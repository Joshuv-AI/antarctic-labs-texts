Impact of simulated glasses noise on facial emotion recognition with deep learning models
Authors: Deniz Baharvand, Fatemeh Jodati, Kimia Shirini, Sina Samadi Gharehveran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Facial emotion recognition (FER) is one of the main fields of research in image processing and artificial intelligence with applications in human–machine interaction, behavior analysis, and intelligent vision. The RAF DB dataset, a widely used benchmark dataset for emotion recognition, is used for the training and testing of deep learning models. In real application conditions, the facial image may be subjected to visual noise. Large glasses that occlude the eye region of the face are one example of environmental or artificial noise which distorts the visual information. If frames covering a large area of an expressive part of the face, like the eyes, are recognized as noise by the emotion recognition network this may lead to noticeable performance degradation. Artificial occlusion noise, similar to large glasses, was applied to the eye region of the facial images from the RAF DB dataset. The impact of this type of structural noise on the performance of deep-learning-based facial emotion recognition models is analyzed. Three state-of-the-art architectures (ResNet-50, Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16) and Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin T)) are tested to determine their robustness to the noisy application conditions. The objective of the study is to determine and suggest possible methods to counter the decrease in recognition performance caused by the occlusion noise. Experimental results indicate that, within our evaluation setup, transformer-based architectures, especially Swin-T, tend to be more robust to the simulated visual noise, which may be useful for the design of facial emotion recognition models intended for more complex conditions.

Introduction

Human emotions play a crucial role in interpreting behavioral aspects and are pivotal for various applications in behavioral science and AI research1. In the driving context, a driver’s mental state directly impacts their ability to concentrate, make decisions, and react to road situations in a timely manner2. Negative emotions such as anger, fatigue, or stress can significantly affect an individual’s risk perception and response, increasing the potential for human error and traffic accidents3. As a result, reliable and accurate emotion recognition has been an important research direction for the development of intelligent transportation and safety solutions4–7.

Emotion recognition in the real-world scenarios still poses significant challenges8. Partial occlusions of different types are common in driving contexts and include sunglasses, masks, hand gestures, or inconsistent lighting in facial images, concealing important facial regions such as eyes, nose, cheeks, and forehead9. When these key features are occluded, the inference of emotion would be more uncertain10–19. Therefore, the models with noisy inputs should still be applicable to work well, which raises the need for performance evaluation and model improvements in this aspect20–23.

Recent years have seen great progress in deep neural network design24. Powerful deep learning architectures have been developed to extract both local and contextual information from images25. The Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Transformer-based models have shown impressive performance in facial emotion recognition tasks as well as in general visual recognition.

Moreover, the integration of statistical feature reduction methods such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) has been shown to improve model stability and reduce redundancy in high-dimensional data representations, as demonstrated in our previous work on electricity consumption forecasting14. Specifically, ResNet-50, Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16) ,and Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin T) represent three different paradigms of neural networks that jointly model local convolutional features and long-range global relationships8,26.

To simulate the real-world occlusion and bridge the gap with lab-clean conditions27, this paper aims to evaluate and compare the robustness of the three models in an occlusion-resilient testing setting, with a new noisy version of RAF DB dataset, referred to as RAF DB noisy. The partial occlusion of the face is simulated by adding a rectangular area noise in the regions of eyes, nose, cheeks, and a portion of the forehead21,28,29. This specific type of structured noise in rectangular shape is different from the traditional random or pixel-level corruptions and better models the noisy conditions in the real world such as wearing big sunglasses or masks, shadows, or reflections on the face21,30,31. This way, we can simulate a more realistic noisy setting and help drive the research and model improvements towards real-world conditions and needs21.

In this noisy setting, a comparative study is conducted to test the resistance to the rectangular occlusion noise among ResNet-50, Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16), and Swin T. It was found that Transformer-based models, especially Swin T, were more robust and had better feature generalization capabilities under various levels of occlusion noise32,33. The hierarchical attention mechanism in Transformers allowed these models to be more adaptive and focus on the available regions to compensate for the missing facial information34,35.

The method proposed in this paper can be extended to other real-world scenarios where visual occlusion and emotion recognition are both common and important. Such scenarios include public surveillance, remote communications, healthcare settings, human–robot interaction21,36, and others where visual information is often impaired. In these contexts, the accurate recognition of emotional information despite the partial occlusion of the face would be critical for safety, empathy, and user experience37.

In conclusion, this paper provides a standardized variant for emotion recognition under structured occlusion noise and contributes to the development of robust, context-aware affective recognition systems for imperfect visual conditions in practical applications8,38.

Related work

Deep learning and computer vision have brought significant progress to facial emotion recognition (FER) in recent years37,39. CNN architectures, in particular, show great capability in extracting hierarchical spatial features. Yet, real-world FER faces a major hurdle: visual noise and occlusion20,21,32,40,41. Elements like masks, hair, or glasses hide parts of the face, leading to sharp drops in accuracy. To tackle this, researchers have explored various techniques, including attention mechanisms, patch-based encoding, GNNs, and image reconstruction21,30,39,42–48. More recently, Transformer models have proven effective at capturing global semantic dependencies, helping to maintain performance even when faces are heavily covered49.

Eye occlusion from glasses or headsets is especially problematic. Eyes provide critical emotional cues like gaze and muscle contractions, and covering them severely disrupts feature extraction21,30,50–52. While VR studies often compensate for Head-Mounted Displays (HMDs) using 3D modeling53, traditional FER studies show that CNNs struggle heavily against dark lenses and glare21,54–56. Furthermore, Although extensive recent research has focused on lower-face occlusion like medical masks57–59, systematic testing of FER models under controlled, glasses-like eye occlusion is still lacking21,60,61.

Some researchers try to bypass these visual limits by building multimodal systems that incorporate physiological sensors (e.g., EEG, ECG, eye-tracking)62–70. These sensor-based methods improve classification stability but require expensive and intrusive hardware. Because of this limitation, our study focuses purely on non-intrusive, image-based models and investigates how they handle extreme simulated visual noise31,37.

To mimic the effect of large eyewear, we applied a structured, non-dark rectangular mask over the eye region in the RAF-DB dataset21,71. This setup forces models to rely predominantly on the lower face, providing a rigorous test for their generalization skills72. Instead of evaluating just one model, we benchmark diverse architectures: ResNet-50 for local spatial modeling27, ViT-Base/16 for global self-attention25,33,39,73,74, and Swin-T for hierarchical processing75. Making FER models more resilient to occlusion is essential for practical applications across security, industry, healthcare, and robotics27,76–78. While standard ViTs are already used in FER, the specific capacity of Hierarchical Transformers (like Swin-T) to handle structural noise through shifted-window attention has not yet been systematically benchmarked against standard CNNs and ViTs.

Methodology

The proposed evaluation framework is designed to systematically assess the robustness of three distinct deep learning architectures—ResNet-50 (augmented with CBAM), Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16), and Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin-T)—against structured upper-face occlusion25,30,77. As illustrated in the schematic overview in Fig. 1 (which utilizes symbolic royalty-free images for visual demonstration), the methodology follows a unified pipeline: inputs from both clean and noisy datasets undergo identical preprocessing, followed by parallel feature extraction through the selected backbones, and subsequent refinement via shared feature enhancement modules (MultiScaleConv and AttentionFusion) before final classification. This section details the dataset generation, model configurations, and the training strategy employed.

Data

We utilize the Real-world Affective Face Database (RAF-DB)27, containing thousands of in-the-wild facial images categorized into seven basic emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, fear, disgust, and neutral)79. Its high diversity in age, gender, ethnicity, and illumination makes it an ideal baseline for evaluating model generalization under realistic conditions80. To ensure compatibility with our baseline architectures (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, and Swin-T), all images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Standard preprocessing steps, including pixel normalization and data augmentation (random rotation, horizontal flipping, brightness adjustment), were applied to mitigate overfitting33,81. Creation of RAF-DB-noisy

To systematically evaluate model robustness under partial occlusion, we introduced a modified variant of the dataset, termed RAF-DB Noisy27,80. Clean and noisy sets share identical identity labels and train-test splits. For the noisy variant, an artificial rectangular occlusion is applied to the upper facial region (eyes, eyebrows, and nose bridge)82, combined with a white Gaussian noise component (σ = 50). This degradation mimics real-world visual obstructions like reflective eyewear, strong glare, or medical shields, forcing the models to rely heavily on uncovered expressive cues such as the mouth region81. This setup provides a controlled testbed for assessing feature-dependency without altering the affective semantics of the original images. The generated dataset will be publicly released for future occlusion-resistant emotion recognition research83. Sample images are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Model architectures overview

In order to compare robustness performance across different deep learning paradigms, three representative architectures were selected: ResNet-50, Vision Transformer (ViT Base/16), and Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin-T). ResNet-50 represents convolutional neural networks with strong local feature extraction enabled by residual connections25,84. Vision Transformer (ViT Base/16) pioneers global token-based self-attention over image patches, while Swin-T employs a hierarchical architecture with shifted window self-attention to capture local contextual information25,85.

ResNet-50

We adopt ResNet-50 as our primary convolutional baseline due to its effective residual learning mechanism, which mitigates gradient vanishing and facilitates hierarchical feature reuse. To enhance its representational power and spatial focus, a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)25,86,87 is integrated after each residual stage. CBAM’s sequential channel and spatial attention allows the network to dynamically emphasize relevant facial regions while suppressing background distractions or occluded areas88.

Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin-T)

Swin Transformer Tiny (Swin-T)3 introduces a shifted window-based self-attention mechanism, offering a localized and hierarchical alternative to the global attention used in conventional Vision Transformers. This design reduces computational complexity to O(n) and progressively merges patches across four stages (from 4 × 4 to 32 × 32 resolutions). This hierarchical structure is particularly advantageous for our task, as it enhances model robustness against localized facial occlusions33. We adopted the lightweight “Tiny” variant (approx. 28 million parameters, ImageNet-1 K pretrained) to balance accuracy with computational efficiency4.

Following our unified adaptation strategy, the original classification head was replaced with a custom two-layer feedforward network: a 512-unit hidden layer (GELU activation, dropout 0.5) and a 7-unit output layer (dropout 0.3)91. Consistent with the overall methodology, partial fine-tuning and standardized augmentations5,92 were applied to adapt the network to the noise patterns of the RAF-DB dataset93. Emotion detection

To enhance robustness against partial facial occlusions, our emotion detection pipeline incorporates two shared auxiliary modules: MultiScaleConv and AttentionFusion. After extracting features from the respective backbones (ResNet-50 + CBAM, ViT-B/16, and Swin-T), the MultiScaleConv module employs parallel convolutional branches to capture both fine-grained local details and broader contextual cues. Subsequently, the AttentionFusion module adaptively reweights these multi-scale features, emphasizing salient facial regions while suppressing occlusion artifacts.

These modules are introduced before global average pooling, yielding a unified tensor of size [32,510,14,14] across all models84. This shared implementation avoids architecture-specific customizations and ensures a fair comparison of robustness under occlusion noise (noise_std = 50)19,78.

Feature enhancement modules

To enhance robustness against structured occlusion noise, two auxiliary feature enhancement modules, namely MultiScaleConv and AttentionFusion, are incorporated after the backbone feature extractors.

MultiScaleConv Module

The MultiScaleConv module extracts complementary facial representations at multiple spatial resolutions. It consists of three parallel convolutional branches operating on the same input feature map:

The padding ensures that spatial dimensions remain consistent. The outputs of all branches are concatenated along the channel dimension and fused via a final 1 × 1 convolution to produce a unified multi-scale representation.

Experimental analysis

This section presents an evaluation of the proposed FER models—ResNet-50, ViT-Base/16, and Swin-T—under both clean and structured-occlusion scenarios generated on the RAF-DB-Noisy dataset. All experiments were conducted following the standardized three-stage fine-tuning strategy described in Sect. 3, employing AdamW optimizer, label smoothing (0.15), and hybrid augmentation (CutMix + MixUp). During training, a stage-wise mixed data strategy was adopted. Models were first fine-tuned on the clean RAF-DB dataset and subsequently adapted to the noisy RAF-DB dataset to improve robustness against occlusion.

Experimental setup

Experiments were conducted on the Google Colab platform (NVIDIA Tesla T4 16GB VRAM, 12GB RAM) using PyTorch 2.3.0. All input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and augmented with random cropping, horizontal flipping, and color jittering.

To ensure a rigorous and fair comparison, a completely unified training protocol was applied across all architectures94,95. Models were trained for 30 epochs with a batch size of 32 using the AdamW optimizer (weight decay 0.01, uniform initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{5\times\:10}^{-5}$$\end{document}) and a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler. Regularization techniques, including label smoothing (0.15) and CutMix/MixUp augmentations (50% probability per batch), were applied identically. No architecture-specific hyperparameter tuning was performed; thus, all performance variations are strictly attributable to the intrinsic structural differences of the models.

Evaluation was based on Accuracy and Weighted F1-Score, with best checkpoints selected via the highest validation F1-Score. To guarantee statistical reliability under occlusion noise, all experiments were repeated five times using different random seeds. The reported metrics represent the mean performance, accompanied by standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals.

Limitations

While the proposed evaluation framework enables a controlled comparison of model robustness under structured occlusion, and statistical significance was validated across five independent runs, evaluating performance across a broader range of noise intensities remains computationally constrained. Furthermore, we simulated glasses-like noise because large-scale, well-annotated datasets with real facial occlusions are scarce. While this synthetic approach guarantees precise control and reproducibility, real-world interferences such as hands, dynamic lighting, or irregular medical masks are naturally more complex. The base RAF-DB dataset, despite its diversity, may not fully capture the extreme distribution shifts present in uncontrolled, real-world environments.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated CNN- and Transformer-based architectures on the RAF-DB-Noisy dataset to analyze their behavior against regional occlusion and corrupted labels. Our results showed that the Swin-T Tiny architecture achieved the highest Accuracy and F1-Score under both clean and noisy conditions. Interestingly, all tested models experienced a similar performance drop when facing extreme noise, showing a stability decrease of ΔF1 ≈ − 0.11 to − 0.12.

Qualitative analysis revealed that Swin-T Tiny maintains a robust balance between Precision and Recall, yielding per-class AUC values over 0.85. This highlights its strong generalization capability under partial occlusion. In contrast, while ViT-B/16 was effective at global feature extraction, it struggled with local facial details. ResNet-50, on the other hand, remained reliable for recognizing coarser facial patterns. Overall, Hierarchical Transformers like Swin-T provide the best trade-off between feature fidelity and noise robustness for emotion recognition.

To ensure a fair comparison across models with fundamentally different learning dynamics, we applied architecture-aware hyperparameter tuning within a unified training protocol. A key limitation of our work, however, is the use of synthetic occlusion. We simulated glasses-like noise because large-scale, well-annotated datasets with real facial occlusions are scarce. While this approach guarantees precise control and reproducibility, real-world interferences—such as hands, medical masks, or dynamic lighting—are naturally more complex. Additionally, the base RAF-DB dataset may not fully capture the extreme diversity of uncontrolled environments.

Moving forward, we plan to address these limitations through two strategic directions. First, since severe visual occlusion inevitably destroys crucial facial cues, integrating non-visual data (such as audio or physiological signals via Multi-modal Data Fusion) is a logical step to compensate for the missing information. Second, to improve training stability on datasets like RAF-DB-noisy, we propose exploring an Occlusion Curriculum Learning strategy. Training models on progressively increasing noise levels, rather than static occlusion, could significantly boost the adaptability and resilience of real-world emotion recognition systems.
