Collagenase Versus Operative Management for Dupuytren Contracture: A Propensity-matched Cohort of 16,888 Patients
Authors: Yousef Tanas, Stephen Chen, Peter Zak, Hamza Bhatti, Samantha Cervantes Valadez, Keyvon Rashidi, Joshua Wang, Philong Nguyen, Sarya Swed, Shari R. Liberman, Anthony Echo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Dupuytren contracture (DC) is a fibroproliferative disorder of the palmar fascia, commonly managed through enzymatic collagenase injections or surgical interventions such as fasciotomy or fasciectomy. This study aims to compare clinical outcomes and complication rates between patients undergoing collagenase injection and those receiving surgical treatment for DC.Methods:Using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a retrospective cohort study was conducted including 8444 patients who underwent collagenase injection and 8444 propensity score–matched patients who received surgical management for DC. Key outcomes assessed included postoperative infection, hematoma, nerve injury, reintervention rates, functional outcomes (occupational therapy utilization), and emergency room visits. Risk differences, risk ratios, and odds ratios (OR) were calculated using risk analysis. Follow-up was 180 days.Results:Collagenase treatment was associated with significantly lower risks of postoperative infection (0.7% versus 1.4%, OR 0.49, P < 0.001) compared to surgery. Nonetheless, collagenase was associated with a significantly higher reintervention rate (31.7% versus 16.2%, OR 2.40, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in iatrogenic nerve injuries (0.2% in both groups), hematoma (0.3% in both groups), functional outcomes (35.9% versus 36.7%, P = 0.284), or emergency room utilization (24.6% versus 24.5%, P = 0.915).Conclusions:Collagenase injection for DC is associated with fewer short-term complications but nearly doubles the risk of reintervention compared to surgical management.

Takeaways

Question: Among patients with Dupuytren contracture, how do complications, reintervention, and healthcare utilization differ between collagenase injection and surgical treatment in real-world practice?

Findings: In a propensity-matched cohort of 16,888 patients from a large database, collagenase was associated with fewer early complications but markedly higher reintervention rates, with similar emergency room and occupational therapy utilization between groups.

Meaning: Both options are effective; collagenase offers lower short-term morbidity in an office-based setting, whereas surgery may yield more durable correction.

INTRODUCTION

Dupuytren contracture (DC) is a benign fibroproliferative disorder of the palmar fascia characterized by nodules and cords that progressively cause flexion contractures of the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints.1 The pathogenesis of this disorder is a mix of genetics and environmental/occupational exposures. Genetic etiology stems from overactivation of the Wnt-signaling pathway, which results in fibroblast proliferation within the dermis and eventually the formation of contractures.1–4 Environmental/occupational exposures, especially vibratory exposure, causes chronic ischemia and inflammation, which promotes fibroblast stimulation to increase production of type 3 collagen for repair.5–8 Normally, type 1 collagen is found in the tissues of the hand; however, in DC, type 1 collagen is replaced by type 3 collagen.9

Currently, no definitive cure for DC exists, as recurrence is possible with any intervention. The goal of treatment is to increase range of motion and grip strength. Current treatment options are divided into invasive and noninvasive procedures. Invasive procedures include open and partial fasciotomy where the diseased fascia is removed. Noninvasive procedures include needle aponeurotomy and collagenase injections. Recently, less invasive treatments such as collagenase injections have gained wider use.10,11 Collagenase clostridium histolyticum (CCH) is an enzymatic treatment injected directly into the Dupuytren cord, followed by a planned manipulation session to rupture the cord and improve extension.11 Compared with operative release, CCH is minimally invasive and may reduce short-term wound-related morbidity, but it may require repeat intervention and does not provide tissue excision. Because real-world comparative safety and early reintervention patterns remain clinically relevant for counseling and shared decision-making, we evaluated short-term outcomes following CCH versus surgical treatment in a large multicenter cohort.

Several randomized trials and meta-analyses have compared collagenase injections with needle fasciotomy and limited fasciectomy, reporting that collagenase injections provide good short-term outcomes but higher recurrence and reintervention rates compared to surgery.12–16 Much of the prior literature has been limited by small sample sizes. Therefore, there is a need for large-scale comparative data that quantifies both short-term complications and long-term durability.

We performed a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, comparing patients who received collagenase injection to a propensity score–matched cohort who underwent surgical treatment for DC. The primary objective was to compare short-term (180 d) postoperative complications following index treatment with CCH versus surgery for DC. Secondary objectives were to compare healthcare utilization within 180 days, including emergency department visits and occupational therapy utilization, and to evaluate early reintervention/reoperation within the same follow-up window. Long-term durability and recurrence are beyond the scope of the present analysis.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a federated electronic health record (EHR) platform containing aggregated, de-identified patient data from over 120 healthcare organizations. This network includes academic medical centers, community hospitals, and outpatient clinics and includes information such as demographics, diagnoses (ICD-9/10), procedures (current procedural terminology [CPT]/healthcare common procedure coding system), medications, laboratory values, and outcomes. All data are de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, so no institutional review board approval was required.

Cohort Definition

Two patient cohorts were identified: the collagenase DC group (patients with ICD code for palmar fascial fibromatosis [Dupuytren] and CPT code 20527 for collagenase injection) and fasciotomy/-ectomy DC group (patients with ICD code for palmar fascial fibromatosis [Dupuytren] and CPT codes 26040, 26045, 26125, 1004628 for fasciotomy and/or CPT codes 26123, 26121, 1014096 for fasciectomy). Patients were included if they had a diagnosis of palmar fascial fibromatosis (Dupuytren) at any time. Procedures included occurred within 6 months before or up to 6 months after diagnosis of DC. This resulted in 8458 patients in the collagenase DC cohort and 18,396 patients in the fasciotomy/-ectomy DC cohort. The database was queried on June 5, 2025.

Index Events and Time Windows

For each cohort, the index event was defined as the first occurrence of the qualifying DC procedure. No end date was specified so all outcomes after the first occurrence of the index event were included. The index event only included events that occurred up to 20 years ago. Therefore, patients whose index event occurred 20+ years ago were excluded. No patients in each cohort were excluded.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included infection following procedure, hematoma, nerve injury, reintervention, functional outcomes (occupational therapy visits), and healthcare utilization (emergency room visits). These outcomes were identified using CPT and ICD-10 codes and assessed within 180 days of the index procedure. Reintervention was defined as any subsequent Dupuytren-related intervention occurring within 180 days of the index procedure, identified using CPT/ICD procedure codes. This included repeat collagenase injections (CPT 20527) and surgical procedures (fasciotomy/fasciectomy CPT codes including 26040, 26045, 26121, 26123, 26125, etc). Functional outcomes/rehabilitation utilization (occupational therapy visits) was assessed as a healthcare utilization outcome. Operation theatre visit counts were used as a proxy for posttreatment rehabilitation needs and resource use; however, operation theatre utilization does not directly measure patient function or range of motion and may be influenced by provider practice patterns and access.

Covariates

Baseline variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, obesity, circulatory system disease, nicotine dependence).

Propensity Score Matching

To minimize confounding, 1:1 propensity score matching was done on baseline demographics, BMI, and comorbidities. After matching, there were 8444 patients in the collagenase DC cohort and 8444 patients in the fasciotomy/-ectomy DC cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean + SD, whereas categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. TriNetX’s “Compare Outcomes-Measures of Association” module was used to calculate absolute risk, risk difference, risk ratio, and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Significance was set to P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Before matching, a total of 26,980 DC patients across 68 healthcare organizations in the TriNetX database were treated with either collagenase injections or surgery. The fasciotomy/-ectomy DC cohort had significantly higher body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus, obesity, and circulatory system disease. After matching, a total of 16,888 DC patients were included in the analysis (8444 patients in each cohort). Both groups were well balanced for all characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, and comorbidities (standardized mean differences < 0.1) (Table 1).

Outcomes

Treatment of DC with collagenase injections was associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative infection (0.7% versus 1.4%, OR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.358–0.666, P < 0.001) compared to surgery. However, there was a significantly higher reintervention rate (31.7% versus 16.2%, OR 2.40, 95% CI: 2.231–2.586, P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in hematoma (~0.3% versus ~0.3%, OR 0.92, 95% CI: 0.513–1.636, P = 0.768), nerve injury (0.2% versus 0.2%, OR 1, 95% CI: 0.489–2.047, P = 1), functional outcomes/rehabilitation utilization (35.9% versus 36.7%, 95% CI: 0.907–1.029, P = 0.284), and emergency room utilization (24.6% versus 24.5%, 95% CI: 0.936–1.077, P = 0.915). DISCUSSION

In this large multicenter cohort of DC patients, treatment with collagenase injections was associated with lower risks of wound complications and infection-related events but higher reintervention rates compared with surgical treatment. These findings are consistent with current literature. Peimer et al’s literature review revealed that the incidence of adverse events (nerve injury, neurapraxia, complex regional pain syndrome, arterial injury) from treatment with CCH was significantly lower than that with treatment with fasciectomy, whereas local events such as skin injury and hematoma were more frequent with CCH.16 Additional transient injection-related adverse events that were not covered in this study, including peripheral edema, extremity pain, injection site pain, hemorrhage and swelling, tenderness, pruritis, and lymphadenopathy were not reported.12,16 Similarly, Smeraglia’s systematic review found that CCH treatment provided better outcomes with lower complications and side effects than fasciectomy.17 However, multiple studies have interestingly found that fasciectomy treatment was associated with lower rates of recurrence, suggesting better efficacy compared to CCH.12,18–20 Although our study evaluates early reintervention within 180 days rather than true long-term recurrence, these studies, together with our findings, suggest that collagenase injections may offer favorable short-term safety but lower long-term durability compared with surgical treatment.

Collagenase injections, CCH, contain collagenase enzymes that cleave collagen types I and III in Dupuytren pathological cords, weakening the cord so it can be mechanically disrupted through the finger extension procedure following injection.21 These injections are minimally invasive, office-based procedures that avoid surgical incisions and wide tissue dissection, which explains the lower rates of infection, hematoma, and nerve injury.16,22–24 However, because these injections do not remove the diseased tissue (as in fasciotomy), they have higher recurrence and reintervention rates.25–27 Functional outcomes are often similar because both collagenase injections and surgical treatment restore extension quickly—injections give faster return to function but over time, recurrence after injection can erode advantage and thus allow surgery to have better long-term hand health.15 Healthcare utilization may also be similar because most adverse events for both treatment options are managed outpatient or are minor.28 Furthermore, multiple collagenase injections may result in dense scarring which can make subsequent fasciotomy difficult and increases operative times.

Strengths of this study include a large sample size, inclusion of diverse healthcare settings, and robust matching. Potential limitations include the retrospective nature of the study design, reliance on administrative coding, and potential unmeasured confounders (surgeon experience, operative time). Specifically, contracture severity (eg, degree of metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal flexion contracture, number of involved digits, Tubiana stage, and primary versus recurrent disease) is not reliably captured in TriNetX and therefore could not be directly adjusted for, introducing potential residual confounding by indication. Also, our “reintervention” endpoint reflects subsequent reintervention identified by procedural codes and may include repeat collagenase or crossover to surgery; digit-level laterality and whether treatment was staged versus for persistence/recurrence cannot be determined. Furthermore, tendon rupture may be under-captured or inconsistently coded in administrative/EHR datasets; the absence of this endpoint may underestimate collagenase-related procedure-specific harms. It is also important to note that the operative cohort includes a range of procedures (eg, fasciotomy and fasciectomy) as captured by CPT codes. This heterogeneity may influence complication rates; therefore, our results should be interpreted as collagenase versus operative management in aggregate, rather than comparison with a single standardized surgical technique. In addition, outcomes in TriNetX are identified through coded clinical events rather than direct chart-level adjudication, which may result in under-capture or misclassification of certain complications. The 180-day follow-up window was intentionally selected to evaluate early comparative safety, healthcare utilization, and short-term reintervention after index treatment; however, this timeframe does not permit robust assessment of longer-term recurrence, durability, or late complications. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting short-term posttreatment outcomes rather than definitive long-term comparative effectiveness. Future studies should aim to analyze long-term outcomes at various time points while adjusting for major confounders (such as contracture severity) to better elucidate efficacy and sustainability.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large real-world cohort, collagenase injection was associated with lower short-term wound-related morbidity but higher early reintervention rates compared with operative management for DC. Given the limitations of EHR-based data, including inability to directly account for baseline contracture severity and digit-level disease characteristics, these findings should be interpreted as associative short-term comparative outcomes that may inform patient counseling and shared decision-making.
