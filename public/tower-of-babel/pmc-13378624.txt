Real‐World Unmet Treatment Needs for Patients With Haemophilia: Results From the Global Adelphi Disease Specific Programme Database
Authors: Víctor Jiménez Yuste, Cléa Percier, Shwetha Prashanth, Nathan Ball, Ella Morton, Giancarlo Castaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionProphylactic factor replacement and non‐factor therapy has improved bleed prevention and clinical outcomes for people with haemophilia (PwH). The remaining burdens faced by PwH in real‐world settings, despite availability of newer therapies, are not well documented.AimWe aimed to describe the haemophilia patient population, assess clinical and humanistic burden of disease, and identify unmest needs associated with current treatments.MethodsData were drawn from the Adelphi Real World Haemophilia Disease Specific Programme, a cross‐sectional survey of haemophilia‐treating physicians, their patients with haemophilia A (HA) or B (HB), and caregivers. Data were collected in multiple countries from July 2023 to October 2024. Physicians reported patient demographics, disease characteristics, treatment details, bleeding episodes and joint health status. Patients and caregivers reported health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). Analyses were descriptive.ResultsOverall, 283 physicians reported on 2227 patients, with 67% of PwH aged 18–49 years. Prophylactic compliance was high: 94% of patients were either fairly or fully compliant (taking 50%–‍80% or >80% of prescribed dose, respectively). However, physicians were not completely satisfied with prophylaxis for 56% of patients with HA and 59% with HB, citing concerns about administration methods, dosing schedules, and efficacy.Joint problems were experienced by 49% of patients; 57% reported ≥1 bleed annually despite prophylaxis. HRQoL impairment was reported by patients on all types of prophylaxis.ConclusionDespite current treatment, many PwH continue to experience limitations to their daily activities, productivity, and physical independence. A patient‐centred approach is needed to ensure clinical effectiveness can be balanced with lifestyle priorities.Keywords: haemophilia, outcomes, prophylaxis, real‐world, treatment burden, treatment satisfaction

Introduction

Prophylactic factor replacement and non‐factor therapies (NFTs) are now standard care for people with haemophilia (PwH), reducing bleeding episodes and improving joint health [1, 2]. Despite its efficacy for preventing bleeding, standard half‐life (SHL) factor replacement therapy requires frequent infusions, posing adherence challenges and affecting health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) [3]. Extended half‐life (EHL) products and NFTs represent promising alternatives by reducing administration frequency and therefore burden [4, 5]. Nevertheless, PwH still face clinical challenges, including joint bleeds and arthropathy, treatment burden, including medication storage and preparation, and venous access issues from recurrent infusions [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. These factors affect HRQoL, a multifaceted concept influenced in haemophilia by pain management, treatment type, age‐related factors, inhibitor status, and mental health [9, 10]. Therapeutic goals now emphasise holistic, patient‐centred well‐being and a ‘haemophilia‐free mindset’ [11]. Achieving this requires understanding how current therapies influence HRQoL.

Following global treatment advancements, real‐world data on residual burdens on patients remain limited. This paper describes the haemophilia patient population, characterises the clinical and humanistic disease burden, and identifies unmet needs in today's therapeutic landscape using the multinational Adelphi Disease Specific Programme (DSP) survey.

Methods

Survey Design

Data were drawn from the Adelphi Real World Haemophilia DSP, a cross‐sectional survey, with elements of retrospective data collection, of physicians and their consulting patients with haemophilia (or the caregivers of patients <18 years). Data were collected in France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States of America (USA) from July 2023 to October 2024. Physicians completed patient record forms (PRFs) for 10 consecutively consulting patients. Patients, or their caregivers, voluntarily completed a self‐completion form, independently of the physician.

Population and Sample

A geographically representative sample of physicians was recruited by local fieldwork agencies and screened for eligibility using predefined selection criteria. Physicians with a primary specialty as haematologist, haematologist‐oncologist, paediatric haematologist or paediatric haematologist‐oncologist were eligible if they were involved in treatment decisions for at least five patients with HA or HB (with or without factor VIII/factor IX inhibitors) in a typical month. To meet specific research aims beyond the scope of this manuscript, in addition to the main sample, an oversample of 84 patients treated with efanesoctocog alfa and 181 patients with nonacog beta pegol was included.

Patients diagnosed with HA or HB of any age were eligible if they were male, consulted with an eligible physician during the study period, and had a physician‐confirmed diagnosis of moderate (2%–5% baseline clotting factor [BCF]), moderately severe (1% ≤ 2% BCF), or severe (<1% BCF) HA or HB.

Data Collection

The DSP included data collected from the following four physician‐, patient‐ and caregiver‐reported forms:

Physician survey: Captured physician characteristics and overall treatment prescribing habits.

PRFs were completed for each individual patient.

Physician‐completed PRFs: Captured patient demographics, disease characteristics, treatments, compliance, treatment satisfaction, bleeding episodes, joint health status and quality of life (QoL), including changes in employment and activity due to haemophilia, use of aids due to haemophilia‐related joint problems, and other symptoms experienced. PRFs were completed for each individual patient.

Patient self‐completion forms (PSCs): Captured treatment details, and disease‐associated difficulties. Patients completed the Haem‐A‐QoL, a validated, disease‐specific, patient‐reported outcome measure assessing HRQoL in the 4 weeks prior to data collection across 10 domains: physical health, emotional well‐being, self‐perception, sports and leisure, work and school, dealing with haemophilia, treatment, future outlook, family planning and partnership and sexuality. Scores per question range from 1 to 5, with responses linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale for each domain, and higher scores indicating greater HRQoL impairment. Domain scores are averaged, and an overall score calculated, with higher total scores indicating worse overall HRQoL. Definitions

Prophylaxis (regular preventive therapy) was ascertained by physicians based on their clinical judgement. Target joints were defined as >4 bleeding episodes in a single joint within any 6‐month period, based on physician assessment. Bleeding episodes were defined as the total number of bleeds experienced, with concurrent bleeds in different anatomical sites counted as separate episodes. Joint pain was categorised as patients experiencing acute pain only, chronic pain only, both, or neither for specified joints.

Treatment satisfaction was assessed independently within PRF and PSC using structured Likert‐type categorical scales (e.g. dissatisfied to completely satisfied). Physicians were asked: ‘Please indicate your overall satisfaction with the current treatment in terms of how well it manages the patient's haemophilia’. If the response was anything other than completely satisfied, further questions were asked to elucidate the reason. Patients were asked: (a) “What is your overall satisfaction with your current medication?” and (b) ‘How satisfied do you feel with your medication's ability to control/prevent bleeds?’.

Treatment compliance was physician‐reported in the PRF and categorised according to the clinical assessment of the proportion of prescribed doses taken by the patient. Compliance was classified as: poorly (<50% of prescribed doses taken), fairly (50%–80%), and fully compliant (>80%).

Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted for the total population and subgroups stratified by age (0–11, 12–17, 18–30, 31–49, 50+ years) and prophylactic therapy (SHL and EHL factor replacement and NFT). Categorical data are reported as frequency and percentage of participants in each category. Continuous and count variables are presented as mean, standard deviation (SD) and range. Age subgroup cut‐offs were selected to define groups of children, adolescents, younger adults and older adults, with distributions reported as n, %. Unavailable data options included ‘don't know’ (online) or leaving blank (paper); missing data were not imputed so the base can vary between variables and is reported where appropriate.

Ethics

The DSP was performed in accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection.

Data were collected in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines and did not require ethics committee approval. Survey materials were reviewed by the Pearl Institutional Review Board (IRB ID 2023‐0209) and granted ethical exemption.

Results

Participant Recruitment and Geographical Distribution

A total of 283 physicians provided data via PRFs for 2227 patients (1596 for PwHA, 631 for PwHB). From the total number of physicians completing PRFs, 3% of physicians were from Japan (0% of PRFs completed for PwHA/7% of PRFs completed for PwHB), 7% from UK (10%/12%), 8% from France (12%/7%), 11% from both Germany (9%/17%) and Italy (11%/14%), 12% from both India (12%/10%) and KSA (12%/10%), 13% from Spain (9%/11%) and 23% from USA (24%/12%).

Overall, patient‐reported data were collected from 487 patients who completed PSCs (22% of patients for whom a PRF was completed), and 137 caregivers completed a CSC (Table S1). Of all patients completing a PSC, 0.2% originated from Japan, 3% were from Spain, 6% were from Italy, 8% were from USA, 9% were from France, 22% each were from India and KSA, and 29% were from Germany. Of all caregivers completing a CSC, 0.7% were from Spain, 2% were from Italy, 3% were from USA, 9% were from Germany, 22% were from France, 30% were from KSA and 33% were from India. Patients with inhibitors comprised 7% of the sample and were included to reflect real‐world heterogeneity; separate analyses were not powered. Therefore, unless otherwise stated, results for PwHA and PwHB include those with and without inhibitors.

Physician Demographics

The physician cohort comprised 55% haematologists, 34% haematologist‐oncologists, 6% paediatric haematologist‐oncologists, and 5% paediatric haematologists. Physicians treated a mean (SD) of 66 (86) PwHA and 37 (66) PwHB. Physicians saw a mean (SD) 58% (44) of their patients in academic hospitals and a mean (SD) 23% (37) of patients in a community hospital.

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics From PRF

Mean (SD) age of patients was 27.1 (15.2) years and 83% were White (Table 1). Most patients (58%) were in employed; 30% were students. According to BCF results, 39% had moderate disease, 24% had moderately severe disease and 37% had severe disease.

Treatment Usage and Satisfaction From PRF

Treatment Summary

Among PwHA who were prescribed a single prophylactic (n = 1418), 39% were prescribed SHL, 34% EHL factor replacement, 21% NFT, and 6% other prophylaxis. For PwHB prescribed a single prophylactic (n = 562), 67% received EHL, 29% SHL factor replacement, and 4% other therapies (Table 1). NFT was not available for PwHB at the time of this survey. Treatment usage categorised by dosing frequencies, where reported, are shown in Table S2.

Compliance With Prophylactic Treatment From PRF

Overall, 94% of patients were fairly compliant or fully compliant with prophylactic medication (94% of PwHA and 96% of PwHB).

Treatment Satisfaction From PRF

Overall, physicians reported being satisfied or completely satisfied with prophylaxis treatment for most patients (∼90%; Figure 1A). Physicians indicated that they were not completely satisfied with prophylaxis treatment for 57% of PwH, 56% of PwHA and 59% of PwHB. Physicians of PwHA were not completely satisfied with SHL factor replacement for 66% of patients, EHL factor replacement for 56% of patients and NFT for 30% of patients. Physicians of PwHB were not completely satisfied with SHL factor replacement for 69% of patients and EHL factor replacement for 54% of patients. Physician satisfaction by prophylaxis and disease severity is shown in Figures 1 and S1, respectively.

Clinical Burden

Bleeding Episodes From PRF

Among patients on prophylaxis, 57% reported ≥1 bleed in the past year, with the highest reported proportion in the 12–17‐year age group (Figure 2). Across all age groups, the majority of patients reported their most recent bleed prior to the survey as spontaneous (58% of PwHA, 57% of PwHB) rather than traumatic (43% of both PwHA and PwHB). The proportion of PwHA and PwHB having ≥1 bleed appeared lower with increasing disease severity, although a numerically greater proportion of these bleeds required factor treatment. Of the 549 PwHA on SHL factor replacement, 332 (60%) experienced ≥1 bleed in the previous year, with 86% of bleeds requiring factor treatment. Of 485 PwHA on EHL factor replacement, 289 (60%) experienced ≥1 bleed with 79% of bleeds requiring factor treatment. Of the 294 PwHA on NFT, 113 (38%) experienced ≥1 bleed with 84% of bleeds requiring factor treatment. Of the 163 PwHB on SHL factor replacement, 112 (69%) experienced ≥1 bleed, with 83% of bleeds requiring factor treatment. Of the 375 patients on EHL factor replacement, 186 patients (50%) experienced ≥1 bleed, with 79% of bleeds requiring factor treatment. Bleed data by country are presented in Table S3.

Joint Health From PRF

Of all PwH, 49% experienced joint problems, more commonly reported in patients >30 years of age (60%) than those aged ≤30 years (45%). Target joints were reported by physicians for 40% of their PwHA and 34% of PwHB receiving a single prophylactic, with knees, ankles and elbows being the most common. Among all PwHA receiving a single prophylactic agent, 19% received a SHL factor replacement and had reported joint pain, 19% received an EHL factor replacement and had reported joint pain, and 10% received an NFT and had reported joint pain. For PwHB, 16% received a SHL and had reported joint pain, and 28% received an EHL factor replacement and had reported joint pain (Table 3).

The Haem‐A‐QoL results indicated the highest reported impairment in the sports and leisure domain (Figure 3). Impairment levels across domains were numerically similar between PwHA and PwHB regardless of SHL or EHL prophylactic therapy class. Scores for physical health and feeling were numerically lower in PwHA receiving NFT and PwHB receiving EHL factor replacement.

WPAI and Physical Activity From PSC

WPAI results indicated PwHA receiving NFT experienced the lowest levels of work and activity impairment due to haemophilia (Figure 4). The percentage of overall work impairment was (mean [SD]) 21.7% (16.2%) for patients receiving NFT compared with 44.8% (26.9%) for those on SHL factor replacement and 54.2% (24.1%) for those on EHL factor replacement. Activity impairment (mean [SD]) for those on NFT/SHL/EHL was 23.0% (21.0%)/40.8% (22.8%)/46.0% (22.8%). PwHB receiving SHL products reported numerically higher impairment of work and activity than those on EHL products, with overall work impairment (mean [SD]) 55.4% (24.6%) versus 37.6% (27.6%), and activity impairment 46.1% (17.7%) versus 37.8% (20.9%), respectively.

Caregiver Information

Overall, 57% of PwHA required a caregiver, with 55% on SHL factor replacement, 60% on EHL factor replacement, and 55% on NFT needing assistance (Table S5). Similarly, 60% of PwHB on SHL factor replacement and 50% on EHL factor replacement required a caregiver. The most common reasons PwH provided for requiring a caregiver were medication management (51%) and transportation (41%).

Discussion

This large, multinational, cross‐sectional survey of physicians included data from >2200 PwH receiving prophylaxis, highlighting real‐world clinical and humanistic burdens. PwHA most commonly received SHL factor replacement, with a fifth receiving NFT, whereas most PwHB received EHL factor replacement. Despite this, 57% reported ≥1 bleed annually, half experienced joint problems, and many reported impairment in QoL and physical activity. Physicians were mostly satisfied or completely satisfied with prophylaxis for the majority of their patients; however, complete satisfaction was less common with physicians, indicating that they were not completely satisfied with prophylaxis for over half of their patients. Administration/dosing and lack of efficacy were cited as the most common issues with SHL and EHL factor replacement. This aligns with an Italian survey that identified administration, frequency of administration, and methods of storage as issues [13]. Differences in administration frequency between SHL/EHL factor replacement and NFT (multiple doses weekly vs. every 1–4 weeks) and administration route (intravenous vs. subcutaneous) may contribute to variability in satisfaction between treatment classes. Given some physicians were not completely satisfied with NFT, further efforts are suggested to develop effective treatments for breakthrough bleeds with more patient‐friendly administration and dosing regimens [14]. Physicians reported complete satisfaction with treatment for a numerically higher proportion of PwHB receiving EHL compared with SHL factor replacement. When physicians were not completely satisfied with prophylaxis, administration/dosing and lack of efficacy were the most common reasons cited. Although prophylaxis has improved patient outcomes, challenges including inhibitor development, breakthrough bleeding, and frequent intravenous infusions persist, likely contributing to a lack of complete satisfaction. The development of longer‐acting therapies and alternative delivery methods, for example, subcutaneous injections, has improved treatment satisfaction for PwH [5, 16]; however, opportunities remain to further optimise treatment regimens to address residual clinical burden. Ongoing bleeding episodes, joint problems, and work and physical activity impairment suggest that clinical burden on PwH persists despite prophylaxis. Most patients continued to experience at least one bleed per year, notably patients with inhibitors, among whom higher bleed frequencies were observed descriptively (PwHA: 57% without inhibitors vs. 74% with inhibitors; PwHB: 55% without inhibitors vs. 90% with inhibitors). This indicates that available therapies reduce, but do not eliminate, bleeding risk, illustrating the need for treatments that can prevent bleeds in more patients. This is particularly true for PwHB with inhibitors, who have limited treatment options; however, inhibitor subgroup analyses were not powered and should be interpreted with caution. Although mean bleed rates were numerically highest among PwHA and PwHB aged 12–17 years, across age groups, the majority of bleeds were spontaneous rather than traumatic. The persistence of spontaneous bleeding episodes while receiving prophylaxis highlights a therapeutic gap. Importantly, even in the USA – where access to the most advanced therapies is high [17] – 48% of patients on prophylaxis still reported at least one bleed in the past year, with 78% of all bleeds requiring factor treatment, highlighting the global nature of this residual burden (Table S4).

Joint problems were experienced irrespective of prophylaxis type. Almost half of the overall population receiving prophylaxis reported joint problems, ranging from 35% in the 0–11‐year age group to 62% in the 31–49‐year age group. Joint problems in older patients likely result from historical ineffective haemostasis when prophylaxis or more effective EHL factor replacement or NFT was unavailable. Chronic joint pain was reported regardless of therapy class. Many patients experienced a combination of acute and chronic pain. These findings likely reflect cumulative damage from earlier treatment eras and underscore limitations of current strategies for preventing musculoskeletal complications. The coexistence of high overall physician satisfaction with persistent bleeding and joint morbidity suggests that current prophylaxis regimens are generally effective but may not fully eliminate disease burden in all patients.

QoL outcomes suggest PwH have a poor QoL irrespective of prophylactic type. Haem‐A‐QoL results showed numerically lower scores for physical health and well‐being/feeling among PwHA receiving NFT, consistent with less reported impairment in these domains. However, more remains to be done for patients to achieve the holistic benefits of haemostatic treatment and maintain an active lifestyle. Approximately a third of all PwH reduced their level of physical activity and avoided playing contact sports to prevent bleeding episodes. For PwHA this pattern appeared similar across treatment classes. WPAI data indicated numerically lower activity impairment and greater work engagement ability in patients receiving NFT compared with those on SHL/EHL factor replacement. However, many still avoided physical activities, particularly those involving higher risk, possibly due to a lack of confidence in treatment efficacy. Avoidance of contact sports and reduced physical activity remain common, suggesting persistent fear of bleeding. Given the benefits of physical activity on functional ability and mental health, these self‐imposed limitations could lead to long‐term consequences for both [18]. Behaviour change towards increased physical activity requires clinician support through individualised advice for PwH [19]. In the USA, almost a fifth of PwH receiving prophylaxis decreased their level of physical activity and up to a quarter avoided playing contact sports (Table S4), again revealing a persistent burden despite treatment advances and availability. Although standards of haemophilia care vary globally, inclusion of countries with differing healthcare systems provides insight into the breadth of real‐world experiences and highlights persistent unmet needs that transcend individual healthcare contexts. The observation of residual bleeding, joint morbidity, and activity limitations across diverse settings suggests that challenges remain, even in regions with access to advanced therapies.

Caregivers provide substantial support to PwH across all age groups and treatment classes, particularly in managing medications. The development of treatment regimens that can be more easily incorporated into patients’ lives may help alleviate caregiver burden and enable patients to be more independent.

Study Limitations

The DSP captures real‐world consulting patterns by collecting data from physicians about their patients under routine clinical care, as well as data from PwH and their caregivers. As such, the study population reflects the spectrum of patients with moderate or severe disease actively consulting physicians, rather than a preselected cohort that may over‐represent individuals with ongoing clinical issues compared with the broader haemophilia population. As with any survey‐based research, the findings may be subject to responder and recall bias and rely on accurate survey completion and interpretation across multiple languages and settings.

The multinational design included patients from nine countries with differing healthcare systems and treatment access; as heterogeneity across countries was not formally accounted for, results should not be interpreted as reflecting uniform standards of care. Comparisons between treatment classes should also be interpreted with caution, because observed differences may be influenced by treatment dosing regimens and underlying patient characteristics, including age, disease severity, inhibitor status, and country of residence. In addition, the targeted oversampling of patients receiving efanesoctocog alfa and nonacog beta pegol may affect treatment class distributions and any descriptive comparisons. Analyses of inhibitor subgroups were limited by the relatively small number of physicians reporting patients having inhibitors.

Treatment compliance was assessed according to physician judgement rather than objective adherence monitoring, which may introduce variability adherence estimates. Analyses were descriptive without formal statistical testing; therefore, observed differences between groups should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.

Despite these limitations, this large, multinational survey provides a comprehensive real‐world perspective incorporating the physician, patient, and caregiver view, enabling comparison between clinical assessments and lived experience. These findings highlight persistent unmet needs and may help inform strategies to improve education, awareness, and access to evolving therapeutic options for people with haemophilia.

Taking into consideration these real‐world differences and limitations, patient associations and clinicians should advocate for implementation of learning courses and local journeys to promote knowledge about haemophilia, inherited bleeding disorders and modern therapeutic options. Collaboration with local authorities should be fostered to increase awareness about the improved treatment options available and how to include them in the therapeutic armamentarium.

Conclusions

Although current therapies have reduced bleeding and improved QoL, many patients continue to experience limitations to their daily activities, productivity, and physical independence. These findings also underscore a key compromise for PwH, namely the trade‐off between realising the benefits of prophylaxis and managing treatment burden. Balancing clinical effectiveness with lifestyle priorities requires patient‐centred care incorporating open communication about treatment preferences and shared decision‐making. Healthcare providers, researchers and policymakers should prioritise developing therapies and implementing strategies to improve global access to effective prophylactic treatment, aiming for a future where patients can live confidently with a ‘haemophilia‐free mindset’.
