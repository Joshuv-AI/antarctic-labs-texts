Supine‐Position Shoulder Arthroscopy Using the Anterior Portal as the Initial Approach
Authors: Weidong Wang, Yingbiao Wang, Sen Li, Mu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional shoulder arthroscopy mostly adopts the beach chair position or lateral decubitus position. Beach chair position is associated with the risk of cerebral hypoperfusion due to the vertical distance between the carotid artery and the heart, whereas lateral decubitus may induce brachial plexus palsy caused by excessive traction. Both positions demand special operating tables, which raises equipment costs and complicates anesthetic management. We describe supine‐position shoulder arthroscopy using the anterior portal as the initial approach. The technical procedure is carried out in a supine position throughout the whole process, with relevant operations implemented via the anterior portal approach. This technique is safe, efficient, and easy to promote, which provides a reliable alternative to traditional surgical positions for shoulder arthroscopy.

Shoulder arthroscopy is a key modality for the diagnosis and treatment of shoulder disorders. The conventional positions are beach chair position (BCP) and lateral decubitus position (LD). Nevertheless, the potential risk of cerebral hypoperfusion in BCP 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 and brachial plexus traction injury in LD 5 , 6 cannot be ignored. Moreover, the need for special tables for both positions limits their application in primary hospitals. In 2016, Lamsam et al. 7 first reported supine shoulder arthroscopy, and in 2021, the Housset team 8 extended its use to rotator cuff repair; however, both adopted the traditional posterior portal as the initial approach. Since July 2025, our team has implemented the “anterior portal‐first” supine shoulder arthroscopy technique internationally. By optimizing patient positioning, traction methods, and portal selection strategies, we have established a standardized and reproducible operational protocol. This article aims to detail the patient positioning, portal selection, anesthetic coordination, and anatomical‐biomechanical basis of this technique, as well as evaluate its early clinical outcomes.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Standard Patient Positioning

Basic Posture

The patient lies supine on a standard operating table without a pillow, with the head slightly turned 20° toward the unaffected side. Adjust the patient's body to form a 15° to 20° angle with the table axis, and shift the upper body toward the operative side so that the affected scapula extends approximately 5 cm beyond the table edge. Ensure the head is properly supported and stabilized to avoid suspension (Video 1, Section 1).

Position Maintenance

Wrap the forearm (above the elbow) with 3 pieces of integrated medical surgical film with a water bag (3L brand). Twist the water bag end into a strap and secure it to an abduction frame to maintain the shoulder in 30° to 40° of forward flexion and 30° to 40° of abduction 9 , 10 (Figure 1A,B).

Traction Protocol (Nonmandatory)

A traction rope may be attached to maintain a traction weight of 3 kg (Figure 1A). Dynamic traction can be applied to widen the subacromial and glenohumeral joint spaces, with the traction weight typically controlled between 3 and 6 kg. 9 , 10 To prevent nerve injury, it is recommended to release traction every 30 min during surgery. 9

Portal Selection and Operational Techniques

Original Anterior Portal‐First Observation Strategy

Localization

Use the coracoid process, short/long heads of the biceps tendon, and the soft spot of the coracoacromial ligament as core anatomical landmarks (Figures 2 and 3, Video 1, Section 2).

The anterior portal is the initial approach, as indicated by the black arrow. This portal is located in the triangular space bounded by the coracoacromial ligament, the long head of the biceps tendon and the coracohumeral ligament. 1, coracoclavicular ligament; 2, long head of biceps tendon; 3, coracobrachialis ligament; 4, short head of biceps tendon; 5, anterior observation approach interval.

Surface marking of the right shoulder: 1, coracoid process; 2, short head of biceps tendon; 3, soft spot interval (anterior portal); 4, long head of biceps tendon; 5, anterolateral portal (2 cm medial and 4 cm lateral to the anterior angle of the acromion); 6, lateral portal (3 cm lateral to the midpoint of the lateral border of the acromion), with the posterolateral portal located 3 cm to its posterior side; 7, anterior angular portal of the acromion.

Establishment

Insert a 14‐gauge lumbar puncture needle vertically through the preset “soft spot” into the glenohumeral joint. Once the glenoid fossa is palpated, make a skin incision (Figure 4), and then use straight forceps for blunt dissection to the glenoid fossa (Figure 5). Avoid direct incision into the joint capsule with a scalpel to protect surrounding tendons and ligaments.

A 14‐gauge lumbar puncture needle is used to vertically pass through the preset “soft spot” to enter the glenohumeral joint. After sensing the glenoid cavity, a skin incision of less than 1 cm is made, ensuring that only the skin is incised and avoiding damage to the subcutaneous ligaments.

After skin incision at the anterior portal of the left shoulder, a straight hemostat is used to perform a blunt puncture into the glenoid cavity. A sense of breakthrough is felt when penetrating the joint, and the glenoid fossa can then be palpated.

Dynamic Switching of Multiple Portals

Routine portals include the anterior portal, acromial anterior angular portal, anterolateral portal, lateral portal, and posterolateral portal. Depending on surgical needs, 2 to 4 incisions are created (Figure 3), and the arthroscope and instruments can be flexibly switched between these portals during surgery (Figure 6A,B, Video 1, Sections 3 and 4). This technique significantly reduces reliance on the traditional posterior portal which was often replaced by the posterolateral portal.

Anesthetic Coordination Protocol

Ultrasound‐guided interscalene brachial plexus block combined with tracheal intubation general anesthesia is used. Closely monitor vital signs during surgery: maintain systolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg and mean arterial pressure between 70 and 80 mmHg, 11 , 12 and ensure oxygen saturation >95% at all times.

DISCUSSION

This technical note describes a supine‐position shoulder arthroscopy technique using the anterior portal as the initial approach. Through optimized postural biomechanics, it integrates surgical safety, efficiency, and adequate surgical field exposure. A core advantage lies in avoiding reliance on specialized operating tables (required for BCP and LD): it uses a standard table, completes patient positioning in 3 to 5 min, and lowers technical access thresholds for primary hospitals. Additionally, the supine position aligns the heart and carotid artery horizontally, eliminating BCP‐related cerebral hypoperfusion risk; 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 it also simplifies airway management and enables accurate blood pressure monitoring, supporting controlled hypotension to improve surgical field clarity (Table 1).

In terms of nerve protection and rehabilitation, the technique adopts 3 kg controllable lightweight traction (with surgical film fixation) to maintain 30° to 40° shoulder flexion/abduction—meeting surgical needs while avoiding LD‐related brachial plexus injury from excessive traction. 6 , 13 Gravity‐driven irrigation fluid flow to the dorsal side also reduces precordial soft tissue edema, preventing postoperative “breast augmentation‐like” swelling and accelerating recovery.

From an ergonomic perspective, surgeons can operate in sitting or standing positions flexibly; the arthroscopic field of view is similar to that of knee arthroscopy (high clinical familiarity, short learning curve), facilitating mastery by beginners. No patient repositioning, redisinfection, or redraping is needed if switching to open surgery or performing bilateral procedures, ensuring surgical continuity (Table 1).

The optimized surgical field exposure stems from precise regulation of scapular and humeral head positions: pillow‐free neck‐chest hyperextension corrects scapular protraction/anteversion, 30° to 40°, shoulder traction induces scapular upward rotation, and gravity causes natural posterior settling of the humeral head. These synergistic effects expand subacromial and glenohumeral anterior spaces (Figures 7 and 8), providing better exposure than traditional positions 7 , 8 (Table 1).

Notably, this technique has limitations and potential risks requiring clinical attention.

Contraindications

Patients with ankylosing spondylitis or kyphosis (unable to lie flat) must be excluded, as supine positioning may increase spinal burden or cause fractures. Reliable head fixation is also mandatory to avoid operative interference (Tables 1 and 2).

Key Points and Pitfalls of the Supine Shoulder Arthroscopy Technique

Pressure Injury Risk

Bony prominences (occiput, scapula, and sacrococcyx) require adequate padding (gel/foam pads); intraoperative monitoring of blood supply to compressed areas is essential (Tables 1 and 2).

Joint Space Exposure Variability

Without sufficient traction, joint spaces may be inadequately expanded. Traction weight can be adjusted to 3 to 6 kg for narrow subacromial spaces, but weights exceeding 6 kg increase brachial plexus injury risk. For obese/muscular patients, 3 kg traction may be insufficient; intraoperative neuroelectrophysiological monitoring is recommended when increasing weight (Tables 1 and 2).

Visual Field Blind Spots

Within the glenohumeral joint, the anterior portal‐first approach may compromise visualization of the intertubercular groove of the long head of the biceps tendon or the insertion site of the supraspinatus muscle; the exposure of the posterior subacromial space is inferior to that of posterior observation in the LD. Combining the posterolateral portal, anterolateral portal, and anterior acromial angle portal can mitigate these blind spots (Tables 1 and 2).

Irrigation Fluid Management

High irrigation pressure or anterior portal leakage may contaminate the sterile field or macerate the back skin. Dynamic pressure adjustment, waterproof surgical towels, and continuous suction are recommended (Table 2).

Operational standardization is critical for promotion: anatomical localization should prioritize the coracoid process, biceps tendons, and coracoacromial ligament; portal establishment must follow the “puncture confirmation‐small incision‐blunt dissection” protocol to avoid scalpel‐induced tissue injury (Table 2).

In conclusion, this supine‐position shoulder arthroscopy technique avoids the inherent risks of traditional positions 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 and offers such merits as no need for specialized equipment, a short learning curve, and rapid postoperative recovery. It serves as a safer, more cost‐effective, and more accessible complementary approach for shoulder arthroscopy positioning and is worthy of routine clinical application (Table 1). Strict adherence to indications/contraindications, standardized operative procedures, and risk control measures can maximize its clinical value (Table 2).
