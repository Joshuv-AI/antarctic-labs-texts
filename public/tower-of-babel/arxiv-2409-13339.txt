On commutators of unipotent matrices of index 2 Journal: Linear Algebra and its Applications
Kennett L. Dela Rosa, Juan Paolo C. Santos

Abstract
A commutator of unipotent matrices of index 2 is a matrix of the form X ​ Y ​ X − 1 ​ Y − 1 XYX^{-1}Y^{-1} , where X X and Y Y are unipotent matrices of index 2, that is, X ≠ I n X\neq I_{n} , Y ≠ I n Y\neq I_{n} , and ( X − I n ) 2 = ( Y − I n ) 2 = 0 n (X-I_{n})^{2}=(Y-I_{n})^{2}=0_{n} . If n > 2 n>2 and 𝔽 \mathbb{F} is a field with | 𝔽 | ≥ 4 |\mathbb{F}|\geq 4 , then it is shown that every n × n n\times n matrix over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} with determinant 1 is a product of at most four commutators of unipotent matrices of index 2. Consequently, every n × n n\times n matrix over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} with determinant 1 is a product of at most eight unipotent matrices of index 2. Conditions on 𝔽 \mathbb{F} are given that improve the upper bound on the commutator factors from four to three or two. The situation for n = 2 n=2 is also considered. This study reveals a connection between factorability into commutators of unipotent matrices and properties of 𝔽 \mathbb{F} such as its characteristic or its set of perfect squares.

Introduction

Throughout this paper,
\mathbb{F}
denotes a field and
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})
its characteristic. Let
M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be the set of all
n\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{F}
,
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be the group of all
n\times n
matrices with nonzero determinant, and
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be the group of all
n\times n
matrices with determinant 1. The
n\times n
identity matrix is denoted by
I_{n}
while the
n\times n
zero matrix is denoted by
0_{n}
. If
X,Y\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, set
[X,Y]:=XYX^{-1}Y^{-1}.

An
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a (
multiplicative
)
commutator
if
A=[X,Y]
for some
X,Y\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. Since the determinant is multiplicative, it follows that

\det(A)=\det(XYX^{-1}Y^{-1})=\det(X)\det(Y)\det(X)^{-1}\det(Y)^{-1}=1
.

Hence, products of commutators are in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. In fact, more is true: Shoda showed in
[
1
]
that for an algebraically closed
\mathbb{F}
, every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a commutator while Thompson in
[
2
]
extended this result for all fields except for the case when
n=2
and
|\mathbb{F}|=2
. Hence, additional conditions are usually imposed on
X
and
Y
, and it is natural to ask in this case if the commutators generate certain matrix groups. For example, commutators of
J
-symmetries were considered in
[
3
]
. In
[
4
]
, it was shown that every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
or
SL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a product of at most two commutators of involutions. The result was extended to arbitrary fields with
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
in
[
5
]
and then in
[
6
]
where the characteristic condition was removed but the assumption is that
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
.

An
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is said to be a
unipotent matrix of index
k
or
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix
if

(A-I_{n})^{k}=0_{n}
and
(A-I_{n})^{k-1}\neq 0_{n}
.

In
[
7
]
, Hou proved that every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a product of at most two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. Since a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices is a product of two
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices (see Proposition
2.3
for a generalization), Hou’s result
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
implies Wang and Wu’s result
[
8
, Theorem 3.5]
, which asserts that every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a product of at most four
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. Recently, Ha and Toan in
[
9
]
extended Hou’s result to matrices in
GL_{n}(\mathbb{H})
where
\mathbb{H}
is the real quaternion division ring. The authors showed that every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{H})
is a product of at most three commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. Products of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices in
GL_{n}(D)
was investigated in
[
10
]
where
D
is a division ring.

The goal of the paper is to generalize the main results in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
for arbitrary fields
\mathbb{F}
. In particular, we prove that every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most four commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices provided that
n>2
and
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
(Theorem
4.8
). For
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, the upper bound can be improved: if
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
, then the upper bound is
three
or
two
depending on
\mathbb{F}
(Theorem
3.14
) but if
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
, then only those in the commutator subgroup
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}
can be considered and in this case,
|\mathbb{F}|-1
is the upper bound (Theorem
3.9
). We also provide conditions on
\mathbb{F}
that improve the upper bound to
three
or
two
(Theorems
5.4
-
5.5
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we prove properties of unipotent matrices and their commutators. In Section
3
, we first make observations for
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
and in Section
4
, we extend some results to the general case
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. In Section
5
, we identify conditions on
\mathbb{F}
that improve the upper bound on the commutator factors in Theorem
4.8
.

2
Unipotent matrices, their commutators, and other preliminaries

Recall that
N\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is said to be a
nilpotent matrix of index
k
if
N^{k}=0_{n}
and
N^{k-1}\neq 0_{n}
. We only consider
n\geq 2
since
\mathbb{F}
has no nilpotent elements and
k\geq 2
since
0_{n}
is the only index
1
nilpotent matrix.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix. Observe that
A=I_{n}+N
for some nilpotent matrix
N
of index
k
. The minimal polynomial of
A
is
m_{A}(x)=(x-1)^{k}
which implies that its characteristic polynomial is
p_{A}(x)=(x-1)^{n}
and
k\leq n
. Since the only root of
p_{A}
is
1\in\mathbb{F}
and its multiplicity in
m_{A}
is
k
, it follows that the Jordan canonical form of
A
is composed of Jordan blocks
J_{s}(1)
corresponding to
1
and the largest Jordan block is
J_{k}(1)
(in general,
J_{s}(\lambda)
denotes the upper triangular
s\times s
Jordan block with eigenvalue
\lambda
). These observations are summarized in the following result where
\bigoplus\limits_{s=1}^{\ell}A_{s}
denotes the direct sum of matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}
and
\sigma(A)
denotes the set of eigenvalues of
A
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix. The Jordan canonical form of
A
is given by
\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}J_{n_{i}}(1)
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k=n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{\ell}>0
. In particular,
k\leq n
,
\sigma(A)=\{1\}
,
\det(A)=1
, and
\textup{tr}(A)=n
.

For
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
and
P\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
,
(PAP^{-1}-I_{n})^{\ell}=P(A-I_{n})^{\ell}P^{-1}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. If
A\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, then
A^{-1}(A-I_{n})=I_{n}-A^{-1}=(A-I_{n})A^{-1}.
Hence,
(A^{-1}-I_{n})^{\ell}=(-A^{-1})^{\ell}(A-I_{n})^{\ell}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. These observations guarantee the next result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
and
P\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. The following are equivalent:

1.

A
is a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix;

2.

A^{-1}
is a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix;

3.

PAP^{-1}
is a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix.

If
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\geq k\geq 2
, then
I_{n-k}\oplus J_{k}(1)
is a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix (only
J_{k}(1)
is present when
n=k
). Hence,
I_{n}=[I_{n-k}\oplus J_{k}(1),I_{n-k}\oplus J_{k}(1)]
. Consequently, the set of commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices is non-empty.

Let
[X,Y]=XYX^{-1}Y^{-1}
where
X,Y
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. By Proposition
2.2
,
YX^{-1}Y^{-1}
is also a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix. The next result is immediate.

Proposition 2.3
.

A commutator of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices is a product of at most two
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices.

Proposition 2.4
.

The identity matrix is the only scalar matrix that is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. Suppose
\lambda I_{n}=[X,Y]
where
X,Y
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. Then
X=[X,Y](YXY^{-1})=Y(\lambda X)Y^{-1}
. By Propositions
2.1
-
2.2
,
\lambda X
is a
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrix and so
\lambda=1
since
1
is the only eigenvalue of
X.

∎

For any
P,X,Y\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, note that
[X,Y]^{-1}=[Y,X]
and
P[X,Y]P^{-1}=[PXP^{-1},PYP^{-1}]
. These imply the following.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
and
P\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. The following are equivalent:

1.

A
is a product of
r
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices;

2.

A^{-1}
is a product of
r
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices;

3.

PAP^{-1}
is a product of
r
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices.

Proof.

Suppose
A=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[X_{i},Y_{i}]
where
X_{i},Y_{i}
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices for all
i\in\{1,...,r\}
. Equivalently,
A^{-1}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[Y_{r+1-i},X_{r+1-i}]
, proving the equivalence of (i) and (ii). For the equivalence of (i) and (iii), observe that
PAP^{-1}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[PX_{i}P^{-1},PY_{i}P^{-1}].
Since for each
i
,
PX_{i}P^{-1}
and
PY_{i}P^{-1}
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices due to Proposition
2.2
, it follows that
PAP^{-1}
is a product of
r
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. On the other hand, if
PAP^{-1}
is a product of
r
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices, then so is
A
by an analogous argument applied to
P^{-1}(PAP^{-1})P=A
.
∎

Suppose
X,Y\in GL_{m}(\mathbb{F})
and
V,W\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. Then
[X\oplus V,Y\oplus W]=[X,Y]\oplus[V,W]
. This observation is used to prove the following.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
A\in SL_{m}(\mathbb{F})
and
B\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be products of
r
and
s
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices, respectively. Then
A\oplus B
is a product of at most
\max\{r,s\}
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. In particular,
A\oplus I_{n}
and
I_{m}\oplus B
are products of at most
r
and
s
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices, respectively.

Proof.

Let
s,r\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
r\leq s
(the argument for
r>s
is analogous). Suppose that
A=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[X_{i},Y_{i}]
and
B=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{s}[V_{j},W_{j}]
where
X_{i},Y_{i}
and
V_{j},W_{j}
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. Observe that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A\oplus B&=&\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[X_{i},Y_{i}]\oplus[V_{i},W_{i}]\right)\left(\prod\limits_{i=r+1}^{s}I_{m}\oplus[V_{i},W_{i}]\right)\\
&=&\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}[X_{i}\oplus V_{i},Y_{i}\oplus W_{i}]\right)\left(\prod\limits_{i=r+1}^{s}[I_{m}\oplus V_{i},I_{m}\oplus,W_{i}]\right)\end{array}

where only the first factor above is present if
s=r
. For all
i
,
X_{i}\oplus V_{i}
,
Y_{i}\oplus W_{i}
,
I_{m}\oplus V_{i}
, and
I_{m}\oplus W_{i}
are
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. Thus,
A\oplus B
is a product of
r+(s-r)=s=\max\{r,s\}
commutators of
{\cal U}_{k}
-matrices. The claim about
A\oplus I_{n}
and
I_{m}\oplus B
follows from the preceding and Proposition
2.4
.
∎

3
Decomposition of matrices in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
into commutators

Suppose
N\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is nonzero and has zero trace. Write
N=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{bmatrix}
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}
not all zero. Since
N^{2}=(a^{2}+bc)I_{2}
, it follows that such
N
is a nilpotent matrix of index
2
if and only if
a^{2}+bc=0
. This characterization implies the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
. Then
A
is a
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrix if and only if

A=\begin{bmatrix}1+a&b\\
c&1-a\end{bmatrix}

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}
are not all zero and
a^{2}+bc=0
.

Proposition
3.1
suggests two types of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices based on the condition
a^{2}+bc=0
. Corresponding to
a=0
, a
type
(
i
) is of the form
\begin{bmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
c&1\end{bmatrix}
where
b,c\neq 0
. Corresponding to
a\neq 0
, a
type
(
ii
) is of the form
\begin{bmatrix}1+a&b\\
c&1-a\end{bmatrix}
where
a,b,c\neq 0
and
a^{2}+bc=0
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
be a nonscalar matrix. Then
A
is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
\textup{tr}(A)=2+\alpha^{2}
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose
A=[X,Y]
where
X,Y
are
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. By Proposition
2.1
,
PXP^{-1}=J_{2}(1)
for some
P\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, and so

PAP^{-1}=P[X,Y]P^{-1}=[PXP^{-1},PYP^{-1}]=[J_{2}(1),PYP^{-1}].

By Propositions
2.2
and
3.1
,
PYP^{-1}
is either a type (i) or type (ii)
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrix. If
PYP^{-1}
is type (ii), i.e.,
PYP^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1+a&b\\
c&1-a\end{bmatrix}
for some
a,b,c\neq 0
such that
a^{2}+bc=0
, then

PAP^{-1}=[J_{2}(1),PYP^{-1}]=\begin{bmatrix}c^{2}+c+ac+1&-(c+ac+a^{2}+2a)\\
c^{2}&-ac-c+1\end{bmatrix}.

Hence,
\textup{tr}(A)=2+c^{2}
where
c\neq 0
. Suppose
PYP^{-1}
is type (i), i.e.,
PYP^{-1}
is either
\begin{bmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
c&1\end{bmatrix}
where
b,c\neq 0
. In the first case,
PAP^{-1}=[J_{2}(1),PYP^{-1}]=I_{2},
and so
A=I_{2}
, contradicting the assumption. In the second case,

PAP^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}c^{2}+c+1&-c\\
c^{2}&-c+1\end{bmatrix}

which implies
\textup{tr}(A)=2+c^{2}
where
c\neq 0
.

Conversely, suppose
\textup{tr}(A)=2+\alpha^{2}
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
. Since
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a nonscalar matrix, the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(x)=p_{A}(x)=x^{2}-\mbox{tr}(A)x+1.
If
m_{A}(x)
is irreducible, then
A
is similar to the companion matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\mbox{tr}(A)\end{bmatrix}
. Otherwise,
m_{A}(x)=(x-\lambda)(x-\lambda^{-1})
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
. By assumption
\alpha\neq 0
, and so
\lambda\neq 1
. If
\lambda=-1,
then
A
is similar to the companion matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&-2\end{bmatrix}
since
A
is a nonscalar matrix. If
\lambda\neq\pm 1
, then
A
is similar to
\textup{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})
; note that
A
is similar to
P\textup{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
where
P=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-\lambda&\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
. In any case,
A
is similar to
B:=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\mbox{tr}(A)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&2+\alpha^{2}\end{bmatrix}
. By Proposition
2.5
, it suffices to show that
B
is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. Direct computations reveal that
B=[X,Y]
where

X=\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha^{2}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\ \textup{and}\ Y=\begin{bmatrix}1-\alpha^{-1}(\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1)&-\alpha^{-1}(\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1)^{2}\\
\alpha^{-1}&1+\alpha^{-1}(\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1)\end{bmatrix}

Note that
X
is a type (i)
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrix while
Y
is either a type (i) or (ii)
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrix (
Y
is a type (i)
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrix if and only if
\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1=0
).

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Any nonscalar matrix
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
that has
\textup{tr}(A)=2
is not a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. In particular,
J_{2}(1)
is not a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then
J_{2}(-1)\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
-1=a^{2}
for some
a\in\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
,
J_{2}(-1)
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
-2=\textup{tr}(J_{2}(-1))=2+\alpha^{2}
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
. Equivalently,
-1=(2^{-1}\alpha)^{2}
since
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
∎

From Proposition
2.4
,
I_{2}
is the
only
scalar matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
which is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. In particular, when
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
,
-I_{2}
is
not
a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. It was observed recently

[
10
, Theorem 8.2]
that writing
-I_{2}
as a product of two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices is equivalent to a sum-of-squares problem in
\mathbb{F}
. A reformulation and an alternative proof are presented here.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then
-I_{2}
is a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
-1=a^{2}+b^{2}
for some nonzero
a,b\in\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
-I_{2}=AB
where
A,B
are commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. Since
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
,
A,B
are nonscalar matrices. By Proposition
2.5
, Theorem
3.2
, and the assumption, there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that

-2-\alpha^{2}=\textup{tr}(-A^{-1})=\textup{tr}(B)=2+\beta^{2}.

If
a:=2^{-1}\alpha
and
b:=2^{-1}\beta
, then
a,b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
and

a^{2}+b^{2}=2^{-2}\alpha^{2}+2^{-2}\beta^{2}=2^{-2}(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})=-1.

Conversely, suppose there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that
a^{2}+b^{2}=-1
. If
\alpha:=2a
and
\beta:=2b
, then
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
and
-I_{2}=AB
where
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
2\alpha^{2}-1&\alpha^{2}\end{bmatrix}
and
B=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha^{2}&1\\
2\alpha^{2}-1&-2\end{bmatrix}
. Since
\textup{tr}(A)=2+\alpha^{2}
and
\textup{tr}(B)=-2-\alpha^{2}=2+\beta^{2}
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
,
A
and
B
are commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Consider
-I_{2}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
where
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
|\mathbb{F}|=5
. Proposition
2.4
and Corollary
3.5
imply that
-I_{2}
is not a commutator nor a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. This does not automatically mean that
-I_{2}
is
not
a product of commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. This issue will be addressed in Proposition
3.13
.

For the meantime, we end this part with a characterization of nonscalar diagonal matrices in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
which are commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
. Then
\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1})
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
a=b^{2}
for some
b\in\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Observe that since
a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
,
\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1})
is a nonscalar matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
.

If
\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1})
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices, then Theorem
3.2
ensures that
a+a^{-1}=\textup{tr}(\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1}))=2+\alpha^{2}
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
. Since
\alpha\neq 0
and
a\neq a^{-1}
,
b:=(a-1)\alpha^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
and

b^{2}=(a-1)^{2}\alpha^{-2}=(a^{2}-2a+1)\alpha^{-2}=a(a+a^{-1}-2)\alpha^{-2}=a.

Conversely, suppose
a=b^{2}
for some
b\in\mathbb{F}
. By assumption on
a
, it is necessary that
b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
. Hence,
\alpha:=b-b^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
and

\mbox{tr}(\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1}))=a+a^{-1}=2+(b-b^{-1})^{2}=2+\alpha^{2}.

By Theorem
3.2
,
\textup{diag}(a,a^{-1})
is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

∎

As we will see, there is stark contrast in the results for fields with
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
and
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
.

3.1
Fields with at most three elements

This subsection involves group theoretic considerations, and so recalling notations and facts is necessary. Let
G
be a group. A subgroup (proper subgroup)
H
of
G
is denoted by
H\leq G
(
H<G
). If
S
is a subset of
G
, then it generates a subgroup which is denoted by
\langle S\rangle.
A normal subgroup
H
of
G
is denoted by
H\trianglelefteq G
while the factor group it defines is denoted by
G/H
. The index of a subgroup
H
in
G
is denoted by
[G:H]
and the normalizer of
H
in
G
is denoted by
N_{G}(H)
. Analogous to the matrix case, a
commutator
in
G
is an element of the form
[x,y]:=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
for some
x,y\in G
. The subgroup generated by all commutators in
G
is called the
derived or commutator subgroup of

G
and is denoted by
G^{\prime}
.

Observe that

|SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})|=\frac{1}{q-1}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i})\ \textup{whenever}\ |\mathbb{F}|=q.

(1)

Indeed, if
\phi:GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})\to\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
is defined by
\phi(A)=\det(A)
, then the First Isomorphism Theorem for groups implies

GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})/SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})=GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})/\ker(\phi)\cong\textup{Im}(\phi)=\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}.

Since
|GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})|=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i})

by
[
11
, Corollary 11.2.14]
, (
1
) holds.

Dickson showed in
[
12
]
that
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}=SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
except for
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
with
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
. In this subsection, we confirm Dickson’s result for
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
with
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
by providing an alternative proof using Sylow theorems and results on commutator subgroups.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|=2
. If
A:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}),
then
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\langle A\rangle.

Proof.

Since
\textup{char}\mathbb{(}\mathbb{F})=2
,
A^{2}\neq I_{2}
but
A^{3}=I_{2}
. Hence,
[SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}):\langle A\rangle]=\frac{|SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})|}{|\langle A\rangle|}=2
due to (
1
). By
[
13
, Exercise I.5.1]
,
\langle A\rangle\trianglelefteq SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
. In particular,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})/\langle A\rangle
is abelian, and so
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\langle A\rangle
by
[
13
, Theorem II.7.8]
. ∎

Let
|\mathbb{F}|=3
and consider the following elements of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
:

\mathbf{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&2\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{j}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
2&0\end{bmatrix},\ \textup{and}\ \mathbf{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

The matrices in (
2
) satisfy

\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=\mathbf{k}^{2}=-I_{2}
,
\mathbf{ij}=-\mathbf{ji}=\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{jk}=-\mathbf{kj}=\mathbf{i}
, and
\mathbf{ki}=-\mathbf{ik}=\mathbf{j}
.

Thus,
\{I_{2},\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k},-I_{2},-\mathbf{i},-\mathbf{j},-\mathbf{k}\}=\langle\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}\}\rangle=:{\cal Q}
is the quaternion group.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|=3
. If
{\cal Q}:=\langle\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}\}\rangle
where
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is as defined in (
2
), then
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

By (
1
),
|SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})|=24=2^{3}\cdot 3
.
Since
|{\cal Q}|=2^{3}
,
[
13
, Corollary II.5.8]
(i) guarantees that
{\cal Q}
is a Sylow 2-subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
. If it can be established that
{\cal Q}\trianglelefteq SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, then the claim follows since
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})/\mathcal{Q}
is abelian, and thus
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
by
[
13
, Theorem II.7.8]
.

To show that
{\cal Q}\trianglelefteq SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, it suffices to prove that
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
has a unique Sylow 2-subgroup due to
[
13
, Corollary II.5.8]
(iii). Suppose not. We arrive at a contradiction by showing that all non-identity elements of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
can only have orders which are powers of
2
or
3
; we then exhibit an element whose order is
6.

By the Third Sylow Theorem,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
has three Sylow 2-subgroups, say
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
. We claim that
|P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}|=4
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\}
. For
P\in\mathcal{P}
, define
\mathcal{S}_{P}
be the set of all subgroups of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
conjugate to
P
. Since each element of
{\cal S}_{P}
is a Sylow 2-subgroup due to
[
13
, Corollary II.5.8]
(ii),
\mathcal{S}_{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
. Equality holds since the Second Sylow Theorem ensures that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{P}
. Hence,

3=|{\cal P}|=|{\cal S}_{P}|=[SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}):N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)]=\frac{24}{|N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)|}

due to
[
13
, Corollary II.4.4]
, and so
|N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)|=8
. Since
P\trianglelefteq N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)
and
|P|=8
by
[
13
, Corollary II.5.8]
(i), it follows that
P=N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)
. Consequently,
P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}=\bigcap_{P\in{\cal P}}N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P).
Now,
let
\Sigma(\mathcal{P})=\{\sigma\,|\,\sigma
is a bijection on
\mathcal{P}\}
. For a given
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, define
\phi(A)(P)=APA^{-1}
for all
P\in{\cal P}
. The mapping
\phi:SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})\to\Sigma(\mathcal{P})
is a group homomorphism (see
[
13
, Theorem II.4.5]
). Note that
A\in\ker(\phi)
if and only if
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
such that
APA^{-1}=\phi(A)(P)=P
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Equivalently,
A\in N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. It follows that
\ker(\phi)=\bigcap_{P\in{\cal P}}N_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})}(P)=P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}
. By the First Isomorphism Theorem for groups,

|P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}|=|\ker(\phi)|=\frac{|SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})|}{|\textup{Im}(\phi)|}\geq\frac{|SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})|}{|\Sigma({\cal P})|}=\frac{24}{6}=4.

To show equality, observe that
4\leq|P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}|\leq|P_{i}\cap P_{j}|\leq|P_{i}|=8
. For distinct
P_{i},P_{j}
,
|P_{i}\cap P_{j}|=4
by Lagrange’s Theorem. Therefore,
|P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}|=4
which proves the claim. Thus, there are
4|{\cal P}|+|P_{1}\cap P_{2}\cap P_{3}|-1=15
non-identity elements in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
that have order a power of 2.

Since
|\langle J_{2}(1)\rangle|=|\langle J_{2}(1)^{\top}\rangle|=3
, both
\langle J_{2}(1)\rangle
and
\langle J_{2}(1)^{\top}\rangle
are Sylow
3
-subgroups of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
due to
[
13
, Corollary II.5.8]
(i). By the Third Sylow Theorem,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
has exactly four Sylow 3-subgroups, say
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
. Since
|R_{i}|=3
for all
i
,
R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\{I_{2}\}
for all
i\neq j
. As a consequence, there are exactly 8 non-identity elements in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
that have order a power of 3.

Since
|SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})|=24=15+8+1
, all non-identity elements of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
can only have orders which are powers of 2 or 3. However, observe that
J_{2}(-1)\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
has order 6. Therefore, there must be a unique Sylow-2 subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
as desired.∎

Lemmas
3.7
-
3.8
are used to prove the exceptional cases in Dickson’s result.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
. Every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}
is a product of at most
|\mathbb{F}|-1
commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices. The upper bound
|\mathbb{F}|-1
cannot be further reduced. Moreover,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}<SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
.

Proof.

Suppose
|\mathbb{F}|=2
. If
A
is as in Lemma
3.7
, then
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\langle A\rangle
. Since
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
and
\textup{tr}(A)=\textup{tr}(A^{2})=1=2+1^{2}
, it follows that
A
and
A^{2}
are commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices by Theorem
3.2
. Hence,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}=\langle A\rangle<SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
. In particular, every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}
is a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Suppose
|\mathbb{F}|=3
. If
{\cal Q}
is as in Lemma
3.8
, then
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
.
Equality holds by showing that every element of
\mathcal{Q}
can be written as products of commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices (necessarily, elements of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
). Note that all elements of
{\cal Q}\setminus\{-I_{2}\}
are commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices due to Proposition
2.4
and Theorem
3.2
. On the other hand, Corollary
3.5
ensures that
-I_{2}
is a product of two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices since
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=3
and
-1=2=a^{2}+b^{2}
where
a=b=1
.
Thus, every matrix in
\mathcal{Q}
is a product of at most two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices and two is the smallest such number since
-I_{2}
is not a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices due to Proposition
2.4
. Consequently,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}<SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
.∎

When
|\mathbb{F}|\leq 3
, Theorem
3.9
implies that there are elements of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, e.g.,
J_{2}(1)
and
J_{2}(1)^{\top}
, which are not products of commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices or matrices in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
.

3.2
Fields with at least four elements

The situation when
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
is different. We show that every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most three commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\notin\{2,3,5\}
. There exists
b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that
b^{2}\neq b^{-2}
.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive of the claim. Suppose that
x^{2}=x^{-2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
. That is, each element of
\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
is a root of the degree four polynomial
f(x):=x^{4}-1
. If
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2,
then
|\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}|\leq 4
; in this case,
|\mathbb{F}|\in\{3,5\}.
Otherwise, if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2,
then
f(x)=(x^{2}+1)^{2}
and
|\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}|\leq 2
; in particular,
|\mathbb{F}|=2.
∎

With the aid of the previous result, we obtain the next one which is analogous to Corollary
3.4
(compare with Corollary
3.5
as well).

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\notin\{2,3,5\}
. If
-1=a^{2}
for some
a\in\mathbb{F}
, then
-I_{2}
is a product of at most two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.10
, there exists
b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that
b^{2}\neq b^{-2}
. Then
-I_{2}=\textup{diag}(b^{2},b^{-2})\ \textup{diag}((ab^{-1})^{2},(ab^{-1})^{-2})

is a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Corollary
3.6
.
∎

Proposition
3.13
will handle the case when
-1
is not necessarily a perfect square.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
. Every nonscalar matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
and
A\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
be a nonscalar matrix. By
[
14
, Theorem 1]
,
A=BC
for some
B,C\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
where
\sigma(B)
and
\sigma(C)
can be specified as long as
\det(B)\det(C)=\det(A)=1
.

If
|\mathbb{F}|\neq 5
(implying
|\mathbb{F}|\notin\{2,3,5\}
by assumption), then there exists
b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that
b^{2}\neq b^{-2}
due to Lemma
3.10
. Take
\sigma(B)=\sigma(C)=\{b^{2},b^{-2}\}
. Since
b^{2}\neq b^{-2}
,
B
and
C
are similar to
\textup{diag}(b^{2},b^{-2})
,
which is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Corollary
3.6
. Hence,
A
is a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Suppose that
|\mathbb{F}|=5
. Take
\sigma(B)=\sigma(C)=\{-1\}
. It follows that the minimal polynomials
m_{B}(x),m_{C}(x)\in\{x+1,(x+1)^{2}\}
. Two cases arise:
m_{B}(x)=m_{C}(x)
or not. Suppose
m_{B}(x)=m_{C}(x)
. In this case,
m_{B}(x),m_{C}(x)
cannot be both linear since
A
is a nonscalar matrix. Hence,
m_{B}(x)=m_{C}(x)=(x+1)^{2}
, and so
B
and
C
are similar to
J_{2}(-1)
. Since
-1=2^{2}
in
\mathbb{F}
, Proposition
2.5
and Corollary
3.4
ensure that
B,C
are commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. Thus,
A
is a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. Suppose
m_{B}(x)\neq m_{C}(x)
. Assume
m_{B}(x)=x+1
and
m_{C}(x)=(x+1)^{2}
(the case
m_{B}(x)=(x+1)^{2}
and
m_{C}(x)=x+1
is analogous). Then there exists
P\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
such that

A=BC=(-I_{2})PJ_{2}(-1)P^{-1}=PJ_{2}(1)^{-1}P^{-1}=(PJ_{2}(1)P^{-1})^{-1}.

Note that
J_{2}(1)=D^{2}
where
D=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Theorem
3.2
. Proposition
2.5
guarantees that
A
is a product of two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.
∎

For some fields, if
-I_{2}
is a product of commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices, then the number of factors is at least three (see remarks after Corollary
3.5
). Such factorization of
-I_{2}
into three commutators is indeed possible.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 5
and
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then
-I_{2}
is a product of at most three commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

If
|\mathbb{F}|=5
, then
-I_{2}=J_{2}(-1)J_{2}(1)
is a product of three commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices due to Corollary
3.4
and Proposition
3.12
. Assume
|\mathbb{F}|>5
. By Lemma
3.10
, there exists
b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that
b^{2}\neq b^{-2}
. In this case,
[-\textup{diag}(b^{2},b^{-2})]^{-1}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a nonscalar matrix. It follows that
-I_{2}=\textup{diag}(b^{2},b^{-2})[-\textup{diag}(b^{2},b^{-2})]^{-1}
is a product of three commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices due to Corollary
3.6
and Proposition
3.12
.
∎

The main result of this subsection follows from Propositions
2.4
,
3.12
,
3.13
, and Corollary
3.5
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
. Every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most three commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. In particular, if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2,
then every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices; if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, then every matrix in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices if and only if
-1=a^{2}+b^{2}
for some nonzero
a,b\in\mathbb{F}
.

4
Decomposition of matrices in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
into commutators

In this section, we show that if
n>2
, then every matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most four commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices provided
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
.

As seen from Corollary
3.3
,
J_{2}(1)
is
not
a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. Interestingly, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

The matrix
I_{n}\oplus J_{2}(1)
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
[1]\oplus J_{2}(1)
is a commutator by Proposition
2.6
. If
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
and
Y=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
-1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
(both
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices), then
[X,Y]=[1]\oplus J_{2}(1)
.
∎

In writing
J_{n}(1)
as a product of commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices, we make use of observations about products of matrices with special patterns of zero entries. To emphasize the size of a
k\times\ell
zero matrix, we denote it by
0_{k,\ell}
(or as defined earlier,
0_{k}
if
k=\ell
). In the next result,
\mathbb{F}^{n}
denotes the set of all
n\times 1
vectors with entries in
\mathbb{F}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
k\geq 2
,
\ell\geq 1
,
x\in\mathbb{F}^{2}
,
B,C\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, let
A_{e}:=[A_{ij}]\in M_{2k}(\mathbb{F})
be strictly block upper triangular matrix where
A_{ij}=0_{2}
whenever
j\geq i+3
,
A_{i,i+1}=B
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, and if
k>2
,
A_{i,i+2}=C
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-2\}
; let
A_{o}:=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2}&Bx\\
0_{1,2}&0\end{bmatrix}
if
\ell=1
and
A_{o}:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{e}&N\\
0_{1,2\ell}&0\end{bmatrix}
if
\ell>1
where
N\in\mathbb{F}^{2l}
is zero except possibly in its last four rows which are given by the rows of
\begin{bmatrix}Cx\\
Bx\end{bmatrix}
. The following hold:

1.

A_{e}^{k}=0_{2k}
and
A_{e}^{k-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2,2k-2}&B^{k-1}\\
0_{2k-2}&0_{2k-2,2}\end{bmatrix}
;

2.

A_{o}^{\ell+1}=0_{2\ell+1}
and
A_{o}^{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2\ell}&B^{\ell}x\\
0_{1,2\ell}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

The claims in (i) follow by repeated multiplication and recognizing that

A_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2}&B\\
0_{2}&0_{2}\end{bmatrix}\ \textup{if}\ k=2\ \textup{and}\ A_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2}&B&C&0_{2}&\cdots&0_{2}\\
0_{2}&0_{2}&B&C&\ddots&\vdots\\
0_{2}&0_{2}&0_{2}&B&\ddots&0_{2}\\
0_{2}&0_{2}&0_{2}&0_{2}&\ddots&C\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&B\\
0_{2}&\cdots&0_{2}&0_{2}&0_{2}&0_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\ \textup{if}\ k>2.

For (ii), the case
\ell=1
is easily verified; if
\ell>1
, then use (i) and the fact that
A_{o}^{i}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{e}^{i}&A_{e}^{i-1}N\\
0_{1,2\ell}&0\end{bmatrix}
for any
i
.

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
n>2
. The matrix
J_{n}(1)
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

Observe that

J_{n}(1)\ \textup{is similar to}\ \begin{cases}[J_{\frac{n}{2}}(1)\oplus J_{\frac{n}{2}}(1)][I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\oplus J_{2}(1)\oplus I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}],&\textup{if}\ n\ \textup{is even}\\
[J_{\frac{n+1}{2}}(1)\oplus J_{\frac{n-1}{2}}(1)][I_{\frac{n-1}{2}}\oplus J_{2}(1)\oplus I_{\frac{n-3}{2}}],&\textup{if}\ n\ \textup{is odd}.\end{cases}

By Propositions
2.5
-
2.6
, and
4.1
, it suffices to prove that the first factor above is similar to a commutator of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Consider

.

In the above proof, even if

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

and

for some

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
S=\{a^{2}|a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\}
. If
|\mathbb{F}|
is even, then
|S|=|\mathbb{F}|-1
. If
|\mathbb{F}|
is odd, then
|S|=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-1}{2}
.

Proof.

The map
\phi:\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
defined by
\phi(x)=x^{2}
is a group homomorphism. By the First Isomorphism Theorem for groups,
\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}/\ker(\phi)\cong\textup{Im}(\phi)=S.

If
|\mathbb{F}|
is even, then
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
and so
\ker(\phi)=\{1\}
. Consequently,
|S|=|\mathbb{F}|-1
. On the other hand,
|\mathbb{F}|
being odd implies
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. In particular,
|\ker(\phi)|=|\{-1,1\}|=2
, and so
|S|=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-1}{2}
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4
. Let

1.

If
n

is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices;

2.

Let

\mathcal{U}_{2}

\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2

\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Let
A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})

for some

. For each

Suppose

for some

. Let

For each

2.4

, and so

. In any case,

-matrices. Now, note that

-

,

Suppose
n=2k
for some

. If

. Assume

. Note that

and

(3)

-

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
|\mathbb{F}|\notin\{2,3,5\}
and consider
S=\{a^{2}|a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\}
. Then
S
can be written as a disjoint union of sets in the following manner:

S=E\cup\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}^{-1},...\}

(4)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}^{-1},...
are distinct and
E
is defined as

E=\begin{cases}\{1\}&\textup{if $\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2$}\\
\{1\}&\textup{if $\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2$ and $-1\notin S$}\\
\{-1,1\}&\textup{if $\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2$ and $-1\in S$}.\end{cases}

If
\mathbb{F}
is infinite, then
S\setminus E
is infinite. If
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then
S\setminus E
is finite and

S\setminus E=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},...,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k}^{-1}\}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
where

1.

k=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-2}{2}
if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
;

2.

k=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-3}{4}
if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
-1\notin S
, and

3.

k=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-5}{4}
if
\textup{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
-1\in S
.

Proof.

Since
|\mathbb{F}|\notin\{2,3,5\}
, Lemma
3.10
implies that there exists
\beta\in S
such that
\beta\neq\beta^{-1}
. In particular,
\beta\notin\{1,-1\}
, and so
\beta\notin E
. This guarantees that
S\setminus E\neq\varnothing
. Both
\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\in S
since
S
is a multiplicative group. We claim that
\alpha\in S\setminus E
if and only if
\alpha^{-1}\in S\setminus E.
Indeed, suppose
\alpha\in S\setminus E
. If, on the contrary,
\alpha^{-1}\in E,
then we arrive at a contradiction in each possible case of
E
as defined; hence,
\alpha^{-1}\in S\setminus E
. The converse is analogous. Moreover, if
\alpha\in S\setminus E
, then
\alpha\neq\alpha^{-1}.
Otherwise,
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}
; checking each case of
E
yields a contradiction. Therefore, we have the disjoint union
S=E\cup(S\setminus E)
, where
\alpha\in S\setminus E
if and only if
\alpha^{-1}\in S\setminus E
and
\alpha\neq\alpha^{-1}
.

If
\mathbb{F}
is infinite, then
S
and
S\setminus E
are infinite. Assume
\mathbb{F}
is finite. By Lemma
4.6
,
S
is also a finite set. There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

S=E\cup\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},...,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k}^{-1}\}

(5)

where
E\cap\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},...,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k}^{-1}\}=\varnothing
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1},...,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k}^{-1}
are distinct. If
|\mathbb{F}|
is even, then by definition of
E
and Lemma
4.6
,
|\mathbb{F}|-1=|S|=1+2k
. Hence,
k=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-2}{2}
. If
|\mathbb{F}|
is odd, then
\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-1}{2}=|S|=|E|+2k
by Lemma
4.6
, and so
k=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|-1-2|E|}{4}
. The claim follows by considering cases for
E.
∎

\lfloor x\rfloor
denote the floor function of

.

Proposition 5.2
.

Every nonscalar matrix in

is a product of at most two commutators of

-matrices provided that any of the following conditions hold:

-1\neq\alpha^{2}
for all

|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+5

and
-1=\alpha^{2}
for some

.

|\mathbb{F}|\geq 4\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+5

is a product of at most two commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Suppose

or

for some

. Let
S
and

be as in Proposition

. If
\mathbb{F}
is infinite, then

is infinite, and so

. If
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then assumptions (i)-(iii) guarantee that

. In any case, there exist distinct
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{-1}...,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k}^{-1}\in S\setminus E

[

, Theorem 1]
,
A=BC
for some
B
and
C
where
\sigma(B)
and
\sigma(C)
can be specified provided that

.
If

while if

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
n
be even and
|\mathbb{F}|>2n+1
. Every scalar matrix in

is a product of at most three commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.
In addition, if
\mathbb{F}
is algebraically closed, then every scalar matrix in
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices.

Proof.

Let
A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
such that
A=\lambda I_{n}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. Define
\Lambda_{i}:=\textup{diag}(\lambda^{i},\lambda^{n-i})
for
i\in\{0,1,...,n-1\}
. Since
\lambda^{n}=\det(A)=1
,
\Lambda_{i}\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
and
\Lambda_{i}\Lambda_{n-i}=I_{2}.
Assume
n=2k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since
|\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}|=|\mathbb{F}|-1>2n

a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
such that

B
and
C
be respectively the first and second factors above. By permuting diagonal entries,
B
is similar to
\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{2i-1}D
while
C
is similar to
\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{2i-2}^{-1}D^{-1}.

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
\lambda^{2-2i}a^{-2}\neq-1
and
\lambda^{2i-1}a^{2}\neq-1
. Otherwise, since
n
is even, either

or

, contradicting the choice of

i\in\{1,...,k\}
,
\Lambda_{2i-2}^{-1}D^{-1}
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Proposition
2.4
or Corollary
3.6
. By Propositions
2.5
-
2.6
,
C
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. On the other hand, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
\Lambda_{2i-1}D\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{-I_{2}\}
. By Propositions
2.4
-
2.6
and
3.12
,
B
is a product of at most two commutators of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices. Hence,
A
is a product of at most three commutators of
{\cal U}_{2}
-matrices.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, there exists

such that

. By Proposition
2.4
or Corollary
3.6
,
\Lambda_{2i-1}D
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices for all

. Hence,
B
is a commutator of
\mathcal{U}_{2}
-matrices by Propositions
2.5
-
2.6
