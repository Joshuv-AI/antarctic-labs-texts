Beyond Confidence: The Rhythms of Reasoning in Generative Models
Deyuan Liu, Zecheng Wang, Zhanyue Qin, Zhiying Tu, Dianhui Chu, Dianbo Sui

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit impressive capabilities yet suffer from sensitivity to slight input context variations, hampering reliability. Conventional metrics like accuracy and perplexity fail to assess local prediction robustness, as normalized output probabilities can obscure the underlying resilience of an LLM’s internal state to perturbations. We introduce the Token Constraint Bound ( δ TCB \delta_{\mathrm{TCB}} ) , a novel metric that quantifies the maximum internal state perturbation an LLM can withstand before its dominant next-token prediction significantly changes. Intrinsically linked to output embedding space geometry, δ TCB \delta_{\mathrm{TCB}} provides insights into the stability of the model’s internal predictive commitment. Our experiments show δ TCB \delta_{\mathrm{TCB}} correlates with effective prompt engineering and uncovers critical prediction instabilities missed by perplexity during in-context learning and text generation. δ TCB \delta_{\mathrm{TCB}} offers a principled, complementary approach to analyze and potentially improve the contextual stability of LLM predictions.

2
Preliminaries: Understanding LLM Predictions and Stability

This section establishes the foundational concepts for analyzing local output stability in LLMs.
We begin by outlining the LLM output mechanism, then explore what it means for a prediction to be stable against internal variations.
This leads to the introduction of the Jacobian matrix as a tool for quantifying sensitivity, and finally culminates in the formal definition of our
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

2.1
Language Model Output and Distribution Concentration

Consider an LLM whose final layer computes a hidden state
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This state is linearly transformed by an output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
to produce logits
\bm{z}=\mathbf{W}\bm{h}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the vocabulary size.
Each row
\bm{w}_{i}^{\top}
of
\mathbf{W}
corresponds to the output embedding for token
i
. The probability distribution over the next token,
\bm{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
, is obtained via the softmax function:

\bm{o}=\text{softmax}(\bm{z}),\quad\text{where }o_{i}=\frac{\exp(z_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_{j})}.

(1)

This distribution satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}=1
and
o_{i}\geq 0
. The model’s prediction is typically the token
i^{*}
maximizing
o_{i}
.
A useful measure of the concentration of this distribution is the
effective vocabulary size

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
:

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{o})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\|\bm{o}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(2)

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
ranges from 1 to
\mathcal{V}
, inversely relating to the
L_{2}
norm squared of the probability vector.
Our analysis hinges on understanding how the characteristics of this output vector
\bm{o}
, including its concentration, relate to its
stability
when the context-derived hidden state
\bm{h}
undergoes small changes.

2.2
Defining Stability: What Does It Mean for a Prediction to Be Stable?

Our core objective is to understand the robustness of an LLM’s next-token prediction.
Specifically, we want to know: if the LLM’s internal summary of the context (represented by the final hidden state
\bm{h}
) changes slightly, how much does its next-token probability distribution
\bm{o}
change?
Let
\Delta\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represent a small internal
"wobble"
or perturbation to the hidden state
\bm{h}
. Such a perturbation to the
model’s internal representation of the context
could arise from minor input variations, noise in the computation, or other subtle disturbances.
Let
\bm{o}^{\prime}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}(\bm{h}+\Delta\bm{h}))
be the perturbed output distribution.
The resulting change in the prediction is
\Delta\bm{o}=\bm{o}^{\prime}-\bm{o}
.

The central question motivating our work, reiterated from the Introduction
\mathbb{Q}
, is how to quantify a
"safety margin"
for
\bm{h}
: how large can the perturbation
\Delta\bm{h}
be before the change in the output
\Delta\bm{o}
becomes unacceptably large?
Answering this requires a way to relate the magnitude of the internal perturbation
\Delta\bm{h}
to the magnitude of the resulting output change
\Delta\bm{o}
.
This safety margin offers insights distinct from interpreting output probabilities
\bm{o}
as direct measures of absolute confidence; instead,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
focuses on the
local integrity and resilience of the current predictive mechanism
itself.

2.3
Quantifying the Impact of Perturbations: The Role of the Jacobian

To precisely relate changes in
\bm{h}
to changes in
\bm{o}
, we utilize the concept of the Jacobian matrix.
To first order, for small
\Delta\bm{h}
, the change in the output distribution
\Delta\bm{o}
can be approximated linearly:

\Delta\bm{o}\approx\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\Delta\bm{h},

(3)

where
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
is the Jacobian matrix of the output probabilities
\bm{o}
with respect to the hidden state
\bm{h}
. It is given by:

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})=\frac{\partial\bm{o}}{\partial\bm{h}}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\bm{o}}{\partial\bm{z}}}_{\mathclap{\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top}}}\quad\underbrace{\frac{\partial\bm{z}}{\partial\bm{h}}}_{\mathclap{\mathbf{W}}}=\left(\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{W}.

(4)

The Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})
essentially captures the sensitivity of each output probability
o_{i}
to infinitesimal changes in each dimension of the hidden state
h_{k}
. Its entries are
\frac{\partial o_{i}}{\partial h_{k}}
. Note that the Jacobian depends on both the current output distribution
\bm{o}
and the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
.
To relate the overall magnitude of the state perturbation
\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}
to the overall magnitude of the output change
\|\Delta\bm{o}\|_{2}
, we use matrix norms. A standard inequality bounds the output change using the Jacobian’s Frobenius norm:

\|\Delta\bm{o}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}.

(5)

The Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\frac{\partial o_{i}}{\partial h_{k}})^{2}\right)^{1/2}
provides a comprehensive, aggregate measure of the sensitivity of all output probabilities to all hidden state dimensions. A larger Frobenius norm indicates that, the output probabilities are more sensitive to changes in the hidden state.

2.4
The Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
): Our Measure of Stability

We are interested in finding the maximum allowable perturbation radius
\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}
such that the resulting change in the output distribution, as measured by its
L_{2}
norm
\|\Delta\bm{o}\|_{2}
, remains below a predefined small tolerance
\epsilon>0
.
That is, we impose the condition
\|\Delta\bm{o}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.
Using the bound from Eq. (
5
), we require
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.
Rearranging for
\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}
gives us:

\|\Delta\bm{h}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}}.

(6)

This naturally motivates our core metric for local output stability, which we define as this upper bound on the perturbation norm:

Definition 1 (Token Constraint Bound
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
) .

Given the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
, hidden state
\bm{h}
, resulting output distribution
\bm{o}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\bm{h})
, and a tolerance
\epsilon>0
for the maximum
L_{2}
change allowed in
\bm{o}
, the
Token Constraint Bound

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
at state
\bm{h}
is defined as:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})\coloneqq\frac{\epsilon}{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}}.

(7)

Here,
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})
is the Jacobian given by Eq. (
4
) and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

The parameter
\epsilon
is a dimensionless scale factor chosen by the user, representing the desired tolerance for output distribution change.
Consequently,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})
quantifies the
L_{2}
-norm radius of the largest hyper-sphere of perturbations
\Delta\bm{h}
around the current hidden state
\bm{h}
that, to a first-order approximation, guarantees the change in the output probability vector
\bm{o}
remains within
\epsilon
.
A larger
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})
signifies that the model’s
current prediction state

\bm{o}
, as induced by the context leading to
\bm{h}
, is intrinsically more robust to small internal variations (
"wobbles"
) in this hidden state. Conversely, a smaller
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})
indicates that the prediction mechanism is more sensitive to such internal perturbations at this specific point.
The crucial term
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}
in the denominator has a fundamental connection to the geometry of the output embeddings, which we explore in detail in
Section
3
.

3
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
via Output Embedding Geometry

Def.
1
introduced the Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
) as a measure of local output stability.
To unlock its full diagnostic power and understand its nuanced behavior, we now dissect its core component: the Frobenius norm of the output Jacobian,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}
.
This section reveals that
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}
, and consequently
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
, is deeply intertwined with the
geometric arrangement
of the model’s output token embeddings
\bm{w}_{i}
relative to the current prediction probabilities
\bm{o}
.
Intuitively, a prediction is expected to be more stable if the leading token’s embedding is well-isolated from competitors, or if the model is highly certain (peaked
\bm{o}
).

3.1
The Geometry of Output: Token Embeddings and Their Mean

Recall that the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
contains the output embedding vector
\bm{w}_{i}^{\top}
for each token
i
as its rows.
A key concept in understanding the geometric influence on stability is the probability-weighted mean embedding vector:

\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\bm{o}.

(8)

Here,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the current probability-weighted locus within the embedding space.
This mean vector,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
, reflects the current probability-weighted locus within the embedding space, effectively representing the
"center of mass"
or the resultant directional influence of the entire output distribution on the embedding geometry.

3.2
Deriving the Jacobian Norm: Connecting Sensitivity to Embedding Spread

We now derive an exact analytical expression for the squared Frobenius norm of the output Jacobian,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}
, which is the crucial term determining
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.
Let
\{\bm{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}
be the output embedding vectors (rows of
\mathbf{W}
) and
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
be the probability-weighted mean embedding as defined in Eq. (
8
).

Proposition 1 (Exact Squared Jacobian Norm
Appendix
I
) .

For a given output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
and hidden state
\bm{h}
, let
\bm{o}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\bm{h})
be the output probability vector.
The squared Frobenius norm of the output Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})=(\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top})\mathbf{W}
is exactly:

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\left\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(9)

This sum represents the squared Euclidean distances between each embedding
\bm{w}_{i}
and the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
, weighted by the corresponding squared probability
o_{i}^{2}
.

Interpretation of the Formula.

Eq. (
9
) is pivotal.
It states that the overall sensitivity of the output distribution to hidden state perturbations (as captured by
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}
) is determined by how
"spread out"
the token embeddings
\bm{w}_{i}
are from their probability-weighted mean
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
, with each squared distance
\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\|_{2}^{2}
being amplified or diminished by the square of its token’s probability
o_{i}^{2}
.
The
o_{i}^{2}
weighting is crucial:

•

Embeddings for tokens with very low probability
o_{i}
(and thus low current ""evidence” or ""belief” from the model) contribute minimally to the sum, even if geometrically distant from
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
. The model effectively de-weights their geometric influence on stability at this state.

•

Embeddings for high-probability tokens (carrying significant ""evidence”) contribute substantially, particularly if they are far from
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
. Their geometric influence on the Jacobian norm is quadratically emphasized by
o_{i}^{2}
.

This
o_{i}^{2}
weighting distinguishes Eq. (
9
) from measures like the trace of the standard probability-weighted covariance matrix of embeddings.
This distinction arises directly from the definition of the softmax Jacobian and is fundamental for correctly interpreting
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
(see
Appendix
G
).

3.3
The Full Form of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
and Its Geometric Meaning

Substituting the exact squared Jacobian norm from
Prop.
1
Eq. (
9
) into the definition of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
Eq. (
7
) yields its complete form:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\left\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\right\|_{2}^{2}}}.

(10)

This equation provides a clear geometric interpretation:
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is inversely proportional to the square root of the
o_{i}^{2}
-weighted sum of squared Euclidean distances between each token embedding
\bm{w}_{i}
and the probability-weighted mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
.
A larger
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
indicates higher local robustness of the prediction generated from
\bm{h}
. This geometric dispersion, weighted by
o_{i}^{2}
, directly dictates the
"safety radius"
around
\bm{h}
, within which the output distribution
\bm{o}
changes by at most
\epsilon
.
Understanding this relationship is key to interpreting how context shapes
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
and, by extension, prediction stability, as conceptually illustrated in
Figure
1
and
Figure
3
.

4.3
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
as a Diagnostic Tool for Prompt Engineering

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
uniquely identifies stability issues that accuracy or conventional confidence metrics (

include: (1)

: correct yet brittle predictions; (2)

P(\text{top1})
or
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
) but low
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
; (4)

: flatter distribution but a resilient underlying state.

.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

To address Large Language Model sensitivity to input context variations, this paper introduces the Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
), a novel metric quantifying the local stability of next-token predictions against internal state perturbations.
Intrinsically linked to the
o_{i}^{2}
-weighted geometric dispersion of output embeddings,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
offers a principled measure of an LLM’s predictive commitment resilience.
Our experiments demonstrate
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
’s utility in assessing prompt effectiveness and its ability to uncover critical prediction instabilities missed by perplexity, thus providing a valuable complementary tool for analyzing and potentially enhancing LLM contextual robustness.
While these findings are promising, our current investigation primarily uses a specific LLM and a focused set of scenarios.
Future work should expand this research across a broader spectrum of models, varying scales, and diverse application contexts to validate and generalize the utility of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.
Exploring its application to understanding perturbations at intermediate layers could also yield deeper insights into representation robustness.
Further investigation into
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
’s role in model editing, fine-tuning, and robustness against more structured attacks remains an important avenue.

Appendix B
Related Work

Our work on the Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
) builds upon and differentiates itself from several lines of research in LLM evaluation, robustness, and interpretability.

LLM Sensitivity and the Need for Robustness Metrics

The pronounced sensitivity of LLMs to subtle input variations is well-documented.
Studies have demonstrated substantial performance fluctuations arising from minor alterations in prompt phrasing
(
27
)
, example ordering and selection
(
38
;
18
)
, or even formatting details
(
28
)
.
This brittleness
(
20
)
highlights an urgent need for evaluation methods that go beyond aggregate task performance to assess the inherent stability of LLM predictions.
Importantly, increased model scale, despite adherence to scaling laws
(
15
;
12
)
, does not invariably confer enhanced robustness to these nuanced changes
(
18
;
35
)
, further motivating metrics like
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
that focus on local stability.
The imperative for reliable deployment in critical applications
(
36
;
10
;
8
)
makes understanding and quantifying such instabilities paramount.

Limitations of Conventional Evaluation Metrics

Conventional metrics offer limited insight into local predictive robustness, a gap
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
aims to address.
Task accuracy, while a primary indicator, provides an aggregate view that can mask underlying prediction fragility due to contextual shifts
(
38
)
.
Perplexity
(
14
)
, though standard for assessing sequence likelihood
(
16
;
13
)
, aggregates probabilities and can obscure local dynamics and the true stability of the internal state underpinning a prediction
(
4
)
. It may not reflect genuine model conviction, especially in cases of
"surface form competition"

(
13
)
or when the softmax normalizes over poorly supported options.
Emerging metrics targeting confidence and calibration
(
37
;
32
;
7
;
38
)
focus on aligning output probabilities with the likelihood of correctness
(
32
)
.
While valuable, these do not directly quantify the resilience of a specific prediction’s dominant rank against perturbations in the model’s internal representation
\bm{h}

(
17
)
.
A well-calibrated, high-confidence prediction might still stem from an internally fragile state.
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
measures this internal representational stability directly.

Internal State Dynamics and Representational Stability

Our work intersects with research exploring LLM internal representations and their stability.
Efforts to enhance robustness by, for example, aligning hidden states of perturbed instructions with original ones
(
1
)
, implicitly underscore the importance of stable internal configurations.

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
provides a direct, quantitative measure of this local stability specifically for the next-token prediction mechanism, assessing the
"safety margin"
or integrity of the current predictive commitment arising from
\bm{h}
.
This connects to broader goals in Explainable AI (XAI) that seek to understand internal model workings
(
23
)
, where
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
can pinpoint internal decision points of high or low resilience.

Prompt Engineering, In-Context Learning, and Stable State Induction

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is particularly relevant to analyzing the effectiveness of prompt engineering and In-Context Learning
(
2
;
5
)
.
Our central hypothesis posits that effective contextualization, such as through well-designed prompts or informative ICL examples, guides LLMs not only to correct answers but also to more
stable
internal states
\bm{h}
, reflected by higher
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
values.
This aligns with findings suggesting ICL’s efficacy often stems from its ability to clarify task structure
(
21
)
.

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
can thus serve as a quantitative tool to assess how effectively different contextual inputs induce robust internal commitments to a predictive path, complementing metrics focused on epistemic "knowledge strength"
(
19
)
.

Output Embedding Geometry and Predictive Stability

A key theoretical underpinning of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
, detailed in
Section
3
, is its intrinsic link to the geometric dispersion of output token embeddings.
While the general importance of embedding space properties for model robustness is acknowledged
(
26
)
,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
establishes a specific, analytical relationship between the geometry of the output embeddings (weighted by current prediction probabilities
o_{i}^{2}
) and the local stability of the next-token prediction against perturbations in
\bm{h}
.
This provides a mechanistic, geometric interpretation of local prediction stability, moving beyond probability-based analyses alone.

Complementarity with Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is positioned as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, existing Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) methods
(
17
;
30
)
.
While UQ approaches typically aim to gauge a model’s confidence or
"knowledge strength"
often based on output probabilities (e.g., entropy, prediction margins),
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
specifically assesses the robustness of the
predictive mechanism
itself to internal state fluctuations.
A prediction can exhibit high output probability (high confidence by UQ standards) yet originate from an unstable internal state (low
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
), indicating a
"confidently unstable"
scenario.
Conversely, a moderately confident prediction might be highly stable.

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
thus offers a distinct perspective on reliability, focusing on the local integrity and resilience of the model’s current predictive commitment rather than solely its expressed certainty.

Perturbation-Based Robustness in Neural Networks and LLMs

Prior work on NN robustness has leveraged Jacobian norms and perturbations to quantify sensitivity and improve generalization.

(
24
)
empirically study sensitivity via the Frobenius norm of the input Jacobian, finding trained NNs more robust near training data and linking it to generalization gaps.
Subsequent methods regularize this norm for adversarial robustness, e.g.,
(
11
)
for classification margins.
In LLMs, recent studies evaluate robustness to input perturbations like typos or rephrasing
(
29
;
22
)
, with benchmarks like RUPBench
(
34
)
showing larger models’ resilience.
\delta_{\text{TCB}}
extends these by deriving an exact closed-form for the softmax-linear Jacobian norm, linking it to output embedding geometry, and repurposing it for contextual stability in ICL and prompt engineering—focusing on hidden state perturbations rather than inputs.

C.3
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
Calculation and Parameters

The Token Constraint Bound
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})
was computed using Eq. (
10
) from the main text:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h})=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\left\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\right\|_{2}^{2}}}

The tolerance parameter
\epsilon
was set to
1.0
for all experiments. This choice normalizes
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
such that it represents the inverse of the Jacobian’s Frobenius norm (scaled by
o_{i}^{2}
-weighted embedding variance). As stated in the main text, relative changes and comparative values of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
are generally more informative than its absolute magnitude, which depends on
\epsilon
. The hidden state
\bm{h}
was taken from the output of the final transformer layer, just before the unembedding layer.

C.4.1
Geometry Sensitivity Simulation

For each input prompt:
1. The original hidden state
\bm{h}_{\text{orig}}
and output probability distribution
\bm{o}_{\text{orig}}
were obtained.
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}(\bm{h}_{\text{orig}},\mathbf{W}_{\text{orig}})

2. The top

competitor tokens (tokens

3. To create

(
j\in K

\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{cluster}}=\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{orig}}+\alpha(\bm{w}_{\text{top1}}^{\text{orig}}-\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{orig}})

\alpha=0.5
(moving it halfway towards the top-1 token’s embedding). Embeddings of other tokens remained unchanged.

\bm{h}_{\text{orig}}

\bm{o}_{\text{orig}}

4. To create
\mathbf{W}_{\text{disperse}}

\bm{w}_{j}

\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{disperse}}=\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{orig}}-\beta(\bm{w}_{\text{top1}}^{\text{orig}}-\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{orig}})

C.4.2
Systematic Prompt Optimization Protocol

1. Baseline Characterization:

A set of questions (e.g., 50-100 from MMLU/GSM8K) was run with a baseline prompt (e.g., zero-shot for MMLU, 5-shot CoT for GSM8K) across multiple random seeds (e.g.,
S=3

S=5

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}

VCQ (Very Confident Questions)
were defined as those with, e.g., Mean Acc
\geq 0.9

\leq 0.05

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}>T_{H}
,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
Var
<V_{L}

AQ (Ambiguous Questions)
were defined as those with, e.g., Mean Acc
<0.6

>0.15

, or
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
Var
>V_{H}

2.Targeted Prompt Enhancement:

For AQ questions, prompt engineering efforts focused on increasing both Acc and
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}

Refining ICL examples (e.g., ensuring CoT steps are clearer, more analogous to the target problem structure, varying reasoning styles).
Modifying instructional phrases (e.g., adding "Be very careful with calculations," "Explain your reasoning clearly").
Switching prompting strategy (e.g., from 5-shot to 2-shot with very high-quality examples, or adding a self-reflection step).
For MMLU, this could involve adding a directive like "Choose the best option and explain why."
For VCQ questions, efforts might focus on further increasing
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}

2.Evaluation (Unperturbed and Perturbed):

Enhanced prompts were evaluated on the same metrics.
Robustness was tested using perturbed inputs (see
Appendix

C.4.3
Perturbation Strategies for Robustness Evaluation

Perturbations were designed to be plausible minor variations:

•

Syntactic Perturbations (applied to the question text):

–

Paraphrasing: Using a pre-trained paraphrasing model (e.g., T5-based) to generate 2-3 variants of the question.

–

Reordering: For questions with multiple clauses/conditions, reordering them if semantically permissible.

–

Synonym Replacement: Replacing 1-2 keywords with close synonyms.

•

Semantic Perturbations (primarily for ICL / Few-Shot setups):

–

ICL Example Reordering: Changing the order of the few-shot examples in the prompt.

–

ICL Example Replacement: Replacing one of the
k
examples with another valid but perhaps slightly less similar or slightly lower-quality example from the training set.

–

Adding a minor distractor sentence to the prompt context.

The goal was not to make the task unsolvable but to test sensitivity to typical input variations.

Appendix D
Further Discussion and Examples

D.1
Elaboration on Accuracy-Stability Conflict Scenarios

The main text (
Section
4.3
) mentioned four key conflict scenarios. Here are more detailed conceptual examples:

1.Accurate but Unstable (High Acc, Low
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
):

Scenario:
The model correctly answers a complex reasoning question, but its internal state
\bm{h}
is near a decision boundary where a slight internal "wobble" could have led to a different (incorrect) reasoning path and answer.

Example (MMLU-like):
Q: "Which of these legal principles is most directly violated by ex post facto laws?" Correct Answer: "Nulla poena sine lege". Model predicts "Nulla poena sine lege" (Acc=1). However, its
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is low (e.g., 2.1). This might be because the embedding for another plausible but incorrect principle like "Stare decisis" is geometrically positioned such that a small perturbation to
\bm{h}
could shift the logits sufficiently to make "Stare decisis" the top choice. The correct prediction is thus brittle.

2.Inaccurate but Stable (Low Acc, High
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
):

Scenario:
The model makes a clear error, often due to a misinterpretation or flawed reasoning pattern, but it is very robustly committed to this error.

Example (GSM8K-like, from
Table
5
, Row 2):
Felix problem with the misleading "7 days" clarification. The model incorrectly calculates the answer (Acc=0) but does so with high
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
(46.97). It has latched onto a stable, but flawed, interpretation/procedure.

3.Confident but Unstable (High
P(\text{top1})/z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
, but Low
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
):

Scenario:
The output probability for the top token is high, and/or the logit margin to the next token is large, suggesting strong confidence. However, the internal state supporting this is not robust.

Example:
Prompt: "What is the primary ingredient in concrete?" Model predicts "Cement" with
P(\text{Cement})=0.95
and a large logit margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}=5.0
. Superficially, this looks very confident. However,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is low (e.g., 1.8). This could occur if:
The embedding
\bm{w}_{\text{Cement}}
is relatively far from the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
(making the
o_{\text{Cement}}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{\text{Cement}}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\|_{2}^{2}
term large despite
o_{\text{Cement}}^{2}
being large too, if the distance is very large).
Or, many other low-probability competitor embeddings
\{\bm{w}_{j}\}
are clustered very tightly around
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
, leading to many small
o_{j}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})\|_{2}^{2}
terms whose sum is significant, contributing to a large Jacobian norm.
The high probability
P(\text{Cement})
might hide an underlying geometric configuration that is sensitive to perturbation.

4.Uncertain but Stable (Low
P(\text{top1})/z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
, High
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, but High
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
):

Scenario:
The model’s output distribution is relatively flat, indicating uncertainty among several top choices. Yet, the internal state representing this uncertainty is stable.

Example:
Prompt: "Which of these is a common pet: A) Dog, B) Tiger, C) Whale, D) Ant". Model output:
P(\text{Dog})=0.5
,
P(\text{Ant})=0.3
(perhaps due to "common"),
P(\text{Tiger})=0.1
,
P(\text{Whale})=0.1
.
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is relatively high. However,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
could be high if the embeddings
\bm{w}_{\text{Dog}}
and
\bm{w}_{\text{Ant}}
are very close to each other (and thus to
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}(\bm{h})
if they dominate), while
\bm{w}_{\text{Tiger}}
and
\bm{w}_{\text{Whale}}
are far away. The model is stably "stuck" deciding between "Dog" and "Ant", but it’s not about to suddenly jump to "Tiger". The uncertainty itself has a stable geometric basis.

D.3
Differentiating
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
from Perplexity (PPL)

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
assesses internal state robustness, a quality distinct from PPL’s measure of sequence likelihood or token-level prediction confidence. Key differentiators include:

Robustness of (Potentially Erroneous) Confident Predictions:
PPL (or low token probability) flags incorrect predictions (if ground truth is known) but doesn’t indicate if the model is
stably committed
to an error.
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
quantifies this "stable incorrectness" (e.g.,
Table
5
, Row 7, where
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is exceptionally high for an incorrect answer).

Sensitivity to Output Embedding Geometry:
PPL is solely a function of probabilities and thus blind to the geometric arrangement of output embeddings, which critically impacts
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
and the true local stability of the prediction (cf.
Section
4.2
and Eq. (
10
)).

Detection of Local Instabilities During Text Generation:
Sequence-level PPL averages token likelihoods, potentially masking transient points of high internal instability that can precede errors or degenerate loops.
Figure
4
illustrates
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
capturing
sharp dips in
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
(often correlating with
spikes in
P(\text{2nd best token})
).
These crucial local dynamics, indicative of the model teetering between alternatives, are often smoothed over by aggregate PPL but are vital for understanding generation failures.

Appendix F
Statistical Derivation of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
Approximation

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of a
statistical approximation
for the Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
).
This approach models the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
as being drawn from a statistical ensemble and aims to approximate the TCB based on expected values, specifically targeting the root mean square (RMS) norm of the Jacobian.
The resulting formula connects
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
to model parameters like dimensionality and weight variance, as well as higher-order moments of the output probability distribution
\bm{o}
, offering potentially greater accuracy than simpler scaling laws, particularly when the output distribution is not diffuse (i.e., when
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is small).
This statistical perspective contrasts with the exact, non-statistical expression derived in
Section
G
, which applies deterministically to a specific instance of
\mathbf{W}
and
\bm{o}
.

The quantity we aim to approximate is:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\frac{\epsilon}{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}},

where the key terms involved are:

•

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
: The Token Constraint Bound (
Section
2.4
), representing a distance in the hidden state space
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

•

\epsilon>0
: A fixed perturbation threshold, representing the target
L_{2}
change in the output probability vector
\bm{o}
. This is typically treated as a dimensionless small value (e.g., 0.01).

•

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
: Jacobian of the softmax output probabilities
\bm{o}
with respect to the hidden state
\bm{h}
. Its Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
has units of (probability change) / (hidden state unit).

•

\|\cdot\|_{F}
: The Frobenius norm of a matrix.

•

\bm{o}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{\mathcal{V}})^{T}\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
: The vector of softmax output probabilities,
\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}=1
,
o_{i}\geq 0
.

•

S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{k}
: The
k
-th moment sum of the output probabilities. Note
S_{1}=1
.

•

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/S_{2}
: The effective vocabulary size, a measure of distribution flatness (strictly
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}
).

•

\bm{h}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
: The hidden state vector (embedding dimension
d
).

•

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
: The output weight matrix (embedding matrix).

•

\sigma^{2}
: The assumed variance of the elements of
\mathbf{W}
under the statistical model. This is a parameter of the theoretical model. When applied to a real model, it might represent an empirical estimate of the variance relevant to the specific forward pass or training state.

•

\mathcal{V}
: The size of the vocabulary.

Step 1: Recap the Jacobian Matrix
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}
.

As established in (
4
), the Jacobian is:
Here, the matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}
is symmetric and depends only on the output probability vector
\bm{o}
. It encapsulates how changes in the pre-softmax logits
\bm{z}=\mathbf{W}\bm{h}
translate into changes in the post-softmax probabilities
\bm{o}
.

Step 2: Introduce Statistical Assumptions on
\mathbf{W}
.

The core of the statistical approach is to model the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
not as a fixed, trained entity, but as a random matrix drawn from a simple ensemble. We make the following simplifying assumptions about its elements
W_{jk}
:

\mathbf{E}[W_{jk}]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{E}[W_{jk}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}\quad(\text{i.i.d.}).

(12)

These imply
\mathbf{E}[W_{jk}W_{lm}]=\sigma^{2}\delta_{jl}\delta_{km}
. The zero-mean assumption simplifies calculations. The constant variance
\sigma^{2}
captures a typical scale.

Caveat 1 (Model Simplification):
Realistically trained
\mathbf{W}
matrices possess significant structure (e.g., semantic clusters, non-zero mean after layer normalization, varying variances per token, correlations between rows
\bm{w}_{i},\bm{w}_{j}
).
This i.i.d. model ignores such structure.
Specifically, it implies
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]=d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
, assuming orthogonality between rows on average, which might not hold empirically.
For enhanced accuracy, one might incorporate an empirical covariance term
\Sigma_{\text{emp}}
such that
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\approx d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\Sigma_{\text{emp}}
, though this complicates the derivation.

Note on
\sigma^{2}
:

\sigma^{2}
should be interpreted either as a fixed parameter of the theoretical ensemble, or, when connecting to a real model, as an estimate of the empirical variance of weights relevant to the context (e.g., measured during the specific forward pass, possibly after normalization layers).

Step 3: Approximate the Squared Frobenius Norm via Expectation.

We want to estimate
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}
. Under the statistical model,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
is a random variable. We approximate it by its expectation
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\Tr(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W})^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{\top})\right]\quad(\text{using }(AB)^{\top}=B^{\top}A^{\top})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{M})\right]\quad(\text{since }\mathbf{M}\text{ is symmetric})

(13)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\sum_{i,j,l=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}M_{ij}W_{jk}W_{lk}M_{li}\right]\quad(\text{Trace expansion})

(14)

Here we introduce the first major approximation:

Approximation 1 (Decorrelation):
The matrix elements
M_{ij}=\delta_{ij}o_{i}-o_{i}o_{j}
depend on
\bm{o}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\bm{h})
, which itself depends on
\mathbf{W}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{M}
is correlated with
\mathbf{W}
. We make the strong approximation that this correlation can be ignored when computing the expectation involving quadratic terms of
\mathbf{W}
, effectively treating
\mathbf{M}
as constant with respect to the expectation
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\cdot]
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]

\displaystyle\approx\Tr\left(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]\mathbf{M}\right)\quad\textit{(Approximation 1 Applied)}

(15)

Caveat 2 (Decorrelation Risk):
This approximation
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{M}A\mathbf{M}]\approx\mathbf{M}\mathbf{E}[A]\mathbf{M}
can introduce bias.
The correlation is likely non-negligible if dimensions
d
or
\mathcal{V}
are small, or if the distribution
\bm{o}
is highly peaked (low
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, where small changes in
\mathbf{W}
affecting the top logits significantly alter
\mathbf{M}
).
The error magnitude requires careful analysis (e.g., via perturbation theory or empirical validation in problematic regimes).

Now, we use the statistical property from (
12
). The
(j,l)
-th element of
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]
is:

(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}])_{jl}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top})_{jl}]=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d}W_{jk}W_{lk}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[W_{jk}W_{lk}]=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma^{2}\delta_{jl}=d\sigma^{2}\delta_{jl}.

Therefore,
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]=d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Substituting this into (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]

\displaystyle\approx\Tr(\mathbf{M}(d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{V}})\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle=d\sigma^{2}\Tr(\mathbf{M}^{2})\quad(\text{Since }\mathbf{M}^{T}=\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle=d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}.\quad(\text{Definition of }\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{T}))

(16)

This indicates that the expected squared norm of the Jacobian is approximately proportional to the squared norm of the probability-dependent matrix
\mathbf{M}
, scaled by
d\sigma^{2}
. This connection relies critically on Approximation 1.

Step 4: Use the Exact Squared Frobenius Norm of
\mathbf{M}
.

The calculation of
\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=\Tr(\mathbf{M}^{2})
is deterministic once
\bm{o}
is known. As derived in
Section
G
, the exact value is:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}(1-o_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}(o_{i}o_{j})^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(o_{i}^{2}-2o_{i}^{3}+o_{i}^{4})+\sum_{i\neq j}o_{i}^{2}o_{j}^{2}

(18)

\displaystyle=(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{4})+((\sum_{i}o_{i}^{2})(\sum_{j}o_{j}^{2})-\sum_{i}(o_{i}^{2})^{2})

(19)

\displaystyle=S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{4}+S_{2}^{2}-S_{4}

(20)

\displaystyle=S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}.

(21)

This expression relates the norm of
\mathbf{M}
directly to the second (
S_{2}
) and third (
S_{3}
) moments of the output probability distribution. Note that this step involves no statistical approximation itself.

Step 5: Approximate the Jacobian Norm using RMS.

We now introduce the second key approximation:

Approximation 2 (RMS Substitution):
We approximate the actual Jacobian norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
for a specific
\mathbf{W}
by its root mean square (RMS) value under the statistical ensemble model:

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]}.

(22)

This relies on the assumption that the random variable
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
concentrates around its RMS value (related to concentration of measure phenomena, plausible for large
d
or
\mathcal{V}
).
However, it remains an approximation.
By Jensen’s inequality,
\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[X^{2}]}\geq\mathbf{E}[X]
, so the RMS value typically overestimates the expected norm.
The magnitude of this overestimation (and the validity of concentration) depends on the variance of
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
, which might be large if the distribution of norms is heavy-tailed (e.g., due to specific weight structures or sparse outputs).
More rigorous analysis might require bounding
\operatorname{Var}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}]
or using concentration inequalities (e.g., Hanson-Wright), which is beyond the scope of this derivation.

Substituting the result for the expected squared norm from (
16
) and the exact expression for
\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}
from (
21
) into (
22
):

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}}=\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2})}.

(23)

This equation provides our refined statistical approximation for the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian.

Step 6: Refined Approximation for
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

Using the definition
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\epsilon/\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
and substituting the RMS approximation for the norm from (
23
), we obtain the refined statistical approximation for the Token Constraint Bound:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2})}}.

(24)

This is the main result of this section. It provides an estimate for
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
based on the model’s embedding dimension (
d
), the assumed variance of its output weights (
\sigma^{2}
), and the second (
S_{2}
) and third (
S_{3}
) moments of its current output probability distribution (
\bm{o}
).

Step 7: Connection to the Simpler
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
-based Approximation.

A commonly cited simpler approximation for
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
relates it directly to the effective vocabulary size
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/S_{2}
, often presented as
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\approx\epsilon\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}/(d\sigma^{2})}
. We see how this arises from (
24
) by introducing an
additional
approximation:

Approximation 3 (Diffuse Distribution):
Assume
\bm{o}
is sufficiently diffuse, meaning
p_{\max}=\max_{i}o_{i}\mathbf{l}1
, corresponding to large effective vocabulary size,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1
.
Under this condition, higher moments
S_{k}
become small relative to lower moments. As argued in
Section
J
((
51
)), if
o_{i}
are roughly uniform over
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
tokens (
o_{i}\sim 1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
), then
S_{3}\approx S_{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
S_{2}^{2}\approx S_{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
. Thus, for large
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
{|-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}|}\mathbf{l}S_{2}
.

This approximation
S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}\approx S_{2}
might fail if the distribution is not sufficiently "uniform-like" even if
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is large (e.g., heavy-tailed distributions where a few moderately high probabilities contribute significantly to
S_{3}
).
Subject to this approximation:

S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}\approx S_{2}\quad(\text{Approximation 3: Diffuse distribution, }\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1).

(25)

Substituting into (
24
):

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}S_{2}}}=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}{d\sigma^{2}}}.

This shows the simpler
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
-based formula is a special case relying on both the statistical model and the diffuse distribution assumption.

Appendix G
Exact, Non-Statistical Expression for
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
and Its Relation to Weighted Variance

This section presents the derivation of the exact mathematical expression for the squared Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
for a
specific
, given output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
and hidden state
\bm{h}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
(which together determine a specific probability vector
\bm{o}=\softmax(\mathbf{W}\bm{h})
). This derivation is purely algebraic and deterministic; it does not rely on any statistical assumptions about
\mathbf{W}
being drawn from a random ensemble. We establish the correct formula and clarify its relationship to, but distinctness from, the trace of the probability-weighted covariance matrix of the embeddings.

Notation Recap:

Step 1: Squared Frobenius Norm as Sum of Squared Row Norms.

The squared Frobenius norm is the sum of the squared Euclidean norms of its rows. Let
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
denote the
i
-th row of the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}
.

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}.

(26)

Step 2: Derive the Expression for a Jacobian Row.

We need the components of the
i
-th row,
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)=[(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{i1},\dots,(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{id}]
. Recall
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}=\bm{A}(\bm{o})\mathbf{W}
. The
(i,k)
-th element is:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}A_{ij}W_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}(\delta_{ij}o_{i}-o_{i}o_{j})W_{jk}\quad(\text{Definition of }A_{ij})

\displaystyle=(o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}^{2}W_{ik})+\sum_{j\neq i}(-o_{i}o_{j})W_{jk}\quad(\text{Splitting sum: }j=i\text{ and }j\neq i)

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(o_{i}W_{ik}+\sum_{j\neq i}o_{j}W_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}\right)\quad(\text{Recombining sum})

\displaystyle=o_{i}(W_{ik}-(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}))

\displaystyle=o_{i}((\bm{w}_{i})_{k}-(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k})=o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k}

(27)

Here,
W_{jk}=(\bm{w}_{j})_{k}
is the
k
-th component of embedding
\bm{w}_{j}
, and
(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk})
is the
k
-th component of the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
.
Thus, the entire
i
-th row vector (transposed to match
\bm{w}_{i},\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
as column vectors) is:

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)^{\top}=o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}).

(28)

This shows that the
i
-th row of the Jacobian represents the deviation of the
i
-th embedding from the mean embedding, scaled by the probability
o_{i}
.

Step 3: Substitute Row Norm back into Frobenius Norm Definition.

Using the row expression (
28
) in the definition (
26
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad(\text{Since }o_{i}\text{ is a scalar}).

(29)

This is the exact, non-statistical expression for the squared Frobenius norm of the Jacobian matrix.

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(30)

Step 4: Introduce the Trace of the Weighted Covariance Matrix.

A related quantity often considered is the trace of the probability-weighted covariance matrix of the embedding vectors:
Its trace represents the total variance of the embedding vectors, weighted by the probability distribution
\bm{o}
:

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\Tr\left[(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\right]\quad(\text{Linearity of trace})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})\quad(\text{Using }\Tr(\bm{a}\bm{b}^{\top})=\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(31)

This can also be expressed using the variance identity:

\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\right]-\|\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(32)

Step 5: Comparing the Jacobian Norm and the Covariance Trace.

Let us compare the exact squared Jacobian norm (
30
) with the trace of the covariance matrix (
31
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(34)

Step 6: The Exact Expression for
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

Using the correct formula for the squared Jacobian norm (
30
), the exact Token Constraint Bound is:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\frac{\epsilon}{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}}=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\left\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}}.

(35)

This is the ground-truth value for
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
given a specific
\mathbf{W}
and
\bm{h}
(determining
\bm{o}
).

Appendix H
Relating the Statistical Approximation and the Exact Expression

This section elucidates the precise relationship between the
refined statistical approximation
for
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
(derived in
Section
F
) and the
exact, non-statistical expression
for
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
(derived in
Section
G
). Understanding this connection is crucial for interpreting the validity and limitations of the statistical approach and for appreciating why it can serve as a useful, interpretable model despite its simplifying assumptions.

Recap: The Two Formulas for
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
.

1. Refined Statistical Approximation (
Section
F
):
This approach models
\mathbf{W}
as a random matrix. The core result approximates the actual squared norm by its expected value under the statistical model:

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]\approx d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}),

(36)

where
\mathbf{M}=\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top}
,
\sigma^{2}
is the assumed variance of
W_{jk}
,
d
is the embedding dimension, and
S_{k}=\sum_{i}o_{i}^{k}
. The final TCB approximation (
24
) uses the root mean square (RMS) value derived from this expectation:
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]}
.

2. Exact Expression (
Section
G
):
For a specific, given
\mathbf{W}
and
\bm{o}
, the squared norm is calculated deterministically using the geometry of the embeddings and the probability distribution:

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}=\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\right]-\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\bm{w}\right]\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2},

(37)

where
\bm{w}_{i}
is the
i
-th embedding vector (row of
\mathbf{W}
),
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\bm{w}\right]=\sum_{j}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}
, and
\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]
is the trace of the probability-weighted covariance matrix of the embeddings. The exact TCB (
35
) uses the square root of this value.

The Fundamental Connection: Expectation Bridge.

The theoretical link between these two expressions lies in taking the expectation of the
exact
squared norm (
37
) over the statistical ensemble assumed for
\mathbf{W}
. When we apply the statistical assumptions ((
12
)) and the key decorrelation approximation (
Approximation 1
from
Section
F
, see (
15
)) to the exact formula, we recover the core quantity from the statistical approximation:

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\underbrace{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}}_{\text{Exact value from \eqref{eq:app_recap_exact_sq}}}\right]=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\left[\Tr(\bm{A}(\bm{o})\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\bm{A}(\bm{o}))\right]\approx\underbrace{d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}}_{\text{Statistical result \eqref{eq:app_recap_stat_sq}}}.

(38)

Let’s briefly trace why this works. The calculation performed in
Step 3
of
Section
F
(starting from
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{M})]
) effectively computes the expectation of the exact squared norm, represented in the trace form old. The crucial step is applying
Approximation 1
:

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{M})]\approx\Tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]\mathbf{M}).

This approximation treats
\mathbf{M}=\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top}
as fixed when taking the expectation over
\mathbf{W}
, even though
\bm{o}
itself depends on
\mathbf{W}
via the softmax. Using
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]_{ij}=\Tr(\mathbf{E}[\bm{w}_{i}\bm{w}_{j}^{\top}])=\sum_{k}\mathbf{E}[W_{ik}W_{jk}]=\sum_{k}\sigma^{2}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kk}=d\sigma^{2}\delta_{ij}
, we get
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]=d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Substituting this yields:

\Tr(\mathbf{M}(d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{V}})\mathbf{M})=d\sigma^{2}\Tr(\mathbf{M}^{2})=d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}.

This confirms that
d\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2})
is indeed the result of calculating the expected value of the exact squared norm under the statistical assumptions and the decorrelation approximation.

Role of Approximations Revisited.

The difference between the final
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
value predicted by the statistical approximation (
24
) and the exact value (
35
) for a specific model arises precisely from the approximations inherent in the statistical derivation:

Statistical Ensemble Model for
\mathbf{W}
:
The actual trained weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
is treated as a typical realization from a simple statistical ensemble (i.i.d., zero-mean, variance
\sigma^{2}
elements, see (
12
)). Real trained matrices have complex structure (correlations, non-zero mean, varying variances) not captured by this model. The approximation’s accuracy depends on how well the ensemble captures the properties relevant to the norm calculation (specifically, the second moments involved in
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}
).

Decorrelation (
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{W}
):
The calculation in (
38
) relies on treating the probability-dependent matrix
\mathbf{M}
as approximately uncorrelated with the quadratic terms in
\mathbf{W}
(like
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}
) when taking the expectation
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{W}}
(
Approximation 1
, (
15
)). This approximation ignores the feedback loop where
\mathbf{W}
determines
\bm{o}
, which in turn determines
\mathbf{M}
. It may be justified by averaging effects in high dimensions (
d,\mathcal{V}
), but it introduces a deviation from the exact expectation.

RMS Approximation (
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}]}
):
The final step in the statistical approximation replaces the actual norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
for the specific
\mathbf{W}
with the ensemble-averaged RMS value (
Approximation 2
, (
22
)). This assumes that the norm of the specific matrix is close to the average norm across the ensemble, relying on concentration of measure phenomena. While often plausible for high-dimensional random matrices, the actual norm can deviate from the average.

In summary, the refined statistical TCB approximation (
24
) estimates the TCB by replacing the exact Jacobian norm
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
with
\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2})}
, which represents the approximate RMS norm expected under the simplified statistical model and decorrelation assumption.

Appendix I
Derivation of the Exact Jacobian Norm Formula

In this appendix, we provide a detailed step-by-step derivation of the exact mathematical expression for the squared Frobenius norm of the output Jacobian matrix,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}
. This derivation is performed for a
specific
instance of the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times d}
and the hidden state
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which together determine the output probability vector
\bm{o}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\bm{h})
. The derivation relies solely on the definitions of the Jacobian and the Frobenius norm, requiring no statistical assumptions about
\mathbf{W}
.

The final result relates
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
to a weighted sum of squared distances involving the output embedding vectors. We also clarify its relationship to the trace of the probability-weighted covariance matrix of the embeddings, a concept central to related analyses but distinct from the Jacobian norm itself.

Step 1: Definition of Squared Frobenius Norm via Rows.

The squared Frobenius norm of any matrix is the sum of the squared Euclidean norms (
L_{2}
norms) of its rows. Let
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)
denote the
i
-th row vector of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}
.

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}.

(39)

Step 2: Jacobian Definition and its Relation to Weights.

Recall the Jacobian matrix is given by
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}=\frac{\partial\bm{o}}{\partial\bm{h}}
. Using the chain rule, we can write:

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}=\frac{\partial\bm{o}}{\partial\bm{z}}\frac{\partial\bm{z}}{\partial\bm{h}}=(\diag(\bm{o})-\bm{o}\bm{o}^{\top})\mathbf{W}=\bm{A}(\bm{o})\mathbf{W}.

The element
(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}
represents
\frac{\partial o_{i}}{\partial h_{k}}
.

Step 3: Explicit Calculation of the
i
-th Row of the Jacobian.

We aim to find the vector
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)=[(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{i1},(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{i2},\dots,(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{id}]
. The component
(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}
is the
(i,k)
-th element of the matrix product
\bm{A}(\bm{o})\mathbf{W}
:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}A_{ij}W_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}(\delta_{ij}o_{i}-o_{i}o_{j})W_{jk}\quad(\text{Definition of }A_{ij})

\displaystyle=(o_{i}W_{ik})+\sum_{j\neq i}(-o_{i}o_{j})W_{jk}

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}o_{j}W_{jk}

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}-o_{i}W_{ik}\right)\quad(\text{Completing the sum over }j)

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}\right)+o_{i}^{2}W_{ik}\quad

Let’s restart the derivation for
(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}
more directly:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}A_{ij}W_{jk}

\displaystyle=A_{ii}W_{ik}+\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij}W_{jk}\quad(\text{Separating diagonal term})

\displaystyle=o_{i}(1-o_{i})W_{ik}+\sum_{j\neq i}(-o_{i}o_{j})W_{jk}\quad(\text{Substituting }A_{ij}\text{ values})

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}^{2}W_{ik}-\sum_{j\neq i}o_{i}o_{j}W_{jk}

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(o_{i}W_{ik}+\sum_{j\neq i}o_{j}W_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}\right)\quad(\text{Recombining sum})

(40)

Now, let’s interpret the terms.
W_{jk}
is the
k
-th component of the
j
-th embedding vector
\bm{w}_{j}
. Let
w_{j,k}
denote this component.

\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}W_{jk}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}w_{j,k}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}\right)_{k}=(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k}

where
(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k}
is the
k
-th component of the mean embedding vector
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
.
Substituting this back into (
40
):

(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}})_{ik}=o_{i}W_{ik}-o_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k}=o_{i}(W_{ik}-(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k})=o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})_{k}

(41)

This expression gives the
k
-th component of the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}
. Therefore, the
i
-th row vector itself is:

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)=o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}.

(42)

This shows that each row of the Jacobian is the deviation of the corresponding embedding vector
\bm{w}_{i}
from the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
, scaled by the probability
o_{i}
.

Step 4: Substitute Row Norm back into Frobenius Norm Definition.

Now we substitute the expression for the
i
-th row (
42
) back into the definition of the squared Frobenius norm (
39
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}\|o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad(\text{Since }o_{i}\text{ is a scalar}).

(43)

This equation provides the exact, non-statistical expression for the squared Frobenius norm of the Jacobian. It is determined by the squared distances between each embedding vector
\bm{w}_{i}
and the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
, weighted by the square of the corresponding probability
o_{i}^{2}
.

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(44)

Step 5: Relationship to the Trace of the Covariance Matrix.

The trace of the probability-weighted covariance matrix of the embedding vectors is a closely related but distinct concept. The covariance matrix is defined as:
Its trace is:

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\Tr\left[(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}\right]\quad(\text{Linearity of trace})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})^{\top}(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}})\quad(\text{Using }\Tr(\bm{a}\bm{b}^{\top})=\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(45)

As derived previously, this trace also equals the variance identity:

\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\right]-\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}}\left[\bm{w}\right]\|_{2}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(46)

This quantity,
\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]
, represents the total variance of the embedding vectors, weighted by the probabilities
o_{i}
.

Step 6: Comparing Jacobian Norm and Covariance Trace.

Comparing the derived exact squared Jacobian norm (
43
) with the trace of the covariance matrix (
45
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(47)

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{o}}\left(\bm{w}\right)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(48)

These two quantities are clearly different, differing by a factor of
o_{i}
in the weighting term inside the sum. While both measure aspects of the dispersion of the embedding vectors relative to their mean
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
, they are not mathematically identical. The Jacobian norm gives more weight (via
o_{i}^{2}
) to the deviation of high-probability embeddings from the mean.

Appendix J
Derivation of Regime-Dependent TCB Behavior

In this appendix, we provide detailed mathematical derivations supporting the regime-dependent behavior of the Token Constraint Bound (
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
), as discussed in
Section
3
and observed empirically
Table
1
. We analyze the behavior of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\epsilon/\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}
in two distinct regimes based on the flatness of the output probability distribution
\bm{o}
: high flatness (large effective vocabulary size,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1
) and low flatness (highly peaked distribution,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1
).

The key is to understand how the squared Frobenius norm of the Jacobian,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}
, behaves in these limits. We will use different but related expressions for the norm depending on the regime: the statistical approximation for the high-
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
regime and the exact formula for the low-
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
regime.

Recap of Key Formulas:

1.

Exact Squared Norm (from
Section
I
, Eq. (
43
)):

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}

(49)

where
\bm{w}_{i}
is the
i
-th embedding vector (row of
\mathbf{W}
),
\bm{o}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{\mathcal{V}})
is the probability vector, and
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}
is the probability-weighted mean embedding.

2.

Refined Statistical Approximation (from
Section
F
, Eq. (
23
)):

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{h})\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}(S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2})}

(50)

where
d
is the hidden dimension,
\sigma^{2}
is the assumed variance of weight elements
W_{jk}
, and
S_{k}=\sum_{i}o_{i}^{k}
is the
k
-th moment sum (
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/S_{2}
). This approximation relies on modeling
\mathbf{W}
statistically and approximating the norm by its RMS value.

J.1
Regime 1: High Flatness (Large
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1
)

Assumptions.

In this regime, the probability distribution
\bm{o}
is spread relatively evenly across many tokens.

•

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/S_{2}
is large.

•

Consequently, the maximum probability
p_{\max}=\max_{i}o_{i}
must be small (
p_{\max}\mathbf{l}1
). Roughly, if probabilities are spread over
\sim\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
tokens, then
o_{i}\sim 1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

•

We use the refined statistical approximation (
50
), which implicitly assumes the statistical model for
\mathbf{W}
(i.i.d. elements, zero mean, variance
\sigma^{2}
) is a reasonable proxy for average behavior.

Approximating the Jacobian Norm.

We analyze the term
\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}
within the statistical approximation (
50
). Since
o_{i}\mathbf{l}1
for all
i
, higher powers of
o_{i}
are much smaller. Let’s assess the magnitude of the terms relative to
S_{2}
:

•

S_{2}=\sum_{i}o_{i}^{2}=1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

•

S_{3}=\sum_{i}o_{i}^{3}\leq(\max_{j}o_{j})\sum_{i}o_{i}^{2}=p_{\max}S_{2}
. If
o_{i}\sim 1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, then
S_{3}\sim\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\times(1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}})^{3}=1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}=S_{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

•

S_{2}^{2}=(1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}})^{2}=S_{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Thus, both
S_{3}
and
S_{2}^{2}
are smaller than
S_{2}
by a factor of approximately
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
. Since we assume
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1
, the terms
-2S_{3}
and
+S_{2}^{2}
become negligible compared to
S_{2}
:

\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=S_{2}-2S_{3}+S_{2}^{2}\approx S_{2}\quad(\text{for large }\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}).

(51)

This simplification corresponds to Approximation 3 discussed in
Section
F
. Substituting this back into the statistical norm approximation (
50
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}(S_{2})}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

(53)

This expression predicts that the Jacobian norm decreases as
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
increases.

Behavior of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

Using the definition
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\epsilon/\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
and the approximation (
53
):

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}{d\sigma^{2}}}.

(54)

Conclusion (High
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
):
In the high-flatness regime,
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is predicted to be approximately proportional to the square root of the effective vocabulary size:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\propto\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}\quad(\text{for }\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg 1)

This provides a clear mathematical basis for the strong positive correlation
r_{\delta,\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}\approx 0.95
observed empirically (
Table
1
) in diverse datasets where distributions are often flat.

Negligible Influence of Margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
.

The margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}=z_{k}-z_{j^{*}}
between the logits of two specific tokens
k
(usually the top prediction) and
j^{*}
(usually the top competitor) directly affects
o_{k}
and
o_{j^{*}}
. In the high-
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
regime, however, both
o_{k}
and
o_{j^{*}}
are typically small (e.g.,
\sim 1/\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
). Changes in
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
primarily redistribute a small amount of probability mass between these two (and possibly nearby) tokens. The impact on the overall sum
S_{2}=\sum o_{i}^{2}
, which aggregates contributions from many small probabilities, is minimal. Consequently, changes in
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
have a very weak effect on
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
via (
53
), and therefore also on
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
. This explains the near-zero correlation
r_{\delta,z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}
observed in diverse datasets (
Table
1
).

J.2
Regime 2: Low Flatness (Small
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1
)

Assumptions.

In this regime, the probability distribution
\bm{o}
is sharply peaked on a single token.

•

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1
. This occurs when one probability, say
o_{k}
, is close to 1.

•

Let
o_{k}=1-\epsilon_{s}
, where
\epsilon_{s}=\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}
is a small positive quantity (
\epsilon_{s}\mathbf{l}1
).

•

All other probabilities
o_{j}
(
j\neq k
) are very small, typically
o_{j}\sim O(\epsilon_{s})
or smaller.

•

We use the exact expression for the norm (
49
) as it directly captures the influence of the specific dominant embedding
\bm{w}_{k}
and its relation to competitors. Statistical averaging inherent in (
50
) is less appropriate here.

Approximating the Jacobian Norm.

We analyze the exact sum
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}
.
First, approximate the mean embedding
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{V}}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}=o_{k}\bm{w}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}

(55)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{s})\bm{w}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}

(56)

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{k}-\epsilon_{s}\bm{w}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}\bm{w}_{j}

(57)

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\quad(\text{using }\epsilon_{s}=\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j})

(58)

This shows
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}
is close to
\bm{w}_{k}
, differing by terms of order
O(\epsilon_{s})
.

Now, consider the terms in the sum for
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
:

•

Term for
i=k
:

We need
\|\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}
. From (
58
):

\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}=-\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})

The squared norm is
\|\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Since each
o_{j}=O(\epsilon_{s})
for
j\neq k
, this squared norm is
O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})
. The contribution to the total sum is
o_{k}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\approx(1-\epsilon_{s})^{2}O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})\approx O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})
. This term is therefore negligible to the leading order.

•

Terms for
i\neq k
:

We need
\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Using (
58
):

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{i}-\left(\bm{w}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k})-\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})

The squared norm is:

\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k})-\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}

Expanding the square:

\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}-2(\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k})^{\top}\left(\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\right)+\left\|\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}(\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}

The middle term is
O(\epsilon_{s})
, and the last term is
O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})
. Thus, to leading order:

\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\approx\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon_{s})\quad(\text{for }i\neq k)

The contribution of the
i
-th term (
i\neq k
) to the total sum
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
is
o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Since
o_{i}^{2}=O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})
, the contribution is:

o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\approx o_{i}^{2}(\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon_{s}))=o_{i}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon_{s}^{3})

Summing the contributions for all
i
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{o_{k}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\approx O(\epsilon_{s}^{2})}+\sum_{j\neq k}\underbrace{o_{j}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\approx o_{j}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon_{s}^{3})}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}^{2}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\quad(\text{keeping leading order terms, }O(\epsilon_{s}^{2}))

(59)

This approximation reveals that for highly peaked distributions, the squared Jacobian norm is dominated by the sum of squared distances between the dominant embedding
\bm{w}_{k}
and competitor embeddings
\bm{w}_{j}
, weighted by the
square
of the competitors’ small probabilities
o_{j}^{2}
.

Connecting to Margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
.

The logit margin between the winning token
k
and any other token
j
is
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}=z_{k}-z_{j}
. The probability
o_{j}
for
j\neq k
can be approximated using the softmax definition when
z_{k}
is large compared to others:

o_{j}=\frac{e^{z_{j}}}{\sum_{l=1}^{\mathcal{V}}e^{z_{l}}}=\frac{e^{z_{j}}}{e^{z_{k}}+\sum_{l\neq k}e^{z_{l}}}\approx\frac{e^{z_{j}}}{e^{z_{k}}(1+\sum_{l\neq k}e^{z_{l}-z_{k}})}\approx\frac{e^{z_{j}}}{e^{z_{k}}}=e^{-(z_{k}-z_{j})}=e^{-z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}

This approximation holds because
\sum_{l\neq k}e^{z_{l}-z_{k}}=\sum_{l\neq k}o_{l}/o_{k}\approx\epsilon_{s}/(1-\epsilon_{s})\approx\epsilon_{s}\mathbf{l}1
.
Substituting this into the norm approximation (
59
):

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}\approx\sum_{j\neq k}(e^{-z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}})^{2}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\neq k}e^{-2z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

(60)

The specific margin defined as
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}=z_{k}-z_{j^{*}}
, where
j^{*}
is the top competitor (highest logit
z_{j}
among
j\neq k
), corresponds to the term with the largest
e^{-2z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}
(smallest
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
) in the sum, which often dominates the sum’s value.

Behavior of
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

As the margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
increases, the corresponding
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
for the closest competitors also increases. This leads to an exponential decrease in the terms
e^{-2z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}
in (
60
). Consequently,
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
decreases strongly (exponentially) as
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
increases.
Since
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}=\epsilon/\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}
, an increase in
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
causes a decrease in the denominator, leading to an
increase
in
\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}
.

Conclusion (Low
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
):
In the low-flatness regime, TCB increases rapidly as the logit margin
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
increases:

\delta_{\mathrm{TCB}}\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j\neq k}e^{-2(z_{k}-z_{j})}\|\bm{w}_{j}-\bm{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}}\approx\text{Increases strongly with }z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}\quad(\text{for }\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1)

This derivation provides the theoretical underpinning for the strong positive correlation
r_{\delta,z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}}\approx 0.62
observed empirically in the low-
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
data subset (
Table
1
).

Negligible Influence of Residual
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
Variation.

In this regime,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is already close to 1. Small changes in the probability distribution (e.g., caused by changing
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
) lead to minuscule changes in
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
. Specifically,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/S_{2}=1/(o_{k}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq k}o_{j}^{2})
. As
o_{k}\approx 1
and
o_{j}=O(\epsilon_{s})
, we have
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1/(1-2\epsilon_{s}+O(\epsilon_{s}^{2}))
. While changes in
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
affect
\epsilon_{s}
and thus cause small fluctuations in
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, these variations are vastly outweighed by the direct exponential impact of
z_{k}-z_{j^{\ast}}
on
\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
via (
60
). This explains why the correlation
r_{\delta,\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}}
drops to nearly zero (
\approx 0.08
) in the low-
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}
regime.
