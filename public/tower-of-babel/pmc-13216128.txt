Influence of a static magnetic field on the antioxidant properties of selected active substances obtained from the pomegranate
Authors: Agata Krawczyk, Agnieszka Synowiec-Wojtarowicz, Agnieszka Kosowska, Magdalena Kimsa-Dudek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackrgroundLittle is known about the use of static magnetic fields in dermatology and cosmetology. It is hypothesized that this type of magnetic field may modify the strength of biological properties of active compounds into skincare products. Among all of the ingredients, those with antioxidant properties are the most sought-after by consumers. The aim of the study was to evaluate the antioxidant properties of selected compounds that had been extracted from pomegranates as well as to assess changes in them following exposure to a static magnetic field.Methods and ResultsThe research model was a human normal epidermal keratinocyte cell culture treated with a stressor—hydrogen peroxide solution—as well as with appropriate concentrations of the test compounds and exposed to a static magnetic field. Antioxidant gene expression (RT-qPCR) and antioxidant enzyme activity were assessed. It has also been determined production of reactive oxygien species During the study, decreases in the expression of the tested antioxidant genes were observed under the influence of a stressor. The use of the tested compounds resulted in statistically significant increases in the expression of these genes in the experimental variants exposed to oxidative stress, while the static magnetic field led to an enhancement of their activity. The activity of antioxidant enzymes was consistent with the molecular results.ConclusionsThe obtained results confirm that ellagic and punicic acids exhibit antioxidant activity, and the expression of antioxidant genes and protein products increases under the influence of a static magnetic field. This indicates a magnetically induced cell defense reaction to neutralize the increased production of reactive oxygen species.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of magnetic fields (MFs) in medicine has been increasingly described [1–2]. Numerous studies are being conducted on their mechanism of action, demonstrating their effectiveness and safety [3]. MFs are used in both the diagnosis [4] and treatment of many types of diseases, such as muscular and skeletal diseases, osteoporosis, and gastrointestinal diseases [5–6]. Obtaining positive therapeutic effects is possible thanks to the anti-inflammatory, anti-edematous, and regenerative properties of MFs [7–8]. Magnetotherapy and magnetostimulation are widely described in the context of physical treatments, while in the field of cosmetology, low-frequency variable magnetic fields (VMFs) are successfully used for wrinkle reduction, improvement of skin firmness and elasticity, regeneration, and the elimination of inflammation including acne vulgaris [9–10]. Static magnetic fields (SMFs) are not as popular, and therefore, have not been widely described in cosmetology. However, there are reports that they may facilitate the action/penetration of active substances [11].

Given the increasing public awareness of health and beauty, interest in active ingredients of a natural origin is growing, especially those that are used in cosmetic products [12]. The pomegranate is becoming a particularly interesting raw material, providing a source of, among others, punicic acid (an unsaturated omega-5 fatty acid), ellagic acid (a phenolic acid), punicin, isoquercetin (polyphenols), and stearic, oleic as well as linoleic acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) [13]. These compounds significantly strengthen blood vessels, nourish the skin, and improve its elasticity. Furthermore, they also strengthen hair and nails. They have been demonstrated to have anti-carcinogenic, anti-aging, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects [14].

The antioxidant properties of biologically active compounds are related to maintaining redox homeostasis. This is crucial for the proper functioning of the body including the skin. Any abnormalities in this matter are associated with oxidative stress, i.e., an imbalance between the activity of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the biological capacity to detoxify reactive intermediates [15]. Although ROS participate in the physiological processes, an excess of them can negatively impact body function. Primarily, it can lead to DNA damage. This situation promotes aging, the development of many diseases including neurodegenerative ones, and the disruption of the protective barrier of the skin and dermis [16]. Therefore, to prevent the adverse effects of free radicals (FR), it is recommended that an adequate supply of antioxidants be ensured in both the diet and in cosmetic products. The mechanism of action of antioxidants has been observed through their influence on molecular processes, which is reflected in the phenotypic picture [17].

Given the increasingly dynamic development of the cosmetics industry, it is becoming increasingly important to understand the properties of active substances that are derived from plants. Considering the high exposure to ROS, from the consumer perspective, the antioxidant properties of cosmetic products seem to be crucial [18]. Cosmetic companies are constantly seeking new formulations and technological solutions.

Furthermore, in light of the information presented, the influence of SMFs on the effect of substances on cells becomes a significant issue. This issue is of interest to practicing cosmetologists and dermatologists who utilize new technologies in their work. Therefore, the primary goal of the study was to assess the antioxidant properties of selected substances obtained from pomegranates: punicic acid and ellagic acid. Furthermore, preliminary assessments were undertaken to assess the effect of SMF generated by permanent magnets on the modification of the antioxidant properties of the tested compounds extracted from pomegranate in terms of skincare. From a molecular perspective, such an effect is best assessed by analyzing changes in the expression of the relevant genes.

Materials and methods

Cell culture conditions

The research was conducted using the normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEK cell line, Clonetics Corporation, 00192627, San Diego, CA, USA) that had been treated with punicic acid (PA) and ellagic acid (EA)—as selected active compounds that are obtained from pomegranates. Additionally, the cell cultures were then exposed to a moderate strength SMF.

The NHEK cells were cultured in a KBM medium (Keratinocyte Cell Basal Medium, KBM-Gold™, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) with supplements and growth factors: bovine pituitary extract (BPE), human epidermal growth factor (hEGF), insulin, hydrocortisone, epinephrine, transferrin, and gentamicin/amphotericin-B (KGM® Gold SingleQuots, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). The cell culture was grown in an incubator (Heraeus, Hanau, Germany) that provided the appropriate culture conditions: 5% CO2 and 37 °C. 0.4% trypan blue was used to assess the cell count and viability. Measurements were taken on a CountessTM Automated Cell Counter (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Cytotoxicity of the tested compounds

The MTT 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide method was used to assess the cytotoxicity of the tested compounds: punicic acid—PA (name: 9(Z),11(E),13(Z)-Octadecatrienoic Acid; Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA) and ellagic acid—EA (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). The following concentrations were used for the test: 1 µg/ml–200 µg/ml for PA and 0.5µM-5µM for EA, the selection of which was made based on the available scientific literature. For this study, the cells were seeded on a 96-well culture plate with a density of 5,000 cells/well. The EA was weighed and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), the concentration of which did not exceed 1%. Stock solutions of the tested compounds were prepared in the culture medium, which formed the basis for the preparation of the individual tested concentrations. Twenty-four hours after cell seeding, the culture medium was changed to one containing the test compounds at various concentrations. After 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was removed and the MTT solution (1 mg/ml) was added. The duration of the incubation was three hours at 37 °C. After removing the MTT solution and adding 100 µl of DMSO to each well, the absorbance (540 nm) was measured using a Wallace 1420 VICTOR microplate reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The effect of the hydrogen peroxide solution H2O2 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) on the keratinocyte cultures, which served as a stressor in the experiment, was also examined. Concentrations between 10µM and 500µM were toxic to the keratinocyte cell cultures.

Exposure to SMF/Experimental variants

The following experimental variants were used: cells that had not been treated with a stressor or test substance, cells that had not been treated with a stressor or test substance and placed in PLACEBO magnets, cells that had been exposed to H2O2, cells treated with a test substance (PA or EA), cells that had been exposed to H2O2 and then treated with a test substance (PA or EA), cells that had been exposed to H2O2 and then treated with a test substance (PA or EA) and simultaneously exposed to SMF, and cells that that had not been treated with a stressor or test substance and then exposed to an SMF.

Based on the available literature and considering the preliminary nature of the study, a 24-hour incubation period was selected for the test cells with an SMF. An SMF with an induction of 0.7 T was generated in special chambers that are designed for in vitro studies of the effects of an SMF [19- 20]. The chambers are constructed of a ferromagnetic yoke and permanent magnets, and include front, back, and side walls. The culture flask is placed in the chamber through a window on the front wall of the chamber. The dimensions of the window correspond to the dimensions of the culture flask. The designed system ensures uniform distribution of magnetic flux density. A magnetic flux density of 0.0 T was achieved in the control chamber. This is due to the different design: the absence of permanent magnets and the presence of steel elements. An illustration of the chamber can be found in our earlier manuscript [21].

Oxidative stress was induced by adding H2O2 at an appropriate concentration for three hours. After this time, the medium was changed to one that was consistent with the experimental variant or the culture flask was placed in an appropriate SMF-generating chamber. Cultivation was conducted under standard culture conditions – 5% CO2 and 37 °C.

Each experimental group included two biological replicates. The experiments for molecular analyses and for biochemical analyses were performed separately.

RNA extraction

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to extract the extract total RNA [22]. The extracts were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively using agarose gel electrophoresis and a MaestroNano MN-913 spectrophotometer (MaestroGen Inc., Las Vegas, NV, USA).

Biochemical assays

The biochemical studies included determining the activity of SOD, CAT, and GPx in the cell lysates. For this purpose, cell lysates were prepared 24 h after from the start of the experiment. The lysis buffer consisted of 14 mg of protease inhibitor, 100 µl of the phosphatase inhibitor, and 10 ml of the PBS solution. Cell pellets were dissolved in the prepared buffer—100 µl of buffer per million viable cells. Dedicated reagent sets were used for biochemical determinations (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol and the results of biochemical analyses were calculated per 106 cells. Measurements were taken spectrophotometrically. The SOD activity, measured at a wavelength of 440–460 nm, was determined by reacting xanthine with a tetrazolium salt to form formazan. Formaldehyde, a product of the CAT reaction with methanol, was used to determine the catalase activity. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 540 nm. GPx activity was determined (at a wavelength of 340 nm) as the result of the reduction of cumene hydroperoxide to oxidized glutathione. All biochemical assays were performed in at least three replicates.

ROS—determining production

Flow cytometry (Attune™ NxT flow cytometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used to determine intracellular ROS production. H2O2 (500 µM) was used as a positive control. The fluorescence intensity of DCF reflected the amount of intracellular ROS.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using STATISTICA 13.3 software (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA) and the level of significance was set at p < 0.05. A one-way ANOVA test and Tukey’s post hoc test or Dunnett’s test were conducted to evaluate the results. All data are expresses as the mean ± the standard deviation of three repetitions (results of the RT-qPCR assay as the number of copies of mRNA/µg of the total RNA; results of the biochemical assay as a conversion to 106 cells).

Results

Cytotoxicity of PA, EA, and H2O2

Of the tested concentrations (PA—1 µg/ml-200 µg/ml and EA—0.5µM-5µM), the concentrations of 1 µg/ml for PA (Dunnett’s test, p = 0.09) and 2.5µM for EA (Dunnett’s test, p=0.0.006) were selected for further studies. These concentrations were not toxic to the tested cells, and considering the primary goal of the study, the selection of concentrations should take into account the therapeutic aspect of the substances when applied to cells (Fig. 1A–B). For the presented study, a concentration of 500µM of H2O2 was selected as the stress factor (Fig. 1C).

Effect of PA and EA and an SMF with a 0.7 T flux density on the antioxidant gene expression using a RT-qPCR Assay

In the conducted experiment (in the case of PA), statistically significant decreases were observed in the expression level of all of the analyzed antioxidant genes (SOD1, SOD2, GPx, CAT, GSR) in the variant of the experiment that involved the induction of oxidative stress (with H2O2) relative to the control culture and the Placebo variant (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001), which would suggest that the intended effect of oxidative stress was achieved. After adding the test compound (PA) to the NHEK keratinocyte culture that had previously been treated with H2O2, statistically significant increases were observed in the expression of the SOD1, SOD2, GPx and GSR genes (Tukey’s post hoc test; p < 0.001). However, the observed increases in the expression were similar (for SOD1) or lower (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 - GPx, CAT) than the expression levels of these genes in the NHEK that had only been treated with the test compound, which was associated with a reduction in its activity due to the induced oxidative stress. Furthermore, after adding PA to the cell culture with oxidative stress, the expression levels of the studied genes were similar to those in the control and placebo cultures for the SOD1 and SOD2 genes, which could indicate an antioxidant and normalizing effects of the tested punicic acid.

Exposure of the cell culture (exposed to H2O2 and treated with PA) exposed to an SMF resulted in statistically significant increases in the analyzed genes compared to the variants with no exposure to an SMF (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 – SOD1, SOD2, GPx, p = 0.01 – CAT). In the case of the GSR gene, the observed increase in expression was not statistically significant. This could indicate an enhanced antioxidant effect of PA under the influence of an SMF. The expression levels of the antioxidant genes in the NHEK cell cultures that had been exposed to an SMF were higher than in the control and placebo conditions, and this increase was statistically significant for the SOD1, SOD2, and GPx genes (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001) thus demonstrating that an SMF alone can positively influence the expression levels of these genes, which indicates a protective effect. A minimal decrease was observed in the CAT expression (Fig. 2A–E).

In the case of EA, decreases in the expression level of all analyzed antioxidant genes were also observed in the H2O2 variant of the experiment compared to the control culture and the Placebo variant (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 – SOD1, SOD2, GPx, p = 0.025 – CAT, p = 0.017 – GSR). After adding EA to the NHEK keratinocyte culture that had previously been treated with H2O2, statistically significant increases were observed in the expression of the SOD1, SOD2, GPx, GSR, and CAT genes (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 – SOD1, SOD2, GPx, GSR, p = 0.002 – CAT). However, the observed increases in expression were similar (for SOD2 and GSR) or lower (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001– SOD1, GPx) than the expression levels of these genes in the NHEK that had been treated with EA alone. As with the previously described PA, the addition of EA to the H2O2-treated cell culture resulted in the normalization (for SOD1, SOD2, GPx) or similarity (for GSR and CAT) of the gene levels compared to the control and placebo cultures. Exposing a cell culture (exposed to H2O2 and then treated with EA) to an SMF resulted in statistically significant increases in the analyzed genes compared to the variants without exposure to an SMF, and these increases were statistically significant for the SOD1 and GPx genes (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 – SOD1, GPx) (Fig. 3A-E).

Effect of PA and EA and an SMF with a 0.7 T flux density on the activity of the antioxidant enzymes

Examination of the activity of the oxidative enzymes: SOD, GPx, and CAT showed that PA caused a statistically significant increase in the SOD levels (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001) compared to the control and placebo cultures. The same trend was observed for GPx and CAT activity (Tukey’s post hoc test, p = 0.005 – GPx, p < 0.001 – CAT). In the cultures that had been exposed to H2O2 and then treated with PA, a statistically significant increase was observed in SOD and GPx (Tukey’s post hoc test, p = 0.01 – SOD, p = 0.039 – GPx) compared to the culture that had only been exposed to H2O2. Importantly, each of the analyzed enzymes (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001) showed a statistically significant increase in the H2O2-treated culture and in the PA-treated culture that had been exposed to an SMF (Fig. 4. Punicic acid A-C). In the case of EA, this compound caused a statistically significant increase in the levels of SOD, GPx and CAT (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001 – SOD and GPx, p = 0.015 – CAT) compared to the control and placebo cultures. Adding EA to the H2O2-treated culture increased the levels of the studied enzymes, but for SOD, this increase was statistically significant (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001). Similarly, for this enzyme, there was also a statistically significant increase as a result of the exposure of the cell culture to an SMF (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.001). The levels of the remaining enzymes also increased, and in the case of GPx, this increase was trended towards statistical significance (Tukey’s post hoc test, p = 0.083) (Fig. 4 Ellagic Acid A-C).

Effect of PA and EA and an SMF with a 0.7 T flux density on the intracellular ROS production

Compared to control cells not treated with the test substances or SMF, exposure to PA and EA resulted in the production of 13% and 16% ROS, respectively. Application of the test substances after prior exposure to H2O2 resulted in lower or comparable ROS production (30% for PA, 24% for EA) than in the positive control (approximately 30%). The addition of SMF caused slight changes in ROS production (approximately 5% decrease for PA, and no significant changes for EA) (Table 3).

Discussion

Widespread access to information, the ubiquity of media, and increasing patient and consumer awareness have led to a growing interest in methods that can help to improve skin health and condition. The prevailing “eco” trend has fueled the dynamic development of the cosmetics and pharmaceutical industries that rely on preparations whose active ingredients are derived from plants. Many of these ingredients, despite being the basis of such preparations, have not been sufficiently described. Furthermore, issues related to product application and possible methods to facilitate or accelerate their action are also becoming an attractive research topic for many scientists.

The aim of this study was to verify whether exposure to a moderate-intensity SMF could potentially influence the biological properties of compounds of natural origin used in cosmetic and therapeutic preparations. We focused primarily on antioxidant properties due to their complex mechanism. To verify this assumption, we selected compounds that are derived from pomegranates: punicic acid and ellagic acid as they are innovative, little studied or described in the scientific literature [23]. Although these compounds belong to different groups in terms of structure, they exhibit a number of health-promoting properties that are primarily used for therapeutic purposes in the pharmaceutical industry. PA has been proven to prevent neurological disorders, combat metabolic diseases, be a therapeutic agent for type 2 diabetes, and has even exhibited anticancer effects [24–25]. It is therefore not surprising that its properties have also been used in skincare products [26] and the treatment of dermatological conditions [27]. As was previously mentioned, PA is a polyunsaturated fatty acid that is obtained from pomegranates. Its mechanism of action is complex. Among the most frequently mentioned are those related to anticancer effects. It has been proven that PA can affect the mechanisms related to protein kinase C pathways and lipid peroxidation, consequently causing a disruption of the mitochondrial membrane potential and apoptosis [28]. Furthermore, in health and beauty care, PA is used in the fight against obesity and for preventive purposes. It can also, among other things, increase the expression and translocation of GLUT4 in adipocytes. Most of these effects are also possible due to its antioxidant properties [23], which were also indicated in our study. We demonstrated that 1 µg/ml of PA exhibited antioxidant properties in the tested model that had been subjected to oxidative stress. This concentration did not induce toxic effects on the tested cells, which is reasonable in the context of obtaining the substance’s skincare effects. Additionally, Liu et al. [29] also demonstrated antioxidant properties, among others. PA and EA demonstrated their protective effects against oxidative stress in HaCaT keratinocyte cell cultures. They also demonstrated the protective effect of these compounds against H2O2 cytotoxicity, which is consistent with our results [29]. These properties were previously described by Schubert et al. [30]. At that time, the strength of these properties was compared to green tea. This study also indicated the possibility of using PA as a preservative, which is interesting and important in the context of a rapidly developing pharmaceutical company. It is worth noting that PA has been present in nutraceuticals, i.e., products that border on food and therapeutics, for many years [28].

EA is a compound that is more commonly found in therapeutic strategies than PA. Its modulating and enhancing effects on neurological disease therapy have been primarily demonstrated [31]. It is used in the treatment of chronic diseases [32–33] and in anticancer therapies [34]. EA has been proven to exhibit antioxidant activity thanks to urolithins. Therefore, they have become a new group of active compounds that are the subject of extensive research [35]. Our study also demonstrated the antioxidant properties of 2.5 µM EA in an NHEK cell culture. Furthermore, Hseu et al. [36] demonstrated strong antioxidant and protective properties of EA in a HaCaT keratinocyte cell culture model, thus indicating an increased expression of the Nfr-2 and HO-1 genes [36]. In cosmetic terms, this acid has been shown to inhibit tyrosinase, which is an important aspect in the fight against discoloration [37–38].

In our study, we used magnetic chambers that emit an SMF, the detailed description of which has already been described by us [21]. Despite the unexplained mechanism of an SMF’s effect on the human body and the varying reports on this topic, we decided to investigate how the antioxidant properties of the tested compounds, as demonstrated in this study, change after exposure to a moderate-intensity SMF. This is an interesting question given the available information as it remains unclear whether the ubiquitous SMFs have a positive or negative impact on the human body. The effects induced by an SMF certainly depend on its induction. An SMF has potential therapeutic applications as its analgesic, anti-edematous, and anti-inflammatory effects have been demonstrated [39]. SMFs are used in combined therapies in physical therapy and rehabilitation [40–41], and their anticancer properties have also been suggested [42].

There are many conflicting reports on the effect of an MF on FR production in various research systems. Studies have shown that an MF can lead to their increase, which can disrupt the antioxidant balance and lead to harmful changes at the cellular level. In our study, we did not observe such a tendency. Pawłowska-Góral et al. [43] demonstrated that an SMF does not cause disturbances in redox homeostasis in human dermal cells [43]. These discrepancies in this regard led to the observation that the effect of an SMF on FR activity in keratinocytes may be bidirectional. Based on numerous scientific reports, this direction depends on the parameters of the magnetic field. These parameters concern the duration of the exposure and its intensity. The type of cells that are exposed to an SMF is also important. It is also possible that there is no significant effect on FR activity in keratinocytes that have been exposed to an SMF [44–45]. In our study, although the magnetic chamber model used ensures a uniform distribution of magnetic flux density in the measurement field, the generation of a gradient magnetic field is possible. Therefore, the observed changes may be related to two possible phenomena: (1) the influence of the SMF, and (2) the gradient of the MF. The situation described concerns the edges of the magnetic chamber model used. According to the available literature, it is concluded that cell fate may be closely related to the gradient magnetic forces that act on paramagnetic free radicals [46]. Furthermore, it has been proven that a moderate magnetic field alters the structure of proteins and lipids in cell membranes, thus triggering cascades of cellular reactions [47]. Studies conducted in animal models also provide information on the potential effect of SMFs on the antioxidant system. Feng et al. [44] demonstrated in a mouse model that an SMF can reduce oxidative stress and promote wound healing in diabetic mice [44], while Yang et al. [48] indicated that a moderate-intensity SMF can positively influence the antioxidant capacity of ovaries in mice [48].

Although SMFs have already been described as a factor in dermatological therapies [49], reports on the use of SMFs in cosmetic procedures are scarce. It has been proven that SMFs can modify the activity of active substances in drugs. These processes are possible due to the influence of this physical factor on all of the biological processes that occur at the cellular level [50]. Current scientific knowledge indicates that SMFs can affect the physicochemical parameters of active substances in preparations, thereby changing the intensity of their action. Changes in their solubility and accelerated transport have been reported, which is crucial in the context of the speed and intensity of therapy [51]. Zyburtowicz-Ćwiartka et al. [52] demonstrated that exposure to an SMF caused changes in the tested compound – ketoprofen administered transdermally (thermal properties, crystalline structure, lipophilicity, and solubility). Although various types of magnetic fields were studied, the weakest effect was observed for a negatively polarized SMF, which they explained as the role of polarity and intensity [52].

Conclusion

Given that SMFs affect the permeability of the cell membranes and potentially increase the transport of active substances to their target sites, the use of SMF in cosmetic therapies may be of considerable interest, yet this has not been adequately addressed. This may be due to insufficient scientific evidence supporting the potential use of SMFs to improve the transepidermal and transdermal transport of active substances in cosmetics. Our study, which was conducted using the NHEK keratinocyte cell line, is a pilot study. It demonstrated that SMFs can influence the antioxidant properties at the cellular level of substances as was reflected by the increased expression of the studied antioxidant genes and the activity of the antioxidant enzymes. The observed changes at the molecular level corresponded to changes at the protein level. Although the obtained results are preliminary in nature, they are promising due to the selected research model. This model permits the examination of the effect of SMFs on the antioxidant properties of a compound, taking into account the stressor that was used – H2O2. The tested substances are potent antioxidants, but scientific reports on combining their antioxidant properties with SMFs are scarce. Our study results suggest that SMFs may likely enhance the antioxidant activity of PA and EA. However, further, more detailed studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis and to explain the mechanism of this synergistic interaction. It is worth noting that the use of permanent magnets in everyday life is becoming increasingly common. Various industries utilize this type of magnetic field. There are numerous scientific reports on magnetic liposomes using SMFs, which enable the rapid and effective transport of therapeutic substances to their target site [53]. These issues are directly related to the therapeutic aspect, particularly in the field of oncology. However, considering the presented preliminary results and other available scientific reports, it is worth expanding the scope of the study studies to verify the possibility of using SMFs as physical agents in cosmetic protocols.

Our pilot study, the results of which seem promising, certainly requires continuation and the use of additional research methods to better explain the effect of SMF on the antioxidant properties of compounds (in terms of skincare). It would be worthwhile to conduct a similar experiment involving other cell lines, such as normal human cells, such as fibroblasts. The results obtained so far concern only the cellular response. Furthermore, to confidently indicate the actual effect of SMF on the transport of active substances, it is worthwhile to conduct a series of membrane studies to confirm the direction of changes.
