Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction in Patients With Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Steatohepatitis
Authors: Joe Hollinghurst, Margarida Augusto, Fotis Tefos, Robert Bauer, Sreelatha Vadapalle, Lise Gehrt Cardél
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to develop the first prediction models for CVD risk in a cohort of patients with MASH.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) database. Accelerated failure time (AFT) models were used to predict CVD risk independently for males and females with MASH. Covariables from the QRisk3 algorithm were included: age, deprivation, body mass index, cholesterol ratio, systolic blood pressure, ethnicity, smoking status, CVD family history, diabetes, treated hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease (CKD), migraine, corticosteroids, anti‐psychotic medication, serious mental illness and erectile dysfunction. Measures of calibration and discrimination were determined. Observed and predicted risks were used to compare the AFT models with the QRisk3 algorithm.ResultsUtilising a cohort of 10 461 patients with MASH (5364 female and 5097 male) models to predict time to CVD were developed with moderate predictive power (C‐statistics 0.7–0.72) identifying age, cholesterol ratio, type 2 diabetes and CKD as risk factors that decrease time to CVD. Comparing the observed and predicted CV risks indicated the AFT models more accurately predicted CVD risk than the QRisk3 algorithm in patients with MASH.ConclusionsWe describe a first‐in‐kind predictive model to assess the risk of CVD in patients with MASH, which has the potential to more accurately inform the treatment and management of this population.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), previously known as Non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [1], is a severe form of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), with the additional complications of inflammation and liver damage [2]. The overall global prevalence of MASLD has already reached epidemic proportions (> 30%) [3], and prevalence continues to increase [4, 5, 6, 7]. MASLD presents considerable social and medical burdens, as the most common cause of chronic liver disease in the US and Europe [4, 5, 6, 7]. The prevalence of MASH, however, is less well documented, with an estimated prevalence of 3%–6% in the United States [8].

Patients with MASH are at high risk of comorbidities including cardiovascular disease (CVD), with CVD accounting for the highest proportion of deaths in MASLD patients (5%–10%) [4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Excess cardiovascular risk in MASH may be driven by multiple biological mechanisms, including systemic low‐grade inflammation, oxidative stress, hepatic and peripheral insulin resistance and pro‐coagulant and pro‐atherogenic signalling [15]. Evidence shows that patients with MASH exhibit increased levels of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), C‐reactive protein (CRP), pro‐fibrogenic mediators and atherogenic dyslipidaemia, all of which contribute to accelerated vascular injury and altered CVD trajectories. Additionally, chronic kidney disease burden, endothelial dysfunction and ectopic fat deposition, particularly in epicardial adipose tissue, further compound cardiovascular risk [15].

Despite the high cardiovascular burden in this population, no CVD risk prediction models currently exist that are specific to individuals with MASH. Widely used tools such as QRisk3 were developed for the general population and do not incorporate the mechanistic drivers of cardiovascular pathology uniquely observed in MASH, nor the interplay between hepatic inflammation, fibrosis severity, insulin resistance and other metabolic derangements that modify CVD risk in this group [16, 17]. As a result, existing models may systematically underestimate risk for these patients.

Therefore, we sought to develop improved CVD risk prediction models tailored to the MASH population to enable more accurate quantification of cardiovascular risk, support clinical decision‐making and inform health‐economic modelling.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a UK‐based retrospective observational cohort study using anonymised linked primary and secondary care electronic medical record data recorded in the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) AURUM and Hospital Episode Statistics (HES), in addition to Office for National Statistics (ONS) mortality and sociodemographic data between January 1997 and January 2021. The CPRD contains linked primary and secondary care patient data from approximately 6.9% of the UK population and is broadly representative in terms of age, sex and ethnicity [18].

Participants

Figure S1 depicts the assessment window for individuals. The study included adults, aged > 18 years of age, with a recorded sex and a MASH diagnosis in the CPRD or HES. In CPRD, patients were eligible for inclusion if their record was labelled as acceptable by CPRD quality control and approved for CPRD and HES linkage [18]. Patients were followed up until the earliest event of either death or the end of study period (2021‐01‐01). Patients without 12 months of primary care data prior to MASH diagnosis were excluded. A 10‐year window prior to MASH diagnosis was used to determine if an individual had a diagnosis code(s) for CVD. Patients with a prior CVD diagnosis were excluded from the primary analysis. As MASH diagnosis was used as the index date, no additional liver aetiologies were used as exclusion criteria. Analysis was repeated on these patients independently. Models were created independently for men and women. Kaplan–Meier curves with the numbers at risk and cumulative numbers for censoring, along with the distribution of number of MASH events, CVD events and censoring per year by data source/type, are provided in Figures S2 and S3.

QRisk3 Models

The variable specifications for models A, B, C from the QRisk3 [17] derivation were replicated to create MASH‐QRisk3 models A, B and C. Model A contains the same variables as the previous version of QRisk, QRisk2 (deprivation, ethnicity, smoking, family history of coronary heart disease, diabetes, treated hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, total:HDL cholesterol ratio and systolic blood pressure). Model B includes additional variables: migraine, corticosteroid use, lupus, atypical antipsychotic use, severe mental illness and erectile dysfunction. Model C is the same as model B except that it also includes the standard deviation of serial systolic blood pressure values.

Variables, Data Sources and Measurements

Variables were defined using medical and product codes within CPRD, and International Classification of Disease version 10 (ICD‐10) codes within HES and ONS.

Population definition: MASH diagnoses were defined using the Read (J61y8—Non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis) and ICD‐10 code (K75.8—Non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis) for NASH from primary and secondary care respectively.

Outcomes: The time to first CVD event following MASH diagnosis was extracted from primary and secondary care and mortality records. The first recorded incidence of CVD in either dataset was used to determine time to CVD. The following ICD‐10 codes were used to identify CVD in secondary care and mortality records: G45 (transient ischaemic attack and related syndromes), I20 (angina pectoris), I21 (acute myocardial infarction), I22 (subsequent myocardial infarction), I23 (complications after myocardial infarction), I24 (other acute ischaemic heart disease), I25 (chronic ischaemic heart disease), I63 (cerebral infarction) and I64 (stroke, not specified as haemorrhage or infarction). Primary care code‐lists identifying these conditions were taken from Guthrie et al. [19].

Baseline covariables: Variables of interest were taken from the derivation of the QRisk3 algorithm [17]. A 10‐year window prior to MASH diagnosis was used to determine covariables except for systolic blood pressure (BP) variation, which was limited to 5‐years. Continuous variables used the most recent measurement prior to index and included age, body mass index (BMI), cholesterol ratio, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and systolic BP variance. Discrete variables were coded as yes/no unless otherwise specified, and included: deprivation (using the Index of Multiple Deprivation [IMD] quintiles, 1–5 [least–most deprived]) [20], ethnicity (Bangladeshi, Black, Indian, Other Asian, Other, Pakistani, White), smoking status (ex‐smoker, never‐smoked, smoker), CVD family history, diabetes (no diabetes, type 1, type 2), treated hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), atrial fibrillation (AF), CKD, migraine, corticosteroids, anti‐psychotic medication, serious mental illness and erectile dysfunction.

Bias

Summary statistics describing the degree of missing data were presented for each variable. To minimise selection bias, multivariate multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) was used to replace missing values [21]. Imputation was performed on baseline data and not on any outcome variables. The distributions for continuous variables before and after imputation are provided in Supporting Information: Appendix B.

Statistical Methods

Descriptive Statistics

Patient characteristics (demographic characteristics, clinical histories, medication usage and clinical measurements) at baseline were summarised univariately. Appropriate summary statistics: mean, standard deviation (SD), median, minimum and maximum were presented for continuous variables; counts and percentages were presented for categorical variables. Proportions of missingness were summarised for all continuous variables and for the IMD. For binary variables, if there was no record of a comorbidity, it was assumed not to be present.

Risk Prediction Models for CVD—Parametric Survival Models

In contrast to the QRisk3 derivation where Cox proportional hazards models were computed, accelerated failure time (AFT) parametric survival models for the exponential, Weibull, log‐normal, log‐logistic, Gaussian and logistic distributions were created for females and males independently. Parametric models were chosen for transparency and to enable a fully specified model to be used in future predictions [22]. Additionally, AFT models also provide fully specified survival functions for any horizon required in health‐economic analyses, obtain time‐ratio effect measures aligned with clinical questions, can handle non‐proportional or crossing hazards and enable explicit, testable extrapolation beyond follow‐up. MASH‐QRisk3 model D was created using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) with an optimal value of lambda determined using cross‐validation with 1000 folds. Polynomial terms were included in the models where non‐linearity in the continuous variables was evident (Supporting Information: Appendix C). The distribution for the parametric models was chosen by observing the Cox–Snell residuals [23] and AIC (Figures S4–S7).

Validation and Calibration of the Multivariable CVD Models

Validation and calibration were assessed to check the predictive ability of the models. Pseudo‐R 2 values (explained variation; where higher values indicate a greater proportion of variation in time to event is explained by the model) [24], D statistic [25] (a measure of discrimination where higher values indicate better discrimination), and Harrell's C statistic at 10‐years were calculated to validate the models. Harrell's C statistic [26] is a measure of discrimination that is similar to the area under a receiver operating characteristic curve but takes account of the censored nature of the data. Calibration was assessed by obtaining smooth semi‐parametric calibration curves for each of our survival models at 10‐years. The goodness‐of‐fit for the multivariable models was assessed using a combination of the calibration plots, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Cox–Snell residuals for the baseline function.

Competing Risks Parametric Survival Models

Aalen–Johansen estimates were created to show the cumulative event probability for the competing risks of CVD and mortality. A parametric cause‐specific hazard competing risks model was created for each of the strata, with non‐CVD‐related mortality as a competing risk for CVD [27]. Mortality was defined as death due to causes other than CVD events, as the CVD outcome already accounted for CVD‐related mortality.

Comparison to QRisk3

MASH‐QRisk3 models A, B, C and D were compared against predictions from the original QRisk3, developed for the general population, by plotting the deciles of observed and predicted risks. The observed risk was computed using the Aalen–Johansen estimator and predicted risks were calculated using the parametric models.

Additional Analysis

The analysis was repeated on MASH patients with a prior CVD diagnosis, using the parameters established above. The analysis was also repeated using Cox proportional hazards models. The parametric models were preferred in this case due to their reproducibility in generating future predictions [22].

Results

Participants

Data from 12 854 patients with a record of MASH in either primary (n = 2020) or secondary care (n = 10 834) were extracted. Following the exclusion of patients with no gender record and those < 18 years at the time of diagnosis (n = 38) and those with a prior CVD diagnosis (n = 2393), 10 461 patients (n = 5, 364 (51%) female and n = 5097 (49%) male; Table 1) patients were deemed eligible for inclusion in the study. Mean (standard deviation) follow‐up time was 5.04 years (4.16) with a maximum follow‐up of 26 years. The highest proportions of patients were resident in the most deprived quintile, IMD‐5 (2439 [23.3%]), had an ethnicity record of white (9075 [86.8%]) and had no record of smoking (4535 [43.4%]). In total, 8493 (81.2%) of patients were classified as having overweight or obesity with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Key Predictors of CVD

For females, age, diabetes, AF, CKD, serious mental illness and systolic blood pressure variation were statistically significant predictors with a negative sign, indicating a higher risk of an accelerated (faster) time to CVD across models A, B, C and D (Table 2). Similarly, for males, age, diabetes, serious mental illness and systolic blood pressure variation were significant predictors of a reduced time to CVD (Table 3). Diabetes was a significant interactor with age for both genders, with BMI, atrial fibrillation and smoking also being significant in females.

Calibration

The calibration plots show the predicted 1‐, 5‐ and 10‐year time to CVD event against the fraction surviving 1‐, 5‐ and 10‐years (where survival refers to a CVD event); good calibration is therefore indicated by a diagonal line on the calibration plots (Figures S12–S35). The Weibull distribution showed the best calibration for males and females. The mean absolute error increased as the time horizon was increased in the calibration points. The 10‐year calibration plots show a reasonable fit for models A, B, C and D, with mean absolute errors ranging from 0.0365–0.038 for females and 0.044–0.046 for males.

Discrimination and Validation

Measures of performance were similar for all four models (Table 4). In each case, the models for males had greater discrimination than for females, with a C‐statistic of 0.72 in males and 0.7 in females, indicating moderate discrimination. Competing Risks of CVD and Non‐CVD Mortality

Aalen–Johansen and parametric curves were plotted, using the Weibull distribution (Figure 1, female; Figure 2, male) to show the transitions between MASH diagnosis and the competing risks of CVD event and non‐CVD‐related mortality. This modelling indicated there was a higher probability of non‐CVD‐related mortality than CVD event, with an estimated 26% probability of non‐CVD death for both females and males at 10 years. The corresponding probabilities for a CVD event at 10 years were approximately 18%–19%. In contrast, Kaplan–Meier estimates indicated an approximate 25% probability of a CVD event at 10 years, highlighting the requirement for the inclusion of the competing risk of non‐CVD death (Figure S2).

Comparison to QRisk3

Models A, B, C and D (MASH‐QRisk3‐A, B, C, D) all had similar predicted risk. The observed versus predicted risk was generally closer to the diagonal line for the models generated in this study than for the QRisk3 algorithm in both males and females without prior CVD, indicating a more optimised prediction in the MASH cohorts (Figures 3 and 4). This modelling revealed that QRisk3 would both under and overestimate the risk of a cardiovascular event in MASH patients with a maximum error of 12% in males and 13% in females, whereas the MASH‐QRisk3 models (A–D) would underestimate the risk with a maximum error of 9% in males and 5% in females.

Discussion

Key Results

Although patients with MASH are at higher risk of CVD morbidity, there are currently no known models that enable the prediction of CVD risk in this population. We developed risk prediction models using retrospective data from CPRD to quantify risk of CVD in patients with MASH. In this study, we found that in 10 461 patients with MASH, 1734 (16.6%, 32.9 per 1000 person years) had a CVD event during 52 675 person‐years of observation.

Predictive Performance

When observing the validation and calibration of the models, there was better discrimination (D xy) and less variation (R 2) in all the models for males compared with for females. The calibration curves indicated the models for individuals were well calibrated, suggesting the models predict the risk of CVD well at 10 years (Supporting Information: Appendix F). The C‐statistic indicated that the models in individuals with MASH were moderately predictive, with C‐statistics of approximately 0.7–0.72. Although this is not as strong as for QRisk3 in the general population, with a C‐statistic of 0.88 (absolute difference; 0.16–0.18), these are the first model looking specifically at patients with a prior MASH diagnosis. Studies that have investigated the C‐statistic in specified subgroups have found performance varies, with excellent performance in patients with type 1 diabetes (C‐statistics: women, 0.83; men, 0.853), and moderate performance in patients with type 2 diabetes (C‐statistics: women, 0.741; men, 0.695) or CKD (C‐statistics: women, 0.755; men, 0.734) [28]. Studies have also found discrimination to vary for different CVD risk scores in type‐2 diabetes patients and worsen for older populations, with C‐statistics ranging from 0.566 (Swedish NDR) to 0.674 (QRISK2) [29]. These discrimination metrics are comparable to those of our models, indicating models in subpopulations generally have reduced performance compared to general populations. This may be due to the characteristics determining the sub‐populations interacting with the predictor variables for CVD.

Comparison to QRisk3 Cohort

Compared with the QRisk3 derivation cohort, the MASH cohort had a higher mean age (60 years for women and 58 years for men compared with 43.3 years and 42.6 years respectively), higher mean BMI (32.8 for women and 31.9 for men compared with 25.4 and 25.9) and more ex‐smokers (27.5% for women and 34.2% for men compared with 14.7% and 15.4%). Similarly, the MASH cohort had much higher proportions of comorbidities than QRisk3, Table S1. Similar observations have been made when comparing the QRisk2 within a T2D population, where it was highlighted that the age distribution may contribute to differences in predictive performance [29].

Comparison in Predictors Between QRisk3 and MASH‐QRisk3

Whilst the parametric methodology presented in the main text differs between this study and the Cox proportional hazards models in the QRisk3 derivation, the increased/decreased risk for the predictors generally remains consistent across models, indicating validity for the models in a MASH population. Specifically, an increased risk of CVD in women can be seen for increased levels of deprivation, age, ex‐smokers, family history of CVD, diabetes, treated hypertension, RA, AF, CKD, migraine, corticosteroid use, serious mental illness and cholesterol ratio. The largest magnitude for predictors is also consistent, with diabetes, AF and CKD having the largest hazard ratios in QRisk3 and acceleration factors in this study. Inconsistencies in the models for women can be seen for antipsychotic drug use; however, this has a small magnitude and remains statistically insignificant, which indicates uncertainty. For men, consistent predictors for a decreased risk of CVD included ethnicity (black), and predictors for an increased risk included deprivation, age, diabetes, treated hypertension, RA, CKD, migraine, corticosteroids, antipsychotic drug use, serious mental illness and cholesterol ratio. Age, diabetes and CKD had a large magnitude in both studies, indicating a consistent and high influence on the risk of CVD in both a MASH and in a general population. Inconsistent predictors for men included smoking, family history of CVD and AF. However, these predictors have high uncertainty in their estimates, and interactions with age were included, which makes their interpretation difficult and requires further considerations as illustrated in the worked examples (Supporting Information: Appendix J).

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this study include the use of a large, linked database to extract demographic and outcome data for patients with MASH and the use of parametric models. The parametric models generated here can predict the probability of an event at any time without specifying a baseline function, thus providing an advantage over QRisk3 (which focuses on CVD risk at 10‐years) for health economics models where alternative time‐horizons are required. Compared to the cohort used for derivation in QRisk3, the MASH cohort had fewer missing data, which means there is less uncertainty around imputation.

Despite these strengths, there are also limitations associated with the study. First, whilst the sample included a large and diverse sample of UK patients with MASH, we acknowledge this may not translate to other countries and recognise diagnosed MASH may only reflect a subgroup of MASH patients. Second, our study utilised observational data, which can suffer from unobserved confounding and missing information. Third, the outcomes and variables used in this study were defined using diagnoses and interactions that occur as part of the patients' routine care. We aimed to mitigate this limitation by using validated code lists. However, we acknowledge that research using electronic health records databases is reliant on the clinical codes available and the accuracy of inputs by physicians, and therefore may not be representative of the entire MASH population and disease onset may vary between individuals. Fourth, MASH case ascertainment relied on Read and ICD‐10 codes corresponding to non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis. Given known limitations in the accuracy and completeness of routine coding for steatotic liver diseases, misclassification bias is possible. This could include (a) false negatives where MASH is present but not coded, and (b) false positives where codes reflect historical or provisional diagnoses or are applied inconsistently. Fifth, we did not have routine histological confirmation of MASH. Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for grading steatohepatitis and staging fibrosis, which are key determinants of cardiovascular risk. Sixth, although the number of MASH patients included in the study was relatively large, the derivation for the QRisk3 algorithm was applied to a general population, enabling a derivation cohort a magnitude larger than what was available for our presented models. We therefore may not have been able to detect the same small changes and variations as the original derivation. Due to the small number of patients with a record of lupus and HIV/AIDS, these variables were not included in any of the models. As in the QRisk3 paper, it was assumed that the reporting of HIV/AIDS may be unreliable due to specialist care not being recorded in routinely collected data [17]. The poor predictive performance for MASH‐QRisk3 in patients with a prior CVD diagnosis was a limitation; the QRisk3 model was derived for patients with no existing CVD, so the predictor variables used may not be appropriate in this population. Similarly, the impact of how predictions for CVD vary for patients with cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) conditions other than MASH was not investigated. Future work in this area may indicate a need for independent CVD risk predictions to be conducted in the wider CKM patient population. We also acknowledge that QRisk3 is not the only CVD risk score available; however, due to the wide use of QRisk3, the time frame analysed (10‐year risk) and the data used within this study, we found that this was the most appropriate comparator to report.

Conclusion

Whilst the QRisk3 algorithm is a well‐known tool for predicting the risk of CVD in a general population, it has not previously been applied to or recalibrated for a MASH population. We created models for CVD with moderate predictive ability (C‐statistics of 0.7 to 0.72) in both males and females with MASH. T2D and CKD were all predictive of an increased risk of CVD across the MASH population. By developing the models from a MASH‐specific population in the UK and determining the risk of CVD at a specified time‐point, our models have the potential to be optimised for the MASH population and more accurately inform health economic models compared to existing prediction models.
