On a system of equations arising in meteorology: Well-posedness and data assimilation
Eduard Feireisl, Piotr Gwiazda, Agnieszka Świerczewska-Gwiazda

Abstract
Data assimilation plays a crucial role in modern weather prediction, providing a systematic way to incorporate observational data into complex dynamical models. The paper addresses continuous data assimilation for a model arising as a singular limit of the three-dimensional compressible Navier-Stokes-Fourier system with rotation driven by temperature gradient. The limit system preserves the essential physical mechanisms of the original model, while exhibiting a reduced, effectively two-and-a-half-dimensional structure. This simplified framework allows for a rigorous analytical study of the data assimilation process while maintaining a direct physical connection to the full compressible model. We establish well posedness of global-in-time solutions and a compact trajectory attractor, followed by the stability and convergence results for the nudging scheme applied to the limiting system. Finally, we demonstrate how these results can be combined with a relative entropy argument to extend the assimilation framework to the full three-dimensional compressible setting, thereby establishing a rigorous connection between the reduced and physically complete models.

Introduction

Data assimilation is an essential approach used to improve the accuracy of numerical simulations, particularly in complex and nonlinear systems such as equations modelling the atmosphere. Reliable predictions depend strongly on the precision of the initial conditions, yet in practice these conditions can only be measured discretely. Weather observations, for example, are collected from satellites, ground stations, LiDAR and radar, but these measurements provide only partial snapshots of the true state of the atmosphere. Unfortunately, the spatial resolution of these measurements is insufficient to accurately reconstruct the initial conditions on the computational grid.
Data assimilation provides a systematic framework to nudge model trajectories toward observational measurements. Since observations are collected over a time interval rather than at a single moment, they help to compensate for the missing spatial information and allow for the reconstruction of an initial condition that leads to a physically meaningful model evolution.

A central mathematical idea behind data assimilation for dissipative systems is that only a limited number of parameters or modes may be sufficient to determine the long-term behavior of a system.

Properties such as existence and uniqueness of solutions,
are of fundamental importance in data assimilation, providing that the model dynamics are well posed, so that observations can be consistently incorporated without ambiguity. For a broad class of models, the long-term behavior of solutions can be characterized using only a finite number of degrees of freedom, the first rigorous proof for two-dimensional Navier-Stokes is due to
[
20
]
. The result demonstrates that, once the first
N

Fourier modes of two Navier-Stokes solutions agree for sufficiently large

N
, the entire solutions necessarily converge to the same limit as time tends to infinity.
To specify what
sufficiently large
N
means, the paper
[
19
]
provides an explicit criterion, which was later refined in
[
27
]
. Interestingly,
[
21
]
demonstrated that an analogous result holds when the degrees of freedom are taken to be quantities other than Fourier modes; in their work, the values of the solution at selected nodes played this role. Subsequent studies
[
22
,
26
]
replaced nodal values with averages over finite volumes. Moreover,
[
10
]
established that, for the two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations, a broad family of determining sets of degrees of freedom exists, including those arising naturally in finite element discretizations.

Various data assimilation methods have been developed to achieve this goal, including variational approaches such as the four-dimensional variational data assimilation (4D-Var) method and ensemble-based techniques like the Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF). Another important class of methods is based on the Azouani, Olson and Titi (AOT) framework, cf.
[
3
]
, which introduces a continuous data assimilation scheme formulated directly at the level of the evolution equations.
Azouani, Olson, and Titi showed that imposing suitable constraints on the finite-dimensional resolution of the measurement data is sufficient to guarantee that the solution reconstructed by their algorithm converges, over time, to the unknown reference state.
Instead of incorporating the observations directly into the nonlinear dynamics of the model, a feedback control mechanism is introduced, which steers the system toward the reference solution consistent with the data. This approach is motivated by the practical difficulty of embedding raw measurements into the governing equations.
To explain the idea in more detail
let
u(t)
denote the reference solution of the system

\frac{du}{dt}=F(u),\qquad u(0)=u_{0},

(1.1)

where the initial condition
u_{0}
is unknown. Observations are available at coarse
spatial resolution through

, for

. The objective is to construct
an approximate solution

, such that

as

increases, thereby recovering a suitable initial

The algorithm is given by

\frac{dv}{dt}=F(v)-\mu I_{h}(v)+\mu I_{h}(u),\qquad v(0)=v_{0},

(1.2)

where

is a relaxation parameter. The feedback term

\mu(I_{h}(u)-I_{h}(v))
forces the coarse spatial scales of

the spatial resolution

The analytical expectations outlined above have motivated a focus on rigorous results for the two-dimensional Navier-Stokes system, as in
[
3
]
. The later result
[
14
]
showed that, for 2D Rayleigh-Bénard convection, measuring and assimilating (nudging) a single velocity component suffices to recover the exact reference solutions for both the full velocity field and the temperature. In other words, there is no need to measure or nudge the temperature, see also
[
16
]
. Moreover, in
[
2
]
the authors showed that, for the same model, nudging the temperature alone does not suffice to recover the velocity field. Nevertheless,
[
15
]
demonstrates that, in the case of the planetary geostrophic model, observing and nudging only the temperature already suffices to reconstruct both the complete velocity field and the temperature. This highlights that the appropriate choice of state variables to observe and nudge is inherently model dependent.
Follow-up work
[
9
]
explored sensitivity analyses and established super-exponential convergence rates for 2D Navier-Stokes data assimilation. Meanwhile,
[
28
]
extended the assimilation framework to settings with partial observability and feedback control of determining parameters. The works
[
6
]
–
[
8
]
further broadened the theory by introducing mesh-free interpolant observables and local-observable schemes, as well as by extending nudging approaches to both 2D and 3D incompressible Navier-Stokes systems not including temperature effects. Note that the 3D case considered in
[
8
]

the assimilation process is conditional under some additional requirement of the observables, e.g. this may fail to hold for observables generated by the Leray Hopf solutions.

In the present work, we examine the rotating Oberbeck-Boussinesq system and demonstrate how continuous data assimilation, applied through the relative entropy framework, can be used to provide insights into the full three-dimensional compressible model.
Our attention is directed to a system of equations arising as a singular limit of three-dimensional fast rotating compressible viscous fluid driven by temperature gradient. The considered system is particularly interesting in the context of continuous data assimilation, as it originates from the limit of a three-dimensional model and, due to the effect of the centrifugal force, reduces to a two-and-a-half dimensional formulation, see
[
13
]
for rigorous estimates based on the relative entropy. This feature is especially relevant for data assimilation techniques, since allows to provide existence of unique solutions and global attractor.
What is however important, the effective reduction in dimensionality is not imposed artificially but rather emerges naturally from the underlying dynamics due to fast rotation of the system. Moreover, the Coriolis force is indeed present in models actually used in weather forecasting, for which data assimilation plays a crucial role, see for e.g. the Unified Model distributed by the UK’s national meteorological service Met Office cf.
[
30
]
, as well as its local versions. e.g Polish Meteo service (https://www.meteo.pl/) of the Interdisciplinary Centre for Mathematical and Computational Modelling of the University of Warsaw. Even though the Coriolis force appears solely in the primitive system considered here and is absent from the target system, it has a significant influence on the asymptotic behavior, to the extent that it effectively removes all vertical motion from the limiting dynamics.

The Oberbeck-Boussinesq approximation provides a physically consistent yet mathematically and numerically tractable framework for modeling rotating stratified flows. It captures the essential features of atmospheric dynamics, while striking a balance between physical realism and mathematical tractability, avoiding the full complexity of the compressible Navier-Stokes system. In the present work, we focus on the so-called rotating Oberbeck-Boussinesq system, which is incompressible and accounts for the horizontal components of the velocity field together with three components of the temperature. For this reason we will often call it
two-and-a-half-dimensional model
.

The system arises as a singular limit of compressible three-dimensional Navier-Stokes-Fourier system. One could, however, argue that the most realistic model for describing atmospheric behavior is given by the three-dimensional compressible equations, and therefore data assimilation should ideally be performed at that level. Interestingly, there is no need to adopt this point of view, as the combination of the relative entropy method and the results presented here for the rotating Oberbeck-Boussinesq system shows that, for well-prepared initial data, it is possible to assimilate data for the three-dimensional model fully with the aid of the considered two-and-a-half-dimensional one while only controlling the assimilation error. This connection is discussed in detail in Section 5, following the detailed analysis (Theorems
2.1
and
3.4
) and rigorous data assimilation procedure (Theorem
4.1
) carried out for the simpler system in Sections 2-4. An important advantage of avoiding data assimilation directly in the three-dimensional regime is the significant reduction in computational cost, given that data assimilation alone may account for a significant part of the total computational expense. This reduction may open a possibility to substantially lower the energy consumption and, consequently, the overall cost of weather prediction.

1.1
Primitive system

Here and herafter, the spatial variables are denoted as

x=(x_{h},x_{3}),\ x_{h}\in R^{2},\ x_{3}\in R.

Consider a viscous, compressible, heat conducting and fluid confined to rotating cylinder

\Omega=B_{r}\times(0,1),\ B(r)=\left\{x_{h}\in R^{2}\ \Big|\ |x_{h}|<r\right\}

(1.3)

The density
\varrho
, the absolute temperature
\vartheta
, and the velocity
{\bf u}
of the fluid are
governed by the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system
written in the rotation frame:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\varrho{\bf u}\times e_{3}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\nabla_{x}F,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta))+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u},

(1.4)

with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\bf u}

\displaystyle=0\ \mbox{for}\ |x_{h}|=r,\ x_{3}\in(0,1),

\displaystyle{\bf u}\cdot{\bf n}

\displaystyle=0,\ (\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})\cdot{\bf n})\times{\bf n}=0\ \mbox{for}\ x_{h}\in B(r),\ x_{3}=0,1,

\displaystyle\vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\vartheta_{B},\ \overline{\vartheta}>0,

(1.5)

and with the prescribed total mass

\int_{\Omega}\varrho\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\overline{\varrho}|\Omega|.

(1.6)

The forcing potential
F
takes the form

F={\bf g}\cdot x+|x_{h}|^{2},

(1.7)

where the vector
{\bf g}
determines the direction and magnitude of the gravitational field, while
|x_{h}|^{2}
represent a potential of the centrifugal force, see
[
13
]
for details concerning the equation of state as well as other relevant constitutive
equations.

The fluid motion is driven by a
simultaneous action of three forces: the gravitational force, the buoyancy force induced by the temperature gradient, and
the rotation represented by the Coriolis and centrifugal forces. Models of this type arise frequently in meteorology, geophysics or astrophysics.
The system of equations (
1.4
) is scaled by a small parameter
\varepsilon
:

•

The origin of the thermal convection flow is the
compressibility
of the fluid.
The Mach number is small proportional to
\varepsilon
, meaning the fluid is nearly incompressible.
In the incompressible limit, where the pressure
becomes constant, the density variation may be replaced by temperature variation with the opposite sign yielding the celebrated Boussinesq relation.

•

For the limit system to feel this change, the Mach and Froude characteristic numbers must be properly scaled, cf.
[
17
]
.

•

If the limit system is influenced by the centrifugal force, the same scaling must be applied. However, the scaled centrifugal force imposes imperatively
smallnes of the Rossby number in the Coriolis force.

•

In the regime of small Rossby number, the Taylor–Proudman theorem applies, enforcing the motion of the fluid to become purely horizontal.
This is in sharp contrast with the conventional and frequently used Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation advocated by Ecke and Shishkina
[
12
]
and the references cited therein.

1.2
Singular limit and the target system

The problem considered in the present paper arises as a singular limit of solutions
(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})
for
\varepsilon\to 0
. As shown in
[
13
]
,

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to[{\bf u}_{h},0],\ {\bf u}_{h}={\bf u}_{h}(t,x_{h})

\displaystyle\frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}-\lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\fint_{\Omega}\frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\to\theta,\ \lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})=1-\frac{c_{v}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}\in(0,1)

(1.8)

where the limit velocity
{\bf u}_{h}=[u^{1}_{h},u^{2}_{h},0]
is purely horizontal and satisfies the incompressible Navier–Stokes system

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{h}{\bf u}_{h}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}\partial_{t}{\bf u}_{h}+\overline{\varrho}{\rm div}_{h}({\bf u}_{h}\otimes{\bf u}_{h})+\nabla_{h}\Pi

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta_{h}{\bf u}_{h}-\left<\theta\right>\nabla_{h}F,\ \mu>0,

(1.9)

in
(0,T)\times B_{r}
, with the no-slip boundary conditions

{\bf u}_{h}|_{\partial B_{r}}=0.

(1.10)

Here, we have denoted

(1.11)

The temperature deviation
\theta
solves a perturbed heat equation

\partial_{t}\theta+{\rm div}_{x}(\theta{\bf u}_{h})=\kappa\Delta_{x}\theta+a{\rm div}_{x}(F{\bf u}_{h}),\ \kappa>0,\ a\in R,

(1.12)

in
(0,T)\times\Omega
, with a nonlocal boundary condition identified in
[
4
]

\theta|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}-\alpha\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\theta\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ \alpha=\frac{c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{c_{v}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}-1.

(1.13)

We suppose that the boundary temperature
\vartheta_{B}
is a restriction of a smooth function
on
\partial\Omega
,

\vartheta_{B}\in C^{2}(R^{3}).

(1.14)

The constants

and

We call the problem (

1.3
Mathematics of rotating Oberbeck–Boussinesq system

Our main goal is to develop a mathematical theory for the rOB system.

•

In section
2
, we show rOB system is well posed in the class of strong solutions for arbitrarily large initial/boundary data.

•

In Section
3
, we discuss the long–time behavior of solutions. We show the system is dissipative in the sense of Levinson
(cf. Haraux
[
25
]
), meaning it admits a bounded absorbing set and the corresponding trajectory attractor.

•

In Section

[
3
]
.

•

Finally, in Section
5
we show that, for well-prepared initial data, the three-dimensional model can be fully assimilated using the rOB system, with only the assimilation error being controlled, however on finite time interval.

.

2.3.5
Regularity of the time derivative of the temperature

Next, we derive estimates on the time derivative of

, where

(2.24)

L^{p}

Going back to (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Z-\kappa\Delta_{x}Z

\displaystyle Z|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

where,

Moreover,

Z\in L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)\ \mbox{for any}\ 1\leq q<\infty.

Now, similarly to Section

3
Long time behaviour

We show the rOB system is dissipative in the sense of Levinson, meaning there is a bounded absorbing set the size of which is independent of the initial data.
To this end, we make an extra assumption

0<\alpha<1

(3.1)

in the boundary condition (
1.13
). Since

\alpha=\frac{c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{c_{v}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}-1

(see
[
13
]
), hypothesis (
3.1
) is physically admissible at least for gases.

3.1
Maximum principle revisited

Applying the standard maximum principle to the parabolic equation (
2.5
) we deduce that
the maximum of
\Theta
in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
is either attained
at
t=0
, in which case

\max_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\Theta(t,x)=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\Theta_{0},

(3.2)

or there exist
0<\tau\leq T
,
\overline{x}\in\partial\Omega

such that

\max_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\Theta(t,x)=\Theta(\tau,\overline{x})=\vartheta_{B}(\overline{x})-\alpha\fint_{\Omega}\Theta(\tau,\cdot)\,{\rm d}{x}\leq\max_{x\partial\Omega}\vartheta_{B}(x)+\alpha\max_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\Theta(t,x).

As
\alpha<1
and
T
arbitrary we obtain

\max_{t\geq 0,\ x\in\overline{\Omega}}\Theta(t,x)\leq\max\left\{\|\Theta_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)};\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\|\vartheta_{B}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\right\}.

Repeating the same argument for minimum, we conclude

\|\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau,\infty)\times\Omega)}\leq\max\left\{\|\Theta(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)};\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\|\vartheta_{B}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\right\}.

(3.3)

In contrast with (
2.21
), estimate (
3.3
) is global in time.

3.2
Bounded absorbing set in
L^{2}\times L^{2}

First we show that solutions become ultimately bounded in
L^{2}

(

Let

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

The proof of Proposition

Proposition 3.2

(

Suppose

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}

\displaystyle\to\Theta\ \mbox{in}\ C_{\rm loc}(R;L^{2}(\Omega)),\ \mbox{weakly-(*) in}\ L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(R\times\overline{\Omega}),\ \mbox{and weakly in}\ L^{2}_{\rm loc}(R;W^{1,2}(\Omega)),

2.4

4
Continuous data assimilation method for rotating Oberbeck–Boussinesq system

Following the idea of Azouani, Olson, and Titi
[
3
]
, see also Carlson, Larios, and Titi
[
9
]
, Farhat, Jolly, M. S., and Titi
[
14
]
,
we develop a data assimilation method for the rOB system.

We start by introducing the interpolant operators

\displaystyle I_{\delta}[{\bf v}],\ \|I_{\delta}[{\bf v}]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{I}\Big(\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\Big),\ \|I_{\delta}[{\bf v}]-{\bf v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta)\|{\bf v}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\delta)

\displaystyle\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ \delta\to 0.

(4.1)

Next, consider an entire solution of the rOB system
({\bf u}_{h},\Theta)\in\mathcal{A}
, meaning

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{h}{\bf u}_{h}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf u}_{h}+{\rm div}_{h}({\bf u}_{h}\otimes{\bf u}_{h})+\nabla_{h}{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta_{h}{\bf u}_{h}-\left<\Theta\right>\nabla_{h}F

(4.2)

in
R\times\Omega_{h}
,

\partial_{t}\Theta+{\rm div}_{x}(\Theta{\bf u}_{h})=\kappa\Delta_{x}\Theta

(4.3)

in
R\times\Omega
,

{\bf u}_{h}|_{\partial\Omega_{h}}=0,\ \Theta|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}-\alpha\fint_{\Omega}\Theta\,{\rm d}{x}.

(4.4)

Now, we introduce a “forced” rOB system

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{h}\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}+{\rm div}_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h})+\nabla_{h}\widetilde{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta_{h}\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-\Lambda I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]-\left<\widetilde{\Theta}\right>\nabla_{h}G

(4.5)

in
(0,T)\times\Omega_{h}
,

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Theta}+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{\Theta}\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h})=\kappa\Delta_{x}\widetilde{\Theta}

(4.6)

in
(0,T)\times\Omega
,
with the boundary conditions

\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}|_{\partial\Omega_{h}}=0,\ \widetilde{\Theta}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}-\alpha\fint_{\Omega}\widetilde{\Theta}\,{\rm d}{x},

(4.7)

and the initial data

\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}(0,\cdot)={\widetilde{u}}_{0,h},\ \widetilde{\Theta}(0,\cdot)=\widetilde{\Theta}_{0}.

(4.8)

In view of (
4.1
), global solvability of the forced problem (
4.5
)–(
4.8
) can be shown repeating the arguments of the proof
of Theorem
2.1
. Here
\Lambda>0
is the so-called nudging parameter to be determined below.

Our ultimate task is to compare the distance of the solutions
(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h},\widetilde{\Theta})
,
({\bf u}_{h},\Theta)
.
Similarly to Section
2.3.2
, we introduce

Z=\Theta+\alpha\fint_{\Omega}\Theta\,{\rm d}{x}-\widehat{\vartheta}_{B},\ \widetilde{Z}=\widetilde{\Theta}+\alpha\fint_{\Omega}\widetilde{\Theta}\,{\rm d}{x}-\vartheta_{B}

rewriting (
4.3
), (
4.6
) in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\widetilde{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\fint_{\Omega}\widetilde{Z}\,{\rm d}{x}\right)+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}(\widetilde{Z}+\vartheta_{B}))

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta_{x}\widetilde{Z}\ \mbox{in}\ R\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(Z-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\fint_{\Omega}Z\,{\rm d}{x}\right)+{\rm div}_{x}({\bf u}_{h}(Z+\vartheta_{B}))

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta_{x}Z\ \mbox{in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle Z|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(4.10)

Subtracting the corresponding equations we get

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})+\nabla_{h}P

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})-\Lambda I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]-\left<\widetilde{Z}-Z\right>\nabla_{h}G

\displaystyle+{\rm div}_{h}({\bf u}_{h}\otimes{\bf u}_{h})-{\rm div}_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}),

\displaystyle(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})|_{\partial\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.11)

and,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left((\widetilde{Z}-Z)-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\fint_{\Omega}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\,{\rm d}{x}\right)+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}(\widetilde{Z}-Z))

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{Z}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})),

\displaystyle(\widetilde{Z}-Z)|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0.

(4.12)

Multiplying equation (
4.11
) on
(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})
and integrating by parts we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}

\displaystyle+\mu\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}+\Lambda\int_{\Omega_{h}}I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{h}}\left<Z-\widetilde{Z}\right>\nabla_{h}G\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{h}}{\rm div}_{h}({\bf u}_{h}\otimes{\bf u}_{h}-\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h})\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(4.13)

Similarly, multiplying (
4.12
) by
(\widetilde{Z}-Z)
and integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\left[\fint_{\Omega}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)^{2}\,{\rm d}{x}-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(\fint_{\Omega}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\,{\rm d}{x}\right)^{2}\right]+{\kappa}\fint_{\Omega}|\nabla_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)|^{2}\,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=\fint_{\Omega}Z(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\,{\rm d}{x}.

(4.14)

In accordance with (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{h}}I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega_{h}}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{h}}\Big(I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]-(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\Big)\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega_{h}}\Big(I_{\delta}[\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}]-(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\Big)\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right|\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta)\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}.

(4.15)

Moreover, we rewrite

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{h}}{\rm div}_{h}({\bf u}_{h}\otimes{\bf u}_{h}-\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h})\cdot(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla_{h}{\bf u}_{h}:(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\otimes(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x},

where, by Ladyzhenskaya interpolation inequality

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla_{h}{\bf u}_{h}:(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\otimes(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right|\leq\|\nabla_{h}{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{L}\|\nabla_{h}{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}.

(4.16)

Finally, we recall Poincaré inequality

\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\leq C_{P}\int_{\Omega_{h}}|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ \|\widetilde{Z}-Z\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\int_{\Omega_{h}}|\nabla_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

Summing up identities (
4.13
), (
4.14
) and making use of the estimates (
4.15
), (
4.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\left(\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}+\left[\fint_{\Omega}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)^{2}\,{\rm d}{x}-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(\fint_{\Omega}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\,{\rm d}{x}\right)^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\mu\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}+\Lambda\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}+{\kappa}\fint_{\Omega}|\nabla_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)|^{2}\,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta)\Lambda\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\ \|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}

\displaystyle+C_{L}\|\nabla_{h}{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{4})}

\displaystyle+\|Z\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\nabla_{x}(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{h}F\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\widetilde{{\bf u}}_{h}-{\bf u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h};R^{2})}\|\widetilde{Z}-Z\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
