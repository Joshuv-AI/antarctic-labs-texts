Introduction

Patient safety is a paramount concern in all healthcare settings, including dentistry. Adverse events (AEs) can cause patient harm, increase healthcare costs, and diminish patient satisfaction [1–4]. Understanding the types and frequencies of AEs is crucial for developing effective risk management strategies and improving care quality.

Analyzing incident reports allows for the identification of preliminary incident patterns, which serves as a foundation for developing and improving risk management systems. Identifying such patterns is essential for understanding the types of risks that exist and implementing targeted interventions to mitigate them. However, the effectiveness of identifying these patterns depends on the completeness and accuracy of incident reports, and under-reporting remains a significant limitation.

From 2019 to 2022, the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, saw an average of 247,638 patients per year. During this period, only 624 patient safety incidents were reported, accounting for approximately 0.06% of all patients. The relatively low rate of incident reports suggests significant under-reporting, which hinders the accurate identification of incident patterns and reduces the effectiveness of risk management strategies.

This study aimed to identify patterns in dental AEs, including patient, personnel, and organizational safety incidents, through a retrospective analysis of 1,618 incidents reported at Chulalongkorn University Dental Hospital from December 2018 to August 2023. This tertiary care center, with diverse dental specialties and a comprehensive incident reporting system, provided an ideal setting for an in-depth analysis of AEs.

We utilized a validated classification system, encompassing 28 main categories and numerous subcategories, to categorize patient safety incidents. This system was developed from a 5-year retrospective analysis of incident reports and approved by a panel of 14 dental risk management experts, aligning with Thailand’s Hospital Accreditation (HA) standards [5]. Additionally, we analyzed personnel and organizational safety incidents in accordance with HA standards.

To address the limitations of under-reporting and enhance the identification of incident patterns, future research is underway to develop an improved incident reporting system. This effort aims to promote a culture of reporting and ensure sufficient data quality, ultimately supporting effective risk management and enhancing patient safety in dental care.

The findings of this study will offer valuable insights into the risk profile of AEs in a dental hospital setting, contributing to the development of targeted risk management strategies and ultimately enhancing patient safety in dental care.

Method

This study employed a retrospective analysis of incident reports submitted to the dental incident reporting system at the Dental Hospital, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, over a five-year period, from December 2018 to August 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All incident reports related to patient safety incidents occurring within the dental hospital during the specified timeframe were included in the analysis. Reports that were duplicates, incomplete, or lacked essential information for categorization were excluded to ensure data quality and accuracy.

Data collection and analysis

Incident report data were retrieved from the incident reporting system database. The incidents were classified into patient safety incidents, personnel safety incidents, and organizational safety incidents. Patient safety incidents were categorized based on a classification system developed from a 5-year retrospective analysis of 1,618 incident reports at the Dental Hospital, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, and approved by a panel of 14 dental risk management experts. This classification system aligns with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Classification for Patient Safety (ICPS) [6] and the Hospital Accreditation (HA) standards of Thailand, encompassing 28 main categories and numerous subcategories. Personnel and organizational safety incidents were classified according to the HA standards of Thailand.

Descriptive statistics

Were used to summarize the frequency and distribution of dental AEs across different categories and subcategories of the classification system.

Result

A 5-year retrospective analysis of adverse events at Chulalongkorn University Dental Hospital revealed a diverse range of incidents. Out of a total of 1,618 incident reports, 66 were repeated reports (3.84%), 35 were incomplete reports (2.04%), 363 were related to organizational safety goals (21.12%), 752 were related to patient safety goals (43.75%), and 503 were linked to personnel safety goals (29.26%).

Documentation errors

The most frequently reported incidents (23.40%) pertained to errors in medical records, encompassing clerical mistakes, documentation discrepancies, and other administrative lapses. This high incidence underscored the need for robust documentation practices and quality control measures to ensure accurate and reliable patient records.

Medical emergencies

Medical emergencies in the dental clinic constituted 8.51% of reported incidents, encompassing a range of conditions such as sepsis, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), stroke, and cardiac arrest. This finding emphasized the importance of preparedness for medical emergencies in dental settings, including staff training in emergency protocols and the availability of appropriate equipment and medications.

Accidental soft tissue damage

Accidental soft tissue damage accounted for 7.58% of reported incidents, highlighting the inherent risks associated with dental procedures and the importance of meticulous technique and patient management to minimize such occurrences.

Infection control

Failure to adhere to aseptic techniques or standard precautions represented 5.85% of reported incidents. This finding underscored the critical importance of infection control protocols in dental practice to prevent healthcare-associated infections and ensure patient safety.

Post-operative infection

Accounting for 5.72% of reported incidents, post-operative infections remained a significant concern in dental practice. This finding underscored the need for stringent infection control measures throughout the entire perioperative period, including pre-operative assessment, surgical technique, and post-operative care.

The remaining top 10 categories included equipment failure (4.92%), incorrect communication regarding radiology examinations or treatment (4.79%), incorrect radiological examinations or reports (3.59%), aspiration or ingestion of foreign bodies (3.19%), and postponing elective surgery (3.19%). These incidents highlighted the diverse nature of risks in dental care and the need for comprehensive risk management strategies.

Organization safety incidents

The data revealed a significant proportion of incidents related to policy, process of work & operation (36.09%); manpower and management (30.30%); and information technology & communication, internal control & inventory (16.25%). These findings underscored the crucial role of organizational factors in overall safety. The organization safety goals, as outlined by Thailand’s Hospital Accreditation standards, emphasize the importance of having clear policies that comply with regulations, efficient manpower management with sufficient training, and effective use of information technology to support data management and communication. Addressing these issues may require interventions that prioritize policy clarity and implementation, streamline work processes, enhance manpower management practices, and ensure robust information technology systems.

Discussion

The adverse events reported in this study, derived from the incident reporting system of the Dental Hospital, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, likely represent only a fraction of the actual incidents occurring. This underreporting is evident in the high frequency of “Medical Record Errors,” which may reflect a reporting bias towards less severe or easily identifiable incidents. However, the significant number of “Medical Emergencies” (64 cases), ranking fourth in frequency, underscores the critical importance of preparedness and vigilance in the dental setting.

While medical record errors can have significant implications for patient care, medical emergencies pose a direct and immediate threat to patient safety. The fact that these events are consistently reported, despite potential underreporting biases, highlights their severity and the need for proactive risk mitigation strategies. Dental practitioners must remain vigilant in identifying and managing medical emergencies, as prompt recognition and intervention can be the difference between life and death.

The remaining categories – accidental damage, post-operative complications, communication errors, and equipment-related incidents – also contribute significantly to the overall burden of adverse events. Addressing these issues requires a multi-faceted approach, encompassing improved documentation practices, enhanced safety protocols, standardized communication procedures, and regular equipment maintenance. By acknowledging the limitations of incident reporting and focusing on high-risk categories like medical emergencies, dental institutions can take meaningful steps towards improving patient safety and quality of care.

Comparison with previous studies

The pattern of adverse events reported in this study differed from that observed by Al-Jewair et al. (2021) [7], who found that the most common AEs reported by dental assistants were related to wrong-site surgery, retained instruments, and wrong tooth extraction, while those reported by dentists were related to nerve damage and failure of endodontic treatment.

The findings from this study indicate a diverse range of patient safety incidents (PSIs) reported in a dental setting. To provide a more comprehensive perspective, these findings were compared with classifications documented in previous dentistry studies. The limited number of available studies on dental PSIs highlights the need for more focused research in this area, as the current knowledge base remains sparse.

For instance, the study by Kalenderian et al. (2021) [8] classified dental adverse events into categories such as pain, infection, hard tissue damage, and nerve injury. Comparatively, the current study also observed frequent medical record errors, post-operative complications, and procedural mistakes, which align with the types of incidents reported in previous research.

The systematic scoping review conducted by Ensaldo-Carrasco et al. (2021) [9] further emphasizes procedural errors, which are similarly reflected in our findings regarding surgical and post-operative incidents. Additionally, Kimura et al. (2021) [10] categorized adverse events related to procedures, aligning with our observations of incidents involving procedural deviations.

The classification system used in our study also draws parallels with the complexity of patient safety reporting systems as described by Renton & Master (2016) [11], who discussed the challenges in consistently classifying and reporting dental adverse events in the UK. The National Reporting and Learning System (NRLS) used in the UK lacks specificity for dental incidents, which makes direct comparison challenging and highlights the importance of developing dental-specific taxonomies.

A consistent finding across studies, including Thusu et al. (2012) [12], was the occurrence of injuries due to dental treatments or procedures. These commonalities suggest that dental practices face similar challenges globally when it comes to managing patient safety, particularly in terms of procedural errors and documentation issues.

The pattern of adverse event (AE) reporting observed in this study, characterized primarily by underreporting, may be attributed to several factors:

Reporting Predominance by Non-Dentists: Most incident reports originated from dental assistants, nurses, and other staff, rather than dentists. This aligns with previous research (Al-Jewair et al., 2021) [7] suggesting that dentists may be reluctant to report their own errors due to fear of legal or professional repercussions (O'Connor et al., 2019; Davies et al., 2017) [13, 14]. This pattern of underreporting by physicians is not unique to dentistry, as Kaya et al. (2020) [15] found that nurses reported AEs more often than physicians in a hospital setting, citing barriers such as lack of feedback and fear of blame. Sari et al. (2019) [16] and Suarthana et al. (2023) [17] further emphasized the role of nurses in reporting AEs, highlighting the influence of patient safety culture and the persistence of barriers like fear of disciplinary action.

Cultural and organizational barriers

While individual factors like fear of repercussions certainly contribute to underreporting, it is crucial to acknowledge the significant influence of organizational culture. In the context of Thai healthcare, this is particularly salient. The hierarchical structure prevalent in many Thai institutions can create a power dynamic where junior staff may feel hesitant to report incidents, especially those involving senior colleagues. This vertical hierarchy can impede open communication and create an environment where concerns are not freely raised.

Furthermore, cultural norms around respect for authority and the desire to maintain harmony can discourage individuals from reporting errors or questioning practices. There may be a reluctance to “lose face” or cause disharmony within the team by highlighting mistakes. This can be particularly pronounced in a collectivist culture like Thailand, where group cohesion and avoiding conflict are highly valued.

These cultural nuances may explain the observed predominance of incident reports originating from non-dentists. Dental assistants and nurses, often occupying less senior positions, may feel more comfortable reporting incidents compared to dentists who may be more constrained by hierarchical pressures and concerns about professional reputation.

Addressing these cultural and organizational barriers requires targeted interventions, such as fostering an open and non-punitive reporting environment, which encourages all members of the dental team to report incidents without fear of blame. Initiatives that promote transparency, supportive leadership, and education on the importance of reporting are critical to overcoming these challenges and enhancing patient safety.

Feedback on reporting system

The pattern of underreporting observed in this study reflects several influencing factors, including cultural norms, hierarchical structures, and individual fears of repercussions. While cultural factors and organizational hierarchies play significant roles in impeding open communication, feedback from reporters themselves underscores the need for a more user-friendly and responsive reporting system.

Many individuals have expressed that the current reporting process is cumbersome and time-consuming, deterring them from reporting incidents, especially those perceived as less serious. These perspectives highlight the importance of streamlining the reporting process and providing clear feedback mechanisms to demonstrate the value and impact of incident reporting. A simplified reporting system, coupled with clear communication about how reported incidents are used to improve patient safety, could encourage greater participation and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of safety risks.

The underreporting of AEs is a pervasive issue across healthcare settings, particularly in dentistry. (Stelfox et al., 2005; Gallagher et al., 2006, Kimura et al., 2021, Thusu et al., 2012, Renton and Master, 2016, Pemberton, 2014) [2, 3, 10–12, 18]. The underreporting of specific incidents in this study underscores the need for continued efforts to raise awareness among dental professionals about the importance of reporting all adverse events, regardless of perceived severity. Additionally, targeted training programs could enhance the ability of staff to identify and report specific types of incidents that are currently underreported.

The influence of the hospital’s specific context, such as the presence of experienced dentists and established risk management protocols, could explain the low incidence of certain preventable complications. However, continuous monitoring and evaluation of incident reports are essential to identify emerging risks and adapt risk management strategies accordingly.

A comprehensive classification system for dental patient safety incidents, integrated with appropriate information technology, can facilitate continuous learning, raise awareness of potential adverse events, and ultimately promote a sustained increase in incident reporting rates. User-friendly technology further enhances this process by enabling healthcare professionals to easily report incidents, understand associated risks, and recognize the value of reporting.

Stavropoulou et al. (2015) [19] conducted a systematic review of the effectiveness of incident reporting systems (IRS) in improving patient safety, highlighting the importance of clarity, ease of use, and feedback mechanisms in encouraging reporting. A comprehensive classification system aligns with these findings, as it can improve clarity and usability.

Wang et al. (2013) [20] demonstrated that the integration of a workflow-based response system with a hospital’s incident reporting system led to a significant increase in reporting rates. This suggests that integrating a well-designed classification system with a suitable information technology system can streamline reporting and encourage participation.

The World Health Organization (WHO, 2020) [21] emphasizes the importance of clear definitions and categories for incident reporting, recommending structured data capture and free-text narrative elements to facilitate data analysis and risk management. A comprehensive classification system would align with these recommendations, contributing to a more effective and informative incident reporting system.

An analysis of dental patient safety incidents, classified according to the Hospital Accreditation Standards and the Thailand National Reporting and Learning System (TNRLS) [5, 22–24] framework, reveals several key insights:

Limited Granularity of TNRLS: While the TNRLS classification provides a broad categorization of incident types (e.g., Safe Surgery, Infection Prevention and Control, etc.), it lacks the granularity necessary for effective risk management in dental settings. The high incidence rates within broad categories like "Patient Care Processes" and "Safe Surgery" underscore the need for a more refined classification system tailored to dental-specific procedures and risks.

Aligning the classification of dental adverse events with the TNRLS framework, as in the present study, facilitates the summarization of results. Integrating this classification with a robust information technology system enables automated reporting, reducing the burden on healthcare providers.

A 5-year retrospective analysis of safety incidents at the Chulalongkorn University Dental Hospital, categorized as either Personnel Safety Incidents or Organization Safety Incidents, reveals a diverse range of concerns. The most frequently reported incidents pertained to the working atmosphere and environment, followed by policy, process, and operational issues, and work-related injuries with potential for contact transmission. These findings underscore the need for a comprehensive risk management approach within the dental hospital setting.

Several factors may contribute to this pattern of incident reporting. The high frequency of incidents related to the working atmosphere and environment suggests that factors such as workload, stress, and physical conditions may contribute to safety risks. The substantial number of policy, process, and operational incidents indicates potential areas for improvement in standardizing procedures and ensuring adherence to safety protocols. The prevalence of work-related injuries, particularly those with potential for contact transmission, emphasizes the necessity for rigorous infection control measures and staff training in safe practices.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its attempt to identify patterns of patient safety incidents specifically in the dental setting, while making efforts to classify these incidents in a manner that addresses the gaps found in general healthcare taxonomies. However, the reliance on descriptive incident reports limits the ability to capture all potential adverse events, especially when considering underreporting by staff who may be unsure of what constitutes a reportable event. The underreporting issue is also consistent with findings from other studies, such as those mentioned earlier, where reluctance to report due to fear of repercussions was noted.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings from this study suggest a critical need for targeted training and awareness campaigns to encourage more comprehensive reporting of adverse events, especially among dental professionals. Developing a user-friendly, dental-specific classification system is essential for improving the accuracy and completeness of incident data. Such efforts can help foster a culture of safety and continuous improvement in dental care practices.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the current patterns of adverse events reported at the Dental Hospital, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University. The observed patterns indicate that underreporting is a significant issue, with incident reports primarily focusing on less severe events, such as medical record errors, while critical incidents like medical emergencies are consistently reported. This suggests a need for increased awareness and cultural change within dental practice to encourage more comprehensive reporting of all adverse events, regardless of perceived severity.

The study’s results emphasize the need for ongoing efforts to improve the accuracy and completeness of incident reporting. Targeted training and education initiatives could help address barriers to reporting, particularly among dental professionals who may be reluctant to report errors due to concerns about professional consequences. Moreover, while the low incidence of certain preventable complications may reflect the hospital’s specific context, such as the presence of experienced staff and established risk management protocols, it may also indicate limitations in the reporting system itself. Therefore, continued monitoring and efforts to strengthen the reporting process are essential for identifying and managing emerging risks effectively.

Developing and implementing a comprehensive, user-friendly, and dental-specific classification system can foster a culture of safety, enhance learning from adverse events, and ultimately improve the quality of patient care. This study primarily focuses on understanding the reported patterns of adverse events, and future research should aim to refine classification methods, reduce underreporting, and enhance the comprehensiveness of dental incident reporting. These efforts will contribute to better risk management and improved patient safety outcomes in dental care.
