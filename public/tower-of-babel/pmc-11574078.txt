Comparison between spectral-domain optical coherence tomography-based and partial coherence interferometry-based biometry
Authors: So Min Ahn, Young Ho Kim, Cheolmin Yun, Jaeryung Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe compared axial length (AL) measurements using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT)-based and partial coherence interferometry (PCI)-based biometry in eyes with various chorioretinal diseases. Medical records were retrospectively analyzed in 123 eyes from 73 patients who underwent AL measurements using SD-OCT and PCI. AL values were highly consistent by both measurements (ICC = 0.996 in SD-OCT and 0.995 in PCI-based biometry). The mean difference in AL between the two instruments was − 0.09 ± 0.17 mm, which was significant (P < 0.001). There was a strong correlation between the measurements obtained using the two instruments (r = 0.982, P < 0.001). The 95% limits of agreement were − 0.43 to 0.25 mm, and the agreement of AL in the Bland–Altman analysis showed a difference. The difference in measurements was not associated with the difference in central retinal thickness (r = 0.033, P = 0.720). However, the presence of an epiretinal membrane affected the difference in measurements, unlike in other chorioretinal diseases (P = 0.014). SD-OCT-based biometry showed a good correlation with PCI-based biometry in the measurement of AL; however, the mean AL between the two instruments was significantly different. Therefore, AL measurements made using SD-OCT-based and PCI-based biometry are not interchangeable.Keywords: Axial length, Optical coherence tomography, Partial coherence interferometry, Correlation Chorioretinal diseaseSubject terms: Visual system, Physical examination

Introduction

Axial length (AL) is a basic parameter in ophthalmology1. AL can be an important factor in differentiating between diseases such as nanophthalmos or pathologic myopia and in differentiating between refractive errors such as hyperopia or myopia2–5. In addition, AL is important in biometry in determining intraocular lens (IOL) power after refractive surgery such as cataract surgery6.

The IOL Master (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) is currently the most commonly used instrument for measuring AL. A partial coherence interferometry (PCI)-based biometry, such as the IOLMaster, uses PCI and infrared light of 780 nm wavelength7. Since its introduction in 1999, the IOL power is relatively accurate, and the refractive error is small after cataract surgery; therefore, it has been widely used8,9. Recently, with the development of optical coherence tomography (OCT) to measure AL, a novel method, has been developed10–12. It is often difficult to measure AL in patients with low vision due to corneal lesions, retinal disease, or severe cataracts because of poor eye fixation10. The advantage of this instrument is that fixation can be confirmed by visualizing the macula on OCT. Several studies have shown a good correlation between OCT-based and PCI-based AL measurements9. However, the effect of chorioretinal diseases on the difference between measurements using these instruments has not yet been widely studied.

This study aimed to assess the differences and consistency in AL measurements between a PCI-based biometry and an instrument using spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT). Furthermore, we evaluated the effect of chorioretinal disease on the difference in AL measurements between two instruments.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Medical Center in Seoul, Korea (approval no. 2021AN0505). All data collection and analyses were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective study, the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Medical Center waived the need to obtain informed consent from the participants. We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who visited the retinal clinic at the Korea University Medical Center and had undergone AL measurements with two different instruments.

AL measurement

The AL was compared between a novel instrument using whole-eye SD-OCT (HOCT-1 F; Huvitz Co., Ltd., Anyang-si, South Korea) and a PCI-based biometry (IOLMaster, version 5.4, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Germany). The Whole-eye SD-OCT is a device capable of performing fundus imaging, OCT scanning, OCT angiography, anterior segment analysis, and biometry. Among its various capabilities, it includes a biometric mode for measuring AL.

The SD-OCT-based biometry system used in this study acquires images with horizontal resolution of 20 μm and axial resolution of 7 μm by a central wavelength of 840 nm and speed of 68,000 A-scans/s. It automatically identifies the macula and cornea using OCT, displaying them as a 2D b-scan image on the screen. Following optimization of these images, AL is then measured. AL was determined using SD-OCT as the distance from the anterior corneal tear film to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). PCI-based biometry uses a 780-nm infrared light with a semiconductor diode laser and has two reflection sites of inference signals (the anterior surface of the cornea and RPE) to measure AL13–15. AL was measured repeatedly at least five times with PCI-based biometry and one to three times with SD-OCT-based biometry. The eye was either phakic or pseudophakic, and the axial length (AL) was measured by selecting the appropriate lens status on both instruments.

Measurements of chorioretinal diseases

We classified chorioretinal diseases into the following seven disease groups: age-related macular degeneration (AMD), pachychoroid spectrum diseases, epiretinal membrane (ERM), diabetic retinopathy (DR), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), macular hole (MH), and retinitis pigmentosa (RP). We obtained the retinal thickness from SD-OCT measurements. Volume scans of SD-OCT were performed on a 12 × 9-mm area containing the macula, and each scan consisted of 256 A-scans. Retinal thickness was measured in the central 1 mm area noted in the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study chart16.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A paired t-test was used to compare the differences in AL measurements between a PCI-based biometry and an instrument using SD-OCT. Measurement consistency between the two instruments was analyzed using the intra-group correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis. Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the correlation of mean AL between the two instruments and the correlation between the mean difference in AL between the two instruments and the central macular thickness (CMT). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the mean difference in AL between the two instruments according to chorioretinal disease. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) was used to assess the association between the mean difference in AL between the two instruments and chorioretinal disease. The mean difference in AL between the two instruments was calculated as the value of the mean AL by PCI minus the value of the mean AL by SD-OCT. Excluding values with a difference of 0, we analyzed whether the negative or positive differences were related to the disease. GEE was used to identify the significant predictors of AL. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

We reviewed 123 eyes of 73 patients; 56.2% of patients were men, and the mean age was 64.32 ± 9.97 years. Of 123 eyes, 119 (96.7%) were phakic eyes, and 4 (3.3%) were pseudophakic eyes (Table 1). The mean CMT was 271.71 ± 77.82 μm. Of the 123 eyes, 109 had chorioretinal disease, whereas the others were normal (Table 1). Among the eyes with chorioretinal disease, 18 had DR, 32 had non-neovascular AMD, 20 had neovascular AMD, 25 had ERM, 9 had RVO, 3 had central serous chorioretinopathy, 9 had pachychoroid pigment epitheliopathy, 1 had IOL dislocation, 1 had MH, and 2 had RP.

The ICC was 0.996 for AL measurements with SD-OCT and 0.995 for measurements with PCI (Table 2). In addition, the coefficient of variation (CV) was 0.25% for AL measurements with SD-OCT and 0.17% with PCI. There were no significant differences in the ICC or CV between the two instruments. Both instruments showed excellent reliability and low variability.

Comparison of AL measurements between the instruments

The mean AL was 23.76 ± 0.88 mm using SD-OCT and 23.67 ± 0.91 mm using PCI. The mean difference in the AL between the two instruments was significant (P < 0.001) (Table 2). AL measurements were highly correlated between the two instruments (r = 0.982, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1a). The mean difference in AL values between the two instruments was − 0.09 ± 0.17 mm (Fig. 1b). The 95% limits of agreement were − 0.43 to 0.25 mm.

Effect of chorioretinal diseases on the difference in AL measurements

The difference in measurements was not influenced by CMT (r = 0.033, P = 0.720) (Fig. 1c). The mean CMT was 243.29 ± 13.43 μm in eyes without chorioretinal diseases and 275.36 ± 81.87 μm with any chorioretinal disease. The mean CMT was 271.19 ± 81.6 μm in eyes without DR and 274.72 ± 52.12 μm with DR, 281.56 ± 86.39 μm without non-neovascular AMD and 243.69 ± 32.74 μm with non-neovascular AMD, 280.50 ± 79.64 μm without neovascular AMD and 226.40 ± 47.53 μm with neovascular AMD, 248.36 ± 48.37 μm without ERM and 363.24 ± 101.88 μm with ERM, and 264.04 ± 69.26 μm without RVO and 368.78 ± 114.74 μm with RVO. There was no significant difference in CMT between eyes with and without DR (P = 0.224). In contrast, there were significant differences in CMT between eyes with non-neovascular AMD, neovascular AMD, ERM, or RVO and without these diseases (P = 0.017, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, and P = 0.003, respectively).

The mean difference in AL between the instruments was − 0.10 ± 0.14 mm in eyes without any chorioretinal diseases and − 0.09 ± 0.18 mm with any chorioretinal disease. There was no significant difference between eyes without any chorioretinal diseases and with chorioretinal disease in mean difference of AL between the instruments (P = 0.845). The mean difference in AL was − 0.09 ± 0.19 mm in eyes without DR and − 0.06 ± 0.07 mm with DR, -0.09 ± 0.19 mm without non-neovascular AMD and − 0.08 ± 0.11 mm with non-neovascular AMD, -0.06 ± 0.15 mm without neovascular AMD and − 0.21 ± 0.24 mm with neovascular AMD, -0.10 ± 0.18 mm without ERM and − 0.05 ± 0.03 mm with ERM, and − 0.09 ± 0.18 mm without RVO and − 0.08 ± 0.12 mm with RVO. The mean difference in AL was not statistically significant between eyes with DR, non-neovascular AMD, and RVO and without these diseases (P = 0.352, P = 0.831, and P = 0.823, respectively) (Fig. 2). The mean difference in AL was statistically significant between eyes with neovascular AMD or ERM and without these diseases (P < 0.001 and P = 0.007, respectively) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

There have been several previous reports with the IOL Master 700 using swept-source OCT (SS-OCT)7,8,10,11,17,18. When comparing the IOL Master 700 and IOL Master 500, the IOL Master 700 demonstrated excellent repeatability and reproducibility, similar to the IOL Master 5008,10,11. Additionally, the IOL Master 700 showed better results when there was severe lens opacity8,10,11. In addition, AL measured by biometry using SS-OCT was also more precise in myopia18. Even when visual acuity is poor owing to various ophthalmic conditions, the accuracy of the equipment using OCT could be improved because the examiner could know the status of fixation through imaging of the fovea on OCT10,18. There have been several reports of SS-OCT-based biometry to measure ophthalmic parameters, as mentioned above, but there are very few reports of SD-OCT-based biometry19,20. Similar to SS-OCT-based biometry, SD-OCT-based biometry also exhibits excellent repeatability19,20. It can be used to verify proper fixation through foveal scan images and to ensure the RPE is well detected in cases of macular lesions12,21. In this study, we used SD-OCT to demonstrate that a novel instrument using SD-OCT showed good consistency in various ranges of AL and in various ocular diseases. Similar to previous studies comparing SD-OCT-based biometry and PCI-based biometry, this study also demonstrated good repeatability21,22. Compared to SS-OCT-based biometry, SD-OCT-based biometry has the advantage of being able to scan a wider range of the macula with better resolution12,21. This is also an important advantage over PCI, where the B-scan cannot confirm whether the RPE in the fovea has been properly detected.

There are few reports on the usability of SD-OCT-based biometry19,20. These studies reported that SD-OCT biometry showed high repeatability and reproducibility and good correlation and agreement with PCI-based biometry. However, these studies only included healthy individuals. Therefore, it was not possible to determine the effect of the presence of chorioretinal diseases on the repeatability of the instrument or the correlation between the two instruments. In this study, mean difference of AL between the instruments was not significant difference between without any chorioretinal diseases and with chorioretinal disease. However, the mean difference in AL between the instruments was about 0.10 mm in eyes without any chorioretinal diseases and 0.09 mm with any chorioretinal disease. It is known that a 0.1 mm difference in AL can cause a refractive error of about 0.27 D23. Therefore, this should be taken into consideration when calculating IOL power in the clinic. Our study included patients with various chorioretinal diseases, in particular, DR, AMD, ERM, and RVO, which are representative chorioretinal diseases commonly encountered in ophthalmology clinics. In cases of severe macular edema or subretinal fluid, individuals may experience vision impairment or difficulty in focusing, potentially affecting the accuracy of axial length measurements. To address this, we conducted an analysis focusing on CMT values. As whole-eye SD-OCT-based biometry involves OCT measurements, we also conducted CMT measurements. We included the CMT value to examine the association between CMT and axial length in this study and evaluated the effects of chorioretinal diseases on AL. Some studies comparing AL measurements obtained with PCI-based biometry have shown that conditions increasing CMT, such as macular edema or retinal detachment, can affect AL measurement results24,25. This may be due to the AL being measured by focusing on retinal layers other than the RPE layer due to macular edema or retinal detachment, or due to poor fixation. However, the other study has reported no significant relationship between CMT and AL26. Except for cases involving ERM and neovascular AMD, where variations in CMT values were noted, our study found no significant differences in axial length measurements between the two instruments across other disease conditions. In this study, we showed that AL was well correlated between PCI- and SD-OCT-based biometry. However, the mean AL was significantly different between PCI and SD-OCT, and the agreement of AL in the Bland–Altman plot showed some differences. The AL measured using SD-OCT was slightly longer than that measured using PCI. The mean difference in AL (AL by IOLMaster and AL by SD-OCT) was − 0.09 ± 0.17 mm. Some studies have reported that AL measured by OCT is longer than that measured by IOLMaster7,18,20. The reason for the difference in values obtained when using the two instruments for measuring AL may be the difference in technologies used. PCI-based biometry determines the depth of each eye structure by measuring the time it takes for laser light to pass through and return. This method allows for relatively rapid measurements. In contrast, SD-OCT-based biometry utilizes SD-OCT technology, sends light from a light source to an interferometer, where it is split into a reference mirror and a sample arm. The light reflected from both the sample arm and the reference mirror returns to the interferometer, where interference resulting from the phase difference between the two lights is measured. This interference data is then analyzed using Fourier transform to reconstruct spectra gathered at multiple wavelengths into a high-resolution image. Therefore, due to its capability to provide detailed measurements at very high resolution, SD-OCT-based biometry can accurately target the RPE layer of the retina for measuring AL. Besides, just as the positioning of the RPE boundary in OCT can vary depending on the device27, the location reflected in the RPE may differ slightly between PCI and SD-OCT. Additionally, the reason for the difference in values obtained when using the two instruments for measuring AL may be poor fixation. Some patients have extrafoveal fixation due to ocular disease and they may have eyes that are out of focus. However, AL measurement using OCT can show the status of foveal fixation by monitoring the macula. Although the novel instrument using SD-OCT and IOLMaster are not interchangeable in terms of AL measurement because of differences in instrument technology, the high correlation between the two instruments is sufficient to trust the values measured by SD-OCT.

The repeatability of SD-OCT was good based on the ICC and CV values. There was no significant difference in repeatability between SD-OCT- and PCI-based biometry, despite the various states of chorioretinal diseases. However, in the eyes with some chorioretinal diseases, there was a significant difference in the AL between SD-OCT- and PCI-based biometry. Upon examining the mean difference in AL values alone, it might appear that there was less variation in eyes with ERM. However, this observation seems to be attributed to the diverse dispersion of AL values resulting from various chorioretinal diseases, as depicted in Fig. 1b and c. In fact, there have been reports that an inaccuracy problem occurs when measuring AL with PCI-based biometry in patients with various chorioretinal diseases in the clinic28–31. PCI-based biometry measures AL as the distance from the cornea to the RPE13–15. However, PCI-based biometry sometimes notes a double peak in macular disease28. In addition, in eyes with ERM, the posterior peak (one of the two peaks) is hidden, making the anterior peak prominent, causing this to be measured as a single peak by PCI-based biometry28,31. In cases where there is a condition that influences an increase in reflectivity of the inner retina, the RPE reflectivity tends to decrease32–36. In cases of macular edema or low vision, focusing and centering of the fovea could be difficult when measuring the AL. Therefore, in cases with chorioretinal disease or with low vision, AL may be more accurately measured with OCT-based biometry than with PCI-based biometry29. For diseases that can affect reflectivity, such as ERM or other diseases, it would be helpful to measure AL while directly monitoring OCT, as shown in Fig. 3c. If the patient has a retinal disease or low vision, loss of fixation could occur, and OCT-based biometry would be helpful to measure AL. Although OCT-based biometry has its advantages, it is well known that PCI-based biometry, which has been widely used, is also accurate. Therefore, despite the differences in AL measurements between the two instruments, by comparing the scatter plot of eyes without chorioretinal disease shown in Fig. 3a with that of eyes with ERM shown in Fig. 3b, it can be seen that although there is one eye with ERM that falls outside the 95% limits of agreement, the scatter plots are relatively well-maintained. However, it is important to note the small number of eyes without chorioretinal disease and, consequently, the limited number of eyes with ERM in this study. Since PCI may lead to double peaks and potentially incorrect detection of reference planes,, future investigations may require a comparison involving a larger sample size of eyes with ERM and normal eyes to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the observed differences.

This study had some limitations. First, data were retrospectively evaluated. However, because of the large sample size, the AL between the two instruments showed a strong correlation. Second, AL by SD-OCT was measured one to three times. Although the mean AL showed a significant difference between instruments, the mean difference in AL was only − 0.09 mm, and the ICC was over 0.9. If the number of repeated measurements is increased, the accuracy of the instrument using the SD-OCT can be further increased. Third, other biometrics such as corneal power or anterior chamber depth were not compared. Therefore, further evaluation of other biometrics and the difference in IOL power between the two instruments is needed. Firth, due to the retrospective nature of our analysis involving measurements from patients at an ophthalmology clinic, the sample size of normal eyes was limited. Consequently, comparing normal and diseased eyes posed a challenge. Future efforts should focus on collecting additional data to facilitate comparative studies between normal and diseased eyes.

In conclusion, AL measurements obtained using the novel biometric instrument based on SD-OCT and the traditional PCI-based biometry are not directly interchangeable. However, SD-OCT-based biometry demonstrates a strong correlation with PCI-based biometry in the measurement of AL and exhibits excellent reliability.
