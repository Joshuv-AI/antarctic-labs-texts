GFI1 enhances MHC-I expression in tumor cells and augments tumor responsiveness to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors
Authors: Yu Xi, Kewei Xiao, Kelin Meng, Taiyan Yu, Tianlai Wang, Xu Wang, Ounan Pan, Chenxi Zeng, Xue Wang, Xiangning Fu, Lequn Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrowth factor-independent-1 transcription repressor (GFI1) is crucial for determining the differentiation and fate of exhausted CD8+ T cells and affects immunotherapy outcomes. However, the effects of tumor cell–intrinsic GFI1 on antitumor immunity and the response to immunotherapy remain unknown.MethodsThe tumor cell-intrinsic expression of GFI1 was examined via RT‒qPCR and Western blotting. Immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissues was performed to assess the relationships among GFI1, CD8, and MHC I expression. A syngeneic mouse model and clinical tumor samples from NSCLC patients undergoing immunochemotherapy were used to evaluate how tumor cell-intrinsic GFI1 affects the response to immunotherapy. Additionally, techniques such as flow cytometry, immunoprecipitation, ChIP-qPCR, immunofluorescence, and both loss- and gain-of-function approaches were employed to investigate the underlying mechanisms involved.ResultsTumor-intrinsic GFI1 expression was positively associated with MHC I expression and the infiltration of CD8+ T cells. Tumors with higher GFI1 levels showed a significantly better response to immunotherapy. The overexpression of GFI1 in tumor cells increases MHC-I expression in a STAT1-dependent manner. Mechanistic analysis revealed that GFI1 inhibits HDAC1 activity in tumor cells by promoting its export to the cytoplasm, thereby increasing H3K9 acetylation and the transcription of STAT1 and MHC-I. Additionally, phenformin, an AMPK activator, increases GFI1 levels by downregulating EZH2 expression. Compared with anti-PD-L1 therapy alone, combining phenformin with anti-PD-L1 therapy enhances antitumor effects.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the crucial role of tumor-intrinsic GFI1 in enhancing antitumor immune responses, suggesting a new target for increasing the effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04430-y.Keywords: GFI1, Immunotherapy, MHC-I, HDAC1, AMPK

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a groundbreaking approach for treating non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), offering long-lasting survival benefits for some patients [1, 2]. Multiple lines of evidence indicate that tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) can predict patient response to ICI therapy [3–5]. Tumors display either a “hot” (immune-inflamed) or a “cold” (immune-desert) phenotype based on the density and functional state of TILs [6]. “Hot” tumors show intense immune cell infiltration and increased effector activity, whereas “cold” tumors are characterized by poor antigen presentation, limited immune cell infiltration, and low responsiveness to ICIs [7, 8].

To understand the role of TIL status in modulating tumor immunity and immunotherapy, a recent study by Shi and coworkers revealed that transcriptional repressor growth factor-independent 1 (GFI1) plays a key role in determining the differentiation and fate of exhausted CD8+ T cells. GFI1 deletion significantly reduces the effectiveness of anti-CTLA-4 therapy, highlighting the critical role of GFI1 in antitumor immunity [9].

GFI1 is an essential regulator of hematopoietic stem cells [10, 11]. During the differentiation of hemogenic endothelial cells into hematopoietic cells, GFI1 interacts with a complex that includes the histone demethylase LSD1, the cofactor CoREST, and histone deacetylases (HDACs) to coordinate the demethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4), which reduces its transcriptional activity [12]. By repressing endothelial genes and cell adhesion molecules, GFI1 facilitates the transition of adherent endothelial cells into hematopoietic stem cells [13].

In addition to its roles in hematopoiesis and in regulating T-cell-mediated immunity, GFI1 can also be expressed in malignant tumor cells [14–16]. In Group 3 medulloblastoma, EZH2 reportedly suppresses GFI1 expression at the transcriptional level and inhibits tumor growth [17]. However, the effects of tumor-intrinsic GFI1 on antitumor immunity and the success of immunotherapy remain unclear. In this study, we focused on the role of tumor cell-expressed GFI1 in regulating antitumor immunity and the response of these cells to anti-PD-L1 therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

A549 (ATCC Cat# CRL-7909) and H2030 (ATCC Cat# CRL-5914) cells were obtained from Cobioer Biosciences (Nanjing, China) and cultured in RPMI medium (HyClone, Omaha, NE, USA) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. LLC (ATCC Cat #CRL-1642) cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All the cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Antibodies and reagents

All the antibodies and reagents used are listed in Table S1.

Overexpression of GFI1

Human and mouse GFI1 cDNA fragments were amplified and ligated into the pLV4ltr-PGK-Puro-FLAG vector to generate lentiviral expression plasmids. 293T cells were cotransfected with psPAX2, pMD2G, or the indicated lentivirus-based plasmids to produce virus-containing medium. After filtration, the medium was used to transfect A549, H2030, and LLC cells. The infected cells were selected with puromycin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

siRNA transfection

siRNAs targeting GFI1 and STAT1 were obtained from PAIVIBIO (Wuhan, China), and the sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S2. After premixing in serum-free medium, the siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) mixture was added to the tumor cell suspensions. The suspensions were then seeded in culture plates and cultured for 48 h. The efficiency of GFI1 and STAT1 knockdown was evaluated via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and immunoblotting.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA from tumor cells was extracted using TRIzol (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). A total of 500‒1000 ng of mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using an RT reagent kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using a SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on an Applied Biosystems thermal cycler (Vilnius, Lithuania). The data were normalized to ACTB expression via the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers used were obtained from Tsingke Biotechnology (Wuhan, China), and the sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blotting analysis

Tumor cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein samples were separated using SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The blots were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Abbkine, Wuhan, China), and the signals were acquired using a cooled CCD imaging system (Tanon 5200, Shanghai, China).

Immunoprecipitation

Tumor cells were lysed using IP lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Approximately 10% of the cell lysates were separated as input, and the remaining lysates were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Then, 20 μL of protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) was added to each sample and incubated for one hour at room temperature. After the magnetic beads were collected using a magnetic column, they were washed three times with IP washing buffer (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20). The immunoprecipitated proteins were boiled in SDS loading buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 min and analyzed by immunoblotting.

Histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity

HDAC activity in the cell lysates was assessed using an HDAC Activity Colorimetric Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Tumor cells seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 15 min. After three washes with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS (Standard Reagent, Hyderabad, India) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). GFI1 and HDAC1 were stained with primary antibodies and fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The cells were imaged using a wide-field fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Baden-Württemberg, Germany), and three randomly selected fields were analyzed.

Flow cytometry

For cell-surface MHC-I staining, tumor cells were incubated with antibodies against HLA-ABC and H-2Db/H-2 Kb for 30 min at 4 °C.

To stain tumor-infiltrating T cells in tissues, the LLC tumor samples were minced and resuspended in PBS. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through 100 μm nylon cell strainers (Biosharp, Hefei, China). Mouse lymphocyte separation medium (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) was used to isolate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Following centrifugation, the lymphocyte fraction was harvested and subsequently incubated with anti-mouse CD45, anti-CD3, and anti-CD8 antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. Next, the cells were fixed with IC fixation buffer (eBioscience, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (Invitrogen), and incubated with an anti-murine perforin antibody for 1 h at 4 °C. An Attune NxT acoustic flow cytometer was used for flow cytometric analysis. The data were analyzed via FlowJo software v10.

ChIP assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were conducted via a SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, tumor cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The nuclei were isolated and degraded by micrococcal nuclease and ultrasonication. The digested chromatin was then immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibodies. The enriched DNA was subsequently purified and quantified by qPCR. The sequences of the primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Animal model

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). To assess the antitumor immunity of GFI1-overexpressing LLC tumors, 1 × 106 NC or GFI1-overexpressing LLC cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of C57BL/6 mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, the mice were randomized into four groups. Anti-PD-L1 antibody (10 mg/kg; BioXcell, Lebanon, USA) or isotype control (10 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 3 times at 3-day intervals. The tumor volume (0.5 × length × width2) was measured every two days via calipers.

To assess the antitumor effect of phenformin combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, 1 × 106 LLC cells were subcutaneously injected into C57BL/6 mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups. Phenformin (100 mg/kg/day; MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) or solvent was administered orally once daily for 6 days per week. Anti-PD-L1 antibody (10 mg/kg) or isotype control (10 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 3 times at 3-day intervals.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and quantification

The tumor samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. After incubation at 75 °C for 90 min, the tumor slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in ethanol. Next, the antigens were retrieved in EDTA solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The antigens were stained with DAB solution. To assess the expression of GFI1 and MHC-I in tumor samples, three representative 200× fields were randomly selected and evaluated using the H-score with the following formula: H-score = 3 × percentage of strongly stained cells + 2 × percentage of moderately stained cells + 1 × percentage of weakly stained cells. The results are reported as the average of three fields. The number of CD8+ cells in each tumor sample was calculated as the mean of three representative 200× fields. For costaining of GFI1 and Pan-keratin, the antigens were visualized using a tyramine signal amplification kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). To evaluate the expression of GFI1 in tumor and nontumor areas, three representative 200× fields were randomly selected from each sample.

CRISPR-Cas9

Stable GFI1-knockout LLC cells were generated using sgRNA targeting the murine Gfi1 gene. sgRNA-Cas9 constructs were assembled in LentiCRISPR v2-puro vectors and obtained from PAIVIBIO (Wuhan, China). Knockout efficiency was verified by western blotting.

Clinical tissue samples

All human lung cancer tissues used were collected at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College. Advanced non-small cell lung cancer patients who received neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy before surgical lung tumor resection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Tongji Hospital, since 2020, were reviewed. Patients with complete pre- and post-immunotherapy radiological data were selected for analysis. Patients were divided into five responders (complete or partial response, or stable disease ≥ 6 months) and four non-responders (stable disease < 6 months or progressive disease) according to the standard Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST v1.1) guidelines. All tissue samples were collected in accordance with the informed consent policy.

Bioinformatics analysis

NSCLC (LUAD, 512 samples; LUSC, 496 samples) data were downloaded from the TCGA database. Samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median GFI1 expression level: those above the median were assigned to the high-expression group, and those below the median were assigned to the low-expression group. The normalized expression of CD8A, CD8B, PRF1, and GZMB was used to define the CD8+ T-cell infiltration signature, as described previously [18]. RNA-seq data (GSE135222 and GSE126044) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The data were divided into responders and non-responders based on clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using Prism v9.0 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was employed. When multiple groups were compared, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. For in vivo experiments, two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to evaluate differences in tumor growth.

Results

GFI1 increases MHC-I expression in tumor cells

We first evaluated GFI1 expression in tumor tissues. Tumor samples from NSCLC patients were co-stained with GFI1 and Pan-keratin. As shown in Fig. 1A, GFI1 was present in neoplastic cells. To investigate the potential role of tumor-intrinsic GFI1, we performed a gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using data from the TCGA LUAD and LUSC cohorts. As shown in Figs. 1B and S1, the antigen presentation and processing gene signature was enriched in lung tumor samples with high GFI1 expression. Consistently, the expression of MHC-I was also significantly upregulated in the GFI1-high group (Fig. 1C).

Since antigen processing and presentation capacity are linked to increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment [19], we used the TCGA dataset to evaluate the gene signatures of CD8+ T-cell infiltration (CD8A, CD8B, PRF1, and GZMB) in tumors with high versus low GFI1 expression. High GFI1 expression was associated with increased expression of genes related to CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 1D). As shown in Fig. 1E, as confirmed by immunohistochemical staining of tumor samples, tumor tissues with elevated GFI1 levels exhibited increased MHC-I expression and greater infiltration of CD8+ T cells.

Next, to determine whether GFI1 directly influences MHC-I expression in lung tumor cells, we generated GFI1-overexpressing A549 and H2030 cell lines. As shown in Fig. 1F and G, A549 and H2030 cells overexpressing GFI1 exhibited significantly higher levels of MHC-I expression at both the protein and mRNA levels than did NC-overexpressing cells. Notably, the cell-surface expression of MHC-I was also increased in GFI1-overexpressing lung cancer cells (Fig. 1H). We then transfected A549 and H2030 cells with small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting GFI1. GFI1 knockdown reduced MHC-I levels in tumor cells (Fig. 1I–K). Therefore, both loss- and gain-of-function experiments demonstrated that GFI1 directly regulates MHC-I expression in lung cancer cells.

GFI1 enhances tumor immunogenicity in an LLC mouse model

To determine whether GFI1 expression levels in tumors affect their responsiveness to immunotherapy, we reanalyzed data from published ICIs clinical trials in NSCLC (GSE135222/GSE126044). GFI1 levels in tumors were associated with increased response rates to ICIs (Fig. 2A). Additionally, we examined a cohort of 9 patients with advanced NSCLC who underwent surgical resection after neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy. Patients were classified as responders or non-responders based on changes observed in CT scans between baseline and follow-up. The specific classification criteria are detailed in the Materials and Methods section. Notably, tumors from responders exhibited higher GFI1 expression than those from non-responders did (Fig. 2B).

Next, we examined how tumor-intrinsic GFI1 levels affect antitumor immunity using an LLC mouse model. First, a Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cell line stably overexpressing GFI1 was generated. Like GFI1-overexpressing A549 and H2030 cells, LLC cells with increased GFI1 expression presented increased MHC-I expression (Fig. 2C–E). Then, control LLC cells or GFI1-overexpressing LLC cells were injected subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice, followed by treatment with either an isotype control or anti-PD-L1 antibody. Compared with control LLC tumors, GFI1-overexpressing tumors had higher MHC-I expression in vivo (Fig. 2F and G). Notably, only GFI1-overexpressing tumors responded to PD-L1 blockade therapy, whereas control LLC tumors did not respond (Fig. 2H and I). As shown in Fig. 2J, the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells was significantly higher in GFI1-overexpressing tumors than in control LLC tumors. Additionally, PD-L1 blockade did not significantly increase CD8+ T-cell infiltration in control LLC tumors, but it markedly increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration in GFI1-overexpressing LLC tumors. These results demonstrate that tumor-intrinsic GFI1 enhances antitumor immunity in vivo.

Notably, overexpressing GFI1 in tumor cells alone did not affect the effector function or proliferation of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, as indicated by perforin and granzyme B expression and by Ki67 expression, respectively. PD-L1 blockade increased the percentages of perforin+CD8+ and granzyme B+CD8+ T cells in GFI1-overexpressing tumors but not in control LLC tumors without affecting cell proliferation (Fig. 2K).

GFI1 regulates MHC-I expression in a STAT1-dependent manner

IFN-γ and its downstream signaling pathway play critical roles in regulating MHC-I expression in tumor cells [19]. GFI1 overexpression significantly increased IFN-γ-induced MHC-I expression (Figure S2A and S2B), whereas GFI1 knockdown reduced IFN-γ–induced MHC-I expression (Figure S2C and S2D), indicating that GFI1 expression in tumor cells acts as a positive regulator of IFN-γ–STAT1-mediated induction of MHC-I expression.

Signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) is a critical transcription factor that mediates IFN-γ–induced MHC-I expression [20]. Correlation analysis of the TCGA dataset revealed that GFI1 expression was positively correlated with STAT1 levels in tumor samples (Figure S2E). To investigate whether STAT1 was involved in GFI1-mediated MHC-I regulation, we examined STAT1 expression in A549 and H2030 cells in which GFI1 was either overexpressed or knocked down. GFI1 overexpression resulted in increased levels of STAT1 (Fig. 3A and B), whereas its expression decreased in GFI1-knockdown cells (Fig. 3C and D). Moreover, the level of MHC-I expression was significantly lower in GFI1-overexpressing A549 and H2030 cells transfected with siRNA targeting STAT1 than in those transfected with negative control siRNA (Fig. 3E and F). These results demonstrate that GFI1 regulates MHC-I expression in a STAT1-dependent manner.

GFI1 decreases HDAC1 activity in tumor cells by affecting its nuclear translocation

Multiple studies have shown that histone modifications regulate IFN-γ-induced STAT1 expression [21–23]. GFI1 has been reported to impact the methylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4) by interacting with LSD1 and affecting the acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9) through its association with HDAC1 [12, 24, 25]. Therefore, we investigated whether GFI1 controls STAT1 expression via histone modifications. First, we examined the interaction of GFI1 with LSD1 and HDAC1 in lung tumor cells. As shown in Fig. 4A, consistent with previous findings in hematologic malignancy cells [26], GFI1 binds to LSD1 and HDAC1. Next, we assessed whether GFI1 levels influence histone H3 modification in tumor cells. Compared with control cells, GFI1-overexpressing or GFI1-knockdown tumor cells presented no change in H3K4 dimethylation (H3K4me2) (Fig. 4B), but H3K9 acetylation (H3K9ac) was significantly greater with GFI1 overexpression and lower with GFI1 knockdown (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, we directly assessed HDAC activity in GFI1-overexpressing tumor cells. As shown in Fig. 4D, HDAC activity was significantly lower in GFI1-overexpressing tumor cells than in control cells. These results indicate that GFI1 regulates HDAC1 activity in lung tumor cells.

Recent studies have shown that, in addition to its role in nucleoli, HDAC1 can also be found in the cytoplasm under some circumstances [27, 28]. Additionally, the nuclear export of HDAC1 reportedly affects its activity [29, 30]. Therefore, we examined the subcellular location of HDAC1 in A549 and H2030 cells overexpressing GFI1. As shown in Fig. 4E, the percentage of cytosolic HDAC1 was significantly increased in GFI1-overexpressing cells, suggesting that GFI1 overexpression might promote the export of HDAC1 from the nucleus. To test this hypothesis, we treated GFI1-overexpressing tumor cells with leptomycin B (LMB), a nuclear export inhibitor that blocks HDAC1 from leaving the nucleus [29]. As shown in Fig. 4F, G, and H, LMB prevented HDAC1 translocation to the cytoplasm, resulting in decreased H3K9 acetylation and lower STAT1 and MHC-I expression than in untreated GFI1-overexpressing cells.

Furthermore, we investigated whether GFI1-driven upregulation of H3K9 acetylation directly influences the transcription of STAT1 and HLA-B. We conducted a ChIP assay at the STAT1 and HLA-B promoters to compare H3K9 acetylation levels in control cells and in cells overexpressing GFI1. GFI1-overexpressing cells presented a significant increase in H3K9ac at the STAT1 and HLA-B promoters in tumor cells (Fig. 4I). These findings indicate that GFI1 facilitates access to the STAT1 and HLA-B promoters by increasing H3K9 acetylation.

Activation of AMPK increases GFI1 expression by downregulating EZH2 levels

Given that GFI1 plays a crucial role in antitumor immunity, we investigated methods to increase its expression in tumor cells. The inhibition of EZH2 has been shown to increase GFI1 expression in medulloblastoma cells and human foreskin fibroblasts [17, 31]. Interestingly, A549 and H2030 cells treated with the EZH2 inhibitor GSK126 displayed reduced H3K27 trimethylation and increased GFI1 and MHC-I expression (Fig. 5A). Additionally, GSK126 enhanced GFI1 transcription (Fig. 5B). To confirm the regulatory effect of EZH2, we transfected A549 and H2030 cells with EZH2-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA). As shown in Figs. 5C and D. Knockdown of EZH2 in tumor cells upregulated GFI1 and MHC-I expression. Moreover, we tested whether H3K27 trimethylation represses GFI1 transcription in lung tumor cells. Compared with untreated cells, A549 and H2030 cells treated with GSK126 presented decreased H3K27me3 binding at the GFI1 promoter (Fig. 5E).

AMPK activation suppresses EZH2 expression and activity [32]. As shown in Fig. 5F and G, the addition of the AMPK activator phenformin to the cell culture significantly decreased EZH2 expression, whereas GFI1 expression was increased compared with that in the untreated cells. Moreover, phenformin increased the transcription of MHC-I genes (Fig. 5H).

To verify the role of GFI1 in the phenformin-induced upregulation of MHC-I, we treated GFI1-knockdown tumor cells with phenformin. As shown in Fig. 5I, GFI1 deficiency prevented the phenformin-induced increase in MHC-I expression. These data suggest that activating AMPK enhances MHC-I expression by inducing GFI1 expression.

Phenformin enhances anti-PD-L1 therapy and increases GFI1 levels in tumors

LLC tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice were subsequently used to investigate whether phenformin enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. Consistent with the results observed in A549 and H2030 cells, phenformin increased the expression of GFI1 and MHC-I in LLC cells (Fig. 6A and B). Subcutaneous LLC tumors treated with phenformin exhibited elevated levels of GFI1 and MHC-I (Fig. 6C and D). Moreover, tumor progression was notably delayed in mice receiving combined treatment with phenformin and an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) compared with those receiving anti-PD-L1 monotherapy (Fig. 6E and F). Additionally, compared with anti-PD-L1 antibody alone, combination therapy increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration and the proportions of perforin+CD8+ and granzyme B+CD8+ T cells without affecting cell proliferation (Fig. 6G and H).

To validate the role of GFI1 in the phenformin-induced antitumor effect, we constructed a GFI1-knockout LLC mouse cell line (Fig. 6I). As shown in Fig. 6J, GFI1 depletion significantly reduced the tumor growth inhibition achieved by the combination of phenformin and PD-L1 blockade, highlighting the essential role of phenformin-induced GFI1 expression in mediating the immunomodulatory antitumor effects of phenformin.

Discussion

Loss of MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation is a key mechanism through which tumors evade immune checkpoint inhibitors [33, 34]. Variations inherited through germline mutations, somatic mutations in MHC-I regions, MHC-I silencing at the transcriptional and translational levels, and posttranslational modifications of MHC-I molecules all contribute to resistance to ICIs [35–37]. Nonetheless, the intrinsic tumor mechanism controlling MHC-I expression in NSCLC remains incompletely understood. We showed that GFI1 in tumor cells enhances MHC-I expression and facilitates the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, thereby improving the response to immunotherapy. Mechanistically, GFI1 promotes the nuclear export of HDAC1, inhibits its activity, increases histone acetylation, and enhances the transcription of the STAT1 and HLA-B genes.

GFI1 is a six-zinc-finger protein that contains a SNAG domain. Its primary role is to act as a transcriptional repressor by interacting with cofactors such as LSD1, G9A, Ajuba, and HDAC1 [12, 24, 38]. Notably, GFI1 can also function as an active regulator of gene transcription. In lung tumor cells, GFI1 regulates RASGRP2 expression, activates the ERK pathway, and promote cancer metastasis [39]. However, the mechanism by which GFI1 promotes RASGRP2 expression is unclear. Our results showed that GFI1 induces the nuclear export of HDAC1 and inhibits its activity, thereby increasing STAT1 and HLA-B transcription. These findings provide new insight into how GFI1 can upregulate rather than suppress gene expression. However, further research is needed to understand how GFI1 induces HDAC1 export and inhibits its activity.

Targeting HDAC1 has shown promising potential in combination with immunotherapy for the treatment of lung cancer [40]. In addition to upregulating MHC-I, HDAC1 inhibitors have been reported to enhance antitumor immunity by increasing the expression of costimulatory and adhesion molecules, such as CD80, CD86, and intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [41, 42]. Moreover, HDAC1 inhibition can suppress FGL1 expression, a recently identified primary ligand of LAG-3, thereby further potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapy in lung cancer [43]. Our data suggest that GFI1 overexpression in lung tumor cells recapitulates the immunomodulatory effects of HDAC1 inhibition, including the upregulation of MHC-I expression and an improved response to immunotherapy. Whether additional immunoregulatory effects mediated by HDAC1 inhibition contribute to the antitumor immune responses induced by GFI1 overexpression remains to be further investigated.

GFI1 is regulated by promoter hypermethylation [16, 17]. Our data showed that inhibiting EZH2 expression significantly increased GFI1 expression in tumor cells. More importantly, although several studies have demonstrated that AMPK activation enhances MHC-I expression, the underlying mechanism is unclear [44]. Here, we showed that AMPK activation increases GFI1 expression in lung cancer cells by downregulating EZH2 expression, thereby increasing MHC-I expression. We revealed, for the first time, that AMPK-driven MHC-I expression depends on increased GFI1 levels in tumor cells.
