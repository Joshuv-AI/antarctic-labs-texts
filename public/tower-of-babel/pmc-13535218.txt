Genomic epidemiology of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli across humans, poultry and wastewater sectors in Douala, Cameroon
Authors: Arsène Djoko Nono, Benjamin Thumamo Pokam, Anna Njunda, Solène Cottis, Thomas Bovagnet, Chiara Crestani, Pierrette Landrie Simo Tchuinte, Ariane Nzouankeu, Tania Crucitti, Bich-Tram Huynh, Sylvain Brisse, Sebastien Breurec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global health threat of antimicrobial resistance involves the human, animal and environmental sectors. Data from Cameroon are scarce.ObjectivesThis study aimed to define extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-Ec) rates and associated risk factors across the three sectors in Douala, Cameroon, and to define molecular characteristics of isolates.MethodsFrom June 2022 to May 2023, we collected blood cultures from hospitalized patients, rectal swabs from healthy pregnant women, caeca from broiler chickens and environmental wastewater. Samples were screened for ESBL-Ec using CHROMAgar™ ESBL and cefotaxime-supplemented Tryptone Bile X-glucuronide agar. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by disk diffusion following EUCAST guidelines. Whole-genome sequencing was carried out using Illumina technology.ResultsOf 628 samples, 374 yielded ESBL-Ec. Prevalence was 54.6% (131/240) in pregnant women, 70.4% (169/240) in chickens and 93.1% (67/72) in wastewater. The proportion of ESBL-Ec among E. coli-positive-blood cultures was 9.2% (7/76). Multi-family household living was independently associated with ESBL-Ec carriage among pregnant women (adjusted odds ratio = 1.7, 95% CI 1.0–3.1, P = 0.03). High co-resistance (>70%) was observed for tetracycline, ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Sequencing of 32 isolates revealed 45 distinct resistance genes, including blaCTX-M-15 (n = 13, 40.6%), blaCTX-M-55 (n = 11, 34.4%) and last-resort antibiotic resistance genes mcr-1 and blaOXA-181. High-risk sequence types included ST131 (pregnant women) and ST10 (chickens). Notably, ST48 was shared between pregnant women and chickens, and ST155 between pregnant women and wastewater.ConclusionCross-sectoral ESBL-Ec in Douala exhibits high genomic diversity and alarming resistance. The occurrence of last-resort genes requires immediate One Health surveillance and coordinated interventions.

Introduction

The widespread and often inappropriate use of antimicrobials has significantly contributed to the global rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).1,2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), this problem is compounded by poor hygiene and infection control practices in healthcare settings, self-medication driven by over-the-counter access to antibiotics, limited access to safe drinking water and inadequate wastewater management.3–5 Extensive antibiotic use in livestock and agriculture for disease prevention and growth promotion further contributes to this burden.6,7 These challenges are particularly acute in urban LMIC settings, where rapid population growth places additional pressures on water, sanitation and health infrastructure, thereby amplifying the conditions that favour the emergence and spread of AMR.8

In Cameroon, AMR is a major public health concern, with nearly 25 000 deaths attributable to AMR in 2021, making it the second leading cause of mortality after cardiovascular diseases.9 National surveillance data further show that E. coli accounted for more than half of reported bacterial infections (52.3%).10 Of particular concern are extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing E. coli (ESBL-Ec), which have emerged as a key sentinel organism for monitoring AMR across the human-animal-environment interface because they are widespread, readily colonize healthy hosts and frequently carry mobile resistance genes.11

However, data on ESBL-Ec in Cameroon remain limited. Most studies have focused on clinical or animal sectors, while carriage among healthy populations and environmental compartments remains poorly characterized.10 Additionally, many investigations rely on phenotypic approaches, which cannot identify the underlying resistance mechanisms involved (resistance genes and chromosomal mutations), genomic context, or strain relatedness.12,13 Consequently, the genomic landscape of ESBL-Ec across hospital, community, poultry and wastewater sectors in Cameroon, as in other African LMICs, remains poorly defined.14 Understanding these patterns, alongside exposure-related behaviours in community groups such as pregnant women and environmental drivers such as rainfall, humidity and temperature, is essential to interpret ESBL-Ec dissemination within a One Health framework.

In the present study, we aimed to fill existing surveillance gaps by conducting a multi-sectoral investigation of ESBL-Ec and associated risk factors in the city of Douala, Cameroon, following the standardized WHO Tricycle protocol,15 combined with whole-genome sequencing for comprehensive molecular characterization.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Cameroon National Ethics Committee for Research in Human Health (2022/04/1455/CE/CNERSH/SP). Written informed consent was obtained from included individuals.

Study design, sample and data collection

This cross-sectional, surveillance study was conducted in Douala, Cameroon, from June 2022 to May 2023. Douala is the largest urban centre in Cameroon, with more than 4 million inhabitants.16 Three sectors were included: human (community and hospital), animal and environment, following the WHO Tricycle protocol for integrated surveillance of ESBL-Ec.17

In the community sector, rectal swabs were collected monthly from the first 20 pregnant women attending their final antenatal visit at the Centre de Santé et Maternité Sainte Léonie de Logbaba. Women presenting with acute and chronic illness or obstetrical and medical emergencies were excluded. Swabs were placed in Cary-Blair medium (Oxoid Ltd, Basingstoke, UK). A standardized questionnaire was administered to collect demographics, behavioural, hygienic and relevant clinical history data. For the risk factor analysis, women with incomplete questionnaire data (n = 12) were excluded, resulting in 228 participants with complete information. In the hospital sector, blood cultures were performed as part of the routine diagnosis workflow in each participating hospital, according to local laboratory practices and independently of the study team. A total of 2234 blood cultures (Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online) from inpatients with suspected bloodstream infections were processed across the three referral hospitals in Douala: the Gynaeco-Obstetric and Pediatric Hospital (308 beds), Laquintinie Hospital (917 beds) and the General Hospital (630 beds) (Figure 1). Only E. coli-positive-blood cultures were included, and the corresponding isolates were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Sampling points for human, animal and environment sectors in Douala, Cameroon.

In the animal sector, 10 broiler chickens were purchased every 15 days from vendors at two major markets (Brazzaville and New-Bell) on an alternative basis. At each biweekly to a single market, chickens were slaughtered on-site, and caecal contents were collected aseptically.

In the environmental sector, raw wastewater sampling was performed at the beginning of each month at six sites: four urban wastewater sites (‘Cité des Palmiers’, ‘Nettoycam’, ‘Cité Sic-Cacao’ and ‘Brazzaville) and two poultry-market sites ‘Brazzaville’ and ‘New-Bell’ (Figure 1). Sampling followed ISO 19458:2006 guidelines.18 Environmental variables, including ambient temperature, rainfall and relative humidity, were obtained from an online weather database.19

All samples were transported at 2°C–8°C and processed within 24 h at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun.

Isolation, identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Rectal swabs and chicken caeca were plated onto CHROMAgar™ ESBL (CHROMAgar, Paris, France) and incubated for 18–24 h at 36 °C± 2°C to isolate presumptive ESBL-producing colonies. Wastewater samples were homogenized and serially diluted (10−2–10−7). For each dilution, two distinct 100 mL aliquot were filtered through separate 0.45 µm membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were plated on Tryptone Bile X-glucuronide (TBX) agar (Biokar, France) without antibiotic to enumerate total E. coli, and on TBX supplemented with cefotaxime (4 mg/L) to enumerate presumptive ESBL-Ec.20 For wastewater, multiple presumptive colonies were picked per plate. Species confirmation for all presumptive colonies was performed using the API 20E system (bioMérieux, France), and ESBL production was confirmed by the double disk synergy test.21

Susceptibility to 17 antibiotics (amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, ticarcillin, aztreonam, cefoxitin, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime, ertapenem, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, amikacin, tobramycin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin and tetracycline) (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) (Table S1) was tested by disk diffusion on Mueller–Hinton agar (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Results were interpreted according to EUCAST 2022 guidelines,22 for all antibiotics except tetracycline, for which CLSI M100, 32nd edition (2022),23 breakpoints were used. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used for quality control.

Sequencing and genomic analysis

Selection of isolates, total DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

A subset of 32 ESBL-Ec isolates was purposively selected rather than randomly sampled to maximize broad sectoral, temporal and spatial diversity. The sequencing set encompassed isolates collected over 9 months of the 12-month sampling period, covering all sectors: 10 isolates from the community site, 5 from each animal market, 4 from two referral hospitals (the third hospital yielded no ESBL-Ec), and 8 from five wastewater sites. Phenotypic resistance profiles were not used as a selection criterion. While this targeted approach does not provide a strict statistical representation of the overall collection, it was designed to capture the broadest possible diversity of circulating lineages and resistance mechanisms across the available reservoirs.

DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). DNA samples meeting a minimum concentration of 20 ng/µL and approximate 260/280 purity ratio of 1.8 were used for library preparation with the Celero DNA-Seq PCR free kit (NuGen Technologies, Redwood, CA, USA) prior to sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Genomic DNA sequences analysis

Reads were quality-checked with FastQC v0.12.124 and trimmed with Trimmomatic v.039.25 Genomes were assembled with SPAdes v3.15.026 and analysed for species confirmation and serotype prediction using ECTyper v1.0.0.27 In silico multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using MLST v2.22.028 with the ecoli_achtman_4 scheme, resistance genes were identified using Abricate v1.0.0 against the ResFinder29 database, and plasmid replicon typing using PlasmidFinder v2.1.6. Phylogroups were assigned with ClermonTyping v20.03,30 genome annotation was done with Prokka v1.14.5,31 pan-genome analysis with Roary version 3.12.032 and phylogenetic trees were built with FastTree v2.1,33 and visualized in iTOL version 7.0.34

Statistical analysis

ESBL-Ec prevalence was calculated as the proportion of positive samples among all samples within each sector, except for blood cultures, where the result was reported as the proportion of ESBL producers among E. coli positive-blood cultures. For wastewater, positivity was defined by the presence of at least one ESBL-Ec isolate. Concentrations of E. coli and ESBL-Ec as well as the relative abundance of ESBL-Ec (ESBL-Ec/total E. coli × 100) were calculated and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Risk factors for ESBL-Ec carriage in pregnant women were identified using univariate logistic regression. The relative humidity variable was converted into a binary categorical factor using the sample median as structural breakpoint (moderate-to-high humidity ≤94% vs. ultra-high saturated humidity >94%) to avoid variance inflation and mathematical instability in the regression model. Variables with P < 0.2 were entered into multivariate logistic regression, and backward stepwise selection retained variables with P < 0.05. Correlations between weather parameters (temperature, rainfall and humidity) and ESBL-Ec relative abundance were assessed with Spearman correlation. Associations between plasmids and resistance genes were tested using the χ2 test when the assumptions were met and Fisher’s exact test otherwise. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate corrections were applied. Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Prevalence of ESBL-Ec in human, animal and environmental sectors, and quantitative distribution of E. coli, ESBL-Ec and ESBL-Ec relative abundance in wastewater

The prevalence of ESBL-Ec was 54.6% (131/240) [95% CI 50–60] among pregnant women and 70.4% (169/240) [95% CI 60–80] among chickens. Among 2234 blood culture samples, 76 (3.4%) cases of E. coli isolates were detected, of which 7 (9.2%) were ESBL producers (Figure S1).

In the environmental sector, ESBL-Ec was detected in 93.1% (67/72) [95% CI 80–100] of wastewater samples. ESBL-Ec concentrations ranged from 104 colony-forming unit (cfu)/100 mL at ‘Cité des palmiers’ to 5 × 105 cfu/100 mL at ‘New-Bell market’ (Table S2). The relative abundance of ESBL-Ec was lowest (4.5%) at ‘Cité des palmiers’ and highest (7.7%) at ‘Nettoycam’ (Table S3). Market sites showed significantly higher contamination compared with urban sites (P = 0.04; Figure S2).

Factors associated with ESBL-Ec carriage

Multivariate logistic regression revealed that pregnant women living in multi-family households had a 1.7-fold increase in odds of ESBL-Ec carriage (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.7, 95% confidence interval (CI) [1.0–3.1], P = 0.03). Ambient temperature was associated with ESBL-Ec carriage among pregnant women in univariate analysis, with 20% increase in odds for each 1°C increase in temperature (OR = 1.2, 95% CI [1.0–1.4], P = 0.02; Table 1), but the association was attenuated in multivariate analysis and did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.08). In wastewater samples, relative humidity was negatively correlated with ESBL-Ec relative abundance (Spearman = −0.3; P = 0.02; Figure S5), whereas temperature and rainfall showed no statistically significant effect (Figures S3, S4).

Antimicrobial resistance profiles of ESBL-Ec isolates across pregnant women, blood cultures, chickens and wastewater

AST was performed on all ESBL-Ec isolates recovered from the various sectors. Beta-lactam resistance was uniformly high, with cefotaxime resistance observed in almost all isolates. Resistance to ceftazidime exceeded 60%. However, resistance to amoxicillin/clavulanate and cefoxitin varied across sectors. Among blood culture isolates, resistance was 57.1% and 42.9%, respectively; whereas, isolates from other sectors were below 36% and 10% resistance, respectively. Aminoglycoside resistance was generally low: resistance to gentamicin and tobramycin was below 40% among isolates from pregnant women, chickens and wastewater, while amikacin resistance remained below 32% across all sectors. Fluoroquinolone resistance was particularly high, with ciprofloxacin resistance exceeding 80% in all sectors. Resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole exceeded 70%, and tetracycline resistance was also more than 70% in all sectors except wastewater isolates (57.6%) (Table 2).

Genomic characterization of ESBL-Ec

Genomic profiles of antimicrobial resistance and sector-specific distribution

Among the 32 sequenced ESBL-Ec isolates (assembly metrics are provided in Table S4) from all three sectors, 45 distinct resistance genes were identified, including 13 (28.9%) associated with beta-lactam resistance (Figure S6). The blaCTX-M-type genes were ubiquitous among all isolates. The blaCTX-M-15 was the most common (40.6%, n = 13/32), present in 100% (n = 4/4) of blood culture isolates, 62.5% (n = 5/8,) of wastewater isolates, 40% (n = 4/10) of pregnant women isolates and was absent in chicken isolates (n = 0/10). The blaCTX-M-55 was the second most frequent (34.4%, n = 11/32), mainly detected in isolates from pregnant women and chickens (n = 5/10, 50% each).

Beyond beta-lactams, the isolates also carried quinolone resistance determinants (96.9%, including gyrA/parC mutations in 34.4% of isolates), resistance genes to trimethoprim (87.5%), tetracycline (84.4%), aminoglycosides and sulphonamides (75% each). Resistance to last-resort antibiotics was also detected in isolates from different sectors: fosA3 (fosfomycin), mcr-1 (colistin) and blaOXA-181 (carbapenem) (Table S5).

Plasmid-mediated resistance

Plasmids were detected in 96.9% (31/32) of isolates, with 11 groups and 39 distinct replicon types identified. Chicken isolates showed the highest relative plasmid replicon richness, with 21 of the 39 distinct replicons identified in the study (53.8%), followed by wastewater isolates (n = 20/39, 51.3%).The predominant plasmid group was IncF (71.9%, n = 23/32), being detected in all blood culture isolates (4/4), as well as in 87.5% (7/8) of wastewater isolates, 60% (6/10) of isolates from pregnant women, and 60% (6/10) of chicken isolates. Additional groups included IncI (IncI1 and IncI2) and IncX (with IncX1 most common) (Table S6).

Most beta-lactam resistance genes (76.5%) were located on the same contigs as plasmid replicon loci, including all blaTEM-1-b genes and 81% of blaCTX-M-55. Statistical analysis showed that blaCTX-M-15 gene was associated with IncFIB and P0111 plasmids (P = 0.02 and P = 0.01, respectively). blaCTX-M-55 gene was associated with P0111 (P = 0.02), and showed an association with IncX1 (P = 0.01). The IncF group was also associated with non-beta-lactam resistance genes such as tetA and tetB (P = 0.008 and P = 0.02, respectively), and qnrB19 (P = 0.02) (Figure 2; Table S7). blaOXA-181 was located on the same contig as plasmid replicon locus ColKP3 in an E. coli blood culture isolate.

Phylogroups and sequence types

Five E. coli phylogroups (A, B1, B2, D and F) were identified among the 32 ESBL-Ec strains. Phylogroup A was predominant (46.9%, n = 15/32) and was mainly found in chickens (90%, n = 9/10), whereas phylogroup B1 (28.1%, n = 9/32) was frequently detected in wastewater isolates (62.5%, n = 5/8). All five phylogroups were detected among isolates from pregnant women (Figure 3).

The 32 isolates were classified into 28 sequence types (ST), including four novel STs. Well known high-risk STs included ST131, detected in an isolate from pregnant women, and ST10, detected in two isolates from chickens. A few STs were shared across sectors, including ST48 (n = 2; pregnant women and chicken), ST354 (n = 3; blood cultures and wastewater) and ST155 (n = 2; pregnant women and wastewater).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is among the first genomic One Health AMR studies in Central Africa to concurrently sample human, animal and environmental sectors, and the first such study from Cameroon focused on ESBL-Ec across these three sectors within a single integrated surveillance design.

ESBL-Ec prevalence varied markedly across sectors. Blood culture samples yielded few E. coli isolates, and only a minority of these were ESBL producers. Although the total number of positive-blood culture samples from the included sites was not available in our study, a recent 13-year review of data from Cameroon35 reported bacterial growth in 25.2% of blood cultures, with only 5.8% yielding E. coli. This proportion of E. coli among blood cultures is consistent with our observation (3.4%). This low recovery likely reflects blood culture performance factors common in routine care, including pre-analytical limitations and reduced sensitivity after antibiotic exposure.36,37

Wastewater samples revealed a strikingly high contamination rate, consistent with observations from the Tricycle project reporting similar ESBL prevalence in Madagascar (100%)38 and Ghana (98%).39 This widespread environmental contamination is driven by a complex interplay of structural, infrastructural and anthropogenic factors. Wastewater effluents from both human and animal sources are frequently discharged untreated into urban water networks in low-resource settings due to limited centralized sanitation infrastructure.39,40 In African urban settings, open drains and street gutters are highly vulnerable to runoff, while poor sanitation coverage promotes leakage and overflow of wastewater and faecal sludge into drains and surface waters close to homes and public spaces.41 Consequently, these intersecting vulnerabilities likely facilitate the circulation of resistant bacteria through the urban matrix, creating plausible community exposure pathways and public health risks.42,43

The 54.6% carriage rate in pregnant women in Douala is particularly notably, surpassing figures reported in similar low- and middle-income settings, such as Ghana (42.0%), Madagascar (34%) and Benin (22.3%).38,44,45 These findings suggest that other localities of Cameroon may exhibit high levels of ESBL-Ec carriage, consistent with the broader trend of rising ESBL-Ec in Sub-Saharan Africa.46,47 Likewise, the 70.4% prevalence found in poultry exceeds documented rates from the Tricycle project in Ghana (50%),48 Madagascar (∼48%),49 and a poultry-based study in Zimbabwe (18.2%).50 This elevated avian reservoir rate indicates a substantial pool of resistance determinants that could enter the human food chain and the urban environment through multiple parallel pathways, including the processing of live birds in open-air-markets and inadequate occupational hygiene among poultry handlers, and the unregulated disposal of farm litter into communal storm water networks.51–53 These structural vulnerabilities may extend the risk of ESBL-Ec dissemination beyond households with direct contact with live animals.12

Among pregnant women, housing type was associated with carriage. Shared living spaces may create a favourable environment for the spread of bacteria, facilitated by limited access to sanitation facilities, waste management practices and reduced hygiene standards.54 In contrast to studies reporting higher carriage during the rainy seasons,38 our data did not show a clear association between rainfall and ESBL-Ec carriage among pregnant women. In a coastal tropical city like Douala, where seasonal contrasts are relatively modest and intermittent rainfall occurs even during the dry season,19 rainfall volume may be an imperfect proxy for environmental exposures, such as sanitation conditions, water quality and availability, drainage contamination and households flooding that influence colonization risk.55 Likewise, the relatively narrow variation of ambient temperature in Douala56 may explain its attenuated association after multivariate adjustment. However, the marginal significance obtained (P = 0.08) should not been neglected; it suggests that macro-environmental thermal stress may act as a force multiplier for carriage risk,57 particularly for vulnerable pregnant women living in overcrowded housing conditions.58 In wastewater, the negative correlation between ambient relative humidity and ESBL-Ec relative abundance may reflect a combination of dilution of contaminated runoff and changes in surface desiccation and bacterial persistence,59,60 although this remains an ecological association rather than evidence of a specific causal mechanism.

Co-resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin and tetracycline was common among the ESBL-Ec isolates, confirming that multidrug resistance is frequently embedded within the ESBL phenotypes. This is concerning in our setting because these antibiotics rank among the top five most commonly used in the human sector in Cameroon.61 Resistance to these antimicrobials, together with beta-lactams, substantially reduces therapeutic options and is associated with increased mortality in hospital-acquired ESBL infections,62 as well as higher morbidity in community settings.63 Isolates from chickens showed the highest tetracycline resistance, likely reflecting the antimicrobial pressure due to their use in poultry farming as growth promoters and for infection prevention in the country.64

Genomic analysis revealed the ubiquity of blaCTX-M genes, especially blaCTX-M-15 in humans and in wastewater, and blaCTX-M-55 in pregnant women and chickens, with these genes showing putative associations with IncFIB and IncX1 plasmid replicons, respectively. The predominance of blaCTX-M-15 in Cameroon, reported for the first time in Yaounde in 2005,65 may be facilitated by intense population mobility and cross-border exchanges with neighbouring countries where it is also prevalent.66,67 The carbapenem resistance gene blaOXA-181 was detected in an ESBL-Ec isolated from a blood culture sample, representing, to our knowledge, its first report in Cameroon. The gene was inferred to be associated with a ColKP3 plasmid based on contig-level analysis, as reported in previous studies.68 Given that only four hospital isolates were sequenced, this finding should be interpreted as a preliminary observation.

Phylogroup A dominated in chickens, while phylogroup B1 predominated in wastewater, as expected for this environmentally adapted lineage.69 Likewise, the high phylogenetic diversity observed among isolates from pregnant women, spanning both commensal and pathogenic lineages, suggests repeated exposure from multiple contamination pathways.70 Notably, the detection of ST131 in a pregnant woman is epidemiologically important because this lineage is a globally disseminated, high-risk clone with well-documented pathogenic potential and a frequent association with extraintestinal infections.71,72 Likewise, the presence of ST10 in poultry is consistent with the broad host range of this lineage.70

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to assess temporal dynamics of ESBL-Ec carriage and to infer causal relationships between environmental, climatic, or behavioural factors and colonization. Second, the selection of pregnant women may not fully represent the broader community population. Nonetheless, as indicated by WHO guidance, such data remain important for understanding what is circulating at the community level. Third, genomic analysis was limited to 32 isolates. Sequencing all isolates would have provided a better understanding of the epidemiology of resistance genes and high-risk clones, and would have strengthened our ability to infer cross-sectoral transmission. Finally, Illumina short-read sequencing does not enable to reliably determine whether resistance genes are located on the chromosome or on plasmids. This limits our ability to fully characterize the mechanisms of resistance dissemination.

Despite these limitations, this One Health study reveals a worrying convergence of ESBL-Ec across human, poultry and wastewater compartments in Douala, with frequent co-resistance to antibiotics commonly used in human. This pattern suggests that these urban human-animal-environment sectors together constitute a dynamic reservoir of resistance determinants, facilitating multiple pathways for onward dissemination. These findings underscore the need for integrated, targeted One Health genomic surveillance. Specifically interventions should prioritize poultry markets, sentinel wastewater sites and community carriage surveys to detect and monitor ESBL-producing strains, alongside hospital-based monitoring for emerging carbapenem-resistant lineages. Implementing such surveillance, together with strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and improved sanitation measures, will enable rapid detection of emerging resistance, clarify transmission routes and support evidence-based interventions to limit the spread of ESBL and carbapenem resistance in urban African settings.
