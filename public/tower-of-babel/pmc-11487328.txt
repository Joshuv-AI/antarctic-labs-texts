A Study on Veterinary Faculty Students’ Opinions on Ethics Education and Career Choices: A Case of Ondokuz Mayıs University
Authors: Sefa Yıldırım, Berfin Melikoğlu Gölcü
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVeterinary ethics is a multifaceted element of the veterinary profession as it deals with various ethical issues related to medical practice, animal welfare, professional behaviour and societal expectations. By focusing on teaching ethics in veterinary medicine education, it will be possible to guide future veterinarians through the ethical dilemmas and conflicts they may encounter in their careers.ObjectivesTo evaluate the opinions of the Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine intern students about ethics education in veterinary medicine. Evaluation and comparison of the perspective on ethics education and career choice.MethodsA cross‐sectional descriptive survey design was used to collect data via a questionnaire. Data obtained from 87 out of 119 students in the intern programme were evaluated. Responses were analysed by statistical methods.ResultsIn the study, it was determined that pet veterinary services (private sector) and government veterinary services (public sector) were the leading career choices of intern veterinary students; female and male students wanted to turn to different fields of work; and the difference in career preferences did not change the views on ethics education. It was observed that students needed applied teaching and measurement‐evaluation methods in ethics education and sought role models in developing ethical attitudes and behaviours. In addition to gaining ethical knowledge in veterinary education, it was observed that they attach importance to professional and legal obligations.ConclusionsThis study is one of the few studies on veterinary ethics education and career choice in Turkey. The study results provide information to guide the development of veterinary ethics education to prepare future veterinarians for the complex ethical issues they will face in their careers.Keywords: career choice, curriculum, education, ethics, ethics education, veterinary students

Introduction

Because veterinary medicine is a profession with various working areas and offers the opportunity to work in many different sectors, the subject of veterinary ethics is quite diverse. Veterinary ethics, which deals with traditional problems arising from the structure of the veterinary medical profession, has also included issues such as animal experiments, euthanasia, informed consent, transgenic animals, patient rights and cloning with the advancement of science and technology in recent years (Başağaç Gül 2011). The subject of ethics has been included among the sine qua non of veterinary education, which is in an effort to teach how to apply basic ethical principles to guide veterinarians in solving ethical problems and how to experience ethical decision‐making (Başağaç Gül 2015).

In Turkey, the faculty members of the ‘Department of Veterinary Medicine History and Deontology’ (DVMHD) give veterinary ethics education. The first DVMHD was established in 1950 at Ankara University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine. Since the 1990s, teaching professional ethics has gained momentum with the establishment of the DVMHD in the newly established veterinary faculties. DVMHDs, a department specific to Turkey, undertake the teaching of veterinary medicine history and deontology courses as well as professional ethics (Başağaç Gül 2015; Melikoğlu Gölcü et al. 2024). As of April 2024, veterinary ethics courses are taught in all 29 veterinary faculties in Turkey. In 16 of these faculties, ethics courses are taught by the DVMHD faculty members. The ethics course in the undergraduate curriculum of Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine is a compulsory course titled ‘Veterinary Medicine Legislation and Professional Ethics’ in the autumn term of the 5th year.

This study aimed to determine and compare the evaluations of the intern students of Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Spring Semester of 2021–2022 Academic Year, regarding ethics education and career choice in veterinary medicine and to contribute to comprehensive studies on the subject.

Materials and Methods

A cross‐sectional descriptive survey method was applied in the study. The students participating in the study were surveyed about their approaches to ethics education in veterinary medicine. The questionnaire form was prepared as a result of literature research. The survey questions were tested on a pilot population and finalised. The questionnaire forms were administered to the participants after they obtained their consent, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

The study population consisted of 119 intern students enrolled in the Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine in the Spring Semester of the 2021–2022 Academic Year. Participants attended a 14‐h theoretical course on professional ethics for one semester. At the time of this study, all participants had completed this course.

The sample size was determined by a simple random sampling method. The study was conducted using questionnaire data from 87 students. It was possible to consider career choice based on data from 84 respondents. Permissions were obtained from the Dean's Office of the relevant faculty and ethics committee for the study. Descriptive statistics related to the data obtained from the study were expressed as frequency and percentage. Then, One Sample Chi‐Square Test was used to determine the significance of the distribution of variables. In addition, Chi‐Square analysis was used to examine whether there is a relationship between the answers given to the survey questions and demographic characteristics and career choices. In Chi‐Square analysis, a Pearson Chi‐Square or a Fisher–Freeman–Halton Exact Test was used according to the distribution of observed and expected values in the cross‐tabulation. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software. The confidence interval is set at 95%. The margin of error is 5.47%. Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic Data and Career Choices of Participants

Of the 87 students who participated in the study, 43.7% (n = 38) were female, and 56.3% (n = 49) were male. After graduation, the most preferred fields of work were pet veterinary services in the private sector (33.3%, n = 28) and government veterinary services in the public sector (33.3%, n = 28). These were followed by food safety services in the private sector and academic careers. None of the participants chose bovine veterinary services in the private sector. Data on Perceptions of Ethical Sufficiency

Although 25.3% (n = 22) of the students considered their ethical knowledge of veterinary medicine to be sufficient, 66.7% (n = 58) found their ethical knowledge to be partially sufficient, and 8% (n = 7) deemed their ethical knowledge insufficient (p < 0.001, One Sample Chi‐Square Test).

Despite 29.9% (n = 26) of the students found their ability to recognise and solve the ethical problems they would encounter while performing their profession sufficient, 63.2% (n = 55) found it partially sufficient, and 6.9% (n = 6) found it insufficient (p < 0.001, One Sample Chi‐Square Test).

Students indicated that the attitudes and behaviours of their lecturers (23.8%, n = 62), case discussions (23%, n = 60) and ethics courses (22.2%, n = 58) were the most influential factors in the development of their attitudes and behaviours towards veterinary ethics. Outside of their veterinary education, ethical attitudes and behaviours were reported to have developed through personal efforts (12.6%, n = 33) and family and societal influences (12.3%, n = 32). Opinions on Veterinary Medicine Ethics Education and Its Relationship With Career Choices

When the content of veterinary ethics education was evaluated, 62.1% (n = 54) of the participants stated that the content of the ethics courses was sufficient, whereas 33.3% (n = 29) considered it partially sufficient. Only 4.6% (n = 4) of the students found the content of the ethics education insufficient (p < 0.001, One Sample Chi‐Square Test).

However, there was no significant difference between the views on the sufficiency of the duration of the ethics courses. Although 37.9% (n = 33) of the students indicated that the duration of ethics education was partially sufficient, 34.5% (n = 30) stated it was sufficient, and 26.4% (n = 23) found it insufficient (p = 0.34, One Sample Chi‐Square Test).

When the data on the adequacy of the content of ethics courses were compared with career choices, no statistical significance was found between the two parameters (p = 0.12, Table 3).

In veterinary faculties in Turkey, the ethics course is usually given as a one‐semester course in the 4th or 5th year. Overall, 58.6% (n = 51) of the students stated that ethics education should be given in the 3rd, 4th and 5th grades and 21.8% (n = 19) in all grades. Discussion

The significant relationship between gender and career choices of the students participating in the study (p = 0.004, Table 1) shows the importance of gender dynamics in career choice processes in veterinary medicine. The study data showed that female veterinary students preferred the pet veterinary services the most and food safety services the least. This preference can be associated with the value judgements of female students regarding animals. Verrinder and Phillips (2014) reported that in some studies, female veterinary students were more concerned about animal welfare than male veterinary students. Serpell (2005) stated that female students were more supportive of the primary duty of a veterinarian to serve the interests of the animal more than the animal owner. Similarly, the lower number of female students who want to work in food safety services compared to male students can be explained by the fact that female students attach more importance to the health of companion animals. When viewed from this aspect, it is thought that the reasons, such as the fact that veterinarians working in food safety services spend most of their working hours in slaughterhouses, the high number of male employees in these workplaces and approaches to animal welfare, are reflected in the professional preferences of female veterinary candidates.

It was observed that pet veterinary services and government veterinary services were the main areas where students wanted to work after graduation (Table 1). It can be said that economic conditions and the number of pets shape this preference. Although 3989 veterinary clinics, 231 polyclinics and 13 animal hospitals were licensed in the private sector in Turkey in 2007 (Aral et al. 2010), these numbers reached 8958 clinics, 125 polyclinics and 108 animal hospitals in 2024 (Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry 2024). In studies (Aral et al. 2010; Yerlikaya 2023), the rapid increase in the number of private sector pet veterinary clinics in Turkey was associated with the increasing service demand of dog and cat owners, the desire of newly graduated veterinarians to earn higher income in a shorter time by opening their own workplace due to the low employment of veterinarians in the market and the desire to work freely. The high demand for government veterinary services in public sector can be attributed to the difficulties encountered in the establishment phase of clinics, especially financing, site selection and staffing (Kaygısız and Akdağ 2004). It is noteworthy that none of the intern students participating in the study thought of providing bovine veterinary services in private sector, which is a different finding from similar studies (Demir, Uğurlu, and Arslan 2016). It is thought that the lack of preference for bovine veterinary services may be related to issues such as difficulty of working life, social life and working time, as well as problems such as the fact that bovine husbandry in Turkey has been dependent on foreign countries in recent years due to reasons such as high costs, lack of organisation and insufficient development (Tapkı et al. 2018).

The fact that there was no statistically significant difference in the comparison of students’ career choices and their views on the sufficiency of the content of ethics courses (Table 3) was found to be positive in terms of the effectiveness of ethics education. However, the fact that the majority of the students defined their ethical knowledge and their ability to recognise and solve the ethical problems they will encounter as ‘partially adequate’ suggests that they feel a lack of self‐confidence in the ethical dilemmas and conflicts they may encounter in the future despite their current knowledge. It seems inevitable that the ethical problems existing in the structure of veterinary medicine will increase continuously with the contribution of science and technology, the changing social status of the animal, environmental problems, the emergence of new ethical conflicts and dilemmas in the field of health, changing value judgements with socioeconomic factors and so on (Başağaç Gül 2015), which will increase the need for awareness of professional ethics. Main, Thornton, and Kerr (2005) stated that ethics education should be one of the basic components of the veterinary medicine curriculum; Wensley (2008) reported that veterinary students do not reach the highest levels of ethical reasoning and moral development and emphasised the importance of adequate integration of ethics and ethical reasoning into veterinary medicine education in order to ensure that future veterinarians are equipped to address ethical dilemmas and defend animal welfare.

Although studies (Ahmady et al. 2022; Lehmann et al. 2004; Maudsley 2001) mention the effect of instructors as role models on students in medical education, it has been reported that there are not enough studies on seeing lecturers as ethical role models in the field of veterinary medicine and the awareness of being a role model has not yet become widespread (Melikoğlu Gölcü et al. 2024). In this study, it was determined that the most important factor enabling the development of ethical attitudes and behaviours of students in veterinary medicine practices was the attitudes and behaviours of the lecturers, which showed that the need for role models for students was prominent (Table 2). Similarly, the findings in Table 6 highlighted the students’ search for a role model in veterinary ethics teaching, showing that ethics education does not only consist of transferring theoretical knowledge but also that the personal characteristics of educators, such as social communication skills, are important for students. In the study, the fact that case discussions were one of the other factors that provide the development of ethical attitudes and behaviours is an indication of the effectiveness of applied ethics education. The importance of case discussions in ethics education has been discussed in studies (Eckles et al. 2005; Magalhães‐Sant'Ana and Hanlon 2016), which has been determined that applied methods can support veterinary and medical students’ ethical awareness and skills. It has been stated (Eckles et al. 2005; Freire et al. 2017) that instead of traditional theoretical courses based on knowledge transfer, it is much more effective to integrate ethics courses into the curriculum in that they are applied, carried out with discussion and interactive. Claudot et al. (2007) also pointed out that most medical students do not want to treat ethics as a black‐and‐white discipline and that they want an education where they can freely discuss ethical problems that arise instead of didactic education. In the ethics education of the faculty where the study was conducted, it can be said that the inclusion of case discussions in the applied ethics education in the 10th semester of the education improves the students’ empathy skills and increases their ethical awareness. This awareness was also reflected in the students’ opinions about the methods that should be used in veterinary ethics education; discussion on cases encountered during clinical practice, case presentation and small group discussion (Table 6) were the most emphasised teaching methods by the participants. Therefore, it is thought that disseminating applied methods in veterinary ethics education within the curriculum will enable students to be more equipped and conscious of ethical problems and dilemmas they will encounter in their professional lives.

The participants stated that the most important aims of ethics education were to introduce professional responsibilities and legal obligations (22.6%), to gain knowledge on basic veterinary ethics issues (informed consent, malpractice, euthanasia and so on) (20.7%) and to gain the ability to solve ethical problems (ethical decision‐making) (18.4%) (Table 4). Professional norms and laws, accepted as the normative component of ethics teaching, have been accepted as one of the main teaching objectives in various studies (Magalhães‐Sant'Ana et al. 2010; Main, Thornton, and Kerr 2005). Melikoğlu Gölcü et al. (2024) reported that ethics and legislation were taught in the same course in 25 of 29 active veterinary faculties in Turkey. In the curriculum of the veterinary faculty where the study was conducted, the same approach was adopted, and the subjects of veterinary ethics and legislation were taught in the same course. This situation enabled the veterinary candidates to realise the importance of their professional obligations in general and to consider moral as well as legal obligations. However, it can be said that the veterinarian has a responsibility to the animal owner in civil and financial terms, as well as criminal and disciplinary responsibility to the animal owner and society.

It is seen that students want to gain the ability to solve ethical problems rather than the ability to recognise ethical problems (Table 4). Kipperman, Rollin, and Martin (2021) pointed out that in different studies conducted among veterinarians, whereas their confidence in their ability to recognise ethical problems was high, these rates decreased at the point of solving ethical problems. In this context, Magalhães‐Sant'Ana et al. (2014) reported that thinking critically about situations in terms of ethical challenges is a skill that veterinary students are not used to doing that veterinary students, who are predominantly trained in science, have difficulty in this regard and therefore need to learn.

On the other hand, when the comparison between the career choices of the students and the aims expected from veterinary ethics education is analysed, it is seen that the subjects that are aimed and desired to be developed in terms of ethics are common (Table 5). However, it can be said that the slight differences in the prioritisation of these objectives are shaped according to the fields of study. As a matter of fact, students, who plan to work in food safety services and government veterinary services, are determined to recognise professional responsibilities and legal obligations as the most important aim of ethics education, whereas students who plan to work in pet veterinary services and academic careers aim to gain the ability to make ethical decisions in addition to this.

The majority of the intern students (58.6%) stated that ethics education should be given in the 3rd, 4th and 5th grades (Table 6). The reason for this preference is thought to be that, according to the curriculum of veterinary faculties in Turkey, students who start clinical practice courses in the 3rd grade start to communicate with patients and patient owners and encounter clinical ethical problems. In the faculty where the study was conducted, ethics and legislation education is given 14 h theoretically in the 9th semester and 10 h practically in the 10th semester, which is the intern programme. However, there are also studies (Eckles et al. 2005; Giubilini, Milnes, and Savulescu 2016; Kavas et al. 2020; Main, Thornton, and Kerr 2005; Melikoğlu Gölcü et al. 2024) advocating that ethics education should be given throughout the entire curriculum in both veterinary and medical faculties.

Melikoğlu Gölcü et al. (2024) reported that multiple‐choice exams were the most preferred measurement‐evaluation methods in ethics education by instructors in Turkey, followed by written exams, in‐class performance and homework. The results of this study (Table 6) show that, unlike the preferences of ethics educators, students want to be evaluated for their ability to apply this knowledge instead of gaining theoretical knowledge. Nevertheless, there is a need to conduct a study across Turkey to compare the preferences of educators and students for ethics education in veterinary faculties fully. It is thought that the measurement‐evaluation method preferred by the students based on practice shows a more factual and fair approach. However, case study discussions were accepted as a method that received positive feedback from students (Eckles et al. 2005) and provided students with multidimensional ethical decision‐making skills (Magalhães‐Sant'Ana and Hanlon 2016). In this respect, case study discussions can be a method that can increase students’ active participation and be used in measuring and evaluating their achievements.

When asked about the most frequently witnessed unethical behaviours during professional practice, 22.6% (n = 59) of the participants chose the option that veterinarians fail to maintain professional solidarity and deontology among themselves, and 16.9% (n = 44) chose the option that veterinarians measure everything with money and act in‐line with material interests (Table 7). This result can be explained by the unplanned development of veterinary medicine in Turkey. With a similar approach, Kızıltepe (2011) associated the reason for the inability of veterinarians in Turkey to maintain professional solidarity deontology with the economic concerns arising from the increasing number of graduated veterinarians and competition for material interests. On the other hand, Başağaç Gül (2011) draws attention to the incompatibilities between what is taught about professional ethics in veterinary faculties and ‘what is experienced in the market’, and examples where professional ethics and professional legislation are contradictory or divergent, which are among the obstacles to the credibility of professional ethics. From this point of view, it can be argued that the problems associated with the market economy are not only the factors that make ethics education in veterinary medicine compulsory but also put the perception of ethics in a deadlock. Overall, 20.7% (n = 54) of the participants stated that mistreatment of colleagues, less senior staff or students by faculty members was one of the most frequently witnessed ethical violations. Yerlikaya et al. (2023) drew attention to the difficulty of social relations in academic life rather than academic career difficulties as the reason why veterinarians did not prefer an academic career and reported that workplace mobbing, which is a critical problem for academics in universities, is a challenging factor not only for academics but also for last year students. With a similar idea, the fact that an academic career is not among the prioritised employment areas in this study may be associated with the negative impact of the ethical violation mentioned above on the idea of an academic career.

Conclusions

This study provides information about the perspectives on ethics education and career choices of intern students at Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine in the 2021–2022 academic year in Turkey. However, the fact that the research was conducted only within the scope of intern students of a veterinary faculty should be accepted as a limitation of our study. As a result of the study, it was seen that female and male students wanted to turn to different fields of work in their career choices. We assume that economic concerns have come to the fore in the career choices of veterinarians in Turkey in recent years due to the effect of current animal husbandry and socioeconomic variables. The study shows that veterinary students do not feel fully adequate regarding ethical dilemmas and conflicts they may encounter in the future, apart from their current knowledge. The need for application areas within the scope of ethics education by veterinary students is consistent with this finding. In addition, the fact that instructors are seen as role models and mentors in the study emphasises their critical role in shaping students’ ethical attitudes and behaviours. On the other hand, it was determined that students equate moral and legal obligations and attach importance to both.

Overall, the data of this study provide information that can guide the development of veterinary ethics education to better prepare future veterinarians for the complex ethical issues they will face in their careers. In addition, the study is thought to contribute to other studies on the subject. The authors suggest that the content and duration of the courses allocated to ethics education should be increased, case discussions should be increased, applied ethics courses should be added to the periods when clinical practice courses are intensive, and common unethical behaviours witnessed by students and veterinarians should be discussed in the courses. However, we think there is a need for comprehensive and holistic studies on ethics education and career choices in veterinary medicine.
