Parametrized equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category and frame-theoretic examples
Nizar El Idrissi

Abstract
We introduce an equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category that extends several classical notions of equivalence in mathematics. We show that the standard group-action equivalence is a special case of our framework. A more interesting example is provided by an equivalence relation defined on the class of Bessel families and based on a coarse invariant that is the pointwise norm of the analysis operator of the Bessel family – this is also a special case of our framework, and the formalism developed here can be seen as a first step toward a categorical invariant theory for Bessel families and continuous frames.

1
Introduction

Equivalence relations in mathematics are often defined via group actions.

In operator theory and harmonic analysis, a common way to define functional equivalence relations is to require that two functions are equivalent if their respective inputs and outputs are respectively interchangeable under a group action.

Unitary equivalence of frames
[
2
]
, which dates back to the work of Han-Larson
[
7
]
, and extends to a group larger than the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(H)
as in the work of Novikov-Sevostyanova
[
10
]
, is an equivalence relation of this sort.

This equivalence relation can be further generalized to invertible equivalence of frames, which is reminiscent of the definition of the notion of Riesz basis, a frame that is invertibly equivalent to an orthonormal basis.

While powerful, this entire paradigm is unnecessarily restrictive.

Indeed, beyond group actions, equivalence relations on morphisms naturally arise in categorical and homotopical contexts where morphisms are compared via factorizations, universal properties, or localization procedures. Classical examples include congruences on categories and localization with respect to classes of morphisms, as developed in Gabriel–Zisman’s calculus of fractions
[
5
]
. In these frameworks, morphisms are not compared through symmetry but through their behavior relative to a chosen structural constraint, suggesting that equivalence relations on morphisms can be parametrized by auxiliary categorical data rather than by automorphism groups.

From a higher-categorical viewpoint, morphisms are naturally organized into 2-categories or bicategories where comparison data between morphisms is encoded as 2-morphisms. This perspective, developed systematically in the work of Bénabou
[
1
]
and later formalized in bicategorical language
[
9
]
, provides a structural environment in which equivalence of morphisms is not necessarily induced by invertible transformations but may arise from more flexible coherence data. In this sense, parametrizing an equivalence relation by a functor into a 2-category fits naturally within higher-categorical thinking.

In operator theory and functional analysis, many classification problems relie on invariants that are strictly weaker than symmetry-based equivalence. For instance, spectral invariants, numerical ranges, or operator norms often serve as coarse classification tools when unitary equivalence is too rigid
[
4
]
. These examples illustrate that abandoning symmetry in favor of invariant-based comparison can reveal structural phenomena invisible under classical group-action equivalence notions.

Hence,
in our paper
, we approach the frame equivalence problem differently. First, we consider an equivalence relation defined not only on frames but on the whole class of Bessel families (called Bessel functions hereafter). Secondly, we discard any group theoretic compatibility between the Bessel functions, retaining only the pointwise norm of the analysis operator, viewed as a function on the Hilbert space. The pointwise norm of the analysis operator is a much coarser invariant that retains no phase information and is almost insensitive to unitary equivalence.

Before developing this approach, we introduce a very flexible notion of equivalence relation defined on the global class of morphisms of a category and parametrized by functions and functors into a 2-category, a notion that is reminiscent of Green’s J relation
[
6
,
3
]
in semigroup theory. We recall that in semigroup theory, Green’s relations provide a paradigm where elements are classified through principal ideals and divisibility relations
[
8
]
. The resulting equivalence relations depend on how elements factor through the ambient algebraic structure. This conceptual shift – from symmetry to structural comparison – motivates the search for equivalence notions on morphisms that depend on categorical parameters rather than on group actions.

Therefore, our article proposes a far-reaching symmetry concept encoded by the structure provided by a 2-category. We synthesize all the previously cited perspectives by introducing a parametrized equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category, defined through functions and functors into a 2-category. This construction isolates the comparison mechanism from group-theoretic symmetry and instead encodes it via a higher-categorical structure. As an application, we show that both group-action equivalence and coarse invariant-based equivalence in frame theory arise as particular instances of this general framework.

Plan of the article.
After this introduction, our paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we define an equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category. In section
3
, we show that group-action equivalence and the aforementioned equivalence based on the coarse invariant of Bessel functions are two special cases of our general definition. Section
4
contains the conclusion and perspectives of this paper.

2
A parametrized equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category

In this section, we propose an equivalence relation on the global class of morphisms of a category that unifies and extends several classical notions arising from group actions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category,
\mathcal{D}
be a 2-category.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be two functors, and let

\sigma:\begin{cases}\mathsf{Ob(\mathcal{C})}&\to\mathsf{Ob(\mathcal{D})}\\
\mathsf{Hom(c_{1},c_{2})}&\to\mathsf{Hom(\sigma(c_{1}),\sigma(c_{2}))}\end{cases}

be a function (not necessarily a functor) such that
\sigma,\tau_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}
coincide on objects of
\mathcal{C}
.

Further, let

m:A\to B

and

\tilde{m}:\tilde{A}\to\tilde{B}

be two morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
.

We say that
m
and
\tilde{m}
are equivalent (with respect to the data
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D},\sigma,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
), or

m\simeq\tilde{m}

if there exist 1-morphisms
u_{1}:B\to\tilde{B}
,
u_{2}:\tilde{A}\to A
,
v_{1}:\tilde{B}\to B
, and
v_{2}:A\to\tilde{A}
, and four 2-morphisms

\Phi:\tau_{1}(u_{1})\circ\sigma(m)\circ\tau_{2}(u_{2})\Rightarrow\sigma(\tilde{m}),

\tilde{\Phi}:\sigma(\tilde{m})\Rightarrow\tau_{1}(u_{1})\circ\sigma(m)\circ\tau_{2}(u_{2}),

\Psi:\tau_{1}(v_{1})\circ\sigma(\tilde{m})\circ\tau_{2}(v_{2})\Rightarrow\sigma(m),

and

\tilde{\Psi}:\sigma(m)\Rightarrow\tau_{1}(v_{1})\circ\sigma(\tilde{m})\circ\tau_{2}(v_{2}).

Remark 2.2
.

•

The relation
\simeq
is defined on the class of all morphisms in the category
\mathcal{C}
, with respect to the given data
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D},\sigma,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
.

Notice that the composites are well-defined since
\sigma
,
\tau_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}
coincide on objects, ensuring the domains and codomains align for the 1-morphisms in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

The definition of

does not require the
1
-morphisms
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},v_{2}
(in
\mathcal{C}
) to be equivalences nor the
2
-morphisms
\Phi,\tilde{\Phi},\Psi,\tilde{\Psi}
(in
\mathcal{D}
) to be isomorphisms. This is not a problem. Whenever one wishes to work in a setting where everything is invertible, it is always possible to replace
\mathcal{D}
by a
2
-groupoid (a
2
-category in which every
1
-morphism is an equivalence and every
2
-morphism is an isomorphism), for example the
2
-categorical localisation of
\mathcal{D}
obtained by formally inverting all
1
- and
2
-morphisms).

Proposition 2.3
.

\simeq
is an equivalence relation.

Proof.

To prove
\simeq
is an equivalence relation, we show that it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. All constructions use the operations in the 2-category
\mathcal{D}
: vertical composition
\circ_{v}
of 2-morphisms and whiskering (horizontal composition
\circ_{h}
) of a 2-morphism with 1-morphisms on the left and/or right.

For any morphism

, let’s prove that
m\simeq m
.

Set
\tilde{A}=A
,
\tilde{B}=B
,

.

Choose

Since

Similarly,

Set

,
\Psi

to be the identity 2-morphism

on the 1-morphism

in
\mathcal{D}
.
This satisfies the conditions.

3.1
Group-action equivalence and group-action equivalence of Bessel functions

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
E
.

Definition 3.1

(Group-action equivalence)
.

We define

f:\simeq\tilde{f}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists g\in G\text{ such that }\tilde{f}=g.f

Then
\simeq
is clearly an equivalence relation.

Now, let’s show that the equivalence relation of definition
3.1
fits into the general frameword of definition
2.1
.

Consider the identity functors
\sigma=\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\mathsf{Id}
on
\mathsf{\textbf{B}\left((E\sslash G)^{\otimes*}\right)}
, where
\mathsf{\textbf{B}\left((E\sslash G)^{\otimes*}\right)}
is the 2-category delooping of the free strict monoidal category generated by the action groupoid
\mathsf{E\sslash G}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
f\in E
,
f
can be seen as a morphism in
\mathsf{\textbf{B}\left((E\sslash G)^{\otimes*}\right)}
. Under this identification,
f\simeq\tilde{f}
as in definition
3.1
if and only if
f\simeq\tilde{f}
with respect to the data
(\mathsf{\textbf{B}\left((E\sslash G)^{\otimes*}\right)},\mathsf{\textbf{B}\left((E\sslash G)^{\otimes*}\right)},\mathsf{Id},\mathsf{Id},\mathsf{Id})
, as in definition
2.1
.

Proof.

This follows from length comparison: for all
f,\tilde{f}\in E
, for all chains
u_{1},u_{2}\in E^{*}
and for all
g\in G
, we have
u_{1}fu_{2}=g.\tilde{f}
if and only if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are the empty chains and
f=g.\tilde{f}
.

Examples of group-action equivalences abound in mathematics, but let us give a very peculiar example that we encountered in

]

Example 3.3

(Example of a group-action equivalence)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space
\mathbb{F}
(
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) and let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space.

Let

\displaystyle\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega,H)

\displaystyle=\{f:\Omega\to H\text{ such that }f\text{ is measurable and}

\displaystyle\quad\quad(\forall x\in H):\int_{\Omega}|\langle x,f(\omega)\rangle|^{2}d\mu(\omega)<+\infty\},

be the set of Bessel functions.

Define an equivalence relation on
\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega,H)
by

f:\simeq\tilde{f}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad(\exists u\in\mathbb{U}(H))(\exists\alpha\in\mathsf{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{S}^{1})),\quad\text{such that }\tilde{f}(\omega)=\alpha(\omega)u(f(\omega)).

This is precisely a group-action equivalence where the group is the semidirect product of the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(H)
with the group
\mathsf{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Consequently, it is a strict special case of our generalized equivalence framework.

3.2
Coarse invariant-based equivalence on the the global class of Bessel functions

Definition 3.4

(Operatorial equivalence in varying
\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega,H)
)
.

For any two Hilbert spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
over
\mathbb{F}
(
\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{C}

\rho(T):=\lVert T(\cdot)\rVert_{H_{2}}:H_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{F}
(
\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{C}

(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space.

For
f\in\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega,H)

T_{f}(x)=(\langle x,f(\omega)\rangle)_{\omega\in\Omega},

\boldsymbol{\rho}(f):=\rho(T_{f})=\|T_{f}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}:H\to\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Let
\mathsf{Hilb_{\mathbb{F}}^{\text{inj}}}
,
\mathsf{Hilb_{\mathbb{F}}^{\text{inj,cl}}}
, and
\mathsf{Hilb_{\mathbb{F}}^{\text{iso}}}
be respectively the categories of Hilbert spaces with bounded injective operators, bounded injective operators with closed range, and isometries as morphisms.

Two functions
f\in\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega,H)
and
\tilde{f}\in\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{H})
are defined to be equivalent (with respect to the category of Hilbert spaces that has been chosen), denoted
f\simeq\tilde{f}
, if there exist morphisms
u:H\to\tilde{H}
and
\tilde{u}:\tilde{H}\to H
such that

\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ f)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{f})\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u}\circ\tilde{f})\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(f),

or equivalently,

\rho(T_{f}\circ u^{*})\asymp\rho(T_{\tilde{f}})\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(T_{\tilde{f}}\circ\tilde{u}^{*})\asymp\rho(T_{f}),

Here,
A\asymp B
(with
A,B:S\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
S
an arbitrary set) means that there exist two uniform constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that

\forall x\in S:\quad\quad K_{1}A(x)\leq B(x)\leq K_{2}A(x).

Remark 3.5
.

•

This equivalence relation ignores pointwise structure (we have heterogeneous indexing sets) and focuses exclusively on global function behavior encoded by the analysis operator. Moreover, this formulation allows for different and not necessarily isomorphic function codomains. Therefore, it is coarser than invertible or unitary equivalence.

•

For this equivalence relation, frames
f\in\mathsf{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega,H)
are the functions that are equivalent to orthonormal bases
\tilde{f}:\operatorname{dim}(H)\to H
(where
\operatorname{dim}(H)
is the cardinality of
H
).

Indeed, if
f\simeq\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{f}
is an orthonormal basis, then, in particular,

(\exists K_{1},K_{2}>0)(\forall y\in\tilde{H}):K_{1}\lVert y\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert T_{f}\circ u^{*}(y)\rVert^{2}\leq K_{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}.

This implies that
u\circ T_{f}^{*}
is surjective, so
u
is surjective, but since it is already injective, it is an isomorphism.

Therefore
T_{f}^{*}
is surjective, which means that
f\in\mathsf{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega,H)
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Proof that the relation is an equivalence relation.

\tilde{r}\simeq r
, take
v:=\tilde{u}:\tilde{H}\to H
and
\tilde{v}:=u:H\to\tilde{H}
.

The first condition for
\tilde{r}\simeq r
is
\boldsymbol{\rho}(v\circ\tilde{r})\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u}\circ\tilde{r})\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)
, which holds by assumption.

The second condition is
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{v}\circ r)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})
, which also holds by assumption.

Hence,
\tilde{r}\simeq r
.

–

Transitivity.
Suppose
f\simeq s
via
u_{1}:H\to K
and
\tilde{u}_{1}:K\to H
, with
s\in\mathsf{\mathcal{B}}(\Lambda,K)
, so
\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{1}\circ r)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)
(both on
K
) with constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
:

\forall z\in K:\quad K_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{1}\circ r)(z)\leq K_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z),

and
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u}_{1}\circ s)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)
(both on
H
) with constants
\tilde{K}_{1},\tilde{K}_{2}>0
:

\forall z\in H:\quad\tilde{K_{1}}\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u_{1}}\circ s)(z)\leq\tilde{K_{2}}\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)(z),

Further suppose
s\simeq\tilde{f}
via
u_{2}:K\to\tilde{H}
and
\tilde{u}_{2}:\tilde{H}\to K
, so
\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})
(both on
\tilde{H}
) with constants
L_{1},L_{2}>0
:

\forall z\in\tilde{H}:\quad L_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)(z)\leq L_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z),

and
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u}_{2}\circ\tilde{r})\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)
(both on
K
) with constants
\tilde{L}_{1},\tilde{L}_{2}>0
:

\forall z\in K:\quad\tilde{L}_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u_{2}}\circ\tilde{r})(z)\leq\tilde{L}_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z),

To show
f\simeq\tilde{f}
, take
u:=u_{2}u_{1}:H\to\tilde{H}
and
\tilde{u}:=\tilde{u}_{1}\tilde{u}_{2}:\tilde{H}\to H
.

Consider the first condition
\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})
(both on
\tilde{H}
).

From
f\simeq s
, we have

K_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{1}\circ r)(z)\leq K_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(z),

Consequently

\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)(z)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)(u^{*}z)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)(u_{1}^{*}u_{2}^{*}z)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{1}\circ r)(u_{2}^{*}z)

so

K_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(u_{2}^{*}z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)(z)\leq K_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)(z).

But
\boldsymbol{\rho}(s)(u_{2}^{*}z)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)
, so

K_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)(z)\leq K_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s).

From
s\simeq\tilde{f}
, we have

L_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u_{2}\circ s)(z)\leq L_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z)
, so

K_{1}L_{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z)\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)(z)\leq K_{2}L_{2}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})(z).

Thus,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(u\circ r)\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{r})
with constants
K_{1}L_{1},K_{2}L_{2}>0
.

The second condition
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\tilde{u}\circ\tilde{r})\asymp\boldsymbol{\rho}(r)
(both on
H
) can be proven similarly.

Hence,
f\simeq\tilde{f}
.

Before moving on, let’s prove the following.

Proposition 3.7
.

Any preordered monoid
M
gives rise to a 2-category
\mathsf{PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set}
defined as follows.

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ob(PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set)}:=

\displaystyle\{\text{preordered sets }X\text{ endowed with a monoid action by }M\text{ such that }

\displaystyle(\forall m\in M^{+})(\forall x\geq y):m.x\geq m.y\}.

For any two objects
X_{1},X_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Hom(X_{1},X_{2})}:=

\displaystyle\{\text{monotone maps from }X_{1}\text{ to }X_{2}\text{ such that }

\displaystyle(\forall m\in M^{+})(\forall x\in X_{1}):f(m.x)=m.f(x)\}

For any two parallel morphisms
f,g\in Hom(X_{1},X_{2})
,

\mathsf{Hom(f,g)}=\{c\in Z(M^{+})\text{ such that }(\forall x,y\in X_{1}):x\geq y\Rightarrow f(x)\geq cg(y)\}.

Composition of 1-morphisms
f,g
in
\mathsf{PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set}
is defined as function composition
f\circ g
.

Horizontal composition of 2-morphisms c,d in
\mathsf{PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set}
consists of the product
dc=cd
.

Vertical composition of 2-morphisms c,d in
\mathsf{PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set}
consists of the product
dc=cd
.

Proof.

We prove that for any preordered monoid
M
, the data described in the proposition form a 2-category.

•

Objects.

Objects are preordered sets
X
with a monoid action by
M
such that for all
m\in M^{+}
and
x\geq y
in
X
, we have
m.x\geq m.y
.

•

1-morphisms.

1-morphisms between objects
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are monotone,
M^{+}
-equivariant maps
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
(i.e.,
f(m.x)=m.f(x)
for all
m\in M^{+}
).

For each object
X
, the identity 1-morphism
\operatorname{id}_{X}
is the identity map, which is clearly monotone and equivariant.
Composition of 1-morphisms is ordinary function composition, which is associative, unital, and preserves the monotonicity and equivariance of 1-morphisms.

•

2-morphisms.

For parallel 1-morphisms
f,g:X_{1}\to X_{2}
, the set
\operatorname{Hom}(f,g)
consists of elements
c\in Z(M^{+})
(the center of
M^{+}
) such that for all
x\geq y
in
X_{1}
, we have
f(x)\geq c.g(y)
in
X_{2}
.

–

Identity 2-morphisms.

The identity 2-morphism
1_{\alpha}
on a 1-morphism
f
is the unit
1\in M^{+}
.

Indeed, since
1\in Z(M^{+})
and
f(x)\geq 1.f(y)=f(y)
by monotonicity of
f
, we have
1\in\operatorname{Hom}(f,f)
.

–

Vertical composition.

Given
c\in\operatorname{Hom}(f,g)
and
d\in\operatorname{Hom}(g,h)
, the vertical composite is defined as
d\circ c=cd
(product in
M^{+}
).

Well-definedness:

*

Since
c,d\in Z(M^{+})
, their product
cd
is also in
Z(M^{+})
.

*

For any
x\geq y
, we have
f(x)\geq c.g(y)
and
g(y)\geq d.h(y)
. Applying monotonicity of the action by
c\in M^{+}
to the second inequality gives
c.g(y)\geq cd.h(y)
. By transitivity,
f(x)\geq cd.h(y)
. Thus
d\circ c=cd\in\operatorname{Hom}(f,h)
.

Associativity and unitality follow from the monoid structure of
M^{+}
.

–

Horizontal composition.

Given
c\in\operatorname{Hom}(f,g)
(where
f,g:X_{1}\to X_{2}
) and
d\in\operatorname{Hom}(h,i)
(where
h,i:X_{2}\to X_{3}
), the horizontal composite is
d*c=cd
.

Well-definedness:

*

Since
c,d\in Z(M^{+})
, their product
cd
is also in
Z(M^{+})
.

*

We must check that
cd\in\operatorname{Hom}(h\circ f,i\circ g)
.

For any
x\geq y
in
X_{1}
, we have
f(x)\geq c.g(y)
.

Applying the monotone map
h
gives
h(f(x))\geq h(c.g(y))
.

By equivariance,
h(c.g(y))=c.h(g(y))
, so
h(f(x))\geq c.h(g(y))
.

Since
g(y)\geq g(y)
(reflexivity), the condition on
d
gives
h(g(y))\geq d.i(g(y))
. Monotonicity of the action by
c
yields
c.h(g(y))\geq cd.i(g(y))
.

By transitivity,
h(f(x))\geq cd.i(g(y))
. Thus
d*c=cd\in\operatorname{Hom}(h\circ f,i\circ g)
.

Associativity and unitality follow from the monoid structure of
M^{+}
.

–

Interchange law.

Given
c\in\operatorname{Hom}(f,f^{\prime})
,
c^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}(f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime})
,
d\in\operatorname{Hom}(g,g^{\prime})
, and
d^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}(g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime})
, we have

(d^{\prime}\circ d)*(c^{\prime}\circ c)=(dd^{\prime})*(cc^{\prime})=cc^{\prime}dd^{\prime}

and

(d^{\prime}*c^{\prime})\circ(d*c)=(c^{\prime}d^{\prime})\circ(cd)=cdc^{\prime}d^{\prime}

Since
c^{\prime},d\in Z(M^{+})
, they commute, so
cc^{\prime}dd^{\prime}=cdc^{\prime}d^{\prime}
.

Thus the two expressions are equal.

All axioms of a 2-category are satisfied. Therefore,
\mathsf{PreOrd\text{-}M^{+}Set}
is a 2-category.

.

T_{f}:H\to L^{2}(\Omega)

, with respect to the present data
(\mathsf{Hilb_{\mathbb{F}}},\mathsf{(PreOrd\text{-}\mathbb{R}^{+}_{*}Set)^{c}},\sigma,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

Proof.

First, for any seminorm

\displaystyle=\left(\underset{\lVert x\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1}{\operatorname{sup}}(\tau_{2}(u_{2})(\phi)(x)\right)\lVert T_{f}(\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\circ u_{1}

4
Conclusion and perspectives

Our generalized equivalence relation (definition
2.1
) provides a flexible and conceptually clean framework that extends classical group-action equivalences (definition
3.1
) and the equivalence relation based on the pointwise norm of the analysis operator of Bessel functions (definition
3.4
).

More broadly, this approach provides a unifying language for equivalence problems where the relevant structure is encoded by 2-categories and observables rather than explicit symmetries.

Before closing this article, let us mention that there are at least two interesting implicit problems that haven’t been tackled in this article:

\simeq
of definition
3.4
.

Problem 4.2

The generalization of the equivalence relation
\simeq
of definition
3.4
to the Banach space setting.
