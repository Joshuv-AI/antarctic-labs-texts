Beneficial Effects of Nuts on Cardiovascular and Metabolic Health: An Insight into the Gut–Cardiometabolic Axis
Authors: Mahak Hosseinikia, Laura M Beaver, Carmen P Wong, Emily Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) are major contributors to global morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiota plays a central role in the pathogenesis and progression of CMDs by regulating cardiometabolic endpoints, including lipid profiles, glucose homeostasis, immunity, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Nuts are nutrient-dense foods rich in unsaturated fatty acids, dietary fiber, polyphenols, vitamins, and minerals that interact with the gut microbiota. Additionally, nut intake has been associated with improved cardiometabolic health outcomes. This review examines current evidence on the effects of tree nut consumption on the gut–cardiometabolic axis, with an emphasis on clinical and mechanistic studies across different types of tree nuts. Clinical evidence of the capacity of nut consumption to affect CMDs is summarized. The bidirectional interaction between nut-derived microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, urolithins, phenolic acids, valerolactones, and gut microbiota, is highlighted. In addition, mechanisms by which changes in the nut-associated gut microbiome affect the CMDs via regulating lipids, glucose, energy homeostasis, and immunity modulation are discussed. The available evidence, although mostly associative, supports the potential favorable effects of nuts in modulating gut microbial composition and function in ways that may improve cardiometabolic health. However, future studies are needed to address limitations in current studies, including substantial heterogeneity across studies, the lack of established causal mechanisms, long-term clinical outcomes, and to investigate personalized responses to nut consumption given interindividual variability in gut microbiota.Keywords: nut consumption, gut microbiome, gut–cardiometabolic axis, cardiometabolic health, polyphenols, microbial metabolites, immune function

Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) are a group of disorders, including type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [1], and cardiovascular diseases (CVD), which are interconnected disorders largely caused by metabolic dysfunctions and associated with gut dysbiosis [2,3]. Increasing prevalence of CMDs imposes a considerable burden on global health [2,4], highlighting the need to prevent and successfully treat CMDs. According to the available evidence, ∼44% of the United States adult population is predicted to be affected by some form of CMDs by 2030 [5]. Similarly, type 2 diabetes affects 34.6–36.5 million people in the United States and represents a major component of CMDs [6], which share common underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [7]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic abnormalities that increases risk of developing CMDs [8]. MetS prevalence is growing, but is variable by country, ranging from 12.5% to 31.4% of the population, with the highest prevalence in the Americas and countries in the Eastern Mediterranean region [9].

Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiome acts as an important regulator linking diet to CMDs [7]. Diet-induced gut dysbiosis contributes to impaired intestinal barrier integrity, endotoxemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and altered host metabolism, all of which play central roles in the pathogenesis of CMDs [10,11]. The gut microbiota is a combination of trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses, residing in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, whereas the gut microbiome refers to the broader microbial community together with the gene content, functional potential, and associated environment [12]. The gut microbiota has been considered an integral component of human health because of its multidirectional relationship with other organs and the physiological processes it performs, including digestion, producing different kinds of metabolites and vitamins, modifying intestinal epithelium, and boosting immunity [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Human health and well-being is maintained by a complex interaction of different biological systems, and increasing evidence indicates that there is a dynamic interplay between the GI microbiota, cardiovascular system, and metabolic processes, collectively defined here as the gut–cardiometabolic axis, which plays an important role in the development of CMDs [17,18]. Gut dysbiosis, an imbalance in the microbial composition of the GI tract that disrupts epithelial barrier function and leads to endotoxemia and systemic inflammation, is heavily influenced by Western diets and is a key risk factor for developing CMDs [3,19]. However, healthy dietary patterns can decelerate the progression of CMDs and can act to prevent CMDs [10].

Given the high incidence and public health burden of CMDs, it is exciting that a growing body of research has found beneficial effects of eating nuts for the prevention and/or treatment of CVDs and metabolic disturbances. Nuts are a rich source of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), protein, fiber, several vitamins and minerals, and phytonutrients, including polyphenols [20,21]. A diet rich in nuts has the capacity to modulate gut microbiota composition and diversity [22], which promotes gut barrier integrity and reduces systemic inflammation [23].

This review aims to explore the health benefits of consuming nuts, focusing on the interplay between nut intake, CMDs, and gut microbiome. In particular, we highlight emerging evidence that nut consumption influences cardiometabolic health through the production of microbiota-derived metabolites, functional microbial changes, and the regulation of host metabolic and immune-mediated pathways, gut barrier integrity, and inflammatory signaling. However, to our knowledge, a causal mediating role of the gut microbiota in improving CMD outcomes remains largely unestablished in humans.

For the purposes of this review, nuts refer specifically to tree nuts, including almonds, walnuts, pistachios, cashews, Brazil nuts, pecans, hazelnuts, and mixed tree nuts [24]. We summarize: 1) evidence supporting the effects of tree nuts on gut–cardiometabolic health, 2) mechanistic evidence of how tree nut intake could beneficially affect CMD outcomes, 3) the most prominent secondary bioactive compounds produced by microbiota from nuts, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), polyphenol metabolites, and their effects on gut health, and 4) the interplay between the gut microbiome and CMDs via affecting gut microbiota composition, immunity, and changing lipid, glucose, and energy homeostasis. This paper is a nonsystematic narrative review. We searched databases including PubMed and Google Scholar, combining terms for tree nuts including almonds, walnuts, pistachios, cashews, Brazil nuts, pecans, hazelnuts, and mixed nuts with terms for CMD outcomes, gut microbiota, gut microbiome, microbial metabolites, secondary metabolites, randomized trials, and clinical interventions. Studies were considered regardless of statistical significance, allowing all findings to be represented. Additional mechanistic and background literature was identified from targeted searches and hand searching the reference lists. For the clinical studies summarized in Table 1, the 2017–2025 timeframe was selected to provide a sufficient body of evidence while keeping the focus on recent papers. The search was restricted to studies conducted on adult populations. Human intervention studies were considered when nuts were either a defined intervention or test food.

Nutritional Composition and Health Benefits of Nuts

Nuts are nutrient-dense foods, and their health impacts have undergone a thorough investigation since the early 1990s, when epidemiological evidence first associated nut consumption with a lower risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) (Figure 1) [24,64]. The unique chemical composition of nuts led them to be considered a good example of a naturally occurring functional food. Nuts are a rich source of UFAs, protein, fiber, several vitamins and minerals, and phytonutrients including polyphenols [21,65]. The nutrient content of nuts varies by type. For example, almonds and hazelnuts have the highest amounts of α-tocopherol, pistachios are abundant in carotenoids, whereas walnuts have the highest phenolic compounds and plant-derived omega-3 (n–3) fatty acids [66]. Nuts, in general, are a good source of minerals like potassium, magnesium, and selenium [21,65]. Phytosterols, commonly known as noncholesterol sterols, which interfere with cholesterol absorption, are abundantly found in nuts, particularly in pistachios and almonds [21]. More detailed nutritional composition of different tree nuts and comparison of nutritional qualities across nut types have recently been reviewed in detail [21,67,68].

Physical form and processing methods may also influence nutritional profile and physiological effects of nuts [69,70]. For example, roasting and greater physical processing increase lipid release during digestion as compared with raw nuts [69]. Additionally, thermal processing may modify the content and bioaccessibility of phytonutrients in nuts [70].

In the following sections, an overview of recent clinical trials that have examined the effects of nut consumption on cardiometabolic health is outlined, and the protective mechanisms of nuts against CVD and MetS will also be discussed.

Evidence linking nut consumption to cardiometabolic health with an emphasis on clinical and mechanistic studies

According to the growing evidence from interventional studies, nut intake can improve risk factors related to CMDs [22]. Table 1 summarizes the most recent clinical studies (2017–2025) examining how nut intake affects CMD outcomes across various populations, including normal and obese subjects and individuals at risk of CMD. The types of nuts tested in clinical trials include tree nuts such as almonds, walnuts, pistachios, cashews, Brazil nuts, pecans, hazelnuts, and mixed nuts, with study durations ranging from 2 d to 20 wk. Incorporating nuts into the diet often improves the plasma lipid profile [71], glycemic control [72], inflammation [73], and/or weight management [74], supporting the beneficial role of nut consumption in cardiometabolic health.

The overall beneficial effects of nut consumption on all-cause mortality [20] and the cardiovascular system, in particular [75], led various health-related organizations, including the United States Food and Drug Administration and the American Heart Association, to recommend nut consumption as a potential means of lowering the risk of CVD [76]. The cardioprotective effects of nuts have been extensively reviewed in prior narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses [20,22,[77], [78], [79], [80]]. Evidence from large prospective cohorts and intervention studies also supports an association between nut-containing dietary patterns and cardiovascular health. In the PREDIMED (Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea) trial, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with mixed nuts was associated with a lower incidence of major cardiovascular events among adults at high CVD risk compared with a reduced-fat control diet [81]. Across human clinical trials, nut consumption improved cardiometabolic health by lowering LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, blood pressure, glycemic markers, and inflammatory indices. In some studies, these benefits are accompanied by favorable shifts in the gut microbiota, including increased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria and other beneficial taxa, reduced proinflammatory bile acids, and improved microbial diversity. However, the effects of nut consumption on the gut microbiota have not been consistent across studies. A systematic review by Creedon et al. [23] reported limited evidence for consistent effects of nut consumption on microbial diversity and composition. For example, walnut interventions have reported changes in specific taxa, including Ruminococcaceae, Bifidobacterium, and Roseburia, whereas other studies have observed different compositional changes [25,27,29]. Similarly, responses to almond consumption are controversial across studies, with some reporting changes in microbial diversity or specific taxa and others showing more limited effects [34,37,39]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in nut type and dose, intervention duration, nut processing and food matrix, target population, and interindividual variability in gut microbiota and microbiome assessment methods. Thus, current evidence does not support a uniform effect of nut consumption on gut microbial composition. Collectively, this literature demonstrates potential associations between regular nut intake and improvements in physiological factors related to cardiometabolic health, including lipid metabolism [82], glycemic control [83], inflammatory status, oxidative stress [84], and endothelial function [85]. More specifically, nut consumption improved lipid and glucose homeostasis by limiting intestinal cholesterol absorption [86], activating nuclear receptors and transcription factors that promote fatty acid oxidation, inhibit lipogenesis, increase systemic insulin sensitivity, and reduce plasma glucose [87]. The low glycemic index of nuts slows carbohydrate absorption and attenuates postprandial glucose responses [88]. As illustrated in Figure 2, nut consumption stimulates appetite regulation hormones and incretins such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) [[89], [90], [91]]. These hormones stimulate insulin secretion and suppress glucagon production [92], which leads to better glycemic control. Nuts also provide antioxidant and anti-inflammatory compounds that suppress proinflammatory signaling pathways such as nuclear factor–κB (NF-κB) directly or by inducing the expression of genes regulated by nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [84,93]. These pathways affect endogenous antioxidant defense systems, limit lipid peroxidation, and reduce systemic inflammatory responses, thereby supporting metabolic health [94]. Nuts support endothelial function through improving nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability and production. The high content of L-arginine in nuts provides a dietary source that supports NO production and cardiovascular health in humans [85].

Increased nut intake could reduce risk of people developing MetS, which considerably increases risk of CMDs [95], by improving metabolic inflammation. Metabolic inflammation, also known as meta-inflammation, refers to chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation primarily induced by hypertrophic adipocytes. Hypertrophic fat cells outgrow their blood supply, and as a result, hypoxia occurs, which increases the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, Hypoxia-inducible factor, and macrophage migration inhibitory factor. This condition activates inflammatory cascades in adipocytes and immune cells, which play a key role in the pathogenesis of CVDs and MetS [96,97]. Nuts may help regulate body weight by inducing satiety and reducing postprandial hunger [91,98]. Human intervention studies suggest that almonds may influence appetite-regulating hormones. Acute almond consumption showed greater postprandial GIP, glucagon, and pancreatic polypeptide responses than an isocaloric carbohydrate-rich snack, although these changes did not affect subsequent food intake [99]. In a longer-term energy-restriction trial, almond consumption was associated with favorable changes in ghrelin and leptin, as well as greater postprandial GIP, GLP-1, glucagon, and pancreatic polypeptide responses; however, subjective hunger and energy intake did not significantly differ between groups [100]. Recent human clinical evidence also suggests that mixed tree nut consumption may modulate adipose tissue metabolism, with changes in the expression of genes involved in thermogenesis, lipolysis, and energy homeostasis [87].

In addition, various studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of ω–3 fatty acids and nut-derived polyphenols on macrophage polarization, promoting a phenotypic shift from proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages [101,102]. In summary, regular nut intake as part of a healthy diet may favorably influence CMD risk factors, reinforcing its role in dietary strategies for the prevention of CMDs.

The Gut Microbiota’s Role in Cardiometabolic Health

Gut microbiota are essential to overall health by affecting various physiological processes, including metabolism, inflammation, immune responses, and homeostasis [14]. Microorganisms metabolize indigestible fiber and other complex nutritional components in nuts to produce more bioavailable and/or bioactive compounds, as illustrated in Figure 2. Beyond that, gut microbiota influence host metabolism and immune function by interacting with other organs through metabolic and signaling pathways. One of the significant ways that links microorganisms with these effects is via the gut–liver–brain axis, an integrative network that transmits signals from gut microbiota, nervous system, and liver to regulate metabolic processes [103]. However, changes in microbial composition do not necessarily correspond to changes in microbial function, because different microbial taxa can share similar metabolic functions [104]. The balance between pathogenic and symbiotic microorganisms residing in the gut determines whether the interaction between gut microbiota and other organs and physiological processes is beneficial or harmful. A reduction in overall microbial diversity, with a decline in beneficial bacteria and their metabolic processes, is characteristic of dysbiosis, which disrupts intestinal homeostasis and alters the production of gut–endocrine hormones. Gut dysbiosis is closely associated with many diseases, including cancer, diabetes, and neurological disorders [14]. Dysbiosis also facilitates the onset and progression of CMDs by promoting metabolic dysfunction, immune dysregulation, and neuroenteroendocrine disruptions, which we will discuss in more detail in the following sections [105,106].

Bidirectional interaction between nut-derived bioactive compounds and gut microbiota

The numerous bioactive compounds in nuts, such as fiber, polyphenols, vitamins, and minerals, may contribute to maintaining gut health by enhancing microbial diversity and gut barrier integrity [23]. Gut microbiota, on the other hand, transform some of these compounds, making them more bioavailable to the nut consumer. Fibers and polyphenols, for example, are poorly absorbed in the upper GI tract, and they undergo bacterial transformation when they reach the colon, producing secondary metabolites such as SCFAs, urolithin, indoles, and related compounds [66,[107], [108], [109], [110]]. As mentioned earlier, the production of microbial metabolites depends on both the microbial composition and their functional and genetic capacity. The following paragraphs will introduce the most important bioactive compounds derived from nuts following microbial biotransformation and their impact on health.

Short-chain fatty acids

SCFAs are microbial byproducts made by bacteria from nondigestible polysaccharides (NDPs) found in high-fiber foods, including nuts. NDPs are not digested or are partly digested in the upper GI tract and pass through the upper GI into the lower part of the small intestine and colon. SCFAs are a good energy source for colonocytes, have anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory properties, and maintain glucose and lipid homeostasis. SCFAs, which act as a major player in human health, consist of a hydrocarbon chain of 1–6 carbon atoms, with acetate (C2), propionate (C3), and butyrate (C4) being the most abundant. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes taxa are the 2 most known polysaccharide-degrading bacteria that transform NDPs into SCFAs [111]. These bacterial metabolites have both intracellular and extracellular actions. Their intracellular effects on the human gut are mediated through 3 main SCFA transporters in colonocytes, including sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporter 1, monocarboxylate transporter 1, and monocarboxylate transporter 4. These GI transporters help absorb the SCFAs under different luminal conditions [112]. These compounds reduce intestinal pH and promote the growth of beneficial bacteria [113]. Fecal and circulating SCFA concentrations reflect different domains of SCFA metabolism. Fecal SCFAs are more closely associated with gut microbial composition and may better reflect microbial SCFA generation, but they do not represent intestinal absorption or circulating levels. In contrast, circulating SCFAs reflect the fraction reaching the circulation after intestinal absorption and host metabolism and show relatively low concordance with fecal concentrations. Therefore, differences in the biological samples assessed should be considered when interpreting SCFA findings across studies [114]. Figure 2 depicts the favorable effects of SCFAs on gut homeostasis, implemented via their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, which are mainly mediated through G protein-coupled receptor/Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition established in preclinical and in vitro studies [115]. HDACs are a group of enzymes that contribute to epigenetic modifications by deacetylating histones and altering chromatin structure. These structural changes affect gene expression patterns and promote proinflammatory cytokine production in macrophages and dendritic cells [116,117]. Butyrate, a potent inhibitor of HDACs, increases the production of IL-10. This anti-inflammatory cytokine reduces claudin-2 production and maintains gut barrier integrity [118,119]. The significant role of SCFAs in regulating intestinal permeability is mediated via various mechanisms, including mucin secretion and upregulating tight-junction proteins such as claudin-1, zonula occludens-1, and occludin. Upregulation of tight-junction proteins that is triggered by nut consumption promotes intestinal barrier function and reduces bacterial translocation and neutrophil infiltration, leading to inflammation reduction and improved metabolic health [113,120]. Human intervention studies suggest that nut consumption may alter fecal SCFA concentrations [34], but the discussed cellular mechanisms attributed to SCFAs in humans are limited; current evidence is mainly supported by preclinical studies.

Polyphenols and microbial-derived secondary metabolites

Nuts are a rich source of polyphenols, including tannins, phenolic acids, flavonoids, and stilbenes, which primarily alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress and protect the body against metabolic disorders [84,121]. There is a bidirectional relationship between polyphenols and the gut microbiota as polyphenols can alter the richness and abundance of microbial species, and the gut microbiota can biotransform polyphenols and increase their bioavailability [122,123]. Intestinal bacterial species contain a wide variety of metabolic enzymes that increase gut metabolizing capacity beyond the metabolic capacity of the liver. These bacterial enzymes modify the polyphenols via catalyzing biotransformation reactions, including decarboxylation, reduction, isomerization, and demethylation of side chains and aromatic rings in the polyphenols [122]. Nuts contain a considerable amount of hydrolyzable tannins, proanthocyanidins, flavonoids, and phenolic acids, which exert their favorable effects directly and via secondary microbial metabolites [124]. Here, we discuss some of the microbial metabolites made from polyphenols in nuts and describe mechanisms by which they support gut health.

Urolithins

Nuts like walnuts, chestnuts, and almonds contain ellagic acid and ellagitannins, which are metabolized by the human gut microbiota to produce urolithins [125]. Urolithins are produced through a sequence of metabolic steps and are well known for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant qualities. In the stomach and intestines, ellagic acid is produced by hydrolyzing ellagitannins. Certain bacteria then convert ellagic acid into urolithins in the colon [125]. Individuals vary in their ability to produce urolithin, mainly because of their distinct gut microbiome profile. Three distinct urolithin metabotypes have been identified: Metabotype-A (UM-A), characterized by the ability to produce urolithin A, Metabotype-B (UM-B), which produces urolithin A, isourolithin A, and urolithin B, and Metabotype 0 (UM-0), or urolithin nonproducers [126]. The fact that people in the UM-0 category do not make urolithins emphasizes how important the composition of gut bacteria is in breaking down ellagitannins and producing substances that could benefit health [125,127]. These distinct urolithin metabotypes provide a useful example of how interindividual differences in gut microbial composition and metabolic capacity may influence responses to ellagitannin-rich nuts, supporting the importance of microbial metabotypes in personalized nutrition.

Urolithins have been associated with potential health benefits for the gut, like enhancing mucin production and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity through the activation of Nrf2 and aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling pathways [126,128]. Uro-A and its synthetic analog UAS03 also showed promising effects in a colitis model through the same mechanism [129]. Uro-A benefits gut microbial composition by increasing the growth of beneficial bacteria [130]. Wild-type C57BL/6 mice developed thicker mucosal layers and increased mucin 2 protein levels after receiving 20–100 mg/kg Uro-A [131]. In a clinical trial, participants who were given 50 mg/d of Uro-A significantly increased gut microbial α-diversity, and the vascular response to Uro-A was associated with baseline gut microbiota characteristics. These results suggest that the effects of Uro-A on vascular function may vary according to individual gut microbial profiles [132]. Uro-B restored lower levels of intestinal tight-junction proteins and alleviated gut inflammation in high-fat/high-sucrose-fed mice by increasing the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila [133]. In human colon cancer cells, urolithin A and urolithin B have also been shown to inhibit prostaglandin E2 production by attenuating inflammatory pathways, whereas ellagic acid had no effect. It is also worth noting that consumption of foods containing ellagic acid, such as walnuts, altered the microbial community structure and bacterial richness among participants [134].

Other phenolic acids

Phenolic acids, including cinnamic acid, phenylacetic acid, benzoic acid, caffeic acid, and vanillic acid, are a group of nut polyphenols [67] with a low molecular weight and a chemical structure that includes an aromatic ring, a carboxyl group, and one or more hydroxyl groups [135]. Walnuts, pecans, and pistachios, for example, are a good source of gallic acid, vanillic acid, and cinnamic acid [136,137]. Some of these products are produced in plants (as a result of lignin breakdown), and some of these phenolic acids are produced as a result of the microbial processes in the gut [135]. The microbial processes involve hydrolyzation and deglycosylation by various enzymes, including β-glucosidase, β-glucuronidase, and α-rhamnosidase, present in different bacterial taxa in the gut, such as Bacteroides spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. [138]. These microbial-derived phenolic acids are assumed to be primarily derived from anthocyanins, procyanidins, and flavanones. However, owing to the individual differences in gut microbial composition, it is hard to specify the parent compounds exactly [139]. These phenolic compounds have been shown to contribute to gut health via different mechanisms. Research in animal models has shown that p-coumaric acid decreased inflammatory responses by reducing COX-2, IL-17, and suppressing mitogen-activated protein kinase/NF-κB signaling [140,141]. 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoic suppressed proinflammatory cytokines and restored colonic edema in colitis mice models by suppressing the NF-κB pathway [138]. Chlorogenic acid attenuated intestinal inflammation by promoting macrophage polarization from proinflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype. The underlying mechanism includes reducing glycolysis in M1 macrophages via inhibiting pyruvate kinase M2, which could lead to blockage of NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [142]. These findings show protective effects of phenolic acids on CMDs via attenuating inflammation, oxidative stress, and improving gut microbial composition [143].

Valerolactones

Gut-derived, plant-based valerolactones, including phenyl-γ-valerolactones (PVLs), 5-(3′,4′-Dihydroxyphenyl)-γ-valerolactone (DHPV), and 5-(3′,4′,5′-trihydroxyphenyl)-γ-valerolactone. These compounds are key secondary metabolites mainly originating from flavan-3-ols found in nuts such as hazelnuts, pecans, almonds, and walnuts, green tea, berries, and grapes [[144], [145], [146], [147], [148]]. The production of valerolactones from nut-derived polyphenols depends on both the flavan-3-ol content and the composition and metabolic capacity of the gut microbiota in different individuals [149]. PVLs are a new and interesting research area, with much literature on their pharmacokinetics and bioavailability [146,150,151] and in vitro and animal studies supporting their health benefits [152], but results are still emerging in human clinical research.

Valerolactones have numerous health-promoting effects, including cardioprotective, antioxidant, and antiproliferative activities [153,154]. These metabolites have the potential to suppress metalloproteinases and showed scavenging and anti-inflammatory activities [155]. In an in vitro study, human umbilical vein endothelial cells were pretreated with DHPV (≤30 μM) for 1 h, then stimulated with TNF-α for 5 h. DHPV suppresses NF-κB signaling pathway and demonstrated an antiatherosclerotic potential [156]. Microbial metabolites, notably 5(3,4,5-trihydroxyphenyl)-γ-valerolactone, derived from (-)-epigallocatechin gallate, which is mainly found in green tea but is also found in smaller amounts in nuts including walnuts, pecans, pistachios, and hazelnuts, demonstrated angiotensin-converting enzyme-inhibitory activity and reduced blood pressure in hypertensive rats [68,[157], [158], [159]]. In other studies, the anti-inflammatory activities of valerolactones were attributed to their inhibitory effects on LPS-stimulated macrophages [149,155]. These microbial metabolites demonstrated cardioprotective effects and may benefit metabolic disorders by suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress. However, direct evidence supporting valerolactones’ impact on lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and glucose regulation remains limited and needs further investigation.

Effect of nut-associated gut microbiota changes on the cardiovascular system by affecting lipid/glucose and energy homeostasis

SCFAs, produced by microbial fermentation from nondigestible carbohydrates in nuts, are key compounds produced by gut microorganisms that also affect energy homeostasis and carbohydrate metabolism. SCFAs activate free fatty acid receptor 2/3 on enteroendocrine cells and stimulate the production of GLP-1 and peptide YY (Figure 2). GLP-1 enhances insulin secretion [160]. Additionally, the vagal nerve terminals in the gut, stimulated by these hormones, regulate energy intake and expenditure by affecting hypothalamic regulatory centers, leading to glucose control and satiety. There is also a potential lipid-driven crosstalk between the host and microbiome species, affecting lipids’ digestion, detoxification, and biotransformation processes. The bioactive lipid metabolites, produced by bacterial taxa, may influence host metabolism, systemic immunity, and the local tissue. Different microbial species contribute to synthesizing major lipid classes, such as sphingolipids, phospholipids, bile acids, and cardiolipin. Microbial enzymes can also transform these lipid compounds into various products, which can affect host signaling functions. In vitro studies showed that conjugated linolenic acid isomers are an intermediate product resulting from bacterial biotransformation of ALA, which can suppress inflammation and oxidative stress [161]. Cholesterol, on the other hand, is converted into coprostanol through bacterial modification, influencing serum cholesterol levels and inflammation [161]. These microbial-derived lipids increase leptin secretion in adipocytes, which induces satiety and facilitates glucose uptake [162,163]. The endocannabinoid system (ECS), present in both the central nervous system and peripheral tissues, has a leading role in energy balance and consists of cannabinoid receptors and lipid-based ligands [164,165]. ECS and gut microbes interactively affect lipid metabolism. Lactobacillus acidophilus, for example, affects adipogenesis via interaction with the ECS. Additionally, Akkermansia muciniphila increased levels of endocannabinoid-like glycerolipids in the intestine of obese mice [166]. However, most of the mechanistic evidence related to the ECS pathway comes from preclinical and cellular studies. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiome may also influence host metabolic pathways by regulating gene expression. In a randomized, controlled-feeding intervention, walnut intake was associated with increased endogenous homoarginine production, potentially mediated through gut microbiota–related upregulation of glycine amidinotransferase. Homoarginine is involved in NO metabolism and may enhance endothelial NO availability, which is closely linked to improved insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake in peripheral tissues [167]. These findings suggest novel mechanisms by which nuts may induce modulation of the gut microbiota and influence the development of CMDs by regulating metabolic pathways, which leads to systemic homeostasis by affecting host energy balance and inflammatory responses [168].

Effect of nut-associated gut microbiota changes on cardiovascular system via immune-mediated mechanisms

The gut microbiome is increasingly recognized as a regulator of cardiovascular health because it shapes immune function, barrier integrity, and the production of microbial metabolites that signal to vascular, hepatic, and immune cells [169]. The GI tract contains a large proportion of the body's immune cells [170], which form a dynamic crosstalk between gut microbiota and other intestinal epithelial cells. Studies using knockout mice and model organisms have underpinned the intricate bidirectional relationship between gut microbiome and immune system. The gut microbiota play a determinative role in the development and activation of certain immune cell types. On the other hand, immunoglobulins and peptides produced from immune cells help maintain gut microbiota balance [[171], [172], [173]]. This mutual interaction could partly explain the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques and other risk factors of CMDs [174,175]. Atherosclerosis and many forms of CMDs are recognized to have a strong immune-inflammatory component, and gut-derived metabolites can influence multiple steps in this process, from endothelial activation and leukocyte recruitment to macrophage foam cell and plaque formation [175]. However, not all microbial metabolites are protective. In contrast to the beneficial microbial metabolites such as SCFAs, gut dysbiosis can impair epithelial barrier function, enabling the translocation of bacterial products such as LPS from the intestinal lumen into the circulation, which forms a major immune-mediated link between the gut and the cardiovascular system. Even mild, chronic elevations in circulating LPS, known as low-grade endotoxemia, can stimulate TLRs, promoting systemic inflammation and prothrombotic pathways that contribute to atherothrombosis [176,177]. TLRs are a group of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognize conserved microbial structures known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Upon binding PAMPs, PRRs undergo conformational changes and trigger downstream intracellular signaling pathways. They initiate the production of immune mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines, which lead to inflammation and phagocytosis. PRRs help distinguish commensal from pathogenic bacteria and maintain intestinal integrity by increasing mucus secretion, antimicrobial peptide levels, and IgA [178,179]. Mice lacking these pathways were predisposed to dysbiosis and inflammation [180]. Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a gut microbiota-associated metabolite, is generated from microbial trimethylamine (TMA) using dietary precursors including choline and phosphatidylcholine [181]. TMA is absorbed and oxidized in the liver by the flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 enzyme, producing TMAO, which has been linked to CVD risk [182]. This compound promotes macrophage foam cell formation, impairs cholesterol transport and reduces bile acid synthesis, triggers vascular inflammation, and promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation and downstream cytokines [183]. Circulating TMAO concentrations are influenced by multiple factors, including dietary components such as nuts. Although TMAO’s causal contribution to human CVD risk remains controversial, nut intake may modulate the TMA/TMAO pathway; however, evidence from human studies remains limited. A recent randomized dietary intervention in adults with overweight and obesity found that plasma TMAO decreased after a 12-wk hypocaloric diet containing either mixed tree nuts or pretzels, with no significant difference in TMAO reduction between the intervention groups. However, among participants with lower baseline diet quality, mixed tree nut consumption was associated with a greater reduction in TMAO than the control. Notably, dietary choline and phosphatidylcholine intake, as known precursors of TMAO, did not differ significantly over time between groups [181]. In a preclinical study, polyphenols such as resveratrol, which can be found in nuts such as pistachios [184], reduced the risk of TMAO-induced atherosclerosis in choline-fed ApoE/mice by improving gut microbiome composition [184]. Although it is limited evidence, a case report in a patient with cardiorenal syndrome showed that Brazil nut consumption was associated with reduced plasma TMAO levels, alongside suppression of NF-κB signaling and increased glutathione peroxidase activity [185]. Limited human evidence suggests that nut intake may affect TMAO-related inflammatory pathways, underscoring the need for future studies to test these potential mechanisms.

In patients with CAD, coadministration of probiotics and prebiotics significantly reduced inflammatory markers, potentially through enhanced SCFA production and suppression of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling. Notably, several components of nuts, including fermentable fibers and polyphenols, can exert prebiotic effects on the gut microbiome, promoting SCFA-producing taxa and attenuating TLR-mediated inflammatory response [186]. Macrophage polarization represents another innate immune mechanism that may contribute to the gut–cardiometabolic axis. As discussed in "Other phenolic acids" section, chlorogenic acid may attenuate intestinal inflammation by suppressing proinflammatory M1 macrophage polarization and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [142]. In addition, in high-fat diet-fed mice, urolithin A reduced macrophage infiltration into adipose tissue and altered M1/M2 polarization, whereas experiments in bone marrow-derived macrophages demonstrated enhanced M2 polarization and mitochondrial respiration, which is the process by which mitochondria generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation [187].

The adaptive immune system is also central to shaping microbiota composition and cardiovascular outcomes. Gut-associated lymphoid tissue effectively acts against pathogens and promotes eubiosis through mechanisms such as IgA secretion and regulatory T cell (Treg) induction, which support the beneficial commensal bacteria [172]. Disruption of these adaptive immune pathways can alter microbial community structure and promote intestinal inflammation [188]. In a controlled animal study, administration of walnut oil modulated gut microbiota composition and enhanced gut mucosal immunity by increasing secretory IgA levels and suppressing proinflammatory cytokine signaling in the intestinal mucosa. These mechanisms are known to influence systemic inflammation relevant to CMD risk [189].

Chronic immune activation driven by dysbiosis can have deleterious cardiovascular consequences [190]. Persistent low-grade inflammation driven by innate immune activation has been implicated in hypertension, myocardial infarction, and renal dysfunction. Early stages of hypertension are characterized by excessive production of proinflammatory cytokines, whereas sustained innate immune activation may exacerbate disease progression by promoting sodium retention and fibrotic remodeling in the kidneys [176]. By promoting microbial communities that support immune regulation rather than immune activation, nut consumption may help shift the balance toward an anti-inflammatory immune phenotype, thereby mitigating immune-mediated cardiometabolic risk and improving cardiovascular health.

Limitations, Research Gaps, and Future Directions

Despite growing evidence that supports the cardiometabolic benefits of nut consumption, the current literature has some limitations, including relatively small sample sizes, short intervention periods, variability in nut processing/nuts tested, and substantial variation in study design and comparator foods. Additionally, only a subset of clinical trials has directly assessed gut microbiota or microbial metabolites, limiting conclusions about their role in mediating CMD effects. Furthermore, most studies have focused on CMD risk factors rather than clinical CMD outcomes, whereas methodological heterogeneity among microbiome studies limits comparisons across trials. Hence, although nut consumption may improve several CMD risk factors, specific microbiome-mediated mechanisms remain largely associative and require further research to establish causation. New methodologies, including mediation analyses, larger, longer-term trials, shotgun metagenomics, metabolomics, and integration of CMD endpoints using a multiomics approach, offer opportunities to advance the field. Future studies should also consider interindividual variability in microbiome composition and metabolic responses, which may influence the CMD effects of nut consumption and provide a basis for personalized nutrition strategies. Large human studies have demonstrated considerable interindividual variability in metabolic responses to identical foods. Zeevi et al. [191] showed that postprandial glycemic responses could be predicted by integrating dietary, clinical, lifestyle, and gut microbiota characteristics, whereas findings from the PREDICT study further demonstrated individual variation in postprandial glucose, triglyceride, and insulin responses and highlighted the contribution of distinct personal characteristics, including the gut microbiome [192]. Available evidence suggests that nut consumption may alter gut microbial composition and specific bacterial taxa, but the direction and magnitude of these changes vary considerably across studies [23]. In addition, people with different ages, health status, and microbiota compositions can respond differently to nut consumption and other dietary interventions. This interindividual diversity could partly explain the controversial results after nuts intake, which led to stratifying human gut microbiomes with the term “enterotypes” based on predominant taxa [193]. Some enterotypes that have been identified and may predict response to nuts are Prevotella-dominated (P-type), which is connected to diets high in carbohydrates, fiber, and non-Western subjects; Bacteroides-dominated (B-type), which is more frequently found in Western subjects and is linked to diets high in animal proteins and saturated fats; and Ruminococcus-dominated, which is prevalent in people following a diet high in resistant starch [[194], [195], [196], [197]]. This gradient of microbiome composition [198] could result in different metabolic phenotypes known as metabotypes, describing specific metabolic reactions of individuals to various foods, potentially leading to different health outcomes [199]. Therefore, identifying this compositional pattern might replace traditional one-size-fits-all approaches with microbiota-based diagnostics aimed at personalizing treatments through dietary recommendations and pharmacological interventions. When it comes to nut consumption, personalized nutrition using metabotyping seeks to maximize health benefits by personalizing dietary recommendations to individual’s specific characteristics. Although research in this field is still developing, more research has to be done to identify comprehensively how these individualized approaches will be effectively implemented [193,199].

In conclusion, nuts are nutrient-dense foods with well-known benefits for cardiometabolic health, primarily because of the high content of UFAs, fiber, polyphenols, vitamins, and minerals. Nut consumption has been associated with healthier lipid profiles, better glycemic control, and reduced inflammation; thus, nuts have the potential to be an essential part of dietary recommendations for preventing and managing metabolic diseases. Recent findings show that these benefits may be partially mediated through the gut microbiome. The food bioactives in nuts interact with the gut microorganisms to increase the production of beneficial metabolites, such as SCFAs, in amounts that influence metabolic and immune pathways. However, the causal contribution of these microbiome-mediated pathways to the cardiometabolic benefits of nuts remains to be established in humans. The complex multidirectional interaction between nut intake, gut microbial composition and function, and CMDs needs further investigation. Future studies should focus more on interindividual variability in microbiome composition and long-term clinical outcomes to translate mechanistic insight into clinically meaningful dietary recommendations and optimize the use of nuts as a functional food within precision nutrition approaches. Ultimately, harnessing the gut microbiota’s potential may introduce new insights for metabolic health improvement and reduce the worldwide burden of CMDs.
