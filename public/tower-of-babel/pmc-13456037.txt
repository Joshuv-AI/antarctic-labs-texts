MgFe-Layered Double Hydroxide-Reinforced Injectable GelMA Hydrogels Promote Calvarial Bone Regeneration with IL-10RA-JAK1-STAT3-Associated Osteoimmunomodulation
Authors: Jiajie Zheng, Kang Wang, Hao Xu, Huanbin Huang, Guangdong Hu, Jin Xing, Ji Li, Zhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritical-sized calvarial defects remain challenging because conventional grafting strategies often fail to conform to irregular defect geometries and insufficiently regulate the immune–osteogenic microenvironment. This study developed an injectable MgFe-layered double hydroxide (LDH)-reinforced gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel and compared it with MgAl-LDH to evaluate the influence of LDH cation composition on calvarial bone regeneration.MethodsMgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH nanoplatelets were synthesized and incorporated into photocrosslinkable GelMA hydrogels. Nanoparticle characterization, cellular uptake, cytocompatibility, macrophage responses, and bone marrow stromal cell osteogenic differentiation were evaluated in vitro. Regenerative efficacy was further assessed in a murine critical-sized calvarial defect model for 12 weeks using micro-computed tomography, histology, immunofluorescence staining, qRT-PCR, and transcriptomic analysis.ResultsBoth LDH formulations showed comparable nanoplatelet morphology, positive surface charge, efficient cellular internalization, and favorable cytocompatibility. Compared with LPS-stimulated macrophages, MgFe-LDH reduced TNF-α and IL-1β expression by 55.7% and 57.8%, respectively, while increasing IL-10 and TGF-β expression by 4.2-fold and 3.9-fold. MgFe-LDH also enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation, increasing ALP activity, mineralized matrix deposition, RUNX2 expression, and OCN expression by 2.3-fold, 1.7-fold, 2.4-fold, and 1.8-fold, respectively. In vivo, GelMA–MgFe-LDH produced the strongest defect bridging and mineralized tissue formation, increasing BV/TV and BMD by 1.5-fold and 1.6-fold compared with GelMA alone. Transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses suggested that IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-associated signaling may participate in MgFe-LDH-mediated osteoimmune remodeling and bone repair.ConclusionMgFe-LDH-reinforced injectable GelMA hydrogels promote calvarial bone regeneration and are associated with pro-resolving immune responses and osteogenic remodeling. These findings support cation-engineered LDH hydrogels as promising injectable biomaterials, although pathway inhibition, protein-level validation, ion-release profiling, and long-term biosafety studies are needed to clarify the proposed mechanism.

Introduction

Critical-sized calvarial (cranial) defects caused by trauma, tumor resection, infection, or congenital malformation remain difficult to repair because they generally do not heal spontaneously and often present with irregular geometries, disrupted vascularization, and a destabilized regenerative microenvironment.1,2 Conventional reconstructive strategies, including autologous bone grafts, allogeneic grafts, ceramic bone substitutes, and prefabricated scaffolds, have been widely used to restore craniofacial bone defects. However, these approaches still face important limitations. Autologous grafting is constrained by limited donor availability, donor-site morbidity, and prolonged surgical time, whereas allogeneic grafts may involve immune rejection, disease-transmission risk, and reduced osteogenic activity after processing.1,3 Ceramic substitutes and preformed porous scaffolds provide structural support but often show poor conformability to patient-specific or irregular defects, limited integration with surrounding tissues, and insufficient capacity to actively regulate the immune–osteogenic microenvironment.2,3 Therefore, there remains a strong need for regenerative matrices that can adapt to complex defect shapes while providing biologically instructive cues for coordinated bone repair.2,4

Injectable, in situ–forming hydrogels have emerged as an attractive alternative strategy for craniofacial reconstruction because they can be delivered in a minimally invasive manner, conformally fill irregular defect spaces, and form extracellular matrix (ECM)-like networks within the defect site.3,4 In particular, photocrosslinkable gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels combine cell-adhesive motifs, tunable gelation, and high water content, making them suitable matrices for cell–material interactions and localized delivery of bioactive components.4,5 Nevertheless, pristine GelMA hydrogels are usually mechanically soft and biologically insufficient for durable cranial ossification, highlighting the need to integrate bioactive nanofillers that can reinforce the hydrogel network and modulate the local immune–osteogenic response.3–5

Intramembranous cranial repair is not a purely osteogenic process, but a tightly coupled immunological and osteogenic program.6 Early after injury or implantation, innate immune cells—particularly macrophages—regulate the transition from inflammatory clearance to constructive remodeling.7,8 Biomaterials that bias macrophage programs toward a pro-resolving phenotype can reshape downstream cytokine and growth-factor profiles, thereby promoting osteoprogenitor differentiation and matrix deposition.9 Recent work further demonstrates that engineering implant interfaces to be simultaneously immunomodulatory and osteoinductive can markedly improve bone repair outcomes.7

Hydrogels provide a uniquely modular platform for such orchestration because their network chemistry, viscoelasticity, degradability, and cargo interactions can be engineered to match the evolving defect milieu.10 Recent advances in mechanically responsive and electroactive hydrogel microspheres have further demonstrated that hydrogel networks can translate endogenous or external physical cues into bioactive signals for musculoskeletal repair, including osteoarthritis pain relief, cartilage protection, macrophage immunomodulation, and chondrogenic regeneration.11–13 Meanwhile, nanomaterial-integrated injectable hydrogels have emerged as a particularly promising strategy for craniofacial bone reconstruction because nanofillers can reinforce soft matrices, modulate immune responses (notably macrophage polarization), and directly enhance osteogenic behaviors.3

Among candidate nanofillers, layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are compelling because their two-dimensional architecture, positive surface charge, exchangeable interlayer anions, and compositionally tunable metal nodes enable programmable bioactivity and controllable interactions with proteins, cells, and the local tissue microenvironment. Recent reviews have emphasized that LDHs are attractive regenerative nanomaterials because their metal-cation composition, anion chemistry, particle size, surface modification, and ion-release behavior can be engineered to regulate drug/cue delivery, cellular uptake, immune responses, and tissue repair.14,15 In bone-regenerative applications, LDH-based systems have been shown to enhance osteoimmunomodulatory and osteogenic signaling: LDH-modified bone cement promoted osseointegration through multiple osteogenic pathways, whereas MgFe-LDH nanosheets incorporated into thermo-responsive hydrogels enabled controllable growth-factor release and enhanced bone regeneration.16,17 These findings indicate that LDHs are not merely passive inorganic fillers but compositionally tunable bioactive nanoplatforms for skeletal repair.

Importantly, LDH cation composition may critically influence biological performance. In the brucite-like layers of LDHs, divalent and trivalent metal cations jointly determine layer charge density, surface chemistry, interlayer interactions, dissolution behavior, and ion-presentation profiles, all of which may affect protein adsorption, cellular uptake, macrophage responses, and osteogenic differentiation.14,15 Mg-containing LDHs are particularly attractive for skeletal repair because magnesium is involved in osteogenic differentiation, matrix mineralization, and tissue remodeling. However, the role of the trivalent cation within Mg-based LDHs remains less clearly defined in osteoimmunomodulatory bone regeneration. In this study, MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH were selected as two compositionally comparable Mg-based LDH systems that share the same divalent Mg node but differ in the trivalent metal cation. Fe-containing LDHs have been explored in bone-regenerative systems, including MgFe-LDH nanosheet-incorporated hydrogels, suggesting that Fe-containing LDH compositions may be biologically useful when maintained within a biocompatible range. However, in the present study, MgAl-LDH was used as a structurally comparable Mg-based LDH reference with a different trivalent cation, rather than as a negative control for Fe-specific activity. Therefore, the direct comparison between MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH was designed to examine whether trivalent-cation identity within Mg-based LDHs is associated with different macrophage-related and osteogenic outcomes. This comparison should not be interpreted as definitive proof that Fe alone accounts for the observed biological differences, because ion-release kinetics, dissolution behavior, surface chemistry, redox-related activity, protein-corona formation, and cell–material interactions may also contribute to the final regenerative outcome.

In parallel, recent osteoimmunology studies have highlighted IL-10-associated macrophage signaling as a potential molecular bridge between inflammatory resolution and bone formation. A recent study showed that a biodegradable magnesium alloy promoted M2-like macrophage polarization and IL-10 secretion, thereby enhancing osteogenesis through JAK1–STAT3-associated signaling; importantly, IL-10 neutralization or STAT3 inhibition attenuated this macrophage-mediated osteogenic effect.18 These findings support the hypothesis that Mg-based biomaterials may couple macrophage immunomodulation to osteoprogenitor activation through IL-10/JAK/STAT3-related pathways. However, for injectable LDH-based nanocomposite hydrogels, clinical translation still requires careful consideration of biodegradation, ion-release kinetics, long-term safety, sterilization, batch-to-batch consistency, injectability, gelation window, and handling compatibility with irregular craniofacial defects.15 Together, these advances motivate the development of Mg-based LDH-reinforced injectable GelMA hydrogels and support a direct comparison between MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH to clarify how LDH cation composition influences osteoimmune remodeling and calvarial bone regeneration.

Here, we prepare an injectable gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel reinforced with Mg-based LDH nanoplatelets as a regenerative matrix for critical-sized calvarial defects. By directly comparing MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH, we aim to determine how LDH cation composition is associated with macrophage-state changes and osteogenic responses. We demonstrate that MgFe-LDH more effectively attenuates M1-like macrophage-associated inflammation, enhances M2-linked reparative polarization, and promotes BMSC osteogenic differentiation. In a murine critical-sized calvarial defect model, GelMA–LDH composites enhance bone regeneration, with MgFe-LDH outperforming MgAl-LDH. Finally, transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses suggest that IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-associated signaling is associated with the regenerative microenvironment induced by GelMA–MgFe-LDH, although direct functional validation is required to determine whether macrophage modulation mediates osteogenic enhancement.

Experimental Section

Materials

Magnesium nitrate hexahydrate was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich/Merck, USA. Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate, aluminum nitrate nonahydrate, sodium hydroxide, and cetylpyridinium chloride were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology, China. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) and lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) were supplied by EFL-Tech, China. Lipopolysaccharide from Escherichia coli O111:B4, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), paraformaldehyde, Triton X-100, ascorbic acid, β-glycerophosphate, dexamethasone, and Alizarin Red S were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich/Merck, USA. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining reagents, DAPI, and calcein-AM/propidium iodide live/dead staining reagents were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology, China. APC Annexin V/7-AAD apoptosis detection reagents were obtained from BioLegend, USA. ELISA kits for mouse TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β1 were supplied by Servicebio, China. Primary antibodies against CD86, CD206, TNF-α, IL-10, osteocalcin (OCN), and RUNX2 were purchased from Proteintech Group, USA/China. Fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were obtained from Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA.

Sterilization and Aseptic Handling

All solutions and materials used for cell culture and animal implantation were prepared under aseptic conditions. LDH stock suspensions were spread as thin layers in sterile culture dishes and exposed to UV-C light (254 nm) for 30 min under aseptic conditions, followed by redispersion in sterile PBS. GelMA and LAP were dissolved in sterile PBS under light-protected conditions and handled in a biosafety cabinet. Autoclavable surgical instruments were steam-sterilized at 121 °C for 30 min before surgery. Non-autoclavable hydrogel molds and auxiliary materials were rinsed with 75% ethanol, air-dried in a biosafety cabinet, and further exposed to UV-C light for 30 min before use. For in vitro experiments, sterilized LDH suspensions were briefly sonicated under aseptic conditions immediately before use to minimize aggregation.

Synthesis of MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH Nanoplatelets

MgFe-LDH nanoplatelets were synthesized through alkaline coprecipitation followed by hydrothermal aging, based on previously reported LDH preparation strategies for biomedical and bone-regenerative applications.15 Briefly, Mg(NO3)2·6H2O (1.538 g, 6.0 mmol) and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.606 g, 1.5 mmol) were dissolved in ultrapure water (20 mL) under magnetic stirring until a clear metal precursor solution was obtained. A NaOH aqueous phase (0.5 M, 40 mL) was preheated to 60 °C under vigorous stirring. The metal precursor solution was then added dropwise into the NaOH solution at approximately 1 mL min−1 using a dropping funnel, while the reaction pH was maintained at 10.0–10.5 by adding 1.0 M NaOH when necessary. After complete addition, the suspension was stirred at 60 °C for another 30 min. The resulting suspension was sealed in a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and aged hydrothermally at 100 °C for 16 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min and washed with ultrapure water at least three times until the supernatant was nearly neutral. The purified product was finally redispersed in water to obtain a homogeneous MgFe-LDH suspension.

MgAl-LDH was prepared using the same procedure, except that Fe(NO3)3·9H2O was replaced with an equimolar amount of Al(NO3)3·9H2O (0.563 g, 1.5 mmol). All other reaction conditions, including precursor concentration, dropwise addition rate, alkaline precipitation pH, hydrothermal treatment, centrifugation, purification, and storage, were maintained identical to those used for MgFe-LDH to ensure compositional comparability. The obtained LDH suspensions were stored at 4 °C and protected from contamination before use. Before biological experiments, LDH dispersions were sterilized as described above and briefly sonicated to obtain homogeneous suspensions.

Structural and Colloidal Characterization of LDH Nanoparticles

The morphology, lateral dimensions, and lattice features of LDH nanoplatelets were examined by transmission electron microscopy and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy using a JEM-F200 field-emission transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). Samples were prepared by depositing diluted LDH dispersions onto carbon-coated copper grids, followed by drying under ambient conditions.

Surface elemental composition and chemical states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy using an AXIS Supra+ spectrometer (Kratos Analytical/Shimadzu, UK/Japan). Crystalline phase and basal reflection patterns were acquired by powder X-ray diffraction on a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with Cu Kα radiation. XRD profiles were collected over a 2θ range of 5–80°. Hydrodynamic diameter and ζ-potential were measured at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Ultra analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Before DLS and ζ-potential analysis, LDH suspensions were diluted in ultrapure water and briefly sonicated to minimize secondary aggregation.

Cell Culture

RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology, China. C57BL/6 mouse bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) were purchased from OriCell/Cyagen Biosciences, China. RAW 264.7 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium, while BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. BMSCs between passages 3 and 6 were used for subsequent experiments.

FITC Labeling and Cellular Uptake of LDH Nanoparticles

To visualize cellular internalization, MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH were labeled with FITC. Briefly, LDH suspensions (5 mg in 5 mL PBS, 1 mg mL−1) were mixed with FITC solution (0.5 mg FITC dissolved in 0.5 mL DMSO) and incubated overnight at room temperature in the dark under gentle shaking. Unconjugated FITC was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min followed by repeated washing with PBS until no obvious fluorescence was detected in the supernatant. The fluorescently labeled nanoparticles were finally redispersed in sterile PBS, briefly sonicated, and protected from light before use.

RAW 264.7 cells or BMSCs were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes and cultured overnight. Cells were then treated with FITC-labeled LDH nanoparticles at 10 µg mL−1 for 12 h. After incubation, the cells were washed thoroughly with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained with fluorophore-conjugated phalloidin and DAPI to visualize the actin cytoskeleton and nuclei, respectively. Fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM 980 laser-scanning confocal microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

In vitro Cytocompatibility Assessment

The cytocompatibility of LDH nanoparticles was evaluated in both RAW 264.7 macrophages and BMSCs. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. MgFe-LDH or MgAl-LDH was then added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 µg mL−1. The in vitro working concentration of 10 μg mL−1 was selected based on previous LDH-based biomaterial studies19–21 and was further supported by the cytocompatibility and cellular uptake results in the present study. At this concentration, MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH enabled effective cell–nanoparticle interaction without detectable cytotoxicity under the tested conditions. The same concentration was therefore used for macrophage repolarization and BMSC osteogenic differentiation assays to maintain consistency across in vitro evaluations. Importantly, this in vitro concentration was used as a controlled screening dose for cellular response assays and was not intended to quantitatively reproduce the local in vivo exposure after hydrogel implantation. In vivo cellular exposure is expected to depend on hydrogel retention, swelling, degradation, nanoparticle distribution, and ion-release kinetics within the defect microenvironment, which were not directly quantified in the present study. After 72 h, CCK-8 reagent was introduced into each well, and the cells were incubated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Cell viability was calculated relative to untreated controls.

Live/dead staining was performed using calcein-AM and propidium iodide. After nanoparticle exposure, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with the staining solution in the dark. Viable and damaged cells were visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Apoptotic and necrotic populations were further quantified by APC Annexin V/7-AAD staining followed by flow cytometric analysis on an SH800S cell sorter/analyzer (Sony Biotechnology, Japan). Data were processed using FlowJo software.

Macrophage Repolarization Assay

To establish an inflammatory macrophage phenotype, RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS at 100 ng mL−1 for 24 h, as commonly used to induce an M1-like inflammatory response in RAW 264.7 macrophages.22 After induction, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing MgFe-LDH or MgAl-LDH at 10 µg mL−1, and the cells were cultured for an additional 48 h.

At the endpoint, cells were harvested for gene expression and flow cytometry, while culture supernatants were collected for cytokine quantification. The expression of M1-like macrophage-associated genes, including Tnf-α and Il-1β, and M2-associated genes, including Il-10 and Tgf-β, was measured by quantitative real-time PCR. For flow cytometry, cells were blocked and stained with antibodies against CD86 and CD206 to determine the proportion of inflammatory and reparative macrophage phenotypes. Secreted TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β in conditioned media were quantified by ELISA.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells or regenerated calvarial tissues using a FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China). RNA concentration and purity were determined by UV spectrophotometry. Reverse transcription was performed using Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). Quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green-based master mix on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with Gapdh used as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence Staining

For cell immunofluorescence, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% donkey serum. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies against OCN or RUNX2 at 4 °C overnight. After washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

For tissue immunofluorescence, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval when required, and blocked before incubation with the indicated primary antibodies (OCN, TNF-α, or IL-10). Fluorescence images were collected using a ZEISS LSM 980 confocal microscope. Quantitative fluorescence analysis was performed using ImageJ software with identical thresholding parameters across comparative groups.

ELISA Analysis

Conditioned media from macrophage cultures were centrifuged to remove cellular debris and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β were determined using mouse-specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard curves were generated for each assay, and cytokine levels were normalized to cell number where appropriate.

Osteogenic Differentiation of BMSCs

For osteogenic induction, BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well for ALP staining/activity and Alizarin Red S staining, on glass coverslips in 24-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well for immunofluorescence staining, and in 6-well plates at 1×105 cells per well for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis. After overnight attachment, cells were cultured until approximately 70–80% confluence and then switched to osteogenic induction medium consisting of α-MEM supplemented with 50 µM ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 100 nM dexamethasone, according to established osteogenic induction protocols for mesenchymal stromal/stem cells. MgFe-LDH or MgAl-LDH was added to the osteogenic medium at 10 µg mL−1. Medium was refreshed every 2 d.

Early osteogenic differentiation was assessed on day 7 by ALP staining and ALP activity measurement. For ALP staining, cells were fixed and incubated with chromogenic ALP substrate solution. For quantitative analysis, cell lysates were collected, and ALP activity was normalized to total protein content.

Late-stage mineralization was evaluated on day 14 by Alizarin Red S staining. After fixation, cells were stained with Alizarin Red S solution, rinsed to remove unbound dye, and imaged. Bound calcium-associated dye was dissolved using cetylpyridinium chloride and quantified by absorbance measurement. Osteogenesis-related genes, including Bmp2, Runx2, Alpl, Opn, Ocn, and Sp7, were analyzed by qRT-PCR. RUNX2 and OCN protein expression was further examined by immunofluorescence staining.

Fabrication of GelMA–LDH Composite Hydrogels

GelMA was dissolved in sterile PBS at 50 °C to obtain a 5% w/v precursor solution. MgFe-LDH or MgAl-LDH suspensions were sterilized and homogeneously incorporated into the GelMA precursor to reach a final LDH concentration of 1 mg mL−1. LAP photoinitiator was added at a final concentration of 0.25% w/v under light-protected and aseptic conditions. The mixtures were gently vortexed and briefly sonicated to ensure uniform nanoparticle distribution. For in vivo implantation, freshly prepared sterile precursor solution was maintained at 37 °C before injection to preserve injectability.

Hydrogels were formed by exposing the precursor solutions to 365 nm UV light at an intensity of approximately 10 mW cm−2 at the sample surface for 60s under sterile conditions.19 GelMA without LDH served as the hydrogel control. For microstructural observation, hydrogels were frozen, lyophilized, fractured, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged using an SU8010 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, Japan).

In vivo Calvarial Defect Model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for laboratory animal welfare and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Pudong Hospital (approval No. KCZX20251128001). Male C57BL/6J mice, 8 weeks old, were obtained from GemPharmatech, China. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water.

A total of 18 mice were used for the in vivo implantation experiment and randomly assigned to three groups: GelMA, GelMA–MgFe-LDH, and GelMA–MgAl-LDH, with 6 mice per group. Randomization was performed using a random-number table before surgery. The sample size was determined based on previous calvarial defect biomaterial studies and the principle of using the minimum number of animals required to obtain interpretable micro-CT, histological, immunofluorescence, and molecular outcomes. No formal a priori power calculation was performed. Each mouse was considered an independent biological replicate and experimental unit.

Inclusion criteria were defined before surgery and included healthy male C57BL/6J mice of the specified age with normal general condition, no visible cranial injury, and no signs of infection or abnormal behavior before implantation. Predefined exclusion criteria included abnormal health status before surgery, accidental injury to the dura mater, uncontrolled intraoperative bleeding, incomplete or irregular defect formation, severe postoperative wound infection, death before the planned endpoint, or sample-processing failure that prevented reliable micro-CT, histological, immunofluorescence, or molecular analysis. Any exclusion, if required, was to be performed before unblinding and reported transparently. Micro-CT, histological, immunofluorescence, and molecular analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocation.

Before surgery, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation using 3–4% isoflurane for induction and 1.5–2.0% isoflurane for maintenance in oxygen. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal reflex before surgery. The cranial surgical region was shaved and disinfected, and the animals were placed on a sterile surgical field. A midline scalp incision was made to expose the parietal bone. A full-thickness circular calvarial defect with a diameter of 5 mm was generated using a trephine drill under continuous sterile saline irrigation, as previously described for critical-sized calvarial defect models used to evaluate bone-regenerative biomaterials.23 Care was taken to avoid injury to the dura mater. Each defect was filled with 20 μL of the corresponding sterile precursor solution, followed by in situ photocrosslinking under the same conditions described above to form GelMA, GelMA–MgFe-LDH, or GelMA–MgAl-LDH hydrogels. The periosteum and skin were closed in layers using sterile sutures.

After surgery, mice were kept on a warming pad until full recovery from anesthesia and were monitored daily for general condition, wound healing, body weight, food and water intake, mobility, and signs of pain or distress. Postoperative analgesia was provided with meloxicam at 5 mg kg−1 by subcutaneous injection once daily for 3 consecutive days. Humane endpoints included severe weight loss, persistent wound infection, impaired mobility, severe distress, or failure to access food or water.

At 12 weeks after implantation, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation using a gradual-fill method at a displacement rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method to ensure death. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, heartbeat, and reflexes before tissue collection. Calvarial samples were then harvested for micro-CT, histological, immunofluorescence, and molecular analyses.

Micro-Computed Tomography

Regenerated calvarial tissues were scanned using a vivaCT 80 micro-CT system (Scanco Medical, Switzerland). Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed to visualize newly formed mineralized tissue within the defect region. A cylindrical region of interest corresponding to the original defect size was selected for quantitative analysis. Bone volume fraction and bone mineral density were calculated using the manufacturer’s analysis software under identical threshold settings for all samples, consistent with established micro-CT-based quantification methods for calvarial bone regeneration.24

Histological and Tissue Immunofluorescence Analyses

After micro-CT imaging, calvarial specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified in 10% EDTA solution at pH 7.4. Decalcified tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at approximately 6 µm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was used to assess tissue continuity, defect bridging, and cellular organization. Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to evaluate collagen deposition and osteoid/bone matrix formation.

For tissue immunofluorescence, sections were stained with antibodies against TNF-α, IL-10, and OCN. Fluorescence signals were imaged by confocal microscopy and quantified using ImageJ. Identical acquisition parameters were maintained for comparative analysis among groups.

Image Acquisition and Processing

All microscopy, histological, immunofluorescence, SEM, TEM, and micro-CT images were acquired using the same imaging parameters within each experiment whenever quantitative comparison was performed. For fluorescence imaging, laser intensity, detector gain, exposure time, pinhole size, and imaging depth were kept constant among comparative groups. For histological and SEM imaging, magnification and acquisition settings were kept consistent within each figure panel. Image quantification was performed using ImageJ software with identical region-of-interest selection rules and thresholding parameters across comparative groups. Only global adjustments of brightness or contrast were applied for representative image presentation, and no selective enhancement, deletion, duplication, or nonlinear manipulation was performed. Scale bars were embedded in all microscopy and histological images, and the corresponding scale bar lengths are stated in the figure legends.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from regenerated calvarial defect tissues. RNA integrity and quality were assessed before library construction. RNA-seq libraries were prepared and sequenced by Novogene, China, using an Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome, and gene expression levels were quantified using a standard transcriptomic analysis pipeline.

Differentially expressed genes were identified based on normalized expression values and statistical significance thresholds. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using KEGG pathway analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and pathway enrichment was interpreted using established enrichment-analysis frameworks.25,26 Protein–protein interaction network analysis was conducted using the STRING database to identify hub genes associated with LDH-mediated osteoimmunomodulation.27 Key genes in the IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3 signaling axis were further validated by qRT-PCR.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. In vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times using separate cell preparations or independently prepared samples, and these were considered biological replicates. For plate-based assays, technical wells were averaged before statistical comparison when applicable. For imaging-based assays, representative images were selected from at least three independent biological replicates, and quantitative analysis was performed using identical acquisition and thresholding parameters across comparative groups. For in vivo experiments, each mouse was considered one independent biological replicate and experimental unit.

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA). Data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed before parametric testing. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and equality of variance was assessed using Brown–Forsythe or Levene’s test where appropriate. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied when the assumptions for parametric analysis were satisfied. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The exact sample size or replicate type for each assay is specified in the corresponding figure legend. Significance levels are defined in each figure legend.

Results and Discussion

Physicochemical Features of Mg-Based LDH Nanoplatelets

To establish a compositionally tunable nanoplatform for osteoimmunomodulation, MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH nanoplatelets were synthesized under identical coprecipitation and hydrothermal conditions. This paired design was intended to minimize differences arising from synthetic procedure and overall LDH architecture, thereby allowing the biological effects associated with trivalent-cation identity, Fe3⁺ versus Al3⁺, to be examined within Mg-based LDH systems. LDHs are particularly attractive for regenerative biomaterials because their positively charged brucite-like layers, exchangeable interlayer anions, and adjustable metal-cation composition provide multiple handles for regulating interfacial interactions, ion-mediated signaling, protein adsorption, and cellular responses.28 Importantly, the Fe/Al comparison in this study should be understood as a cation-composition screening strategy rather than a definitive isolation of Fe-specific activity, because Fe substitution may simultaneously alter dissolution behavior, surface chemistry, redox-related properties, and cell–material interactions.

Transmission electron microscopy revealed that both MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH formed well-defined two-dimensional nanoplatelets with a typical hexagonal morphology and narrow dimensional dispersion (Figure 1A). High-resolution TEM further showed discernible lattice fringes in both formulations, confirming the crystalline ordering of the layered hydroxide (Figure 1B). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the expected elemental composition of each nanoparticle type (Figure 1C). MgFe-LDH displayed characteristic signals corresponding to Mg, Fe, O, and N, whereas MgAl-LDH showed Mg, Al, O, and N signatures. X-ray diffraction patterns further verified the successful formation of layered double hydroxide structures, as evidenced by the basal reflections assigned to the (003), (006), and (009) planes (Figure 1D). Colloidal characterization demonstrated that both LDHs carried a positive surface charge in aqueous dispersion, with ζ-potentials of approximately +23.4 mV for MgFe-LDH and +29.5 mV for MgAl-LDH (Figure 1E). Such positive surface potentials are favorable for interaction with negatively charged cell membranes and extracellular proteins, which may facilitate cellular association and internalization. Dynamic light scattering showed comparable hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 113 nm for MgFe-LDH and 109 nm for MgAl-LDH (Figure 1F). The close size distribution between the two nanoparticle types is important because LDH particle size has been reported to influence cellular uptake, stem cell behavior, and downstream fate commitment. Therefore, the nearly matched hydrodynamic profiles reduce the likelihood that nanoparticle size is the dominant factor underlying the different bioactivities observed later and support further discussion of LDH cation composition as one important contributor to the biological differences between MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH.

Collectively, the physicochemical analyses demonstrate that MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH possess comparable morphology, crystallinity, colloidal size, and positive surface charge, while differing in the identity of the trivalent cation. This provides a rational materials basis for investigating how Fe- versus Al-containing Mg-based LDHs regulate immune remodeling and osteogenic differentiation.

Confocal microscopy of FITC-labeled LDH nanoparticles revealed extensive intracellular fluorescence after 12 h of incubation (Figure 2B). The green fluorescence was distributed throughout the cytoplasm and was particularly enriched around the perinuclear region, suggesting efficient cellular uptake rather than simple membrane adsorption. The actin cytoskeleton remained well organized, and the nuclei retained normal morphology, further supporting the favorable compatibility of both nanoparticle formulations.

The ability of LDH nanoparticles to enter macrophages is biologically relevant because macrophages are highly responsive to physicochemical cues presented by biomaterials. After implantation, macrophages are among the earliest immune cells to interact with a material surface or released nanoscale components, thereby shaping the transition from acute inflammation to tissue repair. Recent studies have emphasized that macrophages are not merely inflammatory effector cells but central regulators of bone repair, capable of determining whether a defect progresses toward regeneration or fibrotic repair.9 Therefore, efficient uptake of LDH nanoplatelets may allow macrophages to sense Mg/Fe- or Mg/Al-derived ionic and surface cues, providing a cellular entry point for subsequent osteoimmune regulation.

To evaluate whether LDH nanoparticles could modulate an inflammatory immune state, RAW 264.7 macrophages were first stimulated with LPS to induce an M1-like phenotype and then treated with MgFe-LDH or MgAl-LDH. LPS priming markedly increased the expression of inflammatory mediators, whereas LDH treatment substantially attenuated this response. qRT-PCR analysis showed that both MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH reduced the expression of M1-associated genes, including Tnf-α and Il-1β, while enhancing the expression of reparative M2-associated genes, including Il-10 and Tgf-β (Figure 2C). Notably, MgFe-LDH induced a more pronounced increase in Il-10 and Tgf-β expression than MgAl-LDH, indicating a stronger capacity to drive macrophages toward an immunoresolving phenotype.

Flow cytometry provided further evidence for this phenotypic transition. LDH treatment decreased the proportion of CD86-positive macrophages and increased CD206 expression relative to LPS-stimulated controls (Figure 2D). These changes indicate that the nanoparticles did not simply suppress inflammatory activation but actively redirected macrophage polarization from an M1-like state toward an M2-like reparative program. Consistently, ELISA analysis of conditioned media showed reduced secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β and increased production of IL-10 and TGF-β after LDH treatment (Figure 2E). Among the two formulations, MgFe-LDH elicited the most robust cytokine shift, suggesting that the MgFe-LDH composition was associated with a stronger immunoregulatory response than MgAl-LDH under the tested conditions. However, this difference should not be attributed solely to Fe incorporation, because Fe/Al substitution may also alter dissolution kinetics, surface chemistry, redox-related behavior, protein adsorption, and macrophage–nanoparticle interactions.

This immunomodulatory behavior is highly relevant to cranial bone regeneration. Bone healing requires an early inflammatory response to initiate debris clearance and recruit reparative cells; however, prolonged inflammatory signaling can impair osteoprogenitor function and favor fibrotic tissue deposition. Recent immune profiling studies in musculoskeletal trauma have identified macrophages as decisive regulators of healing outcomes, with dysregulated macrophage activity contributing to fibrosis and defective regeneration.9 In parallel, immunomodulatory biomaterial strategies have shown that promoting a pro-resolving macrophage phenotype can substantially improve osteointegration and new bone formation.29 The present results therefore suggest that MgFe-LDH may create a more regenerative immune microenvironment by simultaneously limiting inflammatory cytokine production and amplifying IL-10/TGF-β-associated reparative signaling.

The stronger immunomodulatory response observed in the MgFe-LDH group than in the MgAl-LDH group may be related to the different trivalent-cation environment within the Mg-based LDH framework. However, this observation should be interpreted as an association between LDH composition and macrophage phenotype rather than direct evidence of Fe-specific immunoregulatory activity. In addition to possible Fe-associated effects, Fe/Al substitution may influence LDH dissolution kinetics, ion-presentation profiles, surface charge distribution, surface chemical states, protein adsorption, protein-corona formation, and macrophage–nanoparticle interactions. Because Mg/Fe/Al release kinetics, Fe availability, redox-related activity, detailed surface-chemistry changes, and protein-corona profiles were not independently quantified, the current data do not allow us to determine whether the enhanced immunomodulatory effect was driven by Fe ions themselves or by broader composition-dependent physicochemical changes. Rather, these findings indicate that trivalent-cation composition is an important design variable for tuning LDH-mediated macrophage responses and should be mechanistically dissected in future studies.

At the molecular level, LDH nanoparticles upregulated a panel of osteogenesis-associated genes, including Bmp2, Runx2, Alpl, Opn, Ocn, and Sp7 (Figure 4E). These genes represent distinct stages of osteogenic differentiation, ranging from early lineage specification to extracellular matrix maturation and mineral deposition. The broad upregulation of this gene set suggests that LDH nanoparticles enhance osteogenesis across multiple phases rather than affecting a single marker. Immunofluorescence staining for RUNX2 and OCN further confirmed increased osteogenic protein expression, with MgFe-LDH again showing the strongest effect (Figure 4F and G).

These results indicate that MgFe-LDH promoted early osteogenic commitment and late mineralized matrix formation more effectively than MgAl-LDH under the tested conditions. This difference may be associated with the Fe-containing LDH composition, but it should not be interpreted as proof that Fe alone directly drives the osteogenic response. Fe/Al substitution may influence not only potential Fe-related metabolic or redox cues, but also LDH dissolution behavior, ion-presentation kinetics, surface chemistry, protein adsorption, and cell–material interactions. Therefore, MgFe-LDH should be interpreted as a compositionally distinct Mg-based LDH formulation that showed stronger osteogenic bioactivity in the present assays. Additional Mg/Fe/Al ion-release profiling, Fe-specific controls, redox/ROS assays, protein-corona analysis, and pathway inhibition experiments will be required to define the precise physicochemical and biological basis of the enhanced osteogenic response.

Importantly, the osteogenic effects observed in BMSCs should be interpreted alongside, but not as a direct consequence of, the macrophage results. Bone regeneration is influenced by inflammatory cytokines, macrophage-derived mediators, and the temporal resolution of inflammation; therefore, the increased IL-10 and TGF-β production observed in MgFe-LDH-treated macrophages may be consistent with a more pro-regenerative paracrine environment. However, the present study evaluated macrophage responses and BMSC osteogenic differentiation in separate in vitro systems. Because macrophage–BMSC co-culture, macrophage-conditioned medium transfer, IL-10 neutralization, and pathway inhibition experiments were not performed, we cannot conclude that macrophage modulation directly mediated the enhanced BMSC osteogenesis. Instead, the current data indicate that MgFe-LDH is associated with both pro-resolving macrophage marker changes and enhanced osteogenic differentiation, and future functional studies are required to define their causal relationship.

GelMA-LDH Composite Hydrogels Promote Critical-Sized Calvarial Defect Regeneration in vivo

To translate the bioactivity of LDH nanoparticles into an implantable regenerative matrix, MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH were incorporated into photocrosslinkable GelMA hydrogels. GelMA was selected because it provides an extracellular matrix-mimetic environment with cell-adhesive motifs, tunable crosslinking, and injectability, making it suitable for irregular cranial defect filling. Hydrogels have increasingly been recognized as advanced therapeutic matrices because their hydrated polymer networks can be engineered to regulate mechanical support, cargo retention, cell–matrix interaction, and local tissue responses.10

Scanning electron microscopy of lyophilized hydrogels revealed interconnected porous microstructures in GelMA and GelMA–LDH groups (Figure 5A). The incorporation of LDH nanoplatelets did not collapse the porous architecture, suggesting that the composite hydrogels preserved a microenvironment favorable for nutrient diffusion, tissue ingrowth, and cellular infiltration. In vitro cytocompatibility assays further showed that GelMA and GelMA–LDH hydrogels supported the viability of both macrophages and BMSCs (Figure 5B), indicating that the hydrogel network and embedded LDH nanoparticles did not introduce overt cytotoxicity. Histological analysis of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain revealed well-preserved tissue architecture in animals receiving GelMA–LDH implants, with no apparent inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or organ-specific pathological alterations (Figure S1). A murine critical-sized calvarial defect model was then used to evaluate bone repair. This model is particularly stringent because cranial defects exhibit limited spontaneous regeneration and require coordinated immune regulation, osteoprogenitor recruitment, matrix deposition, and mineralization. Recent intravital imaging studies have also demonstrated that calvarial regeneration is not a simple replication of long-bone fracture healing; biomaterials designed for skull regeneration must do more than provide passive filling; they should actively remodel the local regenerative niche.30

At 12 weeks after implantation, micro-CT reconstruction showed limited mineralized tissue formation in the GelMA-only group, indicating that the hydrogel matrix alone was insufficient to fully restore the critical-sized defect (Figure 5C). In contrast, both GelMA–MgFe-LDH and GelMA–MgAl-LDH markedly increased new bone formation across the defect area. The regenerated tissue in LDH-containing groups showed improved defect bridging and a more continuous mineralized structure. Quantitative analysis further confirmed significantly increased BV/TV and BMD in the GelMA–LDH groups, with GelMA–MgFe-LDH producing the highest values (Figure 5D). These data indicate that LDH incorporation converts GelMA from a primarily permissive matrix into an instructive osteoimmunomodulatory scaffold.

Molecular analysis of regenerated tissues provided additional evidence of coordinated immune and osteogenic remodeling. qRT-PCR showed that GelMA–LDH treatment reduced the expression of inflammatory genes Tnf-α and Il-1β, while increasing the expression of Il-10 and Tgf-β (Figure 5E). Simultaneously, osteogenic genes including Runx2, Opn, Ocn, and Sp7 were upregulated in LDH-containing groups compared with GelMA alone (Figure 5F). This combined reduction in inflammatory transcripts and increase in osteogenic markers suggests that LDH-containing hydrogels promoted a transition from an inflammation-dominant defect microenvironment toward a regeneration-competent state.

Tissue-level immunofluorescence further supported this interpretation. GelMA–LDH-treated defects exhibited weaker TNF-α staining and stronger IL-10 and OCN signals compared with GelMA-only defects (Figure 6A and B), indicating that pro-resolving immune-associated signals and osteogenic matrix formation occurred concurrently within the regenerated defect tissues. Histological staining corroborated the micro-CT and immunofluorescence results. H&E staining showed that GelMA-only defects were largely occupied by fibrous tissue with limited bony bridging, whereas LDH-containing hydrogels promoted more extensive tissue continuity and defect filling (Figure 6C). The superior in vivo performance of GelMA–MgFe-LDH is consistent with its stronger in vitro immunomodulatory and osteogenic effects. Recent studies have highlighted that biomaterials capable of self-adaptation, immune regulation, and endogenous cell recruitment are advantageous for skull regeneration.6 Similarly, ion-containing biomaterials have been shown to guide osteoimmune responses by providing instructive biochemical signals to macrophages and osteoprogenitor cells.18 In this context, GelMA provides a conformal and ECM-like structural framework, whereas MgFe-LDH may provide composition-associated material cues that are correlated with macrophage marker changes and osteogenic maturation. However, the specific contribution of Fe-containing composition remains to be clarified because ion release, redox behavior, surface chemistry, and protein-corona formation were not directly assessed. Thus, the resulting composite hydrogel is better interpreted as a promising osteoimmunomodulatory matrix whose enhanced performance is associated with MgFe-LDH incorporation, rather than as a system with a fully defined Fe-specific mechanism. This concept is consistent with recent progress in adaptive hydrogel microsphere systems for degenerative musculoskeletal diseases, where mechanically or ultrasound-activated piezoelectric hydrogels convert physical stimulation into localized electrical cues to regulate cell fate, inflammatory remodeling, mitochondrial homeostasis, pain transmission, and cartilage repair.11–13,31

Transcriptomic Analysis Prioritizes IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-Associated Signaling as a Candidate Pathway During GelMA–MgFe-LDH-Mediated Bone Regeneration

Given the concurrent reduction of inflammatory readouts and enhancement of osteogenic readouts observed in vivo, transcriptomic analysis was performed as an exploratory approach to identify molecular programs associated with GelMA–MgFe-LDH-mediated regeneration. RNA-seq revealed a distinct transcriptional profile in regenerated tissues from the GelMA–MgFe-LDH group compared with GelMA controls (Figure 7A), indicating broader remodeling of the calvarial defect microenvironment. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that differentially expressed genes were associated with several tissue-repair-related pathways, including JAK–STAT signaling, NF-κB signaling, calcium signaling, integrin-mediated interactions, Hedgehog signaling, and Wnt signaling (Figure 7B). These enriched pathways suggest that GelMA–MgFe-LDH-mediated regeneration involves coordinated changes in inflammatory regulation, extracellular matrix interaction, osteogenic differentiation, and mineralization-related programs. Among these pathways, the JAK–STAT pathway was prioritized for further discussion because it was consistent with the increased IL-10 expression observed in macrophage-related assays and regenerated tissues, and because IL-10 receptor signaling is biologically linked to JAK1–STAT3-associated anti-inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, NF-κB, Wnt, Hedgehog, integrin, calcium signaling, and other enriched pathways may also contribute to the regenerative response and should not be considered secondary or irrelevant.

Gene set enrichment analysis further supported remodeling of the JAK–STAT pathway in the GelMA–MgFe-LDH group (Figure 7C). Protein–protein interaction network analysis identified Il10ra as a hub gene associated with the differentially expressed gene network (Figure 7D), and qRT-PCR validation showed increased expression of Il10ra, Jak1, and Stat3 in the GelMA–MgFe-LDH group (Figure 7E). These findings provide convergent transcript-level evidence that IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-associated signaling may participate in the regenerative microenvironment induced by GelMA–MgFe-LDH. Recent studies have linked IL-10/JAK/STAT3-related signaling to inflammation resolution, macrophage-associated tissue remodeling, and bone-regenerative responses.18,32 Therefore, the observed upregulation of Il10ra, Jak1, and Stat3 provides a plausible candidate pathway for interpreting the coexistence of increased IL-10 expression, reduced inflammatory readouts, and improved bone formation in the present study.

Importantly, the transcriptomic results do not establish IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3 signaling as the dominant or required mechanism of GelMA–MgFe-LDH-mediated bone regeneration. The present study did not include IL-10 neutralization, IL-10RA blockade, JAK/STAT pharmacological inhibition, gene-silencing or rescue experiments, macrophage–BMSC co-culture validation, or protein-level assessment of phosphorylated JAK1/STAT3. Therefore, IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3 should be regarded as a prioritized candidate pathway rather than a functionally validated driver. Future studies combining pathway inhibition, cytokine blockade, p-JAK1/p-STAT3 protein analysis, and macrophage–BMSC interaction models will be required to determine whether this pathway is necessary for the observed regenerative effects.

Moreover, the transcriptomic profile indicates that GelMA–MgFe-LDH-mediated regeneration is unlikely to be governed by a single pathway. The enrichment of NF-κB, Wnt, Hedgehog, integrin, calcium signaling, and other pathways suggests multi-pathway remodeling of inflammatory, matrix-related, and osteogenic programs. Accordingly, the superior regenerative performance of GelMA–MgFe-LDH should be interpreted as the integrated outcome of favorable cytocompatibility, nanoparticle–cell interactions, macrophage-associated immunomodulation, enhanced osteogenic differentiation, hydrogel-mediated defect filling, and coordinated tissue remodeling, rather than the consequence of one conclusively proven signaling axis.

Overall, the present findings support GelMA–MgFe-LDH as a promising injectable osteoimmunomodulatory hydrogel for critical-sized calvarial bone regeneration. Compared with GelMA–MgAl-LDH, GelMA–MgFe-LDH showed stronger associations with pro-resolving macrophage marker changes, enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and improved mineralized tissue formation in vivo. These results indicate that LDH cation composition is an important design parameter for developing regenerative nanocomposite hydrogels, but they do not establish that macrophage modulation is the direct upstream cause of enhanced osteogenesis. However, the current evidence should be interpreted as demonstrating biological efficacy and pathway association rather than complete mechanistic causality. In particular, the present study did not directly quantify Mg/Fe/Al ion-release kinetics, ROS/redox changes, hydrogel degradation-mediated exposure, macrophage-to-BMSC paracrine effects, or pathway-specific inhibition. Therefore, GelMA–MgFe-LDH should be regarded as a promising preclinical materials strategy rather than a fully mechanistically validated or translationally optimized platform at this stage.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although MgFe-LDH treatment was associated with reduced inflammatory markers, increased reparative cytokine expression, enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and improved calvarial bone regeneration, the present experimental design evaluated macrophage responses and BMSC osteogenesis mainly in separate in vitro systems. Therefore, the immune-to-osteogenesis sequence remains inferential, and the current data should not be interpreted as direct evidence that macrophage modulation caused the enhanced osteogenic differentiation. Future studies using macrophage–BMSC co-culture systems, macrophage-conditioned medium transfer, macrophage depletion or selective macrophage modulation, IL-10 neutralization, IL-10RA blockade, JAK/STAT pathway inhibition, rescue experiments, and protein-level validation of phosphorylated JAK1/STAT3 will be required to verify whether macrophage-derived IL-10 signaling directly mediates the osteogenic effects of MgFe-LDH and to determine whether the IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-associated transcriptomic signature is functionally necessary for bone regeneration.

Second, although the superior performance of MgFe-LDH over MgAl-LDH supports LDH cation composition as an important design parameter, the specific contribution of Fe remains inferential. The present Fe/Al comparison was designed to compare two Mg-based LDH formulations with different trivalent cations, but it does not isolate Fe-specific effects from other composition-dependent physicochemical changes. Differences in Mg/Fe/Al ion-release kinetics, dissolution behavior, surface charge distribution, surface chemical states, redox activity, protein adsorption, protein-corona formation, and cell–material interactions may all contribute to the observed biological differences. Because ion-release profiling, Fe-release quantification, Al-release quantification, intracellular ROS measurement, redox-related assays, detailed post-incubation surface analysis, long-term nanoparticle storage stability, and protein-corona analysis were not included in the present study, the physicochemical and redox-related basis underlying the enhanced bioactivity of MgFe-LDH requires further investigation. Accordingly, the enhanced performance of GelMA–MgFe-LDH should be interpreted as a composition-associated outcome rather than direct proof of an Fe-specific mechanism. Without ion-release profiling, redox/ROS assays, post-incubation surface characterization, and protein-corona analysis, the precise physicochemical basis underlying the difference between MgFe-LDH and MgAl-LDH remains to be defined.

Third, although the GelMA–LDH hydrogels showed favorable porous architecture, cytocompatibility, and preliminary in vivo biosafety under the present experimental conditions, hydrogel degradation behavior, swelling behavior, mechanical strength, rheological properties, injectability under clinically relevant handling conditions, sterilization tolerance, and long-term local or systemic safety were not systematically evaluated. These characteristics are particularly important for injectable hydrogels because degradation profile, mechanical integrity, gelation behavior, and release kinetics can strongly influence retention, tissue integration, and therapeutic durability in bone defects. Future studies should therefore include ion-release analysis, hydrogel degradation and swelling assays, compression and rheological testing, injectability/gelation-window assessment, nanoparticle stability evaluation, long-term biosafety assessment, and larger-scale animal studies. In addition, the in vitro LDH concentration used for cellular assays should not be considered a direct equivalent of the in vivo exposure level, because cells within the calvarial defect may experience time-dependent exposure governed by hydrogel degradation, nanoparticle retention, local fluid exchange, and ion-release kinetics. Future studies should therefore connect in vitro dose–response assays with in vivo release/exposure profiles to better define pharmacologically and biologically relevant concentration ranges.

Finally, further translational considerations remain to be addressed, including batch-to-batch reproducibility, scalable preparation of LDH nanoplatelets and GelMA–LDH precursors, storage stability of the injectable formulation, compatibility with clinical sterilization procedures, and performance in more clinically relevant irregular or load-influenced craniofacial defect settings. Addressing these mechanistic and translational issues will be necessary to clarify the broader applicability of GelMA–MgFe-LDH hydrogels and to support their further development toward clinically usable osteoimmunomodulatory biomaterials.

To further place the present findings in the context of recent biomaterial-assisted bone regeneration studies, representative related strategies are summarized in Table S2. Previous studies have demonstrated that nanomaterial-integrated injectable hydrogels are promising for craniofacial bone reconstruction because they can combine defect-shape adaptability with mechanical reinforcement, localized bioactive cue delivery, and immunoregulatory or osteogenic functions.3 Recent GelMA-based composite systems, including bioactive glass- or nanofiber-reinforced GelMA hydrogels, have shown enhanced BMSC osteogenesis, angiogenic marker expression, and calvarial bone regeneration.33 In parallel, LDH-based systems have been increasingly explored as ion-responsive or compositionally tunable nanoplatforms for osteogenesis and bone repair.34,35 Compared with these previous strategies, the present study focuses on direct cation engineering within Mg-based LDH nanoplatelets and demonstrates that MgFe-LDH outperforms MgAl-LDH in macrophage-associated immunomodulation, BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and critical-sized calvarial defect regeneration. This comparative design highlights LDH trivalent-cation composition as an important variable for designing injectable osteoimmunomodulatory hydrogels, while also emphasizing the need for future studies that separate Fe-specific effects from composition-dependent changes in dissolution, surface chemistry, protein adsorption, and biological microenvironmental interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that cation-engineered Mg-based LDH nanoplatelets can enhance the osteoimmunomodulatory and osteogenic performance of injectable GelMA hydrogels for critical-sized calvarial defect repair. Compared with MgAl-LDH, MgFe-LDH was associated with stronger biological responses, including attenuated inflammatory macrophage-associated markers, increased pro-resolving cytokine expression, enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and improved mineralized tissue formation in vivo. Transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses further suggest that IL-10RA–JAK1–STAT3-associated signaling may represent a putative pathway associated with GelMA–MgFe-LDH-mediated osteoimmune remodeling, but this pathway should not be considered a confirmed or required mechanism because pathway inhibition, IL-10/IL-10RA blockade, rescue experiments, and protein-level phosphorylation analyses were not performed. Importantly, the present study does not establish Fe as the exclusive determinant of the enhanced biological response. The superior performance of MgFe-LDH over MgAl-LDH may be associated with Fe-containing LDH composition, but alternative explanations, including Mg/Fe/Al ion-release kinetics, dissolution behavior, surface-chemistry evolution, ROS/redox activity, protein adsorption, protein-corona formation, and cell–material interactions, were not systematically excluded. Additional limitations include the absence of macrophage–BMSC co-culture or conditioned-medium experiments, hydrogel degradation assessment, mechanical/rheological characterization, nanoparticle stability evaluation, batch reproducibility assessment, and long-term biosafety and translational evaluation. Future studies integrating these mechanistic and translational analyses will be important to distinguish Fe-specific effects from broader composition-dependent physicochemical changes, define the causal relationship between macrophage modulation and osteogenic enhancement, and support the further development of GelMA–MgFe-LDH hydrogels for irregular craniofacial bone defect reconstruction.
