Linear methods for non-linear inverse problems
Geerten Koers ??, Botond Szabó ??, Aad van der Vaart ??

Abstract
We consider the recovery of an unknown function f f from a noisy observation of the solution u f u_{f} to a partial differential equation that can be written in the form ℒ ​ u f = c ⁡ ( f , u f ) \mathcal{L}u_{f}=c(f,u_{f}) , for a differential operator ℒ \mathcal{L} that is rich enough to recover f f from ℒ ​ u f \mathcal{L}u_{f} . Examples include the time-independent Schrödinger equation Δ ​ u f = 2 ​ u f ​ f \Delta u_{f}=2u_{f}f , the heat equation with absorption term ( ∂ t − Δ x / 2 ) u f = f u f (\partial_{t}-\Delta_{x}/2)u_{f}=fu_{f} , and the Darcy problem ∇ ⋅ ( f ∇ u f ) = h \nabla\cdot(f\nabla u_{f})=h . We transform this problem into the linear inverse problem of recovering ℒ ​ u f \mathcal{L}u_{f} under the Dirichlet boundary condition, and show that Bayesian methods with priors placed either on u f u_{f} or ℒ ​ u f \mathcal{L}u_{f} for this problem yield optimal recovery rates not only for u f u_{f} , but also for f f . We also derive frequentist coverage guarantees for the corresponding Bayesian credible sets. Adaptive priors are shown to yield adaptive contraction rates for f f , thus eliminating the need to know the smoothness of this function. The results are illustrated by numerical experiments on synthetic data sets.

Introduction

Consider the recovery of a function
f:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
on a known domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

from a noisy observation of the function
u_{f}
satisfying a partial differential equation (PDE) of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}u_{f}&=c(f,u_{f}),\qquad&&\text{ on }\mathcal{O},\\
u_{f}&=g,\qquad&&\text{ on }\Gamma\subseteq\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Here
\mathcal{L}
is a linear differential operator and
g:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is a known function
on a subset
\Gamma
of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
of
\mathcal{O}
.
The map
c
transforms the pair of functions
(f,u_{f})
into a function on
\mathcal{O}
.
In most of our examples this map takes the form of a pointwise transformation, such as

c(f,u_{f})(x)=\bar{c}\bigl(f(x),u_{f}(x)\bigr)
, for a map
\bar{c}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, but
c
may also act on
(f,u_{f})
as elements
of given function spaces (and hence also involve derivatives of
f
or
u_{f}
).
The idea of (
1.1
) is to isolate possible non-linearities in the PDE in the term
c(f,u_{f})
,
and to choose the linear transformation
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
rich enough to be able to recover the function
f
from
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
.
We make the latter precise by assuming that there is a (sufficiently regular) solution map
e
such that

f=e(\mathcal{L}u_{f}).

(1.2)

In several examples the map
e
takes a simple, pointwise form, which makes our
general method very easy to implement. More generally, the map
e
may act on
\mathcal{L}u_{f}

as an element of a function space, and practical implementation may involve a numerical
implementation of the recovery of
f
from
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
.

The general idea of the paper is to solve the linear inverse problem of recovering

\mathcal{L}u_{f}
from the observations by the Bayesian method, and next recover
f
by an implementation of
(
1.2
). We prove that the method can attain optimal results,
and confirm the feasibility of the approach also by numerical experiments. The two-step approach
has both computational and theoretical advantages.

The observational model consists of observing the function
u_{f}
plus Gaussian white noise, which we write informally
in the form

\displaystyle Y_{n}=u_{f}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\dot{\mathbb{W}}.

(1.3)

We consider two precise versions of this model. In the white noise model the observation is
the Gaussian process
\bigl(Y_{n}(h):h\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\bigr)
, given by

Y_{n}(h)=\langle h,u_{f}\rangle_{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\dot{\mathbb{W}}(h),

for
\dot{\mathbb{W}}=\bigl(\dot{\mathbb{W}}(h):h\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\bigr)
the mean zero Gaussian process with covariance function

\mathop{\rm cov}\nolimits\bigl(\dot{\mathbb{W}}(h_{1}),\dot{\mathbb{W}}(h_{2})\bigr)=\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{L_{2}}
.
Next to this continuous interpretation of (
1.3
),
we also consider the practically relevant discrete observational setting,
where
Y_{n}
is observed at
n
given design points
x_{n,1},\ldots,x_{n,n}\in\mathcal{O}
. In this case the observation
Y_{n}

is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with coordinates
Y_{n}(i)=u_{f}(x_{n,i})+W_{i}
, where
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
are
i.i.d. standard normal variables. In our general discussion we shall use (
1.3
) to refer to both models.

Examples of the structure (
1.1
)
include the time-independent Schrödinger equation, considered in
[
32
,
30
,
34
]
with
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
the Laplacian,
the heat equation with absorption term, considered in
[
23
,
28
]
,
with
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta_{x}/2+\partial_{t}
, the one-dimensional Darcy problem with
\mathcal{L}
the inverse Volterra operator,
and the general Darcy problem, considered in
[
18
]
, with
\mathcal{L}
equal to the Laplacian.
Details for these examples and additional examples are given in Sections
4
-
8
.
It is not clear that the approach works in general, but the list of examples
is encouraging.

A non-homogeneous boundary condition (
u_{f}=g
on
\Gamma
with
g
nonzero)
still renders the problem of recovering

\mathcal{L}u_{f}
non-linear. To remove also this non-linearity,
define
K
as the solution operator
u\mapsto Ku
of the
homogeneous partial differential equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}Ku&=u,\qquad&&\text{ on }\mathcal{O},\\
Ku&=0,\qquad&&\text{ on }\Gamma\subseteq\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

Thus the linear operator
K
is the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
under the Dirichlet boundary condition.
(For our purposes it suffices that
K
is defined and satisfies (
1.4
) on
the smaller set of functions
\{\mathcal{L}u:u=0\text{ on }\Gamma\}
.)
Furthermore, for the given boundary function
g:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
in (
1.1
),
let
\tilde{g}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function that satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}\tilde{g}&=0,\qquad&&\text{ on }\mathcal{O},\\
\tilde{g}&=g,\qquad&&\text{ on }\Gamma\subseteq\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

Solutions to the equations (
1.4
)-(
1.5
)
exist and are unique under mild regularity conditions on the operator
\mathcal{L}
,
domain
\mathcal{O}
, boundary set
\Gamma
and function
g
.
In particular, suppose that the solution to the problem to (
1.5
) with zero boundary condition
is unique
(i.e. the function
v=0
is the only solution to the problem
\mathcal{L}v=0
on
\mathcal{O}
and
v=0
on
\Gamma
).
Because the definitions (
1.4
) and (
1.5
)
imply that
\mathcal{L}(K\mathcal{L}u_{f}+\tilde{g})=\mathcal{L}K(\mathcal{L}u_{f})+0=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
on
\mathcal{O}
,
and
K\mathcal{L}u_{f}+\tilde{g}=0+g
on
\Gamma
,
it then follows that

u_{f}=K\mathcal{L}u_{f}+\tilde{g}.

(1.6)

Since the function
\tilde{g}
is known, this and (
1.3
) leads to the adapted observational model

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{n}:=Y_{n}-\tilde{g}=K(\mathcal{L}u_{f})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\dot{\mathbb{W}}.

(1.7)

Thus the problem of estimating
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
has been reduced to solving the linear inverse problem

\tilde{Y}_{n}=Kv+n^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbb{W}}
, defined by the operator
K
. The solution
v
to this problem
is next substituted as
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
in the inversion map (
1.2
) to find
f
. In the Bayesian approach,
the induced distribution of
f
when this map is applied to a posterior distribution of
v
is the usual posterior distribution
of
f
for the given prior. (We give a precise statement in Section
2
.)

It is natural to put a prior distribution directly on the function
f
, which is
the basic parameter. Within the context of non-linear inverse problems this approach is taken
in
[
32
,
18
,
30
,
31
,
23
,
33
]
,
who put a Gaussian process prior on
f
or
\log f
or independent priors
on the coefficients of an orthonormal wavelet basis of the domain.
However, in our two-step approach, which uses the Bayesian method to recover
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
in the observational model (
1.7
),
it is easier to put a prior on
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
or
u_{f}
. Because
f
is determined
uniquely by
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
in view of (
1.2
), this induces a prior (and posterior) distribution on
f
.
Methods for computing the posterior distribution of
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
given a prior on this function,
as well as theoretical results on this posterior distribution
are available (e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
38
,
2
,
25
,
19
,
59
,
60
]
).
A main purpose of the present paper is to show
that natural priors on
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
can lead to accurate recovery also of the function
f
. Nothing
appears to be lost at the level of contraction rates of the posterior distribution, and
our numerical experiments also show satisfactory performance.

The posterior distribution is the conditional distribution given the data
Y_{n}
, as usual,
where the distribution of the data given the parameter is determined by (
1.3
).
We denote all posterior distributions by
\Pi_{n}(\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n})
, where the argument

\cdot
can be specialised to a set concerning
u_{f}
,
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
or
f
.

We obtain posterior contraction rates, as usual (
[
15
]
), in the non-Bayesian setup
where the observation is assumed to be generated according to the model
(
1.1
) and (
1.3
) with a fixed parameter
f_{0}
. For instance,
a contraction rate for
f
relative to a given norm
\|\cdot\|
is a sequence
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0

such that
\Pi_{n}\bigl(f:\|f-f_{0}\|\geq M_{n}\epsilon_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\bigr)\rightarrow 0
in probability, for every

M_{n}\rightarrow\infty
.
This rate can be compared to an optimal contraction rate, which is determined,
in a minimax sense, by the smoothness of the
function
f_{0}
. The optimal contraction rate is typically obtainable by using a prior that matches the smoothness of the true parameters.
By mixing over priors of different smoothness levels (hierarchical Bayes) or using
a prior of data-determined smoothness level (empirical Bayes),
optimal contraction rates for a range of different smoothness levels are obtainable by a single prior.
An advantage of our approach is that such “adaptive” contraction rates for the linear inverse problem of estimating

\mathcal{L}u_{f}
carry over into adaptive rates for the recovery of
f
.
Adaptive priors for the linear problem have been
constructed by using a scale of priors of fixed smoothness simultaneously through empirical or hierarchical Bayes
methods (e.g.
[
25
,
19
,
51
,
45
,
3
]
).

Besides for estimating the parameter at a (nearly) minimax rate, a posterior distribution can be
used for uncertainty quantification. This can be justified, or not, from a non-Bayesian point of view by
the coverage of credible sets, i.e. the probability that a (central) set of given posterior probability
covers the true parameter
f_{0}
.
For infinite-dimensional credible sets that involve a bias-variance trade-off, such as balls and bands, this was
studied for linear inverse problems in
[
25
,
50
,
48
,
44
]
. Coverage is different
for priors of fixed smoothness and adaptive priors, and depends on properties of the true parameter
relative to the prior. Since our method is based on transforming the non-linear problem at hand into a linear one,
the positive results showing the existence of reasonable credible sets in the linear problem imply such
results for the non-linear problem of interest (see Section
2
).

The coverage of credible intervals for smooth functionals of the parameter
or of credible balls in a weak norm follow from a suitable version of
the Bernstein-von Mises theorem (
[
7
,
8
,
39
,
32
,
30
]
).
It is an open question whether such results are valid for the main construction in the present paper.

The approach of this paper is partly motivated by computational efficiency. The recent paper
[
35
]
derived
a Langevin type algorithm to compute the posterior mean, which provenly takes only polynomial time.
The algorithm is based on only the forward map
f\mapsto u_{f}
, which is an advantage for many problems.
However, repeatedly evaluating the forward map
may still be heavy for some practical applications in case of medium or large data sets.
A gain of our approach is that the linear problem, to compute
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
,
may be substantially faster to solve than the original non-linear one. If the inverse map (
1.2
) is easy
(as it is in the case of most of our examples), then this advantage is propagated to the
computation of
f
. A further gain is that our approach may combine well with distributed computation
based on partitioning the data in space. Since the
observation is a noisy version of the function
u_{f}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
, a (possibly overlapping) partition of
the domain
\mathcal{O}
, followed by separate reconstruction of
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
on the partitioning sets, is feasible.
Preliminary work shows that such a distributional approach may give theoretically optimal and practically competitive
results compared to other computational shortcuts. In particular, spatial partitioning allows priors and posteriors
to adapt to the smoothness of the true function (for the regression problem without inversion, see
[
49
]
).
In comparison, an approach based on partitioning the domain
of the function of
f
seems impossible, due to the structure of the partial differential equation. Another approach to speed up the computations is
the extended version of the inducing variable variational Bayes approach
[
53
]
.
By optimally choosing the number of inducing variables, this procedure recovers the functional parameter of interest
with the optimal minimax rate
[
37
]
and (in case of the direct regression model)
provides reliable uncertainty quantification
[
36
,
54
]
.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Section
2
we formalise the relation between the non-linear inverse problem of
interest and the auxiliary linear inverse problem. Next in Section
3
we review and extend Bayesian methods for
the linear inverse problem, describing prior choices, with both fixed and data driven choices of the tuning parameter,
and resulting results on contraction rates and frequentist coverage of credible sets.
In Sections
4
–
8

we apply our approach to five examples of PDEs. In Section
9
we present numerical simulations
that illustrate our approach in various settings.
We conclude with a discussion of the approach and open questions in Section
10
.
Sections
A
–
E
provided
technical complements.
Sections
F
and
G

presents posterior contraction rates in smoothness norms for the linear inverse problem in the Gaussian white noise,
and the discrete observation settings, respectively.

Notation

For two sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
we write

if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a_{n}/b_{n}\leq C
, for every
n
.
We write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold simultaneously.
The letters
c
and
C
denote constants not depending on
n
; their values might change from line to line.

A

is an open set
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which throughout the paper we shall take to be bounded and connected
with a Lipschitz boundary.

For
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
the notation
H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
is the Sobolev space
W^{\beta,2}(\mathcal{O})
of functions (or distributions)
f:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
of smoothness
\beta
.
For
\beta=0
this is
L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
, while for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
this consists of the functions
f:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}

with weak derivatives
D^{\alpha}f
up to order
|\alpha|\leq\beta
, normed by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})}:=\sqrt{\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq\beta}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}<\infty.

(1.8)

Sobolev spaces of non-integer smoothness
\beta\notin\mathbb{N}
can be constructed in various equivalent ways
(
[
14
,
55
,
11
]
). Sobolev spaces with negative smoothness are by definition
the dual spaces of the corresponding spaces of positive smoothness. All spaces refer to a given
domain
\mathcal{O}
, which may be dropped from the notation.

2
General method

In this section we present the skeleton of our approach, specialised to rates of contraction and uncertainty quantification.
In the next sections, we verify the generic assumptions of the present section for combinations of priors and PDE models.

We assume given a prior distribution on the function
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
, and consider the two posterior distributions
corresponding to the models (
1.7
) and (
1.3
):

(L)

the posterior
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}(v\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n})
of
v
in the observational model
\tilde{Y}_{n}=Kv+n^{-1/2}\,\dot{\mathbb{W}}
;

(N)

the posterior
\Pi_{n}(f\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n})
of
f
in the observational model
Y_{n}=u_{f}+n^{-1/2}\,\dot{\mathbb{W}}
.

The two models are linked through the identity
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
and equations (
1.2
) and (
1.6
).
Our approach is to construct the posterior distribution
of
v
from model (L) and data
\tilde{Y}_{n}
, and next form the posterior distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}\bigl(e(v)\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n}\bigr)

of
e(v)
induced by the solution operator
e
given in (
1.2
). The latter equation shows that this
is a posterior distribution of
f
in model (N) under the identification
\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}-\tilde{g}
.

To be more precise, a prior distribution on
v
that gives full probability to the set
\{\mathcal{L}u_{f}:f\in{\cal F}\}
, for some set
{\cal F}
, induces a prior on

f\in{\cal F}
through (
1.2
). The induced posterior distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}\bigl(e(v)\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n}\bigr)
of
e(v)
is the
ordinary posterior distribution
\Pi_{n}(f\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n})
of
f
under this prior
(see the proof of Proposition
2.1
for a precise statement and derivation of this correspondence).
In particular, and trivially, this applies to priors on
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
induced from a prior on
f
.

For flexibility we also allow prior distributions on
v
that are not restricted to the range of the map
f\mapsto\mathcal{L}u_{f}
,
requiring only that the inverse map
e
:

(a)

is defined on a set of full probability under the prior of
v
and

(b)

recovers
f
as in (
1.2
) when applied to a function
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
.

The posterior distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}\bigl(e(v)\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n}\bigr)

may then involve functions
f=e(v)
for which existence of the forward solution
u_{f}

to the partial differential equation (
1.1
) may be hard to verify, or does not exist.
In particular, PDE theory may ensure the existence of a solution
u_{f}
of (
1.1
) only under certain conditions on
f
.

For our purposes it is not important to verify such conditions; instead we can extend the definition of the forward map
f\mapsto u_{f}
as follows,
keeping the basic identities.
For a given function
v
in model (L), the function
u=Kv+\tilde{g}
is well defined and satisfies
v=\mathcal{L}u
on
\mathcal{O}
and
u=g
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
,
by (
1.4
)-(
1.5
). If
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
for a function
f
and solution
u_{f}
of (
1.1
),
then
u=u_{f}
by (
1.6
) and
f=e(\mathcal{L}u_{f})
by the definition (
1.2
) of the solution operator. In the other case, if
v
does not belong to the
range of the map
f\mapsto\mathcal{L}u_{f}
, then we
define

f=e(v)
and
u_{f}=u
and
again (
1.2
) and (
1.6
) hold, even if (
1.1
) may not.
(It does if
c\bigl(e(v),Kv+\tilde{g}\bigr)=v
on
\mathcal{O}
.)
An alternative to extending the definition of
u_{f}
in this way is to consider the posterior distribution

\tilde{\Pi}_{n}\bigl(e(v)\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n}\bigr)
as a
projection-posterior
for
f
in the sense of
[
10
]
,
but this disregards that it is an ordinary posterior distribution in most cases.

The observations
\tilde{Y}_{n}
and
Y_{n}
may be interpreted in terms of the white noise model, or be understood as
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for given
x_{n,1},\ldots,x_{n,n}\in\mathcal{O}
, equal to the sum of the
vectors
\bigl(Kv(x_{n,i}):i=1,\ldots,n\bigr)
and
\bigl(u_{f}(x_{n,i}):i=1,\ldots,n\bigr)
and a
standard normal vector, in the linear and non-linear model, respectively.

2.1
Posterior contraction rate

We assume that the posterior distribution of
v
is a Borel law on a normed space
(V,\|\cdot\|)
and
possesses a rate of contraction
\epsilon_{n}
to
v_{0}
in the sense that
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}(v:\|v-v_{0}\|\geq\epsilon_{n}M_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n})\ {\mathchoice{\raise-1.5pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{\scriptstyle P}}$}}{\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{\scriptstyle P}}$}}{}{}}\ 0
,
for any
M_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, under the assumption that
v_{0}
gives the true distribution of
\tilde{Y}_{n}=Kv_{0}+n^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbb{W}}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that the posterior distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{n}(v\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n})
of
v
in model (L) contracts under
v_{0}

to
v_{0}=\mathcal{L}u_{f_{0}}
at rate
\epsilon_{n}
in
(V,\|\cdot\|)

and satisfies
\Pi_{n}(v\in V_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n})\ {\mathchoice{\raise-1.5pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{\scriptstyle P}}$}}{\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{\scriptstyle P}}$}}{}{}}\ 1
for given sets
V_{n}\subset V
.
If (
1.2
) holds for a map
e
such that
e:V_{n}\to L_{2}
is Lipschitz at
v_{0}
,
then the posterior distribution of

f
in model (N) attains a rate of contraction
\epsilon_{n}
under
f_{0}
relative to the
L_{2}
-norm.

By assumption the prior distributions of
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
and
v
are the same, and in view of
(
1.7
) and (
1.6
), the conditional distributions of

Y_{n}-\tilde{g}=K\mathcal{L}u_{f}+n^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbb{W}}
given
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
and
\tilde{Y}_{n}=Kv+n^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbb{W}}
given
v
are the same as well,
under the identification
v=\mathcal{L}u_{f}
.
It follows that the conditional distributions of
\mathcal{L}u_{f}
given
Y_{n}-\tilde{g}
and of
v
given
\tilde{Y}_{n}

are the same. More precisely, if
(y,B)\mapsto L(y,B)
is a Markov kernel such that

v\mathchar 25194\relax\tilde{Y}_{n}\sim L(\tilde{Y}_{n},\cdot)
, then
\mathcal{L}u_{f}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\sim L(Y_{n}-\tilde{g},\cdot)
.

By (
1.2
), we have that
f-f_{0}=e(\mathcal{L}u_{f})-e(\mathcal{L}u_{f_{0}})
and hence

\Pi_{n}\bigl(f:\|f-f_{0}\|_{L_{2}}<\epsilon,\mathcal{L}u_{f}\in V_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\bigr)=\Pi_{n}\bigl(f:\|e(\mathcal{L}u_{f})-e(\mathcal{L}u_{f_{0}})\|_{L_{2}}<\epsilon,\mathcal{L}u_{f}\in V_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\bigr)
.
By the preceding paragraph this is the same as

L(Y_{n}-\tilde{g},B)
, for
B=\{v\in V_{n}:\|e(v)-e(v_{0})\|_{L_{2}}<\epsilon\}
. Since

3.3

The scale of Sobolev spaces

There are several bases that generate this scale,

conveniently indexed by a double index in the form

the base functions can be ordered in a sequence and the resulting scale takes the form as in

The Gaussian prior on this sequence constructed as in Section

\sum_{j}\sum_{k}v_{j,k}h_{j,k}

appears to be not often true for the full Sobolev scale, due to the boundary conditions connected
to the differential operator. For instance, the inverse of the Laplacian is smoothing (of order 2) relative
to the scale

, where

is the space of weak solutions of (

the subscript 0 arising from the Dirichlet boundary condition (see Proposition

We conjecture that an unrestricted wavelet basis would give the same results.

More abstractly, the operator

will be smoothing relative to the scale generated

, and we may construct a Gaussian prior with covariance operator equal to
the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
(see Proposition 8.19 in

). The equivalence of Hilbert scales

and Sobolev spaces has been investigated extensively,
see for instance Theorem 10.1 in
[

for elliptic differential operators

Discrete observations

The preceding results for the white noise model extend to the discrete observational scheme, in which

given by,
for i.i.d. standard normal variables

The key is an interpolation technique,
mapping discrete signals to the continuous domain, as employed in Chapter 8 of
[

and
[

Denote the empirical inner product by

and let

Assume that for every

, there exists an

-dimensional subspace

such that, for constants

, independent of

\displaystyle C_{1}\|v\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|v\|_{\mathbb{L}_{n}}\leq C_{2}\|v\|_{L_{2}},\qquad v\in\mathbb{L}_{n}.

(3.9)

Let

be the map such that
\mathcal{I}_{n}v
is
the unique element of

that interpolates

at the design points, i.e.
\mathcal{I}_{n}v(x_{n,i})=v(x_{n,i})
, for every

.
Then assume that for every

in some interval
(s_{d},S_{d})
, and some sequence

,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}_{n}v-v\|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\delta_{n,s}\|v\|_{G^{s}}.

(3.10)

Theorem 3.4
.

If (

)-(
3.10
)
hold with

and

and

continuous, then under
the conditions of Theorem

the assertions (i)-(ii) of the

The assertion on the contraction rate is an extension of results of
[
59
,
60
]
to smoothness norms,
which is proved in Section

.

The remainder of the proof is the same as the proof of Theorem

.

4
Schrödinger equation

For a bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a
given non-negative function
f\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
and a bounded, measurable function
g:\partial\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
,
let
u_{f}
be the solution to the time-independent
Schrödinger equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{f}&=fu_{f},\qquad&&\text{ on }\mathcal{O},\\
u_{f}&=g,\qquad&&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1)

The goal is to recover the unknown “potential”
f
using a noisy observation of
u_{f}
.

This model can serve as a prototypical, benchmark example for elliptic PDEs, with substantial interest in its own right.
Previous results in the literature placed a Gaussian prior distribution of fixed regularity on the (logarithm) of the functional parameter
f
.
In
[
34
]
minimax convergence rates were derived for
the MAP estimator corresponding to an optimally rescaled Gaussian process prior. Posterior contraction rates were derived for a uniform prior on the coefficients in a series expansion in
[
32
]
and for a rescaled Gaussian
process prior in
[
30
]
. Frequentist coverage guarantees for credible balls in weak Sobolev spaces
were deduced from uniform non-parametric Bernstein-von Mises results in
[
32
,
30
]
.

The solution
u_{f}
to the Schrödinger equation has
a probabilistic expression through the Feynman-Kac formula (see Theorem 4.7 in
[
61
]
):

\displaystyle u_{f}(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[g(X_{\tau})e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau}f(X_{s})\,ds}\right],\qquad x\in\mathcal{O}.

(4.2)

Here
(X_{s})_{s\geq 0}
denotes a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion with starting value
X_{0}=x\in\mathcal{O}
,
and
\tau
is its exit time from
\mathcal{O}
. It is known that
\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}^{x}\tau
is finite (c.f. Proposition 1.16 in
[
61
]
).
If
f
is bounded and
g
is bounded away from zero on
\partial\mathcal{O}
,
then Jensen’s inequality applied to (
4.2
) shows that

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}u_{f}(x)\geq\inf_{y\in\partial\mathcal{O}}g(y)\inf_{z\in\mathcal{O}}e^{-\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{z}\tau}>0.

This justifies an inversion formula of the form (
1.2
):

\displaystyle f=\frac{\Delta u_{f}}{2u_{f}}.

(4.3)

We conclude that to estimate
f
, it suffices to estimate
\Delta u_{f}
and
u_{f}
. In fact,
an estimator for
\Delta u_{f}
suffices, since
u_{f}
can be recovered without error from
\Delta u_{f}

and the known boundary condition
u_{f}=g
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

This motivates to take
\mathcal{L}
as in
(
1.1
) equal to the Laplacian
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
. Its inverse
K
as in (
1.4
)
is then the inverse Laplacian under the Dirichlet boundary condition, and
the function
\tilde{g}
in (
1.4
) is the solution to the Poisson equation with
boundary function
g
. We record the existence of this operator and function in the following lemma.
We assume that the boundary points of
\mathcal{O}
are
regular
in the sense of
[
17
]
, page 25; a sufficient
condition is that
\mathcal{O}
satisfies the exterior sphere condition: for every
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{O}
there exists a closed
ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
B\cap\bar{\mathcal{O}}=\{\xi\}
. This is true in particular for the domain
\mathcal{O}=(0,1)^{d}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
be the Laplacian.
There exists a compact, self-adjoint linear operator
K:L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
such that (
1.4
) holds.
If
\mathcal{O}
is regular, and
g:\partial\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, then there exists a function
\tilde{g}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
(
1.5
).
If
\partial\mathcal{O}
is
C^{m+2}
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, then
K:H^{m}(\mathcal{O})\to H^{m+2}(\mathcal{O})
is continuous.

Theorem 3 in Section 6.2 of
[
14
]
with
L
minus the Laplacian
together with the example following the theorem show that
there exists a unique (weak) solution
Ku\in H_{0}^{1}
to equations (
1.4
) with
\mathcal{L}
the Laplacian,
for every
u\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
.
The linearity of the map
u\mapsto Ku
is clear, while compactness and self-adjointness is noted in the proof of Theorem 1 on page 335 of
[
14
]
.
The continuity
K:H^{m}(\mathcal{O})\to H^{m+2}(\mathcal{O})
follows from Theorem 5 on page 323 of
[
14
]
.

By Theorem 2.14 in
[
17
]
, the Dirichlet problem on a bounded domain has a solution
for every continuous boundary function
g

if and only if the boundary points of the domain are regular. By the remark made below the stated theorem, a sufficient condition for this is that
the domain satisfies the exterior sphere condition.
∎

Thus we can proceed by the general approach outlined in the introduction with
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
the Laplacian.
Because
u_{f}=K\Delta u_{f}+\tilde{g}
, equation (
4.3
)
shows that the solution map (
1.2
) can be written as
f=e(\Delta u_{f})
for

\displaystyle e(v)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{v}{2(Kv+\tilde{g})},\quad&\text{if}\ \mathop{\rm essinf}Kv+\tilde{g}>0,\\
0,\quad&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.2
.

If
u_{f_{0}}=Kv_{0}+\tilde{g}
satisfies
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}u_{f_{0}}(x)\geq c_{0}
, for some
c_{0}>0
,
then the map
e:V\subset L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
, for
V=\bigl\{v:\|Kv-Kv_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{0}/2\bigr\}
,
is Lipschitz at every uniformly bounded
\bar{v}\in V
with Lipschitz constant bounded by a multiple of
1\vee\|\bar{v}\|_{\infty}
.

If
Kv_{0}+\tilde{g}\geq c_{0}
and
\|Kv-Kv_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{0}/2
, then
Kv+\tilde{g}\geq c_{0}/2
.
By the triangle inequality, uniformly in
v\in V
,

\bigl|e(v)-e(\bar{v})\bigr|=\Bigl|\frac{v/2}{Kv+\tilde{g}}-\frac{\bar{v}/2}{K\bar{v}+\tilde{g}}\Bigr|\leq\frac{|v-\bar{v}|}{c_{0}}+\frac{|Kv-K\bar{v}|\,\|\bar{v}\|_{\infty}}{c_{0}^{2}}.

Thus the
L_{2}
-norm of left-side is bounded above by a multiple of

\|v-\bar{v}\|_{L_{2}}+\|Kv-K\bar{v}\|_{L_{2}}\|\bar{v}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|v-\bar{v}\|_{L_{2}}(1\vee\|\bar{v}\|_{\infty})
,
by the continuity of
K
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
u_{f_{0}}=Kv_{0}+\tilde{g}
is bounded away from zero and
\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
If the posterior distribution of
Kv
based on the observation

is consistent for the uniform norm, then the posterior distribution of
f
given

contracts under

at the same rate as the posterior distribution of
v
given
\tilde{Y}_{n}
under
v_{0}
.
Furthermore, the credible sets
C_{n}(Y_{n})
given in (
2.1
) have diameter of the same order in probability under
f_{0}
as
the credible sets
\tilde{C}_{n}(\tilde{Y}_{n})
under
v_{0}
provided the latter sets are contained
in
\{v:\|Kv-Kv_{0}\|_{\infty}<c_{0}/2\}
, for
c_{0}=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}u_{f_{0}}(x)
, and contain
\bar{v}_{n}
with
\|\bar{v}_{n}\|_{\infty}=O_{P}(1)
.

We combine Lemma
4.2

with Proposition
2.1
to obtain the contraction rate, and
with Proposition
2.2
to obtain the coverage of credible sets.
∎

Thus we focus on priors for

in the problem

Theorems

,

It can be verified that the eigenfunctions of the inverse Laplacian on

\displaystyle h_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(i_{j}\pi x_{j}),\qquad(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(4.4)

with corresponding eigenvalues

\displaystyle\kappa_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}=\frac{1}{(\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2})\pi^{2}}.

One-dimensional Darcy equation

For given functions

\left\{\begin{aligned} f^{\prime}u_{f}^{\prime}+fu_{f}^{\prime\prime}&=h,\qquad&&\text{on }(0,1),\\
u_{f}&=g,\qquad&&\text{on }\{0,1\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Because the solutions

(and hence

The equation can be written in the form

Under the assumption that

We conclude that we can write equation (

(and

Let

The adjoint of

7
Darcy equation

For a bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an unknown function
f:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
and given functions
h:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\partial\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider
the solution
u_{f}:\bar{\mathcal{O}}\to\mathbb{R}
of the partial differential equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(f\nabla u_{f})&=h,\qquad&&\text{on }\mathcal{O},\\
u_{f}&=g,\qquad&&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

(7.1)

Here
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits v=\nabla\cdot v
denotes the divergence
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits v=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}v_{i}
of a vector field
v:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
\nabla u
is the gradient of a function
u:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
, so that

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(f\nabla u)=\nabla f\cdot\nabla u+f\Delta u
, with the dot on the right side the ordinary
inner product of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta
the Laplacian. For a sufficiently smooth domain and sufficiently smooth
functions
h
and
g
, a solution
u_{f}
is known to exist for every given positive
f\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
with
\beta>1+d/2

(see
[
33
]
, Proposition 6.1.5 or Chapter 8 in
[
17
]
).
Estimation of
f
in the white noise version of this model was investigated in
[
18
]
,
who obtained contraction rates for certain Gaussian process priors on the function
\Phi^{-1}(f)
for a given link function
\Phi
.

For
d=1
, this model reduces to the model considered in Section
6
, which
already revealed interesting features. The equation for
d>1
arises in many applied settings, but the challenges
are substantial. The function
f
can be recovered from
u_{f}
only under certain conditions, and this may require
that certain boundary values of
f
are pre-given, in addition to the boundary values on
u_{f}
in (
7.1
).
Moreover, if recovery is possible, then the solution map (
1.2
) is not explicit, but only
available as a numerical algorithm. Such difficulties can be avoided by taking multiple measurements
of the solution function
u_{f}
, for the same function
f
, but with different boundary functions
g
. This is
feasible in particular in an experimental setup, when the boundary function is under the control of the experimenter.
Not only do multiple measurements simplify the inverse problem, but also better
recovery rates may be expected. In this section we apply our general method with both a single and multiple measurements,
in both cases with our operator
\mathcal{L}
taken to be the Laplacian.

We start by a brief review of some aspects of the inversion map.
A standard approach is the method of
characteristics
(see e.g.
[
14
]
, p97–115),
the set of curves
x:[\tau_{0},\tau]\to\mathcal{O}
in the domain
\mathcal{O}
defined through the gradient flows

x^{\prime}(t)=\nabla u\bigl(x(t)\bigr)
, with varying initial values
x(\tau_{0})
.
For each characteristic, equation (
7.1
) gives, for
u=u_{f}
,

\frac{d}{dt}f\bigl(x(t)\bigr)+f\bigl(x(t)\bigr)\Delta u\bigl(x(t)\bigr)=h\bigl(x(t)\bigr).

This ordinary differential equation can be solved by integrating factors, to give

f\bigl(x(t)\bigr)=e^{-\int_{\tau_{0}}^{t}\Delta u\bigl(x(s)\bigr)\,ds}f\bigl(x(\tau_{0})\bigr)+\int_{\tau_{0}}^{t}h\bigl(x(s)\bigr)e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\Delta u\bigl(x(r)\bigr)\,dr}\,ds.

(7.2)

This equation completely specifies
f
on the characteristic
\{x(t):t\in[\tau_{0},\tau]\}
in terms of
\Delta u
and the starting value

f\bigl(x(\tau_{0})\bigr)
. (It is attractive that the formula uses only the Laplacian
\Delta u
, which is the starting point
of our reconstruction method, but the method also needs the gradient
\nabla u
to construct the characteristics.)
The method of characteristics is to cover the complete domain
\mathcal{O}
by characteristics, and thus obtain
an explicit solution to the inverse problem. This works best if the gradient
\nabla u
never vanishes,
which (given sufficient smoothness) would allow to extend every given characteristic (one can start one at every interior point of
\mathcal{O}
) to the boundary of
\mathcal{O}
.
The domain is then covered by a set of characteristics starting at some boundary point (said to belong to the
influx boundary
)
and exiting the domain at another boundary point. For a full reconstruction the function
f
then needs to be
initialised on (only) the influx boundary.

A nonvanishing gradient is considered desirable, but depends on the problem, in particular on the boundary function
g
.
If the gradient
\nabla u
does vanish at some interior point of the domain, then a characteristic essentially stops there.
It is shown in
[
41
]
, that the method of characteristics is then still applicable under the condition that the Laplacian
\Delta u
is nonzero at such a point
and has the same sign at all zeros of the gradient, e.g.
\Delta u+\|\nabla u\|>0
throughout
\mathcal{O}
.
In the latter case the domain
\mathcal{O}
can be covered by characteristics as before, between boundary points, and characteristics starting at zeros of
\nabla u
leading
to the boundary (with infinite time set), and
f
can be recovered by integrating over the characteristics, as before. Interestingly, at a zero of
\nabla u
, equation
(
7.1
) specifies that
0+f\Delta u=h
, and hence the initial value of
f
at such a point is given by the value of
h/\Delta u
, which is measured
and need not be externally specified. It may even be that all characteristics emanate from a point, or points, where the gradient vanishes (so that the influx boundary is empty)
and no pre-given values of
f
are necessary for its recovery.

The influx boundary are the points where the gradient
\nabla u
points into the interior of the domain
\mathcal{O}
.
As seen previously, the recovery of
f
requires pre-given values on the influx boundary, and then
f
is determined on
\mathcal{O}
and also at the
remaining boundary points. This is somewhat tricky, as it indicates that one can also specify too many boundary values,
and rule out every function
f
, the more so since the inflow boundary depends on
u_{f}
in general.

The paper
[
41
]
also discusses cases where there is no (unique) solution
f
to the inverse problem.
In practice, such situations may be avoided if the measurements can be taken for specific boundary functions
g
.
The inverse problem can be further simplified by taking measurements for multiple boundary functions.
If
u_{f,1},\ldots,u_{f,d}
satisfy (
7.1
) for
h=0
and boundary functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
, and the
same function
f
, then (with
[a_{1},\cdots a_{d}]
the matrix with columns
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
),

\nabla f\cdot\bigl[\nabla u_{f,1}\cdots\nabla u_{f,d}\bigr]+f\big[\Delta u_{f,1}\cdots\Delta u_{f,d}\bigr]=0.

If the boundary functions can be chosen such that
\det[\nabla u_{f,1}\cdots\nabla u_{f,d}]\not=0
throughout the domain
\mathcal{O}
, then
this gives the explicit inversion formula

\frac{\nabla f}{f}=-\big[\Delta u_{f,1}\cdots\Delta u_{f,d}\bigr]\bigl[\nabla u_{f,1}\cdots\nabla u_{f,d}\bigr]^{-1}.

(7.3)

For a convex domain
\mathcal{O}
, the function
f
(or rather
\log f
) can be recovered from this up to a multiplicative (or additive) constant
by integration along lines starting from a single point. The construction of appropriate
boundary functions

and other aspects of this approach are studied in
[
4
]
.

has the same boundary values.)

Another limitation of (

of (

).
This can be circumvented, in principle, by defining a solution map
e:L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
by

e(v)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{f\in{\cal F}}\Bigl(f\mapsto\|\Delta u_{f}-v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}:u_{f}\text{ solves \eqref{EqDarcy}}\Bigr).

(7.6)

Boundary conditions on

can be inserted through the domain
{\cal F}
of the minimization.
(We assume that the minimum is assumed, uniquely; otherwise, the argument can proceed with a
consistent choice of a near minimiser, within a negligible tolerance.) If

for

, whence (
1.2
) is satisfied for
f\in{\cal F}
.
Furthermore, by (

), provided
\exp(2\|u_{e(v)}\|_{\infty})/C(u_{e(v)})
is bounded,

\|e(v)-f_{0}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|\Delta u_{e(v)}-\Delta u_{f_{0}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}\leq 2\|v-\Delta u_{f_{0}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})},

in view of the triangle inequality and the fact that
\|v-\Delta u_{e(v)}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}\leq\|v-\Delta u_{f_{0}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}
by the
definition of
e(v)
.
If

and
\|u_{f_{0}}\|_{\infty}\leq d_{0}
, then consistency of the posterior distributions of

\Delta u_{f}
,
\nabla u_{f}
and
u_{f}
for the uniform norm, shows
that restricting

to functions such that

and
\|u_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 2d_{0}

has an asymptotically negligible effect on the induced posterior distribution of

.
Then the
L_{2}
-contraction rate of a posterior distribution of the Laplacian
v=\Delta u_{f}
is carried over
into the same
L_{2}
-contraction rate of the induced posterior of the reconstructed function
f=e(v)
.

For a concrete example for the domain
\mathcal{O}=(0,1)^{d}
,
we may use the prior on the eigen expansion of the Laplacian, as discussed in Section
4
.
Let
(G^{s})
be the smoothness scale generated by the eigenfunctions (

).
We assume that the boundary values of

are given and incorporated in such a way that

While definition (
7.6
) proves the feasibility of our approach, it is not practically
useful without an (efficient) algorithm to solve the minimisation problem. This is similar to solving
the well known
deterministic
inverse problem of computing
f
from
u_{f}
, but better conditioned
as our starting point is the Laplacian
\Delta u_{f}
instead of
u_{f}
. Given an estimate
v
for
\Delta u_{f}
, we can use
(
1.6
) to compute
u=Kv+\tilde{g}
and
\nabla u=\nabla Kv+\nabla\tilde{g}

as estimates of
u_{f}
and
\nabla u_{f}
and next
f
by the method of
characteristics (
7.2
). The first two steps are particularly
easy if
v
is given in terms of the eigenexpansion
v=\sum_{i}v_{i}h_{i}
of
\Delta
, in which case

\nabla u=\sum_{i}\kappa_{i}v_{i}\nabla h_{i}+\nabla\tilde{g}
.

A numerical procedure implementing the third step (
7.2
) is already provided in
[
42
]
,
exactly under the condition that
C(u)
is positive, but under the condition that the target function
f
is
C^{2}(\mathcal{O})
(see Theorem 2
in
[
42
]
; note that
f
in the latter paper is our

and
\alpha
is our

).
In the remainder of this section we adapt the algorithm to take the Laplacian
v=\Delta u
and gradient
w=\nabla u
as inputs, and
show that the inversion is stable if the function
f
is absolutely continuous (along discretised characteristics) and contained in
C^{1}(\mathcal{O})
.
(We have not investigated whether the latter condition is always reasonable, in particular in the case of a vanishing gradient, but
it cannot be much improved as the gradient
\nabla f
appears in the defining equation. In Section
D

the assumption is verified in the case
d=1
, even for a vanishing gradient, provided

is sufficiently smooth.)

We consider the case that the domain is the two-dimensional unit square
\mathcal{O}=[0,1]^{2}
. The construction can be extended to
less regular domains along the lines described in Section 4 of
[
42
]
. For a given
\delta>0
so that
1/\delta\in\mathbb{N}
,
consider the grid consisting of the points
x_{i,j}=(i\delta,j\delta)
, for
0\leq i,j\leq 1/\delta
. It is shown in
[
41
]
that for given
u\in C^{2}([0,1]^{2})
,
given values on the influx boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}_{1,u}
and smooth functions
g
and
h
, there exists a function
f
that is absolutely continuous
along the characteristics such that
u=u_{f}
, for
u_{f}
solving the Darcy equation (
7.1
) (and
f
is given by (
7.2
)).
We shall give a numerically efficient algorithm to construct numbers
\alpha_{i,j}
so that
\max_{i,j}|\alpha_{i,j}-f(x_{i,j})|\lesssim\delta^{\eta/2}
, for some
\eta>0
,
thus allowing the recovery of
f
at any precision. The complexity of the algorithm is linear in the number of grid points.

For a function
u:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, abbreviate
u(x_{i,j})
to
u_{i,j}
. We are seeking an approximation to the solution
f

to the discretised equations
(\mathcal{L}_{f}u)_{i,j}=g_{i,j}
, for
\mathcal{L}_{f}
the operator given by
\mathcal{L}_{f}u=\nabla f\cdot\nabla u+f\Delta u
.
To this end, consider the approximation
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{\delta}
, defined by

(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{\delta}u)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j},&\text{if }\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|<\sqrt{\delta},\\
\frac{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|}{\|z_{i,j}\|}(\alpha_{i,j}-\alpha_{k,l})+\alpha_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j},&\text{if }\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta},\end{cases}

(7.7)

where
(k,l)=(k_{i,j},l_{i,j})
are the coordinates of another grid point or a point in
\partial\mathcal{O}_{1,u}
that is linked to
x_{i,j}
, as defined below,
and
z_{i,j}=x_{i,j}-x_{k,l}
. The display mimics
(\mathcal{L}_{f}u)_{i,j}=\nabla f_{i,j}\cdot\nabla u_{i,j}+f_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j}
, where
f
is replaced by
\alpha
. In both cases in the
display the term
f_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j}
is simply copied into
\alpha_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j}
, but the term
\nabla f_{i,j}\cdot\nabla u_{i,j}
is approximated. In the first case,
when
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|<\sqrt{\delta}
, the latter term is approximated by 0, while the second case involves a vector
z_{i,j}
, which is chosen
in the direction of
\nabla u_{i,j}
, as follows. For
f\in C^{1}
the approximation
f(x_{i,j})-f(x_{i,j}-v)\approx\nabla f(x_{i,j})\cdot v
, valid for any small vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, gives
\nabla f_{i,j}\cdot\nabla u_{i,j}\approx\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|/\|z_{i,j}\|\bigl(f(x_{i,j})-f(x_{i,j}-z_{i,j})\bigr)
, for a small vector
z_{i,j}
in the direction of
\nabla u_{i,j}
. We arrive at the approximation in the display by choosing small
z_{i,j}
in the direction of
\nabla u_{i,j}
so that
x_{k,l}=x_{i,j}-z_{i,j}
is a point of the grid or a point of the influx boundary. An explicit choice is

z_{i,j}=\delta\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\frac{\nabla u_{i,j}}{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|}\Bigr],

(7.8)

where for a scalar
x
the notation
[x]=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits(x)\lfloor|x|\rfloor
denotes the integer closest to
x
inside the interval
[-|x|,|x|]
,
and
[x]=([x_{1}],[x_{2}])
for a vector
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
. This choice always gives a point
x_{i,j}-z_{i,j}
in the grid
\delta\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If
this point falls outside the unit square, then we redefine
z_{i,j}
so that
x_{k,l}:=x_{i,j}-z_{i,j}
is the point in
\partial[0,1]^{2}

where the line from
x_{i.j}
to the original
x_{i,j}-z_{i,j}
crosses this boundary. We shall refer
to the latter type of points
x_{k,l}
as belonging to the (discretised) influx boundary
\partial_{d}\mathcal{O}_{1,u}

and assume that the values of
f
at these points are prespecified.

For points
x_{i,j}
in the influx boundary
\partial_{d}\mathcal{O}_{1,u}
we set the value
\alpha_{i,j}
equal to the pre-specified values
f(x_{i,j})
.
For the other points we define
\alpha_{i,j}
as the solution to the equation
(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{\delta}u)_{i,j}=g_{i,j}
, thus
mimicking the discretised Darcy equation
(\mathcal{L}_{f}u)_{i,j}=g_{i,j}
. In view of (
7.7
) this solution takes the form

\alpha_{ij}=\begin{cases}g_{i,j}/\Delta u_{i,j},&\text{if $\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|<\sqrt{\delta}$,}\\
\frac{g_{i,j}+\alpha_{k,l}\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|/\|z_{i,j}\|}{\Delta u_{ij}+\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|/\|z_{i,j}\|},&\text{if $\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta}$}.\end{cases}

(7.9)

Under the condition that
C(u)
given in (
7.4
) is bounded away from zero, these quotients are well defined for small enough
\delta>0
.
In the first case of the display, the value
\alpha_{i,j}
is expressed explicitly in the known values
g_{i,j}
and
\Delta u_{i,j}
, but
the second case involves the solution
\alpha_{k,l}
at the grid point
x_{k,l}
, in addition to these known values and
\nabla u_{i,j}
. The latter recursion
can be solved along a chain of recursions
x_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\to x_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
that connects a point
x_{i,j}
with
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta}

to a point
x_{i_{0},j_{0}}
which either belongs to the influx boundary or satisfies
\|\nabla u_{i_{0},j_{0}}\|<\sqrt{\delta}
. The value

\alpha_{i_{0},j_{0}}
is then determined and
\alpha_{i,j}
can be computed by repeated substitutions. We show below that

u_{i,j}>u_{k,l}
if
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta}
, so that the equations are then solved in the order of increasing
value of
u_{i,j}
. The strict decrease shows that the chain of recursions
x_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\to x_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
cannot visit
any grid point twice and hence must end at a point
x_{i_{0},j_{0}}
after finitely many steps.

The proof of the following lemma is given in Section
E
.

Lemma 7.3
.

For
\mathcal{O}=[0,1]^{2}
and given
u\in C^{2+\eta}(\mathcal{O})
with
C(u):=\Delta u+\|\nabla u\|^{2}>0
and
\eta\in(0,1]

such that
u=u_{f}
for
f\in C^{1}(\mathcal{O})
, the preceding algorithm gives
(\alpha_{i,j})
such that, for small enough
\delta>0
,

\max_{0\leq i,j\leq 1/\delta}|\alpha_{i,j}-f(x_{i,j})|\lesssim\delta^{\eta/2},

where the multiplicative constant depends on
\|u\|_{C^{2+\eta}(\mathcal{O})}
,
C(u)
,
\|f\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}
and
\|g\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}
only.

7.2
Multiple measurements

Suppose given multiple measurements
Y_{n,j}=u_{f,j}+n^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbb{W}}_{j}
, for
u_{f,1},\ldots,u_{f,d}
solutions
to (
7.1

and boundary functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
. If the matrix
[\nabla u_{f,1}\cdots\nabla u_{f,d}]

\nabla f/f
.
We can accommodate this in our setup by replacing the single measurement
Y_{n}
by the
vector
(Y_{n,1},\ldots,Y_{n,d})
and defining a solution operator

as in (
1.2
) as operating on the
vector of Laplacians
\bigl(\Delta u_{f,1},\ldots,\Delta u_{f,d}\bigr)
, as follows.

If
K
is the inverse of the Laplacian
\mathcal{L}=\Delta

Our method is to infer

Then

\displaystyle\bar{e}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})

\displaystyle=-\bigl[v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\bigr]\bigl[\nabla Kv_{1}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\nabla Kv_{d}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{d}\bigr]^{-1}.

By (
7.3
) and the preceding reasoning

\nabla f/f=e(\Delta u_{f,1},\ldots,\Delta u_{f,d})
. The map
\bar{e}
is Lipschitz on the domain of functions
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})

such that the norm of the inverse matrices
\bigl[\nabla Kv_{1}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\nabla Kv_{d}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{d}\bigr]^{-1}
is uniformly bounded.
We can compose
\bar{e}
with a map that recovers
f
from
\nabla f/f

to construct a solution operator (
1.2
).

For a concrete example for the domain
\mathcal{O}=(0,1)^{d}
,
we use the prior on the eigen expansion of the Laplacian, as discussed in Section
4
.
Let
(G^{s})
be the smoothness scale generated by the eigenfunctions (
4.4
).

Theorem 7.4
.

Assume that
\Delta u_{f_{0},1},\ldots,\Delta u_{f_{0},d}\in G^{\beta}
, for
\beta>d/2-1
and that the
(d\times d)
matrix

\bigl[\nabla u_{f_{0},1}(x)\cdots\nabla u_{f_{0},d}(x)\bigr]
is continuously invertible for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
with
inverses of uniformly bounded norms.
(i). The
L_{2}
-contraction rate of the posterior distribution of

to
\nabla f_{0}/f_{0}
in the white noise model is

n^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta)/(2\alpha+4+d)}
if the regularity of the prior is chosen
deterministic and equal to
\alpha
with
\alpha>d/2-1
.
(ii). This
L_{2}
-posterior contraction rate is
l_{n}n^{-\beta/(2\beta+4+d)}
for a slowly varying sequence
l_{n}
,
if
\alpha
is chosen by the empirical Bayes or hierarchical Bayes methods.

Theorems
3.1
and
3.2

give a posterior contraction rate
n^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta-\delta)/(2\alpha+4+d)}
for the Laplacians
v_{j}=\Delta u_{f,j}
relative
to the
G^{\delta}
norm and a posterior contraction rate
n^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta)/(2\alpha+4+d)}
for the Laplacians relative to the
L_{2}
-norm,
with
\alpha=\beta
in the case of (ii) and provided
\alpha\wedge\beta>\delta
.

From the eigen expansion of
K
(see (
4.4
)), we find that
\nabla Kv=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\kappa_{i}v_{i}\nabla h_{i}
, where

\nabla h_{i}
is the vector-valued function with

2^{d/2}\pi i_{k}\prod_{j\not=k}\sin(i_{j}\pi x_{j})\cos(i_{k}\pi x_{k})

The latter functions without the factor

\|\nabla Kv\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}

\|\nabla Kv\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\kappa_{i}|v_{i}|\,\|\nabla h_{i}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}|v_{i}|

\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2},\ldots
be the array of values
(v_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}^{d})

Then in view of Lemma
B.2

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{-1/d}|\tilde{v}_{\ell}|\leq(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{-(2+2\delta)/d})^{1/2}\|v\|_{G^{\delta}}

\nabla K:L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O})

The second shows that the posterior distribution of

\bigl[\nabla Kv_{1}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{1}\cdots\nabla Kv_{d}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{d}\bigr]

for every

We can conclude that the map

Remark 7.5
.

The function
\nabla f/f
determines
f

\nabla f/f\mapsto f

One might hope that the rates for

8
Exponentiated Volterra operator

For a known positive constant
g
, consider the solution
u_{f}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
of the
ordinary differential equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{f}^{\prime}&=fu_{f},\qquad&&\text{on }(0,1),\\
u_{f}&=g,\qquad&&\text{at }0.\end{aligned}\right.

(8.1)

This equation takes the form (
1.1
) with
\mathcal{O}=(0,1)
and
\Gamma=\{0\}
,
differential operator
\mathcal{L}u=u^{\prime}
and
c(u,f)=uf
. The inverse operator
K

defined in (
1.4
) is the standard
Volterra operator
K:L_{2}([0,1])\to L_{2}([0,1])
defined by

\displaystyle Kv(x):=\int_{0}^{x}v(s)\,ds.

The function
\tilde{g}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
as in (
1.5
) is the constant
\tilde{g}=g
.
It is immediate from the differential equation (
8.1
) that
f=u_{f}^{\prime}/u_{f}
, where we note that
the function
u_{f}
is nonzero, as follows from the explicit expression of the solution:

u_{f}(x)=g\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}f(s)\,ds\right)
. Thus the solution map
(
1.2
) is given by

e(v)=\frac{v}{Kv+g}.

Because
\|Kv\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|Kv\|_{\infty}\leq\|v\|_{L_{2}}
, the map

L_{2}
-norm in a sufficiently small neighbourhood

function
v_{0}
such that

-contraction rates for

-contraction rates for

.

One possible prior is the one based on the eigenexpansion.
The eigenfunctions of the operator
K^{\intercal}K:L_{2}(0,1)\to L_{2}(0,1)
take the form (see e.g.
[
20

with corresponding eigenvalues

\kappa_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}(i-1/2)^{2}}.

Hence the operator
K
is smoothing (
3.3
) of order
p=1
in its eigenscale
(G^{\beta})_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}
.

We endow

with the prior distribution
v=\sum_{i\geq 1}v_{i}h_{i}
, for
v_{i}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{ind}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,i^{-1-2\alpha})

\alpha
or with

Theorem 8.1
.

If

-norm at rate

) with

the credible ball

and Propositions
2.1
and

10
Discussion

We have derived a novel, Bayesian linearisation method for non-linear inverse problems. Our approach potentially decreases computation time, as routines for solving linear inverse problems are simpler and faster than for non-linear ones. Even if the linear inverse problem is non-conjugate (e.g. if the prior is not set on the singular value basis of
K
) and iterative methods are needed, our method may be substantially faster as it is does not require to solve the forward problem at each iteration step.

We derived posterior contraction rates for Gaussian process priors, with both fixed and data driven regularity hyper-parameters (using either the empirical or hierarchical Bayes methods). Our results on adaptation are the first one on PDE-constrained, non-linear inverse problems. We also showed that the frequentist coverage guarantees of credible sets in the corresponding linear inverse problems translates to the non-linear inverse problems. Our results apply both to the benchmark Gaussian white noise model and to the practically more relevant discrete observational framework. Our results can be extended to priors going beyond the eigenfunctions of the differential operator
\mathcal{L}
. Our numerical analysis on synthetic data sets shows that the proposed approach has good numerical properties, although
proper handling of boundary conditions appears to require care.

A potential bottleneck of our approach is the solution operator (
1.2
). As a proof of concept, we have covered a collection of benchmark PDE and ODE problems, where this operator takes an explicit form, as well as the Darcy flow problem, where the solution operator only exists in the form of a numerical algorithm. In general,
a numerical implementation of the operator may be necessary. We can borrow here from the field of (numerical) analysis,
where this is an active research area. Another interesting question for future research is scaling up our approach with various machine learning techniques, including distributed and variational inference. Our linearised setup provides a convenient framework to derive such approximation algorithms compared to the original non-linear setting.

Hilbert scales and Sobolev spaces

Recall that
H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
is the Sobolev space of order
s
and that
H_{0}^{s}(\mathcal{O})

is the subset of functions that vanish on the boundary of
\mathcal{O}
(i.e. have vanishing trace).

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a symmetric, second order, elliptic differential operator with
C^{\infty}
coefficient functions
such that the function
v=0
is the only solution to the problem
\mathcal{L}v=0
on
\mathcal{O}
and
v=0
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
. For
u\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
, let
Ku\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
be the weak solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}Ku&=u,\qquad&&\text{ on }\mathcal{O},\\
Ku&=0,\qquad&&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{O}.\end{aligned}\right.

Then
K:L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
is self-adjoint and compact (it is even bounded as an operator

K:L_{2}(\mathcal{O})\to H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
; see
[
14
]
, Theorem 6 in Section 6.2). Given its eigenfunctions
(h_{j})
and
eigenvalues
\kappa_{j}
and
s\geq 0
, let
G^{s}
be the set of functions
v=\sum_{j}v_{j}h_{j}\in L_{2}
for which

\|v\|_{G^{s}}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}v_{j}^{2}\kappa_{j}^{-s}
is finite. For
s<0
, let
G^{s}
be the dual space of
G^{-s}
.

Proposition A.1
.

For
s\geq 0
such that
\partial\mathcal{O}\in C^{\lceil s\rceil+2}
, we have
G^{s}\subset H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
,
with
\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|u\|_{G^{s}}
, for every
u\in G^{s}
, with

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{O})}\asymp\|u\|_{G^{s}}
for every even integer
s
.
For
s=2
we have
G^{2}=H^{2}(\mathcal{O})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})
if
\partial\mathcal{O}\in C^{2}
.

The space
G^{s}
is equivalent to the set of functions
K^{s}v
, for
v\in L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
, with the norm

\|K^{s}v\|_{G^{s}}=\|v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
under the condition that
\partial\mathcal{O}\in C^{m+2}
,
Theorem 5 in
[
14
]
gives that
Ku\in H^{m+2}(\mathcal{O})
if

,
with
\|Ku\|_{H^{m+2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{m}(\mathcal{O})}
.

For
m=0
, this shows that

and
\|Ku\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|u\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{O})}\asymp\|Ku\|_{G^{2}}
.
By the definition of a weak solution with Dirichlet boundary condition, it is
also true that
KL_{2}\subset H_{0}^{1}
and hence
G^{2}\subset H^{2}(\mathcal{O})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})

with
\|u\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|u\|_{G^{2}}
.
Conversely if
uH^{2}(\mathcal{O})
, then
\mathcal{L}u\in L_{2}
by the definitions of
H^{2}(\mathcal{O})
and
\mathcal{L}

(or see (5.12) in Chapter 4.5 of
[
52
]
). If also
u\in H_{0}^{1}
,
then

solves
\mathcal{L}w=\mathcal{L}u
(trivially) on
\mathcal{O}
and

\partial\mathcal{O}
and hence

\|u\|_{G^{2}}=\|\mathcal{L}u\|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{O})}
.
This concludes the proof that
G^{2}=H^{2}(\mathcal{O})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})
and that the norms
are equivalent on this space.

Next we prove by induction that
G^{2m}\subset H^{2m}(\mathcal{O})
with equivalent norms,
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For
m=1
the assertion is proved.
From the definitions it follows that
G^{2m+2}=KG^{2m}
with norms satisfying

\|K^{m+1}u\|_{G^{2m+2}}=\|u\|_{L_{2}}=\|K^{m}u\|_{G^{2m}}
.
By Theorem 5 in
[
14
]
as quoted previously
K^{m+1}u=K(K^{m}u)
is contained
in

with norms
satisfying
\|K^{m+1}u\|_{H^{2m+2}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|K^{m}u\|_{H^{2m}(\mathcal{O})}
.
Therefore if the assertion is true for

, then it is true for

The Sobolev spaces

for non-integer

can be constructed as the

[L^{2},H^{m}(\mathcal{O})]_{s/m,2}
, for

(see
[

]

[

]

is equivalent to

\biggl(\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{1}{t^{s/m}}\inf_{u=u_{0}+u_{m}}\bigl(\|u_{0}\|_{L_{2}}+t\|u_{m}\|_{H^{m}(\mathcal{O})}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\biggr)^{1/2},

with

,
and the space
H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
is exactly the set of functions

for which the expression is finite.
By direct argument it can be seen that the space

is the interpolation

Lemma A.2
.

For
0\leq s\leq m
, the space
G^{s}
is the interpolation space
G^{s}=[L_{2},G^{m}]_{s/m,2}
.

Let
K(t,u)=\inf\bigl\{\|u_{0}\|_{L_{2}}+t\|u_{m}\|_{G^{m}}:u=u_{0}+u_{m}\}
, where the infimum is taken
over
u_{0}\in L_{2}
and
u_{m}\in G^{m}

Because

,

\displaystyle K^{2}(t,u)

\displaystyle\asymp\inf_{u=u_{0}+u_{m}}\bigl(\|u_{0}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+t^{2}\|u_{m}\|_{G^{m}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u=u_{0}+u_{m}}\bigl(\sum_{i}(u_{i,0}^{2}+u_{m,i}^{2}t^{2}\kappa_{i}^{-m}\bigr)=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}\wedge(u_{i}^{2}t^{2}\kappa_{i}^{-m}).

It follows that the square interpolation norm is given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{2}(t,u)}{t^{2s/m}}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1\wedge(t^{2}\kappa_{i}^{-2m})}{t^{2s/m}}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\kappa_{i}^{m/2}}t^{1-2s/m}\,dt\kappa_{i}^{-m}+\int_{\kappa_{i}^{m/2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2s/m+1}}\,dt\Bigr).

The right side can be seen to be up to a multiple equivalent to
\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}\kappa_{i}^{-s}=\|u\|_{G^{s}}^{2}
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
K:L_{2}\to L_{2}
be a self-adjoint linear operator
with eigenvalues
\kappa_{j}\asymp j^{-p/d}
and such
K^{-\beta/p}(H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O}))\subset L_{2}
.
If
f\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
and

for some

, then
K^{-1}(fu_{f})\in G^{\beta}
,
for
(G^{s})
the Hilbert scale generated by the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of
K
.

For every eigenfunction
h_{j}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
K^{-s}h_{j}=\kappa_{j}^{-s}h_{j}
.
Thus the self-adjointness of
K
gives

\displaystyle\langle fu_{f},h_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}=\langle fu_{f},\kappa_{j}^{\beta/p}K^{-\beta/p}h_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{j}^{\beta/p}\langle K^{-\beta/p}(fu_{f}),h_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}.

Since
\kappa_{j}\asymp j^{-p/d}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle fu_{f},h_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}^{2}j^{2\beta/d}\asymp\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle K^{-\beta/p}(fu_{f}),h_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}^{2}.

If
f\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
and
u_{f}\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
, then
fu_{f}\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})
, since

,
and hence

, by assumption. Thus the right side of the display is finite,
and hence so is the left side, which implies

.
∎

.

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}(2\|k\|_{\infty}-1)^{-2\beta}\asymp\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\|k\|_{\infty}^{-2\beta}\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2\beta/d}=O(1).

We conclude the proof of the first statement by applying the upper bounds derived above for the two terms on the right-hand side of (
B.2
) with
m=n^{1/d}
.

To show that
\mathcal{I}_{n}f
coincides with
f
on the design points, note that for
x\in\mathbb{X}=\{(\frac{2i}{2m+1})_{i=1,...,m}\}^{d}
,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m\end{subarray}}\langle f,h_{l}\rangle_{L_{2}}h_{l}(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\langle f,h_{k(2m+1)+l}\rangle_{L_{2}}h_{k(2m+1)+l}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m\end{subarray}}\langle f,h_{l}\rangle_{L_{2}}h_{l}(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m\end{subarray}}\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\langle f,h_{k(2m+1)+l}\rangle_{L_{2}}\Big)h_{l}(x),

where the last equality follows from

\displaystyle h_{k(2m+1)+l}(x)

\displaystyle=2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin((k(2m+1)+l)_{j}\pi x_{j})

\displaystyle=2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(l_{j}\pi x_{j}+2k_{j}\pi r_{j})=2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(l_{j}\pi x_{j})=h_{l}(x),

where
r_{j}=x_{j}(2m+1)/2\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, to prove the equivalence of the
L_{2}
-norm with the empirical Euclidean norm
\mathbb{L}_{n}
, note that in view of Lemma
B.4
, for any
f=\sum_{\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m}c_{l}h_{l}\in\mathbb{L}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathbb{L}_{n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m}\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq m}c_{l}c_{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}}h_{l}(x)h_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\frac{1}{2m}\Big)^{d}\sum_{\|l\|_{\infty}\leq m}c_{l}^{2}=\Big(1+\frac{1}{2m}\Big)^{d}\|f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
h_{l}(x)=2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(l_{j}\pi x_{j})
for
l\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
x\in[0,1]^{d}
. Then for arbitrary
l,k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
\|l\|_{\infty},\|k\|_{\infty}\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle\langle h_{l},h_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{L}_{n}}=\begin{cases}(1+\frac{1}{2m})^{d},&\text{if}\,\,l=k,\\
0,&\textnormal{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where the empirical measure
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is defined on the equidistant grid
\mathbb{X}=\{(\frac{2i}{2m+1})_{i=1,...,m}\}^{d}
.

First we consider the
d=1
case and then extend the result for general
d\in\mathbb{N}
. By standard trigonometric equivalence, for all
l,k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}}2\sin\left(l\pi x\right)\sin\left(k\pi x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\cos\left((l-k)\pi x\right)-\cos\left((l+k)\pi x\right).

Then, in view of Lemma
B.5
, for
1\leq l,k\leq m
, the right-hand side of the preceding display is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle\Big(m1_{2m+1|(l-k)}-\frac{1}{2}1_{2m+1\nmid(l-k)}-\big[m1_{2m+1|(l+k)}-\frac{1}{2}1_{2m+1\nmid(l+k)}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\left(m1_{l=k}-\frac{1}{2}1_{l\neq k}+\frac{1}{2}\right)=(1+\tfrac{1}{2m})1_{l=k}.

This finishes the proof for
d=1
.

To extend the results to
d>1
, note that

\displaystyle\langle h_{l},h_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{L}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}}2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(l_{j}\pi x_{j})2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(k_{j}\pi x_{j})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathbb{X}_{j}}2\sin\left(l_{j}\pi x_{j}\right)\sin\left(k_{j}\pi x_{j}\right)=\begin{cases}(1+\frac{1}{2m})^{d}&l=k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma B.5
.

For any
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cos\left(r\pi\frac{2j}{2m+1}\right)=\begin{cases}m,&2m+1|r,\\
-\frac{1}{2},&2m+1\nmid r.\end{cases}

For
r=v(2m+1)
with
v\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cos\left(r\pi\frac{2j}{2m+1}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cos\left(2v\pi j\right)=m.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r\neq v(2m+1)
, for any
v\in\mathbb{N}
, and
z=\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{2}{2m+1}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cos\Big(r\pi\frac{2j}{2m+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\text{Re}\Big[\exp\Big(ri\pi\frac{2j}{2m+1}\Big)\Big]=\text{Re}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}z^{j}\Big)=\text{Re}\Big(\frac{z(z^{m}-1)}{z-1}\Big).

Furthermore, since
(z-1)(\bar{z}-1)=2-2\text{Re}(z)=2-2\cos(r\pi\frac{2}{2m+1})
, and

\displaystyle z(z^{m}-1)

\displaystyle(\bar{z}-1)=z^{m}-1-z^{m+1}+z

\displaystyle=\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{2m+1}{2m+1}\right)\exp\left(-ri\pi\frac{1}{2m+1}\right)-1

\displaystyle\quad-\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{2m+1}{2m+1}\right)\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{1}{2m+1}\right)+\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{2}{2m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r}\left(\exp\left(-ri\pi\frac{1}{2m+1}\right)-\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{1}{2m+1}\right)\right)-1+\exp\left(ri\pi\frac{2}{2m+1}\right),

so
\text{Re}(z(z^{m}-1)(\bar{z}-1))=\cos(r\pi\frac{2}{2m+1})-1
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(\frac{z(z^{m}-1)}{z-1}\right)=\frac{\cos(r\pi\frac{2}{2m+1})-1}{2-2\cos(r\pi\frac{2}{2m+1})}=-\frac{1}{2}.

finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma C.1
.

Consider the operator
K:L_{2}(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1])\to L_{2}(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1])
defined in (
1.4
)
for
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta
and
\mathcal{O}=(0,1)^{d}
.
For
i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, define
\mu_{i}=\pi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2}
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
\nu_{i,k}
be the (unique) solution
to the equation
\nu_{i,k}/\tan\nu_{i,k}=-\mu_{i}/2
in the interval
\bigl((k-1/2)\pi,k\pi\bigr)
.
Then the eigenvalues of the operator
K^{\intercal}K
are given by
\lambda_{i,k}=1/(\nu_{i,k}^{2}+\mu_{i}^{2}/4)
, for
(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}
,
and satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{-2}}{k^{2}+\pi^{2}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2})^{2}/4}\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\frac{\pi^{-2}}{(k-1/2)^{2}+\pi^{2}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2})^{2}/4},\quad(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}.

The corresponding normalised eigenfunctions are given by

\displaystyle h_{i,k}(x,t):=\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu_{i,k}}\sin(\nu_{i,k}t)+\cos(\nu_{i,k}t)}{\sqrt{\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu_{i,k}}+\frac{\nu_{i,k}}{(\sin\nu_{i,k})^{2}}}{2\nu_{i,k}}}}2^{d/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(i_{j}\pi x_{j}).

(C.1)

The set of eigenfunctions
\{h_{i,k}:(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L_{\infty}
.

Equation (
1.4
) gives
u=\mathcal{L}Ku
on
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{O}\times(0,1)
, whence one or two partial integrations yield

\langle u,K^{\intercal}v\rangle_{L_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{O}})}=\langle\partial_{t}Ku-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta Ku,K^{\intercal}v\rangle_{L_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{O}})}=\langle Ku,-\partial_{t}K^{\intercal}v-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta K^{\intercal}v\rangle_{L_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{O}})},

for every
v
for which
K^{\intercal}v(x,1)=K^{\intercal}v(0,t)=0
for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
and
t\in(0,1)
.
Here we also use the boundary conditions on
Ku
specified in (
1.4
) to see that the boundary values to the partial integrations vanish.
It follows that the adjoint operator
K^{\intercal}:L_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1]\bigr)\to L_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1]\bigr)
of
K
is the solution operator
v\mapsto K^{\intercal}v
of the homogeneous differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad} l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{} }{-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}K^{\intercal}v-\frac{1}{2}\Delta K^{\intercal}v}&=v,&(x,t)\in\mathcal{O}\times(0,1),\\
K^{\intercal}v(x,t)&=0,&x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\,t\in(0,1],\\
K^{\intercal}v(x,1)&=0,&x\in\mathcal{O}.\end{array}\right.

(C.2)

Evaluating this at
v=Ku
, we find
Ku=-\partial_{t}K^{\intercal}Ku-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta K^{\intercal}Ku
. By (
1.4
) this implies
that
\Delta(-\partial_{t}K^{\intercal}Ku-\tfrac{1}{2}K^{\intercal}Ku)=u
on
\mathcal{O}\times(0,1)
, and moreover the function on the right inherits the boundary conditions of
Ku
. We infer that
the operator
K^{\intercal}K
is given as the solution operator
u\mapsto K^{\intercal}Ku
of the homogeneous differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad} l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{} }\big(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{2}\big)K^{\intercal}Ku&=u,&(x,t)\in\mathcal{O}\times(0,1),\\
K^{\intercal}Ku(x,t)&=0,&x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\,t\in(0,1],\\
K^{\intercal}Ku(x,1)&=0,&x\in\mathcal{O},\\
(-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)K^{\intercal}Ku(x,t)&=0,&x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\,t\in(0,1],\\
(-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)K^{\intercal}Ku(x,0)&=0,&x\in\mathcal{O}.\end{array}\right.

(C.3)

Thus an eigenfunction
h
of
K^{\intercal}K
with eigenvalue
\lambda
satisfies
h=(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{2})\lambda h
on
\mathcal{O}\times(0,1)
,
and satisfies the given boundary conditions.

For an eigenfunction of the form
h(x,t)=g(t)f(x)
and
\lambda>0
, this becomes

gf=-\lambda\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}gf+\lambda\frac{1}{4}g\Delta^{2}f.

(C.4)

Dividing both sides by
gf
results in

1=-\lambda\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}g}{g}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda\frac{\Delta^{2}f}{f}.

(C.5)

Since the left-hand side is constant, and the two terms on the right do not share any variable, they must be constants.
Therefore, we are lead to the following systems of equations, for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad} l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{} }\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}g(t)&=-\frac{c}{\lambda}g(t),&t\in(0,1),\\
g(t)&=0,&t=1,\end{array}\right.

(C.6)

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad} l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{} }\Delta^{2}f(x)&=4\frac{1-c}{\lambda}f(x),&x\in\mathcal{O},\\
f(x)&=0,&x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\\
\end{array}\right.

(C.7)

together with the mixed boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad} l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{}l@{} }(-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)g(t)f(x)&=0,&x\in\mathcal{O},t=0,\\
(-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)g(t)f(x)&=0,&x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\,t\in(0,1].\end{array}\right.

We shall show that these equations yield a complete set of eigenfunctions.

The system of equations (
C.7
) describes the eigenfunctions of the square Laplacian with zero boundary condition. These
eigenfunctions are the same as those of the Laplacian with the eigenvalues squared. The system is solved
by the functions
f(x)=2^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sin(i_{j}\pi x_{j})
, for
i_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, with corresponding eigenvalues

4(1-c)/\lambda=\mu^{2}
, for
\mu=\pi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2}
the eigenvalues of minus the Laplacian
-\Delta
.
Since
\Delta f=-\mu f
, the first mixed boundary condition
becomes
(-g^{\prime}(0)+g(0)\mu/2)f(x)=0
, for
x\in\mathcal{O}
.
This implies the boundary condition
-g^{\prime}(0)+g(0)\mu/2=0
of Robin form for the problem (
C.6
).

It follows from the preceding that
c<1
. Under the (Robin) boundary conditions of (
C.6
), partial integration gives

\langle g^{\prime\prime},g\rangle_{L_{2}(0,1)}=-g(0)^{2}\mu/2-\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(t)^{2}\,ds<0
, for
g\not=0
. It follows that the operator
g\mapsto g^{\prime\prime}
is
negative definite and hence possesses only negative eigenvalues. We may thus restrict (
C.6
)
to
c>0
. Under this restriction the general solution of this second order ordinary differential equation is

g(t)=c_{1}\sin(\nu t)+c_{2}\cos(\nu t)
, for
\nu=\sqrt{c/\lambda}
. The boundary conditions at
t=1
and
t=0

give
c_{1}\sin\nu+c_{2}\cos\nu=0
and
-c_{1}\nu+c_{2}\mu/2=0
. A nonzero solution
(c_{1},c_{2})
requires that
(\sin\nu)\mu/2+\nu\cos\nu=0
.
For
\nu>0
this is possible only if
\nu\not=k\pi
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and then
\nu\cos\nu/\sin\nu=-\mu/2
.
The function
\nu\mapsto\nu\cos\nu/\sin\nu
is strictly decreasing on every interval
\bigl((k-1)\pi,k\pi\bigr)
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
with value 0 at
(k-1/2)\pi
and limit
-\infty
as
\nu\uparrow k\pi
.
It follows that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu>0
, there exists a unique
\nu_{k}\in\bigl((k-1/2)\pi,k\pi\bigr)
such that

\nu_{k}\cos\nu_{k}/\sin\nu_{k}=-\mu/2
.

The equations
4(1-c)/\lambda=\mu^{2}
and
\nu=\sqrt{c/\lambda}
imply that
1=(\mu^{2}/4+\nu^{2})\lambda
. Thus for
every
i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have obtained an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i,k}=1/(\mu_{i}^{2}/4+\nu_{i,k}^{2})
,
for
\mu_{i}=\pi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2}
and
\nu_{i,k}/\tan\nu_{i,k}=-\mu_{i}/2
with
\nu_{i,k}
contained in the interval

\bigl((k-1/2)\pi,k\pi\bigr)
. This clearly satisfies the inequalities in the lemma.

The eigenfunctions
f
as given are already normalised in
L_{2}(\mathcal{O})
.
To normalise
(x,t)\mapsto g(t)f(x)
in
L_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1]\bigr)
, we require that
\int_{0}^{1}g(t)^{2}\,dt=1
, which gives

\displaystyle g(t)=\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu}\sin(\nu t)+\cos(\nu t)}{\sqrt{\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu}+\frac{\nu}{(\sin\nu)^{2}}}{2\nu}}}.

For
\nu
satisfying
\nu/\tan\nu=-\mu/2
, we have
\tan\nu=-{2\nu}/{\mu}<0

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu}+\frac{\nu}{(\sin\nu)^{2}}}{2\nu}}\geq\sqrt{0+\frac{1}{2(\sin\nu)^{2}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus the second term of

The absolute value of the constant in front of the first term can be bounded using the definitions of
\nu
and
\mu

\displaystyle\biggl|\frac{\frac{1}{\tan\nu}}{\sqrt{\frac{-\frac{1}{\tan\nu}+\frac{\nu}{(\sin\nu)^{2}}}{2\nu}}}\biggr|=\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{-\tan\nu+\frac{\nu}{(\cos\nu)^{2}}}}=\left(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{2(\cos\nu)^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}\leq\sqrt{2}.

We see that
g
is uniformly bounded, and thus also the eigenfunctions are uniformly bounded.

It remains to show that all eigenfunctions of
K^{\intercal}K
are of the form (
C.1

The functions
(f_{i}(x))_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
form an orthonormal basis of

, and the
(g_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

form an orthonormal basis of

Then the products of these functions form an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{O}\times[0,1]\bigr)
and give
the complete set of eigenfunctions. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\{\kappa_{i,k}:i\in\mathbb{N}^{d},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
be numbers with
C_{1}/(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})\leq\kappa_{i,k}\leq C_{2}/(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})
, for constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
p,q>0
and

\|i\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}^{2}
, for every
i=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and let
k_{1}\geq k_{2}\geq\cdots

denote the same values
\kappa_{i,k}
sorted in decreasing order.
Then there exist positive constants
\tilde{C}_{1},\tilde{C}_{2}
that depend on
C_{1},C_{2},d
only such that

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{1}\ell^{-p/(d+p/q)}\leq k_{\ell}\leq\tilde{C_{2}}\ell^{-p/(d+p/q)},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

Furthermore, if
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
are the values of the multi-indexed array
\{\nu_{i,k}:i\in\mathbb{N}^{d},k\in\mathbb{N}\}

in corresponding order, then
\|v\|_{G^{\beta}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{2\beta/(d+1)}v_{\ell}^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|v\|_{G^{\beta}}^{2}\asymp\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d},k\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl(\|i\|+k^{q/p}\bigr)^{2\beta(d+p/q)/(d+1)}\nu_{i,k}^{2}.

Let
z:\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a bijection that corresponds to an ordering of the numbers
\kappa_{i,k}
. We show in the next paragraph
that
z(i,k)\asymp(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})^{d/p+1/q}
, where the multiplicative constants depend on
C_{1},C_{2},d
only.
If this is true, then
k_{z(i,k)}=\kappa_{i,k}\asymp(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})^{-1}
, by the assumption on the
\kappa_{i,k}
, which is
z(i,k)^{-p/(d+p/q)}
, and the first
assertion of the lemma is proved.
Furthermore
\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\ell^{2\beta/(d+1)}v_{\ell}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d},k\in\mathbb{N}}z(i,k)^{2\beta/(d+1)}v_{z(i,k)}^{2}\asymp\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q}\bigr)^{2\beta(d/p+1/q)/(d+1)}\nu_{i,k}^{2}
,
proving the second assertion of the lemma.

It remains to prove that
z(i,k)\asymp(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})^{d/p+1/q}
, which we achieve by a separate lower and upper bound.
If
(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}
is such that
\|j\|^{p}+l^{q}\leq d_{1}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})
for a sufficiently small constant
d_{1}>0
that depends on
C_{1},C_{2}
only
(
d_{1}<C_{1}/C_{2}
works), then
the assumption on the numbers
\kappa_{i,k}
shows that
\kappa_{j,l}>\kappa_{i,k}
and hence
z(j,l)\leq z(i,k)
. It follows that

z(i,k)\geq\#\bigl((j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}:\|j\|^{p}+l^{q}\leq d_{1}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})\bigr)
,
which is bounded below by
\#\bigl(j\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\|j\|^{p}\leq d_{1}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})/2\bigr)\#\bigl(l\in\mathbb{N}:l^{q}\leq d_{1}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})/2\bigr)
, which is of the order
(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})^{d/p+1/q}

up to multiplicative constants depending on
d_{1}
and
d
only. Conversely, because every
(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}
with
z(j,l)\leq z(i,k)
has

\kappa_{j,l}\geq\kappa_{i,k}
, we have
z(i,k)=\#\bigl((j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}:z(j,l)\leq z(i,k)\bigr)\leq\#\bigl((j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}:\kappa_{j,l}\geq\kappa_{i,k}\bigr)
,
which is bounded above by
\#\bigl((j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}:\|j\|^{p}+l^{q}\leq d_{2}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})\bigr)
for
d_{2}=C_{2}/C_{1}

by the assumption on the numbers
\kappa_{i,k}
, which is bounded above by

\#\bigl(j\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\|j\|^{p}\leq d_{2}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})\bigr)\#\bigl(l\in\mathbb{N}:l^{q}\leq d_{2}(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})\bigr)
,
which is bounded above by a multiple of
(\|i\|^{p}+k^{q})^{d/p+1/q}
.
∎

u_{f_{0}}
. This implies
that there can be at most one zero

, in which case the differential equation (

) can be integrated to find

(f_{0}u_{f_{0}}^{\prime})(x)=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}h(s)\,ds
, for every
x\in[0,1]
, and hence

f_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{H(x)-H(x_{0})}{u_{f_{0}}^{\prime}(x)},&x\not=x_{0},\\
\frac{h(x_{0})}{u_{f_{0}}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})},&x=x_{0}.\end{cases}

The value at
x_{0}
follows by continuity or directly from (
6.1
).
An interesting feature is that this inversion formula requires no boundary value of
f_{0}
, as in Section
6
.

To make this inversion strategy work with noisy data, we would have to replace
u_{f_{0}}^{\prime}
by a draw from the posterior. Because this appears
unstable, we instead define an inverse operator using the second derivative. Let
V
be the set of continuous functions
v:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such
that the map

possesses a single zero at a point
x_{v}\in(0,1)
, with
v(x_{v})>0
,
and define a map

by

e(v)=\begin{cases}\frac{H(x)-H(x_{v})}{\int_{x_{v}}^{x}v(s)\,ds},&\text{if }x\not=x_{v},\\
\frac{h(x_{v})}{v(x_{v})},&\text{if }x=x_{v}.\end{cases}

(D.1)

(The function
v
in this definition stands for an estimate of
u^{\prime\prime}
and the constant

in its primitive function

is chosen so that the double primitive function

x\mapsto u(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,ds+g(0)+bx

satisfies the boundary conditions of (
6.1
) at
x\in\{0,1\}
.)

Lemma D.1
.

Suppose that the solution

\inf_{0<x<1}\bigl(|u_{f_{0}}^{\prime}(x)|+u_{f_{0}}^{\prime\prime}(x)\bigr)>0

for
x_{0}\in(0,1)
.
Then there exists
\eta>0
so that any continuously differentiable

\|v-u_{f_{0}}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}<\eta

satisfies

\|f-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq c_{0}\|v-u_{f_{0}}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

Define

Because any zero of

Because

Since

\displaystyle=\frac{h(x_{v})}{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{v})}+(x-x_{v})\Bigl(\frac{h^{\prime}(x_{v})}{2u^{\prime\prime}(x_{v})}-\frac{u^{\prime\prime\prime}(x_{v})h(x_{v})}{2u^{\prime\prime}(x_{v})^{2}}\Bigr)+o(x-x_{v}),

The solution map (

The proof consists of five steps, (i)-(v).

(i). We have
\|z_{i,j}\|\leq\sqrt{\delta}

\bigl\|{z_{i,j}}/{\|z_{i,j}\|}-{\nabla u_{i,j}}/{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|}\bigr\|\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\delta}

The first assertion is immediate from the definition of
z_{i,j}
. To prove the second, let
\tilde{z}_{i,j}
be as
z_{i,j}
, but without the truncation to the grid.
Thus
\tilde{z}_{i,j}=\sqrt{\delta}\,\nabla u/\|\nabla u\|

\tilde{z}_{i,j}/\|\tilde{z}_{i,j}\|=\nabla u_{i,j}/\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|

Now
\bigl\|z_{i,j}/\|z_{i,j}\|-\tilde{z}_{i,j}/\|\tilde{z}_{i,j}\|\bigr\|\leq 2\|z_{i,j}-\tilde{z}_{i,j}\|/\|\tilde{z}_{i,j}\|

z,\tilde{z}
,
which is bounded above by
2\sqrt{2}\delta/\sqrt{\delta}
in the present case. The possible redefinition of
z_{i,j}
if
x_{k,l}
is a boundary point,
does not change the direction of
z_{i,j}
and hence does not affect
z_{i,j}/\|z_{i,j}\|

(ii). If
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta}

u_{i,j}-u_{k,l}\geq\|z_{i,j}\|\bigl(\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|(1-4\delta)-\|\nabla u\|_{\text{Lip}}\sqrt{\delta}\bigr)
.
In particular,
u_{i,j}>u_{k,l}
, for all sufficiently small

.

The left side
u_{i,j}-u_{k,l}=u(x_{i,j})-u(x_{k,l})
is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\nabla u(x_{i,j}-sz_{i,j})\cdot z_{i,j}\,ds\geq\nabla u(x_{i,j})\cdot z_{i,j}-\|\nabla u\|_{\text{Lip}}\|z_{i,j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\,\|z_{i,j}\|\Bigl(1-{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\Bigl\|\frac{z_{i,j}}{\|z_{i,j}\|}-\frac{\nabla u_{i,j}}{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|}\Bigr\|^{2}\Bigr)-\|\nabla u\|_{\text{Lip}}\|z_{i,j}\|^{2},

since

\|x\|=\|y\|=1

(iii). For

\max_{i,j}|\alpha_{i,j}|\leq\Bigl(\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{D(u)}\vee\|f\|_{\partial_{d}\mathcal{O}_{1,u}}\vee\sqrt{\delta}\Bigr)\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{{3(\max u-\min u)}}{D(u)(1-4\delta)-\|\nabla u\|_{\text{Lip}}\sqrt{\delta}}\Bigr).

If
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|<\sqrt{\delta}

\Delta u_{i,j}\geq 2D(u)-\sqrt{\delta}>0

If

If
\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|\geq\sqrt{\delta}

is defined by the second case of (
7.9
) as a recursion on
\alpha_{k,l}
.
If
\Delta u_{i,j}>D(u)

(a+b)/(c+d)\leq(a/c)\vee(b/d)

|\alpha_{i,j}|\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{D(u)}\vee|\alpha_{k,l}|.

If
\Delta u_{i,j}\leq D(u)
, then

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i,j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}+|\alpha_{k,l}|\,\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|/\|z_{i,j}\|}{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|(1/\|z_{i,j}\|-1)}\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{D(u)\|(1/\|z_{i,j}\|-1)}+\frac{|\alpha_{k,l}|}{1-\|z_{i,j}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{D(u)}\vee|\alpha_{k,l}|\Bigr)\frac{\|z_{i,j}\|+1}{1-\|z_{i,j}\|}.

Combining the two inequalities in the last two displays, we see that whenever

\displaystyle\Bigl|\frac{\|\nabla u_{i,j}\|}{\|z_{i,j}\|}(f_{i,j}-f_{k,l})-(g_{i,j}-f_{i,j}\Delta u_{i,j})\Bigr|

F
Smoothness norm contraction rates in the Gaussian white noise linear inverse problems

In this section we extend the results of
[
19
]
to give rates of contraction relative to
smoothness norms in the context of the Gaussian white noise linear inverse problem (
1.7
). We obtain general results for an abstract scale of spaces of numerical sequences. By taking these sequences equal to the coefficients of functions relative to a suitable basis,
the scale may be identified with concrete function spaces.
For instance, for a suitable wavelet basis the scale can (partially) be identified with
the scale of Sobolev spaces and the general result will give contraction relative to Sobolev norms of positive order.
By Sobolev embedding this implies consistency (at a rate) for other norms such as the uniform norm.
Alternatively, the sequences can be taken equal to coefficients relative to the eigenbasis
of an operator of interest, giving a contraction rate to a norm intrinsic to the problem.

F.1
Smoothness scale

Fix a sequence
1\leq\rho_{1}\leq\rho_{2}\leq\ldots\uparrow\infty
, and for
s\in\mathbb{R}
consider the Hilbert spaces

G^{s}=\bigl\{g=(g_{i}):\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}:\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{2s}g_{i}^{2}<\infty\bigr\},

with inner product and norm defined by

\langle g,h\rangle_{G^{s}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{2s}g_{i}h_{i},\qquad\|g\|_{G^{s}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{2s}g_{i}^{2}}.

The spaces are nested: if
s\geq t
, then
G^{s}\subset G^{t}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
the inner product and norm satisfy, for
s\geq 0
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|g\|_{G^{t-s}}=\sup_{g\in G^{t+s}:\|g\|_{G^{t+s}}\leq 1}\langle g,h\rangle_{G^{t}},\qquad g\in G^{t-s}.

(F.1)

The map defined by
(C_{s}g)_{i}=\rho_{i}^{s}g_{i}
is an isometry
C_{s}:G^{t+s}\to G^{t}
, and

\langle g,h\rangle_{G^{s}}=\langle C_{s}g,C_{s}h\rangle_{G^{0}}
. The subspace
V_{j}=\{g\in G_{0}:g_{i}=0\text{ for }i>j\}

has the properties, for
s<t
, for
P_{j}
the orthogonal projection onto
V_{j}
,

\displaystyle\|g-P_{j}g\|_{G^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>j}\rho_{i}^{2s}g_{i}^{2}\leq\rho_{j}^{-2(t-s)}\|g\|_{G^{t}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|g\|_{G^{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{j}^{2(t-s)}\|g\|_{G^{s}}^{2},\qquad\text{ if }g\in V_{j}.

Thus it satisfies the conditions of a smoothness scale as in
[
19
]
.

We consider a prior distribution on the sequences given as the law of the random
sequence
\mathbb{G}=\bigl(\rho_{i}^{-\alpha}i^{-1/2}c_{i}Z_{i}\bigr)
, for
Z_{i}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{iid}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,1)
and a sequence
(c_{i})

such that
c_{\pi}\leq c_{i}\leq C_{\pi}
, for some constants
0<c_{\pi}<C_{\pi}
, for all
i
. Then

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{G}\|_{G^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{-2(\alpha-s)}i^{-1}c_{i}^{2}.

If
\rho_{i}\gtrsim i^{r}
, for some
r>0
, then this is finite for every
s<\alpha
, and hence the prior concentrates
on the space
G^{s}
, for
s<\alpha
: it is regular of nearly order
\alpha
.

Example
.

Given a wavelet basis
\{\psi_{j,k}:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},k=1,\ldots,2^{jd}\}
for functions on a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we can consider the sequences of coefficients
(h_{j,k})
of expansions of given functions
h
in the basis, linearly ordered
by level and location as
h_{0,1},h_{1,1},\ldots,h_{1,2^{d}},h_{2,1},\ldots,h_{2,2^{2d}},h_{3,1},\ldots
.
The ordering defines the map

(j,k)\mapsto i:=1+2^{d}+\cdots+2^{(j-1)d}+k=\frac{2^{jd}-1}{2^{d}-1}+k.

Set
\rho_{i}=2^{j}
and
\bar{h}_{i}=h_{j,k}
if
i\in\mathbb{N}
is the image of
(j,k)
under this map. Then the square norm

\sum_{j}\sum_{k}2^{js}h_{j,k}^{2}
is equal to
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{2s}\bar{h}_{i}^{2}
and hence the smoothness
space with norm the square root of
\sum_{j}\sum_{k}2^{js}h_{j,k}^{2}
corresponds to the space
G^{s}
.

The wavelet basis can be chosen so that the scale of spaces
G^{s}
, with
s
in a bounded interval,
corresponds (partially) to the scale of Sobolev spaces.

By the preceding display
i\asymp 2^{jd}
, as
j\rightarrow\infty
, and hence

\rho_{i}\asymp i^{1/d}
. The Gaussian prior
\sum_{j}\sum_{k}2^{-j(\alpha+d/2)}Z_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}
, for
Z_{j,k}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{iid}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,1)
,
in the doubly indexed sequence space,
corresponds to the Gaussian prior
\bigl(\rho_{i}^{-\alpha-d/2}\bar{Z}_{i}\bigr)
, for
\bar{Z}_{i}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{iid}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,1)
in the sequence space.
This is the prior
\mathbb{G}
for
\rho_{i}^{-d/2}=i^{-1/2}c_{i}
, i.e. the constants
c_{i}
account for the discrepancy
between
i^{1/d}
and
\rho_{i}
.

From now on we consider a sequence

\rho_{i}
such that
c_{0}i^{1/d}\leq\rho_{i}\leq C_{0}i^{1/d}
, for constants
C_{0}\geq c_{0}>0
and some
d>0
.
The power
1/d
is included so that the index
s
of
G^{s}
can be interpreted as “smoothness”
of a function on a
d
-dimensional domain, but is irrelevant for the results. On the other hand,
the polynomial increase of
\rho_{i}
will be important for the form of the results. Allowing

\rho_{i}\asymp i^{1/d}
rather than setting exactly
\rho_{i}=i^{1/d}
and including the constants
c_{i}
in
the prior ensure that the construction applies to the “exact” weights
2^{j}
in the wavelet basis, but
is inessential for the results.

F.2
Inverse problem

Let
L
be an arbitrary separable
Hilbert space, with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{L}
.
Let
K:G^{0}\to L
be a linear operator such that, for some given
p\geq 0
and constants
C\geq c>0
,

c\|f\|_{G^{-p}}\leq\|Kf\|_{L}\leq C\|f\|_{G^{-p}},\qquad f\in G^{0}.

(F.2)

For a standard white noise process
\xi
in
L
, we observe

Y_{n}=Kf+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi.

We put the
\alpha
-regular prior
\mathbb{G}
as indicated on
f
and denote the corresponding
posterior distribution of
f
by
\Pi_{n}(f\in\cdot\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n})
.

Theorem F.1
.

Assume that
c_{0}i^{1/d}\leq\rho_{i}\leq C_{0}i^{1/d}
, for constants
C_{0}\geq c_{0}>0
and
d>0
.
Let
K:G^{0}\to L
be a linear operator satisfying (
F.2
) for every
f\in G^{0}
.
If
f_{0}\in G^{\beta}
and
0<\delta<\alpha\wedge\beta
, then there exists a constant
M
such that

\mathbb{E}_{f_{0}}\Pi_{n}\bigl(f:\|f-f_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}\leq Mn^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta-\delta)/(2\alpha+2p+d)}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\bigr)\rightarrow 1
.

For positive constants to
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
be determined later in the proof and a sequence of constants
c_{3,n}
set

\epsilon_{n}=c_{1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^{\frac{\alpha\wedge\beta+p}{2\alpha+2p+d}},\quad\eta_{n}=c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^{\frac{\alpha\wedge\beta-\delta}{2\alpha+2p+d}},\quad\rho_{j_{n}}=c_{3,n}n^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+2p+d}}.

(F.3)

By the assumption that
\rho_{i}\asymp i^{1/d}
,
we can find
j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
so that the corresponding
c_{3,n}
is bounded from below and above by positive constants.

The smoothing property of
K
implies that
K
is injective. Therefore its restriction
K:V_{j}\to L
to the
finite-dimensional subspace
V_{j}\subset G^{0}
is invertible on its range
KV_{j}
.
Let
R_{j}:L\to V_{j}
be the map
R_{j}=K^{-1}Q_{j}
formed
by first applying the orthogonal projection
Q_{j}:L\to KV_{j}
of the Hilbert space
L
onto
KV_{j}
and
next applying the inverse
K^{-1}
. (The map
R_{j}K:G^{0}\to V_{j}\subset G^{0}
is called the “Galerkin
solution” to
Kf
.)

Let
p_{f}^{(n)}
be a density of
Y_{n}
relative to a suitable dominating measure.
The proof is based on the following claims: we can choose
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
so that

(1)

The events
A_{n}:=\bigl\{\int p_{f}^{(n)}/p_{f_{0}}^{(n)}(Y_{n})\,d\Pi(f)\geq e^{-2n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}\bigr\}

satisfy
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}(A_{n}^{c})\rightarrow 0
.

(2)

\Pi\bigl(\|Kf-Kf_{0}\|_{L}<\epsilon_{n}\bigr)\geq e^{-n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
.

(3)

\Pi\bigl(\|R_{j_{n}}Kf-f\|_{G^{\delta}}>\eta_{n}\bigr)\leq e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
.

(4)

There exist
M>0
and tests
\tau_{n}
such that
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}\tau_{n}\rightarrow 0
and

P_{f}^{(n)}(1-\tau_{n})\leq e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
, for every
f\in{\cal F}_{n}
, where

{\cal F}_{n}=\bigl\{f:\|f-f_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}>M\eta_{n},\|R_{j_{n}}Kf-f\|_{G^{\delta}}\leq\eta_{n}\bigr\}.

Claim (1) is the usual evidence lower bound,
as in Lemma 8.21 of
[
16
]
. Its validity follows from the prior mass condition (2)
and the fact that the Kullback-Leibler divergence and variation between
P_{f}^{(n)}
and
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
are
equal to
n\|Kf-Kf_{0}\|_{L}^{2}/2
and twice this quantity, respectively (e.g. Lemma 8.30 in the same reference
or Lemma 9.1 in
[
19
]
).
Claim (2) is the content of Lemma
F.3
. Claims (3) and (4) are proved at the end of this proof.

Given claims (1)–(4) the proof of the theorem can be finished as follows.
We bound
\Pi_{n}\bigl(f:\|f-f_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}>M\eta_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y^{(n)}\bigr)
by

\displaystyle\tau_{n}+1_{A_{n}^{c}}+\Pi_{n}\bigl(\|R_{j_{n}}Kf-f\|_{G^{\delta}}\geq\eta_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n}\bigr)+\Pi_{n}({\cal F}_{n}\mathchar 25194\relax Y_{n})1_{A_{n}}(1-\tau_{n}).

The expectation under
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
of the first two terms tend to zero
by the first part of claim (4) and claim (1). The expectation of the third term tends
to zero by claims (3) and (2), in view of the remaining mass condition; see Theorem 8.20(iii’) in

[
16
]
. To bound the fourth term, we use Bayes’ formula to write
the posterior probability of
{\cal F}_{n}

as the quotient of
\int_{{\cal F}_{n}}p_{f}^{(n)}/p_{f_{0}}^{(n)}(Y_{n})\,d\Pi(f)
and
\int p_{f}^{(n)}/p_{f_{0}}^{(n)}(Y_{n})\,d\Pi(f)
,
and bound the denominator on the event
A_{n}
below by
e^{-2n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
. This shows that the expectation of the
fourth term is bounded above by

e^{2n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}\int_{{\cal F}_{n}}\frac{p_{f}^{(n)}}{p_{f_{0}}^{(n)}}\,d\Pi(f)(1-\tau_{n})\leq e^{2n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}\sup_{f\in{\cal F}_{n}}P_{f}^{(n)}(1-\tau_{n}).

This tends to zero by the second part of (4).

Proof of claim (3).
We apply Lemma
F.4

with
j=j_{n}
as specified at the beginning of the proof.
We choose
t
such that
bt^{2}j_{n}\rho_{j_{n}}^{2(\alpha-\delta)}=4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}
, thus
reducing the right side of the lemma to
e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
. We always have that

\eta_{n}=c_{2}(\rho_{j_{n}}/c_{3,n})^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta-\delta)}\geq c_{2}(\rho_{j_{n}}/c_{3,n})^{-(\alpha-\delta)}
. Therefore it suffices to show that
also
\eta_{n}\gtrsim t=2\sqrt{n}\epsilon_{n}j_{n}^{-1/2}\rho_{j_{n}}^{\delta-\alpha}/\sqrt{b}
. This follows, since
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}=c_{1}^{2}n^{(2\alpha-2(\alpha\wedge\beta)+d)/(2\alpha+2p+d)}

and
j_{n}\asymp n^{d/(2\alpha+2p+d)}
.

Proof of claim (4).
To construct the tests in (4), we note that observing
Y_{n}
is equivalent to observing a Gaussian
stochastic process
\bigl(Y_{n}(g):g\in L\bigr)
with mean
\bigl(\langle Kf,g\rangle_{L}:g\in L\bigr)

and covariance function
\mathbb{E}Y_{n}(g)Y_{n}(h)=\langle g,h\rangle_{L}
. In particular,
for a given orthonormal basis
(\psi_{i})_{i\leq j}
of
KV_{j}
, we observe
the variable
\sum_{i=1}^{j}Y_{n}(\psi_{i})\psi_{i}
, which we shall denote by

Q_{j}Y_{n}
. (It can formally be understood to be the projection of
Y_{n}
onto
KV_{j}
.)
It can be represented in distribution as

Q_{j}Y_{n}=Q_{j}Kf+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{j},

for the Gaussian variable
\xi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}\langle\xi,\psi\rangle_{L}\psi

with mean zero and
\mathbb{E}\langle\xi_{j},g\rangle_{L}^{2}=\|Q_{j}g\|_{L}^{2}
.
Then
R_{j}Q_{j}Y_{n}=R_{j}Kf+n^{-1/2}R_{j}\xi_{j}
is a well defined variable with values in
V_{j}\subset G^{0}
, with

R_{j}\xi_{j}
a Gaussian random element in
V_{j}\subset G^{0}
with strong and weak second moments

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\bigr\|_{G^{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{j}\|_{G^{0}}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{j}\|_{L}^{2}=\|R_{j}\|_{G^{0}}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\leq j}\langle\xi_{j},\psi_{i}\rangle_{L}^{2}=\|R_{j}\|_{G^{0}}^{2}j\lesssim\rho_{j}^{2p}j,

\displaystyle\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{0}}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\langle R_{j}\xi_{j},f\rangle_{G^{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\!\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{0}}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\langle\xi_{j},R_{j}^{*}f\rangle_{L}^{2}=\!\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{0}}\leq 1}\|Q_{j}R_{j}^{*}f\|_{L}^{2}\leq\|R_{j}^{*}\|_{G^{0}}^{2}\lesssim\rho_{j}^{2p}.

In both cases the inequality on the norms
\|R_{j}\|_{G^{0}}=\|R_{j}^{*}\|_{G^{0}}
of the operators
R_{j}:L\to G^{0}
and
its adjoint
R_{j}^{*}:G^{0}\to L
at the far right side follow from (
F.7
).
The first inequality implies that the first moment

\mathbb{E}\bigl\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\bigr\|_{G^{0}}
of the variable
\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\|_{G^{0}}
is bounded above by
\rho_{j}^{p}\sqrt{j}
.
By Borell’s inequality (e.g. Lemma 3.1 in
[
29
]
and subsequent discussion or
Proposition A.2.1 in
[
58
]
),
applied to the Gaussian random variable
R_{j}\xi_{j}
in
G^{0}
, we see that
there exist constants
a,b>0
such that, for every
t>0
,

\Pr\bigl(\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\|_{G^{0}}>a\rho_{j}^{p}\sqrt{j}+t\bigr)\leq e^{-bt^{2}/\rho_{j}^{2p}}.

Since
R_{j}\xi_{j}\in V_{j}
, we have
\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\|_{G^{\delta}}\leq\rho_{j}^{\delta}\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\|_{G^{0}}
and hence

\Pr\bigl(\|R_{j}\xi_{j}\|_{G^{\delta}}>a\rho_{j}^{p+\delta}\sqrt{j}+\rho_{j}^{\delta}t\bigr)\leq e^{-bt^{2}/\rho_{j}^{2p}}.

(F.4)

Choose
j=j_{n}
and
t^{2}=4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\rho_{j_{n}}^{2p}/b
to reduce the right side to
e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
. Then

\rho_{j_{n}}^{\delta}t=2\sqrt{n}\epsilon_{n}\rho_{j_{n}}^{p+\delta}/\sqrt{b}\simeq\sqrt{n}\eta_{n}
and

\rho_{j_{n}}^{p+\delta}\sqrt{j_{n}}\lesssim\sqrt{n}\eta_{n}
. It follows that, for a sufficiently large constant
c_{4}
,

\displaystyle P_{f}^{(n)}\bigl(\|R_{j_{n}}Y_{n}-R_{j_{n}}Kf\|_{G^{\delta}}>c_{4}\eta_{n}\bigr)\leq e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}.

(F.5)

We define

\tau_{n}=1\bigl\{\|R_{j_{n}}Y_{n}-R_{j_{n}}Kf_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}>c_{4}\eta_{n}\bigr\}.

It is immediate that
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}\tau_{n}\leq e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}\rightarrow 0
.
Since
f_{0}\in G^{\beta}
and
\delta<\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\|f_{0}-R_{j_{n}}Kf_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}\leq\rho_{j_{n}}^{-(\beta-\delta)}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}\leq c_{6}\eta_{n}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}.

(F.6)

It follows that
\|R_{j_{n}}Kf-f\|_{G^{\delta}}<\eta_{n}
implies that

\|R_{j_{n}}Kf-R_{j_{n}}Kf_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}\geq\|f-f_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}-\eta_{n}-\eta_{n}c_{6}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}.

If also
\|f-f_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}>M\eta_{n}
, then the right side is bounded below by
(M-1-c_{6}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}})\eta_{n}
, and hence

\displaystyle\|R_{j_{n}}Y_{n}-R_{j_{n}}Kf\|_{G^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\geq(M-1-c-6\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}})\eta_{n}-\|R_{j_{n}}Y_{n}-R_{j_{n}}Kf_{0}\|_{G^{\delta}}.

If
\tau_{n}=0
, then the norm on the far right is bounded above by
c_{4}\eta_{n}
.
It follows that
P_{f}^{(n)}(1-\tau_{n})\leq\Pr\bigl(\|R_{j_{n}}Y_{n}-R_{j_{n}}Kf\|_{G^{\delta}}\geq c_{5}\eta_{n}\bigr)

for
c_{5}=M-1-c_{6}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}-c_{4}
, whenever
f\in{\cal F}_{n}
. For
M\geq 1+c_{6}\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}+2c_{4}
, the latter
probability is bounded above by
e^{-4n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

If
K:G^{0}\to L
is a bounded linear operator satisfying (
F.2
) for every
f\in G^{0}
,
then the norms of the operators
R_{j}:L\to G^{0}
and
R_{j}K:G^{0}\to G^{0}
and

R_{j}K-I:G^{t}\to G^{s}
satisfy

\displaystyle\|R_{j}\|_{L\to G^{0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{j}^{p},

(F.7)

\displaystyle\|R_{j}K\|_{G^{s}\to G^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,\qquad s\geq 0,

(F.8)

\displaystyle\|R_{j}K-I\|_{G^{t}\to G^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{j}^{-(t-s)},\qquad t\geq s\geq 0.

(F.9)

For
s=0
the lemma is the same as Lemma A.2 in
[
19
]
. For the extension
of (
F.8
) to general
s\geq 0
,
we first use the triangle inequality to see that
\|R_{j}Kf\|_{G^{s}}\leq\|R_{j}Kf-P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}+\|P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}
, for

P_{j}:G^{s}\to V_{j}\subset G^{s}
the orthogonal projection onto
V_{j}
. Since
P_{j}
simply sets the coefficients
of index larger than
j
to zero, we have
\|P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}\leq\|f\|_{G^{s}}
, for any
s
. Because
R_{j}Kf-P_{j}f\in V_{j}

and
R_{j}K
is the identity on
V_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\|R_{j}Kf-P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{j}^{s}\|R_{j}K(I-P_{j})f\|_{G^{0}}\leq\rho_{j}^{s}\|R_{j}\|_{L\to G^{0}}\|K(I-P_{j})f\|_{L}

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{j}^{s}\rho_{j}^{p}\|(I-P_{j})f\|_{G^{-p}}\leq\|f\|_{G^{s}},

where we used (
F.7
) in the second last step and

\|(I-P_{j})f\|_{G^{-p}}\leq\rho_{j}^{-(p+s)}\|f\|_{G^{s}}
in the last. We conclude
that
\|R_{j}Kf\|_{G^{s}}\lesssim\|f\|_{G^{s}}
, for every
f
, which is (
F.8
).

Because
(R_{j}K-I)f=(R_{j}K-I)(I-P_{j})f
, for every
f\in G^{s}
, we have

\|(R_{j}K-I)f\|_{G^{s}}\leq\bigl(\|R_{j}K\|_{G^{s}\to G^{s}}+1\bigr)\|f-P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}.

The right side is bounded by a multiple of
\|f-P_{j}f\|_{G^{s}}\leq\rho_{j}^{-(t-s)}\|f\|_{G^{t}}
, for
t\geq s
.
This proves (
F.9
).
∎

Lemma F.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,p>0
and let

K:G^{0}\to L
be a bounded linear operator satisfying (
F.2
) for every
f\in G^{0}
.
Let
\mathbb{G}=\bigl(\rho_{i}^{-\alpha}i^{-1/2}c_{i}Z_{i}\bigr)
, for
Z_{i}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{iid}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,1)
and constants
c_{i}

that are bounded away from 0 and
\infty
.
Then for every
f_{0}\in G^{\beta}
there exists a constant
C
so that, for every
\epsilon<1
,

\log\Pr\bigl(\|K\mathbb{G}-Kf_{0}\|_{L}<C\epsilon\bigr)\geq\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\alpha-2\beta+d}{p+\beta}}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigr)^{\frac{d}{p+\alpha}}.

Consequently, there exists a constant
c_{1}
such that
\epsilon_{n}=c_{1}n^{-(\alpha\wedge\beta+p)/(2\alpha+2p+d)}
satisfies

\Pr\bigl(\|K\mathbb{G}-Kf_{0}\|_{L}<\epsilon_{n}\bigr)\geq e^{-n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}
.

By the smoothing property (
F.2
), the left side is bounded below by
\log\Pr\bigl(\|\mathbb{G}-f_{0}\|_{G^{-p}}<C_{1}\epsilon\bigr)
.
This is bounded below by, for
\mathbb{H}
the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of
\mathbb{G}
,

\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}:\|h-f_{0}\|_{G^{-p}}<C_{1}\epsilon}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}-\log\Pr\bigl(\|\mathbb{G}\|_{G^{-p}}<C_{1}\epsilon\bigr).

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space of the Gaussian variable
\mathbb{G}
consists of the sequences

\bigl(\rho_{i}^{-\alpha}i^{-1/2}c_{i}w_{i}\bigr)
, for
w\in\ell_{2}
, with square norm
\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}
, or equivalently
the sequences
(h_{i})
with square norm
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{2\alpha}ih_{i}^{2}/c_{i}
.
Since
i\simeq\rho_{i}^{d}
, this norm is equivalent to the norm of
G^{\alpha+d/2}
.
Thus the first term in the preceding display is bounded above by a multiple of

\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{2\alpha+d}h_{i}^{2}:\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{-2p}(h_{i}-f_{0,i})^{2}<\epsilon^{2}\Bigr\}.

If
\alpha+d/2\leq\beta
, we can choose
h=f_{0}
to see that the infimum is bounded above by

\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}^{2}\leq\|f_{0}\|_{G^{\beta}}^{2}
. For
\alpha+d/2>\beta
, we choose
h_{i}=f_{0,i}
for
i\leq J
and
h_{i}=0
for
i>J

with
\rho_{J}\simeq(1/\epsilon)^{1/(p+\beta)}
. Then

\displaystyle\|h-f_{0}\|_{G^{-p}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>J}\rho_{i}^{-2p}f_{0,i}^{2}\leq\rho_{J}^{-(2p+2\beta)}\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{2\beta}f_{0,i}^{2}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\|h\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq J}\rho_{i}^{2\alpha+d}f_{0,i}^{2}\leq\rho_{J}^{2\alpha+d-2\beta}\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{2\beta}f_{0,i}^{2}\lesssim\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\alpha-2\beta+d}{p+\beta}}.

This gives the first term of the lower bound.

The centered small probability can be bounded using
direct computation or from entropy bound of the unit ball of
G^{\alpha+\delta/2}
in
G^{-p}
.
∎

Lemma F.4
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=\bigl(\rho_{i}^{-\alpha}i^{-1/2}c_{i}Z_{i}\bigr)
, for
Z_{i}\,\raise-2.0pt\hbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox{iid}}}{{\sim}}$}\,N(0,1)
and constants
c_{i}

that are bounded away from 0 and
\infty
.
For all
\delta<\alpha
, there exist constants
a,b>0
so that, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
,

\Pr\bigl(\|R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G}\|_{G^{\delta}}>a\rho_{j}^{-(\alpha-\delta)}+t\bigr)\leq e^{-bt^{2}j\rho_{j}^{2(\alpha-\delta)}}.

Consequently, for every
b_{1}>0
, there exists

such that

, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

By Borell’s inequality a centered Gaussian variable
G
in a separable Banach space
satisfies
\Pr\bigl(\|G\|\gtrsim\mathbb{E}\|G\|+t\bigr)\leq e^{-bt^{2}/\sigma^{2}}
, for every
t>0
, where

\sigma^{2}=\sup_{b^{*}}\mathbb{E}(b^{*}G)^{2}
is the supremum of the second moments of the variables

b^{*}G
if
b^{*}
ranges over the unit ball of the dual space of the Banach space.
The variable
G=(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G}
is centered Gaussian in
G^{\delta}
.
Thus it suffices to show that

\mathbb{E}\|(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G}\|_{G^{\delta}}\lesssim\rho_{j}^{-(\alpha-\delta)}
and

\mathbb{E}\langle(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G},g\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\lesssim j^{-1}\rho_{j}^{-2(\alpha-\delta)}\|g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}
,
for every
g\in G^{\delta}
. Here we can bound the first strong moment by the root of the second strong moment.

We have
\langle\mathbb{G},f\rangle_{G^{\delta}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{-\alpha}i^{-1/2}c_{i}Z_{i}f_{i}\rho_{i}^{2\delta}
and hence

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbb{G},f\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{i}^{-2\alpha+4\delta}i^{-1}c_{i}^{2}f_{i}^{2}=\langle f,C_{2(\delta-\alpha)}D^{2}f\rangle_{G^{\delta}},

for
(Df)_{i}=f_{i}i^{-1/2}c_{i}
.
Therefore, as a map into
G^{\delta}
the variable
(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G}
has covariance operator

(R_{j}K-I)C_{2(\delta-\alpha)}D^{2}(R_{j}K-I)_{\delta}^{*};=SS_{\delta}^{*}
, for
S=(R_{j}K-I)C_{\delta-\alpha}D
, and

(R_{j}K-I)_{\delta}^{*}
and
S_{\delta}^{*}
the adjoint operators of
(R_{j}K-I):G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}
and
S_{\delta}:G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}
.
(The operators
C_{s}:G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}
, for
s\leq 0
, and
D^{2}:G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}
are self-adjoint, and commute,
with roots
C_{s/2}
and
D
.)

The strong second moment is equal to the trace of the covariance operator.
Therefore, for any orthonormal basis
(\phi_{i})
of
G^{\delta}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G}\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\bigl(SS_{\delta}^{*})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\langle SS_{\delta}^{*}\phi_{i},\phi_{i}\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|S_{\delta}^{*}\phi_{i}\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle S_{\delta}^{*}\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle\phi_{i},S\phi_{j}\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\|S\phi_{j}\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}.

We apply this with the orthonormal basis consisting of the sequences
\phi_{i}=e_{i}/\rho_{i}^{\delta}
, for

e_{i}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots)
the sequence with a 1 in the
i
th coordinate and zeros elsewhere, to
see that this is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl\|(R_{j}K-I)C_{\delta-\alpha}De_{i}\rho_{i}^{-\delta}\bigr\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl\|(R_{j}K-I)e_{i}\bigr\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\,\rho_{i}^{-2\alpha}i^{-1}c_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{i>j}\|R_{j}K-I\|_{G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}}^{2}\|e_{i}\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\,\rho_{i}^{-2\alpha}i^{-1}\leq\sum_{i>j}\rho_{i}^{-2(\alpha-\delta)}i^{-1},

since
R_{j}K-I
vanishes on
V_{j}
, the norm of
R_{j}K-I:G^{\delta}\to G^{\delta}
is bounded by a constant by
(
F.9
), and
\|e_{i}\|_{G^{\delta}}=\rho_{i}^{\delta}
. For
\delta<\alpha
, the right side is bounded
by a multiple of
\rho_{j}^{-2(\alpha-\delta)}
.

The weak second moment is the supremum over
\|g\|_{G^{\delta}}\leq 1
of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle(R_{j}K-I)\mathbb{G},g\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\langle g,SS_{\delta}^{*}g\rangle_{G^{\delta}}=\|S_{\delta}^{*}g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}=\|C_{\delta-\alpha}D(R_{j}K-I)_{\delta}^{*}g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\|(R_{j}K-I)_{\delta}^{*}g\|_{G^{2\delta-\alpha-d/2}}^{2}=\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}\leq 1}\langle f,(R_{j}K-I)_{\delta}^{*}g\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2},

by (
F.1
), applied with
t-s=\delta+(\delta-\alpha-d/2)
and
t+s=\delta-(\delta-\alpha-d/2)=\alpha+d/2
.
The right side is equal to

\displaystyle\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}\leq 1}\langle(R_{j}K-I)f,g\rangle_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\leq\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}\leq 1}\bigl\|(R_{j}K-I)f\bigr\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\|g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{\|f\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}\leq 1}\|R_{j}K-I\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}\to G^{\delta}}^{2}\|f\|_{G^{\alpha+d/2}}^{2}\|g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2}\leq\rho_{j}^{-2(\alpha+d/2-\delta)}\|g\|_{G^{\delta}}^{2},

by (
F.9
). Thus the weak second moment is bounded by
\rho_{j}^{-2(\alpha+d/2-\delta)}\lesssim\rho_{j}^{-2(\alpha-\delta)}j^{-1}
.

This concludes the proof of the first probability bound. The second bound follows by choosing

t=\sqrt{b_{1}/b}\,\rho_{j}^{-(\alpha-\delta)}
in the first bound.

Lemma H.5
.

Assume

The derivative of

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}(\log n)^{2}\sup_{\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]}n^{(1+4p{+4\delta})/(1+2\alpha+2p)},
