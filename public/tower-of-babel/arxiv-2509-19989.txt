Ricci Flow on Weighted Digraphs with Balancing Factor
Shuliang Bai, Rui Li, Shuang Liu, Xin Lai

Abstract
Ricci curvature and Ricci flow have proven to be powerful tools for analyzing the geometry of discrete structures, particularly on undirected graphs, where they have been applied to tasks ranging from community detection to graph representation learning. However, their development on directed graphs remains limited, with Ricci flow being especially underexplored. In this work, we introduce a rigorous formulation of Ricci flow on directed weighted graphs, which evolves edge weights while preserving distances, and establish both the existence and uniqueness of its solutions. To capture the essence of asymmetry in directed networks and to enhance the capability of modeling more flexible structures, we incorporate a node-wise balancing factor that regulates between outflow and inflow. Building on the continuous Ricci flow evolution framework, we propose a discrete Ricci flow algorithm that is applicable to numerical computing. Numerical studies on various directed graph examples demonstrate the capacity of the proposed flow to reveal structural asymmetry and dynamic evolutions.

Introduction

Graphs are pervasive in representing data structures in areas such as Internet of Things (IoT)
[
1
,
2
]
, biology
[
3
]
, social network
[
4
]
, supply chain analysis
[
5
]
, etc.
Directed graphs in particular capture causal/preferential/directional relationships between entities.
In recent years, discrete Ricci curvature has emerged as a powerful tool to analyze network structure.

Ricci curvature and Ricci flow are fundamental notions in Riemannian geometry, playing a significant role in understanding manifold geometry.
Introduced by Hamilton
[
6
]
, the Ricci flow evolves a Riemannian metric
g(t)
using Ricci curvature
\mathrm{Ric}(g)
according to

\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}=-2\,\mathrm{Ric}(g),

progressively smoothing metric irregularities and driving the manifold toward canonical geometries.
This technique led to significant breakthroughs, most notably Perelman’s resolution of the Poincarè conjecture and Thurston’s geometrization conjecture
[
7
,
8
]
.
Following these landmark results, Ricci flow has become a central tool in the study geometric evolution.

Motivated by these developments, researchers have sought discrete analogues of Ricci curvature and Ricci flow on graphs.
Several notions of discrete Ricci curvature have been developed for graphs.
Among the most widely studied are Ollivier’s Ricci curvature
[
9
]
on metric spaces using optimal transportation, Lin–Lu–Yau (LLY) curvature
[
10
]
on graphs using a limit form of Ollivier’s Ricci curvature that is better adapted to graphs, and Forman Ricci curvature
[
11
,
12
]
on graphs using combinatorial approaches.
Each of them has solid theoretical foundations and computational properties.
Ollivier and LLY curvatures maintain a strong connection to the continuous theory but require computing Wasserstein distances, which can be expensive for large networks. Forman curvature offers simplicity and scalability but lacks some geometric richness.
Building on these definitions of Ricci curvature, discrete Ricci flows have been proposed for theoretical studies and applications in network analysis, including network robustness, community detection, bottleneck identification
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
However, most existing work has focused on
undirected graphs
, where symmetric interactions simplify the underlying structure.
The study of Ricci flow on
directed graphs
, which inherently exhibit asymmetry and directional dependence, remains largely unexplored. This gap motivates this work to adapt the Ricci flow models to directed and weighted networks, capable of capturing their anisotropic geometry.

Directed weighted graphs are ubiquitous in modeling real-world systems.
They exhibit inherent
asymmetry
that significantly influences network structure and dynamics.
Recent surveys on machine learning for directed graphs highlight that geometric approaches remain far less developed than their counterparts for undirected graphs
[
16
]
.
This scarcity hinders the practical effectiveness for inherently directed data, such as road networks for traffic forecasting and control
[
17
,
18
]
, gene regulatory networks for directional biochemical reaction control
[
19
]
, and social network for information spreading analysis
[
20
]
.
Simply adapting directed data to algorithms which are designed for undirected data is critical because many complex systems cannot be accurately represented without accounting for directional dependencies and imbalanced interactions.
Incorporating directionality into Ricci curvature and Ricci flow algorithms is therefore essential for understanding anisotropic geometry, improving network robustness, and guiding structural optimization.

Furthermore, since the LLY’s Ricci curvature on graphs is based on the optimal transportation between probability distributions, to capture directional heterogeneity, we introduce a node-wise
balancing factor

\beta
, which regulates the ratio of outflow to inflow at each node.
This mechanism provides a richer representation of flow asymmetry and facilitates the directed Ricci flow adapting to both directionality and local structural imbalance.

Another notable limitation of most existing Ricci flow formulations on graphs is that they do not distinguish between
edge weight
and the
distance metric
of the network.
For instance, in the traditional Ollivier Ricci flow
[
21
]
, edge weights are updated by formulas such as

\frac{d}{dt}w_{e}(t)=-\kappa_{e}(t)\,w_{e}(t),\quad\text{or}\quad w_{xy}^{(i+1)}=\bigl(1-\epsilon\,\kappa_{xy}^{(i)}\bigr)\,d^{i}(x,y),

and other variants
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, implying that distances evolve alongside weights, coupling the network’s distance-related geometric structure with its weight-related dynamic properties.
Although such coupling has proven effective for tasks like community detection, it becomes problematic in scenarios where physical distances, such as road lengths, are inherently fixed, while weights (such as road width or capacity) evolve along Ricci flow process.
These formulations conflate the dual role of edge weights, i.e., the connection strength and metric distance, obscuring the distinct functions of them.

In this work, we introduce a Ricci flow that decouples edge weight from the metric distance, allowing the weights to evolve while maintaining the static distance metric.
This design better reflects operational dynamics in real-world systems, such as traffic networks
[
25
]
, communication systems where latency remains constant while bandwidth evolves, and transport or logistics networks where geography is immutable but flow conditions change.
In a nutshell, this separation will enable more applicable scenarios for further studies.

The main contributions of this work are threefold.
Firstly, we extend the concept of Ricci flow to
weighted directed graphs
and propose a new Ricci flow formulation that
preserves the underlying distance metric while evolving edge weights
, decoupling distance-related geometry from weight-related dynamics.
Secondly, we introduce a node-wise
balancing factor

\beta
, which flexibly regulates the bias between outflow- and inflow-based transport distributions, enabling the model to capture local directional properties and flow asymmetry.
Lastly, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the proposed Ricci flow framework for weighted directed graphs, providing a rigorous theoretical foundation for Ricci flow evolution in weighted directed networks.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews definitions and properties of directed graphs. Section
3
introduces the directed LLY’s Ricci curvature with a balancing factor. Section
4
formulates the directed Ricci flow and proves the existence and uniqueness of its solution. Section
5
gives the discrete time version of our Ricci flow formulation, making it applicable to computational network analysis. Section
6
presents illustrative examples and simulations. Finally, Section
7
concludes and outlines future directions.

Preliminaries

A directed graph (digraph)
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
consists of a finite vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}
and a set of directed edges
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
, and
n=|{\mathcal{V}}|
,
m=|{\mathcal{E}}|
.
Different from the edges in undirected graphs, a directed edge is an ordered pair of nodes, denoted as
(x,y)
or
x\to y
, where
x
is called the
tail
and
y
is called the
head
of the edge.
The digraphs in this work are simple, that is,
{\mathcal{G}}
has no loops (
x\to x
) nor multiple edges (
x\rightrightarrows y
).
In this work, we set the edge weight and distance separately, which is more flexible and in line with more application scenarios.
Set
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
as the edge weights, and
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
as the distance function.
In this part, we give the definitions and fundamental settings.

Definition 2.1

(Directed path)
.

For any
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
, a directed path from
x
to
y
is a sequence of distinct vertices, denoted as
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=v_{0}\to v_{1}\to\dots\to v_{k}
such that
v_{0}=x,v_{k}=y
, and for each
i=0,1,\dots,k-1
, the directed edge
(v_{i}\to v_{i+1})\in{\mathcal{E}}
.
The length of the path is defined as

l(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}d_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}.

The set of all directed paths connecting
x
and
y
is denoted as
{\mathbb{P}}(x,y)
.

Definition 2.2

(Directed distance)
.

For any
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the distance between them on
{\mathcal{G}}
is the shortest length of all directed path connecting them, i.e.,

d(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}(x,y)}l(p),

If there is no directed path from
x
to
y
, set
d(x,y)=\infty
.

Note that the directed distance
d(x,y)
satisfies the following properties:

•

Non-negativity:

d(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

•

Triangle inequality:

d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

•

Asymmetry:

d(x,y)
not necessarily equals
d(y,x)
for
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

The diameter of a directed graph is the length of the longest directed path, that is,

\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}}d(x,y).

A directed graph is said to be strongly connected if for any pair of vertices
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
, there exists a directed path from
x
to
y
and vice versa, thus
\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})<\infty
.
By ignoring the directions of edges, a directed graph becomes a related undirected graph. Weak connectivity of a directed graph refers to that the corresponding undirected graph is connected.
In this work, we assume all directed graphs are strongly connected.

Definition 2.3

(Neighborhood)
.

If
x\to y
is a directed edge in
{\mathcal{G}}
, then
x
is an in-neighbor of
y
, and
y
is an out-neighbor of
x
. Thus the in-neighborhood of any vertex
x\in V
is the vertices
x
is pointed to, denoted as

{\mathcal{N}}^{in}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{z\in V|z\to x\in E\}.

Similarly, the out-neighborhood indicates the set of all out-neighbors, denoted as

{\mathcal{N}}^{out}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{z\in V|x\to z\in E\}.

The neighborhood of
x
is
{\mathcal{N}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathcal{N}}^{in}(x)\cup{\mathcal{N}}^{out}(x)
.

Let
\deg^{in}_{x}=|{\mathcal{N}}^{in}(x)|
,
\deg^{out}_{x}=|{\mathcal{N}}^{out}(x)|
and
\deg_{x}=|{\mathcal{N}}(x)|
be the in-degree, out-degree and degree of
x
respectively. If
\deg_{x}=0
, then
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
is called an isolated vertex.

Definition 2.4

(
c
-Lipschitz function on directed graphs)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w,d)
be a weighted directed graph.

d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is a directed distance function.
For any real-valued function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
f
is a
c
-Lipschitz function if and only if there exists a constant
c>0
such that for any pair of vertices
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the following inequalities holds:

f(y)-f(x)\leq cd(x,y).

Specifically, if
f
is a 1-Lipschitz function, that implies
f(y)-f(x)\leq d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Let
\lipv\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}|f\text{ is 1-Lipschitz function}\}
. Note that
\lipv
is not compact because we can drift the functions in it by a constant. Chose a base point
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
\text{Lip}_{1,x_{0}}({\mathcal{V}})=\{f\in\lipv|f(x_{0})=0\}
. Then
\text{Lip}_{1,x_{0}}({\mathcal{V}})
is compact for

(1)

Uniform Boundedness
:
|f(x)|\leq|f(x)-f(x_{0})|+|f(x_{0})|\leq\max\{d(x_{0},x),d(x,x_{0})\}\leq\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})
for
\forall x\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Thus
||f||_{\infty}\leq\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})
.

(2)

Closeness
: Let
\{f_{n}\}\subset\text{Lip}_{1,x_{0}}({\mathcal{V}})
and
f_{n}\to f
as
n\to\infty
. Then
f(x_{0})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x_{0})=0
, and
\forall x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
f(y)-f(x)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}[f_{n}(y)-f_{n}(x)]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(x,y)=d(x,y)
. Thus
f\in\text{Lip}_{1,x_{0}}({\mathcal{V}})
.

3
Ricci Curvature on Directed Graphs

3.1
Probability Distribution and Laplacian

The probability distribution on directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
is a function
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}\mu(x)=1
. For any
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
in a graph, it can interact with others via edge connections. The interaction can be illustrated by a well designed probability distribution. But for directed graph, the interaction is more complicated because of the in-/out-neighboring relationships. Next, we give our definition on directed graph based on the transition perspective.

Definition 3.1

(Probability transition kernel on directed graphs)
.

Given a weighted directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w)
, for any
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
\deg_{x}\neq 0
. If
{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}\neq\emptyset
, the out probability transition kernel
P\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as:

P(x,z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{w_{xz}}{\sum_{u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\to u}w_{xu}},&x\to z;\\
&0,&otherwise.\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly, if
{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}\neq\emptyset
, the in probability transition kernel is defined as

P^{\prime}(x,z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{w_{zx}}{\sum_{u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}u\to x}w_{ux}},&z\to x;\\
&0,&otherwise.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\to[0,1]
be a flexible balance factor. The
balancing probability transition kernel
is defined as:

{\mathcal{P}}(x,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &P(x,z),&&\text{if }{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}=\emptyset\text{ and }{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}\neq\emptyset;\\
&P^{\prime}(x,z),&&\text{if }{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}=\emptyset;\\
&\beta(x)P(x,z)+(1-\beta(x))P^{\prime}(x,z),&&otherwise.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Thus,
\textnormal{supp }P(x,\cdot)={\mathcal{N}}^{out}(x)
,
\textnormal{supp }P^{\prime}(x,\cdot)={\mathcal{N}}^{in}(x)
and
\textnormal{supp }{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(x,\cdot)={\mathcal{N}}(x)
.

Thus we always have
\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathcal{P}}(x,z)=1
for
\forall x\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Since
x
is not an isolated vertex,
{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}
and
{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}
are not
\emptyset
at the same time.

Remark 3.2
.

The factor
\beta(x)
serves as a balancing factor between the contributions of the out-neighborhood and in-neighborhood of a vertex
x
in the definition of
{\mathcal{P}}(x,z)
. Specifically:

•

\beta(x)=\frac{1}{2}
assigns equal attention to both out- and in-neighbors of
x
, which is discussed in
[
26
]
;

•

\beta(x)=\frac{\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{x}}{\deg_{x}}
ensures that each incident edge of
x
, regardless of its direction, receives equal attention, and it summarizes the three cases in Equation (
1
), because
{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}=\emptyset
(
{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}=\emptyset
) implies
\deg^{in}_{x}=0
(
\deg^{out}_{x}=0
);

•

\beta(x)<\frac{1}{2}
emphasizes the
in-neighborhood
of
x
, making in-edges more efficient and likely to
decrease
in-edge weight under the Ricci flow;

•

\beta(x)>\frac{1}{2}
emphasizes the
out-neighborhood
of
x
, making out-edges more efficient and likely to
decrease
out-edge weight under the Ricci flow.

Intuitively, the choice of
\beta(x)
allows one to control the relative importance of outgoing and incoming edges in curvature computation, providing flexibility to adapt to different network structures. See Example
6.2
for an example.

Remark 3.3
.

We only talk about strongly connected graphs in this work, thus for any
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
{\mathcal{N}}^{in}_{x}
and
{\mathcal{N}}^{out}_{x}
are both nonempty. So the balancing probability transition kernel writes

{\mathcal{P}}(x,z)=\beta(x)P(x,z)+(1-\beta(x))P^{\prime}(x,z).

(2)

Definition 3.4

(Probability distribution on directed graphs)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w)
be a weighted directed graph, and
{\mathcal{P}}
be the average probability transition kernel. For any
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the probability distributions is defined as:

\mu_{x}^{\alpha}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha,&z=x;\\
&(1-\alpha){\mathcal{P}}(x,z),&otherwise,\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Definition 3.5

(Laplace operator)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w)
be a weighted directed graph, and
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a function defined on the graph. For any vertex
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the Laplace operator on directed graphs is defined as

{\mathcal{L}}f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{y\sim x}{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))=f(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)f(y),

and

\Delta f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-{\mathcal{L}}f(x),

where
y\sim x
indicates
y
is an in-/out-neighbor of
x
, i.e.,
y\in{\mathcal{N}}(x)
.

Thus
{\mathcal{L}}
is a functional operator which can be written as
{\mathcal{L}}f=(I-{\mathcal{P}})f
. Here,
(If)(x)=f(x)
for
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
is an identity operator, thus continuous.
{\mathcal{P}}
is a continuous operator, because
{\mathcal{G}}
is finite graph, then for any
f,g
, it holds that

||{\mathcal{P}}f-{\mathcal{P}}g||=\max_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left|\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)[f(y)-g(y)]\right|\leq\max_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{V}}}|{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)|\ ||f-g||_{\infty}\leq||f-g||_{\infty},

where
||f||_{\infty}=\max_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}|f(x)|
. Hence
{\mathcal{L}}
is continuous.
Similar as Lemma 3.1 in
[
26
]
, the relation between probability distribution and Laplacian holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{V}}}f(y)\mu_{x}^{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)\sum_{y\sim x}{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)f(y)

(4)

\displaystyle=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)(\Delta f(x)+f(x))

\displaystyle=f(x)+(1-\alpha)\Delta f(x).

This equation is useful when analyzing the dual of Wasserstein transportation distance, which we will discuss in the next section.

3.2
Optimal Transport and Ricci Curvature

The optimal Transport problem is related to minimize the total transportation cost between two probability distributions
[
27
]
.
To formalize this optimal problem, it is straightforward to define a transportation plan and a cost function. Let
\mu
and
\nu
are two probability distributions.
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}\to[0,+\infty)
is a
transportation plan
from
\mu
to
\nu
if it satisfies:

\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{V}}}\pi(x,y)=\mu(x),\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}\pi(x,y)=\nu(y).

We also say that
\pi
is a
coupling
from
\mu
to
\nu
.
The set of all couplings from
\mu
to
\nu
is denoted as
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, which is non-empty since there is a trivial transportation plan
\pi=\mu\otimes\nu\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
(’
\otimes
’ indicates the tensor product). The
cost function
is denoted as
c(x,y)
which indicates the costs to transport one unit from
x
to
y
. It is natural that
c
is non-negative and measurable.
The minimizing problem is formulated as:

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}}c(x,y)\pi(x,y).

(5)

The cost function
c(x,y)
is usually related to the distance between
x
and
y
.

Definition 3.6

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w,d)
is a weighted directed graph where
d
is a directed distance function on
{\mathcal{G}}
.
\mu
and
\nu
are two probability distributions on
{\mathcal{G}}
. The
\bm{L_{1}}
-Wasserstein distance
is the infimum of transportation cost, that is,

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}}\pi(x,y)d(x,y).

(6)

The optimal transportation plan is denoted as
\pi^{\ast}
.

Since the directed distance
d
is generally asymmetric, the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
is also asymmetric, i.e.,
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\neq W_{1}(\nu,\mu)
in general.
However,
W_{1}
still satisfies the non-negativity and the triangle inequality.
Note that for strongly connected finite graphs, by the duality theorem of a linear optimization problem
[
27
]
, the Wasserstein distance has a strong duality representation written as:

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in 1\text{-}Lip}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}}f(x)(\nu(x)-\mu(x)),

(7)

where
1\text{-}Lip
is the set of all 1-Lipschitz function on
{\mathcal{V}}
. Here is a useful convexity property of Wasserstein distance obtained from
[
27
]
(Section 7.4).

Lemma 3.7

(Convexity of Wasserstein distance)
.

For any four probability distributions
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

W_{1}(\lambda\mu_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{1},\lambda\mu_{2}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{2})\leq\lambda W_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+(1-\lambda)W_{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

(8)

The proof can be easily obtained by the dual formula Equation (
7
).
Similar to the definition of Ricci curvature on undirected graphs
[
10
]
, we give the definition of Ricci curvature on directed graphs.

Definition 3.8

(Ricci curvature)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w,d)
be a directed weighted graph, and
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
be any two vertices.

(1)

\bm{\alpha}
-Ricci curvature
: Given
\alpha\in[0,1]
,

\kappa_{\alpha}(x,y)=1-\frac{W_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})}{d(x,y)}.

It is trival that
\kappa_{1}(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

(2)

Lin-Lu-Yau (LLY) Ricci curvature
:

\kappa(x,y)=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}(x,y)}{1-\alpha}

Note that the weights
w
and distance
d
are defined independently.
We prove this is well-defined and can be expressed in terms of a discrete gradient of the Laplacian.

Lemma 3.9

(Concavity)
.

For any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let
\bar{\alpha}=\lambda\alpha_{1}+(1-\lambda)\alpha_{2}
. Then for
\forall x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
,

\kappa_{\bar{\alpha}}(x,y)\geq\lambda\kappa_{\alpha_{1}}(x,y)+(1-\lambda)\kappa_{\alpha_{2}}(x,y).

By the convexity of Wasserstein distance, the concavity of
\kappa_{\alpha}
holds as in
[
10
,
26
]
.
Then we get that
\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}
is an increasing function.
According to the concavity of
\kappa_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\kappa^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}\leq 0
.
Let
f(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}
, then
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{\kappa^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1-\alpha)+\kappa_{\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}
. Let
g(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\kappa^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1-\alpha)+\kappa_{\alpha}
, then
g^{\prime}(\alpha)=\kappa^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}(1-\alpha)\leq 0
. Thus
g(\alpha)
is decreasing. We know that
g(1)=\kappa_{1}=0
, then
g(\alpha)\geq 0
. Thus
f^{\prime}(\alpha)\geq 0
.

Next we show that
\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}
is upper bounded. In 2011, Lin-Lu-Yau
[
10
]
proved that
\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\leq\frac{2}{d(x,y)}
on undirected graphs. Ozawa
et al.

[
26
]
generalized it to directed graphs in 2020. In this work, we generalized the definition of probability transition kernel using a balancing factor
\beta
, but the upper bounded property still holds.

Lemma 3.10

(Upper bounded)
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}(x,y)}{1-\alpha}\leq\frac{2\textnormal{\text{diam}}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}
.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{x}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1
when
z=x
and
\delta_{x}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=0
otherwise.
Notice that

W_{1}(\delta_{x},\mu_{x}^{\alpha})+W_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})+W_{1}(\mu_{y}^{\alpha},\delta_{y})\geq W_{1}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y}).

Thus

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle\geq W_{1}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})-W_{1}(\delta_{x},\mu_{x}^{\alpha})-W_{1}(\mu_{y}^{\alpha},\delta_{y})

\displaystyle=d(x,y)-W_{1}(\delta_{x},\mu_{x}^{\alpha})-W_{1}(\mu_{y}^{\alpha},\delta_{y}).

Where

\displaystyle W_{1}(\delta_{x},\mu_{x}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{N}}(x)}(1-\alpha){\mathcal{P}}(x,z)d(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{N}}(x)}{\mathcal{P}}(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}}).

Similarly,
W_{1}(\mu_{y}^{\alpha},\delta_{y})\leq(1-\alpha)\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})
.
Then we have

W_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})\geq d(x,y)-2(1-\alpha)\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}}).

Here we have

\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}(x,y)}{1-\alpha}\leq\frac{2\textnormal{\text{diam}}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}.

∎

Based on the concavity and upper-bounded property, we know that the Lin-Lu-Yau’s Ricci curvature on a directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},w,d)
is well-defined.
Moreover, there exists an equivalent expression for the directed Ricci curvature that does not rely on limit operation. To introduce this formulation, we first define the gradient operator on directed graphs:

\nabla_{xy}f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{d(x,y)}

Using the Laplacian operator defined earlier, we now give a limit-free expression for the Lin-Lu-Yau’s Ricci curvature on directed graphs.

Theorem 3.11

(Limit-free
\kappa
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a directed graph. For any two distinct vertices
x,y\in V
, the Lin-Lu-Yau’s Ricci curvature from
x
to
y
satisfies:

\kappa(x,y)=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{xy}}\nabla_{xy}\mathcal{L}f

where

\mathcal{F}_{xy}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\in\lipv\mid\nabla_{xy}f=1\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{xy}
is not compact. If we set a base point
y
s.t.
f(y)=0
, then
\mathcal{F}_{xy}
is compact since boundedness is straightforward, and closeness yields because when
\{f_{n}\}\in\mathcal{F}_{xy}
,
f_{n}\to f
as
n\to\infty
, then
f(y)-f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(y)-\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(f_{n}(y)-f_{n}(x))=d(x,y)
.

Münch and Wojciechowski firstly proposed this limit-free representation of Lin-Lu-Yau’s Ricci curvature in
[
28
]
, and Ozawa
et al.
followed the same line to prove this representation in directed graphs
[
26
]
.
We leverage the same technique as in
[
26
]
to prove this theorem, and carefully check that the proof still holds for our generalized definition of probability transition kernel using a balancing factor
\beta
.
Before proving this theorem, we first introduce a useful lemma from
[

]

Lemma 3.12

(

By Equation (

Taking

\kappa(x,y)\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{xy}}\nabla_{xy}\mathcal{L}f

To prove the opposite inequality, let

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(1-\nabla_{xy}f\right)+\nabla_{xy}{\mathcal{L}}f

This indicates that

eliminates a constant drift for

(10)

Next, we are going to verify the continuity of

on

, thus

•

\nabla_{xy}(\cdot)

Hence

By compactness of

Using the compactness of

Now we take a closer look at Equation (

On the left hand side of Equation (

On the right hand side of Equation (

(11)

Then

\displaystyle|\nabla_{xy}{\mathcal{L}}f|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{{\mathcal{L}}f(y)-{\mathcal{L}}f(x)}{d(x,y)}\right|

4
Ricci Flow on Directed Graphs

Given a directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\bm{w}},d)
, and
\kappa_{e}(t)
is the directed Lin-Lu-Yau’s Ollivier Ricci curvature. Let
m=|{\mathcal{E}}|
. Ricci flow deforms the graph weights
{\bm{w}}(t)
according to the Ricci curvature on edges, while the distance
d
between vertex pairs remain unchanged. Formally, we define the Ricci flow as follow:

Definition 4.1

(Ricci flow on directed graphs)
.

For continuous time
t\in[0,+\infty)
, the edge weights on
{\mathcal{G}}
at time
t
is denoted as
{\bm{w}}(t)=(w_{e_{1}}(t),w_{e_{2}}(t),\dots,w_{e_{m}}(t))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{+}

{\bm{w}}^{0}=((w_{e_{1}}^{0},w_{e_{2}}^{0},\dots,w_{e_{m}}^{0})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{e_{i}}^{0}=1
. Then the Ricci flow on directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}
is formulated as the following ODE:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\bm{w}}(0)={\bm{w}}^{0}\\
\frac{dw_{e}(t)}{dt}=-\kappa_{e}(t)w_{e}(t)+w_{e}(t)\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}(t)w_{h}(t).\end{array}\right.

(13)

Equation (
13
) is a scaling normalized Ricci flow of the unnormalized version:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{{\bm{w}}}(0)=\tilde{{\bm{w}}}^{0}\\
\frac{d\tilde{w}_{e}(t)}{dt}=-\kappa_{e}(t)\tilde{w}_{e}(t).\end{array}\right.

(14)

The volume of a graph is defined as
\text{Vol}(t)=\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{e}(t)
. Thus the normalized Ricci flow in Equation (
13
) preserves total volume, because
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{e_{i}}(0)=1
, and

\displaystyle\frac{d\text{Vol}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}\left[-\kappa_{e}(t)w_{e}(t)+w_{e}(t)\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}(t)w_{h}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{e}(t)w_{e}(t)+\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{e}(t)\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}(t)w_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=0.

The solution to Equation (
13
) (denoted as
{\bm{w}}(t)=\{w_{e}(t)\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
) and the solution of Equation (
14
) (denoted as
\tilde{{\bm{w}}}(t)=\{\tilde{w}_{e}(t)\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
) have a relation
w_{e}(t)=\tfrac{\tilde{w}_{e}(t)}{\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}
. The evolution process of curvature
\kappa_{e}
are the same because it is not sensitive to scaling change. Readers may refer to
[
21
]
for more details.

(18)

\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})=\inf_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{xy}^{\ast}}g(f{\bm{w}})
, we show that
|\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})-\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})|\leq L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}
.
Since
g(f,{\bm{w}})
is continuous for
f
in the compact set
{\mathcal{F}}_{xy}^{\ast}
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
f_{\epsilon}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{xy}^{\ast}
such that
g(f_{\epsilon},{\bm{w}})<\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})+\epsilon
. Thus by Equation (
18
),

\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})\leq g(f_{\epsilon},{\bm{w}}^{\prime})\leq g(f_{\epsilon},{\bm{w}})+L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}<\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})+\epsilon+L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}.

As
\epsilon\to 0
,
\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})+L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}
. By exchanging
{\bm{w}}
and
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
, we get the opposite inequality
\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})\leq\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})+L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}
. Thus

|\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})-\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})|\leq L_{g}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty},

(19)

where
L_{g}=\frac{2L_{{\mathcal{P}}}\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}
.

•

|\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})|
is bounded.
As showed in Equation (
12
) and Theorem
3.11
,

|\kappa_{e}({\bm{w}})|=\left|\inf_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{xy}^{\ast}}\nabla_{xy}{\mathcal{L}}f\right|\leq\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{xy}^{\ast}}\left|\nabla_{xy}{\mathcal{L}}f\right|\leq\frac{2\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}.

(20)

Equation (
15
) writes

|h_{e}({\bm{w}})-h_{e}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{2\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}(L_{\mathcal{P}}+1)||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=L_{h}||{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}||_{\infty},

where
L_{h}=\frac{2\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})}{d(x,y)}(\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}+1)
.

Thus
F({\bm{w}},t)
is continuous w.r.t.
t
, and local Lipschitz w.r.t.
{\bm{w}}
. There exists a unique solution for ODE system in Equation (
13
) for
t\in[0,T]
.

2. Long time existence.

According to the Extension Theorem
[
29
]
, let

T^{\ast}=\sup_{T>0}\{Equation(\ref{eq:continue_ricci_flow})\text{ has unique solution in }[0,T]\},

and
[0,T^{\ast})
be the maximal interval of existence, then
T^{\ast}<+\infty
only if
|{\bm{w}}(t)|\to 0
or
|{\bm{w}}(t)|\to+\infty
as
t\to T^{\ast-}
.
Let
d_{min}=\min_{u,v\in{\mathcal{E}}}d(u,v)
and
d_{max}=\max_{u,v\in{\mathcal{E}}}d(u,v)=\text{diam}(G)
. For any
e=(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}
, according to the boundedness of
\kappa_{e}
in Equation (
20
), we have

-\tfrac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}\leq-\tfrac{2\text{diam}(G)}{d(x,y)}\leq\kappa_{e}\leq\tfrac{2\text{diam}(G)}{d(x,y)}\leq\tfrac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}.

Then then evolution of edge weight in Equation (
13
) is bounded by:

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{e}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{e}(t)-w_{e}(t)\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\frac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}\left(1+\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{h}(t)\right)w_{e}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{e}(t),

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{e}(t)}{dt}\leq\frac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{e}(t)+w_{e}(t)\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\frac{2d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{h}(t)=\frac{4d_{max}}{d_{min}}w_{e}(t),

which means
{\bm{w}}_{e}(t)
is bounded by:

w_{e}(0)e^{-\frac{4d_{max}}{d_{min}}}\leq w_{e}(t)\leq w_{e}(0)e^{\frac{4d_{max}}{d_{min}}}.

Here we have
T^{\ast}=+\infty
, hence Equation (
13
) has unique solution for
t\in[0,+\infty).

∎

5
Discrete Ricci flow on directed graphs

5.1
Limit-free Curvature

The Lin–Lu–Yau (LLY) curvature has attracted significant attention in recent years due to its ability to capture geometric and functional properties of complex networks. In particular, the limit-free formulation of LLY curvature has been extensively applied to undirected graphs
[
21
,
23
,
14
]
, providing efficient and practical tools for curvature-based analysis without relying on limiting processes. Motivated by these developments, and in order to facilitate computations, we extend a limit-free definition of curvature to directed graphs, which is similar to the undirected version in
[
30
]
.

Definition 5.1

(
\ast
-coupling on directed graphs)
.

Given a directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\bm{w}},d)
, for any
z\in{\mathcal{V}}
, denote
\mu_{z}^{0}
as
\mu_{z}
. For any distinct nodes
x,y\in{\mathcal{V}}
, coupling
B
is called an
\ast
-coupling if it satisfies:

1.

B(x,y)>0
, and
B(u,v)\leq 0
if
u\neq x
or
v\neq y
.

2.

\sum_{u,v\in{\mathcal{V}}}B(u,v)=0
.

3.

\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}B(u,v)=-\mu_{x}(u)
for
\forall u\neq x
.

4.

\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}B(u,v)=-\mu_{y}(v)
for
\forall v\neq y
.

Moreover,
\ast
-coupling based Lin-Lu-Yau Ricci curvature is defined as:

\kappa^{\ast}(x,y)=\frac{1}{d(x,y)}\sup_{B}\sum_{u,v\in{\mathcal{V}}}B(u,v)d(u,v).

(21)

Similar to
[
30
]
,
\kappa^{\ast}
in Equation (
21
) is a limit-free dual of
\kappa
in Definition
3.8
.
Although the distance is directed and the definition of probability distribution in Definition
3.4
is different, the proof of Theorem 2.14 in
[
30
]
still holds.

5.2
Discrete Ricci Flow

While the continuous Ricci flow on directed graphs is formulated as the system of ODEs in Definition Definition
4.1
, for practical implementations, especially in computational settings, we often need a discrete-time approximation. As the discretization process in
[
23
]
, the idea is to update edge weights iteratively in each iteration by small steps, following the gradient induced by the curvature.

Definition 5.2

(Discrete Ricci Flow on Directed Graphs)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\bm{w}},d)
be a finite directed graph with positive edge weights
{\bm{w}}=(w_{e})_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
normalized such that
\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{e}=1
. Given an initial weight vector
{\bm{w}}^{0}
, the discrete Ricci flow is defined for a sequence of discrete time
t=0,1,2,\dots,T
with step size
s>0
by the iteration:

w_{e}^{t+1}=w_{e}^{t}-w_{e}^{t}\cdot s\Big(\kappa_{e}^{t}-\overline{\kappa}^{t}\Big),\qquad e\in{\mathcal{E}},

(22)

where

\overline{\kappa}^{t}=\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}^{t}\,w_{h}^{t}

is the weighted average curvature at time step
t
.

Remark 5.3
.

The update rule Equation (
22
) is an explicit discretization of the continuous Ricci flow ODE Equation (
13
).

•

It ensures positivity of the weights, i.e.,
w_{e}^{t+1}\geq 0
iff

1-s\big(\kappa_{e}^{t}-\overline{\kappa}^{t}\big)\;\geq\;0

for all
e\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
In particular, according to Equation (
20
), denote

K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{2\text{diam}({\mathcal{G}})}{\min_{e=(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}}\{d(x,y)\}},

then
|\kappa_{e}^{t}|\leq K
uniformly at time
t
.
Thus
\kappa_{e}^{t}-\overline{\kappa}^{t}\leq 2K
and any
s\leq\frac{1}{2K}
preserves nonnegativity of
w_{e}^{t+1}
.
(If
\kappa_{e}^{t}-\overline{\kappa}^{t}\leq 0
the positivity of
w_{e}^{t+1}
always holds).

•

The volume of the graph still preserves as
\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{V}}}w_{e}^{t+1}=1
, making it acts as a proper weight in the average curvature
\overline{\kappa}^{t}
.

Remark 5.4
.

In Definition
5.2
, we assumed the total edge weight (“volume”) is normalized to
1
, i.e.
\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{e}=1
. This normalization is
not essential
. One may equivalently work with any fixed total volumne

\sigma\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\;\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}w_{e}\;>\;0,

provided the average curvature is defined with the same normalization. Concretely, put

\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma}(t)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\;\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}(t)\,w_{h}(t).

Then the modified continuous flow preserving total volumne
\sigma
reads

\frac{dw_{e}(t)}{dt}=-\kappa_{e}(t)\,w_{e}(t)+\frac{w_{e}(t)}{\sigma}\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}(t)\,w_{h}(t)=w_{e}(t)\big(-\kappa_{e}(t)+\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma}(t)\big).

(23)

The discrete Equation (
22
) is modified analogously:

w_{e}^{t+1}\;=\;w_{e}^{t}-s\,w_{e}^{t}\big(\kappa_{e}^{t}-\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma}^{t}\big),\qquad\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma}^{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\kappa_{h}^{t}\,w_{h}^{t}.

(24)

The solution to Equation (
23
) (denoted as
{\bm{w}}(t)=\{w_{e}(t)\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
) and the solution of Equation (
14
) (denoted as
\tilde{{\bm{w}}}(t)=\{\tilde{w}_{e}(t)\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
) have a relation
w_{e}(t)=\tfrac{\sigma}{\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}\tilde{w}_{e}(t)
, since

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{e}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sigma\cdot\frac{d\frac{\tilde{w}_{e}(t)}{\sum_{h}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}}{dt}=\sigma\cdot\left(\frac{d\tilde{w}_{e}(t)}{\sum_{h}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)dt}-\frac{\tilde{w}_{e}(t)\sum_{h}\frac{d\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}{dt}}{\left(\sum_{h}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\sum_{h}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}\left(-\kappa_{e}(t)\tilde{w}_{e}(t)\right)+\frac{\sigma\tilde{w}_{e}(t)}{\left(\sum_{h}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)\right)^{2}}\sum_{h}\kappa_{h}(t)\tilde{w}_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{e}(t)w_{e}(t)+\frac{w_{e}(t)}{\sigma}\sum_{h}\kappa_{h}(t)w_{h}(t).

where
\kappa_{e}(t)
are the same in both equations by scaling change of weights for any
t\geq 0
, and the volume
\sum_{e}w_{e}(t)=\sum_{e}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sum_{h\in{\mathcal{E}}}\tilde{w}_{h}(t)}\tilde{w}_{e}(t)=\sigma
for all
t\geq 0
.

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{v_{1}}^{\alpha},\mu_{v_{3}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=[\alpha-\tfrac{3}{4}(1-\alpha)]d(v_{1},v_{3})=\alpha-\tfrac{3}{4}(1-\alpha).

leading to

\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=1-\alpha,\quad\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{2},v_{3})=1-\alpha,\quad\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{3},v_{1})=\tfrac{7}{4}(1-\alpha),\quad\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{3})=\tfrac{7}{4}(1-\alpha).

Thus, the Lin-Lu-Yau curvatures are

\kappa(v_{1},v_{2})=\kappa(v_{2},v_{3})=1,\quad\kappa(v_{3},v_{1})=\kappa(v_{1},v_{3})=\tfrac{7}{4},

and the average curvature is
\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma}=\tfrac{11}{8}
.
According to the Ricci flow equation, we have for each edge
e_{i}\in{\mathcal{E}}
:

w_{e}(t)=w_{e}^{0}\exp\big(-t(\kappa_{e}-\overline{\kappa}_{\sigma})\big).

increases from

,
w_{e_{1}}
and
w_{e_{2}}
increase simultaneously, while
w_{e_{3}}
and
w_{e_{4}}
decrease simultaneously.

(First row), note that the nonsymmetric essence of the graph is not reflected in this case, as the flow pattern is symmetric,

and
w_{e_{2}}
behave exactly the same, and so do

Case 2:
\beta(x)=\frac{d^{out}_{x}}{d_{x}}
.

Each node treats out-neighbors and in-neighbors proportionally to their degrees, i.e., every edge, regardless of direction, is treated equally.
Specifically,
\beta(v_{1})=\tfrac{2}{3},\;\beta(v_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2},\;\beta(v_{3})=\tfrac{1}{3}

At
t=0

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{v_{1}}^{\alpha},\mu_{v_{2}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=[\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)]d(v_{1},v_{2})+\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\alpha)d(v_{3},v_{2})=\alpha-\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\alpha),

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{v_{2}}^{\alpha},\mu_{v_{3}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\alpha)d(v_{2},v_{1})+[\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)]d(v_{2},v_{3})=\alpha-\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\alpha),

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{v_{3}}^{\alpha},\mu_{v_{1}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=[\alpha-\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\alpha)]d(v_{3},v_{1})=\alpha-\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\alpha),

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{v_{1}}^{\alpha},\mu_{v_{3}}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=[\alpha-\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\alpha)]d(v_{1},v_{3})=\alpha-\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\alpha).

leading to

\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{2})=\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{2},v_{3})=1-\alpha+\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\alpha),\quad\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{3},v_{1})=\kappa_{\alpha}(v_{1},v_{3})=1-\alpha+\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\alpha).

and

Compare to Case 1, by observing the evolution of weights, we find that curvatures and weights in Case 2 converge faster than those in Case 1, i.e., they seem to reach a steady state in a shorter time.

Remark 6.3
.

The above example illustrates how the balancing factor
\beta
influences the Ricci curvature and flow on a directed graph, highlighting its role in capturing the asymmetry of directed edges.
By adjusting
\beta
, we can model different transport preferences at each node, leading to distinct models of flow evolution.
We only presented four specific cases for clarity, but in practice,
\beta
can be set to any value in
[0,1]
for each node.
This flexibility is crucial for accurately representing and analyzing real-world directed networks, where nodes may have varying tendencies towards sending or receiving flow.

Apart from the flexibility incorporated by the balancing factor
\beta
, another key feature of our proposed directed Ricci flow is that the weights evolving while the distances remain unchanged.
For application perspective, this is crucial in scenarios where the physical or geographical distances between nodes are fixed, but the weights (e.g., traffic loads, communication costs) can vary over time.
Other graph Ricci flow approaches that conflate edge weights with distances cannot capture this distinction, limiting their utility for real-world dataset modeling, while our method effectively addresses this challenge.

7
Conclusion

We proposed a Ricci flow framework for
weighted directed graphs
, proved the existence and uniqueness of its solution, introduced a node-wise balancing factor
\beta
to capture directional asymmetry, and designed a distance-preserving flow that decouples geometry from dynamics. This approach better models real-world systems where physical distances remain fixed but flow conditions evolve.

Future research will proceed along both theoretical and practical directions. Theoretically, further investigation into the convergence behavior of the proposed Ricci flow and a deeper analysis of the role of the balancing factor
\beta
in shaping network evolution are of particular interest. Practically, applying this framework to real-world networks, such as transportation systems, communication networks, and microbial interaction networks, etc., will uncover more geometric structural properties.
