The potential of ruxolitinib as a senomorphic therapy in alleviating neovascular age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Yinhao Wang, Zhihua Huang, Yulin Tseng, Huiling Ma, Kuangqi Chen, Xinlei Pan, Hui Ye, Tiantian Cheng, Jiayun Ge, Jingcheng Deng, Xinglin Wang, Jianhua Xia, Jianqin Shen, Chixin Du, Wei Lei, Zebin Mao, Hongguang Cui, Yan Sheng

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCellular senescence plays a key role in the pathogenesis of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Senomorphic therapy, which targets the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), holds promise for treating age-related diseases; however, effective senomorphic treatments for nAMD remain lacking.MethodsWe screened a panel of senomorphic agents in bleomycin- and ionizing radiation (IR)-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells to identify a lead compound for nAMD. Bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed to explore JAK-STAT signaling activation. Ruxolitinib and siRNA were used to validate the role of the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway in senescent retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). A laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) model was established in Brown-Norway rats for in vivo studies.ResultsRuxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor, most effectively suppressed SASP factors and complement C3 expression while restoring pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) levels in senescent ARPE-19 cells. Knockdown of JAK1 or JAK2 significantly reversed the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), C3, and PEDF. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed activation of the JAK-STAT pathway in nAMD patient samples, CNV models, and senescent RPE cells, which was corroborated in the CNV rat model. Inhibition of JAK1/2-STAT3 with ruxolitinib markedly reduced vascular leakage and CNV area, and accelerated recovery of retinal function. The combination of ruxolitinib with anti-VEGF therapy presented a better effect on inhibiting CNV area. Furthermore, intravitreal injection of ruxolitinib showed no significant structural or functional toxicity in normal rat retinas over one month.ConclusionsOur study identifies the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway as a key regulator of the SASP in senescent RPE and confirms its activation in nAMD and experimental CNV. Pharmacologic inhibition of this pathway with ruxolitinib effectively attenuates CNV progression with a favorable short-term safety profile, supporting its potential as a senomorphic therapy for nAMD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08705-x.

Background

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of severe vision loss globally [1]. Neovascular AMD (nAMD) is one form of advanced AMD, which greatly damaged the vision of patients [2]. The main pathological feature of neovascular AMD is macular neovascularization, which is mostly originated from choroidal capillaries, named as choroidal neovascularization (CNV). While anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy is the first-line treatment, a substantial proportion of patients (over 60%) show limited visual improvement, underscoring the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies [3, 4].

Accumulating evidence links nAMD pathogenesis to cellular senescence [5–7]. Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed an enrichment of senescence-like retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells within CNV lesions from nAMD patients [8]. Preclinical studies further support this link; ablation of senescent cells with senolytic agents attenuates CNV in murine models [9]. However, the potential for retinal atrophy following widespread senescent cell elimination raises safety concerns, highlighting the need for alternative approaches.

Cellular senescence, a stable cell-cycle arrest triggered by stress, is defined not only by growth cessation but also by a profound secretome shift. Senescent cells secrete a plethora of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases, collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [10]. The SASP drives diverse pathophysiological processes, including chronic inflammation, tissue remodeling, and aberrant angiogenesis [11]. Targeting the SASP without eliminating senescent cells has thus emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for age-related diseases, which is named as senomorphic drugs [12].

The JAK/STAT signaling pathway is a key regulator of the SASP [13]. Its inhibition can ameliorate the senescent phenotype, suppress SASP factor expression, and has been shown to extend healthspan, reduce tissue inflammation, and mitigate various senescence-associated pathologies [13–16]. Ruxolitinib (Ruxo), a specific JAK1/2 inhibitor approved for myelofibrosis, represents a clinically translatable candidate for such an approach [17].

Based on this rationale, we screened a panel of senomorphic agents and identified Ruxo for its superior efficacy in suppressing the pro-angiogenic SASP in vitro. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of Ruxo, investigating its mechanism of action and efficacy in a laser-induced CNV mouse model.

Methods

Cell culture and senescence induction

Adult retinal pigment epithelial cell line-19 (ARPE-19) was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibico, C11995500BT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, TransGen, FS301-02 C, China), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin. Senescence was induced using bleomycin or ionizing radiation (IR). For bleomycin treatment, cells grown in 10-cm dishes to approximately 50% confluence were incubated with complete growth medium containing 50 µg/ml bleomycin for 24 h, after which the drug-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. For IR-induced senescence, cells received a single dose of 10 Gy (145 s). Following either treatment, cells were maintained in complete medium, refreshed every 2–3 days, for an additional 7 days to establish a senescent phenotype before subsequent experiments.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

Cellular senescence was assessed using an SA-β-gal staining kit (Beyotime, C0602, China). Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and incubated with the staining solution overnight at 37 °C in a CO₂-free incubator. After washing with PBS, stained cells were visualized and imaged using an inverted microscope.

Screening of senomorphic agents

A panel of six senomorphic agents was screened: rapamycin (400 nM), SB203580 (40 µM), Ruxo (20 µM), MM-102 (100 µM), MCC950 (20 µM), and H-151 (5 µM). Compounds were added to the culture medium of senescent ARPE-19 cells (5 days post-induction) and incubated for 48 h. Total RNA was then extracted, and the mRNA expression of key SASP factors (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MMP3, VEGF), along with pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) and complement C3, was analyzed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, 15596018, USA). Reverse transcription was performed with the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (TOYOBO, FSQ-301, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Resulting cDNA was amplified with gene-specific primers (listed in supplementary Table S1) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotech, 11202ES08, China) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA expression was calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B, China) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, B14001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail I/II (MedChemExpress, HY-K0021/HY-K0022, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 12% gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were probed with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime, P0018M, China). The antibodies were listed in supplementary Table S2. Three biological replicates for western blotting were conducted to confirm the activation of JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway and the senescence of ARPE-19 cells.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Culture supernatants from ARPE-19 cells were collected on days 5–7 after induction with bleomycin or IR. The concentrations of VEGF, complement C3, and PEDF were measured using commercial ELISA kits for human VEGF (Servicebio, GEH0022, China), human complement C3 (Elabscience, E-EL-H6054, China), and human PEDF (Elabscience, E-EL-H1875, China), following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, MedChemExpress, HY-K0301, USA) was used to test cell viability of ARPE-19. Briefly, cells were added to 96-well plate. When the cells reached a convergence of 50%, 50 ng/µl bleomycin was added for 24 h. Then the cells were culture for another 5 days in complete medium. A series of gradient concentration of ruxolitinib (160, 80, 40, 20 and 10 µM) was subsequently used to treat bleomycin-treated ARPE-19 cells for 48 h. After washed with PBS, 100 µl FBS-free DMEM with 10 µl CCK-8 regent was added for 1 h, and the OD450 was measured by BioTek Synergy Neo2 Hybrid Multimode Reader (USA).

TUNEL assay

One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1086, China) was used for apoptosis testing. In brief, bleomycin-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells, treated with or without ruxolitinib for 48 h, were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min. After incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100, and washed with PBS, cells were incubated in TUNEL detection solution at 37 ℃ in dark for 1 h.

Transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNA)

siRNA transfection was carried out with Hieff Trans® in vitro siRNA/miRNA Transfection Reagent (Yeasen, 40806ES01, China). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, 30 pmol of siRNA and 4 µL of transfection reagent were separately diluted in 100 µL of OPTI-MEM, mixed gently, and incubated to form transfection complexes. The complexes were added dropwise to ARPE-19 cells at 50% confluence. Subsequently, 500 µL of complete DMEM was added, and cells were incubated under standard conditions. To minimize potential off-target effects, three distinct siRNAs were designed for each gene, and the most potent siRNA was selected for subsequent functional assays. STAT3 overexpression by lentivirus transfection

ARPE-19 cells were seeded into 6-cm culture dishes until they reached 50% confluence, then treated with fresh culture medium containing 5 µg/ml polybrene and lentivirus. After 72 h, cells overexpressing STAT3 were selected using puromycin.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Bleomycin was added in the medium for 24 h to induce senescence of ARPE-19 cells. Ruxolitinib and vehicle were added to the culture medium of senescent ARPE-19 cells (5 days post-induction) and incubated for 48 h. Total RNA was then extracted and prepared for bulk-RNA sequencing by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co.,Ltd. Data analysis was performed using the Majorbio Cloud (www.majorbio.com) [18]. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The sequencing data is available in GEO database (GSE326364).

GEO data mining

The characteristics of the GEO datasets are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Bulk RNA-seq datasets from nAMD patients and CNV models (GSE146887, GSE129743) were downloaded from the GEO database. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software (v4.2.2). The gene sets used in the GSEA analysis were obtained from https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp. A result with |Normalized enrichment score (NES)| > 1, P-value < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25 was considered statistically significant. A single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE271262) was also obtained from GEO. Low-quality cells (< 200 genes/cell, < 500 transcripts/cell, > 70,000 transcripts/cell, or > 15% mitochondrial genes) and genes expressed in fewer than 10 cells were filtered using the Seurat package (v5.1.0). Gene expression levels were normalized using the “NormalizeData” function with the LogNormalize method. A total of 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was performed to visualize the clustering results. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony package (v1.2.4). Doublets were identified using the DoubletFinder R package (v2.0.4). Cell clusters were annotated based on following marker genes: rod (Rho), cone (Arr3), Müller glia (Gpr37), cone bipolar cells (CBCs) (Grik1), rod bipolar cells (RBCs) (Sebox), endothelial cells (Cdh5), horizontal cells (Cntnap2), microglia/macrophage (Aif1), RPE (Rpe65), retinal ganglion cells (Snca), fibroblasts (Col1a1), melanocytes (Mlana), pericytes (Rgs5), Schwann cells (Plp1), epithelial cells (Epcam), and T cells (Cd3d). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted using the GSVA package (v2.0.7). The SenMayo gene set was obtained from a previous study [19], and other GSVA-based gene sets were retrieved from the GSEA website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine and conformed to the Association for Research in Vision Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Male Brown Norway rats (aged 2–3 months) were used in this study. Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) was established as previously described [20], using a 532-nm laser with settings of 340 mW power, 0.05 s duration, and a 50 μm spot size. Under a contact cover slip, six to seven laser spots were applied peripapillary at a distance of two disc diameters from the optic disc in each eye. Successful disruption of Bruch’s membrane was confirmed by intraoperative bubble formation or slight bleeding; only these eyes/spots were retained for analysis. To ensure masking, randomization was performed using a random number table after laser induction. Animals were assigned to receive either 5 µl of Ruxo (200 ng/ml or 10 ng/µl) or vehicle via intravitreal injection on days 2, 5, 8, and 11 post-injury, with 4–6 rats per group. For comparative analysis with standard anti-VEGF therapy, animals were randomized into four groups receiving a single intravitreal injection at day 7 post-laser: 5 µl of vehicle, Conbercept (10 mg/ml), Ruxo (10 ng/µl), and the combination of Conbercept and Ruxo.

Retina and RPE-choroid flatmount staining

Eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h at room temperature. After removal of the cornea and lens, the retina and RPE-choroid-sclera complex were carefully separated and rinsed with ICC buffer [9]. For whole-retina staining, tissues were incubated with Hoechst 33,342 (KeyGEN, KGA1030, China), washed, mounted, and coverslipped. For RPE-choroid complexes, tissues were blocked with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0260, China), incubated with isolectin B4 and primary antibodies overnight, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies and Hoechst 33,342 counterstain. Images were analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

H&E staining

Tissue sections were stained using a standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0105S, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded eye sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating in EDTA buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂. Sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, incubated with anti-pSTAT3 antibody (Abclonal, AP0705, China) overnight at 4 °C, followed by a CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 60 min in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (MedChemExpress, 28718-91-4, USA), and sections were mounted for imaging.

Fundus imaging, OCT, FFA, and OCTA

Fundus images were acquired using a panoramic ophthalmoscope (Optos Daytona-P200T). Infrared, autofluorescence, and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) images were obtained with a scanning laser ophthalmoscope (Heidelberg Engineering). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA) was performed using a TowardPi system (TowardPi Medical Technology Ltd.).

Electroretinogram (ERG)

Full-field ERG was recorded as previously described [20] using a Ganzfeld system (ROLAND Consult). After dark adaptation for ≥ 12 h, animals were anesthetized, pupils were dilated, and recording electrodes were placed on the corneas. Scotopic responses were recorded at flash intensities of 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 3.0, and 10.0 cd·s/m², and photopic responses at 3.0 cd·s/m². Oscillatory potentials (OPs) were derived as the maximum amplitude among the OP1–OP4 wave components.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the figure legends. Data normality was assessed using Q-Q plots in conjunction with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for normal and non-normal distributions, respectively. Subsequently, post hoc tests were performed with Dunnett’s (ANOVA) or Dunn’s (Kruskal-Wallis) correction for multiple comparisons. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown-Forsythe test; ordinary one-way ANOVA was used when variances were equal, whereas Welch’s ANOVA was applied otherwise. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5, with P < 0.05 defined as statistically significant.

Results

The senescent ARPE-19 cell expressed high level of SASP

Senescent retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells are considered a major contributor to neovascularization in nAMD, primarily through the secretion of SASP factors [6]. To establish a cellular model, we induced senescence in ARPE-19 cells using bleomycin (Bleo) and ionizing radiation (IR). Following treatment, the cells exhibited enlarged and irregular morphology, along with a significant increase in SA-β-gal positivity (NC: 2.33 ± 1.16%; Bleo: 94.33 ± 4.04%; IR: 83.67 ± 4.51%) (Fig. 1A). The expression of key senescent markers (p16, p21, and p53) was also significantly upregulated by at least 1.6-fold (Fig. 1B and F). Subsequently, analysis of SASP factors revealed marked upregulation of IL-1β (111.8–145.6-fold), IL-6 (19.3–68.7), IL-8 (107.9–142.5), MMP-3 (120.3–203.5), C3 (20.7–135.2), and VEGF (2.5–3.8), which were implicated in nAMD pathogenesis (Fig. 1C). Conversely, pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), a known angiogenesis inhibitor, was downregulated to 0.34–0.65-fold of baseline levels (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these findings indicate that the transcriptional profile of senescent RPE cells shifts toward a pro-angiogenic state.

Candidate senomorphic drugs for inhibiting SASP of senescent ARPE-19 cells

To identify a potential senomorphic therapy for nAMD, we treated senescent ARPE-19 cells with a panel of senomorphic agents: 400 nM rapamycin (an mTOR inhibitor), 40 µM SB203580 (a p38 MAPK inhibitor), 20 µM Ruxo (a JAK inhibitor), 100 µM MM-102 (an MLL1 methyltransferase inhibitor), 20 µM MCC950 (an NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor), and 5 µM H-151 (a STING inhibitor) (Fig. 1D-E; Fig. S1A-B). Among these, Ruxo demonstrated the most potent effect in suppressing the SASP. It inhibited the transcriptional expression of multiple SASP factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MMP-3, C3, and VEGF, while concurrently restoring the expression of the anti-angiogenic factor PEDF (Fig. 1D-E). To identify the optimal concentration of ruxolitinib for inhibiting SASP and modulating C3, VEGF, and PEDF expression, we treated bleomycin-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells with a range of drug concentrations. Quantitative analysis revealed that 40 µM ruxolitinib exerted the most potent effects (Fig. S2A). Crucially, to rule out nonspecific cytotoxicity or broad transcriptional suppression, we assessed cell viability and apoptosis via cytotoxicity and TUNEL assays. Since no significant cytotoxicity or increase in TUNEL-positive cells was observed at this dose (Fig. S2B-C), 40 µM was selected for all subsequent experiments.

We further validated these findings at the protein level by ELISA. Ruxo treatment notably reduced the secretion of C3 (Bleo: 24.32 ng/ml; Bleo+Ruxo: 5.95 ng/ml; IR: 6.58 ng/ml; IR+Ruxo: 3.86 ng/ml) and VEGF (Bleo: 35.31 pg/ml; Bleo+Ruxo: 29.08 pg/ml; IR: 29.22 pg/ml; IR+Ruxo: 21.62 pg/ml). Conversely, it restored PEDF protein levels (Bleo: 0.86 pg/ml; Bleo+Ruxo: 3.63 pg/ml; IR: 1.87 pg/ml; IR+Ruxo: 3.19 pg/ml) (Fig. S1C-D). Based on its superior efficacy in modulating both the transcriptional and secretory profiles of the SASP, Ruxo was selected for further investigation.

The JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway is activated in senescent ARPE-19 cells and promotes SASP expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the effect of Ruxo, we first examined the status of the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway in senescent ARPE-19 cells. Immunoblot analysis revealed that Bleo- or IR-induced senescence significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of JAK1, JAK2, and STAT3. The ratios of p-JAK1, p-JAK2, and p-STAT3 to β-actin were markedly upregulated (p-JAK1/β-actin: Bleo, 7.32-fold; IR, 6.45-fold. p-JAK2/β-actin: Bleo, 5.27-fold; IR, 4.30-fold. p-STAT3/β-actin: Bleo, 2.62-fold; IR, 2.16-fold) (Fig. 1B and G). Furthermore, the phosphorylation ratios of JAK1 (p-JAK1/JAK1: Bleo, 3.72-fold; IR, 3.13-fold) and STAT3 (p-STAT3/STAT3: Bleo, 2.43-fold; IR, 2.09-fold) were also significantly elevated. In contrast, the phosphorylation ratio of JAK2 (p-JAK2/JAK2) showed no significant change (Fig. 1B and H). These results indicate that cellular senescence activates the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway primarily through upregulating the expression and phosphorylation of JAK1 and STAT3, whereas JAK2 activation involves increased expression without a significant change in its phosphorylation ratio.

We then performed bulk RNA sequencing on Bleo-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells treated with or without Ruxo. Compared with the negative control, Bleo treatment resulted in 3,574 differentially expressed genes (DEGs: 2,671 upregulated, 903 downregulated). Compared with Bleo alone, Bleo + Ruxo (B + R) treatment yielded 490 DEGs (201 upregulated, 289 downregulated) (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 2B) and clustering analysis (Fig. 2C) showed that bleomycin markedly altered the transcriptional profile of ARPE-19 cells, and that Ruxo substantially reshaped this profile, although it remained distinct from that of untreated normal cells. We further defined two core gene sets: “Bleo_Up” (genes upregulated by Bleo vs. NC and downregulated by B + R vs. Bleo) and “Bleo_Down” (genes downregulated by Bleo vs. NC and upregulated by B + R vs. Bleo) (Fig. 2D). KEGG enrichment analysis of the Bleo_Up set highlighted the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and cellular senescence (Fig. 2E). The Bleo_Down set was enriched in metabolic pathways, including retinol metabolism (Fig. 2E), which is relevant to retinal visual function. Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis further revealed that the Bleo_Up set was associated with fundus neovascular diseases, such as diabetic retinopathy and macular degeneration (Fig. 2F).

To establish a causal link, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of JAK1and JAK2. All tested siRNAs (siJAK1 and siJAK2) exhibited high knockdown efficiency and effectively reduced p-STAT3 levels (Fig. 3A-D; Fig. S3A-D). The most effective sequences, siJAK1_1 and siJAK2_1, were selected for subsequent experiments. Knockdown of either JAK1 or JAK2 in senescent ARPE-19 cells significantly reversed the senescence-associated alterations in both mRNA and protein levels of C3 and VEGF. (Fig. 3E-H; Fig. S3E-H). Regarding PEDF, knockdown of JAK1 restored both mRNA and protein levels in senescent ARPE-19 cells. In contrast, JAK2 knockdown led to discordant changes in mRNA and protein expression under IR-induced senescence. This suggests that JAK2 regulates PEDF probably through post-translational mechanisms in this context (Fig. 3E–H; Fig. S3E–H). Then we overexpressed STAT3 to rescue the changes caused by ruxolitinib, and we observed the upregulation of C3 and VEGF, and the downregulation of PEDF (Fig. S3I-J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that activation of the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway is a key driver of SASP factor expression in senescent RPE cells.

The JAK1/2-STAT3-related pathways were enriched in nAMD/CNV and associated with RPE senescence

Given prior evidence linking senescent RPE cells to neovascularization in nAMD patients and CNV models [6, 21, 22], we hypothesized that the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway, activated in senescent ARPE-19 cells, may play a significant role in these pathological contexts. Analysis of public bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE146887, GSE129743) revealed potential activation of the JAK-STAT pathway in diseased tissues. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) based on JAK-STAT pathway-related gene sets revealed positive enrichment in both nAMD patients and CNV models across multiple gene sets, including JAK/STAT cascade (nAMD, NES = 1.265, FDR = 0.139; CNV, NES = 1.347, FDR = 0.098), KEGG_JAK/STAT signaling pathway (nAMD, NES = 1.408, FDR = 0.116; CNV, NES = 1.475, FDR = 0.106), and GOBP_Receptor signaling pathway via STAT (nAMD, NES = 1.230, FDR = 0.227; CNV, NES = 1.486, FDR = 0.120) (Fig. 4A-B). Although certain enrichments in nAMD patients did not reach statistical significance (JAK/STAT cascade, P = 0.059; GOBP_Receptor signaling pathway via STAT, P = 0.101), the collective data suggest potential activation of the JAK-STAT pathway in both nAMD and CNV.

To delineate cellular context, we analyzed a single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE271262) from laser-induced CNV model mice (6–8 weeks). After quality control and batch effect correction (Fig. S4A, B), 21,650 cells were categorized into 16 distinct cell types (Fig. 4C, Fig. S4C). The CNV condition was characterized by increased proportions of fibroblasts (18.0% to 33.7%), endothelial cells (5.5% to 8.4%), and microglia (2.4% to 11.5%), consistent with the fibrotic, neovascular, and inflammatory stats of CNV (Fig. 4D). Focusing on RPE cells, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using the SenMayo senescence signature confirmed a higher senescence score in the CNV group compared to controls (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, we defined the top 10% cells of each celltype by SenMayo score cells as “senescence-like”. A higher percent of senescence-like RPE was noted in CNV (16.3% vs. 6.1% in control) (Fig. 4F). Notably, senescence-like RPE exhibited higher JAK/STAT pathway activity scores compared to their non-senescent counterparts (Fig. 4G).

STAT3 is activated in RPE cells within a laser-induced CNV rat model

To validate the activation of STAT3 in CNV, we established a laser-induced CNV rat model (Fig. 4H). At day 14 post-laser, optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed spindle-like lesions with blood flows, and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed obvious fluorescein leakage from the laser spots (Fig. 4I), confirming successful neovascularization. Staining of RPE-choroid flatmounts with isolectin B4 (IB4) further visualized irregular, multi-processed neovascular lesions (Fig. 4J). To localize STAT3 activation, we performed co-staining for p-STAT3 and IB4 on flatmounts. The p-STAT3-positive and IB4-positive areas were not completely coincident (Fig. 4J), suggesting that STAT3 activation was not confined to the neovascular tufts. Finally, co-immunofluorescence staining of RPE65 and p-STAT3 on paraffin sections revealed a co-localization within RPE cells (Fig. 4K-L), providing direct evidence that STAT3 is activated in the RPE layer during CNV.

Pharmacological inhibition of the JAK-STAT3 pathway mitigates CNV and accelerates functional recovery

Based on our in vitro findings that Ruxo suppresses SASP factors and complement C3 while upregulating PEDF, we next evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in vivo using the laser-induced CNV rat model. Ruxo treatment significantly attenuated CNV progression. FFA analysis revealed a 63.8% reduction in fluorescence intensity (P < 0.001) and a 57.2% decrease in fluorescein leakage area at the laser lesions on day 14 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A-C). Consistent with this, flatmount analysis of RPE-choroid complexes showed a 51.0% reduction in the IB4-positive neovascular area (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5D, E). This attenuation was associated with a significant inhibition of STAT3 activation in the treated group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5D, F). We further assessed retinal function by ERG. At day 7 post-laser, the Ruxo-treated group exhibited significant recovery in functional parameters compared to the vehicle group. Specifically, the amplitudes of both the a-wave and b-wave under higher-intensity scotopic stimuli (3 and 10 cd·s/m²) were significantly greater (Fig. 5G). Photopic b-wave amplitude under 3 cd·s/m² stimulus was also restored (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5G). Although not statistically significant, a trend toward recovery was observed in oscillatory potential (OP) amplitudes (Fig. 5H). Notably, these functional improvements were transient; no significant differences were detected between the treatment and vehicle groups by day 14 post-laser (Fig. 5I, J). These results demonstrate that JAK-STAT3 pathway inhibition with Ruxo effectively suppresses CNV formation and promotes an accelerated, but not ultimate, recovery of retinal function.

To optimize the treatment regimen of Ruxo, we first evaluated a lower concentration to assess its effect on CNV progression. A modest reduction in neovascular area was observed in the 200 ng/µl Ruxo group, although it did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.1636, Fig. S5A). Next, we reduced the injection frequency and compared the effect with anti-VEGF therapy. A single injection of 10 ng/µl Ruxo at day 7 resulted in a 1.4-fold decrease relative to vehicle (P = 0.073), which was comparable to that of a single anti-VEGF injection (Fig. S5B). Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic effect of the combination treatment (Ruxo plus Conbercept). Compared with Ruxo or Conbercept monotherapy, the combination significantly reduced the IB4⁺ area by 1.83-fold and 1.77-fold, respectively (both P < 0.05, Fig. S5B).

Ruxolitinib exhibits no adverse effects on normal retinal structure or function

To assess the long-term safety of Ruxo on the healthy retina, we performed intravitreal injections of the drug into normal rat eyes. Retinal integrity was evaluated after a four-week observation period using multimodal imaging and functional assays. Structural analysis via optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) revealed no significant alterations in retinal vasculature, as evidenced by stable measures of the avascular area, non-perfusion area, and average blood flow density (Fig. 6A, C). Similarly, OCT-based morphometry showed that the thickness and volume of all retinal layers remained unchanged post-treatment (Fig. 6B, C). Histological assessment of retinal flatmounts confirmed that the cellular densities of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), photoreceptors, and the RPE/choroid complex were unaffected (Fig. 6D). H&E staining of retinal cross-sections further showed no significant difference for thickness across all retinal layers (Fig. 6E-H), aligning with the OCT findings. Functionally, full-field ERG recorded at one month post-injection detected no significant differences between treated and control eyes in the amplitudes of the a-wave, b-wave, or OPs under scotopic conditions (Fig. 6I, J). Collectively, these comprehensive analyses demonstrate that intravitreal administration of Ruxo is well-tolerated and does not induce detectable structural or functional deficits in the normal retina over a one-month period.

Discussion

Senescent cells accumulate in various age-related diseases and exacerbate pathological states primarily through SASP, highlighting senomorphic therapies as a promising strategy for intervention [23]. In nAMD, senescent RPE cells have been implicated as key drivers of choroidal neovascularization [9]. Our study identified the JAK1/2 inhibitor Ruxo as the most effective senomorphic agent cells from a panel of candidates, based on its superior ability to suppress the pro-angiogenic SASP in senescent ARPE-19 cells. Furthermore, we demonstrated activation of the JAK-STAT pathway within RPE cells both in nAMD patient data and a laser-induced CNV model. Crucially, therapeutic inhibition of this pathway with Ruxo in the CNV model effectively suppressed lesion formation and accelerated functional recovery, without inducing detectable retinal toxicity.

In this study, we screened a panel of senomorphic agents in bleomycin- or IR-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells. Among the candidates, the JAK1/2 inhibitor Ruxo demonstrated the broadest efficacy, potently suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic SASP factors (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MMP-3, C3, and VEGF) while upregulating the anti-angiogenic factor PEDF. These indicates the anti-inflammation, anti-complement, and anti-angiogenesis capabilities of Ruxo, which we proved to be based on JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway. The JAK-STAT pathway is a principal signaling cascade for numerous cytokines and growth factors, playing a central role in immune regulation, cell proliferation, and survival [24]. Within this family, JAK1 and JAK2 are particularly crucial for transducing inflammatory and other endocrine signals [25]. Notably, JAK-STAT inhibition is an established senomorphic strategy. Kirkland et al. reported that inhibiting JAK-STAT signaling or knocking out JAK proteins reduces the SASP in senescent adipose tissue, attenuates local and systemic inflammation, and improves physiological function in mice [13]. Consistent with this, multiple in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that JAK-STAT pathway inhibition can attenuate cellular senescence, suppress the SASP, and mitigate the progression of age-related pathologies [14–16, 26], solidifying its relevance as a therapeutic target for senomorphic interventions [23, 25].

In addition, we noticed the downregulation of multiple metabolic pathways, including retinol metabolism, in Bleo-induced senescent ARPE-19 cells compared to normal cells or senescent cells treated with Ruxo. Vertebrate light perception begins with rhodopsin activation, a light-sensitive complex formed by a covalent bond between opsin and 11-cis-retinal [27]. Sustained vision requires the visual cycle (retinoid cycle), primarily in the retinal pigment epithelium, which recycles the photoproduct all-trans-retinal back to the 11-cis-retinal chromophore [28]. Therefore, disruption of retinol metabolism may impair the visual cycle and contribute to retinal dysfunction [29]. Besides retinol metabolism, Tyrosine and phenylalanine metabolism were affected as well. A previous study reported that phenylalanine and tyrosine have the possibility of being biomarkers of AMD [30]. Collectively, these altered metabolic pathways in senescent RPE may underlie AMD pathogenesis, and their partial rescue by Ruxo highlights a potential therapeutic mechanism.

To further establish the pathological relevance of this pathway, we integrated bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses. These revealed a significant enrichment of JAK-STAT-associated pathways in nAMD patients, CNV models, and within senescent RPE cells, which was corroborated in our laser-induced CNV rat model. Subsequently, therapeutic inhibition of the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway with Ruxo in this model effectively reduced CNV lesion area and accelerated retinal functional recovery. Our results are supported by a growing body of literature that identifies JAK/STAT3 signaling as a promising therapeutic target for nAMD/CNV [31, 32]. For instance, elevated p-STAT3 levels have been documented in human CNV membranes, indicating pathway activation in the disease [33]. Furthermore, Yang et al. demonstrated that knockdown of STRA6 ameliorated CNV progression by modulating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway [34]. Together, these independent studies align with and reinforce our conclusions, highlighting the translational potential of targeting the JAK-STAT3 axis in nAMD therapy.

Finally, we evaluated the safety profile of intravitreally administered Ruxo. Comprehensive assessments using OCTA, retinal flatmounts, H&E staining, and ERG revealed no significant structural or functional alterations in normal retinas treated with Ruxo compared to vehicle controls, supporting its local ocular tolerability. The retinal and choroidal thicknesses and volumes derived from OCTA were machine-defined and calculated, which may introduce measurement variability. Nonetheless, these data consistently indicated no significant difference between the treatment groups. Notably, first-generation JAK inhibitors, including Ruxo, tofacitinib, and baricitinib, have been approved for systemic clinical use, confirming their general safety within established therapeutic windows [35–37]. However, systemic administration of JAK inhibitors is associated with class-wide adverse events, such as increased risk of infections, hematological abnormalities, and other serious conditions [38–40]. These reports highlight the necessity for further investigation into optimal dosing, mechanisms of potential toxicity, and long-term safety, even for localized ocular delivery. Although intravitreal administration was used in this study to minimize systemic exposure, vigilant monitoring for potential local and systemic side effects remains crucial in future translational research. In addition, we only assessed short-term ocular tolerability under the tested regimen, but did not evaluate long-term safety, safety in diseased eyes, safety of repeated injections, systemic side effects, or local immunosuppressive risks. Therefore, extended observation periods, systemic effect monitoring, optimization of treatment strategies, intraocular pressure stability, and assessment of immunosuppressive risks are warranted in future studies.

Our study identified Ruxo as a promising senomorphic candidate from a panel of agents. Through in vitroand in vivoexperiments, we established the functional importance of the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway in senescent RPE cells and demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of its inhibition in mitigating CNV progression. Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, our findings are based on the ARPE-19 cell line, which may not fully recapitulate the biology of primary human RPE cells. Second, although we have employed several treatment regimens (dosage and timing) of Ruxo in the CNV model, and compared the therapeutic effect with anti-VEGF, it’s not enough to find the optimal therapy regimen. Future studies should optimize therapeutic windows by testing different administration time points, drug concentrations, and injection frequencies. Third, the mechanism depth of this study is in sufficient. In the further researches, experiments demonstrating a direct transcriptional regulation of SASP genes by STAT3 (e.g., ChIP-qPCR or ChIP-seq) are required.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the JAK1/2-STAT3 pathway acts as a key driver of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in senescent RPE cells, and is significantly activated in both nAMD patient samples and CNV models. Pharmacological inhibition of this pathway with Ruxo effectively attenuated CNV progression and promoted early transient functional improvement at day 7 post injury in a laser-induced CNV model, while exhibiting a favorable short-term safety profile. The combination of ruxolitinib with anti-VEGF therapy presented a better effect on inhibiting CNV area. These preclinical findings provide a rationale for the further investigation of JAK inhibitors as a potential senomorphic strategy for nAMD. Future translational studies are warranted to evaluate long-term safety, explore combination therapies with anti-VEGF agents, and optimize local delivery systems.
