1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal function and its zero set

A classical theme in complex and harmonic analysis is that extremal problems for
entire functions of prescribed exponential type encode rigid spectral data: zeros,
canonical products, and Dirichlet series that behave as if they came from a
one–dimensional spectrum. A particularly striking instance is the extremal
problem considered by Hörmander and Bernhardsson
[
18
]
: among entire functions

f
of exponential type at most
\pi
satisfying the normalization
f(0)=1
, minimize
the
L^{1}
–norm on the real line. Equivalently, in Paley–Wiener language, one looks
for the smallest
PW^{1}
–norm under the constraint of a fixed point evaluation.
The unique extremizer (up to the natural symmetries) is an even entire function

\varphi
of type
\pi
, normalized by
\varphi(0)=1
, with real simple zeros

\{\pm\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R},\qquad 0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots\to\infty.

The analysis of this function has recently undergone substantial development in the
works of Bondarenko–Ortega-Cerdà–Radchenko–Seip
[
6
,
7
]
, who obtain a
factorization
\varphi(z)=\Phi(z)\Phi(-z)
into canonical products, as well as a
nonlinear functional equation of inversion type intertwining the values of
\Phi

at
z
and at
(2\pi iCz)^{-1}
for an explicit constant
C>0
. The authors also show precise asymptotic information about
the real zeros
\pm\tau_{n}
of the Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal function.
They indicate that the deviations
\tau_{n}-(n+\tfrac{1}{2})
are very small in a global sense (in particular, of
\ell^{2}
-type), placing
\{\tau_{n}\}
in the classical regime of
stable
perturbations of the half-integer lattice.

One of the most fruitful byproducts of this structure is the emergence of two Dirichlet
series built from the zeros,

L_{+}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-s},\qquad L_{-}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}^{\,s}},\qquad(\Re s>1),

whose analytic continuation, special values, and residues reflect fine information
about
\varphi
and its functional equation.

The present paper is concerned with a different, and in a sense complementary, aspect
of the same zero set: the
admissibility
of sequences extracted from

\{\tau_{n}\}
in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song
[
37
]
.
This notion belongs to the general calculus of zeta regularization and is designed
to capture precisely the analytic input that makes the spectral zeta function and
zeta–regularized products behave as in elliptic spectral theory.
Our goal is to place the Hörmander–Bernhardsson zeros into that calculus, and then
use the resulting zeta machinery to settle conjecture 1 raised in
[
6
]
.

1.2.
Heat traces, zeta regularization, and admissible sequences

Given a sequence
(X_{n})
in the right half–plane, one may form the heat trace

H(t):=\sum_{n}e^{-X_{n}t},\qquad t>0,

together with the associated spectral zeta function

\zeta_{X}(s):=\sum_{n}X_{n}^{-s}.

In geometric and analytic spectral theory, when
(X_{n})
consists of the eigenvalues of a
positive elliptic operator,
H(t)
admits a full small–time asymptotic expansion and

\zeta_{X}(s)
extends meromorphically to the complex plane. This is one of the standard
routes to the zeta–regularized determinant
\det_{\zeta}X:=\exp(-\zeta_{X}^{\prime}(0))
, which
originates in the work of Ray–Singer and its subsequent refinements

[
38
,
39
,
17
,
43
]
.
Quine–Heydari–Song
[
37
]
abstracted precisely the hypotheses on
(X_{n})
needed to
run this Mellin transform machinery without geometric input. Their definition
requires the convergence of
H(t)
for
t>0
, a
full
polyhomogeneous expansion
as
t\to 0^{+}
in pure powers (no logarithms), and a mild growth condition that precludes
pathological cancellations. Under these conditions one recovers, in a purely analytic
setting, the familiar package: meromorphic continuation of
\zeta_{X}(s)
, residue coefficient identities, special value formulae at nonpositive integers, and well–posed zeta regularized products
[
37
, §2–§4]
.

From this viewpoint it is natural to ask whether the sequences derived from the
Hörmander–Bernhardsson zeros behave spectrally. The first obstruction is that the
zero set
\{\tau_{n}\}
is
not
a quadratic lattice; rather, it is a delicate
perturbation of the half–integers. In
[
6
]
it is shown that

\tau_{n}=n+\frac{1}{2}-d_{n},\qquad d_{n}\in\ell^{2},

a perturbative condition of Paley–Wiener type which is powerful for
uniqueness and interpolation in
PW^{1}
and for controlling canonical products.
However,
\ell^{2}
–closeness to a lattice does not by itself imply that the associated
heat trace is polyhomogeneous: logarithmic terms may occur through harmonic weights,
and such logarithms are
forbidden
by the strict Quine–Heydari–Song definition.
Understanding precisely when logarithms appear, and when they do not, is one of the
central points of the present work.

1.3.
Main results: admissibility, parity, and a functional equation for
L_{+}

Our basic spectral object is the squared zero sequence

\lambda_{n}:=\tau_{n}^{2},\qquad n\geq 1,

and the corresponding heat trace

\theta(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}.

Our first main theorem proves that
(\lambda_{n})
is admissible in the sense of
Quine–Heydari–Song.

Theorem 1.1

(Heat trace expansion and admissibility)
.

The heat trace
\theta(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\tau_{n}^{2}t}
admits a full small–time asymptotic
expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
in
pure
half–integer powers:

\theta(t)\sim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}c_{\nu}\,t^{j_{\nu}},\qquad j_{\nu}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z},\quad j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots\to+\infty.

Consequently,
(\tau_{n}^{2})_{n\geq 1}
is admissible in the sense of
[
37
]
.

As in the general QHS calculus
[
37
]
, this admissibility statement immediately yields
a meromorphic continuation of the spectral zeta function

Z(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}^{-s}=L_{+}(2s)
, residue coefficient identities,
and special value formulae at non-positive integers. In addition, it provides a robust
coefficient–extraction mechanism at infinity for the canonical product

W(\lambda)=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1+\lambda/\lambda_{n})
.
These consequences form the analytic backbone of our proof of the conjectural symmetry
of
L_{+}
discovered in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Conjecture 1 of
[
6
]
)
.

Let
C
be the constant appearing in the inversion functional equation for
\Phi
in
[
6
]
.
Then for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

L_{+}(-2k)=\frac{L_{+}(2k)}{(2\pi iC)^{2k}}.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
illustrates the conceptual role of admissibility
in this problem: the functional equation for the extremal function yields a precise
inversion identity for
\varphi
, which enforces a parity constraint on the even
coefficients in the asymptotic expansion of
\log\varphi(Tz)
in a small sector
(
Tz=(2\pi iCz)^{-1}
). Admissibility supplies the missing analytic input that
identifies those even coefficients with zeta special values at negative integers via
coefficient extraction at infinity. In this way, the conjectured relation between

L_{+}(2k)
and
L_{+}(-2k)
becomes an equality between two independently computable
coefficient functionals.

Beyond squares, we establish a sharp parity phenomenon for power sequences.

Theorem 1.3

(Parity dichotomy)
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and consider
\theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\tau_{n}^{\,m}t}
.

(1)

If
m
is even, then
(\tau_{n}^{\,m})_{n\geq 1}
is admissible in the sense of
[
37
]
.

(2)

If
m
is odd, then the small–time expansion of
\theta_{m}(t)
contains a
nonzero

logarithmic term
\kappa_{m}\,t\log t
. In particular
(\tau_{n}^{\,m})
is
not
admissible in the
strict QHS sense.

The appearance of logarithms for odd powers is not an artifact of estimation: it is forced by a
specific
a_{n}^{-1}
term in the asymptotic expansion of
\tau_{n}^{\,m}
(with
a_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
),
and its coefficient can be expressed explicitly through
L_{-}(m)
, reconciling the heat trace
picture with the residue formula for
L_{+}
at negative odd integers recorded in
[
6
, §10.2]
.
Thus the failure of admissibility for odd powers has a precise analytic signature:
logarithmic small–time terms correspond to higher–order pole phenomena in Mellin space and to

\log\lambda
corrections in large–parameter expansions of resolvent traces and canonical products.
This naturally suggests a
log–polyhomogeneous
enlargement of QHS admissibility, parallel to
the way logarithmic heat terms arise in geometric problems with singularities

[
12
,
17
]
.

Finally, our argument yields a general admissibility criterion for analytic perturbations of
quadratic lattices, which should be useful well beyond the Hörmander–Bernhardsson setting.

Theorem 1.4

(Analytic perturbations of quadratic lattices)
.

Let
a>0
and let
q
be analytic in a neighborhood of
[0,a^{-2}]
.
Then the sequence

\lambda_{n}=(n+a)^{2}+q\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr)

is admissible in the sense of
[
37
]
, provided
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n
.

1.4.
Method: analytic normal forms and Mellin analysis of weighted Gaussian sums

A distinctive feature of the Quine–Heydari–Song notion of admissibility is the
strictly
polyhomogeneous
small-time expansion

\sum_{n}e^{-X_{n}t}\sim\sum_{\nu\geq 0}c_{\nu}t^{j_{\nu}}\qquad(t\to 0^{+}),

with
no
logarithmic factors
t^{\alpha}(\log t)^{q}
allowed.
This condition is not cosmetic: it is exactly what forces the associated spectral zeta function

Z(s)=\sum_{n}X_{n}^{-s}
to have at most
simple
poles, and it is what makes the zeta-regularized product calculus behave like a clean Weierstrass theory (e.g. with canonical products controlled by finitely many heat coefficients).

The present problem is subtle for a structural reason: the
Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal zeros
\tau_{n}
are
half-integer perturbations
with a

borderline
first correction of size
1/n
. More precisely, writing
a_{n}:=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
,
the BOCRS parametrization gives

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-\rho(a_{n}^{-1}),\qquad\rho(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m},\quad a_{m}\geq 0,

so that the leading deviation
a_{n}-\tau_{n}
is of order
a_{n}^{-1}
(indeed one has an expansion

\rho(a_{n}^{-1})=\frac{a_{1}}{a_{n}}+O(a_{n}^{-3})
).
This is precisely the regime where a term-by-term linearization of the exponential produces

harmonic weights
:

e^{-\tau_{n}t}=e^{-a_{n}t}\exp\!\bigl((a_{n}-\tau_{n})t\bigr)=e^{-a_{n}t}\Bigl(1+t\,\frac{a_{1}}{a_{n}}+O(ta_{n}^{-3})+O(t^{2}a_{n}^{-2})\Bigr).

Summing the first correction over
n
yields the classical logarithmic amplification

t\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{e^{-a_{n}t}}{a_{n}}\sim t\log\!\frac{1}{t}\qquad(t\to 0^{+}),

so even though the pointwise perturbation
a_{n}-\tau_{n}
is small, the
collective
effect of
the borderline weight
a_{n}^{-1}
creates a genuine
t\log t
term in the heat trace.
In other words: the obstruction is not local in
n
but global in the Laplace summation, and it is
invisible unless one tracks the exact first nonsummable correction. This is exactly why
BOCRS–admissibility (an
\ell^{2}
perturbation of
a_{n}
) does
not
automatically imply
QHS–admissibility (a pure-power heat expansion).

The resolution is to identify a normal form in which the offending harmonic weight cannot occur.
For the squared zeros

\lambda_{n}:=\tau_{n}^{2},

the oddness of
\rho
implies the analytic reparametrization

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-a_{n}^{-1}\sigma(a_{n}^{-2})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{n}=a_{n}^{2}+q(a_{n}^{-2}),

with
q
analytic on a neighborhood of
[0,r_{0}]
(and in particular bounded there).
This is the key cancellation:
squaring removes the
a_{n}^{-1}
term
and replaces it by an
analytic correction in
u_{n}:=a_{n}^{-2}
.
Consequently, in the heat trace

\theta(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\,e^{-tq(u_{n})},

we never encounter the harmonic weight
a_{n}^{-1}
that produces
t\log t
.

The proof then has two robust components.

(i) Taylor expansion in time with a summable remainder.

We expand
e^{-tq(u)}
in powers of
t

uniformly
for
u\in[0,4/9]
,
without expanding
q
in
u
. This yields, for any truncation order
R
,

\theta(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,F_{r}(t)\;+\;O(t^{R+1})\Theta_{0}(t),\qquad F_{r}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}q(u_{n})^{r}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t},

so the error inherits the sharp
t^{-1/2}
growth of the model Gaussian sum

\Theta_{0}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}
.

(ii) Mellin transform and contour shifting for weighted Gaussian sums.

For fixed
r
, the weight
u\mapsto q(u)^{r}
is analytic and admits an absolutely convergent power series on
[0,4/9]
. This reduces
F_{r}(t)
to a convergent linear combination of the weighted theta sums

\Theta_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2m}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}.

We then study
\Theta_{m}(t)
via Mellin inversion and contour shifting:

\Theta_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re s=c}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\Bigl(\zeta(2(m+s),\tfrac{1}{2})-2^{2(m+s)}\Bigr)\,ds,

using the meromorphic structure of
\Gamma
and the Hurwitz zeta function.
Since all poles are simple and do not collide in the even–power regimes, one obtains a
full asymptotic expansion in half–integer powers of
t
with controlled remainder,
and the analyticity of
q
guarantees that the ensuing infinite
m
–summations are
geometrically convergent and may be performed termwise. This produces the full expansion
for
\theta(t)
and hence QHS admissibility.

For odd powers
\tau_{n}^{2\ell+1}
the same Mellin mechanism shows precisely how a logarithm is created:
a harmonic weight
a_{n}^{-1}
appears in the large–
n
expansion of
\tau_{n}^{2\ell+1}
,
and the corresponding weighted Gaussian sum has leading asymptotic
\frac{1}{m}\log(1/t)
, which forces
a nonzero
t\log t
term in the heat trace.

The decisive point is that for
\lambda_{n}=\tau_{n}^{2}
(and more generally for
even powers
\tau_{n}^{2p}
) the spectrum admits the analytic normal form

\lambda_{n}=(n+a)^{2}+q\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr),

which rules out harmonic weights and allows a purely polyhomogeneous Mellin calculus.

The analytic engine behind admissibility proofs is the heat trace

\Theta(t)
and its small-
t
asymptotics.
Standard references for heat kernel expansions and their spectral meaning are

[
17
,
5
,
42
]
.
When logarithmic terms can arise (typically in singular or boundary-value settings),
Cheeger’s analysis provides a useful benchmark
[
12
]
.

To pass from heat asymptotics to zeta information, we rely on Mellin transform and
contour-shift techniques. For sums of theta type and harmonic-sum asymptotics, a
particularly effective toolkit is the Mellin-asymptotics framework of
Flajolet–Gourdon–Dumas
[
15
]
, together
with Mellin–Barnes technology and classical asymptotic-analysis references

[
33
,
30
,
44
]

and standard special-function identities (e.g. Hurwitz zeta) as organized in the
DLMF
[
29
]
.

1.5.
Relation to other works and scope

The extremal function
\varphi
belongs to a long line of Fourier–analytic extremal problems for bandlimited functions, with origins in the Paley–Wiener theory
[
32
]
and the de Branges theory of Hilbert spaces of entire functions
[
13
]
.
The Hörmander–Bernhardsson minimization problem
[
18
]
is a distinguished
PW^{1}
analogue whose solution displays unusually rigid structure. T he recent analysis in
[
6
]
uncovers
new algebraic symmetries and analytic continuations of the associated Dirichlet series. Earlier stages of this program appear in
[
7
]
, which establish
quantitative control on the zero set
\{\pm\tau_{n}\}
of
\varphi
, including an
\ell^{2}
-type proximity of
\tau_{n}
to the half-integers.
Complementary viewpoints on the same extremizer theme in Paley–Wiener
and de Branges settings have emerged very recently in
[
8
,
19
,
4
]
.
In particular, these works develop an operator- and space-theoretic framework for
point evaluation in
PW^{p}
and in model spaces, providing tools that are
useful when one needs stability statements for extremizers and their associated
spectral data.

The notion of admissibility in this paper is formulated in the language of zeta-regularized products. A systematic framework for regularized products of sequences is given by Quine–Heydari–Song
[
37
]
,
which isolates analytic conditions on sequences that guarantee that the associated zeta functions admit meromorphic continuation and that the corresponding
regularized products can be defined by
\exp(-\zeta^{\prime}(0))
.
This sequence calculus aligns with the spectral dictionary developed in the theory
of elliptic operators: Seeley’s complex powers
[
39
]
and the Ray–Singer
analytic torsion program
[
38
]
explain how heat-trace asymptotics control
the meromorphic continuation of spectral zeta functions and determinants.

For context and comparison with broader zeta regularization theories (and
with anomaly phenomena), we also refer to the classical works of Voros and
Kontsevich–Vishik
[
43
,
24
]
, as well as
to the monograph treatments
[
20
,
35
,
23
,
14
]
.

1.6.
On notions of admissibility

The adjective
admissible
refers to genuinely different hypotheses in the two settings at hand.

In the BOCRS framework one considers a strictly increasing sequence

t=(t_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,\infty)
and calls it
admissible
if

d_{n}:=n+\tfrac{1}{2}-t_{n}\in\ell^{2}.

This is a perturbative Paley–Wiener type condition: it guarantees that the
canonical products built from
t
, e.g.

\psi_{t}(z):=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1-\frac{z^{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\Bigr),\qquad\Psi_{t}(z):=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{t_{n}}\Bigr),

have essentially the same exponential type and growth behavior as the corresponding
half–integer model products, and it supports interpolation arguments
for
PW^{1}
.

In the QHS framework one starts from a sequence
(X_{k})
with
\Re X_{k}>0
and assumes
that the heat trace

H(t):=\sum_{k}e^{-X_{k}t}

converges for
t>0
and admits a
full
asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
of the form

H(t)\sim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}c_{j_{\nu}}\,t^{j_{\nu}},\qquad j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots\to+\infty,

together with a mild small–
t
growth condition (e.g.
t^{\beta}H(t)\to 0
for some

\beta>0
).
Crucially, this hypothesis is
purely polyhomogeneous
(no logarithmic terms),
and it is tailored to Mellin-transform arguments yielding meromorphic continuation of
the associated zeta function and zeta-regularized products.

BOCRS–admissibility is a
metric
condition controlling how close the points
t_{n}

are to
n+\tfrac{1}{2}
(an
\ell^{2}
perturbation), whereas QHS–admissibility is an

asymptotic
condition controlling the small-time structure of
H(t)
.
Consequently, neither notion implies the other in general:

•

A QHS–admissible sequence need not be BOCRS–admissible (e.g.
X_{k}=k^{2}
has a
standard heat-trace expansion, but
k^{2}
is not an
\ell^{2}
perturbation of
k+\tfrac{1}{2}
).

•

A BOCRS–admissible sequence need not be QHS–admissible. In particular, for the
Hörmander–Bernhardsson zero sequence
\tau_{n}
one has an expansion of the form

\tau_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}-\frac{a_{1}}{n+\tfrac{1}{2}}+O\bigl(n^{-3}\bigr),

so that
n+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}\asymp 1/n
. Expanding

e^{-\tau_{n}t}=e^{-(n+\tfrac{1}{2})t}\exp\bigl((n+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tau_{n})t\bigr)
yields a correction
term proportional to

t\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{e^{-(n+\tfrac{1}{2})t}}{n+\tfrac{1}{2}}\sim t\log(1/t),

which produces a
t\log t
contribution in
H(t)
and therefore violates the strict
QHS requirement of a pure-power asymptotic expansion.

If one wishes to encompass sequences whose heat traces involve terms

t^{\alpha}(\log t)^{q}
(as happens naturally for odd-power reparametrizations such as

X_{k}=\tau_{k}^{2m+1}
), a natural enlargement of QHS–admissibility is to allow

log-polyhomogeneous
expansions. On the zeta-function side this corresponds to
allowing higher-order poles (simple poles correspond to pure powers), and one then
defines regularized products using zeta-renormalization in that broader
setting.

1.7.
Future directions

The analytic normal form

\lambda_{n}=(n+a)^{2}+q\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr)\qquad(q\ \text{analytic near }[0,a^{-2}])

is not tied to the Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal problem
[
6
]
, it is a common large-parameter structure in several areas. Here are three concrete directions where the same mechanism is expected to apply and where the present admissibility criterion can be used essentially verbatim.

(1)

One-dimensional spectral problems (Sturm–Liouville, Schrödinger).

For regular boundary value problems on an interval, eigenvalues satisfy

\lambda_{n}\sim(n+a)^{2}+\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}(n+a)^{-2j},

with coefficients determined by the boundary conditions and the potential (Liouville–Green asymptotics); see, for instance,

[
45
,
26
,
36
]
.
When the asymptotic series is controlled analytically (e.g. for analytic potentials or
in integrable families), the correction can often be organized as an analytic function of
(n+a)^{-2}
,
placing the spectrum in the scope of the analytic perturbation of a quadratic lattice framework.
A direct payoff is a clean heat expansion and thus determinant-type information; compare
[
16
,
11
,
22
]
.

(2)

Special-function spectra (Bessel and Airy-type zeros and their zeta functions).

Many canonical products in analysis and mathematical physics are built from zeros
x_{n}
of special
functions, with large-
n
expansions

x_{n}\sim(n+a)\pi+\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(n+a)^{2j-1}}.

After squaring, one typically obtains

x_{n}^{2}\sim\pi^{2}(n+a)^{2}+\widetilde{q}\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr),

where
\widetilde{q}
is analytic near
0
under suitable uniformity assumptions.
This is precisely the analytic-quadratic-lattice paradigm and leads to QHS-style admissibility
(compare
[
37
,
39
]
), meromorphic continuation of the associated zeta functions, and controlled canonical products; see representative Bessel-zeta treatments in

[
1
,
41
,
28
,
2
]
.

(3)

de Branges sampling problems with quadraticized lattices.

Zeros of Hermite–Biehler functions (or sine-type functions) frequently form near-lattices

n+a+\varepsilon_{n}
in Paley–Wiener settings; see
[
13
,
40
]
.
In problems where one naturally passes to squares (or even powers) of such zeros
(e.g. via even canonical products, spectral transforms, or functional equations), the resulting
sequence often becomes

(n+a)^{2}+q\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr)

with
q
analytic when the perturbation is produced by an analytic reparametrization.
This provides a portable route to heat-trace asymptotics and zeta-regularized product formulae in
Paley–Wiener settings, parallel to (but distinct from) purely
\ell^{2}
perturbation theory:
compare the density viewpoint

[
25
,
31
,
27
]

with sharp perturbation criteria for exponential Riesz bases

[
21
,
3
,
34
]
.

In all three contexts, the same principle governs whether logarithms appear: odd-power (or unsquared) perturbations may leave a residual
(n+a)^{-1}
harmonic weight and hence
create
t\log t
, while even-power quadraticization tends to eliminate that term and restore a pure-power
heat calculus; see, e.g., the regular-singular heat expansions in
[
9
,
10
]
.

Organization of the paper

Section
2
recalls the notion of admissibility in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song and establishes the basic setup for the squared zero sequence
\lambda_{n}=\tau_{n}^{2}
. In particular, we derive the analytic normal form
\lambda_{n}=a_{n}^{2}+q(a_{n}^{-2})
and reduce the heat trace
\theta(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}
to weighted Gaussian sums (Section
2.3
). The Mellin–Hurwitz zeta analysis of these weighted sums produces full small-time asymptotics (Sections
2.4
and
2.5
), and the admissibility of
(\lambda_{n})
is concluded in Section
2.6
.

Section
3
records the resulting spectral-zeta consequences of admissibility, including meromorphic continuation and special-value identities for the spectral zeta function, as well as coefficient extraction statements and large-parameter asymptotics for the associated canonical product. In Section
4
we combine these admissibility consequences with the inversion identity for
\varphi
to prove Conjecture 1 of
[
6
]
. Section
5
establishes the parity dichotomy for
\theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\tau_{n}^{,m}t}
, showing that even powers yield admissible sequences while odd powers force a nonzero
t\log t
obstruction. Finally, Section
6
discusses the corresponding zeta-theoretic and canonical-product consequences in the log-polyhomogeneous (odd-power) regime.

2.
On admissible sequences in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song

2.1.
Setting

We recall the definition from
[
37
, §2]
.

Definition 2.1

(Admissible sequence)
.

A sequence
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
is called
admissible
if:

(1)

X_{k}
lies in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}X>0
for every
k
;

(2)

the series
\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{-X_{k}t}
converges absolutely for every
t>0
and has
a full asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
of the form

\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{-X_{k}t}\sim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}c_{\nu}t^{j_{\nu}},\qquad j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots,\quad j_{\nu}\to+\infty;

(3)

there exists
\beta>0
such that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}t^{\beta}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{-|X_{k}|t}=0.

Remark 2.2
.

Quine–Heydari–Song index their sequences from
k=0
, whereas we will naturally work with

n\geq 1
. Adding or removing finitely many terms does not affect any of the three conditions
in Definition
2.1
, so we freely switch between these conventions.

Throughout this paper,
admissible
always means admissible in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song.

2.2.
On conditions (1) and (3)

Let
a_{n}:=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
. The following is a reformulation of results proved in

[
6
, Theorem 6.1 and Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Convergent power series representation of
\tau_{n}
)
.

There exists an odd power series

\rho(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m},\qquad a_{m}\geq 0,

such that:

(1)

the series
\rho
converges if and only if
|z|\leq 2
;

(2)

\rho(2)=\tfrac{1}{2}
;

(3)

for every
n\geq 1
,

(2.1)

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-\rho(a_{n}^{-1})=n+\frac{1}{2}-\rho\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr).

Remark 2.4
.

Since the coefficients of
\rho
are nonnegative and the series converges at
z=2
,
it converges absolutely on
[0,2]
and defines a continuous increasing function there.

Throughout we set
\lambda_{n}:=\tau_{n}^{2}
and

\theta(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{n}t}.

Lemma 2.5

(Lower bound for
\tau_{n}
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
one has
\tau_{n}\geq n
. Consequently
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\rho
has nonnegative coefficients and converges at
z=2
(so
\sum_{j\geq 1}\rho_{j}2^{j}<\infty
),
the series defining
\rho
converges absolutely and uniformly on
[0,2]
by the Weierstrass
M
-test.
Hence
\rho
is continuous on
[0,2]
, and because each partial sum is increasing on
[0,2]
,
the limit function
\rho
is increasing on
[0,2]
.

By Theorem
2.3
,
\rho
has nonnegative coefficients and hence is increasing on
[0,2]
.
Since
a_{n}\geq\tfrac{3}{2}
, we have
a_{n}^{-1}\leq\tfrac{2}{3}<2
, so

0\leq\rho(a_{n}^{-1})\leq\rho(2)=\frac{1}{2}.

Using (
2.1
) yields

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-\rho(a_{n}^{-1})\geq\Bigl(n+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}=n,

and squaring gives
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

The series
\theta(t)
converges absolutely for every
t>0
and satisfies

\theta(t)=O(t^{-1/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
.
In particular, Definition
2.1
(3)
holds for
\beta=1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
,
\theta(t)\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{2}t}
.
For
t>0
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{2}t}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}t}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}},

which implies
\theta(t)=O(t^{-1/2})
.
Thus
t\,\theta(t)=O(t^{1/2})\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
, establishing Definition
2.1
(3).
∎

2.3.
Reduction to weighted Gaussian sums

It remains to prove Definition
2.1
(2), i.e. the full small-time asymptotic expansion. We exploit the odd nature of
\rho
to rewrite
\lambda_{n}
as a quadratic main term plus an analytic correction.

Write
\rho
as an odd series

\rho(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{j}z^{2j+1},\qquad b_{j}:=a_{2j+1}\geq 0,

and define

\sigma(u):=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{j}u^{j}.

Then
\rho(z)=z\,\sigma(z^{2})
, so for
x>0
,

(2.2)

\rho(1/x)=x^{-1}\sigma(x^{-2}).

Since
\rho
converges for
|z|\leq 2
, the series
\sigma
converges for
|u|\leq 4
.
Define the analytic function

(2.3)

q(u):=-2\sigma(u)+u\sigma(u)^{2},\qquad|u|\leq 4.

Lemma 2.7

(Analytic correction for
\lambda_{n}
)
.

Let
a_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
and
u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}
. Then

(2.4)

\lambda_{n}=\tau_{n}^{2}=a_{n}^{2}+q(u_{n}).

Moreover,
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,4/9]
.

Proof.

By (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-a_{n}^{-1}\sigma(a_{n}^{-2})=a_{n}-a_{n}^{-1}\sigma(u_{n}).

Squaring gives

\tau_{n}^{2}=a_{n}^{2}-2\sigma(u_{n})+u_{n}\sigma(u_{n})^{2}=a_{n}^{2}+q(u_{n}),

which is (
2.4
).
Finally,
u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}\leq(3/2)^{-2}=4/9
for
n\geq 1
.
∎

Recall that by Lemma
2.7
we have

\lambda_{n}=a_{n}^{2}+q(u_{n}),\qquad a_{n}=n+\frac{1}{2},\qquad u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}\in(0,r_{0}],\quad r_{0}:=\frac{4}{9},

and hence

(2.5)

\theta(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\,\mathrm{e}^{-tq(u_{n})}.

In this part we reduce the asymptotic analysis of
\theta(t)
as
t\to 0^{+}
to the
basic weighted Gaussian sums

\Theta_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t},\qquad m\geq 0.

The crucial point is that we
do not
truncate
q
in the variable
u
, we only
perform a uniform Taylor expansion in the time variable
t
, which produces an
error of size
t^{R+1}\Theta_{0}(t)
and hence ultimately
O(t^{R+1/2})
.

2.3.1.
Uniform Taylor expansion in
t

Since
q
is analytic in a neighborhood of
[0,r_{0}]
, it is bounded there. Set

Q_{*}:=\sup_{u\in[0,r_{0}]}|q(u)|<\infty.

Lemma 2.8

(Uniform Taylor remainder for the exponential)
.

Let
R\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
and all
t\in[0,1]
,

(2.6)

e^{-tx}=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-tx)^{r}}{r!}+O_{R}\!\bigl(t^{R+1}|x|^{R+1}e^{t|x|}\bigr).

Proof.

By the exponential series,

e^{-tx}-\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-tx)^{r}}{r!}=\sum_{n=R+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-tx)^{n}}{n!}.

Taking absolute values and using
t\in[0,1]
gives

\left|e^{-tx}-\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-tx)^{r}}{r!}\right|\leq\sum_{n=R+1}^{\infty}\frac{(t|x|)^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(t|x|)^{R+1}}{(R+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t|x|)^{k}}{(R+2)\cdots(R+1+k)}.

Since
(R+2)\cdots(R+1+k)\geq k!
, we obtain

\sum_{n=R+1}^{\infty}\frac{(t|x|)^{n}}{n!}\leq\frac{(t|x|)^{R+1}}{(R+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t|x|)^{k}}{k!}=\frac{(t|x|)^{R+1}}{(R+1)!}e^{t|x|},

which yields the claimed bound.
∎

Define for
r\geq 0
and
t>0
the auxiliary sums

(2.7)

F_{r}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}q(u_{n})^{r}\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}.

These series converge absolutely for every
t>0
since
|q(u_{n})|\leq Q_{*}
and

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}<\infty
.

Proposition 2.9

(Finite reduction in
t
)
.

Fix an integer
R\geq 0
. Then for
t\in(0,1]
,

(2.8)

\theta(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,F_{r}(t)\;+\;\mathrm{Err}_{R}(t),

where the error satisfies

(2.9)

\mathrm{Err}_{R}(t)=O(t^{R+1})\,\Theta_{0}(t).

Proof.

Insert (
2.6
) with
u=u_{n}
into (
2.5
):

\mathrm{e}^{-tq(u_{n})}=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,q(u_{n})^{r}+O(t^{R+1}),

uniformly in
n
for
t\in(0,1]
. Multiplying by
\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}
and summing over
n\geq 1

gives (
2.8
) with

\mathrm{Err}_{R}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\,O(t^{R+1})=O(t^{R+1})\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}=O(t^{R+1})\Theta_{0}(t),

which is (
2.9
).
∎

2.3.2.
Analytic expansion in
u
and reduction to
\Theta_{m}

For each
r\geq 0
, the function
u\mapsto q(u)^{r}
is analytic in
|u|<4
and hence admits
a power series expansion around
u=0
:

(2.10)

q(u)^{r}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{r,m}\,u^{m},\qquad|u|<4,

with real coefficients
c_{r,m}
(since
q
has real Taylor coefficients).

Lemma 2.10

(Geometric bound for the coefficients
c_{r,m}
)
.

Fix
r\geq 0
and choose any
\rho
with
r_{0}<\rho<4
. Then there exists
C_{r,\rho}>0

such that

(2.11)

|c_{r,m}|\leq C_{r,\rho}\,\rho^{-m}\qquad(m\geq 0).

In particular, the series
\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|\,r_{0}^{m}
converges.

Proof.

By Cauchy’s estimate applied to the analytic function
q(u)^{r}
on the circle
|u|=\rho
,

|c_{r,m}|=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|u|=\rho}\frac{q(u)^{r}}{u^{m+1}}\,du\right|\leq\rho^{-m}\sup_{|u|=\rho}|q(u)|^{r}.

This gives (
2.11
) with
C_{r,\rho}:=\sup_{|u|=\rho}|q(u)|^{r}
.
Since
r_{0}/\rho<1
, the final claim follows.
∎

Proposition 2.11

(Expansion of
F_{r}(t)
in terms of
\Theta_{m}(t)
)
.

For every
r\geq 0
and
t>0
,

(2.12)

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{r,m}\,\Theta_{m}(t),

and the series on the right converges absolutely.

Proof.

Since
u_{n}\in(0,r_{0}]
and (
2.10
) converges absolutely on
[0,r_{0}]
,
we have
q(u_{n})^{r}=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}u_{n}^{m}=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}a_{n}^{-2m}
.
Hence,

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}a_{n}^{-2m}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}.

To justify interchanging sums, note that for each fixed
t>0
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|\,a_{n}^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\leq\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|\left(\sup_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2m}\right)\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}=\Theta_{0}(t)\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|\,r_{0}^{m},

and the right-hand side is finite by Lemma
2.10
. Tonelli’s theorem
therefore applies and yields (
2.12
).
∎

Combining Propositions
2.9
and
2.11
, we obtain for any

R\geq 0
and
t\in(0,1]
the representation

(2.13)

\theta(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{r,m}\,\Theta_{m}(t)\;+\;O(t^{R+1})\Theta_{0}(t).

2.4.
Full asymptotics for
\Theta_{m}(t)

We will use the Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+a)^{-s}
for
\operatorname{Re}s>1
.

Lemma 2.12

(Half-integer identity)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}p>1
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-p}=\zeta(p,\tfrac{1}{2})=(2^{p}-1)\zeta(p).

Hence, as meromorphic functions of
p
,

(2.14)

\zeta(p,\tfrac{1}{2})=(2^{p}-1)\zeta(p).

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}p>1
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-p}=2^{p}\sum_{n\geq 0}(2n+1)^{-p}=2^{p}\Bigl(\sum_{m\geq 1}m^{-p}-\sum_{n\geq 1}(2n)^{-p}\Bigr)=(2^{p}-1)\zeta(p).

Both sides extend meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
, hence (
2.14
) follows by analytic continuation.
∎

Recall

\Theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{-(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}t}.

Proposition 2.13

(Full asymptotic expansion of
\Theta_{m}(t)
)
.

For each integer
m\geq 0
, the function
\Theta_{m}(t)
admits a full asymptotic expansion
as
t\to 0^{+}
with exponents in
\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

More precisely, fix an integer
K\geq 0
and set
s_{K}:=-K-\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then there exist
constants
A_{m,j}
(
0\leq j\leq K
) such that, as
t\to 0^{+}
,

(2.15)

\Theta_{m}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{m=0\}}\,t^{-1/2}\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{K}A_{m,j}\,t^{j}\;+\;\mathbf{1}_{\{1\leq m\leq K\}}\frac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-m\Bigr)\,t^{m-1/2}\;+\;O\!\bigl(t^{K+1/2}\bigr).

In particular,

\Theta_{0}(t)=O(t^{-1/2}),\qquad\Theta_{m}(t)=\Theta_{m}(0)+O(t^{1/2})\ (m=1),\qquad\Theta_{m}(t)=\Theta_{m}(0)+O(t)\ (m\geq 2),

and hence
\Theta_{m}(t)=O(1)
for every
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
. For
y>0
and
c>0
, Mellin inversion gives

\mathrm{e}^{-y}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=c}\Gamma(s)\,y^{-s}\,ds.

Apply this with
y=a_{n}^{2}t
and choose
c>\max(0,\tfrac{1}{2}-m)
so that

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2(m+c)}
converges absolutely. Fubini’s theorem yields

(2.16)

\Theta_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=c}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\,S_{m}(s)\,ds,

where

S_{m}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2(m+s)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-2(m+s)}.

Using Lemma
2.12
with
p=2(m+s)
and subtracting the
n=0
term

(\tfrac{1}{2})^{-2(m+s)}=2^{2(m+s)}
, we obtain the meromorphic identity

(2.17)

S_{m}(s)=(2^{2(m+s)}-1)\zeta(2(m+s))-2^{2(m+s)}.

The only pole of
\zeta
is at
1
, hence
S_{m}(s)
has at most a simple pole at

2(m+s)=1\iff s=\frac{1}{2}-m,

and
\Gamma(s)
has simple poles at
s=0,-1,-2,\dots
.

Fix
K\geq 0
and shift the contour in (
2.16
) from
\operatorname{Re}s=c
to
\operatorname{Re}s=s_{K}=-K-\tfrac{1}{2}
. This crosses the poles at
s=0,-1,\dots,-K
and possibly

s=\frac{1}{2}-m
(which lies to the right of
s_{K}
exactly when
m\leq K
). All poles are simple.
By the residue theorem,

\Theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{0}\in\{\frac{1}{2}-m,\,0,-1,\dots,-K\}\\
s_{K}<\operatorname{Re}s_{0}<c\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{0}}\bigl(\Gamma(s)t^{-s}S_{m}(s)\bigr)\;+\;\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=s_{K}}\Gamma(s)t^{-s}S_{m}(s)\,ds.

We compute the residues.
For
j=0,1,\dots,K
,
\operatorname{Res}_{s=-j}\Gamma(s)=(-1)^{j}/j!
, hence the residues at
s=-j
contribute
terms
A_{m,j}t^{j}
for suitable constants
A_{m,j}
.
At
s=\frac{1}{2}-m
(when
m\leq K
), we use that
\zeta(p)
has residue
1
at
p=1
and

p=2(m+s)
satisfies
dp/ds=2
, while
(2^{p}-1)
equals
1
at
p=1
. Therefore

\operatorname{Res}_{s=\frac{1}{2}-m}S_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\Gamma
is holomorphic at
s=\frac{1}{2}-m
,
this yields the half-integer term

\frac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-m\Bigr)\,t^{m-1/2}.

For
m=0
, this is the leading term
\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})t^{-1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}t^{-1/2}
.

Finally, we bound the shifted integral. On
\operatorname{Re}s=s_{K}
we have
|t^{-s}|=t^{K+1/2}
.
Moreover, by Stirling’s estimate,

|\Gamma(s_{K}+iy)|\ll_{K}(1+|y|)^{-K-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi|y|/2}
.
The function
S_{m}(s)
has at most polynomial growth in
|y|
on vertical lines by
(
2.17
) and standard bounds for
\zeta(\sigma+iy)
in vertical strips.
Hence the integrand is absolutely integrable in
y
and we obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=s_{K}}\Gamma(s)t^{-s}S_{m}(s)\,ds=O(t^{K+1/2})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}),

with an implied constant depending on
K
and
m
.
This proves (
2.15
) and hence the existence of a full asymptotic expansion.
The stated in particular bounds follow by inspecting the first few exponents:
for
m\geq 1
the
t^{0}
term is present and the next exponent is
1/2
if
m=1

and
1
if
m\geq 2
.
∎

Remark 2.14

(Contour shifts and poles on the contour)
.

In Mellin inversion arguments we shift a vertical line
\Re s=c
to the left and pick up
residues. If one of the poles of the integrand lies
exactly
on the intended new
contour (e.g. a zeta pole at
s=s_{0}
coinciding with a target line
\Re s=\sigma
),
we proceed in the standard way: either

(a)

shift instead to
\Re s=\sigma-\eta
for some fixed
\eta>0
, or

(b)

indent the contour around
s_{0}
and take the principal value plus the residue.

Since shifting slightly further left only improves decay of
t^{-s}
as
t\to 0^{+}
, the resulting
error term is unaffected (and in fact improves). We will not repeat this bookkeeping in each
contour shift.

2.5.
Full asymptotics for
F_{r}(t)

We now transfer the asymptotics of
\Theta_{m}(t)
to the weighted sums
F_{r}(t)
.

Proposition 2.15

(Full asymptotic expansion of
F_{r}(t)
)
.

For each integer
r\geq 0
, the function
F_{r}(t)
defined in (
2.7
)
admits a full asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
with exponents in
\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

More precisely, for every integer
K\geq 0
there exist constants
B_{r,j}
,

j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},1,\dots,K\}
, such that

(2.18)

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},1,\dots,K\}}B_{r,j}\,t^{j}+O(t^{K+1/2})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Proof.

Fix
r\geq 0
and
K\geq 0
. By Proposition
2.11
,

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{r,m}\,\Theta_{m}(t),

with absolute convergence for every
t>0
. For each
m\geq 0
, apply
Proposition
2.13
(with the same
K
) and write

\Theta_{m}(t)=\mathcal{P}_{m,K}(t)+R_{m,K}(t),

where
\mathcal{P}_{m,K}(t)
is the truncated part from (
2.15
) and

R_{m,K}(t)=O(t^{K+1/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Uniform control of the remainder and summation over
m
.

Set
M:=K+2
. Split

\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}R_{m,K}(t)=\sum_{m\leq M}c_{r,m}R_{m,K}(t)+\sum_{m>M}c_{r,m}R_{m,K}(t).

The first sum is finite, hence
O(t^{K+1/2})
.

For the tail
m>M
, we use the remainder representation coming from the contour shift
in the proof of Proposition
2.13
. Namely, if we write

s_{K}:=-K-\tfrac{1}{2}
, then (after subtracting residues up to order
K
)

(2.19)

R_{m,K}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re s=s_{K}}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\,S_{m}(s)\,ds,

where

S_{m}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\bigl(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)^{-2(m+s)}.

Since
x\mapsto x^{-\alpha}
is decreasing on
[a_{1},\infty)
for
\alpha>1
, we have the standard bound

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-\alpha}\leq a_{1}^{-\alpha}+\int_{a_{1}}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}\,dx=a_{1}^{-\alpha}+\frac{a_{1}^{-\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1}.

Apply this with
\alpha:=2(m+\Re s)
. On the line
\Re s=s_{K}=-K-\tfrac{1}{2}
and for
m\geq K+2
,
we have
\alpha=2m-2K-1\geq 3
, hence
(\alpha-1)^{-1}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Therefore

|S_{m}(s)|\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2(m+\Re s)}\ll_{K}a_{1}^{-2(m+\Re s)}+a_{1}^{-2(m+\Re s)+1}\ll_{K}a_{1}^{-2m}.

Since
a_{1}=\tfrac{3}{2}
, this gives the geometric bound
|S_{m}(s)|\ll_{K}(4/9)^{m}
uniformly in
\Im s
. Since
a_{1}^{-2}=(\tfrac{2}{3})^{2}=\tfrac{4}{9}=:r_{0}
, this yields the uniform geometric bound

(2.20)

|S_{m}(s)|\ll_{K}r_{0}^{\,m}\qquad(\Re s=s_{K},\ m>M),

uniformly in
\Im s
. Inserting (
2.20
) into (
2.19
) gives, for

t\in(0,1]
and
m>M
,

|R_{m,K}(t)|\leq\frac{t^{-s_{K}}}{2\pi}\Bigl(\sup_{\Re s=s_{K}}|S_{m}(s)|\Bigr)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Gamma(s_{K}+iy)|\,dy\ll_{K}r_{0}^{\,m}\,t^{K+1/2},

since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\Gamma(s_{K}+iy)|\,dy<\infty
for fixed
s_{K}
.
Therefore, using Lemma
2.10
(i.e.
\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|r_{0}^{\,m}<\infty
),

\sum_{m>M}|c_{r,m}||R_{m,K}(t)|\ll_{K}t^{K+1/2}\sum_{m>M}|c_{r,m}|r_{0}^{\,m}=O(t^{K+1/2}).

Hence

\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}R_{m,K}(t)=O(t^{K+1/2})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Summation of the truncated pieces.

The function
\mathcal{P}_{m,K}(t)
is a finite linear combination of the monomials

t^{-1/2}
,
t^{0},t^{1},\dots,t^{K}
, and (when
1\leq m\leq K
) the half-integer monomial
t^{m-1/2}
.
Thus
\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}\mathcal{P}_{m,K}(t)
is a finite linear combination of the same exponents,
once we justify absolute convergence for the integer-power coefficients.

For the integer powers
t^{j}
(
0\leq j\leq K
), Proposition
2.13
gives

A_{m,j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\,S_{m}(-j).

If
m\geq j+2
, then
S_{m}(-j)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2(m-j)}
converges absolutely and the same estimate as
(
2.20
) yields

|S_{m}(-j)|\ll_{j}r_{0}^{\,m}.

Hence
\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}A_{m,j}|<\infty
for each
j
, because only finitely many
m<j+2
remain and

\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|r_{0}^{\,m}<\infty
.

Collecting coefficients of the finitely many exponents

j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},1,\dots,K\}
yields constants
B_{r,j}
such that
(
2.18
) holds.
∎

Lemma 2.16

(Geometric tail control for analytic weights)
.

Let
g(u)=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{m}u^{m}
be analytic on
|u|<\rho
, and suppose
0\leq u_{n}\leq u_{0}<\rho
for all
n
.
Then for each
M\geq 0
one has the uniform remainder bound

g(u_{n})=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}u_{n}^{m}\;+\;O_{g}\!\left(\Bigl(\frac{u_{0}}{\rho}\Bigr)^{M}\right),

and consequently, for every
t\in(0,1]
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}g(u_{n})\,e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}^{m}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\;+\;O_{g}\!\left(\Bigl(\frac{u_{0}}{\rho}\Bigr)^{M}\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\right),

with an implied constant independent of
M
and
t
.

Remark 2.17
.

In applications we take
g(u)=q(u)^{r}
(or
g(u)=q_{p}(u)^{r}
). Since

\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\asymp t^{-1/2}
as
t\to 0^{+}
, the tail term in
Lemma
2.16
is
O((u_{0}/\rho)^{M}t^{-1/2})
. Hence for any fixed truncation
order in
t
one may first expand each finite
m
-sum and then let
M\to\infty
, justifying
termwise summation of the resulting asymptotic expansions.

2.6.
Full heat-trace expansion and admissibility

We now combine the reduction of Section
2.3
with the asymptotics of Sections
2.4
and
2.5
.

Theorem 2.18

(Full small-time expansion of
\theta(t)
)
.

There exist constants
(c_{\nu})_{\nu\geq 0}
and strictly increasing exponents

j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots\to+\infty
with
j_{\nu}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
such that

\theta(t)\sim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}c_{\nu}t^{j_{\nu}}\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

More concretely, for every integer
N\geq 0
there exist constants

c_{-1/2},c_{0},c_{1/2},\dots,c_{N}
such that

(2.21)

\theta(t)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N\}}c_{j}\,t^{j}+O(t^{N+1/2})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Proof.

Fix
N\geq 0
and apply Proposition
2.9
with
R=N
:

\theta(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,F_{r}(t)+O(t^{N+1})\Theta_{0}(t),\qquad t\in(0,1].

By Corollary
2.6
,
\Theta_{0}(t)=O(t^{-1/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, hence

O(t^{N+1})\Theta_{0}(t)=O(t^{N+1/2}).

For each fixed
r\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, apply Proposition
2.15
with

K=N-r
to obtain

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N-r\}}B_{r,j}\,t^{j}+O(t^{N-r+1/2}).

Multiplying by
t^{r}
gives

\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}F_{r}(t)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N-r\}}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}B_{r,j}\,t^{j+r}+O(t^{N+1/2}).

Summing over
r=0,1,\dots,N
(a finite sum) and collecting like powers yields an
expansion of
\theta(t)
involving only half-integer exponents up to
t^{N}
, with total
remainder
O(t^{N+1/2})
. This is exactly (
2.21
). Since
N
is arbitrary,
a full asymptotic expansion follows.
∎

Corollary 2.19

(Admissibility)
.

The sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
\lambda_{n}=\tau_{n}^{2}
is admissible
in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proof.

Condition (1) holds because
\lambda_{n}>0
.
Condition (3) holds by Corollary
2.6
.
Condition (2) holds because
\theta(t)
converges absolutely for
t>0
(Corollary
2.6
)
and has a full asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
by Theorem
2.18
.
∎

3.
Consequences and applications of admissibility

This section records several standard consequences of admissibility which will be useful
in applications (e.g. zeta regularization, coefficient extraction, and large-parameter
asymptotics of canonical products). All statements are proved directly from the heat-trace expansion established in Theorem
2.18
. The Mellin-transform representation, meromorphic continuation of the spectral zeta function, and special-value identities at nonpositive integers are instances of the general Mellin machinery in
[
37
, §2]
(notably their Theorems 3–4). We include proofs for completeness and to record the precise coefficients in our normalization.

3.1.
Leading heat coefficients and a Weyl law

We first isolate the first two coefficients in the small-time expansion of
\theta(t)
.
These coefficients are universal for the present model because the correction
q(u_{n})
is bounded.

Lemma 3.1

(Two-sided bounds for
\tau_{n}
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

n\leq\tau_{n}\leq n+\frac{1}{2},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad n^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}\leq\Bigl(n+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{2}.

Proof.

The lower bound
\tau_{n}\geq n
is Lemma
2.5
.
For the upper bound, note from (
2.1
) that
\tau_{n}=a_{n}-\rho(a_{n}^{-1})
with

\rho(a_{n}^{-1})\geq 0
(nonnegative coefficients), hence
\tau_{n}\leq a_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Squaring yields the corresponding bounds for
\lambda_{n}=\tau_{n}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Model half-integer heat trace)
.

Let
a_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\Theta_{0}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{n}^{2}t}
.
Then as
t\to 0^{+}
,

(3.1)

\Theta_{0}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,t^{-1/2}-1+\frac{1}{4}\,t+O(t^{2}).

In particular,
\Theta_{0}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,t^{-1/2}-1+O(t)
.

Proof.

This is the case
m=0
of Proposition
2.13
with explicit residues. One has

\Theta_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=c}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\,\Bigl((2^{2s}-1)\zeta(2s)-2^{2s}\Bigr)\,ds

for any
c>\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Shift the contour to
\operatorname{Re}s=-\tfrac{5}{2}
.
The poles crossed are:

•

s=\tfrac{1}{2}
(simple pole of
\zeta(2s)
at
2s=1
);

•

s=0,-1,-2
(simple poles of
\Gamma(s)
).

All other possible poles lie to the left of
\operatorname{Re}s=-\tfrac{5}{2}
.
The remainder integral on
\operatorname{Re}s=-\tfrac{5}{2}
is
O(t^{5/2})
and hence
O(t^{2})

by the same bounds used in Proposition
2.13
.

We now compute residues.
Write
S_{0}(s):=(2^{2s}-1)\zeta(2s)-2^{2s}
.
Near
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\zeta(2s)\sim\frac{1}{2s-1}
, hence
S_{0}(s)\sim\frac{1}{2s-1}
.
Therefore

\operatorname{Res}_{s=1/2}\bigl(\Gamma(s)t^{-s}S_{0}(s)\bigr)=\Gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\,t^{-1/2}\cdot\operatorname{Res}_{s=1/2}\frac{1}{2s-1}=\sqrt{\pi}\,t^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,t^{-1/2}.

At
s=0
,
\Gamma(s)
has residue
1
and
t^{-s}=1+O(s)
, so the residue equals
S_{0}(0)
.
Since
\zeta(0)=-\tfrac{1}{2}
and
2^{0}-1=0
, we have
S_{0}(0)=-1
, giving the constant term
-1
.
At
s=-1
,
\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}\Gamma(s)=-1
and
t^{-s}=t
, while

S_{0}(-1)=(2^{-2}-1)\zeta(-2)-2^{-2}=0-\frac{1}{4}=-\frac{1}{4},

because
\zeta(-2)=0
.
Thus the residue at
s=-1
equals
(-1)\cdot t\cdot(-1/4)=t/4
.

Crossing
s=-2
produces an additional residue term of size
O(t^{2})
, which we absorb into the
O(t^{2})
remainder.

Summing residues gives (
3.1
).
∎

Proposition 3.3

(First heat coefficients of
\theta(t)
)
.

As
t\to 0^{+}
,

(3.2)

\theta(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,t^{-1/2}-1+O(t^{1/2}).

In particular, in the notation of Theorem
2.18
one has

c_{-1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2},\qquad c_{0}=-1.

Proof.

Recall
\lambda_{n}=a_{n}^{2}+q(u_{n})
with
a_{n}=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
and
u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}\in(0,4/9]
.
Since
q
is continuous on
[0,4/9]
, set

Q_{*}:=\sup_{u\in[0,4/9]}|q(u)|<\infty.

Then for
t\in(0,1]
and all
n
,

\bigl|e^{-tq(u_{n})}-1\bigr|\leq t\,|q(u_{n})|\,e^{t|q(u_{n})|}\leq t\,Q_{*}e^{Q_{*}}=O(t),

uniformly in
n
. Hence

\theta(t)-\Theta_{0}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\bigl(e^{-tq(u_{n})}-1\bigr)=O(t)\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}=O(t)\,\Theta_{0}(t).

By Lemma
3.2
,
\Theta_{0}(t)=O(t^{-1/2})
, so

\theta(t)-\Theta_{0}(t)=O(t^{1/2})
. Inserting the two-term expansion

\Theta_{0}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}t^{-1/2}-1+O(t)
from Lemma
3.2

yields (
3.2
).
∎

As a byproduct we obtain the sharp one-dimensional Weyl law for the counting function.

Corollary 3.4

(Weyl law for the counting function)
.

Let

N(\Lambda):=\#\{n\geq 1:\ \lambda_{n}\leq\Lambda\},\qquad\Lambda\geq 0.

Then for all
\Lambda\geq 0
,

\lfloor\sqrt{\Lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\ \rfloor\leq\ N(\Lambda)\ \leq\ \lfloor\sqrt{\Lambda}\rfloor,

and in particular
N(\Lambda)=\sqrt{\Lambda}+O(1)
as
\Lambda\to\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
\lambda_{n}\leq(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}
and
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
.
If
(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\leq\Lambda
then
\lambda_{n}\leq\Lambda
, hence
N(\Lambda)\geq\lfloor\sqrt{\Lambda}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rfloor
.
Conversely, if
\lambda_{n}\leq\Lambda
then
n^{2}\leq\Lambda
, hence
n\leq\sqrt{\Lambda}
and
N(\Lambda)\leq\lfloor\sqrt{\Lambda}\rfloor
.
The displayed bounds follow.
∎

3.2.
The spectral zeta function: meromorphic continuation and special values

Define the spectral zeta function associated with
\lambda_{n}
by

(3.3)

Z(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}^{-s},\qquad\operatorname{Re}s>\frac{1}{2}.

Absolute convergence holds for
\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac{1}{2}
because
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
(Lemma
2.5
).

Lemma 3.5

(Exponential decay of the heat trace)
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 1
,

\theta(t)\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}t}.

Proof.

Write

\theta(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}t}\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})t}.

For
t\geq 1
,
\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})t}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})}
, hence

\theta(t)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}t}\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})}.

The series on the right converges because
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
and thus

\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{2}}
.
Absorb the convergent sum into the constant
C
.
∎

Proposition 3.6

(Mellin transform representation of
Z(s)
)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac{1}{2}
,

(3.4)

Z(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta(t)\,dt.

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}s>0
and
\lambda>0
one has

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\lambda^{-s}\Gamma(s).

For
\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the series defining
Z(s)
converges absolutely, so by Fubini,

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta(t)\,dt=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{n}t}\,dt=\Gamma(s)\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}^{-s}=\Gamma(s)Z(s),

which is (
3.4
).
∎

Theorem 3.7

(Meromorphic continuation of
Z(s)
and residues)
.

The function
Z(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
with at most simple poles at

s=\frac{1}{2}-k,\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots.

Moreover, if
c_{k-1/2}
denotes the coefficient of
t^{k-1/2}
in the heat expansion (
2.21
),
then

(3.5)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=1/2-k}Z(s)=\frac{c_{k-1/2}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}-k)}.

In particular,
\operatorname{Res}_{s=1/2}Z(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and write the expansion (
2.21
) as

\theta(t)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N\}}c_{j}t^{j}+R_{N}(t),\qquad R_{N}(t)=O(t^{N+1/2})\quad(t\to 0^{+}).

Split the Mellin integral (
3.4
) at
1
:

Z(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}\theta(t)\,dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta(t)\,dt\Bigr)=:\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\bigl(I_{0}(s)+I_{\infty}(s)\bigr).

By Lemma
3.5
,
I_{\infty}(s)
converges absolutely for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
and defines an entire function.

For
I_{0}(s)
, substitute
\theta(t)=\sum c_{j}t^{j}+R_{N}(t)
:

I_{0}(s)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N\}}c_{j}\int_{0}^{1}t^{s+j-1}\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}R_{N}(t)\,dt.

For
\operatorname{Re}(s+j)>0
,
\int_{0}^{1}t^{s+j-1}\,dt=\frac{1}{s+j}
, hence

I_{0}(s)=\sum_{j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N\}}\frac{c_{j}}{s+j}\;+\;H_{N}(s),

where
H_{N}(s)
is holomorphic in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s>-N-\tfrac{1}{2}
because
R_{N}(t)=O(t^{N+1/2})
.
Thus
Z(s)
extends meromorphically to
\operatorname{Re}s>-N-\tfrac{1}{2}
with possible poles only at
s=-j

for
j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2},\dots,N\}
.
Letting
N\to\infty
yields a meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
with possible poles at

s=-j
for
j\in\{-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\dots\}
, i.e.
s=\tfrac{1}{2}-k
for
k\geq 0
.

To compute residues, note that
\Gamma(s)
is holomorphic and nonzero at
s=\tfrac{1}{2}-k
.
From the decomposition above, near
s=\tfrac{1}{2}-k
the only singular term in
I_{0}(s)
is

\frac{c_{k-1/2}}{s-(1/2-k)}
, so (
3.5
) follows.

Finally, by Proposition
3.3
,
c_{-1/2}=\sqrt{\pi}/2
, hence

\operatorname{Res}_{s=1/2}Z(s)=c_{-1/2}/\Gamma(1/2)=(\sqrt{\pi}/2)/\sqrt{\pi}=1/2
.
∎

Remark 3.8

(Cancellation at nonpositive integers)
.

In the Mellin representation

Z(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta(t)\,dt,

the truncated small-
t
expansion of
\theta(t)
produces apparent simple poles of the integral
at points
s=-j
coming from factors
(s+j)^{-1}
. When
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, this singularity
is canceled by the simple zero of
1/\Gamma(s)
at
s=-j
(since
\Gamma
has a simple pole at
each nonpositive integer). Hence
Z(s)
is holomorphic at
s=0,-1,-2,\dots
.

Theorem 3.9

(Special values at nonpositive integers)
.

For every integer
m\geq 0
, the meromorphic continuation of
Z(s)
is holomorphic at
s=-m
and satisfies

(3.6)

Z(-m)=(-1)^{m}m!\,c_{m},

where
c_{m}
is the coefficient of
t^{m}
in the small-time expansion (
2.21
).
In particular,
Z(0)=c_{0}=-1
.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
and choose
N\geq m
in the decomposition in the proof of Theorem
3.7
.
Then near
s=-m
we have

Z(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\Bigl(\frac{c_{m}}{s+m}+A(s)\Bigr),

where
A(s)
is holomorphic at
s=-m
(it contains the remaining
\frac{c_{j}}{s+j}
terms with
j\neq m
,
the function
H_{N}(s)
, and the entire tail
I_{\infty}(s)
).
Since
\Gamma(s)
has a simple pole at
s=-m
with residue
\operatorname{Res}_{s=-m}\Gamma(s)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}
,
it follows that
1/\Gamma(s)
has a simple zero at
s=-m
and admits the expansion

\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}=(-1)^{m}m!\,(s+m)+O\bigl((s+m)^{2}\bigr).

Therefore,

Z(-m)=\lim_{s\to-m}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\cdot\frac{c_{m}}{s+m}=(-1)^{m}m!c_{m},

and the remaining holomorphic term
A(s)
vanishes in the limit because
1/\Gamma(s)\to 0
.
This proves (
3.6
).
∎

3.3.
Resolvent trace and the canonical product
W(\lambda)

Define the canonical genus-
0
product

(3.7)

W(\lambda):=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}}\Bigr).

Since
\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}^{-1}<\infty
(by
\lambda_{n}\geq n^{2}
), the product converges
absolutely and locally uniformly, hence
W
is entire.

Proposition 3.10

(Logarithmic derivative of
W
)
.

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-\lambda_{n}:\ n\geq 1\}
,

(3.8)

\frac{W^{\prime}(\lambda)}{W(\lambda)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda+\lambda_{n}},

where the series converges locally uniformly in
\lambda
.

Proof.

Fix a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-\lambda_{n}\}
.
Then there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\lambda+\lambda_{n}|\geq\delta\,\lambda_{n}
for all
\lambda\in K
and all
n
.
Consequently,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\sup_{\lambda\in K}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\lambda+\lambda_{n}}\Bigr|\leq\delta^{-1}\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}^{-1}<\infty.

Thus
\sum_{n\geq 1}\log(1+\lambda/\lambda_{n})
converges uniformly on
K
and may be differentiated termwise,
yielding (
3.8
).
∎

Define the resolvent trace

R(\lambda):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda+\lambda_{n}}.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
it admits a Laplace transform representation.

Lemma 3.11

(Laplace representation)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
,

(3.9)

R(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\theta(t)\,dt.

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
and
\lambda_{n}>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda+\lambda_{n})t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\lambda+\lambda_{n}}.

Moreover, for each fixed
\lambda
with
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
, the series

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{|\lambda+\lambda_{n}|}
converges because it is dominated by a constant multiple of

\sum\lambda_{n}^{-1}
. Hence by Fubini,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\theta(t)\,dt=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda+\lambda_{n})t}\,dt=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda+\lambda_{n}}=R(\lambda).

∎

We now derive large-
\lambda
asymptotics for
R(\lambda)
and
\log W(\lambda)
from the small-time heat expansion. This is a classical Watson-lemma mechanism, we include a proof tailored to our situation.

Remark 3.12

(Branch conventions in sectors)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
and set

\Sigma_{\delta}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\ |\arg\lambda|\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta\}.

On
\Sigma_{\delta}
we use the principal branch of
\log\lambda
(so
\arg\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi)
)
and define
\lambda^{\alpha}:=\exp(\alpha\log\lambda)
. Since
\Sigma_{\delta}
is simply connected and
contained in the open right half-plane, these branches are single-valued and holomorphic on

\Sigma_{\delta}
. In particular, the straight segment from

to
\lambda
lies in
\Sigma_{\delta}
,
so term-by-term integration of asymptotic series in
\lambda
along that segment is legitimate.

Lemma 3.13

(Watson-type lemma for Laplace transforms)
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable and assume:

(a)

as
t\to 0^{+}
,

f(t)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{M}a_{\nu}t^{\alpha_{\nu}}+O(t^{\alpha_{M+1}}),\qquad-1<\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{M+1};

(b)

there exist
c,C>0
such that
|f(t)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-ct}
for all
t\geq 1
.

Then for every
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
, as
|\lambda|\to\infty
with
|\arg\lambda|\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(t)\,dt=\sum_{\nu=0}^{M}a_{\nu}\Gamma(\alpha_{\nu}+1)\lambda^{-\alpha_{\nu}-1}+O(|\lambda|^{-\alpha_{M+1}-1}).

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
and set
\Sigma_{\delta}:=\{\lambda:\ |\arg\lambda|\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta\}
.
For
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\delta}
,
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq|\lambda|\sin\delta
.

Split the Laplace integral at
1
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(t)\,dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(t)\,dt=:I_{0}(\lambda)+I_{\infty}(\lambda).

By assumption (b), for
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\delta}
,

|I_{\infty}(\lambda)|\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\operatorname{Re}\lambda\,t}\,C\mathrm{e}^{-ct}\,dt\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(|\lambda|\sin\delta+c)t}\,dt\ll_{\delta}\mathrm{e}^{-|\lambda|\sin\delta},

hence
I_{\infty}(\lambda)
is exponentially small and in particular
O(|\lambda|^{-N})
for every
N
.

For
I_{0}(\lambda)
, substitute the expansion from (a):

I_{0}(\lambda)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{M}a_{\nu}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}t^{\alpha_{\nu}}\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}O(t^{\alpha_{M+1}})\,dt.

The remainder integral satisfies

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}O(t^{\alpha_{M+1}})\,dt\Bigr|

\displaystyle\ll\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\operatorname{Re}\lambda\,t}t^{\alpha_{M+1}}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(|\lambda|\sin\delta)t}t^{\alpha_{M+1}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\alpha_{M+1}+1)\,(|\lambda|\sin\delta)^{-\alpha_{M+1}-1},

so it is
O_{\delta}(|\lambda|^{-\alpha_{M+1}-1})
.

Finally, for each
\alpha>-1
and
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\delta}
,

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}t^{\alpha}\,dt=\lambda^{-\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-u}u^{\alpha}\,du=\Gamma(\alpha+1)\lambda^{-\alpha-1}+O_{\delta}(\mathrm{e}^{-|\lambda|\sin\delta}),

because
\int_{\lambda}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-u}u^{\alpha}\,du=O(\mathrm{e}^{-\operatorname{Re}\lambda})
.
Combining the above completes the proof.
∎

(Asymptotics of the resolvent trace)

3.4.
A robust admissibility criterion: analytic perturbations of quadratic lattices

The proof given above is stable under perturbations and depends only on analyticity of the correction term.
We record a general criterion which may be useful in related extremal-function problems.

Theorem 3.17

(General admissibility criterion)
.

Fix
a>0
. Assume that
q
is analytic in a neighborhood of the closed interval
[0,a^{-2}]

(equivalently, there exists
\rho>a^{-2}
such that
q
is analytic on
|u|<\rho
), and that

q
is real-valued on
[0,a^{-2}]
. Define

\lambda_{n}:=(n+a)^{2}+q\bigl((n+a)^{-2}\bigr),\qquad n\geq 0,

and assume
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n
. Then the sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is admissible in the
sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proof.

Set
a_{n}:=n+a
and
u_{n}:=a_{n}^{-2}
. Then
u_{n}\in(0,u_{0}]
where
u_{0}=a^{-2}
.
Choose
\rho>a^{-2}
such that
q
is analytic on the disk
|u|<\rho
.
Set
u_{0}:=a^{-2}
so that
0<u_{n}\leq u_{0}<\rho
for all
n
.
Then
q
is bounded on
[0,u_{0}]
, say
\sup_{u\in[0,u_{0}]}|q(u)|\leq Q_{*}<\infty
.

(1) and absolute convergence.

Since
q(u_{n})
is real and bounded and
a_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, we have
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
and

\Re\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}>0
. Moreover,
\lambda_{n}\geq a_{n}^{2}-Q_{*}\gg n^{2}
, hence

\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}
converges absolutely for every
t>0
and

\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\ll t^{-1/2}
as
t\to 0^{+}
, implying admissibility (3) as before.

(2) Full heat-trace expansion.

Write

\theta(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}=\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}e^{-tq(u_{n})}.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
|q(u_{n})|\leq Q_{*}
, Taylor’s theorem gives for
t\in(0,1]
,
uniformly in
n
,

e^{-tq(u_{n})}=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,q(u_{n})^{r}+O(t^{N+1}).

Hence

\theta(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,F_{r}(t)+O(t^{N+1})\Theta_{a,0}(t),\qquad F_{r}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}q(u_{n})^{r}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t},

where
\Theta_{a,0}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}
.
Since
\Theta_{a,0}(t)=O(t^{-1/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, the remainder is
O(t^{N+1/2})
.

It remains to show that each
F_{r}(t)
has a full expansion.
Because
q
is analytic on
\{|u|\leq r\}
, the function
q(u)^{r}
has a power series

q(u)^{r}=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}u^{m},\qquad|u|<r,

and by Cauchy’s estimate there is
C_{r}>0
such that
|c_{r,m}|\leq C_{r}r^{-m}
.
Since
0<u_{n}\leq u_{0}<r
,

\sum_{m\geq 0}|c_{r,m}|\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}^{m}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}\leq\Theta_{a,0}(t)\sum_{m\geq 0}C_{r}\Bigl(\frac{u_{0}}{r}\Bigr)^{m}<\infty,

so we may exchange sums and obtain

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{r,m}\,\Theta_{a,m}(t),\qquad\Theta_{a,m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}^{-2m}e^{-a_{n}^{2}t}.

Finally, each
\Theta_{a,m}(t)
has a full small-time expansion by the Mellin/Hurwitz zeta
contour shift (exactly as in your Proposition
2.13
, with
\zeta(\cdot,a)
),
and the
m
-sum above converges absolutely and geometrically, so the expansions may be summed termwise.
This yields a full expansion for each
F_{r}(t)
and hence for
\theta(t)
.
Therefore admissibility (2) holds, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Conjecture 1 in

In this section we prove Conjecture 1 of

Theorem 4.1

(Conjecture 1 of

]

For every

4.1.
The inversion identity for
\varphi

We adopt the notation of
[
6
]
. In particular,

\varphi(z)=\Phi(z)\Phi(-z),\qquad\Phi(z)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{\tau_{n}}\Bigr),

and
\varphi
is the extremal function normalized by
\varphi(0)=1
(hence
\Phi(0)=1

Let

(4.1)

Tz:=\frac{1}{2\pi iCz},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast},

(4.2)

Lemma 4.2

(Gauged functional equation)
.

Let
A
be defined by (
4.2
). Then for every

,

(4.3)

2\sqrt{\pi C}\,z\,A(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi/4}A(Tz)+\mathrm{e}^{i\pi/4}A(-Tz).

Proof.

We start from the functional equation
[
6

for

\Phi(z)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{4Cz}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi/4+\frac{i\pi}{2}z}\,\Phi(Tz)+\mathrm{e}^{i\pi/4-\frac{i\pi}{2}z}\,\Phi(-Tz)}{2\sqrt{\pi C}\,z}.

Using (

) and the identity

\frac{1}{4C(Tz)}=\frac{1}{4C}\cdot(2\pi iCz)=\frac{\pi i}{2}z,

we have
\Phi(Tz)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4C(Tz)}}A(Tz)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi i}{2}z}A(Tz)

\Phi(-Tz)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi i}{2}z}A(-Tz)

[

).
∎

Proposition 4.3

(Inversion identity)
.

For every

(4.4)

\varphi(Tz)=\pi Cz^{2}\bigl(A(z)^{2}+A(-z)^{2}\bigr).

Equivalently, with

\varphi(Tz)=\pi Cz^{2}\,\varphi(z)\,\bigl(R(z)+R(z)^{-1}\bigr).

4.2.
Sectorial logarithms and an even-coefficient invariance

We now extract from (

) a parity statement about the

.

Definition 4.4

(Poincaré asymptotic coefficients with logarithms)
.

Let
S
be a sector with vertex at
0
and fix a holomorphic branch of
\log z
on
S
.
Let
F
be holomorphic on
S\cap\{0<|z|<r\}
.

We say that
F
admits a (logarithmic) Poincaré asymptotic expansion on

if there exist
an integer
n_{0}
and
P\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that for every integer

one has

F(z)=\sum_{p=0}^{P}(\log z)^{p}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{N-1}a_{n,p}\,z^{n}\;+\;O(|z|^{N})\qquad(z\to 0,\ z\in S),

for some coefficients
a_{n,p}\in\mathbb{C}
(depending only on
F
and
S
).
These coefficients are uniquely determined, we write

[z^{n}(\log z)^{p}]_{\mathrm{as}}\,F:=a_{n,p},\qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad[z^{n}]_{\mathrm{as}}\,F:=[z^{n}(\log z)^{0}]_{\mathrm{as}}\,F.

If
F
is holomorphic at
0
(so that no logarithmic or negative-power terms occur),
then
[z^{n}]_{\mathrm{as}}F
coincides with the usual Taylor coefficient, denoted
[z^{n}]_{\mathrm{Tay}}F
.

Remark 4.5
.

If
G
is holomorphic in a sector at
\infty
and admits an asymptotic expansion in inverse powers,
we interpret
[w^{-n}(\log w)^{p}]_{\mathrm{as}}G
as
[z^{n}(\log z)^{p}]_{\mathrm{as}}(G(1/z))
as
z\to 0

in the corresponding sector.

Lemma 4.6

(Odd Laurent structure of
\log R
near
0
)
.

There exists
r>0
such that on
|z|<r
one can choose a holomorphic branch of

H(z):=\log R(z)=\log\frac{A(z)}{A(-z)}

satisfying
H(z)=\frac{1}{2Cz}+h(z)
, where
h
is holomorphic at
0
and odd
(hence
h(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}h_{2m+1}z^{2m+1}
).

Proof.

Using (
4.2
) we have

R(z)=\frac{A(z)}{A(-z)}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2Cz}}\frac{\Phi(z)}{\Phi(-z)}.

Since
\Phi(0)=1
, the ratio
\Phi(z)/\Phi(-z)
is holomorphic near
0
and equals
1
at
z=0
.
Hence we may choose the holomorphic logarithm

h(z):=\log\frac{\Phi(z)}{\Phi(-z)}

with
h(0)=0
. Then
h(-z)=\log\frac{\Phi(-z)}{\Phi(z)}=-h(z)
, so
h
is odd.
Defining
H(z):=\frac{1}{2Cz}+h(z)
gives the desired branch of
\log R(z)
.
∎

We then
define

\log\!\bigl(R(z)+R(z)^{-1}\bigr)
on

by the identity
\log\!\bigl(R+R^{-1}\bigr)=H+\log\!\bigl(1+e^{-2H}\bigr)
, where
\log(1+u)
is the principal holomorphic logarithm on
|u|<1/2
, this choice is compatible with the factorization
R+R^{-1}=e^{H}(1+e^{-2H})
and avoids branch ambiguities.

Lemma 4.7

(Even-coefficient invariance)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\pi/4)
and let

S_{\varepsilon}:=\Bigl\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ 0<|z|<\tau_{1},\ |\arg z|<\varepsilon\Bigr\}.

On
S_{\varepsilon}
we take the branch of

with

.
Then
\log\varphi(Tz)
admits a logarithmic Poincaré asymptotic expansion as

in

in the sense of Definition
4.4
, and for every integer

,

(4.6)

[z^{2k}]_{\mathrm{as}}\log\varphi(Tz)=[z^{2k}]_{\mathrm{Tay}}\log\varphi(z).

Proof.

On
S_{\varepsilon}
we have
\Re(1/z)\geq(\cos\varepsilon)/|z|
. By Lemma

,

.

Now inspect the contributions to the coefficient
[z^{2k}]_{\mathrm{as}}
for
k\geq 1
:

•

\log(\pi Cz^{2})=\log(\pi C)+2\log z
contributes no positive powers of
z
.

•

contributes no positive
even
powers of

because

\frac{1}{2Cz}
is a
z^{-1}
term and

is odd.

•

\log(1+\mathrm{e}^{-2H(z)})
is flat by (
4.7
) and thus contributes no terms to the (logarithmic)
Poincaré expansion.

Therefore, for every
k\geq 1
, the coefficient of

in the logarithmic Poincaré expansion of

\log\varphi(Tz)
equals the Taylor coefficient of

in
\log\varphi(z)
, which is precisely
(
4.6
).
∎

4.3.
Coefficient identifications at

and
\infty

We now compute the even coefficients on the right- and left-hand side of

).

Lemma 4.8

(Taylor coefficients at
0
)
.

For
|z|<\tau_{1}
one has

(4.9)

\log\varphi(z)=-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{L_{+}(2k)}{k}\,z^{2k}.

In particular, for every
k\geq 1
,

[z^{2k}]_{\mathrm{Tay}}\log\varphi(z)=-\frac{L_{+}(2k)}{k}.

Proof.

From
[

, (1.1) and (1.5)]
(or directly from the product for
\varphi
) we have

\varphi(z)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1-\frac{z^{2}}{\tau_{n}^{2}}\Bigr),

and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-2}<\infty
implies the product converges locally uniformly.
For
|z|<\tau_{1}
we may expand
\log(1-w)=-\sum_{k\geq 1}w^{k}/k
and sum termwise to obtain

\log\varphi(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\log\Bigl(1-\frac{z^{2}}{\tau_{n}^{2}}\Bigr)=-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{z^{2k}}{k}\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-2k},

which is (
4.9
).
∎

Lemma 4.9

(Asymptotic coefficients at
\infty
)
.

Let

in a sector

\log\varphi(w)=\textup{(terms involving $w$ and odd powers of $w^{-1}$)}\;-\;\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{L_{+}(-2m)}{m}\,w^{-2m}\;+\;O(|w|^{-2N-1}).

Consequently, for each

[w^{-2m}]_{\mathrm{as}}\log\varphi(w)=-\frac{L_{+}(-2m)}{m}.

This is precisely Corollary

, noting that

.
∎

Lemma 4.10

(Asymptotic coefficients of

Let

Proof.

. Logarithmic terms (coming from

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We first prove the identity for

.
By Lemma

∎

The only genuinely nonlocal input in the argument is the coefficient extraction at infinity (Lemma

), which in our setting follows from admissibility of

together with the Quine–Heydari–Song zeta-regularization calculus. All other steps are algebraic consequences of the functional equation
[

combined with a parity argument in a small sector at

.

5.
Admissibility of higher powers of
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}

Let
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the positive zeros from
[
6
]
and set
a_{n}:=n+\tfrac{1}{2}
.
For each integer
m\geq 1
define the heat trace

\theta_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\tau_{n}^{m}t}\qquad(t>0).

In this section we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1

(Parity dichotomy)
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer.

(a)

If
m
is
odd
, then the heat trace
\theta_{m}(t)
contains a
nonzero
term of the form

\kappa_{m}\,t\log t,

and hence
(\tau_{n}^{m})_{n\geq 1}
is
not
admissible in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song.
In particular,
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(i.e.
m=1
) is not admissible, and neither is
(\tau_{n}^{m})
for any odd
m\geq 3
.

(b)

If
m
is
even
, then
(\tau_{n}^{m})_{n\geq 1}
is admissible in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song.

5.1.
Conditions (1) and (3) for all
m

By
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
there exists an odd power series
\rho
with nonnegative coefficients,
radius
2
, and
\rho(2)=\tfrac{1}{2}
such that

(5.1)

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-\rho(a_{n}^{-1}),\qquad n\geq 1.

Since
a_{n}^{-1}\leq 2/3<2
and
\rho(z)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\alpha_{j}z^{j}
has nonnegative coefficients and
converges on
|z|\leq 2

[
6
, Thm. 6.1]
, the partial sums are increasing on
[0,2]

and hence
\rho
is increasing there. Therefore

0\leq\rho(a_{n}^{-1})\leq\rho(2)=\tfrac{1}{2},

so (
5.1
) yields
\tau_{n}\geq a_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}=n
for all
n\geq 1
.

In particular, for every integer
m\geq 1
we have
\tau_{n}^{m}\geq n^{m}
, hence
\theta_{m}(t)
converges
absolutely for each
t>0
and satisfies
\theta_{m}(t)\ll t^{-1/m}
as
t\to 0^{+}
.
Thus QHS admissibility conditions (1) and (3) hold for
(\tau_{n}^{m})_{n\geq 1}
for every
m
:
condition (1) is immediate since
\tau_{n}^{m}>0
, and condition (3) follows because

t^{\beta}\theta_{m}(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
for any
\beta>1/m
(e.g.
\beta=2
works uniformly for all
m\geq 1
).
Hence only the
pure-power
expansion requirement (QHS condition (2)) remains to be checked.

5.2.
Odd
m
: an explicit
a_{n}^{-1}
term in
\tau_{n}^{m}

Write
m=2\ell+1
with
\ell\geq 0
.
We show that
\tau_{n}^{m}
has an
a_{n}^{-1}
term with a coefficient that can be computed explicitly
from the BOCRS Dirichlet series

L_{-}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}^{s}}\qquad(\Re s>1),

which is denoted
L_{\tau}(s)
in earlier parts of
[
6
]
and is entire in
s
by
[
6
, §10.2]
.

Lemma 5.2

(Coefficient of
a_{n}^{-1}
in
\tau_{n}^{2\ell+1}
)
.

Let
m=2\ell+1
be odd. Then as
n\to\infty
one has an asymptotic expansion

(5.2)

\tau_{n}^{m}=a_{n}^{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{m,j}\,a_{n}^{m-2j}\;+\;d_{m}\,a_{n}^{-1}\;+\;O(a_{n}^{-3}),

for some real constants
c_{m,j}
, where the coefficient
d_{m}
is given by the closed formula

(5.3)

d_{m}=(-1)^{\ell}\,\frac{L_{-}(m)}{2^{m-1}\,\pi^{m+1}\,C^{m}}.

In particular,
d_{m}\neq 0
for every odd
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

Define
\tau(z):=z^{-1}-\rho(z)
, so that (
5.1
) reads
\tau_{n}=\tau(a_{n}^{-1})
.
Then
\tau(z)^{m}
is a Laurent series at
z=0
with leading term
z^{-m}
, and

\tau(a_{n}^{-1})^{m}=a_{n}^{m}+\cdots+\bigl([z^{1}]\tau(z)^{m}\bigr)a_{n}^{-1}+O(a_{n}^{-3}).

Thus
d_{m}=[z^{1}]\tau(z)^{m}
.

To compute this coefficient, we use the auxiliary series
g
introduced in
[
6
, §6]
:

g(w):=\frac{1}{\pi i}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{2\,L_{-}(2k-1)}{2k-1}\,w^{2k-1},\qquad 0<|w|<\tau_{1},

and the defining identity for
\rho
(equation (6.2) in
[
6
]
):

(5.4)

g\!\left(\frac{1}{2\pi iC\,\tau(z)}\right)=\frac{1}{z}\qquad(|z|\ \text{small}).

Justification that
f=1/g
is holomorphic and locally invertible at
0
.

The
k=0
term in the definition of
g
involves
L_{-}(-1)
, which is defined by analytic continuation
and satisfies
L_{-}(-1)=-\frac{1}{4C}

[
6
, §10.2, eq. (10.1)]
. Hence

g(w)=\frac{1}{\pi i}\cdot\frac{2L_{-}(-1)}{-1}\,w^{-1}+O(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi iC}\,w^{-1}+O(w)\qquad(w\to 0),

so
f(w):=1/g(w)
is holomorphic near
0
with
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=2\pi iC\neq 0
.

Set

w(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi iC\,\tau(z)}.

Then (
5.4
) becomes
g(w(z))=z^{-1}
. Equivalently, writing
f(w):=1/g(w)
, we have

(5.5)

f(w(z))=z,

so
w(z)
is the local inverse at
0
of
f
.

Now

\tau(z)^{m}=\frac{1}{(2\pi iC)^{m}}\,w(z)^{-m},

so
d_{m}=[z^{1}]\tau(z)^{m}=(2\pi iC)^{-m}\,[z^{1}]w(z)^{-m}
.

Computation of
d_{m}
via residues.

Since
f
is holomorphic near
0
with
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, the Lagrange–Bürmann
(residue) formula gives, for
\psi(w)=w^{-m}
,

[z^{1}]\psi(w(z))=\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}\frac{\psi^{\prime}(w)}{f(w)}\,dw.

Applying this with
\psi(w)=w^{-m}
(so
\psi^{\prime}(w)=-mw^{-m-1}
) yields

[z^{1}]\,w(z)^{-m}=\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}\left(-mw^{-m-1}\right)\frac{dw}{f(w)}=\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}\left(-mw^{-m-1}\right)g(w)\,dw=-m\,[w^{m}]\,g(w).

Hence

d_{m}=(2\pi iC)^{-m}[z^{1}]\,w(z)^{-m}=-\frac{m}{(2\pi iC)^{m}}\,[w^{m}]g(w).

Since
g(w)
is an odd Laurent series,
[w^{m}]g(w)=0
for even
m
, and for odd
m
we have

[w^{m}]g(w)=\frac{1}{\pi i}\cdot\frac{2\,L_{-}(m)}{m}.

Substituting gives

d_{m}=-\frac{m}{(2\pi iC)^{m}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi i}\cdot\frac{2L_{-}(m)}{m}=-\frac{2L_{-}(m)}{\pi i}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi iC)^{m}},

which simplifies to (
5.3
) because
m=2\ell+1
implies
i^{m+1}=i^{2\ell+2}=(-1)^{\ell+1}
.

Finally,
L_{-}(m)\neq 0
for every integer
m\geq 1
.
Indeed,
\tau_{n}
is strictly increasing because
a_{n}
increases and
\rho(a_{n}^{-1})

decreases (since
a_{n}^{-1}
decreases and
\rho
is increasing).
Hence
\tau_{n}^{-m}
decreases to
0
.
For
m=1
the series defining
L_{-}(1)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}^{-1}
converges by the alternating
series test, and for
m\geq 2
it is absolutely convergent.
In all cases
L_{-}(m)
has the sign of its first term, so
L_{-}(m)<0
and in particular
L_{-}(m)\neq 0
.

∎

5.3.
Odd
m
: a harmonic weighted sum produces a logarithm

Define the harmonic weight sum

H_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}.

Lemma 5.3

(Logarithmic asymptotic)
.

For each integer
m\geq 1
,

H_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}=\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1)\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Proof.

Fix
c>0
. By Mellin inversion,

e^{-y}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re s=c}\Gamma(s)\,y^{-s}\,ds\qquad(y>0).

With
y=a_{n}^{m}t
and summing over
n\geq 1
(Fubini is justified since
\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1-ms}

converges absolutely for
\Re s>0
), we obtain

H_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re s=c}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\,S_{m}(s)\,ds,\qquad S_{m}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1-ms}.

Using
\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-p}=\zeta(p,\tfrac{1}{2})
and subtracting the
n=0
term,
we have the meromorphic identity

S_{m}(s)=\zeta(1+ms,\tfrac{1}{2})-2^{1+ms}.

Since
\zeta(\cdot,\tfrac{1}{2})
has a simple pole at
1
with residue
1
, it follows that

S_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{ms}+O(1)\qquad(s\to 0),

while
\Gamma(s)=\frac{1}{s}+O(1)
and
t^{-s}=1-s\log t+O(s^{2})
. Hence

\Gamma(s)t^{-s}S_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{s^{2}}-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\log t}{s}+O(1),

so the coefficient of
1/s
equals
-\frac{1}{m}\log t+O(1)
.

Now shift the contour from
\Re s=c
to
\Re s=-\tfrac{1}{2}
. The only pole crossed is at
s=0
,
and the integral on
\Re s=-\tfrac{1}{2}
is
O(t^{1/2})
by Stirling’s bound for
\Gamma

and polynomial growth of
\zeta
in vertical strips. Therefore,

H_{m}(t)=-\frac{1}{m}\log t+O(1)=\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1),

as claimed.
∎

5.4.
Odd
m
: a nonzero
t\log t
term in
\theta_{m}(t)

Lemma 5.4

(No logarithm for the polynomial part)
.

Let
m=2\ell+1
be odd and define

b_{n}:=a_{n}^{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{m,j}\,a_{n}^{m-2j}.

Then the heat trace

\vartheta_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-b_{n}t}

admits a full asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0^{+}
in
pure powers
(in particular, it has no

t\log t
term).

Proof.

Write
u_{n}:=a_{n}^{-2}
and set

F(u):=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{m,j}u^{j},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad b_{n}=a_{n}^{m}F(u_{n})=a_{n}^{m}+a_{n}^{m-2}q(u_{n}),

where
q(u):=(F(u)-1)/u=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{m,j}u^{j-1}
is a polynomial (hence analytic on
|u|<\infty
).
Since
u_{n}\leq 4/9
,
q
is bounded on
[0,4/9]
.

Now expand

\vartheta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}\,e^{-t\,a_{n}^{m-2}q(u_{n})}

by a Taylor expansion in
t
with a summable remainder (as in the even-power argument):
for each fixed
R\geq 0
,

e^{-t\,a_{n}^{m-2}q(u_{n})}=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,a_{n}^{(m-2)r}q(u_{n})^{r}+\text{(remainder)}.

Next expand
q(u)^{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{r(\ell-1)}c_{r,k}u^{k}
and note that every resulting weight exponent

(m-2)r-2k\ \geq\ (m-2)r-2r(\ell-1)=r\ \geq\ 0.

Hence
\vartheta_{m}(t)
is a finite linear combination (up to an arbitrarily small remainder in
powers of
t
) of sums of the form

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{q}\,e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}\qquad(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}).

For such
q
, Mellin inversion shows that the only additional pole coming from the Hurwitz zeta factor
occurs at
s=(q+1)/m>0
and therefore cannot collide with the poles of
\Gamma(s)
at
0,-1,-2,\dots
.
Consequently the contour shift produces a full small-
t
expansion in pure powers and no logarithms.
∎

Proposition 5.5

(Odd powers have a logarithmic term)
.

Let
m
be odd. Then as
t\to 0^{+}
,

\theta_{m}(t)=\cdots+\frac{d_{m}}{m}\,t\log t+O(t),

where
d_{m}\neq 0
is the coefficient from Lemma
5.2
. In particular,
\theta_{m}(t)
cannot have
a full asymptotic expansion in pure powers, so
(\tau_{n}^{m})
is not admissible in the sense of
[
37
]
.

Proof.

Let
m
be odd. From Lemma
5.2
, there exists
N\geq 1
and a constant
C>0
such that,
for all
n\geq N
,

\tau_{n}^{m}=b_{n}+d_{m}a_{n}^{-1}+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad b_{n}:=a_{n}^{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{(m-1)/2}c_{m,j}\,a_{n}^{m-2j},\qquad|\varepsilon_{n}|\leq Ca_{n}^{-3}.

The finitely many terms
n<N
contribute a function analytic at
t=0
(hence a pure power series),
so they do not affect the presence or the coefficient of a
t\log t
term. We therefore focus on
n\geq N
.

Fix
t\in(0,1]
. Write

e^{-\tau_{n}^{m}t}=e^{-b_{n}t}\,e^{-d_{m}ta_{n}^{-1}}\,e^{-\varepsilon_{n}t}.

Since
a_{n}^{-1}\leq 2/3
and
t\leq 1
, the quantity
|d_{m}|ta_{n}^{-1}
is uniformly bounded in
n
.
Using the standard estimate
|e^{-x}-1+x|\ll x^{2}e^{|x|}
, we obtain

e^{-d_{m}ta_{n}^{-1}}=1-d_{m}ta_{n}^{-1}+O(t^{2}a_{n}^{-2}),

and similarly (since
|\varepsilon_{n}|t\ll a_{n}^{-3}
)

e^{-\varepsilon_{n}t}=1+O(ta_{n}^{-3}).

Multiplying these and absorbing cross-terms gives, for
n\geq N
,

e^{-\tau_{n}^{m}t}=e^{-b_{n}t}\Bigl(1-d_{m}ta_{n}^{-1}\Bigr)+O\!\left(e^{-b_{n}t}\,(t^{2}a_{n}^{-2}+ta_{n}^{-3})\right).

Summing over
n\geq N
and using
\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-2}<\infty
and
\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-3}<\infty
, we get

(5.6)

\theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-b_{n}t}-d_{m}t\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}e^{-b_{n}t}+O(t).

Comparison of
\sum a_{n}^{-1}e^{-b_{n}t}
with
H_{m}(t)
.

Since
b_{n}=a_{n}^{m}+O(a_{n}^{m-2})
as
n\to\infty
, there exist
K>0
and
N^{\prime}\geq N
such that

|b_{n}-a_{n}^{m}|\leq Ka_{n}^{m-2}
for all
n\geq N^{\prime}
. For
t\in(0,1]
and
n\geq N^{\prime}
,

|e^{-b_{n}t}-e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}|=e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}\bigl|e^{-(b_{n}-a_{n}^{m})t}-1\bigr|\leq e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}\,|b_{n}-a_{n}^{m}|\,t\,e^{|b_{n}-a_{n}^{m}|t}.

Choose
N^{\prime\prime}\geq N^{\prime}
so that
a_{n}^{2}\geq 2K
for all
n\geq N^{\prime\prime}
. Then for such
n
we have

|b_{n}-a_{n}^{m}|t\leq\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{m}t
, hence

|e^{-b_{n}t}-e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}|\ll t\,a_{n}^{m-2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{m}t}.

Therefore

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}\bigl(e^{-b_{n}t}-e^{-a_{n}^{m}t}\bigr)=O(1)+O\!\left(t\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{m-3}e^{-\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{m}t}\right)=O(1),

where the last bound follows by an integral comparison. If
m=1
the sum is
\ll t\sum a_{n}^{-2}=O(t)
,
and if
m\geq 3
(still odd) then

t\sum a_{n}^{m-3}e^{-ca_{n}^{m}t}\ll t\cdot t^{-(m-2)/m}=t^{2/m}=O(1)
.
Consequently,

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}e^{-b_{n}t}=H_{m}(t)+O(1)\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

By Lemma
5.3
,
H_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1)
, hence

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{-1}e^{-b_{n}t}=\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1).

Since
\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-b_{n}t}=\vartheta_{m}(t)
has no
t\log t
term by Lemma
5.4
,
the coefficient of
t\log t
in
\theta_{m}(t)
is exactly
d_{m}/m
.
Insert this into (
5.6
) to obtain

\theta_{m}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-b_{n}t}-d_{m}t\Bigl(\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1)\Bigr)+O(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-b_{n}t}+\frac{d_{m}}{m}\,t\log t+O(t).

Since
d_{m}\neq 0
, the
t\log t
term is nonzero, so QHS admissibility condition (2) fails.
∎

5.5.
Even
m
: admissibility

Let
m=2p
with
p\geq 1
.
Write
\rho(z)=z\sigma(z^{2})
as before, so
\sigma
is analytic in
|u|<4
and

\tau_{n}=a_{n}-a_{n}^{-1}\sigma(a_{n}^{-2})=a_{n}\bigl(1-u_{n}\sigma(u_{n})\bigr),\qquad u_{n}:=a_{n}^{-2}.

Set

F_{p}(u):=\bigl(1-u\sigma(u)\bigr)^{2p},\qquad u\in[0,r_{0}],\quad r_{0}:=4/9.

Then
F_{p}
is analytic on
|u|<4
(hence analytic in a neighborhood of
[0,r_{0}]
) and satisfies

F_{p}(0)=1
. Thus
F_{p}(u)-1
vanishes at
u=0
and we may write

F_{p}(u)=1+u\,q_{p}(u),\qquad q_{p}(u):=\frac{F_{p}(u)-1}{u},

where
q_{p}
is analytic on
|u|<4
. In particular,

(5.7)

\tau_{n}^{2p}=a_{n}^{2p}F_{p}(u_{n})=a_{n}^{2p}+a_{n}^{2p-2}q_{p}(u_{n}).

Define the heat trace

\theta_{2p}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\tau_{n}^{2p}t}=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}\,e^{-ta_{n}^{2p-2}q_{p}(u_{n})}.

Lemma 5.6

(Uniform exponential Taylor bound)
.

Let
R\geq 0
. For all
x\in\mathbb{C}
and all
t\in[0,1]
,

e^{-tx}=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,x^{r}+O_{R}\!\left(t^{R+1}|x|^{R+1}e^{t|x|}\right).

Proof.

Use the exponential series tail:

e^{-tx}-\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}x^{r}=\sum_{n\geq R+1}\frac{(-tx)^{n}}{n!}.

Taking absolute values and bounding the tail by the full exponential series gives

\left|e^{-tx}-\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}x^{r}\right|\leq\sum_{n\geq R+1}\frac{(t|x|)^{n}}{n!}\leq\frac{(t|x|)^{R+1}}{(R+1)!}\,e^{t|x|}.

∎

Step 1: Taylor expansion in
t
with a summable remainder.

Since
q_{p}
is continuous on
[0,r_{0}]
, set
Q_{*}:=\sup_{u\in[0,r_{0}]}|q_{p}(u)|<\infty
and define

A_{n}:=a_{n}^{2p-2}q_{p}(u_{n}),\qquad u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}.

Fix an integer
R\geq 0
. For
t\in(0,1]
and every
n
, Taylor’s theorem with remainder gives

e^{-tA_{n}}=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,A_{n}^{r}+O\!\left(t^{R+1}|A_{n}|^{R+1}e^{t|A_{n}|}\right),

where the implied constant depends only on
R
.
Multiplying by
e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}
and summing over
n
yields

(5.8)

\theta_{2p}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{(-t)^{r}}{r!}\,F_{r}(t)\;+\;\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t),

where

F_{r}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{(2p-2)r}q_{p}(u_{n})^{r}\,e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t},

and

\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t)\ll_{p,R}t^{R+1}\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{(2p-2)(R+1)}\,e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}\,e^{t|A_{n}|}.

Since
|A_{n}|\leq Q_{*}a_{n}^{2p-2}
, we have

e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}e^{t|A_{n}|}\leq\exp\!\left(-t\bigl(a_{n}^{2p}-Q_{*}a_{n}^{2p-2}\bigr)\right)=\exp\!\left(-t\,a_{n}^{2p-2}(a_{n}^{2}-Q_{*})\right).

Choose
n_{0}
so that
a_{n}^{2}\geq 2Q_{*}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Then for
n\geq n_{0}
,

a_{n}^{2p}-Q_{*}a_{n}^{2p-2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}a_{n}^{2p}
, hence

e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}e^{t|A_{n}|}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{2p}t}.

Splitting the sum at
n_{0}
(finitely many terms for
n<n_{0}
) gives

\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t)=O_{p,R}(t^{R+1})+O_{p,R}\!\left(t^{R+1}\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{(2p-2)(R+1)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{2p}t}\right).

By the integral comparison

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{\alpha}e^{-ca_{n}^{2p}t}\ll_{\alpha,p,c}t^{-(\alpha+1)/(2p)}\qquad(t\to 0^{+},\ \alpha\geq 0),

we obtain

\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t)=O\!\left(t^{R+1-((2p-2)(R+1)+1)/(2p)}\right)=O\!\left(t^{(R+1)/p-1/(2p)}\right).

In particular, by choosing
R
large we can make
\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t)=O(t^{N})
for any prescribed
N
.

Step 2: Expand
q_{p}(u)^{r}
in powers of
u
and reduce to weighted theta sums.

Fix
r\geq 0
. Since
q_{p}
is analytic on a disk
|u|<4
, so is
q_{p}(u)^{r}
, hence

q_{p}(u)^{r}=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{r,k}u^{k}

with radius of convergence
4
. Choose
\varrho
with
r_{0}<\varrho<4
, by Cauchy estimates,

|c_{r,k}|\ll_{r}\varrho^{-k}
. Since
u_{n}\leq r_{0}<\varrho
and
|c_{r,k}|\ll_{r}\varrho^{-k}
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 0}|c_{r,k}|\,u_{n}^{k}\ \ll_{r}\ \sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\varrho}\right)^{k}<\infty

uniformly in
n
, so Tonelli’s theorem justifies interchanging the
k
– and
n
–sums.
Using
u_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}\leq r_{0}
, we may interchange sums (absolute convergence) and obtain

F_{r}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{r,k}\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{(2p-2)r-2k}\,e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{r,k}\,\Theta_{p,\,(2p-2)r-2k}(t),

where for
q\in\mathbb{R}
we set

\Theta_{p,q}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}^{q}e^{-a_{n}^{2p}t}.

Step 3: Mellin analysis of
\Theta_{p,q}(t)
and absence of logarithms.

For each fixed
q\in\mathbb{R}
, Mellin inversion gives

\Theta_{p,q}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Re s=c}\Gamma(s)\,t^{-s}\Bigl(\zeta(2ps-q,\tfrac{1}{2})-2^{2ps-q}\Bigr)\,ds,

valid for any
c>(q+1)/(2p)
.
The only pole of
\zeta(\cdot,\tfrac{1}{2})
is at
1
, so the integrand has at most a simple pole at

2ps-q=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s=\frac{q+1}{2p},

and the usual simple poles of
\Gamma
at
s=0,-1,-2,\dots
.
If
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}
then
(q+1)/(2p)
can never be a nonpositive integer, hence this pole
never collides with a pole of
\Gamma
. Therefore contour shifting yields a full asymptotic
expansion of
\Theta_{p,q}(t)
as
t\to 0^{+}

in pure powers
(no logarithmic terms).

Step 4: Assemble the expansion for
\theta_{2p}(t)
.

For each fixed
R
, the finite sum in (
5.8
) is a finite linear combination of the
functions
F_{r}(t)
, each of which is an absolutely convergent series of
\Theta_{p,q}(t)
’s with

q\in 2\mathbb{Z}
. Hence it admits a full Poincaré expansion in pure powers as
t\to 0^{+}
,
and the remainder
\operatorname{Err}_{R}(t)
can be made arbitrarily small (in the sense of powers of
t
)
by taking
R
large. This proves that
\theta_{2p}(t)
has a full small-
t
expansion in pure powers.
Together with the bounds from the beginning of this section, it follows that
(\tau_{n}^{2p})_{n\geq 1}

is admissible in the sense of Quine–Heydari–Song.
∎

6.
Consequences of the parity dichotomy

Throughout this section we assume the parity dichotomy from Theorem
5.1
:
for each integer
m\geq 1
the heat trace

\theta_{m}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{n}^{m}t}\qquad(t>0)

admits a full small-time expansion in
pure powers
when
m
is even, while for
m
odd it contains
a
nonzero
logarithmic term
\kappa_{m}\,t\log t
.

6.1.
Spectral zeta functions attached to
\tau_{n}^{m}

For every
m\geq 1
define the (initially convergent) spectral zeta function

(6.1)

Z_{m}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\tau_{n}^{m})^{-s}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-ms}=L_{+}(ms),\qquad\operatorname{Re}s>\frac{1}{m}.

(The equality
Z_{m}(s)=L_{+}(ms)
holds initially by definition and then as meromorphic functions once
continuation is established.)

Lemma 6.1

(Exponential decay for large
t
)
.

For every
m\geq 1
there exists
C_{m}>0
such that for all
t\geq 1
,

\theta_{m}(t)\leq C_{m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{1}^{m}t}.

Proof.

Write
\theta_{m}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{1}^{m}t}\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-(\tau_{n}^{m}-\tau_{1}^{m})t}
.
For
t\geq 1
,
\mathrm{e}^{-(\tau_{n}^{m}-\tau_{1}^{m})t}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-(\tau_{n}^{m}-\tau_{1}^{m})}
.
Since
\tau_{n}\geq n
(Lemma
2.5
), we have

\mathrm{e}^{-(\tau_{n}^{m}-\tau_{1}^{m})}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\tau_{1}^{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{m}}
, and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{m}}<\infty
.
Absorb the convergent sum into
C_{m}
.
∎

Proposition 6.2

(Mellin representation)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac{1}{m}
,

(6.2)

Z_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta_{m}(t)\,dt.

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}s>0
and
\Lambda>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda t}\,dt=\Gamma(s)\Lambda^{-s}
.
For
\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac{1}{m}
, the series
Z_{m}(s)=\sum\tau_{n}^{-ms}
converges absolutely, hence by Fubini,

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta_{m}(t)\,dt=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{n}^{m}t}\,dt=\Gamma(s)\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-ms}=\Gamma(s)Z_{m}(s).

∎

6.2.
Even powers: clean QHS consequences

Fix
p\geq 1
and set
m=2p
. By Theorem
5.1
(b),

(\tau_{n}^{2p})_{n\geq 1}
is admissible in the sense of
[
37
]
, hence
\theta_{2p}(t)
admits a full
small-time expansion in pure powers:

(6.3)

\theta_{2p}(t)\sim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}c^{(2p)}_{\nu}\,t^{j^{(2p)}_{\nu}},\qquad j^{(2p)}_{0}<j^{(2p)}_{1}<\cdots,\quad j^{(2p)}_{\nu}\to+\infty,

with no logarithmic terms.

Theorem 6.3

(Meromorphic continuation and residues for
Z_{2p}
)
.

The function
Z_{2p}(s)=L_{+}(2ps)
admits a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
with at most simple poles.
More precisely, if
c^{(2p)}_{\alpha}
denotes the coefficient of
t^{\alpha}
in the expansion
(
6.3
), then
Z_{2p}
may have a simple pole at
s=-\alpha
with residue

(6.4)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-\alpha}Z_{2p}(s)=\frac{c^{(2p)}_{\alpha}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}.

In particular, although the Mellin analysis produces possible terms
(s+k)^{-1}
from the coefficient
of
t^{k}
, the factor
1/\Gamma(s)
has a simple zero at
s=-k
and cancels this singularity. Hence,
Z_{2p}
is holomorphic at every nonpositive integer
s=-k
.

Proof.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and truncate (
6.3
) at order
t^{j^{(2p)}_{N}}
:

\theta_{2p}(t)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}c^{(2p)}_{\nu}\,t^{j^{(2p)}_{\nu}}+R_{N}(t),\qquad R_{N}(t)=O\!\bigl(t^{j^{(2p)}_{N}}\bigr)\quad(t\to 0^{+}).

Split the Mellin integral (
6.2
) at
1
:

Z_{2p}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}\theta_{2p}(t)\,dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\theta_{2p}(t)\,dt\Bigr).

By Lemma
6.1
, the
[1,\infty)
–integral converges absolutely for every
s\in\mathbb{C}

and defines an entire function.

On
(0,1]
, insert the truncated expansion and integrate termwise:

\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}\theta_{2p}(t)\,dt=\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\frac{c^{(2p)}_{\nu}}{s+j^{(2p)}_{\nu}}+\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}R_{N}(t)\,dt.

The remainder integral is holomorphic for
\operatorname{Re}s>-j^{(2p)}_{N}
.
Thus
Z_{2p}(s)
extends meromorphically with possible poles only at
s=-j^{(2p)}_{\nu}
, all simple,
and (
6.4
) follows by inspection since
\Gamma
is holomorphic and nonzero at
s=-\alpha

unless
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Finally, for
\alpha=k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the factor
1/\Gamma(s)
has a simple zero at
s=-k
,
which cancels the simple pole coming from
(s+k)^{-1}
. Hence
Z_{2p}
is holomorphic at
s=-k
.
∎

Theorem 6.4

(Special values at nonpositive integers)
.

For every integer
k\geq 0
, the meromorphic continuation of
Z_{2p}(s)
is holomorphic at
s=-k
and

(6.5)

Z_{2p}(-k)=(-1)^{k}k!\,c^{(2p)}_{k},

where
c^{(2p)}_{k}
denotes the coefficient of
t^{k}
in the small-time expansion (
6.3
).
Equivalently,

L_{+}(-2pk)=Z_{2p}(-k)=(-1)^{k}k!\,c^{(2p)}_{k}.

Proof.

Proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
3.9
:
near
s=-k
the only potentially singular contribution from the
(0,1]
integral is
c^{(2p)}_{k}/(s+k)
.
Since
\Gamma(s)
has a simple pole at
s=-k
with residue
\operatorname{Res}_{s=-k}\Gamma(s)=(-1)^{k}/k!
,
we have

\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}=(-1)^{k}k!\,(s+k)+O\!\bigl((s+k)^{2}\bigr)\qquad(s\to-k),

and therefore
Z_{2p}(s)
is holomorphic at
s=-k
and the limit equals (
6.5
).
∎

The identity
Z_{2p}(s)=L_{+}(2ps)
shows that admissibility of
\tau_{n}^{2p}
provides a systematic

heat-kernel mechanism
for producing the special values
L_{+}(-2pk)
(and, via
Z_{2p}^{\prime}(0)
,
determinant-type constants) from the small-
t
expansion of
\theta_{2p}(t)
.
For
p=1
, combining this coefficient extraction at infinity with the BOCRS inversion identity
for
\varphi
yields Conjecture 1 of
[
6
]
(Section
4
).
For
p>1
, admissibility gives the expansion side for
L_{+}(-2pk)
, but a further symmetry
relating
L_{+}(-2pk)
to
L_{+}(2pk)
would require additional functional equations for the corresponding
canonical products (currently only available for
p=1
via
\Phi
and
\varphi
).

6.3.
Even powers: canonical products and coefficient extraction at infinity

For
p\geq 1
define the genus-
0
canonical product

(6.6)

W_{2p}(\lambda):=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Bigl(1+\frac{\lambda}{\tau_{n}^{2p}}\Bigr).

Since
\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{n}^{-2p}<\infty
, the product converges absolutely and locally uniformly.
Set also

R_{2p}(\lambda):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda+\tau_{n}^{2p}}=\frac{W_{2p}^{\prime}(\lambda)}{W_{2p}(\lambda)}.

Lemma 6.5

(Laplace representation)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
,

R_{2p}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,\theta_{2p}(t)\,dt.

Proof.

Identical to Lemma
3.11
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda+\tau_{n}^{2p})t}\,dt=(\lambda+\tau_{n}^{2p})^{-1}
for
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
,
and
\sum_{n\geq 1}|\lambda+\tau_{n}^{2p}|^{-1}\ll\sum\tau_{n}^{-2p}<\infty
on compact subsets of
\{\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0\}
,
so Fubini applies.
∎

Theorem 6.6

(Large-
\lambda
coefficient extraction)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
and write
\Sigma_{\delta}=\{\lambda:\ |\arg\lambda|\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta\}
.
Then
\log W_{2p}(\lambda)
admits an asymptotic expansion as
|\lambda|\to\infty
with
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\delta}
,
and for every integer
k\geq 1
one has the coefficient identity

(6.7)

[\lambda^{-k}]\,\log W_{2p}(\lambda)=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\,Z_{2p}(-k)=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\,L_{+}(-2pk).

Proof.

Combine Lemma
6.5
with Watson’s lemma (Lemma
3.13
)
applied to
f(t)=\theta_{2p}(t)
, using the small-
t
expansion (
6.3
) and the
large-
t
exponential decay from Lemma
6.1
. This yields an asymptotic expansion
for
R_{2p}(\lambda)
in
\Sigma_{\delta}
. Since
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log W_{2p}(\lambda)=R_{2p}(\lambda)
,
term-by-term integration along the segment from
1
to
\lambda
gives the corresponding expansion for

\log W_{2p}(\lambda)
, up to an additive constant depending on the branch.

For integer
k\geq 1
, the
\lambda^{-k-1}
coefficient in
R_{2p}(\lambda)
is

c^{(2p)}_{k}\Gamma(k+1)=k!\,c^{(2p)}_{k}
, hence the
\lambda^{-k}
coefficient in
\log W_{2p}(\lambda)
equals

-\frac{k!\,c^{(2p)}_{k}}{k}
. Using Theorem
6.4
,
Z_{2p}(-k)=(-1)^{k}k!\,c^{(2p)}_{k}
,
which yields (
6.7
).
∎

6.4.
Odd powers: residues and logarithms

Fix an odd integer
m=2\ell+1
.
By Theorem
5.1
(a), the heat trace
\theta_{m}(t)
has a nonzero term

\kappa_{m}\,t\log t
in its small-
t
expansion. In the notation of Proposition
5.5
,
one may take
\kappa_{m}=\frac{d_{m}}{m}
, where
d_{m}\neq 0
is the coefficient of
a_{n}^{-1}
in the
large-
n
expansion of
\tau_{n}^{m}
.

Theorem 6.7

(A pole at
s=-1
for
Z_{m}
)
.

Let
m
be odd and assume that, as
t\to 0^{+}
,

\theta_{m}(t)=\kappa_{m}\,t\log t+O(t),\qquad\kappa_{m}\neq 0.

Then the meromorphic continuation of
Z_{m}(s)=L_{+}(ms)
has a
simple pole
at
s=-1
with

(6.8)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}Z_{m}(s)=\kappa_{m}.

Equivalently,
L_{+}(s)
has a simple pole at
s=-m
with residue

(6.9)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-m}L_{+}(s)=m\,\kappa_{m}.

Proof.

Split the Mellin representation (
6.2
) at
1
as in the proof of
Theorem
3.7
. The
[1,\infty)
integral is entire by Lemma
6.1
.
On
(0,1]
, write
\theta_{m}(t)=\kappa_{m}\,t\log t+O(t)
and compute

\int_{0}^{1}t^{s-1}\,\kappa_{m}\,t\log t\,dt=\kappa_{m}\int_{0}^{1}t^{s}\log t\,dt=-\frac{\kappa_{m}}{(s+1)^{2}}.

Thus the Mellin integral has a double pole at
s=-1
with principal part
-\kappa_{m}(s+1)^{-2}
.
Since
\Gamma(s)
has a simple pole at
s=-1
with residue
-1
, we have

1/\Gamma(s)=-(s+1)+O((s+1)^{2})
near
s=-1
.
Multiplying gives

Z_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\Bigl(-\frac{\kappa_{m}}{(s+1)^{2}}+O\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{s+1}\Bigr)\Bigr)=\frac{\kappa_{m}}{s+1}+O(1),

proving (
6.8
). The statement (
6.9
) follows from
Z_{m}(s)=L_{+}(ms)

by the change of variables
u=ms
.
∎

Corollary 6.8

(Recovery of the BOCRS residue formula)
.

For odd
m=2\ell+1
, using
\kappa_{m}=d_{m}/m
from Proposition
5.5
yields

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-m}L_{+}(s)=d_{m}.

Inserting the explicit value of
d_{m}
from Lemma
5.2
gives

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-m}L_{+}(s)=(-1)^{\ell}\,\frac{L_{-}(m)}{2^{m-1}\,\pi^{m+1}\,C^{m}}=\frac{2i}{\pi}\,\frac{L_{-}(m)}{(2\pi iC)^{m}},

which matches the residue identity for
L_{+}
recorded in
[
6
, §10.2]
.

For
m\geq 2
, the genus-
0
product

W_{m}(\lambda)=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1+\lambda/\tau_{n}^{m})
is well-defined and satisfies

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log W_{m}(\lambda)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\lambda+\tau_{n}^{m})^{-1}
.
The
t\log t
term in
\theta_{m}(t)
forces new
\log\lambda
corrections in the large-
\lambda

asymptotics of the resolvent trace and of
\log W_{m}(\lambda)
, reflecting a genuinely

log-polyhomogeneous
Laplace-transform behavior.

Lemma 6.9

(Laplace transform of
t\log t
)
.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,t\log t\,dt=\lambda^{-2}\,\bigl(1-\gamma-\log\lambda\bigr),

where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant and
\log\lambda
is the principal logarithm.

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
and
\operatorname{Re}\alpha>-1
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}t^{\alpha}\,dt=\Gamma(\alpha+1)\lambda^{-\alpha-1}
.
Differentiate in
\alpha
and evaluate at
\alpha=1
to obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,t\log t\,dt=\Bigl(\Gamma^{\prime}(2)-\Gamma(2)\log\lambda\Bigr)\lambda^{-2}.

Since
\Gamma(2)=1
and
\Gamma^{\prime}(2)=\Gamma(2)\psi(2)=1-\gamma
, the claim follows.
∎

Proposition 6.10

(Logarithms in product asymptotics for odd
m
)
.

Assume
m
is odd and
\theta_{m}(t)=\kappa_{m}\,t\log t+O(t)
as
t\to 0^{+}
with
\kappa_{m}\neq 0
.
Define for
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
the resolvent trace

R_{m}(\lambda):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda+\tau_{n}^{m}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\theta_{m}(t)\,dt.

Then, as
|\lambda|\to\infty
in any fixed sector
\Sigma_{\delta}
,

R_{m}(\lambda)=\cdots-\kappa_{m}\,\lambda^{-2}\log\lambda+O(|\lambda|^{-2}),

so in particular
R_{m}(\lambda)
contains a nonzero
\lambda^{-2}\log\lambda
term.
Consequently, for the genus-
0
product
W_{m}(\lambda)=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1+\lambda/\tau_{n}^{m})
(
m\geq 2
),

\log W_{m}(\lambda)=\cdots+\kappa_{m}\,\frac{\log\lambda}{\lambda}+O(|\lambda|^{-1})\qquad(|\lambda|\to\infty,\ \lambda\in\Sigma_{\delta}),

so
\log W_{m}(\lambda)
contains a nonzero
(\log\lambda)/\lambda
term.

Proof.

Split the Laplace integral defining
R_{m}(\lambda)
at
1
.
The contribution from
[1,\infty)
is exponentially small in sectors by Lemma
6.1
.
On
(0,1]
, write
\theta_{m}(t)=\kappa_{m}\,t\log t+O(t)
and apply Lemma
6.9

(and the elementary bound
\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}t\,dt\ll|\lambda|^{-2}
