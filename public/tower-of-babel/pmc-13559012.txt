Patient‐Reported Satisfaction With Inpatient Palliative Care at a Tertiary Hospital in Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Salma Ahsan Khanam, A.K.M. Motiur Rahman Bhuiyan, Afroja Alam, Mostofa Kamal Chowdhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatient satisfaction is an important indicator of healthcare quality, particularly in palliative care where patient‐centered communication and supportive services are essential. Evidence on patient satisfaction with inpatient palliative care services in Bangladesh remains limited. This study aimed to assess patient‐reported satisfaction with inpatient palliative care at a tertiary level hospital in Bangladesh.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted at the inpatient facility of the Department of Palliative Medicine, Bangladesh Medical University, Dhaka, from November 2023 to June 2024. Data were collected at hospital discharge through face‐to‐face interviews using a structured questionnaire containing the EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 instrument. Spearman's rank correlation assessed relationships between satisfaction subdomains, while Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests explored the associations between satisfaction scores and socio‐demographic factors.ResultsTotal of 91 patients participated in the study, of whom 42.9% were male and 57.1% were female. Overall patient satisfaction with inpatient palliative care services was rated as very good. Doctors and nurses received high ratings for their availability, technical competence, and interpersonal skills, whereas comparatively lower ratings were observed for the provision of information. Within the domain of service organization and delivery, very good levels of satisfaction were reported for waiting time, exchange of information among healthcare staff, and the helpfulness of other hospital personnel. In contrast, relatively lower satisfaction scores were noted for accessibility and environmental comfort. Sociodemographic factors including age, marital status, and educational level showed significant associations with patient satisfaction levels.ConclusionPatients reported a high level of satisfaction with inpatient palliative care services at tertiary hospital in Bangladesh. However, areas such as information provision, communication, accessibility and environmental comfort require further improvement.

Introduction

Patient satisfaction is widely recognized as a core indicator of healthcare quality, reflecting how well care experiences meet patient expectations. It is a multidimensional and abstract concept that is difficult to define or quantify but broadly encompasses all relevant events and processes associated with the provision of healthcare [1]. In palliative care, where the primary goal is to improve comfort and quality of life, patient satisfaction provides a critical measure of whether the service effectively address patient needs. It also serves as an important indicator for monitoring symptom improvement, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes [2].

As disease progresses, palliative patients often experience complex symptom burdens, frequent hospital visits, and considerable psychosocial distress, making satisfaction with care particularly important [3]. Higher levels of patient satisfaction have been associated with improved symptom management, stronger doctor–patient relationships, better treatment adherence, and even increased survival among patients receiving palliative care [2, 3]. Conversely, dissatisfaction may arise from unmet expectations, adverse treatment effects, financial burden, or poor communication between patients and healthcare providers [1, 4].

Patient satisfaction with palliative care is influenced by numerous internal and external factors [5, 6]. Clinical factors such as health‐related quality of life, treatment side effects, symptom burden, functional ability, and mental health may affect patients' perceptions of care and overall satisfaction [1]. Additionally, psychological and demographic characteristics—including age, gender, education level, socioeconomic status, marital status, religion, geographic location, frequency of hospital visits, duration of stay, and individual expectations—have also been associated with patient satisfaction with healthcare services [7]. Hospital‐related factors can further influence satisfaction levels. Positive contributors include efficient admission procedures, cleanliness of facilities, regular visits by doctors and nurses, respectful conduct of hospital staff, prompt service delivery, effective complaint management, and availability of essential medications. In contrast, dissatisfaction may arise due to high treatment costs, unnecessary medical investigations, shortages of specialist physicians or trained nurses, and inadequate communication with healthcare providers [4]. Patients may also report lower satisfaction when treatment outcomes fall below their expectations or when they experience adverse treatment effects [1]. Therefore, assessing patient satisfaction is essential for identifying service strengths and gaps, guiding quality improvement, and ensuring that palliative care remains responsive to the needs of patients and their families [8].

In the context of palliative care, patient satisfaction encompasses multiple domains, including symptom control and pain management, communication and patient education, psychosocial and holistic care, coordination and integrated teamwork, compassionate and skilled healthcare providers, timely and responsive services, patient and family preparedness, and the quality of end‐of‐life care. Incorporating patient preferences into healthcare through advance care planning tailored to individual readiness and disease progression may help align treatment with patient expectations, although evidence regarding its impact on satisfaction levels is mixed [9]. Therefore, assessing patient satisfaction remains essential for evaluating whether care provided is consistent with patient preferences and needs.

In Bangladesh, palliative care is still emerging, with limited specialized centers concentrated in urban areas and shortage of trained professionals. Understanding patient satisfaction in this context is crucial for improving service delivery and shaping national strategies to strengthen palliative care. The objectives of this study are to assess patient‐reported satisfaction with inpatient palliative care services in Bangladesh and to explore the association between satisfaction levels and various factors. The study addressed two research questions: (1) What is the level of satisfaction among patients receiving in‐patient palliative care in Bangladesh? (2) What is the association between sociodemographic factors and the level of satisfaction? Findings from this study would provide evidence to help healthcare providers optimize practices and support policymakers in developing patient‐centered and sustainable model of care.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at the in‐patient facility of the Palliative Medicine Department, Bangladesh Medical University (BMU), Dhaka, from November 2023 to June 2024.

Sample Size

Based on prior research that reported a mean patient satisfaction score of 1.5 ± 0.4 on the EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 scale among individuals receiving palliative care [10], this value was used as a reference for determining the required sample size.

Sample Criteria

Sampling was done using a census method. All patients aged 18 years or older who had received at least seven consecutive days of inpatient palliative care during the study period and were able to communicate directly with the interviewers during data collection were eligible for inclusion. Patients with cognitive impairment, defined as a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score below 24, receiving outpatient consultation, homecare, or daycare management, were excluded from the study.

Data Collection Instrument

A structured questionnaire was used, which included the EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 developed by EORTC Quality of Life Group [2]. This instrument comprises eleven multi‐item scales and three single‐item scales, assessing patient satisfaction across multiple domains:

Doctors: technical skills (items 1–3), interpersonal skills (items 4–6), information provision (items 7–9), availability (items 10–11).

Nurses: technical skills (items 12–14), interpersonal skills (items 15–17), information provision (items 18–20), availability (items 21–22).

Other hospital staff: kindness, helpfulness, and information provision (items 24–26).

Organization of care: waiting time for tests, treatment, and results (items 27–28); access to hospital (items 29–30); exchange of information (item 23); comfort (item 31).

Overall satisfaction: general satisfaction with care (item 32).

Each item was rated on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = poor, 2 = fair, 3 = good, 4 = very good, 5 = excellent). Higher scores indicated greater satisfaction with care.

The original questionnaire was not available in Bangla, so it was translated and pilot‐tested before patient administration. The translation process followed the EORTC guideline [11]. After obtaining the English version of the questionnaire from the EORTC team, two bilingual translators (one medical, one non‐medical) independently performed forward translation, which was reconciled by a third reviewer. The reconciled version was back‐translated by two non‐medical translators (one aware of the tool's concepts, one not). An expert committee, including research team members and language experts, reviewed all versions. After consensus, the final Bengali version was prepared for pilot testing.

Pilot testing was conducted among 50 patients admitted to the oncology department of BMU (≈50% of the calculated main study sample) who were fluent in Bangla, consistent with recommended practices for assessing the clarity, feasibility, and reliability of translated questionnaires. The study objectives were explained, and participants were interviewed before and after completing the questionnaire to identify any confusing terms. Nearly all patients (≈99%) easily understood all questions, so no modifications were needed. The EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 among these patients demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency: Cronbach's alpha was 0.734 for the Doctors' Care subscale, 0.870 for Nurses' Care subscale, 0.603 for Service and Organization subscale, and 0.883 for the total scale.

Data Collection Procedure

Data were collected at the time of hospital discharge through face‐to‐face interviews conducted directly with the patients. The study objectives were first explained to eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria. Then written informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to participate.

Interviewers received structured training on the study objectives, questionnaire administration, and ethical considerations. They were instructed to read each question verbatim, avoid providing additional explanations, and maintain a neutral tone to minimize interviewer bias. To reduce response bias, participants were assured of confidentiality and encouraged to answer honestly without any influence from clinical staff. All respondents were requested to answer as many questions as possible.

All questionnaires were checked for completeness immediately after administration. Missing responses in the EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 were managed according to the standard EORTC scoring guidelines. For multi‐item scales, a scale score was calculated if at least half of the items were completed, with the mean of the completed items used to impute missing responses. If fewer than half of the items were answered, the scale score was considered missing. Single‐item scales were excluded from analysis if responses were missing. No questionnaires were excluded entirely due to missing data, ensuring that all available patient information contributed to the analysis.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics (frequencies and percentages) and EORTC IN‐PATSAT32 satisfaction scores (means with 95% confidence intervals, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test, which indicated non‐normal distributions for most satisfaction variables; therefore, non‐parametric tests were applied. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships among the 14 satisfaction sub‐domains. Associations between demographic and clinical factors and patient satisfaction were analyzed using four main domain scores: doctor's care (items 1–11), nurses' care (items 12–22), service and organization (items 23–31), and overall satisfaction (item 32). Mann‐Whitney U tests compared two independent groups (sex, marital status), while Kruskal‐Wallis H tests were used for comparisons involving three or more groups (age, education, income, duration since diagnosis, and ECOG performance status). Results are presented as mean ranks, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from Institutional Review Board, BMU (Approval no: BSMMU/2023/13906, Date: 13/11/2023) and permission from the Department of Palliative Medicine, BMU. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients eligible for the study. To minimize bias from the presence of hospital staff, all interviews were conducted in a private room with only the research team, the patient, and their family members present.

Result

Nearly equal proportions of male (42.9%) and female (57.1%) patients participated in the study. Most patients were middle‐aged (40–59 years) (50.5%), followed by older (≥ 60 years) (30.8%) and younger patients (18–39 years) (18.7%). Regarding education, 45.0% had completed up to high school, while 30.7% had graduate or higher education. Half of the participants (50.5%) belonged to the lower‐middle economic group, with 25.3% from lower and 24.2% from upper‐middle or higher economic groups. Nearly half of the patients (48.4%) had been diagnosed for more than 10 months. In terms of functional status, most patients had ECOG grade 3 (57.1%), followed by grade 4 (24.2%) and grades 1–2 (18.7%) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated patient satisfaction with in‐patient services at a tertiary palliative care center in Bangladesh. Overall satisfaction was rated as very good, aligning with findings from studies among Bangladeshi patients receiving home‐based palliative care as well as reports from palliative care centers in other countries [12, 13, 14]. These results suggest a generally positive perception of palliative care services across different care settings.

The findings of this study can be interpreted using Donabedian's structure–process–outcome framework, a widely used model for evaluating healthcare quality. In the present study, structural aspects such as hospital access and environmental comfort received relatively lower scores compared with other domains, suggesting possible infrastructural or logistical constraints within the hospital setting. Factors such as inadequate cleanliness, limited manpower, and difficulties in navigating hospital services may explain these findings. Similar associations between environmental conditions and overall patient satisfaction have been reported in other studies [15]. In contrast, process‐related factors—particularly physicians and nurses' technical competence, interpersonal skills, and availability‐ received higher ratings. The favorable outcome, reflected by very good level of overall patient satisfaction, suggests that strong provider–patient interactions may partially compensate for structural limitations [16]. Similar patterns have been observed in healthcare quality studies where positive interpersonal interactions increases health‐related quality of life and satisfaction level despite structural limitations [8].

Patients in this study expressed particularly very good level of satisfaction with doctors and nurses' competence, availability, and interpersonal interactions, which is consistent with findings from previous researches. For example, a study conducted among cancer patients receiving home‐based palliative care in Bangladesh reported high satisfaction with physicians' clinical competence and care coordination [12]. Similar patterns have also been reported in inpatient settings in Europe and Canada, where patients frequently rate healthcare providers highly for competence, empathy, and respect for patient preferences—factors that strongly influence trust and perceived quality of care [14, 17]. These findings suggest that the clinical expertise and interpersonal engagement of physicians and nurses remain central determinants of patient satisfaction in palliative care settings.

However, the present study found relatively lower level satisfaction with information provision with both doctors and nurses. Effective communication and shared decision‐making are important determinants of patient satisfaction in hospital‐based palliative care, particularly among patients with low literacy [18]. In many non‐Western societies, healthcare communication often occurs through family‐mediated decision‐making, where relatives act as intermediaries between healthcare providers and patients. In Bangladesh, families may request physicians to withhold or modify information about diagnosis or prognosis to protect patients from emotional distress [19]. Similarly, a study among terminally‐ill Arab patients reported that families often discouraged healthcare providers from engaging in end‐of‐life discussions directly with patients [20]. Comparable findings have also been reported in Morocco, where patients rated physicians' and nurses' competence highly but expressed lower satisfaction with information sharing [21]. These patterns suggest that cultural influence and family‐centered decision‐making may limit direct communication with patients and influence their perceptions of information provision, resulting in lower satisfaction even when overall care quality is perceived positively.

The study also explored association of socio‐demographic factors with satisfaction scores. Younger patients reported higher satisfaction with physicians' care and organizational aspects of services, while older patients expressed higher satisfaction with nursing care. Differences in expectations across age groups may partly explain these findings. Previous studies have shown that younger patients tend to prioritize efficiency and accessibility, whereas older patients may place greater value on interpersonal support [7]. Additionally, patients with higher education reported lower overall satisfaction. This finding was similar to an oncology setting in Canada where higher education was related to lower satisfaction, due to elevated expectations regarding communication, decision‐making, and information transparency. This suggests that more educated patients may require more detailed explanations, active involvement in care decisions, and clear information to feel satisfied, highlighting the need for tailored communication strategies in palliative care [14]. Marital status was also associated with satisfaction levels: single patients reported greater satisfaction with the care provided by the palliative care team. This may indicate that single patients, who might rely more on professional care than family support, perceive the palliative care team's attention more positively. Although evidence is sometimes contradictory, a meta‐analysis suggested that single or divorced patients were more satisfied in specific aspects of care, such as comfort, visiting policies, and cleanliness. This implies that palliative care services that prioritize environmental comfort and flexible visiting arrangements may particularly enhance satisfaction among patients without immediate family support [7].

Overall, patient satisfaction with inpatient palliative care in the current study was rated as very good. It is significantly associated with healthcare professionals' skill, environmental comfort and exchange of information. However, patient satisfaction primarily reflects subjective perceptions of healthcare experiences, whereas quality of care encompasses broader clinical, organizational, and safety outcomes. Therefore, a high level of satisfaction does not necessarily indicate optimal quality of care, and conversely, high‐quality care may not always result in high patient satisfaction [7]. In the current study, gaps were identified in interpersonal communication among healthcare staff, as well as structural limitations within the care setting. Therefore, patient satisfaction should be interpreted alongside other indicators of healthcare performance, including clinical outcomes and patient‐reported quality‐of‐life measures. Addressing these gaps could involve multiple strategies: strengthening communication training programs for doctors and nurses to improve information provision; expanding the role of nurses in patient education and care coordination; and improving structural aspects of care, including hospital accessibility, environmental comfort, and service navigation. Such measures can improve patient experiences and better align palliative care services with patients' expectations in Bangladesh.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center with a small sample size, which may limit the generalizability. Second, severely ill, cognitively impaired and non‐communicative patients were excluded, potentially leading to an overestimation of satisfaction. Third, data were not collected on several potentially influential factors, including patients' social support, quality of life, staffing ratios, treatment costs, and understanding of their prognosis. Finally, face‐to‐face interviews may have introduced social‐desirability bias.

Conclusion

This study found high overall satisfaction among patients receiving in‐patient palliative care at a tertiary hospital in Bangladesh. Most patients reported very good satisfaction with healthcare staff competency and care coordination; however, gaps in communication and structural limitations were identified. Addressing these through targeted communication training, expanded nursing roles, and improvements in hospital accessibility and comfort could further enhance patient experiences and better align palliative care services with patient expectations.
