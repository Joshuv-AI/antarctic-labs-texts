Origin and evolution of fluids and heatflow in geothermal systems of Indus River Basin (IRB), India
Authors: Gh. Jeelani, Suhail A. Lone, R. D. Deshpande, Abhijit Mukherjee, Santosh K. Rai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Himalayan–Tibetan Orogeny, driven by the convergence of the Indian and Eurasian plates, has resulted in significant crustal thickening, intense deformation and widespread magmatism. This tectonic collision has generated deep-seated faults and shear zones that facilitates fluid circulation and heat transfer, leading to the formation of numerous hydrothermal systems in the region. While Tibetan geothermal systems are largely mantle-derived, the origin of hot springs, geothermal fluids and heat flow in Indus River Basin remained debated. Understanding the sources of solutes, volatiles and heat is significant for constraining the tectonic and geothermal evolution of the region. This study integrates geochemical, geological and geophysical data to examine heat flow and fluid circulation in geothermal systems of Indus River Basin. High concentration of major ions, trace elements and rare alkali metals (Arsenic, Cesium and Lithium) in Puga–Chumathang springs indicate fluid contribution from crystallizing magma from the shallow to mid-crust. Crustal helium and carbon isotopic signatures further supports crustal magmatic influence, particularly near the Indus Suture Zone. Estimated reservoir temperatures of 205–290 °C and circulation depths of ~ 4000 m suggesting a localized heat source in the region. Geophysical evidences, including negative gravity anomalies and low-resistivity zones beneath the ISZ, also indicated the presence of partially molten material in the shallow to mid-crust. These anomalies, combined with minimal seismic activity, highlight zones of high heat flow and active fluid migration pathways. Our findings suggest that partial melting, driven by reduced lithostatic pressure and crustal decompression, enhances geothermal activity. This study provides new insights into the geothermal evolution of the Himalayan–Tibetan Orogenic Belt, emphasizing crustal processes in sustaining high-temperature hydrothermal systems. The results have significant implications for geothermal resource assessment and sustainable energy development, revealing the complex interplay of magmatic, tectonic and hydrothermal processes in shaping these systems.

Introduction

Tibet and the Himalaya form one of the Earth’s great geothermal provinces with 645 thermal springs in Tibet1, and 170 in the Indian Himalaya mostly towards northwest2,3. These hot springs primarily align with major fault structures, including the Karakoram Fault (KKF) and Indus Suture Zone (ISZ), which formed due to the continental collision between the Indian and Eurasian plates, emphasizing their tectonic control4. The orogeny has caused large-scale crustal shortening and deformation5,6, compensated by a series of thrusts and folds7. Hydrothermal manifestation in the form of hot springs along the suture zones and faults suggest their deep-rooted nature required for the origin of geothermal systems in the Himalaya and Tibet. These faults may also become pathways for the upward flow of fluids from the mantle through the crust8,9. The chemical composition of geothermal waters may thus provide a suitable window to understand the origin10–13, hydrogeochemical processes12–15 and circulation system of the fluids11–16. With the average continental heat flux of 60 to 90 mW/m−2, the temperature of some of the thermal springs is largely topography driven with the circulation of recharging water to a depth of a few kilometres11–16. However, the source of heat in some thermal springs is not merely from deep circulation but requires additional source to gain that water temperature11–16. Geochemical constituents and volatiles in geothermal waters are good indicators to decipher their crust or mantle source9,12,14. The geochemical constituents and natural tracers in geothermal fluids have been widely examined in variety of tectonic settings, including: major strike‐slip fault systems, extensional settings and faults along convergent margins9–24.

Among major geochemical constituents and subsurface volatiles, the anomalously high concentration of rare alkali metals (Rb, Cs, Li) , trace metals (As, Se, Cd) and volatiles (He, Ne, CO2 and N2) rich-fluids are considered as critical tracers for understanding and deciphering the origin of hydrothermal fluids and intrusion of mantle-derived melts at shallow depths9–14,16,21. These chemical constituents and volatiles provide explicit measure of mantle-derived fluids due to their significant variation with respect to host reservoir rock10–12,16,19,20. Based on high 3He/4He, the volatiles in the hot springs along KKF are mantle derived12,24,25 and KKF is considered as a pathway for the transfer of mantle fluid25 and heat for these geothermal systems. The higher concentration of volatiles along the KKF e.g., in Pamir10,12,14,16,22,23 and Panamik25, is considered to be derived substantially from the mantle. However, the source of volatiles, chemical constituents and heat in hot springs associated with ISZ is still debatable.

Some studies proposed the source of heat due to deeper circulation of recharging water16,22,23 while others argue the heat source from mixing with hydrothermal fluids along ISZ in Tibet24,25. Similarly, some studies suggest, the chemical constituents and volatiles of geothermal systems along ISZ is due to the leaching of host reservoir rocks26 while others suggest the association of geothermal fluids with the possible uraniferous granitic magma25,27. Recent events, including a notable burst at Chumathang spring expelling volatiles and gases over weeks, and incidents of drilling rig completely damaged during the drilling at Puga geothermal field, have underscored the urgency of comprehending the underlying processes driving this dynamic geothermal system.

Based on a comprehensive dataset of chemical constituents and stable water isotopes of geothermal fluids, supplemented by geological and geophysical data. The main aim of the study is (1) to decipher the sources of solutes and volatiles (2) and to understand the mechanism of high heat flow in the geothermal springs of ISZ, India. We propose that a magma chamber, formed due to decompressional-partial melting within the shallow to mid-crust, serves as a primary heat source for these geothermal systems in UIB. This magmatic activity sustains the high thermal gradient and enriches geothermal fluids with solutes and volatiles, including major ions, trace elements and rare alkali metals. The geothermal systems in this region represent a significant yet untapped energy resource. The prospective exploitation of this vast potential for sustainable energy production could provide substantial societal benefits, including long-term power generation, reduced reliance on non-renewable energy sources and economic development in the region.

Geology and tectonics

The ISZ represents the collisional boundary of the Indian and the Eurasian plates28–30. These collision zones were subjected to intense volcanism and several phases of widespread acidic magma activity from Upper Cretaceous to Upper Tertiary31,32. The ophiolites, seen along the ISZ and KKF, represent the remnants of the uplifted wedge of the oceanic crust31,33–35. On the basis of proximity of these geothermal springs to suture zones, we have classified these geothermal systems as: Puga–Chumathang geothermal system (PCGS) closer to ISZ (although Puga and Chumathang are separate geothermal systems) and Panamik geothermal system (PCGS) nearer to KKF. The Puga geothermal field bounded by Zildat Fault and Kiagar-Tso Fault from eastern and western directions appears as down-faulted block (Fig. 1 modified after33,34), with its north and south bounding faults covered by recent alluvial and colluvium deposits28. The thermal manifestations of the Puga geothermal system is confined to the eastern part of the valley with an aerial extent of ~ 15-km. The Chumathang geothermal system lies in the northern direction of the Puga Valley and is bounded by NNE-SSW local fault, sub-parallel to the Kiagar Tso Fault.

In Chumathang and Puga areas of the UIB, the alluvial cover includes debris cones, sand, alluvial and glacial sediments coated with various types of hot spring minerals (borax and sulphur) extending upto a depth of about 15 to 65 m32,36. On the other hand, the Panamik geothermal system lies close to the KKF, a dextral strike-slip fault extending up to 1000 kms9, separating the western Himalaya from Tibet25. PNGS occurs in strongly deformed tectonic segments, bounded by Ladakh and Karakoram Batholiths to the SW and NE37,38 respectively encompassing a mixture of igneous (acidic and basic) sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks called as accretionary prism37–39.

Materials and methods

Sampling and analysis

Water samples were collected from hot springs of PCGS including Panamik, Pulthang, Changlung and PNGS viz., Puga, Chumathang and Gaik from 2018 to 2020 in four sets for hydrochemical, trace element, 13CDIC, δ18O and δ2H analysis. As there are many discharging points, water samples were collected from the major springs. The in-situ field parameters (pH, EC, TDS, water temperature and ORP) were measured using a multi-parameter probe (HANNA, HI98194). The water samples were filtered through < 0.22 μm nucleopore filter paper (Whatman) for hydrochemical analysis. Samples for cation/trace metal were treated with 6 N-HNO3 to preserve the dissolved cations by preventing their precipitation or adsorption onto the walls of the storage container. The choice of 6 N-HNO₃ is based on its effectiveness in stabilizing dissolved cations and trace metals by maintaining an acidic pH, which minimizes adsorption onto container walls and prevents oxidation or hydrolysis reactions. This standard preservation method ensures consistency in analytical results and prevents potential alteration of elemental concentrations during storage and transport. Whereas, chloroform (CHCl3) was added to the anion samples to inhibit the growth of bacteria and other microorganisms that could alter the concentration of anions through metabolic processes. The water samples for 13CDIC, δ18O/δ2H were collected unfiltered in 125 ml and 10 ml HDPE bottles and filled upto brim to avoid evaporation.

Major cations and trace elements were analysed using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS Thermo Fisher ICAP 6000), with precision for most analyses being less than 3%. Anions were measured using ion chromatography (Dionex Aquion 120), with precision and accuracy both being less than 5%. Total alkalinity, expressed as HCO3⁻, was calculated using the inflection point titration method with the US Geological Survey Calculator. Oxygen and hydrogen isotopes of water were measured using Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer [(IRMS, Delta V plus, Thermo Scientific)] in a continuous flow mode40. The stable water isotopic ratios are expressed in δ notation following Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water. The precisions of δ18O and δ2H was within ± 0.1‰ and ± 1‰, respectively.

The 13CDIC analyses of water samples were carried out using a Delta V (Thermo-Fisher) continuous flow Stable Isotope Ratio Mass spectrometry (IRMS) at the Wadia Institute of Himalayan Geology (WIHG) Dehradun. In this process, a volume of ~ 50–70 µl of the pure phosphoric acid (99%) was put into a clean vial (12-ml) closed with a septa (cap) and placed into the tray attached with a Gas bench, maintained at the temperature of 32 °C. All the vials were treated with 99.99% pure Helium gas by pressurised flushing through needle, attached with the PAL auto-sampler of the gas bench unit. It is ensured to remove all the pre-existing atmospheric gases from the vials before the injection of water samples (~ 700 μl) via a disposable syringe. It is then allowed for ~ 18 h at 32 °C to equilibrate with CO2 of known volume and composition. Finally, the sample stands ready to measure the δ13CDIC on IRMS through analysis needle attached with gas bench. Before the measurements of the samples, NBS-18 (IAEA, a primary carbonate standard collected from Fen locality of Norway) were analysed to check the accuracy of measurements which were consistent within 0.1‰. In addition, with each batch of samples, several laboratory standards (Merck CaCO3 was calibrated against the primary standard of NBS-18) and repeat analyses of samples were also analysed to check the precision (reproducibility) of measurements was found to be better than ± 0.1. Helium data was used from published literature9,14,15,17,23. Bouguer gravity data was downloaded from Geological Survey of India website (https://bhukosh.gsi.gov.in/Bhukosh/) and seismic event data was download from global earthquake catalogue of USGS (https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/search/).

Results and discussions

Origin of geothermal waters

The stable water isotopic characteristics of hot springs along PCGS associated with ISZ and PNGS associated with KKF exhibit significant variability. The PCGS related hot springs exhibit higher isotopic values (δ18O: − 13.4‰ to − 10.3‰ and δ2H; − 112‰ to − 102‰) than PNGS hot springs (δ18O: − 16.4‰ to − 15.3‰ and δ2H; − 121‰ to − 111‰). PCGS waters (except Gaik hot spring, which is a small spring in the area, may be with different flow paths) are located below the GMWL52 and LMWL53. The isotope plot suggest that PCGS waters have additional sources than meteoric water, such as deeper fluids which are plausible in the area being a triple junction region and hence could be responsible for forcing their position off the LMWL. Therefore, the degree of departure of PCGS with respect to LMWL may be a manifestation of efficient mixing of magma components with meteoric waters. The δ18O and δ2H values are higher in PNGS than Nubra River (flowing close to this geothermal system) but similar to snowmelt and glacier melt at an elevation of 3550 to 4090 m, asl suggesting the recharge of these thermal springs by high-altitude snow and glacier melt. We observed a significant positive shift in δ1⁸O values (Fig. 2), similar to trends reported in other geothermal systems worldwide, including Yellowstone, Steamboat Springs and Lassen Volcanic Park11,54–57. This isotopic shift is likely due to high-temperature water-rock interaction, where geothermal waters undergo O18 exchange with the aquifer matrix, as reported in other hydrothermal environments57,58. In the Himalayan geothermal system, the concurrent increase in both δ1⁸O and δ2H values suggests prolonged interaction between hot fluids and the regional silicate-hosted lithology. This process is further influenced by fluid influx and decompression related hydrothermal circulation, which facilitate isotopic modification under elevated temperatures58. The enrichment of both 18O and 2H in the PNGS geothermal system suggests significant interaction of geothermal water with silicate host rocks. However, the observed positive shift in δ18O with minimal effect on δ2H values of PCGS suggests the intense exchange of 18O between the geothermal water and the host rocks under intense temperature. A positive correlation of δ18O and/or δ2H with HCO3−, K+ and As indicates high temperature fluid interaction with silicates and other crustal contaminated minerals or the mixing of fluid interaction with silicates and other crustal contaminated minerals or the mixing of infiltrating meteoric waters with deep saline hydrothermal brine11. This seems applicable to PNGS which clearly shows enrichment of both δ18O and δ2H but this does not hold good for PCGS which showed shift in δ18O only. The higher isotopic value observed in PCGS (δ18O: − 12‰) than PNGS (δ18O: − 14‰) indicates that the thermal waters preserve signal of high temperature oxygen isotope exchange in PCGS.

Circulation of recharging water and reservoir temperature

The geothermal springs of PNGS and PCGS geothermal systems (Fig. 1) expel water with a surface temperature ranging from 73 °C (Panamik) to 89 °C (Puga) at an altitude of 3262 m, asl and 4424 m, asl respectively, where the average annual ambient temperature ranges from − 38 °C to 23 °C. The pH of the thermal springs is circum-neutral to highly alkaline in nature varying from 7 (Changlung) to 10 (Chumathang). The cumulative discharge of these springs ranges from 900 Lm-1 in PNGS and 500 Lm-1 in PCGS. The geothermal waters of PCGS are Na-Cl type whereas PNGS waters are Na-SO4 type suggesting geothermal waters are typically from high mineralisation hosted by felsic reservoir rocks or salinization/brine or mixing with magmatic fluids. Reservoir temperature calculated using chemical geothermometry (e.g. silica based conductive cooling, maximum steam loss, no steam loss, chalcedony and chalcedony based upon field study) and cation based (Na-Li, Na-K and K2/Mg) geothermometers revealed a reservoir temperature of 205 °C to 290 °C with a circulation depth varying from 2020 m (Changlung) to 4000 m (Chumathang). The springs and their reservoir temperatures were higher during low flow period in winter (mean: 82 °C) and lower during high flow period in summer (mean: 64 °C) which is attributed to the dilution of geothermal water with the recharging melt water/streams. The spring temperatures and the resulting reservoir temperatures recorded by us during winter are higher than reported by other studies59–61. The sampling period of most of these studies were in peak flow season when winter accumulated snowfall contributes ~ 70% of water to regional hydrology62,63 thereby diluting the solute chemistry of spring waters. The recharge of PCGS from the Indus River, as supported by the water tracers, significantly decreases the actual reservoir temperature and results in dilution of solute concentration of geothermal water which otherwise would be quite higher. This leads to a systematic underestimation of the actual reservoir temperature and solute concentration within the PCGS. Despite this dilution effect, the recorded reservoir temperature remains notably high at 320 °C, indicating the presence of substantial geothermal activity. Moreover, the circulation depth surpassing 4000 m, seems quite unreasonable under the above scenario when the geothermal system is dominantly recharged locally.

In addition, the noteworthy clustering of PCGS samples along the full-equilibrium line of Na–K-Mg triangular plot (Fig. 3), strongly suggests the existence of an intense localized heat source. A plausible explanation for these observations is the presence of a shallow magma chamber beneath PCGS. This magmatic body, potentially formed by the partial melting driven by reduced lithostatic pressure in the shallow to mid-crust, that provides heat to sustain high reservoir temperatures and geothermal activity along the suture zone in the UIB. Additionally, the recharge of PCGS springs by the Indus River, as supported by water tracers, further modifies the chemical and thermal characteristics of the geothermal waters, diluting solutes and reducing surface temperatures. However, the underlying magmatic heat source maintains high subsurface temperatures, even with surface-level dilution. Water rock interaction v/s partial melting of subducting slab

Geothermal waters of the PCGS exhibited significantly higher concentrations of major ions and trace elements compared to the PNGS. The concentrations of Cl⁻ (80.5 mg L⁻1 to 384.2 mg L⁻1 vs. 30.2 mg L⁻1 to 86.2 mg L⁻1), HCO₃⁻ (420 mg L⁻1 to 817 mg L⁻1 vs. 263 mg L⁻1 to 600 mg L⁻1), As (213 µg L⁻1 to 2256 µg L⁻1 vs. 172 µg L⁻1 to 284 µg L⁻1), and rare alkali elements such as Li (4.5 mg L⁻1 to 24.5 mg L⁻1 vs. 0.32 mg L⁻1 to 0.63 mg L⁻1), Cs (2.7 mg L⁻1 to 9.3 mg L⁻1 vs. 0.30 mg L⁻1 to 0.31 mg L⁻1), and Rb (0.2 mg L⁻1 to 1.1 mg L⁻1 vs. 0.51 mg L⁻1 to 0.52 mg L⁻1) indicate a unique geochemical signature. These elements are commonly associated with arc magmas64, which form due to fluid transfer from a subducting slab and sediment during partial melting in the overlying mid-crust65. The anomalously high concentrations of As, Cs, and Li in PCGS geothermal waters suggest a complex geochemical evolution. Their location in the partial to fully equilibrated field of the Na-K-Mg triangular plot (Fig. 3) indicates interaction with reservoir minerals under high-temperature conditions. Two primary mechanisms could explain the enrichment of these elements: (1) leaching from host reservoir rocks and (2) mixing with magmatic fluids. The positive correlation of several geochemical constituents with reservoir temperature supports the host rock leaching hypothesis10. However, the relatively poor correlation of As (r2 = 0.34) and Cs (r2 = 0.21) with high reservoir temperatures suggests that host rock leaching alone is insufficient to explain their elevated concentrations66. High alkali element concentrations (e.g., Rb, Li, Cs) in PCGS thermal waters likely result from prolonged interaction of geothermal fluids with granitic rocks at temperatures exceeding 200 °C. However, the exceptionally high Cs concentration in PCGS, which exceeds that of Li, suggests an additional source beyond water-rock interaction. A quantitative estimate of Cs leaching from reservoir rocks, considering an average discharge of 30 L s⁻1 and a Cs content of 100 mg kg⁻1 in 40 km3 of granitoids over a period of 10⁶ years, indicates that approximately 1.08 × 1013 kg of Cs could have been leached66. Similarly, for As, given an average granite content of 0.8 mg kg⁻1, approximately 1.30 × 1011 kg of As could have been released. These estimates reveal that the total Cs and As leached from host rocks is two orders of magnitude lower than the observed flux in geothermal waters, ruling out host rock leaching as the sole source of these elements. Instead, we propose that the elevated concentrations of Cs and As in PCGS are primarily sourced from magmatic fluids generated by partial melting in the mid-crust, likely influenced by fluid migration from the subduction zone. Unlike mantle-derived magmas found in rift zones and hotspots, which typically release magmatic fluids low in As and rich in sulphide, the relatively low sulphide content in PCGS (31 mg L⁻1 to 72 mg L⁻1) suggests a significant contribution from crustally contaminated magmas10,16,67. This is a key geochemical factor responsible for the extremely high aqueous As concentrations in these geothermal waters. Furthermore, orogenically sourced continental arc-lithologies are believed to contribute As and other trace elements to groundwater68–70. This As may be trapped, sequestered and enriched in ophiolitic serpentinites and related rocks within the region70. This is corroborated by geophysical studies that reveal widespread partial melting driven by reduced lithostatic pressure within the mid-crust across the Himalayas71,72, further supporting the role of crustal magmatic contributions in the PCGS geothermal system.

Volatiles as indicators of magma source

Noble gases, especially helium isotopes in hot springs are being used as an unequivocal evidence of mantle derived component, owing to its distinct isotopic signature in atmosphere, crust and mantle (R = 3He/4He ratio) in atmosphere (RA = 1.384 × 10–6), crust (0.02 RA) and mantle (8RA)0.02 RA and 8RA9,73. Keeping in view the varied degree of dilution of helium isotopic ratio during its ascent from mantle through crust, a corrected 3He/4He value (RC/RA) > 0.1 is considered to have a mantle component73 with recent transport25. Helium isotopes reported from hot springs associated with KKF e.g., west Tibet (RA = 0.38)9, Changlung hot spring (RA = 2.2)25 and Pamir (RA = 0.88)16 suggested mantle helium. We know that faults and fractures in the Earth’s crust are known pathways for migrating fluids including gases from mantle to the surface and KKF cuts through the lithosphere and serves as a conduit for mantle fluid flow. Lower values of R/RA (< 0.1) reported from the hot springs near Indus Suture Zone (ISZ) e.g., Puga and Chumathang hot springs25 indicate crustal helium without any input from the mantle25. The study suggested that the hot springs associated with ISZ in Tibet and the Indian side do not have mantle-derived helium. We argue that the lower 3He/4He values in these hot springs, which are significantly lower than the mantle-derived signature (8RA) and closer to the crustal range (0.02RA), originates from shallow to mid-crustal partial melting rather than direct mantle input. This partial melting process driven by reduced lithostatic pressure within the crust generates crustal helium signatures, elucidating the observed isotope ratios without requiring a direct contribution from the mantle or the subducting slab.

The δ13C values of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) in the geothermal waters of the Puga–Chumathang Geothermal System (PCGS) range from − 6.1‰ to − 2.5‰, while those of the KKF geothermal springs range from − 0.0‰ to 1.7‰. The carbon isotopic composition of geothermal springs provides critical insights into their origin and the processes influencing their formation. Dissolved inorganic carbon in geothermal waters can originate from four major sources: (i) atmospheric CO₂ (Catm, δ13C ~ − 8‰ to − 7‰), (ii) organic carbon from sediments or soil (Corg, δ13C ~ − 30‰ to − 20‰), (iii) carbonate dissolution (Ccarb, δ13C ~ − 2‰ to 2‰), and (iv) deeply sourced CO2 (Cdeep), which includes contributions from magmatic degassing, mantle-derived CO2 (δ13C ~ − 6‰ to − 4‰), and thermal metamorphism of carbonate-bearing lithologies11,12,51. The δ13C values in PCGS geothermal waters are more negative than those expected from pure carbonate dissolution but do not exhibit a strong mantle signature. This suggests that the primary source of CO2 in PCGS is not direct mantle degassing but rather the thermal decomposition of carbonate-bearing rocks due to shallow to mid-crustal partial melting beneath the Indus Suture Zone (ISZ). This is particularly evident in the Changlung geothermal spring, which exhibits a δ13CDIC signature closely resembling a pure endogenic carbon end-member.

A carbon mass balance model51 focusing on Total Dissolved Inorganic Carbon (TDIC) reveals that 93.5% of the carbon in Changlung is sourced from deep carbon (Cdeep), aligning with its elevated 3He/4He ratio reported in previous studies25. This strongly suggests that the KKF acts as a direct conduit for mantle-derived carbon, enabling the ascent of deep-seated fluids to the surface (Fig. 4). In contrast, the geothermal springs associated with the ISZ exhibit a more complex isotopic signature, with Cdeep contributions ranging between 52.3% and 70.2%. These values suggest significant mixing between magmatic carbon and organic or carbonate-derived carbon. The magmatic carbon component is inferred to originate from the shallow to mid-crust melting beneath the ISZ (Fig. 4). While this process does not produce directly mantle-derived carbon, it results in a distinct endogenic signature associated with subduction zone processes. The presence of metamorphic carbon further complicates the isotopic composition, indicating interactions between ascending magmatic fluids and carbon-bearing crustal rocks. The observed variations in δ13CDIC values reflect the distinct geodynamic settings of the KKF and ISZ. The KKF geothermal system, influenced by deep lithospheric processes, is characterized by a dominant mantle-derived carbon signature, whereas the ISZ geothermal system displays a mixture of magmatic, metamorphic and organic carbon sources. Geophysical evidences of shallow magma chamber

To further explore the connection between isotopic, hydrogeochemical and geophysical characteristics of the PCGS, we compared our results with existing geophysical studies. Across the region, widespread high-amplitude negative gravity anomalies exceeding − 450 mGal (Fig. 5) have been observed. These anomalies are typically associated with high-altitude terrains and tectonic boundaries globally74. The alignment of lower Bouguer anomalies may result from either the higher elevation of the Himalayas or a reduction in crustal density75,76. Furthermore, such anomalies often coincide with major geological structures, which can facilitate fluid circulation and geothermal reservoir formation. The PCGS exhibits significantly lower Bouguer gravity values (Fig. 5) compared to surrounding areas, indicating either crustal thickening or the presence of low-density materials such as hydrothermal fluids or partially molten rock. Magma chambers, known for their lower density relative to surrounding lithologies, are a well-established source of gravity anomalies75–77. The lower elevation of PCGS relative to its surroundings, along with its geomorphological similarity to other basins in the region, provides strong evidence for a localized heat source. Magnetotelluric studies27,71,72 indicated a low-conductivity layer extending to a depth of approximately 8 km, suggesting the presence of a partially molten zone within the shallow to mid-crust. This layer, likely formed from the melting of subducted Indian crustal sediments, that serves as a primary heat source for PCGS. Seismic and geophysical observations further illuminate the tectonic and geothermal dynamics of the ISZ and KKF. The presence of such low-velocity zones plays a critical role in crustal deformation by facilitating strain accommodation, reducing stress transmission to the brittle upper crust and acting as geothermal fluid reservoirs. Contrasting seismic behaviors between the ISZ and KKF highlight the interplay between tectonics, crustal structure and geothermal activity. The KKF experiences seismicity due to active strain accumulation, while PCGS remains seismically quiet, underscoring the influence of shallow to mid-crustal magmatism. Magnetotelluric studies in the region71,72 have identified high-conductance layers in the shallow to mid-crust beneath the ISZ, indicative of partial melts originating from the subducted Indian crust27,72,78,80,82. These melts, in combination with high heat flow and seismic wave attenuation in the Himalayan crust, contribute to the development of geothermal reservoirs. The presence of a localized heat source beneath PCGS, supported by low-resistivity anomalies extending to depths of ~ 8 km, suggests a well-established shallow to mid-crustal melt zone responsible for elevated reservoir temperatures and sustained geothermal activity27,71,72. Geophysical and geochemical data collectively indicate that PCGS is primarily heated by shallow to mid-crustal melting processes associated with subducted Indian crust71,72. The magnetic low over southern Tibet further supports the presence of mid-crustal melts near the ISZ78,80,82. This evidence supports the conclusion that the PCGS geothermal system is fueled by heat from a partially molten shallow to mid-crustal region, emphasizing its potential as a significant geothermal resource with a strong connection to tectono-geothermal processes of the region.

Conceptual model of shallow magma chamber

The Trans-Himalaya, a prominent magmatic belt, marks the tectonic convergence zone between the Indian Plate to the south and the Tibetan Plateau to the north, with the ISZ delineating their boundary31,83. This region has undergone extensive geodynamic evolution driven by the India-Asia collision, characterized by intense volcanic and magmatic activity. During the Cretaceous period, the ISZ experienced significant volcanic episodes, followed by multiple phases of magmatism extending from the Upper Cretaceous to the Upper Tertiary. The E-W trending ISZ is associated with crustal deformation and partial melting within the shallow to mid-crust, which plays an essential role in sustaining the geothermal systems of the region84–88. The initiation of partial melting at shallow to mid-crustal depths is primarily influenced by crustal thickening, radiogenic heat production and tectonic decompression. These processes create zones of high heat flow, facilitating the generation of localized magma chambers that serve as heat sources for overlying geothermal systems. Geophysical studies also reveal the presence of low-velocity, high-conductivity zones within the shallow to mid-crust, indicating active magmatic processes. These zones provide a continuous influx of heat and volatiles, sustaining the high-temperature hydrothermal activity observed at PCGS. Tectonic activities, such as late Tertiary back-thrusting (T3) along the ISZ approximately 15 million years ago31, likely contributed to the decompression and partial melting of crustal materials. This process, coupled with episodic magma ascent, enriched the crust with heat and volatiles, further enhancing hydrothermal circulation. This model identifies shallow to mid-crustal melting as the primary heat source, with geophysical data providing strong evidence for partial melt accumulation beneath the ISZ. Variations in seismic velocity and electrical conductivity point to the presence of interconnected low-velocity and high-conductivity zones at depths of up to 8 to 20 kms, consistent with zones of partial melt or fluid-saturated crust27,71,78,79,89. These melt-rich zones serve as the primary reservoirs of heat, with magma chambers acting as conduits for heat and volatile transfer to the overlying hydrothermal system. The shallow to mid-crustal melting beneath the ISZ plays a pivotal role in the magmatic and hydrothermal processes shaping the region. Isotopic studies (Sr–Nd-Pb) of K-rich volcanic rocks from the ISZ suggest that crustal assimilation significantly influences magma evolution90–92. The interaction between ascending melts and the overlying crust modifies magma composition, leading to distinct geochemical signatures in hydrothermal fluids. Geophysical studies also corroborate the presence of partially molten zones within the shallow to mid-crust, aligning with petrographic evidence of K-rich volcanic rocks91,92, suggesting ongoing magmatic activity. The India-Asia convergence has led to significant strain localization along the ISZ, facilitating the development of partially molten zones within the crust 93,94,95. The continued thickening of the crust enhances heat retention, allowing sustained melting of under thrusted sediments. Ascent of these crustal melts, coupled with crustal assimilation and fractional crystallization, results in the formation of localized magma chambers, that provide thermal energy necessary for high-temperature hydrothermal circulation. These magma chambers serve as persistent heat reservoirs, replenishing the geothermal system and maintaining elevated surface temperatures at PCGS. Trace element compositions in hydrothermal fluids, including anomalously high concentrations of As and Cs, further validate the influence of shallow to mid-crustal magmatic inputs. These elements, enriched in crustal-derived magmas, are transferred to hydrothermal systems through fluid circulation, where they manifest as geochemical anomalies in spring waters. The isotopic and geochemical signatures emphasizing the integration of crustal magmatism and hydrothermal processes in shaping the unique geochemical characteristics of PCGS. The proposed conceptual model underscores the significance of shallow to mid-crustal melting, magma chamber evolution and crustal interactions in driving the hydrothermal and magmatic systems of the ISZ. The heat and volatiles from these crustal magma chambers sustain high-temperature geothermal activity, enrich the geochemical profile of surface springs in UIB and reinforce the strong tectono-geothermal linkages within the region.

Conclusions

Hydrogeochemical analysis of geothermal fluids, complemented by geological and geophysical observations, was used to investigate the primary sources of heat, solutes and volatiles in the Puga-Chumathang (PCGS) and Panamik (PNGS) geothermal systems. The geochemical and isotopic characteristics of these geothermal waters are strongly influenced by their distinct hydrogeological settings. PCGS waters are primarily recharged by the Indus River, whereas PNGS waters originate from snow and glaciermelt at elevations of 3550–4090 m, asl. A notable δ1⁸O enrichment in PCGS waters suggests significant isotopic exchange with carbonate aquifers at high temperatures, while the coupled δ1⁸O and δ2H enrichment in PNGS indicates prolonged interaction with silicate host rocks. Geochemically, PCGS waters exhibit a Na⁺-Cl⁻ dominance, indicative of a magmatic influence linked to partial melting within the shallow to mid-crust beneath the ISZ. In contrast, PNGS waters show Na⁺-SO₄2⁻ signatures, reflecting predominant water-rock interactions with silicate lithology. The elevated concentrations of Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻, As, Li, Cs and Rb in PCGS contrast sharply with the silicate-derived geochemical signatures of PNGS. The high surface temperatures (89 °C) and reservoir temperatures (~ 290 °C) in PCGS suggest localized heat sources associated with shallow to mid-crustal partial melting beneath the ISZ, supported by geophysical indicators, including seismic wave attenuation and discrete helium-carbon isotopic signatures. The presence of mantle helium near the KKF and crustal helium with magmatic δ13C signatures near ISZ reinforces the role of localized magmatic and crustal contribution in sustaining high-temperature geothermal activity. These findings underscore the significant geothermal energy potential of PCGS, driven by high reservoir temperatures and substantial heat contributions from shallow to mid-crustal partial melting. Effective resource management requires a more comprehensive understanding of recharge mechanisms, fluid origins and underlying geodynamics in the region.
