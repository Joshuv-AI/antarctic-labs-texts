Iatrogenic postoperative spinal cord herniation: A report of two cases and review of the literature
Authors: Diogo Gonçalves Mesquita, Inês Almeida Lourenço, Edson Santos Oliveira, Sergio Livraghi, Alexandre Rainha Campos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Iatrogenic spinal cord herniation is an exceptionally rare complication following spinal surgery involving durotomy. Failure of watertight dural closure may lead to pseudomeningocele formation and progressive spinal cord tethering, ultimately resulting in herniation and neurological deterioration.Case Description: We report two cases of postoperative spinal cord herniation. A 36-year-old male developed acute conus medullaris herniation 2 weeks after surgical resection of a cystic lesion, presenting with paraparesis, sensory loss, and sphincter dysfunction. A 68-year-old male presented with delayed cervical spinal cord herniation 19 months after C5–C7 meningioma resection, manifesting with progressive paresthesia on all four limbs. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated herniation of the spinal cord through a dorsal dural defect in both patients. Surgical revision consisted of extension of the previous durotomy, microsurgical lysis of arachnoid adhesions, reduction of the herniated spinal cord, and watertight dural repair using non-absorbable sutures reinforced with a collagen dural substitute and fibrin sealant. Both patients experienced significant neurological recovery, with complete symptomatic resolution in the cervical case.Conclusion: Postoperative spinal cord herniation requires a high index of suspicion in any patient with delayed neurological deterioration after spinal surgery. Early surgical untethering with watertight dural closure is essential to prevent permanent deficits; pseudomeningoceles identified on postoperative imaging warrant radiological surveillance until resolution.

INTRODUCTION

Iatrogenic spinal cord herniation is a rare but serious complication of spinal surgery, associated with dural defects arising from durotomy. Its reported incidence is approximately 0.015%, and delayed recognition may result in permanent neurological deficits.[5]

The pathophysiology of postoperative spinal cord herniation involves a complex interaction between dural defects, arachnoid adhesions, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics. Inadequate dural closure may lead to persistent CSF leakage and pseudomeningocele formation. Over time, arachnoid adhesions may tether the spinal cord to the margins of the dural defect. Pulsatile CSF flow and the resulting pressure gradient across the defect can gradually drive the spinal cord through the dural opening, eventually leading to incarceration of neural tissue and neurological deterioration.[4]

Most of the reported cases of spinal cord herniation occur spontaneously within the thoracic spine due to the natural thoracic kyphosis that presses the spinal cord against the ventral dura. In contrast, iatrogenic cases may occur at any spinal level where a surgical durotomy has been performed.[3]

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A 36-year-old male underwent complete surgical resection of a cystic lesion located at the level of the conus medullaris [Figure 1]. Dural closure was performed with a non-continuous monofilament suture. The immediate postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remained neurologically intact.

Case 1 - T2 sagittal magnetic resonance imaging pre-operative, showing the cystic lesion.

Two weeks after surgery, the patient presented to the emergency department with rapidly progressive lower extremity weakness, bilateral lower limb hypoesthesia, and acute urinary sphincter dysfunction. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrated incarceration of the conus medullaris through the dorsal dural defect [Figures 2 and 3]. The patient was taken emergently to the operating room, where the durotomy was extended to allow adequate exposure. Intraoperatively, the conus was found to be herniated and tethered by arachnoid adhesions; under microscopic visualization, the neural tissue was carefully untethered and reduced into the intradural compartment.

Case 1 - Post-operative (1st surgery) T2 sagittal magnetic resonance imaging showing the spinal cord herniation.

Case 1 - Post-operative (1st surgery) magnetic resonance imaging T2 axial showing spinal cord herniation.

Watertight dural closure was performed using uninterrupted non-absorbable sutures (Prolene), reinforced with a collagen dural matrix (DuraGen) and fibrin sealant (Tisseel).Postoperative MRI confirmed reduction of the herniation and restoration of normal spinal cord anatomy [Figures 4 and 5]. At 1-year follow-up, the patient had achieved complete motor and sphincteric recovery with only minimal residual sensory symptoms.

Case 1 - Post-repair surgery T2 sagittal magnetic resonance imaging showing the restored spinal cord.

Case 1 - Post-repair surgery axial T2 magnetic resonance imaging.

Case 2

A 68-year-old male presented with a cervical intradural extramedullary tumor at C5-C7, radiologically consistent with a meningioma [Figure 6]. He underwent C5-C7 laminectomy and complete tumor resection with dural closure using a continuous monofilament suture. The postoperative course was uneventful. A 2-month follow-up MRI demonstrated a small asymptomatic pseudomeningocele at the surgical site [Figure 7]; no intervention was undertaken.

Case 2 - Preoperative sagittal STIRGad magnetic resonance imaging showing the cervical meningioma.

Case 2 - Two months postoperative sagittal T2 magnetic resonance imaging showing complete removal of the meningioma and the pseudomeningocele.

Nineteen months after surgery, the patient developed progressive paresthesia in all four limbs. Cervical MRI demonstrated herniation of the spinal cord through the dorsal dural defect at C6, with extension into the pseudomeningocele [Figure 8]. Surgical revision revealed dense arachnoid adhesions tethering the spinal cord to the margins of the dural defect; following careful microsurgical lysis, the spinal cord was reduced into the intradural space [Video 1]. Primary dural repair was performed with continuous non-absorbable sutures reinforced with a collagen dural substitute and fibrin sealant. Follow-up imaging confirmed resolution of the herniation and restoration of normal spinal cord anatomy [Figures 9 and 10]. The patient was asymptomatic at 6-month reevaluation.

DISCUSSION

Iatrogenic spinal cord herniation is a rare but increasingly recognized complication of spinal surgery. Most of the cases occur following procedures involving intradural exploration or tumor resection, where a durotomy is performed.[4]

The underlying mechanism involves a persistent dural defect and arachnoid adhesions that tether the spinal cord to the margins of the durotomy. Continuous CSF pulsations generate a pressure gradient that progressively pushes the spinal cord through the dural opening, resulting in herniation and neurological deterioration.[2]

Postoperative pseudomeningocele formation is an important precursor to spinal cord herniation: the accumulated CSF creates a cavity into which the cord may gradually protrude, explaining the delayed presentation seen in some patients.[6] Case 2 exemplifies this directly, with an asymptomatic pseudomeningocele evident at 2 months and clinical herniation manifesting only at 19 months, underscoring the need for serial radiological surveillance until resolution.

Cervical spinal cord herniation is particularly rare, as the natural lordosis tends to maintain the spinal cord away from dorsal dural defects.[3] Our second case documents a delayed cervical herniation after meningioma resection and demonstrates that complete neurological recovery is achievable even after a prolonged symptomatic interval.

Clinical presentation typically includes progressive motor weakness, sensory deficits, and occasionally sphincter dysfunction; MRI is the imaging modality of choice.[4]Our cases reflect this clinical variability: Case 1 presented acutely within 2 weeks, requiring emergency intervention, while Case 2 followed a subacute course over 19 months, reinforcing the need for vigilance across a wide range of postoperative timelines.

The treatment of choice is surgical repair, with goals of spinal cord reduction, lysis of arachnoid adhesions, and watertight dural reconstruction to prevent recurrence.[1] In both cases, we performed microsurgical adhesiolysis, neural tissue reduction, and multi-layer dural repair with non-absorbable sutures reinforced with a collagen substitute and fibrin sealant. The favorable outcomes in both patients align with prior series reporting good recovery when intervention is timely.[1,4]

The contrasting timelines of our cases suggest a biomechanical gradient: The conus medullaris, subject to greater intrathecal pressure fluctuations than the cervical cord, may accelerate herniation at lower spinal levels, warranting earlier surgical vigilance in this region.

CONCLUSION

Iatrogenic spinal cord herniation is a rare but clinically significant complication of spinal surgery; postoperative pseudomeningocele should prompt serial radiological surveillance, and early surgical untethering with watertight dural repair is essential to prevent permanent neurological deficits.
