Longitudinal associations between cybervictimization, generalized problematic internet use, anxiety, and depression: cross-lagged panel analysis
Authors: R Escortell-Sánchez, A Basterra-González, J M Machimbarrena, J Ortega-Barón, J González-Cabrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, several studies, including research with bidirectional and cross-effect designs, have revealed longitudinal associations between cyberbullying, Generalized Problematic Internet Use (GPIU), anxiety and depression. However, few studies have addressed the interrelationship of these four dimensions at different time points, particularly considering the role of cybervictimization. This analysis is crucial for understanding the dynamics between online risks and psychosocial distress in adolescence. This study had the following objectives: (1) to analyze the autoregressive behavior of cybervictimization, GPIU, depression and anxiety; (2) to explore the longitudinal associations and temporal relationships between cybervictimization, GPIU, depression and anxiety. A longitudinal study was carried out with two measurements 6 months apart on 1,640 Spanish students (44% boys; n = 721), with a mean age of 13.90 ± 1.5 years. A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was used to examine longitudinal associations between variables across time. The results yielded positive and significant autoregressive effects, indicating stability in cybervictimization, GPIU, depression and anxiety. Significant cross-lagged associations were also found: cybervictimization at W1 was associated with higher levels of GPIU at W2; anxiety at W1 was associated with subsequent levels of depression at W2; and depression at W1 was associated with subsequent levels of both cybervictimization and anxiety at W2. Digital literacy is essential to prevent Internet-related risks, reduce cybervictimization, and address depressive symptoms as key priorities for intervention. Introduction

Society is facing new challenges related to technologies, with the Internet being an essential element in modern life1. Although its positive influence is undeniable, it is also evident that it has altered traditional forms of social interaction. Likewise, its versatility and constant updates create new problems and potential risks2, especially in the adolescent population due to the psychosocial vulnerability associated with this stage3. In recent decades, probably the most studied phenomenon at this stage has been cyberbullying, defined as the use of electronic devices by one or more people to intentionally and repetitively attack someone who cannot easily defend themselves, with an asymmetry of power between the victim and the aggressor (or aggressors)4. Given its magnitude and prevalence, cyberbullying has become a public health problem5.

Considering the roles involved in this dynamic (victims, aggressors, and bystanders), cybervictims are perhaps the most worrying due to the level of suffering experienced and their participation rates. In the international context, Cosma et al.6 found that between 14 and 57.5% of adolescents have been cybervictims. In Spain, the UNICEF reports that cybervictimization varies between 12.2 and 22.5% between ages 12 and 16 years7. Due to its nature, cybervictimization has been considered a relational risk, along with others such as sexting8, or online grooming problems and online partner abuse9. However, this does not include other dysfunctional risks, such as Generalized Problematic Internet Use (GPIU)10, which is characterized by inappropriate use of technology that generates psychosocial consequences in different life areas (personal, social, relational or academic/work)9.

At the theoretical-conceptual level, GPIU10,11 implies that a preference for online social interaction and mood regulation through the Internet will increase the likelihood of poor self-regulation, with negative consequences in the person’s life12. This term initially arose from the studies of Davis13, who proposed a theory of pathological internet use (PIU) from a cognitive-behavioral perspective14. In this framework, PIU was conceptualised as comprising both generalised and specific forms of maladaptive Internet use. The distinction between Generalized Problematic Internet Use (GPIU) and Specific Problematic Internet Use (SPIU) was later operationalized and further developed in subsequent models10,13.The model was later refined by Caplan10, distinguishing four categories: preference for online social interaction, mood regulation, deficient self-regulation (composed by cognitive preoccupation and compulsive use) and negative consequences10,15. Subsequently, this model has also been applied to other problems such as social networks in adolescents16. Other SPIUs, which encompass various online behaviors, can include online gambling, internet gaming disorder, problematic pornography use, etc.17. Unfortunately, GPIU is prevalent among adolescents, arousing the interest and concern of health professionals and the population as a whole18. International epidemiological studies state that approximately 17% of adolescents present PIU and problematic use of the smartphone19. At the national level, Spain registers a prevalence of 15% in the adolescent population20,21. However, a state-wide study in Spain shows that up to 33% of high school students could develop GPIU7.

Considering that both cybervictimization and GPIU are issues of great concern and circumscribed to digital environments, a relationship between both is theoretically expected. Thus, this relationship could be understood under different theoretical paradigms. One of them would be the problem behavior theory22, which assumes that adolescents who engage in one risky activity tend to engage in other problematic behaviors more frequently. Alternatively, the theoretical framework of compensatory Internet use23 also supports it. Specifically, it considers that stressors (such as cybervictimization) predispose one to the compulsive use of the Internet as an alternative to cope with negative emotions (GPIU has a dimension of mood regulation through the Internet).

On an empirical level, several researchers have attempted to shed light on this relationship. In this regard, Machimbarrena et al.24 observed, in a sample of 25,341 adolescents (10–18 years old), that both cybervictims and cyberbullies presented different profiles of GPIU. Specifically, both presented severe problematic use. Along the same lines, previous cross-sectional research has demonstrated the association between cyberbullying roles and GPIU25. The results derived from longitudinal studies are more scarce, and of great relevance to examining temporal associations between the two phenomena. Among those available in the scientific literature, most studies have confirmed the stability of the two variables over time. In other words, both cybervictimization26–28 and GPIU29–31 have been shown to be associated with their own levels over time. Based on the cross relationships between them, Gámez-Guadix et al.32 found that GPIU was associated with cyberbullying six months later, although cyberbullying in the first wave was not associated with GPIU in the second wave. In the case of cybervictimization, Liu et al.33 found, through a two-time longitudinal study, that cybervictimization was associated with subsequent levels of GPIU. However, more attention should be paid to the relationship between these problems over time and their possible relationship with key psychological variables for adolescents’ emotional well-being. There is still a gap in the research.

Regarding the consequences of the two problems, there is evidence that participation in both cyberbullying (any of its roles) and GPIU is associated with lower quality of life of those involved34,35. In the case of cyberbullying, multiple studies have analyzed its relationship with internalizing variables. Among them, the most studied are anxiety/stress and depression. Thus, numerous studies confirm that experiencing cyberbullying in any of its roles is associated with higher rates of anxiety/stress and/or depression. Specifically, and taking into account the studies utilizing the DASS-2136, this relationship has been found in Spanish34,37, American38, Arab39, Indian40 and Asian41 samples, with stress being the least correlated variable, compared to depression and anxiety. Although the bulk of evidence focuses on cross-sectional designs, some authors report longitudinal relationships between the aforementioned variables. Thus, Zhang et al.42, in a meta-analysis of 35 studies, observed a bidirectional relationship over time between cyberbullying (perpetration and victimization) and depression. Likewise, Lee et al.43, in a sample of 27 longitudinal studies, found that cybervictimization was related over time to depression, anxiety, and/or stress in the entire sample analyzed.

GPIU has also been related to young people’s quality of life35 as well as to internalizing variables, especially depression and anxiety41,44. In this regard, Cai et al.45, after a systematic review analysis of 223 studies, found that PIU is moderately and positively associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, among others. In addition, Aznar-Díaz et al.46, through a systematic review of 62 documents, established that depression is the most studied pathology with the strongest link to PIU. Although most previous literature focuses on cross-sectional studies, some findings measure the relationships between the variables over time. In this regard, Leo et al.47 reported that both depression and social anxiety were associated with the severity of of PIU symptoms. Along the same lines, Zhao et al.48 confirmed the existence of a significant bidirectional relationship between problems with internet use and depressive symptoms, with an increasing trend in the two measured waves, consistent with previous studies49. However, some authors suggest that the relationship is unidirectional, with differences between authors who indicate that depression precedes PIU or addictive Internet use31, and others who observe that PIU is associated with subsequent levels of depression50. Therefore, this issue grants further research to reach a consensus about the direction of the trajectory.

Anxiety and depression have also attracted attention as two disorders that are longitudinally associated with their own levels over time51–54. Furthermore, they are two disorders that coexist comorbidly, presenting some common symptomatology55–57. This relationship can be explained by the tripartite model of Clark and Watson58, who characterized the comorbidity of both disorders based on the presence of core symptoms, present in both anxiety and depression, such as negative affect or physiological hyperexcitability.

Given the above, a step further in the knowledge of these problems is required, providing a more global and multifaceted vision, as there is still a gap because no studies longitudinally relate cybervictimization with GPIU, together with anxiety and depression in adolescents. In this regard, Lee et al.41, through a cross-sectional study, determined that cybervictimization increases the tendency to depression, anxiety, and stress twofold. In addition, studies using path analyses have shown that cybervictimization and GPIU are longitudinally associated six months after the first measurement33. The same is true of GPIU and the internalizing variables of anxiety and depression59. Some consistency was also found in the bidirectional associations between depression and cyberbullying as shown by other studies29, although the role of cybervictim was not analyzed. Very few longitudinal studies have used more advanced techniques, such as the case of the cross-lagged panel model (CLPM). This tool enables the examination of reciprocal relationships between two or more variables over time, offering a more robust framework for examining temporal associations than simple or fixed-effect regression models. It also facilitates the analysis of complex dynamics between variables over time, potentially enhancing some partial approaches of previous studies. In this regard, we are only know of the study by Liu et al.60 which, although not focused on the cybervictim role, provided data on the dynamics of cyberbullying. Thus, it established that cyberbullying was not longitudinally associated with emotional distress (measured by the variables anxiety, stress, and depression). However, emotional distress at W1 was associated with cyberbullying at W2. Despite this evidence, there is still a gap in the longitudinal approach to cybervictimization, GPIU, and anxiety/depression conjointly.

Therefore, the objectives of this study are: (1) to analyze the autoregressive behavior of cybervictimization, GPIU, depression and anxiety; (2) to explore the cross-relationships between cybervictimization, GPIU, depression, and anxiety with an interval of six months. Consequently, it is expected that: (H1) the dimensions of cybervictimization, GPIU, depression and anxiety will remain stable throughout the two measured times32,33,51–53; (H2) cybervictimization will be longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of GPIU, anxiety, and depression after 6 months33,42,43; (H3) GPIU will be longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of cybervictimization, anxiety, and depression32,33,45,49,50; (H4) initial depression levels will be longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of cybervictimization, GPIU and anxiety42,47–49,55,57; (H5) anxiety will be longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of cybervictimization, GPIU and depression 6 months later43,47,55–57.

Method

Design and participants

A two-stage analytical and prospective study was conducted. The initial sample comprised 2,817 participants from seven schools in the province of Valencia (Spain) (three public schools, three private schools, and one state-subsidized school). Due to incomplete questionnaires or problems linking participants across waves, the final sample comprised 1,640 participants (721 boys, 44%) (participation rate: 58.2%). The reduction in the analytical sample was primarily due to two factors: (a) errors or inconsistencies in the anonymous identification codes used to match participants across waves, and (b) cases that could be matched but contained a high proportion of missing data, which prevented their inclusion in the longitudinal analyses.

The initial sample was representative of the reference population with a 99% confidence interval (CI) and a margin of error of 3%. first wave (W1) and the second wave (W2) were carried out at the beginning (November-December) and at the end (April-May) of the same academic year, respectively. Participants were students of Compulsory Secondary Education (CSE) and High School. A total of 390 (23.8%) were in 1 st grade CSE (180 boys), 366 (22.3%) in 2nd grade CSE (140 boys), 356 (21.7%) in 3rd grade CSE (161 boys), 363 (22.1%) in 4th grade CSE (161 boys) and 165 (10.1%) were in High School (79 boys). The mean age was 13.90 ± 1.5 (range 11–18 years) for W1 and 14.29 ± 1.52 for W2 (range 12–19 years).

To further examine potential attrition bias, participants retained in the longitudinal analytic sample (n = 1,640) were compared at baseline with participants who had usable T1 data but were not retained at T2 (n = 170). Because the distributions of the study variables were non-normal, Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted. No statistically significant differences were found for cybervictimization (Z = −1.944, p = 0.052), GPIU (Z = −0.433, p = 0.665), anxiety (Z = −1.159, p = 0.119), or depression (Z = −1.294, p = 0.196). These results suggest that, among participants with observable baseline information, retained and non-retained cases were broadly comparable on the main study variables. However, because some excluded cases could not be compared and losses were partly due to linkage problems and incomplete data, attrition bias cannot be ruled out.

Assessment instruments

Sociodemographic data such as sex, age, school, and grade were collected. Likewise, various questionnaires, which had been validated for the study population, were used.

The reduced version of the Cyberbullying Triangulation Questionnaire61, based on the work of Calvete et al.62 for the dimension of cybervictimization. The questionnaire comprises 9 items, which are symmetrical formulas for each of the behaviors analyzed (e.g., “I have received/sent/observed threatening or insulting messages being sent to other people”). In this study, only the dimension of cybervictimization was used. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost every week), with reference to the past three months. The score for each dimension varies between 0 and 36. CTQ has adequate indicators of reliability and internal validity (Table 1 presents the reliability indicators for each measurement time in the dimension of cybervictimization).

The Spanish version of the Generalized and Problematic Use of the Internet Scale-2 (GPIUS2)10,11 was used. It presents 15 items rated on a six-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree) covering the previous three months. The Spanish version of the Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)36,63. This self-report assesses negative emotional states. It consists of 21 statements distributed in three subscales of seven items each: depression, anxiety, and stress. In the present study, only the items for depression (e.g., “I found it difficult to work up the initiative to do things”) and anxiety (e.g., “I was aware of dryness of my mouth”) were used. Procedure

Participants completed the instrument through the online platform Survey Monkey ©. This process took place in computer classrooms and school classrooms through mobile devices. With the coordination of the guidance departments of each school, the teachers and tutors participated in this process. They received specific instructions to promote the procedure’s standardization in all the schools. The researchers monitored the online collection of data with the records of each school for all groups and classes. In addition, participant detection mechanisms such as those suggested by Niessen et al.64, like the “longstring” maximum response time and the “person-fit statistics,” were enabled. The time required to complete the questionnaires ranged from 15 to 30 min, depending on the students’ age and reading comprehension.

Ethical considerations

The participants and the school directors consented to participate in this study. A consent form was sent to the families of underage students to inform them of the aims and purposes of the study, its characteristics, those in charge of it, and the families’ right to refuse to participate. The parents or legal guardians of minors who did not allow participation returned the signed consent. Less than 1% of the sample refused to participate in the study. Students could always decide not to participate by indicating at the beginning of the assessment that they did not wish to participate (this occurred in less than 0.5% of cases).

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the [masked for review]. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

The means and standard deviations for each dimension, as well as the Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, were calculated with Jamovi 2.2.5. Reliability indices are expected to exceed the threshold of 0.7 proposed by Nunnally65. To fulfill the objectives and hypotheses raised, a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was carried out, which integrates structural equation modeling (SEM) and allows for the analysis of temporal and directional associations between constructs over time. For this purpose, the AMOS 29.0 program was used.

First, the assumptions of normality of the variables were checked, finding that most of them did not comply with this assumption. However, we decided to use the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method for two reasons: (1) non-normality is attenuated in large samples, according to the Central Limit Theorem66; (2) this estimator has performed well in large samples, such as those of this study (n ≥ 500)67. Next, the invariance of the longitudinal measurements of the four dimensions (cyberbu, GPIU, anxiety and depression) was verified to ensure that the scales measure the same at both time points. Configural, metric, and scalar invariance were calculated68. Subsequently, a CLPM model was run to examine the longitudinal bidirectionality of the relationships between the four dimensions. The fit of the model was evaluated through widely used indices, such as the Chi-square (χ²), which evaluates the discrepancy between the proposed model and the matrix of observed covariances. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), which measures the discrepancy between the adjusted model and the population model, was also considered, being an adequate value if RMSEA ≤ 0.05. The comparative fit index (CFI), which compares the fit of the proposed model with a null model was measured, with values greater than 0.90 indicative of a good fit; the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) is also used to evaluate the fit compared to the null model, with values greater than 0.90 considered adequate. Finally, the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) was used, which evaluates the square root of the standardized square error, and is considered adequate if SRMR ≤ 0.08. The model was considered to achieve an adequate fit if these thresholds were met. In addition, the bootstrapping technique was applied with 1000 repeated samples to calculate the confidence intervals of the estimates obtained. To assess the invariance of the longitudinal measures, unconstrained models were compared with constrained models (in terms of factor loads and intercepts) through changes in the CFI and RMSEA indices. According to the criteria established by Cheung & Rensvold69, invariance is considered to be achieved if ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015.

It should be noted that, given the use of a traditional CLPM with two waves, the model does not allow the separation of within-person and between-person variance. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as prospective longitudinal associations rather than as evidence of intraindividual processes or causal effects.

The results suggested that all autoregressive effects were positive and significant, with standardized coefficients ranging from 0.35 to 0.68. In addition, the following cross-effects were significant: (1) from cybervictimization at W1 to GPIU at W2 (β = 0.074); (2) from anxiety at W1 to depression at W2 (β = 0.118); (3) from depression at W1 to cybervictimization at W2 (β = 0.217); and (4) from depression at W1 to anxiety at W2 (β = 0.120). Discussion

The present study, developed through a CLPM, provides findings about the bidirectional and cross-related relationships between cybervictimization, GPIU, and the internalizing variables of depression and anxiety. A particularly noteworthy aspect, unlike in previous literature, is the longitudinal nature of the study, which provides data on the evolution of the variables six months after the initial measurement.

Focusing on the first objective, the variables were stable at both times, thus confirming the first hypothesis (H1). Hence, cybervictimization, GPIU, and internalizing viables were consistently associated with their own levels over time. These results are consistent with previous studies that revealed the stable nature of the variables26–28,32,33,51–53, showing that these phenomena can become chronic. In the case of cybervictimization, being bullied may be associated with the development of dysfunctional social patterns that condition adolescents’ reactions to interactions, developing dysfunctional communication styles that, in turn, may be linked to a higher likelihood of experiencing cybervictimization71.

In the case of the GPIU, the stability of the phenomenon has been robustly revealed in various prior studies29–31,50. This pattern may be interpreted by considering the characteristics of the Internet. Thus, the absence of nonverbal cues or anonymity facilitates communication, creating a false empowering effect that does not always occur in face-to-face interactions72,73. This motivating effect can “blind” young people, preventing them from discerning between moderate and GPIU, as they are more involved in digital environments.

Regarding internalizing variables, anxiety and depression have been the most extensively studied in the literature, likely because they are, unfortunately, the most prevalent throughout childhood and adolescence51. These variables have been shown not only to remain stable over time but also to present symptoms that evolve with different developmental stages, for example, from separation anxiety in childhood to generalized anxiety in adolescence53. Therefore, they are topics that require particular attention from the scientific community in order to create effective interventions that can prevent the chronicity of these disorders and their interference with the holistic development of minors.

As for the second objective, in which the cross-relationships between the variables were analyzed, several hypotheses were formulated in this regard. Concerning Hypothesis 2, cybervictimization was longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of GPIU 6 months later. This finding is consistent with previous literature reporting associations between these constructs24,25,74, including longitudinal evidence32,33. This observed association may be interpreted in light of different theoretical frameworks. Among them, the problem-behavior theory22 stands out, which postulates that adolescents who participate in one risky behavior tend to get involved in other problems more frequently. In the same way, it can be explained by attending to the theory of compensatory Internet use23. Thus, stressors such as cybervictimization may be linked to increased engagement in compulsive Internet use as an alternative way to cope with the negative emotions experienced. Both explanations can shed some light, at least partially, on the cross-relationships between the two problems.

Surprisingly and contrary to our expectations based on prior studies, cybervictimization was not longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of anxiety and depression42,43. Although the relationship between cybervictimization and anxiety/depression has been widely demonstrated, most longitudinal studies tend to use samples with a wide age range, which allows for a more robust examination of temporal associations. In the present study, as we made a single measurement after 6 months, this time lapse may be insufficient to infer directional effects with confidence. Particularly, taking into account that young people may have become desensitized to online violence (especially through social networks and currently, cyber-hate speech is prevailing on the same networks)75,76, it may take a long time to become aware of the consequences experienced. The findings, however, are in accordance with the results reported by Liu et al.60, showing no temporal associations between cyberbullying and emotional distress using a CLPM. Studies with more robust statistical designs, like the one employed in this study with CLPM, tend to analyze the dynamics in a more complex and robust style than others with simple regression models or fixed effects. Therefore, more research in the literature is required to clarify this discrepancy in results.

Hypothesis 3

was not supported, which contrasts with the majority of previous literature41,44,45. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the research domain of generalized problematic internet use (GPIU) remains an evolving field, characterized by ongoing conceptual and methodological refinements. Consequently, there is no stable consensus regarding the adequacy of measurement instruments or the conceptualization of the construct, which complicates the derivation of generalizable conclusions77. An alternative explanation is that the construct of GPIU may be becoming increasingly diffuse as it encompasses more specific forms of problematic use, such as social networking, which is highly normative and prevalent within this age group16. Despite these possible explanations, the findings in this sample indicate that the overall GPIU score did not exhibit prospective associations with the other study variables once autoregressive and concurrent effects were taken into account. Given that the present study relied on a global GPIU score and was conducted across only two time points, it was not possible to determine whether specific dimensions of GPIU follow distinct temporal trajectories. This issue should be addressed in future research rather than inferred from the current data.

Depression (H4) was associated with subsequent higher levels of both cybervictimization and anxiety, although not of GPIU. The bidirectional effects between depression and cybervictimization have been previously established by other authors42,43. Depression is characterized by social isolation, lack of meaning in life, interpersonal problems, and a negative self-image, which can make young people more vulnerable to becoming cybervictims78. The relationship between depression and anxiety has been pointed out previously. In addition, prior evidence highlights that 51% of people diagnosed with major depression also receive a diagnosis of anxiety within the same year56. This relationship can be explained by the tripartite model of Clark and Watson58, who characterized the comorbidity of the two disorders according to the presence of core symptoms (negative affect, physiological hyperexcitability, etc.). Therefore, it is often difficult to separate these two disorders.

In the absence of a relationship between depression and GPIU, the results are contrary to expectations and previous research. Thus, while the bulk of the literature points to a cross-sectional46 and longitudinal47–49 relationship between the two variables, the present study did not show that depression was longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of GPIU 6 months later. The difference in findings may be due to the fact that many of the studies measure broader time intervals and/or focus on different cultural samples, which may prevent a general consensus regarding firm evidence.

Finally, regarding Hypothesis 5 (H5), anxiety was only longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of depression, with no significant associations observed with cybervictimization or GPIU. The reciprocal association between anxiety and depression is well established in the literature55,57, particularly during adolescence. Developmentally, adolescents may be especially sensitive to social evaluation, which may contribute to the co-occurrence of these internalizing symptoms79.

Contrary to our expectations, anxiety was not longitudinally associated with subsequent cybervictimization or GPIU, a finding that differs from prior research43,47,48,60. From an alternative perspective, anxiety may be linked to protective processes through avoidance. In contrast to the “impoverishment” hypothesis (which posits greater vulnerability among anxious youth), anxiety may also be related to avoidance behaviors and increased caution in online contexts. For instance, adolescents with higher levels of social anxiety may feel intimidated by interactions on social media or public forums, which may be associated with reduced exposure to potentially harmful situations. In this context, anxiety would not function as a risk factor but rather as a variable associated with lower engagement in online risky behaviors, potentially reducing the likelihood of victimization80. Furthermore, as noted above, inconsistencies in the literature may be partly explained by variability in sample characteristics (e.g., age range, cultural context, measurement instruments), which complicates the integration of findings and the establishment of consistent patterns. Additionally, future research employing more extensive longitudinal designs with more than two waves may provide a clearer understanding of the temporal dynamics among these variables32.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account. (1) As the measurement instruments are based on self-report questionnaires, the data may be affected by response biases such as social desirability. Future studies could benefit from incorporating multiple sources of information, such as reports from family members or teachers, to improve the robustness of the findings. (2) Although the study is based on a large sample and a longitudinal design, caution is required when interpreting the results, as the sample is not representative of the Spanish adolescent population, which limits the generalizability of the findings. (3) Despite addressing relevant research topics, Internet-related behaviors are dynamic and continuously evolving, which may affect the stability of constructs and contribute to the lack of consensus regarding measurement approaches and theoretical frameworks over time. (4) Although the cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) allows the examination of temporal associations between variables, this approach does not disentangle stable between-person differences from within-person changes. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as intraindividual processes or causal effects, but rather as prospective longitudinal associations. Future research should employ more advanced designs, including at least three waves, to enable the use of models such as the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model, which allows a more precise estimation of intra-individual effects. (5)Although no baseline differences were observed between retained and non-retained participants with usable T1 data, this should not be interpreted as evidence of random attrition. Some excluded cases could not be meaningfully compared, and therefore selective loss remains a potential source of bias.

Regardless of these considerations, several practical implications can be derived at the educational, health, and social levels. First, the observed temporal stability of the variables six months after the initial assessment suggests that earlier patterns of behavior are closely associated with subsequent functioning. From this perspective, early preventive approaches may be particularly relevant. In this regard, promoting constructive, healthy, and responsible Internet use appears essential, especially in terms of fostering a balance between online and offline activities. Accordingly, the implementation of comprehensive digital literacy programs and the prevention of Internet-related risks through holistic and multifaceted approaches is strongly recommended81. Particular attention should be given to cybervictimization and depression, as these variables showed stronger longitudinal associations within the model, highlighting their relevance as key targets for preventive efforts and intervention design. Future research could also expand the focus to include the different roles involved in cyberbullying (bully, observer, and bully-victim), thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of adolescent development. In addition, actions may be integrated within pediatric care frameworks and routine health check-ups to facilitate the identification of potential cases and support early detection of emerging problems. Similarly, these efforts could be aligned with broader school-based screening initiatives.

This longitudinal study highlights the complex relationships between cybervictimization, GPIU, depression, and anxiety. All variables demonstrated temporal stability, with each construct being significantly associated with its own levels six months later. Cybervictimization at baseline was longitudinally associated with subsequent levels of GPIU, suggesting that adverse online experiences may be linked to increased engagement in problematic Internet use as a potential coping response. Depression emerged as a central variable, being associated with subsequent levels of both cybervictimization and anxiety. This pattern may suggest that depressive symptomatology is linked to increased vulnerability to online victimization experiences and to the co-occurrence of anxious symptoms. Anxiety was also associated with subsequent levels of depression, supporting the well-established bidirectional relationship between these conditions. Notably, GPIU was not longitudinally associated with any of the other variables, which contrasts with some previous conceptualizations of its role as a risk factor. This finding may indicate that problematic Internet use is more closely linked to other underlying psychosocial variables rather than acting as an independent driver. Finally, interventions targeting depressive symptoms may be relevant in relation to cybervictimization and anxiety. Prevention programs may benefit from incorporating strategies that address the potential development of problematic Internet use patterns in the context of adverse online experiences.
