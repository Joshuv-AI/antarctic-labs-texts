Differentiation of Benign and Malignant BI-RADS 4–5 Breast Lesions Using Ultrasound Localization Microscopy–Derived Microvascular Biomarkers: A Prospective Study
Authors: Wei Wei, Wenwu Lu, Wang Zhou, Di Zhang, Xiangyi Xu, Ji Zhou, Yuanyuan Gao, Yayang Duan, Chaoxue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate qualitative and quantitative microvascular biomarkers derived from ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) for differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions, and to evaluate their added value in improving BI-RADS-based diagnosis.MethodsIn this prospective study, 295 patients with 301 breast lesions classified as BI-RADS category 4–5 on conventional ultrasound (US) underwent contrast-enhanced ultrasound and ULM prior to surgery or biopsy. Qualitative vascular biomarkers and quantitative parameters were derived from ULM images. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. BI-RADS categories were further re-evaluated by integrating ULM-derived biomarkers.ResultsULM qualitative vascular biomarkers classified microvascular morphology into five patterns: avascular, dot-strip, circular, chaotic, and radial. Benign lesions predominantly exhibited avascular, dot-strip, and circular patterns (5.6%, 23.4%, and 48.6%, respectively), whereas malignant lesions were mainly characterized by chaotic and radial patterns (56.7% and 28.4%, respectively; p < 0.001). The qualitative assessment achieved an AUC of 0.825 (95% CI: 0.780–0.871). All ULM quantitative biomarkers showed significant differences between benign and malignant lesions (all p < 0.05), among which maximum diameter demonstrated the highest diagnostic value (AUC = 0.898, 95% CI: 0.861–0.935). A combined quantitative biomarker (CQB) model further improved performance (AUC = 0.920). Integration of ULM qualitative and quantitative biomarkers with BI-RADS further improved diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.976; sensitivity 0.964; specificity 0.916) and may improve risk stratification beyond BI-RADS.ConclusionULM-derived qualitative and quantitative microvascular biomarkers provide complementary information beyond conventional ultrasound and may improve diagnostic performance and risk stratification in BI-RADS 4–5 breast lesions, but their role in safely reducing unnecessary biopsies requires further clinical validation.Keywords: ultrasound localization microscopy, breast lesions, contrast-enhanced ultrasound, breast imaging reporting and data system

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, underscoring the need for early detection and effective intervention.1 Angiogenesis, a key driver of tumor growth and metastasis, differs markedly between benign and malignant breast lesions: benign lesions typically show orderly, low-curvature microvasculature,2–4 whereas malignancies exhibit dysregulated, permeable, tortuous, and irregularly branched vessels driven by hypoxia and rapid proliferation.5,6 Invasive breast cancer (IBC) requires neovascularization for progression; increased tumor angiogenesis—commonly quantified as microvessel density (MVD)—is associated with aggressive features and, in many studies, poorer outcomes.7,8 Robust quantification of microvascular architecture is therefore clinically relevant for differentiating benign from malignant lesions and for optimizing treatment strategies.

Conventional ultrasound (US) is the first-line modality for breast lesions but has suboptimal performance in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions due to limited depiction of intratumoral microvasculature.9,10 In particular, many BI-RADS 4 lesions proceed to biopsy despite ultimately proving benign, highlighting the need for imaging approaches that improve specificity while preserving sensitivity. Recent advances in breast microvascular imaging, including contrast-free techniques such as superb microvascular imaging (SMI) and high-definition micro-flow imaging (HD-MFI), as well as contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), have improved the visualization of low-flow signals and lesion perfusion beyond that achievable with conventional color Doppler flow imaging.7,11–13 However, these techniques remain constrained by the diffraction limit and by their display mechanisms, which restrict precise characterization of vessel caliber, branching topology, and microvascular flow dynamics at the capillary level.14,15

Ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) is a super-resolution imaging technique based on CEUS that localizes and tracks individual contrast microbubbles to reconstruct microvascular networks beyond the diffraction limit.12,16,17 With spatial resolution on the order of tens of micrometers, ULM enables detailed visualization and quantitative analysis of microvascular architecture and hemodynamics, including vascular density, orientation, and blood-flow velocity.14,16–18 Previous studies have validated ULM in several organ systems, demonstrating its capability to visualize microvascular networks and quantify blood-flow dynamics with high spatial resolution.19–21 These findings suggest strong potential for clinical translation of ULM in vascular imaging.

However, the application of ULM in breast imaging remains relatively limited. Preliminary studies indicate that ULM-derived parameters, including microvascular density, vessel diameter, and flow velocity, may contribute to differentiating benign from malignant breast lesions.18,22,23 Nevertheless, several important questions remain unresolved. In particular, a systematic qualitative classification of ULM microvascular patterns has not been fully established, and the combined diagnostic value of qualitative and quantitative microvascular biomarkers has not been comprehensively evaluated. Moreover, the potential role of ULM-derived biomarkers in refining BI-RADS-based diagnostic assessment and reducing unnecessary biopsies remains unclear.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective exploratory study to investigate the clinical value of ULM-derived microvascular biomarkers for differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions. Specifically, our objectives were to: (1) systematically characterize qualitative microvascular patterns on ULM; (2) evaluate the diagnostic performance of qualitative and quantitative ULM biomarkers across lesion-size strata; (3) assess the incremental value of ULM in assisting experienced radiologists in BI-RADS-based diagnosis; and (4) preliminarily explore the association between ULM quantitative metrics and immunohistochemical subtypes.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This prospective, single-center study was conducted from September 2024 to August 2025. Consecutively enrolled patients with BI-RADS category 4 or 5 breast lesions underwent breast tumor surgery or core needle biopsy at our hospital. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. PJ2024-02-12). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Figure 1 depicts the screening workflow and the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Sample size estimation is provided in Figure S1. A radiologist with 12 years of experience performed the BI-RADS assessment. Investigators responsible for ULM analysis were blinded to BI-RADS assessments and pathological results. ULM findings were not available to the treating clinicians and were not used for patient enrollment or clinical decision-making. In total, 295 patients (301 lesions) were included; demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Ultrasound and CEUS Examinations

Conventional B-mode and CDFI were performed on an Ultimus 9E system (Vinno Technology Co., Ltd.) with a U5–15L linear-array transducer. An experienced breast radiologist (with 12 years of experience in breast US diagnosis) independently performed examinations with patients in the supine position. Based on CDFI findings, the imaging plane with the highest vascular signal was selected for subsequent CEUS acquisition. Minimal transducer pressure was applied to avoid tissue compression and vascular collapse, and patients were instructed to remain still and breathe steadily.

For CEUS imaging, SonoVue (Bracco S.p.A.) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A 2.4-mL bolus injection was administered via the left median cubital vein within 2 s, followed by a 5-mL saline flush. The system timer was started at the time of injection, and continuous cine imaging was recorded for 90 s. When microbubbles entered the lesion field of view, ULM acquisition was initiated while the patient briefly held her breath for approximately 14 s.

ULM Image Processing

ULM image reconstruction followed a standardized post-processing pipeline consisting of motion correction, tissue–microbubble signal separation, microbubble detection, sub-pixel localization, trajectory linking, and map reconstruction.15 First, motion correction was applied to compensate for minor probe- and respiration-related in-plane displacement.24 Second, tissue background and low-intensity noise were suppressed by amplitude- and persistence-based filtering to improve the signal-to-noise ratio of isolated microbubble echoes. Third, candidate microbubble signals were detected frame by frame, and only spatially isolated signals exceeding the predefined amplitude threshold were retained for localization to reduce overlap-related localization error. Fourth, individual microbubbles were localized at sub-pixel precision and linked across consecutive frames using the vendor-provided tracking algorithm under standardized settings, with trajectory continuity constrained by inter-frame displacement and signal consistency.25 Finally, accumulated trajectories were used to reconstruct ULM microvascular density, flow direction, velocity, and angle maps for subsequent qualitative and quantitative analysis. Standardized acquisition and processing settings were applied consistently across all cases, with no manual intervention or parameter tuning during post-processing. ULM image reconstruction and quantitative analysis were performed using a vendor-provided software package (ULM Quantitative Analysis Toolkit). The US parameters were as follows: element pitch 200 μm, center frequency 8.5 MHz, mechanical index 0.06–0.10, depth 3.5–4.0 cm, gain 60–80%, and dynamic range 80 dB.

Regions of Interest (ROI) Delineation

Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated along the lesion margin on the corresponding B-mode ultrasound image and co-registered to the ULM vascular map for quantitative analysis. ROI delineation was independently performed by two experienced US radiologists (Radiologist A and Radiologist B, each with over 8 years of experience in breast US diagnosis). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and when necessary, a senior radiologist (Radiologist C, with over 20 years of experience in breast imaging diagnosis) made the final decision.

Quantitative Biomarker Extraction

Quantitative biomarkers included the following categories:

Microvasculature density: vessel ratio (%), maximum vessel density (MaxDen), mean vessel density (MeanDen).

Vessel diameter: maximum diameter (MaxDiameter) and mean diameter (MeanDiameter) (see Figure S2).

Tortuosity: maximum curvature sum of angles metric (Max curvature SOAM), mean curvature sum of angles metric (Mean curvature SOAM), and complexity level (see Figure S3).

Flow velocity: maximum velocity (MaxVel) and mean velocity (MeanVel).

Composite indices: perfusion index (PI) and blood volume.

Operational definitions are provided in Table S1. Biomarkers with an ICC greater than 0.70 were retained for multivariable model construction; biomarkers with lower reliability were included only in exploratory univariable analyses and interpreted cautiously. All participating physicians were blinded to pathological results.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and Python (version 3.10.19), including relevant packages for statistical analysis and model development. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on distribution, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Differences between benign and malignant lesions were assessed using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Normality was evaluated prior to correlation analysis, and Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were applied as appropriate. Using histopathology from surgical specimens or core needle biopsy as the reference standard, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess diagnostic performance. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were presented descriptively for each model. Multivariable logistic regression was used to construct the combined quantitative biomarker (CQB) model after excluding highly collinear variables. ULM-derived biomarkers were further integrated with BI-RADS assessment to evaluate incremental diagnostic performance. Cases with missing data were excluded from the corresponding analyses. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of Benign and Malignant Breast Lesions Based on ULM Qualitative Biomarkers

Benign lesions were predominantly characterized by avascular, dot-strip, or circular patterns, whereas malignant lesions more frequently exhibited chaotic or radial patterns (p < 0.001; Table 3). Reliability was high with an ICC of 0.897 for the qualitative biomarkers. Among the benign lesions, 23 exhibited a chaotic distribution—adenosis (n = 7), fibroadenoma (n = 4), intraductal papilloma (n = 4), granulomatous mastitis (n = 6), and serous mastitis (n = 2). One additional benign lesion demonstrated a radial distribution (serous mastitis, n = 1). Figure 3 compares multimodal ULM and conventional US images of benign and malignant breast lesions.

Diagnostic Performance of ULM Qualitative and Quantitative Biomarkers Between Benign and Malignant Breast Lesions

Chaotic or radial vascular patterns were defined as positive indicators of malignancy. Across all lesion sizes, the AUC of qualitative biomarkers was 0.825 (95% CI: 0.780–0.871), with sensitivity of 0.851 and specificity of 0.776. The highest performance was observed in lesions <10 mm, with an AUC of 0.908, sensitivity of 0.889, and specificity of 0.882.

Among quantitative biomarkers, MaxDiameter showed the highest overall efficacy (AUC 0.898; 95% CI 0.861–0.935). In size-stratified analyses, AUC was greatest in lesions >20 mm (0.930; 95% CI 0.892–0.968), followed by < 10 mm (0.850; 95% CI 0.634–1.000). Other quantitative parameters showed moderate performance (AUC 0.600–0.703): blood volume, MaxVel, MeanDiameter, complexity level, MeanVel, PI, vessel ratio, Max curvature SOAM, and MeanDen; MaxDen and Mean curvature SOAM performed lower. Lesion Reclassification Based on ULM-Derived Qualitative and Quantitative Parameters

A total of 301 breast lesions were included. Initial BI-RADS categories (assigned by a breast US specialist with 12 years’ experience) were 4A (n = 120), 4B (n = 91), 4C (n = 61), and 5 (n = 29). Using BI-RADS 4B as the positive threshold, overall performance was AUC = 0.797 (95% CI 0.751–0.842). Size-stratified analysis showed lower discrimination for lesions < 10 mm (n = 26; AUC = 0.523), moderate performance for 10–20 mm lesions (n = 119; AUC = 0.736), and the best performance for lesions >20 mm (n = 156; AUC = 0.836). Assessments by the experienced radiologist showed stronger discrimination in larger lesions, with notably limited sensitivity for small lesions (<10 mm) (Figure S6).

After incorporating ULM qualitative biomarkers into BI-RADS assessment, the 301 lesions were reclassified as BI-RADS 3–5 (n= 66, 33, 66, 58, and 78, respectively). Overall diagnostic performance improved to an AUC of 0.905 (95% CI: 0.873–0.938), with sensitivity of 0.907 and specificity of 0.757. Stratified AUCs improved across all size groups: 0.882 (< 10 mm), 0.899 (10–20 mm), and 0.888 (> 20 mm). Notably, seven reclassified BI-RADS 3 lesions were malignant on pathology, indicating residual risk of malignancy within the low-risk category.

After excluding highly collinear variables (r > 0.8) and features with lower reliability (mean curvature SOAM), while retaining the maximum curvature SOAM as a more stable descriptor, the remaining ULM quantitative biomarkers were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. The final CQB model retained six variables: maximum diameter, mean diameter, maximum density, blood volume, maximum velocity, and maximum curvature SOAM. This model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance (AUC = 0.920; 95% CI: 0.887–0.953), significantly outperforming US BI-RADS (DeLong p < 0.001) and showing a higher AUC than the ULM qualitative approach. Size-stratified AUCs ranged from 0.837 to 0.964, with the >20 mm group performing best. Internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling. The apparent AUC of the final multivariable logistic regression model was 0.920, and the 10-fold cross-validated AUC was 0.913, indicating good discrimination with only minimal performance loss after cross-validation. The bootstrap distribution of AUC optimism was centered near zero, suggesting limited overfitting and acceptable model stability (Figure S7).

To further improve diagnostic stratification, a second reclassification was performed based on ULM morphological features and the predicted probabilities from the ULM CQB model. The 301 lesions were subsequently categorized into BI-RADS 3, 4A, 4B, 4C, and 5 (n = 85, 20, 58, 59, 79). Overall performance reached an AUC of 0.976 (95% CI: 0.962–0.991), with sensitivity of 0.964 and specificity of 0.916. High discriminative performance was consistently observed across size-based subgroups, with AUCs of 0.938 (< 10 mm), 0.969 (10–20 mm), and 0.981 (> 20 mm). Notably, two lesions classified as BI-RADS 3 after reclassification were pathologically confirmed as malignant. Discussion

In this study, ULM during microbubble perfusion was used to derive qualitative and quantitative microvascular biomarkers to differentiate malignant from benign breast lesions. Five vascular patterns—avascular, dot-strip, circular, chaotic, and radial—were defined as qualitative biomarkers. Overall, ULM qualitative assessment outperformed conventional US BI-RADS (AUC 0.825 vs 0.797) and was particularly effective for small lesions (<10 mm; AUC 0.908). Quantitatively, MaxDiameter, MeanDiameter, blood volume, and MaxVel discriminated benign from malignant lesions; notably, MaxDiameter alone exceeded US BI-RADS and ULM qualitative biomarkers (AUC 0.898 vs 0.797 and 0.825). When BI-RADS was integrated with both ULM qualitative biomarkers and the CQB model, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity improved across all lesion-size subgroups. These findings indicate that ULM provides complementary microcirculatory information beyond conventional US and may improve diagnostic accuracy when integrated with BI-RADS assessment, with potential implications for reducing unnecessary biopsies.

Previous studies have reported that although ULM improves visualization of breast microvasculature, the evidence base remains early, with small cohorts and mainly exploratory quantitative descriptions.10,18,22 In contrast, we systematically evaluated ULM qualitative biomarkers in a larger cohort spanning a broader spectrum of microvascular phenotypes. We defined five vascular patterns—avascular, dot-strip, circular, chaotic, and radial—and demonstrated strong diagnostic performance. Avascular, dot-strip, and circular patterns were associated with benign pathology, whereas chaotic and radial correlated with malignancy. Overall, morphology-based ULM assessment achieved an AUC of 0.825 (sensitivity 0.851; specificity 0.776). In the <10 mm subgroup, AUC increased to 0.908, addressing a key limitation of conventional US BI-RADS (AUC 0.523; sensitivity 0.222). These results indicate that ULM, by capturing microcirculatory structure and flow patterns, provides clinically meaningful information complementary to US, especially for early, small-volume lesions and distinguishing benign from malignant lesions.

An additional strength is the derivation of a comprehensive panel of ULM-based quantitative biomarkers, with systematic evaluation of 12 indicators including MaxDiameter, MeanDiameter, and vascular density. We defined vascular density as the MBs transit rate within the ROI (MBs counts per unit time), which scales with density values15,26 and reflects local vascular volume and perfusion, a key imaging correlate of tumor angiogenesis.27,28 Consistent with prior reports,18,22,23 malignant lesions exhibited significantly higher vascular density, MaxDen, and MeanDen than benign lesions (all p < 0.05), supporting the greater vascularization of malignancies;29 higher density is also associated with invasion, metastatic risk, and worse prognosis.27 Building on this, we proposed two derived biomarkers—perfusion index and blood volume—and evaluated their diagnostic performance. Overall AUCs were 0.703 and 0.649, respectively; both performed best in small tumors (<10 mm; AUC 0.771 vs 0.814) and declined with increasing size (10–20 mm: 0.658 vs 0.641; >20 mm: 0.619 vs 0.587), suggesting higher information gain for early, small lesions. MaxDiameter and MeanDiameter were also significantly higher in malignant than benign lesions (both p < 0.001), consistent with prior findings.7,18

Vascular tortuosity, quantified by angular deflection, reflects growth factor, mediated vascular wall remodeling (endothelial proliferation, basement membrane reorganization, pericyte and smooth muscle cell loss)30 and is typically higher in malignancy.7,18,31,32 Consistent with Ternifi et al7 and Li et al,18 maximum tortuosity was significantly greater in malignant lesions; prior studies often found no difference in mean tortuosity. Mean tortuosity was paradoxically higher in benign lesions, which may reflect the composition of the benign BI-RADS 4–5 subgroup, including biologically active lesions that increased average tortuosity without exhibiting extreme vascular distortion. ROC analysis supports this: maximum tortuosity outperformed mean tortuosity (AUC 0.631 vs 0.395), indicating that “extreme tortuosity” better captures malignancy-related architectural abnormalities, whereas means are susceptible to dilution.

Complexity level is a quantitative biomarker summarizing geometric irregularity, spatial filling, and network complexity within the ROI. Complexity was significantly higher in malignant lesions overall and across size strata (< 10 mm, 10–20 mm, > 20 mm), in line with prior work.7 Assessing complexity yields diagnostic/prognostic insights and informs mechanisms of tumor angiogenesis.33,34 In contexts where both benign and malignant lesions are highly vascularized, tortuosity and complexity, together with MaxDiameter–capture complementary topological and geometric deviations with added discriminatory value.

Microvascular blood flow velocity is another key ULM biomarker. Unlike conventional US – which is relatively insensitive to low-velocity flow12,35 – ULM can detect low-flow, low-speed components and generates velocity maps depicting magnitude and spatial distribution, thereby better characterizing tumor hemodynamics. Prior studies link angiogenesis-related genes to velocity phenotypes in BC: VEGF165, CTGF, and Gli2 correlate positively with MaxVel, whereas PTEN and MFN2 correlate negatively,36 suggesting velocity partly reflects angiogenic activity and metabolic/signaling pathways. In our cohort, both MaxVel and MeanVel differed significantly between malignant and benign lesions (p < 0.05), consistent with previous reports,7,18 likely reflecting higher density, greater arterialization of feeders, and more arteriovenous shunts in malignant tumors. MeanVel appears more sensitive to global microcirculatory flux, whereas MaxVel highlights extreme channels and high-speed shunts, indicating complementary roles.

Beyond differentiating benign from malignant lesions, ULM-derived microvascular biomarkers may also reflect biological heterogeneity within malignant tumors. Within the malignant cohort, molecular subtypes showed distinct ULM phenotypes: ER/PR-positive tumors exhibited low-perfusion, low-velocity, and low-complexity profiles, whereas HER2-positive tumors showed high-perfusion, high-velocity, and high-complexity profiles, suggesting a correlation between molecular subtype and microvascular remodeling. These observations align with prior studies—higher ER/PR expression associates with lower microvessel density37,38 whereas HER2 amplification associates with higher perfusion, faster flow, and greater complexity, consistent with poorer prognosis.39 Prior studies have suggested that HER2-related signaling may be associated with increased angiogenic activity, partly through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-related pathways, which could contribute to vascular remodeling.40,41 VEGF-A may induce larger “parent vessels” around tumors,42,43 aligning with the “chaotic” and “radial” patterns we observed. More broadly, tumor vascular phenotypes are influenced by complex oncogenic and microenvironmental mechanisms that regulate angiogenic signaling and vascular remodeling, providing a potential biological basis for the vascular patterns observed with ULM imaging.44 Tumor grade analyses were concordant: complexity level, PI, MaxDiameter, and blood volume were significantly higher in high-grade than in low-grade malignancies (all p < 0.05), consistent with denser, more irregular angiogenesis and greater metastatic risk.45 Collectively, these findings suggest that ULM-derived biomarkers may reflect microvascular phenotypes associated with molecular and histologic aggressiveness. However, these observations should be interpreted as imaging correlates rather than direct evidence of specific causal mechanisms, and they warrant further prospective validation and mechanistic investigation. Previous studies have shown that gene expression and the tumor microenvironment are associated with immune infiltration, angiogenesis, and tumor progression, which may influence microvascular features on imaging.46 These observations should be interpreted cautiously because subgroup sizes were limited and no formal correction for multiple testing was applied.

In addition, the integrated ULM-based reclassification showed potential clinical value for improving diagnostic stratification. Its role in safely reducing unnecessary biopsies requires further clinical validation. In our cohort, 85 lesions were reclassified as BI-RADS 3 after integrating ULM morphology and quantitative biomarkers. Among these, 83 were pathologically benign, suggesting that up to 27.6% (83/301) of lesions in this cohort might have avoided immediate biopsy and instead undergone imaging follow-up. Although two malignant lesions were reclassified into this lower-risk category, the integrated model maintained high overall diagnostic performance, supporting its potential role in risk stratification. However, its use for safely reducing unnecessary biopsies requires further prospective validation.

Overall, we systematically evaluated ULM qualitative and quantitative biomarkers in BI-RADS category 4 and 5 breast lesions. ULM qualitative classification, MaxDiameter, and the CQB model outperformed conventional ultrasound and maintained stable diagnostic performance across lesion size strata, consistent with findings from previous breast and cross-organ studies. These findings support the potential value of ULM-derived microvascular phenotypes as complementary imaging markers for improving the assessment of BI-RADS category 4 and 5 lesions, particularly in small breast masses.

This study has several limitations. First, analyses relied on 2D B-mode US with CDFI-guided plane selection, which cannot capture the full three-dimensional vascular architecture and may lead to imperfect estimation of microvascular morphology. Second, the qualitative taxonomy of ULM microvascular patterns was developed at a single center; therefore, its generalizability requires validation in multicenter studies with larger and more diverse cohorts. Third, case selection was restricted to BI-RADS categories 4 and 5, which may introduce spectrum bias and limit generalizability compared with broader screening populations. Because the study population consisted of already suspicious lesions, the apparent diagnostic performance metrics (including AUC and sensitivity) may be higher than those expected in a general screening or diagnostic population. Fourth, only one experienced reader (12 years of breast imaging experience) performed the BI-RADS assessment; future studies including multiple readers with different levels of experience are warranted to evaluate inter-reader variability and improve real-world applicability. Fifth, although internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling, no external validation cohort was available; therefore, the generalizability and robustness of the integrated diagnostic model still require confirmation in independent multicenter datasets. In addition, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied in the univariable between-group analyses, which may have increased the risk of type I error. Furthermore, measurement protocols for ULM-derived quantitative parameters are not yet fully standardized across studies, which may affect the reproducibility and comparability of quantitative metrics. Finally, although ULM shows promising diagnostic potential, the technique remains technically demanding and its integration into routine clinical workflow requires further investigation. Because ULM generates high-dimensional spatial representations of tumor microvasculature, future studies may benefit from data-driven analytical approaches, including artificial intelligence–based methods, to capture complex vascular patterns and spatial heterogeneity that may not be fully represented by handcrafted biomarkers alone.

Conclusion

ULM shows promising potential for differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions and may complement conventional ultrasound in diagnostic assessment. The qualitative and quantitative microvascular biomarkers derived from ULM provide additional information beyond conventional ultrasound and may improve BI-RADS-based assessment when integrated with BI-RADS evaluation. By enabling detailed visualization of tumor microvasculature through microbubble-based imaging, ULM may offer a novel approach for characterizing breast lesions. However, further multicenter studies and external validation are required to confirm the robustness, generalizability, and potential clinical utility of this technique.
