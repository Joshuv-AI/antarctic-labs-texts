Abstract
. We study differentiability conditions on a complex measure ν \nu at a point x 0 ∈ ℝ d x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d} , in relation with the boundary convergence at that point of the Poisson-type integral ℙ t ​ ν = e − t ​ L ​ ν {\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu=e^{-t\sqrt{L}}\nu , where L = − Δ + | x | 2 L=-{\Delta}+|x|^{2} is the Hermite operator. In particular, we show that x 0 x_{0} is a Lebesgue point for ν \nu iff a slightly stronger notion than non-tangential convergence holds for ℙ t ​ ν {\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu at x 0 x_{0} . We also show non-tangential convergence when x 0 x_{0} is a σ \sigma -point of ν \nu , a weaker notion than Lebesgue point, which for d = 1 d=1 coincides with the classical Fatou condition.

1.
Introduction

Let
\nu
be a locally finite complex measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, meaning that
\nu=\nu_{1}+i\nu_{2}
and
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{2}
are signed
Borel measures which are finite in compact sets.

We shall use the notion of Lebesgue point for
\nu
as defined by Saeki in
[
14
]
.
Namely, we say that
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a Lebesgue point of
\nu
, denoted
x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
,
if there exists
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

(1.1)

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\nu-\ell\,dy|\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}=0.

Here
dy(E)=|E|
denotes the standard Lebesgue measure of a set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
B_{r}(x_{0})=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\,\,\,\colon\,\,\,}|x-x_{0}|<r\}
.
If we write
\nu
in terms of the Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym decomposition, that is

(1.2)

\nu=f\,dy+{\lambda},\quad\mbox{with ${\lambda}\perp dy$}

(see e.g.
[
6
, Thm 3.12]
), then using the property that
|\mu+{\lambda}|=|\mu|+|{\lambda}|
when
\mu\perp{\lambda}
,
we see that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
if and only if for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
it holds

(1.3)

\lim_{r\to 0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|f(y)-\ell|\,dy=0{\quad\mbox{and}\quad}\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|{\lambda}|\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}=0.

In particular, if
\nu=fdx
, this is the usual notion of Lebesgue point for the function
f
, while
for a general
\nu
as in (
1.2
) we have

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{f}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}.

Observe also that, if
x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
, we can express the value of
\ell
in (
1.1
) without appealing to the
Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym decomposition,
by letting

(1.4)

\ell=D\nu(x_{0}):=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\nu\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}.

Indeed, this identity follows from the elementary bound

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nu\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}-\ell\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(\nu-\ell\,dy)\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}\right|\leq\frac{|\nu-\ell\,dy|\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}\longrightarrow 0.

The quantity
D\nu(x_{0})
in (
1.4
) is sometimes called the
symmetric derivative
of the measure
\nu
at
x_{0}
;
see e.g.
[
13
, Def 7.2]
.

Consider now the Hermite operator

L=-{\Delta}+|x|^{2},\quad\mbox{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{d}$},

and its associated Poisson semigroup

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}=e^{-t\sqrt{L}}=\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\tau}}\,e^{-\tau L}\,\,\frac{d\tau}{\tau^{3/2}};

see e.g.
[
17
, Ch 2.2]
. This semigroup (and its close relative involving the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator
-{\Delta}+2x\cdot\nabla
)
have been widely studied in Harmonic Analysis; see e.g.
[
11
,
19
,
18
,
9
,
20
]
.
We wish to find mild differentiability conditions on a measure
\nu
at an individual point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

so that the Poisson integrals

(1.5)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\,d\nu(y),\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

have a non-tangential limit when
(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})
.

When
L
is the Hermite operator, the kernel
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)
can be obtained explicitly, see (
2.1
) below for a precise expression,
and its growth (for fixed
t
and
x
) is well determined by the function

(1.6)

\Phi(y):=\frac{e^{-|y|^{2}/2}}{(1+|y|)^{\frac{d}{2}}\,[\log(e+|y|)]^{\frac{3}{2}}},\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Namely, it was shown in
[
8
, Lemma 4.1]
that
for each
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
there exist
c_{j}(t,x)>0
,
j=1,2
, such that

(1.7)

c_{1}(t,x)\,\Phi(y)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\leq\,c_{2}(t,x)\,\Phi(y),\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In particular, let
\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
denote the set of (locally finite) complex measures
\nu
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Phi(y)\,d|\nu|(y)<\infty,

then it follows from (
1.7
) that, if
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
, the function
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)
in (
1.5
) is well-defined for all
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Moreover,
u(t,x)={\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)
is smooth in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}=(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

and satisfies the PDE

u_{tt}=-{\Delta}_{x}u+|x|^{2}u,

see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Our first result in this paper investigates the relation between the Lebesgue point condition,

x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
, and the existence of the non-tangential limit

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x),

for every
{\alpha}>0
. We shall prove the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
and
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then, the following assertions are equivalent

(i)

x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
;

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\big(|\nu-\ell\,dy|\big)(x_{0})=0
, for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
;

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\big(|\nu-\ell\,dy|\big)(x)=0
, for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
and some (all)
{\alpha}>0
.

Morever, if these assertions hold we can take
\ell=D\nu(x_{0})
, and for every
{\alpha}>0
it also holds

(1.8)

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=D\nu(x_{0}).

As a second result, we give a weaker condition than Lebesgue point which still ensures non-tangential convergence.
This is the notion of
\sigma
-point introduced by V. Shapiro in
[
16
]
; see also
[
15
]
.
We say that
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a
\sigma
-point of a (locally finite) complex measure
\nu
, denoted
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\nu}
, if
there exists
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

(1.9)

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|+r\to 0}\frac{\big|(\nu-\ell\,dy)(B_{r}(x))\big|}{(|x-x_{0}|+r)^{d}}=0.

Note that, if
x_{0}
is a
\sigma
-point, then
\nu
has a symmetric derivative at
x_{0}
, and
we can take
\ell=D\nu(x_{0})
in (
1.9
).
This follows by just restricting the above limit to
x=x_{0}
.
Also, since
B_{r}(x)\subset B_{r+|x-x_{0}|}(x_{0})
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{\nu},

and the inclusion can be strict in view of the examples in
[
16
, §3]
.
Finally, when
d=1
there is a simple characterization: write
\nu
as a Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure
\nu=dm_{F}
, with

\nu\big((a,b]\big)=F(b)-F(a);

see e.g.
[
6
, Thm 1.16]
. Then
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\nu}
iff
F
is differentiable at
x_{0}
, in which case

F^{\prime}(x_{0})=\ell=D\nu(x_{0}).

The proof is elementary; see also
[
15
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Our second result has the following statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
and
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a
\sigma
-point of
\nu
. Let
\ell=D\nu(x_{0})
.

(i) If
d\in\{1,2,3\}
then

(1.10)

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=\ell,\quad\forall\,{\alpha}>0.

(ii) If
d\geq 4
then (
1.10
) holds if it is additionally assumed that

(1.11)

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\nu-\ell\,dy|(B_{r}(x_{0}))}{r^{d-3}}=0.

As a corollary, when
d=1
we obtain the non-tangential convergence under the classical Fatou condition, see
[
2
]
or

[
21
, Thm III.7.9.ii]
. This result seems to be new for Poisson-Hermite integrals.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
d=1
and
\nu=m_{F}
be a Lebesgue-Stieljes measure in
\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
. If
F
is differentiable in
x_{0}
then

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=F^{\prime}(x_{0}),\quad\forall\,{\alpha}>0.

Finally, we remark that, when
x_{0}=0
, one can slightly relax the condition (
1.11
) (and even remove it, if
d=4
), due to
the special symmetry of the resulting kernel
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(0,y)
; see a precise statement in Theorem
3.3
below.

We comment on previous results on these matters for the Poisson-Hermite semigroup. When the initial datum is a function, that is
\nu=f\,dy
,
generic results on a.e. convergence of
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}f
go back to
[
11
]
, see also
[
18
,
8
]
. These references contain the main kernel estimates, but do not consider
convergence at individual Lebesgue points of
f
. A statement for these was recently given in
[
7
]
(for the related Laguerre operator),
and requires an argument that we elaborate further here in the new Lemma
2.4
. Concerning measures
\nu
as initial data, we are only aware of
the a.e. results for
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu
in
[
5
, Theorem 1.3]
, which again do not consider the behavior at individual points.

Regarding Theorem
1.2
, no results involving weaker notions than Lebesgue points seem to appear in the literature
for Poisson-Hermite integrals, even when
\nu
equals
f
or
d=1
.
However, there is an extensive literature in the classical setting of harmonic functions
regarding optimal conditions for non-tangential convergence; see e.g.
[
10
,
12
,
1
]
, or the more recent

[
14
,
16
,
15
]
and references therein.
The latter papers consider a general setting of approximations of the identity,
but always associated with convolution kernels with a dilation structure, that is,

K_{t}(x,y)=t^{-d}\phi((x-y)/t),\quad t>0,

and most often with a radially decreasing
\phi
.

One novelty here regards the fact that one must consider kernels
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)
without this convolution structure,
so different arguments are necessary to carry out the proofs. Our arguments will be based on
a suitable kernel decomposition into radial and non-radial parts, and very precise estimates on the kernel
which are optimal for dimensions
d\leq 3
, but require the additional condition (
1.11
) when
d\geq 4
.
We do not know whether Theorem
1.2
may hold without this hypothesis (if
x_{0}\not=0
), although
it seems unlikely in view of examples in
[
4
]
, where a similar condition in relation with the
normal

convergence of
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x_{0})
appears for dimensions
d\geq 4
, and cannot be removed in that setting.
We nevertheless remark that (
1.11
) is quite mild (certainly weaker than the Lebesgue point condition),
due to the factor
r^{d-3}
in the denominator.

The paper is structured as follows. In section
2
we compile the notation and the required kernel estimates,
and present the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We establish in §
2.3
a general Lemma
2.4
, which may have
an independent interest. Finally, in section
3
we give a detailed proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

It is clear that (iii) implies (ii). We shall show in separate subsections the other implications.
Below we use the notation

p_{t}(z)=\frac{t}{(t^{2}+|z|^{2})^{\frac{d+1}{2}}},

which except for a multiplicative constant is the standard Poisson kernel.
Recall also that the Poisson kernel
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)
associated with the Hermite operator
can be explicitly given by

(2.1)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)=\,c_{d}\,t\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\ell(s)}}\,(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{s}+s|x+y|^{2})}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\big(\ell(s)\big)^{3/2}}\,ds,

where
\ell(s)=2\ln\frac{1+s}{1-s}
and
c_{d}>0
; see e.g.
[
8
, (4.1)]
. Note that
\ell(s)\approx s
when
s\in(0,1/2)
, a fact that we shall use often below.

2.1.
Proof of
(ii)\Longrightarrow(i)

We have the following estimate from below for the kernel
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
R\geq 1
, there exists a constant
{\delta}_{R}>0
such that

(2.2)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\,\geq\,{\delta}_{R}\;p_{t}(x-y),\quad\mbox{when $|x|,|y|,t\leq R$}.

Proof.

If we restrict the range of integration in (
2.1
) to
s\in(0,1/2)
, so that

\ell(s)\approx s
, after disregarding inessential terms we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle t\,\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\frac{c\,t^{2}}{4s}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{s}+s|x+y|^{2})}}{s^{\frac{d+3}{2}}}\,ds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}\,t\,\int_{0}^{1/4}\frac{e^{-\frac{c\,t^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{s^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\,\frac{ds}{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{R}\,t}{(ct^{2}+|x-y|^{2})^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\,\int_{ct^{2}+|x-y|^{2}}^{\infty}e^{-u}u^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\frac{du}{u},

performing in the last step the change of variables
u=(c\,t^{2}+|x-y|^{2})/(4s)
.
Using again the assumption
|x|,|y|,t\leq R
, the last integral is bounded below by

\int_{(4+c)R^{2}}^{\infty}e^{-u}u^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\frac{du}{u}=c^{\prime}_{R}.

Combining these expressions one obtains (
2.2
).
∎

Going back to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, assume that (ii) holds, and denote
|\nu-\ell dx|=\mu
.
Pick
R\geq|x_{0}|+1
and
t\leq 1
. Then
the previous lemma gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\big(\mu\big)(x_{0})

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle{\delta}_{R}\,\int_{|y-x_{0}|\leq t}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y).

Since
p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\approx t^{-d}
when
|x_{0}-y|\leq t
, this implies

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\big(\mu\big)(x_{0})\,\gtrsim\,\frac{\mu\big(B_{t}(x_{0})\big)}{|B_{t}(x_{0})|}.

By (ii), the left-hand side goes to 0 as
t\to 0
, hence so does the right hand side.
Since
\mu=|\nu-\ell\,dx|
, this implies that
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
\nu
(and therefore, also that
\ell=D\nu(x_{0})
).

2.2.
Proof of
(i)\Longrightarrow(iii)

We shall use the following upper bound for
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)
, which can be found in
[
8
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a constant
{\gamma}\geq 2
and a continuous function
C(x)
such that

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\leq C(x)\,\Big[p_{t}(x-y){\mathbbm{1}}_{|y|\leq\gamma\max\{|x|,1\}}\,+\,t\,\Phi(y)\Big],

for all
t>0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

We also quote the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
{\alpha}>0
. Then, there exists
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

(2.3)

\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}}\,\big(|x_{0}-y|+t\big)\leq|x-y|+t\leq C_{\alpha}\,\big(|x_{0}-y|+t\big),

whenever
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

The proof is elementary using the condition
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and the triangle inequality.
Indeed, on the one hand

|x-y|+t\leq|x-x_{0}|+|x_{0}-y|+t\leq|x_{0}-y|+({\alpha}+1)t,

which implies the upper bound in (
2.3
) with
C_{\alpha}={\alpha}+1
.
The lower bound follows from the upper one after interchanging the roles of
x
and
x_{0}
.
∎

We turn to the proof of (iii) in Theorem
1.1
. Since we are interested in non-tangential limits at
x_{0}
, we fix
{\alpha}>0
and
consider only points
(t,x)
such that
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t\leq 1
. For such points, Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
imply that

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\lesssim C_{x_{0}}\,\Big[p_{t}(x_{0}-y){\mathbbm{1}}_{|y|\leq\gamma(|x_{0}|+2)}\,+\,t\,\Phi(y)\Big],\quad\forall\,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

for some constant
C_{x_{0}}>0
(which we could take
\max_{|x|\leq|x_{0}|+1}C(x)
).
For simplicity, we write

K=\big\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\,\,\,\colon\,\,\,}|y|\leq{\gamma}(|x_{0}|+2)\big\},

and as before we denote
\mu=|\nu-\ell dx|
. The previous inequalities then imply

(2.4)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mu(x)\,\lesssim\,C_{x_{0}}\,\Big[\int_{K}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y)\,+\,t\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Phi(y)\,d\mu(y)\Big].

The assumption that
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
implies that the last summand is
O(t)
as
t\to 0
, so we must only
estimate the first term

\int_{K}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y).

Here one could use the known results about the standard Poisson kernel to conclude that

(2.5)

\lim_{t\to 0}\int_{K}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y)=0,

under the assumption
x_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
. We briefly sketch the argument for completeness.
Given
{\varepsilon}>0
, we choose
{\delta}>0
such that

(2.6)

\frac{\mu\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)}{r^{d}}<{\varepsilon},\quad r\in(0,2{\delta}].

When
|y-x_{0}|\geq{\delta}
we have
p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\leq t/{\delta}^{d+1}
, and hence

(2.7)

\int_{y\in K\setminus B_{\delta}(x_{0})}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y)\leq\,\frac{t\,\mu(K)}{{\delta}^{d+1}}<{\varepsilon},

if
t<t_{0}:=\min\{{\varepsilon}\,{\delta}^{d+1}/\mu(K),{\delta}\}
. On the other hand, given any such
t
, we can find an integer
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

{\delta}<2^{J}t\leq 2{\delta}.

So letting
S_{j}:=B_{2^{j}t}(x_{0})\setminus B_{2^{j-1}t}(x_{0})
and using that
p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\lesssim t^{-d}2^{-j(d+1)}
in
S_{j}

we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{0})}p_{t}(x_{0}-y)\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{B_{t}(x_{0})}t^{-d}\,d\mu(y)+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{S_{j}}(2^{j}t)^{-d}2^{-j}\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{t}(x_{0}))}{t^{d}}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}2^{-j}\frac{\mu(B_{2^{j}t}(x_{0}))}{(2^{j}t)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\varepsilon},

using (
2.6
) in the last step. This shows (
2.5
).

2.3.
Last assertion in Theorem
1.1

It remains to prove that, under the assumption (iii), it also holds

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=\ell.

This will be a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Then the function

E(t,x):=\,\big({\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\big)-{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(\nu-\ell\,dy)(x)

satisfies

\lim_{(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}E(t,x)=0,\quad\forall\,x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Indeed, assuming the lemma and using (iii) we have

\big|{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\big|\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{t}|\nu-\ell\,dy|(x)+|E(t,x)|\longrightarrow 0,

when
|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0
.

We now prove Lemma
2.4
. We remark that this is not completely straightforward since for the Hermite operator we do not have

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}({\mathbbm{1}})={\mathbbm{1}}
(since
{\lambda}=0
is not an eigenvalue of
L
).
We shall use instead an argument, borrowed from
[
3
, §3.2]
(see also
[
7
, §6]
), which can be adapted to more general
positive self-adjoint operators
L
.
The only tool is the existence of a
regular positive eigenvector
of
L
, that is
\psi\in\mathop{\rm Dom}(L)
such that

(a)

\psi\in C^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

(b)

\psi(x)>0
,
\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(c)

L(\psi)={\lambda}\psi
, for some
{\lambda}\geq 0
.

When
L=-{\Delta}+|x|^{2}
, it is elementary to check that

(2.8)

\psi(x)=e^{-|x|^{2}/2}

satisfies these properties with
{\lambda}=d
.

P
ROOF
of Lemma
2.4
:

Let
\psi(x)
be as in (
2.8
). Since
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}=e^{-t\sqrt{L}}
, we also have

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\psi=e^{-t\sqrt{\lambda}}\psi.

Then

(2.9)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\,=\,\big({\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\,e^{-t\sqrt{{\lambda}}}\big)\,+\,\big(e^{-t\sqrt{{\lambda}}}-1\big)\,\ell.

The last summand goes to 0 as
t\to 0
, so we look at the first summand.
Since
\psi(x_{0})>0
, we may as well consider

A(t,x):=\psi(x_{0})\,\big({\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\,e^{-t\sqrt{{\lambda}}}\big),

which using that
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\psi=e^{-t\sqrt{{\lambda}}}\psi
we can write as

\displaystyle A(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big[\psi(x_{0})\,{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\,{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\psi(x)\big]\,+\,\ell\,\big[{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\psi(x)-{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\psi(x_{0})\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{1}(t,x)+A_{2}(t,x).

Now,

|A_{2}(t,x)|=|\ell|\,e^{-t\sqrt{{\lambda}}}|\psi(x)-\psi(x_{0})|\longrightarrow 0,\quad\mbox{if $|x-x_{0}|\to 0$},

by the continuity of
\psi
. Finally,

\displaystyle A_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(x_{0})\,{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(\nu-\ell\,dy)(x)\,-\,\ell\,{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\big(\psi-\psi(x_{0})\big)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{1,1}(t,x)+A_{1,2}(t,x).

Since the function
g=\psi-\psi(x_{0})
is continuous everywhere (and vanishes at
x_{0}
), using Lemma
2.2
and standard results
on approximations of the identity one can show that

|A_{1,2}(t,x)|\leq|\ell|\,{\mathbb{P}}_{t}|g|(x)\,\lesssim\,\big(p_{t}*|g|\big)(x)+t\int|g|\Phi\longrightarrow 0,

as
|x-x_{0}|+t\to 0
. Thus,

A(t,x)=A_{1,1}(t,x)+O(1).

Dividing this expression by
\psi(x_{0})
, and going back to (
2.9
),
one obtains

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)-\ell\,={\mathbb{P}}_{t}(\nu-\ell\,dy)(x)+O(1).

This proves Lemma
2.4
, and hence it concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

\Box

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

It will suffice to consider the case when
\ell=0
; otherwise, one would apply the reasoning to the measure
\mu=\nu-\ell\,dy

together with Lemma
2.4
.
We fix
{\alpha}>0
and must show that

(3.1)

\lim_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=0.

By the condition
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\nu}
(with
\ell=0
), for any fixed
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists
{\delta}\in(0,1)
such that

(3.2)

\big|\nu\big(B_{r}(x)\big)\big|\leq{\varepsilon}\,(|x-x_{0}|+r)^{d},\quad\mbox{whenever}\;\;|x-x_{0}|+r<2{\delta}.

If the dimension
d\geq 4
, we also assume that
{\delta}
is chosen such that

(3.3)

|\nu|\big(B_{r}(x_{0})\big)\,<\,{\varepsilon}\;r^{d-3},\quad\mbox{when}\;\;r<2{\delta},

in view of the hypothesis in (
1.11
).

The first part of the proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1.1
:
we consider only points
(t,x)
such that
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t\leq 1
; in particular
|x|\leq|x_{0}|+1
.
By Lemma
2.2
we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\lesssim C_{x_{0}}\,\Big[p_{t}(x-y){\mathbbm{1}}_{|y|\leq\gamma(|x_{0}|+2)}\,+\,t\,\Phi(y)\Big],\quad\forall\,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

for some constant
C_{x_{0}}>0
.
In the region
|x-y|\geq{\delta}
, we have
p_{t}(x-y)\lesssim t/{\delta}^{d+1}
, so the above bound gives

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|\geq{\delta}}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\,d\,|\nu|(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{x_{0}}\,\big({\delta}^{-d-1}+\int\Phi\,d|\nu|\big)\cdot t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(x_{0},{\delta})\cdot t\,<\,{\varepsilon},

provided we assume
t<t_{0}:=\min\{{\varepsilon}/c(x_{0},{\delta}),{\delta}\}
. So, in the remainder of the proof we shall aim to show

\Big|\int_{|x-y|<{\delta}}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)\,d\,\nu(y)\Big|=\mathcal{O}({\varepsilon}),

when
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and
t
is sufficiently small. Since
|x|\leq|x_{0}|+1
we have

B_{\delta}(x)\subset\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\,\,\,\colon\,\,\,}|y|\leq|x_{0}|+2\}=:K,

so in the sequel we may assume
\nu
to be supported in the compact set
K
.

We next remove another inessential term: we define
the “local” part of the Poisson kernel by

(3.5)

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{0}(x,y):=\,c_{d}\,t\,\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\ell(s)}}\,(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{s}+s|x+y|^{2})}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\big(\ell(s)\big)^{3/2}}\,ds.

Then we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{1}(x,y):={\mathbb{P}}_{t}(x,y)-{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{0}(x,y)\,\leq\,C\,t\,\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}ds=C^{\prime}\,t,

for some
C^{\prime}>0
(depending only on
d
), and therefore

(3.6)

\int_{K}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{1}(x,y)\,d|\nu|(y)\,\leq\,C^{\prime}\,|\nu|(K)\cdot t=C^{\prime\prime}\,t<{\varepsilon},

if
t<t_{1}=\min\{t_{0},{\varepsilon}/C^{\prime\prime}\}
. So it suffices to show that

A(t,x):=\int_{|x-y|<{\delta}}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t}(x,y)\,d\,\nu(y)=\mathcal{O}({\varepsilon})

when
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and
t
is sufficiently small. We fix
x
in what follows, and changing variables
y=x+h
, we rewrite the above expression as

A(t,x)=\int_{|h|<{\delta}}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}(h)\,d\,\nu_{x}(h),

where for simplicity we denote

{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}(h):={\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{0}(x,x+h){\quad\mbox{and}\quad}\nu_{x}(E):=\nu(x+E),\;\;E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The kernel now takes the form

{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}(h)=\,c_{d}\,t\,\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\ell(s)}}\,(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\big(\ell(s)\big)^{3/2}}\,e^{-\frac{|h|^{2}}{4s}}\,e^{-\frac{s|2x+h|^{2}}{4}}\,ds.

This is not a radial function in
h
, so we shall split it into a radial part and a remainder.
To do so, we write

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{s|2x+h|^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-s|x|^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{s|h|^{2}}{4}}\,e^{-s\,x\cdot h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-s|x|^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{s|h|^{2}}{4}}\,\big(1+R(s\,x\cdot h)\big),

where letting
u=s\,x\cdot h
, we have

(3.7)

|R(u)|=|e^{-u}-1|=\Big|\int_{0}^{u}e^{-v}\,dv\Big|\leq|u|\,e^{|u|}\leq c_{x_{0}}\,s\,|x|\,|h|,

since
s\in(0,1/2)
,
|h|\leq 1
and
|x|\leq|x_{0}|+1
. We now split

(3.8)

{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}(h)=\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)+\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h),

with the “radial part” of
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}
(in the variable
h
) given by

(3.9)

\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)=\,c_{d}\,t\,\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\ell(s)}}\,(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\big(\ell(s)\big)^{3/2}}\,e^{-\frac{|h|^{2}}{4}(s+\frac{1}{s})}\,e^{-s|x|^{2}}\,ds.

The kernel
\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)
has a similar expression with an additional (non-radial) factor
R(s\,x\cdot h)
inside the integral.
Disregarding inessential terms, we can estimate it by

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle t\,\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\frac{c\,t^{2}}{4s}}\,e^{-\frac{|h|^{2}}{4s}}\,s\,|x|\,|h|}{s^{\frac{d+3}{2}}}\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t\,|x|\,|h|}{(ct^{2}+|h|^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\int_{\frac{ct^{2}+|h|^{2}}{2}}^{\infty}e^{-v}\,v^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\,\tfrac{dv}{v},

where in the last step we have changed variables
v=(ct^{2}+|h|^{2})/(4s)
.
At this point we distinguish two cases, if
d>1
, the above integral is bounded by a finite constant, so we have

(3.11)

\big|\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)\big|\lesssim\,\frac{t\,|x|\,|h|}{(ct^{2}+|h|^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

If
d=1
, using that
t,|h|\ll 1
we have

(3.12)

\big|\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)\big|\lesssim\,t\,|x|\,|h|\,\log\tfrac{1}{ct^{2}+|h|^{2}}.

Note that in both cases the involved constants depend only on
x_{0}
(and the dimension
d
), but not on
x
or
{\delta}
.
Note further that

|x|\leq|x-x_{0}|+|x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t+|x_{0}|\leq 1+|x_{0}|,

so below we shall absorb the factor
|x|
in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) into the constants;
however, in the special case that
x_{0}=0
this factor is
\mathcal{O}(t)
and will lead to a slightly
better result; see Remark
3.2
below.

We will now show that

(3.13)

A_{1}(t,x):=\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)\,d\,\nu_{x}(h)=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\mbox{as $|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\searrow 0$.}

The argument is different depending on the dimension.

Case
d=1
. In this case we simply have

\displaystyle|A_{1}(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|h|<{\delta}}|\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)|\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle t\,\log(1/t)\,\int_{|h|<{\delta}}|h|\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t\,\log(1/t)\;|\nu|(K),

which vanishes as
t\searrow 0
.

For higher dimensions
d>1
, the bound in (
3.11
) gives

(3.14)

\displaystyle|A_{1}(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|h|<{\delta}}|\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)|\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle t\,\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\frac{|h|}{(t+|h|)^{d-1}}\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t\,\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\frac{d|\nu_{x}|(h)}{(t+|h|)^{d-2}}.

We again distinguish cases.

Case
d=2
. In this case, (
3.14
) clearly becomes
\mathcal{O}(t)
as
t\searrow 0
(with a bound independent of
x
since

|\nu|(K)<\infty
).

Case
d=3
.
We now have

\displaystyle|A_{1}(t,x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\frac{t}{t+|h|}\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)\,=\,\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{t}{t+|y-x|}\;d|\nu|(y)

(by Lemma
2.3
)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{K}\frac{t}{t+|y-x_{0}|}\;d|\nu|(y)

and the right hand side vanishes as
t\searrow 0
by the dominated convergence theorem (independently of
x
).

Case
d\geq 4
. In this case, rather than (
3.13
), we show that

\limsup_{|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t\to 0^{+}}|A_{1}(t,x)|\leq C\,{\varepsilon}.

To do so, we shall need the additional hypothesis in (
1.11
), in the form (
3.3
).
We break the integral in (
3.14
) into pieces.
Assuming
t\ll{\delta}/2
, we can find
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
2^{J}t<{\delta}\leq 2^{J+1}t
.
We then write

\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\frac{t\;d|\nu_{x}|(h)}{(t+|h|)^{d-2}}\,dh\leq\int_{|h|<t}\cdots\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{J}\int_{2^{j}t\leq|h|<2^{j+1}t}\cdots=:I_{0}+I_{1}.

Then, using (
3.3
) one finds that

I_{0}\leq\frac{1}{t^{d-3}}\,\int_{|h|<t}\,d|\nu_{x}|=\frac{|\nu|\big(B_{t}(x)\big)}{t^{d-3}}<{\varepsilon},

and

I_{1}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{J}\int_{|h|<2^{j+1}t}\frac{t\,\;d|\nu_{x}|}{(2^{j}t)^{d-2}}\,=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{2^{-j}}{(2^{j}t)^{d-3}}|\nu|\big(B_{2^{j+1}t}(x)\big)\leq C\,{\varepsilon},

with
C=2^{d-2}
. Combining these estimates with (
3.14
) we obtain

(3.15)

\limsup_{|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t\to 0}|A_{1}(t,x)|\,\leq\,c_{x_{0}}\,{\varepsilon}.

This finishes the estimate involving the remainder part
\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)
of the kernel
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{t,x}(h)
; see (
3.8
).
We remark that the hypothesis (
1.10
) has not been used in this part.

We now turn to estimate
the piece involving the radially decreasing part
\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)
, which makes a crucial use of this hypothesis (in the form (
3.2
)).
We shall need a lemma from measure theory, whose proof is similar to
[
6
, Proposition 6.23]
, but that we sketch for completeness.
This gives a formula of polar coordinates (
3.18
) for a general measure
\mu
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a (locally finite) complex measure
\mu
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, let

(3.16)

{\varrho}(r):=\mu\big({\overline{B}}_{r}(0)\big)=\mu\Big(\big\{|x|\leq r\big\}\Big),\quad r\geq 0,

and denote by
m_{\varrho}
the associated Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure in
[0,\infty)
, that is

(3.17)

m_{\varrho}\big((a,b]\big)={\varrho}(b)-{\varrho}(a),{\quad\mbox{and}\quad}m_{\rho}\big(\{0\}\big)={\varrho}(0).

Then, for every radial
f(x)=f_{0}(|x|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};|\mu|)
, it holds

(3.18)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}f_{0}(r)\,dm_{\varrho}(r).

Proof.

We may assume that the measure
\mu
is positive. Consider the new
measure
\mu_{0}
defined on Borel sets
A\subset[0,\infty)
by

\mu_{0}(A):=\mu\Big(\big\{x{\,\,\,\colon\,\,\,}|x|\in A\big\}\Big)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(|x|)d\mu(x).

When
A
is an interval
(a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
(or
A=\{0\}
), it is clear by definition that

\mu_{0}(A)=m_{\varrho}(A).

Since these elementary sets generate the
\sigma
-algebra of all Borel sets in
[0,\infty)
,
by uniqueness, the two measures
\mu_{0}
and
m_{\varrho}
will coincide over all such sets; see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 1.14]
.
Thus, for every function of the form
f(x)={\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(|x|)
with
A
a Borel set, it will hold

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}f_{0}(r)\,d\mu_{0}(r)=\int_{[0,\infty)}f_{0}(r)\,dm_{\varrho}(r).

By linearity, this identity extends to all simple functions, and by monotone convergence to all non-negative

f(x)=f_{0}(|x|)
. A further extension by linearity to complex functions in
L^{1}(d|\mu|)
establishes the result.
∎

We shall apply the previous lemma to the measure
\mu=\nu_{x}
, so that

A_{2}(t,x):=\int_{|h|<{\delta}}\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)\,d\,\nu_{x}(h)=\int_{[0,{\delta})}\mathcal{K}_{t,x}^{0}(r)\,dm_{{\varrho}_{x}}(r),

with
\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)=\mathcal{K}_{t,x}^{0}(|h|)
and

(3.19)

{\varrho}_{x}(r):=\nu_{x}\big({\overline{B}}_{r}(0)\big)=\nu\big({\overline{B}}_{r}(x)\big).

Notice that condition (
3.2
) implies

(3.20)

\big|{\varrho}_{x}(r)\big|\leq{\varepsilon}\,(|x-x_{0}|+r)^{d},\quad\mbox{if}\;\;|x-x_{0}|+r<2{\delta}.

Then, integrating by parts (see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 3.36]
) we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle A_{2}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(0){\varrho}_{x}(0)+\int_{(0,{\delta})}\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(r)\,dm_{{\varrho}_{x}}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(0){{\varrho}_{x}}(0)+\Big[\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(r){{\varrho}_{x}}(r)\Big]_{r=0}^{r={\delta}^{-}}-\int_{0}^{\delta}{{\varrho}_{x}}(r)\,\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}({\delta}){{\varrho}_{x}}({\delta}^{-})+\int_{0}^{\delta}{{\varrho}_{x}}(r)\,\Big|\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\Big|\,dr,

using in the last line that (for each fixed
t
and
x
)
the function

r\longmapsto\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(r)

is decreasing in
[0,\infty)
; see (
3.9
). Note also that

(3.22)

\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(h)\lesssim\frac{t}{(t+|h|)^{d+1}},

which can be proved with a similar argument as we did in (
3.10
) for the kernel
\mathcal{R}_{t,x}(h)
(removing the factor
s\,|x|\,|h|

that appears in that kernel due to (
3.7
)). So, using this and (
3.20
), the boundary term in (
3.21
) satisfies

\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}({\delta})\,\big|{\varrho}_{x}({\delta}^{-})\big|=\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}({\delta})\,\big|\nu\big(B_{\delta}(x)\big)\big|\lesssim\frac{t}{{\delta}^{d+1}}\,(|x-x_{0}|+{\delta})^{d}\cdot{\varepsilon}\,\lesssim\,{\varepsilon},

since
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t<{\delta}
(and also
t<{\delta}
). To estimate the integral in (
3.21
) we split it as
\int_{0}^{t}+\int_{t}^{\delta}
.
In the first range we use that

\big|{\varrho}_{x}(r)\big|\leq\,(|x-x_{0}|+r)^{d}\,{\varepsilon}\lesssim{\varepsilon}\,t^{d},

since
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and
r\leq t
. So,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|{\varrho}_{x}(r)|\,\Big|\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\Big|\,dr

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,t^{d}\,\int_{0}^{t}\Big|\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\Big|\,dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,t^{d}\,\Big[\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(0)-\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(t)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,t^{d}\,\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(0)\,\lesssim\,{\varepsilon},

with the last bound due to (
3.22
). Finally, if
r\in(t,{\delta})
we have

\big|{\varrho}_{x}(r)\big|\leq\,(|x-x_{0}|+r)^{d}\,{\varepsilon}\leq({\alpha}t+r)^{d}\,{\varepsilon}\,\lesssim{\varepsilon}\,r^{d}.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\delta}|{\varrho}_{x}(r)|\,\Big|\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\Big|\,dr

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,\int_{0}^{\delta}\Big|\frac{d\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}}{dr}(r)\Big|\,r^{d}\,dr

(parts)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,\Big(-\Big[r^{d}\,\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t,x}(r)\Big]_{0}^{\delta}+d\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathcal{K}_{t,x}(r)\,r^{d-1}\,dr\Big)

(by (
3.22
))

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t\,r^{d-1}\,dr}{(t+r)^{d+1}}\,=\,c\,{\varepsilon}.

This shows that, if
|x-x_{0}|\leq{\alpha}t
and
t<t_{2}=\min\{{\delta},{\delta}/{\alpha}\}
then

|A_{2}(t,x)|\leq C\,{\varepsilon}.

Combining this with the previous estimates (
3.4
), (
3.6
) and (
3.15
), we conclude that, given
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists

\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}({\varepsilon})>0
such that, if
|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t
and
t\in(0,\tau_{0})
then

\big|{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)\big|<{\varepsilon}.

Thus, (
3.1
) holds and we have completed the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

\Box

Remark 3.2
.

As observed above, in the special case that
x_{0}=0
, the estimate of the remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{t,x}
can be slightly improved, due to
the factor
|x|=\mathcal{O}(t)
appearing in (
3.11
). Indeed, this will give a better factor
t^{2}
in the estimate (
3.14
),
which in turn implies that

A_{1}(t,x)=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\mbox{as $t\searrow 0$}

for all dimensions
d\leq 4
.
Moreover, in order to have, for dimensions
d\geq 5
, the estimate

\limsup_{|x-x_{0}|<{\alpha}t\to 0}|A_{1}(t,x)|\leq C\,{\varepsilon},

one only needs the assumption

(3.23)

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\nu-\ell\,dy|(B_{r}(0))}{r^{d-4}}=0,

which at the point
x_{0}=0
is weaker than (
1.11
).
So, overall, we can formulate this special case as a separate theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
and assume that
0
is a
\sigma
-point of
\nu
with value
\ell
.

(i) If
d\in\{1,2,3,4\}
then

(3.24)

\lim_{|x|<{\alpha}t\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\nu(x)=\ell,\quad\forall\,{\alpha}>0.

(ii) If
d\geq 5
then (
3.24
) holds under the additional assumption (
3.23
).
