Triple oral therapy combining metformin, SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors versus dual therapy in type 2 diabetes mellitus: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abdul Fatah Malik, Fnu Kashish, Fnu Shivani, Somesh Kumar, Vanesha Kumari, Abdul Haseeb, Ahmad Mujtaba, Abdur Raheem, Syed Muhammad Sinaan Ali, Usama Saleh, Tagwa Kalool Fadlalla Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Triple oral therapy combining metformin, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, and a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor has been proposed as a synergistic approach to intensify glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of triple therapy compared to dual therapy (metformin plus either sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor).Methods:Following preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science through January 2026. Studies included randomized controlled trials comparing triple versus dual therapy in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Outcomes included hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose, body weight, achievement of HbA1c < 7%, and adverse events (AEs). Pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) and risk ratios (RRs) were calculated using random-effects models.Results:Eight studies encompassing 2606 participants were included. Findings indicate triple therapy significantly reduced HbA1c levels compared to dual therapy, with a SMD of − 0.54 (95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.92 to −0.16; P = .005). Triple therapy resulted in greater reduction in fasting plasma glucose, with an SMD of −0.30 (95% CI: −0.62 to 0.01; P = .06). Patients on triple therapy were more likely to achieve HbA1c levels below 7% (RR: 2.02; 95% CI: 1.55–2.63; P < .0001). Weight reduction was modest, with an SMD: −0.14 (95% CI: −0.22 to −0.07; P = .0002). No significant differences were found in total AEs (RR = 0.97; P = .69) or hypoglycemia (RR = 1.32; P = .32), although there was higher discontinuation due to AEs (RR = 2.62; P = .03).Conclusion:Triple therapy offers superior glycemic control over dual therapy without major safety trade-offs, though tolerability may affect long-term adherence.

1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has reached epidemic proportions, presenting a major global health and economic challenge. In 2021, over half a billion people worldwide had diabetes, with prevalence rising rapidly, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.[1–3] T2DM is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, contributing to complications such as blindness, renal failure, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and limb amputations.[4–6] The pathogenesis of T2DM is multifactorial, with central features including insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, resulting in chronic hyperglycemia.[7] In addition to these defects, patients present with a range of pathophysiological abnormalities collectively termed the “ominous octet.” These include: impaired insulin secretion by pancreatic β-cells, increased glucagon secretion from α-cells, excessive hepatic glucose production, reduced peripheral (muscle) glucose uptake, increased lipolysis in adipose tissue, diminished incretin effect from the gut, increased renal glucose reabsorption, and central nervous system insulin resistance, which contributes to dysregulated appetite and metabolism.[8] This understanding that multiple organs contribute to hyperglycemia has informed therapeutic approaches, extending beyond insulin and sulfonylureas to include antihyperglycemic agents targeting diverse pathways.[8,9]

Metformin, introduced over six decades ago, remains the foundation of 1st-line pharmacotherapy for T2DM in guidelines.[10] Its favourable profile (including ~1% glycosylated hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] reduction, weight neutrality, and low hypoglycemia risk) and evidence for long-term benefit in overweight patients establish metformin as the cornerstone.[10] However, T2DM is progressive; monotherapy often fails to maintain glycemic control due to ongoing β-cell decline.[11–13] Data show that each 1% increase in HbA1c raises the risk of diabetic complications,[14,15] whereas intensive glycemic control can reduce microvascular outcomes.[16,17] Thus, timely therapy escalation is critical. Traditionally, a stepwise approach has been employed, wherein medications are added sequentially. However, this “treat-to-failure” paradigm postpones treatment intensification until glycemic control is compromised. Consequently, patients remain exposed to prolonged hyperglycemia, which accelerates β-cell dysfunction, exacerbates insulin resistance, and heightens the risk of both microvascular and macrovascular complications.[18] Many patients eventually require combinations of 2, 3, or more glucose-lowering drugs to achieve glycemic targets, highlighting the need for effective combination strategies earlier.

Novel oral agents improving glycemic control and addressing metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular risk have emerged in 2 decades. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors have transformed T2DM management.[19–21] DPP-4 inhibitors enhance incretin hormones, increasing insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon.[22,23] They provide HbA1c reductions, are weight-neutral and have low incidence of hypoglycemia.[24] Large cardiovascular trials show DPP-4 inhibitors are cardiovascularly safe, without cardio protection.[25,26] SAVOR-TIMI 53 showed higher heart failure hospitalization with saxagliptin (27% relative increase), requiring caution in at-risk patients.[26] SGLT-2 inhibitors reduce renal glucose reabsorption, lowering HbA1c by ~0.5% to 1.0%, promoting weight loss (~2–3 kg), and reducing blood pressure.[27] SGLT-2 inhibitors showed significant cardiorenal benefits in trials. T2DM patients at high cardiovascular risk receiving empagliflozin showed lower rates of heart failure hospitalization, renal disease progression, and reduced mortality. This positioned SGLT-2 inhibitors and glucagon like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists as preferred therapies for T2DM patients with cardiovascular or kidney comorbidities.[28] Standards recommend metformin initially, with SGLT-2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 agonists added for cardiorenal protections.[29,30] DPP-4 inhibitors remain alternatives when injectable GLP-1 RAs aren’t feasible but lack outcome reductions.[31]

Given T2DM’s multifactorial pathophysiology, combining agents with complementary mechanisms improves glycemic control. Metformin addresses insulin sensitivity and hepatic gluconeogenesis; DPP-4 inhibitors enhance incretin activity; SGLT-2 inhibitors offer insulin-independent glucose-lowering. Triple therapy with these agents targets fasting and postprandial hyperglycemia, reduces glucotoxicity, promotes weight loss, and avoids hypoglycemia. Early combination therapy yields more durable glycemic control than sequential escalation. The EDICT trial showed initial triple therapy (metformin + pioglitazone + exenatide) achieved greater HbA1c reduction, with 70% reaching the target HbA1c.[32] Triple therapy with SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors carries low hypoglycemia risk while improving metabolic factors. Clinical evidence supports metformin–DPP-4–SGLT-2 triple combination efficacy.[24] A 2023 meta-analysis found triple therapy provided better glycemic control than dual therapy, with lower HbA1c and glucose levels.[33] Triple therapy showed weight loss and blood pressure benefits versus dual therapy. Safety remained similar, though genital mycotic infections increased.[33] No differences in fracture risk were noted.[33]

Despite promising evidence for triple therapy, important questions remain. Most trials comparing triple versus dual therapy have been short (24–52 weeks) and focused on intermediate outcomes like HbA1c, weight, and blood pressure. It remains uncertain whether improved glycemic control from triple therapy reduces long-term complications, as no dedicated outcome trials exist. Studies mainly examined adding SGLT-2 inhibitors to dual therapy in patients with suboptimal control, with limited evidence on initiating all 3 drugs simultaneously. Practical considerations like cost, adherence, and quality of life need study. SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors are expensive, making triple therapy burdensome in resource-limited settings. Real-world data show patients on multiple medications face adherence challenges, though fixed-dose combinations offer solutions. Questions remain about patient selection: who benefits most from triple versus dual therapy? Those with high baseline HbA1c might gain more benefit, while others may achieve targets with dual therapy. These uncertainties highlight the need for systematic evidence synthesis.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of triple therapy with metformin, a DPP-4 inhibitor, and an SGLT-2 inhibitor compared to dual therapy (metformin plus either a DPP-4 or SGLT-2 inhibitor) in adults with T2DM. By aggregating data from clinical trials, we aim to quantify the benefits of triple therapy on glycemic control, weight, blood pressure, and other endpoints and assess differences in adverse events (AEs) between regimens. This review addresses an important gap in diabetes management—the comparative value of triple versus dual combination therapy—and seeks to inform treatment strategies for patients not achieving glycemic targets on dual therapy. Our objective is to define the role of triple therapy in the T2DM treatment algorithm, balancing efficacy with safety, cost, and patient-centered considerations.

2. Methodology

2.1. Protocol development

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis guidelines. To enhance methodological rigor, The Cochrane Handbook was consulted for evaluation of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), respectively. This review was previously registered on International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251127855). No ethical/institutional review board approval or consent was required because this study constitutes publicly available and de-identified data.

2.2. Search strategy

A predefined search strategy was employed using keywords such as: (“Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2”) AND (“Sodium-Glucose Transporter 2 Inhibitors” OR “SGLT-2 Inhibitors”) AND (“Dipeptidyl-Peptidase IV Inhibitors” OR “DPP-4 Inhibitors”) AND (“Metformin”) AND (“Triple Therapy”), see File S1, Supplemental Digital Content. The search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify relevant studies published from inception until January 2025. Additional references from bibliographies of relevant articles were manually screened. Only studies published in English were included, with no restrictions on study design, publication date, or geographic location.

2.3. Eligibility criteria and screening

Studies meeting the following inclusion criteria were considered for analysis. Studies that were RCTs; with population of patients older than 18 years diagnosed with T2DM. Studies comparing a triple therapy involving SGLT-2 inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and ipragliflozin), DPP-4 inhibitors (e.g., saxagliptin, sitagliptin, and linagliptin), and metformin with control groups receiving dual therapy (SGLT-2 or DPP-4 inhibitors with metformin). Exclusion criteria included conference abstracts, reviews, observational studies, editorials, case reports, case series, and animal studies.

Screening was conducted in a stepwise manner by 2 independent reviewers, starting with title screening, followed by abstract and full-text assessment. Discrepancies were resolved by consulting a 3rd reviewer. A preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis flowchart was utilized to enhance transparency in study selection and provide a visual summary of exclusions.

2.4. Data extraction and statistical analysis

Data extraction was independently carried out by 2 reviewers, collecting details on study characteristics (authors, year, and study design), demographics (age, sex, sample size, diabetes duration, body mass index, and waist circumference) and outcome measures: changes in HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and body weight, proportion of patients achieving HbA1c < 7%, and incidence of AEs (hypoglycemia, urinary tract infections [UTI], treatment-related side effects, and discontinuation due to adverse effects).

For statistical analysis, RevMan software (Review Manager 5.4) was used. Separate forest plots were generated for dichotomous outcomes, estimating risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for continuous outcomes to compare changes in HbA1c, FPG, and body weight across studies. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the impact of different drug combinations. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics, considering values above 50% as substantial heterogeneity.

2.5. Quality assessment

The risk of bias was independently assessed by 2 reviewers using The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB-1) for RCTs, evaluating selection, detection, reporting, and attrition bias (classified as low, high, or unclear risk), focusing on selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. Studies scoring below 7 out of 9 were considered to have a higher risk of bias, see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

A comprehensive search across multiple databases yielded a total of 1394 records. After the removal of 281 duplicates, 1113 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. During this process, 1080 articles were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The remaining 33 articles underwent full-text review, of which 25 were excluded due to reasons such as irrelevance to the research question, incomplete data, or noncompliance with the study’s methodological requirements. Ultimately, 8 studies were included in the meta-analysis. (Fig. 1).

3.2. Baseline characteristics

The meta-analysis included 9 studies, comprising a total of 2606 participants, with 1307 in the SGLT-2 + DPP-4 inhibitor + metformin group and 1099 in the control group. All included studies were RCTs, see Table 1. The mean age of participants across studies ranged from 42.8 to 59.2 years. The baseline body mass index was comparable between groups, ranging from approximately 23.3 to 32.5 kg/m2. The duration of diabetes varied, with some studies reporting a mean duration of over 7 years, while others did not specify this information. The intervention arms included combinations of different SGLT-2 inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and ipragliflozin) with DPP-4 inhibitors (e.g., saxagliptin, sitagliptin, and linagliptin) and metformin. Control groups generally consisted of placebo or metformin-based regimens.

3.3. Pre and post intervention change in HbA1c% and intervention versus control comparison

Figure 2 depicts HbA1c percentage changes from pre to postintervention at 16 to 26 weeks’ follow-up across studies in the meta-analysis. The overall SMD for HbA1c change was −1.40 (95% CI: −1.82 to −0.99; P < .00001), indicating a substantial reduction in HbA1c levels among patients treated with SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and metformin compared to controls. This finding suggests the combination therapy effectively reduces HbA1c in patients with T2DM. In subgroup analysis, significant reductions were observed for ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −1.22, P < .00001), ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −1.60, P < .00001), and dapagliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −2.46, P = .0003). These results support the efficacy of specific drug combinations in enhancing glycemic control, particularly dual therapy with SGLT-2 inhibitors and DPP-4 inhibitors, which appear more potent in controlling HbA1c compared to other combinations. The most significant reductions were observed with dapagliflozin and sitagliptin, showing the strongest therapeutic effect on HbA1c.

Pre and post-intervention change in HbA1c% at 16 to 26 weeks’ follow-up. CI = confidence interval, HbA1c = hemoglobin A1c, SD = standard deviation.

The data further highlights some interesting findings, such as Softeland et al.’s results for empagliflozin 10 and 25 mg combinations with metformin and linagliptin. Although the reductions in HbA1c were statistically significant (SMD: −0.82 for 10 mg and −0.71 for 25 mg), they were comparatively less pronounced than those seen with dapagliflozin and sitagliptin. This could suggest that while empagliflozin is effective, it may have a less potent effect on HbA1c reduction compared to other combinations like dapagliflozin or ertugliflozin. The general trend observed across the studies is a greater reduction in HbA1c with the combination of an SGLT-2 inhibitor and DPP-4 inhibitor compared to the combination of SGLT-2 inhibitors and metformin alone, further supporting the role of DPP-4 inhibitors in enhancing the therapeutic benefits of SGLT-2 inhibitors.

Figure 3 compares the intervention group (SGLT-2 + DPP-4 inhibitor + metformin) and the control group (SGLT-2/DPP-4 inhibitor + metformin) regarding HbA1c reduction. The overall SMD between groups was −0.54 (95% CI: −0.92 to −0.16; P = .005), showing a statistically significant difference favouring the intervention group, where patients received SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and metformin. Subgroup analysis revealed varying benefits based on drug combination. The study by Han et al. on ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin showed no significant difference between groups (SMD: 0.03, P = .87), possibly due to limited data from a single study. Similarly, Singh et al.’s study on dapagliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin showed no statistically significant difference between groups (SMD: 0.07, P = .59). However, Pratley et al.’s study demonstrated benefit with ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −0.28, P = .002) and ertugliflozin 15 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −1.50, P < .00001), indicating an advantage for the intervention group. The reduction with ertugliflozin 15 mg is notable, showing a substantial effect on HbA1c control compared to the 5 mg dose.

Post intervention HbA1c percent change in triple therapy versus control group at 16 to 26 weeks. CI = confidence interval, HbA1c = hemoglobin A1c, SD = standard deviation.

Dapagliflozin combined with metformin and saxagliptin, as shown in 2 studies by Mathieu et al. and Rosenstock et al., had an SMD of −0.68 (95% CI −1.46 to 0.10; P = .09), which did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that while dapagliflozin in combination with saxagliptin may be effective, the evidence for its superiority over the control group is not as robust as for other drug combinations, possibly due to variability in the studies or sample sizes. The comparison between the empagliflozin combinations also yields some interesting insights: the 10 mg dose (SMD: −0.68, P < .00001) and the 25 mg dose (SMD: −0.55, P = .0001) both show statistically significant reductions in HbA1c, although not as high as those seen with dapagliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin. This suggests that while empagliflozin has a positive effect, its impact on HbA1c reduction may be relatively modest in comparison to other combinations, such as dapagliflozin and ertugliflozin.

Figure 4 presents pre and post-intervention change in FPG percent at 16 to 26 weeks’ follow-up. The overall SMD for FPG change was −0.81 (95% CI −0.91 to −0.71; P < .00001), showing a significant reduction in FPG levels with SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and metformin compared to control treatment. This indicates the combination therapy effectively reduces FPG in T2DM patients. Subgroup analysis revealed significant FPG reductions for several combinations, including ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −0.71, P < .0001), ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −0.81, P < .00001), and ertugliflozin 15 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −1.00, P < .00001). These findings demonstrate that various SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitor combinations significantly improve fasting glucose control, with the strongest reduction from ertugliflozin 15 mg + metformin + sitagliptin. The effect of ertugliflozin 15 mg suggests higher doses may provide greater benefits in reducing FPG levels compared to lower doses or alternative combinations.

Pre and post intervention change in fasting plasma glucose (FPG) percent at 16 to 26 weeks’ follow-up. CI = confidence interval, SD = standard deviation.

In addition to the aforementioned combinations, dapagliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin also showed a substantial reduction in FPG (SMD: −0.97, P < .00001) in Sahay et al. study. Similarly, the empagliflozin combinations (10 and 25 mg) also demonstrated significant improvements in FPG levels (SMD: −0.69 for 10 mg and −0.86 for 25 mg, P < .00001 and P < .00001, respectively), although the effect sizes were somewhat smaller compared to dapagliflozin or ertugliflozin. These findings suggest that while empagliflozin is effective, its impact on FPG reduction may be more modest compared to other drug combinations.

Figure 5 illustrates the comparison between the intervention and control groups for FPG change. The overall SMD between the 2 groups was −0.30 (95% CI −0.62 to 0.01; P = .06), suggesting a trend towards a reduction in FPG favouring the intervention group, although this result did not reach statistical significance. Subgroup analysis showed variable results. For example, the combination of ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin demonstrated a minimal effect (SMD: 0.20, P = .24), which might be attributed to the data from a single study (Han et al.) and a less pronounced reduction in FPG. On the other hand, Pratley et al.’s study on ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin showed a small but significant reduction in FPG (SMD: 0.28, P = .002), while the 15 mg dose of ertugliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin revealed a larger reduction (SMD: −0.32, P = .0005), highlighting the dose-dependent effect of ertugliflozin on fasting glucose control.

Post intervention FPG percent change in triple therapy versus control group at 16 to 26 weeks. CI = confidence interval, FPG = fasting plasma glucose, SD = standard deviation.

For the dapagliflozin + metformin + saxagliptin combination, the 2 studies by Mathieu et al and Rosenstock et al showed a moderate effect (SMD: −0.70, P = .08), although this result did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that while the combination may have some benefit, it may not be as robust as others in reducing FPG. Finally, Softeland et al.’s studies on empagliflozin + metformin + linagliptin (10 and 25 mg) both demonstrated significant reductions in FPG (SMD: −0.47 for 10 mg and −0.62 for 25 mg, P = .0007 and P < .00001, respectively), indicating that these combinations are effective, particularly the 25 mg dose of empagliflozin.

Figure 6, depicts the pre and post-intervention change in body weight percent at the 16 to 26 weeks’ follow-up. The overall SMD for the change in body weight was −0.14 (95% CI −0.22 to −0.07; P = .0002), indicating a modest but statistically significant reduction in body weight following the combination treatment. This suggests that the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and metformin in combination with each other can lead to a slight reduction in body weight in T2DM patients. Subgroup analysis based on the specific drug combinations revealed minimal changes in body weight across most studies. For instance, the combination of ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (Han et al) showed a decrease in body weight, but the effect was not statistically significant (SMD: −0.21, P = .20). Similarly, Pratley et al. observed a small reduction in body weight for ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin (SMD: −0.17, P = .06), which was also not statistically significant. The 15 mg dose of ertugliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin, however, resulted in a statistically significant weight reduction (SMD: −0.20, P = .03), suggesting that higher doses may confer a slightly more significant effect on body weight reduction.

Pre and post-intervention change in body weight percent at 16 to 26 weeks follow-up. CI = confidence interval, SD = standard deviation.

Further analysis showed that dapagliflozin + metformin + saxagliptin, based on 3 studies (Mathieu et al., Rosenstock et al., and Joaqui et al.), demonstrated a small reduction in body weight (SMD: −0.11, P = .08), though this was not statistically significant. Additionally, Sahay et al.’s study on dapagliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin revealed a minimal and statistically insignificant change in body weight (SMD: −0.06, P = .6). Empagliflozin combinations (10 and 25 mg) reported by Softeland et al. also showed minimal weight changes (SMD: −0.15 for 10 mg, P = .28; SMD −0.13 for 25 mg, P = .33), further indicating that while the combination therapies have some effect on weight, the reductions are relatively small and not always statistically significant.

Figure 7 presents the comparison between the intervention and control groups for body weight changes. The overall SMD between the intervention and control groups was −0.53 (95% CI −1.13 to 0.07; P = .08), suggesting a trend toward reduced body weight with the intervention, though the result did not reach statistical significance. The analysis of subgroups revealed more variable results across different drug combinations. Han et al.’s study on ipragliflozin 50 mg + metformin + sitagliptin showed a modest weight reduction (SMD: −0.37, P = .03), with a statistically significant result. The combination of ertugliflozin 5 mg + metformin + sitagliptin also showed a significant reduction in body weight (SMD: −1.10, P < .00001), indicating that this combination is particularly effective in reducing body weight. The 15 mg dose of ertugliflozin + metformin + sitagliptin reported an even more substantial reduction in body weight (SMD: −2.1, P < .00001), suggesting a dose-dependent effect with ertugliflozin, where higher doses lead to greater weight reduction.

Post-intervention body weight percent change in triple therapy versus control group at 16 to 26 weeks. CI = confidence interval, SD = standard deviation.

Dapagliflozin + metformin + saxagliptin, based on 2 studies (Mathieu et al. and Rosenstock et al.), demonstrated a significant weight reduction (SMD: −0.27, P = .009), suggesting that this combination may also be effective in reducing body weight. On the other hand, Softeland et al.’s studies on empagliflozin + metformin + linagliptin (10 and 25 mg) reported a mixed effect. The 10 mg dose showed a slight reduction in body weight (SMD: 0.27, P = .05), while the 25 mg dose resulted in no significant difference (SMD: 0.10, P = .47), indicating that the effect on body weight may not be as robust with empagliflozin compared to other combinations.

Figure 8 shows the RR of achieving HbA1c levels below 7% in patients receiving triple therapy versus the control group. The overall RR across 5 studies was 2.02 (95% CI: 1.55–2.63; P < .0001), indicating that patients on triple therapy had a significantly higher likelihood of achieving HbA1c levels under 7% compared to those receiving the control treatment. This suggests that the combination of SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and metformin may offer a more effective approach for glycemic control in T2DM patients, achieving a lower HbA1c level with greater success than other treatment options.

Risk ratio for HbA1c <7% across studies. CI = confidence interval, HbA1c = hemoglobin A1c.

Figure 9 illustrates the RR for total AEs across 8 studies, with a RR of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.85–1.11; P = .69). This outcome indicates that the incidence of total AEs between the intervention and control groups is comparable, with no statistically significant difference detected. The RR of 0.97 suggests that although AEs were reported, their frequency was not elevated in the triple therapy group relative to the control group. This finding is promising, as it suggests that the combination therapy does not result in an increased overall risk of AEs.

Risk ratio for total adverse events across studies. CI = confidence interval.

Figure 10 analyses the RR for severe treatment-related AEs across 5 studies, where the RR was 1.16 (95% CI: 0.64–2.13; P = .62). This indicates that the risk of severe AEs was slightly higher in the triple therapy group compared to the control group, though the result was not statistically significant. This suggests that while the triple therapy may have a marginally increased risk of severe AEs, the difference between groups is not substantial enough to draw a clear conclusion, and further studies may be needed to evaluate this outcome more definitively.

Risk ratio for severe treatment-related adverse events across studies. CI = confidence interval.

Figure 11 evaluates the RR for hypoglycemia across 8 studies, yielding a RR of 1.32 (95% CI: 0.76–2.30; P = .32). The findings indicate no significant difference in the incidence of hypoglycemic events between the triple therapy and control groups. Although a RR of 1.32 suggests a potential slight increase in the risk of hypoglycemia due to the intervention, the broad confidence interval and lack of statistical significance (P = .32) imply that the clinical relevance of this observation is minimal.

Figure 12 presents the RR for UTIs across 7 studies, with a RR of 1.09 (95% CI: 0.70–1.70; P = .69). This finding suggests that the risk of UTIs was comparable between the triple therapy and control groups, with no statistically significant difference observed. The RR value close to 1 and the high P-value (P = .69) indicate that triple therapy does not significantly increase the likelihood of UTIs compared to the control group.

Figure 13 evaluates the RR for AEs leading to treatment discontinuation across 4 studies, where the RR was 2.62 (95% CI: 1.09–6.30; P = .03). This result indicates that patients receiving triple therapy were more likely to discontinue treatment due to AEs compared to those in the control group, with a statistically significant RR. This finding suggests that while the combination therapy may offer better glycemic control, it may also lead to a higher risk of discontinuation due to side effects, warranting closer monitoring of patients on this regimen.

Overall, the triple therapy (SGLT-2 inhibitors + DPP-4 inhibitors + metformin) appears to offer significant benefits in terms of glycemic control, as evidenced by the higher likelihood of achieving HbA1c levels below 7%, Figure 7. However, the therapy does not show a marked increase in total AEs, severe AEs, hypoglycemia, or UTIs when compared to the control group, suggesting similar safety profiles between the groups. The most notable concern is the higher risk of treatment discontinuation due to AEs, highlighting the importance of monitoring for side effects and patient adherence to the treatment regimen (Fig. 13).

4. Discussion

In our meta-analysis of 8 studies, we assessed the effectiveness and safety of triple therapy compared to dual hypoglycemic treatments involving SGLT2, DPP4 inhibitors, and metformin for individuals with T2DM. Our investigation consolidated evidence by examining glycemic control indicators: changes in HbA1c and FPG, along with metabolic effects in body weight. We evaluated the RR for patients achieving HbA1c below 7%. We tracked the safety profile by examining total and severe AEs, complications like hypoglycemia and UTI, and treatment discontinuations due to adverse effects. Overall, findings indicate triple therapy significantly enhances glycemic markers and promotes weight loss compared to dual therapy, without notable increase in severe safety risks, though with higher discontinuation rates.

Remarkably, a considerably higher percentage of patients on triple therapy reached the target HbA1c of <7% compared to those on dual therapy. Additionally, we noted a slight advantage in weight reduction with triple therapy; although the actual weight loss was minimal, it was favourable considering that many diabetes treatments do not aid in weight management. Crucially, these improvements in efficacy did not result in a higher incidence of overall AEs or hypoglycemia. The occurrence of common side effects, such as genital or UTI, was only marginally higher with triple therapy and did not differ statistically from dual therapy in our analysis. The only significant safety distinction was a greater likelihood of treatment discontinuation due to adverse effects in the triple therapy group, indicating that tolerability issues (like polyuria or other mild effects) might cause some patients to discontinue the therapy.

The enhanced efficacy of triple therapy in lowering HbA1c is supported by multiple randomized trials. Han et al showed that adding ipragliflozin to sitagliptin and metformin produced an HbA1c drop of 0.8% over 24 weeks versus no change with dual therapy.[34] In the VERTIS FACTORIAL trial, co-administration of ertugliflozin and sitagliptin with metformin achieved larger HbA1c declines over 26 to 52 weeks than either agent with metformin alone.[35] Our meta-analysis confirms an overall advantage in glycemic reduction with triple therapy. The HbA1c difference of half to 1 percentage point favoring triple therapy is clinically meaningful, often determining whether glycemic targets are met. In one study, 44% of patients on triple therapy achieved HbA1c.[34] These improvements can reduce microvascular complication risks over time. The combination of SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors addresses both fasting and postprandial hyperglycaemia—SGLT-2 inhibition lowers fasting glucose via glycosuria, while DPP-4 inhibition targets postprandial glucose through incretin effects. Singh et al. reported that triple therapy lowered fasting glucose by 15 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial glucose by 30 mg/dL more than dual therapy.[36] By targeting hyperglycemia through multiple pathways, triple therapy achieves better glycemic response in refractory patients. Early intensive combination therapy may improve long-term glycemic durability.[37–39] Our findings support that “dual add-on” therapy induces more rapid and sustained glycemic control improvement than the traditional “single add-on” approach.

Aside from lowering HbA1c, the triple regimen improves other metabolic parameters. Weight management is crucial in type 2 diabetes therapy. While sulfonylureas and insulin cause weight gain, SGLT-2 inhibitors promote weight loss through caloric loss, while DPP-4 inhibitors are weight-neutral. The triple therapy showed slight weight reduction, consistent with trials. Han et al. observed a 1.7 kg weight decrease over 24 weeks with ipragliflozin added to sitagliptin + metformin.[34] A trial of triple fixed-dose dapagliflozin/sitagliptin/metformin showed small weight loss (1.5 kg over 16 weeks) compared to dual therapy. Weight changes with triple therapy have generally trended downward or neutral.[36,40] In a 52-week study, patients on dapagliflozin + saxagliptin + metformin continued losing weight, while those on dual therapy had less reduction.[40] Similarly, Joaqui et al observational study showed a modest decrease in HbA1c levels.[41] Though weight loss with triple oral therapy is modest, it can benefit overweight T2DM patients and improve blood pressure and lipid profiles. The SGLT-2 component has shown lower systolic blood pressure and improved renal health markers in some populations.[40,42] These benefits make triple therapy attractive for patients needing drugs that worsen weight or blood pressure. While not producing dramatic weight loss like GLP-1 receptor agonists, triple therapy achieves glycemic control without weight gain. Triple therapy provides a “metabolic neutral or mild benefit” profile – lowering glucose while modestly reducing weight and blood pressure – improving upon traditional therapies.

Our analysis found triple therapy was well-tolerated, with safety comparable to dual therapy. Adding a 2nd drug beyond metformin did not substantially increase AEs or risks. The meta-analysis showed triple therapy did not significantly increase overall AEs, severe AEs, or hypoglycemia. Since neither SGLT-2 nor DPP-4 inhibitors stimulate insulin independently, their combination did not increase hypoglycemic episodes versus controls. Mathieu et al. trial showed ~1% minor hypoglycemia incidence in triple therapy, similar to placebo.[24] Our pooled results showed a hypoglycemia RR of 1.32, confirming minimal risk versus insulin or sulfonylureas. Total AEs were similar between triple and dual therapy (RR ~0.97, P = .69). One study found fewer AEs with triple therapy than placebo (52%–55% vs 68%),[43] suggesting improved glycemic control might enhance well-being. While SGLT-2 inhibitors can cause genitourinary infections and DPP-4 inhibitors may cause nasopharyngitis, infection rates were only slightly higher with triple therapy. In a 1-year trial, genital infections remained low in both groups, with UTI incidence marginally higher in triple therapy (8.3% vs 5.0% in women).[40,41,44,45] No serious infections were reported, providing reassurance regarding SGLT-2 inhibitor safety.

Pancreatitis risk with DPP-4 inhibitors was not reported in trials, and outcome studies showed no significant hazard. Euglycemic ketoacidosis with SGLT-2 inhibitors is rare, occurring mainly with insulin deficiency or low-carb diets. When patients are appropriately selected, triple oral therapy can be used safely. The main drawback was higher therapy discontinuation due to adverse effects in the triple therapy group. While severe events are not increased, patients may experience side effects leading to medication discontinuation, with higher risk (RR ~2.6) in triple therapy. Clinicians should counsel patients on side effects and manage them through proper hygiene, hydration, and lower initial doses. Despite increased drop-outs, glycemic benefit remained robust in the triple-therapy group. Long-term trials show triple therapy had low AEs comparable to dual therapy, with no increase in cardiovascular events.[40] This safety profile, with superior efficacy, makes triple oral therapy an attractive intensification option.

Key considerations for triple therapy include cost, adherence, and feasibility. Combining SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors is costly, but a US study found adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor to DPP-4 inhibitor with metformin was cost-effective long-term, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $65,000 per quality-adjusted life year.[46] With patent expiration, DPP-4 inhibitors lose market exclusivity, enabling generic substitution and competitive pricing, which lowers acquisition costs, enhances affordability, and supports sustained use in routine diabetes management. The US Food and Drug Administration’s 2020 approval of Trijardy XR demonstrates progress in fixed-dose combinations (FDCs), which improve adherence by reducing pill burden. Studies show 22% to 29% better adherence with single-pill combinations versus separate pills.[47] Triple therapy FDCs are established in regions with high diabetes prevalence.[48] Triple therapy can be implemented in outpatient care with proper patient education, particularly for those preferring oral medications. Patient factors influence therapy choice – cardiovascular disease patients may benefit from GLP-1 agonists, while those with heart failure or chronic kidney disease might need SGLT-2 inhibitors. Triple therapy can be initiated concurrently or sequentially, showing better control versus dual therapy. Clinicians should weigh costs against benefits and adherence. While viable in well-resourced settings for patients above target on dual therapy, cost remains a barrier until generic versions become available.

This analysis and triple oral therapy strategy have limitations worth discussing. First, most studies in our meta-analysis had follow-up periods of 4 to 6 months (with some extending to 12 months). While sufficient for short-term efficacy and safety assessment, these durations do not inform long-term outcomes like glycemic control durability beyond 1 year or impacts on diabetes complications and cardiovascular/renal events. Initial advantages might diminish if β-cell function declines, though early aggressive control could provide “metabolic memory” benefits. Longer-term studies will determine if triple therapy delays insulin needs or improves macrovascular outcomes over years. Second, there was heterogeneity in comparator regimens. The “dual therapy” control varied between metformin + DPP-4 inhibitor and metformin + SGLT-2 inhibitor (or placebo added to metformin + DPP-4 in some cases).[24] While our subgroup analyses showed a consistent triple versus dual advantage, direct comparison data are limited. Third, study populations were specific – typically middle-aged patients with moderately longstanding diabetes (mean duration ~7–8 years, baseline HbA1c in high 7s to low 9s).[34] Efficacy might differ in early or advanced diabetes. However, a recent study showed promising results: initial triple therapy in newly diagnosed T2DM patients with baseline HbA1c ~11% achieved HbA1c of ~7.0% within 2 years, with over 60% maintaining HbA1c.[42] Triple therapy could be a powerful early intervention, although treating early disease with 3 drugs departs from guidelines and raises concerns about unnecessary medication if fewer drugs might suffice. Some guidelines caution against “overtreatment” in mild disease.[49] Finding optimal timing for triple therapy – neither too early in low-risk patients nor too late after glycemic toxicity – remains an important research topic. A limitation is the lack of data on patient-centered outcomes like quality of life and adherence. While adherence benefits are inferred from FDC studies and avoiding injections, studies measuring patient satisfaction with triple oral therapy would be valuable. Regarding agent combinations, all studies used an SGLT-2 inhibitor + DPP-4 inhibitor, though other class combinations are possible. Our findings support the SGLT-2/DPP-4 combination as the preferred regimen; a 2014 network meta-analysis found that triple therapy with newer agents yields better glycemic control than older regimens, with fewer side effects.[50] Our meta-analysis’s strength lies in focusing on a relevant contemporary strategy using recent trials and diverse populations. Including both randomized trials and real-world data, with subgroup analyses by drug combinations, enhances our conclusions’ robustness. The consistent benefit across studies adds confidence in triple therapy’s effectiveness.

5. Conclusion

Triple oral therapy, which involves the combination of metformin with both an SGLT-2 inhibitor and a DPP-4 inhibitor, demonstrates significantly enhanced glycemic control. This regimen effectively reduces HbA1c levels and facilitates the attainment of HbA1c < 7% when compared to dual therapy. Additionally, it results in modest reductions in body weight and FPG. Notably, the overall incidence of AEs and hypoglycemia is comparable between triple and dual therapy, although there is a higher likelihood of treatment discontinuation due to side effects with triple therapy. While tolerability may present challenges to long-term adherence, triple therapy emerges as an effective and generally safe strategy for intensifying treatment in patients with T2DM.
