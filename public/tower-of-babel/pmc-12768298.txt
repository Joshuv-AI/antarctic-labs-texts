The Development of Neonatal Intensive Care in Belfast, Northern Ireland
Authors: Mark McC Reid, Thomas F Baskett
Published: 2025

Just over one hundred years ago the Edinburgh obstetrician, John William Ballantyne (1861-1923), was among the first to anticipate the need for specialist neonatologists1.

“There is a need for specialists in neonatal medicine. This applies to doctors and nurses as well as teaching and construction of hospitals. The specialist in neonatal diseases and the nurse intensively trained and expert in the management of delicate newborns will be commonplace ere long”.

Until the twentieth century the care and resuscitation of the newborn, if needed, was provided by the accoucheur – midwife or physician. There are references to newborn resuscitation in biblical times, including mouth-to-mouth respiration by Elisha in the Book of Kings2. Indeed, basic resuscitation of the newborn has probably been carried out for millennia as midwives instinctively tried to revive weak and depressed infants at birth. In the eighteenth century two British obstetricians, William Smellie (1697-1763) and Benjamin Pugh (1715-1798), used ‘air pipes’ inserted into the mouth and trachea to initiate early respiration in depressed neonates3,4. Pugh also gave a remarkable description of neonatal mouth-to-mouth respiration that has not been improved upon in the intervening 270 years: “If the child does not breathe immediately upon delivery …. wipe its mouth, and press your mouth to the child’s, at the same time pinching the nose with your thumb and finger, to prevent air escaping; inflate the lungs, rubbing it before the fire: by which method I have saved many”.4

“If the child does not breathe immediately upon delivery …. wipe its mouth, and press your mouth to the child’s, at the same time pinching the nose with your thumb and finger, to prevent air escaping; inflate the lungs, rubbing it before the fire: by which method I have saved many”.4

The benefit of incubators to provide warmth for the neonate followed the observation of the incubation of eggs in nature and in poultry farming. The obstetrician Etienne Tarnier (1828-1897), used heated incubators for premature newborns, after observing their use for hatching the eggs of exotic birds in the Paris Zoo. Incubators were first developed in the mid to late nineteenth century in Russia, France and Germany, and were established in premature baby units in the eastern United States by the early twentieth century5. By the 1950s there was proof from a randomised controlled trial that warming premature infants improved survival6.

Incubators arrived in Belfast in 1959, providing both warmth and a consistent oxygen supply. They reduced the risk of hypothermia, which was a common damaging factor during the transfer of infants from the labour ward to the nursery.

To a degree they also reduced handling of the infant and provided isolation and protection from infection.

While the general importance of nutrition was recognised, the risks of aspiration and poor motility of the premature infant’s bowel led to the belief, particularly in the United States in the 1950s, that the premature infant could be safely starved for the first few days after birth. This was not followed in Belfast where early gavage feeding was adopted. Initially this was via soft rubber catheters, to be supplanted by less irritant polyvinyl feeding tubes in the late-1940s. Early feeding reduced the risks of hypoglycaemia and jaundice. ‘Formula’ feeds were developed with varying proportions of protein, fat, carbohydrate and other nutrients.

Special Care Baby Units

By the early twentieth century some paediatricians were showing an interest in the care of premature infants, with the development of premature baby nurseries. In the United Kingdom, Mary Crosse (1900-1972) a paediatrician with obstetrical training, established the first premature baby unit at the Sorrento Maternity Hospital, Birmingham in 19317. She emphasised the application of warming, humidity, nutrition and the meticulous individual nursing care of each infant8.

In Belfast, Frederick Allen (1898-1972), set up the first premature baby unit at the Royal Maternity Hospital (RMH) in 1927, and was also put in charge of the sister unit at the Jubilee Maternity Hospital in 1938 (Figure 1). Allen, the son of a grocer and a Queen’s graduate, was first appointed consultant paediatrician to the Royal Belfast Hospital for Sick Children (RBHSC) in 1924 and as physician-in-charge of infants at the RMH in 1927. In 1948 he became the first to hold the Nuffield Chair of Child Health at Queen’s and the RBHSC. All of these posts he maintained until his retirement in 1963. His text, Aids to the Diagnosis and Treatment of the Diseases of Children, went through eleven editions up to 19629.

Frederick Allen

At the Belfast City Hospital, the new Jubilee Maternity Hospital was opened in 1935. The previous maternity unit, named Ivy Cottage and founded in 1893, was converted to a neonatal unit for the Jubilee in 194810. The neonatal nurseries at the RMH and Jubilee were among the earliest to be established in Britain and Ireland.

Muriel Frazer

The National Health Service (NHS) was founded in 1948, bringing hope to a war-weary nation. Two paediatricians with an interest in newborn care were appointed consultants: Muriel Frazer at the Jubilee and Wilfred Campbell at the RMH.

Muriel Frazer (1911-2011) was educated at Victoria College and Queen’s – graduating in medicine in 1936 (Figure 2). She was an able student and gained the MRCP in 1938 and the FRCSI in 1939. Blocked by a lack of surgical posts for women she turned to paediatrics. Before Dr. Frazer’s appointment the neonatal unit at the Jubilee was under the supervision of Professor Allen – but his attention was directed mainly to the development of the children’s hospital. Frazer proved to be a strong and determined leader; she placed babies born in the hospital in separate nurseries, with isolation facilities for premature and sick newborns. She admitted sick and premature infants born at home and in GP obstetric units outside the hospital, and also organised surgical treatment of infants under her care rather than transfer them to the children’s hospital11. She did not get on well with Professor Allen and, although she was also appointed to the RBHSC and the RMH, she later confined her work to the City and Jubilee hospitals. Both she and Allen preferred to steer their own units in their separate ways, as was often the case between the City and Royal hospitals.

Dr. Frazer had converted to Catholicism in her early twenties and attended the Mater Hospital for early morning devotional services; following which she did a round of the new babies in the maternity unit there, before returning to the City Hospital. The Mater Hospital did not develop a special care baby unit and infants needing intensive care were transferred to the Jubilee or RMH neonatal units.

As a junior doctor in the 1960s Mark Reid worked closely with Dr. Frazer and found her to be a good teacher and a helpful colleague. It was an honour to take over her position when she retired in 1977. Later the ward was named the Muriel Frazer Neonatal Unit. She died in 2011 at one hundred years.

Wilfred Campbell

Wilfred Campbell (1918-1990) went to school at Campbell College and graduated in medicine from Queen’s in 1940 (Figure 3). In the Second World War he served as a medical officer in the Royal Navy with the Atlantic convoys and in the Far East. Upon his appointment in 1948 he reorganised the neonatal unit at the RMH. Working closely with the professor of obstetrics, Charles MacAfee (1898-1978), he coordinated the care of infants with rhesus isoimmunisation.

Rhesus Isoimmunisation

Campbell highlighted the interface between obstetrics and paediatrics in his review of haemolytic disease of the newborn at the RMH in the 1940s/1950s12: “Haemolytic disease of the newborn is one of the best examples of a condition in the common ground where the spheres of the obstetrician and paediatrician overlap”.

“Haemolytic disease of the newborn is one of the best examples of a condition in the common ground where the spheres of the obstetrician and paediatrician overlap”.

Macafee and Campbell encouraged centralisation of cases of rhesus isoimmunisation in Belfast and by the mid-1950s this was largely achieved – with two-thirds of the provincial cases managed at the RMH and one-third at the Jubilee12. The antenatal selection of patients for pre-emptive induction of labour was based on predictive factors that were, to a degree, arbitrary: previous perinatal outcome, rising anti-Rh antibody levels and, in late pregnancy, reduced fetal movements or an undefined alteration in fetal heart rate13. (As a medical student in the summer of 1963, TFB remembers the almost breathless silence during the professorial round in the open antenatal ward of the RMH as Professor Macafee bent over the patient’s abdomen listening to the fetal heart rate with his ear pressed to a small Pinard fetal stethoscope. After about two minutes he straightened up and said “I think the fetal heart is a little weaker, we should induce labour tomorrow.” So, that was it, the ‘undefined alteration in fetal heart rate’). Following this plan for pre-emptive induction at RMH in the 1950s, Fisher found the fetal loss was 16% in those induced and 29% in those allowed to go into spontaneous labour at term14.

After the pathophysiology of rhesus haemolytic disease was delineated in the early-1940s, simple ‘top-up’ transfusion of the newborn was given to correct anaemia. This was of limited value as it did not reduce the levels of harmful anti-Rh antibodies and could precipitate heart failure. After Wallerstein15 in New York and Diamond16 in Boston refined exchange transfusion this became the mainstay of treatment and halved the neonatal death rate. Wilfred Campbell carried out the first neonatal exchange transfusion at the RMH in 194812.

Nonetheless, Campbell’s nine-year review (1948-1956) at the RMH displayed the high perinatal death rate of Rh isoimmunisation; of 305 affected cases, 64 (21%) were stillborn, 30 (9.8%) neonatal deaths and 211 (69.2%) survived. The era of unlimited reproduction was also shown in the parity of the mothers: 51% were para 5 or above and 12.4% were para 9 or more12.

A more accurate prediction of fetal prognosis became available following the work of William Liley (1928-1983) in Auckland, New Zealand. In 1961 Liley used serial amniocentesis to measure the level of bilirubin and from these results developed a graph that placed the risk to the fetus into one of three prognostic zones – mild, moderate or severe17. He followed this in 1963 with the first intrauterine fetal transfusion (IUFT), which was necessary when the fetus was severely affected but too premature for induction and delivery18. In Campbell’s series, in the era before neonatal intensive care, only 3 of 35 (8.6%) of infants survived when delivered before 34 weeks12.

By the mid-1960s serial amniocentesis was used in Belfast to assess the level of fetal risk. Charlie Whitfield (1927-2018), a Queen’s medical graduate of 1950, was a consultant obstetrician, senior lecturer and reader at RMH from 1964 to 1974; during which time he led the management of Rh cases. He introduced his ‘Action Line’ which was based on two levels of amniotic fluid bilirubin levels superimposed on Liley’s graph19.

The first IUFT in Belfast was carried out at the Jubilee in 196420, and the second at the RMH in 196513. The numbers rose steadily through the late 1960s; at the RMH there were six IUFT in six patients in 1966, rising to 88 IUFT in 55 patients by 197013. The overall perinatal loss from Rh isoimmunisation was reduced dramatically: 1948(57%), 1955(31%) and 1968(19%).

By the mid-1960s the prevention of Rh isoimmunisation using Rh immune globulin (RhIg) was established. Although some patients received it from 1968, it was not until 1971 that routine postpartum prophylaxis with RhIg was available throughout Northern Ireland13. The combination of available contraception and RhIg prophylaxis led to a marked reduction in the number of affected Rh cases delivered at the RMH: from 161 in 1968 to 36 in 198013. The further application of RhIg prophylaxis in the antenatal period and for first trimester loss led to the theoretical prevention of all cases; although system failures continued to occur21.

The main paediatric involvement, after initial resuscitation, was to monitor the neonatal bilirubin levels. In Belfast, guidelines were developed to direct the need for exchange transfusion – often required within two days of delivery. Exchange transfusions were time consuming, taking about two hours, and in the early 1970s there were some 150 performed each year. Phototherapy was introduced in the late-1960s and a local study showed that this did reduce the need for exchange transfusion22.

This obstetric/paediatric cooperation set the scene for improved perinatal care in the future. As the scope of neonatal care expanded Campbell was less involved in direct medical treatment, and by the 1960s he was content to oversee the junior staff providing clinical care in the unit – recognising their youthful grasp of the changing technological advances. He retired to Scotland in 1983.

Oxygen Therapy

When Frazer and Campbell were appointed in 1948 oxygen therapy in premature infants was unrestricted and associated with an increased survival rate23. However, in 1951, the paediatrician Kate Campbell in Melbourne established the link between high concentration and prolonged oxygen therapy and retrolental fibroplasia24.

At the RMH, Oliver Fisher (1921-1997) consultant lecturer in the department of child health, reviewed the local experience25. The improved survival rates of infants weighing less than 3lbs (1360g) was shown over time as the unrestricted use of oxygen therapy increased: 1935 zero; 1947 21%; 1952 55%. The cost of this improved survival however was shown in 1951-1952, when there were eleven cases of retrolental fibroplasia in 109 surviving infants – seven of whom progressed to blindness. By late 1952, at the RMH, the maximum concentration of oxygen was reduced to 40% and the duration of its administration limited as much as possible. From 1954 there were no more cases of retrolental fibroplasia in that era25.

Neonatology as a Specialty

Thus, by the middle of the twentieth century, the care and responsibility for the newborn passed from the obstetrician to the paediatrician – much to the relief of many obstetricians. The terms ‘neonatology’ and ‘neonatologist’ were coined by the Baltimore paediatrician Alexander Schaffer (1902-1981) in his 1960 text, Diseases of the Newborn. He used it to denote that portion of paediatrics concerned with the “art and science of the diagnosis and treatment of disorders of the newborn infant.”26

While the long-term care of the newborn fell to the paediatricians they were less involved with the neonate that needed immediate resuscitation at birth. It was Virginia Apgar (1909-1974), an obstetric anaesthetist in New York, who drew attention to the benefit of routine appraisal of each newborn and the potential need for resuscitation. Her simple system evaluated the condition of the baby by observing five basic signs: colour, heart rate, respiration, reflex irritability and muscle tone27. Each of these signs were scored as zero, one or two at one and five minutes after birth. The simplicity and practicality of the Apgar Score ensured its universal adoption in maternity hospitals, including those in Northern Ireland.

By the early-1960s junior paediatric staff were increasingly asked to attend ‘high risk’ deliveries with the potential need for resuscitation. The resuscitation skills were taught initially by the anaesthetic staff; followed by more structured simulation teaching sessions using plastic model dolls. As was the custom in that era, the youngest staff were taught by those above them in the junior ranks. Better resuscitation equipment was made available in delivery rooms: radiant heaters above the resuscitation table, laryngoscopes, endotracheal tubes, masks, and necessary medications. These basic but critical interventions - mainly the avoidance of hypoxia and hypothermia with rapid transfer to the neonatal unit - improved the outcome for vulnerable infants.

Garth McClure

The appointment of Ivo John Carré (1920-2007) to the chair of child health in 1963 added impetus to the clinical and academic development of paediatrics in Northern Ireland. He was born in Guernsey to a farming family and studied medicine at Cambridge and St Thomas’ Hospital, London – where he was stranded when his home island was occupied by the enemy during the Second World War. After qualification in 1944 he undertook paediatric training in England and Australia before his appointment as senior lecturer in child health at Queen’s in 1956. When he took the chair in 1963, he set about establishing paediatric subspecialists and improving the academic credentials of the department. Although he had no training in neonatology, his subspecialty was gastroenterology with a career-long interest in oesophageal reflux, he recognised the need and, in 1973, appointed Garth McClure to the first joint university lecturer and hospital consultant post in neonatology (Figure 4).

The First Neonatologists

The 1970s saw the first appointment of consultants with specialised training in neonatology to the Jubilee and the RMH: Garth McClure (1973), Mark Reid (1977) and Henry Halliday (1979).

Mark Reid

Garth McClure (b.1940), the son of a nurse, was brought up in Scotland and attended school in Fife. He graduated in medicine from Queen’s in 1965, and took his paediatric training in Belfast with additional experience in neonatology at Cardiff. After his appointment as consultant lecturer in 1973, he was keen to introduce new treatments and inspire others to develop the specialty. He brought energy and enthusiasm to the role and expanded both undergraduate and postgraduate teaching in neonatology – eventually being appointed to the Chair of Neonatology at Queen’s. He was combative and a strong proponent of newborn care, and enjoyed taking on sceptical obstetricians to fight for the newborn cause. In 1993 he was elected president of the British Association of Perinatal Medicine. He retired in 2002.

Mark Reid (b.1937) represented Bangor Grammar School as head boy and as captain of the school rugby team (Figure 5). He qualified at Queen’s in 1962 and undertook paediatric training in the Belfast hospitals. In 1968 he was awarded the Samuel Hazlett Browne Scholarship from Queen’s to spend a year at the neonatal intensive care unit at the Toronto Hospital for Sick Children – this unit being at the forefront of assisted ventilation techniques for the newborn. He returned to Belfast as a senior registrar in 1969. In 1971 he was appointed consultant paediatrician to the new Craigavon Hospital and stayed there until his appointment as consultant neonatologist at the Jubilee and RMH sites in 1977. Reid was president of the Irish Perinatal Society in 1979. He retired in 2001.

Henry Halliday (1945-2022) went to school at Belfast Royal Academy (Figure 6). He was a strong tall man who played rugby for his school and for the former pupil’s side when he was a junior doctor. He graduated from Queen’s in 1970 and did his paediatric training in Northern Ireland. He then spent three years subspecialty training at the Rainbow Babies and Children’s Hospital in Cleveland (1976-78) and in the cardiovascular research unit at the University of California, San Francisco (1978-79). It was here that he worked with Dr. John Clements, one of the foremost authorities on pulmonary surfactant – which became a lifelong focus of Halliday’s research28. He returned to Belfast in 1979 to a consultant post at the RMH.

Henry Halliday

Halliday was a strong advocate of evidence-based medicine and led many of the international randomised clinical trials on surfactant use, to which the Belfast group contributed. His academic output was prodigious with more than 400 peer-reviewed publications. In 1992, he was appointed Honorary Professor of Child Health at Queen’s. He held a number of prominent positions, including: President of the European Society for Paediatric Research (1997); President of the European Association of Perinatal Medicine (2006) and Co-Founding Editor of the journal Biology of the Neonate, now known as Neonatology (2004). Recognition of his contributions to neonatology included the Maternité Prize from the European Association of Perinatal Medicine (2010) and, in 2021, the James Spence medal – the highest honour awarded by the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health. Among the tributes paid after his death in 2022 it was said: “He demonstrated that absolute scientific rigour and humanity can go together”.29,30

The creation of this third consultant neonatologist post at the RMH was stimulated by the increasing burden of neonatal intensive care cover by McClure and Reid. The final straw came when, after three weeks of single-handed cover with repetitive night call, Reid, unannounced, went to see the Medical Officer of Health, Dr. Bob Weir, at his home. He outlined the untenable position and said he and McClure would both resign if a third position was not created. McClure was on holiday and unaware that that he was threatening to resign, but in retrospect agreed with the tactic. To his credit, Weir acknowledged the situation and was able to find the funds to support Halliday’s appointment.

Developments in Neonatal Care

Infants and children had been receiving intensive ventilator care in the Children’s Hospital since 1966, organised by paediatric anaesthetists Harold Love (1920-2010), Gerry Black (1925-2019) and Sam Kielty. However, ventilator treatment in the neonatal units of the Jubilee and RMH did not start until 1978, when Reid and McClure were able to muster the necessary equipment and personnel resources; the addition of which transformed the special care baby nursery into a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). It took some diplomacy to gain acceptance of ventilators in the neonatal units; ultimately the Children’s Hospital staff recognised that the number of neonates needing ventilator care would overwhelm the Children’s ICU. The necessary interhospital cooperation was aided by Reid’s appointment, which included one session a week at the Children’s Hospital ICU.

The 1970s brought major technical advances that improved clinical care of the newborn - particularly very small infants. These included: improved ventilators with methods to deliver continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP); apnoea alarms; infusion pumps; phototherapy; parenteral nutrition and micro methods to assess blood biochemistry, which obviated the need for venipuncture. Micro blood samples permitted measurement of arterial oxygen tension, to be replaced by the even less invasive transcutaneous methods in the 1980s31. Incubators were improved with better temperature control and precise oxygen delivery. Transport incubators for interhospital transfer were developed. Cot-side ultrasound, particularly of the brain, heart and lungs, led to earlier and more focused care of each infant.

By the late-1970s, other than for invasive procedures, staff masks and gowns were abandoned. More important was the change allowing parents, formerly confined to looking through the corridor windows, access to the units to see and touch their babies. This improved communication with the medical staff and gave nurses the opportunity to ‘translate’ some of the medical terminology for the parents. Another important step was the creation of a separate baby chart; confirming that babies were patients in their own right. This also facilitated long term follow up and it was adopted province wide.

Respiratory Distress Syndrome and Surfactant

In 1967 Reid and colleagues reviewed the outcome of all premature infants (<2500gm) looked after at the Royal Maternity Hospital neonatal nursery over the twelve-year period, 1952-1963. There were 3829 such infants with a survival rate of 83.1%32. The commonest cause of death (35.6%) was ‘respiratory disorders’. Comparing the two six-year epochs, 1952-1957 and 1958-1963, there was no significant difference in the mortality rates, reflecting the relative lack of advances in neonatal care during that period.

Most premature infants who die a few days after birth show hyaline membrane disease (HMD) of the lungs at post-mortem examination. Because HMD is a pathological diagnosis, the term respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) came into clinical use33. In 1959, it was shown that HMD was due to a deficiency of surfactant in lung fluid34. The assessment of fetal lung maturity via amniotic fluid analysis using the ratio of lecithin to sphingomyelin (L/S ratio) was introduced by the American pioneer neonatologist, Louis Gluck (1924-1997)35. As a proxy for adequate neonatal lung surfactant this test came into common use as a guide for induction of labour by obstetricians.

The next logical step was to attempt surfactant replacement via the endotracheal tube in premature infants with RDS. A variety of synthetic and animal lung-derived products were tried and after early setbacks there was success with a calf lung extract36. There followed several randomised controlled trials (RCT); the main contenders in the United States being the synthetic product Exosurf and the calf lung-derived Survanta.

In Belfast, Halliday led the group’s RCT of a locally produced synthetic surfactant, Turfsurf, also known as the ‘Belfast surfactant’. Unfortunately, the results were disappointing37.

Halliday, however, developed contacts with the Karolinska University Hospital in Stockholm. In 1984 he spent a month there and was impressed by the results of animal studies with their porcine-derived surfactant, Curosurf. Belfast’s advantage was the provision of a large clinical base for enrolment in the subsequent European RCTs of Curosurf. The results were very successful and the impact of exogenous surfactant on reducing the morbidity and mortality of RDS has been enormous38,39.

Perinatal Collaboration

In the 1970s, with the advent of amniocentesis, ultrasound and fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring, obstetricians started to regard the fetus as a patient whose well-being could be assessed and occasionally treated. Induction of labour increased and ultrasound enabled the diagnosis of some fetal anomalies, ensuring that plans for the appropriate place of delivery and early neonatal treatment could be put in place.

The relationship between obstetrics and neonatology strengthened in the 1970s/1980s. Jack Pinkerton (1920-2013), professor of obstetrics from 1963 to 1985 was keen to promote perinatal research. Two junior obstetric lecturers with additional perinatal training, Knox Ritchie (1944-2022) and Jim Dornan (1948-2021), provided the main impetus for collaborative clinical endeavour and research. One result of this collaboration was a review of the provision and outcome of neonatal care in the province, which was published in the British Medical Journal in 197840. It detailed the poor state of neonatal care in Northern Ireland, where there were no less than 42 units of variable size delivering babies. Moreover, the province had the second highest rate of neonatal death in Europe; a disgraceful distinction in an NHS that prided itself as being one of the best in the world. The paper sparked a reaction at the Department of Health which set up a committee, composed of representatives from obstetrics, paediatrics, midwifery and administration, to advise on the future development and organisation of maternity services. The report (The Advisory Committee on Neonatal Mortality and Handicap, 1979/1980) recommended fewer obstetric units, centralisation of care, enhanced staffing and financing to facilitate these developments. These recommendations were slow to be enacted, as we were warned by the Chief Medical Officer at the time. This slow progress was due to a degree of political cowardice (closing small clinical units is very unpopular), and a failure by medical professionals to adequately explain the advantages of care and delivery in better staffed and equipped units. However, over several decades the number of hospitals providing complete maternity care has fallen from 42 (1978) to seven (2024).

Another major factor leading to improved perinatal outcome was the use of antenatal corticosteroids to reduce the risk of RDS in premature infants, and the subsequent finding of reduced risks of intraventricular haemorrhage and necrotising enterocolitis41.

Over the thirty years, 1962 to 1992, the perinatal mortality rate in Northern Ireland fell from 38/1000 to 8/100042,43.

The 1970s/1980s also saw the expansion of other subspecialties supporting neonatal care. In cardiology, Frank Pantridge (1916-2004) and George Patterson (1927-2013), provided diagnostic and treatment services at the Children’s Hospital since the 1950s. These were refined and made available to the newborn with the appointment in 1976 of Connor Mulholland who, after additional training in Toronto, became the first fulltime paediatric cardiologist. He was later joined by Brian Craig and Frank Casey. Surgical services at the Children’s Hospital were provided by Stephen Brown, Victor Boston and Stephen Potts. (The first ligation of a patent ductus arteriosus was performed in 1947 by Barney Purce (1891-1950), surgeon at the Royal Victoria Hospital44). Neonatal imaging was made possible by portable X-ray equipment in the 1960s and, since 1968, this was overseen by Paul Thomas. He was later joined by Louise Sweeney with expertise in ultrasound. Other contributors to the understanding and development of neonatal medicine included the anatomist, Cowan Meban, who assisted with the early surfactant studies, and John Edgar Morison (1912-2007), with his extensive and detailed work on perinatal pathology45-47.

Nursing

Most medical innovation and progress stand on the shoulders of the nursing staff. In all the units we worked in the nurses were keen to innovate and improve the care of their babies. Without their sustained commitment and willingness to adopt and with skill and compassion apply the new technical advances, the improved neonatal outcomes would not have been possible. It is impossible to name all the leading nursing figures who worked in neonatal intensive care but those such as Marie Crothers, Annemarie Donaghy and Elsie Faux in the Jubilee; Liz Marks, Phil Farrell, Norma Sittlington and Pauline Armstrong in the RMH; and Sadie Greer and Liz Douglas at the Ulster Hospital are a few names among many who deserve full-hearted praise.

Continuing Professional Development

The trio of new neonatologists decided to produce a text on neonatal intensive care to assist the nursing and junior medical staff working in this new specialty. Thus, in 1981, the first edition of Handbook of Neonatal Intensive Care by HL Halliday, BG McClure and M McC Reid was published by Bailliere, Tindall and Cox (Figure 7). The book was a success and further editions followed in 1984, 1989 and 1998. The last edition was co-edited by Angela Bell and Richard Tubman – former trainees. An Italian edition was published in 1990.

Hand Book of Neonatal Intensive care

In the 1960s paediatric training entailed years of junior hospital posts, but the required postgraduate examination could only be taken in adult medicine at one of the Royal Colleges of Medicine. It was not until 1999 that the newly established Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health was able to administer a complete postgraduate exam in paediatrics. So, the first three neonatologists took their royal college certification in adult medicine.

Junior doctors in the postgraduate paediatric training programme rotated through the Belfast NICUs from the 1970s. Some chose a career in neonatology, and over the last twenty years of the twentieth century they produced several MD theses and a plethora of papers on neonatal medicine, including: surfactant trials, nutrition regimes, iron metabolism, infants of diabetic mothers, gastric aspiration, chronic lung disease, energy expenditure and EEG studies48-53. Some of the ‘graduates’ of the programme include: Angela Bell and Anne Black who set up the NICU in the Ulster Hospital to care for all but the smallest of babies. Richard Tubman became the fourth neonatologist at the Jubilee and RMH in 1993 and established a regional neonatal transport service. Desi Brown, at Altnagelvin Hospital, developed a computer-based system to link infants with possible cardiac problems with cardiologists in Belfast and John Jenkins, along with Jarleth McAloon and Joe Lim, developed the NICU at the Antrim Area Hospital.

By the early 2000s, with the closure of smaller maternity units, there were seven hospitals providing full maternity care. The South Western Hospital in Enniskillen and Daisy Hill in Newry had special care baby units; the other five hospitals (Antrim, Craigavon, Derry, Ulster, and the now Royal Jubilee Maternity Hospital) had NICUs. The number of neonatologists expanded from the original three in 1979 to more than twenty. In 2000, the decision was taken to amalgamate the two units in central Belfast, Jubilee and RMH, after a public airing of the relative merits of both hospitals in the media. The decisive factor was the proximity to the Children’s Hospital, and twenty years later the new Royal Jubilee Maternity Hospital was built on the Royal site.

Neonatal Associations

In 1976, Peter Dunn (1929-2021) the first neonatologist in Bristol, wrote to all the teaching hospitals in Britain and Ireland and found only twenty-one paediatricians who devoted a majority of their work to neonatology. This group of twenty-one, including Garth McClure and Mark Reid, became the founding members of the British Paediatric Perinatal Group, which had its first meeting in Bristol in 1976. In 1985 it became the British Association of Perinatal Medicine and had expanded to include a multidisciplinary group of obstetricians, midwives, neonatal nurses, obstetric anaesthetists and paediatric surgeons54.

The Irish Perinatal Society (later to be called the Irish Neonatal Society) was established in 1976. Garth McClure and Mark Reid were founder members, and Reid was elected president in 1979. This was one of the Irish medical societies founded and maintained during the ‘troubles’ which helped foster collaboration and friendship during this fraught political era.

The trio of early neonatologists were made honorary members of the Ulster Obstetrical and Gynaecological Society and became frequent contributors to the scientific programmes.

In Belfast, neonatal mortality has fallen from 40/1000 in 1950, to 28/1000 in 1970, to just over 3/1000 today32,40,42,43. Factors other than medical care have contributed to this, including: better housing, socio-economic conditions and smaller families. From the midwifery and obstetric viewpoint there are three morbid intrapartum factors that should be avoided during the transition from fetus to neonate: infection, hypoxia and trauma. With modern neonatal care, if these three factors can be avoided, the outlook for long-term infant survival without disability is very good. In the 1960s, when we started as junior doctors in the RMH, babies born in the 28 to 34 week gestation group had little or no chance of survival (births less than 28 weeks were classified as ‘abortion’ or ‘miscarriage’) – now, most are assured of long-term intact survival.

The basic principles of neonatology were and remain: keep the baby warm, pink, fed and clean. The big change with modern neonatal intensive care is that this has become feasible for very small infants – less than 24 weeks gestation. Pushing the boundaries of the age of viability continues, with efforts to keep these infants in a stable physiological milieu. The main worry is the potential for long-term disability in these tiny patients. Achieving a balance between what is possible and what is appropriate remains a significant ethical issue.
