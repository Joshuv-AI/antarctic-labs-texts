A reference framework for extremely metal-poor OB star studies: calibrations for stellar parameters and intrinsic colours 2024 A reference framework for extremely metal-poor OB star studies: calibrations for stellar parameters and intrinsic colours – B
M. Lorenzo, M. Garcia, N. Castro, F. Najarro M. Cerviño, A. Herrero, S. Simón-Díaz

Abstract
We provide the first reference framework for extremely metal-poor (XMP) OB-type stars. We parsed a grid of 0.10 Z ⊙ Z_{\odot} fastwind models, covering the parameter space of O stars and early-B supergiants, through contemporary spectral classification criteria to deliver a calibration of key stellar properties as a function of spectral type, and tabulated colours for the most common photometric systems. By using an extensive grid of models, we account for the different combinations of stellar parameters that result in the same spectral morphology and provide a range of parameters and colours compatible with each spectral subtype and luminosity class. We supply updated photometric criteria to optimize candidate selection of OB stars in XMP environments. We find 0.10 Z ⊙ Z_{\odot} OB stars are 1-6 kK hotter and produce higher ionizing fluxes than their Galactic analogues. In addition, we find a bimodal distribution of the He ii -ionizing flux with spectral type; because of its known dependence on effective temperature and the wind, log q HeII q_{\mathrm{HeII}} for individual XMP late-O type stars could be underestimated by up to 4 orders of magnitude by other calibrations, some of them used by population synthesis codes. Finally, we used our calibrated colours to map the extinction of the 0.10 Z ⊙ Z_{\odot} galaxy Sextans A finding that reddening is non-negligible and uneven.

Introduction

In a Universe whose average metal content has been ever-growing since the Big Bang, the study of extremely metal-poor
(
Kunth & Östlin 2000
, XMP, defined as
\leq
0.10
Z_{\odot}
by, e.g., )
massive stars is critical to understanding earlier cosmic epochs
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
)
.
Their characterization is the missing link towards the first, metal-free stars of the Universe, the early chemical enrichment detected at redshifts up to
z

\sim
10
(
Bunker et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, and the reionization epoch, all processes in which XMP massive stars may have played a significant role according to recent observations from the James Webb Space Telescope
(
Endsley et al. 2022
;
Topping et al. 2022
;
Atek et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Our Local Group (LG) and vicinity host star-forming dwarf galaxies with an extreme paucity of metals and resolved stellar populations, offering a unique opportunity to determine the stellar properties of XMP massive stars.
In the future, these properties will be entered into population synthesis and photoionization codes in order to better interpret the observations at
z>
2
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
)
.
However, despite the current efforts to characterize local XMP massive stars
(
Camacho et al. 2016
;
Ramachandran et al. 2021
;
Gull et al. 2022
;
Telford et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, their study is still in its infancy because of i) the small number of high-quality observations and ii) the lack of reference material to guide the studies.

In particular, there are no calibrations of stellar parameters or magnitudes and colours for XMP massive stars as a function of spectral type.
In the absence of such scales, authors usually resort to calibrations for the Milky Way (MW) or the Magellanic Clouds (MC)
(
Martins et al. 2005a
;
Martins & Plez 2006
;
Ramachandran et al. 2019
, such as)
. However, both theory and observations have shown the strong dependency between some stellar properties and metallicity.

Effective temperature (
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
) is one of the most affected parameters due to
line-blanketing
.
A fraction of the flux produced in the stellar interior is blocked by absorption lines in the photosphere, resulting in a steeper
temperature gradient in the inner atmosphere to preserve the total stellar luminosity
(
Pauldrach et al. 2001
;
Lanz & Hubeny 2003
, e.g. )
.
Since most absorption lines are produced by metals due to their more intricate atomic structures, metal-poor stars experience less
line-blanketing
and consequently, need higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
to achieve the same ionization state than their metal-rich analogues, impacting the relation between
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and spectral type (SpT).
This shift in the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
- SpT relation is already measurable in regimes with a slightly decreased metallicity.
The O stars of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC, 0.50
Z_{\odot}
) are 1
\sim
kK hotter than their Galactic counterparts
(
Massey et al. 2004
;
Mokiem et al. 2007a
;
Ramírez-Agudelo et al. 2017
;
Sabín-Sanjulián et al. 2017
, e.g., )
, and the difference increases to 3-4 kK
(
Massey et al. 2004
;
Mokiem et al. 2006
;
Trundle et al. 2007
;
Ramachandran et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
in the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC, 0.20
Z_{\odot}
).
The hotter temperatures impact on their spectral energy distributions (SED), making their colours bluer and their
V
-magnitudes fainter.

Evans et al. 2019
estimated that the absolute visual magnitudes of XMP O stars at the Zero Age Main Sequence (ZAMS) could be 0.5 mag fainter than their metal-rich analogues with the same mass.
Metal-poor massive stars are also more compact, resulting in higher surface gravities (log
g
)
(
Yoon et al. 2006
;
Ekström et al. 2008
)
.
Lastly, because absorption and re-emission of photons by metal lines are the main drivers of radiation-driven winds, metal-poor massive stars are expected to experience weaker mass and angular momentum loss during their evolution
(
Kudritzki et al. 1987
;
Vink et al. 2001
;
Mokiem et al. 2007b
)
.
The different wind behaviour can affect the luminosity of the stars and their SED.

The aims of this work are to construct the first calibration of stellar parameters and colours for XMP O stars and B supergiants.
Because the current sample of known XMP OB stars is reduced, it does not uniformly cover all spectral subtypes
(e.g.
Lorenzo et al. 2022
, hereafter L22)
, and the spectral quality of the data is low, we based this work on a grid of 0.10
Z_{\odot}
,
stellar atmosphere models.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we present our grid of
fastwind
stellar atmosphere models,
and assign a spectral type to each of them.
In Sect.
3
, we obtain calibrations of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and ionizing fluxes as a function of spectral type, and discuss the results. Likewise, we tabulate magnitudes and colours in Sect.
4
.
As an example of scientific exploitation, we build an extinction map of the 0.10
Z_{\odot}
dwarf galaxy Sextans A in Sect.
5
, using our calibration and the spectroscopic sample of XMP massive stars from
44
.
Finally, a summary and general conclusions are given in Sect.
6
.

2
A grid of
fastwind
models to calibrate the stellar parameters of XMP OB stars

To build the theoretical calibrations of stellar parameters and photometry of XMP OB stars, we computed an extensive grid of stellar
atmosphere models described in Sect.
2.1
.
We processed the synthetic spectra to match the observations of spectral standards and classified them (Sect.
2.2
),
and then we discarded unrealistic models from the grid (Sect.
2.3
). The complete process is outlined in Figure
1
.

2.1
Grid description

The grid covers the parameter space of XMP OB-type stars with 13700 models.
We calculated them using
fastwind
(version 10.6), a stellar atmosphere code that accounts for line-blanketing and NLTE effects in "unified", extended and spherically-symmetric atmospheres
(
Santolaya-Rey et al. 1997
;
Puls et al. 2005
;
Rivero González et al. 2012
)
.

fastwind
can provide high volumes of synthetic spectra in reduced periods of time, an essential feature to building our comprehensive grid. In addition, this code provides the continuum of the spectral energy distribution from 20 to 10
7
Å, allowing us to calculate the synthetic photometry and production of ionizing flux of the models.

The grid was computed using detailed model atoms for hydrogen and helium, and treating carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, silicon and magnesium explicitly in the formal solution. All other elements
(
Pauldrach et al. 1998
;
Pauldrach et al. 2001
, those included in the full comprehensive line list of)
were treated as background elements (i.e., allowing them to contribute to line-blocking/blanketing but not calculating their spectral line profiles).

The models have 0.10
Z_{\odot}
metallicity
(
Asplund et al. 2009
, scaled using the solar abundances from)
.
We do not consider any departures from the chemical abundance pattern of the Sun, nor any element enrichment due to stellar evolution.
The grid covers effective temperatures from 15 to 57 kK and surface gravities from 1.6 to 4.4 dex (depending on
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
) in steps of 0.5 kK and 0.1 dex respectively.
The ranges were tailored to cover objects with spectral types between
\sim
O2-B3 and the main luminosity classes (I, III, V, in the case of O stars and class I for the B-types).
In Fig.

, we show the distribution of our grid in the Kiel diagram, along with the evolutionary tracks from
Szécsi et al. 2022
for rotational velocity of 100

and metallicity of 0.10

We initially allowed the wind strength parameter,
defined as
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}=\dot{M}/(R_{*}v_{\infty})^{1.5}

(
Puls et al. 1996
)

where
\dot{M}
represents the mass loss rate (

),

km s
-1
), to range from
log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
= -15 dex
to
log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
= -11.7 dex
.
Although we expect weak winds at the considered 0.10
Z_{\odot}
metallicity, we kept this parameter free in the complete grid
and later selected models according to the expected physical properties of XMP OB stars (Sect.
2.3
).
We also considered smooth, stationary winds with no clumping nor X-ray emission from wind embedded shocks.
The exponent of the wind-velocity law (
\beta
) and the helium abundance (
Y_{\mathrm{He}}
) were kept constant to values of 1.00 and 0.10, respectively.
The microturbulence (
\xi
) for the atmosphere models was set according to log
g
and then was varied from 1 to 30
km s
-1
in the formal solution.

2.2
Spectral classification of the grid models

We assigned spectral type (SpT) and luminosity class (LC) to each model following standard spectral classification schemes for O and B stars

(
Sota et al. 2011
;
Negueruela et al. 2024
, e.g.)
,
adapted to 0.10
Z_{\odot}
metallicity following
Lennon 1997
’s strategy (see Lorenzo et al. in prep).
We previously processed the synthetic spectra to match the
observations of
Sota et al. 2011
’s standard stars, with spectral resolution
R
= 2500 and typical projected rotational velocities of
v\sin i
= 70
km s
-1
(
Sota et al. 2011
;
Holgado et al. 2018
)
. No noise was introduced (but see below).

Assigning spectral subtypes to the grid models in an automatic way required to
first find criteria deciding if key diagnostic lines were detected.
To define the detectability limit, we visually assessed at which intensity with respect to the local continuum (
I_{\mathrm{\lambda}}
=
F_{\mathrm{minimum}}
-
F_{\mathrm{continuum}}
) the lines would no longer be detected.
To this end, we plotted the He

4541 transition with different line-intensities, as shown in Fig.

We then convolved these profiles to
R
= 2500 and
v\sin i
= 70
km s
-1
and introduced zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation (
\sigma
) equal to the inverse of a given signal-to-noise ratio (S/N).
Finally, we smoothed the data with a kernel width of five points to replicate the procedure followed in the spectral classification of poor S/N observations
(
Garcia & Herrero 2013
;
Camacho et al. 2016
;
Lorenzo et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. We applied the procedure for different line intensities and inspected visually whether the line was detected or not.

We repeated this method for different values of S/N to determine its impact on the detectability limit. Figure
4
shows the minimum intensity at which lines are detected for different values of S/N using different random seeds to simulate noise.
By fitting the trend, we approximated that the detectability limit roughly follows S/N as
I_{\mathrm{\lambda}}
= 1/(S/N) or, equivalently,
I_{\mathrm{\lambda}}
=
\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the noise at the continuum.

During the process of classifying the grid,
we adopted the detectability limit corresponding to S/N = 50 sp. pix. for O stars.
With this S/N, we can detect lines with intensities
I_{\mathrm{\lambda}}

\gtrsim
0.02.
The extremely low metallicity of the grid severely hampers the classification of B-type stars, particularly at low S/N and resolution, as it relies on Si and Mg lines that are weaker than the He lines. Therefore, we assessed the detectability limit at S/N = 100 sp. pix., i.e.
I_{\mathrm{\lambda}}
= 0.01, for them.
Because B supergiants are usually brighter than O stars, it is reasonable to expect that higher S/N will be achieved with the same observational set-up.

Finally, we adopted one single value of
\xi
per model,
\xi
= 5 kms
-1
for
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
typical of O stars
and
\xi
= 10 kms
-1
for B supergiants. In Appendix
A
, we discuss that the assigned spectral classification using a higher value of microturbulence for the same model would not differ by more than 0.5 spectral subtypes.

2.3
Selection of models with realistic parameters

Our grid of
fastwind
models serves an additional purpose to perform automatic
\chi^{2}
spectral fitting of observed spectra of XMP OB stars in future works.
In order to minimize edge effects (i.e. the
\chi^{2}
distribution being truncated by the coverage limits), the parameter space is broader than observed from real stars.
For this work, we needed to discard unrealistic models by filtering according to the expected physical properties of the stars.

First, we discarded models with flux conservation errors larger than 3%.

Secondly, we selected models that follow the
modified wind momentum - luminosity relationship
(WLR),

\log D_{\mathrm{mom}}=\log\left(\dot{M}v_{\infty}\sqrt{R/R_{\odot}}\right)=\log D_{\mathrm{0}}+x\log L/L_{\odot}.

(1)

This equation expresses that the mechanical wind momentum of massive stars (
D_{\mathrm{mom}}
) is mainly a function of photon momentum
(
Kudritzki et al. 1995
;
Kudritzki & Puls 2000
, e.g.,)
, translating into a direct dependence on stellar luminosity. The slope (
x
) and intercept (
\log D_{\mathrm{0}}
) vary according to metallicity because metal atoms receive the energy and momentum from photons and carry the wind
(
Kudritzki et al. 1999
;
Vink et al. 2000
;
Puls et al. 2000
)
.

We scaled the intercept of the empirical, unclumped WLR found by
Mokiem et al. 2007b
for stars in the SMC to the metallicity of the grid (0.10
Z_{\odot}
) using the
Z
-dependence relations of mass-loss and terminal velocity given by
Mokiem et al. 2007b
and
Leitherer et al. 1992

(
Hawcroft et al. 2024
, see also)
, respectively.
For luminosities larger than
\log L/L_{\odot}
= 5.6 dex, we accepted all models that followed the WLR allowing for
\pm
1 dex departures to reflect the typical scatter of observational points.
For lower luminosities, we also allowed for models reflecting the weak wind problem.
Weak winds have been detected at
\log L/L_{\odot}\leq
5.2 dex in the Milky Way
(
Martins et al. 2005b
;
Marcolino et al. 2009
)
and at
\leq
5.4 dex in the SMC
(
Bouret et al. 2003
)
, thus we conservatively adapted to the metallicity of the grid and set the luminosity threshold for weak winds to
\leq
5.6 dex.

To check for consistency, we alternatively selected models following the WLR and
\dot{M}\propto Z
scale by
Björklund et al. 2021
.
The selection of models was equivalent to those obtained with
Mokiem et al. 2007b
’s when allowing for
\pm
0.5 dex in the intercept of the WLR.
We note that the different allowed range with respect to
Mokiem et al. 2007b
’s is caused by the different units of
D_{\mathrm{mom}}
used in the two works.

We performed a third selection of models based on the Eddington factor
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
, which evaluates the proximity to the Eddington limit calculated considering that opacity is only due to electron scattering. We used the expression given by
Gräfener et al. 2011
,

\log\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}=-4.813+\log(1+X_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{s}})+\log(L/L_{\odot})-\log(M/M_{\odot}),

(2)

where
X_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{s}}
corresponds to the hydrogen mass fraction at the stellar surface.
For the O stars, we defined an upper limit of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
= 0.55, which is based on the maximum
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
measured in O4-6 supergiants with solar metallicity by
Gräfener et al. 2011
and the results of
Holgado et al. 2018
.
For the B stars, we used the same value, 0.55, based on the results of spectroscopic analysis of B supergiants in the MW and other galaxies of the LG
(
Urbaneja et al. 2002
;
Evans et al. 2004
;
Crowther et al. 2006
, etc)
.

To calculate the mass, luminosity,
\dot{M}
,
v_{\infty}
and
\log D_{\mathrm{mom}}
of the models classified as O stars,
we used the calibrated radii for each spectral subtype of
Martins et al. 2005a
. Radii were subsequently scaled to account for the metallicity difference by considering the calibrated
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for the same spectral type at each
Z
and assuming constant luminosity
(
Evans et al. 2019
, as discussed by)
.
This procedure was done iteratively.
We first adopted
Martins et al. 2005a
’s radii for the spectral types assigned to the models. Then,
we accepted the models based on their
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
and WLR location as explained above,
and obtained a first value of typical
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for each spectral type.
On a second iteration, we accounted for metallicity by scaling the radii based on the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
differences we found between
Martins et al. 2005a
’s MW calibration and our
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale from the first iteration and again selected the models that complied with the
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
and WLR constraints.
This procedure was repeated until the averaged
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for each spectral type remained constant in consecutive iterations.
According to these calculations, a 0.10
Z_{\odot}
O dwarf would be 20% more compact than its Galactic analogue, whereas the radii of O giants and supergiants would be 10% smaller.

Lacking calibrations for B supergiants, we performed a linear regression between the radii and the spectral subtypes determined in spectroscopic analysis of B supergiants
(
Urbaneja et al. 2002
;
Evans et al. 2004
;
Crowther et al. 2006
, etc)
and assigned those.

Finally, we accounted for the large uncertainty of the assigned radii by allowing variations of up to 20% from the central value and propagated these variations to the quantities derived using this parameter.
Therefore, each model with a given (
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, log
g
) was assigned three different radii.
If none of the derived quantities for the three radii (
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
and WLR location) met our physical filters, we discarded the model.

3
Calibration of stellar parameters with spectral type

After assigning a spectral type to the distilled grid models,
we used them to produce calibrations of physical properties as a function of spectral type.
To that aim, we averaged the stellar parameters of all realistic models whose spectral morphology was compatible with a given spectral type.
By leaving all considered parameters free and not fixing them to a specific value in each (SpT, LC) pair, we better reflect the existing degeneracy in reproducing the same He ionization equilibrium (i.e. SpT) and width of the Balmer lines (i.e. LC) when combining different stellar parameters (
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, log
g
and log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
).
Thus, this is the first theoretical calibration that considers that several combinations of stellar parameters can yield the same spectral morphology of a given (SpT, LC) pair.
Our methodology not only allowed us to provide a more realistic average of the stellar properties for each subtype but also the range of values compatible with a given morphology as seen, for example, in Figure
6
for
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Table
1
collects the calibrated stellar parameters for each spectral subtype and luminosity class.
For
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, log
g
, log
q_{\mathrm{HI}}
and log
q_{\mathrm{HeI}}
(defined below), we provide the mean, minimum and maximum values calculated over all the models classified with the same spectral subtype and luminosity class.
For log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
(also defined below), we only include an upper limit (see discussion in Sect.
3.2.1
).
For all other parameters, such as radius, luminosity and ionizing photon production rates, we provide the average.

We excluded subtypes O3 and earlier from the calibration because
typical observations of XMP OB stars cannot detect N
iii
4640 and N
iv
4058 lines (Lorenzo et al. in prep),
hence they cannot provide types earlier than O4.
Indeed, effective temperature is largely undetermined for these objects
(
Garcia & Herrero 2013
, e.g. GHV-62024,)
unless
observations
extend to the space ultraviolet (e.g. Furey et al. in prep).
In addition, since
fastwind
does not provide reliable solutions below 15 kK (Puls, priv. comm), we only consider the spectral subtypes above this temperature (B3 or earlier).

We caution the reader that the physical properties that require the stellar radii were calculated using calibrated values (O stars), or estimates for analogue stars by previous works (B stars), as explained in Sect.
2.3
.
These quantities are provided in Table

3.1
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale

Accurately characterizing the stellar effective temperature
scale of OB stars is fundamental to linking spectral types with physical parameters. Several works have calculated the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale in the MW and MCs
(
Crowther et al. 2002
;
Martins et al. 2005a
;
Massey et al. 2005
;
Trundle et al. 2007
;
Massey et al. 2009
;
McEvoy et al. 2015
;
Markova et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. The works by
Garcia & Herrero 2013
,
Tramper et al. 2014
and
Camacho et al. 2016
opened the way in the sub-SMC metallicity regime but an extensive calibration does not yet exist.

Figure 6:
Effective temperature of all the models as a function of their spectral subtype for different luminosity classes. The black solid lines are the linear fits for O stars excluding the O9.7 subtype. The dashed black line is the linear fit for the early-B supergiants.
We present a histogram on the right side of each panel. Each point represents the number of models with a given effective temperature that are compatible with the same colour-coded spectral subtype, normalized to the maximum of all distributions for visualization purposes.

Figure
6
shows the effective temperature of all our 0.10
Z_{\odot}

fastwind
models as a function of spectral type for different luminosity classes.
For a given spectral subtype, the models exhibit a range of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
with some overlap between adjacent subtypes (as reflected by Table
1
).
As explained earlier, various parameter combinations can replicate the same spectral morphology, leading to dispersion in the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale.
This is illustrated in the histograms of the right subpanels; they show the number of models with a given
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for each subtype, normalized by the maximum of all distributions.
These distributions demonstrate a continuous variation of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
within spectral type, and smooth transitions between subtypes. Assessing these histograms and minimizing numerical asymmetries drove us to use a fine grid of models with
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
-steps of 0.5 kK. However, we note that we do not expect uncertainties below 1 kK even in high-precision spectroscopic analyses
(
Holgado et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.

The distributions are roughly centred around the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
assigned to each subtype.
In addition, some subtypes are less populated than others, which reflects they occupy a smaller fraction of the considered parameter space. We note that these distributions do not represent the frequency of the subtypes in nature.

We built the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale by fitting the points corresponding to all the models using a least squares method, providing separate
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scales for O and B stars, and for the different luminosity classes.
In this linear fit, we did not consider subtype O9.7 because, as discussed in
Martins et al. 2005a
,
the increment of this spectral subtype is not proportional to the corresponding He
i
4471/He
ii
4541 increment.
While the difference between O9.5 and O9.7 is only 0.2 in subtype, the line ratio increment is larger than the one between O8.5 and O9.
Therefore, including O9.7 in the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scale would lead to artificially lower effective temperatures.
For this subtype, we refer the reader to Table
1
.

The linear fits for the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scales are shown in Figure
6
and are expressed as follows,

\mbox{$T_{\mathrm{eff}}$}~(\mathrm{kK})=\begin{cases}62.45\pm 2.05~-3.10~\times~\text{SpT}&\quad\text{(O4 - O9.5~V)}\\
58.38\pm 1.62~-2.97~\times~\text{SpT}&\quad\text{(O4 - O9.5~III)}\\
53.85\pm 1.25~-2.61~\times~\text{SpT}&\quad\text{(O4 - O9.5~I)}\\
65.92\pm 1.69~-3.89~\times~\text{SpT}&\quad\text{(B0 - B3~I)}\\
\end{cases}.

(3)

In these and the following relations, the spectral subtype is represented by a number. O stars range from 4 to 9.5 according to their subtype, while B stars span from 10 to 13.
We included uncertainties in the intercept in all equations. They quantify the dispersion of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
within subtypes and luminosity classes, and express the distributions presented in Fig.
6
, and the minimum and maximum values included in Table
1
.
The provided uncertainties encompass
90% of the models of the grid.

As expected and reflected by other calibrations, given a spectral subtype dwarfs have higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
than giants, and giants are hotter than supergiants.
In addition, we observe that the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
dispersion for O dwarfs is larger than for other classes. This is due to the wider range of log
g
compatible with this luminosity class.
The
\sim
2 kK dispersion of our calibration for dwarfs is in agreement with the scatter seen in observational
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
scales of different environments
(
Simón-Díaz et al. 2014
;
Sabín-Sanjulián et al. 2017
, e.g., )
. The
\sim
1 kK dispersion for giants and supergiants is also consistent with observations
(
Ramírez-Agudelo et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
.

The

3.2
Ionizing photons

Massive stars are key sources of ionization within star-forming regions. Quantifying their production of ionizing photons not only enables studying their feedback but can also be used to calculate the fraction of ionizing photons that escape from a region or galaxy. This quantity is especially interesting in extremely low-
Z
environments as it helps us understand the role of massive stars in the epoch of reionization.

The ionizing flux is calculated by integrating the SED up to the ionization energy,

q_{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{x}}\frac{\lambda F(\lambda)}{hc}d\lambda,

(4)

where
F(\lambda)
is the stellar flux at one stellar radius and
\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}
is the ionization wavelength of the atomic species x.

We calculated the ionizing flux of H
i

(
\lambda_{\mathrm{HI}}
= 911 Å)
and He
i

(
\lambda_{\mathrm{HeI}}
= 504 Å)

for each model of our grids and provided the average (plus minimum and maximum) for each subtype in Table
1
, estimated from all the models with the same assigned spectral subtype and luminosity class (as in Section
3.1
).
We computed the ionizing flux instead of the total photon production rate to avoid scaling by the radius.
Nonetheless, in Table
1
, we also provide the average of the photon production rate (
Q_{\mathrm{x}}
) using calibrated radii (O stars) or radii derived from analogue stars in previous works (B stars). The ionizing flux of He
ii

(
\lambda_{\mathrm{HeII}}
= 228 Å)
will be discussed separately in the next section.

There are few direct estimates of the ionizing photon production of massive stars at extremely low metallicities.
Ramachandran et al. 2021
determined log

for two metal-poor O stars in the Magellanic Bridge (0.20-0.02

Telford et al. 2023
recently calculated the H

) from the nebular emission lines of the H

In Figure

did not estimate the radius of the O7-8 V star, thus we used the average of our calibrated radii for O7 and O8 dwarfs and propagated the radius uncertainty to the error bars of the ionizing flux of this star.
Our calibration is in agreement with both studies.
For the particular case of the Leo P star, our results support
Telford et al. 2023
’s hypothesis that no ionizing photons are leaking from the nebula.

Finally, we discuss whether the estimated ionizing fluxes depend on the stellar atmosphere code used.
Although

does not explicitly include the detailed atomic models of some species, as for instance

(

)
, it does provide an approximate treatment of the full comprehensive line list inherited from WM-basic in the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) and shorter wavelengths
(
Pauldrach et al. 2001
)
.
Because of the severe absorption by interstellar H photons at
\lambda<

column density sight-lines,
only a few stars have been observed in the EUV
(

;
Cassinelli et al. 1994

and
there are scarce observational constraints
on this part of the SED.
However, we estimated that the impact on the resulting ionizing fluxes is not significant. In Figure

and 0.20 dex for log

and log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
.

with

only small differences (

despite

3.2.1
The He
ii
ionizing flux

The log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
scale of O stars exhibits a bimodal distribution in the mid- and late-O types (see Fig.
9
).
In this section, we discuss that this behaviour is a consequence of known the dependence of
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
on
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\dot{M}

(
Schmutz & Hamann 1986
;
Gabler et al. 1989
;
Schaerer & de Koter 1997
, e.g.,)
.

The flux capable of ionizing He
ii
depends on the available photons (
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, log
L
) and on the population of the ground state of this ion.
The more populated the ground state is, the higher the continuum opacity at wavelengths shorter than
\lambda_{\mathrm{HeII}}
, resulting in a reduction of
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
. The population of the ground state is primarily determined by
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, wind density (
\dot{M}
) and log
g
.

For a negligible wind outflow, equivalent to a hydrostatic atmosphere, the radiation field drives the population of the ground state. Thus, the behaviour of
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
as a function of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
or spectral type is similar to
q_{H}
and
q_{\mathrm{HeI}}
.
As
\dot{M}
increases, the wind velocity field desaturates the resonance lines, which enhances the excitation of electrons to the upper levels and depopulates the ground state
(
Gabler et al. 1989
)
.
At a certain
\dot{M}
, however, the wind is so strong that all the resonance lines become saturated and cannot absorb more photons. If
\dot{M}
continues increasing, the ground state will become overpopulated by the enhanced recombination rate, significantly increasing the opacity of the continuum and leading to a strong reduction of the He
ii
-ionizing flux
(
Schaerer & de Koter 1997
)
. The same process also impacts the He
i
-ionizing continuum of early B giants
(
Najarro et al. 1996
)
.

The
\dot{M}
-threshold that defines the transition between resonance lines and recombinations as dominant drivers of the ground-state He
ii
population depends mainly on the effective temperature of the star
(
Schmutz & Hamann 1986
)
.
At higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the He
ii
is almost fully ionized into He
iii
, hence strong
\dot{M}
as high as measured in Wolf-Rayet stars would be needed to repopulate the ground state of He
ii
. Conversely, the number of available ionizing photons is significantly reduced at lower
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
(Wien side of the SED) and even with weak winds the lines will saturate and the He
ii
ground state will become overpopulated.

Figure

illustrates this effect. We show the He

-ionizing flux as a function of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for different values of log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
in our grid.
XMP early-O stars are always in the regime dominated by resonance lines. Thus, the He
ii
-ionizing flux is slightly higher as the wind is stronger, with differences of less than 1 dex.
The transition between a high and low production of He
ii
-ionizing photons occurs at the mid- and late-types, which may lead to differences of log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
of up to 4 dex between the same spectral subtype.
The dependence of
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
on the wind strength explains the bimodal distribution seen in Fig.
9
, since for a given spectral type and luminosity class our collection includes both models with negligible wind outflows (
log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
= -15 dex
) and with weak and moderate winds (
log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
\geq
-14 dex
).

In Figure
14
, the transition between a high and low production of He
ii
-ionizing photons shifts to higher temperatures as
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
increases.
An analogue behaviour was detected by
Martins & Palacios 2021
when they compared the
Q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
/
Q_{H}
ratio for different metallicities. They found that the transition
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
between high and low
Q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
/
Q_{H}
increases with increasing metallicity. This behaviour actually reflects the dependence of
Q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
on the wind strength, which the authors had parameterized as a function of metallicity.

The dependence of
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
on
\dot{M}
imprints large uncertainties on the estimated production of He
ii
-ionizing photons by individual massive stars. If we examine, e.g., an O8 dwarf in Fig.
9
, the star could lie at either side of the bimodal distribution of log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
depending on its stellar parameters.
We now know that winds as weak as
log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}
= -14 dex
already impact
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
(Fig.
14
), hence
\dot{M}
must be accurately determined to estimate
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
from the SED of the best-fitting model.
However, the wind diagnostics in the optical range (Balmer lines and He
ii
4686) are insensitive to these weak wind values (e.g., Lorenzo et al. in prep.). Therefore, additional diagnostics in the UV
(
Garcia et al. 2014
;
Telford et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
or in the NIR
(
Najarro et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
are critical to constrain the wind, and consequently
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
.
Due to the strong dispersion of log
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
within the spectral types, we provide only the maximum values in Table
1
.

Finally, surface gravity also impacts
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
. For constant
\dot{M}
and
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, lower log
g
will result in a larger scale-height, enhancing the escape probability of the ionizing photons and therefore increasing the probability of recombination and impacting the He
ii
continuum. Consequently, the transition between high/low
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
will occur at higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
when log
g
increases.
This is in apparent contradiction with what we observe in Figure
9
, where the bimodal distribution of class III starts at earlier types than in class V.
We note, however, that the impact of log
g
is weaker than the impact of
\dot{M}
and
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, and therefore Fig.
9
rather reflects the different
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
between classes III and V.

Figure
15
illustrates that log
g
has a second-order effect compared to the role of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\dot{M}
.
If the wind strength parameter is enhanced by 0.5 dex, the transition
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
shifts by +2 kK. An increase of 0.5 dex in log
g
only shifts the position of the transition
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
by +1 kK.
We note, however, that outside the log
g
ranges considered in this work, that correspond to
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leq
0.52 for the O stars and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leq
0.55 for the B stars, this parameter could have a stronger impact.

, and in analyses of single-star H

Smith et al. 2002
estimated ionizing fluxes by integrating the SED of a grid of models computed with
wm-basic

(
Pauldrach et al. 2001
)
.
In contrast to our grid,
Smith et al. 2002
assigned one single set of parameters (
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, log
g
,
\dot{M}
, v
∞
) for each spectral type and luminosity class at a given metallicity.
Consequently, as shown in Figure

-ionizing flux.
Their calibration may overestimate
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
from early- and mid-O supergiants.
On the other hand, it underestimates the production of He
ii
-ionizing photons by the more numerous late O dwarfs,
which may also contribute to power nebular He
ii
4686 and He
ii
1640 emission
(
Kehrig et al. 2015
;
Wofford et al. 2021
;
Senchyna et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

There are three key takeaway messages for this section. Firstly, in order to quantify the He
ii
-ionizing flux of O stars all stellar parameters need to be constrained, especially
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\dot{M}
, and calibrations are not reliable. Secondly, this implies that in the case of XMP O stars, observations outside the optical range are needed to reach diagnostics that are sensitive to the expected low
\dot{M}
.
Finally, the stellar grids to be used by population synthesis codes cannot assume one single value of
\dot{M}
because the winds can significantly impact
q_{\mathrm{HeII}}
even if they are weak.

4
Photometric calibration

The grid of
fastwind
models and their SEDs also enabled us to compute calibrations of magnitudes, colours and bolometric corrections for XMP O stars and B supergiants.
Because we usually draw our candidate XMP OB stars from
Massey et al. 2007
’s catalogue
(e.g.,
Camacho et al. 2016
,
44
)
we firstly computed synthetic photometry in the same photometric system: the
UBVRI
filters of the instrument MOSAIC installed at the 4-m Mayall telescope on Kitt Peak National Observatory (KPNO).
In addition, we calculated synthetic photometry in other frequently used filters: the
UBVRIJHK
generic Bessell bands, the
ugri
bands of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SLOAN-SDSS) and a set of optical and UV filters of the Wide-Field Planetary Camera 2 (WFPC2) and the Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) onboard the Hubble Space Telescope (HST).

From the emergent SEDs at the stellar radius [
F(\lambda)
] of each
fastwind
model, we calculated
F_{\mathrm{T}}
,

F_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{\int\lambda F(\lambda)T(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\int\lambda T(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda},

(7)

where
T(\lambda)
is the transmission curve of filter T
1
1

1

The transmission response curves of all filters were downloaded from the Spanish Virtual Observatory
(
Rodrigo et al. 2012
;
Rodrigo & Solano 2020
;
Rodrigo et al. 2024
)
.
.
Then, we computed the absolute magnitude by applying the corresponding zero points (
ZP_{\mathrm{T}}
) and accounting for the radii adopted for each model and a distance of 10 pc,

M_{\mathrm{T}}=-2.5\,\log F_{\mathrm{T}}+2.5\,\log ZP_{\mathrm{T}}-5\log R_{*}+5\log 10~\mathrm{pc}.

(8)

Zero points were calculated with Eq.
7
using the spectrum of Vega (
\alpha
Lyrae)
2
2

2

http://svo2.cab.inta-csic.es/theory/fps/morefiles/vega.dat
.
For the SLOAN-SDSS bands, which are usually in the AB system, we used a reference spectrum of constant flux equal to 3631 Jy.

The bolometric correction in the corresponding band was calculated following the expression,

BC_{\mathrm{T}}=M_{\odot}^{\mathrm{bol}}-M_{\mathrm{T}}-2.5\times\log\frac{L}{L_{\odot}},

(9)

where
M_{\odot}^{\mathrm{bol}}
is the solar bolometric magnitude in filter T.

We provide the calibrated photometric colours, absolute
V
-magnitude and bolometric corrections for the KPNO-MOSAIC system in Table

.

A case example: mapping the extinction of Sextans A

Dwarf irregular galaxies are often assumed to experience negligible and uniform extinction.
However, massive stars in these galaxies enable studying extinction in resolved sightlines and results are challenging this working hypothesis. For instance,
Garcia et al. 2019
measured a colour excess of up to
\mathrm{E}(B-V)
= 0.250 mag towards massive stars in the South of the 0.10
Z_{\odot}
dwarf irregular galaxy Sextans A, a significantly larger value than its foreground extinction
(
Tammann et al. 2011
,
\mathrm{E}(B-V)
fg
= 0.044 mag,)
.
These findings suggest that the local reddening in Sextans A could be non-negligible and uneven.
With a larger sample covering the whole galaxy,
44
showed that the locus of the OB-type stars in the colour-magnitude diagram (CMD) did not match the expected location for their spectral classification, in line with
Garcia et al. 2019
’s results.

Characterizing and mapping the internal extinction of Sextans A is not only useful to search for its massive stellar population or to estimate the completeness of
44
’s catalogue, but it could also aid tracing the molecular gas content of this XMP galaxy.

In the Milky Way and similarly metal-rich environments, the molecular gas is easily traced by CO.
However, the detection of this molecule is challenging in XMP environments, and inferring the molecular gas content from dust emission (from e.g. Herschel observations) is also problematic as the gas-to-dust ratio is poorly calibrated at very low
Z
.

There is only one weak detection of CO (J = 1
\rightarrow
0) in Sextans A with IRAM’s 30-m telescope
(
Shi et al. 2015
)
that has not been confirmed with interferometric observations taken with ALMA or CARMA
(
Warren et al. 2015
)
. This apparent paucity of molecular gas is at odds with the young population of massive stars in the galaxy, although a number of possibilities could explain the non-detection of CO. In this particular example, the CO emission could be extended and therefore be missed by the interferometers.
In general, the CO emission will also be weaker because of the lower metal content of galaxies like Sextans A.
On the other hand, the higher porosity of the XMP interstellar medium makes CO more prone to dissociation
(
Ramambason et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, and may result in a different stratification of the photodissociation region

(
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Madden et al. 2020
, smaller and more compact CO cores surrounded by wider H
2
distributions, e.g., )
. All these factors could make molecular gas in XMP galaxies like Sextans A CO-dark.

6
Summary and concluding remarks

In this work, we present calibrations and material to analyze future observations of
Z
= 0.10
Z_{\odot}
OB stars.

We processed a grid of
fastwind
synthetic spectra to match the observations of
87
’s standards, classified the models,
and removed those with unrealistic stellar parameters.
We used the distilled subset to build the first calibration of stellar parameters and synthetic photometry of 0.10
Z_{\odot}
massive stars as a function of spectral type.
Because we left all stellar parameters free to allow for different combinations to yield the same spectral morphology, we obtained a range of parameters for each (SpT, LC) pair, more realistically accounting for the scatter seen in nature.

Our derived scales of effective temperature and H- and He
i
-ionizing flux are in agreement with results for OB stars and H
ii
regions in sub-SMC metallicity environments. 0.10
Z_{\odot}
OB stars are 1-6 kK hotter (depending on luminosity class) and produce higher H- and He
i
-ionizing fluxes than their Galactic analogues.
As reported in previous works, our
fastwind
grid showed that the He
ii
-ionizing flux depends on the wind density, even if winds are weak (log
Q_{\mathrm{wind}}

\sim
-14 dex). This implies that the He
ii
-ionizing photon production of XMP massive stars is unconstrained unless their
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\dot{M}
are determined. Moreover, in order to study the He
ii
-ionizing photon budget of XMP galaxies, population synthesis codes should be provided with stellar libraries that vary mass-loss rates within the HRD. The calibrations used in some stellar population synthesis codes may be underestimating the production of He
ii
ionizing photons by the more numerous O dwarfs.

This work also delivers calibrated intrinsic colours, magnitudes and bolometric corrections for different photometric systems. The calibrated photometry accounts for spectral lines in the considered ranges and the level dissolution caused by the high hydrogen series thanks to correction factors calculated with
cmfgen
models.
We calculated the relation between
(B-V)_{\mathrm{0}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{phot}}
that can be used to estimate extinction and to identify 0.10
Z_{\odot}
OB-star candidates.
For a given
Q_{\mathrm{phot}}
pseudo-colour, our calibration returns redder
(B-V)_{\mathrm{0}}
than
Massey et al. 2000
’s relation by 0.07 mag on average.

Finally, as an example of the possible application of our theoretical calibrations, we mapped the internal extinction of the 0.10
Z_{\odot}
dwarf irregular galaxy Sextans A. We used
44
’s spectroscopic catalogue, their spectral types and our calibrated intrinsic colours to estimate colour excess. The map shows Sextans A suffers from significant and uneven interstellar extinction, especially in region B that overlaps with the eastern H
i
cloud, but also in other areas with low H
i
column density.

To summarize, this paper unites the tools needed to search and provide a preliminary analysis of OB-type stars in XMP galaxies. Two of the most advanced atmosphere codes,
fastwind
and
cmfgen
, were used in our calculations to provide long-lasting materials. All tables will be available online so they may serve as references for future studies of XMP OB stars with a variety of instruments.
