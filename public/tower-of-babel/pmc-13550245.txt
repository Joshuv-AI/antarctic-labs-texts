A case of Mazabraud’s syndrome identified by FDG PET/CT
Authors: Jie Liu, Xin Li, Jinping Fang, Hu Cai, Heqing Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of 46-year-old man who underwent surgery for a right thigh myxoma 20 years ago. Due to a discovery of cervical metastatic squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary(CMSCCUP), a 18F-FDG-PET/CT examination was taken and it revealed the recurrence of the right thigh intramuscular myxomas and multiple fibrous dysplasia in the right lower extremity bones, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of Mazabraud’s syndrome. Due to its whole-body imaging capability, PET/CT can simultaneously detect the characteristic combination of fibrous dysplasia and intramuscular myxoma in Mazabraud’s syndrome and effectively rule out malignant possibilities. It serves as an important imaging modality for the diagnosis of this syndrome.Keywords: Mazabraud’s syndrome, FDG PET/CT scan, Myxomas

Introduction

Mazabraud’s syndrome is a rare disorder characterized by the coexistence of intramuscular myxomas and fibrous dysplasia, with fewer than 150 cases reported in the literature to date. Pathogenetically, the syndrome arises from inactivating mutations in the GNAS1 gene, which lead to abnormal mesenchymal cell proliferation (Cox et al. 2017; Kašpar et al. 2023). It shows a female predominance, a tendency to affect the lower extremities, and a right-sided predilection. Most patients are asymptomatic, though bone deformity or fracture may occur. Intramuscular myxomas are typically multifocal, adjacent to severely affected bones, and mostly located in the lower limbs (Vescini et al. 2019).

Case presentation

A 46-year-old male who underwent resection of a myxoma in the right thigh 20 years ago due to noticed enlargement of the right thigh, but received no regular follow-up thereafter and remained asymptomatic in the interval. One year ago, he underwent right lobectomy plus central neck dissection for thyroid cancer due to a thyroid nodule. Postoperative pathology revealed papillary thyroid carcinoma with metastasis to 1 out of 2 lymph nodes in the right central neck compartment. Additionally, metastatic squamous cell carcinoma was identified in one lymph node in the suprasternal fossa. Subsequent head and neck MRI, nasopharyngoscopy, and laryngoscopy showed no abnormal findings, and a clinical diagnosis of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary origin was suspected.

To identify the primary site of squamous cell carcinoma, FDG PET/CT was performed. No primary lesion of squamous cell carcinoma was detected; however, recurrence of the right thigh myxoma was observed, presenting as multiple well-defined, homogeneous, hypodense, round-like nodules in the intermuscular space of the right thigh, with a mean CT value of 17 HU, mildly increased homogeneous FDG uptake, and SUVmax ranging from 1.6 to 2.9.

Furthermore, fibrous dysplasia was noted in the right femur, tibia, talus, cuneiform bone, and first metatarsal bone. The lesions appeared as multiple well-defined hypodense and ground-glass opacities growing along the medullary cavity, some with sclerotic margins, and focal thinning but intact cortical bone adjacent to some lesions, with the long axis of the lesions parallel to that of the long bones. Subsequent percutaneous biopsy of the mass confirmed intramuscular myxoma. A final diagnosis of Mazabraud’s syndrome was established.

For CMSCCUP, FDG PET/CT did not detect an occult primary. The patient received routine oncologic follow-up but refused genetic testing and regional targeted therapy. No distant metastasis or primary tumor was identified during short-term follow-up (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Mazabraud’s syndrome is an extremely rare benign genetic disorder. Patients with Mazabraud’s syndrome are often asymptomatic or have mild swelling and bone discomfort, leading to delayed or missed diagnosis (Kašpar et al. 2023). This patient underwent thigh myxoma resection 20 years earlier without follow-up and abnormal symptoms. A 18F FDG PET/CT evaluation for CMSCCUP led to the detection of recurrent myxomas and extensive fibrous dysplasia. The findings suggest a diagnosis of Mazabraud’s syndrome.

This case demonstrates classic clinical and imaging features: lower extremity location, multifocal intramuscular myxomas, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, and right-sided involvement. The diagnosis was incidentally made during oncologic staging for an unrelated malignancy, highlighting the value of FDG PET/CT whole-body imaging. Unlike conventional imaging, PET/CT provides integrated whole-body metabolic and anatomical information, clearly showing typical features: well-circumscribed, hypodense myxomas with mild homogeneous FDG uptake; and ground-glass intramedullary bone lesions with sclerotic margins and cortical thinning (Miyake et al. 2006; Tilkidis et al. 2025).These features allow reliable differentiation from malignant bone lesions, which show cortical destruction, soft-tissue masses, and infiltrative growth.

A major clinical challenge is distinguishing intramuscular myxomas in Mazabraud’s syndrome from low-grade soft tissue sarcomas and soft tissue metastases. Low-grade sarcomas typically present as large, infiltrative masses with ill-defined borders, heterogeneous density, and focal hypermetabolism suggestive of dedifferentiation. Soft tissue metastases usually show a known primary malignancy, multifocal involvement, and markedly increased FDG uptake. In contrast, the mild, homogeneous FDG uptake and typical CT features of myxomas and fibrous dysplasia in this case effectively excluded malignant processes and prevented misdiagnosis, which is particularly critical in patients with concurrent malignancies to avoid inappropriate treatment (Mahajan et al. 2018).On the other hand, Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is regarded as the preferred imaging modality for the accurate diagnosis of Mazabraud’s syndrome (Tilkidis et al. 2025). However, its limited scanning coverage tends to result in missed lesions at distant anatomical sites.

The coexistence of Mazabraud syndrome with malignant neoplasms, although rare, has been reported in several cases (Mahajan et al. 2018; Piciu et al. 2015). Its specific mechanism requires further investigation. The coexistence of papillary thyroid carcinoma, CMSCCUP, and Mazabraud’s syndrome is extremely rare. Mazabraud’s syndrome is associated with inactivating mutations of the GNAS1 gene; In some rare cases, the mechanism underlying malignant transformation of Mazabraud’s syndrome is also associated with GNAS R201H/C mutations (Calleja et al. 2019). However, targeted genetic testing for GNAS or other susceptibility loci was not performed in this patient. Current evidence is insufficient to confirm a shared genetic susceptibility via the GNAS pathway between this syndrome and non-endocrine malignancies or thyroid carcinoma, and it remains unclear whether this triple co-occurrence represents a sporadic event. The potential role of the GNAS pathway in multineoplastic processes warrants further investigation, and long-term multidisciplinary surveillance is essential for such patients.

Conclusion

Mazabraud’s syndrome is a rare benign entity that is easily overlooked due to non-specific manifestations. FDG PET/CT enables whole-body metabolic and anatomical evaluation, allowing simultaneous detection of intramuscular myxomas and fibrous dysplasia—the hallmark combination of this syndrome. It facilitates accurate diagnosis and effective differentiation from malignant lesions, thereby preventing misdiagnosis and inappropriate clinical decisions. In the present case, Mazabraud’s syndrome, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and CMSCCUP occurred concurrently. In the absence of targeted molecular testing, it remains unclear whether this coexistence is attributable to shared genetic susceptibility through the GNAS pathway or is a sporadic occurrence. Long-term multidisciplinary surveillance is recommended for patients with this syndrome and concurrent malignancies.
