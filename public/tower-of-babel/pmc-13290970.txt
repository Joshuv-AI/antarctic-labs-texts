Antibody–Drug Conjugates in Non-small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Lodovica Zullo, Martina Bortolot, Francesca R Ogliari, Stephanie P L Saw, Robin van Geel, Rob ter Heine, Anna Sadowska, Jordi Remon, Lizza Hendriks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) are complex molecules composed of a monoclonal antibody, a linker and a cytotoxic payload. Their design enables the selective delivery of cytotoxic agents to tumoral cells through antibody binding to a tumor-expressed antigen, followed by internalization, intracellular degradation and payload release, ultimately enabling the cytotoxic drug to exert its antitumor activity. ADCs have been evaluated in phase II and III trials in previously treated advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), both in oncogene-addicted and in non-oncogene-addicted tumors, addressing resistance to standard therapies. Ongoing clinical trials are now expanding their use both in the first-line setting for advanced disease and in earlier disease stages. This narrative review summarizes the currently available data for ADC treatment in NSCLC, highlighting the need for improved patient selection to maximize benefit while limiting toxicity, incorporating clinical characteristics, pharmacogenomics and optimal treatment sequencing in the equation. Moreover, the understanding of resistance mechanisms and the development and validation of predictive biomarkers will be of utmost relevance to inform clinical practice.

Key Points

Several clinical trials evaluated antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) in different molecular subtypes of advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), after progression to standard treatment. Now, ADCs are being tested in earlier phases of the disease.To date, evidence on predictive biomarkers and mechanisms of resistance remains scarce, thereby limiting the identification of patient subsets most likely to benefit from a given ADC in a specific clinical setting, as well as the optimal positioning of ADCs along the disease course, including their potential use as rechallenge following prior ADC exposure.Moreover, patient-related factors, disease characteristics and molecular design should be all considered to optimize ADC dosing, with the ultimate goal of minimizing toxicity while preserving efficacy.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths per year [1]. Although targeted therapies and immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved survival over the past decades, NSCLC continues to pose significant challenges due to its molecular heterogeneity and treatment resistance. Novel therapeutic approaches are still needed to further optimize clinical outcomes, both for patients with NSCLC harboring actionable genomic alterations (AGAs) and for those NSCLCs without identifiable AGAs.

Biotechnological advances have led to the design of antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), molecules made of a monoclonal antibody (MoAb), a linker and a cytotoxic agent which is delivered directly to tumoral cells by exploiting specific antigen expression on malignant cells [2]. Efficacy in several solid tumors has accelerated the exploration of ADCs in NSCLC.

This review first outlines the molecular structure and mechanism of action of ADCs, then examines the current evidence supporting their use in advanced NSCLC, as well as the emerging data in early-stage and locally advanced disease. It also discusses key challenges and unresolved questions, including optimal sequencing with existing therapies, mechanisms of resistance, potential predictive biomarkers to guide individualized treatment and strategies to maximize efficacy while limiting toxicity.

Structure and Mechanism of Action

An ADC consists of a MoAb chemically linked to a cytotoxic payload (from any class of anticancer drugs) via a linker, which is cleavable when the payload is released in response to specific conditions (i.e., low pH, enzymatic cleavage, high glutathione concentrations), or non-cleavable if the payload separates from the MoAb only after the molecule degradation [2].

Together, MoAB, linker and payload enable the specific targeting of cancer cells with the cytotoxic drug, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while potentially limiting off-target toxicity [2].

The antitumor activity of ADCs can be conceptualized through four interconnected mechanisms [3]. First, upon binding to its target, the ADC–antigen complex is internalized through receptor-mediated endocytosis and trafficked to lysosomes. Acidic and enzymatic conditions promote linker cleavage or antibody degradation, releasing the payload.

Second, membrane-permeable payloads may diffuse into adjacent cells. This bystander effect enhances efficacy in tumors with heterogeneous antigen expression, but may cause toxicity in surrounding healthy tissues [4]. This effect is reduced with non-cleavable linkers: the payload is released only after complete degradation of the antibody within the target cell and it is stable in the plasma, limiting the drug release into the extracellular environment [5]. In contrast, cleavable linkers can release the payload in a lipophilic and membrane-permeable form capable of diffusing into surrounding tumor sites [6]. It is estimated that only 1–2% of the administered payload finally reaches the intracellular compartment of the target cell [7].

Third, the Fc region of the antibody can recruit immune effector cells by binding Fc receptors. This interaction can trigger antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis and complement-dependent cytotoxicity, thereby adding another layer of tumoral cell elimination [8, 9].

Lastly, ADCs may retain the antibody activity, thereby downregulating cell survival and proliferation signaling pathways mediated by the target antigen [10].

Because of this complexity, many factors can influence the efficacy of ADCs. The internalization is primarily dependent on the antibody’s binding affinity to its target. Although higher affinity generally accelerates uptake into tumoral cells, excessively strong binding can restrict distribution within solid tumors due to the binding site barrier, where antibodies accumulate near blood vessels and fail to reach distal areas [11–13].

Antibody size is another determinant of penetration. Full-length IgG molecules often face difficulties moving through capillaries and the dense extracellular matrix, which may limit their intratumoral distribution [8, 11]. To overcome this limitation, smaller antibody constructs without the Fc region have been developed. However, although these retain target specificity and binding affinity, their reduced half-life may limit systemic exposure, necessitating more frequent dosing [14].

The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR), that is, the average number of payload molecules conjugated to each antibody, is primarily dictated by the conjugation chemistry and influences efficacy through two mechanisms. First, high DAR heterogeneity can lead to receptor competition because antibody fractions with a low payload or no payload at all compete with the higher payload-bearing ADCs for target-binding, thereby reducing the overall therapeutic pressure. Second, the DAR impacts the pharmacokinetic profile of the compound: an excessively high DAR often increases the hydrophobicity of the ADC, which can trigger faster systemic clearance and a shorter half-life. Optimizing DAR is therefore essential to balance maximum cytotoxic delivery with favorable plasma stability [15].

Because each step of the mechanism of action is sensitive to biological variability, the overall efficacy and toxicity of ADCs are influenced by numerous tumor- and patient-specific factors. For instance, ADC targets are often heterogeneously expressed both on tumor cells and healthy tissues [16], and there is no linear relationship between surface antigen expression and efficacy [17, 18]. Genes that affect internalization, linker cleavage and the tumor’s susceptibility to the cytotoxic payload, whose expression can vary across metastatic sites, should also be taken into account [16].

Antibody–Drug Conjugates (ADCs) in Metastatic Non-small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC)

Treatment decisions for previously untreated metastatic NSCLC are guided by the presence or absence of AGAs. Patients whose tumors harbor AGAs receive targeted agents, when available in first line, whereas patients whose tumors do not harbor AGAs for which first-line targeted options are available, in the absence of contraindications, are treated with immunotherapy-based regimens, according to their performance status and Programmed-Death Ligand-1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score [19, 20]. However, resistance inevitably occurs [21]. Therefore, ADCs have first been evaluated in NSCLC progressing upon standard treatments. The following paragraphs will summarize the evidence on ADCs in EGFR-mutated, other-AGA and non-AGA advanced NSCLC.

EGFR-Mutated NSCLC

First-line treatment for NSCLC harboring common EGFR mutations (exon 19 deletion, L858R in exon 21) is based on third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [22, 23] alone or in combination either with platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy [24] or amivantamab (a bi-specific antibody anti-EGFR/MET) [25]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has recently approved datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) for patients with EGFR mutations post-progression on EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy. As new intensification treatment options in the first-line treatment entail fewer later-line options, ADCs represent a promising option to expand the therapeutic toolbox. TROP2: Datopotamab Deruxtecan and Sacituzumab Tirumotecan

Trophoblast cell-surface antigen-2 (TROP2) is commonly overexpressed across solid tumors and is known to predict worse survival outcomes [41]. Dato-DXd is made of an anti-TROP2 MoAb covalently linked to deruxtecan (a topoisomerase-1 inhibitor) via a plasma-stable, tumor-selective, tetrapeptide-based cleavable linker [42].

TROPION-Lung01 was a randomized phase III study comparing Dato-DXd with docetaxel in previously treated NSCLC [29], with two dual primary endpoints: progression free-survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Dato-DXd significantly improved PFS over docetaxel, with modest absolute benefit (4.4 vs 3.7 months; hazard ratio [HR] 0.75, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.62–0.91; p = 0.004); OS was not significantly improved [29]. Subgroup analyses revealed that patients with NSCLC harboring AGA derived a greater benefit from Dato-DXd. This was consistent with results from TROPION-Lung05, where previously treated NSCLC with AGAs received Dato-DXd monotherapy, achieving an overall response rate (ORR) of 35.8% (overall population) and 43.6% in those with EGFR mutations [30]. Thereafter, the results of these two trials were pooled to analyze the data from 117 patients with EGFR mutations, showing an ORR of 43%, median PFS of 5.8 months and median OS of 15.6 months [43]. Notably, 82% of patients had received prior Osimertinib [43]. Based on these results, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted accelerated approval in June 2025 to Dato-DXd for patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC previously treated with EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy. Efficacy of Dato-DXd in previously treated EGFR-mutated NSCLC is supported also by preliminary data of the phase II ICARUS-Lung01 trial (ORR 50%, 95% CI 21.1–78.9) [44].

The original driver alteration persists after acquired resistance to TKIs [45, 46]. Therefore, whether TKI should be continued post-progression while adding on subsequent therapy is an important question. Despite early termination, the phase III COMPEL trial demonstrated improved survival and intracranial control when osimertinib was continued post-progression with platinum-based chemotherapy instead of switching to chemotherapy alone [47]. The feasibility of combining osimertinib with Dato-DXd was evaluated in module 10 of the phase II platform ORCHARD trial, which seemed to achieve longer survival outcomes versus Dato-DXd monotherapy in the pooled analysis of TROPION-Lung01 and TROPION-Lung05 (ORR 43% and median PFS 9.5 months in the 4-mg/kg cohort; ORR 36% and median PFS 11.7 months in the 6-mg/kg cohort) [31, 43]. However, patients in the ORCHARD trial were naïve to chemotherapy, whereas patients in the TROPION trials had progressed on both EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy. Data from ongoing phase II/III trials are awaited to understand the role of ADCs with or without EGFR-TKI in the first-line and post-progression setting (Table 2).

The randomized phase II OptiTROP-Lung03 trial, conducted in China, evaluated sacituzumab tirumotecan (Sac-TMT) versus docetaxel in EGFR-mutated NSCLC with disease progression after EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy [32]. Sac-TMT, an anti-TROP2 ADC with a unique linker-payload system [50], significantly improved ORR (45.1% vs 15.6%), PFS (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.20–0.46) and OS (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27–0.88), even after adjusting for crossover, which occurred in 36.3% of cases [32]. Based on these results, China’s regulatory agency approved Sac-TMT in this setting. Then, the Chinese phase III OptiTROP-Lung04 trial evaluated Sac-TMT versus platinum-based chemotherapy in the post-EGFR-TKI setting. After a median follow-up of 18.9 months, Sac-TMT achieved a median PFS (primary endpoint) of 8.3 versus 4.3 months, median OS not reached [NR] versus 17.4 months and ORR 60.6% versus 43.1% [33] (Table 1).

HER3: Patritumab Deruxtecan, Izalontamab Brengitecan and DB-1310

Patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd) comprises a fully human Human Epidermal Growth Factor 3 (HER3)-MoAb attached to a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload via a cleavable tetrapeptide-based linker [51]. It was the first ADC to be evaluated in EGFR-mutated NSCLC. HER3 overexpression is associated with EGFR-TKI resistance and metastatic progression, making it a rational target for drug development [34]. Early phase trials showed promising efficacy signals: HER3-DXd achieved ORR of up to 41.0% and median PFS of up to 5.5 months even in heavily pre-treated patients [28]. In the phase II expansion study, HER3-DXd demonstrated activity across various HER3 expression levels and diverse mechanisms of EGFR-TKI resistance, as well as intracranial ORR 33.3% in a subgroup of 30 patients with non-irradiated brain metastases [27]. This led to the phase III trial HERTHENA-Lung02 trial, which randomized patients who had progressed on prior third generation EGFR-TKI to HER3-DXd or platinum-based chemotherapy. PFS was marginally improved in the HER3-DXd arm (5.8 vs 5.4 months; HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.94; p = 0.011), with no significant OS benefit (16.0 vs 15.9 months; HR 0.98; 95% CI 0.79–1.22) [26]. For this reason, the Biologics License Application was withdrawn. It is uncertain whether HER3-DXd will be further developed in EGFR-mutated NSCLC or whether the activity of this ADC could be enhanced when combined with an EGFR-TKI, as suggest by preclinical models [52]. However, HER3-DXd is currently under investigation even in non-EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC (Table 2).

Izalontamab brengitecan (Iza-bren/BL-B01D1) is a novel first-in-class bi-specific ADC that targets EGFR and HER3, conjugated to a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload through a stable tetrapeptide-based cleavable linker. In a pooled analysis from two phase I/II studies, Iza-bren monotherapy achieved an ORR of 47.4%, median PFS of 6.9 months and median OS of 24.8 months among 171 patients ranging from treatment-naïve to post-EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy. The combination of osimertinib plus Iza-bren among treatment-naïve patients was explored in another phase I/II trial, with 100% ORR achieved in the 2.5-mg/kg cohort (n = 40) [53]. Iza-bren has obtained FDA breakthrough designation in EGFR-mutated NSCLC post-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy and confirmatory phase III trials are ongoing (Table 2).

DB-1310, another HER3-targeting ADC in development, also utilizes a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload. Among 42 patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC included in the phase I/IIa trial, DB-1310 achieved an ORR of 35.7%, a median PFS of 5.4 months and a median OS of 7.0 months [37]. Further data are required to confirm its efficacy.

c-MET: Telisotuzumab Vedotin and Telisotuzumab Adizutecan

Telisotuzumab vedotin (Teliso-V) is an anti-cMET (the cell surface receptor for hepatocyte growth factor encoded by MET) MoAb linked to a microtubule inhibitor (monomethyl auristatin 3) through a valine-citrulline junction. Teliso-V monotherapy achieved an ORR of 11.4% among c-MET overexpressing (≥ 50% of tumor cells with strong [3+] staining), previously treated (EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy), non-squamous EGFR-mutated NSCLC [17]. When combined with erlotinib or osimertinib in phase Ib trials, ORR improved to 32.1% [34] and 50.0% [35], respectively. Whether Teliso-V will be further developed in EGFR-mutated NSCLC remains uncertain, as trial NCT06093503 (osimertinib plus Teliso-V) has been withdrawn for strategic considerations. Conversely, telisotuzumab adizutecan (Temab-A) demonstrated promising activity; in a phase I trial, Temab-A demonstrated an ORR of 63% (n = 26/41) with duration of response (DoR) ≥ 6 months in previously treated patients with non-squamous histology [40], irrespective of EGFR mutations and c-Met protein expression. Giving these promising results, a phase II/III trial (NCT07005102) is ongoing to evaluate the safety and efficacy of Temab-A in combination with osimertinib as first-line treatment in metastatic EGFR-mutated NSCLC. Another phase II/III trial (NCT07155187) will compare safety and efficacy of Temab-A to standard of care in patients with non-squamous EGFR-mutated NSCLC after progression on a third-generation EGFR-TKI (Table 2).

Other AGA

Other molecular drivers are less prevalent than EGFR common mutations, but they pose similar challenges upon treatment resistance; at exhaustion of targeted treatments, therapeutic options are limited [19].

TROP2: Datopotamab Deruxtecan

The phase II TROPION-Lung 05 trial was designed to assess safety and efficacy of Dato-DXd 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks in previously treated, metastatic NSCLC with AGAs (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, NTRK, BRAF, MET exon 14 skipping, or RET alterations) [30]. Most patients (60%) were previously treated with two or more lines of targeted therapies. ORR, median DoR and PFS were 36%, 7.0 months and 5.4 months, respectively. Eight patients (23.5%) with NSCLC harboring ALK translocation obtained an objective response; median PFS and median DoR were 4.3 (95% CI 2.6–6.9) and 7.0 (95% CI 2.8–8.4) months, respectively [30]. Compared with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, the ALK-rearranged subgroup was more highly pretreated and, overall, had more advanced disease, two factors potentially explaining the inferior outcomes [30]. Three cases of ROS1 rearrangements obtained a partial response [30]. No data is available about efficacy of Dato-DXd specifically in other NSCLC harboring AGAs [30].

HER2: Trastuzumab Deruxtecan, Trastuzumab Rezetecan, Ado-Trastuzumab Emtansine and BL-M07D1

Four phase II studies have evaluated anti-HER2 ADCs in patients with previously treated, advanced HER2-mutated NSCLC. In a basket trial conducted in a single institution, 18 patients received ado-trastuzumab emtansine, achieving a partial response rate of 44% (95% CI 22–69). Eleven cases (61%) harbored an insertion in exon 20, but responses were seen across different molecular subtypes, which included a single nucleotide variant (SNV) in the tyrosine kinase and in the extracellular domain [54]. Based on these results, ado-trastuzumab emtansine may be considered for the treatment of previously treated, metastatic HER2-mutated NSCLC, according to the NCCN guidelines [55]. Then, trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) was evaluated in the phase II DESTINY-Lung02 [56]. ORR was 49% and 56% in the 5.4-mg/kg cohort and in the 6.4-mg/kg cohort every 3 weeks, respectively, with responses seen regardless of the number of previous lines and the presence of CNS involvement. In 149 out of 152 cases (98%), HER2 mutations were in the tyrosine kinase domain, and 141 (93%) were insertions in exon 20 [56]. T-DXd is approved by the FDA and EMA for patients with HER2-mutated NSCLC progressed upon platinum-doublet with or without ICI [19].

To date, no targeted agent is recommended as first-line treatment of advanced HER2-mutated NSCLC. T-DXd is currently being evaluated versus platinum, pemetrexed and pembrolizumab in previously untreated HER2-mutated NSCLC in the phase III DESTINY Lung-04 trial (NCT05048797).

Trastuzumab rezetecan is a HER2-directed MoAb conjugated to a topoisomerase 1 inhibitor via a cleavable linker. In the phase II part of the HORIZON-Lung trial, conducted in China, trastuzumab rezetecan, at the dose of 4.8 mg/kg once every 3 weeks achieved an ORR of 73% (n = 69, 95% CI 63.3–82.0) in advanced or metastatic NSCLC harboring an activating HER2 mutation progressed upon platinum-based chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1 agent [57]. Most HER2 mutations (n = 91, 97%) were in the kinase domain, and 68 (72%) were Ala775_Gly776insTyrValMetAla, but responses were seen even across other exon 20 insertions or SNVs. A phase III trial in the first-line setting is ongoing (Table 2).

Recently, results from a phase II trial of BL-M07D1, an anti-HER2 ADC linked to Ed-04 (a novel topoisomerase I inhibitor) via a cleavable linker, were reported. Fifty-eight patients with previously treated NSCLC harboring HER2 activating mutations were included and treated with BL-M07D1 at the dose of 4.4 mg/kg once every 3 weeks. BL-M07D1 achieved an ORR of 56.9%. Median PFS (95% CI 9.8–not reached [NR]) and median DoR (95% CI 6.9–NR) were not reached at a median follow-up of 10.3 months [58]. A phase III trial is currently ongoing (Table 2).

HER3: Patritumab Deruxtecan

Patients with previously treated, advanced NSCLC harboring non-EGFR AGA were included in a phase I study assessing the efficacy of HER3-DXd 5.6 mg/kg once every 3 weeks [59]. Across all molecular alterations, ORR was 28% (n = 47, 95% CI 16–43) [59]. Both patients harboring EML4-ALK rearrangements had a partial response; two patients with CD74-ROS1 fusion experienced stable or progressive disease as best response; one patient with a RET fusion experienced stable disease. The study also included one patient with an ERBB2 D769Y and an ERBB2 exon 20 insertion, who had stable and progressive disease as the best response, respectively [59].

Recently, the anti-HER3 and anti-EGFR bi-specific ADC Iza-bren demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity in pretreated NSCLC patients with diverse AGA outside of classical EGFR mutations (ORR 39.7%, DCR 85.9%, median PFS 7.0 months). Notably, outcomes were more favorable in patients with EGFR exon 20 insertions or other nonclassical mutations who achieved an ORR of 69.2% and a median PFS of 10.5 months, and in the HER2-mutant cohort (ORR 52.9%, median PFS 7.5 months) [60].

Two TROP2-ADCs bearing a topoisomerase 1 inhibitor (Dato-Dxd [29] and sacituzumab govitecan [SG] [62]) have been compared with docetaxel after first-line chemo-immunotherapy. Both the TROPION-Lung01 (n = 604) [29] and the EVOKE-01 (n = 603) [62] trial did not reach a significant OS benefit (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.78–1.14, p = 0.53; HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.68–1.04, p = 0.053, respectively). Nevertheless, Dato-DXd significantly improved median PFS, a co-primary endpoint, compared with docetaxel, but the absolute benefit was modest (4.4 vs 3.7 months, HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.62–0.91, p = 0.004) [29]. Despite employing payload from the same therapeutic class, the two phase III trials revealed two major differences between Dato-Dxd and SG. Dato-Dxd achieved longer PFS compared with docetaxel in the non-squamous histology (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.51–0.79), whereas it appeared detrimental in the squamous one (HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.95–2.08) [29]. SG did not demonstrate a similar benefit in the non-squamous histology compared with the squamous one, and was associated with higher toxicity: 66.6% of grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs) were registered in the experimental arm of EVOKE-01 versus 25.6% in the Dato-DXd arm of TROPION-Lung01 [29, 62]. The difference in toxicity might be at least partially explained by different molecular structures, since SG has higher DAR (8:1) compared with Dato-DXd (4:1) [61].

In another phase III trial (CARMEN-LC03), patients with advanced pre-treated non-squamous NSCLC and CEACAM5 immunohistochemistry (IHC)-positivity (≥ 2+ intensity involving ≥ 50% of tumor cells) were randomized to receive the CEACAM5-directed ADC tusamitamab ravtansine (Tusa-rav), linked to a cytotoxic maytansinoid payload, versus docetaxel [63]. At the first planned analysis, Tusa-rav did not reach the primary endpoints of PFS (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.86–1.51) and OS (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.64–1.11), leading to the early discontinuation of Tusa-rav’s development program [63].

A growing number of novel ADCs are being explored in this setting, and a non-exhaustive list of ongoing trials is reported in Table 2. To date, only a few of these studies have shown clinically meaningful results in phase II trials. Enfortumab vedotin (EV), an anti-Nectin-4 antibody conjugated to an anti-microtubule payload, achieved an ORR of 14%, a median PFS of 4.1 months and a median OS of 10.5 months in non-squamous pre-treated NSCLC [64]. Similarly, in secondary refractory (progressed after a response lasting at least 3 months or after stability lasting 6 months) NSCLC, brentuximab vedotin, directed against CD30, showed an ORR of 14%, a median PFS of 5.8 months and a median OS of 14.4 months when combined with pembrolizumab [65].

Teliso-V received accelerated approval from the FDA for previously treated, non-squamous NSCLC without EGFR mutations and c-MET high expression, based on the phase II LUMINOSITY trial results, where Teliso-V achieved an ORR of 34.5%, median PFS of 5.5 months and median OS of 14.3 months in this subgroup [66]. The confirmatory phase III TeliMET NSCLC-01 trial (NCT0492884) is ongoing.

Recent advances in this field are shifting toward the first-line setting and exploring combinations of different ADCs with anti-PD-(L)1 agents with or without chemotherapy in treatment-naïve patients (Table 2). For example, the TROPION-Lung02 is a phase Ib/II trial of Dato-DXd plus pembrolizumab ± chemotherapy in the first-line setting. Both the doublet and the triplet strategy achieved high ORR (52% and 55%, respectively) [48]. Similarly, preliminary data on the combination of SG and pembrolizumab show an ORR of up to 75% in patients with high PD-L1 expression [67]. However, survival data remain immature and the overall risk–benefit profile of these combinations has yet to be fully defined.

ADCs in Early-Stage NSCLC

Following the encouraging results achieved with immunotherapy in early-stage NSCLC, both as neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment, there is an increasing interest in assessing ADCs in combination with ICIs in this setting.

In the perioperative setting, the NeoCOAST-2 trial is a randomized, multi-arm, phase II platform study evaluating the safety and efficacy of durvalumab and chemotherapy in combination with novel agents in patients with resectable stage IIA–IIIB NSCLC without EGFR mutation or ALK rearrangement, with the primary endpoints of pathological complete response (pCR) rate and safety [68]. In Arm 4, 54 patients received neoadjuvant durvalumab plus Dato-DXd and single-agent platinum, followed by surgical resection and adjuvant durvalumab. Among them, 40 patients (74%) completed the planned four cycles of all protocol treatments and 51 (94%) underwent surgery [68]. At the time of final pathological response assessment, the pCR rate was 35.2% (95% CI 22.7–49.4), with comparable activity across PD-L1 subgroups (< 1%: 31.3%, 1–49%: 38.1%, ≥ 50%: 35.3%) [68]. Major pathological response (mPR), defined as ≤ 10% residual viable tumor in the resected specimen, was achieved in 63% of patients, with a median residual viable tumor of 4.5% [68]. Although meaningful cross-trial comparisons are limited by differences in trial design, patient selection and response assessment, the addition of Dato-DXd yielded numerically higher pCR and mPR rates compared with chemo-immunotherapy regimens. The AEGEAN [69], CheckMate 816 [70], KEYNOTE-671 [71], CheckMate 77T [72], NEOTORCH [73] and RATIONALE-315 [74] trials reported pCR rates ranging from 17% to 25%, except for the RATIONALE-315 trial conducted in China, reporting a pCR rate of 41% [74]. It should be noted, however, that the relative contribution of each component tested in the NeoCOAST-2 trial cannot be directly determined.

The safety profile of the combination of Dato-DXd, durvalumab and platinum was broadly comparable to that of currently approved regimens. During the neoadjuvant phase, the overall incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs was 18.5% (16.7% considered possibly related to Dato-DXd), with treatment discontinuation occurring in 11.1% of patients [68].

Other ongoing trials are investigating the potential synergistic effect of ADCs combined with immunotherapy in chemotherapy-sparing perioperative strategies, based on the hypothesis that these regimens could achieve comparable efficacy to chemo-immunotherapy, avoiding the chemotherapy-related toxicity (Table 2).

Of note, the NEOVISION trial (NCT06734182) is the only study specifically designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of four cycles of neoadjuvant envafolimab (anti–PD-L1) in combination with disitamab vedotin (anti–HER2 ADC with a microtubule inhibitor as payload), and carboplatin in a selected population with stage II–IIIB, resectable, HER2-mutant NSCLC. This biomarker-selected approach represents a precision-based development model analogous to oncogene-driven therapy paradigms in the advanced setting and may further enhance therapeutic efficacy by maximizing on-target delivery.

A distinctive aspect of current perioperative ADC studies, including NeoCOAST-2, is that the ADC is administered exclusively in the neoadjuvant phase and discontinued after surgery. The only exception is the phase II, single-arm, NeoTRACE study, which is evaluating a neoadjuvant regimen of SG combined with zimberelimab (anti-PD-1 MoAb), followed by surgery and adjuvant treatment: SG + zimberelimab for up to 4 cycles then zimberelimab alone for up to 13 cycles, or zimberelimab alone, according to physician’s choice [75]. This design allows investigation of whether extending ADC exposure beyond resection can help eradicate residual micrometastatic disease and translate pathological responses into durable survival benefits.

In the adjuvant setting, ongoing phase III trials are assessing ADC-based strategies to escalate treatment in patients lacking a pCR after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy (Table 3).

Finally, the phase III TROPION-Lung12 trial is testing adjuvant Dato-DXd in combination with rilvegostomig (a bispecific antibody that targets PD-1 and TIGIT), or rilvegostomig alone, or standard of care in patients with completely resected stage I adenocarcinoma at high risk of recurrence or ctDNA-positive disease [76], potentially paving the way for the introduction of ADCs in an even earlier stage of NSCLC.

Toxicity

While ADCs are designed to deliver cytotoxic agents selectively to tumor cells, thereby improving the therapeutic index compared with chemotherapy, they might cause relevant toxicities that necessitate dose reductions, interruptions, or delays. In many cases, AEs are primarily payload-driven, as the conjugated drug, once released, retains the toxicity of the cytotoxic agent [77]. For example, SN-38–based ADCs such as SG reproduce irinotecan-like neutropenia and gastrointestinal toxicity, whereas DXd-based ADCs frequently cause stomatitis, ocular events and interstitial lung disease (ILD) [78]. Target-mediated effects also contribute to specific contexts, particularly when the target antigen is expressed in normal tissues. This mechanism, known as on-target/off-tumor toxicity, can amplify tissue-specific injury [77]. A notable example is Teliso-V; while its peripheral neuropathy is payload-driven (MMAE causes microtubule-related neurotoxicity [79]), the peripheral edema is target-related, consistent with MET’s physiological role in endothelial integrity and tissue repair [80]. Overall, systemic chemotherapy induces more hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicities, whereas ADCs produce less generalized myelosuppression but more organ-specific AEs [82].

Interstitial Lung Disease: Pathogenesis and Management

ILD represents a significant and potentially life-threatening toxicity associated with different ADCs, and it is challenging in patients with lung malignancies, who might have received thoracic locoregional treatments, and many of whom may also have had a smoking habit history.

ILD starts with an acute or chronic injury to the lung tissue, with alveolitis, disruption of alveolar structure, inflammation and subsequent fibrosis affecting gas exchange [83], and potentially leading to respiratory failure and death.

A recent real-world pharmacovigilance study (n = 1277) reported that ILD accounts for 1.49% of all AEs in patients treated with ADCs, with a fatality rate of 25.8% [84]. Diagnosis is suspected based on the onset or worsening of respiratory symptoms, together with radiological and/or laboratory findings [85]. A pulmonology consultation with function testing is then recommended, and a bronchoalveolar lavage can be useful for differential diagnosis. ADC-induced ILD tends to improve after the drug's discontinuation [85].

The exact mechanism of ADC-induced ILD is not fully understood. A first consideration is that, although most of the ADC-targetable antigens are expressed in normal lung tissues, ILD incidence is not directly correlated with the expression levels of these proteins [86]. On the other hand, some payloads may cause lung injury; for instance, topoisomerase inhibitors can damage lung tissue via a target-dependent and target-independent uptake of the ADC [87], given that even non-cancer cells can uptake the molecule [87]. Therefore, the payload can cause lung tissue injury by bystander killing both when released from cancer and non-cancer cells and when circulating free after deconjugation of the ADC [85, 87].

Of note, especially for T-DXd, lung toxicity was shown to be dose dependent. In the DESTINY-Lung02 trial, ILD occurred in 12.9% (n = 4 G1, n = 7 G2, n = 1 G3 and n = 1 G5) of patients receiving T-DXd 5.4 mg/kg and in 28.0% (n = 4 G1, n = 9 G2, n = 1 G5) of those treated with T-DXd 6.4 mg/kg [56]. Similarly, in the DESTINY-Lung 01, which investigated T-Dxd at a dose of 6.4 mg/kg, ILD rate was 26% (n = 3 G1, n = 15 G2, n = 4 G3, n = 2 G5), with a median range of onset of 80–140 days [81].

Different patient- and treatment-related variables have been identified as risk factors: history of ILD, advanced age, comorbidities, prior anti–PD-(L)1 treatment and radiotherapy. Factors associated with higher mortality include severe initial presentation, smoking history, baseline saturation <95%, older age, pre-existing lung disease and auto-immune disorders [88]. Moreover, the incidence of ILD was 15% (6% G3) in ORCHARD, whereas Dato-DXd monotherapy had 4% incidence of ILD (1% G3) [43, 89]. Although these numbers need to be interpreted with caution given cross-trial comparisons and relatively small patient populations, the increased ILD risk can be explained by overlapping toxicities between osimertinib and deruxtecan. Patients receiving an ADC in combination with an EGFR-TKI must be considered at higher risk of developing ILD.

To date, no recommendations have been released specifically for NSCLC, but previous experience in breast cancer highlighted how patient education, early detection and timely management of ILD are critical [90, 91]. Prior to treatment initiation, baseline pulmonary function testing and high-resolution thoracic CT imaging should be performed to identify pre-existing lung abnormalities [90, 91]. If ILD is suspected, the ADC should be immediately withheld and corticosteroid therapy should be initiated according to the severity, with close monitoring of respiratory symptoms and, when necessary, hospitalization [90, 91]. Reintroduction of the ADC may be cautiously considered only in asymptomatic patients after complete clinical and radiological resolution of pulmonary infiltrates [90, 91].

Strategies to Reduce ADC-Related Toxicity

The first factor to consider when aiming to improve ADC tolerability is the drug’s design and engineering, including the choice of target antigen, linker and payload [92].

Because many ADC targets are also expressed in normal tissues, on-target/off-tumor toxicity is a challenge. Careful selection of tumor-associated antigens with high differential expression, that is, abundant on malignant cells but minimal on normal tissue, is essential to reduce toxicity [77]. Furthermore, the antibody must be engineered for high specificity and stability, to minimize non-specific binding. While high affinity is required for efficient internalization, it must be balanced against the binding site barrier to ensure deep tumor penetration.

Because the linker ensures payload stability and controlled intracellular release, the development of cleavable linkers that are selectively degraded under tumor-specific conditions (e.g., acidic pH, enzymatic activity, or reducing environments) can enhance payload delivery to malignant cells while minimizing off-target effect [77, 93].

Complementary to linker stability, the nature of the payload is critical for ADCs’ dose-limiting toxicities, as specific toxicities are often shared by different ADCs that deliver the same payload. The ideal payload should exhibit high potency, low immunogenicity, stability during blood circulation and modifiable functional groups that enable precise and stable conjugation [77].

Regarding drug dosing, traditionally, acute off-targets effects have been linked to peak plasma concentrations, whereas efficacy depended on exposure. Fractionated dosing may reduce such toxicity while preserving efficacy [94]. However, with improved linker designs that reduce premature payload release, toxicity is increasingly driven by cumulative exposure or on-target/off-tumor effect [94]. Dose regimens of ADCs are currently similar to those employed for chemotherapies, that is, with the drug dosed at the maximum tolerable dose to balance sub-therapy and treatment-limiting toxicity [95]. This approach works on a population level, but optimal individual treatment is influenced by other factors. For instance, ADC targets are expressed by tumor cells, and tumor burden differs: individualized dosing based on tumor burden and response, including dose reductions in responders, may improve the benefit–risk ratio [96].

Cachexia-driven increases in protein turnover accelerate the clearance of monoclonal antibodies and ADCs, potentially necessitating higher doses to maintain therapeutic levels [97]. Conversely, ADC clearance may decrease as patients respond to treatment, suggesting that responders could benefit from dose de-escalation to avoid overexposure. However, a critical paradox exists for cachectic patients: while their increased clearance might suggest a need for higher dosing, their accelerated protein metabolism can also trigger premature payload release. This premature deconjugation increases systemic exposure to the free cytotoxin, thereby heightening the risk of unintended systemic toxicity [97, 98].

Moreover, ADCs are predominantly administered using a total body weight-based schedule in mg/kg. However, this dosing method incorrectly assumes a linear relationship between systemic drug exposure and body weight, which in practice is less than proportional [99]. Consequently, patients with a low and high body weight are relatively under- and overdosed, respectively. Alternative dosing regimens, accounting for this non-linear relationship, may achieve more individualized doses [100]. Dose capping could be used to prevent overdosing in heavier patients, but this neglects the potential underdosing in lighter patients.

Several other patient-related variables (age, ethnicity, baseline organ function, comorbidities, concomitant medications), polymorphisms in metabolism enzymes and drug–drug interactions (DDIs) may affect ADCs’ pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Because ADCs contain both small and large components that are processed into analytes with distinct pharmacokinetic properties, assessing their DDIs is complex [101]. Although the free payload rarely acts as an interaction perpetrator due to its low systemic levels, it can still be a ‘victim’ of interactions [102]. Many payloads, such as camptothecin derivatives or auristatins, undergo hepatic metabolism, therefore CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers can alter systemic exposure. For example, MMAE exposure from telisotuzumab vedotin is predicted to increase 1.4-fold with ketoconazole, and decrease by 70% with rifampicin [81]. In addition, polymorphisms in enzymes involved in payload metabolism are clinically relevant. Patients carrying UGT1A1 *28 alleles have an increased risk of neutropenia, diarrhea and anemia, as this variant impairs SN-38 glucuronidation, predisposing individuals treated with SG to more severe toxicities [103]. Therefore, awareness of potential pharmacogenetic effects and DDIs is important for optimizing individualized ADC therapy.

Biomarkers

Because ADCs bind extracellular proteins, it is logical to search for biomarkers at a protein expression level. In NSCLC, T-DXd has demonstrated efficacy in tumors harboring HER2 exon 20 mutations or insertions [56]. However, gene mutations do not directly imply HER2 protein overexpression, and not all cases of protein overexpression are explained by gene mutations [104, 105]. In the HER2-mutant cohort of the DESTINY-Lung01 trial, HER2 expression was categorized in 0 (not detectable), 1+ (faint or barely detectable), 2+ (weak to moderate), or 3+ (strong) [105]. Granular data from 51 patients having HER2 IHC showed that in 14 cases (27%), T-DXd did not achieve an objective radiological response despite 2+ or 3+ HER2 expression [105]. Conversely, the only complete response was obtained in a patient with score 0 [105]. In the non-HER2-mutant, HER2-overexpressed (2+ and 3+) cohort, ORR by Blinded Independent Central Review (BICR) was 26.5% with T-DXd 6.4 mg/kg, and 34.1% with T-DXd 5.4 mg/kg, respectively [106].

In the phase I DESTINY-Lung03 trial, 36 patients with HER2 IHC 3+ and IHC 2+ NSCLC were treated with T-DXd 5.4 mg/kg; ORR was 56.3% (95% CI 29.9–80.3) and 35.0% (95% CI 15.4–59.2), respectively [36]. To note, 47% (n = 17) of tumors were EGFR-mutated [36].

To date, target expression cannot be considered a reliable predictive biomarker for most ADCs. This is particularly evident for HER2-directed, but also for HER3-directed ADCs, as responses to HER3-DXd were registered across a broad spectrum of HER3 membrane expression in EGFR-mutated NSCLC [28]. Even in breast cancer, not the sole expression of HER3, but its spatial distribution, together with pathological components of the microenvironment (necrosis foci, stroma, immune cells) contribute to the penetration and internalization of the HER3-DXd in cancer cells [107]. HER2 mutations or amplification may predict anti-HER2 ADCs’ activity, even though they do not necessarily translate into protein overexpression [56]. Indeed, the receptor activation induced by gene amplification or mutation promotes the internalization of the molecule via the receptor ubiquitination [56]. Similarly, preclinical models suggest a higher uptake of Sac-TmT and higher activity of the drug in the presence of EGFR mutations [50]. Taken together, these findings reinforce the notion that predictive biomarkers should extend beyond target expression alone, and molecular-defined patient subgroups might achieve higher benefit from ADCs.

TROP2 expression has also been investigated as a predictor of survival to Dato-DXd [44]. Lower PFS was associated with the lowest H-score (< 100) compared with intermediate (100–200) and high (> 200) scores in the phase III ICARUS Lung-01 trial (p = 0.02) [44]. A similar ORR was observed with Sac-TmT in tumors with TROP2 IHC H-score > 200 and ≤ 200, although numerically longer PFS was achieved with higher TROP2 expression [50]. To note, TROP2 can be expressed on both the membrane and the cytoplasm, and deep learning models might enable a more comprehensive and precise quantification of the target expression in both compartments. A quantitative continuous scoring (QCS)-normalized membrane ratio (NMR) model, relative to cytoplasmic expression of TROP2, was trained and validated on tissue slides from the phase III TROPION-Lung01 study. Compared with docetaxel, Dato-DXd achieved higher ORR (32.7%) and longer PFS (6.9 months) in the QCS-NMR+ population [108]. Moreover, in the phase I TROPION-Lung02 study, ORR was 64.9% in QCS-NMR+ and 56.4% in QCS-NMR+ patients, with a median PFS of 12.0 and 8.1 months, respectively. Among patients treated with N Dato-DXd plus pembrolizumab, median PFS was 21.3 and 10.9 months in QCS-NMR+ versus QCS-NMR−. With the addition of platinum-based chemotherapy, median PFS was 8.2 and 5.5 months in QCS-NMR+ versus QCS-NMR− [48].

Finally, different histologies have various sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, including ADC payloads. In this context, tumor histology can represent both a predictive biomarker and mechanism of primary resistance to certain ADCs [109].

Mechanism of Resistance

Resistance to antineoplastic agents might be driven by primary factors, or attributable to characteristics that have been acquired during treatments [110]. Compared with other agents, ADCs have peculiarities due to their complex structure [111, 112].

Resistance to ADCs can be broadly divided into two macro-domains: resistance driven by the payload and resistance driven by the antigen target. Resistance to the payload might be driven by tumor histology, as a specific tumor type might not respond to certain classes of chemotherapeutic agents, or by diverse gene alterations and expression profiles [112], a phenomenon that has been largely investigated in the context of resistance to TOP1 inhibitors [113]. Missense mutations in TOP1 genes have been identified in plasma samples from women with breast cancer who progressed to TOP1 inhibitor-bearing ADCs, suggesting a role in acquired resistance to the treatment [111]. Similarly, in the U31402-A-U102 phase I trial of HER3-Dxd in pretreated EGFR-mutated NSCLC, the TOP1 L721R missense mutation was found in a patient’s post-progression samples, but it has not been functionally characterized as yet [28].

A recent study investigating resistance to T-DXd in HER2-mutated NSCLC identified payload resistance as a major and heterogeneous mechanism. Preclinical analyses showed decreased expression of topoisomerase I together with increased expression of topoisomerase IIα and IIβ as a mechanism of resistance to T-DXd, but other genes might also be affected. SLFN11 is a component of the damage response repair machinery that is recruited to stressed replication forks, where it induces replication block or apoptosis. Its expression is a biomarker for DNA-damaging agents, including TOP1 inhibitors [114], because the downregulation of SLFN11 ensures higher tolerance to DNA replication stress. Therefore, its decreased expression has been identified as a mechanism of acquired resistance to T-DXd in a preclinical model of NSCLC [113], and the pharmacological upregulation of TROP2 and SLFN11 expression increased response to SG in a preclinical model of metaplastic breast cancer [115].Copy number gains in ABCC1, coding for the MRP1 efflux protein, can also drive resistance to multiple drugs, including deruxtecan, by increasing drug efflux [113].

Regarding payloads with different mechanisms of action, TUBB3 is a biomarker of resistance to microtubule inhibitors [116].

Resistance mechanisms can also occur on the gene coding for the target protein, commonly leading to reduced antibody-antigen binding and decreased ADC penetrance in the tumoral cell [117].

Two acquired ERBB3 frameshift mutations (Y789fs and P489fs) were identified in two patients who progressed to HER-Dxd in the U31402-A-U102 phase I study [28]. Y789fs is predicted to cause premature truncation of HER3 within its kinase domain, leading to reduced expression, reduced protein levels and, therefore, reduced drug internalization and payload release. Indeed, treatment with HER3-DXd did not induce payload release in cells expressing ERBB3 Y789fs or frameshift mutations near ERBB3 Y789 [28]. Currently, the functionality of ERBB3 P489fs remains uncharacterized to date.

Similarly, mutations in the ERBB2 IV domain (trastuzumab binding site) might occur upon treatment with trastuzumab-based ADCs. These mutations reduce sensitivity to both T-DM1 and T-DXd, by impairing trastuzumab binding, but, in preclinical models, they do not cause resistance to HER2-TKIs. Moreover, in clinical datasets, these mutations (E580K, D582H) are predominantly subclonal and therefore unlikely to drive resistance on their own [113]. Loss of the target or its reduced surface exposure have also been described as a mechanism of resistance to ADCs [118], although it does not seem to play a major role [113].

The following payload- or target-related mechanisms may further influence a tumor’s sensitivity profile to ADCs: the presence of other ligands competing for the same target antigen (e.g., neuregulin promotes HER2 dimerization with HER3 or HER4); lysosomal function; alterations in molecules that transport the agent from the lysosome to the cytoplasm; or alterations in the genes coding for enzymes responsible for drug metabolism (e.g. UGT1A1 for SN38) [119].

Assessing resistance mechanisms may help inform sequential treatment strategies. First, tumors that develop resistance to a specific payload may retain sensitivity to ADCs carrying alternative payloads [113]. Second, as shown for the HER2-mutated disease, kinase inhibitors may remain effective after HER2-targeted ADCs if payload resistance arises, or if acquired molecular alterations affect antibody binding while HER2 signaling dependency is preserved [113].

Interestingly, off-target resistance to T-DXd has been described, including copy number alterations and single-nucleotide variants in MAPK–ERK and PI3K–AKT pathway genes, as well as epithelial–mesenchymal transition [113]. This underscores the heterogeneity of ADC resistance, which likely extends beyond payload- and antigen-related mechanisms.

The ICARUS program is aimed at shedding light on mechanisms of action and resistance of ADCs. Preliminary data presented at ASCO 2024 show an activation of DNA repair-related pathways and a suppression of immune-related pathways at progression to Dato-DXd in previously treated NSCLC [44].

Challenges and Future Directions

The following paragraphs focus on the current directions in ADC research and the challenges that lie ahead. We will not specifically discuss brain activity of ADCs. Overall, ADCs seem to exert encouraging intracranial activity [120–122], but more data are needed to understand potential synergy and toxicity of intracranial radiotherapy during treatment with ADCs.

ADCs Targeting Immune Checkpoints

B7H3 is expressed by most NSCLC cases, and higher expression is linked to poor survival [123, 124].

The phase I ARTEMIS-001 trial investigated risvutatug rezetecan, a B7-H3–directed ADC with a topoisomerase I inhibitor, in advanced solid tumors. The drug demonstrated encouraging activity in 167 patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC (ORR was 33.3% and 10.7%, with 8.0 mg/kg and 10.0 mg/kg, respectively), and a manageable safety profile.

Similarly, BNT324, a B7-H3–targeting antibody linked to a DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor, demonstrated encouraging tumor responses in pre-treated NSCLC, with an unconfirmed ORR of 22.0% in non-squamous and 16.0% in squamous histology. Activity appeared particularly notable in SCLC (unconfirmed ORR and DCR of 56.2% and 89.0%, respectively) [125].

YL201 is another anti-B7-H3 ADC bearing a topoisomerase 1 inhibitor via a protease-cleavable linker [126]. Its safety, pharmacokinetics and preliminary efficacy have been evaluated in a phase I/II basket trial, including 68 patients with NSCLC. Safety was in line with what has already been observed for topoisomerase inhibitor–bearing ADCs. ORR was 40.8% (95% CI 35.0–46.7) in the overall population, 28.6% (95% CI 13.2–48.7) in lung adenocarcinoma (n = 28) and 8.3% (95% CI 0.2–38.5) in squamous lung cancer (n = 12), respectively [126]. The median number of previous systemic treatments was two (range 1–5) for lung adenocarcinoma, and two (range 1–6) for lung squamous carcinoma, including a previous ICI in most of the cases. Of note, among the 152 patients with available baseline tissue samples, B7-H3 expression was detected in the majority of tumors; however, higher expression levels did not correlate with greater treatment efficacy [126]. Similarly, plasma soluble B7-H3 concentrations (available from 223 patients with different solid tumors) did not correlate with clinical outcomes [126].

MGC018127 [127], another B7-H3-directed ADC, is currently under investigation in a phase I/II trial including a NSCLC cohort, alone and in combination with the anti-PD-1 antibody MGA012 (NCT03729596).

To note, immune-checkpoints are often co-expressed by tumoral cells [128], therefore bispecific ADCs (i.e. anti-B7-H3/PD-L1) may represent a novel promising approach [129].

Positioning ADCs in Early-Stage, Resectable NSCLC

ADC might produce a paradigm shift in early-stage, resectable NSCLC, especially for those whose PD-L1 expression is < 1%, which demonstrated less favorable outcomes with perioperative chemo-immunotherapy.

In the NeoCOAST-2 trial, pCR and mPR rates were consistent across PD-L1 expression levels. With the addition of Dato-Dxd, a pCR rate of 31.3% was observed in patients with PD-L1 expression < 1% (~ 30% of patients) [68], similar to the one achieved in the PD-L1 positive group (36.8%) and notably higher than the ones reported in the AEGEAN and CheckMate 816 trials in the same subgroup (9% and 17%, respectively [69, 70]). This is probably due to the ability of anti-TROP2 ADCs to increase the sensitivity of tumor cells to T-cell–mediated cytotoxicity, thereby enhancing the activity of ICI also in PD-L1–negative disease with a less immunologically active tumor micro-environment [130]. As pCR currently represents the most robust positive prognostic factor among patients treated with neoadjuvant strategies [131], use of Dato-Dxd in this subgroup should therefore be considered.

ADCs could also play a key role in the adjuvant setting, particularly among patients whose tumors do not achieve a pCR after neoadjuvant therapy (~80%). Several trials are currently evaluating different combinations to intensify adjuvant treatment. Notably, many of these studies compare the ADC–ICI combination against ICI (e.g., the TroFuse-019 trial: adjuvant Sac-TMT+ pembrolizumab vs pembrolizumab alone) or even observation (e.g., the ARIAN trial: adjuvant SG + zimberelimab vs zimberelimab monotherapy or observation), comparators that may be suboptimal for this clinical context.

Adjuvant dual therapy raises concerns about treatment adherence and cumulative toxicity. The completion rate of the adjuvant phase in perioperative immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-based regimens is relatively low (~69% for durvalumab in the AEGEAN trial [69], and around 40% for full adjuvant courses of pembrolizumab in real-world settings [70]). Adding ADCs may further complicate adherence to adjuvant treatment.

Positioning ADCs in Unresectable, Locally Advanced NSCLC

At present, non-EGFR mutated and non-ALK rearranged unresectable, locally advanced NSCLC is treated with chemoradiotherapy (CRT) followed by 1-year durvalumab [132]. Despite longer survival achieved with durvalumab consolidation, a large proportion of patients has disease relapses, highlighting the need for novel and more effective approaches. No ADCs are approved or supported by mature clinical data in this setting. Potentially, ADCs could be placed as a consolidation treatment, after CRT, or earlier in the treatment strategy, as induction therapy or during radiotherapy.

The only ongoing trial (NCT06706219) in this setting is a phase II study evaluating the safety and efficacy of Sac-TMT in combination with pembrolizumab as induction therapy for patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC, followed by definitive treatment (radical radiotherapy or surgery, with or without pembrolizumab consolidation or Sac-TMT with pembrolizumab in patients who did not achieve a pCR). Induction strategies, even in unresectable disease, may elicit a broader and more diverse T-cell response owing to the presence of an intact lymphatic network and exposure to the full spectrum of tumor antigens [133]. In this context, the addition of ADCs could synergize with ICI, thereby decreasing the volume of tissue requiring irradiation and potentially allowing for subsequent modulation of chemoradiotherapy, or surgery.

There is significant uncertainty regarding the feasibility of concurrent ADCs and RT, both in terms of efficacy, especially in light of the dismal results from trials integrating ICIs concomitantly to CRT, and in terms of safety, given the potential overlapping toxicities [134]. For instance, pulmonary adverse events can be induced by thoracic radiation, by ADC payloads, and even by ICIs. ADCs might also be explored as post-cCRT consolidation, since ADCs might be effective on residual disease, enhance antitumor immunity and overcome resistance to PD-L1 blockade.

Overall, the incorporation of ADCs in early-stage and locally advanced disease requires careful patient selection. This enhances the importance of predictive biomarkers to identify those most likely to benefit from multimodal treatment strategies. This underscores the need for comprehensive molecular and immunohistochemical characterization at baseline, reassessment after CRT and close disease monitoring throughout treatment to enable timely adaptation of therapeutic strategies.

Positioning ADCs in the Treatment Landscape of NSCLC, and Sequential Treatments

The optimal timing of introducing ADCs into the treatment paradigm of patients with NSCLC with or without AGAs is an important research question. Indeed, for NSCLC harboring AGAs (e.g. EGFR common mutations), the positive results of combination strategies in the FLAURA2 [24] and MARIPOSA [25] phase III trials increased the interest in combining effective therapies in the previously untreated metastatic setting. Moreover, even in non-AGA NSCLC, ADCs are being evaluated in earlier disease settings. Therefore, it is critical to assess their efficacy and safety when administered sequentially. Different ADCs may share similarities across several components, including the target, the antibody, the linker, the drug-to-antibody ratio and the payload. Among these, a shared target or payloads belonging to the same chemotherapeutic class raise concerns regarding potential cross-resistance [119].

For instance, as one potential mechanism of resistance to antibodies is target loss or its reduced expression [28], a tissue re-biopsy with IHC assessment of target expression (or QCS-NMR for TROP2) should be considered at progression on an ADC, before proposing another ADC directed against the same target. Similarly, payload resistance and genomic alterations in alternative signaling pathways may be acquired [113], factors to be considered when choosing a subsequent ADC.

For instance, mutations in TOP1 for topoisomerase inhibitors [111, 113] may limit the efficacy of a sequential topoisomerase inhibitor ADC. No data from clinical trials are available for NSCLC specifically. However, among patients with triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) treated in the phase I TROPION-PanTumor01 trial, Dato-DXd achieved longer median PFS in patients who never received an ADC bearing a TROP1 inhibitor (7.3 months, 95% CI 3.0–18.0) compared with patients who were pre-treated with such agents (4.4 months, 95% CI 3.0–7.3). In TROP1 inhibitor–naive patients with TNBC, confirmed ORR was 40% (12/30, 95% CI 22.7–59.4) compared with 31.8% (14/44, 95% CI 18.6–47.6) of the overall population [135].

From a clinical research perspective, conducting a phase III randomized controlled trial comparing different sequential treatments is not always practical given the speed of drug development with multiple competing treatment options. To that end, and in the light of the evidence, understanding resistance mechanisms and biomarker development is of paramount importance to provide translational insights that may help to inform clinical practice. As such, tissue biopsies at the time of treatment resistance should be obtained as much as possible.

Conclusions

ADCs represent a compelling recent advance in the treatment of NSCLC. However, their true clinical value will not be determined by their novelty, but by their capacity to deliver clinically meaningful and durable benefits while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. Their successful integration into established treatment algorithms will rely less on indiscriminate expansion of indications and more on precise patient selection, optimal timing and rational combination strategies.
