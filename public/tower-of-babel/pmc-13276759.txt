Prevalence and associated factors of head lice (Pediculus humanus capitis) among schoolchildren in Mérida (Yucatán) and Monterrey (Nuevo León), Mexico
Authors: Iram P Rodriguez-Sanchez, Mariana Lizbeth Jiménez Martínez, María de Lourdes Ramírez Ahuja, Felipe A Dzul-Manzanilla, Fabian Correa-Morales, Adriana E Flores, Sergio Dzib, Norma Pavia, Pablo Manrique-Saide, Gerardo Trujillo-Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediculosis capitis is a common parasitic infestation in school settings and can disrupt wellbeing and school attendance. Despite its public-health relevance, epidemiological data describing Pediculosis capitis prevalence and associated factors in Mexico remain limited at the city level. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Pediculosis capitis and identify associated factors among schoolchildren from Monterrey and Mérida, Mexico. Aggregated data from a school-based cross-sectional survey were analyzed. Infestation status was determined through standardized individual scalp inspection performed by the research team. Prevalence and unadjusted odds ratio were calculated for sociodemographic, household, and hygiene-related variables. A total of 6372 schoolchildren were examined (3743 in Monterrey; 2629 in Mérida) with 590 positive cases overall. Prevalence was 16.40% in Monterrey and 6.57% in Mérida, with marked variation among schools. The strongest and most consistent associations were observed for sex and hair length, with higher infestation rates among girls and children with long hair. Associations with household and hygiene factors such as sharing of personal items, bathing frequency, room density, parental education, and employment were heterogeneous across cities and did not follow a uniform pattern. These findings highlight the need for school-home collaboration and continuous surveillance, promoting early detection, hygiene education, and preventive practices adapted to local school environments.Keywords: Pediculosis capitis, Head lice, Schoolchildren, Prevalence, Risk factors, México

Introduction

Pediculosis capitis is an ectoparasitic infestation of the scalp and hair caused by Pediculus humanus capitis (De Geer, 1767), commonly known as the head louse. It is characterized by pruritus and excoriations resulting from a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to louse saliva (Meister and Ochsendorf, 2016). The condition primarily affects school-aged children (5–15 years) and continues to represent a global public-health concern (WHO, 2024).

Head lice are hematophagous ectoparasites within the suborder Anoplura. Transmission occurs primarily through direct head-to-head contact, but may also occur indirectly through shared personal items, including combs, clothing, towels, bedding, or other objects recently used by an infested person (Organizacion Panamericana de la Salud, 2024). Infestation is particularly frequent in overcrowded environments and among populations facing socioeconomic constraints. Contrary to body-louse infestations, which are associated with poor hygiene, head-louse infestation occurs irrespective of hair cleanliness (Durand et al., 2007).

Each clinical form of pediculosis involves a distinct taxon: head-lice (P. h. capitis), body-lice (pehumanus humanus), and pubic lice (Pthirus pubis). The body louse is a recognized vector of Rickettsia prowazekii (epidemic typhus), Bartonella quintana (trench fever) and Borrelia recurrentis (louse-borne relapsing fever) (Sweet et al., 2022). In contrast, P. h. capitis is not a proven vector of human pathogens but causes significant discomfort, social stigma, and excessive use of pediculicidal products.

In the study by Valle Barbosa et al. (2020), which included 720 schoolchildren in Guadalajara, Mexico, Pediculosis capitis was more frequent among girls, who accounted for 82.35% of infested cases. The authors reported an overall prevalence of 4.72% and a cumulative prevalence of 51.94%, indicating that more than half of the students had experienced lice at some point. Infestation was also more common in children with long hair. Together, these observations highlight sex and hair length as potential key determinants of infestation patterns in school-based settings. Although overall prevalence differed between settings, the concordance in sex and hair related patterns suggests that these factors may represent stable epidemiological characteristics of Pediculosis capitis across different regions of Mexico. Understanding the local epidemiology is essential for developing targeted prevention and control strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of pediculosis and to identify sociodemographic, household, and hygiene-related factors associated with infestation among schoolchildren in Mérida (Yucatán) and Monterrey (Nuevo León), Mexico.

Material and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in two geographically and climatically distinct regions of Mexico: the northeastern state of Nuevo León and the southeastern state of Yucatán. In Nuevo León, the research took place in the Monterrey metropolitan area (25.6866° N, 100.3161° W), a highly urbanized region with a semi-arid climate. Sampling was conducted in 14 public elementary schools distributed across the municipalities of Escobedo, General Zuazua, Guadalupe, Santa Catarina, San Nicolás, and San Pedro. In Yucatán, data collection was performed in the city of Mérida (20.9674° N, 89.5926° W), characterized by a tropical, warm-humid climate, and included 8 public elementary schools within the urban area.

Study design and data source

This study followed a cross-sectional, school-based design conducted in Mérida (Yucatán) and Monterrey (Nuevo León), Mexico. The original field survey was carried out by “Laboratorio de Fisiología Molecular y Estructural, Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León” in january 2022 to january 2023, with the purpose of estimating the occurrence of Pediculus humanus capitis infestation and identifying associated factors among schoolchildren. The survey protocol was reviewed and approved by the “COMITÉ DE ÉTICA EN INVESTIGACIÓN Registro CONBIOÉTICA-06-CEI-001-20200721”, under approval number CEICANCL/REV1/PEDIHUM/09/2022. Participation was voluntary. Written informed consent was provided by the parents or legal guardians of all participating children, and verbal assent was obtained from the children prior to examination. All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees, and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

For the present analysis, only anonymized and aggregated data were used. No new field activities or direct human participation were undertaken. The dataset consisted of city-specific spreadsheets in which each row represented a binary variable with four frequency counts (infested and non-infested for each category). City was treated as a grouping variable, and all analyses were conducted separately for Mérida and Monterrey.

Participants and sampling

All students present at each participating school on the day of data collection were invited to take part in this cross-sectional survey. To assess the presence or absence of head lice, each child was examined individually in a private screening area using temporary partitions installed by the research team. Examinations followed a standardized protocol that combined systematic visual inspection of the scalp and hair under adequate lighting with combing using a fine-toothed detection comb. Inspection and combing focused on common predilection sites, including the retroauricular areas and the occipital region. Children were classified as positive when at least one live louse was detected by either visual inspection or combing; otherwise, they were classified as negative. For the evaluation of lice presence, only children confirmed to be positive for pediculosis were included in the analysis (Subahar et al., 2021).

Questionnaire and variables

A structured questionnaire was used to record potential factors associated with pediculosis. Parental education was categorized as basic education (up to middle school) or higher education (bachelor's degree or higher), and parental employment status was recorded as employed or not employed. Type of residence was classified according to construction materials as permanent housing (e.g., concrete structures) or precarious housing. Hygiene-related variables included frequency of full-body bathing (including hair and scalp washing), categorized as once per day or twice per day. Information on bathing frequency and school-related hygiene conditions was obtained with the assistance of teachers to improve reporting accuracy. Sharing of personal hygiene items (fomites) and the number of persons sharing a bedroom were also recorded. Use of control methods referred to the reported use of pediculosis treatment, including permethrin-based shampoo or ivermectin treatment. Child characteristics included sex, age group and hair length. The age cutoff (<12 vs. ≥12 years) was selected to reflect the typical transition from late childhood to early adolescence within the Mexican school system, a stage associated with changes in social interaction patterns (Villalobos et al., 2023). Hair length was classified as short (1–3 cm) or long (≥20 cm) No medium hair-length category was included in the survey instrument, as children were classified during field assessment into these two clearly distinguishable categories. Infestation status was recorded as a binary variable (infested or non-infested) based on standardized visual inspection of the scalp and hair.

Data curation

Data processing was performed in R (version 4.4.1 “Race for Your Life”; R Core Team, 2024). Data were cleaned, reshaped, and summarized using the tidyverse package (Wickham et al., 2019). Empty strata were coded as missing (NA), while reported zeros were preserved. Because the dataset contained aggregated contingency tables, no imputation was conducted, and no individual-level data were inferred.

Statistical analysis

For each variable within each city, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed contrasting infested vs. non-infested children across the two factor levels. Unadjusted odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated on the log-odds scale using the Woolf method. When a cell count was zero, the Haldane–Anscombe correction (+0.5 to all cells) was applied before computing the log-OR and its standard error, to avoid undefined estimates and yield conservative intervals for sparse tables (Aragon et al., 2020). Statistical association was assessed with Pearson's χ2 test with one degree of freedom when all expected counts were ≥ 5, or Fisher's exact test otherwise. In tables with structural zeros, margins (an entire row or column summing to zero), no hypothesis test was performed, and the p-value was recorded as not applicable; the OR was nevertheless reported using the continuity-corrected estimate. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All calculations used base R together with tidyverse and epitools (Aragon et al., 2020).

Visualization

Forest plot of unadjustedOR with 95% CIs were generated on a logarithmic axis; displaying results separately for Mérida and Monterrey, with a vertical reference line at OR = 1. Prevalence by factor level was visualized using a heat map representing the percentage of infested children (infested/total x 100). All figures were produced with ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016).

Results

All students present at the participating schools on the day of evaluation were examined. In total, 6372 schoolchildren were included across both cities, comprising 3743 children in Monterrey and 2629 in Mérida. Among them, 590 children were positive for Pediculosis capitis, including 421 cases in Monterrey and 169 in Mérida. The distribution of sociodemographic, household, and hygiene-related variables by city is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. School-level prevalence was calculated as the number of infected children divided by the total number of examined children at each school, multiplied by 100. In the Monterrey metropolitan area (14 schools; 3743 examined; 421 positives), school-level prevalence ranged from 5.30% to 43.8% (Fig. 1). The highest values were observed at Santa Catarina Elementary School (14/32; 43.80%) and San Pedro Elementary School (15/35; 42.9%), followed by San Nicolás 3 Elementary School (20/70; 28.6%). Nine of the fourteen schools showed prevalences ≥10%, and three schools were ≥ 20%. The lowest value occurred at Escobedo 3 Elementary School (10/189; 5.3%).

In Mérida (8 schools; 2629 examined; 169 positives), school-level prevalence ranged from 5.26% to 9.60%, with the highest value at Mérida 7 Elementary School (24/250; 9.60%) and the lowest at Mérida 6 Elementary School (20/380; 5.3%).

For Monterrey, unadjusted OR with 95% CI are shown in Fig. 2. The OR for sex, was 27.67 (95% CI: 16.31–46.92; p < 0.001), and for hair length was 27.90 (95% CI: 16.45–47.32; p < 0.001). For fomites, the OR was 0.19 (0.14–0.25; p < 0.001), and for control methods, it was 0.004 (95% CI: 0.002–0.011; p < 0.001). Room density yielded 0.61 (95% CI: 0.48–0.78; p < 0.0019, and baths per day yielded an OR of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.52–0.98; p < 0.048). Mother's education, showed an OR of 3.68 (95% CI: 1.84–7.35; p < 0.001), father employed, showed an OR of 2.19 (95% CI: 1.38–3.45; p = 0.001), father's education, showed an OR of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.88–1.61; p = 0.262), and age, showed an OR of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.63–1.02; p = 0.082) (Fig. 2).

For Mérida, similar qualitative patterns were observed, although overall prevalence levels were lower across all factors (Fig. 3). As in Monterrey, higher prevalence was concentrated among females and children with long hair. Variation across hygiene-related, household, parental, and age-related factors was limited, and in some strata no observations were recorded, as reflected by blank tiles in Fig. 3.

Discussion

This study provides city-level evidence on Pediculosis capitis among children in two geographically distinct regions of Mexico. Across both settings, the descriptive profile of the examined population and questionnaire-derived characteristics is summarized in Supplementary Table S1, which contextualizes the patterns observed in prevalence and the unadjusted associations reported here. Overall prevalence differed between cities, and school-level estimates showed substantial heterogeneity, particularly in Monterrey (Fig. 1). In the unadjusted contrasts, sex and hair length consistently showed the strongest associations with PC in both cities (Fig. 2), whereas household and hygiene-related variables showed more heterogeneous patterns across settings (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

In the study by Valle Barbosa et al. (2020) involving 720 schoolchildren in Guadalajara, Mexico, Pediculosis capitis was more frequent among girls, who accounted for 82.35% of infested cases. The authors reported an overall prevalence of 4.72% and a cumulative prevalence of 51.94%, indicating that more than half of the students had experienced lice at least once. Infestation was also more common in long hair, and schools with more cases tended to correspond to a medium marginalization index. In our study, the pattern for sex and hair length was similar, with higher Pediculosis capitis prevalence among girls with long hair in both Mérida and Monterrey. Regarding school socioeconomic context, while Barbosa et al. (2020) emphasized institutions with medium marginalization, Pediculosis capitis in our data was observed across socioeconomic strata, including schools classified as medium/high, supporting the view that infestation is not restricted to low socioeconomic setting.

The findings of this study align with previous reports indicating that head-lice infestation is not limited to low socioeconomic settings. In line with Alzain (2012), our data from Nuevo León and Yucatán did not reveal marked differences in infestation rates between middle-class communities. Rather than socioeconomic status itself, contextual factors such as household conditions and hygiene practices may better explain local variation in PC. Similarly, Madke and Khopkar (2012) highlighted that extreme conditions, including overcrowding and limited access to water, are associated with higher infestation burdens and more severe clinical outcomes.

When comparing regional estimates, our PC infestation rates were lower than those reported in Gaza by Alzain (2012) and fell within the range observed in Turkey (13.4%) and Argentina (29.7%). In our dataset, Pediculosis capitis was concentrated among school-aged children (approximately 5–12 years), which is consistent with previous reports indicating that this age group is more frequently affected (Alzain, 2012).

Regarding household factors, our findings align with Alzain (2012), who reported that a larger family size was associated with a higher prevalence of Pediculosis capitis. This relationship likely reflects the increased frequency of close head-to-head contact among household members, particularly in homes where multiple children share sleeping spaces or personal hygiene items.

A key area where our findings diverge from previous reports concerns hair washing. While Alzain (2012) described a negative association between hair washing and Pediculosis capitis, our data did not reveal a consistent trend across study sites. In Mérida, the contrast of one bath per day versus more than one yielded an OR of 1.88 (95% CI: 1.00–3.56; p = 0.052), whereas in Monterrey, the corresponding contrast produced an OR of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.52–0.98; p = 0.048). Pediculosis capitis was detected even among children who reported routine hair washing, indicating that no single bathing-frequency category was uniformly associated with the absence of infestation.

Fomites, such as shared combs and hats, were associated with Pediculosis capitis in our data, consistent with findings from other regions (Iran) (Alborzi and Shekarriz-Foumani, 2016). Pediculosis capitis prevalence was also higher among girls than boys, a pattern reported in previous studies (East Azerbaijan Province, Iran) (Shayeghi et al., 2010). Longer hair has been proposed to favor louse attachment by providing a larger surface area, and our hair-length contrasts were indeed large and significant in both cities (Shayeghi et al., 2010). Our study found that gender is associated with a higher prevalence. Therefore, when comparing the study by Shayeghi et al. (2010), which mentions a higher percentage of girls than boys, we believe that hair length is linked to gender, as long hair is more common in girls than in boys, with some exceptions.

Although our study did not detect a clear association between socioeconomic status and Pediculosis capitis, targeted health education remains essential. Simple and low-cost programs that strengthen communication between schools and families, as suggested by Shayeghi et al. (2010) and Downs et al. (1999), could help reduce Pediculosis capitis prevalence by promoting routine head inspections, early detection, and preventive hygiene practices across communities, regardless of income level.

In addition to household-level factors, contextual school variables such as classroom type were also recorded. Although these variables did not show a consistent pattern across cities, their inclusion supports a broader interpretation of Pediculosis capitis transmission within both home and school environments.

In the study by Khais Muri Laabusi and Rhadi (2022), conducted between March and October 2020 in Al-Kut, Iraq, and its surrounding urban, rural, and marginal areas, the presence of human lice was investigated in 1632 individuals from 225 families representing different socioeconomic levels. Data were obtained through household surveys and direct visual inspections. Infestation rates varied by family size, 24.2%, 21.4%, 12.5%, and 8.6% for families with 10–15, 7–9, 4–6, and 2–3 members, respectively, and by area, with 11.5% in urban, 33.6% in rural, and 21.6% in marginal settings. Lice infestation was detected across all age groups, with the highest prevalence in children aged 3–17 years (79.04%) and the lowest in adults aged 18–40 years (20.9%). The study also reported higher infestation rates in women (21.8%) than in men (13.3%). Similarly, in our data, Pediculosis capitis was more frequent in females than in males and was concentrated among school-aged children (5–15 years), whereas parents were not included in the sampling.

In southwest Iran, Maramazi et al. (2019) reported Pediculosis capitis in 199 girls (23.38%). Using univariate logistic regression, the authors identified associations between Pediculosis capitis and several variables, including parental education level, father's occupation, sharing of personal hygiene items, number of combing sessions, infestation among family members, and previous infestation. In our data, the associations for fathers' education and sharing of personal hygiene items were consistent with their findings. However, we did not evaluate previous infestation or the number of combing sessions, precluding direct comparison for those variables.

Evidence syntheses have highlighted multiple factors associated with Pediculosis capitis. In the review by Fu et al. (2022), human pediculosis is described as a global public-health problem affecting millions of people, with a substantial burden in developing countries, although cases are also reported in high-income regions. Consistent with this observation, our data showed Pediculosis capitis in schools with medium- to high-socioeconomic levels, supporting the notion that the infestation is not limited to low-income populations (Fu et al., 2022).

Similarly, Valdez and Romero (2023) noted that lice occur worldwide and can affect individuals of any age, sex, race, or socioeconomic status. They reported that Pediculosis capitis is most frequently documented among children aged 3–11 years, including reports from Mexico, and that the prevalence tends to be higher in females and in individuals with long hair. They also indicated a lower frequency of transmission via fomites, including brushes, combs, barrettes, bedding, helmets, and hats. Our results are consistent with these patterns for sex and hair length.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although scalp examination combined visual inspection with fine-toothed combing to improve detection, underestimation of infestation cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between associated factors and infestation. Third, questionnaire-based variables relied on parental and teacher reports and may therefore be subject to recall or reporting bias. Finally, some contextual factors that may influence transmission dynamics, such as detailed behavioral interactions outside school or prior treatment history beyond binary categorization, were not assessed. These considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Pediculosis capitis is a common condition in school environments. In this cross-sectional study conducted in Monterrey and Mérida, the overall prevalence was 16.40% and 6.57%, respectively, with variation at the school level. The most consistent associations were observed for sex and hair length, with higher PC rates among girls and children with long hair in both cities. Associations with household and hygiene-related factors such as sharing personal items, bathing frequency, room density, and parental characteristics were heterogeneous across sites and did not follow a uniform pattern. Pediculosis capits occurred across socioeconomic strata, indicating that infestation is not limited to low-income groups.

These findings highlight the importance of school–home collaboration and routine preventive measures, including regular head inspections, early case detection, and targeted educational campaigns for families and teachers. Continued monitoring in schools may facilitate the detection of temporal trends and support locally adaptive prevention and control strategies.
