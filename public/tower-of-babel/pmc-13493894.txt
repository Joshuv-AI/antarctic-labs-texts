An rRNA-depleted full-length transcriptome strategy using nanopore sequencing for identification of novel lncRNA isoforms
Authors: Tianyuan Zhang, Jie Chen, Huiyu Hou, Salsabeel Yousuf, Jinlong Ji, Yali Li, Zhaoyang Tian, Dengli Guo, Huanyu Tang, Ting Qiu, Xiaojing Li, Weike Zeng, Man Lu, Yao Chen, Liu Yang, Hu Chen, Zhipeng Qu, Kai Wu, Tingyu Ma, Yong-Xin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play crucial roles in gene regulation, but their full-length isoforms are often missed because of the limitations of poly(A)-based enrichment and short-read sequencing. Here, we aim to establish a comprehensive transcriptome profiling that captures both poly(A)+ and poly(A)- RNA isoforms using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) R10.4.1 flowcells. We establish an rRNA-depleted full-length transcriptome sequencing workflow (NanoncRNA-Seq), and use it together with Illumina NovaSeq to profile lncRNA isoforms in Saccharomyces cerevisiae under glucose and ethanol-associated physiological states. We combine multiple analytical tools to evaluate expression levels, splicing patterns, variants, and lncRNA identification. ONT sequencing achieves high accuracy (Q-score: 22.35, 99.42%) and detects fewer SNP and more novel isoforms, while Illumina sequencing reports fewer INDELs. Expression profiles are highly consistent within each platform and moderately across platforms. Notably, NanoncRNA-seq enables isoform-resolved lncRNA discovery and recoveres substantially more lncRNAs than Illumina (Pinfish: n = 260; Illumina: n = 51), including more lincRNAs (n = 201 vs. n = 25), likely because low-abundance transcripts are difficult to reconstruct from short reads. Overall, NanoncRNA-Seq effectively captures full-length lncRNA isoform discovery and highlights the complementary strengths of ONT in transcriptome research.

Introduction

Advances in high-throughput sequencing have greatly increased interest in the role of long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) in gene regulation and various biological processes1. LncRNAs are a class of RNA molecules longer than 200 base pairs that do not encode proteins. They may possess poly(A) tails and a 5’-methylguanosine caps, and can undergo post-transcriptional splicing events2–4. Notably, lncRNAs are defined more by what they are not, namely protein-coding transcripts, than by a shared function or structure. This classification reflects the historical view that RNA primarily serves as a messenger between genes and proteins, whereas other non-coding RNAs (e.g., rRNAs, tRNAs, snoRNAs, and snRNAs) fulfill essential housekeeping roles5,6. As a class of regulatory molecules, lncRNAs exhibit diverse and complex functions. They participate in fundamental biological processes, such as post-transcriptional gene regulation, cell differentiation, and proliferation, and they also contribute to the development and progression of various diseases7,8. Altough extensive work has focused on lncRNAs' function in multicellular organisms, particularly mammals and model organisms, studies of lncRNAs in microorganisms remain relatively limited9. Nonetheless, lncRNAs have been identified in microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, and certain fungi, and may regulate microbial growth, development, metabolism, environmental adaptation, and host interactions10–12.

Because lncRNAs are often expressed at low levels, can share sequence similarity with other transcripts, and may exist as complex splice variants, studies based on conventional short-read sequencing have largely relied on reference-guided strategies. However, many lncRNAs exceed the read length of short-read sequencing technologies, leading to fragmented sequences and limiting accurate reconstruction of full-length transcripts2,13. Long-read sequencing technologies developed by Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) and Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) offer effective ways to address these limitations14–17. PacBio is frequently used for reference-free/novel transcript discovery, whereas ONT is widely used for isoform discovery and quantification; in practice, both technologies can support both applications depending on sequencing depth and analysis strategy18–20. Although ONT has strong potential in revealing complete RNA structure of RNA, several practical challenges remain, including efficient ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion, optimization of library preparation to improve lncRNA capture efficiency, and platform-specific quality control issues21. At present, most poly(A) enrichment long-read approaches for lncRNA analysis rely on mining lncRNAs from cDNA libraries. However, these methods may fail to detect non-polyadenylated lncRNAs because transcripts lacking poly(A) tails are excluded during poly(A) enrichment steps22. ONT currently does not provide a dedicated reagent kit for lncRNA sequencing.

Many existing analytical tools and pipelines were developed using short-read sequencing data or early-generation Oxford Nanopore flow cells such as R9.4.1, which had relatively high error rates and an average base-calling accuracy of approximately 94%23. Accordingly, although these tools performed well on earlier datasets, their generalizability to newer ONT chemistries/basecallers should be empirically re-assessed. With the introduction of Kit 14 (quality-score 20+ (Q20 + )) chemistry and the R10.4.1 flow cell, ONT has substantially improved raw read accuracy, surpassing Q20 and in some cases reaching Q2824,25. However, current ONT transcriptome library-preparation workflows are primarily optimized for poly(A) + RNA. Dedicated, widely adopted kits and standardized protocols specifically optimized for poly(A)- (non-polyadenylated) RNA sequencing remain limited, creating a bottleneck for comprehensive nanopore-based analysis of non-coding RNA, including lncRNA. These limitations highlight the need for further methodological development and systematic, multi-dimensional evaluations tailored to lncRNA studies on ONT platforms. Here, we introduce NanoncRNA-seq, a streamlined approach designed to enhance the detection and representation of both mRNA and ncRNA, particularly lncRNAs. This method leverages the high-accuracy R10.4.1 flow cells and is specifically optimized for eukaryotic microorganisms, particularly yeast species. Using NanoncRNA-seq, we conducted a comprehensive comparison with Illumina sequencing and established a complete workflow encompassing library preparation, sequencing, and automated bioinformatics analysis. This protocol enables the accurate reconstruction of full-length lncRNAs, significantly improving data processing efficiency and accuracy, thereby advancing lncRNA research in microbial systems.

Result

Development of a rRNA-depleted full-length transcriptome approach

To achieve comprehensive transcriptome profiling, including both poly(A)+ and poly(A)- RNAs, we developed a novel approach named NanoncRNA-seq (Fig. 1). This approach includes rRNA depletion, cDNA library preparation, and comparative analysis using both ONT and Illumina sequencing platforms (Fig. 1A). Total RNA was extracted from yeast cultures and subjected to rRNA depletion to minimize interference from highly abundant rRNA molecules. The resulting rRNA-depleted RNA was then used for cDNA library construction. To target both poly(A)+ and poly(A)- RNAs, rRNA probes along with enzymatic digestion using RNase H and DNase I were applied to remove residual rRNA and DNA contaminants (Fig. 1B). For ONT library preparation, a customized forward primer was synthesized with modifications at both ends: the 5’ end carried the App modification, and the 3’ end was modified with a spacer C3 to prevent unwanted ligation and RNA concatemer formation. App is a preactivated diphosphate donor, which can specifically promote the terminal connection of RNA or DNA through the catalysis of T4 RNA ligase 2, thereby reducing the production of non-specific by-products. Additionally, a sequence containing multiple mixed “n” nucleotides was attached to the 3’ end to improve ligation efficiency by reducing steric hindrance and enhancing the compatibility of the RNA ends during enzymatic ligation. A 3’ NV adapter was ligated to the RNA, followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced using ONT R10.4.1 flow cells. To evaluate workflow performance, we also constructed dUTP-based strand-specific libraries using short-read sequencing. Comparative analyses included assessments of R10.4.1 quality scores, rRNA-depletion efficiency, transcript discovery, expression patterns, lncRNA identification, and functional annotation. Furthermore, we examined alternative splicing events and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (Fig. 1C). High-quality data generation and effective rRNA removal by Nanopore R10.4.1 flow cell

The ONT R10.4.1 platform yielded an average of 25,038,764 (mean) ± 2,808,250 (SD) pass reads (Q score ≥10) per sample and an average sequencing yield of 12.64 (mean) ± 1.66 (SD) Gb per sample (Fig. 2A). The mean Q-score for raw data was 20.72 (raw accuracy: 99.15%), while for pass reads excluding primers, it was 22.35 (accuracy: 99.42%) (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table 1). The N50 of the pass reads ranged from 527–607 bp. In comparison, the Illumina Novaseq platform generated an average of 13.91 (SD ± 0.65) Gb of high-quality paired-end data per sample, with Q20 > 96% and Q30 > 91% across all the samples (Supplementary Table 2). To avoid erroneous identification of transcript termini caused by untrimmed adapter sequences, full-length reads containing correctly orientated strand-switching primer (SSP) and anchored oligo(dT) VN primer (VNP) were strand-aligned and trimmed using Pychopper. A total of 72% of quality-filtered reads containing adapters were retained for downstream analysis. The N50 of full-length reads ranged from 449–517 bp (Fig. 2C). The advanced rRNA removal kit used in this study showed high rRNA-depletion efficiency, with rRNA contents reduced to 0.0079% for Illumina and 1.0291% for ONT data (Fig. 2D). All samples achieved genome alignment rates above 90% (Fig. 2E). In the Cen_PK113-7d genome, 96.95% of annotated genes are single-exon. Performance of variants identification by ONT and Illumina platforms

To evaluate the efficacy of variant calling using R10.4.1 data, we compared variant calls from ONT and Illumina datasets across two yeast physiological states (metabolic phases): glucose utilization and anaerobic respiration. Despite the high accuracy of ONT R10.4.1 reads (> Q20), fewer SNPs were detected in the ONT datasets than in the Illumina datasets. Specifically, 158 and 147 SNPs were identified in ONT samples (S1, glucose utilization; S2, anaerobic respiration), whereas 175 and 180 SNPs were detected in the corresponding Illumina datasets (T1, glucose utilization; T2, anaerobic respiration), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). However, the number of INDELs identified in ONT data was approximately five times higher than that detected by Illumina. Cross-platform comparisons revealed substantial overlaps between the two datasets, with 108 and 107 shared variant sites identified during glucose utilization (S1 vs. T1) and anaerobic respiration (S2 vs. T2) phases, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Notably, the ONT-specific variants were predominantly insertions, whereas Illumina-specific variants were mainly SNPs. To further characterize platform-specific differences in INDEL calling, we summarized the length distribution of insertions and deletions (1–2 bp, 3–5 bp, 6–10 bp, and >10 bp) (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). In particular, the ONT datasets contained markedly more 1–2 bp insertions/deletions than Illumina, while longer INDELs ( ≥ 6 bp) were infrequent in both platforms. Finally, we visually inspected representative variant sites in IGV to provide read-level support for the calls. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 4–6, consistent mismatch and/or gapped-alignment patterns were observed at the variant positions across multiple loci on CP046081.1, indicating that the detected variants are supported by two sequencing platforms.

mRNA expression profiling using NanoncRNA-seq

To evaluate the reproducibility of mRNA analysis using this approach, we calculated gene expression correlation between the ONT and Illumina platforms. Spearman correlation analysis revealed strong intra-platform consistency, with coefficients (ρ) exceeding 0.995 for Illumina and ≥0.986 for ONT. Inter-platform rho value ranged from 0.697 to 0.733, indicating substantial concordance in gene expression profiles (Fig. 3A). We further identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from both platforms (Fig. 3B–D). A total of 80.1% of DEGs were shared between ONT and Illumina, while 10.7% and 9.3% were uniquely detected by ONT and Illumina, respectively. Among the top 15 up- and down-regulated DEGs, 57.9% and 66.7% were commonly identified by both platforms, highlighting strong similarity in core gene expression profiles. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed for DEGs and differentially expressed transcript (DETs) identified from the metabolic phase comparisons (T1 vs T2 and S1 vs S2) (Fig. 3E). DEGs identified by ONT were significantly enriched in 13 metabolic pathways, all of which overlapped with those detected by Illumina. For DETs, ONT identified 15 enriched pathways, including three biologically significant pathways: metabolic pathway, ribosome, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Comparative analysis of genes involved in pathways between ONT and Illumina

To investigate the consistency and divergence of gene expression across sequencing platforms, we compared the expression patterns of key metabolic pathway genes between Illumina and ONT platforms. We focused on genes involved in cellular energy metabolism and stress response, including the TCA cycle, glyoxylate cycle, ethanol degradation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and trehalose metabolism (Supplementary Table 3). Overall, gene expression patterns of the majority of genes were markedly consistent between ONT and Illumina platforms. Notably, genes such as IDH1/2, KGD1/2, SDH1-4, FUM1, and MDH1/2/3, all component of the TCA cycle, were significantly upregulated on both platforms. Similarly, ICL1/2 and MLS1, which participate in the glyoxylate cycle, also showed strong upregulation, suggesting a metabolic shift toward gluconeogenesis or non-fermentative growth. However, minor differences in expression levels were observed for a few genes. For example, ADH4 exhibited a log₂FC of −0.76 on Illumina and −1.03 on ONT, while PFK2 showed −0.94 and −1.02, respectively. Despite these small differences, the overall expression patterns remained strongly consistent across platforms, highlighting the robustness of NanoncRNA-seq in identifying consistent metabolic expression profiles. To validate our RNA-seq findings, qRT-PCR was performed on eight randomly selected genes (FPB, mleA, fumC, ACSS1_2, DLD, OGDH, DLST, and ALD1A) from the analyzed set. The expression levels quantified by qRT-PCR exhibited a consistent expression pattern with RNA-seq results, supporting the accuracy of transcriptome-based differential expression analysis (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 7).

Performance of transcript re-construction by different bioinformatics tools

We analyzed the distribution of structural categories and transcript lengths reconstructed by different bioinformatics tools using SQANTI3. The Full Splice Match (FSM) category demonstrated the highest proportionality and a highly consistent distribution of transcript lengths across all approaches (Fig. 4A), indicating a substantial consistency in detecting well-annotated transcripts. Specifically, Illumina yielded the highest proportion of FSM transcripts, suggesting its ability to detect known transcripts. Nevertheless, it can also identify other types of transcripts, including fusion, antisense, genic intron, novel_not_in_catalog (NNC), and novel_in_catalog (NIC). Substantial differences emerged among the structural categories across approaches. For ONT data, Pinfish generated the largest proportions (90.59%) of FSM transcripts, along with a notable amount of intergenic, intronic, and NIC transcripts. Conversely, transcripts categorized as fusion, genic, and NNC were least represented. StringTie_ref generated 89.37% FSM transcripts, the second highest among all approaches, and exhibited a balanced distribution of other categories. Isoquant achieved an FSM proportion of 88.00% and generated a markedly higher proportion of NNC transcripts compared to other approaches. A significant number of antisense and fusion transcripts were identified by both RNAbloom2 and Pinfish_RNAbloom2. Furthermore, StringTie_ref exhibited performance similar to StringTie across the majority of transcript categories. We further examined transcript length distributions across structural classes for each approach (Fig. 4B). The length distribution of FSM transcripts remained relatively stable across approaches. However, significant differences were observed in the median lengths of NNC transcripts: Isoquant and Pinfish exhibited shorter median lengths (672 bp and 535 bp, respectively), whereas PR and Stringtie yielded longer median lengths (955 bp and 728 bp, respectively), indicating differences in reconstruction behavior across pipelines. For antisense transcripts, Isoquant produced a shorter median length (727.5 bp), reflecting lower performance in resolving complex antisense events. Fusion transcripts, which represent highly complex genomic events, were best captured by Stringtie, which reported a significantly longer median length (8397.5 bp) compared to other approaches.

We also evaluated canonical/non-canonical and known/novel splicing events, together with soft-clipping rates (Fig. 4C). Illumina data exhibited the highest proportions of novel_canonical (32.82%) and novel_non-canonical (30.25%) junctions. In contrast, Pinfish reported significantly lower proportions of novel junctions—only 2.60% novel_canonical and 25.00% novel_non-canonical, despite maintaining low soft-clipping rates (0.91% for known_canonical and 0.92% for known_non-canonical). Isoquant demonstrated moderate performance, detecting 12.46% novel_canonical and 23.78% novel_non-canonical junctions, with soft-clipping rates ranging from 0.84–1.01%. Meanwhile, StringTie and StringTie_ref identified 14.10% and 11.03% novel_canonical and 15.45% and 18.75% novel_non-canonical junctions, respectively. Notably, StringTie_ref exhibited slightly better junction accuracy (soft-clipping rates: 0.91% and 0.96%) (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, RNAbloom2 identified 5.60% novel_canonical junctions but 0% novel_non-canonical, possibly due to stringent filtering or limited capacity for detecting complex splicing events, despite maintaining soft-clipping rates comparable to other tools. PR and RNAbloom2 showed similar overall trends. For transcript expression distribution, ONT data consistently exhibited a wider range of expression levels than Illumina data (Fig. 4E), further highlighting platform-specific differences in transcript quantification sensitivity.

Performance of lncRNA identification by different bioinformatics tools

We utilized GFFcompare to evaluate transcripts reconstructed by various approaches against known transcripts. ONT data yielded more novel transcripts than Illumina data (Fig. 5A). De novo approaches such as RNAbloom2 and Pinfish-RNAbloom2, identified the highest numbers of novel transcripts ( > 2000), primarily classified under the “x” and “o” categories. Nonetheless, de novo approaches are generally less suitable for conventional transcriptome analyses due to their lower annotation accuracy and structural ambiguity. Among reference-based reconstruction tools, Pinfish generated the fewest novel transcripts overall but identified the highest number of “u”-type transcripts. In contrast, Isoquant identified the most “o” and “j” type transcripts, while reporting the fewest “u” type transcripts. StringTie_ref demonstrated a more balanced performance across all transcript categories. Despite generating the fewest novel transcripts, Pinfish identified the largest number of lncRNAs (260), including the highest number of lincRNAs (201) (Fig. 5B). Following that, StringTie_ref and Isoquant identified 182 and 163 lncRNAs, respectively. In contrast, Illumina data, despite its higher sequencing depth, yielded only 51 lncRNAs. To further evaluate the performance of bioinformatics tools in lncRNA analysis, we examined lncRNA length and expression attributes. For lncRNA length (Fig. 5C), Isoquant produced the shortest lengths overall. While the average length determined by Pinfish was shorter than that from Illumina, its maximum length exceeded that of Illumina. For differentially expressed lncRNAs (Fig. 5D), StringTie and StringTie_ref produced transcripts with notably longer lengths than other approaches. In terms of TPM distribution (Fig. 5E), both Illumina and Isoquant exhibited a broader expression profile, highlighting their ability to detect transcripts across a wide abundance range. Conversely, Pinfish and RNAbloom concentrated on low-expression transcripts, indicating their strength in detecting low-abundance lncRNAs. Among differentially expressed lncRNAs (Fig. 5F), Isoquant had the highest mean TPM (569.79), significantly surpassing other approaches. Conversely, Pinfish, RNAbloom, and StringTie had lower mean TPMs (31.40, 32.19, and 33.18, respectively), consistent with their general lncRNA expression profiles. These findings highlight the unique strengths and limitations of each approach in reconstructing and analyzing lncRNAs, offering essential guidance for tool selection in transcriptome studies.

We performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (q-value < 0.05) on the identified lncRNA target gene sets to assess functional performance of these genes across approaches (Fig. 5G). The findings indicated that several enriched pathways (eight in all) were uniquely identified by individual approach, suggesting approach-specific functional insights. Approaches employing ONT data, like Pinfish, identified a higher number of shared pathways than other tools. Pinfish exhibited significant enrichment in critical cellular pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, spliceosome, ribosome, and metabolic pathways, sharing enriched pathways with four other approaches. Notably, the ribosome pathway was enriched by five approaches. In contrast, Illumina data exhibited the most significant enrichment in the arginine and proline metabolism pathway (sce00330, q = 0.0018), along with several other metabolism-related pathways. Of all enriched pathways, eight were unique to Illumina. Additionally, Illumina shared one enriched pathway each with Isoquant and PR, indicating a limited yet selective overlap.

Comparison of alternative splicing profiles revealed by Illumina and ONT

To assess the consistency of alternative splicing (AS) detection, we evaluated seven categories of AS events identified by various approaches (Supplementary Table 5). Illumina-based assemblies consistently detected a higher number of AS events, particularly retained introns (RI) and alternative splice sites (A3, A5). Moreover, AS detection using Illumina data demonstrated greater consistency across biological replicates and experimental conditions. In contrast, ONT-based tools exhibited variability in both the number and significance of detected AS events. For instance, Isoquant reported the highest number of A3 and A5 events, whereas Pinfish detected markedly fewer AS events across all categories. Alternative first exon (AF) and alternative last exon (AL) events remained consistently low across all approaches.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a full-length transcriptome sequencing strategy based on the ONT platforms, which includes rRNA removal and enables the simultaneous sequencing and analysis of mRNA and ncRNA, with comparison to the Illumina short read sequencing platform. We systematically evaluated the advantages and limitation of various transcript reconstruction approaches, clarifying their strengths and weaknesses in transcriptome analysis. This strategy supports comprehensive transcriptome analysis, including isoform-level resolution, variant detection, and lncRNA identification. Compared to the Nano-3P-seq26, which relies on N base complementarity, and FLEP-seq227, which employs direct reverse transcription, our Nano-ncRNA-seq workflow better preserves non-poly(A) RNA, enabling more uniform coverage and improved sensitivity for low-abundance non-polyadenylated transcripts. Together with the specialized primer design, this workflow supports efficient reverse transcription across diverse RNA types. The rRNA-depletion kit used in this study was highly effective and reduced rRNA content to minimal levels. Nonetheless, even with the same input RNA following rRNA-deletion, the effectiveness of rRNA removal may vary across sequencing platforms28. Illumina short-read sequencing may fail to detect residual rRNA fragments with slightly lower rRNA abundance. Since the rRNA probes used were specifically tailored to the target species, the future applications of customized probes could further enhance the depletion process, particularly for non-model prokaryotic organisms and complex microbial communities29–31. Historically, ONT has been associated with higher sequencing error rates. However, with the use of R10.4.1 flow cells and the Dorado basecaller with the super model, we achieved a raw read accuracy of 99.15%. Notably, adapter- and primer-trimmed pass reads exhibited even higher Q-scores than those reported in previous studies32, reflecting significant improvements in both basecalling algorithms and the performance of ONT R10.4.1 flow cell. Despite this, the effective data obtained in our experiments indicated an N50 range of 449–517 bp, which was lower than in previously published full-length transcriptome studies32,33, likely due to RNA fragmentation during the rRNA removal. An important caveat of RNase H–based rRNA depletion is the potential for off-target cleavage. Spurious or partial hybridization of depletion probes to non-rRNA transcripts (e.g., lncRNAs and other repetitive or low-complexity RNAs) can recruit RNase H activity and lead to undesired fragmentation, thereby biasing transcript integrity and quantification. This risk can be mitigated by careful probe design and stringent hybridization/enzymatic conditions, and should be monitored using post-depletion RNA integrity assessment. Nevertheless, genome-guided transcriptome assembly partially compensate for this limitation. Overall, our findings indicate that the rRNA-depletion method used here is suitable for both short-read and long-read sequencing libraries, enabling robust and comprehensive analysis of both mRNA and ncRNA, while Illumina data remains advantageous for SNP detection and expression quantification34,35. Although ONT R10.4.1 platform produced highly accurate data in our study, the two platforms showed different variant-calling profiles, with fewer SNP calls but more INDEL calls detected in ONT than in Illumina, consistent with its known error profile23. A moderate overlap in variant calls between the two platforms validates their mutual reliability while also revealing distinct detection preferences. Notably, ONT-specific variants were predominantly insertions, whereas Illumina-specific variants were mainly SNPs. These results underscore the unique strengths of each platform for different genomic applications.

Long-read RNA-seq enables the detection of small variants (SNPs and indels) within RNA and allows these variants to be linked to specific full-length transcript isoforms, significantly advancing the study of allelic variation in gene expression and splicing. Currently, tools such as Clair336, GATK437 and DeepVariant38 demonstrate excellent performance in variant calling from long-read data. Additionally, variant calling tools specifically designed for long-read RNA sequencing, such as Clair3-RNA39 and longcallR (https://github.com/huangnengCSU/longcallR) have also been developed. However, their performance still requires further validation to assess their suitability and reliability across diverse applications40. High intra-platform correlations coefficients (Illumina: ρ > 0.995; ONT: ≥ 0.986) and moderate inter-platform correlations (ρ = 0.697–0.733) underscore the robust expression quantification capabilities of both sequencing technologies. Approximately 80% of DEGs were identified by both platforms, and KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that both datasets encompassed the same 13 metabolic pathways. Discrepancies in DEGs/DETs between pipelines are expected, as read assignment and alignment (e.g., indel/splice-junction handling), together with isoform complexity and read-length differences, can alter whether reads uniquely support a given gene/isoform. Moreover, ONT and Illumina exhibited remarkably consistent expression trends in key metabolic processes such as the TCA cycle, glyoxylate bypass, and ethanol degradation. Although minor differences were noted, for instance, in the expression of ADH4 and PFK2, they did not affect the overall interpretation of metabolic trends. qRT-PCR validation further supported the accuracy of transcriptomic differential analysis. Previous studies have reported a strong correlation between qPCR data and ONT-derived expression levels41, whereas partial correlations with the Illumina platform were reported to be negative occasionally. However, in this study, no such discrepancies were observed, possibly due to the limited number (eight) of genes selected for qPCR validation.

Currently, short-read RNA-Seq technologies are limited by read length, which restricts their ability to resolve transcript complexity42. The advent of long-read RNA sequencing has provided a powerful tool for the identification and quantification of full-length transcripts, significantly enhancing the depth and precision of transcriptomic research. Initially, long-read sequencing technologies faced considerable technical challenges due to high base error rates (ranging from 5% to 20%). However, with advancements in sequencing accuracy, numerous novel tools have been developed to analyze long-read transcriptome data, including StringTie243, TAGET44, Oarfish (https://github.com/COMBINE-lab/oarfish)45, ESPRESSO46, Isoquant47, FLAIR248, Transgram (https://github.com/yutingsdu/TransGram), Pinfish (https://github.com/nanoporetech/pinfish), and Bambu49, among others. These tools are tailored to correct errors inherent in long-read sequencing. For example, Isoquant constructs an intron graph and applies an inexact intron-chain matching algorithm to identify isoforms. This algorithm addresses splice site shifts caused by sequencing errors and assigns reads to known isoforms for accurate transcript structure reconstruction. StringTie2 uses a maximum flow algorithm on the splice graph to identify transcript paths, filtering out erroneous paths and candidate transcripts with low coverage or weak read support. Bambu utilizes a probabilistic model to correct splice site alignments, integrate annotations and sequence data, group reads into read classes (RCs), and quantify transcript expression using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. It further refines accuracy by filtering out RCs with low-normalized detection ratios. FLAIR employs event-level alignment algorithms and splice site correction to build high-accuracy transcript models, iteratively refining them after the initial assembly to reduce errors. It corrects sequencing errors in long-read data and transcript models. Pinfish, tailored for ONT data, performs genome alignment, multi-round error correction, and consensus sequences generation through long-read redundancy. These algorithms can be broadly classified into two main types: guided and unguided modes, offering flexibility to accommodate diverse research needs.

In this study, we compared various transcript reconstruction approaches and found substantial discrepancies in their efficacy for isoform identification and classification. Compared to short-read data, long-read sequencing demonstrated superior performance in identifying full-length isoforms. Structural classification using SQANTI3 revealed that ONT-based approaches identified more intergenic and NIC transcripts, underscoring their effectiveness in capturing transcriptome structural diversity. In contrast, Illumina short-read data achieved the highest percentage of FSM transcripts but showed limitations in identifying fusion, antisense, and novel isoforms. Interestingly, the median transcript lengths of antisense, genic, and NNC categories reconstructed from Illumina data were longer than those from ONT data (Fig. 4B). We note that these differences may reflect platform- and pipeline-dependent effects rather than true biological transcript length. Higher Illumina sequencing depth can provide denser evidence for reconstruction. however, short-read assembly/reconstruction may also inflate transcript span in complex loci by bridging fragmentary signals across overlapping transcription units or alternative paths in splice graphs43,50. Ambiguous read placement in repetitive or paralogous regions can further contribute to extended or incorrect transcript models51. Library preparation may also play a role: random hexamer priming during cDNA synthesis can introduce systematic biases and can capture heterogeneous RNA fragments (including partially degraded molecules), producing dispersed coverage that may be “stitched” into longer transcript models by assembly algorithms52,53. By contrast, ONT-based reconstructions may be biased toward shorter models when reads are truncated (particularly toward the 5′ end) and when platform-specific errors affect alignment and isoform collapsing33.

ONT-based approaches excelled in detecting structural diversity and longer transcripts, especially in intergenic and antisense categories. Although ONT sequencing generated far fewer total reads than Illumina, it identified significantly more lncRNAs, demonstrating the advantages of long-read sequencing in novel transcript detection. Previous studies have shown that full-length, high-accuracy reads are particularly important for transcript discovery, while higher read depth is critical for transcript quantification. This is consistent with our findings, where a relatively small ONT data identified more novel transcripts than a large Illumina dataset54. A prior benchmarking study reported the discovery of 1,531 novel transcripts using long-read sequencing, with 40.6% located in uncharacterized genomic regions41, further highlighting its strength in uncovering novel transcripts. The relatively smaller number of transcripts identified by Pinfish is consistent with earlier studies55. Notably, Pinfish identified the highest number of lncRNAs (n = 260), showing exceptional performance in identifying long intergenic non-coding RNA (lincRNA), suggesting it is a particularly powerful tool for lncRNA research. In a comparative study using simulated data, Isoquant emerged as the best-performing tool for transcript identification overall, while Bambu and StringTie2 also demonstrated excellent performance46. However, Nanopore simulated dataset generally exhibited poor performance across tools, likely due to limited coverage and insufficient accuracy54. Importantly, no single approach has yet emerged as a definitive best for all use cases. Tool selection should be based on specific research tools. While some tools are optimized for quantifying annotated transcript isoforms, others are better suited for identifying novel transcript isoforms. Ultimately, choosing the appropriate software requires considerable of multiple factors, including reference genome quality, analysis accuracy, and study-specific requirements54. Overall, accurately estimating novel transcripts remains a significant challenge across all existing methods. This highlights not only the complexity of transcriptomes but also the need for enhancements in existing tools for the identification and validation of novel transcripts. Future advancements will require a combination of higher-precision technologies, more robust algorithms, and deeper insights into transcriptome biology to address this challenge effectively.

Despite the improved sensitivity of long-read sequencing for transcript discovery, false-positive lncRNA calls may still arise from sequencing/alignment errors, RT template-switching, genomic DNA contamination, or transcript fragmentation. To mitigate this risk, we applied a conservative filtering strategy. Specifically, we excluded transcripts overlapping annotated protein-coding loci that were more consistent with mRNA fragments or extensions, required each retained lncRNA/isoform to be supported by at least 10 full-length reads, and defined lncRNA candidates as those consistently classified as non-coding by multiple independent predictors (CPC < 0, CNCI < 0, and PLEK). Collectively, these orthogonal criteria (genomic context, read-level support, and coding-potential consensus) increase confidence that the reported lncRNAs represent genuine non-coding transcripts rather than technical artifacts. For this analysis, we recommend using Pychopper to retain full-length reads, Minimap2 for spliced alignment to reference, Pinfish for isoform reconstruction/collapsing, and SQANTI3 followed by gffcompare for structural quality control and novelty classification, together with stricter read-support thresholds for novel isoforms (e.g., ≥3 or 10 full-length reads) to reduce false positives. We believe these additions provide readers with a clear starting experimental and analytical configuration for lncRNA isoform studies.

Nevertheless, we note that computational filters cannot completely eliminate all artifacts, especially for lowly expressed single-exon transcripts or loci in repetitive regions. Future work could further validate a subset of novel lncRNAs using junction-spanning RT-PCR/Sanger sequencing, strand-specific assays, and/or independent datasets (e.g., replicate libraries, CAGE/Ribo-seq where available) to provide additional orthogonal support.

For alternative splice detection, Illumina-based approaches yielded more RI events than ONT-based approaches in our analysis. Because S. cerevisiae has limited alternative splicing, indeed, single-exon transcripts accounted for 96.96% of reference transcripts (Fig. 2F). So we did not apply additional RI-specific confidence filtering (e.g., minimum junction support, intron-body coverage constraints, or orthogonal validation). We attribute this difference largely to reconstruction behavior rather than biology: short-read assemblies may generate RI transcript models more readily due to ambiguous mapping and fragmented coverage, whereas long-read pipelines often enforce more conservative isoform definitions focused on well-supported splice junctions and full-length consistency, which can reduce the number of reconstructed RI models. Similar platform- and pipeline-dependent differences in intron retention detection have been reported previously, highlighting that RI estimates from short-read can be sensitive to reconstruction and filtering choices and may not fully agree with long-read-derived isoforms56,57.

This study has several minor limitations. First, the lack of RNA modification data may limit a comprehensive understanding of RNA functions and regulatory mechanisms. This limitation could potentially be addressed by using direct RNA sequencing (DRS) approaches33,58–60. Second, the absence of spike-in standards for calibration may affect the quantitative accuracy and reproducibility of the results to some extent61. Although this study was designed primarily for method development and comparative study rather than to test a specific biological hypothesis, the rRNA-depleted, full-length Nanopore transcriptome strategy has clear biological relevance. Compared with conventional poly(A)-selected RNA-seq, rRNA depletion is, in principle and in practice, better suited to capture non-polyadenylated (non-poly(A)) RNA species, thereby reducing systematic biases introduced by poly(A) selection. For instance, prior work reported that a subset of SatIII transcripts is non-poly(A), and SatIII RNA abundance can increase under stress conditions62; Therefore, rRNA-depleted Nanopore full-length sequencing provides a more comprehensive route to profile both poly(A) and non-poly(A) transcripts, resolve complex isoforms, and assess condition-dependent transcriptomic changes63. Accordingly, experimental validation of newly identified lncRNA candidates was not included in this work. Future studies leverage this approach to validate the functional roles of lncRNA candidates. Although we applied conservative filtering criteria and integrated multiple coding-potential predictors to mitigate false-positive annotations, future studies will be required to experimentally confirm a subset of candidates and to assess their functional relevance. In addition, the rRNA-depletion probes used here were designed for S. cerevisiae, extending the experimental workflow to other species will require additional optimization and validation. Furthermore, the data obtained through this strategy is not recommended for alternative polyadenylation (APA) analysis, as APA analysis requires all reads to possess poly(A) tails. Moreover, the development of tools specifically tailored for prokaryotic species remains an unaddressed necessity. In summary, this study demonstrates that an rRNA-depleted full-length transcriptome strategy enables the accurate identification of novel lncRNA isoforms and reveals deeper insights into transcriptome complexity.

In conclusion, NanoncRNA-seq enables robust transcriptome profiling beyond conventional poly(A)-enriched RNA-seq. By combining rRNA depletion with full-length Nanopore sequencing, this strategy provides broader transcriptome coverage and improved sensitivity for detecting low-abundance and non-polyadenylated RNA species, including unconventional lncRNAs that are likely to be underrepresented in poly(A)-selected datasets. Importantly, long-read sequencing facilitates isoform-resolved characterization of lncRNAs, because many isoforms differ only subtly in splice junction usage or transcription start/end sites. Compared with Illumina short-read approaches, NanoncRNA-seq offers a practical advantage for discovering and describing rare, low-abundance lncRNA isoforms, particularly those that are long, structurally complex, or lack poly(A) tails, thereby providing a methodological foundation for more comprehensive investigations of non-coding transcriptome diversity.

Material and methods

Yeast cultivation, RNA purification, and reverse transcription

Saccharomyces cerevisiae CEN.PK113-7D (MATa MAL2-8c SUC2, purchased from Bio Sci Bio, Hangzhou, China) was cultured overnight in 15 mL of yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) medium containing 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/ L of peptone and 20 g/L of glucose. For RNA extraction, S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-7D was cultivated in 50 mL of defined media composed of 20 g/L of glucose, 7.5 g/L of (NH4)2SO4, and 14.4 g/L KH2PO4, with the pH adjusted to 6.5 using NaOH. To capture transcriptomes under two distinct yeast physiological (metabolic) states, samples were collected at two time points: (i) mid-exponential growth on glucose (~4.3 × 10^7 cells/mL) and (ii) post-glucose oxidative growth on ethanol/fermentation products (~2.6 × 10^8 cells/mL). These two sampling points correspond to the conditions referred to as glucose utilization and oxidative/respiratory growth, respectively, throughout the manuscript. At each sampling point, 15 mL of culture was rapidly transferred into a 50 ml conical tube half-filled with ice pellets, centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was isolated using the TSP0202 RNA Plus kit (Tsingke, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were measured using NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE) and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), respectively. Then, rRNA was subsequently removed using the Ribo-Clean rRNA-Depletion Kit Mega (Fungi) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), which employs specific rRNA probes for efficient depletion. The rRNA-depletion products were purified using RNA Clean beads (N412, Vazyme, China).

ONT library preparation and sequencing

Reverse Adapters (App--GTACTCTGCGAAGATACCTGTGCAA-- 3’ space) were ligated to the 3’ ends of purified rRNA-depleted RNA using T4 RNA Ligase 2, truncated KQ (M0373S, NEB, USA) ligase enzyme. Reverse transcriptase was then added to the ligation products to synthesize full-length cDNA with incorporation of template Switching Oligo (TSO) sequences. The reverse-transcription primer used was 5’- TTGCACAGGTATCTTCGCAGAGTAC(N)n-3’, and the reverse-transcription Strand-Switching Primer (SSP) sequence was 5’-TTTCTGTTGGTGCTGATATTGCTmGmGmG-3’ (where *mG indicates modified guanine bases). Following this, barcoded adapters were employed for PCR amplification and labeling of different cDNA products, using 13 amplification cycles. The amplified cDNA products were then processed for library construction according to the Oxford Nanopore ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK114). The final library was loaded onto an R10.4.1 flow cell and sequenced on a PromethION platform.

Illumina library preparation and sequencing

We used 1 μg of rRNA-depleted RNA per sample as input material for Illumina sequencing. Samples were treated DNase to remove residual DNA and were then purified using magnetic beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). A fragmentation buffer was added to shear the purified RNA into short fragments. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase. The second strand was synthesized using RNase H and DNA polymerase I, during which dTTP was replaced with dUTP in the dNTP mixture and reaction buffer. Next, the 3′ ends of the resulting DNA fragments were adenylated, and an Illumina sequencing adapter was ligated for hybridization. Library fragments were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411-03, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to select cDNA fragments approximately 350-400 bp. PCR amplification was then conducted to complete library construction. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies USA). Sequencing was performed on the Nextseq500 platform (Illumina Company, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Cross-platform data processing and transcriptome analysis

Raw Illumina reads in FASTQ format were processed with Fastp program64,65 to remove adapter sequences, poly-N stretches, and low-quality reads, yielding high-quality clean reads. Summary statistics for raw and filtered reads were generated using SeqKit266. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences were retrieved from the RFam database and used to build a custom rRNA database, including 5S rRNA (RF00001), 5.8S rRNA (RF00002), tRNA (RF00005), eukaryotic small subunit rRNA (RF01960), eukaryotic large subunit rRNA (RF02543), and bacterial large subunit rRNA (RF02541). The decontamination process was performed using the Bowtie2 tool. The strand-specificity of the libraries was assessed using the ‘infer_experiment.py’ script from the RSeQC package67. Clean reads were then aligned to the S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-7D genome (GenBank assembly accession: GCA_002571405.2) using HISAT2 v2.2.168.Transcript identification and assembly were carried out with Stringtie v2.2.143. Transcript assemblies were evaluated with Gffcompare, and FASTA sequences were extracted from the merged assembled transcriptome using gffread69. Finally, transcript abundances were estimated using Stringtie2.

For ONT R10.4.1 reads, simplex basecalling, demultiplexing, and adapter/barcode trimming were performed with Dorado 0.7.0 (http://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado), applying the models dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_super@v5.0.0 (for downstream analysis) and hac@v5.0.0 (for comparative quality score analysis). To identify full-length sequences in pass data, utilized Pychopper software (version: 2.4.0, -Z 50, https://github.com/nanoporetech/pychopper), which removed adapter, barcodes, and primer sequences. Filtered full-length sequence were aligned to the reference genome using minimap2 v2.17-r94 with the parameters -ax splice -uf -k1470. The alignment results were further processed using SAMtools software v1.1571. For gene/transcript identification, Pinfish was used to generate the collapsed transcript annotations. Additionally, we compared transcriptome assemblies generated by the following approaches: (a) Isoquant47 and StringTie2_ref with reference mode; (b) StringTie2 and RNA-Bloom272 with reference-free mode; (c) combined Pinfish and RNA-Bloom2 in a hybrid mode. To evaluate RNAbloom2, we provided the full-length RNA assemblies to PASA73 for transcript structure annotation against the reference genome. For hybrid transcript merging, gffread was used to combine annotations from RNA-Bloom2 and Pinfish69. All tarnscriptome assemblies were assessed using SQANTI374 and GFFcompare69. SQANTI3 was used for quality assessment, classification, and functional annotation of long-reads transcripts. GFFcompare was used to compare transcript annotations and assign class codes (e.g., “i”, “j”, “o”, “u, “x”, “=”, “c”, etc.) based on their relationships to the reference annotation. Coding regions of novel transcripts were predicted using TransDecoder v5.5.0 with the parameters -m 50 and -single_best_only. Gene/Transcript quantification was performed using Salmon75. Differential expression analysis was carried out using the DESeq2 R package (v1.12.3). Genes with a q-value < 0.05 and an absolute Foldchange ≥ 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Expression correlation between datasets was calculated using the Spearman correlation coefficient (rho).

Bioinformatics pipeline for identification of lncRNAs

To identify long non-coding (lncRNAs), transcripts were filtered based on structural characteristics and non-coding functional features. Novel transcripts classified by GFFcompare as “i”, “j”, “o”, “u”, and “x” were retained for further analysis. Transcripts lacking protein-coding potential were identified using the Coding Potential Calculator (CPC, score < 0), Coding-Non-Coding Index (CNCI, score <0), and PLEK. High confidence lncRNAs retained for downstream analysis. For cis-target prediction, protein-coding genes located within 100 kb upstream or downstream of each lncRNA were defined as candidate targets. For trans-target lncRNAs, target genes were identified on the basis of expression correlation between lncRNAs and protein-coding genes using Python scripts76. Genes with an absolute correlation coefficient > 0.95 were considered potential targets. Functional enrichment analysis of lncRNAs target genes was then performed using ClusterProfiler v477.

Bioinformatics pipeline for evaluation of SNPs and alternative splicing

SNPs were identified using DeepVariant (v1.4.0, mode: WES) and filtered with GLnexus (v1.4.1) to retain only variant with a “PASS” filter value78. BCFtools mpileup79 was used to obtain DP4 values, representing the number of REF and ALT reads, for additional SNP filtering. Variant calling quality was further evaluated by visual inspection of a subset of loci in Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV) (v2.19.1)80. AS events were detected using SUPPA2 (parameters: -boundary S -f ioe -e SE SS MX RI FL), which identified splicing types in each sample. Differential AS events between groups were identified using the DiffSplice function in SUPPA2 by calculating ΔPSI (dPSI) values and associated p values. All data were analyzed using R scripts (version 4.1.0), and basic figures were plotted with ggplot2 (v4.1.0)81. A summary of all bioinformatics tools used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Real-time quantitative PCR

The cDNA samples were synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The resulting cDNA was diluted 30-fold and used as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Reactions were performed with 2 × SYBR® Premix Ex Taq ™II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Dalian, China) on a Roche LightCycler 96 real-time PCR machine (Roche, Germany). β-actin was used as the reference gene for normalization, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔct method. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed with three independent biological replicates. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed with independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. For transcriptome sequencing, yeast cultures were grown under two physiological states (glucose utilization and oxidative/respiratory growth on ethanol/fermentation products), and RNA was extracted from independently prepared cultuores for each condition. The same rRNA-depleted RNA preparations were used to construct both ONT and Illumina libraries to enable cross-platform comparisons.

For gene/transcript differential expression analyses, count-based statistical testing was performed using DESeq2. Unless otherwise specified, genes/transcripts with an adjusted p value (q-value) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Correlations of expression profiles were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted using ClusterProfiler with multiple-testing correction; enriched terms were considered significant at q-value < 0.05 unless otherwise stated.

For qRT-PCR validation, reactions were conducted using three independent biological replicates, and relative expression was calculated using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2}^{-\Delta \Delta {Ct}}$$\end{document}2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the reference gene. Statistical significance between conditions was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test with Welch’s correction on relative expression.
