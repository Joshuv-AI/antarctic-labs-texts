Alignment optimization in digital implantology: evaluating the role of scan body modifications and different software
Authors: Damla Bilgin Avsar, Ahmet Atila Ertan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlignment accuracy in digital impressions is critical for implant-supported prostheses. This in vitro study evaluated the effects of scan body modifications and alignment software on digital impression accuracy.Materials and methodsA printed maxillary model with a unilateral distal edentulous area included analogs at positions No. 25 and No. 27. Three scan body groups were tested: unmodified (M0), additive (M+), and subtractive (M−). Each was scanned 10 times using an intraoral scanner. Alignment was performed in Exocad and Medit Link software. Experimental scans were compared with a reference model using the best-fit method. Linear and angular.Results Mean linear deviations ranged from 0.04–0.07 mm and angular deviations from 0.23°–0.63°. Medit Link produced significantly lower deviations than Exocad (p < 0.001, p = 0.001). Unmodified scan bodies showed the highest accuracy, and implant position also influenced results (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBoth scan body modifications and alignment software significantly affected matching accuracy. Appropriate software can reduce implant misfits and improve prosthetic outcomes. These preclinical findings highlight the potential of advanced algorithms-based alignment approaches to enhance reliability under challenging conditions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

In recent years, digital impression systems have become increasingly common in implant dentistry, enabling intraoral or extraoral acquisition of impressions for integration into computer-aided design (CAD) workflows [1]. However, accuracy of digital impressions can be adversely affected by various factors such as the speed of data acquisition, image clarity, the software used and its file-processing capability, compatibility with the digital library of the implant company, scanning strategy, implant position, and patient/operator-related factors [2]. Along with these general parameters, the scan body itself plays a crucial role in the digital workflow of implant dentistry. Several scan body–related factors such as material composition, geometric design, number, position, installation torque, and surface wear were previously reported to influence accuracy of digital implant impressions. Depending on some of these factors, scan data may show deficiencies in the scan body region [3]. In this context, both the scanning process and the accuracy of virtual alignment in CAD software may be compromised, which may result in deviations in implant positioning and prosthetic misfit [4, 5].

In the digital workflow, scan data are imported into CAD software, where the scan body is automatically matched with its corresponding digital library file. Errors during this alignment step can lead to deviations in abutment positioning and subsequent prosthetic restoration. Most dental CAD software programs provide multiple alignment algorithms, among which standard best-fit and landmark-based methods are the most used approaches. However, clear manufacturer guidelines on the preferred use of these algorithms in specific clinical situations are limited, and comparative studies evaluating their effects on alignment accuracy remain relatively scarce in the dental literature [6–10]. The scan body matching process varies across different CAD systems, where automated alignment is typically based on the selection of either one or three points on the scan body, requiring the user to verify and manually adjust the superimposition if necessary [11]. Although this step is computational, it has direct clinical relevance, as inaccurate alignment may cause misfits, screw loosening, or long-term biomechanical complications. Accordingly, achieving precise digital alignment is essential to ensure passive fit and long-term success of implant-supported restorations [12–14]. To facilitate accurate alignment, certain areas of the scan body are intentionally designed with asymmetrical features, thereby helping CAD software to more effectively recognize and align them [15, 16]. Although most dental CAD software offer multiple alignment algorithms, clear guidance on their selection and application is lacking [17]. In recent digital workflows, artificial intelligence (AI) has been incorporated to facilitate the best-fit alignment and scan body matching process. In systems such as Medit Link (Medit, Seoul, Republic of South Korea), the “AI Scan Body Matching” function automatically or manually identifies and aligns scan bodies through geometric recognition. This feature aims to improve alignment consistency, particularly in cases where scanning challenges are present.

Most previous research has focused on evaluating scanning accuracy, and only a limited number of studies have assessed the impact of scan body matching accuracy. Recent work has also highlighted the need to investigate how software-related differences influence the accuracy of digital workflows [18, 19]. Therefore, studies comparing the role of CAD software in scan body alignment accuracy are still needed. Seeking to bridge this gap in the literature, the present study seeks to evaluate accuracy of digital impressions obtained after matching scan bodies with different modifications using two different alignment software.

The null hypothesis of this study is that scan body modifications and the type of alignment software used do not significantly affect the accuracy of digital implant impressions.

Materials and methods

Since this research did not involve human or animal subjects, neither ethical approval nor clinical trial registration was required.

Model fabrication

A maxillary arch typodont was modified by removing the left second premolar (No. 25), and the first (No. 26), second (No. 27), and third molars (No. 28). The edentulous areas were filled with modeling wax and then scanned with a laboratory scanner (Dentalscan 3.9.2, Smartoptics Sensortechnik GmbH, Vinyl UXD, Germany). The data were exported in standard tessellation language (.stl) format. Digital implant analogs (Oxy Implant, Biomec S.r.l, Colico, Italy) were virtually positioned in the model using Blender for Dental (v.3.3.1; Blender Foundation, New York, USA). The analog for site No. 25 was tilted 10° mesially in the vertical plane, while the analog at site No. 27 was aligned at 90° to the horizontal plane. Sockets were virtually designed to fit the physical analogs precisely, with the junction of analogs and scan bodies located on the crest level and at a standardized mesiodistal distance of 17 mm between analogs.

The model was fabricated with a three-dimensional (3D) printer (Photon M3, Anycubic Technology, Hong Kong). After printing, digital analogs and scan bodies (Oxy Implant, SCANK1R, Biomec S.r.l, Colico, Italy; titanium grade 5, opacified) were placed and tightened to 10 Ncm using a torque wrench, and were not removed throughout the entire scanning procedure. Proper fit was verified by ensuring the absence of rotational, vertical, and horizontal mobility. This approach standardized scan body positioning and eliminated positional variability.

Power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany)for the most complex interaction in a repeated-measures ANOVA with three within-subject factors (software, modification, and scanbody). Using an effect size of f = 0.25 and a significance level of 0.05, the analysis showed that having 10 samples for each condition provided a power of 0.91.

Digital impressions were obtained using an intraoral scanner (i600, Medit scan for clinics v.1.12.4, Medit Link v.3.1.1, Medit Corp., Seoul, Korea). The scanner was recalibrated after every 10 scans. Each arch was scanned 10 times, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol, starting from the incisal surface of the left central incisor.

After the unmodified scans (M0 group), red matte plastic beads (3 mm half-spheres) were manually attached with flowable composite to the buccal, mesial, and distal surfaces to create the additive modification group (M+). The purpose of these beads was to introduce distinguishable surface irregularities among the scan bodies; therefore, they were intentionally not placed at identical millimetric points. Instead, beads of identical size and color were positioned within the same general surface regions to produce similar—but not identical—surface modifications for each scan body. Ten digital scans were obtained for this group.

In the M − group, the red color filter in Medit Link was activated so that the scanner would not capture the red-colored areas corresponding to the bead locations. This resulted in unscanned surface voids comparable in size to the bead-occupied regions in the M + group. This method was chosen because it simulates the non-captured areas encountered by optical scanners under real clinical conditions.

Ten scans were also obtained for this group. In total, 30 scans were recorded: 10 each for M0, M+, and M−.

Scan body alignment

The impact of alignment software on impression accuracy was also evaluated. A total of 30 scans (10 per group: M0, M+, M−) were processed under the following two conditions: (1) aligned in Medit Link, and (2) aligned in Exocad. This resulted in six experimental subgroups and a total of 60 matched scan files.

In Medit Link, implant sites (No. 25 and No. 27) were manually identified, appropriate scan bodies were selected from the software library, and three reference points were placed on the triangular flat surface of each scan body. The AI- assisted algorithm then refined the alignment, and files were exported in .stl format (Fig. 1).

In Exocad, alignment was automatically performed using a single-point best-fit algorithm. The .stl files were imported, matched with the corresponding library files, and then exported again for further analysis (Fig. 2). To ensure consistency, all matching procedures were carried out by a single trained operator. Color map analysis was used to visually evaluate deviations following alignment in each software.

Reference Scan

The reference scan was obtained by scanning the master model with scan bodies in place using a high-precision laboratory scanner (Vinyl UXD, Smartoptics Sensortechnik GmbH, Germany) with an accuracy of 4 μm. The file was saved in .stl format for subsequent comparison.

Accuracy assessment

Each experimental scan was compared with the reference using reverse engineering software (Geomagic Control X v.2017.0.3; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, NC) and superimposed via the “reference best-fit” algorithm. For each scan body, the long axis was defined by a vector originating from the center of a circle at its base. Linear deviations were calculated as the displacement of this center point, and angular deviations as the difference in orientation between vectors of the experimental and reference scans (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v.21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was tested with Shapiro–Wilk. A repeated measures ANOVA assessed the effects of software type, modification level, and scan body on angular and linear deviations. Mauchly’s test confirmed sphericities for all measures. For significant results, F-values, degrees of freedom, and partial eta squared (η²) were reported. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted. The methodological workflow is summarized in Fig. 4.

A significant interaction was also found between software and modification (p < 0.001), whereas software × scan body number, modification × scan body number, and the three-way interaction were not significant. These findings indicate that both the software used and the level of modification affected angular deviations, with scan body number having the strongest influence (Table 1). Discussion

With the increasing utilization of digital technologies in implant dentistry, the accuracy of intraoral scanning and scan body matching has become a critical determinant of digital workflow success. Any inaccuracies introduced at this stage may propagate throughout subsequent steps and ultimately compromise the fit of the prosthesis. Yet, the interaction between scan body geometry and alignment algorithms across different software platforms remains insufficiently clarified in the literature [11, 19–21].

In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the influence of scan body modifications on the performance of AI-assisted alignment and to assess potential clinical implications. As noted by Revilla-León et al., intraoral scanner software increasingly incorporates AI-powered functions—such as scan cleaning, smart stitching, and CAD object recognition—that include automatic alignment tools. However, the impact of scan body design on these tools remains largely unclear, warranting further investigation [20]. By introducing additive and subtractive modifications, the present study directly addressed this gap and evaluated their effect on alignment accuracy.

Accordingly, two software platforms were assessed: Medit Link and Exocad. Medit Link was chosen because it allows AI-assisted manual three-point selection or fully automatic alignment within the scanning environment. This feature enables clinicians to identify mismatches in real time and rescan immediately, which may streamline workflows in clinically challenging situations. Yet, previous studies have shown that, compared to other strategies, fully automatic AI-based matching may yield lower accuracy [19], suggesting that Medit Link’s option to combine AI assistance with manual control provides a more reliable approach. By contrast, Exocad, was included not only because all datasets must eventually be transferred to this software for prosthetic design, but also due to its open architecture that ensures broad compatibility and allows the alignment process to be independently assessed. Although Medit Link’s alignment function is marketed as utilizing AI-assisted algorithms, the exact working mechanism is not fully known. Therefore, the observed performance differences between software platforms may stem from their differing alignment methodologies and the level of automation they provide.

Previous studies have indicated that both the type of intraoral scanner and the matching algorithm implemented in CAD software significantly influence digital accuracy [11]. For instance, Exocad’s single-point alignment approach was reported to provide lower accuracy than three-point strategies employed by other platforms. One study further emphasized that the best-fit algorithm itself has a critical effect on the outcome and recommended evaluating multiple CAD systems from different manufacturers [22]. Our results support these observations: in the present study, Medit Link’s manual three-point alignment protocol yielded lower angular and linear deviations as compared with Exocad’s single-point method. This finding not only reflects the advantages of multipoint matching but also demonstrates reliability of Medit’s AI-assisted alignment. That Medit achieved accuracy comparable to—or better than—that of Exocad suggests that clinicians may confidently rely on this feature in difficult scanning scenarios. However, although AI represents a promising technology for implant dentistry, further research is needed to evaluate its performance under diverse clinical conditions. In addition, the ability to scan and match within the same software environment may have reduced the risk of resolution loss or compatibility errors associated with data transfer. This methodological aspect can be regarded as a limitation of the present study.

Scan body modifications also had a measurable influence on accuracy. Additive modifications were introduced to provide additional reference points and minimize skipping during scanning. However, possibly due to the formation of acute angles and overlaps that algorithms could not effectively resolve, these modifications resulted in higher deviations [23, 24]. Subtractive modifications, applied to simulate unscannable areas in challenging clinical conditions, also increased deviations. Previous research has demonstrated that data deficiencies exceeding 10–15% significantly impair accuracy, and the present findings are in line with those results, particularly when subtractive modifications were combined with Exocad [3].

The results were further affected by implant position. The anterior implant (No. 25), supported by adjacent teeth, demonstrated lower deviations than the posterior implant (No. 27) in a distal edentulous region. This outcome is consistent with previous studies suggesting that distal sites, with fewer anatomic landmarks, are more prone to stitching errors [25, 26].

Furthermore, in the present study, deviations observed at tooth No. 27 occasionally exceeded the reported clinically acceptable thresholds (50–150 μm for linear deviations and 0.4° for angular deviations [14, 27–29]), particularly when subtractive modifications were evaluated using Exocad. Although these thresholds are primarily derived from full-arch rehabilitations, exceeding such values even in short-span implant-supported restorations may still be clinically relevant, especially in complex clinical scenarios Overall, the results demonstrated that both scan body modifications and alignment software significantly influence matching accuracy. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was rejected.

These findings highlight the potential of AI in compensating for landmark scarcity and spatial distortion, which may improve distal implant accuracy. Recent evidence indicates that, while AI use in implant planning is expanding, high-quality datasets and standardized protocols remain essential. Nevertheless, automated recognition and segmentation of anatomical structures have shown accurate and time-efficient results, supporting the integration of predictive AI models for implant stability and prosthetic fit [30]. Beyond alignment accuracy, AI-based algorithms may enable a transition from descriptive digital workflows towards data-driven, anticipatory systems in implantology.

From a clinical perspective, in clinically challenging scenarios where scan bodies cannot be fully captured, incorporating AI-assisted support into the alignment process may help identify and correct scanning errors at an early stage. AI-assisted alignment approaches, particularly those providing real-time feedback and enabling the digital matching of scan bodies that are incompletely captured either automatically or manually during the scanning process, may reduce chairside time, minimize unnecessary rescanning procedures, and help prevent over scanning. Furthermore, performing digital alignment in a more controlled manner in areas with incomplete data acquisition or compromised surface integrity may allow more accurate recording of implant positions, thereby contributing to the fabrication of more predictable and better-fitting implant-supported prostheses.

The present study has several limitations. Our research was conducted in vitro, and clinical factors such as saliva, blood, patient movement, and limited intraoral access were not simulated. Only one implant system and scan body design were tested, and prosthetic fabrication or passive fit assessment was not performed. In addition, all scans were obtained by a single operator using a single intraoral scanner. Consequently, operator-related factors such as scanning strategy and user experience could not be evaluated with respect to their potential influence on digital accuracy. Similarly, the use of a single scanner model limited the assessment of hardware-related variability among different optical systems. Furthermore, the analyses were performed using specific software versions, and potential effects of future software updates or changes in matching algorithms on alignment outcomes should be taken into consideration. Additionally, sufficient open-source information about these commercial algorithms was not available, which limited our ability to analyze the specific mechanisms behind the observed differences.

Accordingly, future research should aim to validate these findings under clinical conditions, where intraoral challenges can influence scanning accuracy. To enhance generalizability, comparative analyses involving multiple implant systems, scan body geometries, and materials would be needed. Furthermore, evaluation of different AI-assisted alignment algorithms could provide insight into how algorithmic design affects matching precision. Finally, integration of prosthetic fit and long-term clinical outcomes would establish a stronger link between digital accuracy and functional performance in implant-supported restorations.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this in vitro study, the following conclusions were drawn:

Within the limitations of the present study, scan body modifications were found to significantly influence alignment accuracy, with unmodified scan bodies providing the most consistent results, while additive and subtractive alterations introduced greater deviations. Medit Link outperformed Exocad in both angular and linear measurements, which highlights the clinical value of software with advanced alignment capabilities and suggests that such platforms may improve reliability in challenging scanning scenarios.Furthermore, in vivo studies involving various implant systems, diverse scan body geometries, and complex clinical scenarios would be needed to validate these findings and establish their broader clinical applicability.
