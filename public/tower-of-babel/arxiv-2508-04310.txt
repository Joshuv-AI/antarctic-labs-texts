Quantum Advantage in Identifying the Parity of Permutations with Certainty
A. Diebra 1 , S. Llorens 1 , D. González-Lociga 1 , A. Rico 1 , J. Calsamiglia 1 , M. Hillery 2 , and E. Bagan 1

Abstract
We establish a sharp quantum advantage in determining the parity (even/odd) of an unknown permutation applied to any number n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 of particles. Classically, this is impossible with fewer than n n labels, being the success limited to random guessing. Quantum mechanics does it with certainty with as few as ⌈ n ⌉ \lceil\sqrt{n}\,\rceil distinguishable states per particle, thanks to entanglement. Below this threshold, not even quantum mechanics helps: both classical and quantum success are limited to random guessing. For small n n , we provide explicit expressions for states that ensure perfect parity identification. We also assess the minimum entanglement these states need to carry, finding it to be close to maximal, and even maximal in some cases. The task requires no oracles or contrived setups and provides a simple, rigorous example of genuine quantum advantage.

Introduction
. One of the defining goals of quantum information science is to identify tasks that quantum systems can perform better than classical ones, a phenomenon broadly known as quantum advantage. Over the past two decades, many such examples have emerged: exponential speedups relative to the best known classical algorithms, as in Shor’s factoring

The recent experimental achievement of quantum supremacy

While a significant portion of the literature has focused on quantum algorithms and computational tasks, quantum advantage also arises in a variety of physically motivated settings, from quantum communication and metrology to nonlocal correlations. These instances highlight how quantum resources such as coherence

In this work, we propose a problem that is elementary to state, physically natural, and yet exhibits a sharp separation between classical and quantum capabilities. It requires no complex communication setup, no oracle for function evaluation, and no computational promises—just a set of
n
particles, subject to a hidden permutation, and the simple binary question:
is it even or odd?

Swapping is arguably the most primitive and universal transformation one can perform on a set of objects. It applies regardless of their nature; it is simply a rearrangement. Classically, if each particle carries a distinct label—e.g.,
n
different colors—one can perfectly reconstruct the permutation and read off its parity. But with even one fewer label, perfect parity identification becomes impossible: every permutation has an opposite-parity counterpart producing the same label arrangement, and the best one can do is guess, with success probability
P_{\rm s}=1/2
.

Quantum systems, however, allow us to do fundamentally better. By preparing the
n

|\psi_{e}\rangle
of qudits (each of local dimension

Theorem I
.

Perfect parity identification (
P_{\rm s}=1
) is achievable if

d\geq d_{\rm min}:=\left\lceil\sqrt{n}\,\right\rceil.

(1)

Below this threshold, assuming all permutations are equally likely, parity remains indistinguishable and random guessing is the best one can do (
P_{\rm s}=1/2
).

Thus, from a quantum system in which each of the
n

exists, and the subspaces spanned by the action of the even and odd permutations on

E(|\psi\rangle)=1-\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}
,

supp_mat

(18)

where

\langle\phi|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi\rangle

\rho_{e}
(pure or mixed) supported on this subspace

Summary and outlook.
We have presented a protocol that determines the parity of an unknown permutation on
n
particles with certainty using as few as
\lceil\sqrt{n}\,\rceil
distinguishable states per particle, far below the classical requirement of
n
. We provided explicit states for small
n
and quantified the minimum entanglement required, finding it close to maximal. This work reveals a sharp, provable quantum advantage that relies only on permuting particles, without invoking any specific dynamics. Note that our protocol requires no ancillas, nor can their inclusion reduce the minimum local dimension, as increasing the multiplicities has no effect here.

Future work should explore practical implementations, including preparation of the required states using experimentally accessible Hamiltonians (via ground-state engineering or dynamical protocols) or quantum circuits, along with design of the corresponding measurements. Assessing the robustness of the
\sqrt{n}
quantum advantage under noise will also be essential. Finally, extensions to other symmetry groups may reveal further instances of clear quantum advantage
future_work
.

I.1
The symmetric group
S_{n}

The
symmetric group

S_{n}
is the group of all permutations of
n
objects. Its elements can be represented as bijections
\sigma:\{1,2,\dots,n\}\to\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, with group operation given by composition: applying
\sigma
followed by
\tau
yields the permutation
\tau\sigma
. Its order (number of elements) is
|S_{n}|=n!
, and its identity element is denoted by
e
.

Any permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
can be expressed as a product of cycles. A
cycle

(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
permutes elements cyclically:
a_{1}\to a_{2}
,
a_{2}\to a_{3}
,
\dots
,
a_{k}\to a_{1}
, leaving all other elements fixed. Any permutation can be written uniquely (up to order) as a product of
disjoint cycles
, i.e., cycles with no elements in common.

Any permutation can also be decomposed (non-uniquely) as a product of
transpositions
, i.e., 2-cycles
(a,b)
that swap
a
and
b
. The
parity
(evenness or oddness) of a permutation is defined by whether it can be expressed as an even or odd number of transpositions; this parity is well-defined (independent of the chosen decomposition).

I.1.1
Conjugacy classes

The
conjugacy class
of an element
g
in a group
G
is the set of all elements
hgh^{-1}
with
h\in G
; in
S_{n}
, two permutations are conjugate if and only if they have the same cycle structure when written as a product of disjoint cycles.

Conjugacy classes in
S_{n}
are in one-to-one correspondence with partitions of
n

n
boxes. A
partition
\lambda=[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r}]
of
n
is a sequence of positive integers in non-increasing order summing to
n

Young diagram
(YD) of
\lambda
is a left-justified arrangement of boxes with
\lambda_{p}
boxes in row
p
. Each YD provides a graphical depiction of a partition as rows of decreasing length. In this correspondence, the parts of the partition (or equivalently, the lengths of the rows of its YD) match the lengths of the disjoint cycles in the permutations of that conjugacy class—for example,
(1,2,4)(5,6)(3)\in S_{6}
has cycle type
[3,2,1]

(3)
, typically omitted from the notation), whose YD is:

\lambda

\lambda

m_{p}

I.1.2
Representations

A
representation
of a group
G
is a homomorphism from
G
to the group of invertible linear operators on a vector space
\mathscr{V}
. Since we are interested in physical systems, where
\mathscr{V}
is a Hilbert space, we restrict attention to
unitary
representations.
A representation is
irreducible
if
\mathscr{V}
has no proper, nontrivial subspace invariant under all group elements. As is common practice, we will use the term “representation” to refer to both the operators on
\mathscr{V}
(or their matrices in a given basis) and the space
\mathscr{V}
itself. The operators (or matrices) will be denoted
D(g)
for
g\in G
. Accordingly, we will sometimes denote a representation by the pair
(\mathscr{V},D)
for extra precision.
Any finite group
G
has as many irreducible representations as conjugacy classes.

For
S_{n}
, the irreducible representations (irreps) are also in one-to-one correspondence with partitions of
n
or YDs with
n
boxes. We denote by
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}
the irrep labeled by partition/YD
\lambda
, though usually we will refer to
(\mathscr{K}_{\lambda},D^{\lambda})
simply as “irrep
\lambda
”. Its dimension,
d_{\lambda}=\dim\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Tr}\big(D^{\lambda}(e)\big)
, is given by the
hook-length formula
:

d_{\lambda}=\frac{n!}{\prod_{b\in\lambda}h(b)},

(S2)

where
h(b)
is the hook length of box
b
in the YD. The
hook length
of a box is the total number of boxes directly to its right and directly below it, plus one (counting the box itself).

As for any finite group, any representation
(\mathscr{K},D)
of
S_{n}
can be decomposed as a direct sum of irreps:

\mathscr{K}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m_{\lambda}},

(S3)

where
\mathbb{C}^{m_{\lambda}}
accounts for the multiplicity, that is, the number of copies of
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}
in the decomposition.
This means that there is a basis of
\mathscr{K}
such that

D(\sigma)=\sum_{\lambda}D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\otimes\openone_{m_{\lambda}},\qquad\sigma\in S_{n},

(S4)

and
\openone_{m_{\lambda}}
denotes the identity operator on
\mathbb{C}^{m_{\lambda}}
. Note that
S_{n}
acts trivially on the multiplicity spaces.

I.1.3
Characters and character table

Given a representation
(\mathscr{V},D)
of a finite group
G
, its
character
is the function
\chi:G\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\chi(g)=\operatorname{Tr}D(g).

(S5)

Note that characters are constant on conjugacy classes. Characters provide a powerful tool for studying representations; for instance, the character of a direct sum is the sum of the characters, and two irreps are equivalent iff their characters are the same.

The
character table
of a group
G
is a square array whose rows correspond to
irreducible characters
(i.e., the characters of the irreps), denoted
\chi^{\alpha}
, and columns to conjugacy classes. For a generic group, it has the form shown in Table

\begin{array}[t]{c|ccccc}\text{Class}&C_{1}&\cdots&C_{\beta}&\cdots&C_{r}\\
\text{Size}&1&\cdots&|C_{\beta}|&\cdots&|C_{r}|\\

\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\

\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\

\end{array}\vskip 5.0pt

Irreducible characters,

G

\langle\chi^{\alpha},\chi^{\beta}\rangle:=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\,\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\;\chi^{\beta}(g)=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad 1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq r,

(S6)

where the bar (
\overline{\phantom{m}}

Equivalently, summing over all conjugacy classes
C_{\gamma}

\langle\chi^{\alpha},\chi^{\beta}\rangle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{r}\frac{|C_{\gamma}|}{|G|}\;\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(C_{\gamma})}\;\chi^{\beta}(C_{\gamma})=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad 1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq r.

(S7)

These relations can be used, e.g., to compute multiplicities in a decomposition.

There is also a dual orthogonality relation for the columns of the character table:

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(C_{\gamma})}\;\chi^{\alpha}(C_{\delta})=\frac{|G|}{|C_{\gamma}|}\delta_{\gamma\delta},\quad 1\leq\gamma,\delta\leq r.

(S8)

Both sets of relations can help complete the character table of a group, but they do not fully determine it, particularly for groups of large order.

D^{\lambda^{T}}\cong\mathrm{sign}\otimes D^{\lambda},

D^{\lambda^{T}}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\,D^{\lambda}(\sigma),\qquad\forall\,\sigma\in S_{n}.

\lambda=\lambda^{T}

D^{\lambda}(\sigma_{\rm odd})=0

S9

V\,D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\,V^{\dagger}\;=\;\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\,D^{\lambda}(\sigma),\qquad\sigma\in S_{n}.

\lambda=\lambda^{T}
, then
\chi^{\lambda}(C_{\lambda^{\prime}})=0
for all odd conjugacy classes.

I.1.4
Schur’s lemma

A fundamental result in the classification of irreducible representations is
Schur’s lemma
. In its matrix form, it states that given two irreps,
D^{\alpha}(g)
and
D^{\beta}(g)
, if a matrix
T
satisfies
TD^{\alpha}(g)=D^{\beta}(g)T
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, then
T
is either invertible —implying that the two representations are
isomorphic
— or zero. Moreover, in the special case where both irreps coincide,
\alpha=\beta
, i.e., when
T
commutes with
D^{\alpha}(g)
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
T
must be a scalar multiple of the identity in the corresponding irreducible subspace. In other words,
T=\xi\,\openone_{d_{\alpha}}
for some
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
.

A direct consequence of Schur’s lemma is that the matrix elements of two irreps satisfy the following orthogonality relation, known as the
Great Orthogonality Theorem
,

\sum_{g\in G}\left[D^{\alpha}(g)\right]_{k,l}\overline{\left[D^{\beta}(g)\right]}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\frac{|G|}{d_{\alpha}}.

(S13)

The character orthogonality relation in Eq. (
S6
) follows directly from this theorem.

I.2.2
Schur-Weyl duality

Consider a system of
n
qudits, each with Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The total Hilbert space is the tensor product
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
. The “canonical” basis of this space is the standard computational basis:

|i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}\rangle\equiv|i_{1}\rangle\otimes|i_{2}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{n}\rangle,\qquad i_{l}=0,1,\dots,d-1,\quad l=1,2,\dots,n.

(S15)

Both
\operatorname{SU}(d)
and
S_{n}
act naturally on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
, and their action is conveniently described in the computational basis. The group
\operatorname{SU}(d)
acts diagonally:

U^{\otimes n}|i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}\rangle=\bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}U|i_{l}\rangle,\qquad U\in\operatorname{SU}(d),

(S16)

while
S_{n}
acts by permuting tensor factors (inducing the
tensor permutation representation
):

D^{\rm perm}(\sigma)|i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}\rangle=|i_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}i_{\sigma^{-1}(2)}\cdots i_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}\rangle,\qquad\sigma\in S_{n}.

(S17)

When no ambiguity arises, we will write
\sigma|\psi\rangle
in place of
D^{\rm perm}(\sigma)|\psi\rangle
for any
|\psi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.

The actions of
S_{n}
and
\operatorname{SU}(d)
commute and together generate a representation of
\operatorname{SU}(d)\times S_{n}
on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
. The decomposition of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
into invariant subspaces can be expressed compactly (Schur–Weyl duality) as

(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash n\\
\ell(\lambda)\leq d\end{subarray}}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\lambda},

(S18)

where the sum runs over all partitions
\lambda
of
n
, denoted
\lambda\vdash n
, and
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of rows of
\lambda
, called its
length
. Here,
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}
carries, as above, the irrep of
S_{n}
labeled by
\lambda
, and
\mathscr{H}_{\lambda}
carries the irrep of
\operatorname{SU}(d)
labeled by the same
\lambda
. The two factors are multiplicity spaces for each other:
S_{n}
acts nontrivially only on
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}
, and
\operatorname{SU}(d)
acts nontrivially only on
\mathscr{H}_{\lambda}
.

I.3
Group algebra and projectors

The
group algebra

\mathbb{C}[G]
of a finite group
G
is the vector space spanned by formal linear combinations of its elements with complex coefficients, endowed with multiplication inherited from
G
and extended by linearity. It has dimension
|G|
, with basis
\{\,g\mid g\in G\,\}
.

Any representation
(\mathscr{V},D)
of
G
extends linearly to
\mathbb{C}[G]
:

D\Bigg(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\Bigg)\;=\;\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}D(g)\;\in\;\mathrm{End}(\mathscr{V}),\qquad a_{g}\in\mathbb{C},

(S19)

where
\mathrm{End}(\mathscr{V})
is the space of all linear operators (not necessarily invertible) on
\mathscr{V}
.
In particular, every irrep of
G
is also an irrep of
\mathbb{C}[G]
, and conversely.

This correspondence allows one to construct
projectors onto the irrep subspaces
using characters:

\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\,D(g)\;\in\;\mathrm{End}(\mathscr{V}),

(S20)

These projectors satisfy

\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}\mathscr{P}_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}

and resolve the identity:

\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}\;=\;\openone\qquad\text{in }\mathrm{End}(\mathscr{V}).

(S21)

Proof.
Using the great orthogonality theorem, Eq. (
S13
), one has

\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\,\chi^{\beta}(h^{-1}g)=\sum_{k,l,s}\left(\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\left[D^{\alpha}(g)\right]_{k,k}}\left[D^{\beta}(g)\right]_{s,l}\right)\left[D^{\beta}(h^{-1})\right]_{l,s}\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{|G|}{d_{\alpha}}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{k,l,s}\delta_{k,s}\delta_{k,l}\left[D^{\alpha}(h^{-1})\right]_{l,s}=\frac{|G|}{d_{\alpha}}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{k}\left[D^{\alpha}(h^{-1})\right]_{k,k}=\frac{|G|}{d_{\alpha}}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\,\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(h)}.

(S22)

Then,

\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}\mathscr{P}_{\beta}=\frac{d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)\chi^{\beta}(g^{\prime})}\,D(g)D(g^{\prime})=\frac{d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)\chi^{\beta}(g^{\prime})}\,D(gg^{\prime})\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{g,h\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)\chi^{\beta}(g^{-1}h)}\,D(h)=\frac{d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{h\in G}\left(\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\,\chi^{\beta}(h^{-1}g)\right)D(h)\\[5.0pt]
=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\,\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|G|}\sum_{h\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(h)}\,D(h)=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\,\mathscr{P}_{\alpha},

(S23)

where we changed variables by setting
h:=gg^{\prime}
in the second line.
This proves the orthogonality of the projectors.

Next, we show completeness:

\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{P}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\chi^{\alpha}(e)}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\,D(g)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\chi^{\alpha}(e)}{|G|}\sum_{\beta}\sum_{g\in C_{\beta}}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(g)}\,D(g)\\[5.0pt]
=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\chi^{\alpha}(C_{1})}{|G|}\sum_{\beta}|C_{\beta}|\,\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(C_{\beta})}\,D(C_{\beta})=\sum_{\beta}\frac{|C_{\beta}|}{|G|}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\overline{\chi^{\alpha}(C_{\beta})}\,\chi^{\alpha}(C_{1})\right)D(C_{\beta})\\[5.0pt]
=D(C_{1})=D(e)=\openone\;\in\mathrm{End}(\mathscr{V}),

(S24)

where in the second line we used the orthogonality of the columns of the character table, Eq. (
S8
).
This completes the proof
\blacksquare

When
G=S_{n}
, these projectors act naturally on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
via the permutation representation, isolating the Schur–Weyl subspaces
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\lambda}
, and Eq. (
S20
) becomes

\mathscr{P}_{\lambda}\;=\;\frac{d_{\lambda}}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\overline{\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)}\,\sigma\;\in\;\mathrm{End}\!\left((\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\right).

(S25)

Y_{T}\;:=\;\frac{d_{\lambda}}{n!}\,r_{T}\,c_{T}.

(S33)

For fixed
\lambda
, the
Y_{T}
associated with
T\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]
and, under left multiplication, span a copy of the irrep
\mathscr{V}_{\lambda}
. They are idempotent,

Y_{T}^{2}=Y_{T},

(S34)

but not orthogonal projectors: generally,

Y_{T}Y_{T^{\prime}}\neq 0\qquad\text{for }T\neq T^{\prime}.

(S35)

To obtain orthogonal projectors, one uses
generalized Young symmetrizers
(GYS), also known as Hermitian Young operators
keppeler2014hermitian
. They are defined recursively:

\mathcal{Y}_{T}\;=\;\left(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{pre}(T)}\otimes\openone\right)Y_{T}\left(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{pre}(T)}\otimes\openone\right),\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{T}=Y_{T}\ \ \text{for}\ \ n\leq 2,

(S36)

where
\mathsf{pre}(T)
is obtained by removing the box containing the largest number from
T
.

I.5
The alternating group
A_{n}

The
alternating group

A_{n}
is defined as the subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{n}
consisting of all even permutations:

A_{n}\;:=\;\{\sigma\in S_{n}\,\mid\,\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=+1\}.

(S39)

It is a normal subgroup of
S_{n}
of index 2, with order
|A_{n}|=n!/2
, and can be equivalently characterized as the kernel of the sign homomorphism. The group
A_{n}
is generated by
3
-cycles. For
n\geq 5
, it is
non-solvable
, i.e., it admits no finite sequence of nested normal subgroups with abelian quotients (in fact, it is
simple
, meaning it has no nontrivial normal subgroups). This has important implications. For instance, the general quintic is not solvable by radicals, since its Galois group is generically isomorphic to
S_{5}
, which contains the non-solvable group
A_{5}
as a normal subgroup. For small
n
, one finds
A_{3}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{3}
(cyclic of order 3),
A_{4}
of order 12 (not simple), and
A_{5}
of order 60, the smallest non-Abelian simple group.

The character table of
A_{n}
is obtained from that of
S_{n}
by restricting characters to even permutations. In this process, certain conjugacy classes of
S_{n}
split into two distinct classes in
A_{n}
, namely those whose elements are conjugate in
S_{n}
but not in
A_{n}
. Likewise, irreps of
S_{n}
labeled by non-self-conjugate partitions
\lambda\neq\lambda^{T}
remain irreducible when restricted to
A_{n}
, although they become equivalent,
D^{\lambda}\cong D^{\lambda^{T}}
, within
A_{n}
. In contrast, self-conjugate irreps
\lambda=\lambda^{T}
split into two inequivalent irreps of
A_{n}
of equal dimension, which we denote by
\lambda a
and
\lambda b
(this notation is nonstandard but convenient for our purposes).

Moreover, any odd permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}\setminus A_{n}
swaps the two corresponding irreducible subspaces:

\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda b},\qquad\sigma\in S_{n}\setminus A_{n}.

(S40)

Proof.
Assume
\lambda=\lambda^{T}
. Recalling Eq. (
S11
) we have

V^{2}D^{\lambda}(\sigma)(V^{\dagger})^{2}=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)VD^{\lambda}(\sigma)V^{\dagger}=D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\qquad\mbox{for all $\sigma\in S_{n}$},

(S41)

so by Schur’s lemma and the unitarity of
V
, it follows that
V^{2}=\openone_{\lambda}
. Define orthogonal projectors on
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}
:

\mathscr{P}_{a}={\openone_{\lambda}+V\over 2},\qquad\mathscr{P}_{b}={\openone_{\lambda}-V\over 2},

(S42)

and the subspaces

\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}:=\mathscr{P}_{a}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda},\qquad\mathscr{K}_{\lambda b}:=\mathscr{P}_{b}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}.

(S43)

We can check that these are invariant under the action of
A_{n}
:

D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{P}_{a}={1\over 2}\left[D^{\lambda}(\sigma)+D^{\lambda}(\sigma)V\right]={1\over 2}\left[D^{\lambda}(\sigma)+VD^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right]=\mathscr{P}_{a}D^{\lambda}(\sigma),\qquad\mbox{for all $\sigma\in A_{n}$},

(S44)

and

D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}=D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{P}_{a}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{P}_{a}D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{P}_{a}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a},\qquad\mbox{for all $\sigma\in A_{n}$}.

(S45)

The same holds for
\mathscr{P}_{b}
and
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda b}
.

If instead
\sigma\in S_{n}\setminus A_{n}
(odd),
\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=-1
, and:

D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{P}_{a}={1\over 2}\left[D^{\lambda}(\sigma)+D^{\lambda}(\sigma)V\right]={1\over 2}\left[D^{\lambda}(\sigma)-VD^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right]=\mathscr{P}_{b}D^{\lambda}(\sigma),\qquad\mbox{for all $\sigma\in S_{n}\backslash A_{n}$},

(S46)

which implies

D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}=D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{P}_{a}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{P}_{b}D^{\lambda}(\sigma)\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{P}_{b}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda}=\mathscr{K}_{\lambda b},\qquad\mbox{for all $\sigma\in S_{n}\backslash A_{n}$}.

(S47)

This means that for
\sigma
odd,
\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{K}_{\lambda b}
. The reverse follows identically, proving that odd permutations swap the two subspaces.
\blacksquare

III
How much entangled is a parity-detecting state?

Here we provide further details on how we compute numerical lower bounds on the entanglement required for parity detection for
n=3
,
4
, and
5
.

We quantify entanglement using the geometric measure of entanglement (GME)
Wei2003geometric
;
Lisa2025quantifyin
, which for a pure multipartite state
|\psi\rangle
is defined as

E(|\psi\rangle):=1-\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2},

(S96)

where the maximization is over the set
\mathrm{SEP}
of all separable states,
|\phi\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\phi_{i}\rangle
.

Our goal is to evaluate the GME of the least entangled state within the subspace
\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}:=\mathscr{K}_{\lambda a}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\lambda}
that enables parity discrimination, with associated projector
\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}
. Namely, the quantity

E_{\lambda a}:=\min_{|\psi\rangle\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}}E(|\psi\rangle)=1-\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}\,.

(S97)

We first show that this quantity can be expressed as

E_{\lambda a}=1-\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}\langle\phi|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi\rangle.

(S98)

Proof.

We note that for any
|\psi\rangle\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}

\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\text{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}\leq\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}\langle\phi|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi\rangle.

(S99)

To see this, let
|\phi^{*}_{\psi}\rangle,|\phi^{*}\rangle\in\text{SEP}
be the states that maximize the overlap on the left-hand side and the expectation value on right-hand side, respectively.
Since
\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}
can be written as a uniform convex combination of any orthogonal basis of
\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}
, in particular one including
|\psi\rangle
, the operator
\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}-|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
is positive semidefinite. Thus,

\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\text{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}=|\langle\phi^{*}_{\psi}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\leq\langle\phi^{*}_{\psi}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}_{\psi}\rangle\leq\langle\phi^{*}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle=\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}\langle\phi|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi\rangle,\quad\mbox{for any $|\psi\rangle\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}$}.

(S100)

This inequality is attainable since one can construct a parity-detecting state as

|\psi\rangle=\frac{\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle}{\|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle\|}\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}\,,

(S101)

and choosing
|\phi^{*}_{\psi}\rangle=|\phi^{*}\rangle
, we obtain

|\langle\phi^{*}_{\psi}|\psi\rangle|^{2}=\left|\frac{\langle\phi^{*}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle}{\|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle\|}\right|^{2}=\frac{\langle\phi^{*}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}^{2}|\phi^{*}\rangle^{2}}{\|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle\|^{2}}=\|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle\|^{2}=\langle\phi^{*}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi^{*}\rangle,

(S102)

thus saturating Eq. (
S100
), and in turn showing that the parity-detecting state in Eq. (
S101
) achieves the maximum possible value of

\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\text{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}
over
\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}
, i.e.,

\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\text{SEP}}|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}=\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathrm{SEP}}\langle\phi|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi\rangle,

(S103)

which leads to Eq. (
S98
)
\blacksquare

Evaluating
E_{\lambda a}
exactly is challenging, as the brute-force optimization of Eq (
S98
) is highly nonlinear and yields results with no guarantee of global maxima.
Instead, we derive a rigorous lower bound using semidefinite programming (SDP), as detailed next.

SDP relaxation.

We outer-approximate the set SEP using SDP with symmetric extensions (DPS
k
)
DPS2004
;
NavascuesDPS2009
. In this hierarchy, SEP is approximated at level
k=0
by the set of PPT states
\varrho
, while higher levels impose both PPT and symmetry constraints on an extended system.

Exploiting the invariance of
\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}
under diagonal (local) unitaries,

U^{\otimes n}\,\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}\,{U^{\dagger}}^{\otimes n}=\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}
,
and noting that the maximization in Eq. (
S98
) is over the set of product states, we can fix the state of one subsystem:

\max_{|\phi_{1}\cdots\phi_{n}\rangle}\langle\phi_{1}\cdots\phi_{n}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|\phi_{1}\cdots\phi_{n}\rangle=\max_{|\phi_{2}\cdots\phi_{n}\rangle}\langle 0\phi_{2}\cdots\phi_{n}|\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a}|0\phi_{2}\cdots\phi_{n}\rangle.

(S104)

This leads to the computable bound:

E_{\lambda a}\geq 1-\max_{\varrho\in\mathrm{DPS}_{k}}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\openone^{\otimes(n-1)}\,\mathscr{P}_{\lambda a})\,\varrho\Big).

(S105)

For
n=3
(three qubits),
\varrho
is a two-qubit state; since for two qubits PPT implies separability, the resulting value,
E_{[2,1]a}=5/9
, is exact.

So far, we have lower bounded the entanglement of pure parity-detecting states
|\psi_{e}\rangle
. To extend this to mixed states
\rho_{e}
(supported entirely on
\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}
), we follow Refs.
Wei2003geometric
;
das2016generalized
and define the GME of a generic multipartite density matrix
\rho
via the convex roof:

E(\rho):=\min_{\{p_{i},|\psi_{i}\rangle\}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\,E(|\psi_{i}\rangle),

(S106)

where the minimization is over all pure-state decompositions
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
.

In our case, every decomposition of
\rho_{e}
lies entirely within
\bm{\mathscr{K}}_{\lambda a}
