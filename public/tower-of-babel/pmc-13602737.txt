Engineered nanodendritic sonosensitizers empower sonodynamic-elicited ferroptosis and PANoptosis against platinum-resistant breast cancer
Authors: Jiahui Chen, Yu Shen, Yi Zheng, Jiwen Kang, Xifu Wang, Xuehong Diao, Yu Chen, Rong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCisplatin-resistant breast cancer continues to pose a major clinical challenge, largely due to sophisticated cellular defense mechanisms that compromise the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapy. Ferroptosis and PANoptosis, as programmed cell death pathways, have emerged as promising strategies to circumvent treatment resistance. In this study, Pt56Mn44 nanodendrites have been designed and engineered as high-performance ultrasound-activatable sonosensitizers for efficient sonodynamic therapy of cisplatin-resistant breast cancer. The nanodendrites feature a narrow bandgap structure that facilitates enhanced electron-hole separation under ultrasound irradiation, leading to highly efficient reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Importantly, the resulting nanodendrites effectively suppressed the growth of cisplatin-resistant breast tumors through a coordinated multi-mechanistic action. The released Pt(II) ions induced DNA damage and promoted apoptosis while evading common resistance pathways. Concurrently, Mn(II) ions depleted intracellular glutathione and downregulated glutathione peroxidase 4, precipitating ferroptotic cell death. The sonodynamic effect further disrupted mitochondrial integrity, elevated ROS levels, and activated PANoptosis. A synergistic interaction between metal ion-mediated effects and sonodynamic radical oxidation enabled simultaneous induction of multiple cell death programs, collectively overcoming cisplatin resistance of cancer cells. The engineered nanodendrites also exhibited favorable biocompatibility, supporting their high potential as a translatable therapeutic strategy for cisplatin-resistant breast cancer and other treatment-refractory malignancies.Keywords: Ferroptosis, PANoptosis, Tumor drug resistant, Cisplatin, Sonodynamic therapy

Introduction

Cisplatin (DDP), a pioneering platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent, exerts its cytotoxic effects primarily through the formation of covalent DNA adducts. These interactions induce structural DNA damage, inhibit nucleic acid synthesis and protein translation, disrupt DNA replication, and activate multiple signal transduction pathways, ultimately triggering apoptotic cell death [1,2]. However, the therapeutic success of DDP is often undermined by the emergence of drug resistance [3,4]. The development of DDP-resistant malignancies represents a major obstacle in oncology, as their robust adaptive responses effectively evade the cytotoxic effects of drugs, culminating in treatment failure [5]. Hence, it is imperative to develop novel therapeutic modalities that overcome chemoresistance and reduce recurrence rates by employing synergistic mechanisms of action. The emerging sonodynamic therapy (SDT) exerts its antitumor effects via the ultrasound (US) -triggered generation of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS). This modality provides dual advantages by virtue of its deep-tissue penetration capability (≥10 cm), which enables non-invasive targeting, and its spatiotemporally confined activation, thereby minimizing collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues [[6], [7], [8]]. Notably, emerging evidence has revealed that combining SDT with chemotherapy generates a synergistic effect, which effectively counteracts chemoresistance and significantly enhances antitumor efficacy [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. This chemo-sonodynamic approach thus represents a promising strategy for managing resistant malignancies with substantial translational promise [13].

Ferroptosis is a form of non-apoptotic programmed cell death driven by iron-dependent accumulation of lethal lipid peroxides within cellular membranes. Its execution is critically regulated by the glutathione (GSH) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis, where GSH depletion or GPX4 inactivation disrupts redox homeostasis, leading to unchecked lipid peroxidation and subsequent cell death [14,15]. As an innate tumor-suppressive mechanism, ferroptosis plays a significant role in various tumor-related biological processes. Notably, excessive ROS acts as a potent inducer of ferroptosis, positioning this cell death pathway as an promising therapeutic liability in oncology, especially for reversing chemoresistance in malignancies [[16], [17], [18]]. In addition, PANoptosis represents a recently identified programmed cell death pathway characterized by the convergent activation of pyroptotic, apoptotic, and necroptotic executors [19]. It is mechanistically triggered by ROS and mitochondrial damage, and serves as a coordinated stress-response mechanism capable of inducing multimodal cytotoxic effects. This makes PANoptosis a compelling therapeutic strategy for targeting therapy-resistant cancers [[20], [21], [22]]. Collectively, ferroptosis and PANoptosis constitute mechanistically distinct yet functionally synergistic forms of programmed cell death, both offering exploitable vulnerabilities for cancer treatment.

Herein, we present PEGylated Pt56Mn44 nanodendrites (PM) as a high-performance US-activatable sonosensitizer that addresses the essential clinical challenge of DDP resistance in breast cancer through innovative concurrent activation of ferroptosis and PANoptosis pathways. The Pt56Mn44@PEG nanodendrites (PMP), synthesized by a one-pot solvothermal method and PEGylated for biocompatibility, exhibit excellent sonodynamic performance by producing ROS such as singlet oxygen (1O2) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) under US exposure (Scheme 1a). Importantly, beyond mediating potent sonodynamic action via abundant ROS generation, the PMP also releases functional Pt(II) and Mn(II) ions into the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby enabling a combined therapeutic effect (Scheme 1b). The released Pt(II) ions penetrate the nucleus and induce DNA damage, bypassing conventional resistance mechanisms to re-establish apoptotic signaling. In parallel, Mn(II) ions disrupt cellular redox homeostasis by depleting GSH and inhibiting GPX4 activity, leading to iron-dependent lethal lipid peroxidation characteristic of ferroptosis. Concurrently, the accumulated ROS induce severe mitochondrial damage, resulting in membrane potential collapse. This cellular crisis subsequently activates the PANoptosis pathway, initiating a coordinated form of inflammatory cell death that encompasses elements of apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. The robust antitumor efficacy of this triple-therapy modality was systematically validated in both in vitro and in vivo settings using DDP-resistant MCF-7/DDP cell and animal models. Therefore, these findings collectively pave the way for developing advanced sonosensitizers and exploring innovative combinatorial modalities to effectively treat therapy-resistant cancers.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PM and PMP

PM nanodendrites were synthesized via a solvothermal reduction method, followed by surface modification with DSPE-PEG-NH2 using rotary evaporation to yield PEG-modified PMP (Fig. 1a) [23]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that the as-synthesized PM and PMP exhibited a dendritic architecture assembled from ultrasmall primary nanoparticles (Fig. 1b; Fig. S1, Supporting Information). This dendritic morphology conferred abundant catalytic active sites to the nanoparticles, demonstrating therapeutic potential [24,25]. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging confirmed the crystalline structure of PM, revealing well-defined lattice fringes with a measured spacing of 0.221 nm that corresponded to the (222) crystallographic plane of the Pt56Mn44 alloy (Fig. 1c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous spatial distribution and coexistence of Pt and Mn throughout the nanodendrites (Fig. 1d; Fig. S2, Supporting Information). Combined TEM imaging and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis established an average hydrodynamic diameter of 22.79 nm for the PMP (Fig. 1e). Zeta potential measurements indicated a surface charge shift from +7.73 mV for pristine PM to −2.70 mV after PEG modification (PMP) (Fig. 1f). To evaluate stability, the hydrodynamic diameter of PMP nanodendrites was monitored by DLS over 72 h in PBS, saline, deionized water, and DMEM culture medium. As shown in Fig. S3 (Supporting Information), PMP maintained consistent particle sizes without significant fluctuation or aggregation across all media. This confirms its excellent physiological stability, supporting its suitability for systemic administration.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were acquired to characterize the surface chemical states of the PM nanoparticles. The Pt 4f spectrum exhibits doublets at 71.12 eV (4f7/2) and 74.56 eV (4f5/2), consistent with a predominantly metallic Pt0 state. The Mn 2p spectrum reveals coexisting peaks for metallic Mn0 at 641.28 eV and MnO at higher binding energies, accompanied by characteristic satellite peaks. While the Mn0 core maintains the alloy structure, the presence of surface Mn(II) species indicates air-induced oxidation. This surface Mn(II) layer is functionally significant for therapy, as it serves as a proton-responsive reservoir capable of releasing Mn(II) ions in the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating downstream therapeutic mechanisms. (Fig. 1g). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the crystalline PtMn alloy phase, with characteristic peaks at 40.5° (111), 46.8° (200), and 68.5° (220) that consistently shifted relative to pure Pt (JCPDS 04-0802) and Mn (JCPDS 33-0887) standards, indicating lattice contraction due to alloying (Fig. 1h). Within the acidic and hydrogen peroxide-enriched TME, nanoparticles undergo biodegradation for therapeutic purposes [26,27]. To assess the degradability of PMP, four model systems simulating physiological and TME were established. TEM imaging after 24 h incubation revealed structural disintegration exclusively in the TME-mimetic condition (pH 5.5 + 10 mM H2O2), where PMP underwent decomposition into sub-10 nm particles with dendritic architecture collapse (Fig. 1i and j). To further quantify the degradation behavior under more physiologically relevant conditions, PMP was incubated in PBS buffer under five conditions: pH 7.4 (normal physiological), pH 5.5 (tumor acidic), pH 7.4 with 100 μM H2O2, pH 5.5 with 100 μM H2O2 (simulating tumor microenvironment), and pH 5.5 with 10 mM H2O2 (accelerated degradation control). The release of Pt and Mn ions was quantified by ICP-OES at multiple time points over 48 h. As shown in Fig. S4–S5(Supporting Information), PMP exhibited a clear pH-dependent and H2O2-responsive degradation behavior. To corroborate the valence states of the released ions, XPS analysis of the degradation products confirmed that Pt exists primarily in the Pt2+ oxidation state under tumor-mimetic conditions. Specifically, the Pt 4f spectrum exhibited characteristic doublet peaks at binding energies of approximately 72.5 eV and 75.8 eV, consistent with the formation of Pt(II) species (Fig. S6, Supporting Information). This result, combined with the previously identified Mn2+ species in the Mn 2p spectra, confirms the release of both divalent ions. This degradation pattern correlates well with the observed therapeutic efficacy. The significant ion release under acidic and oxidative conditions facilitates effective intracellular delivery of Pt(II) and Mn(II) ions, which enables DNA damage and GSH depletion, followed by subsequent ferroptosis and PANoptosis activation. These processes collectively contribute to the potent antitumor activity demonstrated in vitro and in vivo.

Sonodynamic performance of PMP

The SDT potential of PMP was evaluated through optical bandgap characterization. Solid-state absorbance spectra analysis via Tauc plots derived from Kubelka-Munk transformations calculated a bandgap (Eg) of 2.67 eV for the material (Fig. 2a; Fig. S7, Supporting Information). Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as spin traps was utilized to detect •OH and 1O2, respectively. Upon US irradiation, characteristic ESR signals were detected in PMP solutions: a 1:2:2:1 quartet indicative of DMPO-•OH adducts and a 1:1:1 triplet corresponding to TEMP-1O2 complexes (Fig. 2b and c).

The sonodynamic performance of PMP was further assessed using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) and methylene blue (MB) as molecular probes for 1O2 and •OH, respectively (Fig. 2d–g). Upon US exposure (1 MHz, 2.0 W cm−2, 50% duty cycle), the PMP/DPBF solution exhibited time-dependent attenuation of the DPBF absorbance peak at 410 nm in UV-Vis spectra (Fig. 2e), confirming 1O2-mediated oxidation of DPBF to colorless 1,2-dibenzoylbenzene (Fig. 2f). Parallel experiments with MB/PMP solutions under identical US conditions monitored •OH generation (Fig. 2h), where progressive reduction of the 670 nm MB absorption band was observed with increasing US duration (Fig. 2i).

GSH, a critical intracellular ROS scavenger, constrains the therapeutic efficacy of US-mediated ROS generation [[28], [29], [30]]. Under US activation, PMP simultaneously generated substantial ROS to deplete GSH and amplify oxidative stress, while synchronously accelerating the redox reaction between Mn(II) and GSH to synergistically deplete intracellular antioxidant reserves, thereby potentiating antitumor efficacy. GSH depletion kinetics were quantified using 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) as a chromogenic probe (Fig. 2j). Upon mixing GSH with PMP under US irradiation (1 MHz, 2.0 W cm−2, 50% duty cycle), residual GSH reacted with DTNB to generate 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoate (TNB2−), exhibiting characteristic absorbance at 412 nm. Time-dependent attenuation of the 412 nm absorption band confirmed US-activated GSH depletion by PMP (Fig. 2k), whereas control groups lacking PMP exhibited negligible GSH depletion (Fig. 2l).

In vitro antitumor activity of PMP

The quantitative assessment revealed dose-dependent cytotoxicity against DDP-resistant MCF-7/DDP cells, with 200 μg mL−1 PMP achieving approximately 60% cell viability (Fig. 3a), while comparable ferroptosis-PANoptosis activation efficacy was validated in murine 4T1 breast cancer models (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). Concurrently, systematic optimization of US parameters revealed time- and power-dependent cytotoxicity profiles under fixed conditions (50% duty cycle, 200 μg mL−1 PMP). Tumor cell viability declined proportionally with increasing sonication duration and intensity, resulting in 80% eradication at the therapeutic threshold of 2.0 W cm−2 for 6 min (Fig. 3b and c; Figure S9-10, Supporting Information). Additionally, comparative analysis revealed no statistical difference in cytotoxicity against MCF-7/DDP cells between the PMP + US and PMP + DDP + US groups (Fig. S11, Supporting Information). Cellular internalization dynamics of PMP in MCF-7/DDP cells were tracked via electron microscopy and confocal imaging. Bio-TEM confirmed PMP endocytosis after 12 h co-incubation (Fig. 3d). Time-lapse confocal imaging of FITC-labeled PMP demonstrated progressive perinuclear accumulation (Fig. S12, Supporting Information). Simultaneously, cellular internalization of PMP was investigated by quantifying intracellular Pt and Mn levels using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Analysis revealed differential accumulation of Pt and Mn across treatment groups. The DDP group exhibited Pt accumulation exclusively, while the PMP + US treatment significantly enhanced the intracellular uptake of both Pt and Mn compared to control groups (Fig. 3g). To further elucidate the mechanism underlying this enhanced intracellular accumulation, we performed inhibition studies using specific pharmacological inhibitors. Pre-treatment with the P-gp inhibitor verapamil and the MRP inhibitor MK-571did not significantly alter intracellular Pt levels following PMP + US treatment, indicating that the enhanced accumulation was not attributable to inhibition of drug efflux pathways. In contrast, pre-treatment with the clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor chlorpromazine and the lipid raft or caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor methyl-β-cyclodextrin (M-β-CD) significantly reduced the intracellular co-accumulation of Pt. These results collectively demonstrate that PMP achieves efficient intracellular delivery through enhanced endocytosis, effectively bypassing the ABC transporter-mediated efflux mechanisms that underlie cisplatin resistance, thereby truly evading the conventional resistance pathways (Fig. S13, Supporting Information).

The efficacy of PMP against MCF-7/DDP cells was further assessed using Calcein-AM/PI co-staining (Fig. 3e and f). Fluorescence imaging revealed a markedly increased red fluorescence (indicating dead cells) and a corresponding decrease in green fluorescence (indicating live cells) in MCF-7/DDP cells treated with PMP + US, compared to all control groups (PBS, US, DDP, and PMP alone; Fig. 3f). This pattern confirmed significantly enhanced cell death specifically induced by the combination of PMP and US. The intracellular mechanism underlying the US-mediated tumor cell-killing effects of PMP was further investigated by monitoring intracellular ROS generation using the DCFH-DA staining assay. Exposure to US combined with PMP induced significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity, indicative of substantial ROS generation, compared to control treatments (Fig. 3h and i). Such potent ROS generation was primarily attributed to the Type I sonodynamic mechanism, which proceeded through electron transfer between the sonosensitizer and surrounding substrates, making it far less oxygen-dependent. The narrow bandgap structure of Pt56Mn44 nanodendrites facilitated efficient electron-hole separation under US irradiation. The generated holes (h+) can directly oxidize H2O molecules to produce abundant •OH, while the electrons react with H2O/O2 to yield superoxide radicals (•O2−), which subsequently leads to •OH generation. Therefore, even under the hypoxic conditions inherent to DDP-resistant tumors, the PMP nanosystem can effectively bypass the oxygen limitation through its dominant Type I pathway to ensure potent ROS generation [31,32].

Given that sustained ROS accumulation can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 probe. JC-1 forms red-fluorescent aggregates in mitochondria with high ΔΨm but remains as green-fluorescent monomers under low ΔΨm conditions. Consistent with ROS accumulation, the PMP + US group showed a pronounced increase in green fluorescence intensity, indicating significant mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 3j; Fig. S14, Supporting Information). Furthermore, to overcome the limitation posed by the TME where excessive GSH scavenges ROS and diminishes therapeutic efficacy, intracellular GSH depletion was evaluated. Quantitative analysis using a GSH/GSSG assay kit confirmed significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels following PMP and PMP + US treatment compared to controls (Fig. 3k). This depletion resulted from the combined action of Mn(II)-mediated GSH redox reactions as well as US-triggered SDT. Annexin V-FITC/PI staining revealed distinct shifts in apoptotic cell populations across treatment groups (Fig. 3l). PMP-containing treatments significantly increased the proportion of cells in both Q2 (late apoptotic, Annexin V+/PI+) and Q3 (early apoptotic, Annexin V+/PI−) quadrants. The PMP + US group exhibited the highest total apoptosis rate, with 24.1% of cells in Q2 and 29.7% in Q3, demonstrating potent US-enhanced apoptotic cell death.

Transcriptomics analysis

To elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying US-induced sonodynamic effect enabled by PMP, transcriptome analysis via RNA sequencing was performed to profile genome-wide mRNA expression in MCF-7/DDP cells. Transcriptome analysis comparing PMP + US-treated cells against PBS-treated controls revealed high-quality sequencing data. Initial quality assessment demonstrated strong intra-group reproducibility, with Pearson correlation coefficients >0.8 across biological replicates (Fig. 4a). Comparable expression density distributions in violin plots further confirmed sample homogeneity (Fig. S15, Supporting Information), validating the suitability of these datasets for differential expression analysis. Volcano plot analysis revealed 3545 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) meeting significance criteria, comprising 1937 upregulated and 1608 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, the heatmap analysis revealed a significant difference in mRNA expression between control group and PMP + US group (Fig. 4c). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) confirmed significant enrichment of apoptosis (ES = 0.37) and ferroptosis (ES = 0.58) gene sets, indicating coordinated activation of these programmed cell death pathways (Fig. 4d; Figure S16, Supporting Information). The protein–protein interaction network highlighted the expression of major genes involved in this mechanism (Fig. 4e).

To functionally characterize transcriptomic alterations, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were performed, elucidating pathway-level dysregulation and biological processes associated with differentially expressed mRNAs. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs are predominantly enriched in microRNAs in cancer, pathways in cancer, DNA replication, p53 signaling pathway, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and ferroptosis (Fig. 4f). Moreover, GO enrichment analysis revealed significant associations of these DEGs with biological processes including negative regulation of cell cycle and cellular response to stress (Fig. 4g; Fig. S17, Supporting Information). Notably, analysis of the 3545 DEGs identified key regulators of four distinct programmed cell death pathways: ferroptosis (MAP1LC3B, HMOX1), apoptosis (JUN, FOS), pyroptosis (GSDMB), and necroptosis (IL1A, VDAC2), revealing a multi-modal death-inducing mechanism of PMP in MCF-7/DDP cells (Fig. 4b). Pathway enrichment analyses confirmed significant involvement of oxidative stress-induced inflammatory cascades, while the concurrent upregulation of core death executors across multiple pathways indicates that cell death is not confined to a single modality. These coordinated alterations collectively define PANoptosis, an integrated inflammation-associated cell death program triggered by redox imbalance [33,34].

Induction of ferroptosis and PANoptosis

Ferroptosis is characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and diminished activity of the antioxidant enzyme GPX4, concomitant with mitochondrial alterations including condensation or swelling, increased membrane density, cristae reduction, and outer membrane rupture [35,36]. To characterize PMP + US-induced ferroptosis, bio-TEM revealed distinct mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations (Fig. 5a) in treated MCF-7/DDP cells (ii) versus PBS controls (i). Test group mitochondria exhibited marked condensation with increased membrane density and cristae loss, confirming ferroptosis execution as a primary cell death pathway. Intracellular lipid peroxidation was quantified using BODIPY 581/591 C11, a ratiometric probe exhibiting a fluorescence shift from red (591 nm, reduced state) to green (510 nm, oxidized state). PMP + US-treated MCF-7/DDP cells displayed significantly enhanced green fluorescence intensity with concomitant red signal attenuation (Fig. 5b; Fig. S18, Supporting Information), confirming elevated lipid peroxide accumulation that mechanistically drives ferroptosis execution. Complementary western blot analysis confirmed molecular-level suppression of ferroptosis defense, with PMP + US treatment significantly reducing GPX4 protein expression versus controls (Fig. 5f; Fig. S19a, Supporting Information). To establish the causal role of ferroptosis in PMP + US-induced cytotoxicity, specific inhibition and chelation assays were performed (Fig. S20, Supporting Information). Pre-treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) restored cell viability from approximately 15.8% to 72.9%, confirming ferroptosis as a primary death pathway. Furthermore, the metal chelator EDTA sequestered released Mn(II) ions, preventing their redox interaction with intracellular GSH, which similarly recovered cell viability [37]. This finding verified that Mn(II)-mediated GSH depletion was the upstream driver of ferroptosis in this system. Collectively, these rescue experiments provide causal evidence that PMP + US-induced ferroptosis is initiated by Mn(II)-dependent GSH depletion and subsequent GPX4 inactivation. Together, these ultrastructural, redox, and proteomic findings establish the robust induction of ferroptosis in MCF-7/DDP cells.

In addition to ferroptotic mitochondrial alterations, bio-TEM analysis of PMP + US-treated cells revealed ultrastructural hallmarks diagnostic of PANoptosis (Fig. 5c). Apoptotic morphology, characterized by plasma membrane blebbing and nuclear fragmentation, was distinctly observed in PMP + US-treated cells as captured in Fig. 5c(i). Concurrently, Fig. 5c(ii) revealed necroptotic morphology in PMP + US-treated cells, characterized by plasma membrane rupture and nuclear dissolution. These cells exhibited distinct pyroptotic ultrastructural features, including nuclear deformation, plasma membrane rupture, cellular swelling, and organelle dissolution, as observed in Fig. 5c(iii).

As γ-H2AX is a core marker of DNA double-strand breaks, elevated or persistent γ-H2AX levels are strongly associated with cellular apoptosis and serve as a significant predictive and correlative indicator [38,39]. To investigate the extent of apoptosis induction by PMP + US, γ-H2AX levels were assessed (Fig. 5d). The results demonstrated significantly higher γ-H2AX expression in the PMP + US-treated group compared to all other control groups. This indicates that PMP + US treatment induced maximal DNA damage and triggered the most potent apoptotic response in MCF-7/DDP cells. Moreover, Caspase-3 is a pro-apoptotic protein, while B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) functions as an anti-apoptotic protein [40,41]. As shown in Fig. 5f, the upregulation of cleaved Caspase 3 (c-Caspase3) expression, concomitant with the downregulation of Bcl-2 expression, provided further confirmation of apoptosis occurrence (Fig. S19b–c, Supporting Information). YO-PRO-1 (YP1)/propidium iodide (PI) co-staining further confirmed PANoptosis occurrence. Apoptotic cells exhibited green nuclear fluorescence, while necroptotic cells displayed orange fluorescence from concurrent YP1/PI staining. PMP + US treatment significantly increased proportions of both apoptotic and necroptotic cell populations (Fig. 5e). Previous studies confirm that mitochondrial ROS induces necroptosis specifically by RIPK3-mediated MLKL phosphorylation, complementing apoptosis and pyroptosis pathways [42,43]. Consistent with this paradigm, PMP + US treatment significantly upregulated phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) and RIPK3 expression in tumor cells compared to controls, conclusively demonstrating necroptosis activation (Fig. 5f; Fig. S19d–e, Supporting Information).

Complementary to bio-TEM analysis, bright-field imaging revealed distinct pyroptotic morphology in PMP + US-treated MCF-7/DDP cells. These cells exhibited characteristic swelling with large bubble-like protrusions, a definitive ultrastructural signature that confirmed pyroptosis execution (Fig. S21, Supporting Information). Given the reciprocal relationship between pyroptosis and ROS generation [44,45], protein expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of NLRP3, N-GSDMD, and cleaved Caspase-1 (c-Caspase1) in PMP + US-treated cells. The upregulation of these proteins demonstrates NLRP3 inflammasome activation and Caspase1-mediated cleavage of GSDMD into its active N-terminal fragment (N-GSDMD) (Fig. 5f; Figures S22a-c, Supporting Information). Pyroptosis initiation triggers cellular swelling and plasma membrane pore formation, resulting in the release of intracellular contents including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-18. This release recruits additional inflammatory cells, amplifying the inflammatory cascade [[46], [47], [48]]. Consistent with this mechanism, PMP + US treatment significantly elevated extracellular levels of IL-1β, LDH, and IL-18 (Fig. 5g–i), confirming robust pyroptotic activation. The coordinated induction of these three distinct cell death modes collectively promoted PANoptosis, which underlies the enhanced antitumor efficacy of the US-triggered therapy (Fig. 5j). These cellular experimental findings demonstrated that PMP-mediated sonodynamic therapy concurrently triggered ferroptosis and PANoptosis in a parallel and synergistic manner. The released Mn(II) ions induced oxidative stress by depleting GSH and inactivating GPX4, which drove the ferroptotic pathway. Meanwhile, US-triggered ROS inflicted mitochondrial damage and activated the PANoptosis cascade. These two pathways formed a positive feedback loop. The lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress from ferroptosis exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction, which amplified PANoptotic signals. Conversely, the execution of PANoptosis disrupted intracellular homeostasis and compromised the antioxidant defense, creating a cellular state that accelerated ferroptotic progression. This reciprocal crosstalk promoted cell death, thereby overcoming the resistance of breast cancer cells to DDP.

Biosafety and biodistribution of PMP

To assess the biosafety of PMP under US exposure, healthy female Balb/c mice received intravenous injections of the following: (i) saline, (ii) US irradiation at the left mammary pad, (iii) PMP (25 mg kg−1), (iv) PMP (25 mg kg−1) with US irradiation at the left mammary pad. After 14 days, blood samples were collected for complete blood count and biochemical analysis, while major organs underwent histopathological examination via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Blood parameters at day 14 showed no significant differences from baseline (day 1) or interim (day 7) measurements, with all values remaining within normal physiological ranges (Fig. 6a; Figs. S23–25, Supporting Information). Concurrently, histopathological analysis revealed normal tissue architecture in major organs without signs of damage or inflammation (Fig. 6b), while body weight trajectories in treated groups remained comparable to controls throughout the 14-day period (Fig. 6c). To further evaluate the long-term clearance and potential accumulation of heavy metals, we performed quantitative analysis of Pt and Mn contents in liver and spleen from the PMP with US treatment group using ICP-MS at days 1, 2, 4, and 7 post injection (Fig. S26, Supporting Information). The results indicate that while the liver and spleen showed the highest initial uptake of Pt and Mn, consistent with reticuloendothelial system clearance, metal levels in these organs exhibited a clear time-dependent decline. By day 7, residual Pt and Mn concentrations in both organs had decreased significantly, demonstrating efficient systemic clearance and minimizing the risk of long-term cumulative toxicity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the favorable in vivo biosafety profile of PMP.

Biodistribution of PMP nanoparticles was evaluated using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) following intravenous administration of Cy5.5-conjugated PMP (PMP-Cy5.5). Cy5.5 fluorescence signals at tumor sites in MCF-7/DDP xenografts exhibited a time-dependent increase, with peak accumulation occurring at 12 h post-injection (Fig. 6d and e), demonstrating efficient nanoparticle accumulation. In addition, imaging of major organs and tumors at 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection revealed progressive clearance of Cy5.5 signals from liver and kidney tissues, with concomitant retention of strong fluorescence in tumors (Fig. 6f). Semi-quantitative analysis confirmed high tumor accumulation of PMP, which was primarily cleared through the reticuloendothelial system (liver and spleen) (Fig. S27, Supporting Information).

In vivo ferroptosis/PANoptosis-based therapy for combating drug resistance in tumors

Given the favorable in vitro efficacy and in vivo biodistribution, the therapeutic performance was evaluated in DDP-resistant tumor models. To further investigate whether the addition of DDP could provide any additional therapeutic benefit under the complex in vivo tumor microenvironment, a PMP + DDP + US group was included in the animal study. When tumor volumes reached ∼100 mm3, MCF-7/DDP tumor-bearing female Balb/c nude mice were randomized into six groups (n = 5/group): (G1) Saline control (IV, equivalent volume); (G2) US; (G3) DDP monotherapy (IV; 2.5 mg Pt kg−1); (G4) PMP monotherapy (IV; 2.5 mg Pt kg−1); (G5) PMP + US (IV; 2.5 mg Pt kg−1); (G6) PMP + DDP + US (IV; 2.5 mg Pt kg−1 each) (Fig. 7a). As illustrated in the survival analysis (Fig. 7b), the therapeutic efficacy of the different treatment groups was evaluated over a 60-day period. The saline control group (G1) exhibited 100% mortality observed by day 22. US (G2) did not significantly improve survival compared to the control. Similarly, DDP monotherapy (G3) showed limited efficacy, with all mice succumbing by day 25, indicating robust drug resistance in the MCF-7/DDP model. In contrast, PMP-administered groups demonstrated significantly extended survival. Specifically, the PMP + US group (G5) achieved a survival rate of 66.7%, reflecting substantial therapeutic benefit. The combination group G6 (PMP + DDP + US) also showed comparable survival outcomes (66.7%), although it experienced earlier mortality events compared to G5. These results confirm that PMP-mediated sonodynamic therapy effectively overcomes cisplatin resistance and provides robust antitumor efficacy in vivo.

Tumor volume served as the primary efficacy metric during the 14-day treatment for evaluating the US-enhanced therapeutic effect mediated by PMP nanoparticles. Over a two-week observation period, the PMP + US group produced a marked suppression of tumor growth compared to the control group, while the PMP-alone group induced a moderate reduction in tumor volume (Fig. 7c–h). These findings were corroborated by dissected tumor weight measurements and macroscopic images (Fig. 7j and k). Analysis of harvested tumors revealed the most pronounced inhibition in the PMP + US group, achieving a tumor-inhibition rate of 77.8%, substantially higher than the PMP group. Notably, compared to DDP monotherapy, the PMP + US combined with DDP treatment significantly attenuated drug resistance, suppressed tumor growth, and reduced tumor volume to 25% of control values. Notably, the absence of a statistical difference between the PMP + US and PMP + DDP + US groups indicates that the addition of free DDP yields no supplementary therapeutic benefit. This explicitly demonstrates that PMP + US neither re-sensitizes resistant tumors to conventional DDP chemotherapy nor acts synergistically with it. Instead, PMP + US independently overcomes cisplatin resistance by concurrently triggering ferroptosis and PANoptosis, thereby bypassing the canonical DDP resistance mechanisms. Throughout the 14-day treatment period, mean body weights across all six experimental groups remained stable with no significant alterations, indicating favorable systemic safety profiles of the therapeutic regimens (Fig. 7l). Histopathological assessment of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) revealed unaltered tissue architecture with no evidence of treatment-related pathology (Fig. S28, Supporting Information), confirming the biosafety of PMP-mediated therapy.

The therapeutic efficacy was further assessed through multiplexed histological analysis of tumor sections, including H&E staining and immunofluorescence for TUNEL, Ki67, GPX4, Bcl-2, c-Caspase3, and N-GSDMD (Fig. 7m). H&E evaluation revealed extensive cellular damage and necrosis in PMP + US-treated tumors. In contrast, control and DDP monotherapy groups maintained intact tissue architecture, while PMP + US administration induced significant histopathological disruption. TUNEL staining, a molecular method for detecting DNA fragmentation as an apoptosis hallmark, revealed significantly intensified red fluorescence in PMP-treated groups compared to controls, confirming nanoparticle-induced programmed cell death. Furthermore, the PMP + US group exhibited the lowest Ki67 immunofluorescence intensity, confirming that PMP + US treatment significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation. Remarkably, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated concurrent induction of various cell death pathways in the PMP + US group, with key markers indicating ferroptosis (reduced GPX4), apoptosis (decreased Bcl-2 and elevated c-Caspase3), and pyroptosis (increased N-GSDMD). This coordinated induction of multimodal cell death underpins the optimal antitumor efficacy of PMP + US, effectively overcoming chemoresistance via US-triggered nanocatalytic ferroptosis and PANoptosis. In addition, in vivo ROS production was assessed via dihydroethidium (DHE) staining of tumor sections. Fluorescence quantification revealed ROS levels consistent with in vitro findings (Fig. 3h), where PMP + US treatment generated the most intense red fluorescence among all groups (Fig. S29, Supporting Information). This demonstrates PMP-mediated sonodynamic effect significantly amplifies intratumoral oxidative stress.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed Pt56Mn44 nanodendrites as a high-performance US-activatable sonosensitizer nanosystem that overcomes DDP resistance in breast cancer by concurrently activating ferroptosis and PANoptosis pathways. The nanodendrites possess a narrow bandgap structure that enhances electron-hole separation under US irradiation, enabling highly efficient production of 1O2 and •OH. This sonodynamic effect also induces mitochondrial damage, heightens ROS production, and consequently initiates PANoptosis. Simultaneously, the released Pt(II) ions induce DNA damage and promote apoptosis while bypassing common resistance pathways. Meanwhile, Mn(II) ions deplete intracellular GSH and downregulate GPX4, thereby inducing ferroptosis. Systematic in vitro and in vivo assessments demonstrate the favorable biosafety profile of PMP nanodendrites and reveal a synergistic interaction between metal ion-mediated effects and sonodynamic radical oxidation. This synergy enables simultaneous induction of multiple cell death programs, thus overcoming cisplatin resistance in cancer cells. The developed SDT approach effectively bypasses DDP-resistant tumors and suppresses tumor growth through a dual ferroptosis/PANoptosis activation strategy. This work establishes a highly informative paradigm for designing multi-pathway synergistic nanotherapeutics to combat drug-resistant malignancies.
