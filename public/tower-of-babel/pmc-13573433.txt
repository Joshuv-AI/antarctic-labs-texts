Effects of a brief sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention incorporating critical-thinking elements on immediate self-reported prejudice against lesbians and gay men: Baseline-adjusted evidence from a randomized experiment
Authors: Quan Zheng, Yang Cao, Bingyu Niu, Xiangen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrejudice against lesbians and gay men (PALG) is associated with poorer well-being among sexual-minority people and with broader intergroup tension. Efforts to reduce it may benefit from pairing accurate information about sexual orientation with guided evaluation of the evidence and reasoning behind prejudicial claims. This study tested whether participants assigned to a brief sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention incorporating critical-thinking elements would display lower immediate self-reported PALG than participants assigned to a neutral-video control condition.MethodsIn a two-condition, between-subjects randomized experiment, 112 Chinese college students were randomly assigned to the instructional intervention or to a neutral-video control condition matched in duration. PALG was assessed before and immediately after the assigned video; prior intergroup contact and critical-thinking disposition were measured at baseline. The primary analysis was a baseline-adjusted analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with post-intervention PALG as the dependent variable, condition as the predictor, and centered baseline PALG and centered prior intergroup contact as covariates. A separate exploratory model added the condition × baseline PALG interaction.ResultsRelative to the neutral-video control condition, the instructional intervention condition showed lower post-intervention PALG, adjusted mean difference = − 20.36, 95% CI [− 25.32, − 15.40], p < 0.001. An exploratory interaction indicated that the adjusted condition difference varied across baseline PALG, F(1, 107) = 250.49, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.70; this variation may partly reflect floor compression. Repeated-measures and expanded-covariate sensitivity analyses were consistent with the condition difference, and analyses by sexual orientation indicated that the difference was concentrated among heterosexual participants.ConclusionsParticipants assigned to a brief sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention that combined sexual-orientation information with two selected critical-thinking elements reported lower immediate self-reported PALG than participants assigned to a neutral-video control condition. The condition contrast did not isolate either selected element, and the outcome was measured immediately by self-report. The findings should therefore be treated as preliminary; active-control comparisons, delayed follow-up, more diverse samples, and less demand-sensitive outcomes are needed before stronger conclusions are drawn.Keywords: prejudice against lesbians and gay men, sexual-orientation-based prejudice, critical-thinking elements, instructional intervention

Introduction

Prejudice against lesbians and gay men (PALG) refers to negative attitudes toward lesbian and gay people and their communities [1]. Despite advances in equal-rights and anti-discrimination protections in many societies, such prejudice persists and remains a concern across social psychology, sociology, and public health [2, 3]. Its costs are well documented. PALG is linked to depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among lesbian and gay people, to higher physical-health risks, and to poorer outcomes in education and work [4, 5]. The social pressure it creates can also push lesbian and gay people into mixed-orientation marriages, at lasting cost to them and their families [6]. More broadly, it deepens intergroup division and strains public-health and mental-health resources. Identifying effective interventions to reduce PALG therefore remains an important theoretical and practical goal.

The Chinese context underscores the relevance of this work. A Chinese version of the Attitudes Toward Lesbians and Gay Men Scale has been validated for assessing such attitudes [7], and research on online discourse and cross-society comparisons documents mixed and unevenly distributed attitudes toward sexual-minority people [8, 9]. Evidence from younger populations is especially pertinent. A national survey of people aged 15–24 years reported mixed attitudes toward same-sex sexuality [10], and a college-based program in southwest China found that sexuality education improved sexual-minority-related attitudes [11]. Together, these findings indicate that PALG remains a meaningful concern among Chinese young adults, and that such attitudes vary across sociocultural contexts rather than remaining uniform [9].

The nature and reduction of prejudice against lesbians and gay men

Research on prejudice reduction has examined a variety of approaches, including intergroup contact, education, stereotype challenge, cognitive reframing, and social-norm change [12–14]. Reviews of this work report substantial progress, but they also note a persistent difficulty in producing effects that are durable, scalable, and clearly attributable to a specific psychological process [13, 14]. One approach has received comparatively little structured attention: guiding people to evaluate the evidence and reasoning that underlie prejudicial claims. Prejudice is sustained in part by cognitive shortcuts and broad categorical judgments that conserve limited processing resources [15]. Moreover, sexual prejudice is not a unitary attitude. Such prejudice may rest on misinformation, on outgroup antipathy, or on moral disapproval, and these components need not respond equally to the same intervention [16]. A reasoning-focused approach should be most relevant to prejudice grounded in inaccurate information or weak argumentation, whereas prejudice anchored in moral conviction or identity-defensive concern may require a different or more intensive approach.

These differing bases of prejudice can be situated within broader theory. The dual-process motivational model of ideology and prejudice offers a useful framework for locating PALG within general accounts of prejudice [17–19]. The model links prejudice to ideological-motivational orientations such as right-wing authoritarianism and social dominance orientation, which are tied to threat-related and hierarchy-related social worldviews [17, 18]. From this perspective, prejudice may be sustained not only by inaccurate information but also by motivationally meaningful beliefs and rigid, stereotype-based information processing. In the present study, this model serves as an orienting context rather than as a source of directly tested predictions: the intervention and the measures targeted the evaluation of PALG-related claims rather than ideological-motivational constructs such as right-wing authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, or need for closure, none of which were assessed here. At the same time, the model locates prejudice partly in cognitive and evaluative habits, such as rigid, stereotype-based processing of social information, that may be open to deliberate reconsideration.

Critical-thinking elements in a sexual-orientation-focused intervention

This cognitive and evaluative dimension of prejudice is where critical thinking becomes relevant. Critical thinking is commonly defined as purposeful, evidence-sensitive, self-regulated judgment involving analysis, evaluation, inference, and reflection [20]. Two features are pertinent to the instructional rationale we tested in the current research. First, aspects of critical thinking can be taught and strengthened through instruction [21–23]. Second, their application to a particular problem depends on relevant background knowledge and explicit guidance rather than on general reasoning ability alone [24]. These observations make selected critical-thinking elements plausible instructional content for addressing prejudice grounded in inaccurate information or weak argumentation; they do not imply that a brief lesson represents critical thinking as a whole.

Two lines of work connect careful reasoning to lower sexual prejudice. Conceptually, careful reasoning has long been described as the antithesis of prejudiced judgment, which forms prematurely and on insufficient evidence [25, 26]; guiding people to weigh the evidence and logic behind a prejudicial claim therefore targets a core source of bias. Empirically, stronger reasoning is associated with lower sexual prejudice: greater abstract-reasoning ability predicts lower PALG [27], and a stronger disposition toward critical thinking predicts support for sexual-minority people [28]. Together, these observations motivate testing guided evaluation of PALG-related claims, but they do not establish which instructional element would account for any condition difference.

Because the intervention targeted PALG, we drew on Liu and colleagues’ higher-education assessment framework, together with broader work on teaching critical thinking [21–23], to define two selected, prejudice-relevant elements: analysis and synthesis. The analysis element concerns the evaluation of the evidence and reasoning in a PALG-related claim, including whether the evidence is relevant, reliable, biased, overgeneralized, or insufficient to support the conclusion, and the identification of logical fallacies, inferential gaps, and unsupported generalizations. The synthesis element concerns the integration and evaluation of broader meaning: drawing on reliable information, weighing alternative viewpoints, identifying implicit conclusions, and considering what a prejudicial claim implies beyond its literal content. We treat this last activity, evaluating the consequences of a claim, as part of synthesis: it requires reasoning about what follows from the claim rather than imagining another person’s experience, which distinguishes it both from empathic perspective-taking and from PALG itself, the attitudinal outcome.

These selected elements are not applied in the abstract; their use depends on accurate domain knowledge [21–24]. Evaluating stereotypes, misinformation, or weak arguments about lesbian and gay people requires a factual understanding of sexual orientation against which such claims can be assessed. The intervention therefore paired scientifically grounded information about sexual orientation with guided practice in analysis and synthesis. We describe it as a sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention incorporating critical-thinking elements rather than as a domain-general reasoning exercise. On this basis, we hypothesized that participants in the intervention condition would display lower immediate self-reported PALG, compared with a neutral-video control condition (Hypothesis 1).

Correlates of PALG and the choice of covariates

Several variables measured before the intervention are associated with PALG, and taking them into account allows the condition effect to be estimated more precisely. Greater prior contact with lesbian and gay people is associated with weaker prejudice [29]. Men, on average, report stronger sexual prejudice than women [30, 31], whereas sexual-minority individuals tend to report weaker prejudice than heterosexual individuals [3]. Younger people generally hold more accepting attitudes toward sexual minorities [31], and individual differences in cognitive ability and reasoning dispositions have been linked to lower prejudice [27, 28]. Baseline prejudice, as the pre-intervention level of the outcome itself, is likewise expected to predict post-intervention scores.

Because participants were randomly assigned, these variables are expected to be balanced across conditions, so adjusting for them is not necessary to obtain an unbiased estimate of the condition difference; it can, however, improve precision and aid interpretation. Our analytic strategy therefore comprised three clearly separated steps. The primary model adjusted only for the two covariates most directly tied to the outcome: baseline PALG, because the same scale was administered before and after the intervention, and prior intergroup contact, a well-established predictor of sexual prejudice [29]. An exploratory analysis then added a condition × baseline PALG interaction to examine whether the adjusted condition difference varied across baseline PALG. Finally, we ran two robustness checks: a condition × time repeated-measures model and an expanded covariate-controlled model that additionally adjusted for the remaining correlates, namely age, gender, sexual orientation, and baseline critical-thinking disposition. Additional sensitivity analyses addressed the sample’s sexual-orientation composition.

The present study

The present study provided an experimental test of our key hypothesis. Chinese college students were randomly assigned to the instructional intervention or to a neutral-video control condition matched in total duration, and completed the PALG measure both before and immediately after the assigned video. We estimated the adjusted condition difference while accounting for baseline PALG and prior intergroup contact. As an exploratory analysis for which no directional hypothesis was specified, we examined whether the adjusted condition difference varied across values of continuous baseline PALG. The study was not designed to identify psychological mechanisms or beneficiary subgroups.

Methods

Participants

A sample-size benchmark was computed with G*Power 3.1 [32, 33]. Because no closely matched prior effect-size estimate was available for a sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention on PALG, we adopted an approximate small-to-medium planning benchmark for the adjusted condition effect, f² = 0.07. This value is close to that derived from Cohen’s conventional medium ANOVA effect size, f = 0.25, which corresponds to f² = 0.0625 in the R²-increase metric [34]. For an F test in linear multiple regression (fixed model, R² increase) with α = 0.05, power = 0.80, one tested predictor, and four total predictors, this specification indicated a minimum of 115 participants. A total of 117 Chinese college students were recruited and randomly assigned to the instructional intervention condition or the neutral-video control condition. Attention checks consisted of three pairs of items that presented the same statement in positive and reversed form (e.g., that same-sex sexual orientation is a choice and that it is not a choice) and one instructed-response item (“Please select ‘strongly agree’”), embedded in the questionnaires; the five participants who gave contradictory answers to any pair or did not select the instructed option were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 112 participants (mean age = 19.57 years, SD = 1.43), with 57 in the neutral-video control condition and 55 in the instructional intervention condition.

As Table 1 shows, the sample consisted mostly of women (75.00%). Gender identity was recorded with women and men response options, and sexual orientation as heterosexual versus non-heterosexual; finer gender-identity and sexual-orientation categories were not collected. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Central China Normal University, and all participants gave informed consent before taking part.

Design and procedure

The study used a two-condition, between-subjects design, with participants randomly assigned to the instructional intervention or the neutral-video control condition. It was administered through the enterprise edition of Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), a Chinese online survey platform, and took approximately 45 min. After providing informed consent, participants completed baseline measures of PALG, critical-thinking disposition, prior intergroup contact, and demographic variables. They were then assigned to a condition and viewed the corresponding video. Immediately afterward, they completed the post-intervention PALG measure, the primary outcome, followed by the lesson-content assessment. The outcome was assessed before the lesson-content assessment so that its retention items and near-transfer item would not cue the evaluative procedures before PALG was measured. A structured debriefing concluded the session.

Intervention materials

The instructional condition received an approximately 27-minute multimedia lesson that combined sexual-orientation information with guided practice in two selected critical-thinking elements: analysis and synthesis. The video first introduced basic concepts and current scientific understanding of sexual orientation, drawn from authoritative sources [35], to provide accurate background knowledge for evaluating prejudice-related claims. The analysis element directed participants to examine the evidence and reasoning used in PALG-related claims, including whether the evidence was relevant, reliable, biased, overgeneralized, or insufficient to support a conclusion, and to identify logical fallacies and inferential gaps. The synthesis element guided participants to integrate reliable information, weigh alternative viewpoints, identify implicit conclusions, and consider the broader consequences of prejudice-related claims. The consequence-evaluation activities were designed as evidence-guided reasoning exercises rather than as exercises in imagining the subjective experiences of lesbian or gay individuals. The instructional design followed established guidance on teaching the evaluation of arguments and evidence [21, 36, 37].

Because applying the selected critical-thinking elements requires a specific focal object [24], six authentic posts expressing PALG, drawn from the Chinese social-media platform Weibo, were used as case materials to preserve ecological validity. Five posts were embedded in the instructional video as examples for guided analysis, and one additional post was reserved for the post-outcome near-transfer item. The posts were presented as screenshots to retain realism, and a brief pilot with six college students was used to confirm that the selected materials resembled typical social-media content and did not contain extreme or confounding elements.

Participants in the neutral-video control condition viewed four short animated popular-science videos drawn from a Chinese video platform (Bilibili). The four clips were combined so that the control session matched the instructional session in total duration. They addressed everyday topics with no bearing on sexual orientation, PALG, or the selected critical-thinking elements, namely why bubble-tea prices have risen, how to choose a color-rich computer monitor, how to select a comfortable pillow, and several food-taste curiosities. To support the content neutrality of these materials, they were first reviewed by five psychology doctoral students and then by five undergraduate students, all of whom judged the videos to be unrelated to PALG and the selected critical-thinking elements. This screening supports content neutrality with respect to the focal topic; it does not establish equivalence between conditions on expectancy, engagement, emotional salience, topic salience, perceived purpose, or demand. No participant ratings of these dimensions were collected.

Because exposure to authentic prejudiced content carries some risk of discomfort, all participants received a structured debriefing immediately after the experiment. The rationale for using real-world examples was explained, the scientific consensus on sexual orientation was reiterated with an emphasis on respect and inclusion, and additional informational resources were provided.

Measures

Prejudice against lesbians and gay men

PALG was measured with the Chinese version of the Attitudes Toward Lesbians and Gay Men Scale [7, 30]. The 20 items are rated on a 9-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 9 (strongly agree). Item scores were summed (possible range 20–180; theoretical midpoint 100), with higher total scores indicating greater prejudice. A sample item is “Lesbians are disgusting.” The scale was given at baseline and again immediately after the video (Cronbach’s α = 0.94 and 0.95, respectively).

Prior intergroup contact

Prior intergroup contact was assessed with six items adapted from Poteat [28]. The items covered exposure to information about lesbian and gay people on social media and other platforms; exposure to related films, television, and other cultural materials; awareness of lesbian and gay public figures and acquaintances; number of lesbian and gay friends; perceived closeness to them; and time spent with them. A sample item is “I have many friends who are gay or lesbian.” Each item used a 6-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), and the items were summed, with higher scores indicating greater prior contact (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Critical-thinking disposition.

Critical-thinking disposition was measured only at baseline, using the 70-item Chinese Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory [38], rated on a 6-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Item scores were summed (possible range 70–420), with higher total scores indicating a stronger disposition (Cronbach’s α = 0.90). A sample item is “I always identify the key issue in a problem before trying to solve it.” It served as a baseline individual-difference covariate in the expanded-covariate model and was not reassessed after the intervention.

Lesson-content assessment

After completing the post-intervention PALG measure, participants answered 15 multiple-choice items assessing their performance on procedures taught in the lesson. The 14 retention items referred to the prejudiced posts used during instruction, whereas one near-transfer item required participants to apply the same procedures to a new PALG-related post. Across the assessment, participants identified evidence sources, logical fallacies, and potential negative consequences. Some items contained more than one correct option. Scoring was conducted at the option level: each correct option received 0.5 or 1 point, and each incorrect option received 0 points. Option scores were summed (possible range 0–37), with higher totals indicating better lesson-aligned performance. Because the assessment closely mirrored the lesson and remained within the PALG topic, it was interpreted as retention and near transfer of the taught procedures, not as a manipulation check or a measure of general critical-thinking ability.

Statistical analysis

Analyses used SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). The primary outcome was analyzed with a baseline-adjusted analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) implemented within the general linear model framework. Post-intervention PALG was the dependent variable, condition the focal predictor, and centered baseline PALG and centered prior intergroup contact the only covariates; they were chosen for their theoretical relevance to the outcome. In a separate exploratory model, a condition × baseline PALG interaction term was then added to examine whether the adjusted condition difference varied across baseline PALG. Baseline adjustment of this kind is standard for pretest–posttest designs [39–41]. Because the interaction was significant, we probed the adjusted difference at one standard deviation below the baseline PALG mean, at the mean, and at one standard deviation above the mean.

We ran two robustness checks and one additional set of sensitivity analyses. The first used repeated-measures models on baseline and post-intervention PALG to test the condition × time interaction, a repeated-measures analogue of a difference-in-differences estimate, with and without prior intergroup contact as an added covariate. The second was an expanded-covariate model that also adjusted for age, gender, sexual orientation, and baseline critical-thinking disposition. The additional sensitivity analyses addressed the sample’s sexual-orientation composition: the primary model was re-estimated among heterosexual participants only, and a condition × sexual orientation interaction model was estimated in the full sample. We assessed homogeneity of post-intervention variances with Levene’s test and, because the assumption was violated, checked the unadjusted condition difference with a Welch comparison, which does not assume equal variances; we also inspected the post-intervention score distribution for floor effects.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Randomization produced comparable groups. The conditions did not differ at baseline in age, t(110) = 0.72; gender, χ²(1) = 0.11; sexual orientation, χ²(1) = 1.44; critical-thinking disposition, t(110) = 0.60; baseline PALG, t(110) = − 0.47; or prior intergroup contact, t(110) = 0.55 (all ps > 0.05). Participant characteristics by condition appear in Table 1.

Lesson-content assessment

Scores on the lesson-content assessment were higher in the instructional intervention condition (M = 21.86, SD = 4.52) than in the neutral-video control condition (M = 3.83, SD = 2.63), t(110) = − 25.91, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 4.90. Given the close correspondence between the assessment and the lesson, this difference documents better immediate performance on lesson-aligned retention and near-transfer tasks only; it does not establish that a critical-thinking construct was manipulated, that general critical-thinking ability changed, or that the selected elements were the active ingredient responsible for the PALG condition difference.

Primary analysis

Before adjustment, post-intervention PALG was lower in the instructional intervention condition (M = 28.47, SD = 8.52) than in the neutral-video control condition (M = 47.18, SD = 28.11). In the primary ANCOVA, the instructional intervention condition reported lower post-intervention PALG than the neutral-video control condition, B = − 20.36, SE = 2.50, 95% CI [− 25.32, − 15.40], p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.38 (Table 2); the adjusted means were 47.99 in the control condition and 27.63 in the intervention condition. Baseline PALG predicted post-intervention PALG, B = 0.46, 95% CI [0.33, 0.58], p < 0.001, and prior intergroup contact was associated with lower post-intervention PALG, B = − 0.78, 95% CI [− 1.24, − 0.33], p = 0.001. The model accounted for 67.5% of the variance in post-intervention PALG.

Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses yielded the same pattern. In a repeated-measures analysis of variance using baseline and post-intervention PALG, the condition × time interaction was significant, F(1, 110) = 40.60, p < 0.001; the corresponding difference-in-differences estimate showed that the intervention condition’s pre-to-post reduction in PALG exceeded the control condition’s, B = − 21.07, 95% CI [− 27.62, − 14.52]. The interaction remained significant after prior intergroup contact and its interaction with time were added, F(1, 109) = 40.54, p < 0.001, B = − 20.68, 95% CI [− 27.12, − 14.24]. In the expanded-covariate model, which additionally adjusted for age, gender, sexual orientation, and baseline critical-thinking disposition, the condition × baseline PALG interaction was essentially unchanged, F(1, 103) = 245.86, p < 0.001; among the added covariates, only age significantly predicted post-intervention PALG (Table 5). Post-intervention scores were more variable in the control condition than in the intervention condition; Levene’s test rejected the homogeneity-of-variance assumption, F(1, 110) = 39.79, p < 0.001; a Welch comparison that does not assume equal variances nonetheless reproduced the effect, with the control condition scoring 18.70 points higher than the intervention condition on unadjusted post-intervention PALG, 95% CI [10.93, 26.48], t(66.53) = 4.80, p < 0.001. The condition difference was therefore consistent across covariate specifications and in the Welch comparison. Post-intervention scores ranged from 20 to 58 in the instructional intervention condition, with 24 of 55 participants (43.64%) scoring at or below 25, and from 20 to 125 in the neutral-video control condition, with 12 of 57 participants (21.05%) scoring at or below 25.

The additional sensitivity analyses addressed the sample’s sexual-orientation composition. When the primary model was re-estimated among heterosexual participants only (n = 84), the condition effect remained significant and was larger than in the full sample, B = − 25.33, SE = 3.17, 95% CI [− 31.63, − 19.02], p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.44. In the full sample, a condition × sexual orientation interaction was significant, F(1, 106) = 13.07, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.11: the condition difference was concentrated among heterosexual participants, whereas non-heterosexual participants (n = 28) scored near the scale floor at both assessments (pooled baseline M = 28.61, corresponding to 1.43 per item) and showed a small condition difference, B = − 2.74, SE = 1.17, 95% CI [− 5.15, − 0.33], p = 0.027.

Discussion

The present study tested Hypothesis 1: whether participants assigned to a brief sexual-orientation-focused instructional intervention incorporating two selected critical-thinking elements would display lower immediate self-reported PALG than participants assigned to a neutral-video control condition. After adjustment for baseline PALG and prior intergroup contact, the instructional intervention condition reported lower post-intervention PALG. In a separate exploratory analysis, the adjusted condition difference varied across values of continuous baseline PALG. Repeated-measures and expanded-covariate sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary condition difference, while the sexual-orientation sensitivity analyses indicated that the difference was concentrated among heterosexual participants. Prior intergroup contact was independently associated with lower post-intervention PALG.

Interpreting the intervention effect

The observed condition difference pertains to the specific lesson tested, which combined accurate sexual-orientation information with guided practice in two selected critical-thinking elements, analysis and synthesis. It does not provide evidence about critical thinking as a whole, does not isolate analysis or synthesis as an active ingredient, and cannot separate these lesson elements from sexual-orientation knowledge, topic exposure, factual correction, normative framing, or demand. The direction of the difference is consistent with an earlier conceptual argument [25] and with correlational evidence linking stronger reasoning to lower sexual prejudice [27, 28]. However, the neutral-video control does not identify which feature or combination of features accounts for the lower immediate self-reported PALG.

The design also does not identify the psychological process producing the adjusted condition difference. No candidate process was assessed at a separate, earlier time point, and the outcome was measured by immediate self-report. Because a process and an outcome measured concurrently cannot establish temporal precedence, a mechanism account cannot be supported here [42, 43]. Corrected beliefs, closer attention to the evidence behind a claim, perceived norms, topic salience, and demand are all plausible contributors, but the present design cannot distinguish among them. Within these constraints, the findings for Hypothesis 1 are preliminarily encouraging: the condition difference was sizable and remained evident in the repeated-measures, expanded-covariate, and heterosexual-only analyses, and it warrants further intervention research targeting selected aspects of critical thinking under more rigorously controlled comparisons.

Baseline prejudice and prior contact

Prior intergroup contact, entered as a covariate, was itself associated with lower post-intervention PALG, consistent with the broader contact literature [29]. The way the adjusted condition difference varied across baseline PALG should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory model feature rather than a tested boundary condition. Post-intervention scores in the instructional intervention condition clustered near the lower end of the scale (possible range 20–180): they ranged from 20 to 58, with 24 of 55 participants (43.64%) at or near the floor (a score of 25 or below), compared with a range of 20 to 125 and 12 of 57 participants (21.05%) near the floor in the control condition. Because the adjusted instructional-intervention means changed little across the three probe values whereas the adjusted control means tracked baseline more closely, the widening condition difference may partly reflect floor-related compression and limited room for lower scores. The present data therefore do not support subgroup-specific or differential-utility claims.

Theoretical and practical implications

These findings make three contributions within the scope of the present design. First, the study provides a randomized, baseline-adjusted comparison of immediate self-reported PALG following a brief, theory-informed lesson in a Chinese college-student sample for which experimental evidence has been scarce. Second, by pairing accurate sexual-orientation information with guided analysis and synthesis of prejudicial claims, it describes a concrete instructional format that incorporates two selected critical-thinking elements in a particular prejudice domain. Third, the adjusted condition difference remained similar across the specified repeated-measures and expanded-covariate sensitivity analyses. Taken together, these findings provide a basis for more rigorous comparative tests of this domain-focused instructional format. In this respect, the evidence for Hypothesis 1, although preliminary, is encouraging and supports future intervention studies that target aspects of critical thinking. These contributions should nevertheless be interpreted within the specific lesson and immediate self-reported outcome; the study does not establish general critical-thinking change, active ingredients, mechanisms, or boundary conditions.

Two conceptual boundaries further temper the theoretical reading. Although the dual-process model helped to frame PALG [17, 18], we did not measure its ideological-motivational constructs, such as right-wing authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, need for closure, or perceived threat, so the results do not speak to those pathways. A further boundary is that the analysis relied on the PALG total score, which captures overall prejudice but not its distinguishable components [16]. Because component-level outcomes were not measured, the study cannot determine whether the condition difference varied across misinformation, outgroup antipathy, or moral disapproval. Evaluating such variation would require measures that directly distinguish these components.

Turning to application, the practical implications are modest. Within a literature that has examined many approaches to prejudice reduction [13, 14], structured materials pairing accurate sexual-orientation knowledge with guided analysis and synthesis of prejudicial claims warrant further testing as a candidate instructional format. Such materials ask learners to examine common misconceptions, evaluate the basis of prejudiced statements, and apply lesson-aligned procedures to realistic cases. This study does not establish durable attitude change or differential effects across participant characteristics. Evidence from content-matched active controls, delayed follow-up, more diverse samples, and outcomes less susceptible to demand is needed before implementation or policy claims would be warranted.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations constrain these conclusions. The most important is construct confounding. The conditions were matched only in total duration. No participant ratings of expectancy, engagement, emotional salience, topic salience, perceived purpose, or demand were collected, so equivalence on these nonspecific dimensions was not empirically established. The conditions also differed in sexual-orientation knowledge, topic content, factual correction, normative framing, and guided analysis and synthesis. The contrast therefore cannot identify which feature or combination of features accounts for the lower immediate self-reported PALG. Stronger comparisons would include content-matched informational controls, empathy-focused controls, controls applying the same analysis and synthesis procedures to non-sexual-orientation material, and factorial or dismantling designs.

Floor effects, social desirability, and demand are closely related concerns. On the summed PALG scale (range 20–180; midpoint 100), the baseline mean of 47.25 equals 2.36 per item. The high probe value (the mean plus one standard deviation, approximately 73.71 from rounded descriptive statistics) equals 3.69 per item and remains below the item midpoint of 5. The value 73.59 in Table 4 is instead the model-estimated post-intervention control mean at that probe, not a baseline subgroup mean. The instructional-intervention post-intervention mean of 28.47 equals 1.42 per item. These low values may reflect genuinely low reported prejudice, socially desirable responding, or both. Moreover, participants completed the same attitude scale immediately before and after a lesson explicitly addressing sexual orientation and prejudice, making the expected direction potentially apparent. Lower post-intervention self-reports may therefore reflect short-term compliance or recognition of study purpose rather than genuine attitude change [44–46]. No awareness probe, social-desirability scale, delayed follow-up, or implicit or behavioral measure was included.

Sample characteristics also limit generalizability. The participants were young Chinese college students, and 84 of 112 (75.00%) were women; because men tend to report higher sexual prejudice [30, 31], this imbalance bears on the interpretation of the group means. In addition, 28 of 112 participants (25.00%) reported a non-heterosexual orientation, so for a quarter of the sample lesbian and gay people may have been perceived as members of a shared sexual-minority ingroup rather than as an outgroup; these participants scored near the scale floor at both assessments, and the sensitivity analyses indicated that the condition difference was concentrated among heterosexual participants. Gender identity was recorded only as women or men and sexual orientation only as heterosexual or non-heterosexual, which precludes finer subgroup analyses. Future studies should recruit more diverse, community-based samples and use more detailed gender-identity and sexual-orientation measures.

Two further issues concern measurement and precision. The lesson-content assessment documents only closely aligned task performance; it does not establish a specific psychological process, general critical-thinking change, or broad transfer. Broader transfer requires validated measures and tasks independent of the training and PALG topic. The analytic sample also fell slightly below the planning benchmark. The present design does not provide direct evidence about mechanisms or boundary conditions; testing them requires larger samples, temporally separated process measures, and direct measures of well-defined candidate moderators.

Finally, the findings pertain only to immediate self-reported PALG. Whether comparable materials would affect sexual-orientation prejudice more broadly, or prejudice toward other stigmatized groups, remains a question for future comparative research.

Conclusions

Participants assigned to a brief sexual-orientation-focused lesson combining sexual-orientation information with two selected critical-thinking elements reported lower immediate self-reported PALG than those assigned to a neutral-video control, after adjustment for baseline PALG and prior intergroup contact. By specifying the lesson content and using randomized allocation with baseline adjustment, the study provides an initial test of a domain-focused instructional format in a Chinese college sample. In an exploratory analysis, the adjusted condition difference varied across continuous baseline PALG probe values, but floor compression limits interpretation of this pattern. The result concerns the specific lesson, not critical thinking as a whole or the isolated contribution of analysis or synthesis. Because nonspecific features were not matched and the immediate self-report was vulnerable to demand and social desirability, the study cannot establish genuine attitude change or active ingredients. Content-matched controls, delayed follow-up, more diverse samples, and less demand-sensitive outcomes are needed before stronger conclusions are drawn.
