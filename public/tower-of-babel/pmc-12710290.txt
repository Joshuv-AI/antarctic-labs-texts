Comparison of Biparametric MRI and Multiparametric MRI Using Split Dynamic MRI in Prostate Cancer Diagnosis
Authors: Bo Kyung Kim, Dal Mo Yang, Geon-Ho Jahng, Hyun Cheol Kim, Sang Won Kim, Hyung-Lae Lee, Gyeong Eun Min, Kyu Yeoun Won, Hyeok Jung Kwon, Atle Bjørnerud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the diagnostic performance between biparametric MRI (bpMRI) and multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) using split dynamic MRI for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) with Prostate Imaging and Reporting Data System (PI-RADS) v2.1, using prostatectomy as the reference standard of pathologic analysis.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 77 PCa cases in 60 patients who underwent radical prostatectomy for biopsy-proven prostate cancer. The MR Images of the prostate were interpreted in consensus by two experienced radiologists (aged 3 and 35 years). We retrospectively reviewed the MRI images according to PI-RADS v2.1 and recorded the scores from T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging, and peak relaxation rate (R2*) for each lesion at three different time points: 1) bpMRI (T2WI, DWI, and ADC), 2) mpMRI1 (T2WI, DWI, ADC, and DCE), and 3) mpMRI2 (T2WI, DWI, ADC, DCE, and peak R2*). The diagnostic performances of bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 in detecting csPCa using PI-RADS V2.1 were evaluated. Additionally, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of DCE-MRI and peak R2* for csPCa detection.ResultsThe area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for diagnosing csPCa with PI-RADS v2.1 in all lesions and peripheral zone (PZ) lesions were not significantly different between the bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 protocols (p > 0.05). DCE-MRI was better than the peak R2* for diagnosing csPCa in PZ lesions (p < 0.001).ConclusionmpMRI using split dynamic MRI has diagnostic performance similar to that of bpMRI for detecting csPCa using PI-RADS v2.1.Keywords: Prostate, Prostate Cancer, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Biparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging (bpMRI), Multiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging (mpMRI), Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced (DCE) Imaging, Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC), Dynamic Susceptibility Contrast (DSC) Imaging, Split Dynamic Magnetic Resonance Imaging

INTRODUCTION

MRI is a well-established diagnostic tool for prostate cancer and is increasingly used before biopsy in patients with elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels (1). Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) is a crucial imaging technique for the detection of targeted biopsies, local staging, risk classification, and monitoring of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) (2,3,4,5,6). According to the Prostate Imaging and Reporting Data System (PI-RADS version 2.1 [v2.1]) in 2019, prostate mpMRI includes T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) in at least two of three orthogonal planes: axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging (7).

In the diagnosis of prostate cancer, DCE-MRI is highly sensitive, especially for peripheral lesions. In PI-RADS v2.1, a lesion with positive DCE-MRI results is classified as PI-RADS 4 when it demonstrates an equivocal appearance on DWI and ADC maps (PI-RADS 3) (7). However, a high false-positive rate may result from the enhancement effect of DCE-MRI in benign prostatic conditions such as prostatitis and fibrosis in the peripheral zone (PZ) (8). These results thus revealed that biparametric MRI (bpMRI) offered comparable diagnostic performance to mpMRI. Several studies have reported that the diagnostic accuracy of bpMRI for detecting csPCa is similar to that of mpMRI using PI-RADS v2 (9,10,11,12,13) and PI-RADS v2.1 (14,15,16).

An accurate diagnostic tool for qualitative evaluation of signal loss in T2*-weighted images is dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) MRI (17,18). Investigators have revealed that using DSC-MRI in conjunction with DCE-MRI may help distinguish malignant from benign breast lesions (19,20,21). The need for an additional contrast bolus to measure the susceptibility-induced signal loss using a dynamic T2/T2*-weighted sequence is the main limitation of this technique.

Recently, a split dynamic MRI technique was introduced (22,23,24) that alternately captures high spatial and temporal resolution data through DCE and DSC imaging sequences. Split-dynamic MRI allows both conventional T1-weighed DCE-MRI as well as DSC imaging in a single acquisition. This approach yielded both morphological and functional insights from a single-bolus injection. Furthermore, multi-echo DSC imaging was incorporated within the high temporal resolution series to facilitate quantitative measurements of the dynamic change in the transverse relaxation rate (R2*) during the passage of the contrast bolus. Although this innovative split-dynamic MRI method has been used for breast (22,23) and rectal cancers (24), its application in patients with prostate cancer remains unexplored. The peak R2* value may play a role in the diagnosis of prostate cancer.

This study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of bpMRI and mpMRI using split dynamic MRI for detecting csPCa with PI-RADS v2.1, using prostatectomy as the reference standard for pathological analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB No. 2023-04-020).

STUDY POPULATION

Between January 2020 and December 2021, a consecutive series of 69 patients who underwent radical prostatectomy for biopsy-proven prostate cancer was identified after searching the institutional database. The inclusion criteria of the patients were as follows: patients who had a csPCa histopathologically (tumor with a Gleason score ≥7 and and/or a volume ≥0.5 cm3 [tumor diameter ≥8 mm] and/or extraprostatic invasion) evaluated by a urologic pathologist with a 17-year experience and patients who had multiparametric MRI of the prostate. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who had non-csPCa histopathologically (tumor with a Gleason score <7 and/or a volume <0.5 cm3), did not undergo dual MRI, had inadequate MR images, and received hormonal therapy before MR examination.

The final study population comprised 60 patients with a mean age of 68 (43–78) years and a median PSA level of 7.6 (1.8–91.5) ng/mL. Non-csPCa was reported in nine patients (Table 1).

MRI PROTOCOL

A 3T MRI scanning system (Ingenia; Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands) equipped with a 16-channel abdominal surface array coil was utilized for imaging. The MR protocol included routine and specialized scans. The routine scan protocol included a T1W turbo spinecho (TSE) sequence in transverse orientation without fat suppression; a T2W TSE sequence in sagittal, transverse with fat suppression, and coronal orientations; and DWI with b-values of 0, 100, 1,000, and 1,500 s/mm2.

For the special scan, a split dynamic sequence was employed alternating between 3D T1-wighted high-resolution isotropic volume examination (3D THRIVE) for DCE imaging and 3D T2*-weighted imaging for DSC imaging. In the scanning order, one set of high spatial resolution baseline images was acquired using a 3D THRIVE sequence. This was followed by a high-temporal-resolution 3D multishot turbo field echo (TFE) echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence, resulting in seven baseline images prior to the administration of the contrast agent. High-temporal-resolution acquisition was repeated 63 times during the wash-in phase immediately after the first post-contrast 3D THRIVE acquisition. Throughout the intermediate and late postcontrast phases, five split sessions were conducted to acquire five EPI images and an interleaved THRIVE image. POST-PROCESSING OF SPLIT DYNAMIC SCANNING IMAGES

Using the multi-channel module of the nordicICE software package, images acquired from the split dynamic scan were processed (NordicNeuroLab, Bergen, Norway). The multi-channel module is designed for analyzing dynamic multi-echo data in T2*-weighted contrast MRI. First, TFE EPI images were registered for each participant. Second, within the TFE EPI images, the time points for baseline, time-to-maximum, and last scan were delineated using the R2* contrast option, which was based on the signal intensity curve derived from the specified volume of interest. At this step, changes in relaxation times as a function of contrast agent concentration was estimated using a contrast agent specific relaxation rate constant, as reported in the literature. This allowed for the estimation of the dynamic R2* changes. The accuracy of describing the dynamic change in R2* was measured by the ability to capture the first-pass peak. The resulting maps were then visualized with MRIcro software, employing an RGB color scheme.

HISTOPATHOLOGICAL ANALYSIS

For all 60 patients, the reference standard for tumor localization was determined through step-section histological analysis of radical prostatectomy specimens. For each patient, one genitourinary pathologist at our institution with >17 years of combined experience verified the histology and assigned the histology slides.

If a lesion extended into more than one pathological slice, the areas of the tumor foci on all slices were summed to obtain an estimate of the histopathological diameter of the entire lesion. Tumors that covered both the TZ and PZ were considered TZ tumors if more than 70% of the tumors were in the TZ, and all others were considered PZ tumors.

IMAGE EVALUATION

The MR Images of the prostate were interpreted in consensus by two experienced radiologists (aged 3 and 35 years). They were aware that the patients had PCa but were blinded to the histopathological results. They retrospectively reviewed the MRI images according to PI-RADS v2.1 and recorded the scores from T2WI, DWI, DCE, and peak R2* for each lesion at three different time points: 1) bpMRI (T2WI, DWI, and ADC), 2) mpMRI1 (T2WI, DWI, ADC, and DCE), and 3) mpMRI2 (T2WI, DWI, ADC, DCE, and peak R2*). Readers were blinded to the clinical, laboratory, and histopathological results. The readers interpreted the datasets, waiting 4 weeks between interpretations of each dataset. Peripheral lesions with PI-RADS category 3 on bpMRI were upgraded to PI-RADS category 4 when early enhancement was noted on DCE and/or peak R2*. In controversial cases, the two radiologists jointly negotiated and reached an agreement on the bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 scores.

Using Picture Archiving and Communication System software, the tumor diameter was measured in consensus by two radiologists. After detection and measurement, the tumor site was considered to match the histological findings if the tumor depicted on the image was present in the same region of the prostate, as indicated in the prostatectomy specimen. A size ≥8 mm for correlation between histology and MRI was considered.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Using the pathological results as the reference standard, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMR2 in all lesions and PZ lesions were plotted. A comparison of the area under the curve (AUC) obtained from the ROC analysis using the Delong test was conducted between bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 in all lesions and PZ lesions. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and 95% confidence interval were calculated to evaluate the diagnostic performance. For accuracy in detecting PCa, PI-RADS v2.1 category 1–3 were considered negative, whereas PI-RADS v2.1 category 4–5 were considered positive.

Data analysis was performed using MedCalc (version 15.2.2; MedCalc Software Ltd., MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05.

RESULTS

CLINICOPATHOLOGICAL DATA

In this study, 77 csPCa cases in 60 patients were analyzed, including one lesion in 44 patients, two lesions in 15 patients, and three lesions in one patient. Based on the histopathological results, 13/77 patients (16.9%) had Gleason 3 + 3, 46/77 (59.7%) had Gleason 3 + 4, 13/77 patients (16.9%) had Gleason 4 + 3, 3/77 patients (3.9%) had Gleason 8, and 2/77 patients (2.6%) had Gleason nine tumors (Table 1). Of the 77 csPCa cases, 43 (55.9%) originated from the PZ, and 34 (44.1%) originated from the transition zone.

COMPARISON OF THE DIAGNOSTIC PERFORMANCES OF bpMRI, mpMRI1, AND mpMRI2

The AUCs for the bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 protocols for diagnosing csPCa in all lesions were 0.663 (0.559–0.757), 0.674 (0.570–0.767), and 0.674 (0.570–0.767), respectively, and the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 3, Fig. 1). The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy of bpMRI for diagnosing csPCa were 63.6%, 55.6%, 86.0%, 26.3%, and 62.1%, respectively, whereas those of mpMRI1 and mpMRI2 were 67.5%, 55.6%, 86.7%, 28.6%, and 65.3%, respectively. The AUCs, sensitivity, and specificity for diagnosing csPCa in all lesions were not significantly different between the bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 protocols (p > 0.05) (Table 3, Figs. 1, 2, 3).

DISCUSSION

In our study, we explored the value of mpMRI using split dynamic MRI compared with that of bpMRI in the detection of csPCa in patients who underwent radical prostatectomy. Split-dynamic MRI can simultaneously acquire DCE and DSC images with a single administration of a gadolinium contrast medium. In our results, compared to the histological findings, no statistically significant difference was found between bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 for the detection of csPca with PI-RADS v2.1 in all lesions and PZ lesions.

Currently, one of the most commonly used perfusion techniques for tumor evaluation is the DSC-MRI. Technically, it is performed using an intravenous bolus injection of a paramagnetic contrast agent that changes the magnetic susceptibility in and around the microvasculature perfused with the contrast agent. The transient change in magnetic susceptibility causes shortening of T2 or T2*, thereby decreasing the MR signal of the tissue (25,26). The extent of signal decrease depends on the local concentration of the contrast agent. DCE-MRI is fundamentally different from DSC-MRI in that it assesses T1 signal shortening following the administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (23). DCE-MRI assesses perfusion and permeability. Contrast agents in the vessels and extracellular space shorten T1 relaxation times, leading to signal brightening (27).

We hypothesized that the detection rate of prostate cancer could be increased by combining DSC-MRI and DCE-MRI. This approach allows for the simultaneous acquisition of both T1-shortening and T2-shortening images following the administration of a contrast agent. Peak R2*, a key parameter of DSC-MRI, directly reflects perfusion and may provide unique information regarding tumor hemodynamics, particularly perfusion not obtainable with conventional T1-weighted DCE-MRI (22). Additionally, malignant lesions show a strong increase in R2* values, whereas benign lesions show little to no R2* enhancement (22). However, in our study, despite adding the Peak R2*, no significant difference was observed in the detection of csPCa using PI-RADS v2.1. Interestingly, DCE imaging was better than the peak R2* for the diagnosis of csPCa. On DSC-MRI, a larger susceptibility effect with significant signal loss due to blood products or calcification may affect the value of the data, such as the R2* value and the calculated hemodynamic parameters (25). We believe that these results may have been influenced by blood products from a previous prostate tissue biopsy prior to MRI examination.

DCE-MRI in PI-RADS v2.1 is to serve as a modifier to upgrade PZ lesions from PI-RADS category 3 to 4 in the presence of early focal enhancement (7). In our study, for mpMRI of PZ lesions, PI-RADS assessment category 3 was upgraded to category 4 in three cases (30%, 3/10) by applying mpMRI. However, no statistically significant difference was found among bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 for the detection of Pca with PI-RADS v2.1 in all lesions and PZ lesions because of the small number of upgraded cases. Cases with a DWI score of 3 to PI-RADS 4 given a positive DCE-MRI result did not have a positive impact on the diagnostic performance of MRI for csPCa detection owing to the high frequency of false-positive results (28). The high false-positive rate of DCE-MRI for prostatitis and fibrosis may be the cause of its low accuracy in detecting csPCa. In our study, although there were no false-positive cases in positive DCE-MRI upgraded to PI-RADS 4, there was no statistically significant difference between bpMRI, mpMRI1, and mpMRI2 for the detection of csPca with PI-RADS v2.1 in all lesions and PZ lesions.

Several studies using PI-RADS v2.1 have reported that the diagnostic accuracy of bpMRI for detecting csPCa is similar to that of mpMRI (14,15,16). Compared with mpMRI, bpMRI without DCE-MRI has potential benefits, including the elimination of side effects such as nephrogenic systemic fibrosis due to the administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents, reduction in the time required for MRI, and improvement of cost-effectiveness for patients (7,10,16).

However, DCE-MRI has been reported to improve the sensitivity of mpMRI for diagnosing prostate cancer (29,30). Furthermore, it serves as a safety net or backup sequence when T2WI and DWI are degraded by artifacts or an inadequate signal-to-noise ratio (7). DCE-MRI remains essential for patients with suspected local recurrence after prior treatment, such as radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy, for whom the current PI-RADS assessment criteria do not apply (7). The indications for DCE-MRI in PI-RADS 2.1 are determining PI-RADS 3 lesions that include csPCa, assist in analyzing MRIs with inadequate diagnostic quality on T2WI and DWI sequences, and aid radiologists with limited expertise in interpreting prostate MRI (31). DCE-MRI has many clinical applications and requires multicenter research to determine the expected value of PI-RADS v2.1.

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with a single-center design and a relatively small number of patients. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these results. Second, in our study, we believe that in most patients, MRI conducted after confirmation of prostate cancer through prostate tissue biopsy may have been affected by blood products from the tissue biopsy, particularly the Peak R2*. Third, this study relied solely on the agreement between the two radiologists, without calculating inter-rater variability as a potential source of bias. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the utility of mpMRI combined with split-dynamic MRI for detecting csPCa before prostate tissue biopsy.

In conclusion, mpMRI using split dynamic MRI has diagnostic performance similar to that of bpMRI for detecting csPCa using PI-RADS v2.1. Additionally, there was no difference in csPCa detection even with the addition of peak R2* to DCE-MRI.
