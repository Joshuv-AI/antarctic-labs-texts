National trends in the prevalence of depression and suicidal ideation stratified by academic achievement among adolescents, 2006 to 2022 in South Korea
Authors: Jinyoung Jeong, Jaeyu Park, Jaehyeong Cho, Hyesu Jo, Jeongseon Oh, Hyunjee Kim, Jiyeon Oh, Sooji Lee, Jiseung Kang, Hanseul Cho, Lee Smith, Ho Geol Woo, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although severe depression and suicidal ideation among adolescents could be driven by excessive academic competition, research on this problem is still insufficient. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the relationship between academic achievement, depression, and suicidal ideation based on large-scale data. This study utilized data from the nationwide Korea Youth Risk Behavior Web-based Survey conducted from 2006 to 2022, investigating a total of 1,138,802 adolescents. Weighted proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and crude numbers and percentages were utilized to analyze and present the results in various formats. To accurately assess the association of academic achievement with depression and suicidal ideation, weighted odds ratios and their 95% CIs were calculated using weighted logistic regression analysis. Compared to students with high academic achievement, those with moderate achievement showed a 1.12 (95% CI = 1.11–1.13) times higher risk of depression, while those with low achievement had a 1.54 (1.52–1.56) times higher risk. Similarly, students with low academic achievement had a 1.49 (95% CI = 1.47–1.51) times higher risk of suicidal ideation. The prevalence of both depression and suicidal ideation decreased before the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic but steadily increased during the pandemic. Females experienced higher rates of depression in each academic achievement group (high: weighted odds ratio = 1.49 [95% CI = 1.46–1.52]; middle: 1.59 [1.56–1.63]; low: 1.72 [1.69–1.75]) and suicidal ideation (high: 1.60 [1.57–1.64]; middle: 1.63 [1.59–1.68]; low: 1.82 [1.78–1.86]) than males. This study suggests that academic achievement can be associated with depression and suicidal ideation. Therefore, implementing mental health and educational programs targeting students with lower academic achievement may be crucial.

1. Introduction

Modern society is characterized by various forms of competition, which have contributed to its advancement. However, when competition becomes excessively intense, it can adversely influence mental health and threaten social stability.[1] Therefore, appropriately managing competition is a critical responsibility of society. In particular, competition during adolescence significantly influences mental health, including self-esteem, stress, and emotional issues, as well as social development.[2] Mental health problems in adolescence can persist into adulthood, leading to damage in various critical psychosocial areas, which may result in more severe negative outcomes than physiological issues encountered in adulthood.[3,4] Moreover, adolescent depression and suicidal ideation have been increasing each year, with the risks further heightened due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.[4] Despite this alarming trend, there has been insufficient research on the association between academic achievement, the most common form of competition faced by adolescents, and mental health.

Previous studies have suggested a bidirectional relationship between academic achievement and depression, with depressive symptoms predicting subsequent declines in academic achievement and lower academic achievement being associated with subsequent depressive symptoms.[5,6] However, previous studies were based on relatively limited sample sizes and did not examine long-term temporal trends in the association between academic achievement and depression. Additionally, although a previous study reported an association between academic performance and suicidal ideation, it was conducted among university students, and evidence regarding this relationship in adolescents remains limited.[7]

Therefore, this research aims to fill this gap by analyzing the associations between academic achievement and depression, as well as between academic achievement and suicidal ideation, among a large sample of Korean adolescents from 2006 to 2022. This study also analyzed the influence of various risk factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, on adolescent mental health. The ultimate goal of our study is to contribute to helping adolescents maintain better mental health.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient selection and data collection

This study analyzed data from the nationwide Korea Youth Risk Behavior Web-based Survey (KYRBS) to investigate the association between academic achievement, depression, and suicidal ideation among adolescents. This study is based on annual surveys conducted over 17 years from 2006 to 2022, with a total sample of 1,138,802 adolescents. Although the KYRBS was first established in 2005, the survey items relevant to the present study, including academic achievement, household income, and parental educational attainment, have been consistently collected since 2006. We therefore analyzed data from 2006 to 2022 to ensure comparability across all survey years and to capture long-term trends spanning both the pre-pandemic and COVID-19 pandemic periods. The KYRBS dataset, jointly administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency and the Ministry of Education, covers students aged 13 to 18 years from 800 schools across South Korea.[8] The data collection was based on voluntary, anonymous online questionnaires with an average response rate exceeding 95%.[8] The KYRBS data were anonymous. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (2014-06EXP-02-P-A). All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2. Ascertainment of academic achievement, depression, and suicidal ideation

This study aimed to examine the association of academic achievement with depression and suicidal ideations among adolescents. Three key terms were defined to address the objective of this study: academic achievement, depression, and suicidal ideation. In this study, academic achievement was classified into 3 groups: high, middle, and low. The classification criteria were as follows: participants were asked, “How would you rate your academic performance over the past 12 months?” Those who responded “high” or “middle-high” were categorized into the high group; those who answered “middle” were placed in the middle group; and those who responded “middle-low” or “low” were classified into the low group.[9] In this study, depression refers to participants who responded affirmatively to the question, “In the past 12 months, have you felt sad or hopeless for 2 consecutive weeks to the extent that you stopped your usual activities?”[9] Suicidal ideation was defined based on the question, “In the past 12 months, have you seriously considered suicide?” Participants who answered affirmatively were classified as having had suicidal thoughts within the past 12 months.[10] In addition, this study considered the COVID-19 pandemic to have begun in 2020. This is based on the fact that the first confirmed case of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection in South Korea was identified in January 2020, and the World Health Organization declared a pandemic in March 2020.[11]

2.3. Covariates

This study analyzed the following 9 covariates: sex, age, grade level (7th–9th grade: middle school; 10th–12th grade: high school), household income level (high, high-middle, middle, middle-low, and low), residential area (urban or rural), alcohol consumption within 1 month before the survey, smoking status within 1 month before the survey, and parental educational attainment (high school diploma or less and bachelor’s degree or higher). These variables were selected based on their potential relevance to the study, as identified in previous research.[12]

2.4. Statistical analyses

Weighted proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and crude numbers and percentages were utilized to analyze and present the results in various formats. Initially, the overall characteristics of the study population were described using unweighted crude results in terms of frequencies and percentages. Furthermore, the study period was divided into 2 distinct phases to account for the unique period of the COVID-19 pandemic.[12] To accurately assess the association of academic achievement with depression and suicidal ideation, weighted odds ratios (wORs) and their 95% CIs were calculated using weighted logistic regression.[13] Trends in depression and suicidal ideation based on academic achievement were examined using weighted percentages and 95% CIs derived from weighted composite sample analyses provided by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency.[13] In addition, to identify trends and changes before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, binomial and linear logistic regression models were employed to estimate 95% CIs for the β coefficients and the β difference (βdiff).[14] Moreover, the influence of various factors on depression and suicidal ideation, both before and after the pandemic, was assessed by calculating wORs for each risk factor.[15] All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute), and a two-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

This study analyzed a total of 1,138,802 adolescents (587,254 [51.57%] males) from 2006 to 2022, including the COVID-19 period. Among them, 418,440 (36.74%) individuals had low academic achievement, 319,481 (28.05%) individuals had middle academic achievement, and 400,881 (35.20%) individuals had high academic achievement (Tables 1 and S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). The analysis of the association between academic achievement and depression showed that, compared to the group with high academic achievement, students with moderate academic achievement had a 1.12 (95% CI = 1.11–1.13) times higher risk of depression, and students with low academic achievement had a 1.54 (1.52–1.56) times higher risk. This indicates that lower academic achievement is associated with a higher risk of depression. Similarly, in the case of suicidal ideation, the risk increased as academic achievement decreased, with students in the low achievement group having a 1.49 (95% CI = 1.47–1.51) times higher risk (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Similar results were obtained from the analysis using weighted values. Adolescents with lower academic achievement experienced higher levels of depression (high: 26.70% [95% CI = 26.50–26.89]; middle: 28.88% [28.66–29.10]; low: 36.06% [35.84–36.28]). In addition, the prevalence of depression decreased before the COVID-19 pandemic (high: β = −3.64 [95% CI = −3.79 to −3.50]; middle: −3.83 [−4.00 to −3.66]; low: −3.86 [−4.03 to −3.69]) but increased during the COVID-19 pandemic (high: 0.44 [0.16–0.73]; middle: 0.37 [0.04–0.70]; low: 0.67 [0.32–1.01]). Lower academic achievement was associated with a higher risk of suicidal ideation (high: 13.72% [95% CI = 13.57–13.87]; middle: 13.73% [13.57–13.89]; low: 19.12% [18.94–19.30]). In addition, the number of individuals experiencing suicidal ideation decreased before the COVID-19 pandemic (high: β = −2.29 [95% CI = −2.40 to −2.17]; middle: −2.34 [−2.48 to −2.21]; low: −2.88 [−3.02 to −2.73]) but increased during the COVID-19 pandemic (high: 0.65 [0.42–0.87]; middle: 0.28 [0.05–0.52]; low: 0.49 [0.22–0.77]; Tables 3 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2).

Furthermore, an analysis of the changes in depression risk based on academic achievement across several variables suggested that both males (high: 22.94% [95% CI = 22.69–23.19]; middle: 24.15% [23.87–24.44]; low: 30.03% [29.75–30.32]) and females (high: 30.99% [30.71–31.28], middle: 33.76% [33.44–34.09]; low: 42.67% [42.35–42.98]) showed an increasing trend of depression with lower academic achievement. However, females experienced higher levels of depression and exhibited a greater difference according to academic achievement. Furthermore, individuals with a higher grade level, lower household income, recent alcohol consumption, or smoking status were found to experience higher levels of depression. A similar analysis of suicidal ideation suggested that both males (high: 11.01% [95% CI = 10.83–11.19]; middle: 10.81% [10.61–11.01]; low: 14.64% [14.43–14.85]) and females (high: 16.81% [16.59–17.04]; middle: 16.74% [16.48–17.00]; low: 24.02% [23.75–24.30]) showed an increasing trend in suicidal ideation with lower academic achievement. However, females experienced higher levels of suicidal ideation and exhibited a greater difference based on academic achievement. Additionally, it was found that individuals with lower household income levels, those living in urban areas, recent alcohol consumers, and smokers experienced higher levels of suicidal ideation (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3).

An analysis of the risk factors for depression showed that the risk of depression was higher among females (high: wOR = 1.49 [95% CI = 1.46–1.52]; middle: 1.59 [1.56–1.63]; low: 1.72 [1.69–1.75]), individuals with a high school education (high: 1.29 [1.27–1.32]; middle: 1.21 [1.18–1.23]; low: 1.11 [1.09–1.13]), those with lower household income levels (high: 2.27 [2.15–2.39]; middle: 2.48 [2.32–2.66]; low: 2.04 [1.95–2.14]), alcohol consumers (high: 2.06 [2.01–2.10]; middle: 1.93 [1.88–1.97]; low: 1.86 [1.83–1.90]), and smokers (high: 2.34 [2.26–2.42]; middle: 1.94 [1.88–2.01]; low: 1.78 [1.74–1.82]). In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic particularly increased the risk of depression in females and individuals with a smoking status. Similarly, for suicidal ideation, the elevated risk was observed among females (high: wOR = 1.60 [95% CI = 1.57–1.64]; middle: 1.63 [1.59–1.68]; low: 1.82 [1.78–1.86]), those with lower household income (high: 2.43 [2.29–2.58]; middle: 2.96 [2.73–3.20]; low: 2.33 [2.21–2.45]), alcohol consumers (high: 1.92 [1.87–1.97]; middle: 1.88 [1.83–1.93]; low: 1.76 [1.73–1.80]), and smokers (high: 2.35 [2.26–2.44]; middle: 1.97 [1.89–2.05]; low: 1.81 [1.76–1.85]). Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of suicidal ideation in females, individuals with lower household income, recent alcohol consumers, and smokers (Table 4).

4. Discussion

4.1. Key finding

This study is the first to analyze the association of academic achievement with depression and suicidal ideations from 2006 to 2022, including the COVID-19 pandemic, considering multiple risk factors among the 1,138,802 population. As academic achievement decreases, levels of depression and suicidal ideation increase. This finding implies that academic performance may be associated with both depression and suicidal ideations. The risk of depression and suicidal ideation was higher among females, individuals with a high school education, alcohol consumers, and smokers. The prevalence of both depression and suicidal ideation decreased before the COVID-19 pandemic but steadily increased during the pandemic. This suggests that the risk of depression and suicidal ideation among adolescents is currently growing and that COVID-19 has an adverse influence on the emotional well-being of adolescents. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated the risk of depression among females and individuals with a smoking status. Similarly, with regard to suicidal ideations, the risk increased for females and smokers due to COVID-19, and individuals from low-income households and recent alcohol consumers also faced a higher risk.

4.2. Plausible underlying mechanisms

The results of this study indicate that lower academic achievement is associated with higher levels of depression. The increased occurrence of depression among individuals with lower academic achievement can be explained by various models, including the failure model, the competency-based model, and the life stress model.[16] Among these, the competency-based model provides the most compelling explanation for this phenomenon, as individuals who fail to develop skills in social, academic, and behavioral domains are more likely to receive negative feedback, which can trigger depression.[17] Furthermore, this phenomenon can also be explained by the academic incompetence hypothesis. When students experience academic failure or receive poor grades, negative self-perceptions increase, which can lead to a rise in depressive symptoms.[18]

This study also found that lower academic achievement is associated with higher levels of suicidal ideation. Students with lower academic achievement tend to experience lower self-esteem and may feel like a burden to their families or schools, leading to a sense of loss of belonging.[19] This loss of belonging and the feeling of being a burden can ultimately lead to suicidal ideation.[18]

A wide range of factors may have contributed to the overall and continuous increase in depression and suicidal ideation during the COVID-19 pandemic. Low social support,[20] high physical and mental exhaustion,[21] quarantine, loneliness, and mental health difficulties[22] can be seen as contributing factors to the increase in depression and suicidal ideation during the COVID-19 pandemic.[23] Therefore, the COVID-19 pandemic has had an additional adverse influence on mental health, further exacerbating the issues of depression and suicidal ideation caused by low academic achievement.

4.3. Comparison of previous studies

Previous studies have suggested a link between academic achievement and depression.[5,6,18,24] However, research in this area has been notably limited, with existing studies being either outdated or based on relatively small sample sizes, which has hindered a clear understanding of the relationship between academic achievement and depression.[5,6] In addition, some studies have represented academic achievement as a binary variable, making it difficult to comprehensively assess its connection to depression.[5] Furthermore, certain research focused exclusively on individuals in early childhood, specifically during primary school, and therefore did not explore the relationship between academic performance and depression during adolescence.[18]

Several studies have suggested an association between suicidal ideation and academic achievement.[7,25] However, most studies were conducted exclusively on university students, which limited assessment of the relationship between academic achievement and suicidal ideation during adolescence.[7,25] Both previous studies were limited by small sample sizes, which constrained causal inference. By contrast, the present study used data through 2022, encompassing the COVID-19 pandemic, and thereby enabled assessment of pandemic-related changes and recent trends. The large adolescent population improved the precision and robustness of the estimates, while adjustment for a broad range of covariates allowed assessment of multiple potential risk factors.

4.4. Clinical and policy implications

The results of this study indicate that the number of adolescents experiencing depression and suicidal ideation is increasing, and the severity of these issues is escalating. Depression and suicidal ideation during adolescence can persist into adulthood, making the issue particularly concerning.[26] Furthermore, it was found that students with lower academic achievement are more likely to experience depression and suicidal ideation during adolescence. Therefore, the implementation of school-based mental health screening and early intervention programs is necessary. Annual mental health screenings should be conducted in middle and high schools, specifically targeting students with lower academic achievement. These screenings would assess the mental health status of adolescents, and immediate counseling services should be provided to students exhibiting signs of depression or suicidal thoughts.

In addition, to reduce academic competition, it is necessary to strengthen process-oriented assessments rather than focusing solely on academic achievement and to introduce collaborative learning campaigns that promote cooperation among students.[27] These policies will help students view education not as mere competition but as a learning process. This shift will allow students to focus on learning itself rather than feeling pressured by grades, thereby reducing stress and suicidal ideations related to academic achievement.[28] Through collaborative learning, students can develop supportive relationships by helping one another with their studies, which can provide emotional stability and positively impact their mental health.[29]

Furthermore, although the rates of depression and suicidal ideation are steadily increasing, the rate of seeking treatment remains low, largely due to the stigma associated with mental health disorders.[30] Therefore, awareness campaigns aimed at reducing stigma and improving access to treatment for those experiencing depression and suicidal ideations should be expanded.

4.5. Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to examine the relationship between academic achievement, depression, and suicidal ideation over a long period, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a large sample. Additionally, it identifies the associations by utilizing various risk factors. However, despite these strengths, there are several limitations.

First, this study conducted the analysis using data based on self-reports. Therefore, some responses may not accurately reflect actual circumstances, which may lead to bias. However, as the study focuses on subjective perceptions such as academic achievement, depression, and suicidal thoughts, self-reported data may be more appropriate. Self-reported data allows for an understanding of students’ actual experiences of depression and suicidal thoughts, thereby enabling a more effective examination of the relationship between their psychological burden and subjective academic achievement.[9] Second, this study utilized data from Korean students, which may limit its applicability to other countries. However, the previous studies referenced in this research include those from various countries, such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and China, exhibiting similar trends despite cultural differences.[5,17,18] In addition, according to the World Health Organization, adolescent suicide and depression are global issues. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude that this study is limited to South Korea alone. Third, the cross-sectional design precluded assessment of causality or temporal direction. Although our interpretation was informed by previous studies, reverse causation remains possible, whereby depression and suicidal ideation adversely affect academic achievement. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and to assess potential mediators, including self-esteem, academic pressure, and peer relationships.

5. Conclusion

This study is the first to analyze the long-term association of academic achievement with depression and suicidal ideation in a large population over an extended period from 2006 to 2022. The prevalence of both depression and suicidal ideation decreased before the COVID-19 pandemic but steadily increased during the pandemic. Adolescents with lower academic achievement showed higher rates of depression and suicidal ideation, suggesting that academic performance may influence both conditions. The risk of depression and suicidal ideation was higher among females, individuals with a high school education, alcohol consumers, and smokers. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic worsened the risk of depression among females and smokers and further heightened the risk of suicidal ideation in females, those with lower household incomes, recent alcohol consumers, and smokers. Therefore, this study highlights the need to implement mental health and educational programs targeting students with lower academic achievement and expand awareness-raising initiatives.
