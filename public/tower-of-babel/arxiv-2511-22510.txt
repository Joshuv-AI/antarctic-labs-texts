Efficient Pseudo-spectral Algorithms for Statistical Field Theories
Martin Kjøllesdal Johnsrud, Navdeep Rana

Abstract
We present stochastic variants of the exponential time differencing schemes for stiff stochastic differential equations. We derive three explicit schemes that offer better stability compared to Euler-Maruyama and Milstein’s method, and achieve strong convergence up to order 𝒪 ⁡ ( h ) \mathcal{O}({h}) in the time step h h . We combine these schemes with a pseudo-spectral approach to outline efficient algorithms for simulating stochastic field theories with additive noise. To illustrate the effectiveness of this approach, we study several systems in and out of equilibrium, including Model A, Model B, the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation, and the Complex Ginzburg-Landau equation. We outline procedures for computing physical observables such as the critical exponents, correlation functions, and dynamic linear response, and provide our implementation as open source code.



I
Introduction

Stochastic field theories (SFT) are a cornerstone of statistical physics, and are widely used to study collective behaviour in systems in and out of equilibrium
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
;
Kardar et al. 1986
;
Kardar 2007a
;
Zinn-Justin 1989
;
Täuber 2014
.
The notion of
effective field theories
, building on the insights from the renormalization group
Wilson 1971
, implies that the large scale behaviour of many interacting degrees of freedom is well described by field theories with a limited number of terms
Weinberg 1979
.
This approach has been successfully applied to systems which are in, close to, or relax towards, equilibrium
Wilson and Fisher 1972
;
Forster et al. 1977a
.
When the system relaxes towards equilibrium, we have access to the steady state distribution given by the Boltzmann factor, which allows for both well developed theoretical approaches and efficient numerical methods such as the Monte-Carlo approach
Binder 1995
.
In recent years, non-equilibrium systems have been under intense investigations
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Young et al. 2020
;
Young et al. 2024
;
Zelle et al. 2024
;
Diessel et al. 2025
.
A broad class of examples are the field theories for active matter, where the individuals consume energy at the microscopic scales, resulting in inherently dynamical steady state
Toner and Tu 1995
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Ramaswamy 2010
;
Tiribocchi et al. 2015
;
Stenhammar et al. 2013
;
Doostmohammadi et al. 2018
;
Nardini et al. 2017
;
Cates 2019
;
Saha et al. 2020
;
Johnsrud and Golestanian 2025a
;
Mahdisoltani et al. 2021
.
Out of equilibrium systems lack the universal Boltzmann description and the analytical tools of (near-)equilibrium statistical physics are in general not applicable. Extensive direct numerical simulations thus are crucial to study such systems, which emphasises the importance of accurate, fast, and scalable numerical algorithms to investigate SFTs.

A crucial property of effective field theories is their locality—the equation of motion for each point in space and time is well described by the value of the field, and its lowest order derivatives, at that point
Weinberg 1979
.
Fourier space, therefore, provides a natural description of statistical field theories, both for analytical approaches such as the renormalization group
Wilson and Fisher 1972
and numerical methods.
Pseudo-spectral algorithms are an excellent candidate for efficient and precise numerical integration of SFTs.
However, in Fourier space, SFTs become stiff
Cox and Matthews 2002
; the timescales at which various Fourier modes evolve become smaller and smaller as the wave number grows.
For example, for Model A (see
Section

V.1
), the Fourier amplitude of the wave number
\bm{k}
has an exponential decay whose timescale is proportional to
1/k^{2}
. For Model B the timescale is proportional to
1/k^{4}
, which exacerbates the stiffness.
Numerically integrating spatially well-resolved systems using simple, explicit methods, such as the Euler-Maruyama (EM) method would then require extremely small time steps.
Implicit integration methods require inversions of the matrices that typically scale quadratically with the number of spatial degrees of freedom, or multiple iterative Newton method solves per update.
While this is feasible for smaller system sizes, it becomes prohibitively expensive when the number of degrees of freedom are large.
Implicit methods are therefore infeasible for investigating the long time, large wavelength hydrodynamics, for example, near critical points and phase transitions.

This motivates the search for fast and stable explicit methods.
For deterministic PDEs, Exponential Time Differencing (ETD) provide explicit numerical integration schemes that alleviate the stiffness issues and provide better stability and accuracy for larger time steps
Cox and Matthews 2002
;
Kassam and Trefethen 2006
.
The stiff terms are handled exactly, maintaining accuracy and avoiding the numerical damping introduced by implicit methods.
These schemes have been used in numerical integration of a broad class of deterministic partial differential equations
Bratanov et al. 2015
;
Rana and Perlekar 2022
;
Rana et al. 2024
;
Jain et al. 2024
;
Pandey et al. 2020
;
Padhan et al. 2024
.
Recent approaches use mixed implicit-explicit methods
Zwicker 2020
;
Caballero 2024
, but to our knowledge, there are no fully explicit schemes for stiff stochastic PDEs.

In this paper, we present generalizations of the ETD schemes, the Stochastic Exponential Time Differencing (SETD) scheme, to numerically integrate stiff stochastic differential equations (SDEs).
In
Section

II
, we derive SETD schemes for a generic stiff SDE and illustrate it’s implementation for two examples SDEs,
viz.
, the Stochastic Anharmonic Oscillator, and the Geometric Brownian Motion.
We discuss the convergence and stability of these schemes in
Section

III
, and illustrates their advantages compared to the Euler-Maruyama and the Milstein methods
Kloeden and Platen 1999
;
Higham 2001
.
In
Section

IV
, we outline how to efficiently apply the SETD schemes to numerically integrate SFTs with additive noise, as well as describe practical details on how to compute various observables of interest, such as correlations and Susceptibility.
Finally, in
Section

V
, we show how to apply the SETD scheme to
we apply the proposed algorithms for various equilibrium and out of equilibrium SFTs, namely Model A and Model B
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
, the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation
Kardar et al. 1986
, and the Complex Ginzburg-Landau (CGL) equation
Aranson and Kramer 2002
.

II
Stochastic Exponential Time Differencing Schemes

We begin with the stochastic ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=cu(t)+F(u(t),t)+G(u(t),t)\eta(t),

(1)

which defines a stochastic trajectory
u(t)
.
The functions
F(u(t),t)
and
G(u(t),t)
depend on the trajectory
u
at time
t
, and possibly explicitly on the time
t
itself.
Here,
\eta(t)
is a Gaussian white noise that satisfies

\displaystyle\left<\eta(t)\right>=0,~\left<\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\right>=\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(2)

and we assume the Itô convention.
For large
|c|
, the timescale
\mathcal{O}(1/|c|)
at which the
u(t)
evolves is very small, rendering the equation
stiff
.
This can results in numerical schemes blowing up.
To capture this dynamics with explicit Euler-like methods will require very small time steps
Cox and Matthews 2002
. This problem is further exacerbated for SFTs, where
|c|
increases quickly with the wavenumber (see
Section

V
for examples).

We begin our derivation by rewriting the equation as a infinitesimal increment in
u
,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(t)=cu(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+F(u,t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+G(u,t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t),

(3)

where
W(t)
is the standard Wiener process
Gardiner 2002
;
Van Kampen 1976
;
Kloeden and Platen 1999
. Multiplying by
e^{-cs}

and integrating between
[t,t+{h}]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left[u(s)e^{-cs}\right]=\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}F(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}G(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s).

(4)

Integrating the left hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle u(t+{h})=e^{c{h}}u(t)+I_{D}(u,t,h)+I_{S}(u,t,h)

(5)

where

\displaystyle I_{D}(u,t,h)

\displaystyle=e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}F(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\text{~and~}

(6)

\displaystyle I_{S}(u,t,h)

\displaystyle=e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}G(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)

(7)

are the deterministic and the stochastic integral, respectively. Equation (
5
) is exact and is the starting point to derive SETD schemes of different order. Here, we will derive schemes that have a strong convergence of
\mathcal{O}({h})
. Using Itô’s lemma, for a general
H(u(t),t)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H(u,t)=\left[\partial_{t}H(u,t)+F(u,t)\partial_{u}H(u,t)+\frac{1}{2}G(u,t)^{2}\partial_{u}^{2}H(u,t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+G(u,t)\partial_{u}H(u,t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t).

(8)

Integration between
[t,s]
yields

\displaystyle H(u,s)=H(u,t)+\int_{t}^{s}\left[\partial_{z}H(u,z)+F(u,z)\partial_{u}H(u,z)+\frac{1}{2}G(u,z)^{2}\partial_{u}^{2}H(u,z)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z+\int_{t}^{s}G(u,z)\partial_{u}H(u,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(z),

(9)

which is used to approximate
F(u,s)
and
G(u,s)
in (
5
) to obtain various numerical approximations of (
1
). In the deterministic integral

\displaystyle I_{D}(u,t,h)=e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}F(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

(10)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
is
\mathcal{O}({h})
, thus we require only an
\mathcal{O}({h}^{0})
approximation of
F(u,s)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F(u,s)\simeq F(u,t)+\mathcal{O}({h}^{1/2}),

(11)

which after integration yields

\displaystyle I_{D}(u,t,h)=\frac{e^{c{h}}-1}{c}F(u,t)+\mathcal{O}(h^{3/2}),

(12)

which is the ETD1 scheme in the absence of the stochastic term
Cox and Matthews 2002
. Since
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)
is
\mathcal{O}({h}^{1/2})
, for
G(u,s)

we need to keep the terms up to order
\mathcal{O}({h}^{1/2})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle G(u,s)\simeq G(u,t)+G(u,t)\partial_{u}G(u,t)\int_{t}^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(z)+\mathcal{O}({h}).

(13)

Plugging in the stochastic integral
I_{S}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{S}(u,t,h)

\displaystyle=e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}G(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)

\displaystyle=e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}\left[G(u,t)+G(u,t)\partial_{u}G(u,t)\int_{t}^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(z)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)

\displaystyle=G(u,t)I_{1}(t,h)+G(u,t)\partial_{u}G(u,t)\left[I_{2}(t,h)-I_{1}(t,h)W(t)\right]+\mathcal{O}({h}^{3/2}),

(14)

where we now need to evaluate the following two integrals

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,h)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s),

(15)

\displaystyle I_{2}(t,h)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{c(t+{h})}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-cs}W(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s).

(16)

We can evaluate these integrals exactly using the methods of Stochastic calculus, or, for numerical integration, we can compute approximations that are correct up to certain order in the time step.
Consider first the integral
I_{1}
, which is a stochastic integral of a deterministic function
Gardiner 2002
. It can be evaluated exactly as

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,h)

\displaystyle=e^{c(t+{h})}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\int_{t}^{t+{h}}e^{-2cs}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2c{h}}-1}{2c}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(17)

We can make approximations for
I_{1}
by evaluating the integrand at the left point, i.e.
e^{-cs}\simeq e^{-ct}
which gives

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,h)\simeq I_{1,l}(t,h)=e^{c{h}}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)=e^{c{h}}\left[W(t+{h})-W(t)\right]=e^{c{h}}{\Delta W}.

(18)

Comparing the statistical properties of the exact integral and the approximation, we find that mean vanishes for both,
whereas the variances are

\displaystyle\left<I_{1}^{2}(h)\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2c{h}}-1}{2c}={h}+c{h}^{2}+\mathcal{O}({h}^{3}),

(19)

\displaystyle\left<I_{1,l}^{2}(h)\right>

\displaystyle=e^{2c{h}}{h}={h}+2c{h}^{2}+\mathcal{O}({h}^{3}).

(20)

Thus the variance of the left point approximation is correct up to
\mathcal{O}({h})
. To improve the approximation, we evaluate the integrand at
the midpoint, i.e.,
e^{-cs}\simeq e^{-c(t+{h}/2)}
, which leads to

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,h)\simeq I_{1,m}(t,h)=e^{c{h}/2}\int_{t}^{t+{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(s)=e^{c{h}/2}{\Delta W},

(21)

and
\left<I_{1,m}^{2}\right>={h}+c{h}^{2}+\mathcal{O}({h}^{3})
, that is correct up to
\mathcal{O}({h}^{2})
. Similarly, for

I_{2}(t,h)
, we have the following mid point approximation

\displaystyle I_{2,m}(t,h)=\frac{e^{c{h}/2}}{2}\left[W(t+{h})^{2}-W(t)^{2}-{h}\right].

(22)

Putting everything together, we obtain the following approximations for the full stochastic integral
I_{S}(u,t,h)
,

\displaystyle I_{S,m}(u,t,h)

\displaystyle=e^{c{h}/2}G(u,t)\left[{\Delta W}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}G(u,t)\left({\Delta W}^{2}-h\right)\right],

(23)

which gives us the following SETD-Milstein scheme, an ETD variant of the Milstein method
Kloeden and Platen 1999
;
Higham 2001

\displaystyle u(t+{h})=f_{1}(h)u(t)+f_{2}(h)F(u,t)+f_{3}(h)G(u,t)\left[{\Delta W}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}G(u,t)\left({\Delta W}^{2}-h\right)\right].

(24)

Here, we have define the factors

\displaystyle f_{1}(h)=e^{c{h}},~f_{2}(h)=\frac{e^{c{h}}-1}{c},\text{~and~}f_{3}(h)=e^{c{h}/2}

(25)

for book keeping. Ignoring the
\mathcal{O}({h})
correction to the stochastic term, we can also write down the SETD-EM scheme as

\displaystyle u(t+{h})=f_{1}(h)u(t)+f_{2}(h)F(u,t)+f_{3}(h)G(u,t){\Delta W}.

(26)

Note that in the small
{h}
limit, the SETD-Milstein and SETD-EM schemes map to the standard Milstein and Euler-Maruyama schemes respectively.

For additive noise, i.e.
G(u,t)=G_{0}
and
G^{\prime}(u,t)=0
, the SETD-EM and SETD-Milstein methods are identical and we simply have

\displaystyle u(t+{h})=f_{1}(h)u(t)+f_{2}(h)F(u,t)+f_{3}(h)G_{0}{\Delta W}.

(27)

Further, we can use the exact result for the
I_{1}
integral and drop the
I_{2}
integral to obtain the SETD1 scheme

\displaystyle u(t+{h})=f_{1}(h)u(t)+f_{2}(h)F(u,t)+f_{4}(h)G(u,t)\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(28)

where we have defined

\displaystyle f_{4}(h)=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2c{h}}-1}{2c}}.

(29)

The advantage of using SETD1 scheme is that the stochastic integral is evaluated exactly, which improves the statistical properties in the steady state (see
Fig.

1
, for example). Similarly one can write down mixed schemes, that offer higher order approximations for the deterministic terms. For example, SETD2 scheme, the stochastic variant of the ETD2 scheme in
Cox and Matthews 2002
can be written as

\displaystyle u(t+{h})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(h)u(t)+\frac{(1+c{h})e^{c{h}}-1-2c{h}}{c^{2}{h}}F(u,t)+\frac{1+c{h}-e^{c{h}}}{c^{2}{h}}F(u,t-{h})+f_{4}(h)G_{0}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(30)

To our knowledge, this is the first full generalization of the exponential time stepping algorithm to stochastic systems.
Some schemes for specific applications have been proposed, such as by
Gillespie 1996
for the linear Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and
Ta et al. 2015
have derived integration factor based schemes for stiff stochastic reaction-diffusion systems.

We now show numerically the strong and weak convergence for the SETD schemes

;
Kloeden and Platen 1999
. Given the true solution
U(t)
of a stochastic process and it’s numerical approximation
U_{n}(t)

\gamma

\displaystyle\left\langle\left|U_{n}(t)-U(t)\right|\right\rangle\leq C_{1}{h}^{\gamma},

(40)

for
{h}
sufficiently small. Here
\left<\ldots\right>
defines the ensemble average. In other words, the strong convergence measures how far on average the numerical approximation is from the true solution. Similarly, weak convergence is defined as

\displaystyle|\left\langle U_{n}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle U(t)\right\rangle|\leq C_{2}{h}^{\zeta},

(41)

and it quantifies the decay of the error of the averages. In
Fig.

and

for the SETD-Milstein and
\gamma=1/2
and
\zeta=1
for the SETD-EM scheme

, as shown in

Pseudospectral Algorithms for Stochastic Field theories

We now showcase how SETD schemes can be used to efficiently simulate SFTs with additive noise using pseudospectral algorithm.
We consider a general statistical field theory for an order parameter
\phi(\bm{x},t)
—real or complex—driven by deterministic dynamics and a noise term
\eta(\bm{x},t)
.
This gives us a field Langevin equation (or Stochastic PDE), which has the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)=K[\phi](\bm{x},t)+\sqrt{2D}\eta(\bm{x},t).

(44)

Here,
K[\phi]
specifies the forces acting on the field
\phi
, and is typically a local functional of
\phi
and its derivatives,
\bm{\nabla}\phi
,
\nabla^{2}\phi
, and so on. The noise
D
may in general also be an operator, as is the case for conserved models. Some examples of (
44
) are Model A, Model B, and other models considered in
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
, the KPZ equation
Kardar et al. 1986
, Navier-Stokes equations with stochastic forcing
Forster et al. 1977a
;
DeDominicis and Martin 1979
;
Frisch and Kolmogorov 1995
;
Pope 2000
, and its generalizations
Toner and Tu 1995
;
Chatterjee et al. 2021
.

We will assume that the Fourier amplitudes
\phi(\bm{k},t)=\mathcal{F}[\phi(\bm{x},t)]
(see
Appendix

A
for definitions), satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{k},t)=c(\bm{k})\phi(\bm{k},t)+F[\phi](\bm{k},t)+\sqrt{2D(\bm{k})}\eta(\bm{k},t),

(45)

where
c(\bm{k})
is typically a function of
k\equiv|\bm{k}|
only,
F[\phi](\bm{k},t)
represents the nonlinear terms (or linear terms not included in
c(\bm{k})
), which typically involves convolutions of
\phi(\bm{k},t)
, and
D(\bm{k})
specifies the correlators of the noise.
In general, the noise in Fourier space satisfies

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{k},t)\right>

\displaystyle=0,

(46)

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{k},t)\eta(\bm{k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=0,\text{~and~}

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{k},t){\eta}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

where
∗
implies the complex conjugate. For
\phi(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e., when the order parameter is real,
\phi(\bm{k},t)
and
\eta(\bm{k},t)
satisfy the Hermitian property

\displaystyle{\phi}^{*}(\bm{k},t)=\phi(-\bm{k},t)\text{~and~}{\eta}^{*}(\bm{k},t)=\eta(-\bm{k},t),

(47)

which can be used to recast (

) in the familiar form for the real order parameter. For concrete examples of (
44
) and (
45
), we refer the reader to
Section

V
.

Stochastic Field Theories are, in the continuum and thermodynamic limit, considered in an infinite system with a continuous coordinate
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
But our computers are finite, so we consider the field on a finite grid of size
L^{d}
discretized with
N^{d}
points.
The continuous Fourier Transform is thus replaced by the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), and care must be exercised while discretizing the fields and the noise correlators, which we explain in details in
Appendix

A
.
In the discretized form,
Eq.

45
becomes a set of coupled, stiff, SDEs, each labelled by the Fourier index
\bm{a}
(see
Appendix

A
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\bm{a}}(t)=c_{\bm{a}}\phi_{\bm{a}}(t)+F_{\bm{a}}(\{\phi_{\bm{a}}\},t)+\sqrt{2D_{\bm{a}}}\eta_{\bm{a}}(t).

(48)

for which the schemes derived in
Section

II
apply directly. Given the configuration
\phi_{\bm{a}}
in Fourier space at time
t
, the pseudo-spectral algorithm to integrate for a time step using SETD1 is as follows:

For each grid-point
\bm{a}
in the Fourier lattice:

1.

Compute the non-linear term
F_{\bm{a}}(\{\phi_{\bm{a}}\},t)
.
This is typically done by first transforming the Fourier amplitudes back to real space, computing the non-linear term and then taking it’s Fourier transform, which lends the algorithm the name
pseudo
-spectral algorithm.
To avoid aliasing errors, it is necessary to de-alias appropriately, for example by truncation of the Fourier amplitudes
Orszag 1969
;
Orszag 1971
;
Gottlieb and Orszag 2009
;
Canuto et al. 1988
;
Yoffe 2013
or by phase-shift de-aliasing
Rogallo 1981
;
Canuto et al. 1988
;
Boyd 2001
;
Yoffe 2013
.

2.

Draw uncorrelated, normally-distributed random numbers
\eta_{\bm{a}}(t)
.
For real fields,
\eta_{\bm{a}}(t)
needs to satisfy the Hermitian property, thus some corrections must be made when drawing random deviates in Fourier space (see
Appendix

B
).
Alternatively, one can draw the random deviates in real space
\eta_{\bm{n}}(t)
and obtain
\eta_{\bm{a}}(t)
by performing an additional FFT per iteration step.

3.

Compute the following factors:

\displaystyle f_{1,\bm{a}}=e^{c_{\bm{a}}h},~~f_{2,\bm{a}}=\frac{e^{c_{\bm{a}}h}-1}{c_{\bm{a}}},\text{~~and~}G_{\bm{a}}=\left(\frac{N^{2}}{L}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{D_{\bm{a}}}{c_{\bm{a}}}\left(e^{2c_{\bm{a}}h}-1\right)}.

(49)

For the derivation of the additional factor
(N^{2}/L)^{d/2}
in
G_{\bm{a}}
, see
Appendix

A
.

4.

Integrate the Fourier amplitudes using the SETD1 scheme:

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{a}}(t+h)=f_{1,\bm{a}}\phi_{\bm{a}}(t)+f_{2,\bm{a}}F_{\bm{a}}(\{\phi_{\bm{a}}\},t)+G_{\bm{a}}\eta_{\bm{a}}(t).

(50)

Since, in the
c{h}\to 0
limit, the SETD1 schemes reduces to the EM method, one should use it instead for small
c_{\bm{a}}
to avoid overflow errors.

This method has the convergence and stability properties derived in the previous section, which comes especially handy for conserved models such as model B, where
c(\bm{k})\sim k^{4}
. Furthermore, explicit methods are faster than implicit schemes—the most costly operation here is the Fourier transform, which requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{d}\log N)
operations per iteration and the time stepping in the Fourier space requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{d})
operations. Implicit methods, in addition to the FFT costs, either need matrix inversions that cost
\mathcal{O}(N^{2d})
operations, or multiple iterative Newton method solves that each cost
\mathcal{O}(N^{d})
operations, per iteration.

Examples

We now illustrate the use of the SETD1 scheme for the numerical simulation of various SFTs.
The properties of the SFTs are best described in terms of various statistical measures which we now discuss.
The dynamic correlation function
C(\bm{k},\omega)
is defined as

\displaystyle\left<\phi(\bm{k},\omega){\phi}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right>\equiv C(\bm{k},\omega)(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(51)

and the equal time correlator
C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k},t)
is defined as

\displaystyle\left<\phi(\bm{k},t){\phi}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime},t)\right>\equiv C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k},t)(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}\right).

(52)

In the steady state, equal time correlators are independent of time, i.e.,
C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k},t)\sim C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k})
and then it can be easily shown that

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k})=\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}C(\bm{k},\omega).

(53)

When the order parameter is real, the Hermitian property can be used to recast the above expressions to their familiar form
Kardar 2007b
, for example,

\displaystyle\left<\phi(\bm{k},t)\phi(-\bm{k}^{\prime},t)\right>\equiv C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k},t)(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}\left(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime}\right).

(54)

For a real order parameter, we further define the Susceptibility, which gives the linear response of a system to an external force,
f(\bm{k},\omega)
as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left<\phi(\bm{k},\omega)\right>_{f}}{\delta f(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}\bigg|_{f=0}\equiv\chi(\bm{k},\omega)(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime}),

(55)

where
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{f}
is the expectation when the force
f
is added to the right hand side of the equation of motion (
45
).
In
Appendix

C
, we describe how these quantities can be obtained from simulations.

The Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation

The Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation
Kardar et al. 1986
describes out of equilibrium growth of an interface. The height of the interface
h(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}
(not to be confused with the symbol for time step,
h
, used in previous sections) obeys the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\bm{x},t)=\nu\nabla^{2}h(\bm{x},t)+\frac{\lambda}{2}|\nabla h(\bm{x},t)|^{2}+\sqrt{2D}\eta(\bm{x},t),

(66)

where
\nu
is the surface tension,
\lambda
controls the lowest-order nonlinear term, and
\eta(\bm{x},t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{x},t)\eta(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>=\delta^{d}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(67)

In Fourier space, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\bm{k},t)=-\nu k^{2}h(\bm{k},t)+F[h](\bm{k},t)+\sqrt{2D}\eta(\bm{k},t).

(68)

With
C(\bm{k})=-\nu k^{2}
,
D(\bm{k})=D
, and

\displaystyle F[h](\bm{k},t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathcal{F}\left[\left|\nabla h(\bm{x},t)\right|^{2}\right](\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{q}}{2\pi}\,\left[\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{q}-\bm{k})\right]h(\bm{q},t)h(\bm{q}-\bm{k},t),

(69)

we identify the KPZ equation as another form of (
45
). The evolution of the interface can be quantified by the width function
Barabási et al. 1995
which is defined as

\displaystyle w_{\ell}(t)\equiv\sqrt{\left\langle h(\bm{x},t)^{2}-\left\langle h(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle_{\ell}^{2}\right\rangle_{\ell}},

(70)

where the
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{\ell}
implies ensemble average over partial domain of size
\ell
. It measures the average variance of the height within a box of width
\ell
, at time
t
after initialization, and satisfies the following scaling form

\displaystyle w_{\ell}(t)=\ell^{\alpha}f\left(\frac{t}{\ell^{z}}\right),\text{~where~}f(u)\sim\begin{cases}u^{\beta},&u\ll 1,\\
1,&u\gg 1.\end{cases}

(71)

Here,
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
z
are the critical exponents for the KPZ equation, which satisfy the identities

\displaystyle z=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\text{~~and~~}\alpha+z=2.

(72)

The first identity is a consequence of the scaling form (
71

Kardar et al. 1986
;
Forster et al. 1977b
;
Barabási et al. 1995
.
Thus, in reality, there is only one independent exponent (say
\beta
).
In 1D, the exponents can be obtained exactly, and we have
\beta=1/3

The Complex Ginzburg-Landau equation

So far we have considered SFTs with a real valued order parameter.
We now focus on the Complex Ginzburg-Landau equation, which describes the out of equilibrium dynamics of a non-conserved complex order parameter field
Aranson and Kramer 2002
.
In real space, we have for
\left(\phi(\bm{x},t),\eta(\bm{x},t)\right)\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi=\phi-(1+ic)|\phi|^{2}\phi+(1+ib)\nabla^{2}\phi+\sqrt{2D}\eta,

(73)

where
b,c,D\in\mathbb{R}
, and the noise satisfies

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{x},t)\right>

\displaystyle=0,

(74)

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{x},t)\eta(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=0,\text{~and~}

\displaystyle\left<\eta(\bm{x},t){\eta}^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\delta^{d}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

For
D=0
, CGL equation admits travelling wave solutions of the form
\phi(\bm{x},t)=R\exp\left[i(\bm{q}\cdot\bm{x}-\Omega t)\right]
, where
R=\sqrt{1-q^{2}}
and
\Omega=bq^{2}+c(1-q^{2})
.
These solutions are linearly stable against small perturbations
Aranson and Kramer 2002
as long as

and

\displaystyle q^{2}<\frac{1+bc}{3+2c^{2}+bc}.

(75)

Let’s now consider the stochastic evolution of the system initialized with a stable travelling wave state in 1D. If
D

is small, the exact equal time correlations can be derived from a linear fluctuating theory. For simplicity, we consider

b=0
, where all the travelling waves with
q^{2}<1/(3+2c^{2})
are stable. After linearizing (
73
) for the perturbations of the form

\displaystyle\phi(x,t)=\left[R+u(x,t)\right]\exp\left[i(qx-\Omega t)\right],

(76)

where
u(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}
, we obtain the following equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=-c_{R}\left(u+{u}^{*}\right)+\partial_{x}^{2}u+2iq\partial_{x}u+\sqrt{2D}\eta,

(77)

where we have defined
c_{R}=R^{2}(1+ic)
.
In the Fourier space, we obtain the following dispersion relation

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-i\omega+c_{R}+k_{+}^{2}&c_{R}\\
{c_{R}}^{*}&-i\omega+{c_{R}}^{*}+k_{-}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u(k,\omega)\\
{u}^{*}(-k,-\omega)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta(k,\omega)\\
{\eta}^{*}(-k,-\omega)\end{pmatrix},

(78)

with

\displaystyle C(k,\omega)=2D\frac{\left|-i\omega+{c_{R}}^{*}+k_{-}^{2}\right|^{2}+\left|c_{R}\right|^{2}}{\left|\mathrm{det}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)\right|^{2}},

(79)

where

Plugging in the expressions we obtain

\displaystyle C(k,\omega)=\frac{2D\left(1+c^{2}\right)R^{4}}{\left[4ckqR^{2}+2\omega\left(k^{2}+R^{2}\right)\right]^{2}+\left[k^{4}+2k^{2}\left(R^{2}-2q^{2}\right)-\omega^{2}\right]^{2}}.

(80)

The equal time correlator is

(81)

For long wavelength fluctuations

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{ET}}(k)\sim\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

(82)

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented stochastic variants of the ETD schemes—the Stochastic Exponential Time Differencing (SETD) schemes—and outlined fast, scalable pseudo-spectral algorithms for numerical investigations of stochastic field theories.
These schemes treat stiff linear terms exactly and thus offer better numerical convergence and a broader range of stability compared to the Euler-Maruyama and the Milstein method.
We have investigated the convergence and stability properties of the variants of the SETD scheme in the context of stochastic ODEs, and then described how to apply the schemes for SFTs.
We have outlined the pseudo spectral approach, derived the necessary conversion factors that appear for discretized versions of stochastic PDEs, and methods for sampling observables of interest, such as the dynamical linear response function and correlation function.
Finally, we applied the SETD1 scheme to numerically investigate of various field theories and compared our numerical results with analytical predictions with an excellent agreement.

The schemes considered here offer convergence of order
\mathcal{O}({h})
for additive SFTs. One could extending these algorithms to derive higher order schemes by using repeated Itô-Taylor expansions. However, for the over-damped SFTs considered here, quadratic integrals of noise terms that obey non-Gaussian statistics appear at
\mathcal{O}({h}^{3/2})
and above, which makes numerical integration unfeasible
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2025
. For a particular class of under-damped SFTs, such as the ones considered in
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2025
, non-Gaussian terms are absent up to
\mathcal{O}({h}^{4})
, thus it is possible to devise higher order SETDs for these subclass of systems
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2025
;
Hershkovitz 1998
;
Milstein and Tretyakov 2021
.
Finally, we have considered SFTs with additive noise. In future, our work could be extended to include multiplicative noise, relevant for reaction-diffusion systems
Doering et al. 2003
and Dean-Kawasaki type of models
Dean 1996
;
Kawasaki 1994
.

Appendix A
Observables for Discrete Fourier Transforms

For the notational simplicity, we choose to represent the fields and their Fourier amplitudes with the same symbol and explicitly specify their arguments to specify if we are working in real space or Fourier space.
The real-Fourier domain pairs are
(\bm{x},\bm{k})
and
(t,\omega)
.
For example,
\phi(\bm{x},t)
implies real space and time,
\phi(\bm{k},t)
implies that the field is transformed in space, and
\phi(\bm{k},\omega)
implies that the field is transformed in both space and time.
Similarly, for discrete fields, the real-Fourier domain pairs are
(\bm{n},\bm{a})
and
(m,b)
, i.e.,
\phi_{\bm{n},m}
is the order parameter in real space and time and
\phi_{\bm{a},b}
implies that the field is in the wave number and frequency space.
We define the forward and inverse Fourier transforms in space and time as

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[\phi(\bm{x},t)](\bm{k},\omega)=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~e^{-i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}-\omega t)}\phi(\bm{x},t),

(83)

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\phi(\bm{k},\omega)](\bm{x},t)=\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}~e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}-\omega t)}\phi(\bm{k},\omega).

In this Appendix, we drop time, and consider only transformations in space, the generalization to time dependent fields is straight forward.
For numerical simulations, we consider a
d
-dimensional periodic domain with each side of length
L
, discretized over
N^{d}
collocation points.
Thus, a grid point
\bm{x}_{\bm{n}}
in real space labelled by
\bm{n}\in[1,2,\cdots,N]^{d}
has the spatial coordinates
\bm{x}_{\bm{n}}=\Delta x\bm{n}
,
where
{\Delta x}=L/N
. Similarly,
\bm{k}_{\bm{a}}=\Delta k\bm{a}
is the wavenumber in Fourier space, where
\bm{a}\in[-N/2,\cdots,N/2-1]^{d}
and
\Delta k=2\pi/L
.

\phi(\bm{x}_{\bm{n}})
then implies the value of the continuous order parameter at
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{\bm{n}}
, and
\phi(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}})
is the Fourier amplitude for the wavenumber
\bm{k}=\bm{k}_{\bm{a}}
.

For Discrete Fourier Transforms (DFT), we use the following convention,

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{n}}e^{2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{n}}{N}}\phi_{\bm{n}},

(84)

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{a}}e^{-2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{n}}{N}}\phi_{\bm{a}},

where
\phi_{\bm{n}}
and
\phi_{\bm{a}}
are the discrete fields in real and Fourier space respectively.
Note that in the definition of the inverse DFT we omit a factor of
1/N
, i.e., we define unnormalized inverse transforms, to stay consistent with the numerical libraries
Frigo and Johnson 2005
.
In the limit
L,N\rightarrow\infty
with
{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0
, the discrete domain maps to the continuous limit as

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{n}}\left(\frac{L}{N}\right)^{d}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{n}})\sim\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,f(\bm{x}),

(85)

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{a}}\left(\frac{1}{L}\right)^{d}g(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}})\sim\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,g(\bm{k}).

If we consider the limit of the Fourier transform,

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{a}}=\sum_{\bm{n}}e^{2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{n}}{N}}\phi_{\bm{n}}\sim\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,e^{2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}}{L}\cdot\bm{x}}\phi(\bm{x}),

(86)

and as a consequence, the discretized fields maps to the continuous fields on a finite grid as

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle\sim\phi(\bm{x}_{\bm{n}}),

(87)

\displaystyle\phi_{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d}\phi(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}}).

The discretized Kronecker-Delta functions are related to the continuous Dirac-Delta function as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d}\delta_{\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\sim\delta^{d}(\bm{x}_{\bm{n}}-\bm{x}_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}),

(88)

\displaystyle L^{d}\delta_{\bm{a},\bm{a}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\sim(2\pi)^{d}\delta(\bm{k}_{a}-\bm{k}_{a^{\prime}}).

Thus, in real space the noise is to be discretized as

\displaystyle\eta(\bm{x}_{\bm{n}},t)\sim\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d/2}\eta_{\bm{n}}(t),

(89)

where
\eta_{\bm{n}}(t)
is still continuous in time and obeys
\left\langle\eta_{\bm{n}}(t)\eta_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. In
Fourier space, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\eta(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}},t){\eta}^{*}(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}}-\bm{k}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(90)

and the correlations for the discretized noise becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle\eta_{\bm{a}}(t){\eta}^{*}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\left(\frac{N^{2}}{L}\right)^{d}\delta_{\bm{a},\bm{a}^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(91)

it means that the noise in Fourier space is to be discretized as

\displaystyle\eta(\bm{k}_{a},t)\sim\left(\frac{N^{2}}{L}\right)^{d/2}\eta_{\bm{a}}(t),

(92)

where
\left\langle\eta_{\bm{a}}(t)\bar{\eta}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. As a sanity check, we can directly compute the statistics of the noise using DFT. Ignoring the dependence of noise on time for now, in real space we have

\displaystyle\left<\eta_{\bm{n}}\eta_{{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}\right>=\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d}\delta_{\bm{n},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}},

(93)

and in Fourier space we obtain

\displaystyle\left<\eta_{\bm{a}}{\eta}^{*}_{{\bm{a}}}\right>=\left<\sum_{\bm{n}}e^{2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{n}}{N}}\eta_{\bm{n}}\sum_{{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}e^{-2\pi i\frac{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{n}^{\prime}}{N}}\eta_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}\right>=\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{d}\sum_{\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime}}\delta_{\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{N^{2}}{L}\right)^{d},

(94)

which agrees with the noise statistics described above.

Appendix C
Measuring quantities in simulations

After simulating a time series
\phi_{\bm{n},m}
over
M+1
time steps
m\in(0,...M{h})
, we are interested in measuring various statistical quantities—expectation values of observables
A[\phi]
, which are functions of the field
\phi
.
In this appendix, we describe how to do this consistently with the conventions laid out above.

Equal-time correlation functions—
For translationally invariant systems, such as considered in this paper, the equal-time correlation function is defined as

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi(\bm{k},t){\phi}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime},t)\right\rangle=C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k})(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}).

(97)

In the steady state,
C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k})
is independent of the time it is measured at. Thus, assuming ergodicity, we can approximate the ensemble average
\left<A[\phi(\bm{k},t)]\right>
of an observable
A[\phi(\bm{k},t)]
as the average over a simulated time series,

\displaystyle\left<A[\phi]\right>\sim\left<A[\phi]\right>_{T}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,A[\phi]\sim\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}A[\phi_{\bm{a},m}].

(98)

Plugging in the discrete forms of
\phi(\bm{k},t)
and
\delta^{d}(\bm{k})
, we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}})=\left(\frac{L}{N^{2}}\right)^{d}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=0}^{M}|\phi_{\bm{a},m}|^{2}.

(99)

The 2D SFTs we have considered in this manuscript are rotationally invariant. Thus, to improve the statistics, we can average over a shell in
\bm{k}
-space to obtain

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{ET}}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{\bm{q}_{a}\in\Omega_{k}}C_{\mathrm{ET}}(\bm{q}_{\bm{a}}),~~\text{where}~~\Omega_{k}=\{\bm{q}_{a}~|~k-\Delta k<|\bm{q}_{\bm{a}}|\leq k\}

(100)

and
N_{k}=\sum_{\bm{q}_{a}\in\Omega_{k}}1
is the number of modes
\bm{q}_{\bm{a}}
that contributes to the shell.

Dynamic correlations—
To measure the dynamic observables such as the correlation function
C(\bm{k},\omega)
, we need to perform an ensemble average over a set of time series,
\smash{\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},m}}
, where
s=[1,\ldots,S]
and
S
is the size of the ensemble. We then perform a DFT over the time as well, which yields

\displaystyle\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},b}=\sum_{m}e^{2\pi i\frac{bm}{T}}\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},m}.

(101)

Then the dynamical correlation function
C(\bm{k},\omega;S)
is simply measured as

\displaystyle C(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}},\omega_{b};S)\approx C_{\bm{a},b}(S)\equiv\left(\frac{L}{N^{2}}\right)^{d}\frac{T}{M^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{S}|\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},b}|^{2}.

(102)

Susceptibility—
Together with the correlation function, the susceptibility
\chi(\bm{x},t)
, also known as the linear response function, is crucial to characterise the statistical properties of a system.
As the name suggests, it measures the linear response of the system to an external force
f(\bm{x},t)
.
One way of measuring susceptibility is obvious—we let the system reach steady state, apply a small force, and simulate its response.
This approach, however, is very costly: one would have to apply a sinusoidal force
f_{\bm{k},\omega}(\bm{x},t)=\epsilon\sin(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{k}-\omega t)
for each component of
\chi(\bm{k},\omega)
one intends to measure, and simulate enough runs to get good statistics and extrapolate to
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
This is, indeed, the experimental approach.
However, when doing simulations we may exploit the fact that we have access to the exact equation of motion.
The system responds in the same manner to a small perturbation whether it is external or arises from fluctuations.
Since we have the equation of motion, we can infer the form of the noise that affects the system—it is the deviation away from the deterministic forcing.
We can therefore measure the susceptibility using an ensemble of simulated time series, without ever perturbing the system.
This approach works even out of equilibrium and is as follows. For the Langevin equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)=K[\phi](\bm{x},t)+\sqrt{2D}\eta(\bm{x},t),

(103)

the Onsager-Machlup functional gives the probability distribution of a path
Täuber 2014
,

\displaystyle P[\phi]\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4D}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,\left[\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)-K[\phi](\bm{x},t)\right]^{2}\right\}.

(104)

Thus, we can write the expectation value of an observable
A[\phi]
as a path integral

\displaystyle\left<A[\phi]\right>=\int\mathcal{D}\phi(\bm{x},t)\,P[\phi]A[\phi].

(105)

An external force
f
modifies the equation by
K\rightarrow K+f
, which means that the Susceptibility in real space is given as

\displaystyle\chi(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\delta\left<\phi(\bm{x},t)\right>_{f}}{\delta f(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\bigg|_{f=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta f(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\int\mathcal{D}\phi(\bm{x},t)\,\phi(\bm{x},t)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4D}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,\left[\partial_{t^{\prime\prime}}\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime})-K(\bm{x},t^{\prime\prime})-f(x^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\right]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2D}\left<\phi(\bm{x},t)[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})-K(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})]\right>.

(106)

Thus, in Fourier space, we can measure the Susceptibility as

\displaystyle\chi(\bm{k}_{\bm{a}},\omega_{b};S)\approx\chi_{\bm{a},b}(S)\equiv\left(\frac{L}{N^{2}}\right)^{d}\frac{T}{M^{2}}\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},b}{\left[-i\omega_{b}\phi^{(s)}_{\bm{a},b}-K^{(s)}_{\bm{a},b}\right]}^{*}.

(107)

The average over wave-numbers and frequencies, as is done to quantify the average absolute deviation from the FDT [see (
65
) in
Section

V.1
], is calculated as

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}(S)\equiv\left\langle\left|\Delta(\bm{k},\omega;S)\right|\right\rangle_{\bm{k},\omega}\approx\frac{1}{N^{d}M}\sum_{\bm{a},b}|\Delta_{\bm{a},b}(S)|.

(108)
