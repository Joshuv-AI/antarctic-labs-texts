Thyroid hormone regulatory system in the mouse retina: integrated transcriptional and translational analysis of deiodinase enzymes, thyroid hormone receptors and thyroid hormone transporters
Authors: Alberto Melecchi, Elisa Angeloni, Silvana Balzan, Sara Calcagno, Alessio Canovai, Francesca Forini, Franziska Brinkmann, Matteo Lulli, Giuseppina Nicolini, Chiara Posarelli, Milena Rizzo, Rosario Amato, Giovanni Casini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid hormone (TH) signaling is involved in complex processes regulating development and energy balance, especially in organs with high-rate metabolism such as the retina. Despite evidence of TH involvement in regulation of retinal functions and disease susceptibility, a comprehensive analysis of the tissue distribution of the components of the retinal TH regulatory system is lacking. The aims of the present study were (i) to provide a detailed description of the retinal expression sites and localization patterns of deiodinases (DIO2 and DIO3), TH receptors (THRα and THRβ), and TH transporters (monocarboxylate transporter 8, MCT8, and organic anion-transporting polypeptide 1C1, OATP1C1); (ii) to recapitulate the retinal alterations following genetic deletions of specific TH regulatory elements in specific knockout (KO) mice.MethodsWe first predicted the cell-specific expression of TH regulatory elements in mouse and human retina via bioinformatic analysis of single-cell RNAseq databases. The results were subsequently validated in mouse retinas with RNAscope and protein localization was assessed with immunofluorescence. Cone photoreceptor morphology and retinal layer thicknesses were evaluated in the retinas of KO mice.ResultsDio2 mRNA was expressed by Müller glial cells and neuronal populations of the inner retina, while prominent DIO2 immunoreactivity was in cone photoreceptors, with an evident mismatch between mRNA and protein localization. DIO3 was expressed by virtually all neural cell populations of the retina. THRα and THRβ were predominantly localized to the inner and outer retina, respectively, with prominent expression of THRα in putative amacrine cells and ganglion cells, and of THRβ in cone photoreceptors. Both MCT8 and OATP1C1 were detected in the retinal pigment epithelium, in Müller glia, and in blood vessels. MCT8 was also expressed in some retinal ganglion cells. In KO mice, drastic alterations were observed in cone density and morphology and in retinal layer thickness following either Dio2 or Dio3 genetic deletion. In contrast, deletion of Thrs or TH transporters resulted in only minor changes, indicating potential compensatory mechanisms.ConclusionsThe present mapping of the TH regulatory system in the mouse retina provides a framework for understanding molecular and cellular mechanisms regulating local TH availability and signaling in health and disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02913-0.Keywords: RNAscope, Immunofluorescence, Confocal microscopy, DIO2, DIO3, THRα, THRβ, MCT8, OATP1C1

Background

Circulating levels of thyroid hormones (THs) depend primarily on the activity of the thyroid gland, which produces both 3,5,3′-triiodothyronine (T3), the biologically active form of TH, and its precursor thyroxine (T4). In the retina, as in other organs, a regional regulation of TH signaling is constantly provided thanks to the activity of local deiodinase enzymes (DIO2 and DIO3). DIO2 is responsible for the activation of T4 into T3, while DIO3 converts T4 and T3 into reverse T3 and 3,5-diiodothyronine (T2), respectively, which have only low biological activity [1]. Through these reactions, deiodinase enzymes regulate local T3 availability and provide a tissue-specific control of TH signaling. Local TH signaling in the retina is further influenced by hormone transport. Indeed, THs reach the retina through both the outer and the inner blood retinal barrier [2] and enter their target cells using specific transporters, such as the monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8) encoded by the Slc16a2 gene, and the organic anion-transporting polypeptide 1C1 (OATP1C1) encoded by the Slco1c1 gene [3, 4]. At cell level, THs may exert genomic effects by directly binding specific nuclear receptors (THRs), namely THRα and THRβ [5], or modulate non-genomic, regulatory effects mediated by membrane receptors such as integrin αvβ3 [6].

As demonstrated in a variety of studies, TH signaling in the retina is crucial for multiple aspects of cone photoreceptor biology, including their development, dorso-ventral patterning, and survival in the mature retina, (see [7–9] for review). Since THs affect cellular metabolism, their regulation is likely to be of fundamental importance not only for photoreceptor development / survival but also for a variety of retinal functions both in health and in disease. For instance, TH deficiency during fetal or postnatal development may affect retinal neuronal migration and differentiation, optic nerve myelination, eye growth, and alteration of retinal and visual cortex processing of visual stimuli [10–12]. Recent data have also revealed a role of DIO2 expressed in Müller glial cells for the regulation of T3 in a mechanism of light adaptation [13]. In addition, TH signaling is involved in retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration [7, 9]. In this respect, local modulation of DIO2 and DIO3 expression in the diabetic retina has been reported to establish a retinal low T3 state, which is likely to represent an adaptive mechanism aimed at preserving cellular energy and mitigating the metabolic stress induced by hyperglycemia [14].

Despite the likely involvement of TH signaling in a variety of retinal functions and diseases, a comprehensive analysis of the tissue distribution of the components of the retinal TH regulatory system is lacking. The expression of TH signaling elements has been reported in retinas of amphibians [15, 16], zebrafish (see [17] for references), salmonids [18, 19], and chickens [20–22]. In mammals, the only well-established evidence is the presence of DIO2 expression in Müller cells [8, 13, 23–26], while there are reports of the presence of both THRβ1 [27] and THRβ2 [28, 29] in cone photoreceptors.

The goals of the present work were: (i) to verify the translational relevance of the observations in the mouse tissue through a comparative prediction of cell-specific expression of the main regulators of TH signaling in murine and human retina; (ii) to provide a validation of the expression predictions through a detailed description of the expression sites and localization patterns of deiodinases, TH transporters, and TH receptors in the mouse retina; (iii) to recapitulate the effects of genetic deletion of specific components of TH signaling on the maturation of the retinal structure and of selected retinal cell populations such as cone photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells. A thorough mapping of the retinal TH regulatory system in the mouse model would provide a crucial framework for understanding how TH availability and signaling are locally controlled within the retina. Identifying the cell type- and layer-specific distribution of deiodinases, TH transporters, and TH receptors would clarify the circuits and developmental processes directly governed by THs and highlight cell populations vulnerable to TH imbalance. Such an atlas would not only refine mechanistic models of TH-dependent retinal development and function but also guide targeted therapeutic strategies for TH-related visual disorders.

Methods

Bioinformatic analysis

A predictive analysis of deiodinase, TH receptor and TH transporter expression in different cell populations of mouse and human retina was performed using the PLatform for Analysis of scEiad (PLAE) dataset (https://plae.nei.nih.gov/). scEiaD (single-cell Eye in a Disk) meta-atlas includes around 1.1 million single-cell eye transcriptomes across 44 different datasets, provided by 35 different publications. A total of 1.1 million single-cell eye and body tissue transcriptomes underwent deep metadata mining, rigorous quality control analysis and deep learning-based batch effect correction to obtain single-cell differential gene expression. The predicted expression of Dio2, Dio3, Thra, Thrb, Slc16a2 (encoding the MCT8 protein), Slco1c1 (encoding the OATP1C1 protein), Slc16a10 (encoding the MCT10 protein), Slc7a5 (encoding the L-type amino acid transporter 1, LAT1), and Lat2 mRNAs in different retinal cell populations of both mouse and human retina were retrieved applying “Cell type Predict” and “Organism” (Homo sapiens and Mus musculus) as two sorted-filters. Data was plotted as the mean ± SEM of the percentage of cells expressing a given marker within a specific retinal cell type. Only study combinations with more than 50 cells were included in this study, displayed as Expression Plot and In Situ Projection diagrams. The analysis did not consider the range of ages as a variable as this information was not available in the meta-atlas.

Animals and tissue preparation

Two month-old C57BL/6J wild type (WT) mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Italy (Calco, Italy). Thra 0/0 (hereinafter referred to as Thra knockout [KO]) and Thrb -/- (hereinafter referred to as Thrb KO) mice were bred in the animal core facility of the Dresden University of Technology. Mice with a homozygous mutation of Thra 0/0 and Thrb -/- do not express any THRα isoforms or functional THRβ1 or THRβ2 variants, respectively [30, 31]. All the animals used in these studies were treated in accordance with the ARVO statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and with the European Communities Council Directive (2010/63/UE). Animal procedures and organ collection were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (decree number 256/2024-PR) and by the institutional animal care committee of the Technische Universität Dresden and the Landesdirektion Sachsen (TVT 03/2023), according to the 3Rs principles for ethical use of animals and ARRIVE guidelines. The animals were maintained in a regulated environment (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity) with 12/12 h light/dark cycles and fed with a standard diet and water ad libitum. Eyes from KO mice for Dio2 [32], Dio3 [33], Slc16a2 [34], or Slco1c1 [35] as well as eyes from Dio2/Slc16a2 KO mice [36] were donated by the colleagues listed in the Acknowledgments section.

Mouse euthanasia and tissue collection were all performed between 10:00 and 12:00 pm in order to limit possible influences of the light/dark cycle. The mice were deeply anesthetized with Avertin (1.2% tribromoethanol and 2.4% amylene hydrate in distilled water, 0.02 mL/g body weight, i.p.), they were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde/0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and their eyeballs were enucleated and postfixed in the same solution for 2 h at room temperature. Fixed eyeballs of WT mice were transferred to sucrose/0.1 M PBS at increasing concentrations (10% − 30% every 1 h) and stored at 4 °C overnight. These eyes were embedded immediately into cryo-gel medium and stored at -80 °C to preserve mRNA stability for RNAscope or RNAscope / immunofluorescence double labeling. Fixed KO eyes, instead, were received in 30% sucrose and they were used only for immunofluorescence. All eyes were cut into 10 μm thick coronal sections and mounted onto superfrost slides. Alternatively, for whole mount retinal preparations, dissected retinas were immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h at 4 °C and then stored in 25% sucrose in 0.1 M PBS at 4 °C until use.

RNAscope

All the materials used for the RNAscope experiments were purchased from Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Inc., Newark, CA, USA. Single or duplex in situ hybridizations were performed using RNAscope probes for Dio2 (Cat. No: 479331-C1), Dio3 (Cat. No: 561641-C2), Thra (Cat. No: 519431-C3), Thrb (Cat. No: 451441-C1), Slc16a2 (Cat. No: 545291-C2), and Slco1c1 (Cat. No: 491821-C3). The hybridization was performed using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Retinal sections were pretreated with heat and protease (30 min at 40 °C) using the HybEZ Oven before hybridization with the appropriate mRNA probes listed above. After incubation with the primary amplification probes (AMP1 and AMP2 for 15 min, AMP3 for 30 min, at 40 °C), retinal sections were washed with RNAscope wash buffer and incubated with TSA vivid fluorophores 520, 570, or 650 for 2 h at room temperature. Additional retinal sections were incubated with triplex positive and negative probes (provided by the manufacturer) as positive or negative controls. The slides were coverslipped with Fluoroshield mounting medium containing 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No: F6057) and stored overnight at 4 °C before microscope examination. The observations were confirmed in at least 3 retinas deriving from different animals.

Immunofluorescence

Retinal sections were incubated with primary antibodies (listed in Table 1) diluted in 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS overnight at 4 °C. The specificity of the primary antibodies directed to DIO2, DIO3, THRα, THRβ, SLC16A2, and SLCO1C1 was tested with immunolabeling of retinas from Dio2 KO, Dio3 KO, Thra KO, and Thrb KO, Slc16a2 KO, and Slco1c1 KO mice, respectively. The specificity of antibodies directed to DIO2 or to SLC16A2 was also tested using retinas from Dio2/Slc16a2 double KO mice. After 3 × 5 min washes in 0.1 M PBS, retinal sections were incubated with the appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody (Table 1) diluted in 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Double immunolabeling with primary antibodies from different host species were performed incubating the retinal sections with both primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with the appropriate Alexa Fluor 546 or Alexa Fluor488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Conversely, double immunolabeling with both primary antibodies produced in rabbit were conducted by first incubating the sections with one of the primary antibodies diluted in 0.5% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS for 3 h at room temperature, and then with an anti-rabbit fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated Fab Fragment for 1.5 h at room temperature. After an overnight blocking step at 4 °C with an anti-rabbit unconjugated/unlabeled Fab Fragment (see Table 1 for details on anti-rabbit Fab fragment), retinal sections were incubated with the second primary antibody for 3 h at room temperature, and then with a secondary anti-rabbit Alexa-Fluor 546-conjugated antibody (1:400) for 1.5 h at room temperature. The slides were coverslipped with Fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI and stored overnight at 4 °C before microscope examination.

Double label RNAscope / immunofluorescence

Combined RNAscope / immunofluorescence analysis was performed using a specific protocol provided by the manufacturer (Manual pre-treatment PRO protocol, Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Retinal sections underwent a pretreatment protocol with heat and pretreatment PRO solution (30 min at 40 °C) with no protease incubation, and the antigen retrieval was performed with a co-detection buffer. At the end of the RNAscope procedure described above, retinal sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C diluted in 0.1% bovine serum albumin in 0.1 M Tris-buffered saline. The sections were then incubated with appropriate Alexa-Fluor 546-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After 3 × 5 min washes in 0.1 M PBS, the slides were coverslipped with Fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI and stored at 4 °C before microscope examination. The observations were confirmed in at least 3 retinas deriving from different animals.

Labeling of retinal blood capillaries

In some retinal sections, RNAscope labeling was evaluated in retinal blood capillaries. After the RNAscope procedure described above, retinal vessels were visualized using FITC-conjugated Isolectin B4 (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA, Cat. No: FL-1201-0.5) diluted 1:200 in 0,1% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PBS overnight at 4 °C. After 3 × 5 min washes in 0.1 M PBS, the slides were coverslipped with Fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI and stored at 4 °C before microscope examination. The observations were confirmed in at least 3 retinas deriving from different animals.

Confocal microscopy and figure preparation

The slides were analyzed using a Nikon A1 confocal inverted microscope (Nikon A1, Eclipse Ti). Confocal images were acquired at similar laser powers and black levels using a 40× objective (numerical aperture: 1.15) at a detector resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels, with a z-step of 0.5 μm. Stacks of confocal slices (18–25 steps each) were processed with ImageJ (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) to obtain Z Projections using the “Projection type” option “Max Intensity”. The final images were then corrected for brightness and contrast using ImageJ. Adjustments were performed using linear scaling and were applied uniformly to all pixels in each image. Comparable brightness and contrast settings were applied across images within the same experiment, with background signal used as a reference to ensure appropriate comparisons. Images representing the staining patterns across multiple biological replicates were trimmed and arranged in figure panels using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Photoshop CS3; Adobe Systems, Mountain View, CA, USA).

Quantitative analyses of retinal ganglion cells, cone photoreceptors, and retinal layers

Retinal sections from C57BL/6J mice and from KO mice were immunolabeled with RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS) [37]. Ganglion cell density was evaluated by counting the number of RBPMS immunopositive cells per section unit length (approximately 300 μm per section) in the peripapillary area of the optic nerve head. The total number of ganglion cells in each retinal section was divided by the section length and then expressed proportionally as ganglion cells / mm. The density of cone photoreceptors was evaluated by counting the number of cone arrestin immunopositive cells per section and it was then expressed as cones /mm. The size of cone pedicles was evaluated by measuring the maximum diameter of cone arrestin immunostained cone pedicles visualized in maximum projection images along 100 μm per section and the diameters of the pedicles from each section were averaged. Quantitative measures of RBPMS immunolabeled ganglion cells, cone density, and cone pedicle diameter were obtained using ImageJ. The thickness of retinal layers was measured in images of DAPI stained retinal sections using the Analysis menu of Adobe Photoshop. Briefly, in each image the measurement scale was set and the outline of the retinal layers was drawn on a separate image layer. Then, the area of each layer was measured and divided by the length of the section to obtain the average layer thickness. Values obtained from different sections belonging to the same retina were averaged. A total of 5 retinas were considered in all quantitative analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). The normal distribution of the data was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests were used to analyze the differences of cone density, cone pedicle diameter, ganglion cells density and retinal layer thickness between WT and KO mouse retinas. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was instead used to compare the cell-specific expression of each TH signaling component between mouse and human. Differences with P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Bioinformatic analysis: cell-specific expression of components of the TH regulatory system

The differential cellular expression of the genes encoding the main regulators of retinal TH signaling in the mouse retina was retrieved via bioinformatic analysis of available single cell RNAseq datasets as specified in the methods section. This analysis was performed to set up a working model summarizing predicted cell-specific mRNA expression in the mouse retina to be used as a reference for the subsequent localization of specific mRNAs and proteins in the tissue. The resulting outputs, with 10% of the total cells used as the threshold value for cell-specific positive/negative expression, are shown in Fig. 1. High Dio2 mRNA expression was predicted in Müller cells (Fig. 1A), while Dio3 mRNA expression levels were the lowest among all the genes analyzed and no Dio3 mRNA expression could be predicted in any of the retinal cell types considered in the analysis (Fig. 1B). Thra mRNA was predicted in cells of the inner retina, including horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, ganglion cells, and Müller cells (Fig. 1C). Thrb mRNA was likely to be maximally expressed by rod and cone photoreceptors, while lower levels were also expected in all other retinal cell types, except cells of retinal blood vessels (Fig. 1D). Among TH transporters Slc16a2 expression was predicted in cells of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), Müller cells, and blood capillaries (Fig. 1E), while that of Slco1c1 was mainly in RPE and Müller cells (Fig. 1F). The expected cell-specific expression of the TH regulatory system components in the mouse retina are summarized in Table 2. Bioinformatic data were also retrieved to compare the predicted expression of components of the retinal TH regulatory system in mice with that in humans. The results, summarized in figure S1, showed a high degree of similarity between mice and humans relative to the expression patterns of deiodinase, receptor, and transporter mRNAs, with the only notable difference regarding a prominent predicted expression of Slc7a5 mRNA (encoding LAT1) in different cell types of the human retina, which was not detected in mice.

mRNA localization using RNAscope in situ hybridization

Both RNAscope positive and negative controls were as expected (Fig. S2), indicating specific labeling of the used probes. As shown in Fig. 2A, the Dio2 mRNA signal was densely packed in cell bodies in the mid-inner nuclear layer (INL) and more sparsely distributed in proximity to the GCL. Interestingly, within the inner plexiform layer (IPL) labeled puncta appeared to be organized along vertically oriented processes (also evident in Fig. 2G and J) seemingly connected with the labeled cell bodies in the mid-INL. Overall, the Dio2 mRNA localization pattern suggested the presence of the transcript in Müller cell bodies and processes in the inner (but not in the outer) retina. The Dio2 mRNA labeling near the GCL is consistent with its localization in Müller cell endfeet near the inner limiting membrane or in somata of the GCL (ganglion cells and/or displaced amacrine cells).

Sparse Dio3 mRNA labeling was observed throughout the nuclear layers of the retina (outer nuclear layer [ONL], INL, and GCL), while some fluorescent puncta were also observed in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) and in the IPL (Fig. 2B). This localization pattern suggested Dio3 mRNA expression by virtually all cell types of the retina.

Regarding Thra mRNA staining (Fig. 2C), we observed densely packed fluorescent clusters in the most proximal portion of the INL and in the GCL, while the outer retina (OPL and ONL) was devoid of any Thra transcript signal. This pattern suggested the expression of Thra mRNA in a variety of inner retinal cell populations, and in particular in amacrine cells and in ganglion/displaced amacrine cells.

Thrb mRNA appeared to be mainly distributed to the outer retina. As shown in Fig. 2D, the Thrb mRNA signal was particularly dense at the level of cone photoreceptor somata and in cone outer segments (OS). Fluorescent puncta were also evident in the ONL, likely indicating Thrb mRNA expression in rod photoreceptors, and, more sparsely distributed, in the INL and in the GCL. These observations suggested the presence of Thrb mRNA mainly in photoreceptors (particularly in cones) and, in lower amount, in cells of the inner retina.

The Slc16a2 mRNA signal was mainly localized to the mid-INL (Fig. 2E), in a pattern reminding that of Dio2 mRNA. Therefore, Slc16a2 mRNA was likely to be expressed in Müller cells; however, different from Dio2 mRNA, it did not appear in putative Müller cell processes in the IPL. In addition, sparse Slc16a2 mRNA labeled cells were observed in the GCL. Considering the possible involvement of MCT8 in the transport of THs through the outer blood retinal barrier, the expression of Slc16a2 mRNA was evaluated not only in the neural retina but also in the RPE, where it appeared to be extensively distributed, as shown in Fig. 1E.

Slco1c1 transcripts (Fig. 2F) were primarily confined to a narrow band in the mid-INL, in a pattern suggestive of Slco1c1 mRNA expression in Müller cells. Some sparse Slco1c1 mRNA labeling was also in the GCL and in elongated profiles localized to the OPL and IPL resembling blood capillaries. In addition, Slco1c1 mRNA labeling was distributed throughout the RPE.

Components of the TH signaling system have been reported to have differential regional distributions in the retina during development [38]. In particular, a dorso-ventral patterning would contribute to the differentiation of two different cone photoreceptor populations in mice [8, 38, 39], therefore we checked for possible variations in the dorsal and ventral expression patterns of TH component mRNAs. As shown in figure S3, no appreciable differences were observed in the RNAscope patterns of deiodinase enzymes, THRs and TH transporters in dorsal and ventral retinal regions.

Since both Dio2 and Thra mRNAs were localized to the INL and to, or near, the GCL, we compared the two patterns in the same retinal sections using duplex RNAscope. As shown in Fig. 2G, the two fluorescent signals in the INL were clearly distinct from each other, which further supports Dio2 expression by Müller cells and Thra expression by putative amacrine cells. In the GCL, there was a partial overlap of the two signals.

The simultaneous detection of the mRNAs of Thra and Thrb (Fig. 2H) confirmed that Thra was expressed exclusively in the inner retina, while Thrb transcript was mostly detected in the outer retina. Interestingly, there was an extensive overlap of the two signals in the INL and in the GCL, indicating that putative amacrine and ganglion cells are likely to express both types of THRs.

The localization patterns of Dio2, Slc16a2, and Slco1c1 mRNAs shared important features. Indeed, in duplex RNAscope experiments the signals of Dio2 and Slc16a2 mRNAs (Fig. 2I) showed numerous sites of overlap in the mid-INL, suggesting the expression of both genes in Müller cells. In addition, Dio2 and Slc16a2 appeared to be co-expressed in sparse cells of the GCL. Similar to the Slc16a2 mRNA signal, also that of Slco1c1 mRNA showed extensive overlap with Dio2 mRNA in the mid-INL (Fig. 2J), suggesting co-expression in Müller cells of Dio2, Slc16a2, and Slco1c1 mRNAs. This possibility was further supported by observations of Slc16a2 and Slco1c1 mRNA double labels (Fig. 2K), in which extensive colocalization of the two signals was observed throughout the mid-INL. In addition, sparse cells in the GCL were also double labeled. Profiles resembling blood capillaries were mostly labeled only by the Slco1c1 mRNA probe, although colocalization with Slc16a2 mRNA staining was sporadically observed. Finally, both mRNAs appeared to be co-expressed in RPE cells.

Similarly, smaller DIO2 immunostained somata were observed in the proximal INL (Fig. 3F). Effects of genetic deletions of specific components of the retinal TH regulatory system

Cone photoreceptors were immunolabeled using an antibody directed to cone arrestin. As shown in Fig. 8A, in WT retinas strong immunolabeling was observed in cone OS, in cone somata in the distal ONL, in their axons spanning through the ONL, and in cone pedicles in the OPL. In the different KO retinas, the most notable changes were (i) various degrees of fragmentation and disorganization of the OS; (ii) presence of displaced cone somata in the mid-ONL; (iii) reduced number of cones (Fig. 8B); (iv) swelling of cone pedicles (Fig. 8C). In particular, significant decreases in cone numbers were detected in Dio2 KO, Dio2/Slc16a2 KO, Dio3 KO, and Thrb KO retinas (23%, 19%, 70%, and 29% vs. WT, respectively), while significant swelling of cone pedicles was observed in Dio2 KO, Dio2/Slc16a2 KO, Dio3 KO, Slc16a2 KO, and Slco1c1 KO retinas, as evidenced by increases in cone pedicle diameters of about 48%, 30%, 42%, 37% and 36%, respectively, as compared to those of WT retinas.

Since most, if not all, of the players related to the retinal TH system considered in the present studies were likely to be expressed in retinal ganglion cells, a quantitative analysis was performed to ascertain whether the genetic deletion of any of these factors could influence ganglion cell survival. RBPMS immunoreactive ganglion cells were visualized in retinal sections from WT and KO retinas and their densities did not appear to differ significantly in any of the experimental groups (Fig. S6).

As shown by the representative images of Fig. 8D, retinas from WT and from KO mice displayed variable thicknesses of retinal layers. According to quantitative analysis, summarized in Fig. 8E, both Dio2 KO and Dio3 KO retinas exhibited significant reductions in the thickness of GCL + IPL (25% and 35%, respectively), INL (36% and 37%, respectively), OPL (43% and 71%, respectively) and ONL (37% and 50%, respectively) as compared to WT. Both Dio2 / Slc16a2 KO and Slc16a2 KO retinas instead showed significant thinning limited to the INL (32% and 24% vs. WT, respectively) and the ONL (24% and 17% vs. WT, respectively). In addition, the ONL of Slco1c1 KO retinas was reduced by 18% compared to WT, while Thra KO and Thrb KO retinas did not show appreciable alterations in any of the layers. Figure 8F represents an analysis of the relative layer thickness in each experimental group and indicates that, despite changes in absolute thickness, the general pattern of retinal coverage by each layer was similar to that of WT in most of the KO retinas. Notable exceptions were Dio2 / Slc16a2 KO retinas, which displayed a 23% increase in the relative thickness of the GCL + IPL and a 20% decrease of that of the INL. Similarly, Dio3 KO retinas showed a 18% increase in the relative thickness of the GCL + IPL in conjunction with a 50% reduction of the coverage of the OPL. Discussion

The studies reported here provide a detailed and extensive description of the localization patterns of the main elements controlling TH availability in retinal neurons. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive description of the retinal TH signaling system, which includes a predictive bioinformatic analysis of scRNA-seq data followed by validation through evaluations of both the sites of mRNA expression and those of protein localization. In addition, the observations in KO retinas, which confirm and expand those of previous studies, contribute to the understanding of the involvement of the retinal TH system in retinal cell development and survival. The translational value of the findings reported in the present study is supported by the bioinformatic data indicating important similarities between mouse and human expression patterns.

Comparison with previous studies

The largest availability of localization data on the retinal TH system is about Dio2 expression in Müller cells. Indeed, Dio2 mRNA has been reported in Müller cells of mouse retinas using a variety of techniques, including microarray [24], conventional in situ hybridization [25], RNAscope and scRNA-seq [13] or cre drivers for Dio2 [7]. In addition, Dio2 expression in Müller cells has been reported also in human and monkey retinas using RNAscope and scRNA-seq [23, 26]. Our results expand these observations providing convergent data from bioinformatic analysis, in situ hybridization (RNAscope), and immunofluorescence. In addition, our findings also indicate DIO2 expression (both mRNA and protein) in ganglion cells. As noted above, the immunofluorescence patterns of DIO2 display impressive mismatches with the localization of Dio2 mRNA. In particular, DIO2 immunoreactivity, but not Dio2 mRNA, was observed in cone photoreceptors, in horizontal cells, and in populations of amacrine cells. These mismatches are discussed below, but it is interesting to note that another immunofluorescence study in rat retinas using a different DIO2 antibody reported a similar pattern of DIO2 immunostaining [44]. Different from DIO2, very scarce evidence of DIO3 expression / localization in retinas is available in the literature. To our knowledge, only one immunofluorescence study reported DIO3 immunoreactivity in the OS of cone photoreceptors [45], in a pattern similar to that observed in the present study also in Dio3 KO retinas. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Although the bioinformatic data indicate very low expression of Dio3 mRNA in mouse retinas, our experimental observations establish that virtually all cells in the retina may express low levels of Dio3 mRNA and the immunofluorescence findings confirm the presence of DIO3 protein in rods, horizontal cells, ganglion cells and GABAergic amacrine cells.

A study using a β-gal reporter system described the presence of T3 signaling in the inner retina of mice, mainly in glycinergic, AII amacrine cells and in ganglion cells [46], which is consistent, at least in part, with our findings regarding THRα and THRβ localization. A previous paper reported the presence of mRNA of the Thra1 isoform in the ONL, INL, and GCL using RNAscope in situ hybridization in retinas of mice at postnatal day 20 [47], while scRNA-seq indicated the presence of Thra mRNA in rod and cone bipolar cells, Müller cells, amacrine, horizontal, and ganglion cells of mouse retinas [13]. Overall, our findings confirm and expand these observations, revealing a previously unnoted prevalence of Thra mRNA expression in the inner retina together with an abundant THRα protein localization in ganglion cells. The expression of Thrb in cone photoreceptors has been assessed in different reports. The THRβ2 isoform was detected using immunohistochemistry in developing cones of mouse retinas [48], while Thrb2 mRNA expression in cones was also described using reporter mice with a lacZ knockin [28, 29]. The THRβ1 isoform was also localized to cone photoreceptors as well as to amacrine and putative ganglion cells by lacZ reporter transgenes [27]. In addition, diffuse localization of Thrb mRNA was reported in ONL, INL, and GCL using RNAscope [47], while scRNA-seq indicated the presence of Thrb mRNA in cone bipolar cells of mice [13] and of primate [26] retinas. Overall, our observations are consistent with these previous data and also add some previously unreported features of Thrb expression patterns in the retina. For instance, both the bioinformatic analysis and the RNAscope observations indicate Thrb mRNA expression not only in cone but also in rod photoreceptors and in cells of the inner retina, in particular in retinal ganglion cells. Finally, the bioinformatic data indicate possible expression of both Thra and Thrb mRNAs in the RPE.

Concerning TH transporters, the distinctive traits of Slc16a2 and Slco1c1 expression patterns were the presence of their mRNAs and/or proteins in the RPE, in Müller cells, and in ganglion cells, together with the presence of strong RNAscope signal of Slco1c1 in blood capillaries. Similar features were reported, at least in part, by previous studies using t-sne [13], lacZ reporter transgenes [49], and immunofluorescence in mouse [50] or in rat [4] retinas.

Divergent localization patterns between mRNA and proteins of TH regulatory elements

As noted above, the data from the bioinformatic analysis, the RNAscope investigation, and the immunofluorescence study are not always convergent in a unitary picture. In particular, three different types of mismatches could be identified: (i) a mismatch between the bioinformatic predictions and the mRNA localization; (ii) a mismatch between mRNA and protein localization with presence of the mRNA but absence of the protein; (iii) a mismatch between mRNA and protein localization with absence of mRNA but presence of the protein. i) Mismatch between the bioinformatic predictions and the mRNA localization

Evident differences were those concerning the lack of bioinformatic evidence of Dio2 and Dio3 mRNA expression in ganglion cells as compared to the presence of the RNAscope signals in these cells and those at the level of retinal blood vessels, where bioinformatic data seem to indicate absence of Slco1c1 mRNA while a strong Slco1c1 RNAscope signal was detected. On the other hand, RNAscope in situ hybridization failed to validate bioinformatic results on the presence of Thrb mRNA in RPE. These mismatches may be explained considering that there may be differences in the expression patterns of cells within a tissue and isolated cells in a single cell suspension [51], or that the used techniques have different resolutions [52].

ii) Mismatch between mRNA and protein localization: presence of mRNA and absence of protein

High density of RNAscope signal but lack of immunofluorescence was observed for MCT8 in the RPE and in Müller cells, and for OATP1C1 in the RPE and in vascular endothelial cells. A mismatch between mRNA expression and protein synthesis may depend on different factors, for instance the presence of regulatory molecules, such as miRNA, that may bind to mRNA and block its translation or promote its degradation [53], or different rates of mRNA turnover [54]. On the other hand, the lack of immunofluorescent signal might also be due to limited ability of the primary antibodies to detect their specific antigens in some cell types due to differential post-translational processing, as typically observed for proteins of the Slc transporter family [55].

iii) Mismatch between mRNA and protein localization: absence of mRNA and presence of protein

The most impressive mismatch of this kind was the presence of specific DIO2 immunostaining in cone photoreceptors and in amacrine cells, which were devoid of any RNAscope signal. Similar to our observations, an immunofluorescence study in rats reported DIO2 immunoreactivity localized to photoreceptors, the INL, and the GCL in dark adapted retinas, although specificity controls of the DIO2 antibody used in those studies were lacking [44]. Another study suggested the presence of a circadian control of DIO2 expression in cones, although direct demonstration of Dio2 mRNA in cones was not provided [56]. The possibility exists that a protein is detected in the absence of its mRNA. Indeed, it is known that some proteins may have high stability and long half-life, so that they can persist in the cell after degradation of their mRNA [57]. However, the reason underlying the different stability of Dio2 mRNA in cone photoreceptors as compared with other DIO2-expressing cells (i.e. Müller cells) would be difficult to explain. Another intriguing possibility is that DIO2 protein reaches cone photoreceptors and other cells through extracellular vesicles (EVs) released by Müller cells. Indeed, it has been shown that Müller cells may release EVs in vivo [58], which have been shown by in vitro studies to contain small amounts of proteins and mRNAs as well as miRNAs [59]. The EVs released by Müller cells can be internalized by both neural and non-neural retinal cells and may play important roles in communication between glial and neuronal cells both in physiological and in pathological conditions [59, 60]. If Müller cells were able to deliver DIO2 containing EVs, then these cells would act as main controllers of T3 production in virtually all types of retinal cells, and in particular in highly metabolically active cells such as the cone photoreceptors.

Functional implications

TH signaling relies on three main processes: hormone transport from circulation to target cells, hormone processing by deiodinases (activation by DIO2 or inactivation by DIO3) and hormone binding to its receptors [6]. Similar to findings in the brain [61, 62], the present data allow to propose a possible reconstruction of the functional organization of the local TH system in the retina, as reported in Fig. 9B-D.

In the outer retina, both MCT8 and OATP1C1 are expressed by RPE cells. This is not surprising, since the RPE is the major component of the outer blood-retina barrier acting as the primary checkpoint for the selective transport of molecules from choriocapillaris circulation [63]. Then, circulating T3 and T4 would reach the outer retina through MCT8 and OATP1C1, respectively, expressed by the RPE. T4 would be internalized in Müller cells through OATP1C1 to be converted into T3 by DIO2 expressed by Müller cells. In contrast to neurons of the inner retina, photoreceptors do not seem to express either MCT8 or OATP1C1, but they display a marked expression of Thrb, confirming a strong influence of TH in their physiology, as extensively described in previous reports [7]. Therefore, T3 released by Müller cells through MCT8 would be taken up by photoreceptors via transporters other than MCT8 or OATP1C1 [64]. Rods may use DIO3 to exert a negative regulation of T3 by transforming it into T2. On the other hand, in cones the presence of DIO2 protein, although not of Dio2 mRNA, suggests that these photoreceptors might also receive T4 directly from RPE and exert a positive control of TH signaling by converting T4 into T3.

In the inner retina, the vasculature displays a marked expression of OATP1C1, whose affinity for T4 is higher than that for T3. In particular, OATP1C1 would facilitate both the influx of T4 into endothelial cells and the efflux of T4 from endothelial cells to the extracellular matrix, thus allowing the translocation of T4 from the vascular lumen to retinal cells. Circulating T3, although minimally represented as compared to T4, could still reach retinal cells by carrier-mediated transport through MCT8 expressed at the level of blood vessels. Among the cells of the inner retina, Müller cells are the only type displaying high levels of OATP1C1 expression, indicating that these cells are likely to be the main acceptors of T4 from the circulation. Müller cells also have a prominent expression of DIO2, thus suggesting that they act as the primary retinal converters of T4 into active T3. Our observations show that Müller cells also express MCT8, which has higher affinity for the T3 than for T4 [65]. Therefore, after DIO2 mediated conversion from T4, T3 may exit Müller cells through a carrier-mediated mechanism involving MCT8 and, as MCT8 is also expressed by inner retinal neurons, T3 released by Müller cells would be taken up by neurons of the INL and GCL. These neurons have been found to express DIO3, although at low levels, and therefore they could participate in the regulation of TH function by allowing inactivation of T3 into T2. On the other hand, the presence of DIO2 protein in inner retinal neurons, although not fully confirmed by Dio2 mRNA, raises the possibility that T4 may directly enter retinal neurons. For instance, the uptake of T4 by ganglion cells may take place via OATP1C1, although it is expressed only by sparsely distributed ganglion cells, or via low affinity transport through MCT8. T4 would then be either converted into T3 by DIO2 or into rT3 by DIO3. Therefore, the effective T3 levels available to bind THRs and activate TH signaling would depend upon the combined effect of DIO2 and DIO3 in retinal neurons. Although the present reconstruction based on localization data should be validated with in depth mechanistic analyses (for instance using cell-specific gene knock out models), still it remains a key reference for upcoming studies focusing on TH signal transduction in the retina.

Effects of genetic deletions

The observation of retinal alterations caused by the genetic deletion of specific components of the retinal TH system may provide information on the roles played by these elements, mainly during retinal development. In this regard, retinas from Dio2 or Dio3 KO mice displayed an overall damage of the cone structure, in line with the known functional roles played by DIO2 in photoreceptor maturation and in the maintenance of photoreceptor physiological function [56, 66]. In particular, in Dio2 KO retinas we noted an evident increase in size of cone pedicles, indicating presynaptic or axonal swelling typical of degenerating synapses, as observed in rats after brain damage [67] and in a mouse model of Alzheimer disease [68]. Still, since the deletion of Dio2 results both in decreased T3 tissue levels and accumulation of circulating T4 [69], the analysis of Dio2 KO alone could not establish the differential contribution of these two conditions. Therefore, we used the Dio2 / Slc16a2 double KO model, which combines the Dio2 KO effects (low T3 tissue levels and accumulation of circulating T4) with those of Scl16a2 KO, consisting in reduced release of T4 into the circulation from thyroid gland with unaltered tissue T3 [69–71]. This model would reproduce a low T3 state in the retina with attenuated T4 accumulation, thus allowing to estimate the T4 contribution to the phenotypical alterations. Interestingly, the effects on cone pedicle hypertrophy were reduced in retinas from Dio2/Slc16a2 KO mice as compared to Dio2 KO, suggesting a possible exacerbating role of T4 accumulation on cone alterations.

On the other hand, a total absence of cones was observed in Dio3 KO retinas, in line with previous findings describing the key role of DIO3 for the survival and the maturation of cone photoreceptors through the control of T3 availability [72].

Regarding the effects of genetic deletions of the Thrs, it seems that a normal overall retinal development may still occur, despite the key role of Thrb in cone differentiation and maturation as reported by previous studies [39, 73]. These observations are consistent with only minor changes in opsin expression and normal electroretinographic responses reported in Thrb1 KO mice [27]. A slight, but statistically significant, decrease in ONL thickness was observed in both Slc16a2 and Slco1c1 KO retinas, which was likely to be the result of cone suffering, as indicated by the presence of cone pedicle hypertrophy detected in these retinas. Indeed, the expression of both Slc16a2 and Slco1c1 in the RPE is extremely important to ensure cone differentiation during development. In this respect, MCT8-deficient mice have been found to display reduced electroretinographic responses, demonstrating an overall photoreceptoral loss of function [49].

Most studies regarding the role of TH in retinal development and the effects of genetic deletions of deiodinases, THRs or TH transporters have been dedicated to the maturation of cone photoreceptors. However, THs may play an important role for the overall retinal development. Indeed, the alterations induced by genetic deletion of deiodinases observed in the outer retina were concomitant with a reduction of the whole retinal thickness, affecting all retinal layers. These findings suggest that the functional role of DIO2 and DIO3 are not limited to the maturation and survival of photoreceptors but may also affect the inner retina inducing a dysregulation of TH signaling. Interestingly, in line with this view, a significant correlation between insufficient thyroid function markers and retinal thinning has been reported recently in women affected by type 1 diabetes [74]. Similar to the observations in cone photoreceptors, the less evident alterations observed in Dio2/Slc16a2 KO retinas compared to those in Dio2 KO retinas suggest possible negative effects of circulating T4 on the development of the retinal layers.

The genetic deletion of either the Thrs or the TH transporters did not result in notable alterations in retinal layer thickness as compared to retinas with genetic deletion of the deiodinases. The lack of overt retinal damage or effects on retinal layer thicknesses in Thra or Thrb KO mice was probably the result of compensatory mechanisms involving one receptor isoform in the absence of the other, as previously reported [75, 76]. The hypothesis of compensatory mechanisms involving different elements of the TH regulatory system in the retina would be particularly relevant for the retinal ganglion cells, as they appear to express all the elements of this system. In this line, our findings indicate no changes in ganglion cell density in any of the KO retinas analyzed in this study, thus supporting the hypothesis of a sort of cooperation involving the existing elements of the TH system in response to the lack of one of them.

These observations in KO retinas are in line with those of previous studies and indicate that TH functions in retinal development may go well beyond regulation of cone photoreceptor maturation and affect the whole retinal structure. However, from this data it is difficult to ascertain whether the observed effects reflect either direct or indirect effects of altered TH signaling on retinal cells. To establish this point, analysis involving cell-specific KO of each TH signaling component for each major retinal cell type should be realized in a series of dedicated studies. Likewise, conditional KO mice for each TH signaling component could be used to ascertain whether the effects observed might derive from retinal progenitors and/or mature retinal cells. In addition, if any of the morphological changes observed in the KO models have an impact on visual function remains an open question that will be considered in future studies using electroretinography and behavioral tests.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, the present work is the first detailed, comprehensive analysis of the expression and localization of the elements comprising the local TH regulatory system in the mouse retina. This data allow us to recapitulate some mechanisms that are likely to regulate T3 availability in the retina and provide a solid structural basis for past [14], ongoing, and future studies on the functional roles played by THs in retinal health and disease.
