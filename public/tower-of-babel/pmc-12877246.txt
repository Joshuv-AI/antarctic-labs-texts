The association between continuing work after retirement and the incidence of frailty: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Linsu Sun, Guangrui Deng, Xi Lu, Xinlan Xie, Long Kang, Tao Sun, Xinhua Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesRetirement represents a significant life transition, with post-retirement status serving as a pivotal aspect of aging research. Despite its potential significance, little research has delved into the relationship between continuing work after retirement and the frailty. This study aims to investigate the association between continuing work after retirement and the incidence of frailty among older individuals.DesignA nationally representative cohort study.Setting and participantsWe utilized data from 4 waves (2011, 2013, 2015 and 2018) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study and a total of 5,960 participants were included in the study after applying specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.MethodsFrailty was assessed using a Frailty Index. To balance baseline covariates between workers (n = 3,170) and non-workers (n = 2,790), we employed inverse propensity of treatment weighting. The relationship between work status and the incidence of frailty was examined using Cox proportional hazards analysis, with results reported as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 5,960 participants (mean age 64 years; 42.1% male) were included in the analysis. Over a mean follow-up of 6.9 years, 2,105 cases of frailty were identified. In the cohort analysis, following adjustment using the inverse propensity of treatment weighting (IPTW), continuing work after retirement showed a negative association with frailty incidence, with an HR of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.65−0.79). Subgroup analysis revealed a more significant protective effect of continuing work beyond retirement age among individuals aged 65 or older, males, smokers, and those with limited social activities.ConclusionsIn summary, this study identified a significant association between continuing work after retirement and a decreased risk of frailty. The findings underscore the potential benefits of policies promoting social engagement and extending working life in enhancing the quality of life for the aging population.Keywords: Frailty, Work Status, Retirement, Longitudinal study, CHARLS.

Introduction

Facing the challenges of global aging, it is imperative to address the health and quality of life concerns of the older population [1]. Sociobehavioral factors play a crucial role in maintaining health among older individuals, with sustained physical activity [2,3], social interaction [4], and exercise [5] being of paramount importance. Moreover, retirement represents a significant life transition that often leads to notable changes in social behavior patterns and lifestyles among individuals [[6], [7], [8]], making the post-retirement period an important subject for scholarly discourse.

Increasing attention has been placed on frailty as a comprehensive indicator of the health status of older individuals [9]. Frailty, defined as a multi-dimensional syndrome that diminishes an individual's physiological reserves, heightens their susceptibility to adverse health outcomes such as falls, fractures, disability, and hospitalization [10,11]. This condition reflects the cumulative health deficits over an individual's lifespan, often assessed using tools like the Frailty Index (FI) [12,13]. Beyond its impact on older people, frailty presents challenges to both society and healthcare systems [14], Therefore, in rapidly aging societies, it is considered one of the primary public health challenges [15]. While previous research indicated a positive association between continued work status post-retirement and health outcomes, such as improved physical functioning, cognitive status, and psychological well-being [[16], [17], [18], [19]], existing evidence primarily stems from cross-sectional studies. Notably, the specific relationship between continuing work after retirement and the incidence of frailty still needs to be explored.

Therefore, this study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the relationship between continuing work after retirement and the incidence of frailty among older individuals. The analysis also aimed to identify the variations in this association across different demographic groups. By shedding light on these aspects, our study seeks to inform the development of targeted health management and intervention strategies for the older population, ultimately enhancing their quality of life and mitigating health risks. Additionally, the findings may offer valuable insights for guiding progressive retirement policies.

Material and methods

Study design and population

The data for this study is sourced from CHARLS, which collects coordinated and life history data. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey covering 28 provinces in China. In general, CHARLS is a biennial national research initiative that aims to obtain a representative sample of the Chinese population through a multistage, stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling technique. Enrolled residents contribute high-quality data for the study. 17,708 Chinese people who were middle-aged or older participated in the baseline survey conducted in 2011–2012. Subsequent surveys were conducted biennially (2013, 2015, and 2018). CHARLS has received approval from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (#IRB00001052-11015). Additional information on the recruitment strategies and research sampling of CHARLS can be found in previous publications [20]. This study utilized data from the CHARLS database, spanning from the first wave (2011–2012) to the fourth wave (2018), with the first wave serving as the baseline. Measurements included frailty status, self-reported work status, and covariates. Specifically, frailty events were recorded at waves 1 through 4 (W1-W4). All other measurements, including self-reported work status and covariates, were documented based on wave 1 data.

The inclusion criteria for this study were based on gender-specific age thresholds established by the statutory retirement age in China, incorporating participants from the CHARLS database recorded in 2011 who were over 55 years old for women and over 60 years old for men. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) individuals without baseline age information or those aged 90 years and above, due to the higher specificity of this age group; (ii) individuals missing baseline information on employment status and age, as well as those who had died at baseline; (iii) individuals with frailty at baseline; (iv) to maximize data utilization, we excluded individuals with missing values for more than 20% of frailty indicators(i.e. 32*20%, more than 6 items) in any wave, a criterion widely adopted in the literature [21]. Based on these criteria, a total of 5,960 participants were included in the study. Details of the participant selection process are provided in Fig. 1.

The flow chart of participants through the study.

Definitions of continuing work after retirement

To evaluate individuals' work status, we utilize the variable R1WORK (w1 r currently working) from the baseline dataset (wave 1). Individuals who reported being currently employed were categorized as "continuing work after retirement" (coded as 1); while those not currently employed were classified as having "retired status" (coded as 0). This classification allowed us to distinguish between individuals actively working beyond the retirement age threshold and those already retired.

Assessment of frailty

This study defines frailty as the accumulation of health deficits in individuals as they age [22], with the FI serving as the tool for assessing the frailty status of each participant. Frailty involves the accumulation of diseases, symptoms, signs, disabilities, or any health deficits as one ages, with a higher number of health deficits indicating greater frailty [23]. Although health deficits increase with age, the FI is more effective than chronological age [24] in reflecting age-related declines in health. Furthermore, the FI has been shown to predict adverse outcomes more accurately than chronological age [25] and even biological age [26]. Therefore, the FI provides a more comprehensive assessment of overall health in older adults, particularly in predicting adverse health outcomes. While variations exist in the number and types of indicators used in FIs across studies, consistency is maintained in incorporating key health domains such as activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living, chronic diseases, psychological characteristics, and cognitive function. By including these fundamental health aspects in the index, this study ensures a comprehensive evaluation of frailty status among the participants.

In this study, the FI calculation adheres to a standardized procedure, encompassing all major health domains. A selection of 32 items was made to construct the FI [27], covering variables such as comorbidities, physical function, disabilities, depression, and cognition. This FI has been validated in the CHARLS database and across other cohorts [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Except for the 32nd item, each item is dichotomized into 0 or 1 based on specific cutoff values, where 0 indicates the absence of a deficit, and 1 indicates its presence. The 32nd item is a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating poorer cognitive abilities. Further details regarding the FI are provided in Supplemental Table S1. For each participant, the 32-FI is calculated as the sum of current health deficits divided by 32. Therefore, the FI's generated range is from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating a higher level of frailty. As described in previous research [31], frailty is defined as 32-FI ≥ 0.25. The computation of 32-FI is conducted for each wave of the CHARLS dataset.

To address missing values and optimize data utilization while ensuring indicator quality, we allowed a maximum of 20% missing variables (≤6 items) in the calculation of the FI, a practice widely adopted in previous literature [14,32]. If participants have more than 6 missing values on these indicators, their FI will be considered as missing. For those with missing values equal to or fewer than 6, the average score of non-missing values is computed. For example, if a participant has 28 valid deficits with a total score of 6, the FI would be calculated as 6/28 = 0.21.

Covariates

In our analysis, we controlled for a series of demographic variables, including gender (male/female), age, education (less than primary school/high school and vocational training/higher education), marital status (married or partnered/other marital status (single, separated, divorced, or widowed)), and current residence (urban/rural). Health behaviors: smoking status (current smoking vs nonsmoking), alcohol consumption status (current drinking vs. no drinking), and body mass index (BMI) defined as the weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m) (BMI categorized as underweight and normal [≤23.9 kg/m²] or overweight and obese [>23.9 kg/m²]) [33].

Moreover, self-reported doctor-diagnosed chronic diseases, including lipid disorders, liver disease, kidney disease, and digestive system disorders. Social characteristics: Health insurance status (participating in at least one health insurance, including Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI), and New Cooperative Medical Scheme (NCMS)/not participating in health insurance). Social activities (participation in at least one of 11 social activities in the past month signifies that the respondent is socially active ("interacting with friends," "playing mahjong, chess, cards, or going to community clubs," "helping family, friends, or neighbors not living with you," "engaging in sports, social, or other clubs," "participating in community-related organizations," "volunteering or charity work," "caring for sick or disabled adults not living with you," "participating in educational or training courses," "stock investment," "using the internet")/not participating in social activities).

Furthermore, family information: average household income (defined as total household income divided by the number of household members) and the number of surviving children (0, 1−3, ≥4).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as mean (SD (standard deviation)) or median (IQR (interquartile range)) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) test (for normally distributed data) and Kruskall–Wallis test (for non-normally distributed data) were employed to examine inter-group differences for continuous variables. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Additionally, trend p-values for normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables were computed using Pearson and Spearman tests, respectively. Furthermore, a descriptive analysis was conducted on the number of times reporting being in a working state before the onset of frailty and the FI at the follow-up endpoint, corresponding to the time when the follow-up concluded.

For longitudinal analysis, the association between late-life work and the incidence rate of frailty was explored using Cox regression models, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to demonstrate these relationships. Schoenfeld residuals were used to test the proportional hazards assumption. CHARLS provides only the interview year and month for participants, without specifying the interview date. We calculated the follow-up time for individuals who became frail in W2 by subtracting the interview date in W1 (year and month) from that in W2 (year and month). For instance, if a participant was interviewed in W1 (July 2011) and became frail in W2 (August 2013), their follow-up time was 25 months. Similarly, for those who did not experience frailty until the fourth wave (W4), the follow-up time was calculated as W4 (year, month) - W1 (year, month).

Due to the use of non-randomly selected samples, concerns persist about potential self-selection bias, which may introduce normative issues [34]. In the context of this study, older individuals with better-predetermined health conditions may more easily opt for delayed retirement. Previous research indicates that poor health conditions are associated with intentions to exit the labor market prematurely [35]. In such circumstances, older individuals opting for delayed retirement may inherently suggest better predetermined health conditions compared to those not delaying retirement. To address this self-selection bias, the inverse propensity of treatment weighting (IPTW) method is employed, allowing the creation of a pseudo-population by assigning weights corresponding to the inverse of the probability of being employed based on observed covariates. If the standardized mean differences are ≤0.10 [36], covariate imbalances are considered trivial. This step aims to ensure the adjustment for potential selection bias when comparing different groups, enhancing the reliability of study outcomes.

The impact of various job categories on the prevalence of frailty was also investigated. Self-employment (coded as 1), non-self-employment (coded as 2), and non-working (coded as 0) are the three categories into which we divided employment kinds. Furthermore, we classified employment as follows: work in agriculture (coded as 1), work in non-agricultural areas (coded as 2), and non-working (coded as 0). Under these two classification approaches, the connection between different forms of employment and frailty was examined independently.

Additionally, subgroup analyses will be conducted using age, gender, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and social activities to explore whether the correlations among subgroups remain consistent. Interaction testing will be performed using the likelihood ratio test.

We conducted five sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the results: (1) excluding individuals who reported frailty in wave 2 to eliminate the possibility of reverse causation; (2) selecting individuals who reported being worked and those who reported being unemployed in all four waves, analyzing the relationship between continuing work after retirement and the risk of frailty in these two groups; (3) utilizing Propensity Score Matching (PSM) with a 1:1 ratio and a caliper set at 0.2, examining the association between continuing work after retirement and the risk of frailty; (4) employing PSM with a 1:1 ratio and a caliper set at 0.05, investigating the relationship between continuing work after retirement and the risk of frailty; (5) using a cross-lagged model to estimate the relationship between employment status and frailty post-retirement by repeatedly measuring the same sample across three waves (T1 (wave 1), T2 (wave 2), and T3 (wave 3); see Supplemental Fig. S2). Within the model framework, we focused on the cross-lagged effects of employment status on frailty (T1 work → T2 frailty and T2 work → T3 frailty). These sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings under different scenarios and methodological approaches.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corporation), IBM SPSS Amos version 26.0 (IBM Corporation), and R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All p-values are two-sided, and statistical significance is defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 5,960 participants were included in the cohort analysis, with a median follow-up time of 83 months [IQR: 48–84]. The median age was 64 years [61, 70], with 2,508 individuals (42.1%) being male. Participants in employment, compared to those not working, were more likely to be male, have a BMI ≤ 23.9 kg/m², suffer from digestive system disorders, have an education level of primary school or below, be married, reside in rural areas, currently smoke, consume alcohol, have no social activities, and have 1−3 surviving children. During the four follow-up assessments, we conducted a descriptive analysis of the relationship between the frequency of work and the FI. The results revealed a clear trend, showing that increased work engagements corresponded to a gradual reduction in the median FI (Supplemental Fig. S1). Specifically, the median FI associated with work frequencies ranging from 0 to 4 were 0.27, 0.27, 0.26, 0.18, and 0.13, respectively (P < 0.001). This trend suggests a significant negative correlation between the frequency of work and the FI over the four follow-up periods.

Association between work status and frailty

Table 2 shows the association between work status and the risk of frailty incidence. Over a 7-year follow-up period, among 5,960 initially non-frail participants, 2,105 incidents of frailty were observed. In the crude model, we observed that continuing work after retirement was significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty incidence. In Model 2, after adjusting for all covariates, it was found that continuing work after retirement had a significant protective effect against frailty, with an HR value of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.65−0.79, P < 0.001). Additionally, as shown in Table 3, even after IPTW adjustment, an inverse association between work status and frailty persisted. Continuing work after retirement continued to show a significant protective effect against frailty, with an HR value of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.65−0.79, P < 0.001). Compared to non-working, HR for agricultural work was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.68−0.84), and for non-agricultural work was 0.60 (95% CI: 0.51−0.71) as shown in Table 4. This suggested that labor in both agriculture and non-agriculture is linked to a lower incidence of frailty. Furthermore, Table 4 illustrates that while self-employment was linked to a protective impact against frailty (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.67−0.82) in comparison to not working, non-self-employment showed a stronger protective effect (HR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.54−0.75).

Subgroup analysis

The findings of the subgroup analysis performed following IPTW adjustment were shown in Supplemental Table S9. Following stratification by gender, age, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and social activities, a significant negative association between work status and frailty was observed across all subgroups. Interestingly, in people aged 65 and older, males, smokers, and those without social activities, continuing work after retirement showed a more marked protective effect against frailty (after adjusting for relevant confounders, P < 0.05 for the interactions).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis consistently supported our findings, even after excluding participants who experienced frailty in the second wave (HR = 0.81; 95% CI 0.71−0.92, P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S3). Additionally, when selecting individuals who consistently reported working in all four assessments and those consistently reporting not working, we observed an HR of 0.46 (0.37−0.56, P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S4). Furthermore, by utilizing PSM to directly create a 1:1 matched cohort with a caliper set at 0.2, we successfully matched 1,705 workers with 1,705 non-workers, creating a propensity score-matched cohort of 3,410 patients. After adjusting for all confounding factors, the HR was 0.79 (95% CI, 0.71−0.89) (Supplemental Table S5). Similarly, setting the caliper at 0.05 for 1:1 PSM resulted in a matched cohort of 3,368 individuals, with 1,684 workers and 1,684 non-workers; after correcting for confounding factors, the HR was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.70−0.87) (Supplemental Table S6). Supplemental Table S7 presents the baseline characteristics of the data used in the cross-lagged model. The findings of the standardized estimates for the relationship between work status and frailty were shown in Supplemental Table S8. The cross-lagged analyses indicated that T1 work status negatively impacted T2 frailty (β = −0.10, P < 0.001) and T2 work status negatively affected T3 frailty (β = −0.07, P < 0.001). According to these results, continuing work after retirement may help to either lower the risk of frailty or slow its advancement. These strong outcomes validate our findings even further.

Discussion

In this study, we observed a negative association between maintaining work after retirement age and the risk of frailty incidence before and after IPTW adjustment. Subgroup analyses indicated a more pronounced protective effect of continuing to work beyond retirement age among individuals aged ≥65 years, males, smokers, and those with limited social activities. Importantly, our findings remained consistent and robust across various sensitivity analyses. This study represents the first investigation into the relationship between work status and frailty incidence among individuals beyond retirement age.

Previous research has consistently shown an association between delayed retirement or continuing to work after retirement age and health outcomes [16,19,37]. For instance, several studies have suggested that late-life working may contribute to alleviating depression [38,39] and have positive effects on mental health [16,17,40,41]. Furthermore, a study examining the effects of postponing retirement on the health of older adults found that engaging in late-career occupations may benefit their physical and cognitive abilities [18]. These findings are consistent with our primary results, providing further support for the protective role of continuing work after retirement in reducing the risk of frailty incidence.

Both continued engagement in agricultural and non-agricultural work were associated with a reduced risk of frailty, with non-agricultural work demonstrating a more pronounced protective effect. Although agricultural work involves significant physical labor, which helps maintain physical activity, the higher occupational risks and intensity may diminish its protective effect. In contrast, non-agricultural work generally involves lower physical intensity and reduced occupational risks, providing a more comfortable and secure working environment, which may contribute to a more pronounced health protection effect.

Similarly, both self-employment and non-self-employment exhibited protective effects against frailty, with non-self-employment providing slightly stronger protection. This could be partially explained by the fact that independent contractors frequently take on more risk and responsibility, such as handling management concerns, making decisions, and handling substantial job-related obligations. Such elevated stress levels could potentially diminish the protective effect against frailty [42]. Consequently, more older adults could benefit from actively engaging in social activities through flexible and varied employment options, such as part-time work, short-term contracts, and remote work. In order to guarantee stable income streams and a comfortable living environment for older individuals, comprehensive social care and welfare support—which includes housing aid, health insurance, and pension insurance—are essential.

To address the baseline characteristic imbalance between the late-life working group and the non-working group, we employed IPTW to control for covariates. According to our findings, there is a significant correlation between working after retirement and a reduced risk of frailty occurrence. Maintaining work after retirement age can provide a person with a continuing social network and source of income, which can support the preservation of their physical and mental well-being. Previous studies have suggested that employment is associated with higher life satisfaction and a healthier life for older adults [43]. Furthermore, regular physical activity has been associated with better physical and cognitive function in older age and longevity [44]. The social interactions and cognitive challenges in the work environment may contribute to maintaining brain activity, thereby slowing the rate of cognitive decline and subsequently reducing the risk of frailty [45]. For many older individuals, working beyond retirement age not only ensures a stable economic source of income but also fosters social connections, alleviating feelings of loneliness and contributing to psychological well-being, ultimately lowering the risk of frailty [46]. Additionally, serum inflammatory markers are significantly elevated in older individuals [47]. Research indicates that increased inflammatory burden is associated with frailty [48,49], suggesting that chronic inflammation may be a key mechanism underlying frailty in older adults. Therefore, future studies should investigate how continuing work after retirement influences inflammatory levels and, consequently, the incidence of frailty.

In our study, we observed that the protective effect of late-life work on frailty was more pronounced within specific subgroups. More specifically, the positive impact of late-life work was more significant in participants aged ≥ 65, males, smokers, and those with fewer social activities. Several factors may contribute to this phenomenon. Firstly, among individuals aged 65 and above, late-life work may play a more crucial role in alleviating health challenges and risks commonly encountered by the older population. By providing cognitive stimulation and economic support [50], late-life work contributes to mitigating age-related health issues, thus reducing the vulnerability to frailty. Secondly, for male participants, the protective effect of late-life work could be associated with the more common economic responsibilities and social roles that males often undertake in late-life work [50]. Maintaining employment may provide steady financial support while preserving social duties, which would have a stronger protective effect against frailty. For smokers, the significant protective effect of late-life work might be attributed to the heightened health risks faced by smokers. Continuing to work may serve as motivation for smokers to adopt healthier lifestyles, reduce smoking, and increase physical activity, thereby lowering the risk of frailty [2,51]. Lastly, among individuals with fewer social activities, the protective effect of late-life work may be attributed to its structured social environment, helping alleviate feelings of loneliness and mental health issues, thereby reducing the risk of frailty [52]. These findings imply that the possible effects of retirement on the health of older people should be carefully taken into account, especially in light of China's progressive and diversified retirement plans. The impact of age and social-behavioral factors should be carefully considered when developing policies to support older people's health and quality of life, with the goal of improving their standard of living and reducing health risks.

Moreover, work in China is not just a matter of economic activity; it is also strongly associated with social roles and individual beliefs. An additional reason for the positive impact of ongoing employment on health is the fact that many older people decide to work after retirement in order to maintain their social networks and find psychological fulfillment. One of the main reasons why older individuals continue to work is economic pressure. Due to low income, older persons in economically deprived areas frequently continue to work, which maintains their economic independence and quality of life and slows the onset of frailty. Currently, China is confronted with challenges related to population aging, primarily characterized by labor market shortages and rising pension and healthcare costs. To address the substantial burden of aging, a proposal was put forward in 2021 to implement a phased increase in the statutory retirement age in the future. Based on our research findings, these measures may help further delay the onset and progression of frailty in the older adults. Future retirement plans, however, also need to take individual differences in health, economic standing, and working conditions into consideration. Creating a more inclusive and scientifically grounded retirement management system is important in order to enhance both the health of the aging population and the pension system at the same time. These results, however, can alter in various cultural and economic circumstances. Therefore, future research should compare retirement policies and work statuses across various countries and cultures to assess the generalizability and applicability of these results.

Several high-quality studies have validated the use of the FI within the CHARLS database, underscoring its applicability and robustness for the Chinese population. Prominent studies by He, Di et al. (2023) [27] and He, Di et al. (2024) [28], as well as Sun, Boran et al. (2024) [29], have utilized this FI in various contexts, including frailty progression and other health outcomes. All of these research supported the usefulness and applicability of the FI in studying frailty and associated disorders in Chinese cohorts. This validation supported the use of the FI in our study to investigate the relationship between maintaining work status post-retirement and frailty incidence.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it utilized a nationally representative sample of community-dwelling older adults in China and conducted comprehensive a seven-year follow-up on the occurrence of frailty. Secondly, it is the first to explore the relationship between working beyond the retirement age and the risk of frailty among the older population. However, the study does exhibit certain limitations. Firstly, participant information was obtained through questionnaire surveys, and screening tools such as the activity of daily living (ADL), the instrumental activity of daily living (IADL), and the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) were self-reported assessments rather than clinical diagnostic evaluations. Secondly, although we utilized IPTW to balance baseline confounding variables, IPTW can only account for observed confounders; unobserved confounders may significantly impact individuals' work decisions and frailty risk. For instance, personal health history, including the age of onset, duration, and severity of chronic diseases, is not comprehensively recorded or detailed in our dataset. However, we also utilized the E-value methodology to further evaluate the impact of unmeasured confounding on the results, and the calculated E-value was 1.84. In our study, considering that key risk factors for frailty, such as age, gender, and chronic diseases, had HRs below 1.84, it was unlikely that an unmeasured confounder with an effect large enough to overturn the observed impact of maintaining work status after retirement on frailty incidence exists. Finally, the sample size was limited because late-life workers still constitute a relatively small group. Future research could benefit from tracking the labor participation of older workers in different periods and circumstances in a larger population, providing a more in-depth and comprehensive investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, this prospective analysis suggests an inverse association between continuing work after retirement and the risk of frailty. Therefore, to improve the quality of life in the aging population, policies encouraging social engagement and extending working life should be advocated.
