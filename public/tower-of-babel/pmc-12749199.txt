Novel m5C-dependent post-transcriptional regulation of KIF11 drives lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer
Authors: Yi Zhang, Ruofan He, Jianxin Ye, Xiaoxin Xu, Xinyi Gui, Yi Wu, Xinjian Lin, Xu Lin, Kunqi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymphatic metastasis is a critical determinant of poor prognosis in gastric cancer (GC), yet the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. While kinesin family member 11 (KIF11) is known to promote lymphangiogenesis, its upstream post-transcriptional regulation in GC has not been elucidated. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that KIF11 is a direct target of 5-methylcytosine (m5C) RNA modification, which is mediated by the methyltransferase NSUN2 and recognized by the m5C reader YBX1. Mechanistically, NSUN2 deposits m5C marks at specific sites on KIF11 mRNA, particularly at C94413695 and C94413832, which are subsequently recognized by YBX1 to enhance mRNA stability and elevate KIF11 expression. Functionally, upregulated KIF11 promotes lymphangiogenesis by facilitating the Golgi localization and secretion of pro-lymphangiogenic proteins, thereby accelerating lymph node metastasis in GC. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized NSUN2/YBX1–KIF11 regulatory axis and establish m5C-dependent post-transcriptional regulation as a key mechanism linking KIF11 upregulation to lymphangiogenesis and metastatic progression in GC. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking fifth in both incidence and cancer-related mortality worldwide1. A key driver of GC lethality is its high rate of lymphatic metastasis, which is present in over 50% of patients at diagnosis and significantly worsens prognosis2–4. While many molecular pathways involved in lymphangiogenesis and metastasis have been identified5–7, the post-transcriptional regulation of key pro-metastatic genes in GC is still poorly understood.

Recent studies have highlighted the involvement of kinesin family member 11 (KIF11) in tumor progression and metastasis, particularly in lymphatic dissemination8. KIF11, a member of the kinesin superfamily, encodes the molecular motor protein Eg5, which is essential for mitotic spindle formation during cell division9,10. Beyond its role in cell division, KIF11 facilitates the intracellular transport of proteins from the Golgi apparatus to the extracellular space, a process that may be critical for tumor progression11. Clinically, mutations in KIF11 are associated with lymphatic vessel abnormalities and disease development10. Notably, elevated KIF11 expression has been observed in various cancers, where it strongly correlates with lymphatic metastasis and poor prognosis8,12,13. Furthermore, KIF11 is highly expressed in metastatic lymph nodes, suggesting a functional role in promoting lymphatic dissemination14. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling KIF11 expression in GC remain largely unknown.

Post-transcriptional modifications such as 5-methylcytosine (m5C) are emerging as critical regulators of gene expression in cancer biology. m5C is deposited on mRNAs by the methyltransferases NSUN2 and NSUN615, and read by specific RNA-binding proteins such as Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1), which stabilize target transcripts and promote translation16,17. While the NSUN2/YBX1 axis has been reported to regulate oncogenic mRNA stability, its role in governing the expression of pro-metastatic genes such as KIF11 has not been previously explored.

In this study, we provide the first evidence that NSUN2-mediated m5C modification directly regulates KIF11 expression in GC by facilitating YBX1-dependent mRNA stabilization. We show that this epitranscriptomic axis promotes GC lymphangiogenesis and lymph node metastasis, revealing a novel mechanism of post-transcriptional gene regulation in cancer and providing potential new targets for therapeutic intervention.

Results

KIF11 promotes lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer

To identify genes associated with lymph node metastasis, we collected gastric cancer tissue samples from patients with (N = 3a or N = 3b, M = 0) or without (N = 0, M = 0) lymph node metastasis in advanced gastric cancer (T = 3 or 4a) and performed high-throughput transcriptome sequencing. We retrieved 79 genes previously reported to be involved in lymph node metastasis (Table S3). Upon analyzing the sequencing data, we identified seven genes that exhibited significant differential expression. Among them, KIF11 demonstrated the most significant upregulation in primary gastric cancer tissues with lymph node metastasis compared with primary gastric cancer tissues without lymph node metastasis (FC = 2.395, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1A). We further examined the expression levels and prognostic significance of KIF11 using TCGA and KMplotter databases. As expected, KIF11 was highly expressed in gastric cancer and significantly correlated with poor prognosis (Fig. S1A, B). To investigate the functional role of KIF11 in lymphatic metastasis, we established a nude mouse hindfoot lymphatic drainage model using shRNA-mediated stable KIF11 knockdown cells and corresponding control cells, which were implanted into the footpads of nude mice. After 30 days, the mice were sacrificed, and we evaluated tumor growth, footpad tumors, and lymph node metastasis in the inguinal and popliteal lymph nodes. Notably, the inguinal and popliteal lymph nodes in the shKIF11 group were significantly smaller compared to the control group (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1C). To further confirm lymph node metastasis, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) using human mitochondria-specific antibodies to detect metastatic tumor cells in the lymph nodes. The results showed a significant reduction in the lymph node metastasis rate and a lower number of human mitochondria-positive cells in inguinal lymph nodes following KIF11 knockdown (Fig. 1C-E, Fig. S1D). Additionally, we assessed LYVE-1 expression, a marker of tumor microlymphatic vessels, in xenograft tissues. The shKIF11 tumors exhibited significantly fewer LYVE-1-positive lymphatic vessels compared to control tumors (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that KIF11 plays a crucial role in promoting lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer.

Loss of KIF11 impairs lymphangiogenic activity and invasive behavior in vitro

Given the correlation between lymph node metastasis and the invasive, migratory, and lymphangiogenic capacity of cancer cells, we further explored the functional role of KIF11 in vitro.

First, we examined the impact of KIF11 knockdown on gastric cancer cell migration and invasion using wound healing and transwell assays. In the wound healing assay, AGS and HGC27 cells transfected with siRNAs targeting KIF11 exhibited significantly wider scratch widths, indicating reduced migration ability (Fig. S2A). Similarly, in the transwell assay, Matrigel was added to the upper chamber to assess invasion ability. The results showed a significant reduction in the number of AGS and HGC27 cells that invaded the lower chamber following KIF11 inhibition (Fig. S2B). Next, we investigated the role of KIF11 in modulating HLEC migration and tube formation. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected from KIF11-knockdown AGS or HGC27 cells and used to culture HLECs. In the wound healing assay, HLECs treated with KIF11-knockdown CM exhibited significantly wider scratch widths compared to the control group (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2C). Similarly, in the transwell assay, HLECs treated with KIF11-knockdown CM exhibited significantly reduced migration (Fig. 2B, Fig. S2C). Additionally, the tube formation assay revealed a marked decrease in lymphatic tube formation when HLECs were treated with KIF11-knockdown CM (Fig. 2C, Fig. S2C). These findings suggest that KIF11 plays a key role in gastric cancer cell invasion and lymphangiogenesis. Given that KIF11 is not a secreted protein, we hypothesized that it may modulate protein secretion by affecting its localization in the Golgi apparatus11. Using mass spectrometry, we identified several lymphangiogenesis-associated proteins that interact with KIF11 (Fig. S2D). To validate these findings, we measured the extracellular protein levels of HNRNPA2B1, IGFBP3, ANXA2, and ALDOA using ELISA. The results showed that the secretion of these proteins was significantly reduced in the KIF11-knockdown cells (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed the interaction between KIF11 and these lymphangiogenic proteins in gastric cancer cells (Fig. S2E). Since GM130 is a key marker of the Golgi apparatus, we performed an additional Co-IP experiment using a GM130-specific antibody. The results showed that HNRNPA2B1, IGFBP3, ANXA2, and ALDOA had reduced binding to GM130 upon KIF11 silencing (Fig. 2E). Additionally, laser confocal microscopy demonstrated that KIF11 knockdown reduced the co-localization of IGFBP3 with the Golgi apparatus (Fig. 2F). These findings suggest that KIF11 modulates protein secretion by regulating the localization of lymphangiogenesis-associated proteins in the Golgi apparatus, ultimately promoting lymphangiogenesis.

NSUN2 and YBX1 promotes lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer

Since KIF11 mRNA upregulation may be influenced by transcription factors or post-transcriptional mechanisms, we analyzed transcription factor expression in gastric cancer samples with lymph node metastasis. No significant changes were observed in reported KIF11-associated transcription factors (Table S4), suggesting that post-transcriptional regulation might play a key role. Further analysis revealed that NSUN2 (an m5C methyltransferase) and YBX1 (an m5C reader protein) were significantly upregulated in gastric cancer patients with lymph node metastasis, highlighting their potential role in post-transcriptional regulation of KIF11 (Fig. 3A). StarBase database analysis confirmed that KIF11 mRNA interacts with NSUN2 and YBX1 (Fig. S3A). Additionally, TCGA and KMplotter analyses demonstrated that NSUN2 and YBX1 were highly expressed in gastric cancer and were significantly associated with poor prognosis (Fig. S3B, C). To further explore their functional role, we generated stable NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown gastric cancer cell lines and performed the nude mouse hindfoot lymphatic drainage model. The results showed that inguinal and popliteal lymph node sizes were significantly reduced upon NSUN2 or YBX1 silencing (Fig. 3B, Fig. S3D). IHC analysis confirmed a significant reduction in metastatic tumor cells in lymph nodes (Fig. 3C-E). Moreover, LYVE-1 staining revealed a decrease in tumor microlymphatic vessels in NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown tumors (Fig. 3F). Together, these findings demonstrate that NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate KIF11 expression via m5C modification, thereby promoting lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer.

NSUN2 or YBX1 knockdown suppresses lymphangiogenesis and invasion in vitro

To investigate the role of NSUN2 and YBX1 in the migration and lymphangiogenesis of gastric cancer cells, we assessed their effects using wound healing and transwell assays in AGS and HGC27 cells. Silencing NSUN2 or YBX1 significantly reduced wound closure and decreased the number of cells that migrated into the lower chamber in the transwell assay. However, these effects were reversed upon KIF11 overexpression (Fig. S4, S5). We further examined the role of NSUN2 and YBX1 in regulating HLEC migration and tube formation. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected from NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown AGS and HGC27 cells and used to treat HLECs. In the wound healing and transwell assays, HLECs treated with NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown CM displayed wider scratch widths and fewer migrating cells in the transwell assay compared to the control group (Fig. 4A, Fig. S6A). Furthermore, tube formation assays revealed a significant reduction in lymphatic tube formation in HLECs treated with CM from NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown cells (Fig. 4A, Fig. S6A). To determine whether KIF11 mediates these effects, we overexpressed KIF11 in NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown cells and repeated the experiments. The results showed that KIF11 overexpression restored tube formation, migration, and invasion in gastric cancer cells (Fig. 4B, Fig. S6B). These findings strongly suggest that NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate gastric cancer cell migration and lymphangiogenesis through KIF11.

(B) Rescue experiments showing the effect of KIF11 overexpression on HLEC migration and tube formation when cultured with CM from NSUN2- or YBX1-knockdown AGS cells. *p < 0.05.

Post-transcriptional regulation of KIF11 by NSUN2 and YBX1

To confirm the involvement of NSUN2 and YBX1 in KIF11 expression, we transfected siRNAs targeting NSUN2 or YBX1 into AGS and HGC27 cells. Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses revealed a significant reduction in both KIF11 protein and mRNA levels upon NSUN2 or YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 5A, B). Furthermore, RNA stability assays demonstrated that KIF11 mRNA half-life was significantly shortened following NSUN2 or YBX1 knockdown, indicating that NSUN2 and YBX1 stabilize KIF11 mRNA (Fig. 5C).

m5C modification of KIF11 by NSUN2 and YBX1

We next investigated whether NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate KIF11 through m5C modification. RIP-qPCR analysis confirmed that both NSUN2 and YBX1 directly bind to KIF11 mRNA ((Fig. S7A)). Additionally, m5C-MeRIP-qPCR confirmed the presence of m5C modification on KIF11 mRNA, which was significantly diminished upon NSUN2 or YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 6A). To identify the specific m5C modification sites on KIF11 mRNA, we queried the m5C-Atlas database and identified five candidate sites (Table S5). Among them, two sites were located in the coding sequence (CDS) at chr10:94,373,140 and chr10:94,373,323, while three sites were found in the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) at chr10:94,413,693, chr10:94,413,695, and chr10:94,413,832. To functionally validate these sites, we constructed wild-type (WT) and mutant KIF11 plasmids, in which cytosine (C) was replaced with adenine (A) at each candidate m5C site. Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that NSUN2 knockdown significantly reduced luciferase activity in KIF11-CDS-WT, C94373323A, KIF11-3’UTR-WT, and C94413693A constructs, but had no effect on C94373140A, C94413695A, and C94413832A mutants (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, NSUN2 knockdown did not alter KIF11 protein or mRNA levels in the C94373140A, C94413695A, and C94413832A mutants, indicating that these three sites are critical for NSUN2-mediated m5C modification (Fig. S7B, C). RIP-qPCR assays further confirmed that mutations in C94373140, C94413695, and C94413832 disrupted NSUN2 binding affinity to KIF11 mRNA (Fig. 6C). Additionally, m5C-MeRIP-qPCR analysis showed a significant reduction in m5C modification at these sites (Fig. 6D), confirming that NSUN2 catalyzes m5C modification at these specific KIF11 mRNA sites. To determine the role of YBX1 as an m5C reader, we examined whether YBX1 recognizes NSUN2-modified sites. Luciferase reporter assays and qPCR analysis revealed that YBX1 knockdown had no effect on luciferase activity and mRNA expression in the C94413695A and C94413832A mutants (Fig. 6E, Fig S7D). Additionally, RIP-qPCR analysis showed that YBX1 binding affinity to KIF11 mRNA was disrupted in C94413695A and C94413832A mutants (Fig. 6F). To provide further evidence that NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate KIF11 expression in a methylation-dependent manner rather than through indirect or scaffolding effects, we overexpressed wild-type NSUN2 or a catalytically inactive NSUN2 triple mutant (K190M, C271A, C321A)18 in NSUN2-depleted gastric cancer cells, and likewise overexpressed wild-type YBX1 or a reader-defective mutant (W65A)17 in YBX1-depleted cells. The results showed that wild-type NSUN2 and YBX1 both restored KIF11 protein expression, while their respective mutants failed to do so (Fig. S7E), indicating that the m5C catalytic activity of NSUN2 and the m5C-recognition function of YBX1 are both essential for KIF11 upregulation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that NSUN2-mediated m5C modification at C94413695 and C94413832 is recognized by YBX1, which stabilizes KIF11 mRNA, promoting its expression.

Correlation between KIF11 and NSUN2, YBX1, and lymphatic vessel density in gastric cancer specimens

To assess the clinical relevance of KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1 in gastric cancer lymphangiogenesis, we performed IHC staining on 22 gastric cancer tissue samples to evaluate the expression levels of KIF11, NSUN2, YBX1, and LYVE-1. The IHC results showed that the expression levels of KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1 were significantly higher in gastric cancer tissues with lymph node metastasis compared to those without lymph node metastasis (Fig. 7A, B). Consistently, microlymphatic vessel density was elevated in metastatic tissues, further supporting the role of these factors in lymphangiogenesis. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between KIF11 expression and NSUN2 or YBX1 expression (Fig. 7C). TCGA data analysis using the TIMER database further validated these findings, showing a positive correlation between NSUN2, YBX1, and KIF11 expression in gastric cancer tissues (Fig. S8). Furthermore, we observed a positive correlation between microlymphatic vessel density and KIF11, NSUN2, or YBX1 expression, indicating their role in promoting lymphangiogenesis in gastric cancer (Fig. 7D). These findings collectively support the hypothesis that NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate KIF11 expression via m5C modification, ultimately promoting gastric cancer lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis (Fig. 8).

Proposed model of NSUN2/YBX1-mediated m5C modification regulating KIF11 to promote lymphangiogenesis in gastric cancer. NSUN2 catalyzes m5C methylation of KIF11 mRNA in gastric cancer cells, which is recognized by YBX1 to enhance mRNA stability and KIF11 expression. Elevated KIF11 promotes Golgi localization and exosomal secretion of pro-lymphangiogenic proteins (IGFBP3, ANXA2, ALDOA, hnRNPA2B1), which activate lymphangiogenic signaling in lymphatic endothelial cells, including PROX1 promoter activation, thereby driving lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis.

Discussion

Lymphangiogenesis plays a crucial role in tumor lymphatic metastasis19,20. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that KIF11 is highly expressed in gastric cancer specimens with lymph node metastasis. Under physiological conditions, KIF11 is involved in lymphatic vessel formation, and its mutations can lead to lymphatic vessel abnormalities and genetic disorders such as MLCRD syndrome (microcephaly, primary lymphatic dysplasia, and chorioretinopathy) and CDMMR syndrome (chorioretinopathy, microcephaly, and mental retardation)10. Additionally, KIF11 mutations have been identified in patients with chorioretinopathy and lymphedema, highlighting its role in maintaining retinal and lymphatic structures21. In cancer, KIF11 overexpression has been linked to lymph node metastasis in multiple malignancies and is associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, KIF11 activates the ERBB2/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to promote cell proliferation22 and inhibits apoptosis via the p53 pathway23, which may enhance tumor cell survival within the lymphatic system. Given its clinical significance, KIF11 has been explored as a potential drug target for cancer therapy24. However, its precise role in lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis remains largely unknown. Due to its close association with lymphatic system disorders and tumor lymphatic metastasis, research has focused on understanding the regulation of KIF11 expression. While transcriptional regulation of KIF11 has been reported25,26, the post-transcriptional regulation of KIF11, particularly the role of RNA methylation, remains largely unexplored.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of m5C modification in tumor metastasis and lymph node dissemination. For example, m5C modification of mitochondrial tRNA has been implicated in lymph node metastasis in head and neck cancer, and inhibiting its modification significantly reduces metastatic spread27. In serous ovarian cancer, high m5C methylation levels are positively correlated with tumor metastasis28. In cholangiocarcinoma, NSUN2-catalyzed m5C modification enhances the interaction between NKILA (NF-kappa B interacting lncRNA) and YBX1, thereby promoting lymph node metastasis29. However, the role of m5C modification in gastric cancer lymphatic metastasis, particularly in the regulation of KIF11, has not been well characterized.

In this study, we investigated the role of KIF11 in lymphatic metastasis of gastric cancer, focusing on its biological significance and regulatory mechanisms. Our results confirmed that KIF11 is highly expressed in gastric cancer specimens with lymph node metastasis. Using the hindfoot lymphatic drainage model, we demonstrated that KIF11 enhances lymphatic metastasis of gastric cancer cells, while immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of footpad tumors revealed that KIF11 promotes lymphangiogenesis. At the cellular level, KIF11 knockdown reduced gastric cancer cell migration, invasion, and lymphangiogenesis, as evidenced by wound healing, transwell, and tube formation assays. These findings prompted further investigation into KIF11’s role in lymphangiogenic protein secretion. Using mass spectrometry, we identified HNRNPA2B1, IGFBP3, ANXA2, and ALDOA as potential KIF11-interacting proteins. Each of these proteins has been previously implicated in lymphangiogenesis and metastasis. HNRNPA2B1 promotes lymphangiogenesis by mediating TBK1 mRNA translation30 and enhancing extracellular vesicle (EV) release of circTLCD4-RWDD331. IGFBP3, regulated by FASN, has been shown to accelerate lymphangiogenesis in cervical cancer32. Both ANXA233 and ALDOA34 have been associated with lymph node metastasis in various cancers. Our findings indicate that KIF11 knockdown inhibits the secretion of these proteins and reduces their binding to and co-localization with the Golgi apparatus, suggesting that KIF11 facilitates lymphangiogenesis by modulating the secretion of lymphangiogenesis-associated proteins.

To further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing KIF11 expression, we investigated the role of NSUN2 and YBX1. Our results confirmed that both NSUN2 and YBX1 promote lymphatic metastasis and lymphangiogenesis in gastric cancer, as demonstrated by the hindfoot lymphatic drainage model. At the cellular level, silencing NSUN2 or YBX1 inhibited gastric cancer cell invasion, migration, and lymphangiogenesis, while KIF11 overexpression rescued these effects. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that NSUN2 and YBX1 regulate KIF11 expression by stabilizing its mRNA. Knockdown of NSUN2 or YBX1 significantly reduced KIF11 mRNA stability and expression levels in AGS and HGC27 cells. Combining m5C-Atlas database analysis with luciferase reporter assays, we identified three key m5C modification sites on KIF11 mRNA (C94373140, C94413695, and C94413832), catalyzed by NSUN2. Importantly, YBX1 specifically recognized the m5C modifications at C94413695 and C94413832, thereby enhancing KIF11 mRNA stability and promoting its expression. Interestingly, the m5C site at C94373140 was not recognized by YBX1, suggesting that NSUN2 may co-regulate KIF11 mRNA with other m5C reader proteins. This finding highlights the possibility that different m5C modifications within the same mRNA transcript may be regulated by distinct reader proteins, warranting further investigation into the context-dependent roles of m5C modifications in RNA stability and translation.

Despite the mechanistic strengths and multi-level validation in this study, we acknowledge certain limitations. The number of patient tissue samples used for immunohistochemical analysis was relatively small, which may affect the robustness and generalizability of the observed correlations between NSUN2, YBX1, KIF11, and lymphatic vessel density. Furthermore, although we confirmed key expression patterns using public databases such as TCGA and KMplotter, independent validation in larger, clinically annotated patient cohorts is warranted to fully establish the prognostic and therapeutic relevance of the NSUN2/YBX1–KIF11 axis in gastric cancer. Future prospective studies involving multi-center clinical samples will be crucial to validate the translational potential of these findings.

It may be noteworthy that the translational relevance of our findings highlights the NSUN2/YBX1–KIF11 axis as a promising therapeutic target in gastric cancer. KIF11 is a mitotic kinesin essential for bipolar spindle formation, and its druggable ATPase domain has made it a focus of antimitotic drug development. Several KIF11 inhibitors, including ispinesib (SB-715992) and filanesib (ARRY-520), have undergone clinical trials in solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, demonstrating manageable toxicity profiles and some clinical efficacy35–37. While these agents primarily act by blocking cell proliferation, our findings suggest that KIF11 also promotes lymphangiogenesis by regulating the Golgi-mediated secretion of pro-lymphangiogenic factors. Thus, repurposing or optimizing KIF11 inhibitors for patients with lymph node–positive gastric cancer may offer dual anti-proliferative and anti-metastatic benefits. In parallel, targeting the upstream m5C RNA methylation machinery offers complementary therapeutic opportunities. NSUN2 has been identified as an oncogenic methyltransferase in multiple cancers, and its depletion sensitizes tumor cells to chemotherapy and oxidative stress17,38. Although specific NSUN2 inhibitors are not yet clinically available, early-stage efforts to screen or design small-molecule NSUN2 inhibitors are underway39. In addition, RNA-based approaches such as siRNAs or antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting NSUN2 or YBX1 could be used to modulate m5C-dependent post-transcriptional networks. Delivery via lipid nanoparticles or GalNAc-conjugated carriers—technologies already used in RNA therapeutics—could enhance tumor selectivity and reduce systemic toxicity40,41. Given that the NSUN2/YBX1–KIF11 axis plays a central role in promoting GC cell migration, lymphangiogenesis, and lymphatic dissemination, its inhibition may provide a valuable strategy to block metastasis and complement current cytotoxic or anti-angiogenic therapies. Together, our study not only reveals a novel m5C-driven mechanism of lymphatic metastasis but also proposes actionable targets within this regulatory cascade that merit further preclinical and translational exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that KIF11 promotes lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer by regulating the secretion of lymphangiogenesis-associated proteins through its interaction with the Golgi apparatus. Additionally, we identify KIF11 as a direct target of NSUN2/YBX1-mediated m5C modification, in which NSUN2 catalyzes m5C methylation at C94373140, C94413695, and C94413832, while YBX1 recognizes C94413695 and C94413832 to enhance KIF11 mRNA stability. These findings provide novel insights into the RNA modification-driven regulation of lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer. Moreover, targeting KIF11, NSUN2, or YBX1 may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing lymph node metastasis in gastric cancer, offering new avenues for clinical intervention.

Methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The AGS and HGC27 gastric cancer cell lines, along with human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs), were purchased from the Shanghai Institutes of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were maintained in their respective recommended growth media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ST30-3302; PAN Seratech, Eidenbach, Bavaria, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Generation of stable shRNA knockdown cell lines

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1, along with a non-targeting negative control (NC), were chemically synthesized and cloned into the AgeI and EcoRI sites of the pLKO.1-puro vector (8453; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA). Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting the recombinant plasmids with lentiviral packaging plasmids p-VSVG and p-PHR into HEK293T cells. Viral supernatants were collected and used to infect HepG2 cells. Forty-eight hours post-infection, cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (A1113802; Invitrogen) for 72 h to establish stable knockdown cell lines. The efficiency of KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1 knockdown was validated by Western blot analysis. The sequences of shRNAs are listed in Table S1.

Hindfoot lymphatic drainage model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval number: IACUC FJMU 2023-Y-1139) and strictly followed the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. The study involving animals was conducted following the Basel Declaration. Male nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were used for the lymphatic metastasis model. A total of 1 × 10⁶ cells suspended in 20 µL of PBS were injected into the footpads of the mice. After 30 days, mice (weighing 26.10 ± 1.16 g) were euthanized by exposure to a gradually rising concentration of carbon dioxide in a dedicated chamber, with a flow rate displacing 30% of the chamber volume per minute. Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method to ensure death. And primary tumors in the footpads, as well as lymph nodes (LNs) from the popliteal and inguinal regions, were harvested. Tumors and LNs were sectioned and subjected to immunohistochemical staining using anti-KIF11, anti-NSUN2, anti-YBX1, and anti-mouse LYVE-1 antibodies (ab218535, Abcam, Shanghai, China). The number of LYVE-1-positive lymphatic vessels was quantified, and metastatic tumor cells were detected using an anti-human mitochondria antibody. The LN metastasis ratio was calculated accordingly.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed following standard protocols. Briefly, freshly excised tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at room temperature and embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval using sodium citrate buffer. After blocking for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (PV-9001/2; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) and developed using a DAB substrate kit (ZLI-9018; ZSGB-BIO). Staining intensity was assessed using a 4-tiered scale based on color intensity in 200 × fields: 0 = no staining, 1 = weak (light yellow), 2 = moderate (yellow–brown), 3 = strong (brown).

RNA interference

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1 were synthesized (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) and transfected into AGS and HGC27 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000008; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). A non-targeting siRNA (siRNA-NC) was used as a control. The sequences of siRNAs are listed in Table S1.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

AGS cells were lysed on ice using Western/IP lysis buffer (P0013; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010; MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) and PMSF (ST505, Beyotime Biotechnology). Cleared lysates (1 mg) were incubated with 20 µL Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (HY-K0202; MCE, Shanghai, China) and specific antibodies. Immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted and analyzed via Western blotting.

Western blot analysis

AGS and HGC27 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B; Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors and PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (E112; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Fifty micrograms of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (IPV00010; Millipore), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (K720; Amresco). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (7074/7076; CST, Boston, MA, USA) for 1 h. Detection was performed using an ECL system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The primary antibodies used in this study included anti-NSUN2 (20,854–1-AP, ProteinTech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-YBX1 (20,339–1-AP, ProteinTech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-GAPDH (2118S, Cell Signaling Technology, Shanghai, China), and anti-KIF11 (ab124930, Abcam, Shanghai, China). Original full-length blot images with membrane edges are not available due to the need for multiple antibodies for incubation. All original blot image files used for analysis are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Measurement of extracellular protein levels by ELISA

The levels of HNRNPA2B1, IGFBP3, ANXA2, and ALDOA in culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits (Jianglai, Shanghai, China; Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Confocal microscopy

AGS cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. Cells were incubated overnight with GM130 (F0080, Selleck, Shanghai, China) and IGFBP3 (10,189–2-AP, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China) antibodies, followed by secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor® 488 or Alexa Fluor® 594 (CST). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1002; Beyotime). Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM-410 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Preparation of conditioned medium

AGS and HGC27 cells were transfected individually with siRNAs targeting KIF11, NSUN2, or YBX1 following the method described earlier. After transfection, cells were incubated in serum-free medium for 24 h, and the culture supernatants were collected as conditioned medium (CM). CM was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min to remove debris and then stored at − 80 °C until further use. For subsequent assays involving HLECs, CM was used as a substitute for endothelial cell medium (1001; Science Cell, San Diego, California, USA).

Wound healing assay

A total of 5 × 105 cells per well were seeded in a 6-well culture plate. After 12 h of incubation, when the cells reached near confluence, a 200-μL sterile pipette tip was used to create a scratch across the monolayer. The wells were washed three times with PBS to remove detached cells and then replaced with fresh CM supplemented with 2% FBS. Images of the wound were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h, and the gap distance was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA)[Available at: https://imagej.net/ij/].

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using transwell inserts (3422; 24-well, 8-μm pore size; BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA). For migration assays, 4 × 105 cells were suspended in 200 μL of serum-free CM and seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 600 μL of CM supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were gently removed with a cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. For invasion assays, Matrigel (40,183, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was added to the upper chamber before seeding the cells. The number of migrated or invaded cells was counted under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Tube formation assay

To assess lymphangiogenesis in vitro, 50 μL of Matrigel (356,234; BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) was pre-coated onto 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 1 × 105 HLECs per well were cultured in CM for 4 h. Tube formation was visualized under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the total number of formed tubes was quantified from five random fields at 100 × magnification.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HCC cells using TRIzol reagent (15,596,026; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701; Accurate Biology, Changsha, China). GAPDH served as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

RNA stability assay

To assess RNA stability, AGS and HGC27 cells were treated with Actinomycin D (M4881; Abmole, Shanghai, China) at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL for 0, 2, 4, and 6 h. Total RNA was extracted, and RT-qPCR was performed to quantify KIF11 mRNA levels. The degradation rate (K) was calculated using the equation: NtN0 = e−κt, where N₀ represents the initial RNA quantity, Nₜ represents the RNA quantity at time t, and κ is the degradation rate. The RNA half-life (t₁/₂) was determined as: t1/2 = ln2/κ.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR

RIP was performed using the RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (P0102; Geneseed, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoprecipitation was conducted using anti-NSUN2, anti-YBX1, and rabbit IgG (A7016; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) as a negative control. The co-precipitated RNAs were analyzed via RT-qPCR.

m5C-MeRIP-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from AGS cells using TRIzol reagent, followed by m5C RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) using a GenSeq® m5C MeRIP Kit (GS-ET-003; CloudSeq Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Immunoprecipitated RNA was subjected to RT-qPCR as described earlier.

Luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type or mutated KIF11 3’UTR or CDS regions were cloned into the XhoI and SalI sites of the pmirGLO luciferase reporter plasmid. Cells were co-transfected with the reporter constructs and specific siRNAs targeting NSUN2 or YBX1. After 48 h, the cells were lysed, and intracellular luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (DL101; Vazyme, Nanjing, JiangSu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was detected using a luminometer (Berthold Technologies, Oak Ridge, TN, USA).

Clinical samples

A total of 11 paired gastric cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of of Fujian Medical University (approval number: 202–166), and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were pathologically diagnosed with primary gastric adenocarcinoma, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using anti-KIF11, anti-NSUN2, anti-YBX1, and anti-LYVE-1 (ab219556; Abcam, Shanghai, China) to evaluate protein expression and microlymphatic vessel density.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) [Available at: https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics]. GraphPad Prism (version 7, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) [Available at: https://www.graphpad.com] was used for data visualization. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by LSD-t post hoc tests. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted on eight tissue samples, including four GC tissues with lymph node metastasis and four without (N = 0, M = 0). RNA-seq was performed by BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. Raw sequencing data were processed using Trim Galore for adapter removal and read cleaning. Reads were aligned to the GRCh37 human genome reference using HISAT2, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using StringTie (version 1.3.6, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD, USA) [Available at: https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie] and DESeq2 (EMBL, Heidelberg, Germany) [Available at: https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html]. Data regarding RNA-binding proteins and m5C modification sites were retrieved from ENCORI and m5C-Atlas, respectively. Additionally, data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database were analyzed to assess the differential expression of KIF11, NSUN2, and YBX1 in normal and GC tissues, as well as their correlation in GC progression. The survival analyses for KIF11 and YBX1 were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter platform (https://www.kmplot.com/analysis) by integrating five publicly available gastric cancer datasets: GSE14210, GSE15459, GSE22377, GSE29272, and GSE51105. For NSUN2, survival analysis was performed using GSE15459, GSE22377, and GSE51105, as these datasets contain available NSUN2 probe data.
