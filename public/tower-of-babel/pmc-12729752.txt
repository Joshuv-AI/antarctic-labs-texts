Successful conversion of percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage to endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy combined with antegrade stenting for a malignant distal biliary obstruction due to invasive intraductal papillary mucinous carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Taiji Yoshimoto, Takeshi Takajo, Hiroshi Takihara, Ryuichi Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Blood test results revealed increased levels of white blood cell counts and hepatobiliary enzymes. Background

Although endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the first-line treatment for malignant distal biliary obstruction, when ERCP cannot be successful, endoscopic ultrasound-guided rendezvous and endoscopic ultrasound-guided antegrade stenting can be performed as salvage [1–4]. However, in cases of inaccessible papilla due to duodenal obstruction, percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) and endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD), including endoscopic ultrasound-guided choledochoduodenostomy (EUS-CDS) and endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS-HGS), are alternative treatment options [5–8]. Among EUS-BD techniques, EUS-HGS has extensive indications because biliary access can be obtained from the stomach even when duodenal obstruction occurs. Although EUS-HGS is gradually becoming more common, it is currently performed only at high-volume centers owing to its technical challenges and the high risk of complications [9–12]. Some studies have reported that performing PTBD initially relieves cholangitis and secures a biliary drainage route in terms of safety, after which it can be converted to EUS-HGS; however, there are limited case reports describing the conversion of percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD) to EUS-HGS [13–16]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reported cases of conversion of PTGBD to EUS-HGS combined with antegrade stenting (EUS-HGAS).

Herein, we describe a case in which ERCP could not be performed owing to severe duodenal stenosis caused by duodenal invasion of an intraductal papillary mucinous carcinoma (IPMC), and PTGBD was performed for biliary drainage before conversion to EUS-HGAS.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old Japanese woman was referred to our hospital with jaundice and loss of appetite. She had no relevant past medical history, family history, or psychosocial history. Physical examination revealed upper abdominal tenderness with scleral icterus and jaundice. Laboratory examinations revealed the following data: white blood cell count 10,000/μL, total bilirubin level 11.8 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase level 239 U/L, alanine aminotransferase level 57 U/L, alkaline phosphatase level 1031 U/L, and CA19-9 level 4168 U/mL. Abdominal contrast computed tomography (CT) disclosed a pancreatic tumor composed of a cystic and solid component, measuring approximately 5.5 cm, that caused a malignant distal biliary obstruction (Fig. 1). An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy demonstrated that the tumor had invaded the duodenal bulb and second portion, causing severe duodenal stenosis (Fig. 2). The gastroscope was passed through the duodenal bulb with difficulty, accompanied by mild bleeding due to contact, and the papilla could not be identified owing to severe infiltration of the tumor. We performed a biopsy from the duodenal bulb, on the basis of which the histopathological diagnosis was villotubular adenocarcinoma. The final diagnosis was invasive unresectable IPMC. We could not perform ERCP owing to severe duodenal stenosis. Despite the severe duodenal stenosis, there were no symptoms of gastric outlet obstruction. Because PTBD was unsuccessful owing to insufficient dilation of intrahepatic bile ducts, we performed PTGBD for biliary drainage. Following the successful placement of the PTGBD catheter, the patient’s symptoms resolved, and her white blood cell count and hepatobiliary enzyme levels improved. Conversion of PTGBD to EUS-HGS combined with antegrade stent placement was performed after 2 weeks. First, contrast medium diluted with normal saline was injected into the gallbladder through the PTGBD catheter, and the swollen gallbladder and expanded intrahepatic bile ducts were confirmed by fluoroscopic imaging (Fig. 3A). The expanded B2 intrahepatic bile duct was punctured by a 19-gauge fine needle aspiration (FNA) needle (EZ Shot 3 Plus; Olympus Medical Systems) under EUS guidance (Fig. 3B). We then inserted a 0.025-inch guidewire (EndoSelector; Boston Scientific, MA, USA) through the FNA needle and advanced it beyond the distal biliary obstruction and major papilla to the duodenal lumen with corresponding movements of the ERCP catheter (MTW Endoskopie, Wesel, Germany). The location of the biliary obstruction and major papilla was confirmed by a cholangiogram (Fig. 3C). Next, the puncture tract was dilated using a Tornus ES (Olympus Co, Tokyo, Japan). Finally, an uncovered self-expandable metal stent (6Fr, 8 mm × 10 cm, Niti-S Large Cell SR Slim Delivery; TaeWoong Medical, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) was placed in an antegrade manner across the distal biliary obstruction and major papilla, and a 7Fr dedicated HGS plastic stent (7 Fr, 14 cm, Through & Pass Type IT, Gadelius Medical Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) was placed across the hepaticogastrostomy tract (Fig. 3D). The procedure was completed without any complications. The patient showed a good clinical course and was subsequently discharged after removal of the PTGBD catheter. Discussion and conclusions

Although EUS-HGS has increasingly been adopted as a rescue procedure when ERCP is unsuccessful in patients with acute cholangitis secondary to biliary obstruction, it is technically challenging and has a high rate of adverse events [17, 18]. Oh et al. suggested that more than 33 cases are required to acquire technical proficiency for EUS-HGS to reach a stabilization level in terms of procedure time and adverse events [19]. Some studies have also indicated that, when EUS-HGS is performed at low-volume centers, the technical success rate is lower and the adverse event rate is higher than those when EUS-HGS is performed at high-volume centers [20, 21]. Moreover, the incidence of adverse events associated with EUS-HGS has been reported to range from 14.9% to 23.8% [9, 10]. Consequently, EUS-HGS is primarily performed at high-volume centers. Conversely, PTBD and PTGBD are established treatments that are widely available at the majority of centers. Although there are some reports showing that performing initial PTBD before conversion to EUS-HGS can reduce the rate of adverse events, there are limited case reports describing initial PTGBD before conversion to EUS-HGS [13–16]. In addition, we could convert to EUS-HGAS because the latter technique can achieve a longer time to recurrent biliary obstruction than EUS-HGS alone [22–24].

In our patient, we used a 7Fr dedicated HGS plastic stent rather than a metal stent for EUS-HGS because this stent is easy to deploy with a lower risk of stent migration [25, 26].

There are several advantages in performing initial PTGBD before EUS-HGS. First, PTGBD can resolve the patient’s cholangitis before performing EUS-HGS. Second, injecting the contrast medium through the PTGBD catheter before performing EUS-HGS can help in expanding the intrahepatic bile duct, which facilitates puncture under EUS guidance. Third, the bile duct cholangiogram obtained through the PTGBD route can help in identifying the optimal puncture site in the intrahepatic bile duct and aid manipulation of the guidewire. Finally, even when EUS-HGS fails, the risk of adverse events, including bile peritonitis and cholangitis, can be reduced because the biliary drainage is secured through the PTGBD route. Therefore, this approach may be useful for endoscopists early in their learning curve for EUS-HGS—as it simplifies the EUS-HGS procedure—and PTGBD can act as a salvage drainage route when adverse events occur. We believe that this approach is useful when both PTBD and EUS-HGS cannot be performed owing to insufficient dilation of intrahepatic bile ducts.

However, this approach also has a few disadvantages. First, performing both PTGBD and EUS-HGS will lead to longer hospital stays and higher medical expenses. Moreover, despite being temporary, the external drainage catheter can impair the quality of life of the patient.

In conclusion, conversion of PTGBD to EUS-HGAS is feasible and safe for patients who cannot undergo ERCP owing to a malignant duodenal obstruction; nevertheless, large-scale studies are required to confirm the efficacy of this approach.
