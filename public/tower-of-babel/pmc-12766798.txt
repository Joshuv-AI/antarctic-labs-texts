Concordance between presenting features and relapse in granulomatosis with polyangiitis: implications for risk assessment and counselling
Authors: Michele Iudici, Zubeyir Salis, Pascal Cohen, Antoine Néel, Achille Aouba, Francois Lifermann, Marc Ruivard, Olivier Aumaître, Bernard Bonnotte, Maxime Samson, Francois Maurier, Thomas Le Gallou, Eric Hachulla, Alexandre Karras, Vincent Cottin, Noemie Jourde-Chiche, Jean-François Viallard, Amandine Perier, Pascal Godmer, Thomas Quéméneur, Claire de Moreuil, Loïc Guillevin, Benjamin Terrier, Xavier Puéchal, French Vasculitis Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the concordance between organ involvement at diagnosis and relapse in granulomatosis with polyangiitis and factors associated with new disease features at relapse.MethodsData from a national database of newly diagnosed patients was analysed. Clinical features were recorded at diagnosis and relapse, grouped by organ system. ORs and HRs were used to assess associations between baseline features and first relapse. Factors independently associated with new organ involvement at relapse were identified using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 795 patients (median follow-up 3.5 years), 394 (50%) relapsed; organ involvement at relapse was available for 376 patients. Relapses most often affected ear, nose and throat (ENT), lungs and kidneys. Organ involvement at diagnosis was associated with a higher likelihood of relapse in the same organ: eyes (OR 6.69), lungs (OR 3.35), kidneys (OR 3.58), nervous system (OR 2.90), and mucocutaneous (OR 4.53). Major manifestations associated with a higher likelihood of recurrence were scleritis, pachymeningitis, subglottic stenosis and worsening renal function. For 56% of patients, the first relapse affected only the initially involved organs. Of the 165 patients with new organ manifestations, these were rarely isolated (n=34) and usually occurred alongside involvement of at least one previously affected organ (n=131). In multivariable analysis, systemic, ENT and lung manifestations at diagnosis were associated with a lower risk of new organ disease at relapse.ConclusionAlthough new features can still emerge, organ involvement at diagnosis is associated with a higher likelihood of relapse in the same organ.Keywords: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Vasculitis, Epidemiology

Introduction

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) is a granulomatous vasculitis involving small-to-medium vessel, frequently associated with the positivity of antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs).1 GPA is a multisystem disease that primarily affects the respiratory tract and kidneys, but may also involve the musculoskeletal system, skin, nervous system and cardiovascular (CV) system.2 In recent years, several new treatment options have become available, significantly improving disease control.3,5 However, GPA remains a severe vasculitis that requires continuous monitoring.2 6 7 Despite advancements in therapy, the disease still carries a high risk of relapse. About 50–65% of patients experience at least one flare during the course of the disease.2 It is crucial to be aware of this risk and recognise the early warning signs. This enables clinicians to educate patients and initiate treatment in a timely manner.

One of the main concerns for patients and clinicians is not only understanding the likelihood of disease recurrence, but also being able to anticipate the pattern of relapse, particularly in terms of which organs may be affected. A key question is whether the clinical features present at diagnosis tend to recur during relapse or if new manifestations typically emerge. Most existing studies do not specifically address this question. Instead, they provide general information about the overall risk of relapse and identify associated risk factors,8 9 often grouping together both microscopic polyangiitis and GPA.10

To address this gap, we conducted a study on a large cohort of patients with GPA, consecutively enrolled in a national database. Our aim was to evaluate the concordance between clinical features at diagnosis and those observed during relapse and to characterise patients more likely to develop new disease features at relapse.

Methods

Patients

We studied newly diagnosed patients with GPA from the French Vasculitis Study Group (FVSG) registry, enrolled between May 1983 and April 2018. All patients fulfilled the 2022 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) criteria11 and/or revised Chapel Hill nomenclature.12 For the patients to be classified as fulfilling those criteria, their entire clinical histories were considered. Only newly diagnosed patients, entered in the database at diagnosis, and followed up for ≥6 months or who died within 6 months of entry, were included. The database collects patient’s information at baseline, thereafter every 6 months or in case of refractory/relapsing disease requiring treatment escalation. Patients were enrolled in randomised controlled trials613,18 or received standard-of-care therapy. Agents used to induce and maintain disease remission were recorded. Details on the main features of patients enrolled and their main outcomes over time have been described elsewhere.2

Definitions

Disease activity was assessed at diagnosis and each visit using the original 1994 Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score (BVAS).19 For patients enrolled before its publication, BVAS was calculated retrospectively. According to the EULAR recommendations,20 relapse was defined as the reoccurrence or new onset of disease attributable to active vasculitis; remission as the absence of disease activity attributable to GPA manifestations for >3 consecutive months, corresponding to BVAS=0. According to that definition, patients in remission could still be taking immunosuppressant or prednisone. Clinical manifestations at diagnosis and at relapse were grouped into the following categories: systemic, mucocutaneous, ear, nose and throat (ENT), lungs, CV, gastrointestinal, kidney, neurological. Major manifestations were defined according to the BVAS19 and included the following: scleritis, hearing loss, mesenteric ischaemia, alveolar haemorrhage, haematuria, renal function worsening (increased creatinine >30%), tracheobronchial involvement, pachymeningitis, stroke, cranial nerve palsy, peripheral nerve involvement.

Outcomes and follow-up

For each patient, organ involvement and major manifestations were assessed at baseline and at each follow-up visit. The main outcome was the assessment of the concordance between initial presenting features and those observed at the time of relapse.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics included the number (%) for categorical variables and means±SD or medians (IQR) for continuous variables. We estimated the proportion of patients, based on the presence or absence of a given presenting feature at diagnosis, who developed organ involvement at relapse across various organs/systems (eg, the proportion of patients with or without renal involvement at diagnosis who experienced a relapse involving the skin or kidneys or CV system). To examine associations between presenting features and relapse, we used two complementary approaches: (1) ORs and (2) HRs. The primary analysis evaluated organ concordance between baseline and first relapse using ORs. This model was chosen because the research question is cross-sectional at the time of first relapse among relapsing patients. Cox proportional hazards models were used as secondary analyses to explore whether baseline organ involvement was associated with time-to-first-relapse.

For ORs, we constructed 2×2 contingency tables to estimate the odds of organ involvement or major organ manifestation at the first relapse, based on its baseline status. These analyses were restricted to patients who experienced a relapse of the disease. ORs with 95% CIs were calculated, and statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests. McNemar’s test was used to evaluate within-patient changes between diagnosis and relapse of the disease.

For each baseline–relapse organ pair, we constructed a 2×2 table and calculated the conditional probabilities of relapse organ involvement as P (relapse=1 | baseline=0) and P(relapse=1 | baseline=1), applying a 0.5 continuity correction when necessary. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the concordance models stratified by ANCA subtype (ANCA-proteinase 3+ (PR3+) and ANCA-myeloperoxidase+ (MPO+)), and within three diagnostic eras (1983–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2018). Eras were defined according to major changes in diagnostic tools and treatment strategies, such as the introduction of BVAS scoring in routine practice (around 2000) and the wider use of rituximab from 2010 onward. To estimate the time-to-event association between baseline organ involvement or major manifestation involvement and the first relapse for each specific organ or major organ involvement, we calculated HRs, using Cox proportional hazards models. These time-to-event models included all patients, regardless of whether they relapsed, and accounted for censoring at last follow-up. The models were adjusted for age, sex and ANCA status. For both approaches, multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR). We also identified among the relapsing patients those who developed or did not develop a new organ manifestation at first relapse and performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify features associated with the risk of developing new disease feature at first relapse. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Because relapse with new organ involvement and relapse without new organ involvement are mutually exclusive outcomes, we additionally performed time-to-event competing-risk analyses. Three states were defined: no relapse, relapse without new organ involvement and relapse with new organ involvement. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted for each relapse type, treating the alternative relapse type as the competing event. A two-sided p value ≤0.05 after adjustment was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics at diagnosis

The study included 795 patients with GPA (mean age, 53.2±16.3 years), of whom 446 (56.0%) were male. Their main demographic characteristics and clinical manifestations are described elsewhere.2 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria were fulfilled by 659 patients.11 The main presenting clinical manifestations were ENT (80.3%) and lung (67.6%) involvement; 56.2% of patients had renal involvement, of whom 5.8% required haemodialysis. Of the 795 patients who were followed up for a median of 3.6 years (IQR 1.8–6.6), 394 (50%) experienced at least one relapse and 147 (23.5%) experienced more than one relapse. The first relapse occurred after a median of 18 months (IQR 12–36). Information about organ involvement at the time of relapse was available for 376 patients. Their main features at diagnosis are reported in table 1. First relapse affected mainly ENT (n=189), kidneys (n=139), lungs (n=133), nervous system (n=64). The most common major manifestations at the time of the first relapse were haematuria (n=69), peripheral neuropathy (n=38), alveolar haemorrhage (n=31) and worsened renal function (n=22).

We found strong concordance between the presenting features and the clinical manifestations at first relapse (table 2). Several organ systems exhibited a statistically significant tendency to recur on first relapse, particularly the eyes, mucocutaneous tissue, kidneys, lungs and nervous system, which remain significant after FDR correction. Some other baseline organ involvements were associated with increased or decreased odds of first relapse in different organs, but they did not remain significant after FDR correction (table 2 and online supplemental table 1). Both ANCA-stratified and era-stratified analyses demonstrated similar patterns of organ-level concordance across the three periods, although the CIs were wider in the smaller MPO-ANCA subgroup and in the earliest years (online supplemental tables 2–6)

Comparison of patients with or without new organ manifestations at first relapse

Among relapsing patients, 211/376 (56.1%) experienced a first relapse limited to the organs affected at diagnosis, while 165 (43.9%) developed at least one newly involved organ (figure 2). Table 1 compares the main characteristics of patients with versus without new organ manifestations at first relapse. Of the 165 patients with new organ involvement, 131 (79%) had both new organ involvement and recurrence in an organ affected at baseline, while 34 (21%) had relapse confined exclusively to one or more previously unaffected organ(s) (figure 2). These 34 patients accounted for 9% of all relapsing patients and 4% of the entire study cohort. In this subgroup, relapses most frequently involved a single organ: ENT (n=5), nervous system (n=4), lung (n=4), kidney (n=3) or eye (n=2). The remaining cases presented with multiorgan involvement. Detailed organ-level data for each patient is available in online supplemental table 9. In the multivariable analysis, the presence of systemic signs/symptoms, as well as ENT and lung involvement at diagnosis, was associated with a lower risk of developing a new organ involvement (table 4). The Fine–Gray competing-risk model confirmed these associations (online supplemental table 11). ANCA status, induction remission treatment and the number of organs involved at baseline were not significant predictors.

Discussion

Here, we have described the patterns of relapse in patients with GPA and provided information that can be easily interpreted by patients regarding the likelihood of experiencing specific organ involvement or major manifestations at relapse, based on initial presenting features. Our findings show that, although new manifestations can develop during a flare, relapse more frequently represents a recurrence of clinical features already present at diagnosis. New organ manifestations without concurrent involvement of previously affected organs were rare, occurring in less than 5% of all patients and less than 10% of those who relapsed. The highest concordance between initial presentation and relapse was observed in cases involving eyes, mucocutaneous tissues, kidneys, lungs and nervous system. The same pattern was observed for major manifestations such as scleritis, alveolar haemorrhage, pachymeningitis and tracheobronchial disease.

The literature on relapse in GPA has primarily focused on quantifying the risk of flare and its associated risk factors. For example, studies have shown that PR3-ANCA positivity, younger age and upper respiratory tract involvement are associated with a higher risk of relapse.21,23 Others have examined the predictive value of ANCA persistence or reappearance,24 25 or the impact of induction regimens on relapse rates.6 However, to the best of our knowledge, these studies have not addressed whether patients are likely to relapse with the same manifestations or develop new organ involvement, and few have distinguished between minor and major relapses. While the available information has certainly improved our ability to stratify patients according to their overall risk of flare, it has lacked the granularity needed to inform patients about their individual likelihood of developing serious relapses, such as alveolar haemorrhage, glomerulonephritis or central nervous system involvement during relapse. Furthermore, it is very difficult to give patients precise answers about which symptoms they would experience if they relapsed or which symptoms should be considered warning signs because there is no precise data in the literature.

In our previous analysis of approximately 800 patients with GPA from the FVSG,2 we provided a general description of patterns of disease relapse. We observed that nearly half of the relapses involved the same organ systems initially affected, with the ENT system, lungs and kidneys being the most frequently involved. Another study, published more than 10 years ago,10 has described the most common observed manifestation at relapse in patients with six types of vasculitis and their relative frequencies. Despite these insights, these studies did not explore the relationship between baseline disease features and specific relapse patterns in detail. Nor did they focus on severe manifestations, which are the most alarming and potentially life-threatening for patients and pose significant management challenges for clinicians. The present work aimed to address this gap. Our data indicate that relapses predominantly mirror the initial disease phenotype. Most involve recurrence of manifestations present at diagnosis, and new manifestations typically occur in combination with baseline features. Educating patients about this characteristic disease pattern may facilitate timely recognition and management of relapse. These concepts could now be used by therapeutic education programmes to help patients know when to seek specialist advice.

This observation suggests that the processes triggered at disease onset in specific organs may be more readily reactivated over time. The underlying reasons for this pattern remain unclear, but several mechanisms are likely to contribute. Organ-specific involvement and recurrence may be influenced by genetic predisposition.726,28 Environmental factors can also play a role as relapse triggers. For example, changes in the nasal microbiota have been observed during active GPA,29 30 and inhaled irritants, such as silica or occupational particulates, may promote recurrent pulmonary flares.31

The reasons why some relapses resemble the initial presentation, while others involve new organs, are unknown and the involved mechanisms remain purely speculative.32,39 Tissue-resident memory immune cells could facilitate rapid reactivation in previously involved organs,32,34 while damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)-driven inflammation might amplify local immune responses.35 36 Other forms of immune memory, such as circulating T and B cells or innate immune training, may also shape relapse patterns. Together, these mechanisms may explain why many relapses mirror the initial phenotype, while a subset of patients develops new organ involvement. The latter may be driven by epitope spreading, systemic immune activation and/or exposure to new environmental triggers.37,39

Given these findings, it remains unclear why some patients experience flares in the same organs involved at diagnosis, while others develop new organ involvement. A better understanding of how genetic, epigenetic, environmental and treatment-related factors interact may help guide more targeted therapeutic strategies in the future.

In our cohort, ENT and lung involvement at diagnosis emerged as protective factors against the development of new organ manifestations at relapse, even when we adjusted for the number of organs affected at diagnosis. Clinically, these findings are relevant because they indicate that patients presenting with ENT or lung disease might follow a more localised relapse pattern, which has been associated with a better overall survival rate.40 Recognising this dual disease behaviour and communicating it to patients is critical. It provides important guidance for long-term monitoring, alerting clinicians and patients to the possibility of serious relapses even in previously unaffected organs.

This study has several strengths, including its large and diverse patient population drawn from multiple specialties across France. This broad representation allowed for comprehensive qualitative descriptions of clinical characteristics and long-term relapse patterns. The extended follow-up period further strengthens the reliability of the findings.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort was recruited from tertiary referral centres, which may have introduced selection bias by over-representing patients with more severe disease. A proportion of patients did not fulfil the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, although they could still be considered as having GPA according to the Chapel Hill nomenclature. This was due to missing data, mainly related to the lack of histological confirmation, as biopsy is not always feasible in routine clinical practice. An additional limitation is that haematuria was considered a sign of relapse, without biopsy confirmation for all patients. Nevertheless, haematuria was interpreted by the treating physicians as suggestive of active GPA, and treatment was escalated in all cases. Additionally, some non-significant associations may reflect limited statistical power due to the low number of events for certain outcomes. Finally, changes in treatment strategies over time may have influenced the observed relapse patterns and affected the generalisability of temporal trends.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights that can help both patients and healthcare professionals better anticipate the type of flares throughout the disease course. Patients should be informed of the increased risk of recurrence in the organs initially involved at diagnosis, while also remaining vigilant for warning signs of new organ involvement. This education may facilitate earlier recognition of symptoms, leading to prompt medical advice and initiation of treatment, which could improve outcomes, although this remains a hypothesis that warrants further study.
