Abstract
This article travels one century into the future—from 2025 to 2125—through the analytical lens of the Information–Curvature Efficiency Law (ICEL), an organizing ansatz that reframes wireless capacity around the curvature of the information manifold. It contends that wireless evolution will not proceed through incremental generations such as 6G or 7G, but through a curvature-managed integration of electromagnetics, biology, and thermodynamics. The technical instantiation of ICEL for phase-coded continuous apertures—where curvature is realized as the affine-quotient second derivative of the aperture phase, with a compact synthesis operator and a Fredholm-determinant capacity—is developed rigorously in a companion theory paper [ 25 ] and stress-tested against SVD, Fourier, Zernike-like, matched-focus, and RIS baselines in a companion benchmark paper [ 26 ] . The present essay supplies the physical intuition, the century-scale narrative, and a set of cross-domain extensions (biology, thermodynamics, ecology) that are explicitly labeled as illustrative extrapolations, not independent derivations.



ICEL as introduced here is an organizing ansatz. Its operator-theoretic instantiation for phase-coded apertures is derived and verified in the companion papers; the biological, thermodynamic, and social extensions of this essay are extrapolations calibrated to preserve the flat-limit Shannon reduction but are not derived from first principles.

A. From Shannon Flatlands to the Curved Manifold of Transmission

Under the Information–Curvature Efficiency Law (ICEL),
transmission capacity generalizes to the curvature-augmented form

C_{\text{ICEL}}=B\,\log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{S/N}{1+\alpha\,(C/C_{0})^{2}}\right),

(1)

where
C
represents the total information curvature—
a composite of environmental topology, quantum interference structure, and algorithmic feedback introduced by adaptive controllers
[
13
,
18
]
—
and
C_{0}
is a reference curvature that renders the ratio dimensionless.
The coefficient
\alpha
is the dimensionless curvature–noise coupling. It is not a free parameter: for phase-coded apertures it is now derived rather than fitted, as set out below.

Why this form, and why not the subtractive one.

Earlier statements of ICEL used the subtractive penalty

-\lambda\,C/(1+C^{2})
. That term is
bounded
: it attains its maximum

\lambda/2
at
C=1
and decays thereafter, so arbitrarily large curvature
costs at most
\lambda/2
bits per second and is asymptotically free. The
justification offered was that no single-source channel should extract
unbounded rate from geometry alone — but that is an argument for bounding the

benefit
of curvature, and it was applied to the
cost
.

The decisive objection is consistency with the companion theory paper rather
than taste. The
companion theory paper’s derivative-noise theorem proves that the receiver-side curvature cost
grows as
\Theta(\Delta x^{-4})
, without bound. A headline equation whose
penalty saturates therefore contradicts the companion paper it claims to
summarise. The multiplicative divisor above drives capacity to zero as

C\to\infty
and is consistent with that theorem; it also preserves the three
qualitative requirements the subtractive form was chosen for — it vanishes in
the flat limit, is monotone in
|C|
near the origin, and admits no unbounded
geometric gain.

The coupling is not free.

Once the abstract curvature
C
is given its concrete meaning for a phase-coded
aperture,
\alpha
stops being a constant to be fitted. The companion theory
paper now derives it. For an aperture driven at
N
control points about a bias
phase
\phi
, expanding the exact phase-only law to second order in the
modulation excursion returns precisely the divisor of (
1
), with

\alpha(\phi)\;=\;\frac{2\,\operatorname{tr}g(\phi)-\lVert s(\phi)\rVert^{2}}{4\,(N+2)\,\operatorname{tr}g(\phi)},

(2)

where
g(\phi)
is the Gram matrix of the tangent channel and

\lVert s(\phi)\rVert^{2}
is the coherent received power at the operating point

[
25
]
.

Two consequences follow that this essay could not have asserted on its own.
First,
\alpha
is a property of the aperture and of where it is operated, not a
universal constant; it takes the value
1/(4(N+2))
exactly when the radiated
element fields are mutually orthogonal. Second, and more consequentially, it

changes sign
. When coherent array gain exceeds twice the summed element
power,
\alpha<0
and curvature stops being a tax. The claim made further below — that
geometry can either penalize or amplify — is therefore not a rhetorical
flourish. The crossing is an equality between two measurable powers,
and dense apertures sit on the amplifying side of it.

The reference curvature.

1+\alpha(C/C_{0})^{2}
requires its argument to be dimensionless, and
C
is
not. Under the companion paper’s instantiation
C=\|D^{2}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

carries units of
\mathrm{rad}\cdot\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}
. Earlier versions of this
essay supplied no normalisation anywhere, so dimensional consistency could not
be checked at all. We fix

C_{0}\;=\;\frac{\varphi_{\text{peak}}}{L^{3/2}},

(3)

the curvature of a profile that uses the full phase excursion

\varphi_{\text{peak}}
once across an aperture of length
L
. Every appearance
of curvature in this essay is to be read as the dimensionless
C/C_{0}

On the status of (

Equation (

rather than a first-principles derivation. For one instantiation that is no longer the case: the second-order form of the divisor, and the value of
\alpha

C\to 0

|C|
near the origin, so that small departures from flatness carry a proportional cost — a requirement (

not
universal, since
\alpha

B\log_{2}(1+S/N)\cdot e^{-\alpha(C/C_{0})^{2}}
satisfies the same three and remains admissible. The subtractive form
-\lambda\,C/(1+C^{2})
does not, and is retired: its penalty saturates, so it contradicts the companion theory paper’s unbounded
\Theta(\Delta x^{-4})
receiver cost. Distinguishing the two survivors is an empirical exercise, and the derivation above does not perform it:

The rigorous operator-theoretic instantiation of ICEL for the specific case of phase-coded continuous apertures—where
C

\|D^{2}\phi\|_{L^{2}}
on the affine-quotient phase space, and the corresponding capacity is a Fredholm-determinant supremum characterized by a dual-multiplier generalized water-filling law—is developed in the companion theory paper

[

What would falsify this, and what would not.

An earlier statement of the cross-paper binding named the companion theory
paper’s
\Theta(\Delta x^{-4})
receiver-noise law as ICEL’s falsifier: ICEL
commits to a
\Delta x^{-4}
cost, the theory paper delivers

\|K_{c}\|_{2}\leq 16\sigma^{2}/\Delta x^{4}
, and a measurement contradicting it
would sink both. That binding is void. The second-difference operator has
symbol
(2-2\cos\theta)/\Delta x^{2}\in[0,4/\Delta x^{2}]
, so

\|D_{2}D_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{2}\leq 16/\Delta x^{4}
holds for
any
scheme
that estimates a second derivative from uniformly sampled noisy data. It is a
property of finite differencing, it appears in numerical-analysis textbooks,
and it would hold identically under every competing framework. A prediction
that no conceivable experiment could refute, and that no rival theory denies,
is not a falsifier.

The discriminating prediction is the two-regime scaling law. The companion
theory paper derives, and its diagnostics measure, a crossover in the
high-SNR capacity exponent as the link moves between the differentiation-limited
and aperture-limited regimes: the fitted exponent is
0.2175
on the plateau
branch and
0.0576
on the saturated branch, with the transition located by the
Fresnel number rather than fitted. Flat-channel theory predicts neither branch
and no crossover; a measurement that found a single exponent across the
transition, or found the transition at a Fresnel number materially away from
unity, would refute the curvature account while leaving Shannon’s untouched.
That is the shape a falsifier has to have, and it is the one this programme
should be judged on.
