Integrated proteomic and immune subtyping: a two-tier framework for biomarker-guided therapy in high-grade serous ovarian cancer
Authors: Jiagui Song, Lixiang Xue
Published: 2026

Ovarian cancer remains one of the deadliest gynaecological malignancies, with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) accounting for the majority of deaths.1,2 Despite advances in surgery and chemotherapy, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, and the 5-year survival rate has stubbornly remained below 50% for decades.3 A major obstacle is that the traditional FIGO staging system, while useful for prognosis, does not explain why patients within the same stage often follow dramatically different clinical trajectories.4 More importantly, the molecular events that drive the shift from early to advanced disease, a transition that could offer a window for more effective intervention, have not been systematically mapped at the protein level yet. Over the past decade, transcriptomic and proteomic efforts by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Clinical Proteomic Tumour Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) have produced several molecular classifications of HGSOC.5,6 However, none of these subtypes have been translated into routine clinical practice, largely because of lacking clear and reproducible associations with patient outcomes or therapeutic vulnerabilities. In parallel, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown only modest activity in HGSOC, in sharp contrast to their success in some other solid tumours.7 This therapeutic resistance is largely attributed to the poorly elucidated tumour immune microenvironment, in which the protein-level differences between “hot” and “cold” tumours remain insufficiently defined.8 Thus, the precise molecular tipping point from early to advanced HGSOC, the protein networks driving this transition, and effective patient stratification strategies for optimised immunotherapy represent critical, urgently warranted research priorities.

In the recent issue of eBioMedicine, Tu and colleagues addressed these gaps by performing comprehensive proteomic profiling of 116 primary HGSOC tumours.9 They identified FIGO stage IIA as a critical molecular tipping point between early and advanced disease, marked by a shift from oxidative stress to cell cycle-driven programs. Mechanistically, the GOSR2-SEC24D axis was found to suppress CXCL9/CXCL12 secretion, thereby limiting CD8+ T cell infiltration and driving both tumour progression and immune evasion; functional experiments confirmed that targeting GOSR2 reduced tumour growth and restored cytotoxic T cell activity. The authors further classified HGSOC into three proteomic subtypes with distinct biological features: S-I (immune-active), S-II (metabolic), and S-III (ECM-rich, highly invasive, and prognostically unfavourable). Parallel immune clustering yielded three immune-contexture subtypes (IC1, IC2 and IC3) with distinct outcomes: IC1 was NKT/Th1-enriched and associated with good prognosis, IC2 represented a “cold tumour” phenotype with the worst survival, and IC3 fell in between. By integrating these two classifications, the authors proposed a two-tier stratification system: immune subtypes define baseline prognosis, and within the IC2 microenvironment, proteomic subtypes guide therapy, IC2/S-II being immune-hot and potentially sensitive to immunotherapy, whereas IC2/S-III being immune-evasive and possibly amenable to anti-stromal agents. Notably, elevated levels of ISG15, ITGB2 and RELA were associated with improved survival and denser CD8+ T cell infiltration in advanced HGSOC, pointing to their utility as candidate biomarkers for immunotherapy response. These findings provide a framework for biomarker-guided stratification and precision therapy in HGSOC.

These findings suggest that the integrated proteomic and immune subtyping framework offer a promising avenue for biomarker-guided precision therapy in HGSOC. Although the study by Tu and colleagues has thrown much-needed light on the molecular tipping point and immune evasion mechanisms, many fundamental questions remain unanswered. Future research is required to validate these findings in larger, more balanced cohorts and immunocompetent models, and to develop simplified, cost-effective assays for routine clinical implementation.
