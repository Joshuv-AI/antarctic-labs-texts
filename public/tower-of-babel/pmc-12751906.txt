Genome-wide adaptive selection and functional annotation of regulatory variation in the Yangxin pig
Authors: Sha Liu, Qiong Chen, Huanhuan Zhou, Caichun Yang, Liangyu Shi, Hongbo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocal adaptation drives complex traits in domestic animals, but the roles of positive and balancing selection in Chinese indigenous pig breeds remain unclear. We performed whole-genome resequencing of 79 Yangxin pigs and applied positive and balance selection, integrated findings with PigQTLdb and FarmGTEx.ResultsIn this study, we detected ~ 98 Mb of the genome under adaptive selection, mainly involving non-coding variants. Positive selection signals were concentrated in regulatory regions affected to muscle traits, whereas balancing selection was enriched at loci associated with reproduction. The strongest overlap with regulatory elements was observed in muscle tissue. Additionally, significant enrichment of the Hippo signaling pathway was detected in testis. DNA-level annotation revealed reproductive QTL to be most overrepresented in balancing-selection regions. Notable candidate genes and pathways include VGLL3 and Hippo signaling, and oxytocin, circadian entrainment, vascular smooth muscle contraction, and adrenergic signaling.ConclusionThese results underscore a regulatory basis for muscle phenotype variation, and the role of balancing selection in preserving fertility-related diversity. Our findings provide genomic markers for breeding and conservation strategies aimed at enhancing productivity in the Yangxin pig.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12364-5.Keywords: Yangxin pig, Selective signatures, Adaptative selection, Muscle trait, Reproductive QTL, Non-coding variants

Background

Environmental adaptation is a key driver of species survival and distribution [1]. Domestic animals, shaped by both natural selection and artificial breeding, have accumulated rich genetic diversity and unique adaptive characteristics [2–4]. Pigs have undergone intense selective pressure during domestication, resulting in genetic variations in pathways related to immunity, reproduction, and energy metabolism [5]. East Asian and European wild boars diverged approximately one million years ago. Subsequent human-driven migration and hybridization facilitated extensive gene flow and global dispersal of pigs [6]. China possesses abundant indigenous pig breeds adapted to diverse ecological conditions, from the Tibetan Plateau to tropical lowlands, each exhibiting distinct environmental adaptability [7].

Yangxin pig is a representative local breed from Hubei Province, China. It is characterized by medium body size, tolerance to coarse feed, and superior meat quality. Yangxin pigs show higher genetic polymorphism and distinct genetic differentiation from Tongcheng and Qingping pigs in Hubei Province, suggesting an independent genetic background and potential localized adaptation pathways. However, there is still a lack of systematic genomic studies investigating positive and balancing selection during its domestication and local adaptation, as well as variations associated with meat quality and disease resistance.

Positive selection increases beneficial allele frequencies rapidly via hard or soft sweeps, whereas balancing selection maintains genetic diversity, enhancing population resilience to environmental fluctuations. Large-scale genomic analyses has revealed persistent positive selection on NPR3 associated with body size in pigs, and long-term balancing selection on the MUC13 gene in multiple breeds [8]. Genome-wide analyses also indicate that production‑related genes, particularly those affecting growth and meat quality [9, 10], show strong positive‑selection signatures, while immune‑related regions, including the MHC (or SLA), exhibit high diversity consistent with balancing selection [11, 12]. These processes shape the genomic basis of adaptation in domestic animals.

The Pig Quantitative Trait Loci database (PigQTLdb) compiles extensive QTL information associated with growth, reproduction, meat quality, and disease resistance traits [13]. Integrating adaptive selection regions with QTL data can clarify sources and impacts of selection, providing insights into the evolutionary and breeding history of livestock.

Advances in functional genomics further make it possible to investigate relationships in greater detail. Non-coding regulatory elements play important roles in shaping phenotypes [14, 15]. Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTL) analyses link genetic variation to changes in gene expression or RNA splicing, offering a functional perspective on genomic variation [16]. In humans, adaptive selection signals have been found enriched in immune-related eQTLs among European populations [17]. In livestock, resources such as FarmGTEX [18] offer tissue-specific eQTL and sQTL data across multiple species, facilitating the functional interpretation of genetic variation. In pigs, integration of selection mapping with eQTL and sQTL data facilitate the clarification of molecular mechanisms underlying adaptive evolution. Combining adaptive selection regions with eQTL and sQTL analyses can benifit interpretation of functional mechanisms underlying genomic variation in adaptive evolution.

In this study, a combining strategy of whole-genome resequencing, comprehensive population genetics analyses, adaptive selection and multi‑omics datasets from PigQTLdb and FarmGTEX was performed for comprehensive view of the adaptive genomic architecture in Yangxin pigs. The findings contribute to understanding the mechanisms of adaptation in this breed and provide a basis for better conservation and utilization of local porcine genetic resources.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and whole genome resequencing

In this study, 79 Yangxin pigs were sampled from the Yangxin pig breeding herd at Huangmei Qiangli Livestock Co., Ltd. (Huangmei County, Hubei Province, China). Based on detailed family and pedigree records, individuals with minimal kinship were purposively selected to reduce close relatedness and improve representation of the genetic diversity of the Yangxin pig population. All animals were kept under standardized housing conditions with identical feed and water. Tail-tissue samples were collected for DNA extraction. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted and purified using the Bioer 32-channel Automatic Nucleic Acid Extraction System (Bioer, Nanjing, China). DNA quality was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a gel-imaging system (Monad Biotech Co., Ltd.).

Whole-genome resequencing was carried out on the BGI MGI-T7 platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end reads, ~ 20× coverage).

Quality control, sequence alignment, and variant calling

Raw reads were filtered using fastp (v0.24.0) [19] to remove adapter sequences. Clean reads were mapped to the pig reference genome (Sus_scrofa.Sscrofa11.1.113) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [20]. Aligned sequences were sorted and indexed with Samtools (v1.16.1) [21], and duplicated reads were removed using Picard’s MarkDuplicates tool (v3.1.0) [22].

Genomic variants were called with GATK (v4.2.6.1) [23]. For each sample, HaplotypeCaller [24] generated GVCF files, which were combined with CombineGVCFs and jointly genotyped using GenotypeGVCFs. SNP filtering was conducted using SelectVariants and VariantFiltration with the following criteria: QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, SOR >3.0, FS >60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. Non-biallelic SNPs, minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.05 and SNPs with more than 10% missing genotype were excluded. Haplotype phasing was performed with BEAGLE (v5.4) [25], retaining SNPs with DR2 ≥ 0.9.

Population structure and demographic history analyses

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was performed using PLINK (v1.9) [26] to remove high LD regions (--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1) for population structure. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using PLINK (v1.9) [26]. Ancestry proportions were estimated with ADMIXTURE (v1.3) [27] for K = 2 to K = 5. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using vcf2phylip (v2.9) [28] and FastTree [29] tools, and visualized in iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

The effective population size (Ne) was estimated with SMC++ (v1.15.2) [30], assuming a mutation rate of 1.25 × 10− 8 per generation.

Individual inbreeding levels were assessed using runs of homozygosity (ROH). ROH segments were identified using the following criteria: (1) minimum length of 300 kb containing ≥ 61 SNPs; (2) a maximum gap of 500 kb between adjacent SNPs; (3) at most 1 heterozygous and 2 missing calls allowed per window; (4) SNP density ≥ 100 SNPs/kb; and (5) window homogeneity threshold of 0.01 [31]. Identification of adaptive selection signals

Six methods were used to identify genomic regions under positive and balancing selection in Yangxin pigs. Recent hard selective sweeps were identified with iHS [33], nSL [34], and CLR [35]. Soft sweeps were detected with iHH12 [36]. All tests were implemented with selscan (v2.0.0) [37, 38], using SNPs with MAF >0.01. For |iHS|, nSL and iHH12, significant selection signals were defined at P < 0.0001 (Z-test). CLR calculations were performed in 50 kb windows with 20 kb steps, identifying significant selection signals with P values less than 0.0001.

Balancing selection was detected in genomic regions with MAF ≥ 0.15 [39]. Beta statistics combined with Tajima’s D [40] values greater than 2 were used to detect significant balancing selection signals at the right-tail significance threshold (P < 0.0001). All analyses were based on the reference genome Sscrofa11.1 to determine ancestral alleles [39].

Definition and functional annotation of adaptive regions

Adaptive regions were defined as windows or sites that showed a significant selection signal in at least one method. For window-based analyses, adaptive regions were the genomic windows exhibiting significant selection signals. For site-based analyses, 1 kb upstream and downstream flanking sequences were included. Candidate regions under positive selection were defined by merging adjacent windows or sites that exceeded the significance thresholds in any of the four statistics (iHS, nSL, CLR, iHH12). Regions under balancing selection were defined as windows that concurrently showed a significant Beta statistic and a positive Tajima’s D value. Regions exhibiting both positive and balancing signals were classified as subject to ‘positive and balancing selection’.

Functional annotation of adaptive regions was performed in ANNOVAR (v2025Mar02) [41] based on the Sscrofa11.1.113 reference genome. Variants were categorized into six functional regions: exonic and splice-site regions, non-coding RNA regions, intronic regions, untranslated regions (5’ and 3’ UTRs), regulatory regions (upstream and downstream), and intergenic regions. Using the pig QTL database (PigQTLdb), overlaps between adaptive regions and QTLs associated with growth, reproduction, meat quality, and disease resistance traits were examined. The regioneR package [42] was employed to calculate Z-values and perform permutation tests (100 permutations) to assess significance of overlaps between adaptive regions, functional genomic regions, and QTLs.

Integration with eQTL and sQTL analyses

To explore functional implications, adaptive regions were integrated with eQTL and sQTL data from the FarmGTEX, a livestock transcriptome resource providing tissue-specific regulatory information across species [18]. Overlapping regions were used to identify regulated genes and their tissue expression patterns.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using WebGestaltR (v0.4.6) [43]. The significance threshold was defined as adjusted FDR < 0.05.

Results

Resequencing data

A total of approximately 5.41 TB of raw sequencing data was generated. After filtering, an average of 233,510,120 clean reads per sample were obtained. The GC content per sample ranged between 40.0% and 43.0%. The average genome coverage was 96.70%, varying from 96.38% to 97.11%. The average sequencing depth was 22.20×, ranging from 15.27× to 47.21× (Supplementary Table S1). SNP annotation indicated that most variants were located in non-coding regions: 52.43% in intergenic regions and 39.51% in intronic regions (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Population structure

To analyze the genetic structure of Yangxin pigs, principal component analysis (PCA), phylogenetic tree, and ADMIXTURE analyses were performed. PCA revealed genetic stratification within Yangxin pigs (Fig. 1a). ADMIXTURE showed the lowest cross-validation error at K = 4, indicating four ancestral components (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. S2). The phylogenetic tree supported this genetic differentiation (Fig. 1c).

Population structure of Yangxin pigs. a PCA. b Effective population size (Ne). c Phylogenetic tree based on the neighbor-joining method. d ADMIXTURE of the population using LD-pruned SNPs, with K values ranging from 2 to 4. e Histogram of ROH length distribution. f LD decay plot

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) showed that most segments were 0 ~ 5 Mb (Fig. 1e). The genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH) ranged from 0.033 to 0.148 (Supplementary Table S2). Effective population size (Ne) indicated a historical population bottleneck followed by population expansion in recent generations, especially over the past 10,000 generations (Fig. 1b). LD decayed rapidly within 100 kb across chromosomes. Chromosome 15 exhibited the slowest LD decay, whereas chromosome 10 showed the fastest (Fig. 1f).

Identification of adaptive genomic regions

To identify genomic regions under adaptive selection, six selection detection methods (iHS, nSL, CLR, iHH12, Beta, and Tajima’s D) were applied. The total size of adaptive selection regions identified in Yangxin pigs was approximately 98.05 Mb (Fig. 2a). Chromosomes 1, 5 and 7 show the strongest footprints of positive selection, whereas chromosomes 1, 7 and 15 carry the most pronounced signatures of balancing selection. Moreover, the longest region under positive selection is located on chromosome 11, and the longest region under balancing selection is found on chromosome 2. Most of the genome (95.672%) was classified as neutrally evolving, whereas 3.949% was under positive selection, 0.384% under balancing selection, and 0.005% under both positive and balancing selection simultaneously (Fig. 2b). Regions under positive selection cover 89.35 Mb and contain 2,855 genes, while regions under balancing selection cover 8.81 Mb with 220 genes. The overlap between them is 0.11 Mb, harboring 23 genes. Statistical analyses of these gene sets were conducted (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Distribution of eQTL and sQTL

The distribution of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTL) showed tissue-specific patterns. The highest proportion of eQTL was found in muscle tissue, suggesting that regulatory variants influencing muscle gene expression represent important targets of selection, followed by blood (Fig. 3a). eQTLs were also relatively abundant in liver, testes, and brain. Functional enrichment analysis (GO/KEGG) of these eQTL-associated genes identified 82 significantly enriched pathways (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the Hippo signaling pathway (ssc04392), associated with embryonic development, was significantly enriched in testes tissue. The highest proportion of sQTL was observed in embryonic sac tissue, followed by the frontal cortex, indicating selection on splicing during embryogenesis and in the brain (Fig. 3b).

Functional annotation of adaptive regions

Genomic regions identified as adaptive were annotated (Fig. 4). A total of 6,180 regions under positive selection, 113 regions under balancing selection, and 46 regions under both types of selection were identified. Most adaptive variants were located in intergenic (38.44%~50.63%) and intronic regions (40.88%~42.57%). Only a small proportion overlapped with exons (0.74%~5.03%), untranslated regions (UTRs, 0.06%~1.42%), or regulatory regions (upstream/downstream; 2.03%~11.79%; Fig. 4a). Adaptive regions significantly enriched exonic and intergenic regions (Z-values: 2.01 ~ 2.71). Positive selection regions were enriched in exonic, while balancing selection and both positive and balancing selection regions were enriched in intergenic regions (Fig. 4b).

Genomic annotation of adaptive regions in Yangxin pigs. a Distribution of genomic annotation by regions grouped according to different types of selection. b Z-score heatmap of adaptive regions in different genomic feature regions. c Distribution of adaptive regions overlapping with quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with various traits, where the Y-axis represents the percentage of adaptive regions detected in each feature region on the X-axis out of the total number. d Z-score heatmap of the distribution of adaptive regions in different types of QTL regions

Overlap analysis between adaptive regions and previously reported quantitative trait loci (QTL) revealed that most overlaps were associated with reproductive performance traits. Approximately 3.01% of adaptive regions overlapped reproductive QTL, with balancing selection regions showing the highest overlap (Fig. 4c). Adaptive selection regions showed significant enrichment with QTL associated with appearance, meat quality and carcass traits, production performance, and reproductive traits (Fig. 4d). Reproductive trait QTL exhibited the strongest enrichment signal, with positive selection regions being underrepresented in this category of QTL (Z = −3.33) and balancing selection regions being enriched (Z = 3.16). There were differences in the enrichment of adaptive selection regions across QTL of all different trait categories. Positive selection was underrepresented in QTL associated with appearance, meat quality and carcass traits, production performance, and reproductive traits (Z = −3.33 to −1.13), while balancing selection was significantly enriched only in reproductive traits (Z = 3.16), indicating that loci associated with fertility-related traits are less prone to classic hard sweeps, likely due to the need to maintain genetic diversity for reproductive performance.

Functional enrichment analysis

To understand potential functions of genes located in adaptive regions, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted. KEGG pathway enrichment identified 33 significantly enriched pathways (Supplementary Table S4). Top enriched pathways included circadian entrainment (P = 2.90 × 10− 4), oxytocin signaling pathway (P = 3.16 × 10− 4), vascular smooth muscle contraction (P = 6.12 × 10− 4), and adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes (P = 6.12 × 10− 4). Additionally, significant enrichment was observed in pathways involved in axon guidance and parathyroid hormone synthesis. These results suggest roles for adaptive genes in regulating circadian rhythms, hormone signaling, neural development, and muscle function. We further performed enrichment analyses on genes from distinct selection classes. In the positive-selection gene set, KEGG identified 36 significantly enriched pathways, while GO detected no significant terms. No significant enrichment was found for the other selection classes (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

Population analyses indicated that Yangxin pigs retain high genetic diversity with limited recent inbreeding, differing from extensively selected commercial breeds [44, 45].PCA, ADMIXTURE, and the phylogeny support a distinct genetic background among Chinese breeds with little foreign introgression, consistent with local adaptation [46]. The ~ 98 Mb of genomic regions identified under adaptive selection fit the expectation that domestication and breeding shape production traits through selective sweeps while maintaining diversity at loci important for fitness [11, 47, 48]. These signals can be interpreted from two complementary angles: a regulatory perspective that examines how selected variants influence gene expression, and a “DNA-level” perspective that links genomic regions to complex traits using QTL resources [49–51].

A dominant signature of positive selection in Yangxin pigs points to the enhancement of production and resilience traits. For instance, the VGLL3 gene, a critical regulator of muscle fiber composition and meat quality, was located within a strong positive selection peak [52]. Our eQTL analysis indicates that this region is associated with carcass traits, suggesting that regulatory variants affecting gene expression contribute to carcass characteristics. Signals from GALNT15 and MGAT5 further indicate that protein glycosylation is linked to protein metabolism, thereby regulating muscle development and energy utilization [53, 54]. We also identified genes related to reproductive performance in these regions, including ABTB2, which is associated with litter size, alongside G2E3 and ANLN, which are crucial for early embryonic development [55–57]. Concurrently, positive selection appears to have targeted genes for environmental resilience, such as XRN1 [58] (antiviral defense) and VPS13D (mitochondrial quality control) [59], likely contributing to robustness and feed efficiency under coarse feeding conditions.

In contrast to directional selection, our analysis revealed signals of balancing selection acting to maintain genetic diversity at loci critical for fitness, particularly within QTLs for reproductive performance. The prominent signal was the enrichment of balancing selection regions within QTLs for reproductive performance. This pattern suggests the long-term maintenance of diversity at fertility loci, where different alleles may be favored under different conditions. SUSD4 [60] shows both directional and balancing selection, hinting at recent adaptation while keeping immune- and fertility-related diversity.

Pathway analyses based on the entire set of adaptive regions revealed enrichment for several key biological processes. The enrichment of Oxytocin and neuropeptide signaling points to maintained variation in the neuroendocrine regulation of reproductive processes and maternal behaviors [61]. Additionally, the enrichment of circadian entrainment suggests that timing pathways influence energy balance and behavior in local production environments [62]. These findings highlight how selection has acted across multiple interconnected biological systems to affect the unique adaptive profile of the Yangxin pig.

The Hippo signaling pathway offers a compelling synthesis of Yangxin pig adaptation, demonstrating how a single, conserved pathway can be targeted by selection in a tissue-specific manner to optimize both fitness and production. In the testis, Hippo-related genes showed testes-specific pathway enrichment. In muscle, however, the downstream effector VGLL3 lies within a positive-selection peak [52]. This dual pattern highlights a sophisticated adaptive strategy: the same pathway supports reproductive robustness through balancing selection while enabling rapid improvement of carcass traits via positive selection on a tissue-specific effector.

While multi-omics integration greatly clarified the biological interpretation of these adaptive signals, our interpretation relies on functional annotations from public databases like FarmGTEx and PigQTLdb, which are primarily based on commercial pig breeds [45, 63–66]. Because allele frequencies and LD patterns differ between indigenous and commercial pigs, and because these databases lack broad coverage of certain tissues, enrichment at the category level may be diluted. Thus, Yangxin-specific regulatory variants, especially in reproductive tissues, may be present but missed by current datasets. The functional links proposed here represent strong, cross-breed candidate mechanisms that require direct validation through expression and functional studies within the Yangxin breed itself. Recent pangenome work underscores how structural and regulatory diversity across pig lineages can contribute to these complex trait architectures, highlighting a promising avenue for future investigation [67].

Conclusion

We integrated whole-genome resequencing of 79 Yangxin pigs with adaptation selection, QTL and eQTL/sQTL to identify adaptive genetic variation. About 98.05 Mb were under adaptation selection, with most variants in non-coding regions. Positive selection signals were mainly found in regulatory regions related to muscle traits, while balancing selection was enriched at loci affected reproduction. Many adaptive regions overlapped muscle eQTLs and involved genes and pathways related to myogenesis, meat quality, fertility and hormone signaling. Immune- and metabolism-related genes were also detected, indicating potential roles in disease resistance and adaptation to local management. These findings identify key regulatory factors for muscle development and QTL affecting fertility, providing targets for genomic selection to enhance productivity and resilience in Yangxin pigs.
