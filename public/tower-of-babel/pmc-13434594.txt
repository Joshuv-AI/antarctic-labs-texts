Optimization study of light olefins production in gasoil cracking over HZSM-5 catalyst: temperature and catalyst loading as key parameters
Authors: Naemeh Yasrebi, Jafarsadegh Moghaddas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe catalytic cracking of atmospheric gasoil over protonic HZSM-5 zeolite (SiO₂/Al₂O₃ = 33.3) was studied in a fixed-bed reactor. The effects of temperature (500, 550, and 600 °C) and catalyst loading (0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 g) on conversion of gasoil and selectivity of ethylene and propylene were thoroughly investigated. Both ethylene and propylene selectivities increased with rising temperature at all catalyst loadings, whereas the propylene-to-ethylene (P/E) ratio declined. The highest total light olefins selectivity (60.0 wt%) was achieved at 600 °C with 0.5 g of catalyst. Gasoil conversion exhibited a non-monotonic trend at low-to-moderate catalyst loadings, reaching a minimum at 550 °C, attributed to rapid coke deposition and pore blockage; where it recovered at 600 °C due to the decomposition of these reaction intermediates or volatilization. Conversion increased steadily with temperature at 0.5 g catalyst loading owing to greater availability of active sites and improved coke tolerance. Stability tests at 500 °C and 0.3 g revealed stable light olefin selectivity over 6 h time on stream, despite progressive deactivation (conversion dropped from ~ 87% to ~ 35%). The results highlight that there is an optimal catalyst loading-temperature point and further demonstrates the need for consideration of the balance between light olefins yield and catalyst stability.Keywords: HZSM-5, Gasoil cracking, Light olefins, Temperature effect, Catalyst loadingSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The chemical industry depends considerably on light olefins- particularly propylene and ethylene- as the necessary building blocks for the manufacturing of various chemicals and polyolefins1–3. Among them, ethylene appears to be the most in-demand light olefin on the global market, with an annual production of 155 million tons4. However, the ongoing increase in propylene products worldwide could potentially exceed ethylene in the market in the future5. The substantial rise in propylene consumption is primarily driven by the increasing demand for its main downstream product, polypropylene6.

Currently, both ethylene and propylene are primarily produced as major by-products of naphtha steam cracking, a process that accounts for over 80% of total light olefins production. Light hydrocarbons, such as naphtha and light cycle oil, are used as feedstock in this process. However, there is a shortage of light oil supplies because of the worldwide increasing demand. Consequently, the use of heavier feedstock is increasingly preferred as alternatives for the production of light olefins7. The type of feedstock and the operating parameters have a significant effect on the product yield in steam cracking process. However, steam cracking, the most energy-intensive process in the petrochemical sector, produces much CO2 with reaction temperatures of up to 800 °C8–10. Incorporation of a catalyst during hydrocarbon cracking decreases the temperature as well as CO2 emission11. On the other hand, catalytic cracking provides significant flexibility and high propylene selectivity at around 500 °C12. Fluid Catalytic Cracking (FCC) remains vital in today’s market due to its capability to produce valuable light olefins (ethylene, propylene, butenes) and aromatics from hydrocarbons13. In order to increase the production yield of particular products or prevent undesired side reactions, shape-selective catalysts such as zeolites are essential in catalytic cracking14–17. Among zeolites, ZSM-5 stands out due to its exceptional activity, hydrothermal stability, and resistance to carbon deposition18–22. The effect of ZSM-5 additives on the selectivity of FCC products has been the subject of numerous investigations23–25. In most cases, it is reported that the ZSM-5 can improve the light olefins yields when used in FCC operation26–28. In addition to its catalytic role, ZSM-5 can act as an effective sorbent because of its high surface area and microporous structure which gives the ability to selectively adsorb and separate hydrocarbon species29–31. However, in catalytic cracking, the MFI framework and Brønsted acid sites are the dominant factors determining product distribution and light-olefin formation32. The sinusoidal pore channels prevent the formation of large hydrocarbons, leading to reduced carbon deposits and increased catalyst life1. The feed characteristics, operating environment, and catalysts all have a significant effect on the amount of FCC propylene. Operating parameters, particularly temperature, significantly influence the distribution of products33.

Optimization of a process involves altering the process to maximize an identified number of parameters while maintaining a particular set of constraints. It is frequently employed to increase the production cost-efficiency, productivity and throughput34. Various studies have surveyed the conversion of naphtha35, gasoil cracking, or other feedstock over ZSM-5 in order to increase the yield of light olefins by adjusting these factors36. Apart from assessing various feedstocks, scientists have also evaluated how important operational parameters—like reaction temperature, residence time, and weight hourly space velocity (WHSV)—affect product distribution. These parameters are essential in order to maximize the production of valuable light olefins like ethylene and propylene and optimize reaction pathways. Feng et al. studied how reaction temperature affects the catalytic cracking of naphtha over F/HZSM-5 reporting the yield of ethylene and propylene. They found that conversion ethylene yield increases continuously by increasing temperature. In contrast, the yield of propylene exhibits a maximum at 600 °C, beyond which it decreases because of secondary reactions37. Similarly, Lu et al.‘s work on CrHZSM-5 zeolites highlighted the importance of reaction temperature in enhancing light olefins yield in iso-butane cracking by monomolecular cracking38. Jung et al. found that for MFI zeolites with different Si/Al ratios, the conversion and olefin selectivity both increased with increasing temperature (200–600 °C), and the olefin selectivity increased gradually regardless of Si/Al ratio1. Despite the fact that ZSM-5 has been thoroughly investigated in FCC process and naphtha cracking, the performance of unmodified ZSM-5 without metal loading, as a function of temperatures and catalyst loading has not been explored. To fill this gap, the present work attempts to investigate the effect of these parameters on feed conversion and light olefins selectivity to provide an insight on behavior of catalytic cracking of gasoil.

For this purpose, the Na-ZSM-5 was ion-exchanged to produce the protonic form. Then, the resulting catalyst was characterized by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), X-ray Fluorescence (XRF), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analyses, X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Temperature-Programmed Desorption of ammonia (NH3-TPD). Finally, the catalyst was evaluated in catalytic cracking of gasoil and the effect of temperature and catalyst loading was investigated.

Experimental

Catalyst preparation

A commercial sample of Na-ZSM-5 zeolite (SiO₂/Al₂O₃ molar ratio = 36.7) was ion-exchanged to get the proton form following the procedure reported in Ref39. Typically, 5 g of Na-ZSM-5 was added to 150 ml of 1 M ammonium nitrate solution and stirred for 3 h at 80 °C under reflux. Then, the solution was filtered and washed thoroughly with hot deionized water (80 °C). The sample was subsequently dried at 110 °C, and this procedure was performed two more times. Finally, it was calcined for four hours at 550 °C in static air to transform the NH4-ZSM-5 catalyst into HZSM-5. For catalytic tests, the powder was pelletized (3 mm in diameter and 5 mm in height) using 3 wt% bentonite as binder, followed by drying at 110 °C for 12 h.

Characterization

XRD patterns were recorded on a Philips PW1730 diffractometer using Cu Ka radiation at 40 kV and 30 mA, and a scanning rate of 1 s.

Textural properties of the samples were determined by nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms using the Belsorp mini II instrument (Japan). Prior to experiments, samples were degassed at 120 °C for 2 h. The specific surface area of the sample was calculated based on the BET isothermal equation, whereas the pore size distribution was calculated by using Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method40 obtained from the adsorption data of isotherm.

The morphology of the samples was characterized using a MIRA TESCAN scanning electron microscope. Elemental analysis was determined using XRFanalysis.

The acid site density and acidic strength of catalysts were measured by NH3-TPD using a TPD/ TPR 2900 analyzer equipped with an online Thermal Conductivity Detector (TCD). The sample was first degassed at 500 °C under a helium flow (40 mL min⁻¹). After it was cooled to 50 °C, the sample was exposed to ammonia until saturation. The adsorbed ammonia was removed by flushing the helium for about 30 min. Then, the sample was heated at a heating rate of 10 °C min− 1 up to 750 °C. The TCD detector monitored the desorption of ammonia as a function of temperature to quantify the adsorbed amount.

The gaseous products obtained from the catalytic cracking were analyzed using a gas chromatography (Agilent Technologies 7890 A GC) equipped with HP-Plot Q (30 m, 0.53 mm, 40 μm) capillary column and FID detector.

The feed was injected into a Pyrex round-bottom vessel at a regulated flow rate of 2.74 mL/h using a syringe pump (702 SM Titrino, Metrohm). Nitrogen was introduced into the Pyrex vessel at a controlled flow rate, regulated by a flowmeter. The tubular reactor (10 mm inner diameter) was heated using a programmable furnace. Under constant nitrogen flow, the furnace was ramped up to the target temperature for each experiment at a rate of 10 °C/min, and it was maintained at targeted temperature for the predetermined time. The Pyrex vessel was heated by electric mantle and gasoil was injected upon the temperature had reached the desired level. The vessel was maintained within a temperature range of 360–362 °C during the experiments. The generated vapors flowed into the reactor and contacted the catalyst bed. To minimize heat loss, the vessel and connections were insulated with rock wool. The effluent from the reactor was quenched by one water-cooled condenser, followed by a separatory funnel placed in an ice bath. After separating the products, the liquid products were collected, and the non-condensable gases were analyzed using the gas chromatography equipment.

Based on gas chromatography (GC) data, the molar selectivity was calculated as the ratio of the moles of a particular product formed to the total moles of all products. Each component’s molar selectivity (mol%) was multiplied by its corresponding molecular weight to determine the weight% selectivity (wt%). Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

XRD analysis

Figure 2 presents the XRD pattern of HZSM-5 sample, which exhibits sharp peaks at 2θ = 7–9° and 22–25°, characteristic of the MFI-type framework41. The high crystallinity and associated peaks indicate that there were no impurities. It also confirmed that the HZSM-5 crystalline structure was preserved after repeated ion-exchange process42–45.

SEM analysis

The ion-exchanged sample displays the distinctive uniform hexagonal shape of ZSM-5 zeolite with particle sizes in the range of 1–3 μm (Fig. 3). The crystal morphology remained unchanged following ion exchange, suggesting that the zeolite’s structural integrity was preserved. The surfaces of the HZSM-5 crystals stay smooth and show no signs of collapse or cracking, The particles seem to have agglomerated slightly. The morphology of the catalyst is good for catalytic cracking reactions because it has good crystallinity.

FE-SEM images of the HZSM-5 catalyst.

N2 adsorption-desorption analysis

Figure 4a illustrates the Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm of HZSM-5 catalyst. As observed, the HZSM-5 catalyst exhibits a type I adsorption isotherm, with a plateau at high relative pressures, which is a characteristic of microporous materials with limited mesopores45–47. The mesopores observed in the catalyst are likely due to either intracrystalline mesopores and/or the crystallites clustering together48. The pore size distribution curve is a plot showing the pore size variation as a function of pore volume. Using this curve, considerable insight can be achieved regarding the textural properties of synthesized HZSM-5. As demonstrated in Fig. 4b, it can be noted that most pore volumes are found within a specific pore radius range close to 1–2 nm. As such, it can be deduced that based on the calculated mean pore diameter (reported in Table 2), which was found to be 1.83 nm, most pores exist at the micro-mesopore boundary. The narrow pore distribution indicates a uniform structure. A uniform structure is advantageous in diffusion and mass transfer of the reaction molecules. A uniform structure benefits the catalytic cracking performance. Table 2 summarizes the physicochemical properties of the HZSM-5 catalyst, including its textural and structural properties. The catalyst exhibited high surface area and porosity, typical of ZSM-5 catalyst and correspondingly favorable for catalytic applications49. The XRF data confirmed the complete removal of Na⁺ ions from the ZSM-5 framework after ion exchange process (< 0.05 wt%). Moreover, the SiO2/Al2O3 ratio decreased slightly from 36.7 to 33.3, indicating limited desilication rather than dealuminum from the zeolite framework. This SiO2/Al2O3 ratio indicates an intermediate acidic level, which maintains the acid density needed in cracking reactions and helps suppress unwanted coke formation.

NH3-TPD analysis

The NH3-TPD profiles for HZSM-5 zeolites typically show two distinct peaks, which are indicative of two different types of acidic sites (Brønsted and Lewis) that exist within the framework of the zeolite50. Brønsted acid sites are derived from bridging hydroxyls (Si-OH-Al), while Lewis acid sites are aluminum species in the framework of the zeolite. The Brønsted acid sites are usually assumed as the dominant active site in HZSM-5 and chemically interact with weakly basic species such as ammonia via hydrogen bonding49.

The NH₃-TPD analysis curve (Fig. 5) for the HZSM-5 sample indicats two distinct desorption peaks: one low-temperature (157 °C), corresponding to weak acid sites, and one high-temperature (375 °C) strong acid sites. The total acidity of the sample (Table 2) is typical of a HZSM-5 with SiO2/Al2O3 ratio of 33.351.

Catalytic results

Impact of process variables

Initial identification of critical process parameters is necessary for effective optimization. Based on the literature data and preliminary tests, the following process variables with catalytic cracking were considered: (i) reactor temperature (T); and (ii) catalyst loading (C). These parameters were investigated in the range of 500–600 °C (a typical operating range for gasoil cracking over zeolites), and 0.1–0.5 g, respectively. The catalyst loading was determined on the basis of the restrictions of the experimental setup, as well as to ensure there was no gas leakage during the reactions. The effect of the mentioned variables on gasoil conversion, ethylene selectivity, propylene selectivity and total light olefins selectivity were investigated. The conversion–temperature profiles for catalyst loadings of 0.1 and 0.3 g exhibit similar trends as illustrated in the graphs (Fig. 6(a)). The conversion decreased when the temperature was increased from 500 to 550 °C for catalyst loadings of 0.1 and 0.3 g. At 600 °C the conversion recovered. The reduction in conversion at 550 °C could be attributed to the accumulation of reaction intermediates or partially cracked molecules on the catalyst surface, which occupy active sites and thus lower the overall rate of reaction. At 600 °C, the higher temperatures facilitate faster cracking rates, leading to the decomposition of these reaction intermediates or volatilization, thus releasing active sites and resulting in a subsequent increase in conversion. Hence, the non-monotonic variation in conversion can be a result of the competition between the formation of reaction intermediates and their subsequent decomposition at higher temperatures. In contrast, the highest loading of the catalyst (0.5 g) shows a monotonic increase in conversion with temperature. This trend is in agreement with previous studies52 as higher temperatures provide increased chances of VGO cracking and hence higher conversion of VGO33. As evident from Fig. 6(b), the amount of catalyst has a pronounced effect on the conversion at different reaction temperatures. The highest conversion occurs at medium catalyst loading of 0.3 g for both 500 and 550 °C. The statistical analysis also verified that the catalyst loading exerted a more dominant effect than temperature on conversion within the investigated conditions.

Effect of (a) reaction temperature and (b) catalyst loading (%) on conversion of gasoil.

Subsequently, the effect of temperature and catalyst loading on product selectivity was investigated (Figs. 7, 8 and 9). The selectivity of ethylene illustrates a monotonic increase with temperature (Fig. 7a), indicating that thermal cracking predominates as the primary mechanism for ethylene formation37,38. At higher temperatures, the increased C–C bond scission results in the preferential formation of smaller olefinic molecules, thereby increasing the ethylene selectivity. Furthermore, the 0.5 g catalyst exhibits the most pronounced rate of temperature increase for ethylene selectivity, suggesting that temperature and catalyst loading synergistically promote the formation of ethylene. Higher catalyst-to-oil ratios provide more active sites facilitating the cracking of heavier hydrocarbons via carbenium-ion cracking which subsequently are converted to ethylene via thermal cracking. In line with these results, Zhao et al. reported that an increase in ZSM-5 loading amount led to a linear increase in ethylene production. The authors also observed the similar increasing trend for propylene and butene yields53.

As an intermediate species, moderate reaction conditions are needed to achieve high yield of propylene. However, propylene selectivity increased steadily with temperature at all catalyst loadings, reaching a maximum at 600 °C (Fig. 8a). This continued increase with increasing temperature confirms that secondary reactions consuming propylene via hydrogen transfer and cracking remained relatively controlled or suppressed under current conditions. According to previous reports, propylene formation typically peaks between 550 °C and 600 °C, which is consistent with this pattern. At higher temperatures (not investigated here), selectivity may decrease due to increased secondary cracking and hydrogen transfer reactions converting light olefins into lighter gases or parrafins54,55. While, propylene selectivity increased steadily with temperature at all catalyst loadings, it remained relatively insensitive to catalyst loading (Fig. 8b). This difference emphasizes the distinct formation pathways: although, the production of ethylene benefits from a higher availability of acid sites as it promotes the secondary cracking to smaller fragments, which then undergoes thermal cracking; in the case of propylene, it is mainly produced through the monomolecular protolytic mechanism, which is less dependent on acid-site density within the investigated range. Among the gaseous products, propylene exhibited the highest selectivity at all temperatures33, resulting in propylene-to-ethylene (P/E) ratios above 2 at all experimental conditions. However, as the temperature increased, the P/E ratio decreased steadily, indicating that high thermal energy favors the formation of ethylene over propylene. These findings are in excellent agreements with observations of AfsharEbrahimi et al.56 and Kubo et al.44. Kubo et al. reported a decrease in the propylene-to-ethylene ratio from 3 at 723 K to 1.4 at 923 K, along with a higher ethylene and propylene selectivity at higher temperatures. In the light of the results shown in Figs. 7 and 8, the observed trends for light olefins selectivity can be mechanistically explained through the competition between monomolecular and bimolecular cracking pathways. At lower temperatures (500 °C), the cracking of gasoil primarily proceeds via the bimolecular mechanism (hydrogen transfer), likely because the acid sites in HZSM-5 are close enough to facilitate these reactions leading to higher C3/C2 ratio. However, as the temperature rises to 600 °C, the monomolecular protolytic cracking becomes dominant. This is consistent with the higher activation energy required for the direct C-C bond scission of the components existing in the gasoil feed. The increased thermal energy facilitates the β-scission of carbenium ions, significantly boosting ethylene formation and leading to the observed P/E ratio57,58. Increasing the catalyst loading from 0.1 to 0.3 g decreased the P/E ratio, indicating the improvement in ethylene yield favored by more active sites and increased cracking activity.

The total light olefins selectivity (ethylene + propylene) is depicted in Fig. 9. The total selectivity increased with increasing temperature rising from ~ 41–45 wt% at 500 °C to ~ 54–60 wt% at 600 °C20,43. The maximum light olefins selectivity (60. 66 wt%) was obtained at 600 °C and 0.5 g catalyst loading, reflecting an optimum balance between thermal cracking (favoring ethylene) and monomolecular protolytic cracking (favoring propylene). Higher catalyst loading (0.5 g) reduced light olefins selectivity, implying that excessive catalyst loading may result in secondary reactions (hydrogen transfer and aromatization) that lower light olefins yield. Across all catalyst loadings, ethylene selectivity increases monotonically with temperature, reaching ~ 20 wt% at 600 °C with 0.5 g. This result confirms that higher temperatures promote cracking pathways that favor the formation of ethylene. However, propylene remained the dominant product (31–41 wt%) in the light olefins distribution.

Methane selectivity (Fig. 10) decreased steadily with increasing catalyst loading; the maximum selectivity was observed at 500 °C and 0.1 g. Increased catalyst loading creates a larger density of acid sites which promotes secondary reaction of the methane precursors (e.g., oligomerisation–cracking or hydrogen transfer) prior to desorption, therefore resulting in lower net methane selectivity59,60. At a catalyst loading of 0.5 g, methane production increased with the temperature, as expected from literature, because of the increased cracking of larger molecules in the feed to produce smaller hydrocarbons.

Effect of reactor temperature and catalyst loading on methane selectivity (wt%).

The primary objective of this study was to optimize light olefins production and gasoil conversion along with minimizing coke formation. Despite achieving the highest selectivity of light olefins (~ 58 wt%) at 600 °C (Fig. 9), the optimal conditions were identified as 500 °C with a catalyst loading of 0.3 g. Results from statistical analysis (one-way, not presented here) showed that the gasoil conversion at both 500 °C and 600 °C was statistically similar (p > 0.05). The advantages of carrying out the reactions at 500 °C included the following: lower energy consumption and decreased thermal stress on reactor materials. All these features make 500 °C particularly appealing from an industrial perspective, where process stability, catalyst lifetime, and operating costs are often more important than the absolute maximum olefin yield. Accordingly, the selected temperature lies in the lower end of a typical operating condition for light-olefin production in the presence of HZSM-5 (500–600 °C)55.

As reported in the literature, the accumulation of coke on the acid sites of the catalyst causes a gradual decrease in conversion61,62. With regard to this mechanism, it is crucial to investigate how catalyst performance changes over time under fixed operating conditions. The gasoil conversion pattern (Fig. 11) over the ZSM-5 catalyst at 500 °C and 0.3 g catalyst loading showed a sharp fall with time, from 70% at first hour to 35% at 360 min, with a rapid initial coke formation followed by a gradual decrease. However, the selectivity of ethylene and propylene (Fig. 12) remained comparatively stable over the entire 6 h time-on-stream, with only minor variations (10–12%). This indicates that the loss of activity is primarily due to the loss of access to the catalyst sites via pore entrances blockage and external carbon aggregation rather than due to the selective poisoning or blocking of the active sites of the catalyst that produce light olefins. The two different stages of catalyst deactivation (i.e., rapid drop then gradual decline) are representative of catalysts like ZSM-5 used for fixed-bed gas oil and VGO cracking and have been well documented in the scientific literature.

Consistent with the recent findings of Maity et al.11, our results confirmed that temperature is the most influential factor in shifting the product distribution toward light olefins. The increasing trend of ethylene with temperature is in excellent agreement with established trends1,11,35,37. While Maity et al. reported an increase in the conversion of n-hexane over H-ZSM-5, our study revealed that when using a heavier feedstock like gasoil, the catalyst loading plays a more critical role in managing the trade-off between conversion and coke-induced deactivation. Specifically, at a catalyst loading of 0.5 g, the observed increase in conversion with temperature aligns well with previous studies1,11,33. Furthermore, the stability results at 500 °C corroborate the mechanistic behavior reported by Liu et al.17, suggesting that although conversion drops due to external surface coking, the intrinsic selectivity of ZSM-5 toward light olefins remains remarkably stable due to its shape-selective MFI framework. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the obtained results for light olefin selectivity and conversion are comparable with established data although no modification has been made to the catalyst.

Conclusion

Conversion exhibited a non-monotonic dependence on temperature for 0.1 and 0.3 g catalyst loading, decreasing from 500 to 550 °C and then recovering at 600 °C. In contrast, at a catalyst loading of 0.5 g, conversion increased steadily with temperature. Increasing catalyst loading enhanced conversion up to 0.3 g, and then with a slight decrease at 0.5 g. Ethylene selectivity depended more strongly on temperature than propylene. Although the maximum yield of light olefins (~ 60 wt%) was achieved at 600 °C, the optimal operating conditions were found to be 500 °C with 0.3 g of catalyst, which resulted in lower coke deposition, reduced energy consumption, and slower catalyst deactivation. These results demonstrate the complex relationship between reaction conditions and catalyst performance, which can provide valuable insights for optimizing light olefin production in industrial applications.
