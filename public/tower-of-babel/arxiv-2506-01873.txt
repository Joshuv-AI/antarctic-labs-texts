A gradient-enhanced approach for stable finite element approximations of reaction-convection-diffusion problems Journal: arXiv
Soheil Firooz, B. Daya Reddy, Paul Steinmann

Abstract
We develop a micromorphic-based approach for finite element stabilization of reaction-convection-diffusion equations, by gradient enhancement of the field of interest via introducing an auxiliary variable. The well-posedness of the coupled-field approach is established, together with an error estimate. Through a set of 1D and 2D numerical examples the high accuracy and enhanced stability of the approach in approximating solutions associated with complex problems is demonstrated, for situations of varying reactivity and convection.



1
Introduction

Reaction-convection-diffusion
equations play a central role in physical and mathematical modeling of transport and reaction phenomena across a wide range of disciplines from heat transfer and fluid flow to mass transport in environmental and chemical/biological systems.

Diffusion
commonly represents the spreading of species within the domain due to concentration gradients.

Convection
accounts for the transport of species driven by the movement of the fluid medium.

Reaction
refers to the chemical, biological or physical interactions through which species are produced or consumed during the process.

In reaction-convection-diffusion problems, if the diffusive effects are negligible compared to convective or reactive effects, the problem is referred to as convection-dominated or reaction-dominated, respectively
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
A well-known challenge that arises in such problems is that the solutions develop sharp gradients and steep internal or boundary layers.
In such scenarios, standard Galerkin finite element methods (FEM) fail to fully resolve the localized features leading to nonphysical, oscillatory solutions with spurious overshoots and undershoots
[
4
,
5
]
.

Over the past few decades, a variety of stabilization techniques have been developed to address the instabilities associated particularly with convection-diffusion equations.
A classical remedy to eliminate such oscillations is to introduce artificial diffusion in the equations.
However, excessive artificial diffusion can smear the solution which affects the solution accuracy.

One of the most well-established stabilization techniques to overcome instabilities in convection-dominated problems is the streamline-upwind Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG) introduced by Brooks and Hughes
[
5
,
6
]
.
The SUPG method adds a perturbation term aligned with the flow/streamline direction to the test functions which effectively introduces a minimal targeted diffusion along streamlines.
The resulting formulation maintains consistency and greatly reduces global oscillations for high Peclet number flows.
In narrow regions such as sharp internal layers or boundary layers, the SUPG solution may still exhibit small overshoot/undershoot profiles because the stabilization is primarily in the flow direction and lacks dissipation in the crosswind direction
[
7
]
.

Another approach developed to address the numerical instabilities that arise in convection-dominated and reaction-dominated problems is the Galerkin/Least-Squares (GLS) method
[
8
,
9
]
.
The GLS method enhances the standard Galerkin formulation via incorporating additional least-squares terms involving the residual of the governing equations
[
10
]
.
Unlike upwinding or artificial diffusion methods, the GLS method retains the variational consistency of the formulation.
A critical and nontrivial aspect of the GLS method is the choice of the stabilization parameter which typically depends on local mesh size, the magnitude of convection and reaction coefficients, and sometimes solution features such as local gradients.
Moreover, due to its global formulation which is advantageous for convergence analysis, GLS fails to offer fine local control over the stabilization effects.
For such cases, other methods such as SUPG or the discontinuous Galerkin finite element method (DGFEM)
[
11
,
12
]
may provide better adaptability and sharper resolution.

The so-called unusual stabilization method
[
13
,
14
]
maintains the original Galerkin framework but augments it with stabilizing terms that are not necessarily derived from a least-squares or residual-based interpretation.
A key feature of this method is its variational consistency where the added stabilization terms vanish when the exact solution is substituted into the formulation thereby preserving convergence and error properties.
Many studies have been carried out to improve the stability and accuracy of the unusual stabilization method, see
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
among others.

Several other techniques have been developed to deal with instabilities associated with convection-dominated and reaction-dominated problems such as the variational multiscale (VMS) method
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, local projection stabilization (LPS) method
[
22
,
23
]
, the symmetric stabilization method
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, the residual bubble free (RFB) method
[
27
,
28
]
, high-resolution Petrov–Galerkin (HRPG) method
[
29
,
30
]
, weak Galerkin FEM
[
31
]
, and variational subgrid scale method
[
32
]
.
See
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
for comparative studies on some stabilizing finite element methods.
Despite these advances, challenges still remain in achieving stability without compromising accuracy.
Some methods require tuning of problem-dependent parameters such as the SUPG weighting parameter or shock-capturing coefficients.
Such limitations motivate the development of improved stabilization strategies that selectively target oscillations and internal layers without smearing the sharp gradients.

In a recent contribution
[
36
]
, a micromorphic-based artificial diffusion (MMAD) method has been proposed for stable and accurate finite element approximation of convection-diffusion problems.
The method is, in principle, a gradient-enhanced extension of the mean-zero artificial diffusion method presented in
[
37
]
, and which is equivalent to a projected artificial diffusion approach. Motivated by the micromorphic approach
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, the modification entails the introduction of a micromorphic-type variable and enhancement in terms of the variable and its gradient.
A number of numerical examples demonstrate the excellent performance of the approach, in the convection-dominated range and for pure advection problems.

In this work the formulation in
[
36
]
is extended to treat reaction-convection-diffusion problems.
Of particular interest are situations in which reaction or convection dominate.
The formulation is shown to be well-posed and convergent for equal-order (linear) approximation of the original variable and that introduced for the enhancement.
A number of numerical examples are presented, for situations of reaction- or advection- dominated flows.
These illustrate the accurate and stable performance of the method, with oscillations or wiggles largely suppressed in regions of steep gradients or localized layers.

The structure of the rest of this work is as follows.
Section 2 introduces the problem and presents the governing equations for reaction-convection-diffusion problems.
The micromorphic-based artificial diffusion method together with details of the well-posedness and convergence of the method are the subject of Section 3.
Section 4 illustrates the performance of the MMAD method through a set of numerical examples.
Section 5 summarizes the the key contributions of the work.

2
Reaction-convection-diffusion problems

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bounded domain with the boundary
\partial\mathcal{B}
and
\boldsymbol{n}
be the outward unit normal to
\partial\mathcal{B}
.
For a scalar quantity
\varphi
, the general
dimensionless
form of the steady-state reaction-convection-diffusion equation reads

\displaystyle\text{Da}\,\varphi+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\Delta\varphi=F\quad\text{in }\mathcal{B}\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to:}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi=\varphi_{\text{p}}\quad&&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}\,,\\
&\nabla\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=t_{\text{p}}\quad&&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{N}}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle\text{Pe}=\frac{UL}{D}\,,\qquad\text{Da}=\frac{BL}{U}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{u}=\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{U}}{U}\,,

where Pe is the Peclet number, Da the Damköhler number,
\boldsymbol{u}
the dimensionless velocity vector,
\boldsymbol{U}
the divergence-free velocity,
D
the diffusion coefficient,
B
the reaction coefficient,
L
a characteristic length of the spatial domain,
U
a characteristic velocity,
\boldsymbol{n}
the unit vector normal to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{B}
,
\varphi_{\text{p}}
the prescribed value of
\varphi
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{p}}
the prescribed flux.

\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}
and
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{N}}
denote the Dirichlet and Neumann parts of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{B}
, respectively with
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}\cap\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{N}}=\emptyset
and
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}\cup\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{N}}=\partial\mathcal{B}
.
The first term in Eq. (
1
) accounts for reaction, the second term accounts for convection, the third term accounts for diffusion and the term on the right is a source term.

The Peclet number quantifies the relative influence of convection to diffusion whereas the Damköhler number quantifies the relative influence of reaction to convection.
If
\text{Pe}\gg 1
and
\text{Da}\ll 1
, convection dominates over diffusion and reaction; sharp gradients or boundary layers are likely to emerge in the solution.
This is common in high-speed flows or large-scale systems.
If
\text{Da}\gg 1
and
\text{Pe}\ll 1
, reaction dominates over convection and diffusion; reaction kinetics are fast compared to transport processes and the species may be consumed near boundaries or in localized regions before being transported further, which also leads to sharp concentration gradients and boundary layers.
This is common in catalytic reactors, biological systems or porous media transport systems.
If
\text{Da}\gg 1
and
\text{Pe}\gg 1
, the system is advection- and reaction-dominated; the solution often exhibits steep concentration gradients along streamlines and thin reactive boundary layers.
This regime is commonly encountered in high-speed combustion processes, industrial chemical reactors or chemical vapor deposition systems.
If
\text{Da}\ll 1
and
\text{Pe}\ll 1
, the system is diffusion dominated; the solution exhibits uniform concentration distributions and smooth profiles where sharp gradients or boundary layers are generally absent.
This regime is typical in microfluidic devices, low-speed flows or dilute biological systems.

To calculate the weak form of Eq. (
1
), we multiply it with a test function
\delta\varphi
and integrate it over
\mathcal{B}
which, using the divergence theorem and Neumann boundary condition, reads

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\text{Da}\,\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{B}}F\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{N}}}t_{\text{p}}\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}a\,,

(2)

for
\varphi=\varphi_{\text{p}}
on
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}
where the test function
\delta\varphi
satisfies
\delta\varphi=0
on
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\text{D}}
.

3
The micromorphic-based artificial diffusion (MMAD) method

The MMAD formulation for reaction-convection-diffusion problems follows closely that for convection-diffusion problems, as set out in
[
36
]
, but for convenience is presented in self-contained fashion.
We assume in this development a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on all of
\partial\mathcal{B}
.
The extension to more general boundary conditions is straightforward.

We start by defining the micromorphic-type variable
\boldsymbol{g}
and the generalized strain-like variable
\boldsymbol{e}=\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}
.
Then for the special case of a reaction-diffusion problem, for which a minimization problem exists, the total potential is given by

\Psi=\Psi_{0}+\Psi_{{}_{\text{\!MM}}}\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\begin{cases}\Psi_{0}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\text{Pe}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\text{Da}}{2}|\varphi|^{2}+F\varphi,\\[8.0pt]
\Psi_{\text{MM}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{g}:\text{A}:\nabla\boldsymbol{g}\end{cases}

(3)

with
\boldsymbol{H}
the micromorphic-type (second-order) coupling tensor,
\boldsymbol{K}
the micromorphic-type (second-order) tensor and
A
the micromorphic-type (fourth-order) “stiffness” tensor.
We define
\boldsymbol{H}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
A
such that they are symmetric and positive-definite; that is, for any vector
\boldsymbol{a}
and second-order tensor
\boldsymbol{A}
we have

\left[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\geq H_{0}\,|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2},\quad\left[\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\geq K_{0}\,|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2},\quad\left[\boldsymbol{\text{A}}:\boldsymbol{A}\right]:\boldsymbol{A}\geq A_{0}\,|\boldsymbol{A}|^{2},

(4)

where
H_{0}
,
K_{0}
and
A_{0}
are positive constants.
The micromorphic-type coupling tensor
\boldsymbol{H}
enforces compatibility between the primary field and the micromorphic variable.
In the discrete formulation,
\boldsymbol{H}
is selected such that it incorporates the effects of upwinding and reaction terms, ensuring appropriate stabilization.
On the other hand, the micromorphic-type “stiffness” tensor
A
governs the spatial scale of micromorphic effects.
Moreover, through appearing in the gradient terms, it also controls the degree to which the micromorphic effects influence the solution.
A key feature of our proposed MMAD method is that the micromorphic effects can be easily controlled by choosing appropriate values for
\boldsymbol{H}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
A
thereby ensuring stable and accurate solutions suited to the problem at hand.

Remark:
The choice
\text{A}=\text{O}
,
\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{H}=p\boldsymbol{I}
reduces the MMAD formulation to the MZAD approach for pure advection coupled-problems introduced in
[
37
]
.

The weak formulation or first variation corresponding to the resulting energy formulation

\Pi(\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\Psi_{0}(\varphi)+\Psi_{{}_{\text{\!MM}}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})-F\varphi\right]\mbox{d}v\,.

(5)

to which is then added the convective term, is then given by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\text{Da}\,\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}\right]\Big]\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{B}}F\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[-\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}\right]+\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}\Big]\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{g}\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[\text{A}:\nabla\boldsymbol{g}\Big]:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}\,\mbox{d}v=0\,.

We define the spaces and the norms

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{B})=\left\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B})\,\middle|\,\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}),\;\varphi=0\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{B}\right\},\quad

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\Phi}=\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,\mbox{d}v\right)^{1/2},

(7)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\,\middle|\,g_{i}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})\right\},\quad

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\right)\,\mbox{d}v.

Our objective is to seek
\varphi\in\Phi
and
\boldsymbol{g}\in G
that satisfy Eq. (
6
) for all
\delta\varphi\in\Phi
and
\delta\boldsymbol{g}\in G
.

3.1
The discrete MMAD formulation

We denote the discrete conforming approximations of
\varphi
and
\boldsymbol{g}
as
\varphi_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{h}
, respectively, together with their corresponding test functions
\delta\varphi_{h}
and
\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}
.
For the discretized MMAD formulation, the objective is: for all
\delta\varphi_{h}\in\Phi^{h}
and
\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\in G^{h}
, find
\varphi_{h}\in\Phi^{h}\subset\Phi
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\in G^{h}\subset G
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\!\!\text{Da}\,\varphi_{h}\,\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\!\!\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}\,\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\nabla\varphi_{h}\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\!\Big[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}-\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\Big]\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\!\!F\,\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[-\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}-\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]+\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\Big]\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\,\mbox{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[\text{A}:\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\Big]:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\,\mbox{d}v=0\,.

3.1.1
Well-posedness and convergence analysis

To elaborate on the well-posedness of the formulation, we define the space
\widehat{\Phi}=\Phi\times G
.
For
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}:=(\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})\in\widehat{\Phi}
the norm
||\bullet||_{\widehat{\Phi}}
is defined by
||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}||^{2}_{\widehat{\Phi}}=||\varphi||^{2}_{\Phi}+||\boldsymbol{g}||^{2}_{G}
.
Then the fully discrete version of Eq. (
8
) can be written as follows:
for all
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}\in\widehat{\Phi}^{h}
, find
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}\in\widehat{\Phi}^{h}
such that

B\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}\right)=\ell(\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})

(9)

where

\displaystyle B\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}\right)\!=\!\!\!\!\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[\text{Da}\,\varphi_{h}\delta\varphi_{h}\!+\!\boldsymbol{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla\varphi_{h}\delta\varphi_{h}\!+\!\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\nabla\varphi_{h}\!\cdot\!\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}+\Big[\boldsymbol{H}\!\cdot\!\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\Big]\!\cdot\!\left[\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}\!-\!\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\!+\!\left[\boldsymbol{K}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\!\cdot\!\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\!+\!\left[\text{A}\!:\!\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\!:\!\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\bigg]\mbox{d}v\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\ell(\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})=\int_{\mathcal{B}}F\,\delta\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v\,.

To show well-posedness, one needs to show that
B(\bullet,\bullet)
is coercive and continuous, and
\ell(\bullet)
is continuous.
That is,

\displaystyle B(\varphi_{h},\boldsymbol{g}_{h};\varphi_{h},\boldsymbol{g}_{h})\geq M\;||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||^{2}_{\widehat{\Phi}}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle B(\varphi_{h},\boldsymbol{g}_{h};\delta\varphi_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h})\leq m\;||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\;||\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\,,

\displaystyle\ell(\varphi_{h},\boldsymbol{g}_{h})=c\;||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\,,

with
M
,
m
and
c
being positive constants.
For the coercivity of
B(\bullet,\bullet)
, we start with

B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})=\!\!\!\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[\text{Da}\,|\varphi_{h}|^{2}\!+\boldsymbol{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla\varphi_{h}\varphi_{h}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}|\nabla\varphi_{h}|^{2}+\left[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\right]\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]+\left[\boldsymbol{K}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{h}+\left[\text{A}:\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\!:\!\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\bigg]\,\mbox{d}v\,.\\

(12)

For every
\varphi_{h}\in\Phi^{h}
, using the divergence theorem, we have

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}\right]\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v=\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}}\varphi_{h}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mbox{d}a-\int_{\mathcal{B}}\varphi_{h}\bigg[\underbrace{\left[\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\right]}_{=0}\varphi_{h}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}\bigg]\,\mbox{d}v\,.

(13)

Since
\varphi_{h}=0
on
\partial\mathcal{B}
, we can write

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}\right]\varphi_{h}\,\mbox{d}v=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}}\varphi_{h}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\mbox{d}a=0\,.

(14)

From Eq. (
12
) and using Eq. (
14
) we have

B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})\geq\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\text{Da}\,|\varphi_{h}|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}|\nabla\varphi_{h}|^{2}+H_{0}|\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}+K_{0}|\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}+A_{0}|\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}\right]\mbox{d}v\,.

(15)

Using the inequality

2ab\leq\varepsilon a^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}b^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left[a-b\right]^{2}\geq\left[1-\varepsilon\right]a^{2}+\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right]b^{2}\,,

(16)

which holds for any
\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi_{h}-\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}\geq\left[1-\varepsilon\right]|\nabla\varphi_{h}|^{2}+\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right]|\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}\,.

(17)

Consequently, Eq.(
15
) becomes

\displaystyle B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[\text{Da}\,|\varphi_{h}|^{2}+\Big[\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}+H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon\right]\Big]|\nabla\varphi_{h}|^{2}+\Big[H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right]+K_{0}\Big]|\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}+A_{0}|\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}\bigg]\mbox{d}v

(18)

\displaystyle\geq\left[\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}$}}+H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon\right]\right]||\varphi_{h}||^{2}_{\Phi}+\Big[H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right]+K_{0}\Big]|\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}+A_{0}|\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left[\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}$}}+H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon\right]\right]||\varphi_{h}||^{2}_{\Phi}+\text{min}\Big(H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right]+K_{0},A_{0}\Big)||\boldsymbol{g}_{h}||^{2}_{G}\,,

choosing
\varepsilon
such that
0<\varepsilon<1
, and
\boldsymbol{K}
such that
K_{0}+H_{0}(1-\varepsilon^{-1})>0
.
Then

B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})\geq M||\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\,||_{\widehat{\Phi}}^{2}\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad M=\text{min}\left(\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}$}}+H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon\right],\,\text{min}\left(K_{0}+H_{0}\left[1-\varepsilon^{-1}\right],\,A_{0}\right)\right)\,.

(19)

For continuity we have

B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})\!=\!\!\!\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[\text{Da}\,\varphi_{h}\,\delta\varphi_{h}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}\delta\varphi_{h}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\nabla\varphi_{h}\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}+\left[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\right]\cdot\left[\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}\!-\!\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]+\left[\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}+\left[\text{A}\!:\!\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\right]\!:\!\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}\bigg]\mbox{d}v\,,

(20)

Then using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle|B(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h},\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[|\varphi_{h}|\,|\delta\varphi_{h}|+u_{\text{max}}|\nabla\varphi_{h}|\,|\delta\varphi_{h}|+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}|\nabla\varphi_{h}|\,|\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+H_{\text{max}}|\nabla\varphi_{h}\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|\,|\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}\!-\!\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|+K_{\text{max}}|\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|\,|\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|+A_{\text{max}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|\,|\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}|\bigg]\mbox{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\text{Da}||\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}}||\delta\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}}+\left[u_{\text{max}}||\delta\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\,||\nabla\delta\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}}\right]||\nabla\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2H_{\text{max}}||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}||\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}+K_{\text{max}}||\boldsymbol{g}_{h}||_{L^{2}}||\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}||_{L^{2}}+A_{\text{max}}||\nabla\boldsymbol{g}_{h}||_{L^{2}}||\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{g}_{h}||_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\text{Da}+u_{\text{max}}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}+2H_{\text{max}}+K_{\text{max}}+A_{\text{max}}\right]||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}||\delta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\,,

(21)

with

\displaystyle u_{\text{max}}=\underset{i}{\text{max}}\,|u_{i}|\,,\qquad H_{\text{max}}=\underset{ij}{\text{max}}\,|H_{ij}|\,,\qquad K_{\text{max}}=\underset{ij}{\text{max}}\,|K_{ij}|\,,\qquad A_{\text{max}}=\underset{ijkl}{\text{max}}\,|A_{ijkl}|\,.

(22)

Therefore
B(\bullet,\bullet)
is continuous with constant

m=\text{Da}+u_{\text{max}}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}+2H_{\text{max}}+K_{\text{max}}+A_{\text{max}}\,.

(23)

The proof of continuity of
\ell(\bullet)
is straightforward and is omitted here.

3.1.2
Error analysis

The continuity and coercivity of
B(\bullet,\bullet)
imply the standard finite element error estimate for
V^{h},G^{h}
comprising continuous piecewise-linear polynomial is
[
41
]

||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{h}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\leq Ch\,.

(24)

Thus, we have convergence at a linear rate and the constant
C
is given by
C=c\left[m/M\right]
, where
m
and
M
are the continuity and coercivity constants, respectively, and
c
a constant that depends on the
\mathcal{H}^{2}
semi-norm of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
.
Next, we consider the modelling error.
The original problem is to find
\varphi_{0}\in\Phi
such that

\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\bigg[\text{Da}\,\varphi_{0}\,\delta\varphi+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{0}\delta\varphi+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Pe}}\nabla\varphi_{0}\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi\bigg]\mbox{d}v}_{a\left(\varphi_{0},\delta\varphi\right)}=\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{B}}F\,\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v}_{\ell\left(\delta\varphi\right)}\,.\\

(25)

On the other hand, the MMAD problem is given by (
6
).
Thus we have an error
\left[\varphi-\varphi_{0}\right]
between the original (or actual) and MMAD solutions.
We now estimate this error.
Setting

a\left(\varphi,\delta\varphi\right)+b\left(\varphi,\,\boldsymbol{g};\,\delta\varphi\right)=\ell(\delta\varphi)\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad b\left(\varphi,\,\boldsymbol{g};\,\delta\varphi\right)=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big[\boldsymbol{H}\cdot\left[\nabla\varphi\!-\!\boldsymbol{g}\right]\Big]\cdot\nabla\delta\varphi\,\mbox{d}v\,,

(26)

from Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
) we have

a\left(\varphi-\varphi_{0},\,\delta\varphi\right)+b\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\,\delta\varphi\right)=0\,.

(27)

Now
a\left(\bullet,\,\bullet\right)
is coercive, i.e.

a\left(\varphi,\,\varphi\right)\geq M_{0}\,||\varphi||^{2}_{\Phi}\,,

(28)

with
M_{0}=1/\text{Pe}
using Eq. (
14
).
Next, set
\delta\varphi=\varphi-\varphi_{0}
in Eq. (
27
); this gives

a\left(\varphi-\varphi_{0},\,\varphi-\varphi_{0}\right)+b\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\,\varphi-\varphi_{0}\right)=0\,.

(29)

Thus, from Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
),

M_{0}\;||\varphi-\varphi_{0}||^{2}_{\Phi}\leq b\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\,\varphi_{0}-\varphi\right)\leq\beta_{0}\;||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\;||\varphi-\varphi_{0}||_{{\Phi}}\,,

(30)

where
\beta_{0}=H_{\text{max}}
.
Hence,

||\varphi-\varphi_{0}||_{\Phi}\leq\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{0}}{M_{0}}||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}\,.

(31)

Finally, combining Eqs. (
31
) and (
24
) we have, for the error between the original solution and its MMAD finite element approximation,

||\varphi_{0}-\varphi_{h}||_{\Phi}\leq||\varphi_{0}-\varphi||_{\Phi}+||\varphi-\varphi_{h}||_{\Phi}\leq\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{0}}{M_{0}}||\widehat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}||_{\widehat{\Phi}}+Ch\,.

(32)

Remark:

The modelling error depends on
\beta_{0}/M_{0}=H_{\text{max}}\text{Pe}
.
Thus it can be controlled in the limit of small diffusivity (
\text{Pe}>\!\!>1
) through an appropriate choice for
\boldsymbol{H}
.
The finite element approximation error depends on
m/M
, where these constants are given in (
23
) and (
19
).
The choices for
\boldsymbol{H}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
A
will be discussed in the following sub-section.

Remark:

We note that we have stability in the limit of vanishing reaction (
\text{Da}\rightarrow 0
).

3.2
The choice of
\boldsymbol{H}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
A

The next step to complete our approach is to elaborate on the choice of
\boldsymbol{H}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
A
.
Following the works
[
5
,
42
]
, we set

\boldsymbol{H}=\overline{k}_{\text{c}}\,\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}+\overline{k}_{\text{r}}\boldsymbol{I}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{I}\,,\qquad\text{A}=\text{I}\,.

(33)

where

\displaystyle\overline{k}_{\text{c}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{PD}}u_{i}h_{i}{\gamma}_{i}/\text{PD}\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad{\gamma}_{i}=\coth(\alpha_{i})-1/\alpha_{i}\,,\qquad\alpha_{i}=u_{i}h_{i}/2D\,,\qquad u_{i}=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\overline{k}_{\text{r}}=D\left[\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\beta^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{\left[\sinh(\beta)\right]^{2}}-1\right]\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\beta=\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{B}{D}\displaystyle\frac{h^{2}}{4}}\,,

with the subscripts “c” and “r” referring to convection and reaction, respectively, PD being the problem dimension,
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
the unit vectors in the finite element natural coordinate system and
h_{i}
the element characteristic length in each natural coordinate direction.

In this section we evaluate the performance the MMAD formulation through a set of numerical examples in one- and two-dimensional settings.
Six different examples are examined.

The first example is 1D and the rest of the examples are 2D.
In order to effectively examine the influence of reaction or convection dominance in the problem, different cases with various Damköhler numbers and Peclet numbers are considered.
All simulations are carried out using our in-house finite element code.
Throughout the examples, continuous piecewise-linear functions are employed for the finite element approximation of all fields.

Example 1:

In this example we consider a 1D reaction-convection-diffusion problem.
As shown in Fig.
1

\varphi=0
and the source term is
F=1

Fig.

The domain is discretized into
100

The solid black line in each plot shows the exact solution and the dashed line with points shows numerical solutions.
Five different cases are considered; two extreme cases in the top row and the bottom row representing a highly reaction-dominated regime and a highly convection-dominated regime, respectively; and three intermediate cases with comparable convection and reaction contributions.
For all these cases the diffusion is very small.
It is observed that the FEM solution shows small oscillations in the vicinity of the sharp gradients for the reaction-dominated case.
For the three intermediate cases, increasing the Peclet number damps out the oscillations on the left side but exacerbates the oscillations on the right side.
For the convection-dominated case, the FEM solution is completely oscillatory throughout the entire domain.
The MMAD method for all cases gives stable, accurate and oscillation-free solutions.

5
Summary and outlook

We have proposed a novel micromorphic-based artificial diffusion (MMAD) method to circumvent the instabilities commonly observed in standard Galerkin finite element approximations of reaction-convection-diffusion problems.
The key idea in our approach is to introduce an auxiliary micromorphic-type variable, which enriches the formulation through the addition of terms involving the variable and its gradient.
Sufficient conditions for well-posedness and convergence of the method have been presented.
A number of examples comprising reaction- or convection-dominated situations, as well as combinations of two, illustrate the stability and accuracy of the MMAD approach.
