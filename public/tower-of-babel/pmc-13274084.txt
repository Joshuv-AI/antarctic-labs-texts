Interpretable machine learning for postoperative nausea and vomiting prediction in elderly orthopedic patients: a comparative study
Authors: Li-Heng Li, Hao Guo, Hao Wang, Yu-Bo Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) prolongs hospitalization and reduces patient satisfaction. Introduction

Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting (PONV) remains one of the most frequent complications following surgery, directly impacting patient recovery and satisfaction [1–3]. While the baseline incidence ranges from 20% to 30%, it can escalate to 80% in specific high-risk cohorts, such as female patients or those with a history of motion sickness [4, 5]. Despite the availability of prophylactic antiemetics, clinical interventions often remain generalized. A significant challenge is the timing of symptom onset; although many cases occur early, delayed PONV, manifesting within a 7-day window, is often overlooked once patients transition to general wards or are discharged [6, 7]. This gap in monitoring increases the healthcare burden and complicates postoperative management.

Current predictive tools primarily rely on traditional logistic regression or nomograms [8–11]. While these models are easy to implement, their reliance on a limited set of 5 to 8 clinical indicators often fails to capture the multi-dimensional nature of PONV. Furthermore, traditional statistical methods struggle to process non-linear relationships or high-dimensional data, leading to suboptimal generalization across different clinical settings. There is also a notable subjectivity in variable selection, which frequently depends on clinical experience rather than algorithmic rigor.

Machine Learning (ML) has emerged as a robust alternative for processing complex clinical datasets [12–15]. Unlike traditional models, ML algorithms can integrate diverse features, ranging from physiological markers to surgical variables to uncover hidden patterns. However, existing ML studies for PONV have limitations: many lack standardized preprocessing, fail to optimize base-model selection, or ignore the critical 7-day postoperative window. Perhaps most importantly, the “black-box” nature of many ML models hinders clinical trust. To address these issues, this study developed an interpretable ML framework. We utilized a three-phase stacked architecture combined with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to provide a high-accuracy, clinically transparent tool for PONV risk identification. Because the final model incorporates critical intraoperative variables, it is specifically intended for deployment at the end of surgery or upon admission to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU), enabling personalized prophylactic antiemetic interventions for elderly patients undergoing hip or knee surgery.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective observational investigation, conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between May 2023 and August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 2023-K182-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study has also been registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, with the registration number ChiCTR2300074920. All study procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national ethical guidelines. Notably, this study represents a retrospective secondary analysis of the aforementioned registered clinical trial data, and the variables of interest, analytical framework, and clinical endpoints/outcome measures adopted in the current study differ from those specified in the original trial registration protocol; all analyses were conducted using de-identified existing data without additional intervention of participants.

Study participants

This study included patients aged 65 years or older scheduled for elective hip or knee surgery under general anesthesia. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 65 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–IV; and (3) undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria were: (1) a preoperative history of dementia or psychiatric disorders that could compromise the accuracy of subjective symptom reporting; and (2) substantially incomplete medical records. After applying these criteria, 1216 eligible patients were included in the final analysis.

Data collection and variable definition

We extracted preoperative demographic characteristics, clinical features, and laboratory results from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Preoperative demographic and clinical features

Extracted demographic data included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), educational level, and ASA physical status. Comorbidities and social histories (smoking and alcohol consumption) were retrieved from past medical charts.

Baseline sleep status was documented using the sleep Numerical Rating Scale (sleep-NRS) and the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS). Preoperative frailty status was evaluated using the FRAIL scale, classifying patients into non-frail (0 points), pre-frail (1–2 points), and frail (> 2 points). Cognitive function was assessed via the Mini-Cognitive Assessment Instrument (Mini-Cog), with scores < 2 indicating mild cognitive impairment. Preoperative pain intensity was measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Symptoms of depression and anxiety were quantified using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales. Metabolic syndrome (Mets) was defined as meeting at least three of the following criteria: obesity, elevated fasting triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), elevated blood pressure, and elevated fasting blood glucose.

Preoperative laboratory test data

Laboratory data obtained within 72 h prior to surgery were included to reflect the immediate preoperative physiological status. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated as: platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) was calculated as: serum albumin (g/L) + 5×lymphocyte count (×109/L). Other extracted laboratory indicators included baseline hemoglobin, random blood glucose, serum sodium (Na⁺), serum potassium (K⁺), and serum calcium (Ca²⁺).

Intraoperative data

Intraoperative variables were retrieved from anesthesia and surgical nursing records. These included the timing of surgery (morning [08:00–12:00] or afternoon [14:00–18:00]), duration of surgery, duration of anesthesia, intraoperative blood loss, fluid infusion volume, and blood transfusion status. We also recorded the use of dexmedetomidine, intraoperative analgesic dosages, the implementation of peripheral nerve blocks (PNB), and the setup of patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) decrease of > 20% from the baseline or an absolute MAP < 60 mmHg.

Definition of the outcome indicator

The primary outcome was the occurrence of PONV within 7 days postoperatively. Because some patients might be discharged before day 7, PONV was identified comprehensively based on anesthesia recovery records, postoperative nursing notes, documented patient complaints in the general ward, and standardized post-discharge telephone follow-up records. To distinguish true PONV from non-PONV gastrointestinal symptoms, diagnoses were cross-referenced with antiemetic administration logs. An outcome event was positively recorded if a patient experienced at least one episode of nausea (subjective gastric discomfort with an urge to vomit) or vomiting (expulsion of gastric contents) during the 7-day observation window.

Data preprocessing and splitting

The dataset was strictly partitioned into a training set (70%), a validation set (10%), and a testing set (20%) prior to any imputation or feature selection to prevent data leakage. The exact roles of each subset were predefined: the training set was exclusively used for base-model hyperparameter tuning and feature selection; the validation set served as an architecture sanity check to monitor diversity and prevent evaluation bias during the selection of the meta-model; and the 20% testing set remained locked and was evaluated only once for the final clinical benchmarking. No clinical variables were excluded due to excessive missingness, as all candidate features had missing data rates below 30%. For the remaining missing values, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) using Bayesian Ridge regression was performed. Crucially, the imputation model was fitted exclusively on the training set and subsequently applied to transform the validation and testing sets. Following imputation, continuous variables were standardized (mean of 0, variance of 1) using a scaler fitted solely on the training data. Finally, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was conducted strictly within the training set to identify core predictive features for subsequent machine learning modeling. The rationale for applying LASSO-based feature selection across all candidate algorithms, including tree-based models that possess intrinsic feature selection capabilities, was to standardize the input feature space, ensure fair model comparison, avoid dimensional misalignment, and reduce integration bias in the StackNet meta-model. This design is strictly leakage‑safe because LASSO was fitted only on the training set. A sensitivity analysis comparing model performance with and without LASSO feature selection was performed to validate the robustness of this feature selection strategy. Uniform feature inputs also improve the stability, reproducibility, and interpretability of the final stacked ensemble.

Base model development and hyperparameter tuning

Given the retrospective nature of this study, data regarding prior history of PONV and motion sickness, two essential components of the Apfel score, were not consistently documented in the electronic health records. Consequently, the Apfel score could not be calculated. Additionally, nitrous oxide exposure was not considered as it is not utilized in our institution’s standardized anesthetic protocols. Factors such as the specific anesthetic maintenance method and antiemetic prophylaxis strategy were also excluded from the predictive variables, as these were highly homogenized across the study population according to the hospital’s clinical pathways for elderly orthopedic surgery, thereby offering limited discriminatory power for the machine learning models. Instead, a conventional Logistic Regression (LR) model, developed using the same clinical features, was utilized as the established clinical prediction benchmark (the most widely used standard model in perioperative risk assessment).Twelve machine learning algorithms (including the LR baseline) were evaluated. Logistic Regression was included as one of the base learners in the StackNet framework for meta‑feature construction, and an independent LR model was separately trained for performance comparison. To optimize predictive performance and prevent overfitting, each model underwent systematic hyperparameter tuning using GridSearchCV combined with 5-fold Stratified K-fold cross-validation. This tuning process was strictly confined to the training set. We tested predefined ranges and distributions for critical parameters, such as learning rate, maximum depth, and L2 regularization for tree-based models, and penalty terms for linear models. The models configured with their optimal hyperparameters were then evaluated on the independent validation and testing sets. The detailed hyperparameter search spaces, best parameters, and cross-validation strategies for all models are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The detailed hyperparameter search spaces, best parameters, and comprehensive performance metrics of all 12 candidate machine learning models are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Since the Apfel score could not be computed due to missing key variables, logistic regression (LR) was used as the established clinical prediction benchmark, representing the most widely used conventional model in perioperative risk assessment.

Construction of the Diversity-Driven Dynamic StackNet (Fig. 1)

Flowchart of PONV prediction model construction. This flowchart details the sequential phases of the study, including data preprocessing, rigorous dataset splitting to prevent leakage, LASSO-based feature selection, and the development of the StackNet ensemble architecture

To maximize predictive efficacy and clinical reliability without increasing the risk of overfitting, this study developed a novel “Diversity-Driven Dynamic Restacking Network.” To strictly prevent data leakage and evaluation bias, the entire architecture was constructed with rigorous dataset isolation. The updated framework consists of five sequential phases:

Phase 1: Leakage-safe preprocessing and strict data splitting

To ensure absolute isolation, the cleaned cohort data first undergoes strictly isolated imputation using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE, M = 5) and standardization. Following preprocessing, the dataset is rigorously partitioned into three distinct groups: a Training Set (70%) for dynamic feature allocation, a Validation Set (10%) for architecture sanity checks, and a strictly locked Test Set (20%).

Phase 2: Base learner generation and diversity filtering

Within the training set, a diverse pool of 12 machine learning base models is generated and tuned via 5-Fold Cross-Validation. In traditional stacking architectures, high similarity among base models often leads to severe multicollinearity. To address this, out-of-fold (OOF) prediction probabilities are extracted and passed through a strict collinearity filter. Any predictive sequences exhibiting a Pearson correlation greater than 0.90 are eliminated to remove highly homogenized outputs.

Phase 3: Restacking integration

To preserve the authentic clinical context during final decision-making, the framework employs a feature augmentation strategy. The filtered, diverse OOF probabilities are directly concatenated with the patients’ raw clinical features sourced from the training set. This creates augmented meta-features that provide high-quality, complementary inputs for the next layer.

Phase 4: Meta-model competition

To identify the optimal top-level architecture, we implemented a dynamic competition mechanism evaluating multiple algorithms: Logistic Regression (LR), Ridge, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), XGBoost, and LightGBM. The augmented meta-features are fed into this candidate pool. Crucially, the isolated 10% Validation Set is introduced during this phase to actively monitor and prevent evaluation bias, ultimately determining the winning optimal Meta-Model (Dynamic StackNet).

Phase 5: Final clinical benchmark

The strictly locked 20% Test Set—which deliberately bypasses all prior training and fusion layers—is utilized only once for the final evaluation. The finalized StackNet is benchmarked against a Clinical Baseline Model (LR) to validate its superior performance. Comprehensive predictive and clinical utility is assessed using a suite of metrics including Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), Calibration plots, McNemar’s test, and SHAP value interpretations to successfully break the “black-box” limitation.

Model evaluation and statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics (Table 1) were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests. Continuous variables were presented as medians [interquartile ranges] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, as they did not follow a normal distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For model evaluation, discrimination was assessed using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, F1-Score, Balanced Accuracy, and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), with 95% confidence intervals calculated where applicable.

Results

Patients characteristics and data preprocessing

This study included 1216 elderly patients who underwent elective hip or knee surgery. A total of 1,216 patients were included in the final analysis. Within 7 days post-operation, the overall incidence of PONV was 33% (401/1216). Following the 7:2:1 split, the training set comprised 851 patients with a PONV incidence of 33.0% (n = 281), the validation set included 122 patients with an incidence of 32.8% (n = 40), and the independent test set consisted of 243 patients with an incidence of 32.9% (n = 80). There were no statistically significant differences in the baseline clinical characteristics or the primary outcome incidence among the three datasets (all P > 0.05), confirming the efficacy of the stratified partitioning strategy. Significant baseline differences between the groups were observed in gender, education level, metabolic syndrome, smoking and drinking history, preoperative sleep disorders, preoperative anxiety and depression, PCA use, and Dexmedetomidine administration (Table 1).

Clinical baseline and single model evaluation

To establish a benchmark against standard clinical risk frameworks, we evaluated a Logistic Regression model as the conventional clinical baseline. On the independent test set, the Logistic Regression model yielded an AUC of 0.7564, an Accuracy of 0.6584, a Sensitivity of 0.6750, and a Specificity of 0.6503.

Concurrently, we evaluated 12 machine learning algorithms to map the dataset’s non-linear relationships. To optimize performance and prevent overfitting, each model underwent hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation strictly on the training set. Among the single machine learning models evaluated, CatBoost demonstrated the strongest overall discriminative performance, achieving an AUC of 0.9323 and an Accuracy of 0.9012 on the test set. StackNet development and validation

We developed a StackNet meta-model to fuse the predictive probabilities of the base learners with the original clinical features. To mitigate the severe overfitting frequently observed in tree-based meta-models, we restricted the top-layer meta-model to highly regularized linear architectures. On the independent test set, the StackNet model achieved an accuracy of 0.7860, sensitivity of 0.9250, specificity of 0.7178, and AUC of 0.9338, while the Logistic Regression baseline achieved an accuracy of 0.6584, sensitivity of 0.6750, specificity of 0.6503, and AUC of 0.7564 (Table 2).

Compared to the Logistic Regression baseline, the StackNet model demonstrated a substantial improvement in discriminative ability (AUC 0.9338 vs. 0.7564). To evaluate the statistical significance of this improvement, we applied McNemar’s exact test to compare the diagnostic classifications between the StackNet model and the Logistic Regression baseline. Utilizing the optimal Youden thresholds (0.3319 for the baseline model and 0.4182 for the meta-model), the test indicated that the reduction in classification errors achieved by StackNet was highly statistically significant (p < 0.0001; contingency table off-diagonal elements: b = 72.0, c = 7.0).

Model interpretability (SHAP analysis)

To break the “black-box” nature of the prediction, SHAP values were used to quantify feature contributions. Figure 3A and B display the mean absolute SHAP values for the best single model (CatBoost) and the StackNet model, respectively, detailing the primary predictive drivers.

(B) Bar chart of the mean absolute SHAP values for the best-performing single model (CatBoost). (C) SHAP beeswarm plot for the StackNet model, illustrating the density and directional impact (positive or negative) of each feature on PONV prediction. (D) SHAP beeswarm plot for the CatBoost model. (E) SHAP force plot (Data from a randomly selected patient) for the CatBoost model, showing the competing forces of individual risk factors pushing the base value toward the final predicted probability

In the StackNet framework, predicted probabilities from individual base models (e.g., Prob_CatBoost) are integrated with the original clinical data as meta-features. This dual-feature space enables the meta-learner to utilize both the aggregated consensus of multiple models and the granular, patient-specific details of the raw attributes. Consequently, the ensemble exploits model diversity more effectively to mitigate individual algorithmic biases. The beeswarm plot (Fig. 3C) illustrates the directional impact of these clinical features in the StackNet model.

For individualized clinical application, the SHAP decision plot (Fig. 3D) and force plot (Fig. 3E) map how the model synthesizes multiple competing risk factors to arrive at a specific probability for a single patient. This allows anesthesiologists to trace the exact physiological rationale behind each risk alert. Furthermore, stochastic stability analysis revealed that the StackNet meta-model possesses superior robustness. As illustrated by the cross-validation AUC distributions, the StackNet model exhibited a noticeably narrower variance across multiple random seeds compared to the best single model, indicating highly stable predictive performance that is not reliant on a fortuitous data split.

Clinical utility analysis

To assess the practical value of the model in clinical decision-making, we evaluated probability calibration and net benefit against the clinical baseline. While discrimination metrics indicate a model’s ability to rank patients, clinical interventions require accurate absolute risk estimates.

The calibration curve (Fig. 4A) demonstrates the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual PONV observation rates. The StackNet model achieved an excellent Brier Score of 0.102, substantially outperforming the Logistic Regression baseline (0.1976) in prediction reliability.

Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA, Fig. 4B) confirms that the StackNet model provides a markedly higher clinical net benefit across practical threshold probabilities compared to the Logistic Regression baseline, as well as both “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies. This demonstrates that using StackNet to guide prophylactic antiemetic administration results in a highly accurate identification of high-risk patients while minimizing false positives, confirming its true clinical added value over established standard baseline frameworks.

Discussion

PONV affects nearly one-third of surgical patients and remains a leading cause of patient dissatisfaction and delayed discharge [16–19]. Our study focused on elderly patients undergoing hip and knee surgery, a population with unique risk profiles due to multiple comorbidities and reduced physiological reserves [20–22]. It is important to note that the Apfel score, a widely utilized clinical tool, could not be applied to our retrospective cohort due to missing data on prior PONV history and motion sickness. To ensure a robust clinical benchmark, we employed LR as the commonly used model in clinical practice. By developing and validating the StackNet model, we achieved superior predictive performance compared to this conventional baseline and standard single-algorithm models.

In our comparative analysis, tree-based ensemble algorithms, particularly XGBoost and CatBoost, outperformed linear models. This is consistent with previous literature suggesting that tree models are better suited for clinical data where non-linear interactions between variables are common [23]. Linear models like Ridge Classifier performed poorly here, likely because they require strict assumptions about data distribution that clinical reality rarely satisfies. The developed StackNet model further enhanced these results. By integrating multiple base learners, the ensemble approach compensates for individual model biases, resulting in a more stable and accurate predictor. Our framework aligns with recent advancements in the field, such as the “stacked weight-based meta-learning” approach proposed by Moradi et al. [24], which demonstrated that integrating multiple base models significantly enhances predictive performance. However, our Diversity-Driven Dynamic StackNet advances this concept by incorporating a hybrid feature fusion layer, which simultaneously processes out-of-fold probabilities and raw clinical variables at the meta-level to ensure superior probability calibration [25, 26].

Our findings confirm that focusing on department-specific populations, rather than a general surgical cohort, yields more precise risk assessments for orthopedic patients [27–30].

The SHAP analysis provided critical insights into the drivers of PONV in this elderly cohort. Preoperative frailty and baseline Hb levels emerged as primary predictors. Interestingly, while frailty did not show a statistically significant difference in the univariable baseline analysis (Table 1, p = 0.956), it emerged as a primary driver in the SHAP multivariable space. This discrepancy highlights the advantage of machine learning over traditional univariable statistics: ML algorithms can capture complex, non-linear interactions between frailty and surgical stressors that a simple baseline comparison would overlook. Frailty reflects a patient’s overall physiological reserve, while low Hb levels are often associated with postoperative anemia, which can impair gastrointestinal blood supply and trigger nausea [31–34]. These findings suggest clear clinical targets: optimizing transfusion thresholds, implementing multidisciplinary care for frail patients, and refining hemostasis techniques could directly lower PONV incidence [35, 36].

Despite the strengths of the StackNet model, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the data originated from a single center, which may limit the model’s applicability to institutions with different anesthetic protocols or patient demographics. Second, while SHAP improves transparency, the clinical adoption of such models still requires user-friendly interfaces, such as web applications, to provide real-time risk thresholds. Future work will focus on external validation and the development of a user-friendly web application, as well as its integration into electronic medical record systems, to facilitate personalized perioperative care publicly.

Conclusion

The StackNet framework provides significantly better predictive performance and calibration than conventional Logistic Regression for PONV prediction in elderly orthopedic patients. We developed and validated this model specifically for patients undergoing hip and knee surgery. By leveraging SHAP interpretability, the model identifies high-risk individuals based on core clinical drivers like frailty and hemoglobin levels. This framework provides a technical foundation for early clinical intervention and more efficient allocation of medical resources, ultimately improving the quality of postoperative recovery for high-risk elderly patients. Given the inclusion of intraoperative parameters, this model is designed to be deployed at the end of surgery or upon admission to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU), serving as a real-time risk stratification tool for delayed PONV.
