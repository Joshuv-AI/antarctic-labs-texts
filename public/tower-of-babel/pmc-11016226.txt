Stakeholder perspectives on the Kisoboka intervention: A behavioral and structural intervention to reduce hazardous alcohol use and improve HIV care engagement among men living with HIV in Ugandan fishing communities
Authors: Rose Naigino, Amanda P. Miller, Michael Ediau, Asha Anecho, Umar Senoga, Nazarius Mbona Tumwesigye, Rhoda K. Wanyenze, Barbara Mukasa, Judith A. Hahn, Elizabeth Reed, Katelyn M. Sileo, Susan M. Kiene
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:East Africa’s fishing communities experience a high burden of two interrelated and frequently co-occurring health issues: HIV and hazardous alcohol use. Nearly two-thirds of Ugandan fisherfolk men meet the criteria for harmful alcohol use. We developed a multilevel intervention to reduce hazardous alcohol use and improve HIV care engagement among fisherfolk men living with HIV (LWHIV) in Wakiso district, Uganda.Methods:This is a qualitative study of stakeholder perspectives on the appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility of a multilevel intervention for fisherfolk men LWHIV. The proposed intervention, Kisoboka (“It is possible!”), combines a structural component [changing the mode of work payments from cash to mobile money] with a behavioral component [motivational interviewing-based counseling combined with content using behavioral economic principles to promote behavior change]. We conducted one focus group (n=7) and eight in-depth interviews with fisherfolk men LWHIV and 19 key informant (KI) interviews with health workers, employers, and community leaders. These explored the appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility of specific key intervention components.Results:Overall, stakeholders’ perspectives supported high intervention acceptability and perceived appropriateness of the proposed intervention. It was perceived to be feasible with some caveats of recommendations for overcoming potential implementation challenges identified (e.g., having a friend assist with documenting savings and alcohol use if an individual was unable to write themselves) which are discussed.Conclusion:This work highlights the potential of the Kisoboka intervention and the importance of early engagement of key stakeholders in the intervention development process to ensure appropriateness, acceptability, feasibility, and socio-cultural fit.

Introduction

Hazardous alcohol use (defined as a score of ≥8 on The Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test-AUDIT) (Saunders et al., 1993) and HIV represent a syndemic in East Africa’s fishing communities (Smolak, 2014). In the villages that lie along the shores of Lake Victoria and its islands in Uganda, HIV incidence among persons who drink alcohol is five times higher than among those who do not drink alcohol (Kiwanuka et al., 2014) and hazardous alcohol use and alcohol use disorders are prevalent among persons living with HIV (LWHIV) (Duko et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2021a, 2021b). Co-occurrence of hazardous alcohol use and alcohol use disorders is common among fisherfolk men and is associated with increased odds of LWHIV or having an unknown status (Kiene et al., 2019b). Nearly two-thirds (64.8%) of men in Ugandan fishing communities engage in hazardous alcohol use (Kiene et al., 2019b; Tumwesigye et al., 2012), and among fisherfolk men, heavy alcohol use is associated with suboptimal HIV treatment adherence (due to missed doses) and poor HIV care engagement (Sileo et al., 2019b). Poor HIV care outcomes in this population are further exacerbated by their high mobility, HIV-related stigma, and lack of access to HIV clinics (Bogart et al., 2016).

Normalization and social reinforcement of heavy alcohol use among fisherfolk men (Sileo et al., 2016a) occurs at multiple levels of the socioecological model (Melius, 2015; Olaniyan et al., 2021): individual/intrapersonal, interpersonal, and environmental/structural. Social norms around the acceptability of this behavior (Ssebunnya et al., 2020), easy access to alcohol in fishing communities (availability), and a surplus of cash from the lucrative fishing industry (affordability) have all been identified as facilitators of heavy drinking among fisherfolk men (Kuteesa et al., 2022). This normalization of heavy alcohol use may reduce motivation to change, influencing continued use on the individual level. It may also influence alcohol use on the interpersonal level, resulting in a lack of social support to enact change in alcohol use. On the environmental/structural level, the lack of formal banking infrastructure in fishing communities increases “cash in the pocket” and discourages the establishment of formal savings (Sileo et al., 2016a), exacerbating economic vulnerability, which is associated with increased alcohol use in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) (Morojele et al., 2006; Tumwesigye et al., 2012) and elsewhere (Keyes et al., 2011). Prior studies exploring motivations for alcohol use among fisherfolk men found that, although many acknowledged the link between heavy alcohol use and negative health outcomes, men still cited the need to use alcohol to relieve stress related to their work, families, and finances (Breuer et al., 2019; Seeley et al., 2012; Sileo et al., 2016a). As Uganda scales up HIV prevention and strives to meet national 95–95–95 targets of 95% of people LWHIV knowing their status, 95% of people who know their status receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of people on treatment have a suppressed viral load in efforts towards global goals to end the AIDS epidemic by 2030 (UNAIDS, 2015), more work is needed to address the HIV-alcohol syndemic in this key population.

Contextualizing the HIV-Alcohol syndemic among men in Ugandan fishing communities

Consistent with fishing communities in the broader East African region (Kapiga et al., 2021; Kwena et al., 2019), Uganda’s fishing communities are HIV hotspots with a prevalence ranging from 22%–37% (Musumari et al., 2021). Engaging fisherfolk in HIV care remains a significant challenge (Bogart et al., 2016; Seeley and Allison, 2005). Prior studies in SSA suggest heavy alcohol use among fisherfolk men is associated with poor HIV treatment outcomes, including poor ART adherence (Sileo et al., 2019b, 2016b; Velloza et al., 2020), lower CD4 count levels and increased HIV viral load (Chander et al., 2006). Harmful alcohol use among fishermen is also associated with HIV risk behaviors (e.g., condomless sex) (Smolak, 2014) and research suggests nearly two-thirds (64%) of new HIV infections in the fishing communities can be attributed to alcohol use (Kiwanuka et al., 2014). Other studies have identified the lucrative cash economy in this setting as a facilitator of alcohol use and HIV risk behavior (Kiene et al., 2019a, 2019b; Lubega et al., 2015; Sileo et al., 2016a).

Prior research suggests that the high risk of HIV that fisherfolk men face, coupled with HIV fatalism and the perception that their profession (being on the lake) presents a great danger to their safety fosters a short-term (“life is short”) future outlook with regard to decision making (Breuer et al., 2019; Kwiringira et al., 2019; Lubega et al., 2015; Sileo et al., 2019a). Such attitudes predispose this population to increased risk of HIV acquisition (Kissling et al., 2005) and heavy drinking (Tumwesigye et al., 2012), suggesting the need for context-specific interventions to address these issues.

Recent systematic reviews highlight the limited number of efficacious psychosocial interventions to address alcohol use that have been implemented in SSA (Madhombiro et al., 2019; O’Cleirigh and Safren, 2008; Scott-Sheldon et al., 2008; Sileo et al., 2020, 2021). Further, these reviews uncovered reliance on individual level approaches, with only a single structural intervention to reduce alcohol use identified (out of 24 relevant studies). Many of the interventions followed the WHO brief intervention model and five utilized motivational interviewing (MI) (L’Engle et al., 2014; Marais et al., 2010; Rendall-Mkosi et al., 2013; Takahashi et al., 2018; Wandera et al., 2016), a client-centered counseling approach intended to build on one’s inherent motivation for change through feedback on behavioral patterns and long-term goals (Miller and Rollnick, 2013).

In well-resourced health sector settings, brief MI-based interventions have successfully reduced alcohol use in several populations (Myers et al., 2017; Parsons et al., 2007). Prior systematic reviews suggest that this approach is also promising in SSA in less-resourced health sector settings (Sileo et al., 2020, 2021). Brief-MI based interventions have also successfully improved ART adherence and reduced incident HIV in SSA (DiIorio et al., 2008; Gwadz et al., 2015; Holstad et al., 2011; Samet et al., 2005). Given the high mobility of fisherfolk (Rosen et al., 2020), brief interventions are also more feasible than longer-term cognitive behavioral therapy-based interventions in this setting. More evidence is needed to support the effectiveness of MI-based interventions dually aimed at achieving alcohol reduction and improving HIV care engagement among fisherfolk. In addition, the effect size from MI-based alcohol reduction interventions in SSA have been small (e.g. pooled OR of 1.91 for abstinence at long term follow up of 12–60 months) (Sileo et al., 2021) suggesting the need for inclusion of supplemental intervention approaches to bolster intervention impact (Sileo et al., 2020, 2021). Research from the United States (US) suggests that the addition of a behavioral economic supplement to brief MI-based alcohol reduction interventions can improve incremental efficiency. Murphy et al.’s addition of a single behavioral economics guided MI session focused on counseling to increase the salience of delayed rewards (e.g., performing well academically and achieving graduation and career goals) and changing patterns of behavior that hinder one’s ability to achieve those rewards (i.e., heavy drinking which may preclude optimal academic performance) led to a greater reduction in alcohol misuse among college students (Murphy et al., 2012).

A review of economic strengthening interventions (i.e., interventions to support, protect and improve the economic livelihood of individuals) among persons LWHIV in low resource settings found this approach can improve ART adherence, retention in HIV care, and quality of life measures (Bateganya et al., 2015). Prior work exploring the potential value of commitment savings programs (through a bank account, savings program or mobile banking) in fishing communities found that use of such programs was associated with decreased HIV risk behavior, particularly among men who use alcohol (Kiene et al., 2019a). Moreover, economic strengthening programs that focus on improving economic stability using commitment savings programs may reduce the risk of impulsive spending on things like alcohol use through “soft commitments” (restrictions on withdrawals, small transaction fees) (Dupas and Robinson, 2013; Ky et al., 2017). Additional prior research has also linked savings interventions to positive health and behavioral outcomes (Witte et al., 2015). While evidence-based interventions utilizing MI counseling can promote individual behavior change around alcohol use and ART adherence, they do not address structural barriers to escaping economic vulnerability, a risk factor for alcohol use and poor HIV care engagement experienced by fisherfolk. Recognizing the need for a multifaceted approach, we developed a multilevel intervention that addresses both structural and behavioral barriers to HIV care engagement and reducing heavy alcohol use among fisherfolk men. The present study was conducted pre-implementation to inform intervention refinement for Kisoboka (It is Possible!), a MI-based and behavioral economic theory informed brief intervention with a supplemental structural component, which aims to reduce hazardous alcohol use and improve HIV care outcomes among men LWHIV in Uganda’s fishing communities. We qualitatively explored stakeholder perspectives on the appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility of the proposed intervention to inform intervention refinement as well as the potential for future scale-up of the intervention.

Methods

Description of the proposed intervention

Two existing evidence-based interventions targeting individual behavior change served as the foundation of the proposed intervention. The first was a six-session MI intervention to address alcohol use and HIV/STI risk, which successfully reduced alcohol consumption among female commercial sex workers in Kenya (L’Engle et al., 2014). The second intervention included a single standard brief MI counseling session followed by a supplemental behavioral economics guided MI counseling session and successfully reduced alcohol misuse among college students in the US (Murphy et al., 2012). We also planned to add a structural economic strengthening component to increase the accessibility of savings through mobile phone-based banking-related services where employers will pay men receiving the Kisoboka intervention via mobile money instead of cash in order to reduce “cash in the pocket” (the tentative intervention structure could be modified based on feed-back from this qualitative assessment). In addition to this structural piece, in our initial Kisoboka intervention plan, we envisioned four counseling sessions (two individual sessions [one in-person and one by phone] and two group sessions) plus twice-weekly text message reminders (to reinforce alcohol and savings goals and increase the salience of delayed rewards) and use of a daily diary to self-monitor earning, savings, and expenditures. Individual and group session topics will focus on several core components of MI and behavioral economics including client-centered goal setting for savings, alcohol reduction, and optimal HIV care engagement including ART adherence, self-monitoring of savings (via the earnings, spending, and savings diary), identification of alcohol-free leisure activities, increasing the salience of delayed rewards, identifying, and reinforcing discrepancies between actions and goals, motivating change, and financial literacy. Fig. 1 illustrates aspects of the Kisoboka motivational interviewing and behavioral economic intervention components by socioecological model level including hypothesized intermediate outcomes which will provide insights into mechanisms of change. Consistent with the socioecological model, levels interact to affect outcomes, therefore, intervention components listed at one social ecological level are also expected to affect intermediate outcomes illustrated at other levels. For example, the intermediate outcome of increased savings may be affected by increasing the accessibility of savings, economic strengthening, and addressing delayed reward discounting.

Study setting

We conducted the present qualitative study between January and July 2019 in Wakiso district, Uganda. The district lies on the northern side of Lake Victoria, where most residents of fishing villages work in the fishing industry or in jobs supporting the fishing industry and receive daily cash payments for their work. We conducted the study in partnership with Mildmay Uganda, a not-for-profit organization providing comprehensive healthcare, including HIV treatment services. We conducted the study at five of the fifty-nine Mildmay Uganda supported health facilities in Wakiso district that provide ART. By 2019, over 1800 adult male patients were enrolled on ART at these selected facilities.

Study population

We included several strata of key stakeholders in the study, including our intervention’s target population, male fisherfolk, as well as other key informant groups that could provide unique insight into the feasibility, appropriateness, and acceptability of the proposed intervention, including healthcare workers, employers of fisherfolk and group leaders in fisherfolk communities. Eligibility criteria for our target population paralleled criteria for the future pilot intervention trial: fisherfolk men LWHIV, aged 18–50 years, enrolled on ART for >1 month at any one of the five Mildmay supported health facilities approved for this study. Fisherfolk men were defined as men who worked in the fishing industry such as fishermen, fish traders, fish processors, fish loaders, and boat/fishing equipment repairmen. We chose this age range (18–50) because prior research suggests hazardous alcohol use is prevalent in this group (Kabwama et al., 2016). Eligibility included reporting missing ≥1 dose of ART in the prior 14 days and engaging in heavy alcohol use (i.e., reported consuming ≥5 drinks per occasion at least once in the prior 30 days). Men were ineligible if they were visibly intoxicated at the time of enrollment (eligible to enroll when sober) and if they were unable to speak Luganda or English.

We also conducted key informant (KI) interviews with (i) group leaders (representing individuals employed in a particular occupation such as fishermen or fish traders), (ii) employers of fisherfolk (e.g., boat owners and engine owners), and (iii) health workers who were directly involved with ART delivery at the Mildmay sites included in this study.

Participant recruitment

We identified fisherfolk men using two methods: 1) After a potentially eligible patient completed his clinic appointment, the clinic staff referred him to a study interviewer for full eligibility screening. 2) Clinic staff also passively reviewed clinic records to identify potentially eligible patients who they would then contact via phone. If the patient was interested in study participation, the clinic staff obtained permission to share his phone number and name with our study interviewer who briefly described the study and sought verbal informed consent to conduct eligibility screening. To protect participants’ confidentiality, we masked the purpose of the study by including questions about the frequency of their use of a variety of other health services including immunization, outpatient, and in-patient care. Eligible men were offered participation in the study and provided written or thumb-printed informed consent.

We recruited healthcare workers and employers for KI interviews purposively based upon their role in the fishing community or provision of healthcare to fisherfolk men. To recruit health workers, a study interviewer approached the ART clinic supervisors and asked them to help identify clinic staff for our KI interviews. To minimize the possibility of clinic staff feeling coerced, the study interviewer directly contacted those who were recommended to us by their clinic supervisor and asked if they were willing to participate in the study. To recruit group leaders, the study interviewer asked the fisherfolk men who were enrolled in the study to identify their different group leaders/team leads. Those who were identified were contacted by the study interviewer and asked if they were willing to participate in the study. We selected employers (boat/engine owners selected from each landing site) with support from the chairperson(s) of their respective Beach Management Unit (BMU, a group of community stakeholders in a given fishing community who guide decision making in that community) who identified and referred them to our study team. We obtained written informed consent from potentially eligible participants and an interview was conducted on the same day where possible or rescheduled at an appropriate time.

Data collection

We conducted one focus group discussion (FGD) (n=7) with fisherfolk men LWHIV. We experienced logistical challenges in trying to coordinate a second FGD; therefore, we next conducted eight individual in-depth interviews (IDIs) with the same population. We also conducted nineteen KI interviews with employers (n=5), group leaders (n=6), and health workers (n=8). Interviews were conducted by an experienced qualitative interviewer bilingual in Luganda and English. Sample size for each strata of participants was based on reaching saturation of themes. We audio recorded interviews and the FGD which were conducted in Luganda except for a subsection (n=4) of the health worker KI interviews which were conducted in English.

Following the informed consent process, the interviewer provided a detailed description of the proposed Kisoboka intervention to solicit feedback on intervention content and structure. To protect the confidentiality of future intervention participants, we did not provide the full description of the intervention purpose and content to the employers and group leaders (i.e. content related to alcohol and HIV was omitted). Interview guides covered themes specific to the proposed intervention including client-centered goal setting for savings, alcohol reduction, and HIV care engagement and adherence, text messages to increase the salience of delayed rewards and reinforce discrepancies between behaviors and goals, alcohol-free leisure activities available, social support through group counseling sessions, modes of work payments (i.e., cash vs. mobile money), and self-monitoring of earning and spending using a written daily diary.

All procedures were approved by San Diego State University Institutional Review Board, Makerere University School of Public Health Research and Ethics Committee, and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology.

Data management and analysis

We transcribed and translated all recordings into English (where applicable). We analyzed transcripts using a thematic analysis approach which was guided by the preliminary intervention protocol. The first two authors reviewed transcripts and developed a coding scheme based on the interview guides, with domains organized by key Kisoboka MI and BE intervention components. The first two authors then independently applied an initial set of codes to represent each core MI and BE-informed component included in the intervention (see description section above) and to reflect feedback on appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility, utilizing Proctor et al.’s conceptual definitions (Proctor et al., 2011). We defined acceptability as the perception among stakeholders that the proposed intervention was “agreeable, palatable, or satisfactory” (Proctor et al., 2011). We defined appropriateness as “the perceived fit, relevance, or compatibility” of the proposed intervention (Proctor et al., 2011). We defined feasibility as the extent to which the proposed intervention “could be successfully used or carried out” in the setting (Proctor et al., 2011). After initial coding, the team met to discuss emergent themes before reviewing the transcripts a subsequent time to group feedback by intervention activity within each core component. During analysis, we merged core component categories if content had sufficient overlap to warrant reporting results collectively. We organized and reported our findings below by these broad core intervention component categories.

We took steps to promote trustworthiness (i.e., rigor) of the data according to the four-dimension criteria defined by (Lincoln and Guba, 1985) including use of purposive sampling (improves transferability); a detailed study protocol and use of a team of multiple researchers to independently review and code the transcripts (promotes dependability), application of a reflexive approach (i.e. being self-aware of our positionality and how it may impact both the research itself and our understanding of it, which promotes confirmability) and regular peer debrief meetings to serve as a check on researcher bias (promotes credibility).

Results

Participant characteristics

We screened 73 potentially eligible fisherfolk men, identified 23 eligible subjects and successfully recruited 15 participants to participate in either IDIs (n=8) or a FGD (k=1, n=7). Challenges in scheduling precluded inclusion of the other 8 eligible fisherfolk men. Participants’ ages ranged from 25 to 49 years, and resided in one of five villages: Kigungu, Kasenyi, Nakabugo, Bendegere, Entebbe. All fisherfolk received their payment from employers in cash at the time of the interview.

We conducted a total of 19 KI interviews, including five with employers, six with group leaders and eight with health workers. Employers were men, aged 31–43 years, and employed between four and eightemployees each. Group leaders were men, whose ages ranged from 31 to 50 years. Health workers were mostly women (88%), aged 26–50 years.

Motivating change and client-centered goal setting

There was high perceived acceptability and appropriateness for the motivating change and client-centered goal-setting aspect of the intervention, especially oriented around financial wellbeing, from both fisherfolk and healthcare workers. Fisherfolk men generally indicated an interest in improving their finances and savings and receptiveness to receiving support to motivate such changes in behavior. Many of the men shared concrete personal financial goals, indicated a willingness to share these goals with others as a form of accountability, and expressed that seeing the success of their peers in achieving goals served as a motivator for them to pursue their own [Q1-Q3, see quotes 1–3 in Table 1]. Healthcare workers also saw the potential for positive behavior changes as a result of motivating counseling and goal setting. Many indicated that fisherfolk men had goals but were unsure how to achieve them in their current financial circumstances. They also saw increased attention (and perceived concern and compassion) from care providers at counseling sessions as a potential motivating factor for behavior change (improved ART adherence) [Q4-Q5 in Table 1].

Increasing the salience of delayed rewards and reinforcing discrepancies between behaviors/actions and goals

Many of the fisherfolk men placed great value on establishing savings. While some were unsure how to get started, others were already members of savings clubs or were saving money “in their homes” [Q6-Q7 in Table 1]. Some men indicated that fisherfolk generally don’t have goals, choosing instead to focus on short-term outlook which is reflected in their spending and drinking patterns [Q8 in Table 1]. However, the majority indicated that they personally had savings goals while acknowledging the inherent disconnect between their daily living and achievement of these longer-term objectives. MI-based counseling (in-person or over the phone) and text message reminders were generally perceived as potentially useful strategies in helping men to stay focused on achieving these savings goals and highlighting when their short-term actions may interfere with meeting them [Q9–11 in Table 1]. These themes across fisherfolk, healthcare workers, and employers illustrated strong acceptability and appropriateness of intervention content on increasing the salience of delayed rewards via text message reminders and goal setting to reinforce discrepancies between behaviors and goals.

Identification of alcohol-free leisure activities

When asked about alcohol-free leisure activities in their communities, fisherfolk men indicated that options were very limited. Participants mentioned Ludo (a board game), playing pool, and watching movies or sports as options that could be done without drinking. However, several participants indicated that these activities were not always alcohol-free [Q12 in Table 1]. Although participants identified a few activities that were not centered around alcohol use, which speaks to the acceptability of alcohol-free leisure time, the limited availability of such activities suggests potential feasibility challenges in implementing this aspect of the intervention content in the environment of the fishing communities.

Social support/promoting financial literacy

Social support via group sessions was deemed acceptable and appropriate. Fisherfolk men were particularly receptive to the idea of peer group sessions to provide social support and receive peer feedback, citing the “oneness of the lake” and shared experience that many fisherfolk who work and live in the same community have. The opportunity to collectively share how they were taking steps to improve their lives and learn from one another was appealing [Q13 in Table 1]. Health workers also felt that highlighting testimonials of men who were successfully saving money through the proposed Kisoboka program and achieving their savings goals, would be a great addition to the proposed intervention [Q14 in Table 1].

Self-monitoring of spending and savings activities (via daily diary)

Most participants were receptive to the idea of documenting their expenses in a diary and thought this could aid them in self-reflection around spending and alcohol use [Q15–17 in Table 1], although some indicated an initial hesitation to truthfully record alcohol expenses [Q18 in Table 1]. However, participants were quick to propose solutions to identified challenges in using the daily diary. In the FGD, when one participant indicated his wife may be angered by the amount he spends on alcohol, another suggested the diary could be left with an employer or friend for safekeeping. When another indicated he was unable to write, a different participant suggested he get a friend to help him document expenses [Q19 in Table 1]. Despite some fishermen’s initial perceptions of lower acceptability and feasibility of self-monitoring of daily spending and savings, particularly related to expenditures on alcohol, the majority felt this intervention component would be appropriate and would be amenable to problem-solving, and thus, acceptable and feasible to implement.

Structural component: direct deposit of work payments and savings into the mobile money account

When asked what mode of savings program they would prefer (e.g., traditional banking), many fisherfolk men vocalized a preference for a mobile money platform, citing limited bank access and challenges accessing money through savings clubs in a timely fashion, including instances of improper fund management [Q20 in Table 1]. Some also described how the use of mobile money could curb impulsive spending [Q21 in Table 1].

Healthcare workers, group leaders and employers also saw the value in switching from cash to mobile phone-based payment [Q22–23 in Table 1]. Group leaders emphasized the importance of sensitization messaging to overcome potential reluctance to switch from a cash-based pay system [Q24-Q25 in Table 1]. Potential concerns regarding the use of mobile money payment that were mentioned included the taxes or fees levied on mobile transactions [Q25 in Table 1] and fears regarding what would happen to savings if a phone was lost [phone accounts are secured by PINs and tied to national identity cards]. Solutions provided by participants to reduce risk associated with lost phones included saving money on the phone of a trusted family member or having a secondary phone for banking [Q26 in Table 1]. Most participants expressed high perceived appropriateness with some modest cautions about potential acceptability and feasibility challenges to be overcome.

Implementation suggestions for group sessions

Stakeholders had several logistical suggestions around implementation of the group sessions to increase feasibility, including scheduling them on the same day as routine clinic visits to pick up ART refills [Q27 in Table 1], considering off-site group sessions for men who resided far away from the clinic (if the session was not aligned with their routine clinic visit), utilizing the male participants themselves as a tool to help to mobilize and coordinate attendance [Q28 in Table 1], and encouraging peer leadership in engaging men at group sessions [Q29 in Table 1].

Discussion

HIV and alcohol use are inextricably linked public health issues and prior research demonstrates the need for context-specific and culturally relevant interventions to address them (Barrera et al., 2013; Bryant et al., 2010; Castro et al., 2010, 2004). This is especially true when considering interventions to address the HIV-alcohol syndemic among specific key populations in SSA such as fisherfolk, who face unique contextual risks and challenges in their lived environments. Stakeholders indicated a high perceived need for such an intervention and provided several suggestions for intervention refinement, including: 1) sensitization of fisherfolk men to highlight the value of savings; 2) using a peer-led approach to mobilize group sessions; and 3) including testimonials of men who achieved savings goals through the proposed Kisoboka intervention. This work has important implications for intervention roll out in the fishing communities and speaks to the importance of community engagement in the development of interventions to address this syndemic in Eastern and Southern Africa more broadly.

Individual and group counseling to motivate behavior change, promote goal setting around savings and provide ongoing reinforcement of these goals was widely accepted by stakeholders as needed and beneficial to fisherfolk men. High acceptability of multi-session brief interventions that utilize MI-counseling approaches to improve health (and address alcohol use specifically) is consistent with prior work in Uganda’s fishing communities (Miller et al., 2021a, 2021b). We also found high levels of acceptability and appropriateness of intervention components derived from behavioral economics, such as increasing the salience of delayed rewards and addressing delayed reward discounting, which had not previously been explored in this context. The acceptability, and perceived need for inclusion of a structural economic strengthening component (mobile savings) suggests that the multilevel Kisoboka approach has potential in this setting. Among persons LWHIV, structural economic strengthening interventions also have demonstrated success at improving ART outcomes and care engagement in developing countries (Bateganya et al., 2015), another improved health objective of Kisoboka.

Alcohol use, economic vulnerability, and HIV care outcomes in fishing communities are all affected by factors across levels of the socioecological model. In this context, interactions between individuals and their lived environment create a “risk environment” where heavy drinking and impulsive spending are reinforced and access to HIV care services is impeded. In risk environments, a confluence of multi-level factors interact to increase the odds of an individual experiencing harm (e.g., poor health outcomes) (Rhodes, 2002). Participants described how individual decision making (drinking daily at paying establishments) and beliefs (that spending was fine because they could fish again tomorrow), which are reinforced by interpersonal/social circumstances (stress of the lifestyle of fishermen) and structural barriers (lack of formal banking system) contribute to alcohol use and a lack of economic safety net. Fisherfolk men in our study and previous work expressed interest in establishing savings (Breuer et al., 2019; Sileo et al., 2016a), while simultaneously recognizing that daily drinking and impulsive spending were widely normalized parts of their daily lives. The lack of alcohol-free leisure activities identified by fisherfolk men in interviews underscores the central role that drinking plays in social life in fishing communities. While many participants indicated a willingness to switch to mobile money for pay and an interest in establishing savings, the importance of counseling and sensitization around how this directly benefits fisherfolk men was underscored by stakeholders as critical to implementation success. This suggestion is in line with the proposed intervention content, which will focus on increasing the extent to which individuals’ behaviors are motivated by and consistent with their long-term goals such as saving money for the future, and be further bolstered by the structural intervention component (work payments via mobile money). Taking note of the lack of existing alcohol-free leisure activities in the community identified, Kisoboka content was revised to include encouraging peers to support one another in engaging in informal alcohol-free activities during the group sessions.

Fishermen and health workers also suggested the need to employ peer-led approaches for mobilizing group sessions. Prior studies in SSA demonstrate the efficacy of using peer-led group approaches to improve HIV service uptake among different HIV subgroups (Baron et al., 2020; Benoit et al., 2017; Bogart et al., 2020; Carbone et al., 2019; Holstad et al., 2012). The accompanying suggestion to highlight participant testimonials for peer role modeling is also well supported in the literature. In Malawi and South Africa, mentor mothers [mothers LWHIV who had successfully completed the Prevention of Mother-To-Child Transmission care continuum and delivered HIV-negative babies] played a vital role in reducing vertical HIV transmission (Baron et al., 2020; Carbone et al., 2019). Peer mentorship is also the foundation of 12-step recovery programming which has demonstrated success in reducing alcohol use globally through shared experience and systems of accountability (Kelly et al., 2020).

Collectively, the suggestions and feedback provided by participants and stakeholders provided a roadmap for finalizing intervention content and implementation to ensure intervention fit. Suggestions were carefully reviewed and integrated into the final Kisoboka intervention protocol. This work underscores the need for the proposed intervention and the potential and importance of community engagement in intervention development and rollout and provides an example of such activities in the context of a proposed multilevel combination intervention.

Conclusion

Our pre-implementation assessment suggests that Kisoboka might be an acceptable, appropriate, and feasible intervention to improve health in fishing communities and demonstrates the value of seeking feedback from community members and stakeholders early in the research process. Replication of this research in future adaptations of Kisoboka will support successful delivery of this intervention in other populations in SSA that are affected by the HIV-alcohol syndemic (such as sex workers and boda boda (moto taxi) drivers) but occupy different risk environments and contexts. Successful implementation of Kisoboka is dependent on integration of participant feedback provided in this formative assessment. Rigorous evaluation of both implementation and intervention outcomes post-implementation will determine intervention efficacy and if further changes to the intervention are needed. Future research should continue to center the intervention’s audience and key stakeholders at all stages of the intervention design, implementation process, results interpretation, and dissemination to promote intervention fit, ensure implementation success, and enhance potential scalability.
