Impact of Age at Surgery on Perioperative Outcomes After Posterior Spinal Fusion for Neuromuscular Scoliosis
Authors: Vivien Chan, Prudhvi Kodali, Sophie Nuytten, Justin L Reyes, Mehdi Elfilali, Michael G Vitale, Zeeshan M Sardar, David L Skaggs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile surgical outcomes for posterior spinal fusion (PSF) for pediatric patients with neuromuscular scoliosis (NMS) have been well described, less is known about how perioperative outcomes differ when surgery is performed later, during young adulthood. The purpose of this study was to compare perioperative outcomes between adolescents and young adults undergoing PSF for NMS using a large, multi-institutional clinical registry.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) and NSQIP-Pediatric databases from 2016 to 2024. Patients aged 10-40 years who underwent PSF for NMS were included. Two cohorts were defined: adolescents (aged 10-17 years) and young adults (aged 18-40 years). Outcomes of interest included operative time, length of stay (LOS), allogeneic transfusion, any adverse event, 30-day readmission, and non–home discharge. Outcomes were compared between the two groups using the chi-squared test and Student’s t-test. Multivariable logistic and linear regression analyses were performed adjusting for sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA) class, and the number of fusion levels.ResultsThere were 8,005 adolescent patients (mean age: 13.8 years) and 69 young adult patients (mean age: 20.5 years) who met inclusion criteria. Compared with adolescents, young adults experienced significantly longer operative times (350.4 vs 422.9 min, P < .001). Young adults also had higher rates of non–home discharge (4.9% vs 10.1%, P = .04) and any adverse event (13.3% vs 24.6%, respectively, P = .006). No significant differences were observed in mean LOS, rate of unplanned readmission, or rate of allogeneic transfusion. On multivariable logistic regression, surgery in young adulthood was independently associated with increased odds of any adverse event (odds ratio [OR]: 2.3, P = .003), allogeneic transfusion (OR: 1.7, P = .03), and non–home discharge (OR: 2.3, P = .04). Multivariable linear regression demonstrated that surgery in young adulthood was associated with longer operative time (+75.2 min, P < .001) and increased LOS (+1.3 days, P = .01).ConclusionsYoung adults undergoing PSF for NMS experience higher perioperative morbidity and resource utilization than adolescents, including longer operative times, increased risk of adverse events, and non–home discharge. These findings describe higher perioperative risk among young adults but should not be interpreted as demonstrating that delayed surgery itself causes worse outcomes.Key Concepts(1)Young adults undergoing posterior spinal fusion for neuromuscular scoliosis experience significantly greater perioperative morbidity than adolescents.(2)Surgery in young adulthood is independently associated with longer operative time, increased length of stay, and higher odds of allogeneic transfusion.(3)Young adults have more than double the adjusted risk of perioperative adverse events and non–home discharge than adolescents.Level of EvidenceIII, Retrospective Cohort StudyKeywords: Neuromuscular scoliosis, Spinal deformity, Risk stratification, Perioperative outcomes, Age-related outcomes

Introduction

Neuromuscular scoliosis (NMS) is a progressive spinal deformity arising from neuromuscular disorders like cerebral palsy, spina bifida, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and spinal muscular atrophy [1,2]. Unlike the short, segmental curves typical of idiopathic scoliosis, NMS is characterized by long, sweeping C-shaped curves, kyphosis, and pelvic obliquity, with progression driven by asymmetric muscle weakness and continued skeletal growth through adolescence and early adulthood [1]. As NMS advances, patients often develop pain, mobility difficulties, pressure ulcers, and cardiopulmonary complications, profoundly reducing health-related quality of life [[1], [2], [3]].

The initial treatment typically consists of nonoperative management, including bracing and seating modifications. However, surgical intervention is indicated for severe deformity, progressive functional decline, pain, or respiratory compromise [[4], [5], [6]]. Posterior spinal fusion (PSF) is the standard operative treatment and has been shown to improve sitting balance and quality of life in pediatric patients [6]. Despite these benefits, NMS patients represent a medically complex population with elevated perioperative risk due to respiratory insufficiency, cardiovascular comorbidities, and nutritional deficiencies [4,5].

While surgical outcomes in pediatric NMS populations are well described, outcomes in young adults remain poorly characterized [7]. Delayed surgery may increase curve magnitude, curve rigidity, operative complexity, and perioperative morbidity, yet the impact of age on surgical outcomes is not well defined [8]. This gap limits effective counseling and perioperative planning for patients undergoing surgery beyond adolescence.

The purpose of this study is to compare the perioperative outcomes between adolescents and young adults undergoing PSF for NMS using the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) adult and pediatric databases.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This was a retrospective cohort study. The American College of Surgeons NSQIP adult and pediatric databases for years 2016-2024 were used for this study. The NSQIP is an outcome-based program that measures the quality of surgical care. In each participating hospital, a trained surgical clinical reviewer collects preoperative through 30-day postoperative data. Data are collected through random sampling. Data are anonymized. Institutional review board approval is not required for use of NSQIP data.

Patients aged 10-40 years who received PSF for NMS were included in this study. PSF for deformity was defined using Current Procedural Terminology codes. The diagnosis of NMS was defined using International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Patients who received anterior-only fusion were excluded from this study. Two cohorts were defined: (1) adolescents (aged 10-17 years) and (2) young adults (aged 18-40 years). Age cutoffs were absolute: patients aged 17 years or younger were included only in the adolescent cohort, and patients aged 18 years or older were included only in the young adult cohort, with no overlap between groups.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Outcomes of interest included operative time (minutes), length of hospital stay (days), and rates of allogeneic red blood cell transfusion, any adverse event, 30-day unplanned readmission, and non–home discharge. Adverse events included superficial wound infection, deep wound infection, wound dehiscence, pneumonia, unplanned reintubation, renal failure, urinary tract infection, thromboembolism, stroke, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, sepsis, and septic shock. Non–home discharge included discharge to a facility other than the patient’s home or residence, including rehabilitation center, acute care hospital, extended care facility, and other health care facilities. The mean and standard deviation was determined for operative time and length of stay (LOS) for each age cohort and compared using Student’s t-test. The rates of allogeneic transfusion, any adverse event, 30-day unplanned readmission, and non–home discharge were determined as proportions and compared between the two age cohorts using the chi-squared test. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed, adjusting for sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA) class, and the number of fusion levels, to determine the association between age and operative time and LOS. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed, adjusting for sex, ASA score, and the number of fusion levels to determine association between age and rates of allogeneic transfusion, any adverse event, 30-day unplanned readmission, and non–home discharge.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Study population characteristics

There were 8,074 patients who met inclusion and exclusion criteria. There were 8,005 adolescent patients (mean age: 13.8 ± 2.0 years) and 69 young adult patients (mean age: 20.5 ± 4.0 years) with NMS (Table 1). The proportions of female patients in the adolescent cohort and the young adult cohort were 50.8% and 34.8%, respectively. A majority of patients in the adolescent cohort (79.1%) and young adult cohort (76.8%) had an ASA score of 3. A majority of patients in the adolescent cohort (82.6%) and young adult cohort (76.8%) had more than 12 surgical levels.

Operative time and LOS

In the adolescent cohort, the mean operative time was 350.4 ± 119.7 min. In the young adult cohort, the mean operative time was 422.9 ± 150.2 min. The young adult group had significantly longer operative time (t = 5.0, P < .001) with a mean difference of 72.6 min (Table 2). In multivariable linear regression analysis, adjusting for sex, ASA score, and the number of fusion levels, the young adult cohort was significantly associated with an increased operative time of 75.2 min (95% confidence interval [CI]: 47.3-103.1, P < .001) compared to the adolescent cohort (Table 3).

The mean length of hospital stay for the adolescent cohort and young adult cohort were 6.4 ± 4.4 days and 7.4 ± 4.6 days, respectively. There was no significant difference in the LOS between the two groups in unadjusted analysis (P = .06) (Table 2). However, in adjusted multivariable linear regression analysis, the young adult cohort was significantly associated with increased LOS by 1.3 days (95% CI: 0.3-2.3, P = .01) compared to the adolescent cohort (Table 3).

Allogeneic transfusion and any adverse event

In the adolescent cohort, the rate of allogeneic transfusion was 49.1%. In the young adult cohort, the rate of allogeneic transfusion was 58.0%. There was no significant difference in the rate of allogeneic transfusion between the two cohorts in the unadjusted analysis (χ2 = 2.2, P = .14). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusting for sex, ASA score, and the number of fusion levels, the young adult cohort had higher odds of transfusion (odds ratio [OR]: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.06-2.85, P = .03) than the adolescent cohort (see Table 4).

The rates of any adverse event for the adolescent cohort and young adult cohort were 13.3% and 24.6%, respectively. The young adult cohort had a significantly higher rate of any adverse event in unadjusted analysis (χ2 = 7.6, P = .006). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, the young adult cohort had significantly higher odds of any adverse event (OR: 2.32, 95% CI: 1.33-4.05, P = .003).

Rates of the individual adverse events comprising the composite outcome are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

30-day unplanned readmission and non–home discharge

The rates of unplanned readmission for the adolescent cohort and young adult cohort were 8.8% and 10.1%, respectively. There was no significant difference in the rate and odds of unplanned readmission in unadjusted (P = .68) and adjusted (P = .61) analyses.

The rates of non–home discharge for the adolescent cohort and young adult cohort were 4.9% and 10.1%, respectively. In the unadjusted analysis, the young adult cohort had a higher rate of non–home discharge (χ2 = 4.0, P = .04). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, the young adult cohort had significantly higher odds of non–home discharge than the adolescent cohort (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.04-5.08, P = .04).

Discussion

This study provides a large comparative analysis of perioperative outcomes following PSF for NMS between adolescent and young adults. Our results demonstrated that young adults had significantly higher early surgical morbidity after adjusting for sex, ASA physical status, and the number of vertebrae fused. Specifically, young adults carried more than double the odds of an adverse event (OR: 2.32, 95% CI: 1.33-4.05, P = .003) or non–home discharge (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.04-5.08, P = .04), spent an additional 75 min in the operating room, and were more likely to require allogeneic blood transfusion. These findings suggest that as NMS patients transition into adulthood, they are associated with a more resource-intensive and higher-risk perioperative course. However, these associations should not be interpreted as establishing a causal effect of delayed surgery.

The 75-min increase in operative time observed in young adults may reflect greater technical complexity in this group; however, the absence of radiographic measures prevents determining whether this was due to larger or more rigid curves. Critically, this progression does not halt at skeletal maturity. Among patients with cerebral palsy and severe motor deficits or curves exceeding 40-50° at skeletal maturity, curves advance at an average of 1.4-3.5° per year throughout adulthood [9]. Yoshida et al. [10] demonstrated that preoperative curves exceeding 80° were associated with approximately 80 additional minutes of surgical or anesthesia time and more than 4 times the likelihood of requiring adjunctive techniques such as intraoperative halo traction or temporary rods (OR: 4.6; 95% CI: 2.8-7.2; P < .001). Further compounding operative complexity is the characteristically severe 3-dimensional deformity of NMS, such as the long C-shaped curves, pelvic obliquity, and kyphotic deformity, compared to adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). A retrospective analysis of 3-dimensional spinal reconstructions in 28 NMS and 284 AIS patients found that NMS patients had significantly greater thoracic kyphosis (20 ± 18° vs 10 ± 15°, P = .001), further increasing technical complexity [11]. These factors provide a potential explanation for the observed operative-time difference, but the present data cannot determine whether age, curve severity, disease phenotype, or prior treatment history is responsible.

Allogeneic red blood cell transfusion rates were high in both cohorts (49.1% in adolescents vs 58.0% in young adults), reflecting the substantial surgical exposure and hemostatic demands inherent to long-segment posterior fusion for NMS [12,13]. However, young adults demonstrated significantly higher adjusted odds of transfusion (OR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.06-2.85, P = .03). This finding is consistent with prior literature demonstrating greater intraoperative blood loss during correction of neuromuscular deformity than during idiopathic deformity [14,15], as well as the elevated hemorrhage risk observed in patients with cerebral palsy [16]. Prior work has also suggested an age-related increase in transfusion burden among patients with NMS, with individuals older than 15 years reported to be more than 3 times as likely to require transfusion as younger patients [17]. These findings suggest that increasing age is associated with, but may not itself be the cause of, greater transfusion burden in this cohort. Because the adolescent and young adult cohorts were drawn from pediatric and adult NSQIP data sets, differences in institutional blood management protocols or care pathways may also contribute to the observed difference. These system-level factors are not captured in the NSQIP and cannot be separated from age-related effects in the present analysis. Collectively, these findings underscore the universal importance of using evidence-based blood conservation strategies like tranexamic acid and intraoperative cell salvage while highlighting the young adult NMS population as a group warranting heightened perioperative vigilance [18,19].

Compared to adolescents, young adults had more than double the odds of experiencing a perioperative adverse event, reflecting a potentially greater operative complexity and diminished recoverability across a spectrum of complications including wound infection, pneumonia, unplanned reintubation, urinary tract infection, thromboembolic events, sepsis, and cardiac events. This higher complication burden is consistent with the progressive nature of underlying neuromuscular pathology, in which cardiopulmonary, nutritional, and functional decline compounds over time [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Respiratory insufficiency represents a particularly critical comorbidity in this population as NMS is frequently associated with progressive pulmonary compromise that worsens both with curve severity and advancing age [24,25]. These observations reinforce the importance of comprehensive preoperative cardiopulmonary optimization and multidisciplinary perioperative management when NMS surgery is undertaken in adulthood.

While the reported mean LOS following NMS surgery is approximately 6.5 days, young adulthood was independently associated with a 1.3-day increase in our multivariable model (95% CI: 0.3-2.3, P = .01) [26]. This is consistent with the higher complication burden and greater operative complexity observed in the young adult cohort, both well-established drivers of prolonged hospitalization after spinal deformity surgery [3]. Young adults were also twice as likely to require non–home discharge (OR: 2.30, P = .04), a finding that likely reflects a convergence of factors unique to this population. Unlike pediatric patients who are typically discharged home under parental or guardian supervision, young adults lack equivalent caregiver infrastructure more often, increasing reliance on rehabilitation or skilled nursing facilities and amplifying per-patient health care costs [27]. These findings highlight the importance of early discharge planning and proactive counseling regarding home support needs, particularly when an extended recovery is anticipated. Differences in discharge pathways between pediatric and adult systems may also contribute to the observed non–home discharge difference and cannot be evaluated in the NSQIP.

Thirty-day unplanned readmission rates did not differ significantly between cohorts in unadjusted or adjusted analyses (8.8% vs 10.1%, P = .68; adjusted OR: 1.23, P = .61). Notably, both rates exceed the reported 2.7% readmission rate for idiopathic scoliosis, underscoring the inherently greater complexity of NMS surgery regardless of age [28]. The absence of a between-group difference may partly reflect limited statistical power given the relatively small young adult cohort. However, it may also indicate that the elevated acute perioperative risk observed in young adults does not uniformly translate into excess readmission within 30 days. Complications arising during the index hospitalization may be more frequently managed through prolonged observation or extended inpatient care rather than prompting a discrete readmission event. Additionally, the higher rate of non–home discharge in young adults raises the possibility that postdischarge complications are captured and managed at receiving facilities, effectively obscuring readmission to the index hospital. Adequately powered studies with extended follow-up will be necessary to determine whether the higher complication burden in young adults ultimately manifests as increased 90-day or 1-year readmission rates.

There are several significant limitations inherent to our retrospective review of NSQIP and NSQIP-Pediatric databases. Although these databases provide robust, multicenter data, they lack NMS-specific variables such as curve magnitude, pelvic obliquity, Gross Motor Function Classification System level, bone density, ambulatory status, nutritional markers, respiratory support, and seizure burden. The absence of these variables does not allow for us to adjust for disease severity and curve rigidity, each of which may independently influence perioperative outcomes. Furthermore, because the NSQIP captures only 30-day postoperative data, this study cannot account for late complications including hardware failure, proximal junctional kyphosis requiring revision, pseudoarthrosis, functional decline, or caregiver-reported outcomes. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as a reflection of acute perioperative outcomes rather than a longitudinal or comprehensive assessment of surgical morbidity. Another limitation is the substantial imbalance in cohort size (8,005 adolescents vs 69 young adults). While this disparity likely reflects the epidemiological reality that NMS surgery is performed far less frequently in young adults than in adolescents, it carries meaningful statistical implications. The limited young adult cohort results in widened CIs and reduced statistical power for rare outcomes and subgroup analyses, whereas the considerably larger adolescent cohort may amplify statistical significance for differences that are small in absolute terms and potentially limited in clinical relevance. To mitigate these concerns, adjusted linear and logistic regression models were used to account for key confounders. Despite the sample size imbalance, the perioperative observations in the young adult cohort remain clinically meaningful, and it is unlikely that the observed associations are attributable solely to sampling disparity. Nevertheless, residual confounders cannot be excluded. Since NSQIP and NSQIP-Pediatric are separate data collection instruments, residual differences in variable definitions or ascertainment may introduce misclassification when comparing the cohorts. The study also cannot determine whether young adults represented patients with more severe curves, different neuromuscular disease phenotypes, or different prior treatment histories; therefore, age cannot be isolated from disease phenotype or disease severity. Similarly, differences between pediatric and adult health systems, including preoperative optimization, transfusion practices, and discharge pathways, may contribute to the observed associations. Finally, the non–home discharge model contained sparse cells with complete or quasi-complete separation for ASA class and should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

Young adult patients with NMS present with higher rates of adverse events, blood transfusions, and extended recovery times in the perioperative period of a PSF. These results identify young adulthood as a marker of greater perioperative morbidity and resource use in this cohort but do not establish that delaying surgery causes worse outcomes.
