Author Response: Timing Matters: Caution and Opportunity in Early Vasopressin for Septic Shock
Authors: Anirban Bhattacharjee, Priyankar K Datta, Rajathadri H Ravikumar, Prachee Sathe, Vivek Kumar, Riddhi Kundu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
How to cite this article: Bhattacharjee A, Datta PK, Ravikumar RH, Sathe P, Kumar V, Kundu R. Author Response: Timing Matters: Caution and Opportunity in Early Vasopressin for Septic Shock. Indian J Crit Care Med 2025;29(12):1059–1060.

Dear Editor,

We appreciate the authors of the letter titled “Timing matters: caution and opportunity in early vasopressin for septic shock” for their thoughtful analysis of our recently published systematic review and meta-analysis examining the timing of vasopressin initiation in septic shock. Their comments highlight both the clinical importance of this topic and the persistent uncertainties surrounding vasopressor sequencing in septic shock.1

To begin with, they correctly point out that only one of the included studies was a randomized controlled trial, while the rest were observational cohorts with moderate to serious risk of bias. We explicitly acknowledged this limitation and evaluated each study using the ROBINS-I and ROB-2 frameworks. This concern was one of the reasons we incorporated trial sequential analysis (TSA), which demonstrated that the available evidence remains underpowered for firm conclusions, despite favorable trends in the conventional meta-analytic results.2

Regarding the 6-hour threshold used to define “early” vasopressin initiation, we agree that it is an imperfect construct. Nevertheless, given the variability in reporting across studies, it served as the most practical and reproducible cut-off for pooled analysis. Until physiologically informed thresholds or validated biomarkers become available, such standardization remains necessary for comparative evidence synthesis.

We also share the authors' concern about digital and mesenteric ischemia, which may be more common with vasopressin receptor agonists.3 As noted in our review, these outcomes could not be pooled because the primary studies lacked sufficient and consistent reporting. Future trials should incorporate structured, standardized safety monitoring to better inform patient-centered decision-making.

The authors further reference recent systematic reviews that similarly report encouraging signals but low-certainty evidence.4 This aligns with our primary conclusion: Early vasopressin initiation may reduce short-term, ICU, and hospital mortality and may lower the need for renal replacement therapy, but the existing evidence remains hypothesis-generating. Of note, a recent large-scale target trial emulation by White et al. found that initiating vasopressin within 6 hours was associated with reduced 30-day mortality compared with later or no vasopressin use.5 Additionally, a reinforcement-learning model has suggested a possible benefit from earlier vasopressin initiation in a large cohort of patients with septic shock.6

We are grateful for their emphasis on physiologic markers such as diastolic arterial pressure, the diastolic shock index, and the vascular norepinephrine responsiveness index (VNERi). These may offer more individualized, physiology-based strategies for determining vasopressin initiation, rather than relying on rigid time-based definitions. This perspective mirrors our call for tools that can reliably identify norepinephrine non-responders early in the course of shock. In fact, we are currently planning a multicenter trial (the TIVAS trial) that will incorporate physiology-driven thresholds to evaluate vasopressin initiation in septic shock.

In summary, we thank the authors for their constructive observations. Our results should be viewed as promising but not yet sufficient to change practice. Large, pragmatic randomized trials that stratify patients by norepinephrine dose, shock physiology, and perfusion status are urgently needed to determine whether early vasopressin truly alters clinical outcomes in septic shock.
