Intervention of pharmaceutical care on the quality-adjusted life years in diabetic foot ulcer patients–A randomized controlled study
Authors: Barma Naga Raju, Uday Venkat Mateti, Rajashekar Mohan, Caren D’Souza, Neevan D’Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the reasons for hospitalization among diabetic patients. The objective is to study the impact of pharmaceutical care on quality-adjusted life years (QALY) in DFU patients.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The eligible DFUs patients were randomized into the General Care (GC) and Pharmaceutical Care (PC) Group in a 1:1 ratio using the block design method. The study was carried out for 2 years at a tertiary care teaching hospital. The PC group received pharmaceutical care from the clinical pharmacist along with general care. The sociodemographics, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and economic data were collected from the both groups at baseline, 6th month, and 12thmonth. The changes in the outcomes within groups were analyzed using repeated measures of ANOVA.RESULTS:Out of 174 DFU patients, 87 each were allocated to PC and GC groups. At baseline, there was no statistically significant difference in HRQoL scores; in contrast, during 6th and 12thmonth, follow-ups showed a statistically significant improvement in domains like dependence/daily life (P = 0.006), negative emotions (P = 0.030) and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.024) in the PC group compared to the GC group. The PC group gained (-) Rs. 9984.3 and (-) US$.119.83 QALY at less cost when compared with the GC group DFU patients.CONCLUSION:The clinical pharmacist who provided structured pharmaceutical care services had significant positive impact on the HRQoL and economic outcomes in the PC group compared to the GC group. The study intervention was cost effective based on India’s GDP per capita of 2022.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a leading long-lasting metabolic condition affecting 537 million adults worldwide and is expected to raise the number to 643 million by the end of 2030.[1] The prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20–79 at the global level was 10.5%, whereas in India, it was at 8.3%, slightly lower than the global level. The most prevalent complication of DM is diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) caused by peripheral arterial disease or peripheral neuropathy, and it shows an adverse effect on health-related quality of life (HRQoL).[2] The prevalence of DFU in the United States ranged between 1.0% and 4.1%, in Kenya 4.6%, in India 15%, and 20.4% in the Netherlands. DFU constitutes a key cause of hospital admission, amputation, resultant adverse effects on living conditions, relationship status and HRQoL, and premature mortality among diabetic patients.[34] HRQoL is a comprehensive measure that encompasses physical and mental health, independence, social relationships, individual beliefs, and environment, according to the World Health Organization.[5] Among the DFU patients, various HRQoL domains, such as mobility, leisure, and emotions, were affected due to the presence of gangrene, osteomyelitis, and amputation. Healthcare professionals increasingly highlight HRQoL measurement and monitoring as a key outcome measure in DFU care. Pharmaceutical services are essential for attaining good therapeutic outcomes and enhancing patient HRQoL.[6] DFUs carry a significant economic burden, not only in India but also in the rest of the world. DFUs are the most common and serious complication due to diabetes, with exorbitant treatment costs, considerable indirect costs associated with loss of productivity, and a rising prevalence that threatens to overwhelm healthcare systems.[78] Pharmacist-led pharmaceutical care initiatives have been developed globally to improve clinical outcomes and HRQoL, frequently working with other healthcare providers.[6] Implementing educational interventions for diabetic patients showed a significant improvement in behavioral changes toward disease, HRQoL, and increased medication adherence among DFU patients.[910] Patients who adhered to self-care showed an improved HRQoL, achieved glycemic control, and reduced diabetic complications.[11] Several methods exist for determining the burden of chronic diseases, such as DFU, that represent HRQoL, including the widely used quality-adjusted life year (QALY). The QALY integrates the morbidity and mortality of a disease into a single indicator.[12] Some studies have reported that preventive measures, including education on foot care practices and healthy dietary practices, may reduce the incidence of DFUs in India. This can lessen the economic burden on patients and healthcare systems.[1314] In India, very few studies have been conducted to assess the QALY and HRQoL in patients with DFU. A few studies have reported that implementing patient education about foot ulcers will improve HRQoL and decrease the financial burden of the patients. However, there were very few studies conducted on the impact of pharmaceutical care on HRQoL and QALY among DFU patients. Hence, this study aim is to assess the impact of pharmaceutical care on QALY in patients with DFU.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

An open-label randomization-controlled study was carried out for 2 years (September 2021–September 2023) at a tertiary care charitable teaching hospital in Mangaluru, Karnataka.

Study participants and sampling

Based on physical component summary scores of HRQoL in DFU patients, the standard deviation for general care is 5.1, and the pharmaceutical care group is 9.7 for 90% power, the level of significance is 5%, mean difference of 4, the effect size is 0.5405 for two-sided tests. Anticipating the dropout rate of 10%, the minimum sample required in each group was 87, i.e., 174.[15]

Study criteria

Patients diagnosed with DFU visiting outpatient department (OPD) and inpatients were included based on the study criteria. The study’s inclusion criteria were patients above 18 years, either gender, who are diagnosed with DFUs, patients with three-foot complications, peripheral vascular disease, foot deformities, and abnormal plantar foot pressures, and inpatients and outpatients. Patients excluded from the study were psychiatric, other terminal illnesses, cognitive impairments, trauma-related amputation patients, and pregnant and lactating women. A random sampling method was used in the study.

Randomization and intervention

All the DFU patients included were randomized into the general care (GC) and pharmaceutical care (PC) group in a 1:1 ratio using the block design method. A total of 174 patients were grouped into 29 blocks, and each block contains six patients, i.e., three from the GC group and three from the PC group. The sequence was created by a person not directly involved in conducting the study. They were allocated by using the sealed opaque envelope method. Randomization of the groups was balanced based on the grading of DFUs using Wagner classification grades I and II and the University of Texas classification.

General care group (GC): GC group received the general care provided by the hospital team, including doctors, nurses, and other allied health care professionals. The standard care includes consultation with doctors, prescription of medications and routine medical tests, review of disease and drugs, refilling prescriptions, and referral during hospital visits.

Pharmaceutical care group (PC): PC group periodically received pharmaceutical care from the clinical pharmacist and general care. At each pharmaceutical care visit, the clinical pharmacist conducted the one-to-one patient interview, created the medication therapy profile, identified and resolved drug-related problems (DRPs) through a review of drugs, medical records, and laboratory data. PC group patients received special foot care education (foot wash procedure, daily visual assessment of the foot, wound dressing, toenail cutting, and importance of footwear) by providing the video and patient information leaflet (PIL).[16]

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected from patient case sheets, patient and patient caregiver interviews, laboratory reports through a data collection form and included the sociodemographic details of the enrolled patient, such as age, gender, and social history, past medical and medication history, BMI (weight/height readings), occupation, education, monthly income of the family, place of living (Urban/Rural), duration of DM was collected from the medical records, and patient interview. Modified Kuppuswamy’s Scale 2019 was used to assess patients’ socioeconomic status.[17] The recruitment and follow-up details of DFU patients during the study are shown in the flowchart [Figure 1].

Assessment of HRQoL: In this study, HRQoL was assessed by using a disease-specific instrument like Diabetic Foot Ulcer Scale - Short Form (DFS-SF). The scores per domain were transformed on a scale from 0 to 100 (Higher score = Better HRQoL) based on the sum of all items associated with that domain.[18]

Assessment of QALY: In this study, the direct medical, nonmedical, and indirect costs were collected. The direct medical costs were calculated based on the expenses of medications, laboratory investigation charges, consultation charges (OPD charges), charges of amputations, wound dressing, hospitalization charges (admission, bed, amenities, doctor, nurse, and miscellaneous charges), and medical insurance. The direct nonmedical costs were calculated based on the expenses of transportation costs to the hospital and food costs during the hospital visit. The indirect costs were assessed based on daily earnings and type of job. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was used to assess the economic outcomes. The utility values, QALY, and costs of the DFU patients were used for the calculation of the ICER values. EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) questionnaire was used to calculate the utility values.[19]

ICER

The cost-effectiveness grid (consisting of nine cells) and cost-effectiveness plane (pictorial technique consisting of four quadrants) using QALY outcomes were used to compare the results of the PC and GC groups in terms of cost and effectiveness and treatment alternatives.[20]

The primary and secondary outcome of the study is to assess the impact of pharmaceutical services on QALYs and HRQoL of the patients with DFU. The GC and PC group patients were followed for 12 months, and the outcomes were assessed at baseline, 6th month, and 12th months.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were shown as mean and standard deviation (SD), whereas qualitative variable data were shown as frequencies and percentages. An unpaired t-test was performed to analyze the change in the HRQoL outcomes between the two groups, and repeated measures of ANOVA were conducted to analyze the change in the HRQoL outcomes within the GC and PC groups. Mann-Whitney U test was performed to find the difference between the GC and PC groups in the annual economic outcomes. The data were analyzed using SPSS V29.0 software, and the P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

QALY of the PC and GC group patients with DFU

In this study, the total annual costs of the PC group (Rs. 57412.6 and US$689.23) were less compared to the GC group (Rs. 58011.66 and US$696.42) with a P value of 0.801. The detailed direct medical, nonmedical, and indirect costs are shown in Table 2. The ICER values of the PC group compared to the GC group were (-) Rs. 9984.3/QALY [(-) US$119.83/QALY]. The PC group gained QALY at less cost to obtain better health outcome when compared with the GC group patients with DFU. The cost-effectiveness grid analysis using QALY outcome showed that the PC group in the G cell was dominant, which indicates that intervention was more effective at lower cost, and the cost-effectiveness plane analysis using QALY outcome showed that the PC group in quadrant II was dominated, which indicates intervention is less costly and more effective. HRQoL scores of the PC and GC group patients with DFU

At baseline, no statistically significant difference was observed in the domains of both the PC and GC group patients. During the 6th month follow-up, there was statistically significant improvement among the domains of dependence or daily life (P = 0.006), negative emotions (P = 0.030), and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.024). During the 12th month follow-up, there was statistically significant improvement among the domains of dependence or daily life (P = 0.007), negative emotions (P = 0.017), and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.017). Discussion

In India, this is the premier study conducted to assess the intervention of pharmaceutical care services on QALY in DFU patients. The annual direct and indirect costs were calculated in the study, and no statistical significance was seen in either the PC group’s direct and indirect costs compared to the GC group. The cost per QALY would be less than three times the nation’s yearly GDP per capita, which is deemed as cost-effective according to the WHO cost-effective method.[20] In the current study, the yearly cost spent by the PC group to gain QALY is less compared to the GC group, which shows that pharmaceutical care services provided to the PC group had an impact compared to the GC group. According to the Committee on Electronic Information and Communication, India’s GDP per Capita was 2,301.418 USD in Mar 2022.[21] In this study, the PC group DFU patient cost per QALY is 3 times less than India’s annual GDP per capita, which means that intervention provided to the PC group is cost-effective. The results of the current study were comparable to study conducted by Mateti et al.[20] among CKD patients, where the intervention provided to the PC group was adequate compared to the UC group. The results of the present study are supported by the previous studies conducted by Cheng et al. and Hirsch et al.[2223] A study conducted by Obreli-Neto et al.[24] reported that providing pharmaceutical care services was cost-effective and improved the health outcomes of the intervention group compared to the control group. Jódar-Sánchez et al.[25] study among older adults with polypharmacy showed that medication review with follow-up service was cost-effective and that optimizes prescribed medications in the study population. Contrary to previous studies, Ji et al.[26] reported no effect of pharmaceutical care among the patients admitted to the neurology department.

HRQoL is one the important aspects which needs to be addressed in patients suffering from chronic diseases such as DM and its complications like DFU. In this study, the HRQoL of DFU patients were assessed using the disease-specific DFS-SF and the EQ-5D scales were used to determine the utility value. In this study, the baseline HRQoL domains [leisure (P = 0.494), physical health (P = 0.812), dependence/daily life (P = 0.664), negative emotions (P = 0.906), worried about ulcer (P = 0.943), and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.390)] scores of the PC group were statistically not significant compared to the GC group HRQoL scores. The baseline results of the study were comparable to studies conducted by Shekar et al.,[15] Aghakhani et al.,[27] and Mateti et al.[28] During the 6th month follow-up, the PC group HRQoL domains, such as dependence/daily life (P = 0.006), negative emotions (P = 0.030), and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.024), showed a statistically significant difference compared to the GC group. Similar to the current study, the result of a study by Shekar et al.[15] and Sakthong et al.[29] reported an improvement in HRQoL of interventional group compared to the control group after patient education. These results aligned with a previous study conducted by Mateti et al.,[28] which also reported an improvement in HRQoL of the PC group CKD patients compared to the UC group from baseline to 6th month after the pharmaceutical care services. Similar to previous studies, Okafor et al. also reported a significant improvement in HRQoL of the interventional group compared to control DM patients after the 6th month follow-up.[30] There was a statistically significant difference observed during the 12th month follow-up in PC group HRQoL domains such as dependence/daily life (P = 0.007), negative emotions (P = 0.017), and bothered by ulcer care (P = 0.017) compared to the GC group. These results were supported by the previous studies conducted by Shekar et al. and Aghakhani et al.[1527] Studies conducted by Adibe et al.[31] and Chandrasekhar et al.[32] among Type-2 DM and stroke patients showed a statistically significant improvement in HRQoL of the PC group compared to the GC group in the 12th month follow-up after the implementation of pharmaceutical care services. Schulz et al.[33] also reported improvement in HRQoL after pharmacist and physician care among the patients with the heart failure intervention group compared to the control group. According to Correr et al.,[34] the HRQoL of the T2DM interventional group had significantly improved through pharmaceutical care services, when compared to the control group. The significant improvement in HRQoL of the PC group in comparison with the GC group patients with DFU could be due to the pharmaceutical care services provided by clinical pharmacists in the form of educational video and printed materials in regional languages preferred by the participants. The presence of unhealed ulcer, fear of amputation, and pain were the factors that led to the lack of improvement in domains such as leisure, physical health, and ulcer among the intervention group.

The major strength of the study was the research design adopted and that it was a premier study conducted on pharmaceutical care intervention on QALY. However, the study had limitations with respect to the sample size, duration as well as on the generalizability of its results, since it was conducted in a single center. Despite the HRQoL measured by using the disease-specific questionnaire, HRQoL could be influenced by the comorbidities present and recall bias during the follow-ups.

Future scope of the study

This study further can be done on large number of the patients at multicenters for longer duration with a greater number of follow-ups. The study economic data further can be used in policy making of the DFU patients.

Conclusion

The structured pharmaceutical care services and patient education provided by the clinical pharmacist had a significant positive impact on the HRQoL and economic outcomes of DFU patients after the implementation of the pharmaceutical care services. The inclusion of clinical pharmacists in the multidisciplinary team optimizes management strategies, reduces healthcare costs, and enhances patients’ overall well-being. The health policy makers should consider the implementation of pharmaceutical care service intervention at national level for the benefit of the DFU patients.
