Diagonal Linear Networks and the Lasso Regularization Path
Raphaël Berthier

Abstract
Diagonal linear networks are neural networks with linear activation and diagonal weight matrices. Their theoretical interest is that their implicit regularization can be rigorously analyzed: from a small initialization, the training of diagonal linear networks converges to the linear predictor with minimal 1 1 -norm among minimizers of the training loss. In this paper, we deepen this analysis showing that the full training trajectory of diagonal linear networks is closely related to the lasso regularization path. In this connection, the training time plays the role of an inverse regularization parameter. Both rigorous results and simulations are provided to illustrate this conclusion. Under a monotonicity assumption on the lasso regularization path, the connection is exact while in the general case, we show an approximate connection.

Introduction

Context.

The composition of layers enables a neural network to learn a modular representation of the data. However, the reasons why learning the components of this representation is computationally amenable through gradient descent methods and why it leads to excellent generalization properties are still not fully understood
[
3
]
.

In order to address this questions, machine learning theory has studied the gradient flow training of
linear
networks—where the activation is linear—and
diagonal
linear networks (DLNs)—where, in addition, weight matrices are diagonal. These studies have pointed out that a certain
implicit regularization
phenomenon appears when training neural networks: the gradient descent minimizes certain regularization penalties, although not explicitly present in the loss function
[
31
]
. This phenomenon has been shown to be beneficial for the generalization properties of the trained networks, and thus suggested to be a key ingredient in the success of neural networks. See, e.g.,
[
16
,
21
,
9
,
20
]
for contributions to the implicit regularization of neural networks and
[
32
,
34
,
33
,
17
,
20
,
2
,
27
,
28
,
25
,
10
,
4
,
26
]
for more contributions specifically on DLNs.

We now briefly recall the form of this phenomenon in the case of DLNs. Consider the minimization of quadratic function:

\ell({x})=\frac{1}{2}\langle{x},M{x}\rangle-\langle r,{x}\rangle\,,\qquad{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

We assume that this function is convex, i.e. that
M
is positive semidefinite, and that it is lower-bounded, i.e. that
r\in\Span M
.
The minimization of such functions arises, e.g., when solving linear least-squares problems.

A DLN (with two layers) consists in parametrizing
x=u\circ v
, where
\circ
denotes the component-wise product of the two vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and training the parameters
u
and
v
with the gradient flow on
\ell(x)=\ell(u\circ v)
. This induces a trajectory
u(t),v(t)
as well as an effective linear parameter
x(t)=u(t)\circ v(t)
. When initialized from a suitable infinitesimal initialization
x(0)
, the training of DLNs is shown to converge to the minimizer of
\ell
with mininimal
1
-norm
[
33
]
. As a consequence, the minimizer selected by the training of a DLN benefits from a sparsifying effect, which is beneficial for the generalization properties of the trained network.

Contributions.

For a regularization parameter
\lambda\geqslant 0
, we define the lasso objective as

\Lasso({x},\lambda)=\ell({x})+\lambda\|{x}\|_{1}\,.

In other words, the result above states that DLNs converge to a minimum of the lasso objective with infinitesimal regularization parameter
\lambda=0^{+}
.

In this work, we show that there is in fact a deeper connection between the training trajectory of DLNs and the lasso regularization path. The connection holds in the same infinitesimal initialization limit. When
x(0)
is of magnitude
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the time
t
is jointly rescaled as
t(s,\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)s
. Consider the average of the trajectory of
x
:

\overline{x}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}x(u)\mathrm{d}u\,.

We then have the following informal result:

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, in this joint regime, for all
s>0
,
\overline{x}(t(s,\varepsilon))
(approximately) minimizes the lasso objective
\Lasso\left(.,\frac{1}{s}\right)
.

In the informal result above, the parenthesized word “approximately” refers to a more technical discussion. Under a monotonicity assumption on the lasso regularization path, we show that
\Lasso\left(\overline{x}(t(s,\varepsilon)),\frac{1}{s}\right)
converges to the minimum of
\Lasso\left(.,\frac{1}{s}\right)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. However, when the lasso regularization path is not monotone, we only show that
\overline{x}(t(s,\varepsilon))
is an approximate minimizer of
\Lasso\left(.,\frac{1}{s}\right)
, even in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, where the suboptimality gap is controled by the deviation from the monotonicity assumption. This distinction is also apparent in simulations.

This result shows that early stopping the training of DLNs can offer a tradeoff between sparsity and data fitting. An earlier stopping time leads to a sparser linear model. This deepens the connection between DLNs and sparse regression.

Our results also cover the case
x=u\circ u
, that is less motivated by the theoretical study of neural networks, but that is algebraically more elegant. As a consequence, we provide the proofs in the
u\circ u
case first, and then deduce the proofs in the
u\circ v
by a reduction to the
u\circ u
case. This systematic reduction strategy might be of independent interest for the study of DLNs.

The
u\circ v

Setting.

Diagonal linear networks consist in parametrizing
{x}
as a product of two vectors
{x}=u\circ v
. We study the training of such networks with the gradient flow, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{u}\left(\ell(u\circ v)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{v}\left(\ell(u\circ v)\right)\,.

(2.1)

We study this flow in the limit of small initialization. More precisely, we denote
u^{\varepsilon}(t)
and
v^{\varepsilon}(t)
the solutions of Eqs. (
2.1
) with initial conditions
u^{\varepsilon}(0)=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\beta
and
v^{\varepsilon}(0)=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\gamma
, where
\beta
and
\gamma
are fixed vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon
is a small parameter. We denote
{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)=u^{\varepsilon}(t)\circ v^{\varepsilon}(t)
. The scalings in
\varepsilon
are chosen so that
{x}^{\varepsilon}(0)
is of order
\varepsilon
. For non-degeneracy reasons, we assume that for all
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,
\beta_{i}\neq\pm\gamma_{i}
.

We also define
\overline{{x}}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}{x}^{\varepsilon}(u)\mathrm{d}u
the average of the trajectory of
{x}^{\varepsilon}
. The goal of our analyses is to connect the average of the trajectory to the lasso optimization problem

\mindot_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lasso(z,\lambda)\,.

(2.2)

We denote
\Lasso_{*}(\lambda)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lasso(z,\lambda)
the minimum of this optimization problem.

Define a rescaled time

\displaystyle s=\frac{2}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}t\,.

(2.3)

Below, we frequently abuse notations and use the same notation for rescaled versions of functions of time. For instance,
\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)
denotes
\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
where
t=\frac{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}{2}s
.

2.1
Connection to the lasso under a monotonicity assumption

Theorem 2.1
.

For all
s>0
, let
{x}(s)
denote a minimizer of the lasso

\underset{{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mindot}\Lasso\left({x},\frac{1}{s}\right)\,.

Assume that
s>0\mapsto s{x}(s)
is coordinate-wise monotone. Then

\Lasso\left(\overline{{x}}^{\varepsilon}(s),\frac{1}{s}\right)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\Lasso_{*}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\,.

As
\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}{x}(s)=0
, a sufficient condition that ensures
s\mapsto s{x}(s)
to be monotone, is to have the lasso regularization path
s\mapsto{x}(s)
itself monotone. Under the latter condition, the lasso regularization path has already been shown to be equivalent to computationally cheaper algorithms such as the least angle regression algorithm
[
15
]
, incremental forward stagewise regression
[
18
]
or boosting
[
29
]
. Moreover, it is also argued that even when
s\mapsto x(s)
is not monotone, the solutions of these different algorithms might not be very far, see for example Figure
1
in
[
15
]
. A sufficient condition for the lasso regularization path to be monotone, derived in the latter article, is to have
S(M_{II})^{-1}S\mathbbb{1}\geqslant 0
for all subsets
I\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
and for all diagonal sign matrices
S\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|\times|I|}
(see also
[
18
, Sec. 6]
for a succinct statement).

The monotonicity of
s\mapsto sx(s)
specifically has been studied, to the best of our knowledge, only in the case of the positive lasso, which corresponds to the parametrization
x=u^{2}
, see Sec.
3.1
.

2.3
Approximate connection to the lasso in the general case

We now provide an approximate optimality results in the nonmonotone case that comforts the simulations.

Theorem 2.2
.

For all
s>0
, let
{x}(s)
denote a minimizer of the lasso

\underset{{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mindot}\Lasso\left({x},\frac{1}{s}\right)\,.

Assume that
s>0\mapsto{x}(s)
is absolutely continuous on compact subsets of
(0,+\infty)
. Let

z^{\downarrow}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{s}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}(u{x}_{i}(u)_{+})\right)_{-}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}(u{x}_{i}(u)_{-})\right)_{-}\right]\mathrm{d}u\,.

(2.4)

Heuristically,
z^{\downarrow}(s)
quantifies the deviation from monotonicity of
z(s)=s{x}(s)
.

Then, there exists a constant
C=C(d,M,r,\alpha)>0
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Lasso\left(\overline{{x}}^{\varepsilon}(s),\frac{1}{s}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lasso_{*}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)+C\left[\frac{(1+s)^{1/2}}{s}z^{\downarrow}(s)^{1/2}+\frac{1+s}{s^{2}}z^{\downarrow}(s)\right]\,.

This theorem predicts that the suboptimality gap spikes when the lasso regularization path is not monotone (when
z^{\downarrow}(s)
is large) and then decays afterwards (as
s
increases). This is consistent with the observations in Figure
1
.

2.4
Related work and final remarks

Additional related work.

In contrast with many works on diagonal linear networks, this paper does not assume any statistical model for the data that generated the loss
\ell
, and focuses only on the optimization analysis. In particular, our results could cover noisy or noiseless data, and general design matrices. This is in contrast with many works that require a restricted isometry property, or a coherence assumption on the design matrix
[
32
,
34
,
20
]
.

Under a statistical linear model with additive noise, early stopping is used in DLNs to achieve optimal rates
[
32
,
34
,
20
]
. Similarly, in the same setting, the regularization parameter of the lasso is usually chosen proportional to the magnitude of the additive noise
[
19
, Chapter 11]
. Our work sheds light on this correspondence: a non-zero regularization parameter corresponds to a finite stopping time.

More specifically, some earlier works have noted the similarity between the trajectory of DLNs and the lasso regulization path
[
32
,
4
,
26
]
. Vaskevicius et al. noted that
t/\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
plays the role of an inverse regularization parameter in their statistical guarantee
[
32
]
(see discussion below their Thm. 1). However, they note that “the gradient descent optimization path [of DLNs] […] exhibits qualitative and quantitative differences from the lasso regularization path.” Our contribution argues that the qualitative differences are only due to the fact that the lasso regularization path corresponds to the
averaged
gradient descent optimization path, with mild differences discussed in this paper (see also Fig.
1
). Finally, the author proved Thm.
3.1
, the equivalent of Thm.
2.1
in the case
x=u\circ u
, but only when
M
is a matrix with non-positive off-diagonal entries
[
4
]
. (In this case, the monotonicity assumption is guaranteed.)

Final remarks.

This works strengthens our understanding of the implicit regularization of neural networks. Not only the asymptotic convergence point is implicitely regularized, but also the full trajectory of the neural network. The earlier the neural networks is stopped, the more regularized the solution is.

Our proof technique provides a way to analyze the implicit regularization in a
dynamical
way, that is, as time evolves. We hope that this new perspective will help to tackle a major difficulty of the implicit regularization line of research: we do not know how to describe the implicit regularization of more complex neural networks, beyond DLNs. For instance, the implicit regularization of matrix factorization problems can not be described by the nuclear norm, as one would expect from a natural generalization of the results on DLNs
[
1
,
22
]
. We hope that this discrepancy can be understood through a dynamical study of the deviation between matrix factorization dynamics and nuclear norm minimizers.

4.1
Mirror flow interpretation

We recall the interpretation of the DLN dynamics as a mirror flow
[
23
]
. For a general introduction on mirror flows, we refer to
[
7
, Chap. 4]
.

Eq. (
3.2
) can be interpreted as a mirror flow

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\ell(x^{\varepsilon})\,,

(4.1)

where the mirror map

\displaystyle h(x)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(x_{i}\log x_{i}-x_{i}\right)

is the entropy of the vector
x>0
.

A central tool in analyzing mirror flows is the Bregman divergence associated to the mirror map
h
. For
x,y>0
, it is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}D(x,y)&=h(x)-h(y)-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(x_{i}\log\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}-x_{i}+y_{i}\right)\,.\end{split}

(4.2)

Note that, by convexity of the mirror map
h
, the Bregman divergence
D
is always nonnegative. In our case, this Bregman divergence can be interpreted as a relative entropy.

The mirror variable
\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon})
has an initialization
\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon}(0))
that is diverging as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon}(0))=\frac{1}{4}\log x^{\varepsilon}(0)=-\frac{1}{4}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathbbb{1}+O(1)\,.

For this reason, we define a rescaled mirror variable

\displaystyle w^{\varepsilon}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon}(s))=-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\log x^{\varepsilon}(s)\,.

We have
w^{\varepsilon}(0)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\mathbbb{1}
. This rescaled version particularly fits the analysis in the rescaled time
s
. For instance,

\frac{\mathrm{d}w^{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s}=-\frac{4}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}s}\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla h(x^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{d}t}\underset{\text{(Eq.~\eqref{eq:mirror-flow-2})}}{=}\nabla\ell(x^{\varepsilon})=-r+M{x}^{\varepsilon}\,.

(4.3)

4.2
Uniform bound on the trajectories

The goal of this section is to prove that the trajectories
{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
of the DLN dynamics are bounded uniformly in
t\geqslant 0
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists
C=C(d,M,r,\alpha)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)>0
such that

\forall\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}),\forall t\geqslant 0,\quad\|{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|\leqslant C\,.

To prove this result, we first note that as the DLN dynamics are a gradient flow, the function values must be nonincreasing along trajectories.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
,
t\mapsto\ell({x}^{\varepsilon}(t))
is nonincreasing.

As a consequence, there exists
C_{1}=C_{1}(M,r,\alpha),C_{2}=C_{2}(M,r,\alpha)>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, for all
t\geqslant 0
,
\ell({x}^{\varepsilon}(t))\leqslant C_{1}
and
\|M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|\leqslant C_{2}
.

Proof.

Using Eq. (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\ell({x}^{\varepsilon})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\ell({x}^{\varepsilon})\frac{\mathrm{d}{x}_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-4\sum_{i=1}^{d}{x}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\left(\partial_{i}\ell({x}^{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\leqslant 0\,.

This proves that
t\mapsto\ell({x}^{\varepsilon}(t))
is nonincreasing.

Moreover, the continuous function
\ell
is bounded by a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(M,r,\alpha)
on the compact interval
[0,\alpha^{2}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As a consequence, for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\ell({x}^{\varepsilon}(t))\leqslant\ell({x}^{\varepsilon}(0))=\ell(\varepsilon\alpha^{2})\leqslant C_{1}\,.

Finally, let
{x}_{*}={x}_{*}(r,M)
be the minimum norm minimizer of
\ell
. Then for any
{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|M{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|M\|\|M^{1/2}{x}\|^{2}\leqslant 2\|M\|\left(\|M^{1/2}{x}_{*}\|^{2}+\|M^{1/2}({x}-{x}_{*})\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\|M\|\left(\|M^{1/2}{x}_{*}\|^{2}+2\ell({x})-2\ell({x}_{*})\right)\,.

Thus the lemma holds with

C_{2}(M,r,\alpha)=2\|M\|\left(\|M^{1/2}{x}_{*}\|^{2}+2C_{1}(M,r,\alpha)-2\ell({x}_{*})\right)\,.

∎

This result proves Prop.
4.1
when
M
is positive definite. However, the proof in the positive semidefinite case requires more work. Our strategy consists in characterizing the flow
{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
from its image
M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
, and, as a consequence, showing that
{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
is bounded by a bound on its image
M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
. These arguments are the two lemmas below.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
t\geqslant 0
,

{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\argmin_{{x}\geqslant 0,\,M{x}=M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)}D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\,,

(4.4)

where
D({x},y)
is defined in Eq. (
4.2
). Note that we extend
D({x},y)
to coordinates
{x}_{i}=0
by setting by continuity
0\log 0=0
.

Proof.

A point
{x}>0
is a minimizer of
\mindot_{{x}\geqslant 0,\,M{x}=M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)}D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))
if and only if
M{x}=M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
and
\nabla_{x}D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\in\Span M
. For
{x}={x}^{\varepsilon}(t)>0
, only the second condition needs to be checked. As
\nabla_{x}D({x},y)=\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(y)
and by Eq. (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}D({x}^{\varepsilon}(t),{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))

\displaystyle=\nabla h({x}^{\varepsilon}(t))-\nabla h({x}^{\varepsilon}(0))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(r-M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t^{\prime})\right)\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\in\Span M\,.

Moreover,
{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)
is the unique minimizer as
D(.,.)
is marginally strictly convex in its first variable. This justifies using the notation
\argmin
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)>0
and
C=C(d,M)>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
, for all
y=Mu
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u\geqslant 0
, denoting

\displaystyle x(y)=\argmin_{x\geqslant 0,\,Mx=y}D(x,{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\,,

(4.5)

we have

\|x(y)\|\leqslant C(1+\|y\|^{2})\,.

Before we prove this last lemma, let us combine the above lemmas to prove Prop.
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

By Lemma
4.3
,

{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\argmin_{{x}>0,\,M{x}=M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)}D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\,.

(4.6)

Using Lemma
4.4
and then Lemma
4.2
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,

\|{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|\leqslant C(d,M)(1+\|M{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2})\leqslant C(d,M)(1+C_{2}(M,r,\alpha)^{2})\,.

∎

We are now only left with the proof of Lemma
4.4
to finish this section. This result requires a Caratheodory theorem on cones. Let
a_{i}
,
i\in I
, denote a finite family of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The cone generated by
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
is defined as

\Cone(a_{i},i\in I)=\left\{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}a_{i}\,\middle|\,\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0,i\in I\right\}\,.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\Cone(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
.

There exists a linearly independent subfamily
a_{i}
,
i\in I
,
I\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
such that
y\in\Cone(a_{i},i\in I)
.

In particular,
y
is in the cone generated by a subfamily of at most
d
vectors.

To be precise, Carathéodory’s theorem usually refers to the last statement. However, below, we are interested in the linear independence statement, which follows easily from the same proof.

Proof.

As
y\in\Cone(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
,
y
can be decomposed as
y=\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}a_{i}
with
I\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
and
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
,
i\in I
. Consider such a decomposition with
I
of minimal cardinality. We will show that in this case, the
a_{i}
,
i\in I
are linearly independent.

By contradiction, assume that there exists
\mu_{i}
,
i\in I
, not all equal to
0
, such that
\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}a_{i}=0
. Then for all
t
, we have that
y=\sum_{i\in I}(\lambda_{i}-t\mu_{i})a_{i}
. We will use this degree of freedom in
t
to remove an element from
I
, and thus contradict the minimality of
I
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that there exists a positive
\mu_{i}
. (We can consider
-\mu
instead of
\mu
.) Consider

\tau=\inf\left\{t\geqslant 0\,\middle|\,\lambda_{i}-t\mu_{i}=0\text{ for some }i\in I\right\}\,.

Note that
\tau<\infty
as there exists
i\in I
such that
\mu_{i}>0
. Then for all
i\in I
,
\lambda_{i}-\tau\mu_{i}\geqslant 0
, with equality for at least one
i\in I
, that we denote
i_{0}
. Then
y=\sum_{i\in I\backslash\{i_{0}\}}(\lambda_{i}-\tau\mu_{i})a_{i}
. Thus
I
is not minimal, which gives a contradiction.
∎

We now use Caratheodory’s theorem to show a central lemma. Let us give first the motivation. Consider a feasible linear system
Ax=b
. The minimum norm solution
x=A^{\dagger}b
is bounded in norm by
\|A^{\dagger}\|\|b\|
. We now consider the same question for the system
Ax=b
with the additional constraint
x\geqslant 0
(assuming there exists at least one such solution). How can the minimum norm solution be bounded? Carathéodory’s theorem enables to prove that, the non-negative minimum norm solution is, again, bounded by a constant proportional to the norm of
b
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. There exists
C=C(A)>0
such that for all
y=Au
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
u\geqslant 0
, denoting

\displaystyle x(y)=\argmin_{x\geqslant 0,Ax=y}\|x\|\,,

(4.7)

we have

\|x(y)\|\leqslant C\|y\|\,.

Proof.

First, we note that
\|x\|
diverges as
\|x\|\to\infty
,
x\geqslant 0,Ax=y
. As a consequence, the minimizer in Eq. (
4.5
) is well-defined as the unique minimizer of a strictly convex function on a compact set.

In this proof, we show that the lemma holds with
C:=\max_{I\subset\{1,\dots,k\}}\|(A_{I})^{\dagger}\|
. Denote
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
the columns of
A
. Let
y=Au
for some
u\geqslant 0
. Then
y\in\Cone(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
. By Caratheodory’s theorem, there exists a linearly independent subfamily
a_{i}
,
i\in I
, such that
y\in\Cone(a_{i},i\in I)
, i.e.,
y=A_{I}v
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
,
v\geqslant 0
. The matrix
A_{I}
has full column rank, thus
v=(A_{I})^{\dagger}y
. Consider
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
x_{i}=v_{i}
if
i\in I
, and
x_{i}=0
otherwise. Then
Ax=y
and
x\geqslant 0
. Thus

\|x(y)\|\leqslant\|x\|=\|v\|=\|(A_{I})^{\dagger}y\|\leqslant\|(A_{I})^{\dagger}\|\|y\|\leqslant C\|y\|\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma
4.4
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

First, we note that
D(x,x^{\varepsilon}(0))
diverges as
\|x\|\to\infty
,
x\geqslant 0
. As a consequence, the minimizer in Eq. (
4.5
) is well-defined as the unique minimizer of a strictly convex function on a compact set.

The only difference between Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.6
is that the former minimizes
D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))
while the latter minimizes
\|{x}\|
. However, the two quantities are comparable in the sense that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)
,
C_{1}=C_{1}(d)
,
C_{2}=C_{2}(d)>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
, for all
{x}\geqslant 0
,

\frac{1}{8}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|{x}\|-C_{1}\leqslant D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\leqslant C_{3}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|{x}\|+\frac{1}{4}\|{x}\|^{2}+1\,.

(4.8)

Indeed,

\displaystyle D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left({x}_{i}\log\frac{{x}_{i}}{\varepsilon\alpha_{i}^{2}}-{x}_{i}+\varepsilon\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{x}_{i}\log{x}_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-1\right)\|{x}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\log\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,.

For the lower-bound, we use that
u\mapsto u\log u
is bounded from below on
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
. Thus there exists
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}(\alpha)
such that, for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
,

\displaystyle D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\geqslant-C_{1}(d)+\frac{1}{8}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|{x}\|\,.

For the upper-bound, we have

\displaystyle D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left({x}_{i}\log\frac{{x}_{i}}{\varepsilon\alpha_{i}^{2}}-{x}_{i}+\varepsilon\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{x}_{i}\left(\log{x}_{i}+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,.

We use
\log y\leqslant y
for
y>0
. Thus there exists
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{2}(\alpha)
,
C_{2}=C_{2}(d)
such that, for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{2}
,

\displaystyle D({x},{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\|{x}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}{x}_{i}+1\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\|{x}\|^{2}+C_{2}(d)\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|{x}\|+1\,.

Thus Eq. (
4.8
) holds for
\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha):=\min(\varepsilon_{1}(\alpha),\varepsilon_{2}(\alpha))
.

We now define
\beta(y)=\argmin_{\beta\geqslant 0,M\beta=y}\|\beta\|
. By Lemma
4.6
,
\|\beta(y)\|\leqslant C_{3}(M)\|y\|
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|x(y)\|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}(d)+D({x}(y),{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\qquad\text{by Eq.~\eqref{eq:aux-1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}(d)+D(\beta(y),{x}^{\varepsilon}(0))\qquad\text{by the variational definition of $x(y)$}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{4}(d)+C_{2}(d)\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|\beta(y)\|+\frac{1}{4}\|\beta(y)\|^{2}\qquad\text{by Eq.~\eqref{eq:aux-1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{4}(d)+C_{5}(d,M)\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\|y\|+C_{6}(d,M)\|y\|^{2}\,.

Assuming that
\varepsilon_{0}
is small enough so that
\frac{1}{8}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\|x(y)\|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{4}(d)+8C_{5}(d,M)\|y\|+C_{6}(d,M)\|y\|^{2}\leqslant C(d,M)(1+\|y\|^{2})\,.

∎

4.3
The positive lasso and linear complementarity problems

The positive lasso is in fact a constrained quadratic optimization problem:
\mindot_{x\geqslant 0}\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Mx\rangle-\langle r,x\rangle+\lambda\langle\mathbbb{1},x\rangle
. As a consequence, its primal dual formulation is a linear complementarity problem
[
13
]
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The vector
x
is a minimizer of the optimization problem (
3.3
) if and only if there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(v,x)
is a solution of

\displaystyle\begin{split}v&=-r+\lambda\mathbbb{1}+Mx\,,\\
v&\geqslant 0\,,\ x\geqslant 0\,,\ \langle v,x\rangle=0~.\end{split}

(4.9)

The system above has the form of a
linear complementarity problem
(LCP)
[
13
]
.

This result is classical and its proof follows from convex duality
[
6
, Section 5]
. We recall it for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote
q=-r+\lambda\mathbbb{1}
. The Lagrangian associated to (
3.3
) is

L(x,v)=\Lasso(x,\lambda)-\langle v,x\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Mx\rangle+\langle q,x\rangle-\langle v,x\rangle

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated to the constraint
x\geqslant 0
. As the optimization problem (
3.3
) is convex, the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for optimality. The stationarity condition is

0=\nabla_{x}L(x,v)=q+Mx-v\,,

the feasibility conditions are
x\geqslant 0
and
v\geqslant 0
, and the complementary slackness condition is
\langle v,x\rangle=0
. This proves Prop.
4.7
.
∎

We now study the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the LCP (
4.9
). Note that if
M
is positive definite, there exists a unique solution
(v,x)

[
13
, Thm. 3.1.6]
. In the more general case where
M
is positive semidefinite, existence still holds but uniqueness is more subtle. For instance, the positive lasso (
3.3
) might not have a unique minimizer, thus we might not have uniqueness of the solution in
x
. However, we have uniqueness in
v
.

Proposition 4.8
.

There exists a solution of the LCP (
4.9
). Moreover, the solution of the LCP is unique in
v
in the sense that if
(v,x)
and
(v^{\prime},x^{\prime})
are two solutions of the LCP, then
v=v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Existence.

By
[
14
]
, it suffices to show that
\Lasso({x},\lambda)=\ell({x})+\lambda\langle\mathbbb{1},{x}\rangle
is bounded from below on the set
\{{x}\,|\,{x}\geqslant 0\}
. The first term
\ell({x})
is bounded from below on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(as
r\in\Span(M)
), and the second term is nonnegative on
\{{x}\,|\,{x}\geqslant 0\}
.

Uniqueness.

The result is provided in
[
13
, Thm. 3.1.7(d)]
.
∎

4.4
The positive lasso regularization path and parametric linear complementarity problems

In Sec.
3
, we consider a solution
{x}(s)
of the positive lasso with regularization parameter
\lambda=\frac{1}{s}
,
s>0
. For varying
s
, we obtain a certain parametrization of the regularization path of the positive lasso. In this section, we draw a connection with so-called
parametric
LCPs.

From Prop.
4.7
, there exists
v(s)
such that
(v(s),{x}(s))
is a solution of the LCP

\displaystyle\begin{split}v(s)&=-r+\frac{1}{s}\mathbbb{1}+M{x}(s)\,,\\
v(s)&\geqslant 0\,,\ {x}(s)\geqslant 0\,,\ \langle v(s),{x}(s)\rangle=0~.\end{split}

(4.10)

Denote
w(s)=sv(s)
and
z(s)=s{x}(s)
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}w(s)&=-sr+\mathbbb{1}+Mz(s)\,,\\
w(s)&\geqslant 0\,,\ z(s)\geqslant 0\,,\ \langle w(s),z(s)\rangle=0~.\end{split}

(4.11)

This is a
parametric
LCP, where the parameter is
s

[
13
, Sec. 4.5]
.

Note that while the LCP (
4.10
) is defined only for
s>0
, the LCP (
4.11
) is also defined for
s=0
. This extension is rather trivial, the solution of both LCPs being simple for
s
small enough, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Assume
0\leqslant s<\frac{1}{\|r\|_{\infty}}
. Then the LCP (
4.11
) has a unique solution
(w(s),z(s))=(\mathbbb{1}-sr,0)
. If further
s>0
, this means that the LCP (
4.10
) has a unique solution
(v(s),{x}(s))=\left(-r+\frac{1}{s}\mathbbb{1},0\right)
.

Proof.

(w(s),z(s))=(\mathbbb{1}-sr,0)
is a solution of (
4.11
). As the solution is unique in
w
,
\mathbbb{1}-sr
is the unique solution in
w
. But as
\mathbbb{1}-sr>0
, this imposes that
z(s)=0
is the unique solution in
z
by complementarity.
∎

We now prove a simple regularity result on solutions of parametric LCPs.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
w(s)
be the unique solution in
w
of the parametric LCP (
4.11
). Then
s\geqslant 0\mapsto w(s)
is Lipschitz continuous and thus absolutely continuous,
\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\in\Span M
and
\|\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\|_{M^{\dagger}}\leqslant\|r\|_{M^{\dagger}}
.

Proof.

Let
s,s^{\prime}\geqslant 0
and
(w,z)=(w(s),z(s))
,
(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})=(w(s^{\prime}),z(s^{\prime}))
denote solutions of the parametric LCP (
4.11
) for the respective values
s,s^{\prime}
. Then, we have

w=-sr+\mathbbb{1}+Mz\,,\quad\text{and}\ \ w^{\prime}=-s^{\prime}r+\mathbbb{1}+Mz^{\prime}\,.

Hence,
w-w^{\prime}=-(s-s^{\prime})r+M(z-z^{\prime})\in\Span M
and

\displaystyle\|w-w^{\prime}\|^{2}_{M^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\langle w-w^{\prime},-(s-s^{\prime})r+M(z-z^{\prime})\rangle_{M^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-(s-s^{\prime})\langle w-w^{\prime},r\rangle_{M^{\dagger}}+\langle w-w^{\prime},z-z^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=-(s-s^{\prime})\langle w-w^{\prime},r\rangle_{M^{\dagger}}+\langle w,z\rangle-\langle w^{\prime},z\rangle-\langle w,z^{\prime}\rangle+\langle w^{\prime},z^{\prime}\rangle\,.

We can use Hölder’s inequality to bound the first term. Furthermore, by complementarity slackness
\langle z,w\rangle=\langle z^{\prime},w^{\prime}\rangle=0
. Finally, all vectors
w,w^{\prime},z,z^{\prime}
are nonnegative, thus
\langle w^{\prime},z\rangle,\langle w,z^{\prime}\rangle\geqslant 0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|w-w^{\prime}\|^{2}_{M^{\dagger}}\leqslant|s-s^{\prime}|\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{M^{\dagger}}\|r\|_{M^{\dagger}}\,,

i.e.,

\displaystyle\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{M^{\dagger}}\leqslant|s-s^{\prime}|\|r\|_{M^{\dagger}}\,.

This proves that the map
s\mapsto w(s)
is Lipschitz continuous. As for all
s,s^{\prime}
,
w(s)-w(s^{\prime})\in\Span M
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\in\Span M
. The inequality above also shows that
\|\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\|_{M^{\dagger}}\leqslant\|r\|_{M^{\dagger}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s}

(4.12)

Integrating Eq. (

We compute the derivative of

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta^{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s}

):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta^{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s}&=\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s)-\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w^{\varepsilon}(s)-w(s)+\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle-\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),w(s)\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle\\
&\qquad+\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w(s)\right\rangle+\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)\right\rangle\,.\end{split}

(4.14)

We now upper-bound each term separately:

•

The function
u>0\mapsto u\log u
is uniformly lower bounded by a universal constant thus

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\log x_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\leqslant\frac{C(d)}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\,.

•

We have
x^{\varepsilon}(s)\geqslant 0
and
w(s)\geqslant 0
thus
-\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),w(s)\rangle\leqslant 0
.

•

If
\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\geqslant 0
, we use that by Prop.
4.1
,

w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)=-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\log x_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\geqslant-\frac{C(d,M,r,\alpha)}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\,.

If
\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\leqslant 0
, we use Eq. (
4.3
) and Prop.
4.1
to obtain

w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)(1+s)\,.

Thus

-\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)_{+}+(1+s)\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)_{-}\right]\,.

•

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. We show that
\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}w_{i}(s)=0
. From Eqs. (
4.11
), we have
w_{i}(s)z_{i}(s)=0
. If
w_{i}(s)=0
, then the conclusion is obvious. Otherwise
z_{i}(s)=0
. Differentiating
w_{i}(s)z_{i}(s)=0
, we obtain
\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}z_{i}(s)+w_{i}(s)\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}=0
. As
z_{i}(s)=0
, this gives the desired conclusion.

Thus, we have
\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w(s)\right\rangle=0
.

•

Note that
\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)=\mathbbb{1}+\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\log x^{\varepsilon}(0)=\frac{2}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\log\alpha
. This enables to bound the last two terms, using again Prop.
4.1
.

Recall that
z^{\downarrow}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(u)}{\mathrm{d}u}\right)_{-}\mathrm{d}u
. Denote also
z^{\uparrow}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}(u)}{\mathrm{d}u}\right)_{+}\mathrm{d}u
. Integrating the bounds obtained between
0
and
s
, we have

\Delta^{\varepsilon}(s)\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)\left[\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\left(s+z^{\uparrow}(s)\right)+(1+s)z^{\downarrow}(s)\right]\,.

At this point, we have bounded
\|z^{\varepsilon}(s)-z(s)\|^{2}_{M}
, or, equivalently,
\|\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\|^{2}_{M}
, where
{x}(s)
is a minimizer of the positive lasso with regularization
\lambda=\frac{1}{s}
. However, as
M
might not be positive definite, we can not conclude directly.

The positive lasso objective function
\Lasso(x,\frac{1}{s})
is a quadratic function on the set of nonnegative vectors
x\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\Lasso\left(x,\frac{1}{s}\right)=g_{s}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Mx\rangle-\langle r,x\rangle+\frac{1}{s}\langle\mathbbb{1},x\rangle\,.

As a consequence, we can compute
\Lasso(\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s),1/s)=g_{s}(\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s))
using the second-order approximation of
g_{s}
at
{x}(s)
:

\displaystyle\Lasso\left(\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s),\frac{1}{s}\right)-\PosLasso_{*}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)=g_{s}(\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s))-g_{s}({x}(s))

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle\nabla g_{s}({x}(s)),\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\|^{2}_{M}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle M{x}(s)-r+\frac{1}{s}\mathbbb{1},\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\|^{2}_{M}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle v(s),\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s)-{x}(s)\|^{2}_{M}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{s^{2}}E^{\varepsilon}(s)

where
E^{\varepsilon}(s)=\langle w(s),z^{\varepsilon}(s)-z(s)\rangle+\Delta^{\varepsilon}(s)
. We compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E^{\varepsilon}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}=\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}w(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},z^{\varepsilon}(s)-z(s)\right\rangle+\left\langle w(s),x^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle-\left\langle w(s),\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\rangle+\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta^{\varepsilon}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\,.

For the first term, we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
4.10
. Moreover, several terms cancel out with the expression of the derivative of
\Delta^{\varepsilon}(s)
in Eq. (
4.14
). We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E^{\varepsilon}(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|r\|_{M^{\dagger}}\|z^{\varepsilon}(s)-z(s)\|_{M}+\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},w^{\varepsilon}(s)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle x^{\varepsilon}(s),\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s},\mathbbb{1}-w^{\varepsilon}(0)\right\rangle\,.

All of these terms have been bounded above. Integrating between
0
and
s
, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}(s)

\displaystyle\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)\left[\int_{0}^{s}\Delta^{\varepsilon}(u)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}u+\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\left(s+z^{\uparrow}(s)\right)+(1+s)z^{\downarrow}(s)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)\Bigg[s\left(\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\left(s+z^{\uparrow}(s)\right)+(1+s)z^{\downarrow}(s)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\left(s+z^{\uparrow}(s)\right)+(1+s)z^{\downarrow}(s)\Bigg]\,.

This allows use to conclude

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Lasso\left(\overline{x}^{\varepsilon}(s),\frac{1}{s}\right)-\PosLasso_{*}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{s^{2}}E^{\varepsilon}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant C(d,M,r,\alpha)\left[\frac{(1+s)^{1/2}}{s}z^{\downarrow}(s)^{1/2}+\frac{1+s}{s^{2}}z^{\downarrow}(s)\right]\,.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

Thm.
3.1
is an application of Thm.
3.2
. However, to apply Thm.
3.2
, we need to prove that
s\mapsto{x}(s)
is absolutely continuous. This is proven in the following lemma (in combination with Lemma
4.9
).

Lemma 4.11
.

Under the assumptions of Thm.
3.1
, the function
s\geqslant 0\mapsto z(s)
is absolutely continuous on compact subsets of
[0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.10
, we have that
s\geqslant 0\mapsto w(s)
is
C_{1}(M,r)
-Lipschitz continuous for some constant
C_{1}(M,r)>0
. Using the linear relationship
w(s)=-sr+\mathbbb{1}+Mz(s)
, this implies that
z\geqslant 0\mapsto\P_{\Span M}z(s)
is
C_{2}(M,r)
-Lipschitz continuous for some constant
C_{2}(M,r)>0
, where
\P_{\Span M}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\Span M
.

Denote also
\P_{\ker M}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\ker M
. We have the complementarity slackness
\langle w(s),z(s)\rangle=0
. Recall that
w(s)\in\mathbbb{1}+\Span M
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\langle w(s),z(s)\rangle=\langle w(s),\P_{\Span M}z(s)\rangle+\langle w(s),\P_{\ker M}z(s)\rangle\\
&=\langle w(s),\P_{\Span M}z(s)\rangle+\langle\mathbbb{1},\P_{\ker M}z(s)\rangle\,.\end{split}

(4.15)

Consider
s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}
. As
s\mapsto z(s)
is monotone, we have

\displaystyle\|z(s_{2})-z(s_{1})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbbb{1},z(s_{2})-z(s_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbbb{1},\P_{\Span M}z(s_{2})-\P_{\Span M}z(s_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\mathbbb{1},\P_{\ker M}z(s_{2})-\P_{\ker M}z(s_{1})\rangle\,.

We use Hölder’s inequality and Eq. (
4.15
):

\displaystyle\|z(s_{2})-z(s_{1})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{d}\|\P_{\Span M}z(s_{2})-\P_{\Span M}z(s_{1})\|

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle w(s_{1}),\P_{\Span M}z(s_{1})\rangle-\langle w(s_{2}),\P_{\Span M}z(s_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{d}C_{2}(M,r)|s_{2}-s_{1}|+\langle w(s_{1})-w(s_{2}),\P_{\Span M}z(s_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle w(s_{2}),\P_{\Span M}z(s_{1})-\P_{\Span M}z(s_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{d}C_{2}(M,r)|s_{2}-s_{1}|+C_{1}(M,r)|s_{2}-s_{1}|C_{2}(M,r)s_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\sqrt{d}+C_{1}(M,r)s_{1}\right)C_{2}(M,r)|s_{2}-s_{1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{3}(d,M,r)(1+s_{1}+s_{2})|s_{2}-s_{1}|\,.

This proves that
z\geqslant 0\mapsto z(s)
is Lipchistz, and thus absolutely continuous, on compact subsets of
[0,+\infty)
.
∎

5.1
Reductions

5.1.1
Reduction of the lasso to the positive lasso

We study the minimization of the lasso objective:

\displaystyle\mindot_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\Lasso(x,\lambda)=\ell(x)+\lambda\|x\|_{1}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\ell(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Mx\rangle-\langle r,x\rangle\,.

We want to connect the lasso minimization problem to a positive lasso minimization problem (
3.3
). This can be seen as a complexification of the lasso minimization problem, as contrained minimization problems are arguably more delicate to deal with than unconstrained ones. However, the lasso objective is quadratic on nonnegative vectors, while it is not on the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(when
\lambda>0
). The constrained minimization of a quadratic has some advantages over the unconstrained minimization of a non-smooth function.

The strategy is to decompose
x
as the difference
x=y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}}
of two nonnegative vectors
y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}}\geqslant 0
. For instance, a canonical choice would be to take
y^{\text{pos}}=x_{+}
and
y^{\text{neg}}=x_{-}
. Note that for such a decomposition,

\ell(x)=\widetilde{\ell}(y)\,,\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}y^{\text{pos}}\\
y^{\text{neg}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
\widetilde{\ell}
is the quadratic function

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}(y)=\frac{1}{2}\langle y,\widetilde{M}y\rangle-\langle\widetilde{r},y\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}=\begin{pmatrix}M&-M\\
-M&M\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
-r\end{pmatrix}\,.

We define the lasso objective associated to
\widetilde{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lasso}(y,\lambda)=\widetilde{\ell}(y)+\lambda\|y\|_{1}\,.

We denote
\widetilde{\PosLasso}(\lambda)=\min_{y\geqslant 0}\widetilde{\Lasso}(y,\lambda)
the minimum of the positive lasso optimization problem associated to
\widetilde{\ell}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

(1)

For any
y=(y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
,

\Lasso(y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}},\lambda)\leqslant\widetilde{\Lasso}(y,\lambda)\,,

with equality if and only if
\lambda=0
or
\langle y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}}\rangle=0
.

(2)

If
z
minimizes
\Lasso(.,\lambda)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
y=(z_{+},z_{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
minimizes
\widetilde{\Lasso}(.,\lambda)
over
\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
.

(3)

\Lasso_{*}(\lambda)=\widetilde{\PosLasso}_{*}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

(1)

Consider
y=(y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
.

\displaystyle\Lasso(y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\ell(y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}})+\lambda\|y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell(y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}})+\lambda\left(\|y^{\text{pos}}\|_{1}+\|y^{\text{neg}}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\ell}(y)+\lambda\|y\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Lasso}(y,\lambda)\,.

There is equality if and only if
\lambda=0
or there is equality in the triangle inequality, which happens if and only if
\langle y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}}\rangle=0
.

(2)

Let
z
be a minimizer of
\Lasso(.,\lambda)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider
y=(y^{\text{pos}},y^{\text{neg}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
. Then, from
(1)
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lasso}(y,\lambda)

\displaystyle\geqslant\Lasso(y^{\text{pos}}-y^{\text{neg}},\lambda)\geqslant\Lasso(z,\lambda)=\widetilde{\Lasso}((z_{+},z_{-}),\lambda)\,,

where the equality holds in the last step as
\langle z_{+},z_{-}\rangle=0
. As a consequence,
(z_{+},z_{-})
minimizes
\widetilde{\Lasso}(.,\lambda)
over
\mathbb{R}^{2d}_{\geqslant 0}
.

(3)

From
(1)
, we have
\Lasso_{*}(\lambda)\leqslant\widetilde{\PosLasso}(\lambda)
. Further, if
z
is a minimizer of
\Lasso(.,\lambda)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\displaystyle\Lasso_{*}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\Lasso(z,\lambda)=\widetilde{\Lasso}((z_{+},z_{-}),\lambda)\geqslant\widetilde{\PosLasso}(\lambda)\,.

This proves the equality.

∎

5.1.2
Reduction of dynamics in the
u\circ v
case to the
u\circ u
case

Recall that the dynamics in the
u\circ v
case are defined by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{u}\left(\ell(u\circ v)\right)=-v\circ\nabla\ell(u\circ v)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{v}\left(\ell(u\circ v)\right)=-u\circ\nabla\ell(u\circ v)\,.

Consider

\displaystyle p^{\text{pos}}=\frac{1}{2}(u+v)\,,

\displaystyle p^{\text{neg}}=\frac{1}{2}(u-v)\,.

Then

\displaystyle u\circ v=(p^{\text{pos}})^{2}-(p^{\text{neg}})^{2}\,.

Define
\widetilde{t}=\frac{t}{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{\text{pos}}}{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{t}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}=-v\circ\nabla\ell(u\circ v)-u\circ\nabla\ell(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=-2p^{\text{pos}}\circ\nabla\ell((p^{\text{pos}})^{2}-(p^{\text{neg}})^{2})=-2p^{\text{pos}}\circ\nabla_{y^{\text{pos}}}\widetilde{\ell}((p^{\text{pos}})^{2},(p^{\text{neg}})^{2})

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{p^{\text{pos}}}\left(\widetilde{\ell}((p^{\text{pos}})^{2},(p^{\text{neg}})^{2})\right)\,.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{\text{neg}}}{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{t}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{p^{\text{neg}}}\left(\widetilde{\ell}((p^{\text{pos}})^{2},(p^{\text{neg}})^{2})\right)\,.

Thus we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{t}}\begin{pmatrix}p^{\text{pos}}\\
p^{\text{neg}}\end{pmatrix}=-\nabla_{(p^{\text{pos}},p^{\text{neg}})}\left(\widetilde{\ell}\left(\begin{pmatrix}p^{\text{pos}}\\
p^{\text{neg}}\end{pmatrix}^{2}\right)\right)\,.

This is an equation of the form (
3.1
) in the
u\circ u
case. As a consequence, we have reduced a gradient flow on
\ell
in the
u\circ v
case to a gradient flow on
\widetilde{\ell}
in the
u\circ u
case.
