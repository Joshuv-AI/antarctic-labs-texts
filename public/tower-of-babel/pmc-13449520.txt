Frailty and associated factors in women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shurui Wang, Shumei Feng, Aomei Shen, Wanmin Qiang, Qiang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the prevalence of frailty and describe factors associated with frailty among women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer.MethodsThis single center cross-sectional study included women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer who were recruited from a tertiary cancer hospital between May 2023 and November 2023. Frailty was assessed using the Fried frailty phenotype. Introduction

Breast cancer represents a significant and growing global health challenge. With an estimated 2.3 million new cases diagnosed annually, it has become the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, surpassing lung cancer in incidence according to the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates. Notably, breast cancer ranks as the fifth leading cause of global cancer mortality, accounting for approximately 685,000 deaths each year, thereby posing a substantial threat to women’s health across all regions [1]. This burden is particularly pronounced in aging populations. In China, as in many other countries undergoing demographic transition, the absolute number of older women diagnosed with breast cancer is projected to increase rapidly in the coming decades [2], creating urgent needs for specialized geriatric oncology care.

In geriatric oncology, the concept of frailty has gained paramount importance. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines frailty as “a clinically recognizable state of increased vulnerability resulting from aging-associated decline in reserve and function across multiple physiologic systems such that the ability to cope with everyday or acute stressors is compromised” [3]. This multidimensional syndrome serves as a robust predictor of numerous adverse health outcomes in older populations, including postoperative complications, chemotherapy toxicity, unplanned hospitalizations, functional decline, and increased mortality [4–6]. Beyond its physical manifestations, frailty encompasses psychological and social dimensions that collectively diminish quality of life and treatment tolerance [7].

The intersection of breast cancer and frailty in older women presents a complex clinical scenario. Advancing age is a primary risk factor for both conditions [4, 8]. Moreover, a cancer diagnosis and its associated treatments can accelerate the development or worsening of frailty through mechanisms such as sarcopenia, nutritional deficiencies, chronic inflammation, and toxicity from chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery [9–11]. Supporting this, Gilmore et al. [12] found that breast cancer survivors had a higher prevalence of frailty compared to non-cancer counterparts. Specific treatment modalities, including adjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy, have been identified as significant risk factors for functional decline and the onset of frailty in this population [13, 14].

Despite these recognized risks, there remains a notable gap in research specifically focused on the prevalence and the factors associated with frailty among women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer. A deeper understanding of the factors associated with frailty, including sociodemographic, clinical, nutritional, and social characteristics, is important for supportive care planning.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the prevalence of frailty and describe factors associated with frailty among women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer, thereby providing a basis for early identification of frailty and supportive care planning in this population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted using a convenience sampling approach to recruit participants from a tertiary cancer specialty hospital in Tianjin, China. The study population consisted of female breast cancer patients aged 65 years or older who attended the hospital between May 2023 and November 2023. The age threshold of ≥ 65 years was selected based on existing literature indicating that breast cancer in this age group exhibits distinct epidemiological patterns and clinicopathological characteristics compared to younger populations, warranting specific investigation [15, 16]. Participants were recruited from a tertiary cancer center because it provides specialized follow-up care for a large volume of older women with breast cancer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: Pathologically confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer; Aged ≥ 65 years; Had completed active anticancer treatments (including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy); Possessed adequate cognitive and communicationabilities; and voluntarily participated with signed informed consent. Patients receiving active anticancer treatment were not included in order toreduce the influence of acute treatment-related effects on frailty assessment and to evaluate patients in a relatively stable post-treatment condition.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: Severe hearing, visual, or cognitive impairments that could hinder communication or assessment; Inability to ambulate (temporarily or permanently); Diagnosis of cachexia or severe functional decline; Presence of major psychiatric disorders.

Data quality control

Standardized protocols were implemented throughout the study. All researchers received uniform training to ensure consistent data collection procedures. Face-to-face interviews were conducted to minimize missing data, and immediate verification of responses. Identical equipment from the same manufacturer and batch was used for all physical measurements. Handgrip strength was measured twice per participant, with the average value recorded. These measures ensured data consistency and enhanced the reliability of the study results.

Questionnaires with more than 10% missing entries or showing patterned responses were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining missing values (≤ 10%), mean imputation was applied, where missing values were replaced with the mean value of that variable from participants with complete data.

Instruments

The general information and disease-related questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the study objectives, a review of the relevant literature, and clinical experience in geriatric oncology. Sociodemographic information was collected through structured interviews, and disease-related variables were verified using electronic medical records.

Survey of general information

Data regarding sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle factors were obtained through structured interviews supplemented with medical record verification. The collected variables encompassed age, marital status, educational attainment, occupational category, religious affiliation, ethnic background, personal monthly income, residential location, living arrangement, health insurance type, history of falls, smoking status, alcohol consumption patterns, anthropometric measurements (height, weight, and body mass index), daily activity profiles, sleep quality, and self-rated general health status.

Sleep quality and self-rated health status were assessed using single-item self-report questions. Sleep quality was categorised as good, fair, or poor according to the participant’s overall subjective evaluation of recent sleep. Self-rated health status was categorised as Excellent, Good, Fair, or Poor based on the participant’s overall perception of her general health. The oral medication variable was the number of oral medications currently taken regularly. Comorbidity burden was derived from medical records and included physician-diagnosed chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and rheumatological disease.

Disease-related Information

Disease-related clinical data were systematically collected from electronic medical records. These parameters included tumor characteristics (laterality and pathological stage), treatment history (time since diagnosis, surgical history and approaches, as well as prior chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy), comorbidity burden, and current oral medication regimens.

Measurement of frailty

The frailty phenotype, as originally proposed by Fried et al. [5] in 2001, comprises five physical criteria: unintentional weight loss (≥ 4.5 kg or ≥ 5% of body weight in the past year), self-reported exhaustion, slow walking speed (≥ 7 s for women ≤ 159 cm tall or ≥ 6 s for those > 159 cm tall, assessed over a 4.57-meter walk), weak grip strength (defined by BMI-stratified cutoffs: ≤17 kg for BMI ≤ 23 kg/m², ≤ 17.3 kg for BMI 23.1–26 kg/m², ≤ 18 kg for BMI 26.1–29 kg/m², and ≤ 21 kg for BMI > 29 kg/m²), and low physical activity (< 270 kcal/week in energy expenditure). Individuals meeting three or more of these criteria are classified as frail. This phenotype has been widely validated and is commonly used for frailty screening and assessment among older adults in both community and hospital settings [17].

Assessment of anxiety and depression

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), developed by Zigmond and Snaith [18] in 1983, is a 14-item self-report instrument designed to screen for symptoms of anxiety and depression over the past week. The scale consists of two subscales: seven items evaluate anxiety, and seven items assess depression. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21. A subscale score of ≥ 8 is indicative of clinically significant anxiety or depression symptoms.

The Chinese version of HADS has been validated by Sun et al. [19], who reported excellent psychometric properties, with a total Cronbach’s α of 0.879 and test-retest reliability ICC of 0.945; item-level test-retest correlations ranged from 0.871 to 0.988. In the present study, the scale also demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.919.

Screening for nutritional risk

Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form (MNA-SF), a widely used screening tool that has been validated in older adults and oncology populations [20–24]. The MNA-SF is designed to identify older adults at risk of malnutrition or with existing malnutrition. Based on established cut-off values, scores are interpreted as follows: 12–14 points indicate normal nutritional status, 8–11 points indicate risk of malnutrition, and 0–7 points indicate malnutrition.

Evaluation of cognitive function

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a widely used 30-point cognitive screening instrument, originally developed by Nasreddine et al. [25]. It assesses multiple cognitive domains and is particularly sensitive in detecting mild cognitive impairment. In this study, we employed the MoCA Beijing version, which incorporates cultural and linguistic adaptations for Chinese older adults as described in prior work [26]. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with a one-point adjustment applied for individuals with ≤ 12 years of education. A higher final score indicates better cognitive functioning. Previous studies have supported the reliability and validity of the MoCA Beijing version in Chinese older adults [27]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the MoCA was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.818.

Social support assessment

The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), developed by Zimet et al. in 1988 [28], was used to evaluate participants’ subjective perception of social support. This 12-item instrument measures support across three dimensions, with each item rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). Total scores range from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.

The Chinese version, translated and validated by Jiang Qianjin et al. [29], has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties in previous studies. The subscales showed Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.87, 0.85, and 0.91, respectively, with an overall internal consistency of 0.88 for the full scale, indicating high reliability for use in Chinese populations.

Data collection

Data collection was rigorously conducted in accordance with the predefined inclusion, exclusion, and elimination criteria. Before participation, all eligible patients were fully informed about the study’s purpose and significance. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with assurance that their responses would not affect their current or future medical care or treatment.

Physical measurements, including handgrip strength, were performed using standardized procedures. Handgrip strength was measured using a calibrated hand dynamometer by trained research staff. Each participant completed two measurements on the dominant hand, with a 30-second interval between assessments. The mean of the two measurements was used for analysis. To avoid overstatement, we describe these procedures as standardized measures intended to reduce measurement error and improve consistency. Questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site during hospital visits. Research staff provided assistance and clarification when participants had questions regarding the questionnaire items.

To ensure data accuracy, a double-entry system was employed. Two independent researchers performed data entry; one researcher entered the data from the completed questionnaires, while the second researcher cross-checked the entries against the original documents to verify completeness and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Frailty status was compared between the non-frail and frail groups. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables available only in grouped form were analyzed as categorical variables. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating hospital (Approval No: bc2021084). All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study purpose and procedures.

Comparison of participant characteristics by frailty status

Frailty status differed significantly according to age, occupation, number of oral medications, history of falls, sleep quality, self-rated health status, tumor stage, radiotherapy history, and number of comorbidities (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed for religion, monthly income, education, marital status, living arrangement, residence, smoking, alcohol use, body mass index, tumor laterality, surgery history, time since diagnosis, type of surgery, chemotherapy history, targeted therapy history, endocrine therapy history, anxiety, depression, cognitive category, nutritional risk category, or perceived social support score (all P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in Table 1.

To further improve the transparency of the frailty assessment, handgrip strength and gait speed were additionally summarized according to frailty status and are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Compared with the non-frail group, the frail group showed significantly lower handgrip strength (median [IQR], 14.8 [12.3–17.6] vs. 21.4 [18.9–24.6] kg, P < 0.001) and slower gait speed (0.68 [0.59–0.79] vs. 0.94 [0.85–1.05] m/s, P < 0.001).

Discussion

The prevalence of frailty among the 227 women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer included in this study was 60.8%, a figure notably higher than the pooled prevalence of 43% reported in our previous meta-analysis [13]. That analysis, which included 24 studies (7 cross-sectional and 17 cohort studies) involving 13,510 breast cancer patients, revealed frailty rates ranging widely from 5% to 71% [14, 30, 31]. This variability may be attributed to the lack of consistency in frailty assessment tools, divergent conceptual definitions of frailty, and differences in the intended use of instruments (e.g., for clinical screening versus prognostic prediction). Importantly, the meta-analysis indicated that studies using the deficit accumulation model reported the lowest frailty prevalence (19%), whereas those applying the Fried phenotype reported the highest (60%), which is consistent with the findings of the present study. These results underscore the urgent need to standardize the definition and assessment of frailty in oncology settings to improve clinical utility and facilitate cross-study comparisons.

Frailty was common in this cohort of women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer, and this finding is broadly consistent with previous studies showing that frailty is highly prevalent among older adults with cancer, including patients with breast cancer [4, 6, 13, 32]. In the present study, frailty status differed significantly according to age, occupation, number of oral medications, history of falls, sleep quality, self-rated health status, tumor stage, radiotherapy history, and number of comorbidities. Taken together, these findings suggest that frailty in older women with breast cancer is related not only to cancer-related characteristics, but also to broader geriatric and general health factors.

The additional physical performance findings in our cohort were also broadly consistent with previous studies in older breast cancer populations. In a cross-sectional study of 230 Thai older breast cancer survivors [33], the overall mean handgrip strength was 18.2 ± 4.6 kg and the overall mean gait speed was 1.15 ± 0.32 m/s; values were lower in frail survivors (13.36 ± 4.2 kg and 0.83 ± 0.31 m/s) than in robust survivors (21.53 ± 2.4 kg and 1.32 ± 0.21 m/s). In another study comparing older breast cancer survivors with cancer-free controls, survivors had lower handgrip strength (22.3 ± 5.0 vs. 24.3 ± 4.4 kg), whereas gait speed was similar between groups (1.1 ± 0.2 vs. 1.1 ± 0.1 m/s). Against this background, the lower handgrip strength and gait speed observed in our frail group appear clinically plausible and further support the physical vulnerability captured by the frailty phenotype in this population. The relatively slow gait speed in our cohort, particularly in the frail group, may also suggest a greater burden of functional impairment, comorbidity, or post-treatment vulnerability than that reported in some survivorship cohorts [34].

Several of the observed associations are clinically plausible and broadly consistent with previous studies in older adults and older patients with cancer [4, 13, 32]. Age is a fundamental biological correlate of frailty, as advancing age is accompanied by progressive decline in physiological reserve and resilience [35]. History of falls, poor sleep quality, and worse self-rated health are less likely to represent isolated complaints than integrated manifestations of declining physical function, symptom burden, and heightened vulnerability, and together they may reflect a trajectory toward impaired recovery capacity [36, 37]. Likewise, a higher number of oral medications and comorbidities probably reflects not only greater multimorbidity burden, but also more complex long-term health management and a higher likelihood of treatment–disease interactions. This pattern is consistent with the view that frailty arises from cumulative multisystem deficits rather than from a single disease process [32, 38–41]. The association with tumor stage may similarly indicate greater disease burden, more intensive treatment exposure, and heavier symptom load, all of which may contribute to functional decline in older women with breast cancer [42]. In addition, the association with occupation may partly reflect differences in lifelong socioeconomic position, work-related physical demands, health literacy, and access to supportive resources. Although this relationship has been less consistently examined than other geriatric factors, it may still provide useful contextual information for understanding heterogeneity in frailty risk and warrants further investigation.

Radiotherapy history was also associated with frailty in this study, but this finding should be interpreted with caution. Because all participants had completed active anticancer treatment and the present study was cross-sectional, the temporal direction of this association cannot be established. On the one hand, radiotherapy history may reflect greater cumulative treatment burden, more advanced disease, or a more complex treatment trajectory. On the other hand, baseline vulnerability and treatment-selection factors may also have contributed to this association, as frailer older patients may differ systematically in treatment tolerance, supportive care needs, and post-treatment recovery. Therefore, the observed relationship should not be interpreted as evidence that radiotherapy itself directly causes frailty. Rather, it is more appropriately understood as a marker of the broader interaction among disease burden, treatment exposure, and geriatric vulnerability. This cautious interpretation is consistent with previous work suggesting that frailty is closely related to treatment tolerance and treatment-related toxicity in older adults receiving radiotherapy [43]. From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of incorporating routine frailty assessment into the care of older women with breast cancer, not only to identify patients with increased vulnerability, but also to support more individualized follow-up, symptom monitoring, and supportive care planning after treatment. Further multicenter prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between treatment exposure and frailty, validate these associations in broader populations, and determine whether earlier frailty screening and targeted supportive care can improve treatment tolerance and functional outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single tertiary cancer center using convenience sampling, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, because of the cross-sectional design, causal relationships between frailty and associated factors cannot be established. Third, some variables, such as history of falls, sleep quality, and self-rated health status, were based on self-report and may therefore be subject to recall bias. Fourth, patients undergoing active anticancer treatment were excluded; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to that subgroup. Finally, frailty was defined using the Fried phenotype, which focuses primarily on physical frailty. Although cognitive, psychological, nutritional, and social dimensions were measured separately, they were not integrated into the frailty definition.

Conclusion

In conclusion, frailty was common among women aged 65 years and older with breast cancer in this study. Several demographic and clinical characteristics were associated with frailty. Routine frailty assessment may help identify patients who could benefit from more comprehensive supportive care. Further multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
