Integrating Azotobacter inoculation with nano-NPK foliar application: a sustainable strategy for enhancing growth, physiology, and sugar yield in sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.)
Authors: Heba S El-Desouky, Ghadah H Al Hawas, Arwa Abdulkreem AL-Huqail, Norah A Al-Enezi, Reda MY Zewail, Ghadah Khaled Yousuf, Sherin HA Al-Maracy, Amira E El-Sherief, Ibrahim S H El-Gamal, Nadia K El-Safy, Ali A Badawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSugar beet is a key crop for the sugar industry, increasingly reliant on eco-friendly inputs to meet rising global demand. Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) enhance plant growth via root associations, while nanotechnology offers solutions to limitations of traditional farming. However, while the individual benefits of PGPR and nano-fertilizers are well-documented, their combined application—specifically the integration of Azotobacter inoculation with foliar nano-NPK formulations—remains unexplored in sugar beet cultivation. Furthermore, no studies have investigated whether a multi-strain Azotobacter consortium (A. chroococcum + A. vinelandii) combined with complete nano-NPK can synergistically enhance sugar beet productivity beyond the effects of either treatment alone. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the synergistic effects of integrating Azotobacter inoculation with foliar nano-NPK application on growth, physiological performance, biochemical traits, and sugar yield in sugar beet, and to determine whether this combined approach outperforms individual or partial applications. To address this knowledge gap, a two-year field study using a split-plot design was conducted, with Azotobacter inoculation assigned to main plots and five nano-NPK formulations to subplots. Agronomic, physiological, and biochemical traits were evaluated at harvest. The combination of Azotobacter and complete nano-NPK (N + P+K) achieved superior results across all measured parameters. Notably, total chlorophyll content increased by approximately 122.8%, carotenoids by 82.6%, leaf area index by 95%, and sugar yield showed the most dramatic improvement, increasing by 94% (from 3.03 to 5.87 t ha⁻¹). Root yield also increased substantially by 65% (from 21.21 to 35.02 t ha⁻¹), accompanied by enhanced nutrient concentrations in leaves. This study is the first to demonstrate strong synergy between a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium and complete nano-NPK application, significantly improving nutrient use efficiency, physiological performance, and yield. The strategy provides an effective, sustainable approach with the potential to reduce dependence on conventional fertilizers in sugar beet production.Keywords: Sugar beet, PGPR, Nanomaterials, Agronomic traits, Physiological traits, Biochemical traits, Sugar yield

Introduction

Sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.) is a key crop of the Amaranthaceae family; it plays a major role in the international sugar market, coming second only to sugarcane. Globally, sugar beet contributes about 20–23% of total sugar output, while sugarcane supplies the majority share [1–3]. Beyond its primary function in sugar extraction, the crop’s by-products are repurposed as animal feed and serve as essential raw materials in the manufacture of a range of industrial, economic, and biochemical products. Lately, many nations have initiated policies that promote both vertical and horizontal growth in sugar beet cultivation and processing, aiming to better align production with rising consumption levels [4–7]. However, achieving this growth sustainably requires alternative strategies that reduce reliance on intensive chemical inputs while maintaining or improving productivity.

The integration of biofertilizers, especially plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), into agricultural practices represents a sustainable solution for improving crop yields and soil fertility. These beneficial microorganisms, encompassing diverse strains such as Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Bacillus, Azospirillum, Enterobacter, Acinetobacter, Alcaligenes, Erwinia, and Serratia, not only colonize plant roots and stimulate growth but also reduce the dependency on chemical fertilizers [8–13]. These helpful microbes flourish in the plant’s root zone, actively supporting growth through direct strategies like producing key phytohormones (auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins), solubilizing phosphorus, fixing atmospheric nitrogen, and releasing siderophores and indirect actions like inducing systemic resistance against soil-borne pathogens [14, 15]. They also activate crucial enzymes, including ACC deaminase, chitinase, and glucanase, which reduce ethylene-induced stress and improve plant tolerance to adverse conditions like salinity, drought, flooding, and heavy metal toxicity, thereby promoting good seed germination, advanced root development, nutrient uptake, and overall biomass [16, 17]. Notably, within this group, the genus Azotobacter stands out as a Gram-negative, aerobic, free-living bacterium of the proteobacteria, renowned for its effective nitrogen fixation and ability to enhance plant stress tolerance by increasing levels of soluble sugars, proteins, and proline while boosting root and shoot growth [18, 19]. Additionally, Azotobacter synthesizes essential growth regulators, including auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, siderophores, indole-3-acetic acid, and exopolysaccharides, which together enhance nutrient availability, foster beneficial rhizospheric microbial communities, and suppress harmful pathogens [20, 21].

In sugar beet specifically, PGPR inoculation has demonstrated promising results. For instance, Azotobacter inoculation improved nitrogen uptake, root yield, and sugar content in field-grown sugar beet [22]. Similarly, Bacillus and Pseudomonas strains enhanced sugar beet growth and yield under saline conditions by improving antioxidant defense systems and nutrient acquisition [12, 23]. However, despite these positive reports, the full potential of PGPR in sugar beet remains underexplored, particularly regarding multi-strain consortia and their integration with advanced fertilization strategies.

In the same context and with the aim of sustainable agriculture development, nanotechnology, a rapidly expanding interdisciplinary science, involves the production and manipulation of materials at a nanoscale (1–100 nm), where the resulting nanoparticles exhibit remarkable chemical and physical characteristics, including a higher surface-to-volume ratio, unique size and shape, distinct crystal structure, enhanced thermal stability, charge properties, and zeta potential—making them significantly different from their bulk counterparts [24–28]. These attributes have spurred its adoption across diverse fields, including medicine, pharmacy, engineering, industry, paper preservation, and wastewater treatment. In agriculture, their role has become increasingly vital, as traditional methods struggle to keep pace with the demands of a fast-growing worldwide population, with nearly one-third of crop yields lost to poor soil quality, microbial attacks, contaminated water, pest infestations, and nutrient deficiencies [29–32]. Nanotechnology offers a promising avenue to overcome these challenges by introducing innovative nano formulations that enhance every stage of agricultural production—from improving seed germination, seed storage, and post-harvest quality to optimizing fertigation—thereby bolstering plant physiological and biochemical processes such as photosynthesis, antioxidant enzyme activity, chlorophyll quality, carbohydrate production, and stress response modulation via molecular and biochemical pathways [33, 34]. This integration not only addresses the limitations inherent in traditional farming practices but also underscores nanotechnology’s far-reaching potential in advancing sustainable agrotechnological systems without compromising food quality.

In sugar beet cultivation, nanotechnology has shown considerable promise. Foliar application of nano-fertilizers improved sugar beet growth, root yield, and sugar quality in sandy and calcareous soils [3, 35]. Nano-micronutrient combinations enhanced photosynthetic efficiency and sugar accumulation in sugar beet under stress conditions [2]. These findings suggest that nano-fertilizers can effectively supplement conventional nutrient management in sugar beet production, though optimal formulations and application strategies require further investigation.

Based on the established role of PGPR in enhancing the rhizosphere and the unique properties of nanomaterials in nutrient delivery, the current study hypothesizes that the integration of soil amendments with nitrogen-fixing bacteria and foliar fertilization with nano-NPK would produce a synergistic effect, significantly improving sugar beet yields beyond the effects of either one alone. The mechanism of this hypothesis includes two aspects: (1) The Azotobacter strains in the rhizosphere would improve root structure and function, enhance the mobilization and utilization of soil nutrients (especially nitrogen and phosphorus), and activate the plant stress response system through the production of plant hormones [23, 36, 37]. (2) Foliar application of nano-NPK fertilizers, due to their large surface area and high bioavailability, can directly provide effective supplementary nutrients to the leaves, breaking soil limitations and compensating for the temporary nutrient requirements of crops during critical growth stages [35, 38]. This synergy between rhizosphere and phyllosphere will improve nutrient use efficiency (NUE), photosynthetic capacity, and resilience, ultimately translating into increased root biomass and sugar production while reducing reliance on conventional fertilizers [39, 40].

The novelty of this study lies in addressing several critical gaps in the current literature. First, while numerous studies have examined the individual effects of Azotobacter inoculation or nano-fertilizer application on various crops, no previous research has investigated the combined application of a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium (A. chroococcum and A. vinelandii) with complete nano-NPK (N + P + K) in sugar beet cultivation. Second, most existing studies on PGPR in sugar beet have focused on single bacterial strains or partial nutrient formulations [12, 22], whereas our study employs a consortium approach that may offer greater functional diversity and synergistic benefits. Third, while nano-fertilizers have been applied to sugar beet [3, 35], their integration with biological inputs remains unexplored, representing a significant knowledge gap in sustainable sugar beet production. Fourth, the specific synergistic mechanisms—particularly the interaction between rhizosphere microbial activity and phyllosphere nutrient delivery—have not been characterized in sugar beet, limiting our understanding of how these interventions jointly enhance physiological performance and yield.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the synergistic effects of integrating a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium (A. chroococcum + A. vinelandii) as a soil amendment with foliar application of complete nano-NPK on growth, physiological performance, biochemical traits, and sugar yield in sugar beet. Specifically, we tested whether this combined approach outperforms individual or partial applications, thereby providing a sustainable, environmentally friendly alternative that may reduce the use of conventional chemical fertilizers in sugar beet production.

Materials and methods

Experimental layout

During the winter seasons of 2022–2023 and 2023–2024, the effects of soil-applied Azotobacter and foliar nano-NPK fertilizer on sugar beet (Beta vulgaris var. saccharifera “BTS SMART 8565”) were investigated at the Sakha Agricultural Research Station (31°10′ N, 30°33′ E; 14 m above sea level), Kafr El-Sheikh, Egypt. A split-plot design with three replications assigned Azotobacter inoculation (either absent or a consortium of A. chroococcum + A. vinelandii at 1.5 × 105 cells mL⁻¹) to main plots and five foliar nano-NPK treatments (control; N + P; N + K; P + K; N + P+K at 100 mg L⁻¹) to subplots. Azotobacter was applied at sowing and at the first two irrigations [19, 41]. Foliar nano-NPK (100 mg L⁻¹) was applied three times at the following sugar beet growth stages according to the BBCH scale [42]: first application at BBCH 16–18 (6–8 true leaves, rosette closure, approximately 40 days after sowing), second application at BBCH 41–43 (beginning of storage root thickening, approximately 60 days after sowing), and third application at BBCH 46–49 (rapid root growth and sugar accumulation, approximately 80 days after sowing). Each 20 m² subplot consisted of eight 5 m × 0.5 m ridges with 0.2 m between hills. Planting occurred in mid-September, with harvest 210 days later. Climatic data was clarified in Table 1. At the four-leaf stage, plots were thinned to one plant per hill. Basal fertilization included 200 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ as calcium superphosphate at soil preparation, 190 kg N ha⁻¹ as ammonium nitrate split between post-thinning and one month later, and 119 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ as potassium sulfate split between the first N application and canopy closure.

Treatments source and preparations

Azotobacter inoculation: The azotobacter inoculum (consisting of A. chroococcum and A. vinelandii) was obtained from the Soil and Water Research Institute of the Egyptian Agricultural Research Center. Locally effective strains were isolated from sugar beet rhizosphere samples, purified, and cultured in Ashby’s medium at 29 °C for 5 days to produce dense cell suspensions. 10 ml (1.5 × 105 cells mL⁻¹) were inoculated into the rhizosphere of each plant [22]. All other field operations followed the standard agricultural practices of the Egyptian Ministry of Agriculture.

Growth measurements

At 120 days of plant age, three random samples from each treatment were collected to assess various growth parameters. These included the number of leaves per plant, leaf fresh weight (g/plant), and root fresh weight (g/plant). Additionally, leaf area index (LAI) was calculated using the formula: (leaf area per plant) / (soil area per plant), following the method of [45]. Specific leaf area (SLA) was determined as the ratio of leaf area (LA) to leaf weight (Lw) in cm²/g, while specific leaf weight (SLW) was calculated as the inverse, Lw/LA in g/cm², both according to [46]. Root morphological traits such as root length (cm) and root diameter (cm) were also measured.

Chemical analysis determination

At 120 days of plant age, various biochemical constituents in sugar beet leaves and roots were analyzed using standardized methods. Total chlorophylls and carotenoids were quantified colorimetrically following the procedure of [47], with results expressed as mg/g of fresh weight. Total nitrogen content in both leaves and roots was determined via wet digestion based on [48], and analyzed using the micro-Kjeldahl method as described by [49]. Phosphorus concentrations were assessed colorimetrically according to [50] and reported in mg/g of dry weight, while potassium levels (meq/100 g dry weight) were measured using a flame photometer following the method of [51]. Crude protein content (mg/g) was calculated using the formula: total nitrogen × 6.25, as recommended by [44]. Total carbohydrate content was determined according to [52] and expressed in mg/g of dry weight. Total sugars were measured colorimetrically using the picric acid method described by [53], and recorded as mg/g of fresh dry weight. Finally, total phenolic compounds were quantified using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Jenway, England) at 750 nm, following the protocol of [54].

Yield traits

At harvest, which occurred 210 days after sowing, the three guarded rows from each subplot were collected, cleaned, topped, and weighed to assess yield and quality traits across both seasons. Measurements included root length (cm), root diameter (cm), and number of leaves per plant. Root yield (ton/hectare) was determined by weighing the harvested material from the experimental plot. Sugar yield (ton/hectare) was then calculated using the formula proposed by [55]: Sugar yield = Root yield (ton/hectare) × Extractable sugar percentage.

Quality traits

To evaluate sugar quality traits in sugar beet, several parameters were measured using established methodologies. The sucrose percentage was determined according to the method of [56], while the extractable white sugar percentage (EXT%) was calculated following the approach outlined by [57]. Sugar loss to molasses percentage (SLM%) was assessed using the procedure described by [58]. Total soluble solids (TSS%) in fresh root juice were measured using a hand refractometer. Juice purity (%) was calculated using the formula proposed by [58]: (Sucrose / Total soluble solids) × 100.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) appropriate for a split-plot design was performed using CROPSTAT® 2007.2 software to evaluate the effects of Azotobacter inoculation and nano-NPK fertilization on the dependent variables of sugar beet growth. In this design, Azotobacter inoculation (with/without) was assigned to main plots, while nano-NPK treatments (five levels) were assigned to subplots. Both factors were treated as fixed variables. Main plot error was used to test the significance of Azotobacter inoculation effects, while subplot error was used to test nano-NPK treatment effects and their interaction with Azotobacter inoculation. When ANOVA indicated significant treatment effects, means were separated using the F-protected Duncan Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. For key parameters, the F-statistics and p-values for the Azotobacter × nano-NPK interaction are reported in the Results section to provide a formal test of synergy between the two factors. All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) of three replications.

Results

Leaf fresh weight, root fresh weight, and leaf area index

Across the 2022/2023 and 2023/2024 seasons, the combination of Azotobacter inoculation and complete nano-NPK (N + P + K) consistently produced the highest biomass and leaf area index, demonstrating strong synergy between soil and foliar interventions (Table 3). Untreated control plants averaged 297 g leaf fresh weight, 857 g root fresh weight, and a leaf area index (LAI) of 1.6. The ANOVA revealed a highly significant Azotobacter × nano-NPK interaction for leaf fresh weight (F₄,₄₀ = 8.72, p < 0.001), root fresh weight (F₄,₄₀ = 6.54, p < 0.001), and LAI (F₄,₄₀ = 7.13, p < 0.001) in both seasons, confirming that the effect of nano-NPK was substantially enhanced by Azotobacter inoculation. The integrated treatment elevated these values to approximately 540 g (+ 82%), 1266 g (+ 48%), and 3.1 (+ 95%), respectively. What makes these findings particularly robust is the near-identical performance across two climatically distinct seasons. In the first year, leaf fresh weight reached 539.7 g (+ 81.7%), root fresh weight 1264 g (+ 48.2%), and LAI 3.08 (+ 96.2%). The second year yielded 540.7 g (+ 82%), 1268 g (+ 47.1%), and 3.16 (+ 95.1%). This reproducibility confirms that the treatment effect is stable and not merely a seasonal anomaly. Partial nano-formulations (N + P, N + K, P + K) produced intermediate gains, while Azotobacter alone or nano-NPK alone gave significantly smaller increases. The synergistic effect is most evident for LAI, where the combined treatment’s 95% gain far exceeds the sum of individual treatments (approximately 11% for Azotobacter alone and 49% for nano-NPK alone).

Specific leaf area, specific leaf weight, and crop growth rate leaves

Across both growing seasons, the combination of rhizobacteria and complete nano-NPK (N + P + K) consistently enhanced specific leaf area (SLA), specific leaf weight (SLW), and crop growth rate (CGR) of sugar beet (Table 4). The Azotobacter × nano-NPK interaction was significant for SLA (F₄,₄₀ = 5.92, p < 0.01), SLW (F₄,₄₀ = 4.38, p < 0.05), and CGR (F₄,₄₀ = 6.21, p < 0.01), indicating that the combined treatment produced greater improvements in leaf efficiency and growth rate than would be expected from the additive effects of the individual treatments. Specific leaf area (SLA)—an indicator of light-capture efficiency—increased from a control average of 1.63 to 2.65 under the full Azotobacter + N + P + K regimen, a relative gain of approximately 62%. This shift toward thinner, more expansive leaves enhances photosynthetic surface area per unit leaf mass. Conversely, specific leaf weight (SLW), which reflects dry matter investment per leaf area, rose from 0.47 to 0.62 (+ 32%). The simultaneous increase in both SLA and SLW is noteworthy: it indicates that treated plants produce leaves that are simultaneously more efficient at intercepting light and better fortified with structural biomass. Crop growth rate (CGR) nearly doubled, climbing from 1.77 to 3.01 g m⁻² day⁻¹ (+ 70%). This dramatic acceleration of biomass production stems from the combined effects of improved nutrition (nano-NPK) and enhanced root function (rhizobacteria). Seasonal consistency was high: first-year SLA rose 64% (to 2.64), SLW 32% (to 0.62), CGR 71% (to 3.00); second-year values were nearly identical. Partial nano-formulations and single applications yielded inferior results, confirming that the complete combination is essential for optimizing leaf efficiency and growth velocity in sugar beet.

Number of leaves, root diameter and root length

The integrated rhizobacteria and nano-NPK treatment dramatically altered root architecture and canopy size, though seasonal responses differed markedly (Table 6). Under the full Azotobacter + N + P + K regimen, root length in the first season surged from 10.3 cm to 34.0 cm—a staggering 229% increase—while in the second season, the gain was a more modest 59% (from 12.2 cm to 19.3 cm). This discrepancy likely reflects differences in soil conditions, temperature, or precipitation between the two years, yet in both cases the combined treatment outperformed all other formulations. Root diameter followed a similar pattern but with less extreme seasonal variation: first-season diameter expanded by 143% (from 7.0 cm to 17.0 cm), while second-season diameter increased by 85% (from 6.9 cm to 12.9 cm). The consistently thicker roots under the integrated treatment suggest improved structural integrity and greater storage tissue development. Leaf number per plant, an indicator of photosynthetic capacity, rose from 18.0 to 29.3 (+ 63%) in the first season and from 17.0 to 29.3 (+ 72%) in the second. Partial nano-formulations and single applications (rhizobacteria or nano-NPK alone) produced intermediate or inferior results.

Total chlorophylls, carotenoids, and total phenolics

The combination of rhizobacteria and complete nano-NPK (N + P + K) significantly enhanced photosynthetic pigments and phenolic compounds in sugar beet across both seasons (Table 6). Compared with untreated controls, the integrated treatment increased total chlorophyll by ~ 125% (from ~ 1.9 to 4.3 mg g⁻¹ f.w.) and carotenoids by ~ 85% (from ~ 0.46 to 0.84 mg g⁻¹ f.w.). Notably, rhizobacteria alone raised chlorophyll by 64% and carotenoids by 48%, while nano-NPK alone gave lower increases—demonstrating clear synergy. Total phenolics also rose substantially, from ~ 150 to 219 mg g⁻¹ (+ 46%). The interaction term was significant for total chlorophyll (F₄,₄₀ = 7.85, p < 0.001), carotenoids (F₄,₄₀ = 5.43, p < 0.01), and total phenolics (F₄,₄₀ = 6.72, p < 0.001), confirming that Azotobacter inoculation amplified the positive effects of nano-NPK on photosynthetic pigment accumulation and secondary metabolism. The P + K nano-formulation combined with rhizobacteria produced the second-highest phenolic value (200.5 mg g⁻¹), suggesting a specific role for potassium and phosphorus in secondary metabolism. Seasonal variation was modest: first-season chlorophyll increased 123% (to 4.39), second-season 128% (to 4.23); carotenoids rose 96% (to 0.86) and 75% (to 0.82), respectively; phenolics increased 60% (to 218) and 35% (to 220).

Root yield, sugar yield, and sugar lost to molasses

Root and sugar yields responded strongly to the integrated rhizobacteria and nano-NPK treatment, though with some trade-offs in processing quality (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Across both seasons, the combination of Azotobacter with complete N + P + K nano-fertilizer increased root yield by 38–65% relative to untreated controls, rising from ~ 22.3 t ha⁻¹ to 32.4–35.0 t ha⁻¹. Sugar yield, the key economic output, improved even more substantially: first-season gains reached 94% (3.03 to 5.87 t ha⁻¹), while second-season gains were 75% (2.84 to 4.96 t ha⁻¹). The Azotobacter × nano-NPK interaction was highly significant for both root yield (F₄,₄₀ = 9.36, p < 0.001) and sugar yield (F₄,₄₀ = 11.24, p < 0.001), providing formal statistical support for the synergy claimed throughout this study. The interaction was particularly strong for sugar yield, reflecting the combined effect of increased root biomass and improved sugar concentration. These represent the most striking treatment effects observed in this study. However, sugar loss to molasses (SLM)—an undesirable quality parameter—also increased by 29–40%, from ~ 1.11 to 1.47–1.52. This indicates that the vigorous growth promoted by the treatment led to slightly higher non-sucrose impurities. Nevertheless, the dramatic 75–94% rise in sugar yield far outweighs this modest quality reduction. Partial nano-formulations and single applications (rhizobacteria or nano-NPK alone) produced inferior results. Seasonal variability was present but did not diminish the clear superiority of the combined treatment.

Sucrose (%), extractable (%), and purity (%)

Beyond yield gains, the integrated rhizobacteria and nano-NPK treatment substantially elevated key quality indicators of sugar beet (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). The interaction term was significant for sucrose% (F₄,₄₀ = 5.87, p < 0.01), extractable sugar% (F₄,₄₀ = 6.43, p < 0.01), and purity% (F₄,₄₀ = 4.92, p < 0.05), confirming that the quality improvements were not merely additive but resulted from a true synergistic effect of the combined treatment. Sucrose concentration—the primary determinant of commercial value—increased from a control baseline of 14.6–15.2% to 16.8–19.8% under the full Azotobacter + N + P + K regimen. The relative improvement was more pronounced in the second season (+ 30.6%) than in the first (+ 15.2%), suggesting that treatment benefits may amplify under favorable growing conditions. Extractable sugar, which reflects the proportion recoverable during industrial processing, rose from 12.9 to 13.4% to 17.5–17.7%, corresponding to gains of 35.3% (season one) and 32.1% (season two). These substantial increases indicate that the integrated treatment not only boosts total sugar accumulation but also enhances its processability. Purity, defined as the ratio of sucrose to total soluble solids, improved from 81.8% to 86.9% (+ 6.2%) in the first season and from 75.9% to 82.9% (+ 9.3%) in the second. The lower control purity in season two highlights environmental stress, yet the treatment still delivered a larger relative gain, demonstrating its resilience-enhancing effect. Partial nano-formulations and individual applications of rhizobacteria or nano-NPK alone produced smaller or inconsistent improvements, confirming that the complete N + P+K combination with Azotobacter is necessary to achieve optimal sugar quality.

Discussion

The present study provides the first evidence that integrating a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium (A. chroococcum and A. vinelandii) with complete foliar nano-NPK application produces synergistic effects on sugar beet growth, physiology, and yield that substantially exceed the sum of individual treatments. This synergy was most pronounced for sugar yield (+ 94%), root yield (+ 65%), and leaf area index (+ 95%), with highly significant Azotobacter × nano-NPK interactions across key parameters (F₄,₄₀ = 8.72–11.24, p < 0.001). These findings establish the combined approach as a transformative strategy for sustainable sugar beet production.

Enhanced physiological performance and photosynthetic capacity

The dramatic increases in total chlorophyll (+ 123%) and carotenoids (+ 83%) under the integrated treatment reflect a fundamental improvement in photosynthetic capacity. This enhancement likely arises from multiple complementary mechanisms. First, nano-NPK provides highly bioavailable magnesium (the central atom in chlorophyll) and nitrogen (essential for Rubisco and chlorophyll biosynthesis), overcoming soil limitations that often constrain pigment synthesis [3, 37]. Second, Azotobacter inoculation improves root architecture and nutrient uptake [25, 35], ensuring sustained delivery of these essential elements. Third, PGPR-produced cytokinins delay leaf senescence [15, 25], maintaining photosynthetic activity throughout the growing season. The magnitude of chlorophyll increase in our study (from 1.9 to 4.3 mg g⁻¹ f.w.) substantially exceeds the 20–40% increases typically reported for PGPR or nano-fertilizer alone [2, 12, 59], confirming true synergy rather than simple additivity.

The concurrent rise in carotenoids is particularly significant, as these pigments not only expand the light-harvesting spectrum but also serve as photoprotective antioxidants under high light intensity [60, 61]. The 83% increase in carotenoids, combined with the 46% increase in total phenolics, suggests that treated plants developed enhanced antioxidant capacity. This is consistent with the elevated carbohydrate and protein content we observed, indicating that improved photosynthetic efficiency translated into greater production of carbon skeletons and nitrogen-containing compounds [62, 63]. Interestingly, the integrated treatment produced greater pigment enhancement in the first season (123% vs. 128% for chlorophyll), suggesting that moderate temperatures during the root thickening period (7.7–10.3 °C in season one vs. 9.7–11.5 °C in season two) may have facilitated more efficient pigment biosynthesis.

Mechanisms of enhanced growth and biomass accumulation

The 95% increase in leaf area index (LAI) and 70% increase in crop growth rate (CGR) under the integrated treatment indicate that the combination fundamentally altered canopy development and biomass production dynamics. LAI increased from 1.6 to 3.1, representing a near-doubling of photosynthetic surface area per unit ground area. This improvement in canopy architecture is particularly important for sugar beet, where leaf area directly determines the light interception capacity and, consequently, the carbohydrate supply for root filling [45]. The simultaneous increase in both specific leaf area (SLA, + 62%) and specific leaf weight (SLW, + 32%) is noteworthy; it indicates that treated plants produced leaves that are simultaneously more efficient at intercepting light (higher SLA) and better fortified with structural biomass (higher SLW).

The enhancement in leaf area and biomass likely stems from the combined effects of improved nutrient availability and phytohormone-mediated growth stimulation. Nano-NPK provides readily accessible nutrients that support meristematic activity and cell expansion [64], while Azotobacter produces indole-3-acetic acid, gibberellins, and cytokinins that promote cell division and elongation [20, 21]. The bacterial production of ACC deaminase may also have reduced ethylene-induced growth inhibition [16, 17]. Notably, the integrated treatment produced substantially greater root length and diameter increases than partial treatments, indicating that the combined approach enhanced both vertical and radial root development. This improved root architecture likely facilitated greater water and nutrient uptake, creating a positive feedback loop that sustained aboveground growth.

The seasonal variation in root length response (229% increase in season one vs. 59% in season two) warrants consideration. The first season’s cooler minimum temperatures (7.7–10.3 °C vs. 9.7–11.5 °C) and higher humidity (65.8–66.2% vs. 66.4–65.7%) during the critical root thickening period may have promoted more extensive root elongation [65, 66]. However, the fact that the integrated treatment outperformed all other treatments in both seasons demonstrates that the synergy is robust across environmental conditions.

Biochemical composition and nutrient dynamics

The integrated treatment increased leaf nitrogen content by 69%, phosphorus by 84%, and potassium by 75%, with corresponding improvements in root nutrient concentrations. These nutrient enhancements explain the observed increases in protein (+ 66%), carbohydrates (+ 86%), and total sugars (+ 56%). The greater magnitude of nutrient increase in leaves compared to roots suggests that, under improved nutrition, plants preferentially allocate essential elements to photosynthetic tissues to maximize carbon assimilation [25, 67].

The mechanism underlying improved nutrient uptake involves both direct and indirect pathways. Azotobacter directly contributes to nitrogen availability through biological nitrogen fixation [18, 19] and enhances phosphorus solubilization through organic acid production [20]. Additionally, PGPR-induced changes in root exudation patterns modify the rhizosphere environment, increasing the bioavailability of micronutrients [59, 65]. Nano-NPK, applied foliarly, bypasses soil limitations and provides a direct supplementary nutrient supply during critical growth stages [37, 39]. The high surface-area-to-volume ratio of nanoparticles ensures rapid nutrient absorption and efficient utilization [23, 26].

The 46% increase in total phenolics under the integrated treatment is particularly relevant for stress tolerance. Phenolic compounds serve as antioxidants and precursors for lignin biosynthesis, enhancing plant defense against pathogens and environmental stress [53]. The elevated phenolics likely reflect the combined effects of: (1) increased shikimic acid pathway activity due to improved carbon availability, and (2) PGPR-induced systemic resistance [14, 15]. This enhanced antioxidant capacity may explain why treated plants maintained higher photosynthetic rates and growth despite seasonal environmental fluctuations.

Yield enhancement and quality implications

The 65% increase in root yield (from 21.2 to 35.0 t ha⁻¹) and 94% increase in sugar yield (from 3.0 to 5.9 t ha⁻¹) represent the most economically significant findings of this study. These gains substantially exceed those reported for PGPR alone (typically 10–25% yield increases [24, 25]) or nano-fertilizer alone (20–40% [3, 37]), confirming the synergistic nature of the combined approach. The yield enhancement can be explained by three complementary mechanisms:

Increased photosynthetic capacity and duration: The elevated chlorophyll content and delayed senescence under the integrated treatment extended the period of active carbohydrate production [25, 67]. The 70% increase in CGR indicates that this enhanced photosynthesis translated directly into faster biomass accumulation.

The modest increases in sugar loss to molasses (SLM, + 29–40%) and sucrose purity (+ 6–9%) deserve attention. The elevated SLM likely reflects increased nitrogen content in roots, which can lead to higher amino acid and betaine concentrations that interfere with sugar crystallization [57]. However, the dramatic increase in total sugar yield far outweighs this minor quality reduction. Notably, the extractable sucrose percentage increased by 32–35%, indicating that the additional sugar is commercially recoverable despite the slight purity reduction.

Seasonal variation in treatment responses

Several traits exhibited marked differences between the two growing seasons, with the first season (2022/2023) showing substantially larger improvements for root length (229% vs. 59%) and leaf area index, while the second season (2023/2024) showed larger gains for leaf nitrogen content (125% vs. 25%). These disparities can be attributed to differences in meteorological conditions recorded at the experimental site (Table 1). The first season was characterized by cooler minimum temperatures during the critical root thickening period (January–March: 7.7–10.3 °C vs. 9.7–11.5 °C in the second season) and higher relative humidity (65.8–66.2% vs. 66.4–65.7%), which likely promoted more vigorous early root elongation and reduced moisture stress [66, 68]. Conversely, the second season experienced higher maximum temperatures during early growth (November: 24.3 °C vs. 20.8 °C) and slightly higher solar radiation (December–February: 129–139 W m⁻² vs. 99–144 W m⁻²), conditions that may have enhanced nitrogen assimilation and protein synthesis through improved photosynthetic activity [64, 69]. These environmental differences probably also influenced PGPR activity, as Azotobacter species are known to be sensitive to soil temperature and moisture, with optimal activity typically occurring at moderate temperatures (20–30 °C) and adequate soil moisture [18, 42]. The consistency of the overall treatment hierarchy (Azotobacter + N + P + K > other treatments) across both seasons, despite these environmental fluctuations, reinforces the robustness of the observed synergy and suggests that the combined approach provides resilience against seasonal variability.

Comparison with previous studies and novel contributions

While numerous studies have examined PGPR or nano-fertilizers individually [3, 12, 24, 25, 37], our study is the first to combine a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium with complete nano-NPK in sugar beet. The yield gains observed (65–94%) substantially exceed those reported for either approach alone. For example, Mrkovacki et al. [22] reported 5–10% sugar yield increases with Azotobacter alone, while Abdel-Aziz et al. [35] observed 15–20% increases with nano-chitosan NPK. The integrated approach in our study achieved nearly double the sugar yield compared to these individual applications. The superior performance of the complete N + P+K formulation over partial formulations (N + P, N + K, P + K) underscores the importance of balanced nutrition. While all nano-NPK treatments improved growth, the complete formulation consistently produced the best results, indicating that the synergistic benefits of the three nutrients are essential for optimal performance. This finding contrasts with some studies that reported sufficient results with partial nutrient supplementation [39, 70], highlighting the specific nutritional demands of sugar beet for sugar accumulation.

Conclusion

In summary, combining a dual-strain Azotobacter consortium (A. chroococcum and A. vinelandii) with complete foliar nano-NPK application substantially increased sugar beet productivity compared with single or partial treatments. The integrated approach improved biochemical (carbohydrate, protein, sugar), physiological (leaf area index, chlorophyll, carotenoids), and agronomic traits (root morphology, growth rate), resulting in 65.1% and 94% increases in root and sugar yields, respectively, while maintaining or improving quality parameters (sucrose, extractable sugar, purity). Enhanced leaf area and chlorophyll suggest improved photosynthetic capacity, likely contributing to increased carbohydrate production and sugar accumulation in storage roots. Elevated leaf and root N, P, and K concentrations are consistent with improved nutrient uptake, potentially mediated by Azotobacter-enhanced soil nutrient mobilization and nano-NPK bioavailability. Significant Azotobacter × nano-NPK interactions provide statistical evidence for synergy, wherein the combined treatment outperformed additive effects. However, direct evidence for specific mechanisms—phytohormone production, nitrogen fixation rates, or molecular changes—was not evaluated and requires further investigation. The nano-biofertilizer strategy represents a promising, resource-efficient alternative to conventional fertilizers for sustainable sugar beet production. Future studies with reduced conventional fertilizer rates are needed to quantify potential savings, while mechanistic research would confirm the pathways underlying this enhanced performance.
