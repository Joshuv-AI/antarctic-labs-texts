Mask-Based Window-Level Insider Threat Detection for Campaign Discovery
Jericho Cain*, Hayden Beadles

Abstract
User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) systems commonly detect insider threats by scoring fixed time windows of user activity for anomalous behavior. While this window-level paradigm has proven effective for identifying sharp behavioral deviations, it remains unclear how much information about longer-running attack campaigns is already present within individual windows, and how such information can be leveraged for campaign discovery. In this work, we study unsupervised window-level insider threat detection on the CERT r4.2 dataset and show that explicitly separating activity presence from activity magnitude yields substantial performance gains. We introduce a dual-channel convolutional autoencoder that reconstructs both a binary activity mask and corresponding activity values, allowing the model to focus representational capacity on sparse behavioral structure rather than dense inactive baselines. Across multi-day attack campaigns lasting between one and seven days, the proposed approach achieves a window-level precision–recall AUC of 0.71, substantially exceeding standard unsupervised autoencoder baselines and enabling high-precision operating points with zero false alarms. Building on these window-level scores, we further evaluate campaign-level detection by aggregating anomaly evidence across multi-day sequences of windows. Empirically, insider attack campaigns in CERT are highly temporally sparse, with malicious behavior concentrated in a small number of anomalous days. As a result, simple aggregation strategies that emphasize extreme evidence, such as top- k k pooling, yield strong campaign-level detection performance across multiple attack scenarios, achieving a precision–recall AUC of up to 0.84. These results demonstrate that effective campaign discovery can be achieved without explicit temporal or sequence modeling, provided that window-level representations align with the sparsity structure of enterprise audit data. Overall, our findings show that explicitly modeling activity presence is critical for effective unsupervised insider threat detection and that high-quality window-level anomaly scores can be directly leveraged for reliable campaign discovery through principled aggregation.

While window-level detection is effective for identifying sharp behavioral spikes, such as sudden bursts of data exfiltration or clearly anomalous sessions, it is less clear how well this paradigm supports detection of longer-running attack campaigns. In many realistic insider scenarios, no single window appears strongly malicious in isolation. Instead, malicious behavior manifests as a small number of anomalous events embedded within otherwise normal activity over time

Together, these results suggest a practical path toward insider threat detection systems that combine high-precision window-level alerts with effective campaign discovery over longer time horizons.

II
Problem Formulation

We consider the problem of unsupervised insider threat detection from enterprise audit logs. Let
\mathcal{U}
denote a set of users, and let
\mathcal{E}_{u}=\{(e_{i},t_{i})\}
denote the sequence of raw audit events generated by user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, where each event
e_{i}
has a timestamp
t_{i}
and belongs to one of several behavioral modalities such as logon activity, file access, device usage, email, or web activity.

II-A

Window Construction

To obtain a regular temporal representation, we aggregate raw events into fixed-length time windows. Specifically, we partition time into windows of duration
W
hours, each subdivided into
T
buckets of width
\Delta t=W/T
. For each user
u
and window
w
, we construct a feature matrix

\mathbf{X}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F},

where
F
is the number of behavioral features and each entry
\mathbf{X}_{u,w}(t,f)
represents the count or summary statistic of feature
f
observed in bucket
t
of window
w
. Buckets with no activity are explicitly filled with zeros, resulting in highly sparse representations.

Each window is treated as a single observation for window-level detection. Importantly, the aggregation discards fine-grained ordering of events within each bucket, preserving only coarse temporal structure at resolution
\Delta t
.

II-B

Window-Level Anomaly Detection

In the unsupervised window-level detection setting, the goal is to learn a scoring function

s_{w}:\mathbf{X}_{u,w}\mapsto\mathbb{R},

such that windows associated with malicious behavior receive higher anomaly scores than normal windows. The model is trained exclusively on windows assumed to be normal, without access to attack labels. Performance is evaluated by comparing anomaly scores to ground-truth labels on held-out test windows using metrics such as precision–recall AUC.

This formulation reflects the dominant evaluation paradigm in prior UEBA work, where each window is scored independently and user-level decisions, if required, are derived through simple aggregation rules.

II-C

Mask–Value Decomposition

Enterprise audit data is characterized by extreme sparsity, with the majority of window entries corresponding to inactivity. To explicitly model this structure, we decompose each window
\mathbf{X}_{u,w}
into two components:

\mathbf{M}_{u,w}\in\{0,1\}^{T\times F},\quad\mathbf{V}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F},

where
\mathbf{M}_{u,w}(t,f)=\mathbb{I}[\mathbf{X}_{u,w}(t,f)>0]
indicates the presence of activity, and
\mathbf{V}_{u,w}(t,f)=\mathbf{X}_{u,w}(t,f)
encodes the corresponding magnitude. This decomposition separates the problem of detecting when activity occurs from the problem of modeling how much activity occurs, allowing models to focus on sparse behavioral structure rather than dense inactive baselines.

II-D

Campaign-Level Aggregation

Beyond independent window scoring, we consider the problem of detecting extended insider attack campaigns that unfold across multiple time windows. For each user
u
, we obtain a temporally ordered sequence of window-level anomaly scores

s_{u,1},s_{u,2},\ldots,s_{u,N_{u}},

where
s_{u,i}
denotes the anomaly score assigned to the
i
-th window for user
u
.

A campaign is defined as a contiguous time interval during which malicious activity occurs. Rather than assuming that malicious behavior is sustained across all windows in such an interval, we explicitly allow campaigns to be temporally sparse, consisting of a small number of highly anomalous windows embedded within otherwise normal behavior.

To evaluate campaign-level detection, we extract fixed-length sequences of consecutive windows and assign each sequence a label based on its overlap with known attack periods. A sequence is labeled as malicious if it overlaps with at least one malicious window. While this labeling scheme may dilute malicious signal, it reflects the realistic ambiguity inherent in campaign boundaries and allows us to study aggregation behavior under conservative assumptions.

Given a sequence of window-level scores, campaign-level detection is performed by applying an aggregation function that maps the set of scores within the sequence to a single scalar. In this work, we focus on simple aggregation strategies, such as summation and top-
k
selection, to assess whether window-level anomaly scores alone are sufficient to support effective campaign discovery.

III
Model Architecture and Training Objective

This section describes the dual-channel autoencoder architecture used for window-level insider threat detection and the associated training objectives. The design explicitly reflects the sparsity structure of enterprise audit data and the mask–value decomposition introduced in Section 2.

III-A

Input Representation

Each window
\mathbf{X}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
is decomposed into a binary activity mask
\mathbf{M}_{u,w}
and a corresponding value matrix
\mathbf{V}_{u,w}
. These two components are stacked along the channel dimension to form a two-channel input tensor

\mathbf{X}^{(2)}_{u,w}=\left[\mathbf{M}_{u,w},\;\mathbf{V}_{u,w}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times T\times F}.

The value channel
\mathbf{V}_{u,w}
is standardized using z-score normalization with statistics computed from the training data, while the mask channel remains binary. This representation allows the model to reason separately about the presence of activity and its magnitude.

III-B

Autoencoder Architecture

Figure
1
summarizes the dual-channel architecture and the three training loss components.

We employ a convolutional autoencoder to encode each input window into a fixed-dimensional latent representation. The encoder consists of three convolutional blocks operating over the temporal and feature dimensions, followed by a fully connected projection to a latent space of dimension
d
:

\mathbf{z}_{u,w}=f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{X}^{(2)}_{u,w}),\quad\mathbf{z}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Convolutions are applied with shared weights across users and windows, enabling the model to learn local temporal and cross-feature patterns while remaining agnostic to user identity.

The decoder mirrors the encoder architecture and reconstructs both channels from the latent representation:

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{u,w},\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{u,w}=f_{\text{dec}}(\mathbf{z}_{u,w}),

where
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
represents logits for the reconstructed mask, and
\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
represents reconstructed activity magnitudes. No activation function is applied to the value output, while the mask output is interpreted through a sigmoid during loss computation.

III-C

Training Loss

The model is trained exclusively on windows assumed to be normal. The total training loss combines three components:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{mask}}+\lambda_{\text{value}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}}+\lambda_{\text{temp}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}.

Mask Reconstruction Loss.

The mask loss encourages accurate reconstruction of activity presence and is defined as a weighted binary cross-entropy:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{mask}}=\mathrm{BCEWithLogits}\left(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{u,w},\mathbf{M}_{u,w};\;\text{pos\_weight}\right),

where the positive class weight compensates for the heavy imbalance between inactive and active entries. This term dominates the total loss and is the primary driver of detection performance.

Value Reconstruction Loss.

The value loss penalizes reconstruction error only where activity is present:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(t,f)\in\Omega}\left(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{u,w}(t,f)-\mathbf{V}_{u,w}(t,f)\right)^{2},

where
\Omega=\{(t,f):\mathbf{M}_{u,w}(t,f)=1\}
. This conditional loss prevents the model from wasting capacity on reconstructing inactive baselines. The weight
\lambda_{\text{value}}
is chosen to be small to avoid introducing noise into the latent representation.

Temporal Consistency Loss.

To encourage smooth latent trajectories, we optionally apply a temporal consistency regularizer during training. For consecutive normal windows
(w,w+1)
belonging to the same user, we define:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{z}_{u,w+1}-\mathbf{z}_{u,w}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

This term penalizes large latent jumps between adjacent windows and is applied only to normal training data. The regularization strength
\lambda_{\text{temp}}
controls the tradeoff between temporal smoothness and representational flexibility.

III-D

Anomaly Scoring

At test time, each window is assigned an anomaly score based on reconstruction error. For mask-only detection, the score is given by the mask reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{mask}}
. For combined detection, mask and value losses are linearly combined using fixed weights.

For campaign-level analysis, window-level anomaly scores are aggregated across consecutive windows using simple pooling functions. Importantly, neither the autoencoder nor the latent space is optimized directly for campaign detection. This separation ensures that window-level detection remains the primary modeling objective, while campaign discovery is achieved through post hoc aggregation of window-level evidence.
.

IV
Experimental Design

This section describes the dataset, window construction procedure, train–test splitting strategy, evaluation metrics, and experimental protocol used to assess the proposed window-level detection model and its aggregation-based extensions.

IV-A

Dataset

All experiments are conducted on the CERT r4.2 insider threat dataset, which contains synthetic but behaviorally realistic audit logs for a large enterprise environment. The dataset includes multiple behavioral modalities, including logon activity, device usage, file access, email, and web activity, along with ground-truth labels for several insider threat scenarios. Following prior work, we focus on scenarios that exhibit temporally extended attack behavior rather than isolated anomalous events.

We restrict our analysis to attack campaigns lasting longer than one day and shorter than one week. This restriction reflects the practical focus of this work on sustained misuse behavior rather than single-session anomalies. Under this constraint, only Scenario 1 (gradual data exfiltration) and Scenario 3 (IT sabotage) contain qualifying attacks. The restriction is applied uniformly across all experiments and is not tuned to individual users or attack instances.

To control for user heterogeneity and avoid population shift, we further restrict training and evaluation to the subset of users who are compromised at least once during the simulation. Normal behavior from users who are never compromised is excluded. This ensures that both training and testing data are drawn from the same underlying user population, and avoids conflating insider threat detection with differences between compromised and non-compromised users.

We note that this protocol evaluates detection performance within a controlled compromised-user population, and does not model the open-world setting in which the compromised population is unknown in advance. This choice aligns with the intended deployment setting of UEBA systems, where the objective is to detect deviations from a user’s own historical behavior rather than to classify users based on static attributes such as role or department. Training on a mixed population of never-compromised users would confound anomaly detection with population-level differences and obscure whether a model can detect the onset of compromise for a given user. More broadly, our experimental protocol reflects a user-centric detection objective: identifying when an individual user’s behavior becomes anomalous relative to a shared baseline informed by their own normal history, rather than distinguishing compromised users from the general population.

IV-B

Window Construction and Features

Raw audit logs are aggregated into fixed-length windows of duration
W\in\{12,24,48\}
hours. Each window is subdivided into
T
equal-sized buckets, where the bucket resolution
\Delta t
is fixed at one hour. For each bucket, we extract
F=12
behavioral features capturing activity counts and unique entity statistics across five modalities. Buckets with no recorded activity are explicitly filled with zeros, resulting in highly sparse window representations.

Unless otherwise stated, results are reported for the 24-hour window configuration, which serves as the primary experimental setting. The 12-hour and 48-hour windows are evaluated as a sensitivity analysis to assess robustness to temporal aggregation choices. All other aspects of the model architecture, optimization procedure, and data processing pipeline are held constant across window durations.

IV-C

Train–Test Split

To prevent temporal leakage, we employ a strict chronological splitting strategy at the user level. For each compromised user, windows occurring strictly before any attack activity, excluding a fixed temporal buffer, are treated as normal. These normal windows are sorted chronologically, and the earliest 80% are used for training.

The remaining 20% of normal windows, along with all attack windows and normal windows occurring near and between attack campaigns, are reserved for testing. Including attack-adjacent normal windows in the test set yields a more challenging and operationally relevant evaluation, as these windows are often behaviorally similar to malicious activity.

The model is trained exclusively on normal windows without access to attack labels. All test-time evaluations are performed on windows that occur later in time than the training data. This protocol ensures that the model does not observe future behavior during training and avoids contamination from attack-related behavioral drift.

IV-D

Evaluation Metrics

Detection performance is evaluated primarily using precision–recall area under the curve (PR-AUC), which is more informative than ROC-AUC in highly imbalanced settings. Window-level PR-AUC is computed by comparing per-window anomaly scores against ground-truth labels. ROC-AUC is reported as a secondary metric for completeness.

In addition to aggregate performance, we report scenario-specific results for Scenario 1 and Scenario 3 to assess robustness across different attack dynamics. Precision and recall at selected operating points are also reported to illustrate practical tradeoffs, including high-precision regimes with zero false alarms.

To evaluate campaign-level detection, window-level anomaly scores are aggregated across fixed-length sequences of consecutive windows. A sequence is labeled as malicious if it overlaps with any malicious window (i.e., at least one window in the sequence is malicious). Campaign-level performance is reported using PR-AUC and ROC-AUC computed over aggregated sequence scores. This evaluation reflects the goal of identifying users involved in extended attack campaigns rather than issuing independent window-level alerts.

IV-E

Baselines and Ablations

We compare the proposed mask-based autoencoder against standard convolutional autoencoder baselines trained on z-scored value representations using mean squared error. A series of ablation studies examine the contributions of the mask channel, the value channel, and temporal consistency regularization. All ablations share the same architecture, optimization settings, and data splits to ensure fair comparison.

IV-F

Implementation Details

All models are implemented in PyTorch and trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and batch size 32. Models are trained for up to 50 epochs with early stopping based on training loss. Feature normalization statistics are computed using training data only and applied consistently to validation and test sets. All reported results are obtained using fixed random seeds to ensure reproducibility.

V-C

Summary

The window-level results demonstrate that explicitly modeling activity presence is critical for effective unsupervised insider threat detection in sparse enterprise data. Across all evaluations, the mask-based autoencoder substantially outperforms value-only autoencoder baselines, achieving strong precision–recall performance and supporting operating points with zero false alarms.

Performance is robust across attack scenarios and is not driven by a small number of extreme outliers. In particular, the proposed approach maintains high detection quality for Scenario 1, which involves gradual and low-amplitude misuse behavior and is often challenging for unsupervised methods.

An ablation over window duration shows that temporal aggregation plays a decisive role in detection performance. Short windows fail to provide sufficient behavioral context, while overly long windows dilute sparse attack activity. A 24-hour window provides the best balance between noise reduction and signal preservation, yielding the strongest performance for window-level detection and downstream campaign-level aggregation.
.

Overall, these results establish the mask-based autoencoder as a strong and reliable foundation for unsupervised window-level insider threat detection. They also motivate further investigation of multi-scale and trajectory-aware methods that build on high-quality window representations without sacrificing detection precision.

VI
Campaign-Level Detection via Sparse Aggregation

While the primary objective of this work is high-precision window-level insider threat detection, real-world insider attacks typically unfold over multiple days. In such settings, security analysts are less interested in isolated anomalous windows than in identifying users engaged in extended attack campaigns. We therefore evaluate whether window-level anomaly scores produced by the proposed model can be aggregated to support campaign-level detection.

VI-A

Aggregation-Based Campaign Modeling

Rather than modeling detailed temporal trajectories or sequential dynamics, we explicitly consider the sparsity of insider attack behavior in enterprise audit data. Empirically, attack campaigns in the CERT dataset consist of a small number of highly anomalous windows embedded within longer periods of normal activity. This observation suggests that campaign detection should emphasize extreme evidence rather than sustained deviation.

For each user, we group consecutive windows into fixed-length sequences of
L=6
days using a sliding window with stride 3. Each sequence is assigned a campaign label if it overlaps with at least one malicious window. Campaign-level anomaly scores are computed by aggregating the window-level mask reconstruction scores within each sequence using simple pooling functions, including summation and top-
k
selection.

VI-C

Interpretation

These results demonstrate that effective campaign-level insider threat detection can be achieved without explicit temporal modeling or trajectory-based analysis. Instead, the dominant factor is the quality of window-level anomaly scores and the use of aggregation functions that reflect the sparse nature of insider attack behavior. In particular, top-
k
aggregation is critical for scenarios where attacks manifest as a small number of highly anomalous events rather than sustained deviation.

Taken together, these findings show that sparse aggregation of window-level evidence is both necessary and sufficient for campaign discovery in this setting, and that more complex trajectory modeling is unnecessary for achieving strong performance.

VII
Discussion

In a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on single-channel, z-scored telemetry, activity presence and activity magnitude are implicitly entangled within the same input representation and loss function. Because enterprise audit data is extremely sparse, the dominant contribution to the reconstruction loss arises from inactive regions, causing the encoder to organize the latent space primarily around modeling baseline inactivity. As a result, latent distances are heavily influenced by small deviations in normalized zero-valued cells, while the spatial structure induced by patterns of activity occurrence is weak and unstable. Although activity magnitude is technically encoded in the latent representation, it competes with inactivity reconstruction for representational capacity and gradient signal, leading to latent geometries that reflect average behavior rather than discriminative behavioral structure.

In contrast, the proposed dual-channel architecture factorizes the learning objective into two semantically distinct components that shape the latent space in complementary but asymmetric ways. The binary mask channel is trained with a classification loss that directly penalizes errors in activity presence, forcing the encoder to preserve the sparsity structure and co-occurrence patterns of events in latent space. The value channel is optimized conditionally, contributing reconstruction error only where activity is present, and therefore refines the latent representation without dominating its geometry. Although both channels are encoded into a shared latent vector, their gradients impose different geometric constraints: the mask channel determines the primary organization of the latent manifold, while the value channel induces local refinement along directions already defined by presence patterns. This separation yields latent spaces that are structured by where behavior occurs rather than by the reconstruction of inactivity, producing representations that are more stable, more discriminative, and better suited for both window-level scoring and downstream campaign analysis.

This work demonstrates that explicitly modeling activity presence is a critical and previously underexplored factor in unsupervised insider threat detection. Enterprise audit data is dominated by inactivity, and conventional autoencoder approaches trained with mean squared error tend to devote most of their capacity to reconstructing inactive baselines rather than informative behavioral structure. By separating activity presence from activity magnitude, the proposed mask-based autoencoder aligns the learning objective with the true sparsity characteristics of enterprise telemetry. This design choice yields substantial gains in window-level detection performance, including strong precision–recall behavior and operating points with zero false alarms.

The results confirm that window-level detection remains a powerful and operationally relevant capability. The proposed model performs robustly across attack scenarios, including Scenario 1, which involves gradual and low-amplitude misuse behavior and is frequently described as challenging for unsupervised methods. Importantly, these gains are not driven by a small number of extreme outliers, but reflect consistent separation across a broad range of recall values. The window duration ablation further emphasizes the role of temporal aggregation, with 24-hour windows providing the best balance between contextual stability and signal dilution.

Direct quantitative comparison to prior UEBA studies is inherently difficult due to substantial differences in evaluation protocols. Most existing work reports window-level ROC-AUC over heterogeneous mixes of attack types and durations, often dominated by short, high-amplitude events, and does not provide scenario-level precision–recall breakdowns
[
8
]
. In contrast, our evaluation is explicitly restricted to multi-day attack campaigns lasting between one and seven days, a setting in which anomaly signals are weaker and class imbalance is more severe. As a result, aggregate metrics reported in prior work may reflect sensitivity to gross deviations rather than the ability to detect subtle, temporally extended misuse. Our goal is therefore not to claim superiority under identical conditions, but to characterize detection behavior under a stricter and operationally relevant regime that is largely unexamined in the literature.

Beyond window-level detection, we evaluate campaign-level detection by aggregating window-level anomaly scores across multi-day sequences. A key empirical finding is that insider attack campaigns in this setting are highly temporally sparse: positive sequences often contain only a small number of anomalous windows embedded within predominantly normal activity. Under such sparsity, aggregation functions that emphasize extreme evidence dominate those that assume sustained deviation. In particular, top-
k
aggregation of window-level mask anomaly scores yields strong campaign-level performance across both Scenario 1 and Scenario 3, while simple summation can degrade substantially when malicious activity is concentrated in only one or two days. Although any-overlap labeling treats sequences with one or many malicious windows equivalently, this choice substantially increases task difficulty by diluting positive examples with normal behavior, making strong campaign-level performance more—not less—meaningful.

This result shifts the interpretation of campaign detection in sparse enterprise telemetry. Rather than requiring increasingly complex temporal models, effective campaign discovery can often be achieved by combining high-quality window-level scores with aggregation functions that match the event sparsity structure of insider behavior. From an operational standpoint, this suggests a complementary two-stage workflow: (i) high-precision window-level alerts suitable for immediate response, and (ii) campaign-level aggregation suitable for retrospective discovery and threat hunting over longer horizons.

Overall, the results point toward a complementary view of insider threat detection. High-precision window-level detection provides timely and actionable alerts, while aggregation over windows provides an effective mechanism for campaign discovery. Treating these capabilities as distinct but related tasks enables clearer evaluation, avoids conflating objectives that require different assumptions, and motivates future work on multi-scale campaign definitions and aggregation strategies beyond a single temporal resolution.
