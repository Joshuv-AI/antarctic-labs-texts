Wide Awake Facial Reconstruction: A Literature Review, Cost Analysis, and Practical Guide
Authors: Aidan Shulkin, Yossi Cohen, Gabriel Bouhadana, Jordan Gornitsky, Daniel E. Borsuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:General anesthesia (GA) has been the standard for complex facial reconstructive surgery but is associated with perioperative risks and increased resource consumption. Wide awake facial reconstruction (WAFR), performed under local anesthesia (LA)/regional anesthesia (RA), offers an alternative that avoids these limitations. Nonetheless, WAFR remains underreported, and best practices are not well established.Methods:A literature review was conducted to identify studies involving WAFR, from which data were analyzed. Additionally, the costs of performing forehead flap reconstruction using LA/RA were compared with those under GA. Finally, a practical guide was developed to highlight key strategies for optimizing patient comfort.Results:Thirty studies involving 1249 patients and 1262 flaps were included. Forehead (n = 443, 35.1%) and nasolabial (n = 387, 30.7%) flaps were the most frequently implemented. Minor (n = 126, 10.0%) and major (n = 34, 2.7%) complications were uncommon. Most patients expressed minimal to no pain during surgery and were consistently satisfied with their experience. No conversions to GA were required. The total cost of performing a forehead flap using WAFR is considerably lower when compared with GA ($1280.03 versus $6646.74; difference: $5366.71).Conclusions:WAFR is a safe, well-tolerated and effective alternative to facial reconstruction under GA. Advanced procedures, such as loco-regional flaps, can be successfully performed under LA/RA. This approach also improves access to revision surgery, a key factor in achieving the best possible final outcomes. The avoidance of GA significantly reduces healthcare costs. Further research is needed to incorporate patient-reported outcomes and expand the role of WAFR across diverse techniques.

Takeaways

Question: Does Wide Awake Facial Reconstruction (WAFR) reduce healthcare costs while maintaining gold-standard outcomes in the treatment of facial deformities?

Findings: A literature review of 30 studies (1249 patients, 1262 flaps) demonstrated low complication rates, high patient satisfaction, and no conversions to GA. Forehead flap reconstruction using WAFR resulted in $5366.71 in cost savings compared with GA. A practical step-by-step guide was developed to enhance patient comfort.

Meaning: WAFR is a safe, well-tolerated and effective alternative to facial reconstruction under GA.

INTRODUCTION

Facial reconstructive surgery is essential for restoring form and function following oncologic resection, trauma, and other acquired defects, and plays an important role in patient quality of life.1,2 Although minor defects are typically repaired primarily under local anesthesia (LA)/regional anesthesia (RA), more complex interventions, such as locoregional flaps, are less commonly attempted in this setting, and frequently require general anesthesia (GA).3,4 Recently, however, both LA and RA have gained traction as viable alternatives for select patients requiring advanced facial reconstruction.5–7

At our institution, wide awake facial reconstruction (WAFR), defined as facial reconstructive surgery performed under LA/RA without GA, has become standard practice. WAFR is particularly advantageous in older adult or comorbid patients, who are both disproportionately affected by facial lesions and often deemed poor candidates for GA.8–12 Moreover, the use of LA/RA has proven to be a cost-effective alternative, necessitating less personnel and anesthetic materials/drugs, and eliminating the need for a formal operating room (OR) and monitoring, ultimately decreasing the use of healthcare system resources.13–17 Despite these advantages, WAFR remains underutilized, and best practices are not well defined in the literature.

This article aimed to comprehensively review the existing literature on WAFR, highlighting current practices, complications, and outcomes. Additionally, we present a cost-analysis comparing WAFR to GA for forehead flap reconstruction, illustrating the potential economic advantages of this approach in clinical practice. Finally, we provide an overview of our institutional technique, focusing on key procedural considerations to maximize patient comfort.

METHODS

Literature Review

A comprehensive literature review was conducted in July 2025 using the PubMed database. The following search terms were applied in English: (“local” AND anesth*) AND (“face” OR “facial” OR “nose” OR “nasal”) AND (reconstruct* OR “repair” OR “flap”). Studies were included if they reported on the use of LA/RA for advanced facial reconstructive surgery in adult patients (>18 years of age). Eligible procedures involved local or regional flaps used to repair defects of the nose, cheek, peri-oral, peri-orbital, or forehead regions following oncologic resection or other acquired causes.

Studies were excluded if they focused solely on aesthetic procedures (eg, facelift, rhinoplasty, blepharoplasty); reconstruction of the scalp, ear, or oral cavity; or minimally invasive techniques (eg, primary closure, skin grafting, dermal regeneration matrices). Additionally, studies that did not stratify outcomes by anesthesia type were excluded when applicable. No restrictions were placed on publication date or language.

Search results were imported into Covidence for screening.18 After removing duplicates, titles/abstracts were reviewed, and full texts of potentially relevant studies were assessed based on inclusion/exclusion criteria. Although no language restrictions were applied, all retrieved studies presented their titles/abstracts in English, and only 2 full-text manuscripts were published in French. Data extracted from eligible studies included patient characteristics, surgical techniques, complications, and outcomes (ie, pain, satisfaction, and aesthetics). All included studies were subsequently appraised using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence.19

Cost Analysis

A cost analysis was conducted to evaluate the economic impact of performing a forehead flap using LA/RA, in a minor procedure room (MPR), compared with GA, in a traditional OR. This was based on a standardized procedural model, constructed using two representative scenarios rather than relying on case-based averages. The analysis considered three primary categories: infrastructure, materials, and personnel. Infrastructure costs encompassed overhead expenses related to the MPR, OR, surgical day center, and postanesthesia care unit, with data drawn from local institutional accounting estimates. Material costs included expenses for personal protective equipment, disposable surgical supplies, and anesthesia-related items, based on our local institution’s pricing catalog. Personnel costs were determined according to the staffing needs specific to each anesthetic approach and were calculated using standardized wage data from the Régie de l’Assurance Maladie du Québec20 and the Government of Canada Job Bank.21 All costs were expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars. Finally, the total estimated cost per procedure was compared between LA/RA and GA.

Practical Guide

Based on the findings of our literature review, insights from the wide awake anesthesia no tourniquet technique, and our clinical experience, we developed a step-by-step practical guide to performing WAFR with a focus on optimizing patient comfort.

Patient Characteristics and Procedures

Overall, 1249 patients underwent 1262 locoregional flaps using WAFR. The mean reported age of patients was 65 years (range: 22–95 y). Most patients required facial reconstruction secondary to tumor resection (n = 1247, 99.8%). The primary anatomical site of reconstruction was unspecified in most cases (n = 724, 58.0%), followed by the nose (n = 393, 31.5%).

Interpolated flaps were the most frequently implemented, including forehead flaps (n = 443, 35.1%), and nasolabial/melolabial flaps (n = 387, 30.7%). The most common local anesthetic used was 1% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:100,000) (n = 729, 57.8%) (overview in Table 2; individual characteristics in Supplemental Digital Content 2). Complications

Minor complications were observed in 10.0% of cases, mainly attributable to epidermolysis (n = 50, 4.0%) and surgical site infections requiring antibiotics (n = 27, 2.1%). Major complications were observed in only 2.7% of cases, including partial necrosis (n = 26, 2.0%) and total necrosis (n = 5, 0.4%) (overview in Table 3).

Pain

Three studies utilized custom pain scales. One study including 62 patients reported that 95% experienced only mild pain during infiltration, and 90% had no pain during the procedure itself. The remaining patients experienced moderate but tolerable discomfort.22 Similarly, another study assessing 22 patients found mean pain scores of 3.4 of 5 during infiltration, 1.9 of 5 during surgery, and 2.8 of 5 postoperatively.46 A third study comprising 39 patients reported that both intraoperative and postoperative pain and anxiety levels were consistently minimal to mild.49 One study involving 12 patients relied on subjective observations and noted that no pain was observed during surgery, whereas fewer than half reported slight to moderate pain during the night following the procedure.39 Notably, no studies reported intolerable pain during the infiltration, during surgery, or postoperatively, and no conversion from LA/RA to GA was required.

Satisfaction

Overall, patient satisfaction with the WAFR approach was consistently high. In one study including 35 patients, all who underwent an initial procedure using LA/RA opted for subsequent revisions with the same technique.25 Another study assessing 22 patients reported favorable satisfaction ratings, with mean scores of 4.3 of 5 for convenience, 3.8 of 5 for perceived efficacy, and 3.4 of 5 for comfort. When asked about future procedures, the majority of patients expressed willingness to undergo WAFR again.46 Perioperative satisfaction was also elevated in a separate study comprising 39 patients, with a mean score of 95 of 100.49

Aesthetics

Aesthetic results following WAFR were consistently rated favorably across studies, regardless of the flap design implemented. In one study including 62 patients, 90% rated their outcomes as “good” and 10% as “excellent” following reconstruction with a range of local flaps.22 This aligns with findings from other studies employing various techniques in 705 patients, where 94%–97% were reported to have “good,” “satisfactory,” or “excellent” aesthetic outcomes.34,35 A study using transposition flaps in 13 patients reported a high mean aesthetic score of 9.38 of 10 (range: 8–10) on a visual analog scale.31 In another study utilizing paramedian forehead and other local flaps in 22 patients, the mean custom aesthetic rating was 4 of 5, and the mean FACE-Q score was 83.9 of 100.46

DISCUSSION

Rationale and Key Findings

The prevalence of facial skin cancer continues to increase, particularly among older individuals.52,53 Given that advanced age presents a risk for perioperative complications, the use of GA in this demographic introduces further challenges and concerns.8–12 In parallel, rising medical costs underscore the need to adopt strategies that reduce strain on the healthcare system.54,55 Facial reconstruction foregoing systemic anesthetic techniques has proven to be one of those avenues. Although underrepresented in the literature, our findings support WAFR as a safe, well-tolerated and effective technique. A variety of reconstructive procedures have been successfully performed under LA/RA, with low complication rates, little to no pain, and consistently high patient satisfaction. An additional advantage of this approach is facilitated access to an operative setting, allowing surgeons to offer patients revision surgery more freely and, thus, the best possible final results.

Avoidance of GA

The potential risks associated with GA in older adult patients undergoing surgery warrant careful consideration. Age-related declines in organ reserve, combined with the presence of comorbidities, contribute to a heightened risk of perioperative complications, including cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and neurological events.9,11,12 Among these, delirium and cognitive dysfunction are particularly concerning, as they can significantly delay recovery and are linked to increased morbidity/mortality.8 Additional GA-related complications such as nausea, vomiting, and airway challenges further add to the burden of care.13 In contrast, many facial reconstructive procedures, despite their complexity and duration, can be safely and effectively performed under LA/RA, provided the patient is well-prepared, cooperative and appropriately selected,10 as demonstrated in our findings.

Reduced Resource Utilization and Cost Savings

A systematic review comparing procedures performed under LA/RA versus GA reported substantial cost savings for a wide variety of interventions.16 These savings were primarily attributed to the elimination of formal OR use, reduced need for postanesthesia monitoring, and shorter hospital stays. Our findings align with these observations, demonstrating that performing forehead flap reconstruction under LA/RA results in an approximate cost reduction of 80.1%, largely due to the same contributing factors. Furthermore, although not explicitly calculated in our study, the true cost of surgery under GA is likely even higher when accounting for opportunity cost, namely, the use of limited OR resources that could otherwise be allocated to more complex cases.56 Additionally, indirect costs to patients, such as lost income or reduced productivity may further contribute to the overall financial burden.

Infection Control Achieved in Minor Procedure Settings

Recent studies have demonstrated that performing procedures in MPRs with less stringent regulations, compared with traditional ORs, does not increase the postoperative infection risk.57–60 In our study, the infection rate was 2.1%, comparable to published rates for facial reconstruction under GA, which range from 1.5% to 6.5%.61,62 Because most infections were derived from isolated cohorts,25,35,38,45 we believe the true infection rate is likely even lower. These findings support that, when established guidelines are followed and sterility protocols are strictly adhered to,63–66 infections can be effectively avoided. It is worth mentioning that careful attention should be given to vulnerable groups, including immunocompromised patients, older individuals, and those with other risk factors/comorbidities, as they often represent a substantial portion of the facial reconstructive population.67–69

Management of Antiplatelet and Anticoagulant Therapy

The perioperative management of antithrombotic agents is an important aspect of surgical care and requires particular attention in the context of WAFR.70 In our experience, patients can safely continue antiplatelet therapy, such as aspirin, a practice supported by existing evidence.71,72 However, we elect to discontinue anticoagulation, as the associated risks, including uncontrolled bleeding, impaired visualization of the operative field, and increased psychological distress for wide awake patients, justify a more cautious approach.71 Decisions regarding continuation/cessation should be made in consultation with internal medicine or other relevant specialties, ensuring an individualized plan that reflects each patient’s specific risk profile.73,74 If cessation is not recommended, the choice to proceed with WAFR ultimately relies on surgeon preference, provided that appropriate caution is maintained.75

Expanding the Boundaries

Although WAFR has typically been reserved for conventional locoregional flaps, recent reports suggest a broader range of applications, including more advanced and challenging reconstructions. One study reported the use of LA/RA for the repair of a full-thickness lower lip defect using a modified Webster flap in a 68-year-old patient.50 In another report, a modified cervicofacial flap was successfully performed under LA/RA to close a large cheek defect.43 These cases highlight the growing interest in extending WAFR principles to complex reconstructions and underscore its safety and feasibility, particularly in older adult patients with multiple comorbidities. It is important to note that as reconstructions become more extensive, the volume of LA required will inevitably increase. Careful attention must be given to cumulative dosing to remain within established safety limits and prevent systemic toxicity.76,77 Measures to avoid these complications include employing targeted regional blocks, using epinephrine-containing or diluted solutions, and administering anesthesia in incremental doses.

Considerations to Maximize Patient Comfort

Despite its many advantages, WAFR is not without limitations and important considerations. A key concern for surgeons is patient compliance. WAFR Technique to Optimize Patient Comfort

Reassuring communication and the use of appropriate language can help establish trust and maintain patient engagement.78 In addition, playing calm music before and during the procedure may create a more soothing environment.79 Warm blankets and pillows may also provide an added level of comfort.80 In select cases, benzodiazepines may be considered to reduce anxiety and enhance cooperation, though their use should be limited to carefully chosen individuals due to potential side effects.50

Ensuring the proper technique for administering LA/RA is arguably the most critical aspect of WAFR, as a pain-free first-hand experience is essential for maintaining patient cooperation. The goal is to limit discomfort to the initial needle prick, ensuring that the patient feels no pain throughout the rest of the anesthetic administration or the procedure.81 Applying topical anesthetic creams (eg, benzocaine/tetracaine/lidocaine mixture) before injection may provide pain relief during needle insertion.82,83 A waiting period of approximately 30 minutes should be observed before proceeding.84 One key technique involves buffering the LA with sodium bicarbonate, which reduces its acidity and the associated burning sensation during injection.85 Another method uses sensory distraction, such as vibration devices or skin pinching, to downregulate nerve fibers responsible for transmitting pain signals.86,87 Moreover, the use of small gauge needles (eg, 30G, 32G) is preferred for injections, as it further reduces discomfort.88 In our experience, we use a 32G needle for initial, superficial injections, followed by a 30G needle for deeper structures.

A regional block targeting the V1 and/or V2 branches should be performed before injecting the open wound and surrounding tissue. To ensure optimal anesthetic and vasoconstrictive effects, a waiting period of approximately 5 minutes should be observed between administration of the LA/RA and the first incision.89 Before proceeding, the surgeon should gently test the anesthetized region using forceps. At this stage, disinfection should be performed using a clear antiseptic solution, carefully avoiding leakage into the eyes, ears, nose, or neck. Appropriate draping should be applied, leaving the entire face exposed. This maximizes visualization of the defect, facilitates assessment of symmetry and changes, and enhances patient comfort. Moist gauze compresses can be positioned on top of the patient’s eyes to decrease discomfort associated with the surgical lights. During the procedure, local anesthesia should be administered as necessary.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this study represents the most comprehensive synthesis of WAFR to date, integrating a literature review, cost analysis, and practical guide within a single framework. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Most included studies were retrospective cohorts or case series/reports, which introduces the potential for bias. It must also be noted that key outcomes such as pain and satisfaction were available from only a limited number of studies and were often measured using nonvalidated instruments. Additionally, our cost-analysis reflects the practices of a single institution, which may limit its generalizability to other healthcare settings/regions. Furthermore, the cost estimates were derived from a standardized procedural model rather than case averages. Although this does not account for variability, it still provides a reliable basis for evaluating relative cost differences.

CONCLUSIONS

WAFR represents a safe, well-tolerated, and effective alternative to traditional facial reconstruction under GA. Our findings demonstrate that advanced procedures, such as locoregional flaps, can be successfully performed under LA/RA, with low complication rates, little to no pain, and high patient satisfaction. This approach also improves access to revision surgery, a key factor in achieving the best possible final outcomes. In addition, avoiding GA significantly reduces resource utilization and healthcare costs while mitigating risks that disproportionately affect older adult and comorbid patients.

Despite these promising outcomes, there remains a notable gap in the literature. Future research should incorporate patient-reported outcome measures and explore strategies to optimize the patient experience. In this context, we propose a practical guide aimed at standardizing perioperative care and enhancing patient comfort throughout the reconstructive process. Continued investigation is also necessary to further define and expand the boundaries of WAFR across a broader range of reconstructive techniques.
