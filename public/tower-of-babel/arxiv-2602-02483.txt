Abstract
This paper studies how labor market conditions around high school graduation affect postsecondary skill investments. Using administrative data on more than six million German graduates from 1995–-2018, and exploiting deviations from secular state-specific trends, I document procyclical college enrollment. Cyclical increases in unemployment reduce enrollment at traditional universities and shift graduates toward vocational colleges and apprenticeships. These effects translate into educational attainment. Using large-scale survey data, I identify changes in expected returns to different degrees as the main mechanism. During recessions, graduates expect lower returns to an academic degree, while expected returns to a vocational degree are stable. Keywords: college enrollment, expectations, business cycles, apprenticeships JEL Codes: D84, E24, E32, I23, I24, I26

This article exploits deviations from secular state-specific trends in the unemployment rate around high school graduation to study how economic conditions affect postsecondary skill investments. Using administrative microdata on German higher education, I construct a panel at the commuting zone level on the share of more than six million high school graduates who enroll in and graduate from different institutions between 1995 and 2018. To distinguish between expected returns and outside options as mechanisms, I supplement my analysis with administrative data on the apprenticeship system, large-scale survey data on the expected returns to college, and a decomposition of business-cycle fluctuations into their national and local components.

I find that economic shocks reduce overall college enrollment, particularly at traditional universities. A 1 percentage point (pp) deviation of the state unemployment rate (UR) from its long-term quadratic trend in the senior high school year reduces first-time college enrollment by 2.10 pp. Enrollment effects are concentrated at traditional universities and result in a long-term reduction in educational attainment at these institutions (-1.04 pp). In contrast, attainment at more vocationally oriented colleges increases by 0.40 pp. Survey data on expectations and decompositions of the state UR show that these effects are mainly driven by national shocks that reduce the expected returns to an academic degree. However, especially low-performing young men expect returns to a vocational degree to be stable and even to increase during downturns. While state-specific shocks have no effect on students’ expectations, both national and state-specifc shocks have opposing effects on outside options. Capacity and credit constraints play a negligible role.

II. Institutional Background

I focus on high school graduates who obtained the highest leaving qualification (
allgemeine Hochschulreife
, aka
Abitur
). Typically, this takes 12–13 years of schooling. These graduates represent the largest subgroup, with about 75 percent of all high school graduates (including, e.g.,
Fachgebundene Hochschulreife
, aka
Fachabitur
) and 40 percent of the population aged 18–19. Graduates with
Abitur
have the full choice set: They can enroll at any institution, in any degree, and, sometimes, depending on their GPA, in any major. In the main analysis, I abstract from the specific institutional type and major and distinguish between three options that vary in their underlying skill content:

1.

University
: Traditional universities in Germany are publicly financed, offer a wide range of academic programs, and are known for providing a comprehensive and theoretical education. University degrees are typically full-time and emphasize the acquisition of general skills. Internships are allowed but usually not mandatory. There is, hence, greater emphasis on general skills.

2.

Other colleges
: All other colleges, such as universities of applied sciences (
Fachhochschule
), typically offer a more practice-oriented education. They focus on engineering, business, design, media, and technology programs. Their curriculum often includes internships. They can be privately financed and typically offer bachelor’s degrees through dual study programs, in which students spend much of their studies in paid on-the-job training. Depending on the degree type chosen (dual or full-time), the focus on specific skills is stronger.

3.

Apprenticeship
: High school graduates who enter the labor market typically start an apprenticeship at a firm. Apprenticeships combine on-the-job training at the firm with theoretical education at a vocational school (
Berufsschule
). These vocational training programs cover various occupations, e.g., traditional crafts, technical fields, and healthcare. By design, apprenticeships are highly firm- and occupation-specific, as is their skill content.

Higher education has low direct costs, as there are no general tuition fees in Germany.

6

(
14

. Private institutions can still charge tuition fees.
A means-tested federal aid program (
BAFöG
) supports around 25 percent of full-time students. However, only 28 percent of formally eligible students (i.e., those under 30) receive any
BAFöG
, either because they failed the means test, or because they did not apply
(

. Similarly, only five percent of students take on a private loan. Of the average monthly income of students under 21, 66 percent is parental allowances, 13 percent is own earnings, twelve percent stems from federal aid

The largest reform in the European Higher Education sector was arguably the Bologna Process, which began in 1999
(
30
)
. In Germany, the main change, alongside the introduction of the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS), was the transition from a single-tier to a two-tier degree system. Adoption accelerated in the mid-2000s: by 2008, bachelor’s programs had become the dominant entry route, with the sharpest compositional shift around 2005 (see

A.1

(

Consistent with this, the mid-2000s mark a structural change in both levels and slopes of the enrollment series (see

IV.C Identification

Against this background, the main challenges for identification are secular, state-specific trends in unemployment and enrollment rates, as well as structural breaks in these variables. To estimate the causal effect of labor market conditions on postsecondary education choices, I exploit state-specific business-cycle variation and account for a structural break in 2005. Explicitly, I regress enrollment rates
E

t

in the corresponding state

ii. Identifying assumptions.
Under the conditional independence assumption, i.e.,
E\bigl(\varepsilon_{rt}\mid\text{UR}_{s(r)t},\alpha_{s(r)},g_{s(r)}(t)\bigr)=0
, my estimates reflect the causal effect of a one percentage point deviation from the state-specific UR trend on CZ-level enrollment shares

Explicitly, I assume that, conditional on state fixed effects, quadratic time trends, and educational reforms, the unemployment rate at graduation is exogenous to other changes in the relative returns to certain institutional choices or cohort characteristics. As variation in unemployment likely reflects changes in state-level labor demand, reverse causality seems implausible. However, all unobserved within-year across-state shocks that affect both enrollment rates and unemployment may cause omitted variable bias.

A first omitted variable suspect is technology shocks. For example, high-skill augmenting technology shocks may simultaneously increase the expected return to general skill investments and affect unemployment rates. Yet, larger technology shocks often take time to be adopted and cause reallocations in the labor market
(
29

(

Overall, while I cannot rule out that single technology shocks may affect enrollment choices in specific periods, a systematic bias due to unobserved technology shocks is unlikely.

A second main suspect are firm’s expectations. If firms were to lower their economic expectations, e.g., due to political uncertainty, then they may hire fewer workers and apprentices
(
18
,
77
)
, and the unemployment rate would increase. As a result, high school graduates with
Abitur
may fail to find an apprenticeship and enroll at college instead. If this were the case, my finding of procyclical college enrollment would be attenuated and may represent a lower bound. Importantly, however, such expectation-driven changes in firms’ hiring and training behavior are arguably part of the fluctuations in labor demand that my analysis intends to capture. Negative firm expectation shocks would only generate omitted variable bias if they reduce apprenticeship availability relative to other local labor-market opportunities at a given unemployment rate. Consequently,
Section

V.C
studies the cyclicality of the apprenticeship market.

3

A zero effect on posted positions may still be consistent with this, e.g., when the true demand for apprentices is lower, but more of the remaining positions are posted du to frictions. Procyclical effects of state-level labor demnd on the apprenticeship market align with the international evidence (e.g.,
71
,
20
as discussed above). Together with the decomposition results on college enrollment, these effects suggest that national labor demand shocks cause students at the margin between enrollment at a traditional university and other colleges to enroll at other college, while more localized shocks operate at the margin of college going.

i. Data.
The Roy model described in

To measure the expected returns to college and apprenticeship degrees as representing general and more specific human capital, respectively, I rely on the

(

The data cover more than 100,000 high school students scheduled to graduate in 2008, 2012, 2015, and 2018 in a given state. Students are surveyed in the calendar year before and half a year after high school graduation on their expected returns to (i) a college degree and (ii) an apprenticeship.
19
19

19

Students are asked (i): “In general, how do you value the job perspectives for graduates with a college degree?” and (ii) “In general, how do you value the job perspectives for graduates with a vocational degree, without a college degree?”, on a five-point Likert scale, with the option to state “do not know.” From these measures, I compute (iii) a proxy for the implicitly expected relative general value of academic vs. vocational education, i.e., the standardized difference between the two. I refer to this measure as “relative return,” even though it does not resemble the returns to college in a stricter sense. The raw measure ranges from -4 (negative returns to college) to 4 (very high returns to college), with a mean of 0.52. I consider students who are uncertain about either option to have a weak preference for the other. If a student values the return on a college degree as good (4) and is uncertain about apprenticeships, the difference would be equal to 1.

ii. Capacity constraints.
Another alternative mechanism for procyclical enrollment is supply-side adjustment, for example, a reduction in the maximum number of students a university would admit to a given program. For the U.S.
36
show that a 10 percent increase in spending increases enrollment by 3 percent. In Germany, traditional universities are financed through state budgets and multi-year framework agreements that use upper-bound projections of student numbers
(
45
)
. Around 90 percent of university funding comes from public sources, with roughly three-quarters provided by the states and the remainder by the federal government through joint programs such as the Higher Education Pact (
Hochschulpakt
) and its successor, the Contract for the Future of Higher Education and Teaching (
Zukunftsvertrag Studium und Lehre stärken
)
(
54
,
64
)
. It is explicitly aimed at maintaining need-based study capacity, and provides dynamically increasing federal co-financing, largely independent of short-run state-level unemployment shocks.

Consistent with this institutional background, there is only a small and statistically insignificant correlation between the state UR and the log total annual spending across college types in the current year (see
Table

A.11
) However, since the expenditure data do not separate teaching from research, the estimates in
Table

A.11
only have limited information value for any cyclical adjustment on the teaching margin. Overall, this suggests that federal funding arrangements largely stabilize aggregate capacity, and that supply-side restrictions play a secondary role at best. This is consistent with
73
, who find no effects of large double cohort shocks (see
Section

IV.C
) on the number of study programs using any GPA-based admission cutoffs, as a proxy for capacity constraints.

VIII. Discussion

What explains the opposing enrollment patterns between Germany and the U.S.? And how representative is the German case? First and foremost, Germany’s apprenticeship system poses a fundamentally different outside option for high school graduates. While apprenticeships are certainly not acyclical per se, they are relatively less cyclical than direct labor market entry in the U.S.
(see,e.g.,
89
, for an overview)
. Moreover, wages in an increasing number of apprenticeships are bound to collective agreements, and marginal high school graduates who choose an apprenticeship in a recession would often compete with school graduates from lower tracks for remaining positions. Lastly, apprenticeship training in regions with high unemployment is subsidized by the federal and state governments
(see, e.g.,
79
, for an overview)
. As a result, Germany may be comparable to other countries with an established dual education system, such as Switzerland, Austria, and Denmark.
22
22

22

Evidence from Scandinavian countries finds countercyclical enrollment
(for Sweden and Denmark, see
48
,
85
, respectively)
.

Figure

A.8
shows that Germany may also be representative of several countries with centrally funded higher education and less cyclical labor markets. One example along these two dimensions is Spain. For Spain, the time series presented in
3
shows a procyclical pattern for university enrollment. Her regression analysis, however, relies on within-year variation in unemployment across regions and yields a positive effect of unemployment on enrollment. My decomposition helps reconcile this tension: macroeconomic unemployment fluctuations are the main driver of belief changes in my data, whereas more local deviations primarily shift outside options and need not generate the same cyclicality as aggregate shocks.
23
23

23

Different levels of variation in unemployment may also reconcile conflicting findings on an intensive margin. For the U.S.,
17
use detrended state UR and find that recessions reduce enrollment in education majors. In contrast,
37
finds the opposite when using CZ-level UR variation conditional on CZ and cohort fixed effects.

Another difference between Germany and the U.S. is that enrollment effects may occur at a different margin. In the U.S., effects are concentrated at two-year and weaker at four-year colleges
(
26
, see e.g.,)
. Individuals who would have otherwise started or continued employment now enroll for more short-term degrees or retrain to wait out the recession. However, the German higher education system does not offer comparable associate degrees. Instead, more vocational institutions offer formally equivalent bachelor’s degrees. Thus, college enrollment in Germany is generally a longer-term commitment, and retraining at college is less common. The de facto study duration of a bachelor’s degree in Germany is about four years, but it used to be about six years before the stepwise Bologna reform in the early 2000s
(
14
)
. However, retraining alone is unlikely to explain the different results. Like this study, work from the U.S. mostly focuses on first-time enrollment at college-going age
(
70
,
26
, e.g.,)
and papers that consider retraining generally have similar conclusions
(
11
, e.g.,)
.

IX. Conclusion

This paper examines how labor market conditions at high school graduation affect postsecondary skill investments, distinguishing between general academic education and more specific vocational pathways. Using administrative microdata on the universe of students in German higher education, as well as administrative data on the apprenticeship market, I find that higher unemployment at graduation can cause students to shift toward vocationally oriented colleges and apprenticeships.

I embed my analysis in a simple Roy model where students weigh general and specific skill investments based on their expected returns. My empirical analysis shows that high school graduates respond mainly to macroeconomic, rather than local labor market conditions, consistent with the role of salient macroeconomic conditions in shaping expectations. Survey data on subjective expectations shows that for the average student, downturns reduce the expected value of an academic degree, while low-performing male students even expect greater returns to a vocational degree in a recession. I find that capacity and credit constraints only play a negligible role.

My findings contrast with conventional wisdom on countercyclical college-going and demonstrate that downturns can cause a shift to investments in more specific skill investments. The results highlight that students’ adjustment to business cycle conditions depends critically on the available outside options. In systems with established vocational pathways, recessions can shift students toward investments in specific skills, while overall college enrollment declines. Countries that wish to adopt elements of the German apprenticeship system should be aware of how these changes affect the responsiveness of human capital investments to economic shocks. Ultimately, my findings highlight the need to clearly distinguish between the effects of macroeconomic and local conditions on human capital investment.
