Aryl hydrocarbon receptor as a molecular mediator in the induction of human aldehyde oxidase gene expression
Authors: Szu Ling Yeap, Cassandra Ching Lin Yap, Shi Han Tay, Linghua Harris Zhang, Aik Jiang Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRelatively little is known about receptor-mediated regulation of aldehyde oxidase (AOX). 2,3,7,8-Tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin, an aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonist, increases mouse Aox expression. However, given the species differences in ligand-dependent AHR activation and AOX regulation, it remains to be investigated whether human AHR regulates the expression of AOX1 (the human AOX isoform). Therefore, the present study was designed to determine the effect of modulating the functionality of human AHR on AOX1 gene expression in cell culture models (HepG2 and MCF-7) known to express AHR and AOX1. Treatment of HepG2 or MCF-7 cells with a noncytotoxic concentration of a classical AHR agonist (β-naphthoflavone, benzo[a]pyrene, or 3-methylcholanthrene [3-MC]), an endogenous AHR agonist (indoxyl sulfate or 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole), or a selective AHR modulator (quercetin, 3,3′-diindolylmethane, or omeprazole) increased AOX1 and AHR-regulated CYP1A1 mRNA expression. The increases in AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA levels were not correlated among our panel of AHR activators. The induction of AOX1 mRNA by 3-MC was time- and concentration-dependent. It was accompanied by an increase in AOX1 protein expression and AOX1-mediated enzyme activity (O6-benzylguanine 8-oxidation). An AHR antagonist (3′,4′-dimethoxyflavone) completely abolished the increase in AOX1 and CYP1A1 expression by an AHR agonist (3-MC). Gene knockdown by an AHR small interfering RNA substantially decreased AHR mRNA expression and attenuated AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA induction by 3-MC. Overall, based on the experimental approaches involving AHR activation, AHR antagonism, and AHR gene knockdown by small interfering RNA, our novel data indicate that AHR plays a role in mediating the induction of human AOX1 gene expression.Significance StatementThe deployment of multiple experimental approaches involving receptor activation, receptor antagonism, and gene knockdown by small interfering RNA led to the identification of the human aryl hydrocarbon receptor as a molecular mediator of aldehyde oxidase gene induction. This novel discovery expands our limited knowledge of the identity of pharmacological receptors contributing to the functionality of human aldehyde oxidase.Key words: Aldehyde oxidase, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, Cytochrome P450 1A1, Receptor activation, Receptor antagonism, RNA interference

Introduction

Originally discovered with an important role in toxicology, research on the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) in the past 5 decades have shown that this ligand-dependent transcription factor also has significant roles in physiology, pathogenesis, and, more recently, therapeutics, as a target for a specific immunological condition.1 In the inactive state, AHR is part of a cytosolic complex, consisting of several proteins, including a dimer of chaperone protein Hsp90 (a heat shock protein of 90 kDa), AHR-interacting protein (also known as X-associated protein 2), p23 (a cochaperone protein of 23 kDa), and a tyrosine kinase, c-Src.1 The cellular and molecular mechanisms of AHR activation are complex, involving canonical and noncanonical pathways.1, 2, 3 The general steps in the classical, canonical pathway for the ligand-dependent AHR activation are1 (1) ligand binding initiates dissociation of p23 and c-Src from the ligand-AHR multiprotein complex and a conformational change in AHR, resulting in translocation of the AHR complex to the nucleus; (2) dissociation of the Hsp90 dimer and AHR-interacting protein from the complex to release the ligand-AHR components, which then form a heterodimer complex with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT); (3) binding of the AHR/ARNT heterodimer to the specific DNA recognition site, the cis-acting xenobiotic response elements (also known as dioxin-response elements), located upstream of AHR-responsive genes; and (4) stimulation of transcription of target genes, eg, CYP1A1,4 CYP1A2,4 and CYP1B1.5 In the noncanonical pathway, rather than heterodimerization with ARNT, other transcription factors (eg, nuclear factor κB) are involved in AHR signaling to increase gene transcription and elicit biological actions, including those involved in inflammation, immune response, development, and antioxidative defense.2

Since the initial discovery of 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) as a classical AHR agonist,6 other metabolically stable halogenated aromatic hydrocarbons, such as dibenzofurans6 and polychlorinated biphenyls,7 have also been identified as AHR agonists. These ligands have binding affinities in the pM to nM range, representing the most potent class of AHR agonists.6,7 Other classical exogenous AHR agonists are polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, such as 3-methylcholanthrene (3-MC) and benzo[a]pyrene (BaP), which are widely studied as environmental toxicants, and β-naphthoflavone (BNF), which is a synthetic flavonoid.8 Endogenous AHR agonists have also been identified, including 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (FICZ)9 and indoxyl sulfate,10 which are tryptophan metabolites.11 Another group of AHR ligands is referred to as selective AHR modulators, which have partial agonistic activity in some cell types but antagonistic activity in other cell types.12 Selective AHR modulators include 3,3′-diindolylmethane (DIM),13 which is a gut microbial metabolite of a phytochemical known as indole-3-carbinol14; quercetin,15,16 which is a naturally occurring plant flavonoid17; and omeprazole,18 which is a synthetic drug that functions as a proton pump inhibitor.19 Another group of AHR ligands are the pharmacological antagonists of AHR; for example, 3′,4′-dimethoxyflavone (DMF).20

Aldehyde oxidase (AOX) is a cytosolic molybdo-flavoenzyme with a broad substrate specificity and it is expressed abundantly in mammalian liver.21 The number of AOX isoenzymes present varies between species, with humans possessing a single isoform (AOX1) and mice expressing 4 isoforms (Aox1, Aox3, Aox4, and Aox3L).22 Mouse AHR has been linked to the regulation of Aox1 gene expression. TCDD, a prototypical AHR agonist,6 increases Aox1 mRNA in cultured Hepa-1 mouse hepatoma cells.23 It also increases Aox1 mRNA,23 protein,23 and enzyme activity24 in C57BL/6 mouse liver cytosol. However, there are demonstrated species differences in ligand-dependent activation of AHR25 and AOX expression and activity.26 Therefore, it remains to be determined whether human AHR is a molecular regulator of AOX1.

In the present study, we tested the hypothesis that human AHR regulates AOX1 gene expression. The experimental approaches involved the use of receptor activation, receptor antagonism, gene knockdown by small interfering RNA (siRNA), and human cell culture models (HepG2 and MCF-7 cells) known to express AHR27 and AOX1.28 Our novel findings suggest a role of AHR in the induction of human AOX1 gene expression.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

BNF, BaP, 3-MC, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, DIM, omeprazole, quercetin, and DMF were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. AHR siRNA (Silencer Select validated AHR siRNA #S1200), positive control siRNA (Silencer Select GAPDH Positive Control siRNA), and negative control siRNA (Silencer Select Negative Control #2 siRNA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All other chemicals and reagents were bought from sources indicated previously.29,30

Cell culture

HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HB-8065), MCF-7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells (HB-22), and Hepa1–6 mouse hepatoma cell line (CRL-1830) were purchased from and authenticated by the American Type Culture Collection. HepG2 cells were cultured as described previously.29 MCF-7 cells were additionally supplemented with 0.01 mg/mL human recombinant insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hepa1–6 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium high glucose supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. The passage number of the cells was kept between 4 and 20 in all experiments.

Lactate dehydrogenase assay

HepG2 cells were plated onto 12-well plates, with each well having a seeding density of 200,000 cells per well in 1 mL of supplemented culture medium. Cells were then incubated in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% carbon dioxide and allowed to proliferate before treatment with a chemical for 24 or 72 hours. The culture medium was aspirated and replaced with new culture medium containing a chemical once every 24 hours: BNF (5–20 μM), BaP (0.5–2.0 μM), 3-MC (0.1–1.0 μM), DMF (1–10 μM), both 3-MC (0.5 μM) and DMF (1–10 μM), DIM (1–50 μM), FICZ (1–30 μM), indoxyl sulfate (1000–2000 μM), omeprazole (50–300 μM), or quercetin (10–30 μM), dextran (1% v/v; negative control), Triton X-100 (0.005%–0.01% v/v; positive control), or DMSO (0.1% v/v; vehicle control), as stated in each figure legend.

The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay was performed using the Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH), according to the manufacturer’s instructions and as described previously.31 The LDH assay was performed on the culture medium collected every 24 hours and after the last chemical treatment. At the end of the treatment period, cells were lysed, and the cell lysates were also assayed. The absorbance of the contents was measured at 490 nm using a multimode microplate reader (GloMax Explorer System, Promega Corporation). Net absorbance was calculated by subtracting the background absorbance value, which was obtained in the vehicle-treated group in the absence of cells. To determine the percentage of LDH release, the amount of LDH in the culture medium (sum of LDH content from culture medium collected every 24 hours for 72 hours) was expressed as a percentage of the total cellular LDH content (culture medium plus cell lysate) in each well.

Chemical treatment of cells for mRNA assays

Cultured HepG2, MCF-7, and Hepa1–6 cells were seeded onto 12-well plates at a cell density of 200,000, 300,000, and 50,000 cells per well, respectively, in 1 mL complete culture medium. At 24 hours after plating, cells were treated with BNF, BaP, 3-MC, DMF, both 3-MC and DMF, DIM, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, omeprazole, quercetin, or DMSO (vehicle control), at the concentrations indicated in the figure legend. The chemical-containing culture medium was replaced once every 24 hours for 72 hours. In the time-course experiment, HepG2 cells were treated with an AHR agonist for 24 hours (on day 4; or 72 hours after plating), 48 hours (on day 3; or 48 hours after plating), or 72 hours (on day 2; or 24 hours after plating). For the CYP1A1 gene expression assay in the DMF antagonism experiment, HepG2 cells were seeded onto 12-well plates at 1.6 × 106 cells per well in 1 mL complete culture medium. Chemical treatment of cells (6 hours) was initiated at 24 hours postplating.

Isolation and quantification of total RNA

At the end of the chemical treatment period, cells were washed with 500 μL of ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4). Total cellular RNA was isolated using the ReliaPrep RNA Cell Miniprep (Promega Corporation). Ice-cold lysis buffer (175 μL per well × 3 wells or 250 μL per well × 2 wells) was added to each well, and the cell lysates from 2 or 3 wells (of the same treatment group) were pooled and syringed through a 21G needle. Total cellular RNA was isolated and purified according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA pellet was suspended in 30 or 50 μL of nuclease-free water. To determine the concentration and purity of each RNA sample, the ratio of the absorbance at 260 nm to that at 280 nm was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc).

Reverse transcription and quantification of total cDNA

Total cellular RNA was reverse transcribed using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega), according to the manufacturer’s protocol and as described previously.32 The concentration of cDNA was quantified using the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Calibration curves (10–1000 ng/mL DNA) were constructed by serial dilution of the lambda DNA standard (100 μg/mL) with Tris-EDTA buffer, and cDNA samples were diluted 100 times with Tris-EDTA buffer. Quantification of standards and cDNA samples was done in duplicates. The fluorescence of the cDNA samples and lambda DNA standards were measured at an excitation wavelength of 475 nm and emission wavelength of 500 to 550 nm, using a multimode microplate reader (GloMax Explorer System, Promega Corporation).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

The primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies Pte Ltd. The sequences of primers used for amplifying the cDNAs of AOX1, CYP1A1, human hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT1) (reference gene), 18s rRNA (reference gene), mouse Aox1, mouse Hprt1 (reference gene), and mouse β-actin (Actb) (reference gene) are shown in Supplemental Table 1. The primers for AOX1 were designed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information PrimerBLAST primer designing tool. The specificity of AOX1 primers was experimentally verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequencing the purified amplicons at Axil Scientific Pte Ltd. In the melting curve analysis of the AOX1 amplicon, a single peak was obtained at a melting temperature of 81 to 81.5 °C.

To obtain purified cDNA amplicons, human liver QUICK-Clone cDNA (Clontech Laboratories, Inc) was amplified with gene-specific primers and the respective polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions shown in Supplemental Table 1. The individual amplicons for each DNA were pooled and purified using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified DNA was quantified using the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega), as described above. The calculated concentration was converted to copy numbers of DNA, using the formula: copy number = [concentration (g/μL) × 6 × 1023 (copies/mol)] / [number of base pairs × 660 Da/base pair]. The purified DNA master stock was stored at −80 °C until subsequent use. The purified DNA was serially diluted to construct the calibration curve for real-time PCR analysis.33

AOX1, CYP1A1, AHR, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), HPRT1, 18s rRNA, Aox1, Hprt1, and Actb cDNA were amplified using a real-time DNA thermal cycler (Rotor-Gene Q [2-Plex] [Qiagen GmbH] or CFX Connect Real-time PCR Detection System [Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc]). Each 20-μL PCR reaction volume contained 1 ng of total cDNA (all except 0.1 ng for GAPDH); 0.2 μM forward and reverse primers; MgCl2 (total concentration of 4 mM for HPRT1 and AHR, 3 mM for CYP1A1 and 18s rRNA, and 2 mM for AOX1, HPRT1, GAPDH, Aox1, Hprt1, and Actb); and GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (1×) (Promega Corporation). All amplifications were initiated with an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 minutes. The temperature and duration for the denaturation, annealing, and extension steps (40 cycles) for each gene are shown in Supplemental Table 1. A calibration curve (cross point vs log cDNA copies) was constructed by plotting the cross point (Ct) versus log copies of purified cDNA. AOX1, CYP1A1, AHR, and GAPDH mRNA levels were normalized to those of HPRT1 or 18s rRNA (human reference genes), whereas Aox1 mRNA levels were normalized to those of Hprt1 or Actb (mouse reference genes).

Chemical treatment of cells and quantification of total protein concentration and AOX1 protein concentration

Cultured HepG2 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at a cell density of 600,000 cells per well in 2 mL complete culture medium. At 24 hours after plating, cells were treated with BNF (10 μM), BaP (1 μM), 3-MC (0.5 μM), or DMSO (0.1% v/v). The chemical-containing culture medium was replaced every 24 hours for a period of 72 hours.

At 24 hours after the last chemical treatment, cells were washed with PBS (1 mL/well) and 0.3 mL of cold Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay Lysis and Extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (2 tablets per 10 mL) (cOmplete ULTRA Tablets, mini, EDTA-free, Easypack Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche Diagnostics GmbH) was added to each well. The cells were scrapped using a cell scraper, and the pooled lysates (3 or 6 wells per treatment group) were transferred to a microtube. The lysates were kept on ice and mixed for 5 seconds every 10 minutes for 30 minutes and centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes. The total protein concentration in the supernatants was analyzed by Coomassie (Bradford) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cell lysates were diluted 20× with PBS, and the bovine serum albumin standards (25–1000 μg/mL) were serially diluted with PBS with 5% Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay Lysis Buffer. Notably, 5 μL of each standard or sample in a 96-well microplate was mixed with 250 μL of Coomassie Reagent and incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a multimode microplate reader (GloMax Explorer System, Promega Corporation). After total protein quantification, the cell lysates were stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The amount of AOX1 protein in cell lysates was quantified in a capillary nano-proteomic immunoassay (SimpleWestern System, ProteinSimple) as described in our previous study.29

AOX1 enzyme activity assay (O6-benzylguanine 8-oxidation)

O6-Benzylguanine 8-oxidation was used as a catalytic marker for AOX1.29 HepG2 cells were seeded onto 12-well plates at a density of 200,000 cells per well in 1 mL complete culture medium. At 24 hours after plating, cells were treated with BNF (10 μM), BaP (1 μM), 3-MC (0.5 μM), or DMSO (0.1% v/v; vehicle control). The chemical-containing culture medium was replaced once every 24 hours for 72 hours. At the end of the 72-hour treatment period, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with 0.5 mL/well of PBS. The cells were incubated in situ with O6-benzylguanine (150 μM in 200 μL Hanks’ balanced salt solution) for 10 minutes in a humidified, 37 °C CO2 incubator. At the end of the incubation period, 160 μL of the supernatant solution from each well was transferred into a microcentrifuge tube and stored at −20 °C until analysis. To obtain cell lysate, cells were washed with 0.5 mL/well of PBS and mixed on a shaker for 1 hour at room temperature with 200 μL/well of a lysis buffer containing 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and 20 mM EDTA in. The protein concentration of the cell lysates from each well was quantified using the Coomassie (Bradford) Protein Assay Kit, as described above.

On the day of analysis by ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, the calibration standard samples (8-oxo-O6-benzylguanine; 0.1–1000 nM or 0.016–160 pmol in 160 μL) were prepared. To each of the standard and sample solution, 80 μL of tolbutamide (75 nM in acetonitrile; internal standard) was added to give a final volume of 240 μL. All the samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C and the amount of enzymatically generated 8-oxo-O6-benzylguanine (metabolite) and synthetic tolbutamide (internal standard) was quantified by an ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method.29 The rate of metabolite formation in pmol metabolite (8-oxo-O6-benzylguanine) per minutes per mg total cellular protein was calculated for each well, and the data were expressed as fold increase of metabolite formation over the vehicle control group.

siRNA transfection and treatment of cells

Cultured HepG2 cells were seeded onto 12-well microplate at a density of 300,000 cells per well in 1 mL complete culture medium. At 24 hours after plating, cells were transfected with 100 μL of a siRNA transfection master mix containing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (3 μL per well), serum-free Opti-MEM I reduced serum medium (100 μL per well), and a siRNA, ie, AHR siRNA (1 μL or 10 pmol per well), GAPDH (positive control) siRNA (1 μL or 10 pmol per well), or negative control siRNA (1 μL or 10 pmol per well) for 24 hours. Transfected HepG2 cells were treated with 1 mL of fresh culture medium containing 3-MC (0.5 μM) or DMSO (0.1% v/v; vehicle). The chemical-containing culture medium was replaced every 24 hours for a period of 72 hours. At 24 hours after the treatment period, total RNA was isolated and gene expression assays were conducted as described above.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by one-way or two-way ANOVA with Student–Newman–Keuls multiple comparison post hoc test and Pearson product-moment correlation analysis was performed on AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA expression (SigmaPlot 12.5; Systat Software, Inc). The statistical significance level was set a priori at P < .05.

Results

AHR agonists increased AOX1 mRNA expression in mouse Hepa1–6, human HepG2, and human MCF-7 cells

Treatment of Hepa1–6 mouse hepatoma cells with a noncytotoxic concentration of BNF (10 μM), BaP (1 μM), or 3-MC (0.5 μM) once every 24 hours for 72 hours (Supplemental Fig. 1) increased mouse Aox1 mRNA expression by 3.7-, 3.0-, and 2.7-fold, respectively, over the vehicle-treated control group (Fig. 1A). At the same concentrations, BaP and 3-MC increased AOX1 mRNA levels by 3.9- and 3.4-fold, respectively, whereas BNF had no effect in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 1B). By comparison, treatment of HepG2 human hepatoma cells with BNF, BaP, or 3-MC increased human AOX1 mRNA expression by 2.1-, 3.2-, and 5.2-fold, respectively (Fig. 1C), and AHR-regulated CYP1A1 mRNA expression by 84-, 67-, and 184-fold, respectively (Fig. 1D). All subsequent experiments were performed on HepG2 cells.

HepG2 cells (E, F) were treated with DMSO (0.1% v/v; vehicle), FICZ (10 μM), indoxyl sulfate (1750 μM), DIM (30 μM), omeprazole (200 μM), or quercetin (15 μM) once every 24 hours for 72 hours. Aox1 (A), AOX1 (B, C, E), CYP1A1 (D, F), and HPRT1 (normalization control) cDNAs were amplified by real-time PCR. Data are expressed as mean ± SD for 3 or 4 independent experiments. ∗P < .05, ∗∗P < .01, ∗∗∗P < .001, significantly different from the vehicle-treated control group. (G) Correlational analysis of AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA induction by AHR activators in HepG2 cells (C–F).

To determine whether other AhR activators also induce AOX1, cultured HepG2 cells were treated with noncytotoxic concentrations (Supplemental Fig. 2) of DIM, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, omeprazole, or quercetin once every 24 hours for 72 hours. As shown in Fig. 1E, DIM, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, omeprazole, and quercetin increased AOX1 mRNA expression by 2.3-, 2.7-, 3.3-, 4.7-, and 2.0-fold, respectively, over the vehicle-treated control group. A similar pattern of induction by DIM, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, omeprazole, and quercetin was evident for the AhR-regulated CYP1A1 mRNA expression (Fig. 1F). Correlational analysis of AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA induction by our panel of AhR activators yielded a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.47 (P = .2) (Fig. 1G).

Time- and concentration-dependent effect of AHR agonists on AOX1 mRNA expression in cultured HepG2 cells

To characterize the time course for the induction of AOX1 mRNA expression, cultured HepG2 cells were treated with an AHR agonist for 24, 48, or 72 hours. As shown in Fig. 2, A–C, BNF (10 μM), BaP (1 μM), and 3-MC (0.5 μM) increased AOX1 mRNA expression after 48 and 72 hours of treatment, but not after 24 hours of treatment. The induction of AOX1 mRNA expression by BNF (1–10 μM), BaP (0.5–1 μM), and 3-MC (0.1–0.5 μM) occurred in a concentration-dependent manner over the range of concentrations indicated (Fig. 2, D–F).

AHR agonists increased AOX1 protein expression and enzyme activity in cultured HepG2 cells

To determine whether the increase in mRNA expression by AHR agonists is translated to an increase in protein expression in cultured HepG2 cells, we investigated the effect of BNF, BaP, and 3-MC on AOX1 protein expression, using a capillary nano-proteomic immunoassay (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, BNF (10 μM), BaP (1 μM), and 3-MC (0.5 μM) increased AOX1 protein expression by 1.5-, 2.0-, and 2.2-fold, respectively, over the vehicle-treated control group. The same pattern of response was obtained on the effect of each of these AHR agonists on AOX1-mediated O6-benzylguanine 8-oxidation activity (Fig. 3C).

AHR antagonist attenuated the induction of AOX1 mRNA and protein expression by an AHR agonist in cultured HepG2 cells

To determine whether AHR plays a role in the induction of AOX1 mRNA expression by AHR agonists, the pharmacological antagonism approach was used to answer this research question. Noncytotoxic concentrations of DMF (Supplemental Fig. 3), which is a selective AHR antagonist,20 and 3-MC, which is an efficacious inducer of AOX1 mRNA expression (Fig. 2F), were selected. Figure 4A shows that 3-MC (0.5 μM) increased AOX1 mRNA expression by 6.1-fold. DMF (1, 5, and 10 μM) attenuated the increase in AOX1 mRNA expression by 3-MC in a concentration-dependent manner. As expected of an antagonist, DMF by itself had no effect (Fig. 4A). Control analysis indicated that DMF (10 μM) also attenuated the induction of CYP1A1 mRNA expression by 3-MC (Fig. 4B). The increase in AOX1 protein expression by 3-MC was abolished by DMF (Fig. 4C), consistent with the effect of DMF on 3-MC induction of AOX1 mRNA expression (Fig. 4A).

AHR siRNA attenuated the induction of AOX1 mRNA and protein expression by an AHR agonist in cultured HepG2 cells

RNA interference was used to corroborate the data obtained from the pharmacological antagonism experiment. HepG2 cells were transfected with a validated AHR siRNA.34 The AHR siRNA decreased AHR mRNA level by 70% in cells treated with DMSO (vehicle) (Fig. 5A). In the control siRNA-transfected groups, the AHR agonist 3-MC increased the mRNA expression of AHR (Fig. 5A), AOX1 (Fig. 5B), and CYP1A1 (Fig. 5C). The AHR siRNA attenuated the 3-MC induction of AHR mRNA (82%, Fig. 5A), AOX1 mRNA (45%, Fig. 5B), and CYP1A1 mRNA (47%, Fig. 5C). As a positive control for siRNA transfection efficiency, GAPDH siRNA decreased GAPDH mRNA expression in the DMSO-treated group and 3-MC-treated group by 80%–82% (data not shown), which was within the specified range provided by the supplier of the siRNA.

Discussion

A novelty of the present study is the demonstration that AHR activation leads to the induction of human AOX1 gene expression. This conclusion is based on the evidence obtained from experiments performed on AHR-27 and AOX1-expressing28 HepG2 cells indicating that (1) AHR activators BNF, BaP, 3-MC, FICZ, indoxyl sulfate, DIM, omeprazole, and quercetin increased AOX1 mRNA levels; (2) the increase in AOX1 mRNA expression by 3-MC was accompanied by an increase in AOX1 protein expression, as determined by a capillary nano-proteomic immunoassay,29 and an increase in AOX1-mediated enzyme activity, as determined by the O6-benzylguanine 8-oxidation assay29; (3) pharmacological antagonism of AHR by DMF attenuated the effect of AHR agonism by 3-MC on the induction of AOX1 gene expression; and (4) gene knockdown of AHR by siRNA lessened the inducibility of AOX1 gene expression by an AHR agonist (3-MC).

A key finding in the present study is the relative differences in the magnitude of the response of the AOX1 and CYP1A1 genes to AHR activation, as demonstrated for HepG2 cells. Our data showed that AHR activation by classical agonists (BNF, BaP, and 3-MC), endogenous AHR agonists (indoxyl sulfate and FICZ), and selective AHR modulators (DIM, omeprazole, and quercetin) increased AOX1 mRNA expression to a considerably smaller extent than the induction of CYP1A1 mRNA expression by the same panel of AHR ligands. CYP1A1 has been shown to be highly inducible by prototypical AHR agonists.18 However, other AHR-regulated genes, including UGT1A1,35 UGT1A6,36 GSTA1,37 ALDH3A1,38 and NQO1,39 are considerably less inducible by the same AHR agonists. For example, treatment of human Caco-2 colorectal adenocarcinoma cells with 10 nM TCDD increased CYP1A1 mRNA expression by 250-fold, whereas the same chemical treatment increased UGT1A1 mRNA expression by only 3-fold.16 The differential inducibility of CYP1A1 and other AHR-regulated genes by AHR agonists is neither species-specific40 nor cell type-dependent.41 As shown by our experimental findings, AOX1 is inducible by AHR activators, but it is among the group of lesser inducible AHR target genes. It should be noted that not all AHR-regulated genes are subject to induction by an AHR agonist. Stanniocalcin 2 is an AHR-regulated gene, but typical AHR agonists (TCDD, 3-MC, and BNF) have little or no effect on mouse hepatocyte stanniocalcin 2 mRNA expression, whereas each of these chemicals substantively increases CYP1A1 mRNA level in the same hepatocyte samples.42 Overall, the quantitative differences in the inducibility of AOX1 and CYP1A1 by AHR activators suggest that the AOX1 gene is devoid of structural features, such as the dense cluster of multiple high-density xenobiotic response elements in the promoter and enhancer regions, that are associated with the hyperinducibility of CYP1A1.43

DMF is a selective AHR antagonist.20 In the current study, concentration-dependent effect of DMF on AHR agonist (3-MC) induction of AOX1 was observed. The inductive effect of 3-MC on AOX1 mRNA and protein expression was completely abolished by DMF (10 μM), whereas the same concentration of DMF only attenuated by half the induction of CYP1A1 mRNA expression by 3-MC. A plausible reason for this difference in the effect of the AHR antagonist (DMF) in the induction of AOX1 and CYP1A1 by 3-MC may relate to the reported AHR-independent mechanisms of CYP1A1 induction by 3-MC postulated to explain the sustained transcriptional activation of CYP1A1 promoter.44 Overall, the pharmacological antagonism data indicate a role of AHR in the upregulation of human AOX1 gene expression by 3-MC.

RNA interference by siRNA was deployed as an experimental approach to specifically knockdown AHR gene expression in HepG2 cells. The decrease (70%) in AHR mRNA expression in the basal state and the decrease (47%) in 3-MC induction of CYP1A1 mRNA by the AHR siRNA preparation used in the present study are comparable to the decrease in AHR (78%)45 and the decrease (57%)46 in 3-MC induction of CYP1A1 mRNA by the AHR siRNA preparation used in previous studies conducted on HepG2 cells. The partial attenuation of 3-MC induction of AOX1 by AHR siRNA (44%) is in contrast to the complete abolishment of 3-MC induction of AOX1 by pharmacological antagonism (DMF) of AHR reported in the present study. Reasons for the lesser effect of AHR siRNA on 3-MC induction of AOX1 include the transient nature of RNA interference by siRNA because of the cell division that continues to occur and the on-going de novo synthesis of new mRNA because the impact of siRNA is on mRNA, not on DNA.47 The data from our siRNA experiment provide additional evidence that AHR plays a role in the induction of human AOX1 gene expression by 3-MC.

A differential pattern of AOX1 and CYP1A1 gene induction was evident in response to AHR activators in HepG2 cells, as shown by (1) a lack of correlation in the increases in AOX1 and CYP1A1 mRNA levels by our panel of AHR activators; and (2) temporal profiling indicating that BNF, BaP, and 3-MC did not increase AOX1 mRNA expression at 24 hours postdosing; in contrast, an increase in CYP1A1 mRNA expression in HepG2 cells has been reported to occur well before 24 hours postdosing with the same AHR agonist, eg, 1 hour post BNF,48 6 hours post BaP,49 and 6 hours post 3-MC.44 Collectively, these data suggest that AHR activators induce AOX1 and CYP1A1 gene expression by distinct mechanisms. Future investigations will be needed to elucidate the mechanism responsible for the induction of human AOX1 gene expression by AHR activators.

In summary, to our knowledge, our novel discovery of a role for AHR in human AOX1 induction provides the first demonstration that the upregulation of human AOX1 gene expression is controlled by a ligand-activated transcription factor (AHR) in the basic helix-loop-helix/per-arnt-sim superfamily of environmental and physiological sensors.50 The identification of AHR as a mediator of human AOX1 gene induction adds to our limited knowledge of receptor-mediated upregulation of human AOX1 that also includes a role for pregnane X receptor,30 which is a member of the superfamily of nuclear receptors.51
