The clinical dilemma of high-risk stage II colon cancer: are we truly prepared to withdraw oxaliplatin?
Authors: J Taieb, A Gandini, JF Seligmann, C Gallois
Published: 2024

We read with interest the article published by Chibaudel and colleagues in the 4 September issue of the Journal of Clinical Oncology.1 By pooling the individual data of two clinical trials (MOSAIC2 and NSABP 07,3) the authors analyze the oncological outcomes of 1595 stage II colon cancer (CC) patients, treated with fluoro-uracil with and without oxaliplatin.

The authors reported that the addition of oxaliplatin conferred no overall survival (OS) benefit in either low- or high-risk stage II CC, and concluded that oxaliplatin should not be the standard of care for stage II CC—even in high-risk patients. Interestingly, this advice conflicts with their own arguments 10 years ago for the use of oxaliplatin in high-risk stage II.4

We do not agree with their new conclusions and would like to highlight limitations in the presented analyses and urge colleagues to continue to refer to the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO)5/National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines to optimally treat stage II CC patients.

First is the use of OS as the primary endpoint. The authors state that it was decided due to the lack of correlation between disease-free survival (DFS) and OS in stage II patients. Instead, DFS/time to recurrence (TTR) better judge the efficacy of an anticancer treatment, as they are unaffected by non-cancer events. This is especially important in stage II CC which has a low disease recurrence rate, so the competing risks of death have a significant impact. This is highlighted by the strongest prognostic factor in the presented dataset being age >70 years.

Despite the promise of being an ACCENT pooled analysis including 55 000 patients, the relevant populations for the sub-groups in the analysis are small and limit interpretation: there are 220 treated T4 patients and for the high-risk category, <350 per group. This risks misinterpretation or overinterpretation of underpowered analyses. For example, consider the 0.75 hazard ratio (P = 0.054) observed in the Kaplan–Meier curves comparing high-risk patients treated with or without oxaliplatin for DFS and the 0.75 (P = 0.077) observed for TTR. In these high-risk patients, disease recurrence is reduced by 25% by the addition of oxaliplatin; this is highly clinically relevant despite not achieving statistical significance in this unplanned post hoc analysis. It is likely that patients would additionally consider this clinically relevant. The ESMO guidelines recommend the use of FOLFOX/CAPOX limited just to these stage II patients.5

Next, this dataset does not represent contemporary multidisciplinary management of CC, with trials dating back over 20 years. Surgical standards (particularly lymph node yield) and histopathological staging have evolved since the 1990s, with parallel evolution of TNM (tumor–node–metastasis) staging. Furthermore, the absence of molecular data and especially of microsatellite instability (MSI) status is surprising given that it has been published previously by the authors in the JCO.4 Indeed, MSI is a major prognosticator in stage II CC, and is likely to represent a significant proportion of the locally advanced but node-negative (T4 N0) patients reported and are less likely to respond to chemotherapy. In addition, RAS and BRAF status in microsatellite stable patients is likely to influence disease recurrence, as recently published in stage III patients.6

The use of adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II CC has been controversial due to limited absolute survival gain (2%-3%) but growing appreciation of significant clinical and biological heterogeneity when utilizing TNM for classification. T4 tumor, <10/12 lymph nodes examined, and perforation have been constantly associated with poor oncological outcomes, with recurrence risk being similar or worse than that of some stage III patients, all treated with a combination of fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin.

The landscape of stage II CC may be shifted by ongoing trials which will establish the role of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) to identify high-risk patients who benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy.7,8 The Australian DYNAMIC trial,9 the only one dedicated to stage II CC, showed that testing ctDNA resulted in less adjuvant chemotherapy with no impact on DFS; however, in >60% of cases oxaliplatin was prescribed. However, T4 patients with negative ctDNA post-operatively and without adjuvant treatment had a risk of recurrence of ∼20%. This contemporary analysis highlights that this population should be considered distinct and this risk is sufficient to discuss adjuvant treatment while we await further data.

In summary, we strongly recommend that for fit patients with resected stage II CC and tumor perforation, T4 tumor stage or <10/12 lymph nodes examined, FOLFOX/CAPOX should still be considered as per current guidelines. We find the conclusions reached by Chibaudel et al. are too strong and debatable given the limitations described.
