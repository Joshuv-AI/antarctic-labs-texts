From Measurement to Expertise: Empathetic Expert Adapters for Context-Based Empathy in Conversational AI Agents
Erfan Shayegani 1 2, Jina Suh 1, Andy Wilson 1, Nagu Rangan 1, Javier Hernandez 1

Abstract
Empathy is a critical factor in fostering positive user experiences in conversational AI. While models can display empathy, it is often generic rather than tailored to specific tasks and contexts. In this work, we introduce a novel framework for developing and evaluating context-specific empathetic large language models (LLMs). We first analyze a real-world conversational dataset consisting of 672 multi-turn conversations across 8 tasks, revealing significant differences in terms of expected and experienced empathy before and after the conversations, respectively. To help minimize this gap, we develop a synthetic multi-turn conversational generation pipeline and steer responses toward our defined empathy patterns based on the context that more closely matches users’ expectations. We then train empathetic expert adapters for context-specific empathy that specialize in varying empathy levels based on the recognized task. Our empirical results demonstrate a significant gap reduction of 72.66% between perceived and desired empathy with scores increasing by an average factor of 2.43 as measured by our metrics and reward models. Additionally, our trained empathetic expert adapters demonstrate superior effectiveness in preserving empathy patterns throughout conversation turns, outperforming system prompts, which tend to dramatically diminish in impact as conversations lengthen.



I
Introduction

Empathy is a cornerstone of meaningful human interactions and plays a crucial role in the quality of conversations, particularly when dealing with sensitive and personal topics. As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into everyday applications, their ability to effectively exhibit empathy becomes paramount. AI agents are being deployed across a wide variety of contexts—from programming assistance to mental health support—that require varying levels of empathy. However, existing conversational AI systems struggle to demonstrate context-appropriate empathy and tend to provide uniform empathy levels, leading to unsatisfactory user experiences. This challenge is even more pronounced when empathy needs to be tailored based on specific user contexts or tasks, such as providing emotional support during a distressing life event versus offering guidance for professional development.

Several studies have explored empathy in conversational AI, with many relying on the inherent reasoning and commonsense abilities of LLMs to produce generic empathetic responses, often lacking task-specific focus and contextual adaptability. Most of these works are prompt-based, investigating the impact of techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), self-reflection, and similar prompting methods to enhance empathetic expression

Motivated by these limitations, in this work, we introduce a novel framework for developing and evaluating contextually empathetic LLMs. We start by analyzing a real-world conversational dataset

These insights reveal that empathy is highly context-dependent and that the perceived quality of conversations is significantly correlated with the level and type of empathy demonstrated by the assistant. We leverage these findings to inform the design of empathy measurement methods and reward models that evaluate and refine the empathetic capabilities of LLMs.

To address the limitations of data scarcity and variability in empathy-driven conversations, we develop a synthetic multi-turn conversation generation pipeline. By clustering tasks with similar empathy requirements, we generate diverse empathetic conversations, steering responses toward well-defined empathy patterns based on real-world user insights. To further enhance empathy modeling, we employ empathetic expert adapters trained specifically for context-sensitive empathy, ensuring that the LLM is capable of adapting its empathetic tone and style across a range of user needs.

Our evaluation spans multiple experimental setups, including a Baseline LLM without empathy-specific training used in its inherent form, a System Prompt approach where empathy guidelines are injected into the context of the baseline LLM, and our proposed Empathetic Expert Adapter approach. Empirical results demonstrate that the Empathetic Expert Adapters outperform both the Baseline and System Prompt settings, consistently aligning with the defined empathy patterns and maintaining empathy across multi-turn conversations. The ability of Empathetic Expert Adapters to sustain empathy throughout extended dialogues makes them a powerful tool for improving user satisfaction in conversational AI systems.

This study not only showcases the potential of training contextually empathetic agents but also introduces practical methodologies for empathy measurement, synthetic data augmentation, and context-specific fine-tuning. By advancing the empathetic abilities of LLMs, we take an important step towards creating conversational agents that can more effectively adapt to the users’ needs, enhancing their experience in emotionally meaningful and impactful ways.

II
Background

Empathy in AI agents has been a focal point of active research, particularly in the development of chatbots and social robots that aim to support and engage users more effectively. Empathetic AI agents are designed to recognize and respond to human emotions and experiences, thereby fostering more natural and meaningful interactions. For instance, Leite et al.
[
20
]
emphasized the role of empathy in human–robot interaction, suggesting that empathetic behaviors can significantly enhance user engagement. Similarly, Paiva et al.
[
28
]
provided a comprehensive overview of emotion modeling for social robots, highlighting the potential of empathetic agents in various applications. Other studies have also explored the integration of empathy in conversational agents to improve user satisfaction and trust. For example, Lubis et al.
[
25
]
developed an empathetic dialog system that elicits positive emotions through affective backchannels.

To facilitate research in empathetic AI agents, several studies have been conducted to collect relevant datasets. For instance, Rashkin et al.
[
30
]
introduced the “EmpatheticDialogues” dataset of 25K conversations between two people grounded in emotional situations, which has been instrumental in training models for empathetic response generation. In a separate effort, Liu et al.
[
23
]
introduced the “Emotional Support Conversation” dataset, consisting of 10K multi-turn dialogues focused on providing emotional support, aiming to model how humans offer assistance in distressing situations. While these and other datasets have enabled much research in the context of empathetic AI agents, they do not fully capture the nuances of context-specific empathy required in diverse interaction scenarios.
To address this gap, our work builds on the recently released
SENSE-7
dataset by Suh et al.
[
35
]
, which contains 672 conversations where 109 participants provided per-turn subjective ratings of empathy while interacting with AI agents. Using this dataset, we conduct a detailed analysis that expands on their findings and provides deeper insights into how people’s expectations and perceptions of empathy vary across different interaction contexts.

Studies on context-specific empathetic agents have typically focused on exploring one application at a time, tailoring the agent’s empathetic responses to a specific domain. For instance, Lisetti et al.
[
22
]
developed an empathetic virtual counselor to promote behavior change in healthcare settings, while Fitzpatrick et al.
[
13
]
evaluated a conversational agent named Woebot for delivering cognitive behavioral therapy to young adults with symptoms of depression and anxiety. These agents demonstrate the effectiveness of empathy in specialized contexts but are limited in their ability to adapt to different user tasks or scenarios. In contrast, our work aims to understand the user’s context dynamically and adjust the agent’s empathetic displays accordingly, providing a more versatile and naturalistic interaction experience across various applications.

III
Methodology

Our approach consists of multiple stages, beginning with extracting insights from our real-world conversational dataset, followed by the development of synthetic data generation, steering, reward models, and training empathetic expert adapters for context-specific empathy.

III-A

Insights from Real Conversations

We extend the analysis of a real-world conversational dataset (
SENSE-7

[
35
]
), which includes 672 multi-turn interactions between users and AI assistants across eight distinct tasks conducted “in the wild” with participants interacting naturally with the chatbots who reported their desired and perceived level of empathy before and after each conversation, respectively. In particular, participants reported perceived empathy along multiple dimensions such as Affective, Cognitive, and Contextual. The eight tasks include navigating work issues, career/self-improvement challenges, overcoming personal issues, writing assistance, thinking through a work assignment, learning new skills, handling distressing situations, and navigating social situations.
Participants also responded to several 5-point Likert scale questions focused on assessing the level at which the AI assistant achieved success, the level of user engagement, the positivity of the interaction, and the likelihood of future use among others. In addition, participants also provided qualitative feedback on their experience. The dataset includes 151 participants, with 109 completing at least five conversations and the exit survey, resulting in 672 fully labeled conversations. Our analysis of the dataset yielded two primary findings with further details included in the Appendix A:

- Average perceived empathy highly correlates with self-reported satisfaction after the conversation (Pearson’s Correlation = 0.64).

- Desired empathy varies significantly depending on the task and context. For example, as shown by the pre-desired empathy bars in Figure
5
, tasks around overcoming personal issues and distressing situations require much more empathy compared to tasks on learning new skills or thinking through work assignments.

These insights were crucial for shaping the empathy measurement models and guiding our synthetic data generation and training processes.

III-B

Synthetic Conversational Pipeline

Based on our analysis of the real-world dataset, we observe consistent patterns in the relationship between the gap in pre-desired empathy levels and the expected post-perceived empathy, as well as user satisfaction signals (Appendix B-B). Leveraging these insights, we group the eight tasks into four distinct clusters—
Distressing/Social/Personal Situations
,
Learning Skills
,
Work Issues/Career/Self-Improvement
, and
Work Assignment/Help with Writing
—each sharing similar levels and types of empathy; thereby streamlining the process of generating new synthetic conversations as well as designing and steering the responses based on the shared empathy patterns within each group.

Multi-Turn Conversation Generation

Our multi-turn generation pipeline consists of several stages. We first leverage GPT-4o to come up with initial questions per task cluster, simulating the opening prompts of the users. To ensure proximity to our real dataset distribution, we design detailed prompts consisting of In-Context Learning (ICL) examples of our real dataset. Figure 10 shows the prompt used with GPT-4o for the generation of the first user turns mimicking the initial prompt at the start of a conversation.

Figure 11 shows a sample of the corresponding generated questions. In order to be close to the original distribution of our real dataset, we incorporate several In-Context Learning (ICL) examples in the body of the prompt as well as a description of the task category at the beginning of the prompt which in Figure 10 is the “Distressing/Social/Personal Situations” task cluster.

To maximize the diversity of the generated questions and to avoid generic questions, we ask GPT-4o to produce 30 different questions and call the LLM 40 times with varying decoding parameters (Temperature and Top-p). We also expand the exploratory space of the LLM by asking it to:

“
When generating questions, consider those that individuals from diverse backgrounds, such as men and women, managers, researchers, CEOs, parents, engineers, family members, and workers aged 20 to 65 might ask an AI assistant.
Don’t ask general questions; be very specific and detailed in your questions, depicting real-life scenarios, situations, people, concepts, and emotions.
”
Next, we utilize Llama-3-8B-Instruct’s
[
11
]
auto-regression capabilities to generate subsequent assistant and user turns. The assistant’s response is generated directly from the initial user input. For user turns, we employ the Chat Template, appending “
<|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>\n\n
” to the previous context. The LLM’s auto-regressive nature then predicts the next user tokens. This process continues iteratively until reaching the desired number of turns.

Steering Toward Empathy Preferences

Building on our earlier insights, we define specific empathy levels and patterns for each task cluster. Two of the authors of this paper, drawing on their backgrounds in empathy and affective sciences, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), and computer science, collaborated to design the target empathy patterns, ensuring that responses within each task cluster align with the desired empathy goals.

Incorporating these defined empathy patterns, we craft prompts that guide the assistant’s responses toward either empathetic or non-empathetic behaviors within each task cluster. We utilize GPT-4o, first presenting the entire conversation context, and then simulating the dialogue turn by turn, instructing the model to follow the empathy guidelines embedded in our prompts. Finally, we steer both the real and synthetic conversations. Please see Appendix B-B for more details.

III-E
2
Evaluation Metric

We utilize our developed reward and preference models, as mentioned in Subsection
III-C
to evaluate and measure empathy patterns across the three experimental settings.

As discussed earlier, our task-specific learning-based reward models assign a “
Reward Score
” from 0 to 1 for each conversation, with higher scores indicating closer alignment to predefined empathy patterns. The models are trained on data separated by task clusters, providing a measure of empathy performance across the Baseline, System Prompt, and Empathetic Expert Adapter settings depending on the task’s context. These reward models are designed to be context-specific, allowing them to evaluate empathy patterns within each task cluster individually.
Additionally, we train a generic reward model on combined data from all tasks, which is used to assess empathy-level adjustments across the entire dataset in relation to users’ pre-desired empathy levels as shown in Figure
5
.

Figure
4
and Table
II
show the evaluation statistics of the generic trained reward model. Figure
3
shows the prediction distribution of the trained preference model on the test set consisting of 147 conversations corresponding to the
Distressing/Social/Personal Situations
task cluster. We experiment with different
\beta
values during training and witness its reverse relation with the sharpness of the predictions. As shown in Figure
3
, with smaller
\beta
the distributions are clearly distinguishable and we use this model for the evaluations of our adapters.

In addition, we use our task-specific LLM-based reward models, which also assign a score from 0 to 1 for each conversation. Unlike the learning-based reward models, this score is derived from how we prompt the LLM to reason about the input, without additional training. This “
LLM Score
” metric serves as an additional metric to assess empathy alignment with the pre-defined empathy patterns for each setting within each task cluster.

Finally, we employ task-specific preference models, each trained on conversations within a particular task cluster, to evaluate which of the three settings generates the most preferred responses. From this, we define a metric called “
Preference Win Rate (P-WR)
”, which indicates the percentage of times a setting’s conversation is preferred over the other two for a given task cluster.

The use of three evaluation metrics ensures a comprehensive and multi-faceted assessment of empathy. The Reward Score from the task-specific reward models provides a quantitative measure of how well responses align with predefined empathy patterns, while the Preference Win Rate provides a comparative perspective, indicating which setting generates responses most preferred by users in each task cluster. Additionally, the LLM Score leverages the reasoning capabilities of a large language model to evaluate empathy without additional training, serving as an independent validation metric. Together, these metrics complement each other, addressing the limitations of individual measures and providing deeper insights into the model’s empathetic behavior. V
Discussion

Empathetic Expert Adapters vs. System Prompts

In this section, we discuss the key findings of our study, highlighting the effectiveness of context-specific empathetic expert adapters in enhancing empathy alignment across multi-turn conversations. We compare the performance of different settings and models, explore the implications of model architecture and scale, and analyze the challenges in sustaining empathy over longer dialogues. Finally, we outline the broader implications of our work and identify promising directions for future research.

In our experiments, we observe that Empathetic Expert Adapters consistently outperform System Prompts. This advantage can be directly attributed to another key finding: empathetic expert adapters are far more effective at sustaining empathetic behavior as conversations get longer. The reason for this is partly due to the length of the system prompts we use for guiding empathy patterns. These prompts, in order to capture all of the detailed empathy patterns, tend to be long, and as the conversation context expands, the system prompt’s influence diminishes. The large context dilutes the effect of the empathy patterns, and with each turn, less empathy is expressed in the generated response. Due to the auto-regressive nature of LLMs, this drift accumulates, causing the model to further stray from empathy with every turn, also known as “context contamination”
[
3
]
.

We observe this phenomenon even in our synthetic conversation generation pipeline (Appendix B-A), where shorter and more focused system prompts result in more coherent and empathetic conversation turns. Longer prompts often lead to inconsistencies in the generated dialogues, highlighting how challenging it can be for models to balance context with empathy when the prompt becomes too extensive.

Even in the controlled setup of Table
V
, where we condition all settings with an equal empathetic context, we still see that Empathetic Expert Adapters maintain empathy far better than the system prompt, while the Baseline setting remains almost entirely non-empathetic. This is because the empathetic expert adapters are actually trained across varying conversation lengths to predict assistant responses that align with predefined empathy patterns, making them more adaptable to different conversation dynamics.

Impact of the Underlying Model

Our findings also reveal that the underlying architecture of the model impacts its ability to follow instructions and handle empathy. Llama-3-8B-Instruct, with its larger parameter count, superior instruction-following capabilities, and better long-context handling, consistently outperforms Phi-3-mini-128k-instruct (3.8B) across the corresponding settings. Llama-3’s architecture allows it to manage complex empathy patterns and long dialogue contexts more effectively, while Phi-3, although compact and efficient, struggles with sustaining empathy, especially in prolonged conversations.
This becomes even more evident when comparing the performance gap between the System Prompt and Empathetic Expert Adapter settings for Phi-3 versus Llama-3. The larger gap for Phi-3 indicates that the System Prompt setting relies heavily on the underlying model’s ability to handle long contexts and follow instructions, which are less effective in Phi-3 compared to Llama-3.

This performance difference highlights the importance of factors such as model size, training data, and efficiency trade-offs in empathy-driven tasks. As Llama-3 demonstrates, models that can handle complex instructions and retain context over multiple turns are more suited for tasks requiring sustained empathy. Figure
6
summarizes the insights discussed above, visually representing the empathy-maintaining performance across the different settings and models.

Privacy-Preserving Analysis

Another important implication of our learning-based reward modeling approach is its ability to enable “eyes-off” analysis, which is critical for preserving privacy in empathetic conversations. Given the sensitive nature of such data
[
12
]
, our method allows organizations to provide embeddings extracted by the frozen backbone
f_{\theta}
, enabling the training of the regression head without direct access to raw conversation content. This ensures that empathy evaluation can be conducted without compromising user privacy.

Future Work and Research Directions

While our approach focuses on improving average empathy in conversations, empathy is multidimensional as shown in Figure 7. Future research could explore the seven dimensions of empathy in greater depth, their interplay, and how they can be tailored to individual user needs, particularly for personalization purposes.

In addition, we train and evaluate the empathetic expert adapters independently in this work. One promising direction for future research involves developing Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures
[
14
]
that embed multiple empathetic expert adapters and fuse them with varying weights depending on the task progression or conversation context. This approach would enable more nuanced emotional engagement, allowing for a fully end-to-end pipeline that adapts to shifting conversational needs.

Moreover, our reward models, which are currently used to evaluate empathy, could be utilized as aligners within Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) frameworks to optimize for empathy as part of the training process directly. Instead of relying solely on supervised fine-tuning, as we did in this work, integrating reward models into an RL-based approach would allow for more dynamic alignment with empathy patterns, continuously refining the model’s responses based on user feedback and predefined empathy metrics. This could lead to more adaptive and contextually aligned conversational systems.

Limitations

While our study shows promising results for fusing context-specific empathy into LLMs, it also has some limitations.

Firstly, due to resource constraints, we focused on models with 8 billion and 3.8 billion parameters, using 4-bit quantization for the underlying LLMs. Larger-scale models with full 32-bit precision may provide more comprehensive insights. Plus, because our data consists of multi-turn conversations, the memory resources were easily saturated in our experiments after a certain number of turns. Conducting experiments with much longer conversations and more turns could offer deeper insights, especially regarding the models’ empathy-maintaining capabilities and the generalization potential of the empathetic expert adapters.

Another potential limitation of our approach is the models’ ability to generalize empathy effectively across less-presented scenarios in the dataset. Even though the defined empathy patterns were based on comprehensive analyses of the dataset conversations and interactions, there may still be certain task types or topics that were underrepresented or not present at all in the dataset which demand different patterns of empathy.
Furthermore, because our synthetic conversations were generated using in-context examples from the real conversations, the synthetic data closely mirrors the distribution of the original dataset. This grounding helps maintain consistency but also means that any gaps in the real data could be reflected in the synthetic data as well. Thus, the generalization of our empathy patterns and empathetic expert adapters to unfamiliar topics or less common scenarios is not entirely clear.

Lastly, while our metrics and synthetic datasets offer valuable insights into the agents’ empathy alignment, further human evaluation is essential to determine the real-world impact on user satisfaction. Specifically, human feedback would validate whether aligning empathy levels with user expectations consistently enhances satisfaction and engagement. Although our findings are promising, incorporating user studies in future work would provide a more nuanced understanding of how empathy alignment translates to improved conversational experiences.

Ethical Considerations

Empathetic conversational agents can indeed foster trust and enhance user interactions, but there is a risk of misuse, particularly in emotionally sensitive or vulnerable situations. It is crucial to ensure that AI systems are not leveraged to manipulate or exploit users by overusing empathy, especially in ways that could harm the user’s well-being. Transparency must be maintained throughout the deployment of such systems, ensuring that users are fully aware they are interacting with AI and understand how their data might be used for future improvements. In addition, while designing empathetic conversational agents, it is crucial to strike a balance between providing empathetic responses and avoiding excessive anthropomorphism. Overly human-like interactions can risk deceiving users into believing they are engaging with sentient beings, potentially fostering over-reliance on AI systems for emotional support. Maintaining transparency about the AI’s nature and purpose, while ensuring responses are empathetic yet grounded, is essential to fostering trust and minimizing ethical concerns.

Our synthetic conversational generation and steering pipelines also introduce ethical concerns. While it is designed to flexibly generate task-specific conversations, there is a potential risk that malicious actors could exploit this flexibility to simulate harmful behaviors of any topic. For example, scenarios could be generated where users or AI assistants engage in manipulative or harmful behaviors (a.k.a., dark empathy). Throughout the course of the conversation, either party could strategically and covertly influence the other to participate in dangerous or unethical actions. However, throughout this study, we strictly limited the pipeline to generate benign, task-specific conversations, where empathetic interactions were the goal.

All in all, we hope our research on empathetic conversational AI contributes positively by enhancing user experiences, fostering more meaningful interactions, and helping people feel understood and supported. By addressing ethical considerations and ensuring responsible deployment, we aim for this work to have a beneficial impact, both in practical applications and in promoting trust between users and AI systems.

VI
Conclusion

This study conducts a comprehensive analysis of a real-world conversational dataset to understand the vital role of empathy in enhancing user satisfaction. Empathy emerges as context-specific, with its patterns and levels varying depending on the task. Through these insights, tasks with similar empathy requirements are clustered, and distinct empathy patterns and levels for each group are defined with guidance from experts in empathy, affective sciences, and human-computer interaction.
Our methodology introduces several key innovations: the development of steering prompts inspired by our defined patterns to guide conversations towards desired empathy behaviors, the creation of reward models leveraging real dataset annotations and steered conversation preferences, a synthetic conversation generation pipeline for task-specific data augmentation, and fine-tuning of empathetic expert adapters on empathy-steered clusters allowing each adapter to specialize in the appropriate empathy patterns and levels for nuanced, contextually relevant responses.

Comparative experiments across baseline, system prompt, and empathetic expert adapter settings with Llama-3 and Phi-3 LLMs reveal the superior improvement of empathetic expert adapters in empathy alignment, particularly in sustaining empathy over longer, multi-turn conversations. While system prompts outperform the baseline, they rely heavily on the underlying model’s instruction-following and long-context capabilities. Expert adapters, however, maintain empathetic patterns more reliably as conversations progress.
This work opens several avenues for future research, including in-depth exploration of various dimensions of empathy in AI interactions, investigation of Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures with multi-expert fusion algorithms, and integration of reward models into reinforcement learning frameworks for further refinement of empathetic and personalized AI systems.

Appendix D
Maintaining Empathy

Figure
6
clearly illustrates how the three settings—Empathetic Expert Adapter, System Prompt, and Baseline—perform in terms of maintaining empathy as the number of conversation turns increases. The Empathetic Expert Adapter setting consistently shows the least distribution shift for both Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Phi-3-mini-128k-instruct (3.8B) models, indicating its robustness in sustaining empathy even as conversations grow longer. Phi-3, with its weaker instruction-following and context-handling abilities, is more severely impacted, showing a significant shift in empathy distribution as conversations lengthen, though it still performs better than the Baseline setting.

The Baseline setting is the most vulnerable, showing the largest distribution shift for both models, especially in extended conversations. This lack of empathy maintenance underscores its limitations in generating empathetic responses over multiple turns, making it the least reliable setting for empathy-driven tasks.
