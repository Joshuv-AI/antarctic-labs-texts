Ultrahigh charge utilization of C−C bridge-dependent MOF@COFs empowering sustainable removal of trace pharmaceuticals
Authors: Suxin Zhou, Cheng Mao, Yixin Kuang, Liwu Gan, Jinglin Chen, Xiaoying Feng, Luyi Chen, Juan Zheng, Gangfeng Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pharmaceutical contaminations pose serious threats to water quality and human health, while photocatalysis technology provides a solution to the decontamination. However, limited light energy transformation and severe charge recombination hamper the application of photocatalysts for broad-spectrum removal of trace pharmaceuticals. Here, the C − C bond as connection junction served to trigger the versatile synthesis of core-shell MOF@COFs with superior charge separation capability than corresponding parent COFs and MOFs, including MIL@V-TZ, MIL@DaV-TZ, and MIL@DaV-TAPB. The C − C bridge induced lower exciton binding energy (Eb, 42.66 meV) and overlap integral of electron-hole distribution (Sr, 0.57), endowing MIL@V-TZ with photocatalytic efficiencies of 9.8 − 70.2 min−1·g−1 for tetracycline, diclofenac, acetaminophen, and difloxacin. Based on the in-situ grown MIL@V-TZ film, a flow-through system was constructed to achieve ultra-efficient (>99%) and sustainable (up to 6000 min) pharmaceuticals removal without sacrificial agents. In-situ experiments revealed that the electrons were rapidly transferred and highly utilized to form •O2−, dominating the degradation of pharmaceuticals with reduced toxicity. By unveiling the role of C − C bridge in controllable assembly of heterostructures and boosting electron transfer, this work presents an avenue for water decontamination.

Introduction

Tackling the water scarcity or insecurity is a particularly pivotal challenge worldwide, while the ubiquitous emerging organic contaminants with high toxicity, bioaccumulation and strong durability exacerbate the water quality issue, for instance, pharmaceuticals1–3. A global-scale study of pharmaceutical pollution reveals that the detected concentrations are higher than the safe level for aquatic organisms at 270 locations in 104 countries4. Particularly the COVID-19 pandemic increases dosage of pharmaceuticals, amplifying concerns over the water pollution5. It is urgent to effectively eliminate these pollutants for the fresh water supply6–9. The flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration are conventional technologies for the initial treatment of pharmaceuticals, and special processes such as ozonation, activated carbon adsorption, and reverse osmosis can further promote their removal with the average efficiency of 65%, likely involving the excessive energy consumption and strict management of aqueous or solvent waste streams10. Consequently, the development of cost-effective and sustainable techniques to improve the decontamination performance is ongoing.

Responding to the call of the Sustainable Development Goals of the United Nations, photocatalysis technology provides an alternative way to address pharmaceutical contaminants by the environmentally benign appeal of light as a source of reactivity11–13, depending on the high-quality and high-performance photocatalysts. As an extensively studied class of crystalline and porous materials, metal organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have distinctive merits including large specific surface area, abundant active site, and fine-tunable pore dimension, which render them promising candidates for the treatment of contaminated water through photocatalysis14–16. Extensive efforts have been devoted to persistently enhancing photocatalytic activity of MOFs and COFs, such as donor-acceptor engineering17–19, metal-doping20–22, and heterostructure constructing23–25. In particular, MOF/COF heterostructures are expected to exhibit synergistic advantages from hierarchical pores and enriched functional sites of parent components26–29. For instance, the photodegradation rate of NH2-MIL-68@TPA-COF, as the first MOF@COF core-shell hybrid, for Rh B has been 1.4-fold faster than that of NH2-MIL-6830. Recently, an ionic ZIF-8@iCOF has obtained antibacterial efficiency of 99.99999% benefiting from the improvement of solar energy utilization31. Besides, the interface between MOFs and COFs influences the trajection of charge flow, and is also critical to regulate photocatalytic performance32–34. Therefore, exploring effective and versatile strategies to controllably construct MOF/COF heterostructures with an intimate interface that enhances charge separation is highly desired.

As the bridge for MOFs and COFs, interfacial linkages/interactions are important to rationally manipulate interface formation and interframework connectivity32,35. The mismatches of morphology, porosity, and crystallinity between different components present great challenges for the construction of MOF/COF heterostructures, and current studies focus on the construction by π−π interaction, imine bond, and boroxine ring26,36. The reported interfacial linkages/interactions may cause the local structural polarization or electron delocalization effect, leading to fast charge transfer in the designed heterostructures33,37,38. Currently, the effect of interfacial connections on structures and properties of MOF@COFs still lacks exploration due to the finite accessible connections and the challenges from electron beam-sensitive nature of MOF/COF. In our previous work, an alternative strategy employing C − C bond has been proposed to construct MOF@COFs with local interpenetrated structure and superior performance than corresponding imine linked composite39, while the crucial role of C − C bond in feasible interface engineering and the behavior of charge transfer still remains elusive. Inspired by it, the feasibility of introducing varied blocks in this C−C-linked strategy is expected to promote a deeper understanding of the dynamics of photogenerated charge separation and migration, promising to yield highly efficient MOF@COFs.

Herein, we successfully synthesized three different MOF@COF heterostructures with a strongly coupled interface, including MIL@V-TZ, MIL@DaV-TZ, and MIL@DaV-TAPB. Moreover, the interfacial C−C connection enabled weakening the exciton effect and facilitating charge separation. In situ experiments (such as DRIFTS and XPS) and DFT theory calculations gained comprehensive insights into the swift generation of superoxide radical (•O2−) benefiting from ultrahigh charge utilization, thereby endowing these MOF@COFs a dramatic enhancement of photocatalytic activity. Remarkably, by strategically organizing the building blocks of COFs, the MOF@COF of MIL@V-TZ displayed higher removal efficiencies for diverse pharmaceutical contaminants (k = 0.049 − 0.351 min−1), which significantly outperformed the state-of-the-art catalysts, including the corresponding imine-linked composite. It was further assembled into a continuous-flow-through device to degrade trace pharmaceuticals into chemicals with reduced toxicity, underscoring its practical applicability. This work demonstrates a universal strategy to fabricate ultra-efficient photocatalysts of MOF/COF heterostructures and provides a sustainable avenue for the degradation of pharmaceutical pollutants in the ambient environment.

Results and discussion

Characterization of C − C linked MOF@COFs

The C − C bond as the connection junction served to trigger the versatile synthesis of MOF@COFs. As a typical MOF, MIL-68 (In), with a Kagomé-like lattice consisting of InO4(OH)2 infinite chains linked through terephthalic acid (H2BDC) and 2-vinylterephthalic acid (BDC − C = C) ligands, was chosen as the core due to the excellent thermal and chemical stability, high surface area and abundant porosity, as well as low toxity40–42. The morphology of C − C linked MOF@COFs was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1b−d). The noticeable change could be found compared with their pure components (Supplementary Fig. 1), where rougher ribbon-shaped, honeycomb-like, and granular shell could be tightly anchored onto the smooth rod-like core, respectively for MIL@V-TZ (Fig. 1b), MIL@DaV-TZ (Fig. 1c), and MIL@DaV-TAPB (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, low-dose high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) was adopted to more intuitively reveal the microstructure of MOF@COFs (Fig. 1b−d and Supplementary Figs. 2–11). For MIL@V-TZ, the fringe with a spacing of 2.90 nm could be assigned to the pore of V-TZ (Supplementary Fig. 2), well matching the distance of two carbon atoms in the structure mode (Supplementary Fig. 3)43,44. The distinct lattice fringes with the d-spacing of 3.04 and 1.48 nm, respectively, corresponded to (100) and (200) planes of V-TZ (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting the formation of V-TZ shell45. In the MOF core, the (110) crystal plane of MIL with a lattice spacing of 1.92 nm could be observed (Supplementary Fig. 2), verifying the well-retained crystallinity of MIL in hybrid process46. Besides, the fringe with a spacing of 2.73 nm also emerged in the core (Supplementary Fig. 5), which was similar to the pore size of V-TZ shell, indicating the formation of local interpenetrated structure47,48. Similarly, for MIL@DaV-TZ, the fringe with a spacing of 2.66 nm (Supplementary Fig. 6) attributed to the pore of DaV-TZ was observed in MOF core, and the lattice fringe with a spacing of 3.01 nm (Supplementary Fig. 7) attributed to DaV-TZ (100) plane appeared in shell. For MIL@DaV-TAPB, the features of DaV-TAPB also were found in MOF core and COF shell (Supplementary Figs. 8–11).

In addition, the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images showed the uniform distribution of C, N, and In elements in the C − C linked MOF@COFs (Supplementary Fig. 12). The line-scan results demonstrated the shell thicknesses of these three materials were about 286, 153, and 154 nm (Supplementary Fig. 13). In the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 14), new peaks which were attributed to (100) and (200) planes of COFs also appeared at ≈ 2.8 ° and 5.7 °, accompanied by the well-retained characteristic peaks (at ≈ 4.7 ° and 9.4 °) of MOF, indicating the successful formation of highly crystalline MOF@COFs. The above results verified that three highly crystalline core-shell MOF@COFs with local component interpenetration were successfully synthesized by utilizing C − C covalent connection.

From Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy results (Supplementary Fig. 15), the new C = N stretching band (1602 cm−1) and obvious attenuation of C = O stretching band (1686 cm−1), indicated the successful completion of Schiff-base condensation. Moreover, the co-existence of MIL and COFs was also corroborated by N2 adsorption and desorption measurements (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Figs. 16–18). The results manifested that obtained MOF@COFs demonstrated the combination of type-I and type-IV isotherms (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17a and 18a), and pore diameter distribution centered at 1.2 nm and 2.5 nm (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Figs. 17b, 18b), respectively originating from the micropore of MIL and mesopore of COFs39. Compared with parent MIL, decreased pore size and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of MIL-V or MIL-DaV were attributed to the incorporation of aldehyde monomers into the pores or adjacent layers of MIL, which was in favor of subsequently growing COFs. To further verify the tight combination of MIL and these COFs, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Figs. 19, 20). In the high-resolution XPS spectra of N 1 s for MIL@V-TZ, it could be obviously found that the peaks assigned to C = N, C − N, and N − H showed negative shifts compared to the parent V-TZ (Fig. 1f). Likewise, the peaks assigned to C = N and C − N in C 1 s XPS spectra also displayed negative shifts (Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast, compared to parent MIL, two peaks in In 3 d XPS spectra of MIL@V-TZ, underwent the significant migration to higher energy level (Fig. 1g). The results implied charge redistributed between MIL and V-TZ components in as-prepared heterostructure, and partial electron transferred from MIL to V-TZ.

Additionally, emergence of a peak corresponding C − C stretching vibration (1107 cm−1)49 in the Raman spectrum of MIL@V-TZ further substantiated the formation of C − C bridge to connect MIL and V-TZ (Supplementary Fig. 21). In 1H NMR results (Supplementary Figs. 22–24), the peak at 8.03 ppm was assigned to H2BDC, and the peaks at 8.14, 7.87 − 7.89, 7.33 − 7.40, 5.78 − 5.82 and 5.40 − 5.42 ppm were attributed to BDC − C = C. Compared with the 1H NMR of MIL, it could be found that the amount of C = C from BDC − C = C decreased, and peaks of methylene appeared (2.72 and 2.88 ppm) in which of MIL-V. These results provided evidence for the successful coupling of BDC − C = C in MIL and V molecules. And from the 1H NMR of MIL-V (Supplementary Fig. 24), the remaining NMR signals from the non-coupled BDC − C = C could also be detected, demonstrating that part of BDC − C = C was involved in the coupling reaction. The incorporation of V monomers was in favor of subsequently growing COFs. Moreover, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Supplementary Fig. 25) indicated the excellent thermal stability of as-fabricated MOF@COFs. These results verified the formation of strongly-coupled interface between MOF and COFs by the C − C junction, breaking the shackle of interface mismatches and favoring for electron transfer.

Optical properties and charge carrier behaviors of MOF@COFs

To explore the role of C − C junction in boosting photocatalytic performance (Fig. 2a), multiple spectroscopic techniques were employed. From the spectra of UV-Visible (UV-Vis) diffuse reflectance (DRS) (Supplementary Figs. 26, 27) and calculated bandgap energies (Eg) in the Tauc plots (Fig. 2b), C − C linked MOF@COFs presented apparent red shifts and narrower optical bandgaps in comparison with MIL (3.54 eV) due to the integration with COFs (2.24 − 2.41 eV), implying higher efficiency of solar energy utilization. And calculated Eg were 2.39, 2.40, and 2.45 eV for MIL@V-TZ, MIL@DaV-TZ, and MIL@DaV-TAPB, respectively, indicating that electrons of MIL@V-TZ were more easily excited and transitioned under light irradiation. Temperature-dependent photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded to investigate the behavior of excitons (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29). On account of the thermally activated nonradiative recombination process50, the integrated PL intensities of all samples gradually decreased with the increased temperature, and calculated exciton binding energy (Eb) of MIL@V-TZ (42.66 meV) was lower than those of other MOF@COFs (Fig. 2c) and its parent components (Supplementary Fig. 29). From the results of steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 30), all C − C linked MOF@COFs demonstrated stronger quenching emission in comparison to MIL, accompanied by decrease in average PL lifetime from 3772 ps of MIL to 328, 679, 742 ps, respectively for MIL@V-TZ, MIL@DaV-TZ, and MIL@DaV-TAPB. Particularly for MIL@V-TZ, it showed weaker PL signal and shorter lifetime than those of other MOF@COFs and pure V-TZ (Supplementary Fig. 30), inhibiting the electron-hole recombination. Additionally, the photoexcited carrier behaviors were further investigated by femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS). As depicted in Fig. 2f, MIL@V-TZ featured an obvious negative signal assigned to the ground state bleaching, and a broad positive signal attributed to the excited-state absorption. All C − C linked MOF@COF samples exhibited stronger TAS signals than that of MIL, indicating higher population of excited-state carriers in MOF@COFs (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 31). Through the relaxation kinetics of excited-state charge carriers at 740 nm (Supplementary Fig. 32), it could be noticed that MIL@V-TZ possessed a significantly prolonged average lifetime of charge separated state (240.5 ps) compared to V-TZ (31.0 ps), effectively decreasing the probability of charge recombination and promoting the charge transfer51,52. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) for MIL@V-TZ and its pure components was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 33), and a smaller semicircular radius in the Nyquist plot of MIL@V-TZ implied the lower charge transfer resistance. Thus, these results verified the higher efficiency of solar energy utilization, weaker exciton effect and inhibited charge recombination in constructed MOF@COFs. In particular, the MIL@V-TZ possessed the highest charge separation capability.

Further insights came from the time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations, which disclosed electron transfer pathway and electron-hole separation capability for MIL@V-TZ. The overlap integral of the electron-hole distribution (Sr), and centroid distance of electron-hole (D) of MIL@V-TZ and its parent components were calculated (Fig. 2g–i). The photogenerated electrons mainly located around the In atoms, while the holes almost distributed on the C − C covalent junction (Fig. 2i), revealing that the electrons transferred from V-TZ to MIL via the C − C junction. Moreover, the Sr of three materials were in the order of MIL@V-TZ (0.570) < V-TZ (0.797) < MIL (0.976), and the D displayed an inverse trend, following the order of MIL@V-TZ (4.407 Å) > V-TZ (1.343 Å) > MIL (0.281 Å), which further demonstrated more efficient charge separation capability for MIL@V-TZ.

Based on the above experimental results and theoretical calculations, it could be concluded that C − C bridge induced stronger visible light absorption, weaker exciton effect, accelerated charge transfer and separation, endowing C − C linked MOF@COFs with ultrahigh charge utilization capability and exceptional photocatalytic activity. Compared with MIL@DaV-TZ and MIL@DaV-TAPB, the MIL@V-TZ exhibited more superior photocatalytic activity, due to the lower Eg (2.39 eV), Eb (42.66 meV) and average PL lifetime (328 ps), and extended average lifetime of charge separated state (240.5 ps).

Photodegradation performance of MOF@COF catalysts

The photocatalytic performance of all samples in regard to degradation of tetracycline (TC), a model pollutant of pharmaceutical, was investigated. As depicted in Fig. 3a, as-prepared three MOF@COFs demonstrated exceptional photocatalytic removal efficiencies. The corresponding TC degradation rate constants (k) were calculated using the pseudo-first order kinetic model (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 34), and the k values were in the order of MIL@V-TZ (0.0773 min−1) > MIL@DaV-TZ (0.0517 min−1) > MIL@DaV-TAPB (0.0300 min−1) > V-TZ (0.0229 min−1) > MIL (0.0011 min−1) with statistically significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 35), further evidencing more rapid reaction kinetics of C − C linked MOF@COFs. According to the determined amounts of COFs by 1H NMR (Supplementary Figs. 23, 36–38), it demonstrated less effect on the activity of the as-prepared MOF@COFs (Supplementary Fig. 39). Remarkably, the k value of MIL@V-TZ was 3.4 times greater than that of parent V-TZ and 70.3 times greater than that of parent MIL, illustrating the drastically boosted catalytic activity. Moreover, the degradation performance of MIL@V-TZ significantly outperformed that for the physical mixture of MIL and V-TZ, further confirming the superiority of integrating MOFs and COFs as heterostructures (Supplementary Fig. 40). Besides, the apparent quantum yield of MIL@V-TZ for TC degradation at 450 nm was measured to be 0.97% with light intensity of 40 mW·cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 41). The determined apparent activation energy of TC degradation by the Arrhenius equation (Supplementary Figs. 42, 43) was 6.9 kJ·mol−1, and was smaller than that in a typical reaction directly breaking the chemical bond53. Moreover, we assessed the practicability of MIL@V-TZ for dealing with multiple pharmaceutical pollutants including TC, diclofenac (DFC), acetaminophen (APAP), and difloxacin hydrochloride (DIX), under varied operating conditions (pH, pollutant and photocatalyst concentrations, Supplementary Figs. 44–48). The MIL@V-TZ could achieve nearly complete degradation within 20, 10, 60, and 60 min, respectively for TC, DFC, APAP, and DIX, with corresponding k values of 0.188, 0.351, 0.049, and 0.061 min−1 (Fig. 3c–f), indicating its high decontamination efficiencies for broad-spectrum pharmaceuticals. In addition, the degradation performance of MIL@V-TZ was compared with that of the state-of-the-art materials recently reported (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Tables. 1–3). Even without the assistance of sacrificial or oxidizing agents, the degradation efficiency of MIL@V-TZ was much higher. Combined with the adsorption and degradation results for these pharmaceutical pollutants (Supplementary Fig. 49), their synergistic effects made MIL@V-TZ as excellent material for the treatment of pharmaceutical-contaminated water. The excellent stability and reusability of MIL@V-TZ were also verified by satisfactory cycling performance, as well as the PXRD patterns, FTIR and XPS spectra, SEM and TEM images without significant change after ten cycles (Supplementary Figs. 50–55). Besides, MIL@V-TZ as photocatalyst also demonstrated outstanding photodegradation performance in complex matrices including diverse natural organic matter and inorganic ions, indicating the superior practical application potential (Supplementary Fig. 56). All the above results revealed that MIL@V-TZ possessed the exceptional decontamination efficiencies for broad-spectrum pharmaceuticals, which was ascribed to the ultrahigh charge utilization, holding a potential for treating water contaminated by organic pollutants.

Insight into the catalytic mechanism using MIL@V-TZ

Moreover, the intermediates involved in the catalytic process were intuitively identified in real time by in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 57, 58). After introducing O2 flow into the MIL@V-TZ system for 60 min, FT-IR spectra of MIL@V-TZ were recorded at different light irradiation times (Fig. 4a). Noticeably, the new peaks at 1157 cm−1, 948 cm−1, and 1222 cm−1, respectively ascribed to •O2−, O − O and endo-peroxide, gradually enhanced with increased irradiation time17,54. Besides, the degradation rates of target pharmaceuticals were improved after bubbling oxygen (Supplementary Fig. 59). It confirmed that the MIL@V-TZ was capable of effectively transferring electrons to adsorbed O2 for further generation of •O2−, suggesting its ultrahigh charge utilization capability. Additionally, the peaks at 1590 cm−1 and 1521 cm−1 respectively assigned to C = N and C = C varied in intensity with irradiation time, which might be as active sites involved in the formation of •O2−17,55. The presence of •O2− could also be verified from the results of in situ DRIFTS employing the parent components and other MOF@COFs (Supplementary Figs. 57, 58). Furthermore, the p-benzoquinone (BQ), tert-butanol (TBA) and L-histidine were respectively used as quenching agents for •O2−, •OH and 1O2. Obviously, compared to the addition of TBA and L-histidine, the inhibition effect from BQ was more prominent, with a decrease extent of 73.6% for TC degradation (Fig. 4b). Besides, the typical sextuplet peaks for DMPO-•O2− could also be observed in the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra (Fig. 4c). To gain deep insight into the generation of •O2−, in situ irradiated XPS (in situ XPS) was performed (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 60). Clearly, in the In 3 d XPS spectra of MIL@V-TZ (Fig. 4d), two peaks significantly shifted to lower binding energy by about 0.28 eV under 10 min irradiation, indicating a largely increased electron cloud density on MIL. In contrast, the peaks assigned to C = N and C − N in the N 1 s XPS spectra (Fig. 4e) underwent an evident migration to higher binding energy by about 0.20 eV, indicating a decreased electron cloud density on V-TZ. Meanwhile, the peaks attributed to C − C bond in C 1 s XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 60) also showed the positive shift, implying the decreased electron density. Combining with the XPS results under dark (Fig. 1f, g), where displayed partial electron transfer from MIL to V-TZ, it provided evidence for the bridging role of C − C junction in the photogenerated charge transfer. Besides, in situ XPS results coincided with the TD-DFT calculations (Fig. 2g–i), further corroborating that MIL served as electron capturer, V-TZ was as hole receptor and C − C junction was the bridge for the high-efficient charge transfer.

DFT calculations showed the theoretical gap between highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) for V-TZ was 2.10 eV (Supplementary Fig. 61), which was approximately closed to the corresponding gap estimated by the Tauc plot (2.24 eV). And valence band (VB) potentials for V-TZ and MIL obtained from the calculated work functions and XPS valence band spectra were 1.01 and 2.41 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 62). Thus, combined with the Eg obtained by Tauc plots, the corresponding conduction band (CB) potentials of V-TZ and MIL were −1.23 and −1.13 eV, respectively. Based on above theoretical calculations and experimental results, a possible transfer pathway of photogenerated electrons was illustrated in Fig. 4f. Owing to excellent visible-light adsorption, the electrons were excited more easily and transitioned from VB to CB of V-TZ. Whereafter, due to lower CB potential of MIL, the photogenerated electrons would be further transferred to the CB of MIL through C − C bond, facilitating the electron-transfer process towards O2 to form •O2−.

With the help of •O2−, the active sites with lower electron cloud density in pharmaceuticals would be attacked, and possible degradation pathways were elucidated according to the ultraperformance liquid chromatography-Orbitrap-high resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS) results and DFT calculations56,57 (Supplementary Figs. 63–79 and Supplementary Tables 4–11). Therefore, we proposed a reaction mechanism for MIL@V-TZ catalytic system. This heterostructure included the electron capturer of MIL, the hole receptor of V-TZ, and the bridge of C − C junction. Under light irradiation, the electrons of V-TZ were excited and transitioned from the VB to CB. The photogenerated electrons would be readily transferred to the CB of MIL with lower potential through C − C bridges. Whereafter, MIL@V-TZ was capable of effectively transferring electrons to the adsorbed O2 for further swift generation of •O2−, achieving high charge utilization. The generated •O2− dominated the ultra-efficient and broad-spectrum pharmaceutical removal, endowing MIL@V-TZ with superior photocatalytic activity.

Long-term removal of pharmaceutical pollutants

The wide availability and abuse cause increasingly severe pharmaceutical pollution. TC, DFC, APAP, and DIX as typical pharmaceutical pollutants, can persist in the environment because of their continuous release and resistance to traditional treatment, and bioaccumulation in ecosystem. They may cause severe visceral gout and renal failure, even lethal consequences for wildlife58,59. Thus, it is urgent to develop cost-effective and sustainable techniques to improve the decontamination performance. Given that the superior photocatalytic performance of MIL@V-TZ and urgent requirement of water purification with sustainability, simplicity and scalability, a flow-through device was assembled for continuously removing pharmaceutical pollutants. The MIL@V-TZ film was directly grown on the surface of spiral or straight glass tubes, bypassing the redundancy of catalyst recovery (Supplementary Fig. 80). The SEM, XRD, and atomic force microscope, verified the successful fabrication of uniform MIL@V-TZ film (Supplementary Fig. 81). Subsequently, the river water samples containing TC, DFC, APAP and DIX were respectively pumped into the glass tubes, which could be degraded with high efficiency over 99% and long-term operation up to 6000 min, confirming its exceptional photodegradation capability and stability for broad-spectrum pharmaceutical removal in natural water (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 82). Furtherly, the results from Ecological Structure-Activity Relationship (ECOSAR) class program (ECOSAR v2.2, EPA) and Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (TEST v5.1.2, Supplementary Tables 12, 13 and Supplementary Figs. 84–86), predicted reduced toxicity for TC, DFC, APAP, and DIX after degradation. In addition, treated water from MIL@V-TZ flow-through photocatalytic system exhibited less adverse effects on Malabar spinach plant growth, compared with raw pharmaceutical-containing river water (Supplementary Fig. 87). From the Orbitrap-HRMS results, no obvious chromatographic peaks for TZ (m/z = 355.16629) and V (m/z = 187.02173) could be observed in the treated solution from the continuous flow system (Supplementary Fig. 88). The In-leaching concentration detected by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was low (Supplementary Fig. 89), and about 2.46×10−3 wt% of In was leached after reaction for 6000 min, implying the excellent chemical stability of this catalyst in long-term removal of pharmaceutical pollutants. Benefiting from the facilely and mildly in-situ growth strategy, the exploration of large-scale operation for this continuous flow-through system is promising by scaling up the dimension of glass tube. To avoid the catalyst slipping, it will be expected to enhance the robustness with the help of the responsive polymers62,63. The results underscored the great potential of MIL@V-TZ flow-through photocatalytic system for future high-efficiency water purification in a sustainable and green way.

In this work, the C − C connection strategy was utilized to universally synthesize multiple crystalline core-shell MOF@COFs with local component interpenetration and strongly-coupled interface. The C − C bridge induced fast charge separation and promoted O2 to form •O2−, which dominated the high-efficiency and broad-spectrum removal of four pharmaceuticals according to the DFT theoretical calculations and in-situ experiments. The MIL@V-TZ with highest charge separation capability, exhibited superior photocatalytic activity. It achieved rapid pharmaceutical removal (e. g. 10 min for DFC), and continuous degradation of pharmaceutical pollutants in river water with high efficiency (>99%) and long-term sustainability (up to 6000 min), based on in-situ grown films. Furthermore, the toxicity of pharmaceutical pollutants has been significantly reduced after degradation, revealed by UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS, toxicity estimation and plant growth. The broad applicability of C − C connection strategy promotes the development of multivariate frameworks, enlightening more intelligent and tractable design of heterogeneous catalyst. Beyond treating pharmaceutical pollutants, ultrahigh charge utilization and distinguished adsorption capacity greatly extend the potential of MOF@COF in addressing more recalcitrant organic pollutants. Additionally, the continuous flow-through system equipped with feasibly grown film enhances the practicality while reduces the treatment cost, and also presents opportunities to achieve efficient pollutant removal in open-water sources without the need for chemical activators (hydrogen peroxide and persulfate). Future studies will focus on enhancing light utilization by rational structure design of MOFs and COFs, exploring scalability of in-situ grown film, and ensuring long-term reliability with the aid of responsive polymer, thereby facilitating the engineering application. Overall, this study underscores the versatility of proposed strategy and superiority of C − C junction, and discloses the potential of MOF@COFs in continuously addressing pharmaceutical pollutants, providing an avenue for water treatment.

Methods

Synthesis of MIL

H2BDC (124.5 mg), BDC − C = C (144.0 mg), and indium nitrate hydrate (In(NO3)3·xH2O, 451.2 mg), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 200 mL) were added to the vial. Then the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 15 min to prepare MIL64. As-prepared MIL was purified for 24 h by Soxhlet extraction with methanol, and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 24 h.

Synthesis of V-TZ

TZ (56.7 mg) and V (44.7 mg) were added into a 50 mL flat-bottomed flask containing 20 mL acetonitrile. After sonication for around 10 min, 3 mL acetic acid (12 M) was subsequently added into the flask39. And the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 days to produce pure V-TZ. As-prepared V-TZ was purified for 24 h by Soxhlet extraction with methanol, and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 24 h.

Synthesis of MOF@COFs

After the mixture including MIL suspension (120 mg of MIL), 2,2’-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN, 40.4 mg), aldehyde monomers of COF (24.8 mg of V or 32.8 mg of DaV), and DMF (200 mL), was degassed under a flow of N2 gas, functionalized MIL was obtained by heating the mixture at 65 °C for 12 h39. Subsequently, the resulting functionalized MIL reacted with monomers of COFs at room temperature for 3 days to obtain MOF@COFs. Specifically, 60.0 mg of MIL-V, 56.7 mg of TZ, 44.7 mg of V were added into a 50 mL flat-bottomed flask containing 20 mL acetonitrile. After sonication for around 10 min, 3 mL acetic acid (12 M) was subsequently added into the flask. And the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 days to produce MIL@V-TZ. Likewise, MIL@DaV-TZ and MIL@DaV-TAPB were prepared by replacing V with DaV, or replacing TZ with TAPB. Specifically, for MIL@DaV-TZ, 60.0 mg of MIL-DaV, 35.4 mg of TZ and 36.9 mg DaV were dispersed in 20 mL acetonitrile, and 2 mL acetic acid (12 M) was subsequently added. For MIL@DaV-TAPB, 60.0 mg of MIL-DaV, 35.1 mg TAPB and 36.9 mg DaV were dispersed in 20 mL acetonitrile, and 0.4 mL acetic acid (12 M) was subsequently added. As-prepared MOF@COFs was purified for 24 h by Soxhlet extraction with methanol, and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 24 h.

Fabrication of continuous flow-through photocatalytic system

The continuous flow-through photocatalytic system consisted of a pump, a Xenon lamp, a spiral glass tube and straight glass tube respectively coated with MIL@V-TZ film (Supplementary Fig. 82). During the flow-through operation, the pharmaceutical-containing river water was pumped through the MIL@V-TZ photocatalytic system under light irradiation. The MIL@V-TZ film was fabricated by an in-situ growth method64. Specifically, after washing in sequence with sodium hydroxide (1.0 M), hydrochloric acid (0.1 M), and deionized water, the glass tubes were filled with toluene and 3-merraptnpropyltrimethnxysilane (V: V = 25: 1), standing for 12 h at room temperature. The mixture of 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propylmethacrylate, ammonium hydroxide, and ethanol (V: V: V = 1.0:2.5:29) was then added, standing for 12 h at room temperature as well. Subsequently, tubes modified with poly(4-vinylpyridine) (P4VP) were fabricated by heating the degassing mixture of 4-vinylpyridine (6.67 mL), AIBN (50.0 mg), and DMF (100 mL) at 65 °C for 12 h. Through Coulomb attraction between P4VP modified on the surface of tube and In3+ ions, MIL was in-situ grown on the tube after introducing In(NO3)3·xH2O, H2BDC, and BDC − C = C (Supplementary Fig. 80a). And following MIL@V-TZ films were obtained by using the C − C linked strategy (Supplementary Figs. 80b, 80c).

Performance evaluation of continuous flow-through photocatalytic system

Water sample was collected from Guhe River in Guangzhou (113°23'9"E, 23°4'20"N, Supplementary Fig. 90), and the initial concentrations of pharmaceuticals in the river water were 10, 10, 5, and 10 mg·L−1, respectively for TC, DFC, APAP, and DIX. Under light irradiation, pharmaceutical-containing river water flowed through MIL@V-TZ film, and treated water samples from the effluents were taken at 4 hours intervals for HPLC-MS/MS analysis. The Malabar spinach plants, collected from a local vegetable garden (Guangzhou, China), were used to evaluate the environmental impacts of degradation products in MIL@V-TZ flow-through photocatalytic system. These plants were distributed to two planting baskets, and cultivated respectively with raw water and treated water. The growth of plants was recorded on the 1 st, 7 th, and 16 th day.
