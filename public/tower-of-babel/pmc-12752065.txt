Genomic and functional characterization of a lipopeptide-producing endophytic Bacillus velezensis EL isolated from Euphorbia cyparissias L
Authors: Drożdżyński Piotr, Rutkowska Natalia, Popińska Kinga, Boruta Tomasz, Marchut-Mikolajczyk Olga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLipopeptides, are emerging as sustainable alternatives to synthetic surfactants due to their biodegradability, low toxicity, and broad application in bioremediation, agriculture, and medicine. Endophytic microorganisms, especially those adapted to degraded environments, represent a valuable and underexplored reservoir of such bioactive compounds. Euphorbia cyparissias L., known as pine spurge, is a perennial herbaceous plant from the Euphorbiaceae family, and is highly resistant to environmental stresses, which may result from the presence of specialist endophytic microorganisms that support its adaptation.ResultsA novel endophytic strain, Bacillus velezensis EL, was isolated from surface-sterilized tissues of Euphorbia cyparissias L., a hardy plant growing in degraded soil. Preliminary screening revealed its high biosurfactant activity, including emulsification, oil dispersion, and surface tension reduction. Whole-genome sequencing and functional annotation identified thirteen biosynthetic gene clusters, including nonribosomal peptide synthetase pathways responsible for lipopeptide production. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed characteristic functional groups of lipopeptides, while ultra-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry validated the presence of surfactin and bacillomycin homologs. Process optimization using the Taguchi design significantly enhanced production, yielding 3.841 g/L of crude biosurfactant under optimal conditions. The product exhibited strong surface activity, with high emulsifying and oil displacement performance, and favorable biochemical composition with high protein content.ConclusionsThis study highlights Bacillus velezensis EL as a promising producer of lipopeptide biosurfactants. It also identifies Euphorbia cyparissias L. as a novel and ecologically significant host plant harboring endophytes with industrial biotechnological potential. These findings support further development of endophyte-based systems for sustainable biosurfactant production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02882-y.Keywords: Bacillus velezensis EL, Biosurfactant, Lipopeptides, Surfactin, Endophytes, Euphorbia cyparissias L.

Background

Endophytic microorganisms, including both bacteria and fungi, colonize internal, healthy plant tissues without causing disease symptoms [1]. They can be isolated from plant tissues previously subjected to surface sterilization [2]. These organisms are crucial in promoting the growth and development of plants and enhancing their ability to adapt to unfavorable environmental conditions. Endophytes are primarily found in roots but can also inhabit aerial plant parts such as leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits. Due to the nature of their interactions with the plant-host, these microorganisms are divided into obligate, which cannot thrive outside the plant, and facultative, which reside in plants only at specific stages of their development [3–6].

Endophytes isolated from plants growing in hydrocarbon-contaminated areas demonstrate the ability to degrade toxic aromatic compounds, which is the result of their evolutionary adaptation to the host’s secondary metabolites and the development of appropriate enzymatic systems [7, 8]. Owing to their high lipophilicity, hydrocarbons, readily adhere to root surfaces, enter plant cells, and move within tissues. As a mechanism of host protection, endophytes participate in the detoxification of these compounds, converting them into less hazardous forms. Moreover, endophytes can synthesize various bioactive compounds with antibiotic, anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and surface active properties. Among these bioactive metabolites, biosurfactants have garnered particular interest due to their multifunctional roles in host-microbe interactions and environmental adaptability [9–11].

Biosurfactants are a structurally diverse group of surface-active compounds synthesized by microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and yeasts. Unlike synthetic surfactants - amphiphilic molecules composed of hydrophilic and hydrophobic moieties, which are widely used but often toxic to living organisms and the environment - biosurfactants offer a more sustainable and biocompatible alternative [12–15]. Their amphiphilic nature enables them to reduce surface and interfacial tension, facilitate emulsification and solubilization of hydrophobic substances, and support microbial colonization and biofilm formation [13, 15] It is feasible to reduce or entirely eliminate this issue through the application of biosurfactants, which are surface-active compounds created by microorganisms including fungi, yeasts, and bacteria [13, 16, 17].

Among biosurfactants, lipopeptides have gained particular attention due to their multifunctionality and favorable biological properties. These low-molecular-weight compounds, consisting of a cyclic or linear peptide linked to a fatty acid chain, exhibit not only surface activity but also potent antimicrobial, antifungal, anticancer, and immunomodulatory effects [18, 19]. Key representatives include surfactin, iturin, and fengycin, primarily produced by Bacillus spp. Their synthesis is influenced by microbial strain, medium composition, and culture conditions. Despite their broad biotechnological potential in medicine, agriculture, and environmental remediation, the commercial use of biosurfactants remains limited by challenges related to production yield and downstream processing costs [19, 20].

Euphorbia cyparissias L., known as pine spurge, is a perennial herbaceous plant from the Euphorbiaceae family, found mainly in Europe and western Asia. It prefers dry, sandy soils, meadows and open areas, often inhabiting degraded or marginal sites. It is characterized by narrow, needle-like leaves and yellow-green flowers surrounded by characteristic bracts. The plant produces a milky sap containing toxic substances that serve as a defense against herbivorous organisms [21]. Pine spurge is highly resistant to environmental stresses, which may result from the presence of specialist endophytic microorganisms that support its adaptation. Plant secondary metabolites like diterpenes and phenols may influence the composition of endophytic communities and shape their symbiotic interactions. Although the literature on E. cyparissias endophytes is limited, studies on other Euphorbia species (e.g. E. trigonas and E. antisyphilitica) indicate the presence of endophytic microorganisms, including Bacillus sp. bacteria [22, 23]. Due to its ability to adapt to unfavorable conditions and potentially rich endophytic microflora, E. cyparissias is a promising object of research on microorganisms producing bioactive compounds, including lipopeptides [24].

The primary objective of this study was to isolate and identify endophytic bacteria associated with Euphorbia cyparissias L., and to evaluate their capacity for biosurfactant production. Particular attention was given to the selection of the most promising strain based on its biosurfactant activity, followed by its taxonomic identification and assessment of its potential to synthesize lipopeptides. The study further aimed to perform a preliminary characterization of the biosurfactant compounds, with an emphasis on their biological activity and prospective applications in environmental and medical biotechnology.

Methods

Plant material and plant material surface sterilization procedure

The pine spurge (Euphorbia cyparissias L.) specimens were rinsed thoroughly under running water, and sectioned into roots, stems, and leaves. Surface sterilization was performed under sterile conditions based on the protocol described by Marchut-Mikołajczyk et al. (2018), with minor modifications [10]. Plant fragments were immersed in 90% ethanol for 3 min, followed by 6.25% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, and finally rinsed again in 90% ethanol for 30 s. The samples were subsequently washed five times with sterile distilled water. To verify sterilization efficacy, 0.2 ml of the final rinse water was plated onto LB agar with composition as follows in [g/l]: 5 yest extract, 10 peptone, 10 sodium chloride, 25 agar, 1000 distilled water, pH 7.0. No microbial growth was observed after 7 days of incubation at 30 °C.

Conditions for growth and obtaining pure cultures of endophytic isolates

Sterile plant fragments were aseptically placed on LB agar plates (composition: as above) and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. Emerging bacterial colonies were cultured to obtain pure isolates, which were maintained on LB agar at 30 °C for 5 days.

Screening for lipopeptide production

Endophytic isolates were cultivated in a rotary shaker for 7 days in LB medium, with a composition similar as that previously presented, at 30 °C and 180 rpm to assess surfactant production by the strains. Subsequent to this duration, the bacterial biomass of all strains was separated, and the supernatants were examined for the presence of biosurfactants.

Emulsification activity

The emulsifying activity was evaluated using a slightly modified Pearce and Kinsella method [25]. A test mixture contained 1 ml of phosphate buffer (pH 7), 1 ml of diesel oil, and 3 ml of the cell-free supernatant. The mixture was homogenized at 24 000 rpm for 30 s. Immediately, 0.1 ml of homogenate was mixed with 1 ml of 1% SDS solution (added after homogenization to stabilize the emulsion for spectrophotometric measurement). The absorbance of the resulting mixture was measured at λ = 500 nm (OD500) against distilled water as the blank. Negative controls containing only phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and diesel fuel, without cell-free supernatant, were included in all tests, and no emulsifying activity was observed. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Turbidimetric assay

Crude lipopeptide content was evaluated using a turbidimetric method described by Mukherjee et al. [26]. Samples of post-culture supernatant and cell-free extract were acidified to pH 2.0 with concentrated HCl. The resulting suspensions were vortexed and subjected to spectrophotometric analysis at λ = 600 nm (OD600) against a distilled water control. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Oil displacement test

Surface activity of the biosurfactant was evaluated using the oil displacement method [27]. A Petri dish was filled with 25 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 200 µL of crude engine oil. Subsequently, 20 µL of either post-culture fluid or cell-free extract was gently applied to the oil layer. The diameter of the clear zone formed by displacement of the oil was measured using a digital caliper. Measurements were performed in triplicate.

Emulsion index (EI₂₄)

Emulsion stability was determined by the emulsion index method [28]. Equal volumes (2 mL) of diesel oil and post-culture supernatant or cell-free extract were transferred into a test tube and vortexed at 280 rpm for 3 min. After 24 h at room temperature, the height of the emulsion layer (x) and the total height of the liquid column (y) were measured. The emulsion index was calculated using the following equation:

Genome sequencing, assembly and annotation

Genomic DNA of strain EL was extracted after 16 h cultivation in LB broth (30 °C, 120 rpm) using phenol-chloroform method [29]. The concentration and purity of DNA were checked using spectrophotometric absorbance measurement and Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Life Technologies), followed by the qualitative assessment by agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing was conducted using both MGISEQ-2000 Sequencer (MGI, Schenzen, China) and MinION Mk1b device (Oxford Nanopore Technology) platforms.

Short reads were processed through quality filtering and trimming using Trimmomatic v0.36 [30]. Nanopore-filtered reads were obtained by following base calling, demultiplexing, and debarcoding with Dorado v7.2.13 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). The genome was assembled de novo using Unicycler v0.5.1 in bold mode, incorporating an additional polishing step with Pilon [31]. Assembly metrics and contig quality were evaluated with QUAST v5.2.0 [32], while genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM v1.2.2 and BUSCO v5.5.0 [33, 34].

The genomic DNA was annotated through Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) by NCBI. Initially, Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) online server was used for taxonomic identification, as well as JSpeciesWS [35, 36]. The Reference sequence Alignment based Phylogeny builder (REALPHY) tool was used for genome-wide comparisons of the closest related species, then MEGA v11.0.9 for the construction of a phylogenetic tree by the Neighbor-joining method with bootstrap values of 1,000 replications [37, 38]. The final genome, annotated by Prokka, was plotted using Proksee to show gene locations, GC-skew, and GC content [39, 40]. Antibiotics and Secondary Metabolites Analysis SHell (antiSMASH 7.0) tool was used to predict gene clusters involved in secondary metabolite production, with the search of putative products in NORINE database [41, 42].

Lipopeptides production in optimal conditions

To enhance biosurfactant production under optimized conditions, large-scale laboratory cultivation of Bacillus velezensis EL was performed in a 6-L stirred-tank bioreactor (Minifors 2, Infors HT, Switzerland). The fermentation medium consisted of mineral salts supplemented with 5% (w/v) carbon and nitrogen sources previously optimized using the Taguchi method. The medium was loaded into the bioreactor and sterilized in situ at 121 °C for 20 min under constant agitation at 160 rpm.

After cooling, the sterile medium was aseptically inoculated with a 3% (v/v) seed culture (OD600 = 1.8) prepared in LB broth. Fermentation was carried out for 72 h under controlled conditions, with continuous monitoring of temperature, agitation, and aeration parameters.

Isolation and purification of lipopeptides

The biomass obtained after fermentation of Bacillus velezensis EL in Minifros 2 was first centrifuged (18,000 rpm, 4 °C, 15 min). The cell-free supernatant was subsequently acidified to pH 2 with 6 M HCl and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequent to incubation, the mixture underwent centrifugation once more, and the crued biosurfactants were dissolved in 0.5 M NaHCO3. The solution was thereafter put into separatory funnels, to which a 1:1 methanol: chloroform solvent mixture, amounting to one-third of the sample’s volume, was introduced. The extraction procedure was conducted thrice. The organic phase was transferred to a bottle and underwent solvent evaporation (40 mbar, 30 °C, the time was dependent on the rate of solvent evaporation) [10].

Antimicrobial potential of obtained lipopeptides

Antimicrobial activity was evaluated using the agar disc diffusion method. Sterile filter paper discs (6 mm) were soaked in 20 µl of lipopeptide precipitate at defined concentrations (10, 5, 2.5 mg/ml), air-dried under aseptic conditions, and placed on LB agar plates inoculated with Gram-negative and Gram-positive strains of Escherichia coli DH5α, Pseudomonas A34, and Bacillus licheniformis and Bacillus mycoides NS1020, respectively. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 18–24 h. Zones of growth inhibition were measured in mm. All tests were performed in triplicate.

Fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR)

Chemical structure and functional groups of the crude lipopeptides were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Measurements were conducted in transmittance mode over the spectral range of 4000–500 cm⁻¹ using a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Between measurements, the crystal surface was cleaned with isopropyl alcohol to prevent contamination. Data analysis and peak assignment were performed using Omnic 8 software [43, 46].

UPLC-MS lipopeptide structure confirmation

The UPLC-MS analysis was carried with the use of AQUITY-UPLC system and SYNAPT G2 high-resolution mass spectrometer (WATERS, USA). The BEH Shield RP18 2.1 mm × 100 mm × 1.7 μm column (WATERS, USA) was employed for chromatographic separation. The elution was performed with water and acetonitrile as eluents, both containing formic acid at the concentration of 0.1% (v/v). The flow rate through the column was set to 0.2 mL min − 1. The following water: acetonitrile gradient was applied: initial 100:0; 5.00 min 80:20 (curve 6); 11.00 min 70:30 (curve 6); 15 min 60:40 (curve 6); 25.00 min 40:60 (curve 6), 35.00 min 20:80 (curve 6); 45.00 min 0:100 (curve 6). During the run, the temperature of the column was equal to 40 °C, while the samples were kept at 12 °C, and the lock mass solution of leucine enkephalin was used as mass reference. The MS parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 3 kV, sampling cone voltage 40 V, extraction cone voltage 4 V, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation temperature 200 °C, desolvation gas flow rate 500 L h − 1. All samples were analyzed under ESI + mode. As a standard the surfactin (BOCSCI Inc., CAS no. 24730-31−2) was used.

Genome characterization and taxonomic identification

The EL strain was assembled de novo using hybrid approach from both short and long reads. The obtained genome assembly consists of 6 chromosome contigs spanning 3,914,388 bp, with a mean G-C content of 46.5% (Fig. 2). A total of 3,862 genes were predicted, including 3,672 protein-coding sequences, 84 tRNA and 25 rRNA genes. The genome is available in the NCBI GenBank database under the accession number GCA_036964795.1.

The circular genome map of Bacillus velezensis EL

The taxonomic identification of strain EL was determined using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) online server, which employs digital DNA: DNA hybridization (dDDH) values above 70% as a delineating parameter for classification as a specific species. Additionally, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was also calculated; its threshold for classification is above 96%. Strain EL surpassed both thresholds recognized as the cut-offs for species delineation and was classified as Bacillus velezensis [47, 48]. Strain EL is closely affiliated with B. velezensis KACC 13,105, which was previously known as B. methylotrophicus, now its later heterotypic synonym [49] of the results are presented in Fig. 3. Optimization of biosurfactant production

To optimize lipopeptides production, a Taguchi L16 orthogonal array was applied, testing combinations of carbon and nitrogen sources, temperature, pH, and incubation time with designation according Table 1. Results of the optimization according Taguchi method is presented in the Figure S3 and representative data from performed runs are presented in Table S1.

A Taguchi design using the “larger-the-better” criterion was applied to optimize lipopeptides production. The signal-to-noise ratio (ETA) was used to evaluate performance, with higher ETA values indicating more favorable conditions. The “Average Eta by Factor Levels” plot showed that the nitrogen source had the strongest effect, with a wide range of ETA values clearly exceeding the ± 2 standard error threshold. This was confirmed by ANOVA results, where nitrogen had the highest F-value (F = 811.20). The carbon source also had a significant impact (F = 209.45), as did temperature and pH, both showing comparable contributions and high F-values (F > 310). Though incubation time had the smallest influence, it was still statistically significant (F = 146.07). Minimal residual error indicated strong experimental consistency.

The optimal conditions for lipopeptide production were identified as the use of ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3) as the nitrogen source, cultivation at 25 °C, initial pH adjusted to 8.0, malt pulp as the carbon source, and an incubation period of 48 h. The production of biosurfactant by Bacillus velezensis EL in bioreactor under optimized culture conditions reached a concentration of 3.841 g/L, indicating a high production efficiency (Table 4). Biochemical analysis revealed that the biosurfactant contained 0.62 µg/mL of carbohydrates and a relatively high protein content of 3623.65 µg/mL, suggesting a lipopeptide nature of the compound.

Antimicrobial potential of obtained lipopeptides

The disc diffusion results (Table 5) clearly demonstrate concentration-dependent antimicrobial activity of the tested biosurfactants at 10, 5 and 2.5 mg/ml (C1–C3). The highest concentration (C1) resulted in the largest inhibition areas across all susceptible strains, with Bacillus licheniformis (12.29 ± 1.08 mm) and Pseudomonas sp. (9.70 ± 1.10 mm) showing the greatest efficiency. Activity decreased proportionally with concentration, and lower doses showed limited or no inhibition against examined strains (e.g., Bacillus mycoides and B. licheniformis at 2.5 mg/ml), indicating that 2.5 mg/ml approaches the threshold of efficacy. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

The FTIR spectra of the biosurfactant produced by Bacillus velezensis EL under optimized conditions revealed characteristic absorption bands associated with lipopeptide structures, and were very similar to the commercial surfactin standard – Fig. 5. A broad absorption band observed in the region of 3300–3400 cm− 1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of NH groups, indicating the presence of peptide bonds. Distinct peaks at 2920 cm− 1 and 2850 cm− 1 are assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of CH2 and CH3 groups, confirming the aliphatic hydrocarbon chains in the lipid moiety of the lipopeptide. The strong absorption band near 1650 cm− 1 is attributed to C = O stretching vibrations, typical of peptide bonds, while the band observed around 1540 cm− 1 corresponds to N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations, further indicating the peptide structure [50, 51]. Additional absorption in the 1400–1450 cm− 1 region is associated with CH3 bending vibrations. Notably, a series of bands within 1000–1200 cm− 1 are linked to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations, indicative of ester linkages present in the cyclic lipopeptide structure [52]. The overall spectral profile of the biosurfactant closely resembles that of the surfactin standard, thereby confirming its lipopeptide nature.

The UPLC-MS analysis of obtained lipopeptides

The UPLC-MS analysis revealed the presence of surfactin lipopeptides in the sample. Altogether, six distinct m/z values corresponding to the molecular formulae of surfactins were detected, ranging from 994.6390 to 1064.7247 (Table 5).

To confirm that the identified molecules belonged to the surfactin family of lipopeptides, the m/z values recorded for the sample were compared with the results obtained for the surfactin standard. Importantly, no differences in m/z were observed (fig. S4). Furthermore, the consecutive molecular formulae of the detected surfactins (Table 5) differed by the “CH2” group, in agreement with literature reports [53, 54] (Table 6).

Discussion

Endophytes are gaining growing interest as valuable sources of bioactive metabolites, including biosurfactants, with potential applications across various industries, including agriculture, medicine, and environmental remediation. The present study obtained four bacterial strains, from Euphorbia cyparissias L. (pine spurge), a perennial herb recognized for its adaptability to challenging, degraded soils. Scientific studies on the endophytic microbiome of pine spurge are scarce; nevertheless, it has been previously demonstrated that related Euphorbia species harbor an extensive number of bacteria, mainly from the genus Bacillus [57]. Bacteria from the genus Bacillus may be the most prevalent and demonstrate the highest adaptability. Moreover, they produce broad spectrum of bioactive chemicals like biosurfactants, antibiotics and other compounds. A wide variety of biosurfactant-producing bacteria from the genus Bacillus sp. may be indeed responsible for the plant’s resilience and capacity to withstand harsh conditions in the environment [58–60].

The initial evaluation of four endophytic strains revealed that strain EL demonstrated the greatest surfactant production capability among the isolated bacteria. The elevated emulsion index (66.5%), notable oil droplet displacement (5.49 cm), and pronounced emulsification activity (OD₆₀₀ = 2.55) indicated substantial biosurfactant generation. Comparable results were obtained by Marchut-Mikołajczyk et al. (2018). The emulsifying activity OD500 of Bacillus pumilus 2 A strain was at 1.96 and the emulsion index obtained was in excess of 37%, during the biodegradation of diesel oil [10].

As strain EL was prominent among all isolated bacteria, it was subjected to genome sequencing and analysis. Bacillus genus contains multiple strains of high similarity grouping together, i.e. B. cereus, B. subtilis or B. amyloliquefaciens group. Use of single or even few house-keeping genes for proper identification up to species level is inconclusive as those genes are highly conserved in species belonging in the same group [61]. However, with whole genome sequence, it is possible to resolve that issue. Due to the increasing availability of DNA sequencing and, thus, possibility of comparative genomic analysis, many reclassifications of species occurred, especially within Bacillus genus [62].

Strain EL was indisputably characterized as Bacillus velezensis, since both ANI and dDDH values surpassed necessary thresholds. Genome mining of Bacillus velezensis EL revealed thirteen regions with biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), comprising NRPS clusters that encode surfactin, fengycin, and bacillomycin. The existence of several hybrid NRPS-PKS and transAT-PKS clusters reflects findings from investigations on other biosurfactant producers and aligns with the significant metabolic plasticity of B. velezensis [63, 64]. The surfactin BGC exhibited 78% of genes as similar to the reference cluster in the MIBiG database, whereas the fengycin, bacillibactin, and bacillisin BGCs demonstrated total (100%) identity to established clusters.

The production of lipopeptides by strain EL was further optimized with the Taguchi method, utilizing the “more is better” criterion. The optimization technique markedly enhanced the production of lipopeptides, achieving 3.841 g/L (YP/S=285.3 mg/g) at optimal conditions. This yield is competitive with other literature studies on the manufacture of bioactive chemicals by Bacillus strains, particularly given the low-cost substrates utilized. Vahidinasab et al. (2022) cultured a different strain, B. velezensis UTB96, in a specially design bioreactor utilizing glucose as a carbon source; nonetheless, the production yield did not surpass YP/S=120.6 mg/g [56]. The B. pumilus 2 A strain, which synthesizes biosurfactants from diverse carbon sources, exhibited 4.069 g/l from molasses, with emulsifying activity not surpassing an OD500 of 2.1 [43].

Further analysis of the optimization process highlights the crucial importance of medium composition and environmental factors in regulating lipopeptide production. This work indicates that nitrogen sources, particularly ammonium nitrate, influence the activity of nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) and, consequently, lipopeptide yield. Carbon sources such as malt extract provide essential precursors for peptides and lipid fragments, but phosphate content can influence cellular energy metabolism and the availability of enzyme cofactors, thus affecting overall biosynthetic efficiency. In addition to nutritional optimization, factors such as pH, temperature, aeration, and incubation time likely influence the kinetics of lipopeptide synthesis and isoform distribution. Further research will focus on methodologically assessing these variables using factorial models or response surface methods to enhance yield, regulate isoform profiles, and achieve industrial-scale production [43, 65].

Nitrogen was identified as a key factor influencing lipopeptide production in the present study, consistent with findings by Leconte et al. (2022), who similarly highlighted its significance in optimizing surfactin and fengycin biosynthesis. In addition to nitrogen, carbon source and phosphate concentration were also shown to affect lipopeptide yield in both studies. Leconte et al. demonstrated that specific combinations of carbon and nitrogen sources, alongside optimal phosphate levels, led to enhanced biosynthetic performance. These observations align with our results, where malt-based carbon and ammonium nitrate as the nitrogen source supported the highest lipopeptide production by Bacillus velezensis EL, confirming the importance of nutrient pairing in maximizing biosurfactant output [66].

The biochemical characteristics of the crude extract, distinguished by elevated protein levels and diminished carbohydrate levels, aligns with the characteristics of conventional lipopeptide biosurfactants [50, 54, 56]. FTIR spectral analysis confirmed the presence of key functional groups characteristic of lipopeptides, including N–H, C–H, and C = O bonds. The biosurfactant produced by Bacillus velezensis EL exhibited a spectrum that closely overlaps with that of the commercially available surfactin standard (BOCSCI, USA), indicating structural similarity. The major absorption bands observed at 3299.67 cm− 1 (N–H stretching), 2927.30 cm− 1 (C–H stretching in long alkyl chains), 1650.14 cm− 1 (CO-N stretching,), 1390.15 cm− 1 (aromatic C = C stretching), and 1207.74 cm− 1 (C–O deformation vibration) are consistent with typical lipopeptide profiles. These findings are further supported by the study of Meena et al. (2018), who reported comparable peaks for a lipopeptide produced by B. velezensis KLP2016 at 3284.32, 2958.30, 1658.30, 1408.42, and 1233.45 cm− 1 [67]. The slight shifts in wavenumber may result from strain-specific structural variations or experimental conditions, yet the high degree of spectral similarity supports the identification of the EL biosurfactant as a surfactin-type lipopeptide.

The use of UPLC-MS analysis is widely utilized for the profiling and structural elucidation of microbial secondary metabolites and has been successfully applied in previous studies. In our study this approach allowed the successful identification of lipopeptide homologues produced by Bacillus velezensis EL, during which the synthesis of six surfactin isoforms and four bacillomycin homologues was verified, showing molecular weights consistent with other reports [53, 54]. For example, in the work of Bacillus velezensis DMW1 strain characterization [68], a combination of HPLC and UPLC-MS allowed for the resolution and identification of seven distinct lipopeptides based on their retention times and specific m/z values. Similarly, our results revealed six surfactin homologs with molecular masses ranging from 994.6390 to 1064.7247 [M + H]+ and four bacillomycin variants ranging from 1017.5314 to 1059.5703 [M + H]+. These values were in close agreement with previously published data [53, 54], and the detection of consistent + 14 Da shifts confirmed the presence of CH2 group extensions - a well-documented feature in lipopeptide compounds. Importantly, comparison with a surfactin standard confirmed the identity of the corresponding molecular species in the sample, as no differences in m/z values or retention profiles were observed. These findings not only validate the use of B. velezensis EL as a potent biosurfactant producer but also underscore the reliability of UPLC-MS for the discrimination of structurally similar lipopeptide compounds.

The identification of both families indicate the induced expression of two lipopeptide BGCs under the examined conditions, hence affirming the functional relevance of the genomic data. Collectively, these results illustrate the significant potential of B. velezensis EL for biosurfactant synthesis and validate E. cyparissias L. as an innovative host plant for endophyte exploration. The integration of effective biosynthesis, verified structural identity, and strong surface action underscores the biotechnological prospective of this strain, especially in applications that requires multifunctional lipopeptides (antimicrobial, emulsifying, and antifungal). Future research will concentrate on extensive fermentation processes, comprehensive antibacterial efficacy, and formulation for industrial applications.

Conclusions

This study highlights that Bacillus velezensis EL, an endophytic strain isolated from tissues of Euphorbia cyparissias L., is a highly efficient biosurfactant producer, generating structurally diverse lipopeptides, primarily from the surfactin and bacillomycin families. Initial screening assays revealed significant surface activity, which was further confirmed by UPLC-MS analysis and FTIR spectral characterization, confirming the existence of functionally significant peptide and lipid components. A thorough genome search identified numerous biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), including those responsible for surfactin, fengycin, and difficidin production, indicating a diversity of secondary metabolites. Taguchi’s approach to optimizing culture parameters significantly increased production, achieving a biosurfactant yield of 3.841 g/L culturing EL strain in a bioreactor. The resulting product demonstrated strong emulsifying and oil-displacing properties, suggesting significant application potential. The resulting product demonstrated strong emulsifying and oil-displacing properties, positioning B. velezensis EL as a viable and sustainable candidate for commercial biosurfactant production with promising applications in agriculture, environmental remediation, and medical biotechnology and highlighting the potential of E. cyparissias L. - a previously unexplored source of endophytic biosurfactants—as a reservoir of metabolically active bacteria.
