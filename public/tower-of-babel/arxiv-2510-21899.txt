On de Sitter Space, Scalar Fields and Inflation
Eyad H. Al-Samra

Abstract
An introductory, self-contained overview, with pedagogical figures, is provided to acquaint readers unfamiliar with the subjects with key aspects of de Sitter space and inflation. The connection between de Sitter space and cosmology is reviewed. The embedding of a hyperboloid surface in higher-dimensional Minkowski space is analysed, and the Killing vectors of de Sitter space are derived in both global and planar coordinate systems; their integral curves are visualised on the de Sitter Penrose diagram, illustrating the static patch and cosmological horizon. The inflaton scalar field is quantised in the Bunch–Davies vacuum within the slow-roll regime. The two- and three-point correlation functions are computed using the ADM decomposition and the in–in formalism. These computations are related to the observed small temperature anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background.

An important and powerful concept is that of symmetries and Killing vector fields

The corresponding Lie group is the isometry group, which consists of the finite transformations that leave the metric unchanged, or equivalently, map the metric to itself. This powerful concept often simplifies calculations by using the conserved quantities associated with symmetries, as guaranteed by Noether’s theorem. De Sitter space is maximally symmetric, meaning it possesses the maximum number of Killing vector fields. As discussed in detail later, the formulas for these Killing vectors can be explicitly computed by embedding de Sitter space, as a hyperboloid surface, in higher-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. Similarly, the classical geometry of de Sitter space emerges naturally from this embedding picture as explored later.

Understanding the quantum field theory of the inflaton and the dynamics of inflation is crucial for explaining the rapid expansion of the early universe, the formation of large-scale structures, and the cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies. However, constructing such a theory in de Sitter space introduces complexities not present in Minkowski spacetime
[
6
,
7
]
. For instance, in Minkowski space, the Poincaré symmetry group and globally defined, time-like Killing vector naturally lead to a well-defined vacuum state. In contrast, de Sitter space lacks such a globally defined, time-like Killing vector, making the definition of the vacuum state more nuanced. Additionally, solutions, such as the two-point function (a measure of field correlations), in de Sitter space are often more intricate and challenging to work with compared to the simpler Minkowski case. Similarly, setting up perturbation theory in de Sitter space, particularly when considering higher-order interactions like the three-point function, present additional challenges. For example, the expanding nature of de Sitter space complicates the behaviour of perturbations, especially when coupling to gravity is considered. These complications necessitate more sophisticated techniques and approximations to analyse interactions and extract meaningful physical predictions. These subjects are explored in more detail later.

4
Symmetries and Killing vectors of dS
4

As mentioned before, de Sitter space is maximally symmetric, i.e., it possesses the maximum number possible of Killing vectors. To discuss this, one can start from the following definition of the Lie derivative of the metric with respect to an infinitesimal vector field
\delta\xi
[
33
,
29
,
30
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\xi}\,g_{\mu\nu}=\delta\xi^{\sigma}\partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}+g_{\mu\sigma}\partial_{\nu}\delta\xi^{\sigma}+g_{\sigma\nu}\partial_{\mu}\delta\xi^{\sigma}\ .

(44)

If the metric compatibility,
\nabla_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}=0
, is used, the last equation can be rewritten as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\xi}\,g_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}\delta\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\delta\xi_{\mu}\ ,

(45)

where
\nabla_{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative:

\nabla_{\mu}\delta\xi_{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\delta\xi_{\nu}-\Gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu\nu}\delta\xi_{\sigma}\ ,

(46)

where
\Gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu\nu}
are the Christoffel symbols:

\Gamma^{\sigma}_{\ \mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\sigma\lambda}(\partial_{\mu}g_{\lambda\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\mu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu})\ .

(47)

When the Lie derivative of the metric is equal to zero
\mathcal{L}_{\delta\xi}\,g_{\mu\nu}=0
, referred to as the metric being Lie transported with respect to
\delta\xi
, then the vector field
\delta\xi
is a Killing vector satisfying the Killing equation:

\nabla_{\mu}\delta\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\delta\xi_{\mu}=0\ .

(48)

The Lie derivative represents the leading change in the metric under the infinitesimal coordinate transformation implied by the underlying vector field. Therefore, the imposed condition on the Lie derivative to be zero is essentially equivalent to the condition that the metric is unchanged under the infinitesimal coordinate transformation implied by the corresponding Killing vector. Given a metric field
g_{\mu\nu}
, the set of all Killing vectors, together with the Lie bracket of two Killing vectors given as follows:
3
3

3

The usual properties of Lie brackets are satisfied.

[\delta\xi,\delta\chi]^{\mu}=\delta\xi^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\delta\chi^{\mu}-\delta\chi^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\delta\xi^{\mu}\ ,

(49)

defines Lie algebra. The Lie group associated with this Lie algebra is the isometry group, the set of finite coordinate transformations that leave the metric invariant. Specifically, the transformations
x\longmapsto x^{\prime}
such that:

g^{\prime}_{\mu\nu}(x^{\prime})=\frac{\partial x^{\rho}}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}}\frac{\partial x^{\sigma}}{\partial x^{\prime\nu}}\,g_{\rho\sigma}(x)\ .

(50)

The last equation can be viewed as the finite version of Eqn.
48
. It is worth mentioning, to emphasize the significance of the above notion, that the homogeneity and isotropy properties are simply manifestations of the presence of isometries
[
30
]
.

A relevant question is how to determine the maximum number of Killing vector. Briefly, the commutator of covariant derivatives is defined as follows
[
29
,
30
,
32
]
:

[\nabla_{\mu},\nabla_{\nu}]\delta\xi_{\sigma}=-R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\sigma\mu\nu}\,\delta\xi_{\lambda}\ ,

(51)

where
R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\sigma\mu\nu}
is the Riemann tensor which has the following, cyclic sum property:

R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\sigma\mu\nu}+R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\nu\sigma\mu}+R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\mu\nu\sigma}=0\ .

(52)

Combining the last two equations and using the Killing equation, Eqn.
48
, results in:

\nabla_{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\delta\xi_{\nu}=-R^{\lambda}_{\ \,\sigma\mu\nu}\,\delta\xi_{\lambda}\ .

(53)

Eqn.
51
applies to any vector field
\delta\xi_{\mu}
while the last equation applies only if
\delta\xi_{\mu}
is a Killing vector. The significance of the last equation is that it indicates that any higher-order covariant derivative of the Killing vector
\delta\xi_{\mu}
can be written as a combination of
\delta\xi_{\mu}
and
\nabla_{\nu}\delta\xi_{\mu}
. For example, applying a covariant derivative on both sides of the last equation will result in two terms on the right-hand side: one term includes
\delta\xi_{\lambda}
and the second includes
\nabla_{\rho}\delta\xi_{\lambda}
. Therefore, the Taylor expansion of a Killing vector
\delta\xi_{\mu}
around some point
y
is given as follows
[
30
]
:

\delta\xi_{\mu}(x)=A_{\mu}\!^{\nu}(x;y)\,\delta\xi_{\nu}(y)+B_{\mu}\!^{\lambda\nu}(x;y)\,\nabla_{\lambda}\delta\xi_{\nu}(y)\ ,

(54)

where the functions
A_{\mu}\!^{\nu}(x;y)
and
B_{\mu}\!^{\lambda\nu}(x;y)
depend on the point of expansion
y
and also on the metric. Given a metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, the set of Killing vectors
\delta\xi_{\mu}\!^{n}
is linearly independent if the following equation:

\sum_{n}{c_{n}\delta\xi_{\mu}\!^{n}}=0\ ,

(55)

has only one solution,
c_{n}=0
for all
n
. For any of the Killing vectors in the set, a corresponding version of Eqn.
54
applies. The last two equations indicate that the maximum possible number of linearly independent Killing vectors in a space of
d
dimensions is
d+d(d-1)/2=d(d+1)/2
. In Eqn.
54
,
\delta\xi_{\nu}(y)
and
\nabla_{\lambda}\delta\xi_{\nu}(y)
are viewed as components of the vectors. There are
d
terms of the former and
d(d-1)/2
terms of the latter (antisymmetry is due to Eqn.
48
), bringing the total number of components to
d(d+1)/2
. If the number of Killing vectors in the set exceeds this value, the set becomes linearly dependent.

The above formula for calculating the maximum number of Killing vectors is a well-known result

5.4
Two-point function

In conclusion of the above, while there are similarities in the quantisation of scalar fields in Minkowski spacetime and de Sitter spacetime, there are also important differences. For example, the symmetry group in Minkowski spacetime is the Poincaré group, whereas in de Sitter spacetime, the symmetry group is SO(1,4), known as the de Sitter group. This difference in symmetry groups leads to distinct definitions of the vacuum state in each spacetime. A similar pattern arises when considering other aspects of the theory, such as the two-point function discussed in this subsection. The two-point function, also referred to as the Wightman function, is defined as follows

5.5
Relationship to the CMB (Gaussian)

Inflation exponentially stretches space and extends the conformal time coordinate towards negative infinity, preceding the onset of the conventional Big Bang cosmology, which begins at the reheating surface. By rapidly expanding space, inflation allows regions that appear causally disconnected at the time of last scattering to have been in causal contact earlier during the inflationary epoch. This mechanism resolves a central problem in standard cosmology: the observed homogeneity of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature across vast regions of the sky

The small anisotropies observed in the CMB temperature are understood to originate from primordial quantum fluctuations in the inflaton scalar field, which is thought to have driven inflation. The inflaton field is quantised by decomposition into Fourier modes in the Bunch–Davies vacuum, as expressed in Eqn.

The fluctuations generate slight spatial variations, effectively causing inflation to end marginally earlier in some regions than in others. This process seeds the initial density perturbations that imprint tiny temperature anisotropies in the CMB and give rise to cosmic structures later. In other words, the inflaton fluctuations act as the quantum seeds—or initial conditions—for the temperature profile in the CMB. The quantum fluctuations of the inflaton field are fundamentally characterised by the two-point correlation function and a dimensionless power spectrum for the curvature perturbation

Early research into de Sitter space was primarily driven by its rich symmetry properties, the de Sitter group
\mathrm{SO}(1,4)
, which contains the Lorentz group
\mathrm{SO}(1,3)
as a subgroup. Over time, evidence has accumulated that the Universe has entered an era of accelerated expansion, suggesting an asymptotic approach to a de Sitter state.

Additionally, the theory of inflation, which gained traction in recent decades, was developed to explain the uniformity of the CMB and to address issues in the standard cosmological model.

The classical geometry of de Sitter space can be elegantly understood by embedding a hyperboloid surface within higher-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. Different parametrisations of the hyperboloid equation correspond to different patches of de Sitter space, which is particularly useful when focusing on specific regions of interest rather than the entirety of the space. Penrose diagrams are a powerful tool for visualising the causal structure of de Sitter space, thereby enhancing the understanding of its fundamental properties. For instance, de Sitter space features an event horizon, similar to that of a black hole. However, unlike a black hole’s horizon, which acts as an inescapable boundary, the cosmological horizon in de Sitter space arises from the universe’s expansion, effectively limiting the observable region for any given observer. The study of the cosmological horizon has garnered significant attention in recent years
[
67
,
66
,
190

In the context of this embedding picture, the ten Killing vectors of dS
4

8

9

Scalar fields played a central role during inflation. In the slow–roll regime the inflaton is near massless, and its homogeneous background obeys the Klein–Gordon equation with a Hubble friction term. Quantum fluctuations are quantized in the Bunch–Davies vacuum. The two–point (Wightman) function of a massive scalar in de Sitter is generally complex: its imaginary part is nonzero for timelike separations and vanishes for equal–time (or spacelike) separations by symmetry. In the light–mass (near–massless) limit it becomes approximately real in agreement with the form obtained in the explicit massless calculation. The (dimensionless) power spectrum is defined from the equal–time two–point function, and via the relationship with the curvature perturbations, it directly connects to the small CMB temperature anisotropies. Non-Gaussianity during inflation can be studied by introducing cubic interactions obtained via including the lapse and shift in the ADM formalism. This yields the inflaton bispectrum via the three-point function. The computed three-point function indicates slow-roll suppression, and any such effect will be very weak
[
137
,
168
]
.

Appendix A
Appendix: ADM formalism

The Arnowitt–Deser–Misner (ADM) formalism
[
147
]
provides a convenient framework to analyse cosmological perturbations and to show that, in single-field slow-roll inflation, the lapse and shift are non-dynamical constraint variables that can be solved for and integrated out (in particular, one does not ignore all metric perturbations; rather, the lapse
N
and shift
N_{i}
carry no propagating degrees of freedom at linear order)
[
137
,
168
]
. In the ADM approach, spacetime is foliated by spacelike hypersurfaces, each carrying a positive-definite three-metric
h_{ij}
, treated as a dynamical variable. The hypersurfaces are labeled by a time parameter
t
(a coordinate time, not necessarily proper time). The ten components of the spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}
are repackaged into six components of
h_{ij}
, the scalar lapse
N
, and the shift vector
N_{i}
(with
N^{i}=h^{ij}N_{j}
). The line element reads
[
226
,
227
]
:

ds^{2}\;=\;-N^{2}dt^{2}\;+\;h_{ij}\,\big(dx^{i}+N^{i}dt\big)\big(dx^{j}+N^{j}dt\big)\ .

(171)

To discuss inflation, one can start from the Einstein–Hilbert action minimally coupled to a scalar field
\Phi_{\rm T}
:

S=\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}{2}\,R-\frac{1}{2}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{\rm T}\,\partial_{\nu}\Phi_{\rm T}-V(\Phi_{\rm T})\right]\ ,

(172)

where
M_{\rm pl}
is the reduced Planck mass, which will be set to unity for convenience, and
V\left(\Phi_{\rm T}\right)
is the potential, which depends on the field. The total scalar field may be decomposed into a homogeneous background
\bar{\Phi}\left(t\right)
and a fluctuation
\delta\Phi_{\rm T}\left(t,x\right)=\Phi\left(t,x\right)
, consistent with Eqn.
103
of the main body. The three-dimensional hypersurfaces are embedded in the four-dimensional spacetime with an extrinsic curvature
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
related to the Ricci scalar
R
as follows (up to a total derivative):

\sqrt{-g}R=N\sqrt{h}\left(R^{(3)}+\mathcal{K}_{ij}\mathcal{K}^{ij}-\mathcal{K}^{2}\right)=\sqrt{h}\left(NR^{(3)}+\frac{1}{N}\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)\right)\ ,

(173)

where
h
is the determinant of
h_{ij}
,
R^{(3)}
is the Ricci scalar compatible with
h_{ij}
.
E
is the trace of
E_{ij}
, which is given as follows:

E_{ij}=N\mathcal{K}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{h}_{ij}-D_{i}N_{j}-D_{j}N_{i}\right)\ ,

(174)

where the dot denotes the derivative with respect to
t
, and
D_{i}
is the covariant derivative compatible with
h_{ij}
. Substituting back into the action and using Eqn.
171
yields:

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,d^{3}x\,\sqrt{h}\,\bigg[

\displaystyle NR^{(3)}+\frac{1}{N}\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{N}\left(\dot{\bar{\Phi}}+\dot{\Phi}-N^{i}\partial_{i}\Phi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-Nh^{ij}\partial_{i}\Phi\,\partial_{j}\Phi-2NV\left(\bar{\Phi}+\Phi\right)\bigg]\ ,

(175)

which reproduces the standard ADM form. The lapse
N
and shift
N^{i}
enter the action without time derivatives, and therefore, act as Lagrange multipliers; varying with respect to them enforces the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints. Varying the action with respect to
N
gives the Hamiltonian constraint:

R^{(3)}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\dot{\bar{\Phi}}+\dot{\Phi}-N^{i}\partial_{i}\Phi\right)^{2}-h^{ij}\partial_{i}\Phi\,\partial_{j}\Phi-2V=0\ ,

(176)

and varying the action with respect to
N^{i}
yields the momentum constraint:

D_{i}\left(\frac{1}{N}\left(E^{i}_{\ j}-\delta^{i}_{\ j}E\right)\right)-\frac{1}{N}\left(\dot{\bar{\Phi}}+\dot{\Phi}-N^{i}\partial_{i}\Phi\right)\partial_{j}\Phi=0\ .

(177)

To solve these at first order,
N
and
N^{i}
are written as follows:

N=1+N_{1}\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}N^{i}=\partial^{i}\psi+T^{i}\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}D_{i}T^{i}=0\ .

(178)

Additionally, the gauge-freedom is used to impose the following (conformally flat slicing):

h_{ij}=e^{2\mathscr{H}}\left(\delta_{ij}+\gamma_{ij}\right)=a^{2}\left(t\right)\left(\delta_{ij}+\gamma_{ij}\right)\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\gamma^{i}_{\ i}=0\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\partial^{i}\gamma_{ij}=0\ ,

(179)

where
a\left(t\right)
is the scale factor and
\dot{\mathscr{H}}=H
. The tensor mode
\gamma_{ij}
(gravitational waves) will be disregarded in what follows. The extrinsic-curvature variables can be computed:

E_{ij}=a^{2}\left(\delta_{ij}H-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\right)\ ,

(180)

E^{i}_{\ j}=\delta^{i}_{\ j}H-\partial^{i}\partial_{j}\psi\ ,

(181)

E=E^{i}_{\ i}=3H-\partial^{2}\psi\ ,

(182)

E^{ij}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\left(\delta^{ij}H-\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi\right)\ .

(183)

Substituting into the momentum constraint, Eqn.
177
, and keeping only first-order terms gives the following:

2H\partial_{i}N_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\,\partial^{2}T_{i}-\dot{\bar{\Phi}}\,\partial_{i}\Phi=0\ .

(184)

Choosing the transverse piece to vanish,
T_{i}=0
, the solution is (up to a spatially homogeneous function of
t
):

N_{1}=\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}T_{i}=0\ .

(185)

Substituting this into the Hamiltonian constraint, Eqn.
176
, and keeping first-order terms yields the following:

\partial^{2}\psi=-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{2}}{2H^{2}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{H}{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}\,\Phi\right)\ ,

(186)

where
R^{(3)}=0
for the gauge choice of
h_{ij}
, and the equations for the background (Eqs.
108
–
110
with
M_{\text{pl}}=1
) were used. The definition of the slow-roll parameter
\epsilon
in Eqn.
111
indicates that
N_{1}
and
\psi
are slow-roll suppressed and can be integrated out at leading order as discussed and implemented in subsections
5.2
–
5.5
.

However,
N_{1}
and
\psi
play a pivotal role when considering non-Gaussianity as they will generate the interaction terms in the action. For example, keeping up to third order in fluctuations, one finds the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+N_{1}}

\displaystyle\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)=-6H^{2}+2H\left(2\partial^{2}\psi+3H\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\Phi\right)

\displaystyle+\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi-\left(\partial^{2}\psi\right)^{2}-4H\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\partial^{2}\psi\,\Phi-6H^{2}\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{2}\Phi^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\left(\partial^{2}\psi\right)^{2}\Phi-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi\,\Phi+4H\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{2}\partial^{2}\psi\,\Phi^{2}+6H^{2}\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{3}\Phi^{3}\ ,

(187)

where the last line is cubic and the terms left unsimplified to easily track slow roll. Computing the remaining pieces in Eqn.
A
, substituting and keeping only cubic terms yields:

\displaystyle S^{\left(3\right)}_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,d^{3}x\,a^{3}\left(t\right)\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\left(\partial^{2}\psi\right)^{2}\Phi-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi\,\Phi+4H\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{2}\partial^{2}\psi\,\Phi^{2}

\displaystyle+6H^{2}\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{3}\Phi^{3}-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\,\dot{\Phi}^{2}-2\dot{\Phi}\,\partial^{i}\psi\,\partial_{i}\Phi

\displaystyle+2\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{2}\dot{\bar{\Phi}}\,\dot{\Phi}\,\Phi^{2}-\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\right)^{3}\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{2}\,\Phi^{3}+2\,\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\dot{\bar{\Phi}}\,\partial^{i}\psi\,\partial_{i}\Phi\,\Phi

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\,h^{ij}\,\partial_{i}\Phi\,\partial_{j}\Phi-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,V^{\prime\prime}\left(\bar{\Phi}\right)\Phi^{3}-\frac{1}{3}V^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\bar{\Phi}\right)\Phi^{3}\bigg]\ .

(188)

Therefore, to leading order in slow roll:

S^{\left(3\right)}_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,d^{3}x\,a^{3}\left(t\right)\bigg[-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\,\dot{\Phi}^{2}-\frac{1}{a^{2}\left(t\right)}\,\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\Phi\,\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\Phi\,\partial_{j}\Phi-\frac{2}{a^{2}\left(t\right)}\,\dot{\Phi}\,\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\,\partial_{j}\Phi\bigg]\ .

(189)

The interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}
is written as follows (up to a spatial boundary term arising from
\partial\left(\partial_{i}\psi\right)/\partial\dot{\Phi}
after applying the properties of the inverse Laplacian
\partial^{-2}
):

H^{\left(3\right)}_{\text{int}}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\,a^{3}\left(t\right)\bigg[-\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\,\dot{\Phi}^{2}+\frac{1}{a^{2}\left(t\right)}\,\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}}{2H}\,\Phi\,\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\Phi\,\partial_{j}\Phi\bigg]\ ,

(190)

where the terms involving
\psi
cancel out. The last equation is the form used to compute the three-point function via the in-in formula as discussed in the main body.

As discussed in the main body, the three-point function is most often computed using the comoving curvature perturbation

(often denoted

,

\displaystyle\delta\Phi_{\text{T}}=\Phi=0\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}h_{ij}=e^{2\mathscr{H}}\left(\delta_{ij}\,e^{2\mathcal{R}}+\gamma_{ij}\right)=a^{2}\left(t\right)\left(\delta_{ij}\,e^{2\mathcal{R}}+\gamma_{ij}\right),

\displaystyle\gamma^{i}_{\ i}=0\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\partial^{i}\gamma_{ij}=0\ ,

(191)

in which

appears explicitly. For example, to linear order in perturbations, the extrinsic-curvature variables are now given as follows (ignoring

E_{ij}=a^{2}\left(\delta_{ij}\left(H+2H\mathcal{R}+\dot{\mathcal{R}}\right)-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi-\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\partial_{j}T_{i}+\partial_{i}T_{j}\right)\right)\ ,

(192)

(193)

(194)

E^{ij}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\left(\delta^{ij}\left(H-2H\mathcal{R}+\dot{\mathcal{R}}\right)-\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi-\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\partial^{j}T^{i}+\partial^{i}T^{j}\right)\right)\ ,

(195)

where it is assumed that
N
and

are decomposed as in Eqn.

. The momentum and Hamiltonian constraints are obtained by respectively varying the action with regard to
N^{i}
and

:

D_{i}\left(\frac{1}{N}\left(E^{i}_{\ j}-\delta^{i}_{\ j}E\right)\right)=0\ ,

(196)

R^{(3)}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{2}-2V=0\ ,

(197)

where
D_{i}
is the covariant derivative compatible with

. Substituting the extrinsic-curvature variables in the momentum constraint yields the following:

2\partial_{i}\left(\dot{\mathcal{R}}-N_{1}H\right)+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}T_{i}=0\ .

(198)

Therefore, it is possible to write:

N_{1}=\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{H}\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}T_{i}=0\ ,

(199)

where
N_{1}
depends explicitly on
\mathcal{R}
in this gauge. Using this in the Hamiltonian constraint results in the following expression for
\psi
:

\partial^{2}\psi=-\frac{1}{Ha^{2}}\,\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}+\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{2}}{2H^{2}}\dot{\mathcal{R}}\ ,

(200)

where the following expression for the Ricci scalar was used (only the linear terms):

R^{(3)}=-\frac{2}{a^{2}}\,e^{-2\mathcal{R}}\left(2\,\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}+\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}\,\partial_{j}\mathcal{R}\right)\ .

(201)

Substituting
N
,
N_{i}
and
\psi
back into the ADM action and expanding to second order gives the standard quadratic action for the comoving curvature perturbation:

S^{(2)}_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,d^{3}x\,a^{3}\,\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{2}}{H^{2}}\left(\dot{\mathcal{R}}^{\,2}-\frac{1}{a^{2}}\,\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}\,\partial_{j}\mathcal{R}\right)\ ,

(202)

which reproduces the Gaussian profile quoted in the main text, now directly in terms of
\mathcal{R}
(and, in particular, yields the usual nearly scale-invariant power spectrum)
[

]
. To discuss non-Gaussianity and the three-point function, one expands terms up to third order. For example,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left(E_{ij}E^{ij}-E^{2}\right)=

\displaystyle-6H^{2}-6H\dot{\mathcal{R}}+12H\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}\,\partial^{i}\psi+4H\partial^{2}\psi-4\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}\,\partial^{i}\psi\,\partial^{2}\psi

\displaystyle+\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi-\left(\partial^{2}\psi\right)^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{H}\right)\ .

(203)

Expanding the ADM action, Eqn.

and

\displaystyle S^{(3)}_{\mathcal{R}}=\int dt\,d^{3}x\;\Big\{

\displaystyle-a(t)\,e^{\mathcal{R}}\!\left(1+\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{H}\right)\left(2\,\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}+\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}\,\partial^{i}\mathcal{R}\right)

\displaystyle+\,a^{3}(t)\,e^{3\mathcal{R}}\,\frac{\dot{\bar{\Phi}}^{\,2}}{2H^{2}}\,\dot{\mathcal{R}}^{\,2}\!\left(1-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{H}\right)

\displaystyle+\,a^{3}(t)\,e^{3\mathcal{R}}\Big[\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\psi\;-\;(\partial^{2}\psi)^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{H}\right)

Appendix C
Appendix: In-in formalism

This appendix is based primarily on reference
[
168
]
and aims to write the expression for the evolution operator
U\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)
as applied to cosmological fluctuations, thereby determining how any operator in the theory evolves with time. To avoid any confusion with the main body, the notations used in this appendix are kept separate, the field is denoted as
\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)
, the conjugate momentum as
\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)
and the Hamiltonian as
H[\varphi,\pi]
, where the square brackets indicate functional dependence. The equal-time commutation relations are:

\displaystyle\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}[\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=i\delta^{3}(\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{2})\ ,

\displaystyle[\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=[\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=0\ .

(206)

The equations of motion, in the Heisenberg picture, are given by commutation with the Hamiltonian:

\dot{\varphi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[H[\varphi,\pi],\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\dot{\pi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[H[\varphi,\pi],\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,

(207)

where the dot refers to the time derivative. Next, it is assumed that the field and conjugate momentum can be decomposed as follows:

\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\bar{\varphi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)+\delta\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\bar{\pi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)+\delta\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)\ .

(208)

It is important to note that the
\bar{\varphi}
and
\bar{\pi}
are merely functions representing the expectation values of the field and conjugate momentum respectively, while the fluctuations
\delta\varphi
and
\delta\pi
are operators. Therefore,
\delta\varphi
and
\delta\pi
obey the following commutation relations:

\displaystyle\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}[\delta\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\delta\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=i\delta^{3}(\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{2})\ ,

\displaystyle[\delta\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\delta\varphi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=[\delta\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{1})\,,\delta\pi(t\,,\textbf{x}_{2})]=0\ .

(209)

Additionally, the functions
\bar{\varphi}
and
\bar{\pi}
satisfy the classical equations of motion in the Hamiltonian formalism:

\dot{\bar{\varphi}}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\frac{\delta H[\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}]}{\delta\bar{\pi}}\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\dot{\bar{\pi}}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=-\frac{\delta H[\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}]}{\delta\bar{\varphi}}\ .

(210)

Next, to obtain the equivalent of Eqn.
207
for the fluctuations
\delta\varphi
and
\delta\pi
, the Hamiltonian is expanded around
H[\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}]
as follows:

H[\varphi,\pi]=H[\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}]+\left(\frac{\delta H}{\delta\varphi}\right)_{\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}}\delta\varphi+\left(\frac{\delta H}{\delta\pi}\right)_{\bar{\varphi},\bar{\pi}}\delta\pi+\widetilde{H}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi]\ ,

(211)

where
\widetilde{H}
contains the higher-order terms in fluctuations (second order and higher). Substituting into Eqn.
207
and using Eqs.
208
-
210
, the equations of motion for the fluctuations are written as follows:

\delta\dot{\varphi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[\widetilde{H}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi],\delta\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\delta\dot{\pi}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[\widetilde{H}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi],\delta\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,

(212)

indicating that the time dependence of the fluctuations is determined by
\widetilde{H}
, which is a key conclusion up to this point. Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\widetilde{U}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\varphi\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)\widetilde{U}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\delta\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\widetilde{U}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\pi\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)\widetilde{U}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(213)

where
\widetilde{U}
is the solution of the following differential equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\,\widetilde{U}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)=-i\widetilde{H}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\widetilde{U}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(214)

here, the intrinsic dependence of
\widetilde{H}
on time is made explicit, and where the fluctuations are evaluated at some initial time
t_{0}
.

The interaction picture needs to be developed next. The term
\widetilde{H}
in the expansion given in Eqn.
211
can be split into two components: the first term, denoted as
H_{0}
, is second-order in fluctuations, while the second term, denoted as
H_{I}
, accounts for higher-order fluctuations:

\widetilde{H}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi]=H_{0}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi]+H_{I}[\delta\varphi,\delta\pi]\ .

(215)

Next, the fluctuations
\delta\varphi^{I}
and
\delta\pi^{I}
are introduced, defined in such a way that:

\delta\dot{\varphi}^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[H_{0}[\delta\varphi^{I},\delta\pi^{I}],\delta\varphi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\delta\dot{\pi}^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=i[H_{0}[\delta\varphi^{I},\delta\pi^{I}],\delta\pi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)]\ ,

(216)

where, for some initial time
t_{0}
, it is assumed that:

\delta\varphi^{I}\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\delta\varphi\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)\ ,\textrm{\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt}\delta\pi^{I}\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)=\delta\pi\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)\ .

(217)

It follows from Eqn.
216
that the fluctuations with superscript
I
satisfy:

\displaystyle\delta\varphi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=U_{0}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\varphi^{I}\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)U_{0}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\delta\pi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=U_{0}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\pi^{I}\left(t_{0}\,,\textbf{x}\right)U_{0}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(218)

where
U_{0}
is the solution of the following differential equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\,U_{0}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)=-iH_{0}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)U_{0}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(219)

here, the intrinsic dependence of
H_{0}
on time is made explicit, where the fluctuations are evaluated at some initial time
t_{0}
. Finally, one can substitute Eqn.
217
in Eqn.
C
, and then use Eqn.
C
to write the following:

\displaystyle\delta\varphi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=U_{I}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\varphi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\delta\pi\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)=U_{I}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\delta\pi^{I}\left(t\,,\textbf{x}\right)U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(220)

where

U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)=U_{0}^{-1}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\widetilde{U}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ .

(221)

Combining the last equation with Eqn.
214
and Eqn.
219
results in the following differential equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\,U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)=-iH_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)\ ,

(222)

which has the following known solution:

U_{I}\left(t\,,t_{0}\right)=T\exp\left(-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}H_{I}\left(t\right)dt\right)\ ,

(223)

where
T
refers to time ordered. Now, it is possible to express the evolution of any operator of the theory
Q\left(t\right)
as follows:

Q\left(t\right)=\tilde{T}\exp\left(i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}H_{I}\left(t\right)dt\right)Q^{I}\left(t\right)T\exp\left(-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}H_{I}\left(t\right)dt\right)\ ,

(224)

which is the desired expression, where
\tilde{T}
is the anti-time ordering.
Q
can be a product of operators and generally depends on spatial coordinates. The above discussion considers a single field theory. However, this framework can be extended to accommodate multiple fields by appropriately summing over all relevant fields where necessary
[
168
]
.
