Application of the PECARN prediction rule for febrile infants up to 90 days of age: a multi-center study
Authors: Tahir K Hameed, Salma H Almadani, Walaa A Shahin, Husam I Ardah, Walaa A Almaghrabi, Mohammed A Alhabdan, Ahmed M Alfaidi, Asma M Abuthamerah, Manar M Alahmadi, Malik H Almalki, Mona A Aldabbagh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrediction rules using biomarkers to stratify young febrile infants at low risk for bacterial infections have been developed over the last decade in North America and Europe. The aim of this study was to validate the Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) prediction rule for febrile infants 90 days of age and younger presenting to the emergency department (ED) in tertiary care centers in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA multi-center retrospective study was conducted on febrile infants who presented to the ED at 3 Saudi hospitals between January 2018 and June 2021. Patients were included if they were full-term, 0–90 days of age, had documented fever, and procalcitonin (PCT) performed.ResultsA total of 327 patients met inclusion criteria. Fifty-three patients (16.2%) had serious bacterial infections (SBIs), 33 with urinary tract infection (UTI) alone and 20 with invasive bacterial infections (IBIs) (bacteremia and/or bacterial meningitis). The mean absolute neutrophil count was 6.6 × 109/L in infants with SBIs as compared to 4.3 × 109/L in infants without SBIs (p = 0.0015). The mean PCT was 8.7 ng/mL in those with SBIs versus 0.5 ng/mL in those without SBIs (p < 0.0001). Nine patients who were classified as low-risk according to the PECARN rule had SBIs; 7 were infants with UTIs and 2 were infants under 3 weeks of age with IBIs. The sensitivity and negative predictive value of the PECARN prediction rule for SBIs was 80.4% and 92.1%, respectively.ConclusionsSBIs are common in our population, and the PECARN prediction rule performed well in classifying febrile infants at low risk for SBIs. The prediction rule was very accurate in ruling out IBIs, with no misclassified cases in infants 3 weeks of age and older. Our study helps validate the applicability of the PECARN prediction rule in our setting.Keywords: Fever; Infant, newborn; Urinary tract infections; Bacteremia; Meningitis, bacterial

Introduction

A large number of emergency department (ED) visits are due to fever in the neonatal or infantile age. Serious bacterial infections (SBIs) are an important cause of fever in this population [1]. The overall risk of the most commonly studied SBIs (urinary tract infection, bacteremia, and meningitis) is approximately 10–15% in young infants under 90 days of age who present to the ED with fever [2–4]. Invasive bacterial infections (IBIs), which include bacteremia and bacterial meningitis, occur in approximately 2–3% of young febrile infants [5]– [6].

Untreated infants with SBIs are at risk of morbidity [7]. Over the last 3 decades, several prediction rules (or criteria) have been developed to identify those febrile young infants at low risk of SBIs. By identifying the population at low risk of SBIs, fewer infants may need lumbar punctures, receive antibiotics, and require hospitalization [8]. Criteria developed in the past include the Philadelphia criteria [9], the Rochester criteria [10], the Boston criteria [11], the Yale Observation Scale [12] and the Lab-score [13]. Most of these criteria take into account the risk factors from the history, clinical examination and specific laboratory investigations [14].

Recent prediction rules on the febrile young infant have incorporated the use of procalcitonin (PCT). PCT appears to be the most reliable biomarker in predicting invasive infections in infants and young children, especially in those with short duration of fever [15, 16]. The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) prediction rule to identify infants at low risk for SBIs was derived from febrile infants 60 days and under presenting to EDs in the United States from 2011 to 2013. SBIs were defined by bacterial meningitis, bacteremia, or urinary tract infection (UTI). Low risk for SBI was defined as normal urinalysis, absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≤ 4090/uL, and PCT ≤ 1.71 ng/mL. The sensitivity of the rule was 97.7% with a negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.6%. Using rounded numerical thresholds for ANC (4,000/uL) and PCT (0.5 ng/mL) resulted in nearly identical sensitivity and NPV [8]. The Step-by-Step approach, validated in European EDs, included febrile infants up to 90 days of age and also used CRP as part of the algorithm. It uses a sequential approach in which low-risk for IBI is defined as a well-appearing febrile infant, 22–90 days of age, no leukocyturia, and PCT < 0.5 ng/mL; intermediate risk was defined as ANC >10,000/uL or CRP >20 mg/L. The tool had a sensitivity and NPV of 92.0% and 99.3%, respectively [16].

Although there are multiple new clinical prediction rules to stratify low risk febrile young infants, there is paucity of data on the application of these tools to populations outside North America and Europe. The aim of this study is to evaluate the applicability of the PECARN prediction rule in febrile young infants 90 days and younger in 3 hospitals in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was done on febrile infants presenting to the ED at 3 tertiary care hospitals in Saudi Arabia between January 1, 2018 and June 30, 2021. Two of the hospitals were in the city of Riyadh (King Abdullah Specialized Children’s Hospital and Prince Sultan Military Medical City) while one was in the city of Jeddah (King Abdulaziz Medical City, Jeddah). The number of pediatric ED visits per year in each hospital is approximately 100,000, 25,000, and 15,000 respectively. Patients were included if they were 0–90 days of age, had documented fever (temperature ≥ 38.0 °C) recorded in the ED, in a prior health care setting or at home within the last 24 h, and had serum PCT performed. Patients were excluded if they were premature (gestational age < 37 weeks), had a NICU admission for more than 5 days, had received antibiotics in the preceding 48 h, had indwelling devices or soft tissue infections, or had pre-existing conditions (like major chromosomal anomalies, congenital heart disease on anti-failure medication, or immunodeficiency). Regarding appearance (“well” versus “ill”), patients were categorized based on the clinical exam as “ill” if any of the following was noted in documentation (“ill-appearing,” “toxic,” “limp,” “unresponsive,” “gray,” “cyanotic,” “apnea,” “weak cry,” “poorly perfused,” “grunting,” “listless,” “lethargic,” or “irritable.”). Patients with the following SBIs were included: UTI, bacteremia or bacterial meningitis. A UTI was defined as the growth of a single uropathogen (≥ 1,000 cfu/mL for cultures obtained by suprapubic aspiration or ≥ 50,000 cfu/mL for cultures obtained by catheterization). If colony counts were between 10,000 and 50,000 cfu/mL, they were considered significant if there was an abnormal urine dipstick or urinalysis (defined by the presence of leukocyte esterase, nitrite, or > 5 white blood cells per high-power field [WBC/hpf]). Bacteremia and bacterial meningitis were defined by the growth of a single pathogenic bacteria in the blood culture or CSF culture (or CSF multiplex PCR panel), respectively. Pathogens considered contaminants included Coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium spp., Bacillus spp., Propionibacterium acnes, and Viridans group streptococci. If a patient was found to have a contaminant they were not considered to have a SBI. Infants were considered to be at low risk of SBIs according to the PECARN prediction rule if all of the following were present: absolute neutrophil count ≤ 4.0 × 109/L, PCT ≤ 0.5 ng/mL, and negative urinalysis or dipstick (negative leukocyte esterase, negative nitrate and WBC ≤ 5/hpf).

Means and proportions of the study participants were calculated to characterize the study participants, overall and in groups. The study participants were divided into two groups based on the diagnosis of SBI: SBI positive and SBI negative. The two groups (SBI positive, SBI negative) were compared using Chi square or fisher exact test for categorical factors (age, gender, appearance) and t-test or Kruskal Wallis Test for continuous variables as appropriate. To determine the detection validity of PECARN low risk criteria for a positive SBI, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), NPV, and overall accuracy were calculated. Level of significance was set at p = 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Overall 327 infants met inclusion criteria over the study period; 172 males (52.6%) and 155 females (47.4%). One hundred and twenty-five patients (38.2%) were aged 0–28 days, 123 (37.6%) were 29–60 days, and 79 (24.2%) were 61 to 90 days. Fifty-three patients (16.2%) had SBIs and of these 8 (15.1%) were ill-appearing on examination. Of those ill-appearing, 6 (75%) had IBIs and 2 (25%) had UTI alone. Patients with SBI had significantly higher mean ANC count (6.6 × 109/L vs. 4.3 × 109/L; p = 0.0015), PCT (8.7 ng/mL vs. 0.5 ng/mL; p < 0.0001), and CRP levels (72.9 mg/L vs. 17.2 mg/L; p < 0.0001) compared to those SBI negative.

Of the 53 infants with SBIs, 33 (62.3%) had urinary tract infections alone and 20 (37.7%) had IBIs. Amongst the 20 infants with IBIs, 11 patients (55%) had bacteremia (7 with isolated bacteremia and 2 with concurrent UTI), and 9 patients (45%) had bacterial meningitis (5 with concurrent bacteremia, 3 with isolated bacterial meningitis, and 1 with concurrent UTI) (Table 2). Escherichia coli was the most common pathogen in infants with UTIs alone (20 patients; 60.6%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (10 patients; 30.3%). The most common pathogens in infants with IBIs were Streptococcus agalactiae (GBS) (9 patients; 45%), Escherichia coli (5 patients; 20%) and Streptococcus pyogenes (GAS) (2 patients; 10%) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this multi-center study, we assessed the prevalence SBIs in febrile infants 0–90 days of age who had PCT measured and evaluated the validity of the PECARN prediction rule for classifying febrile infants as low risk for SBIs. Our main findings were that SBIs are common in our population and the PECARN prediction rule was relatively accurate in ruling out SBIs in young febrile infants.

The overall prevalence of SBIs in our study was 16.2%. This prevalence is higher than that reported in the original PECARN study (9.3%) but lower than the 20% range reported in other recent studies [17]– [18]. In a single-center in Saudi Arabia of the epidemiology of respiratory viruses in febrile young infants in the emergency room, the prevalence of bacterial infections was 19.2%; however this study included some bacteria usually considered as contaminants as true positive cultures [19]. In our study, IBIs accounted for over one-third of SBIs and 6% of the total cohort of patients, which is higher than the usual reported rate of IBIs in young febrile infants of 2–3% [5]– [6, 8, 16]– [17, 20]. The high rate of IBIs in our study may be due to the fact we included only those patients that had PCT obtained, and we did not exclude patients that were unwell appearing. The ill-appearing young febrile infant is more likely to have an IBI as compared to the well-appearing febrile infant [20].

In our study, infants with SBIs had higher ANC and PCT levels as compared to SBI negative patients, which supports the PECARN prediction rule. Applying this prediction rule to our patient population, we found the sensitivity to be 80.4% and the NPV to be 92.1%. In the original PECARN study, the validation group had a very high sensitivity and NPV (97.7% and 99.6%, respectively). While our NPV as not as high as in the original study, 7 out of the 9 infants with SBIs that were classified as low risk had UTIs and all had negative urinalysis or urine dipstick testing. This makes us believe that some of these infants, though diagnosed with UTIs, may actually have asymptomatic bacteriuria. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) on febrile UTIs, the diagnosis of a UTI should be made by the presence of pyuria along with growth of a single uropathogenic organism [21]; this approach is also recommended in the new AAP CPG on the evaluation of young febrile infants [22]. If these young infants with positive urine culture and negative urinalysis/dipstick were excluded from the “misclassified patients”, the sensitivity and NPV of the PECARN prediction rule would be much higher in our population. The 2 patients with IBIs that were classified as low risk based on the PECARN criteria were 14- and 15-day old babies with bacterial meningitis (caused by Escherichia coli and Streptococcus agalactiae, respectively). This suggests that, as neonates are at the highest risk for IBIs [23], very young febrile infants (especially those 21 days and younger) should have a full septic work-up done, regardless if they are well-appearing or have normal inflammatory markers [22]. In addition, repeating inflammatory marker measurement for young febrile infants, especially those with a short duration of fever, may increase the reliability of blood biomarkers [24].

There are several strengths of this study. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study evaluating a clinical prediction rule for febrile young infants in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf region. In addition we involved multiple study sites. The main limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (327 patients). In addition, due to the retrospective nature of the study, we are unable to determine if procalcitonin was ordered more in certain clinical situations (for example in more ill-appearing febrile infants). Moreover, our study design included infants up to 90 days of age whereas the original PECARN study included infants up to 60 days of age. Given the limited number of patients in our study that were 61–90 days of age, we are unable to validate the PECARN criteria in this age group. However as there were no misclassified patients in this age group, we are confident that the PECARN criteria would perform well in the 61–90 days of age group. Furthermore, the role of viral testing was not analyzed in the present study as it was not uniformly performed at the study sites; how it would influence the evaluation of the febrile young infant needs to be studied further. There is an increased awareness of viral sepsis [25], and the reliability of the PECARN criteria on infants with high-risk viral infections has not been described.

Conclusions

SBIs are common and potentially life-threatening infections in young febrile infants. We found the PECARN prediction rule to be accurate in febrile infants 90 days of age and younger in Saudi Arabia. However, larger prospective studies will be needed to further validate the PECARN prediction rule in other populations.
