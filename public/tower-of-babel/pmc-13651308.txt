Trauma burden, coping patterns, and psychological symptoms in Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents displaced following the Ukraine war: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elif Abanoz, Mehmet Karadag, Bahadir Turan, Omer Faruk Tevrizci, Onur Burak Dursun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWar-related displacement may affect youth mental health through direct trauma exposure, family-level trauma burden, and coping processes. Ahiska Turkish families displaced after the Ukraine war are a historically important group because recent displacement may occur against a background of repeated forced migration and intergenerational adversity. This study examined associations between trauma burden, coping patterns, posttraumatic stress symptoms, child trauma-related symptoms, and general psychological distress in displaced Ahiska Turkish youth.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study analyzed data from 50 Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents brought to Türkiye following displacement related to the war in Ukraine. Participants completed the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), Children’s Revised Impact of Event Scale (CRIES-8), 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12), Turkish Ways of Coping Inventory, and Perceived Ability to Cope With Trauma (PACT) scale. Derived variables included child trauma load, parent trauma load, family total trauma load, adaptive coping, and maladaptive coping. Spearman correlations, hierarchical ordinary least squares regression models with HC3 robust standard errors, and sensitivity regression models were used.ResultsThe mean age was 15.60 years (SD = 0.76), and 54.0% of participants were male. Witnessing explosions was the most frequent exposure (80.0%), and in 60.0% of cases both the child and parent were exposed. Child, parent, and family trauma load were positively associated with GHQ-12 scores in bivariate analyses. CRIES-8 scores were associated with helpless coping (rho = 0.37, p < .01) and maladaptive coping (rho = 0.34, p < .05). In hierarchical models, no individual predictor significantly explained PCL-5 scores. In sensitivity analyses, maladaptive coping predicted higher CRIES-8 scores (B = 0.57, SE = 0.25, p = .024), whereas no predictor remained significant for GHQ-12 scores.ConclusionsFamily-level trauma burden was associated with general distress in bivariate analyses, while maladaptive coping was the most consistent correlate of trauma-related symptoms. These findings support trauma-informed and family-oriented mental health interventions that directly address helpless and submissive coping patterns.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Ahiska Turks, Refugee children, Trauma exposure, Coping, PTSD, Intergenerational trauma

Background

War, forced displacement, and family separation place children and adolescents at an elevated risk of posttraumatic stress, anxiety, depression, and broader psychosocial impairment. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that refugee and asylum-seeking minors have substantial rates of mental health problems, especially posttraumatic stress and depressive symptoms [1]. Simultaneously, symptom severity in conflict-affected youth is not determined only by direct exposure to violence; it is also shaped by family-level adversity, post-migration stressors, and individual coping processes [2].

The Ahiska Turks, also known as Meskhetian Turks, represent a historically distinctive population in the forced migration literature. Their collective history includes the 1944 Stalin-era deportation, prolonged dispersion across multiple Soviet and post-Soviet contexts, recurrent discrimination, and repeated relocation processes across generations [3, 4]. This historical pattern makes Ahiska families particularly relevant to contemporary trauma research because current war-related displacement may occur against the background of cumulative, collective loss and intergenerational adversity.

Recent studies on refugee populations suggest that the intergenerational transmission of trauma-related risk may operate through parental psychopathology, emotional availability, family relationships, and broader contextual factors [5–7]. Such psychosocial mechanisms may shape children’s adaptation following war and forced displacement and may help explain individual differences in trauma-related symptoms and psychological distress. In this context, parental trauma exposure may offer additional insight into the broader familial environment in which children’s psychological responses to war and displacement emerge.

Coping processes are central to understanding the variability in youth adaptation after trauma. Trauma-related outcomes may differ according to whether individuals rely on confident and support-seeking strategies or helpless and submissive strategies, and perceived coping flexibility may also influence this adjustment [8, 9]. In refugee children, coping patterns may function not only as correlates of distress but also as potential intervention targets that are more modifiable than historical trauma exposure.

Against this background, the present study examined war-related trauma burden, family-level trauma burden, coping patterns, and psychological symptoms in displaced Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents following the war in Ukraine. The primary aim of this study was to test whether child trauma burden, parent trauma burden, and coping variables were associated with posttraumatic stress symptom severity. The secondary aim was to evaluate associations with child trauma-related symptoms and general psychological distress and to discuss the findings within the broader context of intergenerational adversity.

We hypothesized that greater child trauma burden, parent trauma burden, and maladaptive coping strategies would be associated with higher levels of posttraumatic stress symptoms, trauma-related symptoms, and psychological distress, while adaptive coping strategies would be associated with lower symptom levels. We additionally examined whether parent trauma burden moderated the association between child trauma burden and psychological outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional, observational, analytic study was based on an existing dataset obtained from Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents who were brought to Türkiye following displacement related to the war in Ukraine. Data were collected by the first author between September and December 2022, following ethics approval. After the outbreak of the war in Ukraine, a temporary container settlement was established in Elazığ Province for displaced Ahiska Turkish families, and participants were recruited from this settlement. The first author visited the settlement and attempted to reach all children and adolescents aged 15–17 years residing there during the study period. Both children and their parents participated in the assessment process. All participants spoke Turkish and were able to understand and complete the study measures administered in Turkish. Participation was voluntary, and all eligible individuals who agreed to participate were included in the study. Participants with incomplete questionnaire data were excluded from the analyses. The analytical sample included 50 participants. The available dataset permitted the examination of child symptoms, parent and child trauma exposure, and coping-related variables.

The study sample is clinically and historically noteworthy because it reflects not only recent war-related displacement but also the broader context of a community with a history of forced migration. Nevertheless, the present analyses are strictly limited to variables that were directly observed in the dataset.

Measures

Posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), a 20-item self-report instrument aligned with DSM-5 PTSD symptoms and yielding intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, hyperarousal, and total scores [10, 11]. Child trauma-related symptoms were assessed using the Children’s Revised Impact of Event Scale (CRIES-8), an eight-item child-focused screening measure comprising intrusion and avoidance domains [12]. General psychological distress was assessed using the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12); the binary GHQ scoring method was used (theoretical total range, 0–12) [13]. Coping styles were assessed using the Turkish adaptation of the Ways of Coping Inventory, which yields self-confident, optimistic, helpless, submissive, and social support-seeking subscale scores [9]. Perceived coping flexibility was described using the Perceived Ability to Cope with Trauma (PACT) scale, which includes forward-focus and trauma-focus dimensions [8]. All measures were administered in Turkish.

Item-level responses were not retained in the analytical dataset available for this secondary analysis; only total, domain, and subscale scores were available. Consequently, item-level Cronbach’s α or McDonald’s ω could not be validly reconstructed for the PCL-5, CRIES-8, GHQ-12, or individual Ways of Coping subscales. As an explicitly aggregate component-score check, Cronbach’s α was 0.880 across the four PCL-5 cluster totals, 0.839 across the two CRIES-8 domain totals, 0.717 across the three subscales comprising adaptive coping, and 0.410 across the two subscales comprising maladaptive coping. These coefficients assess coherence among aggregate components and should not be interpreted as item-level internal consistency; the low coefficient for the maladaptive composite was considered when interpreting the results.

Derived variables and statistical analysis

Based on the event exposure data, child trauma load was the count of event categories endorsed for the child, parent trauma load was the corresponding count for the parent, and family total trauma load was the count endorsed by either member of the child–parent dyad. Two theory-derived coping indices were constructed from raw Ways of Coping subscale totals: adaptive coping was the sum of self-confident, optimistic, and social support-seeking scores, and maladaptive coping was the sum of helpless and submissive scores. This grouping followed the effective/problem-oriented versus ineffective/passive distinction in the Turkish adaptation [9] and was not derived by exploratory factor analysis in the present sample. Because every contributing item used the same response metric, summing raw subscale totals gave equal weight to each item; subscales with more items therefore contributed proportionally more to the composite. Individual subscale results were retained in the bivariate analyses to show whether a composite association was concentrated in a specific coping dimension. Given the low component-score α for maladaptive coping, this index was treated as a theory-derived summary rather than evidence of a strictly unidimensional construct.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for sociodemographic variables, traumatic exposures, and scale scores. Means and standard deviations were supplemented by medians and interquartile ranges for clinical, coping, and trauma-load variables. Spearman correlations were used for bivariate analyses because of the small sample size and non-normality in several variables. The primary multivariate analysis consisted of hierarchical ordinary least squares regression models with HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors predicting the PCL-5 total score. Model 1 included age and sex; Model 2 added child and parent trauma load; Model 3 added adaptive and maladaptive coping; and Model 4 added the child trauma × parent trauma interaction term after mean-centering the trauma variables.

Secondary-outcome analyses used ordinary least squares models with HC3 robust standard errors for CRIES-8 and GHQ-12 total scores. Linearity was evaluated using residual-versus-fitted plots and Ramsey RESET tests; residual distributions were assessed using Q–Q plots and Shapiro–Wilk tests; heteroscedasticity was evaluated using Breusch–Pagan tests; multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors (VIFs); and leverage and influence were examined using hat values and Cook’s distance. Leave-one-out analyses were used to assess the stability of the CRIES-8 maladaptive-coping coefficient. Given N = 50 and seven predictors in the final models, these analyses were considered exploratory. A sensitivity calculation (α = 0.05, seven predictors) indicated that 80% power was available only for an overall R² of approximately 0.25; consequently, small-to-moderate associations and individual coefficients were vulnerable to Type II error. Statistical interpretation therefore emphasized effect estimates, confidence intervals, robustness checks, and clinical plausibility rather than statistical significance alone.

Results

Sample characteristics

The mean age of the sample was 15.60 years (SD = 0.76), and 54.0% of the participants were male. Most participants were enrolled in high school (66.0%), whereas 34.0% were not enrolled in high school. The family structure was predominantly intact, with 92.0% of participants living in families in which the parents were married. No prior child, maternal, or paternal psychiatric admissions were recorded in this dataset.

Maternal education was most commonly at the primary school (50.0%) or high school (44.0%) level, and paternal education was most commonly at the high school (54.0%) or primary school (40.0%) levels. The detailed sociodemographic characteristics are shown in Table 1.

War-related traumatic exposures

War-related traumatic exposure was not evenly distributed across event types. Witnessing explosions was by far the most frequent exposure, present in 80.0% of the sample, and in 60.0% of cases, both the child and parent were exposed. Other event categories were markedly less frequent, with serious injury or accident reported in 10.0% of the sample and torture or violence exposure reported in 8.0%. This pattern suggests that traumatic exposure in the sample was concentrated primarily around direct war experiences and that many exposures were shared within the family context (Table 2).

Psychological symptoms and coping profiles

The mean PCL-5 total score was 35.26 (SD = 17.62; median = 36.00, IQR = 23.50–46.75), indicating a substantial posttraumatic stress symptom burden in the sample. Bivariate associations

Bivariate analyses indicated that greater trauma burden was primarily associated with higher general psychological distress rather than PTSD symptom severity. Child trauma load, parent trauma load, and family total trauma load were all positively correlated with GHQ-12 total scores, with the strongest association observed for family total trauma load (rho = 0.37, p < .01). In contrast, CRIES-8 scores were significantly associated with helpless coping (rho = 0.37, p < .01) and maladaptive coping (rho = 0.34, p < .05) composite scores. No significant bivariate associations were observed between PCL-5 total scores and the trauma or coping variables included in the analysis (Table 4).

Multivariable analyses

In hierarchical robust linear regression analyses predicting PCL-5 total scores, neither sociodemographic nor trauma-burden variables significantly predicted the primary outcome. Adding child and parent trauma load increased the explained variance from R2 = 0.018 to R2 = 0.084, and the inclusion of coping composites modestly increased the value to R2 = 0.115. In the final model, the child trauma x parent trauma interaction term was negative but not significant (B = -8.00, SE = 6.31, p = .204). Overall, the final model explained 16.3% of the variance in PCL-5 scores, with no individual predictor reaching statistical significance (Table 5).

Across the final models, VIFs ranged from 1.09 to 4.45. Ramsey RESET tests did not indicate marked nonlinearity (all p ≥ .088), and Breusch–Pagan tests did not detect heteroscedasticity (all p ≥ .112). Residual distributions were compatible with normality for PCL-5 and CRIES-8 (Shapiro–Wilk p = .542 and 0.295, respectively) but not for GHQ-12 (p = .027). Cook’s distance exceeded 4/N for five, three, and four observations in the PCL-5, CRIES-8, and GHQ-12 models, respectively; maximum values were 0.335, 0.383, and 0.760, and one observation had high leverage (h = 0.739). HC3 standard errors were therefore retained, and the models were interpreted as exploratory. In leave-one-out analyses of the CRIES-8 model, the maladaptive-coping coefficient remained positive (B range, 0.47–0.66) and met p < .05 in 47 of 50 iterations.

Discussion

This study examined psychological symptoms in Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents displaced following the Ukraine war, with particular attention to family-level trauma burden and coping processes. Three findings are central to this study. First, the sample showed a meaningful burden of trauma-related symptoms. Second, trauma exposure was clustered primarily around explosion-related war experiences and was often shared between children and parents. Third, maladaptive coping was the most consistent multivariate correlate of trauma-related symptoms, whereas direct predictors of the PCL-5 total score did not emerge clearly in the primary models.

The absence of significant predictors for the PCL-5 total score should not be interpreted as indicating that trauma burden is clinically irrelevant. Although none of the individual predictors reached statistical significance, the final model retained meaningful explanatory value in the context of psychosocial research. Rather, this finding may be understood in light of several explanations that are consistent with ecological models of refugee mental health. First, the modest sample size and the concentration of exposure variance within a limited number of event categories may have constrained statistical power. One possible explanation is that PTSD symptom expression may be shaped not merely by the number of traumatic events experienced, but also by the interplay of family-level and contextual mechanisms. Comprehensive systematic reviews on the mental health of refugee children have shown that family cohesion and parental mental health, particularly maternal mental health, may function as stronger risk or protective factors than children’s individual trauma exposure itself [14]. In small refugee samples, direct event counts may therefore provide an incomplete representation of risk when symptom development is shaped or obscured by parental functioning, contextual instability, and individual coping styles [2, 7]. Moreover, studies of refugee families have reported that parental psychopathology and disrupted family dynamics may predict child mental health problems more strongly than direct traumatic experiences [15]. In the present Ahiska Turkish sample, this pattern may be consistent with the possibility that cumulative trauma burden influences symptomatology through mechanisms extending beyond a simple dose-response relationship, including individual coping processes and broader family and contextual factors.

The final PCL-5 model explained 16.3% of the outcome variance, which may be meaningful in psychosocial research. Nevertheless, with seven predictors in only 50 observations, the model had limited degrees of freedom and was powered mainly for relatively large overall effects; smaller independent associations could have been missed. The absence of statistically significant coefficients should therefore be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no association. Moderate VIFs for the trauma terms and the influential-case diagnostics further indicate that coefficient magnitudes may be sample-specific and require replication.

The most robust finding of the present analyses was the association between maladaptive coping and CRIES-8 total score. Clinically, this suggests that helplessness, passivity, and related ineffective coping responses may be particularly actionable targets in displaced youth. This interpretation is consistent with the broader trauma literature, which emphasizes coping flexibility and regulatory processes in posttraumatic adaptation [8]. Converging meta-analytic evidence underscores the strong association between emotion-regulation strategies and psychopathology in children and adolescents, confirming that maladaptive strategies such as passivity and avoidance are among the most robust predictors of symptom severity [16]. Mechanisms such as helplessness and emotional suppression are known to maintain PTSD and anxiety symptoms following trauma exposure and to impede the recovery process [17]. Furthermore, trauma-informed neurodevelopmental models propose that clinical recovery in refugee youth depends on their transition from a survival-oriented, reactive state of mind - the “survival brain” - toward a more flexible and controlled capacity for self-regulation - the “learning brain” [18]. From this perspective, interventions targeting the maladaptive patterns observed among Ahiska Turkish children should move beyond symptom reduction alone and focus on rebuilding the child’s regulatory capacity in the face of traumatic stress.

The divergence between CRIES-8 and PCL-5 is theoretically informative but should be interpreted cautiously. CRIES-8 was developed specifically for children and adolescents and concentrates on intrusion and avoidance, whereas PCL-5 samples the broader DSM-5 symptom architecture, including negative alterations in cognition and mood and hyperarousal [10–12]. Helpless and submissive coping may be more proximal to intrusion–avoidance cycles than to this broader, heterogeneous symptom total; the shorter, developmentally focused CRIES-8 may also have been easier for 15–17-year-olds to map onto a salient war event. However, the present design cannot establish that CRIES-8 is more sensitive than PCL-5. Differences in content coverage, the low coherence of the two-component maladaptive index, sampling variability, and limited power are alternative explanations. Replication with item-level psychometric data and adequately powered models is needed before the divergence is treated as a stable instrument-specific effect.

The Ahiska context adds conceptual depth to these findings. Because Ahiska families have a historical trajectory marked by repeated displacement and collective rupture, current psychological outcomes may be embedded in a broader pattern of cumulative adversity rather than in a single acute war event [3, 4]. In refugee populations, the intergenerational transmission of mental health risk has been linked to parental trauma exposure, parental psychopathology, and relational availability [5, 6]. The present findings are compatible with this framework but do not directly support it. However, parental psychopathology, family functioning, and broader contextual mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore remain hypothetical explanations for the observed associations.

The main clinical implication is that assessment and intervention planning for Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents should not focus exclusively on the number of traumatic events experienced by a child. Evaluation should also include family-level trauma burden and, in particular, maladaptive coping patterns. Trauma-focused psychoeducation, emotion-regulation work, family-oriented interventions, and approaches that strengthen parent-child relational functioning may be particularly valuable for this population.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is that child and parent trauma exposures were evaluated within the same dataset, allowing for a family-level framing that is often absent from acute war-exposure studies. The Ahiska sample also provides a distinctive historical context that enriches the interpretation beyond immediate conflict exposure. In addition, the use of robust standard errors improved the analytical caution in a small sample.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences. The sample was modest (N = 50), and the final models included seven predictors; this unfavorable ratio increased the risks of overfitting, unstable coefficients, and Type II error. Several observations met conventional influence criteria, and one had high leverage, although the direction and approximate magnitude of the CRIES-8 maladaptive-coping coefficient were maintained in leave-one-out analyses. Item-level responses were not retained in the analytical dataset; therefore, current-sample item-level α or ω coefficients could not be estimated for the principal scales or the individual Ways of Coping subscales. Aggregate component-score coefficients are not substitutes for item-level reliability, and the low coefficient for the two-component maladaptive index further limits inference regarding that composite. Historical trauma and repeated migration were central to the conceptual framing but were not directly measured as standalone variables. Parent psychopathology, family functioning, post-migration stressors, and social support were also not assessed directly and remain possible, rather than demonstrated, influences on the observed associations.

Conclusions

In this sample of Ahiska Turkish children and adolescents displaced following the Ukraine war, the most consistent multivariate signal was the association of maladaptive coping with child-focused trauma-related symptoms rather than with the broader PCL-5 symptom total. Given the small sample, model complexity, aggregate-score reliability constraints, and cross-sectional design, this finding should be considered preliminary. It nevertheless supports further evaluation of helpless and submissive coping within trauma-informed and family-oriented assessment. Larger longitudinal studies with item-level psychometric data are needed to determine whether the CRIES-8/PCL-5 divergence replicates and to clarify the psychosocial mechanisms associated with repeated forced migration.
