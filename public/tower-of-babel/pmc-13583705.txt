Operational efficiency in public infectious disease hospitals across 31 provinces in China: a three-phase data envelopment analysis
Authors: Xin Ou, Jiangping Wu, Junyu Yin, Xianxu Huang, Yonghong Zhang, Yingmin Ma, Qingkun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveInfectious disease hospitals (IDHs) are core components of the national public health system, and high-quality development (HQD) of IDHs is a priority in this system. This study aimed to systematically analyse the operational status of public IDHs in China and provide empirical references for HQD.Design and settingA comprehensive survey approach was adopted to collect operation-related data from 133 public IDHs across 31 provinces in China in 2023. A three-phase data envelopment analysis (DEA) was applied to measure the operational efficiency (OE) of IDHs in each province. Phase 1 calculated raw efficiency and input slacks. Phase 2 eliminated environmental interference to adjust inputs. Phase 3 recalculated adjusted OE.ResultsThe third-phase DEA results indicated that the average values of technical efficiency (TE), pure technical efficiency (PTE) and scale efficiency (SE) of public IDHs in China were 0.610, 0.681 and 0.914, respectively. The IDHs in Beijing, Hainan, Tianjin, Sichuan and Tibet maintained an effective OE after adjustment for environmental factors. The adjusted mean TE and PTE exhibited a geographic variation of ‘East>Central > West > Northeast’, while the average SE showed a reverse trend of ‘Northeast>Central > West > East’. The stochastic frontier analysis regression results showed that per capita GDP (p<0.001) was significantly positively correlated with OE, while urbanisation level (p<0.001) and the number of participants in basic medical insurance (p=0.025) both exhibited significant negative associations with OE.ConclusionsThe OE of public IDHs in China was generally low. TE was mainly constrained by PTE and exhibited significant regional heterogeneity. Each region should adjust measures to local conditions to reasonably determine the construction scale of IDHs, improve infrastructure and medical equipment and focus on enhancing hospitals’ capabilities in refined management and medical technology standards, thereby promoting the HQD of IDHs in China.Keywords: Health Services, Public health, Public Hospitals, Health policy

Introduction

The outbreaks of emerging and reemerging infectious diseases have posed unprecedented challenges to global healthcare systems1 and underscored the urgency of public health system (PHS) development. As a core component of the national PHS, infectious disease hospitals (IDHs) exert key functions in prevention and control, severe case treatment, epidemic surveillance and public health emergency response to infectious disease. Their operational efficiency (OE) was directly related to the allocation efficiency of public health resources (PHRs) and the overall effectiveness of the service system. In recent years, the Chinese government attached great importance to enhancing infectious disease prevention, control and treatment capabilities and released a series of policies to promote the high-quality development of IDHs, including the ‘Plan for Strengthening Public Health Prevention and Control Capabilities’,2 the ‘Opinions on Promoting the High-Quality Development of Public Hospitals’,3 the ‘Guiding Opinions on Promoting the High-Quality Development of Disease Prevention’,4 the ‘Control Undertakings, the National Action Plan for Disease Prevention and Control (2024–2025)’5 and other documents. However, despite the progress in IDH construction driven by these policies, OE-related issues such as unbalanced scale and layout and misplaced resource allocation still existed.6 Therefore, studies on IDHs’ OE have great significance for promoting the rational allocation and efficient utilisation of resources in national IDHs.

Currently, existing studies in IDHs have mainly focused on the operational management models,7 development trends,6 website operational quality,8 development of intelligent management evaluation schemes,9 service model development,10 hospital management,11 economic operations12 and risk prevention.13 The studies on the OE were seldom reported in IDHs, making it difficult to meet the demand for efficiency improvement in the phase of high-quality development. Data envelopment analysis (DEA) is a non-parametric systematic analysis method used to evaluate the relative efficiency among effective decision-making units (DMUs) with multiple input and output indicators. It quantifies the gaps in resource allocation and OE of DMUs by constructing a production frontier.14 As an effective tool for efficiency evaluation in the healthcare field,15 16 it had been widely applied to assess the OE of different types of hospitals, including public hospitals,17 traditional Chinese medicine hospitals18 and maternal and child healthcare hospitals.19 The three-phase DEA model, which combined the advantages of DEA and stochastic frontier analysis (SFA), could eliminate the impact of external environmental factors.20 In view of this, this study aimed to systematically measure the OE of public IDHs in 31 provinces in China by constructing a three-phase DEA model, analysing the influence of environmental factors and providing empirical evidence for the management efficiency and high-quality development of IDHs.

Methods

Data collection and processing

This study was permitted by the National Administration of Disease Prevention and Control to collect operational data of public IDHs in 31 provinces from China in 2023.

We took all 182 national public IDHs listed in the 2024 China Health Statistical Yearbook as the sampling frame. Questionnaires were sent to all 182 hospitals, yielding 133 valid replies with a 73.08% response rate. Reminders and telephone follow-ups were applied to reduce non-response bias. The collected data could well represent national conditions (online supplemental figure 1). First, the collected data were logically checked to identify outliers. Seven logically invalid outliers and six randomly distributed missing values were imputed by referencing corresponding provincial average indicators from the statistical yearbooks published by the Bureaus of Statistics of the provinces where the hospitals were located. The variable-stratified proportion and provincial distribution of hospital samples that required data imputation are presented in online supplemental figures 2 and 3. Subsequently, data related to medical and health resources and service outputs of public IDHs in each province were aggregated. The provincial aggregated bed occupancy rate was calculated via the bed-number weighted average method for further provincial analysis. Data on environmental variables were derived from the local 2023 Statistical Communique on National Economic and Social Development and the 2024 China Statistical Yearbook. According to the standards recommended by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, the 31 provinces were divided into four regions: (1) The Eastern Region covered 10 provinces (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong and Hainan); (2) The Central Region involved 6 provinces (Shanxi, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei and Hunan); (3) The Western Region included 12 provinces (Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia and Xinjiang) and (4) The Northeastern Region comprised 3 provinces (Jilin, Heilongjiang and Liaoning).

Phase 3: recalculation of the DEA-BCC model

DEA was performed again using the adjusted input indicator values and original output indicators, with the input-oriented BCC model still adopted, to recalculate the actual OE of IDHs, excluding the impact of the external environment and random disturbances. The efficiency values of the DEA model range from 0 to 1. An efficiency value equal to 1 indicated efficient status, whereas a value less than 1 indicated inefficient status.

Selection of indicators

Selection of appropriate input and output variables in DEA was crucial. Global empirical studies defined input indicators from human, material and financial resources of medical institutions, with total beds and health workforce size serving as the most popular proxy indicators.23 24 Correspondingly, output indicators reflected medical service supply by integrating service scale and quality, covering inpatient and outpatient volumes alongside clinical performance metrics.25 26 Based on three core indicator selection principles of scientific validity, representativeness and data availability, this study adopted the number of beds and health personnel as input variables to reflect institutional service scale.27 The number of outpatient and emergency visits and bed utilisation rate served as output variables to measure service volume and OE of hospitals.28 Additionally, this study analysed the OE of IDHs in 31 provinces nationwide; the number of DMUs set exceeded three times the total number of input and output variables, conforming to Hollingsworth’s principle.29

In selecting environmental variables, factors such as regional economic development, medical insurance policies and market competition were generally considered to affect the efficiency of medical services in China.30 31 Considering data availability, this study selected four quantifiable environmental factors from the perspectives of regional economic development level, urbanisation degree, policy support, market competition and the impact of neighbouring hospitals. These four factors were per capita GDP, urbanisation level, the number of participants in basic medical insurance for urban and rural residents and the number of general hospitals. Per capita GDP was a core indicator for evaluating the living standards of various regions, calculated as total GDP divided by the permanent population of the region. Urbanisation level was used to reflect the degree of urbanisation development in different regions, expressed as the proportion of urban population in the permanent population. The number of participants in basic medical insurance for urban and rural residents reflected the coverage of medical insurance policies, with its statistical scope including participants in basic medical insurance for both urban and rural residents and urban employees. The number of general hospitals could reflect the competitive situation of the local medical service market, usually measured by the number of general hospitals in the region. All data corresponding to the above-selected environmental variables were obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook in 2024.

Statistical methods

Shapiro-Wilk tests were adopted to verify the normality of efficiency data of IDHs. ANOVA was used for data conforming to a normal distribution, and the Kruskal-Wallis H non-parametric test was applied for data not conforming to a normal distribution to compare the efficiency of IDHs in different regions. The paired rank sum test was adopted to compare differences in the efficiency of IDHs before and after adjustment. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to test the multicollinearity among environmental explanatory variables in the second-phase SFA model. The VIF values of per capita GDP, urbanisation level, number of general hospitals and number of participants in basic urban and rural medical insurance were 3.45, 2.78, 7.46 and 7.12, respectively, all lower than the critical threshold of 10, indicating no severe multicollinearity problem among variables. To verify the stability and robustness of the three-phase DEA model, sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding samples with outliers and missing values, followed by repeated three-phase DEA procedures for re-estimation. R software version 4.4.2 (https://www.r-project.org/) was used for descriptive analysis, statistical tests of the data and second-phase SFA analysis; DEAP 2.1 software was used for measurement and analysis of first and third-phase DEA. The statistical significance threshold was set at p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement statement

No patient involved.

Phase 1: traditional DEA-BCC model analysis

In 2023, the average technical efficiency (TE), pure technical efficiency (PTE) and scale efficiency (SE) of IDHs in 31 provinces were 0.525, 0.591 and 0.914, respectively (table 2). Among the 31 provinces, Beijing, Hainan, Tianjin, Sichuan and Tibet exhibited approximately constant returns to scale (CRS), with all DEA efficiency values equal to 1 (table 2). Although Hebei Province also showed CRS, the extremely low PTE meant it could not be considered DEA efficient (table 2). Twelve provinces, including Fujian, Guangdong, Jiangsu, Shandong, Anhui, Hunan, Gansu, Guangxi, Guizhou, Yunnan, Chongqing and Liaoning, were in a state of decreasing returns to scale, suggesting that IDHs in these provinces exhibited reduced efficiency at their current scale, and further expansion would not yield higher efficiency (table 2). Thirteen provinces, including Shanghai, Zhejiang, Henan, Hubei, Jiangxi, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Qinghai, Shaanxi, Xinjiang, Heilongjiang and Jilin, were in a state of increasing returns to scale, indicating that IDHs in these provinces might be in a phase of rapid growth, requiring more resource input to support their growth and potentially achieving greater economies of scale through expansion (table 2).

Additionally, the γ values for the two input slack variables were close to 1, suggesting appropriate selection of environmental variables and that input slack stemmed primarily from managerial inefficiency, with negligible influence from random errors.

Specifically, per capita GDP exhibited a significant negative correlation with input slack in both the number of beds and health personnel (p<0.05, table 3), indicating that IDHs in regions with higher economic development had higher resource utilisation rates and lower source waste. In contrast, urbanisation level showed a significant positive correlation with input slack in both beds and health personnel (p<0.05, table 3). This suggested that accelerated urbanisation led to rapid urban population growth, while residents’ demand for infectious disease medical services declined, thereby increasing input slack in beds and health personnel at IDHs. Furthermore, the number of participants in basic urban-rural medical insurance exhibited a significant positive correlation with health personnel slack (p<0.05, table 3), indicating that a larger insured population correlated with higher health personnel slack in such hospitals. Although the number of general hospitals showed a negative correlation with input slack in both beds and health personnel at IDHs, this relationship lacked statistical significance (p>0.05, table 3), meaning no confirmation could be made as to whether the number of general hospitals affected medical resource input slack in IDHs.

Phase 3: DEA model analysis with adjusted input variables

Following the SFA regression analysis, the DEA-BCC model was used to recalculate the OE of IDHs in 31 Chinese provinces for 2023. Adjusted TE, PTE and SE stood at 0.610, 0.681 and 0.914, respectively (table 2), with TE significantly higher than in phase 1. Paired rank-sum tests on TE, PTE and SE between the first and third phases revealed statistically significant differences (TE: p<0.001, PTE: p<0.001, SE: p<0.05, figure 2). This further confirmed the necessity of the second-phase SFA regression analysis, as the TE of IDHs was partially influenced by external environments.

Paired rank-sum test of three kinds of efficiency in the first and third phases. PTE, pure technical efficiency; SE, scale efficiency; TE, technical efficiency.

Adjusted TE increased in over half of the provinces (online supplemental figure 4), indicating that low TE in phase 1 was largely driven by unfavourable external environments. Without accounting for environmental variables and random disturbances, the OE of IDHs in these provinces would have been underestimated. Notably, TE rose significantly in Hebei, Shandong, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Inner Mongolia, Qinghai, Chongqing, Heilongjiang and Jilin, indicating that OE in these provinces was more strongly affected by environmental factors (online supplemental figure 4).

Three-phase DEA results showed significant differences in both TE and PTE of IDHs across China’s four regions after adjustment (p<0.05, figure 3). By regional averages, adjusted TE ranked as Eastern China (0.752)>Western China (0.609)>Central China (0.468)>Northeastern China (0.422). Adjusted average PTE followed the same trend: Eastern China (0.852)>Western China (0.680)>Central China (0.517)>Northeastern China (0.446), indicating that management and medical technology of IDHs in Eastern and Western China outperformed those in Central and Northeastern China. In contrast, adjusted average SE showed Northeastern China (0.949)>Central China (0.943)>Western China (0.905)>Eastern China (0.899), suggesting better scale status in Northeastern and Central China than in Eastern and Western China.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis revealed that the efficiency scores estimated by the first- and third-phase DEA exhibited negligible discrepancies relative to the primary analysis after excluding outliers and samples with missing values (online supplemental table 2). In the second-phase SFA regression, the coefficient signs and statistical significance of per capita GDP, urbanisation rate and the number of general hospitals remained largely consistent with the main analysis, demonstrating robust core conclusions of the model (online supplemental table 3). The number of urban and rural basic medical insurance enrollees was the only variable sensitive to sample selection. This variable showed no statistically significant correlation in the sensitivity subsample, which indicated that the effect of the medical insurance coverage scale was vulnerable to extreme observations and missing data.

Discussion

This study was the first national cross-sectional study to comprehensively evaluate the OE of IDHs in China. The overall OE of healthcare services and resource allocation in IDHs was relatively low (61%). TE is mainly driven by PTE and exhibits significant provincial disparities. These findings collectively provided empirical evidence for the subsequent optimisation of the operation and management of IDHs.

With adjustment of environmental factors, the low TE of IDHs was consistent with two previous studies reported in China32 and India.33 However, the study performed by Zhao et al34 reported a higher TE of public health service than this study. This underscored that the OE of China’s IDHs remained at low levels; nearly 40% of available health resources were not leveraged efficiently, rendering the issue of resource idleness in urgent need of redress. Deeper analysis of efficiency components revealed that PTE increased from 0.591 before adjustment to 0.681 after adjustment, while SE exhibited no significant fluctuations; thus, TE was primarily influenced by PTE, which was similar to the previous observations.35 PTE was the pivotal breakthrough for the OE enhancement of IDHs, and constructing a performance evaluation index system was a key approach to promote PTE and address their current operational difficulties.36

PTE improvement was mainly constrained by inadequate capital investment and talent supply.37 At the capital level, although IDHs possessed dual compensation channels, specifically government funding and medical service revenues, their financial subsidies were not only insufficient in aggregate compared with general hospitals but also lacked a differentiated allocation mechanism anchored in efficiency and service quality.38 If indicators such as ‘resource utilization efficiency’ were integrated into the performance evaluation system and linked to the scale of financial subsidies and the distribution of medical service revenues, the ‘efficiency-oriented’ evaluation logic could guide hospitals to optimise capital allocation, prioritise the upgrading of key technical equipment and alignment with clinical needs and preclude capital idleness or misallocation. Concerning talent-related challenges, the root cause lay in hospitals’ lack of robust mechanisms to attract and retain talent, compounded by practical predicaments such as high workload pressure and elevated occupational exposure hazards; talent attrition became prominent.39 In response, the performance evaluation index system could be augmented with a targeted talent-focused dimension, with evaluation outcomes deeply tied to salary incentives and career advancement pathways. This not only delineated the core objectives of talent management through evaluation but also strengthened talent retention via positive incentives, thereby mitigating the constraints imposed by talent shortages on PTE. In fact, numerous studies had corroborated that adequate human resources and systematic training could substantially boost the efficiency of public health institutions,40–42 and performance evaluation served as the bridge to translate ‘talent development goals’ into ‘practical operational actions’, facilitating the conversion of talent advantages into TE.

Furthermore, against the backdrop of uncertainties in the external environment, hospitals struggled to adapt their management protocols flexibly to meet infectious disease prevention and control requirements across different phases. This further led to a disconnection between resource allocation mechanisms and operational needs, ultimately reducing OE.43 At this point, the ‘dynamic adaptability’ of the performance evaluation index system could exert a pivotal function. For instance, during the epidemic emergency phase, indicators related to emergency response could be designated as core evaluation criteria to guide hospitals in prioritising the optimisation of emergency management workflows. In the regular prevention and control phase, the focus could shift to indicators such as ‘synergy between routine diagnosis and treatment and prevention’, steering resources toward refined operations. Through this dynamic evaluation mechanism linked to prevention and control phases, hospitals could be impelled to continuously refine their management processes, achieve precise alignment between resource allocation and operational needs10 and ultimately contribute to the enhancement of TE.

Our study further found that IDHs in the eastern region had the highest levels of TE, which was consistent with previous studies.32 44 In contrast, the northeastern region had the lowest efficiency, in line with the measurements by Yi et al.45 The study performed by Lyu et al46 also observed the characteristic that the eastern region had significantly higher efficiency. Xu et al47 further pointed out that the coupling coordination degree between health resource allocation and health service utilisation in the eastern region was generally higher. This phenomenon could be explained from two aspects. First, public health institutions in the eastern region had a higher density of health resources allocated by geographical area, resulting in better accessibility of health services for residents.48 Second, the eastern region’s higher economic level could provide more sufficient financial support for improving the efficiency of PHR,49 enabling a priority guarantee of investments related to PTE. Before adjustment, the TE of IDHs in the western region was approximately 51%, which was similar to the findings of Li et al.50 The underlying reason might be that the western region had relatively backward economic development and weak medical and health infrastructure. Moreover, the complex inter-provincial geographical characteristics and sparse population distribution further reduced the accessibility of PHR, making it difficult to concentrate resource utilisation and ultimately affecting residents’ effective access to public health services. As for the low TE of IDHs in the northeastern region, one reason might be that local residents preferred tertiary general hospitals, leading to the diversion of service demand from IDHs.51 The other reason might be the dual constraints of economic conditions and government input,52 which made it difficult to support the update of technical equipment and the introduction and cultivation of talents. Notably, Wu et al53 analysed China’s professional PHR supply from 2012 to 2021 via the entropy weight method and found it exhibited a ‘low in the east, high in the west’ pattern. This study also observed this similar phenomenon in terms of SE. Perhaps because although the economic development level could provide basic support for improving the efficiency of public health services, the excessive resource investment scale had instead become a key bottleneck constraining the optimisation of PHR allocation efficiency.54 Therefore, the government should formulate differentiated policies based on the efficiency disparities in different regions. For the northeastern regions, it was necessary to strengthen the construction of hardware facilities in IDHs while focusing on promoting the optimisation and upgrading of software facilities, such as increasing financial support to update technologies and equipment and enhancing staff training and skill improvement.55 At the same time, it should guide hospitals to build a culture of humanistic care, improve the occupational security of medical staff and optimise the working environment to enhance the cohesion and centripetal force of employees.39 For the eastern region, it was necessary to rationally control the expansion scale of hospitals to avoid resource idleness and waste. Hospitals could also adopt approaches such as ‘multidisciplinary collaboration’ and ‘comprehensive development of specialized hospitals’ to improve the breadth and depth of utilisation of existing resources,56 thereby promoting the TE to move closer to the optimal level.

In addition, external environmental factors indicated certain associations with the OE of IDHs. Specifically, the positive correlation between per capita GDP and OE of IDHs might stem from the dual empowerment of economic development on both the medical supply side and the health demand side. On the supply side, abundant economic resources provided material guarantees for IDHs in terms of infrastructure construction, introduction and cultivation of professional talents, upgrading of digital diagnosis and treatment systems and refined operation management. On the demand side, the improvement of residents’ economic level significantly enhanced their health awareness, thereby increasing their proactive demand for early prevention and control of infectious diseases and diagnosis and treatment.57 In contrast, urbanisation level was negatively correlated with the OE of IDHs. This might be because residents in regions with a higher level of urbanisation had a stronger demand for comprehensive and high-end medical services after the improvement of their quality of life; when experiencing symptoms related to infectious diseases, they were more inclined to seek medical treatment in tertiary general hospitals with richer diagnosis and treatment resources.58 Against the background of medical insurance payment reform, Zhao et al59 found that during the implementation of China’s DRG/DIP payment reform, the case mix index of specialised hospitals was generally lower than that of general hospitals, reflecting that specialised hospitals had a relatively weaker advantage in resource acquisition within the medical insurance payment system. Our study further found that the number of participants in urban and rural basic medical insurance was related to the slack of health personnel in IDHs. The potential reason might be that the expansion of medical insurance coverage significantly improved residents’ accessibility to medical care, but patients preferred the general diagnosis and treatment capabilities of general hospitals in their medical choices, resulting in the failure of synchronous growth in the demand for medical treatment in IDHs and thus reducing the utilisation efficiency of health personnel. Therefore, the government could optimise resource allocation through preferential medical insurance policies. In addition, the results of this study showed that the number of general hospitals exhibited no statistically significant association with the OE of IDHs, which was similar to the finding by Chong et al60 that there were small differences in economic operation indicators between IDHs and general hospitals. This suggested that the two had relatively independent functional positioning and operational logic in the medical service market, and variations in the count of general hospitals bore no direct statistical link to the resource utilisation efficiency of IDHs.

In summary, aggregated provincial estimation results help hospitals benchmark their efficiency against provincial efficiency frontiers, distinguish external environmental constraints from internal management defects and tackle common operational weaknesses across the industry so as to support targeted daily resource allocation decisions.

Limitations

This study also had limitations. First, the analysis relied on single-year data and could not fully capture the long-term trends and annual fluctuations in the OE of IDHs. Window analysis for TE scores is suggested for follow-up trend exploration. Second, we imputed outliers and missing values using the provincial average data corresponding to each hospital’s location, which may introduce certain bias into the estimation results. Third, the selected variables might not fully reflect the actual operation of hospitals, particularly the output indicators failing to cover the public health functions of IDHs beyond inpatient care. Besides, efficiency estimation results are sensitive to the selection of input and output indicators, and this study did not perform sensitivity analysis across alternative indicator specifications to verify result robustness. Meanwhile, the current model ignored medical service quality differences when measuring OE,61 which might also generate a certain degree of bias. Provincial aggregated data might have caused ecological fallacy, and findings applied only to regional rather than hospital-level analyses. Limited by single-year provincial aggregate data, we only used one DEA specification without cross-checking alternative assumptions. Future studies could incorporate multi-year data, expand the scope of input and output indicators and control variables, conduct sensitivity analysis using diverse indicator combinations together with window analysis, integrate various analytical models across different DEA assumptions and carry out empirical analyses at the hospital micro level to deeply explore the long-term impact of policy interventions, thereby providing more precise empirical support for optimising the OE of IDHs.

Conclusions

China’s IDHs had not yet achieved full-scale efficient operation, with substantial room for PTE improvement. Additionally, environmental factors exerted a significant impact on OE, and IDHs also exhibited obvious regional heterogeneity in OE. Therefore, each region needs to consider the local economic conditions, urbanisation level, medical insurance coverage and hospital-specific characteristics to scientifically determine the construction scales of IDHs and prioritise strengthening the hospitals’ refined management and improving medical technology standards, thereby promoting the high-quality development of China’s IDHs.
