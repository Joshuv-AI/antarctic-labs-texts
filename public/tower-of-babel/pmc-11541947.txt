Introduction

Periductal stromal tumors (PDST) are rare biphasic breast neoplasms with a unique combination of epithelial and stromal (mesenchymal) tissue. Although the classification of breast tumors according to the recent World Health Organization identifies periductal stromal tumors as a rare type of phyllodes tumor, the determination of precise incidence rates remains challenging. This is attributed to their infrequent occurrence and frequent misdiagnosis as other breast lesions [1].

Based on previously published data, PDST predominantly affects the peri/post-menopausal age group [2]. A single case of PDST has been documented in a 14-year-old female [3]. Notably, one case of PDST in the literature was reported in the vulva [4].

Each newly diagnosed case of PDST presents a unique opportunity to expand knowledge and understanding of this rare disease and to identify potential diagnostic challenges, thereby facilitating the development of effective care plans for future patients. By presenting the distinctive and informative case details of our patient, we aim to contribute to the existing body of knowledge about this rare tumor and enhance awareness among the medical community.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old lady was presented to our center complaining of right breast swelling. She has no relevant medical or surgical history, but her sister was treated for breast cancer. By examination, there was a right breast large retro-areolar well-defined swelling with regular borders. It measured about 4 cm in diameter. The right breast mammogram (Fig. 1) described a right breast central lesion of mixed density, while the ultrasound described a right breast well-circumscribed, retro-areolar lesion of mixed fatty and glandular tissue, measuring 38 × 20 mm. The provisional diagnosis of the sonomammogram was a hamartoma BIRADS 3. A core needle biopsy was performed. The prepared slide revealed breast lobules and ducts lined by bland epithelial and outer myoepithelial cells. There was a proliferation of spindle cells with mild atypia, an elongated nucleus, and an indistinct cell border. These cells are negative for CK. This led to the diagnosis of bland spindle cell proliferation. The patient underwent wide local excision under general anesthesia. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on the same day of surgery. Gross examination revealed labeled breast mass measuring 6x5x4.5 cm, with lobulated firm yellowish-white cut sections and areas of calcification. All surgical margins were inked. Microscopic examination of the excised tumor (Fig. 2) revealed biphasic fibroepithelial proliferation, formed of multiple nodules separated by fatty tissue. The nodules were composed of breast ducts lined by both epithelial and myoepithelial layers; some ducts were opened, and others compressed into a slit-like manner; however, they lacked the leaf-like architecture. Surrounding spindle cell stroma was seen with variable cellularity, and focal areas showed a moderate degree of nuclear atypia and pleomorphism. There were no detected significant mitotic figures or tumor necrosis. The overlying skin and all the surrounding surgical cut margins were free. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 3) for CD34 was positive in stromal cells, while Ki67 was positive in about 5 % of cells. The tumor cells were negative for Pan-CK and S-100. This led to the diagnosis of a right breast periductal stromal tumor with free all surgical margins. The patient did not develop any postoperative complications. She attended a follow-up 6 months after the surgery date. By clinical examination and breast ultrasound, she was free from any local recurrence. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Imaging studies, including mammography and ultrasound, are not definitive in diagnosing PDST due to the absence of specific radiological findings [9]. The tumor in the presented case typically manifested as a well-defined mass of mixed fatty and glandular tissue, making it difficult to distinguish from other breast lesions. Further investigations in the form of a core needle biopsy followed by excision were necessary. This case represents the second published instance of a periductal stromal tumor with mammographic findings that mimic breast hamartoma. The fatty density observed in the mammogram could be attributed to interlobular benign adipose tissue islands infiltrated by spindle-shaped tumor cells [10].

Both PDST and hamartoma exhibit overlapping imaging features, appearing as well-circumscribed, mixed-density masses on mammography, which may lead to misdiagnosis [10].

Due to its low incidence, radiologists may possess limited experience in encountering PDST, rendering recognition more challenging. This underscores the importance of compiling the radiological reports of all published cases in the literature to formulate a comprehensive radiological interpretation guideline for PDST; this will facilitate proper diagnosis and differentiation from other mimics.

PDST belongs to a group of tumors classified as fibroepithelial tumors, which also encompass fibroadenomas, phyllodes tumors, and mammary hamartomas. According to the recent classification system, periductal stromal tumors are analogous to phyllodes tumors but lack characteristic leaf-like structures. Due to their extreme rarity, there is limited knowledge of their radiological and clinical features [10].

To diagnose periductal stromal tumors, pathologists adhere to guidelines established by the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP). These criteria include 1) a tumor with spindle-shaped cells of varying numbers and abnormalities surrounding open tubes and ducts without a leaf-like pattern; 2) one or more clumps of tumor cells that may merge or be separated by fatty tissue; 3) at least three cell divisions observed in a high-powered microscope field; and 4) the tumor spreading into the surrounding fatty and connective tissue of the breast [11]. In this case, the histopathological findings of lacking the leaf-like architecture were consistent with PDST. Immunohistochemical staining in the form of a diffuse positive reaction to CD34, and a low Ki-67 proliferation index (5 %) further corroborated the diagnosis.

Although PDST typically follows a benign course, based on the data in the literature, the majority of published cases and case series have demonstrated a potential for recurrence and malignant transformation if not completely excised [3]. This underscores the importance of complete surgical removal of the mass with negative margins, and their propensity for recurrence necessitates follow-up. In our case, the patient underwent wide local excision, and all safety margins were free without postoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. This aligns with the previously reported literature, which indicated that therapies such as chemotherapy or radiation are typically not necessary and wide local excision with a safety margin is sufficient [9].

Although there is limited data regarding the prevalence of a family history of breast cancer in patients with periductal stromal tumors (PDSTs), the majority of published cases have been associated with a personal and family history of breast disease, either benign or malignant. A review of six patients over 22 years at one institution reported that 3 out of 6 patients had a family history of malignant breast tumors [2]. Our patient also had a family history of breast cancer, which is consistent with the data in the literature. While having a family history of breast cancer is a well-established risk factor for many types of breast cancer, this rare disease requires large-scale studies to investigate its associated risk factors. This highlights the importance of collecting detailed family history information from all individuals diagnosed with PDST, as most available data are derived from case reports and small case series, which often lack comprehensive data on family history.

Conclusion

Periductal stromal tumor is a rare breast neoplasm with potentially benign behavior. It presents challenges in differentiation at the clinical and imaging level from other benign breast lesions, including hamartoma, fibroadenoma, and phyllodes tumors. Complete excision is the standard therapeutic approach, which not only provides the definitive diagnosis but also may contribute to reducing the incidence of local recurrences and malignant transformation.
