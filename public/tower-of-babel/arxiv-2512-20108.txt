Generative Bayesian Spectrum Cartography: Unified Reconstruction and Active Sensing via Diffusion Models
Yuntong Gu, Xiangming Meng, Zhiyuan Lin, Sheng Wu, Linling Kuang

Abstract
High-fidelity spectrum cartography is pivotal for spectrum management and wireless situational awareness, yet it remains a challenging ill-posed inverse problem due to the sparsity and irregularity of observations. Furthermore, existing approaches often decouple reconstruction from sensing, lacking a principled mechanism for informative sampling. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a unified diffusion-based Bayesian framework that jointly addresses spectrum reconstruction and active sensing. We formulate the reconstruction task as a conditional generation process driven by a learned diffusion prior. Specifically, we derive tractable, closed-form posterior transition kernels for the reverse diffusion process, which enforce consistency with both linear Gaussian and non-linear quantized measurements. Leveraging the intrinsic probabilistic nature of diffusion models, we further develop an uncertainty-aware active sampling strategy. This strategy quantifies reconstruction uncertainty to adaptively guide sensing agents toward the most informative locations, thereby maximizing spectral efficiency. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed framework significantly outperforms state-of-the-art interpolation, sparsity-based, and deep learning baselines in terms of reconstruction accuracy, sampling efficiency, and robustness to low-bit quantization.



I
Introduction

Electromagnetic spectrum cartography—the process of constructing continuous radio environment maps from sparse observations—is a fundamental enabler for spectrum situational awareness, interference management, and cognitive radio networking
[
1
,
27
,
25
]
. In practice, this task is cast as a highly ill-posed inverse problem: a satellite-mounted receiver acquires power spectral density (PSD) measurements over a vast geographic area, from which the underlying spatial spectral field must be recovered
[
15
,
16
]
.
However, operational realities impose two distinct yet coupled challenges. First,
reconstruction fidelity is severely limited by data degradation
. Observations are typically sparse due to revisit time constraints and are often subject to low-bit quantization to accommodate limited downlink bandwidth. Recovering high-dimensional spectrum maps from such sparse, quantized, and noisy measurements pushes the limits of classical estimation theory.
Second,
sensing resources are intrinsically scarce
, necessitating an efficient mechanism to select future measurement locations. Random or pre-determined sampling trajectories are suboptimal; instead, the sensing agent must adaptively target areas that maximize information gain. Addressing these dual challenges—robust reconstruction under quantized/sparse data and uncertainty-aware active sensing—is central to advancing next-generation spectrum monitoring systems.

Existing solutions to spectrum cartography broadly fall into model-based and data-driven categories. Model-based approaches, specifically spatial interpolation techniques such as Shepard interpolation

]
, inverse distance weighting (IDW)
[
8
]
, thin-plate splines (TPS)
[
3
]
, radial basis functions (RBF)
[
11
]
, and Kriging
[
4
]
, rely on smoothness assumptions that often fail in complex or interference-rich environments. Similarly, compressive sensing (CS) and matrix/tensor completion methods
[
19
,
13
,
2
,
39
]
depend on sparsity or low-rank priors that may not hold universally across heterogeneous spectrum landscapes.
Conversely, deep learning (DL) methods
[
26
,
37
,
24
]
learn priors directly from data, offering improved performance. However, deterministic DL models typically yield a single point estimate, failing to quantify the reconstruction uncertainty. Without a reliable measure of uncertainty, it is impossible to formulate a principled active sampling strategy that guides the sensor toward informative, unobserved regions.

Recently, generative modeling, particularly diffusion probabilistic models

,

,

[

,
34
,
14
]
.
Despite these potentials, applying diffusion models to satellite-based spectrum cartography faces two systemic bottlenecks. The primary obstacle is the
incompatibility with quantized measurements
. State-of-the-art diffusion solvers, such as Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS)
[
5
]
, DMPS
[
21
]
, and PiGDM
[
29
]
, rely on approximating the likelihood score
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
via gradient backpropagation. This approach fundamentally breaks down when measurements undergo low-bit quantization (e.g., 1-3 bits), as the step-wise constant nature of quantization yields gradients that are either zero or undefined, rendering gradient-based guidance mathematically invalid or highly unstable. Compounding this issue is the
prohibitive computational latency
. Even if gradients were computable, existing solvers require iterative backpropagation through the physical measurement operator at every diffusion time step. This imposes a heavy computational burden, making them ill-suited for on-board processing where real-time sensing decisions are required.

To transcend these limitations, this paper proposes a unified Bayesian framework for spectrum cartography that jointly addresses reconstruction and active sampling.
Our approach fundamentally differs from the aforementioned score-based methods. We do not attempt to approximate the likelihood score
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Instead, we modify the reverse diffusion transition itself: rather than using the standard unconditional denoising estimate
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t}]
, we derive and utilize the measurement-conditioned posterior mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
.
Crucially, we show that for spectrum cartography, this conditional expectation admits a simple, closed-form analytical solution for both linear Gaussian and non-linear quantized observation models. This yields a
gradient-free
algorithm that is not only mathematically robust to quantization but also significantly more computationally efficient than gradient-based solvers.

Furthermore, this Bayesian formulation provides a natural bridge to active sensing
[
10
,
40
]
. Unlike deterministic methods, our framework explicitly models the posterior distribution, enabling us to quantify the
epistemic uncertainty
of the reconstructed map. We exploit this by designing an
uncertainty-guided active sampling policy
that dynamically directs the satellite to locations with the highest predictive entropy. This forms a closed-loop “sense–reconstruct–plan” system, where reconstruction uncertainty drives efficient data acquisition, and new data further reduces uncertainty.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

Generative Bayesian Framework for Spectrum Cartography:
We establish a unified framework that treats spectrum reconstruction as a Bayesian inverse problem solved via diffusion priors. By leveraging a powerful generative models as the prior, the proposed method significantly improves fidelity in sparse sensing regimes compared to traditional methods.

•

Unified Closed-Form Posterior Inference for Linear and Non-Linear Measurements:

Distinct from classic diffusion solvers (e.g., DPS) that rely on computationally expensive and potentially unstable likelihood score approximations, we propose a novel posterior sampling mechanism by deriving tractable closed-form solutions for the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
under both linear and quantized models. This results in a fast, gradient-free algorithm that efficiently handles diverse degradation mechanisms without relying on gradient approximations.

•

Uncertainty-Guided Active Sampling Strategy:
Leveraging the intrinsic probabilistic nature of the diffusion process, we formulate an uncertainty estimation mechanism that captures the spatial ambiguity of the reconstructed map. We then design an active learning based sampling algorithm that utilizes this uncertainty map to select optimal future sensing locations, creating a closed-loop system where reconstruction and sensing mutually reinforce each other.

Section IV presents the uncertainty-guided active sampling strategy. Section V reports experimental evaluations on reconstruction accuracy and sampling efficiency, and Section VI concludes the paper.

II
Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

II-A

Problem Formulation

Electromagnetic spectrum cartography fundamentally aims to reconstruct a continuous spatial power spectral density (PSD) map from sparse, noisy, and potentially quantized sensor measurements. In satellite-based monitoring, this reconstruction is an ill-posed inverse problem due to severely limited sensing resources and irregular observation trajectories.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
denote the two-dimensional PSD map at a fixed frequency, following the configuration in
[
33
]
. We define its vectorized form as
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N=IJ
. Due to sensing sparsity, only a subset of entries in
\mathbf{x}
is observed, forming the measurement vector
\mathbf{y}
.

II-A
1
Linear Observation Model

In standard sensing scenarios, observations are modeled via a linear masking operator. Let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
be the sampling matrix, implemented as a binary selection mask corresponding to the
M
observed locations. The measurement model is given by:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n},

(1)

where
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{M})
represents additive Gaussian thermal noise with variance
\sigma_{y}^{2}
. The objective is to recover
\mathbf{x}
from the incomplete and noisy observations
\mathbf{y}
.

II-A
2
Quantized Observation Model

In practical satellite or low-power sensing systems, the measurements are further constrained by low-bit anolog-to-digital converters and thus undergo nonlinear quantization. We model this by extending the linear formulation to include an element-wise scalar quantization operator
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)
:

\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}),\qquad

(2)

where
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)
maps continuous inputs to discrete levels based on predefined thresholds. This introduces a non-linear and non-Gaussian likelihood, posing significant challenges for reconstruction methods relying on differentiability
[
20
,
42
,
22
]
.

II-B

Denoising Diffusion Models

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)
[
12
,
28
]
are generative frameworks that learn the data distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{0})
via two coupled processes.

The
forward process
gradually corrupts the clean data
\mathbf{x}_{0}
into Gaussian noise through a Markov chain. The transition kernel is defined as:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}\right),

(3)

where
\{\beta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is a variance schedule. As
t\to T
, the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{T}
approaches a standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.

The
reverse process
aims to invert this diffusion. The generative transition is parameterized as:

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\right),

(4)

where a neural network
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is trained to predict the noise added at step
t
.

Crucially, DDPM defines the tractable posterior of the reverse step given the clean signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
as:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}),\tilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),

(5)

where the mean takes the affine form

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}]

\displaystyle=a_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}+b_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t},

(6)

with

a_{t}=\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}},\qquad b_{t}=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}},

and the variance
\tilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}
determined by the noise schedule
\beta_{t}
.

In the standard unconditional sampling,
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is unknown and is estimated from the noisy state via Tweedie’s formula:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\right).

(7)

Substituting this estimate into (
6
) yields the standard sampling step:

\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})+\tilde{\sigma}_{t}\,\mathbf{z},\qquad\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),

(8)

which provides the full transition rule for generating
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
from

\mathbf{x}_{t}
during sampling. However, for spectrum cartography, we must refine this
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
to be consistent with the observations
\mathbf{y}
.

III
Posterior Inference for Spectrum Reconstruction

This section presents the core of the proposed Generative Spectrum Cartography (GSC) framework, a unified methodology designed to harmonize pre-trained generative priors with diverse physical measurement constraints. Unlike heuristic guidance methods that rely on approximate gradient optimization to nudge the sampling trajectory, our approach rigorously embeds the observation model directly into the reverse diffusion dynamics. By deriving exact analytical posterior transitions, we establish a general-purpose inference engine capable of handling both standard linear projections and challenging non-linear quantizations within a single, theoretically grounded framework.

The reverse transition
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
in diffusion
probabilistic models is inherently constructed by first estimating the
clean signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
at the current step
t
. This arises because
the true reverse mean
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
in
(
6
) is a deterministic function of the pair

(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
produced by the forward diffusion process.
Consequently, accurate evaluation of the reverse distribution requires an
accurate estimate of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

In the unconditional setting, the estimator
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}
is obtained
from (
7
). Once external measurements
\mathbf{y}
are introduced, the reconstruction must first satisfy the physical constraints imposed by the observation model(
1
) and (
2
). Consequently, the target distribution is no longer the unconditional prior but the posterior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})
.
This makes the unconditional estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}
insufficient for
measurement-consistent reconstruction.

To enable posterior sampling in the conditioned setting, we show that under the
linear–Gaussian forward diffusion model, the reverse transition

p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})
retains exactly the same Gaussian
form as in the unconditional case. The only component that changes after
conditioning is the clean-signal estimate
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. In particular, we prove
that posterior sampling can be achieved simply by replacing the unconditional
predictor with the conditional posterior mean

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
,
as formalized in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Under the observation models
(
1
)–(
2
),
the reverse diffusion update maintains the DDPM affine structure when
conditioning on
\mathbf{y}
.
In particular, replacing the unconditional clean-signal predictor

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]
in the DDPM reverse step with the
conditional predictor
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]

yields a valid posterior reverse diffusion process whose final state
follows the posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})
.

Proof.

We begin from the conditional one-step reverse kernel

p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{0}),

(9)

which describes the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
given the current
noisy state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, the observation
\mathbf{y}
, and the clean
signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. This is the fundamental object governing one-step
posterior transitions when
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is known.

In the DDPM generative process with observations, the reverse diffusion
step from
\mathbf{x}_{t}
to
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
is determined solely by the
pair
(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
, while the measurement
\mathbf{y}
is
generated only from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
through the observation models
(
1
)–(
2
). Once

(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
are fixed, the value of
\mathbf{y}
therefore
carries no additional information about
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
, and we obtain

p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{0})=p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})=q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(10)

The unconditional DDPM kernel
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})

is Gaussian with variance
\tilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}
and affine mean

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})=a_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}+b_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t},

(11)

where
a_{t}
and
b_{t}
are the standard DDPM coefficients from
(
6
). Combining (
10
) and
(
11
), we see that for each fixed

\mathbf{x}_{0}
the posterior conditional kernel

p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{0})
coincides with
the unconditional DDPM kernel and has mean

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

In practice, however,
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is never observed during sampling.
In the standard unconditional DDPM sampler, this clean signal is therefore
approximated by an estimator of the unconditional predictor
in (
7
). Once the observation
\mathbf{y}
is introduced and the target distribution
changes from the prior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0})
to the posterior

p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})
, this unconditional predictor is no longer
consistent with the underlying generative model: the information from

\mathbf{y}
must be used when inferring the clean signal.

The identity (
10
) implies that, after conditioning on

(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})
, all remaining uncertainty in the one-step
kernel
p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})
comes solely
from the latent clean signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, which now follows the
posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})
.
When we summarize this latent variable by a single point estimator under
mean-squared error, the optimal choice is precisely the posterior mean

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
. Therefore, to
construct a posterior reverse update that respects the DDPM affine
structure (
11
), it is natural to
replace the unknown
\mathbf{x}_{0}
in
a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+b_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}

by the conditional posterior mean

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=a_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]+b_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t},

(12)

which completes the proof.
∎

III-A

Generative Posterior Inference under Linear Observations

Theorem
1
establishes that introducing the
measurement
\mathbf{y}
does not alter the affine structure of the DDPM
reverse step: conditioning affects only the clean-signal predictor, which is
updated from the unconditional estimator
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]

to the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
.
Consequently, substituting the posterior clean-signal
mean into the reverse transition yields a Markov chain whose stationary
distribution is exactly the posterior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})
, ensuring
correct posterior sampling.

In our framework, the clean-signal posterior predictor is obtained through a
direct Bayesian update rather than a heuristic approximation.
The resulting estimator, denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}
,
corresponds to the conditional posterior mean

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
.
For clarity, we use
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}
throughout the
remainder of this subsection to denote this posterior mean.
Substituting
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}
into the reverse kernel
yields a measurement-consistent diffusion update.
The following result provides the closed-form expression under the
linear–Gaussian observation model (
1
).

Theorem 2
.

Assume that at diffusion step
t
, the generative model implies a Gaussian conditional prior for the clean signal:

\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t},\,\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),

(13)

where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}
is the unconditional estimate from (
7
). Under the linear Gaussian observation model (
1
), the posterior mean admits the closed-form expression:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}),

(14)

where the gain matrix is given by
\mathbf{K}_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{\gamma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}}\,\mathbf{H}^{T}
.

Proof.

We treat
\mathbf{x}_{t}
as a fixed condition. By Bayes’ rule, the posterior density is given by:

p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\frac{p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})}{p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})}.

(15)

Since the denominator
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
is independent of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, it serves as a normalizing constant. Thus,

p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}).

(16)

Under the generative model assumption(
1
), the measurement
\mathbf{y}
is conditionally independent of the noisy state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
given the clean signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, implying
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})=p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Substituting this into (
16
) yields:

p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})\,p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(17)

Both terms on the right-hand side are Gaussian: the diffusion prior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
given in (
13
) and the linear observation likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y};\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I})
. The product of two Gaussian densities is also Gaussian. In this Linear-Gaussian setting, the posterior mean is provided by the standard Linear Minimum Mean Square Error (LMMSE) estimator:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{H}^{T}\big(\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}\right).

(18)

In the spectrum cartography problem, the observation operator
\mathbf{H}
is a binary diagonal masking matrix selecting observed locations. Such a matrix satisfies the idempotent property
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}=\mathbf{H}^{2}=\mathbf{H}
.
Substituting this into the gain term:

\mathbf{K}_{t}=\gamma_{t}^{2}\,\mathbf{H}^{T}\big(\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{H}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}.

(19)

Since
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{I}
are diagonal, they commute, and the inversion is performed element-wise. For observed entries (where
H_{ii}=1
), the diagonal element becomes
(\gamma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2})^{-1}
; for unobserved entries, it is
\sigma_{y}^{-2}
. However, the pre-multiplication by
\mathbf{H}^{T}
zeroes out the unobserved components. Thus, the gain simplifies to:

\mathbf{K}_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{\gamma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}}\,\mathbf{H}^{T}.

(20)

This matches the closed-form expression in (
14
), completing the proof.
∎

Based on the derivation above, we have obtained an explicit closed-form expression for the posterior mean of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
given the diffusion state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and the linear observation
\mathbf{y}
. This result provides a rigorous data-consistency update that can be seamlessly integrated into each reverse diffusion step, ensuring that the generative reconstruction process remains anchored to the physical measurements.

III-B

Posterior Inference under Quantized Observations

Having established the framework for linear observations, we now extend the derivation to the more challenging case of quantized measurements. Following an analogous Bayesian structure, the posterior distribution under quantization retains the factorization property shown in (
17
). Specifically, the diffusion prior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
remains Gaussian at every step. The fundamental difference lies in the likelihood term
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
: due to the non-linear quantization operator, the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is no longer Gaussian. Consequently, the standard LMMSE update derived for the linear case is not directly applicable.

Despite this non-Gaussian likelihood, the structure of scalar quantization allows the likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
to be expressed explicitly as an interval probability derived from a Gaussian latent variable. This observation is key: it implies that the posterior density acts as a Truncated Gaussian, which admits analytical moment calculations. We exploit this property to derive a closed-form expression for the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
. The resulting estimator, summarized in Theorem
3
, provides an exact element-wise Minimum Mean Square Error (MMSE) update, serving as the non-linear counterpart to the linear update in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 3

(Closed-form posterior mean under quantized observations)
.

Consider the quantized observation model
\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n})
. For each observed pixel
i
(where
\mathbf{H}_{ii}=1
), let the quantized measurement
y_{i}
correspond to the interval
(l_{i},u_{i}]
. Let
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}
denote the unconditional prior mean derived from the diffusion model, and define the aggregate noise standard deviation
s=\sqrt{\gamma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}}
.
Defining the normalized interval boundaries as:

a_{i}=\frac{l_{i}-(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t})_{i}}{s},\qquad b_{i}=\frac{u_{i}-(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t})_{i}}{s},

(21)

the posterior mean
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}]
admits the following closed-form expression:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\text{post}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t}+\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{s}\,\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Delta},

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is a correction vector whose
i
-th entry is given by:

\Delta_{i}=\frac{\phi(a_{i})-\phi(b_{i})}{\Phi(b_{i})-\Phi(a_{i})},

(23)

with
\phi(\cdot)
and
\Phi(\cdot)
denoting the PDF and CDF of the standard normal distribution, respectively.

Proof.

Since the masking matrix
\mathbf{H}
is diagonal, the observation model implies that the noise and quantization operate independently on each pixel. Thus, the likelihood factorizes across coordinates. For a specific observed pixel
i
, the latent measurement is
r_{i}=x_{0,i}+n_{i}
, with
n_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2})
. The observed quantization level
y_{i}
implies the constraint
r_{i}\in(l_{i},u_{i}]
. Consequently, the likelihood function is given by the probability of the latent variable falling into this interval:

p(y_{i}\mid x_{0,i})=\Phi\!\left(\frac{u_{i}-x_{0,i}}{\sigma_{e}}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{l_{i}-x_{0,i}}{\sigma_{e}}\right).

(24)

Combining this likelihood with the Gaussian prior provided by the diffusion model,
x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\gamma_{t}^{2})
where
\mu_{i}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0|t})_{i}
, the posterior density takes the form:

p(x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},y_{i})\propto\mathcal{N}(x_{0,i};\mu_{i},\gamma_{t}^{2})\cdot p(y_{i}\mid x_{0,i}).

(25)

This describes a Gaussian distribution modulated by an error function, which is mathematically equivalent to the marginal distribution of a Truncated Gaussian. Let
C_{i}
denote the normalizing constant (or partition function) of this posterior:

C_{i}=\int\mathcal{N}(x_{0,i};\mu_{i},\gamma_{t}^{2})p(y_{i}\mid x_{0,i})\,dx_{0,i}.

(26)

It can be shown that
C_{i}
corresponds to the probability of the interval under the convolved Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},s^{2})
, i.e.,
C_{i}=\Phi(b_{i})-\Phi(a_{i})
.
Leveraging the property of exponential families, the posterior mean can be computed via the derivative of the log-normalizing constant with respect to the prior mean
[
36
]
:

\mathbb{E}[x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},y_{i}]=\mu_{i}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\frac{\partial\ln C_{i}}{\partial\mu_{i}}.

(27)

Computing the derivative explicitly:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln C_{i}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Phi(b_{i})-\Phi(a_{i})}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi(b_{i})}{\partial\mu_{i}}-\frac{\partial\Phi(a_{i})}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\frac{\phi(a_{i})-\phi(b_{i})}{\Phi(b_{i})-\Phi(a_{i})}.

(28)

Substituting this back yields the element-wise update:

\mathbb{E}[x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},y_{i}]=\mu_{i}+\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{s}\Delta_{i}.

(29)

For unobserved pixels where
\mathbf{H}_{ii}=0
, the likelihood is uniform, and the posterior mean reverts to the prior mean
\mu_{i}
. Stacking these results into vector form yields (
22
), completing the proof.
∎

This theorem provides a rigorous foundation for handling quantized data within diffusion models. Unlike approximate methods that rely on unstable gradients of the quantization operator, our result gives an analytical solution to the Bayesian inverse problem. This closed-form posterior mean is then directly integrated into the reverse diffusion process, as detailed in the unified GSC in Algorithm
1
.

IV
Uncertainty-Guided Active Sampling

A distinct advantage of our Bayesian framework is its intrinsic ability to quantify reconstruction uncertainty, which we exploit to enhance sensing efficiency. Unlike deterministic interpolation or point-estimate deep learning methods, our generative model approximates the full posterior distribution. By sampling this posterior, we can estimate the
epistemic uncertainty
of the reconstruction—identifying regions where the model’s knowledge is most ambiguous—and direct future observations to these locations.

IV-A

Epistemic Uncertainty Estimation

To quantify uncertainty, we employ a Monte Carlo sampling strategy using the pre-trained diffusion model. Given a fixed observation
\mathbf{y}
, we run the GSC reconstruction process
N
times with different random seeds for the initial noise
\mathbf{x}_{T}
and the stochastic transition noise
\mathbf{z}
. This yields an ensemble of candidate reconstructions
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}\}
.

We define the spatial uncertainty map
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
as the pixel-wise variance of this ensemble:

\mathbf{V}(i,j)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{s}_{k}(i,j)-\bar{\mathbf{s}}(i,j)\right)^{2},

(30)

where
\bar{\mathbf{s}}(i,j)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{s}_{k}(i,j)
is the empirical posterior mean.
As illustrated in Fig.
1a
, regions with high variance (brighter pixels) correspond to areas where the generative model produces diverse plausible solutions, indicating a lack of sufficient observational constraints.

IV-B

Diversity-Aware Sampling Strategy

Given the uncertainty map
\mathbf{V}
, the objective is to select a set of
Q
new measurement locations to maximize information gain.
A naive approach—simply selecting the
Q
pixels with the highest variance—often proves suboptimal. As shown in Fig.
1b
, high-uncertainty points tend to be spatially clustered, typically concentrating around the edges of unobserved interference sources. Sampling multiple points within such a small neighborhood yields diminishing marginal returns due to the high spatial correlation of the electromagnetic field. This phenomenon leads to
information redundancy
, reducing the overall efficacy of the sensing budget.

To address this, we propose a diversity-aware sampling strategy based on K-means clustering. This method balances the need to sample high-uncertainty regions with the requirement for spatial coverage.
We construct a feature set
\mathcal{D}=\{(i,j,\tilde{v}_{ij})\}
for all unobserved pixels, where
(i,j)
are spatial coordinates and
\tilde{v}_{ij}=\mathbf{V}(i,j)/\max(\mathbf{V})
is the normalized uncertainty.
We partition
\mathcal{D}
into
Q
clusters using the K-means algorithm. Within each cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, we select the single representative point with the maximum uncertainty:

\mathbf{q}_{k}=\arg\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbf{V}(i,j),\quad k=1,\ldots,Q.

(31)

This procedure ensures that the selected samples are distributed across different regions of high ambiguity rather than being clumped together. As demonstrated in Fig.
1c
, the K-means approach successfully disperses the sampling points, covering multiple uncertainty hotspots simultaneously. The complete procedure is detailed in Algorithm

.

Experiments

In this section, we present a comprehensive empirical evaluation of the proposed Generative Spectrum Cartography (GSC) framework. The assessment is conducted across two distinct scenarios: (1) a large-scale simulated electromagnetic spectrum dataset designed to rigorously test reconstruction fidelity under diverse degradation conditions, and (2) a high-fidelity satellite-based emulation reflecting realistic orbital sensing geometries.
These experiments aim to validate three core hypotheses: the superiority of generative priors over classical interpolation and deterministic deep learning; the robustness of our closed-form posterior updates under low-bit quantization; and the information-theoretic efficiency of the proposed uncertainty-guided active sampling strategy.

V-A

Simulated Electromagnetic Map

Dataset construction.
The simulated electromagnetic map dataset is generated
using a collaborative model combining Spatial Loss Function (SLF) and Power Spectral
Density (PSD). The SLF accounts for path loss, modeled as distance-dependent decay
between transmitter and receiver, and log-normal shadowing, characterized by a
zero-mean Gaussian distribution with spatial correlation controlled by a decorrelation
distance parameter. The PSD is modeled as a sum of randomly scaled sinc functions,
with center frequency, sidelobe width, and power intensity following predefined
uniform distributions. The electromagnetic field distribution over a

50\times 50\,\text{m}^{2}
grid is computed as

E=\sum_{r=1}^{R}S_{r}\odot\mathcal{F}(C_{r})+N,

(32)

where
S_{r}
and
C_{r}
represent the SLF and PSD matrices of the
r
-th
signal source, respectively, and
N
denotes additive noise. This model
incorporates noise interference to emulate real-world electromagnetic propagation.

The dataset consists of 100,000 simulated electromagnetic maps generated from
single-transmitter spatial loss functions (SLFs) on a
50\times 50
grid.
Transmitter locations are uniformly distributed across the spatial area, and the
path-loss exponent
\gamma_{r}
is sampled from a uniform distribution to emulate
diverse propagation environments. Multiple transmitters are incorporated by
superimposing independently generated SLFs, with the number of transmitters

R\in\{1,2,\dots,10\}
. Each SLF is further associated with a randomly generated
power spectral density (PSD), resulting in a set of three-dimensional electromagnetic
tensors
X(:,:,k)
. These 100,000 samples are used to
train the diffusion model, enabling it to learn the underlying statistical
structure of electromagnetic field distributions.

VI
Conclusion

This paper has established a unified Bayesian framework for satellite-based spectrum cartography, jointly addressing the twin challenges of high-fidelity reconstruction under degraded observations and information-efficient active sensing.
Departing from standard diffusion-based inverse solvers that rely on computationally expensive and potentially unstable gradient approximations, we proposed a novel gradient-free posterior sampling paradigm.
By deriving tractable, closed-form posterior transition kernels, our method incorporates both linear and non-linear (quantized) measurement constraints directly into the reverse diffusion process. This not only resolves the differentiability bottleneck inherent in low-bit quantized sensing but also significantly reduces computational latency. Furthermore, we bridged the gap between reconstruction and sensing by exploiting the epistemic uncertainty intrinsic to the generative diffusion prior.
By quantifying the spatial ambiguity of the reconstructed map, we developed an uncertainty-guided active sampling strategy that creates a closed-loop system where sensing decisions are driven by the maximization of information gain.
Extensive experiments on both synthetic and realistic datasets demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms state-of-the-art interpolation and deep learning baselines in terms of reconstruction accuracy, particularly in regimes with extreme data sparsity and coarse quantization. Future work will focus on extending this probabilistic framework to spatiotemporal spectrum cartography to capture dynamic radio environments, and on exploring distributed multi-agent collaborative sensing where communication constraints interact with posterior updates.
