Sugar caps protect mRNAs from decay
Authors: Rebecca Forbes, Boris Görke
Published: 2025

The modification state at the 5′ end of RNA is a key determinant of RNA fate and function. For decades, a N7-methylguanosine at the 5′ end was considered a hallmark of eukaryotic mRNAs, whereas bacterial mRNAs were believed to merely possess phosphate groups at the 5′ terminus. Curiously, recent research revealed that RNA 5′ ends can also be modified (or “capped”) with various nucleotide metabolites, most of which are incorporated by the RNA polymerase during transcription initiation (1, 2). A study from the Belasco lab, published in the current issue of PNAS by Banerjee et al. (3), investigated one of these mysterious noncanonical caps, namely uridine diphosphate glucose (UDP-glucose)—an RNA modification that is detectable in bacterial as well as eukaryotic RNA (2). UDP-glucose is an activated form of glucose and is involved in numerous important glycosylation events in all domains of life. Yet, it was not known that UDP-glucose can also be incorporated at the 5′ end of mRNA. Additionally, which particular RNAs carry such a cap, to what extent, and for what purpose was unknown. Banerjee et al. demonstrate that mRNAs in Escherichia coli beginning with a uridine (U) are specifically and extensively modified at their 5′ ends with glucose (3). They also show that these glucose-capped mRNAs are highly protected from degradation as compared to their unmodified counterparts (Fig. 1). The increased RNA stability conferred by UDP-glucose capping could potentially result in elevated levels of the encoded proteins. Intriguingly, this feature can be transferred to another mRNA that naturally starts with a cytidine (C) by substitution of the latter with a U. Thus, the capacity for glucose capping and the subsequent stability it confers is contingent on U being the nucleotide used for initiating transcription. Consequently, bacteria might be able to program mRNA half-life by selecting a suitable start nucleotide for the RNA polymerase.

5′ UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs escape rapid degradation enabling their accumulation. During canonical transcription initiation, RNA polymerase utilizes a nucleoside triphosphate to generate 5′ triphosphorylated primary transcripts. The RNA pyrophosphohydrolase RppH converts 5′ triphosphate to 5′ monophosphate groups, rendering the dephosphorylated transcripts susceptible to degradation. However, RNA polymerase can alternatively incorporate UDP-glucose at the 5′ ends of RNAs that initiate with uridine. These transcripts bearing a 5′ UDP-glucose cap are protected from degradation, and their enhanced stability likely enables their accumulation within the cell.

Approaches to identify RNA molecules carrying a particular 5′ cap often rely on the development of capturing methods allowing for their selective isolation from cells, followed by RNA sequencing for identification (4, 5). Unfortunately, such high-throughput pipelines are not available for many of the previously identified noncanonical RNA caps including UDP-glucose. Circumventing these limitations, Banerjee et al. selected two model U-initiated mRNAs and adapted biochemical and molecular biology methods to identify their 5′ modification status. Utilization of a programable ribozyme directed against these specific mRNAs enabled the release of fragments from the 5′ ends that were short enough to separate modified and nonmodified variants by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Advantageously, this approach detects both variants of a given mRNA concomitantly, allowing for their direct comparison. The authors find that 30 to 40% of each mRNA examined are modified at their 5′ terminal U residue. They provide in vitro and in vivo evidence that this modification refers mainly to UDP-glucose. The extent of capping was markedly reduced in an E. coli mutant that was unable to synthesize UDP-glucose, indicating that the majority of the observed caps are derived from this UDP-sugar. Additionally, the authors compared the biophysical and biochemical properties of the cellularly capped mRNAs with in vitro generated mRNA standards carrying different caps. The in vivo capped mRNAs perfectly recapitulated the electrophoretic mobility properties of the UDP-glucose-capped standard, confirming the identity of this modification. Moreover, the glucose-capped mRNAs were only weakly susceptible to RppH, an enzyme that initiates the degradation of many RNAs. RppH converts 5′ triphosphorylated transcripts to their 5′ monophosphorylated variants. Thereby, RppH-processed transcripts including mRNAs and small regulatory RNAs, become vulnerable to attack by degrading endoribonucleases (Fig. 1; 6, 7).

In line with their low susceptibility to decapping by RppH, the UDP-glucose-modified mRNAs exhibited enhanced stability compared to their 5′ triphosphorylated (unmodified) counterparts, allowing for their accumulation in the cell (Fig. 1; 3). Notably, it is demonstrated that a C to U substitution of the nucleotide at which transcription initiates confers the ability to become capped and protected from decay. This is a far-reaching observation as it suggests that the transcription initiation site determines the incorporation of UDP-glucose at the 5′ end of an mRNA. The findings by the Belasco lab imply that a significant proportion of the primary transcripts beginning with U in E. coli could carry this cap. However, it remains to be seen whether all UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs are protected from degradation, given that not all transcripts follow the RppH-dependent decay pathway. Nonetheless, bacteria might be able to program mRNA stability solely through selecting a suitable initiating nucleotide for the RNA polymerase. As bacteria often possess multiple promoters for the transcription of a gene, this may even allow for differential expression of mRNA isoforms with distinct stabilities as needed by the cell. The rate of UDP-glucose incorporation by RNA polymerase is likely determined by the cellular concentration of this metabolite, which may vary during the life cycle of a bacterium. UDP-glucose is an important precursor for synthesis of several components of the cell envelope and for polysaccharide components of biofilms. The mechanism discovered here would therefore provide an elegant means of coupling mRNA half-life directly to metabolite availability.

Whether the increased stability of the UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs results in elevated levels of the encoded proteins remains to be seen, however, this is a reasonable assumption because the 5′ modification state of mRNAs is not known to interfere with translation in bacteria. It also remains unclear how cells eliminate the otherwise stable UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs when physiological conditions change, and rapid degradation is required to enable genetic reprogramming and adaptation. An enzyme capable of removing the UDP-glucose caps rapidly is not present under normal growth conditions. However, it cannot be excluded that such an enzyme is only produced when needed. Alternatively, cells could eliminate UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs by employing small regulatory RNAs that expose the transcripts to endoribonucleases that degrade them (7, 8).

The results of Banerjee et al. are also surprising because a prior global study targeting various noncanonical cap structures in RNA hydrolysates found uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) to be much more common than UDP-glucose at the 5′ ends of U-initiated RNAs in E. coli (2). Although the E. coli RNA polymerase incorporates UDP-glucose and UDP-GlcNAc with comparable affinities into a U-initiated transcript in vitro (9), the intracellular concentration of UDP-GlcNAc is ~fourfold higher than UDP-glucose in exponentially growing E. coli cells, explaining the prevalence of UDP-GlcNAc caps. Contrary to expectation, Banerjee et al. did not observe any UDP-GlcNAc caps on the mRNAs they investigated. One possibility is that UDP-GlcNAc caps were indeed incorporated, but the corresponding RNAs are rapidly degraded. By contrast, the greater stability of UDP-glucose-capped mRNAs as compared to the 5′ triphosphorylated variants may allow them to accumulate over time (Fig. 1), even though the RNA polymerase incorporates these caps with a lower frequency. It also appears feasible that the UDP-GlcNAc caps are posttranscriptionally converted to other caps including UDP-glucose by yet unknown mechanisms.

Beyond UDP-glucose, several other noncanonical 5′ RNA caps have been identified in bacteria. For example, the Belasco lab has previously shown that RNA polymerase can also incorporate the adenosine-derived dinucleotide tetraphosphates (Np4As) into the 5′ end of RNA during transcription initiation (10). As was shown for UDP-glucose caps, the Np4A caps confer a protective effect against RNA degradation. However, unlike UDP-glucose, the Np4 caps are steadily removed by a cognate decapping enzyme and are therefore not detectable in unstressed wild-type cells. Yet, under disulfide stress, the decapping enzyme becomes inactivated, thereby promoting accumulation of Np4A-capped RNAs, suggesting a possible role in stress adaptation (11). It has been known for decades that Np4As accumulate upon stress. The finding of the Belasco lab showed that the target of the accumulating Np4As upon disulfide stress was not a protein, as was presumed, but rather the Np4A metabolite becomes incorporated into the 5′ end of RNA. A role for another RNA cap, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), was recently elucidated in bacteria. NAD is the most intensively studied noncanonical RNA cap and exists in prokaryotes as well as eukaryotes. No prominent role for RNA stability, translation, or function could be assigned to the bacterial NAD cap (12). However, the Höfer lab recently discovered that the NAD cap can be used to link RNA covalently with proteins—a process that was named RNAylation, which is thought to play a role during viral infection of bacteria (13).

In the current issue of PNAS, the Belasco lab pushes forward the efforts to decode the functions of these 5′ metabolite caps on RNA. Rather than identifying RNA modifications on a global scale, the authors employ a targeted approach to identify 5′ RNA modifications present on specific RNA molecules of interest. By focusing on specific model mRNAs, the authors could infer the function of an RNA modification, providing an approach that can be extrapolated to other RNAs.
