Comparative Effectiveness of Fluid Absorption Between Superabsorbent and Foam Dressings in Split‐Thickness Skin‐Graft Donor Sites: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Thanapat Chaisudhiphongskul, Akaradech Attainsee, Chairat Burusapat, Suttisun Jankajorn, Nutthapong Wanichjaroen, Nuttadon Wongpakob, Rapeepat Sapruangthong, Chatchai Pruksapong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute wounds with high exudate output, such as split‐thickness skin‐graft donor sites, require dressings that effectively manage fluid, reduce pain, and support timely epithelialization. Foam dressings are widely used, but superabsorbent materials may provide advantages in exudate handling. This study compared the absorption performance, wound healing, and pain outcomes of foam versus superabsorbent dressings in donor‐site wounds. Thirty patients undergoing split‐thickness skin grafting were enrolled, with no exclusions or loss to follow‐up. Each donor site was divided into two equal zones, with one receiving a foam dressing and the other a superabsorbent dressing according to randomized allocation. Data were collected using coded identifiers. Clinical outcomes, including absorption capacity, wound epithelialization, pain scores, and complications, were evaluated at 3‐day intervals through postoperative day 15. Experimental testing showed that a 10 × 10 cm foam dressing weighed 7.5 g and absorbed 75 mL of saline, whereas the superabsorbent dressing weighed 5.5 g and absorbed 93 mL. Clinically, absorption power was significantly higher with the superabsorbent dressing on day 3 (104.09 vs. 71.87 mg/cm2/day; p = 0.034), day 6 (84.45 vs. 40.91 mg/cm2/day; p < 0.001), and day 9 (76.59 vs. 19.74 mg/cm2/day; p = 0.022). Pain scores tended to be lower with the superabsorbent dressing on days 6 and 9, although the differences were not statistically significant. Wound epithelialization was comparable between groups, with no allergic reactions, infections, or other complications observed. Superabsorbent dressing demonstrated superior absorption capacity compared with foam dressing, while achieving comparable epithelialization and healing outcomes. Pain scores tended to be lower with the superabsorbent dressing during the early postoperative period, although the differences were not statistically significant.Level of Evidence1.

Background

Acute wounds are injuries to the skin or underlying tissues that occur suddenly. Common examples include lacerations, cuts, abrasions, burn wounds, and surgical wounds. These injuries occur frequently from routine daily activities, occupational exposure, or accidental events [1, 2]. In most uncomplicated situations, acute wounds heal within a few weeks under standard wound care using simple gauze and saline, particularly in low‐income countries where advanced products may not be readily available. Although this basic treatment is effective, it does not actively enhance the wound environment.

Numerous experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that a moist wound environment accelerates healing [3, 4, 5]. Moisture helps maintain cellular viability, prevents desiccation at the wound base, and supports faster epithelial migration. In contrast, dehydration of the wound surface leads to necrosis and eschar formation. Because necrotic material acts as a medium for bacterial growth and delays tissue repair, it should be removed at the initial presentation and during follow‐up when present. Maintaining optimal moisture balance is therefore a key principle in modern wound care [6, 7].

Wound exudate is often viewed negatively because uncontrolled drainage can damage the peri‐wound skin, deteriorate the wound bed, and contribute to an increase in wound size. These complications can prolong healing and adversely affect patient comfort. Uncontrolled exudate also increases healthcare costs, as frequent dressing changes—sometimes daily or twice daily—are required to prevent leakage and maceration. This increases both the material cost and the overall treatment burden for patients and healthcare systems.

An ideal open‐wound dressing should maintain a moist and clean environment, protect the area from external trauma, reduce edema, and support tissue repair while remaining cost‐effective. In addition, a suitable dressing should minimize dressing‐change frequency and prevent skin irritation. Although many dressing types exist, no single product provides all these characteristics across all wound types.

Superabsorbent dressings incorporate materials designed to rapidly absorb and retain large amounts of fluid, which helps create a more stable healing environment [8, 9, 10]. Previous studies have shown benefits of superabsorbent dressings in managing wounds with moderate to heavy exudate [11, 12, 13, 14]. However, despite these advantages, their use remains limited, and comparative data between superabsorbent and foam dressings in acute surgical wounds are insufficient.

The donor site of a split‐thickness skin graft is a common iatrogenic acute wound and can be relatively large, depending on the defect being reconstructed. Although considered a routine part of reconstructive surgery, donor‐site wounds frequently cause significant discomfort, including pain and excessive drainage, which may impact patient quality of life and slow healing. Effective exudate control at the donor site is therefore an important clinical issue.

This study aims to compare the fluid‐absorption capacity of superabsorbent dressings and foam dressings in donor sites of split‐thickness skin grafts. A secondary objective is to evaluate pain experienced during dressing changes and to assess overall healing duration between the two dressing types.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Study Design

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted at Phramongkutklao Hospital between June 2023 and October 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Royal Thai Army Medical Department (IRBRTA 1933/2568). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, including consent for the use and publication of clinical photographs obtained during the study.

Eligible participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: age over 20 years and undergoing a split‐thickness skin grafting procedure for any clinical indication. Exclusion criteria were established to minimize confounding factors and included a documented allergy to any component of the superabsorbent polymer dressing (Superabsorbent polymer, Polyethylene, Polypropylene, or Trilaminate fabric), a history of using other wound dressing products that might influence wound healing characteristics, the presence of a known bleeding disorder, and pregnancy.

Sample size determination was performed prior to participant enrollment. Reference data were extracted from previous literature, and the calculation followed a clinical superiority trial framework. Using the G*Power software and applying the appropriate sample size formula, a minimum of 15 participants per group was determined to be necessary to achieve adequate statistical power. This ensured that the study would be sufficiently powered to detect clinically meaningful differences between the two dressing types evaluated.

Randomization

After enrollment, each participant's donor‐site wound was divided into two equal zones (upper and lower). One zone received a foam dressing (Allevyn; Smith & Nephew, Watford, England) as the control, and the other received a superabsorbent dressing (Innosorb Superabsorbent; PTT Innovation Institute, Bangkok, Thailand) as the study intervention. Data were collected using coded numbers rather than patient identifiers. Stratified block randomization was performed, with computer‐generated sequences stratified by the study center at Phramongkutklao Hospital. Variable block sizes of 4 and 6 were used to minimize allocation predictability, and sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) were prepared. Randomization was executed in the operating room: before the procedure began, the first assistant obtained the allocation code from the central center and informed the surgeon accordingly (Figure 1).

Study Protocol

Experimental Study

Before the clinical trial commenced, a preliminary laboratory assessment was carried out to compare the absorption capacity of the two dressing materials. Normal saline (NSS) was selected as the testing fluid because its physical properties closely resemble the serous exudate commonly produced from donor site skin graft wounds. To ensure standardization, each dressing type was prepared in uniform dimensions of 10 × 10 cm, providing an identical surface area for controlled comparison.

NSS was applied gradually onto each dressing using a calibrated syringe, allowing precise measurement of fluid volume delivered. The saline was gently distributed across the entire surface of the dressing while additional NSS was added until the material reached full saturation. Saturation was defined as the point at which the dressing could no longer absorb additional fluid and visible pooling or leakage occurred. The total volume of NSS (mL) absorbed at saturation was recorded as the primary indicator of absorption performance.

To enhance measurement accuracy, each dressing sample was weighed immediately before and after the absorption test using a high‐precision digital scale. The weight difference represented the total volume of fluid retained within the material. This combination of volumetric assessment and pre−/post‐test weight analysis provided a comprehensive and reliable evaluation of the absorption characteristics of both dressing materials prior to their clinical application (Figure 2).

Weight measurement of dressing material before applying it to the patient's wound.

Clinical Study (RCT)

The surgical field was prepared under strict sterile conditions to ensure optimal safety and minimize contamination. The donor site designated for split‐thickness skin graft harvesting was carefully marked and anaesthetised using 1% xylocaine with adrenaline, which was infiltrated throughout each study area. After waiting approximately 5–7 min to achieve effective vasoconstriction and anaesthesia, the donor site surface was lubricated with K‐Y Jelly to facilitate smooth, uniform advancement of the dermatome.

Skin graft harvesting was performed using an electric dermatome (Aesculap, Tuttlingen, Germany), with careful adjustment to achieve the required graft thickness. In all cases, the dermatome depth was set at 0.015 inches (0.38 mm) for both study areas.

Following completion of the harvest, the donor site was thoroughly cleansed with povidone–iodine (betadine) solution. Each wound area was then dressed with the allocated study dressing material according to randomization. The allocation determined whether the upper or lower zone received the superabsorbent dressing, with the opposite zone receiving the foam dressing (Figure 3). Before application, both types of dressing materials were weighed and documented, allowing subsequent calculation of fluid absorption based on weight differences. Elastic bandages were used to secure the dressings in both study groups, and no additional absorbent materials were applied.

(a–c) Split‐thickness skin‐graft donor sites were randomly divided into two areas, each receiving either a Super‐absorbent dressing or a foam dressing, allowing intra‐patient comparison.

Baseline demographic data, relevant medical history, and detailed donor site wound characteristics were recorded for all participants. To reduce potential bias, independent outcome assessors—not involved in patient care—evaluated all outcomes. The primary outcome, absorption rate, was assessed on day 0 and then at regular 3‐day intervals (day 3, day 6, day 9, day 12, and day 15), completing a two‐week postoperative evaluation period. Absorption rate was calculated by comparing pre‐ and post‐use dressing weights.

Secondary outcomes included patient‐reported pain scores and the percentage of wound healing (Figure 4). These parameters were recorded consistently at each dressing change, allowing accurate monitoring of clinical progression in both study groups.

(a and b) Wound size was measured using a mobile application–based digital planimetry system.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA/SE version 15.1. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Because of the intra‐patient paired study design and non‐normally distributed continuous data, comparisons between the superabsorbent and foam dressing groups at each time point were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Changes in absorption capacity, wound healing, and pain scores across repeated time points were evaluated using the Friedman test. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In the experimental study, one 10 × 10 cm piece of foam dressing weighed 7.5 g, whereas one piece of superabsorbent dressing weighed 5.5 g. In the volume absorption test, a single piece of foam dressing absorbed 75 mL of normal saline, while a single piece of superabsorbent dressing absorbed 93 mL of solution.

This study included 30 patients, with no exclusions or loss to follow‐up. The median patient age was 59.5 years (IQR, 48.5–70.8 years). There were 21 male patients (70%) and 9 female patients (30%). All patients completed the scheduled follow‐up. The decreasing number of evaluable cases at later time points reflected complete wound epithelialization and subsequent discontinuation of dressing use rather than loss to follow‐up. Once a donor‐site wound had completely epithelialized and no longer produced measurable exudate, dressing application and subsequent absorption assessment were discontinued.

Absorption capacity was significantly higher in the superabsorbent group than in the foam group during the early postoperative period. On day 3, the median absorption capacity was 104.09 (IQR, 46.29–214.90) mg/cm2/day in the superabsorbent group compared with 71.87 (IQR, 45.69–158.11) mg/cm2/day in the foam group (p = 0.034). On day 6, the corresponding values were 84.45 (IQR, 66.52–121.95) and 40.91 (IQR, 24.45–51.24) mg/cm2/day, respectively (p < 0.001). Discussion

Wound dressing plays a crucial role not only for donor sites in split‐thickness skin grafts but also for various types of open wounds that involve large surface areas. These include traumatic injuries such as extensive abrasion wounds commonly seen in motorcycle accidents, large burn wounds, and infected wounds requiring aggressive debridement, such as those seen in necrotizing fasciitis. Because these wounds expose a substantial area of dermis and subdermal tissue, proper wound dressing becomes essential to protect the wound bed, control exudate, maintain moisture balance, and support optimal wound healing dynamics.

A favourable wound environment is widely recognized as a key determinant of successful healing. Numerous clinical and experimental studies have confirmed that wound healing proceeds more rapidly in a moist environment compared with a dry one. Moisture preserves cellular viability at the wound base, facilitates keratinocyte migration, and supports microcirculatory perfusion, allowing oxygen and nutrients to reach the wound bed more effectively. Conversely, excessive moisture is equally detrimental. Highly exudative wounds may accumulate clots, fibrinous debris, and devitalized tissue beneath the dressing. These materials impair epithelial migration and can delay healing in selected areas. Surgeons often attempt to prolong dressing intervals—commonly 5–7 days for donor sites—to minimize patient discomfort and avoid unnecessary manipulation. However, upon dressing removal, it is frequently observed that some areas exhibit delayed healing due to clot accumulation beneath the dressing.

For this reason, a dressing material that can effectively manage early postoperative exudate without requiring premature dressing changes is critical, especially in donor site wounds that typically produce substantial serosanguinous drainage during the first few days. Foam dressings have traditionally been employed because of their relatively high absorption capacity, conformability, and ease of application.

Our findings are consistent with Orlov et al. [14] who demonstrated superior fluid handling properties of superabsorbent dressings under laboratory conditions. Similarly, Barrett et al. [12] reported favourable exudate control in clinical practice. In contrast, our randomized study demonstrated that although fluid management improved, healing outcomes remained comparable.

No wound complications, wound infection, dressing leakage, or periwound maceration were observed in either group throughout the study. We believe that this may be related to our standardized postoperative protocol, in which both dressings were secured with an elastic bandage to ensure close contact with the wound surface, and dressings were routinely changed every 3 days, thereby preventing excessive exudate accumulation.

Pain control is another fundamental aspect of donor site wound care. Reduction of wound‐related pain has consistently been rated as a priority by plastic surgeons. The survey by Geary and Tiernan [15] emphasized that pain control is a major factor influencing the choice of donor site dressing. Dressing removal, in particular, is a well‐known trigger of acute pain, as adherence to the wound bed or dried exudate can cause significant discomfort. In our study, although statistical significance was not achieved, patients clinically reported less pain with superabsorbent dressings during the early postoperative period. A limitation of intraindividual randomization is that patients may have difficulty distinguishing pain between adjacent wound areas due to proximity, which can influence subjective reporting.

The present study selected superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) as a comparator due to their unique properties. SAPs are cross‐linked polymer matrices capable of absorbing and retaining extraordinarily large amounts of exudate—often several hundred times their own weight—while preventing fluid leakage or backflow. They function by generating a strong osmotic gradient that rapidly draws exudate into the polymer network. Once absorbed, the polymer chains hydrate, swell, and convert the fluid into a stable hydrogel, effectively trapping the exudate even under external pressure, such as compression bandaging. This ability to lock fluid in a gel core minimizes strike‐through, protects the peri‐wound skin from maceration, and maintains an optimal moist environment. Advanced SAP products can also bind proteases and inflammatory mediators, potentially benefiting chronic or highly exudative wounds.

Experimental analysis in our study demonstrated that SAPs had an absorption capacity approximately 1.24 times greater than standard foam dressings.

In our local market, the SAP dressing was approximately 50%–60% less expensive than the foam dressing used in this study.

These findings correspond well with the clinical results, in which SAPs effectively managed exudate from the early postoperative period until full re‐epithelialization. Beyond absorption capacity and cost‐effectiveness, SAPs also reduced pain during dressing changes. This may be explained by their superior surface contact, faster absorption, and reduced formation of clots or debris beneath the dressing—factors that otherwise cause adherence and pain during dressing removal.

An important strength of this study is its robust methodology and low risk of bias. The intra‐individual randomized design minimized interpatient variability, while the sample size exceeded the minimum required number of participants (n = 15), further strengthening the reliability of the findings.

This study has several limitations. Patient satisfaction was not assessed using a validated questionnaire. Dressing leakage and periwound maceration could not be compared because neither occurred during the study period. In addition, a formal cost‐effectiveness analysis was not performed. Finally, the fixed three‐day dressing‐change protocol did not allow assessment of whether the greater absorptive capacity of the superabsorbent dressing could permit longer dressing intervals or reduce dressing‐change frequency.

Future randomized trials should investigate whether improved exudate management results in fewer dressing changes, lower rates of periwound maceration, improved patient satisfaction, and greater cost‐effectiveness.

Conclusion

Superabsorbent dressing demonstrated superior exudate absorption compared with foam dressing in split‐thickness skin‐graft donor‐site wounds, while epithelialization and healing outcomes were comparable between groups. Pain scores tended to be lower with the superabsorbent dressing during the early postoperative period, although the differences were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that superabsorbent dressing may be a useful alternative for exudate management in acute donor‐site wounds.
