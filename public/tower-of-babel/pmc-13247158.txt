PSMC2 serves as a potential regulatory target of EZH2 in promoting glioma progression via epithelial-mesenchymal transition
Authors: Yufeng Zhu, Xinlong Li, Peng Feng, JunQiang Dai, Juncheng Wang, Guoqiang Yuan, Yawen Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteasome 26 S Subunit, ATPase 2 (PSMC2) was initially thought to be associated with the removal of misfolded or damaged proteins and the removal of excess proteins that are no longer necessary. However, recent studies have found that PSMC2 acts as an oncogene in the human body. In our research, PSMC2 serves as a tumor-initiating factor in glioma. Bioinformatics analysis of public databases, along with an examination of PSMC2 expression in glioma patients, revealed its high expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in this type of tumor. The expression of PSMC2 exhibits a negative correlation with glioma patient prognosis. Modulating PSMC2 levels through inhibition or overexpression alters glioma cell morphology and suppresses both proliferation and motility. Silencing PSMC2 impedes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), concurrently altering cellular polarity and cytoskeletal architecture. Mechanistically, proteomics analysis, corroborated by co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) and fluorescence co-localization assays, identified a interaction between PSMC2 and Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2). Our research findings indicate that PSMC2 can promote the occurrence and development of glioma, and this process is closely related to EZH2. Targeting the PSMC2-EZH2 axis may become a promising option for glioma treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48884-7.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive and prevalent tumor in the central nervous system, accounting for approximately 57.7% of all glioma cases. The median survival time of patients is 14.6 months, which posing significant challenges to clinical treatment1. Currently, clinical treatment remains based on conventional surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Although several novel treatment modalities, including immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and tumor-treating fields, have shown certain efficacy in recent years, these approaches remain in the early stages of development2–4. The high proliferative and invasive capacities of GBM remain major bottlenecks for suppressing tumor progression, and further molecular biology research is required to provide a theoretical basis.

PSMC2 (Proteasome 26 S Subunit, ATPase 2) is a component of the multicatalytic proteasome complex. Proteasomes are highly concentrated throughout eukaryotic cells and cleave peptides through an ATP/ubiquitin-dependent, non-lysosomal pathway. Hence, the proteasome is involved in numerous cellular processes, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and DNA damage repair5–7. Inhibition of PSMC2 has been reported to attenuate joint inflammation and bone destruction in rats by upregulating RUNX2 and COL1A18. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that PSMC2 functions as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor gene in different cancer types. In ovarian cancer, the PSMC2/CCND1 axis promotes cancer cell progression by regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration9. Moreover, inhibition of PSMC2 has been shown to suppress the malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma, gallbladder cancer, and colorectal cancer10–12. Notably, recent studies have highlighted a significant upregulation of PSMC2 in gliomas and have proposed PSMC2 as a prognostic biomarker for this type of brain tumor13,14. Yet, its precise function and the mechanisms underlying in glioma are still not well understood. In this study, we found that PSMC2 promotes the proliferation and motility of glioma cells by regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and this regulatory process is associated with the expression of EZH2. EZH2 is a member of the polycomb family and plays a pivotal role in tumor initiation and progression15. Our study demonstrated that PSMC2 can influence the expression of EZH2. Furthermore, overexpression of EZH2 in the context of shPSMC2 knockdown can partially reverse the inhibitory effects of shPSMC2 on glioma cell proliferation and motility. Our findings suggest that the PSMC2-EZH2 axis may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for gliomas.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and tissue acquisition

Human glioma cell lines (A172, U87, U118, LN229, U251) and normal human astrocytes (NHA) were obtained from the Institute of Neurology, Second Hospital of Lanzhou University. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The brands of all reagents used in this study are listed in Table S3 of the supplementary materials. The clinical tissue specimens used in this study were obtained from the Institute of Neurosurgery, Second Hospital of Lanzhou University. Among these specimens, normal brain tissues were obtained from patients who required clinical brain tissue fistula surgery, and all patients providing the specimens had given informed consent prior to surgery.

Construction of stable cell lines

Lentiviral vectors expressing PSMC2-shRNA, Scramble-shRNA, Empty Vector (EV), and PSMC2 overexpression (OE) were constructed by Hanbio (Shanghai, China). The shPSMC2 sequence was designed based on the second targeting sequence of siPSMC2. U251 and LN229 cells were used to establish stable PSMC2 knockdown cell lines, while U118 cells were utilized to generate a stable PSMC2 overexpression cell line. Full-length transcript sequences are detailed in the supplementary materials. U251, LN229, and U118 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. The viral dose was determined according to the multiplicity of infection (MOI) values provided by the reagent manufacturer for each different cell line. Basal medium (2 mL/well) and viral stock solution (10 µL/well) were mixed and incubated with the cells at 37 °C for 4 h, after which 10% FBS was added and the cells were further incubated in a cell incubator. At 48 h post-incubation, the cells were selected with 1 mg/mL puromycin for 2 weeks to establish stable cell lines. Transfection efficiency was evaluated by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (WB).

CCK-8 assay

CCK-8 analysis assessed cell viability. Cells were seeded in quadruplicate wells (0.8–2 × 10³ cells/well) of a 96-well plate and cultured for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. Then, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS and 10% CCK-8 solution. Work solution was added to different culture wells every 24 h and incubated at 37℃ in the dark for 2 h until the end of the fifth day. Plates were read at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Bio Tek, USA).

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was evaluated by the cell wound healing assay. Cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates, and by the second day, the cells had grown to confluence (95% confluency) in the wells. A wound was created by scratching two vertical lines on the cell monolayer with a 200 µL pipette tip, using a ruler as a guide. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were maintained in FBS-free DMEM. The bottom of each plate was marked with a marker pen. The cells were observed under a microscope (Leica DM4B, Germany) at 0 h and 12–36 h, and ImageJ (NIH, Rockville, USA) software was used for statistical analysis of wound healing.

Invasion and migration

For the cell invasion assay, Transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8 in DMEM) and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The upper chambers were filled with FBS-free DMEM containing 1–3 × 10⁴ cells, while the lower chambers were loaded with DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS. The cells were incubated in a cell incubator for 24–48 h. Subsequently, the chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 1% crystal violet solution. Cells remaining on the Matrigel and in the upper chambers were gently removed using cotton swabs. Images of invasive cells were captured under a light microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software. Cell migration assays were performed using the same protocol, except for the omission of Matrigel coating.

Flow cytometry analysis

Apoptosis rate was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, harvested with EDTA-free trypsin, washed with PBS, and transferred to Eppendorf tubes. After incubation with Annexin V-FITC/PI at room temperature in the dark for 15 min, the apoptotic rate was determined using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

The cell cycle distribution was evaluated using a Cell Cycle Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were harvested and fixed in pre-chilled 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 500 µL of staining solution (containing 25 µL propidium iodide (PI) and 10 µL ribonuclease) for 30 min in the dark. The stained cells were immediately analyzed using a CytExpert flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Clone formation assay

The colony formation assay was performed by seeding 2000 cells per well (2 mL medium/well) in 6-well plates, followed by incubation in a cell incubator for 2 weeks. After incubation, the plates were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% PFA, and subsequently stained with crystal violet solution.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA purification kit. Briefly, approximately 50–100 × 10⁴ cells were collected, and 300 µL of lysis buffer was added; the mixture was gently pipetted 8–10 times until the cell suspension was completely dissolved. An equal volume of binding solution was added, and the mixture was transferred to a purification column and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. The column was then sequentially washed with washing solution, followed by an additional centrifugation step at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. The purification column was placed into a new elution tube, and eluent was added. After incubation at room temperature for 3 min, the column was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 s to elute total RNA. A 20 µL reverse transcription reaction system was prepared using a Reverse Transcription Kit, and the reaction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was stored at − 20 °C. For RT-qPCR, the obtained cDNA was mixed with SYBR Green qPCR Mix containing UDG, nuclease-free water, and specific primers to prepare a 10 or 20 µL reaction mixture. The amplification reaction was detected using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (BIO-RAD, USA). Relative RNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1 and were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China).

Western bloting

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors at a 1:100 dilution. Subsequently, the total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. The sample loading volume for each electrophoresis lane was adjusted according to the measured protein concentration. Proteins were then separated by 6–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk, washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST), and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies. After washing with TBST, the membrane was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Information on all antibodies used is listed in Table S2.

Phalloidin staining and Immunofluorescence assay

Glioma cells were seeded in 24-well plates (3 × 10⁴ cells/well), with a 14-mm diameter glass coverslip placed at the bottom of each well. The cells were transfected with PSMC2 siRNA, EZH2 siRNA, or Scramble siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. After confirming complete cell adhesion to the culture vessel walls (> 24 h post-seeding), the cells were washed with pre-chilled PBS and fixed with 4% PFA solution for 30 min. Subsequently, the cell membranes were permeabilized by incubation with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature (RT) for 5 min, followed by washing with PBS three times for 10 min each.

For Phalloidin staining, the cytoskeleton was stained with tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate (TRITC)-conjugated Phalloidin solution. Specifically, 200 µL of TRITC-Phalloidin solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at RT in the dark for 1 h. After washing with PBS, the cell nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and the stained cells were then observed using a fluorescence microscope in a darkroom. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were blocked with 10% goat serum at RT for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against the target protein at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at RT in the dark for 2 h. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the coverslips were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany) in a darkroom. Additionally, the brightness and contrast of images from the siPSMC2 group were moderately adjusted to facilitate the visual observation of cell morphological changes.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 1 week, dehydrated using a fully enclosed tissue processor (Leica ASP300), and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin Sect. (4 μm thick) were cut using an automatic microtome and baked at 65 °C for 6 h. The sections were then dewaxed through a xylene-ethanol gradient and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with 5% goat serum at RT for 1 h. The tumor tissue sections were then incubated with a PSMC2 primary antibody (1:200 dilution, Affinity) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, a HRP-labeled rabbit secondary antibody was added, and the sections were incubated in the dark for 30 min. Following washing with PBST, fresh DAB solution (G1212, Servicebio) was applied to the sections at RT for 3 min, followed by incubation with hematoxylin for another 3 min. After washing, the sections were dehydrated with a graded ethanol series, absolute ethanol, and xylene sequentially, and then mounted with neutral balsam.

Immunoprecipitation and co-immunoprecipitation (co-ip)

Wild-type U251 and LN229 cells were cultured in Petri dishes. When the cell confluency reached 70%, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer on ice for 40 min. Centrifugation was performed at 15,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. The protein supernatant was then incubated with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) for 2–4 h at 4 °C to pre-clear non-specific proteins. According to the measured protein concentration, an appropriate amount of PSMC2 antibody (Santa Cruz, 4–8 µg) and IgG control antibody (Abcam, 4–8 µg) were separately incubated with fresh Protein A/G magnetic beads for 2–4 h. Subsequently, the protein lysate was added separately to the tubes containing Protein A/G magnetic beads pre-incubated with IgG control antibody and PSMC2 antibody, respectively. After gentle rotation overnight at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the Protein A/G magnetic beads were washed three times with NP-40 lysis buffer. An appropriate amount of 2× SDS loading buffer was then added to the beads, and the mixture was boiled for 10 min to elute the immunocomplexes. After separating the magnetic beads using a magnetic stand, the eluted supernatants were collected and used for subsequent WB analysis.

Proteomics analysis

The purified PSMC2 protein obtained from Wild-type LN229 cells was subjected to SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie blue. The different electrophoresis lanes were cut into proteomics analysis (iProteome, Shanghai, China). Compared with the IgG group, the results with an abundance value > 1 × 106 and a difference of > 10-fold were analyzed (project number: PDB-2024-0353).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.3; http://www.r-project.org). For continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences between the two subtypes, whereas the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the correlation between two variables. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to compare overall survival rates among different subtypes, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0) was additionally used for statistical analyses. Unpaired Student’s t-tests were performed to compare differences between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post hoc test was used to assess differences among multiple groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. All Western blot (WB) results were normalized and statistically analyzed.

Results

High expression of PSMC2 is associated with poor prognosis of glioma

The expression of PSMC2 in gliomas of different WHO grades was analyzed using the CGGA database, and higher-grade gliomas were associated with significantly higher PSMC2 expression (Fig. 1A). Multivariate models revealed that PSMC2 serves as an unfavorable prognostic factor for glioma, with age stratified using a cutoff value of 42 years in CGGA database (Fig. 1B). Glioma patients with high PSMC2 expression exhibited a significantly poorer prognosis (Fig. 1C). RT-qPCR and WB results showed that the mRNA and protein expression of PSMC2 in different glioma cells (U87, A172, U251, LN229) was significantly higher than NHA. However, there was no significant increase in PSMC2 expression in U118 cells (Fig. 1D, E). IHC revealed significantly higher PSMC2 expression in grade III-IV glioma tissues compared to non-tumor brain and grade II glioma tissues (Fig. 1F). These findings imply that elevated PSMC2 expression may drive glioma progression.

Inhibition of PSMC2 reduced the proliferation, invasion and migration of glioma cells

To investigate the functional role of PSMC2 in glioma cells, U251 and LN229 cells were transfected with PSMC2 siRNA or Scramble siRNA. The transfection efficiency was verified by WB, which confirmed a marked reduction in PSMC2 expression (Fig. S1C). CCK-8 assay demonstrated that PSMC2 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation of glioma cells (Fig. 2A, B). Wound healing and Transwell assays indicated that PSMC2 contributed to the migratory and invasive capacity of glioma cells (Fig. S2A; Fig. 2C). silencing PSMC2 also significantly increased the apoptosis of glioma cells (Fig. S2B). Furthermore, Flow cytometry analysis revealed that silencing PSMC2 induced G1 phase cell cycle arrest in glioma cells (Fig. S2C). Since siRNA transfection cannot achieve stable gene silencing, to investigate the effect of PSMC2 on the clonal proliferation of single glioma cells, the PSMC2-siRNA#2 sequence was used to construct a lentiviral vector, which was then transfected into U251 and LN229 glioma cells. RT-qPCR and WB analyses confirmed that the lentiviral vector exhibited good transfection efficiency (Fig. S1A-C). Colony formation assay results demonstrated that PSMC2 knockdown significantly reduced the single-cell clonogenic proliferation and division capacity of glioma cells (Fig. 2D, E).

Overexpression of PSMC2 can enhance the proliferation, invasion and migration of glioma cells

Among several glioma cell lines, we found that PSMC2 expression was relatively low in U118 cells (Fig. 1D); therefore, we overexpressed PSMC2 in U118 cells. The efficiency of PSMC2 overexpression was verified by RT-qPCR and WB analyses (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast to PSMC2 knockdown, overexpression of PSMC2 enhanced the proliferation of U118 glioma cells (Fig. 3C). Transwell and wound healing assays showed that overexpression of PSMC2 significantly enhanced the migratory and invasive capacities of U118 glioma cells (Fig. 3D, E). In contrast to PSMC2 knockdown, U118 glioma cells overexpressing PSMC2 exhibited a cell cycle transition from the G1 phase to the G2/M phase, with an accelerated cell division cycle (Fig. 3G). As expected, colony formation assay results demonstrated that overexpression of PSMC2 significantly promoted the single-cell clonogenic proliferation and division capacity of U118 glioma cells (Fig. 3H).

PSMC2 is involved in regulating the EMT process and altering the cytoskeleton of glioma cells

During the previous experiments, we unexpectedly observed that PSMC2 knockdown not only suppressed cell growth but also induced obvious alterations in cell morphology. Under bright-field microscopy, cells with PSMC2 knockdown displayed loss of cell polarity and adopted a more rounded morphology (Fig. 4A). We hypothesized that this morphological change might be associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). To test this hypothesis, we performed GSEA using the online CGGA database and found that PSMC2 expression was significantly correlated with the EMT pathway (Fig. 4B). The cytoskeletal protein F-actin was stained with phalloidin and observed under a fluorescence microscope. We observed that PSMC2 knockdown converted glioma cells from a long spindle shape to a rounded morphology, and actin filaments became fragmented and sparse (Fig. 4C). Vimentin, an intermediate filament protein, is a key component of the cytoskeleton that plays an important role in maintaining cell morphology and structural integrity16. Next, immunofluorescence double staining was performed for Vimentin and PSMC2. The results showed that PSMC2 knockdown significantly disrupted cellular structural integrity (Fig. 4D). Several EMT-related proteins were detected by WB17, and the results showed that the expression of vimentin and N-Cadherin was decreased while E-Cadherin was increased after PSMC2 knockdown (Fig. 4E).

PSMC2 have interaction with EZH2

To identify genes associated with high PSMC2 expression, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and the detailed analysis process is shown in Fig. S3A. Based on the median PSMC2 expression level, the samples were divided into high and low PSMC2 expression groups, and β = 19 was selected as the soft threshold for gene module construction (Fig. S3B-E). We identified that the genes could be clustered into eight distinct modules (lightgreen, red, green, purple, saddlebrown, brown, darkturquoise, and grey). Gene modules with a P value < 0.001 and a correlation coefficient (COR) > 0.4 were selected, yielding a total of 138 genes. Subsequent intersection with proteomic profiling results identified 8 core genes (Fig. S3F-I). Through a literature review of the 8 molecules identified from the Venn diagram, we found that EZH2 is closely associated with the stemness and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process of various tumor cells and can be regulated by multiple substances, including but not limited to long and short non-coding RNAs18–20. Further investigation revealed that EZH2 and PSMC2 expression levels were both elevated with increasing tumor grade, and a strong positive correlation was observed between them (Fig. 5A, B). Subsequently, immunofluorescence double staining for PSMC2 and EZH2 was performed, and the results demonstrated their co-localization (Fig. 5C). Knockdown or overexpression of PSMC2 downregulated or upregulated EZH2 expression, respectively (Fig. 5D). Co-IP assays demonstrated that PSMC2 interacted with EZH2 (Fig. 5E). In addition, we analyzed the mRNA expression levels of PSMC2, EZH2, and Ki67 from the CGGA database and explored their pairwise correlations using the corrplot package in R. The results showed that the correlation coefficients among PSMC2, EZH2, and Ki67 were all greater than 0.4, indicating a significant positive correlation between these three genes (Fig. S4). Furthermore, EZH2 also activated the EMT pathway, which was consistent with the function of PSMC2 (Figs. 4B and 5F). These results indicate that EZH2 is closely associated with PSMC2.

EZH2 inhibitors significantly inhibited the proliferation, invasion, migration, and EMT of glioma cells

We further investigated the effects of EZH2 on the proliferation, invasion, and migration of glioma cells. U251 and LN229 cells were transfected with EZH2 siRNA. CCK-8 assay were performed to evaluate cell proliferative capacity, and the results showed that proliferative activity was significantly reduced in glioma cells following EZH2 inhibition (Fig. 6A). Cell cycle analysis demonstrated that the cell division cycle was arrested at the G1 phase following EZH2 inhibition (Fig. S5A). The results of cell apoptosis detection were consistent with the above findings, and the apoptosis rates of U251 and LN229 cells were significantly increased following EZH2 inhibition (Fig. S5B). Findings from the wound healing and Transwell assays revealed a significant reduction in the migratory and invasive capacities of the cells following EZH2 knockdown (Fig. 6B, S5C). Consistent with previous reports21, inhibition of EZH2 inhibited the EMT process of tumor cells, which was manifested as increased E-Cadherin and decreased N-Cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 6C). Phalloidin staining assay showed that cell morphology was significantly altered after EZH2 knockdown, which was consistent with the changes observed following PSMC2 knockdown. Tumor cells exhibited reduced polarity, enlarged size, a rounder morphology, and diminished intercellular junctions (Fig. 6D).

E-cadherin serves as a key regulator in stabilizing intercellular adhesion during EMT and plays an important role in suppressing tumor invasion and migration20. Snail (Zinc finger protein SNAI1) as transcriptional repressing factors of E-Cadherin, are highly expressed in tumor cells, which inhibit E-Cadherin expression and promote EMT process and accelerate tumor malignant progression22. WB analysis demonstrated that Snail protein levels were decreased following both PSMC2 and EZH2 knockdown (Fig. 6E). These results suggest that PSMC2 and EZH2 may regulate Snail expression to repress E-cadherin expression, thereby modulating the EMT process of glioma cells.

EZH2 can rescue the inhibitory effect of PSMC2 knockdown on glioma cells

To confirm the role of EZH2 in PSMC2-induced glioma malignant behavior, we overexpressed EZH2 in the established shPSMC2 cells to observe whether the malignant phenotype of the cells was reversed. CCK-8 assays were performed to evaluate cell proliferation. The results demonstrated that EZH2 overexpression reversed the suppressive effect of PSMC2 knockdown on cell proliferation (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, EZH2 overexpression partly counteracted the inhibitory impact of PSMC2 knockdown on glioma cell invasion and migration, as indicated by wound healing and Transwell assays (Fig. 7C–F; Fig. S6A, B).

PSMC2-based glioma subtype classification

To identify and integrate distinct glioma subtypes linked to PSMC2, datasets (CGGA301, GEO16011, GEO18494 + GEO4412, and TCGA) served as validation sets. NMF (Non-negative Matrix Factorization) analysis was conducted using the Brunet algorithm, with K values ranging from 2 to 10 (Fig. 8A). For each K, NMF decomposition was performed, and statistical indicators (Cophenetic coefficient, silhouette score) were calculated, along with biological significance assessment using sample clinical info. After evaluation, k = 2 was determined to be optimal. Cross-validation and independent dataset validation further confirmed the stability and reliability of this clustering model. The optimal clustering analysis identified two transcriptional subtypes, namely C1 and C2 (Fig. 8B). Subtype-specific survival analysis showed C1 had a significantly better prognosis than C2 (Fig. 8C). To ensure the classification wasn’t overfitting and had biological relevance across patient groups, we used multiple independent external datasets (GEO16011, CGGA301, TCGA, GEO18494, GEO4412) for validation (Fig. 8D–G). The results indicated samples in these external datasets could also be split into two subgroups with distinct prognostic implications. Clinical characteristic analysis revealed a higher prevalence of high-grade gliomas in the C2 subtype (Fig. 8H).

Discussion

Among central nervous system tumors, glioma remains one of the most intractable challenges in clinical practice. Few tissue sites are as highly vascularized and densely packed with functional areas as brain tissue. However, the highly invasive nature of glioma allows it to infiltrate these critical neural structures, posing substantial challenges to complete surgical resection. Consequently, the recurrence rate of glioma remains extremely high, particularly for glioblastoma. Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying glioma cell invasion and migration is urgently needed. EMT is a biological process closely associated with alterations in cell morphology and the migration and invasion of tumor cells23,24. Our results provide mechanistic evidence that PSMC2 functions as an EMT promoter to facilitate the invasion and migration of glioma cells.

PSMC2, located at 7q22.1-q22.3, is an essential component of the 26 S proteasome25. PSMC2 is a large proteolytic complex responsible for the clearance of regulatory and damaged proteins. It catalyzes the unfolding and translocation of ubiquitinated protein substrates, facilitates their association with the 26 S proteasome, and regulates various biological processes, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and signal transduction26. Recently, PSMC2 has been identified as an oncogene in various tumors. Liu et al. demonstrated that it increases RPS15A levels by targeting hsa-let-7c-3p, thereby activating the mTOR pathway to promote gastric cancer progression27. Wang et al. found that PSMC2 predominantly reduces apoptosis in oral squamous cell carcinoma cells by inhibiting the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins and apoptotic pathways28. Su et al. showed that knockdown of PSMC2 inhibits the malignant progression of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells, and this effect can be reversed by an activator of the AKT signaling pathway29. Importantly, PSMC2 has also been identified as a glioma oncogene by bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation in glioma30,31. In the present study, we found that PSMC2 was highly expressed in LN229 and U251 cells. Moreover, PSMC2 knockdown resulted in slower cell growth and decreased cell viability. PSMC2 knockdown suppressed glioma cell proliferation and motility, as determined by proliferation, invasion, migration, cell cycle, and apoptosis assays. We then overexpressed PSMC2 in U118 cells, which exhibit low endogenous PSMC2 expression. The results were opposite to those following PSMC2 knockdown: cell proliferation, invasion, and migration were significantly enhanced. However, the magnitude of these changes was less pronounced than that observed in the PSMC2 knockdown group. This may be because U118 cells are already highly malignant glioma cells. The effect of PSMC2 overexpression on accelerating the cell proliferation cycle may have reached the limit of protein regulation. Nevertheless, the complex regulatory mechanisms underlying this phenomenon warrant further exploration.

EMT represents a key mechanism by which tumor cells penetrate the basement membrane and Infiltrate other surrounding tissues. During this process, tumor cells undergo cytoskeletal rearrangement, upregulate the expression of migration and invasion related proteins such as N-Cadherin and Vimentin, and downregulate the epithelial marker E-Cadherin, thereby promoting tumor cell migration and invasion32,33. In addition, EMT not only affects tumor cells themselves but also remodels the tumor microenvironment. By secreting various cytokines and growth factors, and recruiting immune cells, fibroblasts, and other components, EMT promotes the formation of the tumor-associated matrix and provides a favorable microenvironment for tumor cell growth, proliferation, and invasions34,35. Some studies have demonstrated that tumor cells with EMT characteristics exhibit enhanced resistance to various therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapeutic drugs, targeted drugs, and radiotherapy. This phenomenon may be attributed to factors such as alterations in intracellular signaling pathways during EMT, increased expression of drug efflux pumps, and changes in the cell cycle36–38. In the present study, we observed that silencing PSMC2 resulted in the loss of cell polarity, cytoskeletal reorganization, and the formation of larger, more rounded cells. Combined with the downregulation of N-cadherin and vimentin expression and the upregulation of E-cadherin expression, these findings suggest that PSMC2 may be involved in regulating the EMT process in glioma cells.

The continuous advancement of sequencing technologies and the rapid expansion of molecular databases have revolutionized research paradigms in molecular biology. These advances not only improve the accuracy and efficiency of profiling genes, proteins, metabolites, and other biological molecules, but also provide investigators with a vast resource of accessible data. Integration of these approaches has yielded numerous studies aimed at dissecting the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying cancer progression. In this study, we aimed to investigate the mechanism by which PSMC2 regulates the EMT process in glioma cells. We identified 8 candidate genes through WGCNA analysis, combined with PSMC2 pull-down and proteomic assays, followed by Venn diagram construction. According to previous studies, EZH2 functions as a histone methyltransferase subunit of the Polycomb repressive complex and is frequently upregulated in various human malignant tumors, including glioma, pancreatic cancer, and colorectal cancer15,39,40. Notably, a growing body of evidence indicates that EZH2 participates in biological processes governing tumor-associated EMT41–44. On this basis, we proposed the hypothesis that PSMC2 may facilitate EMT progression by modulating EZH2 expression. Our experimental results demonstrated that PSMC2 and EZH2 were co-localized, and the protein level of EZH2 varied with changes in PSMC2, presenting a positive correlation. We analyzed the potential mechanism by which PSMC2 affects EZH2 protein expression. Co-IP assays suggested the existence of a spatial protein-protein interaction between these two proteins. We speculate that altering PSMC2 expression may expose the protein sites that normally bind to EZH2, thereby impairing EZH2 stability. Given that PSMC2 belongs to the proteasome family, the exposure of its binding sites with EZH2 may render EZH2 more susceptible to recognition by ubiquitin ligases, ultimately leading to its degradation via the proteasome pathway. To verify this speculation, further mechanistic exploration is required, including the use of cycloheximide to detect the half-life of EZH2 protein degradation after PSMC2 knockdown. In addition, proteasome complex inhibition experiments using MG132, as well as further exploration of the specific amino acid sites involved in EZH2 ubiquitination modification, are needed.

Loss of E-cadherin expression is associated with enhanced invasiveness induced by EMT; Snail acts as a transcriptional repressor of E-cadherin20,45. Our experimental results demonstrated that inhibition of PSMC2 and EZH2 reduced Snail expression, which may contribute to the upregulation of E-cadherin. To further exploration the regulatory relationship between PSMC2 and EZH2 in glioma cells, we performed EZH2 overexpression rescue experiments in PSMC2-knockdown glioma cells. Cell function assays showed that PSMC2 knockdown significantly impaired the proliferation, invasion, and migration capabilities of glioma cells, whereas subsequent EZH2 overexpression effectively partly reversed these impaired malignant phenotypes, restoring cell proliferation, invasion, and migration but not achive the levels of the control group. This suggests that other mechanisms may also be involved in the regulation of EZH2. These findings identify a novel regulatory pathway through which PSMC2 exerts its oncogenic effects—at least partially by regulating EZH2 expression in glioma. Finally, we constructed two subtypes with independent prognostic significance by employing the NMF algorithm, Based on the validation using external datasets, we found that PSMC2 can serve as an indicator for predicting the prognosis of glioma patients.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Clinically, it is challenging to obtain normal non-tumor brain tissues (NBTs); most of the available non-tumor brain tissues are derived from patients with hydrocephalus who require stoma surgery, and it is difficult to collect a larger number of fresh NBT samples within a short period. Additionally, the detailed regulatory mechanism between PSMC2 and EZH2 proposed in this study remains to be further clarified. Furthermore, this study only conducted in vitro experiments, and additional in vivo experiments are needed to verify the findings. Finally, this study explored multiple phenotypes, including proliferation, invasion, migration, and EMT, and involved numerous related molecules. The underlying molecular mechanisms may be far more complex than those elaborated in this study, and we anticipate that more in-depth and detailed mechanistic investigations will be conducted to address these aspects individually.
