Ultrafast synthesis of α-aminophosphonates using ternary deep eutectic solvent in water
Authors: Najmedin Azizi, Navid Habibnejad, Nasim Nourazar, Mehran Shahiri Haghayegh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A remarkably rapid synthesis of α-aminophosphonate medicinally privileged scaffold is developed using a ternary deep eutectic solvent (TDES) of urea/SnCl2/HCl catalyst. Newly promoted TDES enabled a sustainable pathway to a library of the desired compounds with divergent electronic and steric characteristics simply using water as the solvent. The TDES-promoted protocol boasts unusual reaction times of 48–120 s, particularly for a batch chemistry process of organic synthesis, selectively churning out quantitative yields of a range of α-aminophosphonates. The catalyst entails total atom economy in its synthesis, and with a kinematic viscosity of 2.68 mm2.s-1, it is freely flowing at room temperature, making it a worthwhile candidate for utilization in large-scale process chemistry. The protocol is amenable to scaled-up synthesis as the use of the stable catalyst enabled its retrieval and reuse in at least three successive runs. 119Sn-NMR and13C-NMR spectroscopy techniques were employed to fully characterize the TDES catalyst produced from cheap, abundant chemicals. Additionally, UV-VIS analysis was adopted for monitoring the extraordinarily swift synthetic procedure, and stereochemical assignment analysis was implemented for α-aminophosphonate products using NMR spectroscopy, highlighting the synthetic selectivity of the process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31806-4.

Introduction

α-Aminophosphonates share structural resemblance with amino acids, rendering them important organophosphorus building blocks1. Versatility of α-aminophosphonates makes them relevant in various fields, including medicinal chemistry2, agriculture3, and advanced materials science4. In medicinal chemistry, α-aminophosphonates have garnered interest due to their potent pharmaceutical properties5, as certain derivatives exhibit antimicrobial, antiviral, antifungal, and anticancer activities. They can function as enzyme inhibitors, as bioisosteres of natural amino acids, offering opportunities for drug development6,7. As agrochemicals, some α-aminophosphonate derivatives act as plant growth regulators, herbicides, fungicides, or insecticides8. In addition, by influencing various physiological processes in plants and pests, these compounds contribute to crop protection3. In the field of materials science, α-aminophosphonates hold promise for diverse functionalities, as suitable building blocks for polymers, dendrimers, and coordination complexes9 with multifaceted utility in catalysis, sensing, and drug delivery, among others10,11.

The synthesis of α-aminophosphonates involves several methods, such as Kabachnik-Fields reaction and Michaelis-Arbuzov reaction12. These reactions typically involve the condensation of an imine with a phosphite or phosphonate compound13,14. The Kabachnik-Fields reaction is indeed a well-known method for synthesizing α-aminophosphonates and has attracted a great deal of attention in the scientific community15. This reaction can be carried out using either two components, involving an imine and a phosphorus nucleophile, or three components, which include an amine, a carbonyl compound, and a phosphorus moiety16. However, previous research has identified certain challenges associated with Kabachnik-Fields reaction, including but not limited to high reaction temperatures, low yields, steric effect constraints, and long reaction times. To address these challenges, researchers have explored various catalysts and solvents to enhance the efficiency of the Kabachnik-Fields reaction17–19. Recent investigations have incorporated different types of catalysts, including Brønsted acids, Lewis acids, organocatalysts, and photocatalysts20–23 in order to improve reaction conditions, increase yields, and overcome steric hindrance limitations, as the most persistent issues. By employing alternative catalysts and solvent systems, researchers have been striving to optimize the Kabachnik-Fields reaction and make it more efficient and practical24,25. The most recent studies have remarkably employed single electron transfer processes enabled by photocatalysts of different nature, including TiO2nanowires26, MoS2quantum dots27, and carbazole based organic dyes23. However, their adaptability for non-photoactive substrates and to current industrial reactors is unknown. Other issues often include prolonged reaction times, the use of volatile organic solvents, handling of hazardous radical species, and self-sacrificing agents.

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) are considered environmentally friendly materials, mostly formed by combining hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs)28. They offer advantages, such as total atom economy, tunability, strongly polar solvating capability, and facilitation of synthetic reactions29–35. Additionally, DESs have gained attention as potential alternatives to conventional solvents due to their lower toxicity, low vapor pressure, cost-effectiveness, usual biodegradability, and ability to dissolve a wide range of compounds36,37. They have found applications in various fields, including chemical transformations, extraction processes, and electrochemistry, with ongoing research focused on exploring their potential while developing new targeted DES compositions38–40. Delving into the state of the art in the application of DESs for the synthesis of α-aminophosphonates, including non-metal all-organic compositions, indicates compromised energy requirements and/or non-optimal reaction times41–43, which raises the need for more efficient alternatives.

Ternary deep eutectic solvents (TDESs) constitute a subclass of deep eutectic solvents composed of three components: an HBD, an HBA, and an additional component that enhances the properties or functionality of the solvent, often via regulation of the H-bonding network within the DES structure44. TDESs offer unique and tailored properties compared to binary deep eutectic solvents45. The addition of a third component in TDESs allows for further customization of the solvent’s characteristics, such as polarity, viscosity, and stability46. This additional component can be chosen to impart specific properties, such as increased ionic conductivity, improved solubility for several compounds, or enhanced catalytic activity. TDESs have shown great potential in various applications. They have been explored as solvents for chemical reactions, extraction processes, and separation techniques. TDESs have also been studied for their use in electrochemistry, as electrolytes in energy storage devices, and as reaction media in green and sustainable processes. The design and development of TDESs involve careful selection of components and their corresponding ratios to achieve the desired properties. Researchers continue to investigate the synthesis and characterization of TDESs, as well as their application in different fields to unlock their full potential as efficient, environmentally friendly solvents47.

The Kabachnik-Fields reaction is commonly performed using simple phosphorus nucleophiles like trimethyl or triethyl phosphite, and there has been limited exploration of bulky phosphorus nucleophiles such as triphenyl phosphite due to dominant steric hindrance. In this study, we focused on utilizing a TDES system to achieve ultrafast synthesis of bulky α-aminophosphonates as lead chemotypes of anti-leishmanial48 and mycobacterial49 applications, leveraging a combination of catalytic activity from SnCl2 Lewis acidic species and an ammonium salt of urea, as a Brønsted-Lowry acid. This research demonstrates the potential of TDES systems in facilitating the synthesis of α-aminophosphonates and highlights the benefits of using water as a reaction medium. The overarching objective of this study is to expand the toolbox of synthetic chemistry to develop selective synthesis of α-aminophosphonates via bulky electron-deficient triphenyl phosphite in fast conversions, while eliminating the need for expensive catalysts, unnecessarily sophisticated reactors, hard-to-remove organic/inorganic additives, harmful solvents, or the handling of harmful radical species (Scheme 1).

Experimental section

All chemicals, including amines, aldehydes, triphenyl phosphite, SnCl2·2H2O, urea, and HCl 37%, were commercial products of synthesis grade. Structural validation of products and TDES was performed using various nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. DMSO-d6 was used as the solvent for NMR experiments. The TDES chemical structure was characterized using13C-NMR (75 MHz, Bruker) and 119Sn-NMR (112 MHz, Bruker). The chemical structures of the products were confirmed using1H-NMR (300 MHz, Bruker), 13C-NMR (75 MHz, Bruker), and31P-NMR (121 MHz, Bruker) spectroscopy. Melting points of the compounds were determined using a Büchi 535 melting point apparatus. Kinematic viscometry experiments were performed using an Ubbelohde type capillary viscometer (type 0 A, Schott).

TDES Preparation

A round bottom flask was used to mix 50 mmol (1.13 g) of SnCl2·2H2O, 50 mmol (3.00 g) of urea, and 50 mmol (4.93 g) of HCl. The components were thoroughly mixed until a clear solution was obtained (Scheme 2).

Synthesis of urea/SnCl2/HCl ternary deep eutectic solvent (TDES).

Kinematic viscosity measurement of TDES

Measurements were performed using an Ubbelohde capillary viscometer at 18.0 ± 0.1 °C. Efflux times were measured in triplicate using a digital stopwatch. Kinematic viscosity values were calculated from the average flow times multiplied by the calibration factor, K, 0.004991 mm2.s- 2, constant at all temperatures.

Synthesis of α-aminophosphonate

The reaction was carried out in a round bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stirring bar. 50 µl (µL) of the TDES catalyst was added to 5 mL of water. Subsequently, aldehyde (1 mmol), amine (1 mmol), and triphenyl phosphite (1 mmol) were added, respectively, to the mixture. Upon addition of the reactants, the reaction proceeded rapidly, and the desired α-aminophosphonates were obtained within a few seconds. At the end of the reaction, the product was collected from the aqueous solution, and in a few cases was purified via recrystallization from ethanol. All compounds were characterized using melting point determination as well as 1H-NMR and13C-NMR spectroscopy.

Results and discussion

Synthetic procedure to urea/SnCl2/HCl TDES offers full atom economy in a straightforward manner without any need for further workup and thus loss of materials. In addition, due to the water content in its structure, regulating intermolecular H-bonding network, it has a desirable practical viscosity for a DES, measured by an Ubbelohde glass capillary kinematic viscometer. Chemical structure of the TDES was characterized using various techniques, including 119Sn-NMR, 13C-NMR, and FTIR. In 119Sn-NMR spectrum (Fig. 1), the TDES exhibited a singlet signal at −256 ppm, indicating the presence of tin in the complex. This chemical shift is different from the singlet signal observed at −358 ppm for pure SnCl2·2H2O, confirming the formation of the TDES complex. Similarly, in 13C-NMR spectrum, the TDES showed a singlet signal at 161 ppm, which is distinct from a singlet signal observed at 160 ppm for urea (Fig. 2). This difference in chemical shifts further supports the formation of the TDES complex. Formation of the complex can be explained by a nitrogen atom in urea acting as a ligand and donating an electron lone pair to the unoccupied orbital of SnCl2, resulting in a positive charge on the nitrogen atom. This positive charge leads to deshielding the carbonyl carbon in urea, as evidenced by the change in chemical shift observed in the13C-NMR spectrum.

After preparation and characterization of the TDES, its catalytic activity was evaluated in Kabachnik-Fields reaction using benzaldehyde, aniline, and triphenyl phosphite as the reactants. A remarkable efficiency of 100 µL of the catalyst in a quick 200 s promotion of the reaction in water, using 1 mmol of the reactants, prompted us to further explore optimal conditions for the transformation. The initial step in optimizing the reaction parameters involved identifying the most suitable solvent. As reflected in Table 2, various polar, non-polar, protic, and non-protic solvents were tested on this reaction. Among them, water (98%) and ethyl acetate (98%) demonstrated the highest possible yields, however, at different rates, which could be attributed to the extended H-bonding network within the TDES structure in water. Ethanol (95%) and chloroform (89%) also showed excellent yields, while n-hexane exhibited the lowest yield (72%). In the next step, the optimization of the catalyst volume was examined. Different volumes of TDES were tested, and it was found that reducing the volume to 50 µL of TDES furnished the same quantitative yield as in the initial 100 µL volume. However, further decrease of the catalyst volume resulted in a significantly compromised yield in the transformation (Table 2, entry 13).

To determine the optimal reaction time, UV-VIS monitoring was employed, thanks to the extraordinarily rapid reaction rate, realized via the benign catalyst-solvent system. In this connection, the synthesized product from the modal reaction was dissolved in ethyl acetate, and the λMax was recorded at 262 nm. Then, 0.2 mmol benzaldehyde, 0.2 mmol aniline, and 0.2 mmol triphenyl phosphite were added in a quartz cell, prior to adding 50 µL of TDES, and product absorption was scanned per second at 262 nm. The absorption increased extremely after adding TDES till 48 s, after which the absorption reached a plateau and followed the constant value of 3.272 (Fig. 3). This observation indicated that the reaction reached completion within this astounding timeframe. This is also consistent with our complementary experiments through our general synthetic procedure, where uncompromised quantitative yields were collected with reaction runs tested in timeframes down to 48 s (Table 2, entries 15–16).

By systematically probing the solvent, catalyst volume, and reaction time, the optimized conditions for the promotion of Kabachnik-Fields reaction using TDES as a catalyst were established. In the next step, otherwise substituted aldehydes and amines with electron-donating groups (EDG) and electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) were tested to examine the limitations of our synthetic protocol. The highest yields result from the reactions of benzaldehyde with aniline (98%) and 4-nitrobenzaldehyde with aniline (98%). Generally, EWG substituted aromatic aldehydes like nitro and chlorine (by inductive and resonance effects) when partnered with aniline, produced the quantitative yields of 98% and 98% (Table 3, entries 2–4). On the other hand, the lowest yield among aromatics is from the reaction of benzaldehyde and 3,4-dichloroaniline (89%), which still produced the desired product in a high-yielding manner. Other aromatic aldehyde and aniline reactants with different substituents furnished similarly excellent yields without undergoing undesired conversions on the corresponding functional groups, thus further indicating the generality of this swift protocol in Kabachnik-Fields reaction for these substituents. However, the use of n-octanal as an aliphatic aldehyde did not proceed to the formation of the desired product, which indicates that a minimum level of electron deficiency on the aldehyde is necessary for fast conversion of aldehydes. On the other hand, the use of benzylamine as an aliphatic amine furnished the corresponding α-aminophosphonate compound with a 73% yield (Table 3, entry 14).

A mechanistic pathway for the catalytic synthesis of α-aminophosphonates by urea/SnCl2/HCl TDES in water is proposed in Scheme 3. Herein, the catalytic activity is ascribed to electrophilic activation of the aldehyde carbonyl group via tin salt Lewis acid species, as well as H-bond donation and Brønsted-Lowry acidic role of the urea ammonium salt, which make the aldehyde more susceptible to a nucleophilic attack by the amine, followed by condensation and the formation of the Schiff base intermediate. In the second step, the nitrogen atom in the Schiff base intermediate undergoes similar electrophilic activation by the TDES catalyst, paving the way for a nucleophilic attack by triphenyl phosphite and the formation of phosphonium cation which is stabilized by H2O solvation and lone pair electrons of chlorine atoms from tin salt Lewis acid species as well as the electron lone pairs of nitrogen atoms in urea. The final step is the oxidation of the phosphonium intermediate to form the product by the addition of water under ambient conditions.

Chemical structures of the desired α-aminophosphonate products were confirmed by 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and31P-NMR spectroscopy. The methine hydrogen in all of the products was split into a doublet of doublet (dd), with the first splitting occurring as a result of phosphorous and hydrogen coupling (2JPH ≈ 25 Hz), and the second splitting by hydrogen-hydrogen coupling with methine and hydrogen on the secondary amine (3JHH ≈ 10 Hz)1. Further evidence was observed by adding D2O and therefore deuterium exchange process, which caused the second splitting be disappeared (Supporting Information, 1H-NMR spectra and data for the experiment in DMSO and D2O regarding the compound corresponding to entry 7, Table 3). In 13C-NMR, two signals were seen at approximately 53–55 ppm, nevertheless, these are originally split from one signal (doublet, 2JPC ≈156 Hz). Although ipso carbon as the most deshielded was seen in the form of a doublet of doublet, in fact the peaks arise from two distinct signals. This phenomenon was ascribed to diastereotopic carbons as each ipso carbon feels a different steric hindrance (Fig. 4). Ortho carbons also split into two distinct signals, with each signal spilt into a doublet because of P-O-C-C coupling. Furthermore, amine ipso carbon was also seen as a doublet signal.

In order to assess the scalability and stability of the TDES catalyst, we explored the possibility of its recovery from a 10 mmol scale procedure using the model reaction of benzaldehyde, aniline (1 eq.), and triphenyl phosphite (1 eq.) using 0.5 ml of urea/SnCl2/HCl TDES in 50 ml of water. As a result, the reaction’s completion was achieved in 5 min, as confirmed by tlc. The mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (3*15 ml), rendering a 97% isolated yield of the α-amino phosphonate product after workup. The aqueous phase containing the TDES catalyst was used for two additional runs at the same scale, successfully yielding remarkable 95 and 92% yields of the desired organophosphorus product in repeated runs, which indicates a notable recovery and reuse feature of the catalyst on a large scale (Fig. 5). UV-VIS monitoring analysis was also implemented to further examine the efficiency of the recycled TDES. In this regard, the aqueous phase from the third 10 mmol scale run was evaporated in a running fume hood and then using a rotary evaporator. The resulting mixture was charged with a magnetic stir bar, and concentrated HCl was added dropwise until the clear TDES solution was recreated. As per the initial UV-VIS monitoring experiment discussed above, 50 µL of the resulting TDES was subjected to a 0.2 mmol scale of the modal reaction in a quartz cell, and the mixture was scanned per second at 262 nm. A similar trend in the absorption was observed, except that the mixture of the recycled TDES reached the absorption plateau at a longer period of 62 s (supporting information). These results indicate the efficient recovery and reusability of the catalytic system.

Conclusion

In summary, a newly developed TDES of urea/SnCl2/HCl proved efficient in catalytic synthesis of Kabachnik-Fields reaction for straightforward production of α-aminophosphonate compounds, which furnished the significant phosphorous pharmacophores in 48–120 s in water. Synthesized from cheap, abundant chemicals, TDES swiftly furthered the reaction of bulky triphenyl phosphonate with a number of aromatic aldehydes and amines bearing electron-donating and electron-withdrawing functional groups without undergoing undesired transformations towards the product formation (14 examples). Use of water as the solvent for the remarkably fast reaction highlights the mild and eco-sustainable virtue of the protocol, along with employing a water-compatible TDES of organic and metallic nature. 119Sn-NMR and13C NMR techniques confirmed the successful synthesis of the TDES catalyst, and31PNMR, 1HNMR, and13NMR provided information on the selective synthesis of phosphonated compounds of interest. Furthermore, UV-VIS monitoring technique supported the brisk kinetics of converting the starting materials into the α-aminophosphonates upon introducing the TDES catalyst into the reaction milieu. The protocol also supports scaled-up synthesis, and the catalyst could be retrieved from the process and reused for at least three successive runs without significant loss of activity.
