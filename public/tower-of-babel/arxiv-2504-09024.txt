A Robust Lattice Boltzmann Method for Interface-Bound Transport of a Passive Scalar: Application to Surfactant-Laden Multiphase Flows
William Schupbach, Kannan Premnath

Abstract
The transport of a passive scalar restricted on interfaces, which is advected by the fluid motions have numerous applications in multiphase transport phenomena. A prototypical example is the advection-diffusion of the concentration field of an insoluble surfactant along interfaces. A sharp-interface model of the surfactant transport on the interface (Stone, Phys. Fluids A, 111, 1990) has been modified and further extended to a diffuse-interface formulation based on a delta-function regularization by Teigen et al. (in Comm. Math. Sci. , 4:1009, 2009). However, the latter approach involves singular terms which can compromise its numerical implementation. Recently, Jain and Mani (in Annual Research Briefs , Center for Turbulence Research, Stanford University, 2022) circumvented this issue by applying a variable transformation, which effectively leads to a generalized interface-bound scalar transport equation with an additional interfacial confining flux term that prevents lateral diffusion into the bulk phase regions. The resulting formulation has similarities with the conservative Allen-Cahn equation (CACE) used for tracking of interfaces. In this paper, we will discuss a novel robust central moment lattice Boltzmann (LB) method to simulate the interface-bound advection-diffusion transport equation of a scalar field proposed in Teigen et al. by applying Jain and Mani’s transformation. It is coupled with another LB scheme for the CACE to compute the evolving interfaces, and the resulting algorithm is validated against some benchmark problems available in the literature. As further extension, we have coupled it with our central moment LB flow solver for the two-fluid motions, which is modulated by the Marangoni stresses resulting from the variation of the surface tension with the local surfactant concentration modeled via the Langmuir isotherm. This is then validated by simulating insoluble surfactant-laden drop deformation and break-up in a shear flow at various capillary numbers.



Investigations on this research were presented at 32nd International Conference on Discrete Simulation of Fluid Dynamics (DSFD), Albuquerque, New Mexico, July 2023 and at American Physical Society (APS) 76th Annual Division of Fluid Dynamics (DFD) Meeting, Washington D.C., November 2023 (https://meetings.aps.org/Meeting/DFD23/Session/T16.6).

1
Introduction

Fluid flows in which a combination of materials existing in multiple thermodynamic phases are classified as multiphase flow, such as the flow of water entrained with water vapour bubbles that often occur in industrial applications such as power generation, water treatment systems, the production of oil, as well as refrigeration and air conditioning systems and in emerging microfluidics. On the other hand, these phase components can also consist of chemically distinct matter such as oil drops in water, and are sometimes classified as multi-fluid flows, which occur in emulsification and flotation processes that have numerous industrial applications. In general, multiphase flows contain a continuous phase and a dispersed phase that are separated by an interface in which inter-molecular force balances between the bulk fluids manifest as surface tension forces at continuum scales.

There exist an interesting class of situations where the advection-diffusive transport of a scalar field is confined by the dynamics of the interfaces, with the surface active agents or surfactants being a prototypical example. As such, surfactants are molecules with a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail that tend to preferentially adsorb on fluid interfaces that further disrupt the existing imbalance of inter-molecular forces at the interface and tend to decrease the surface tension locally, which in turn can influence the dynamics of bulk fluids significantly
[
1
]
. There are a wide variety of surface acting agents that exist, both natural and synthetic, but they can be broadly characterized by the head group polarity which leads to a complex range of adsorption and solution properties. The most commonly observed condition for the use of surface active agents, is when the hydrophilic portion is separated into the aqueous phase while the hydrophobic part adsorbs onto a liquid interface. When this occurs, the surfactant effectively acts as a wetting or dispersion agent, as an emulsifier, as a foaming or anti-foaming agent, or as a lubricant such as those found in detergents, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics, and many other industrial processes. Of interest in this work, is the so called insoluble surfactant, which can be thought of as a surfactant with weak solubility in the bulk phase, while the hydrophobic part of the tail-head edifice still plays an active role in adsorbing to liquid interfaces as they undergo surface diffusion and advection and thereby modifying the surface tension locally.

Due to the complex nature of such surfactant-laden multiphase flows, studying the associated fluid dynamics requires experimental investigations or numerical simulations with appropriate continuum models for their transport. Stone
[
2
]
derived a time dependent advection-diffusion equation for interfacial surfactant transport by assuming a ‘sharp’ interface model
[
2
]
, where the interface serves as a discontinuity for the bulk fluids with zero thickness. This theoretical framework was then used in simulations using a variety of numerical techniques, such as the boundary integral method
[
3
]
, volume-of-fluid method
[
4
,
5
]
, level set method
[
6
]
, finite element method
[
7
]
, and finite difference method
[
8
]
.

On the other hand, diffuse-interface models, which are endowed with interfaces of finite thickness across which fluid properties vary continuously and also known as the phase field models, have been used widely in recent years for simulation of multiphase flows (see e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
) as they overcome numerical challenges associated with interface tracking with methods that are based on sharp-interface models. Many different formulations to incorporate the effect of surfactants in such phase field models have been developed in recent years (see e.g.,
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
). In the context of modeling insoluble surfactants with diffuse interfaces, Teigen et al.
[
19
]
modified the formulation of Stone
[
2
]
by introducing a surface delta function, which was solved by various different numerical schemes, including the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) recently
[
20
,
21
]
. However, the Teigen et al.
[
19
]
’s approach was found to have singularity issues as they involve computation of the inverse of the surface delta functions outside the interface region. Recognizing this, Jain and Mani
[
22
]
recently circumvented such an issue via a variable transformation based on their computational model for transport of immiscible scalars in two-phase flows
[
23
]
. This leads to a regularized formulation to represent the advection-diffusion of interface-confined scalars and interestingly has a mathematical structure similar to the conservative Allen-Cahn equation (CACE) used for capturing interfaces
[
24
]
. It forms the basis for further extensions and numerical implementations using an advanced lattice Boltzmann (LB) approach in this paper.

The lattice Boltzmann method (LBM)
[
25
,
26
]
is a computational approach based on kinetic theory and involves collide-and-stream steps of the particle distribution functions on a discrete lattice. Due to algorithmic simplicity and natural parallelization capabilities as well as its ability to serve as a rich platform to incorporate models based on kinetic theory at mesoscopic scales, it has been used for simulations of a wide range of fluid mechanics applications, including multiphase flows. The choice of the collision model in LBM plays a major role in its fidelity and numerical stability. In particular, the central moment-based formulations such as the cascaded model utilizing the Maxwell distribution
[
27
]
and the Fokker-Planck model
[
28
]
have been shown to result in significantly improved numerical characteristics with robust implementations. Moreover, the central moment-based LB approach has recently been used for interface capturing and simulation of multiphase flows (see e.g.,
[
29
]
).
Thus, in this paper, we develop a new central moment LB method for the transport of an interface-bound scalar field representing the insoluble surfactant concentration. Moreover, as a further extension of
[
22
]
, we couple this approach with two other LB solvers based on central moments for interface capturing and the computation of two-fluid motions with variable surface tension effects; the latter involve the normal capillary and the tangential Marangoni forces, and the dependence of the local surface tension on the surfactant concentration is parameterized via the Langmuir isotherm. The resulting numerical schemes are tested and validated for a series of benchmark problems to demonstrate their accuracy in simulations of surfactant-laden multiphase flows.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In the next section (Sec.
2
), we discuss the derivation of the regularized interface-confined transport of a scalar field, as well as the governing equations for CACE-based interface capturing and two-fluid motions with a variable surface tension modeling. Next, Sec.
3
presents the development of a novel central moment LBM for solving the interface-confined scalar transport equation, while the other two LB solvers for interface capturing and two-fluid motions are discussed in the appendix sections. Then, the results for a series of validation benchmark cases are discussed in Sec.
4
. Finally, a summary of this paper and the main conclusions drawn are presented in Sec.
5
.

2
Governing Equations

We now discuss the essential elements of the development of the interface-restricted transport of the scalar field, viz., the concentration of an insoluble surfactant, that was proposed by Jain and Mani
[
22
]
. Furthermore, we also outline the governing equations of the evolution of diffuse interfaces represented by the conservative Allen-Cahn equation and the multiphase Navier-Stokes equations written in a single-field formulation with variable surface tension forces, with the latter being modulated by the local surfactant concentration field through the Langmuir isotherm discussed at the end of the section. These governing equations and models then form the basis of the development of our novel LB schemes based on central moments in the next section.

2.1
Interface-bound scalar transport equation for diffuse interfaces

The “sharp” interface-based conservation equation of the interfacial surfactant concentration field, which serves as the starting point of this discussion, was first derived by Stone
[
2
]
and also elaborated further in
[
30
]
, is written as

\frac{\partial\hat{c}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\hat{c}=\bm{\nabla\!\!_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\cdot(D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\bm{\nabla\!\!_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\hat{c})-\hat{c}\bm{\nabla\!\!_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\cdot\bm{u_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}-\hat{c}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\hat{n}},

(1)

where
\hat{c}
is the interfacial surfactant concentration,
\bm{\hat{n}}
is the unit normal of the interface,
\bm{\nabla\!\!_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}=(I-\bm{\hat{n}}\bm{\hat{n}})\bm{\nabla}
is the surface gradient operator,
D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
is the interfacial surfactant diffusion coefficient and
\bm{u}
is a velocity field. This equation represents the advection and diffusion of surfactant concentration
\hat{c}
exclusively on the interface, which is henceforth denoted by
\gamma
.

On the other hand, for practical implementations based on phase field models, we need the equivalent formulation of Eq. (
1
), which was developed by Teigen et al
[
19
]
via using the surface delta function
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
that effectively distributes the scalar field across a narrow region spanning the“diffuse” interfaces. More precisely, this is accomplished by satisfying the conservation relation given by

\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\gamma}\hat{c}\,d\gamma=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\,d\Omega,

(2)

where
\gamma
represents the interface, and
\Omega
represents the overall domain (see Fig.
1
).

Figure 1
:
Schematic of an interface
\gamma
separating two fluid domains
\Omega_{A}
and
\Omega_{B}
, which, when combined becomes the total domain
\Omega
, i.e.,
\Omega=\Omega_{A}\cup\Omega_{B}
.

Physically, this means that the total amount of surfactant on the interface is equivalent to the total amount found in the extended domain where such an extension is achieved via the surface delta function. The resulting formulation, which is valid everywhere in the domain
\Omega
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s})+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\bm{u}\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s})=\bm{\nabla}\cdot(D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\bm{\nabla}\hat{c}).

(3)

Then again, Eq. (
3
) leads to robustness issues since its solution leads to
\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
and to recover
\hat{c}
, one needs to divide
\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
by
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
, which is singular away from the interface.

2.2
Regularized interface-bound scalar transport equation for diffuse interfaces

Alternatively, Jain and Mani
[
22
]
, proposed to regularize the formulation given in Eq. (
3
) by introducing a transformation of variables
c=\hat{c}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
. Then, Eq. (
3
), in terms of the new variable
c
, becomes

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\bm{u}c)=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\left(\bm{\nabla}c-\frac{c}{\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\bm{\nabla}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\right)\right].

(4)

In what follows, we consider that the interface
\gamma
is tracked by the conservative Allen-Cahn equation (CACE) for the phase field variable
\phi
with bounds
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}\leq\phi\leq\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}
, which is given by
[
24
]

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\bm{u}\phi\right)=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}\left(\bm{\nabla}\phi-\theta\bm{\hat{n}}\right)\right],

(5)

where
\bm{\hat{n}}=\bm{\nabla}\phi/|\bm{\nabla}\phi|
is the unit normal,
\phi=\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}
in
\Omega_{A}
and
\phi=\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}
in
\Omega_{B}
, and the coefficient
\theta
is given by

\theta=\left(\frac{4}{W}\right)\frac{(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi)(\phi-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b})}{(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b})}.

Here,
W
is the width of the interface. Then, an equilibrium profile across the interface can be derived from Eq. (
5
) by letting the interfacial diffusion term balance with the sharpening term, i.e.,
M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}\left(\bm{\nabla}\phi-\theta\bm{\hat{n}}\right)=0
or
\bm{\nabla}\phi=\theta\bm{\hat{n}}
, and the resulting hyperbolic tangent profile reads as

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle m}+\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle o}\tanh{\left(\frac{2\zeta}{W}\right)},

(6)

where
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle m}=(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}+\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b})/2
and
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle o}=(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b})/2
,
\zeta
is the interfacial normal coordinate with
\zeta=0
where
\phi=\phi_{m}
. Then, following
[
19
]
, we obtain the surface delta function
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
via
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}=|\bm{\nabla}\phi|
, and in view of the above, and noting
|\bm{\hat{n}}|=1
, it follows that

\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}=|\bm{\nabla}\phi|=\theta.

(7)

Then, we can crucially observe that

\frac{1}{\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\bm{\nabla}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}=\frac{1}{\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}\bm{\nabla}\phi=\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}\bm{n}

(8)

Finally, using this last equation in the second term on the right hand side of Eq. (
4
) and simplifying we arrive at the regularized form of the interface-confined scalar transport equation, which reads as

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\bm{u}c\right)=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\left(\bm{\nabla}c-\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}\bm{\hat{n}}\right)\right],

(9)

where the coefficient
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}
is given by

\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}=\frac{8c}{W}\frac{(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle m}-\phi)}{(\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b})}.

(10)

Here, the term involving
D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}\bm{n}
in Eq. (
9
) can be interpreted as the “confining” flux term, which prevents the diffusion of the scalar field (i.e, surfactant) on both sides of the interface and restricts it exclusively to the interface region. It is important to point out that Eq. (
9
) does not require dividing by
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
and avoids the singularity issue associated with Eq.(
3
), and is therefor more robust. Moreover, unlike Eq. (
1
), there is no need to deal with the surface gradient operator
\bm{\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\!s}}
, and hence Eq. (
9
) is simpler to use in numerical implementation. Equation (
9
) represents a slight generalization of the model derived in
[
22
]
, which uses
0\leq\phi\leq 1
whereas here we have
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}\leq\phi\leq\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}
.

We note here that the equilibrium concentration profile across the interface
c=c(\zeta)
can be readily obtained from Eq. (
9
) using the same approach as that used to derive Eq. (
6
) and is given here as
[
22
]

c=\frac{c_{0}}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{2\zeta}{W}\right)}.

(11)

Here,
c_{0}
is the reference concentration field at the interface where
\zeta=0
and very rapidly decays to zero away from the diffuse interface region with
c=0
as
\zeta\rightarrow\pm\infty
. In practice, we use Eq. (
11
) for the initialization of the surfactant concentration field
\hat{c}
in simulations.

2.3
Navier-Stokes equations for multiphase flows with variable surface tension forces

In order to simulate more general situations where both the interface and the concentration field is advected by the motion of two-fluids in multiphase flows, thereby extending the recent work
[
22
]
, we need to be able to compute the hydrodynamic velocity fields. In this regard, within the phase field framework, we utilize the single-field formulation of the Navier-Stokes equations with a distributed form of surface tension forces. Thus, the corresponding mass and momentum equations are given, respectively, as

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})=0,

(12)

and

\frac{\partial(\rho\bm{u})}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{u}\bm{u})=-\bm{\nabla}P+\bm{\nabla}\left[\mu\left(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right)\right]+\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle t}},

(13)

where the total force
\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}
consists of the combination of the surface tension force
\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}
and any external body force
\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext}}
given as

\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}=\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}+\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext}},

(14)

and
\bm{u}
and
P
are the fluid velocity and pressure, respectively. Here, the density
\rho
and viscosity
\mu
are taken to vary smoothly across the diffuse interfaces.

2.3.1
Modeling of variable surface tension force

In the presence of surfactants on the interface, the surface tension force, shown below in Eq. (
15
), contains both the capillary force, which is normal to interface, and the Marangoni force, which is tangential to the interface, and they depend on the local interfacial concentration
c
. Thus, we can resolve the distributed form of the local surface tension force as

\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}=\underbrace{-\sigma(c)|\bm{\nabla}\phi|^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{n}\right)\bm{n}}_{\mbox{capillary force}}+\underbrace{|\bm{\nabla}\phi|^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bm{\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle s}}\sigma(c)}_{\mbox{Marangoni force}}

(15)

The local surface tension
\sigma(c)
dependence on the surfactant concentration is modeled by means of a surface equation of state given by the standard Langmuir isotherm equation of state, which reads as

\sigma(c)=\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle o}\left[1+\beta\log\left(1-\frac{c}{c_{\scriptscriptstyle max}}\right)\right],

(16)

where
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
is the surface tension for the clean interface, i.e., in the absence of surfactants,
\beta
is the Gibbs elasticity parameter, which represents the sensitivity of surface tension on the surfactant concentration, and
c_{\scriptscriptstyle max}
is the maximum possible concentration of the latter. Figure
2
shows an illustration of the Langmuir isootherm surface for ranges of the surfactant concentration and the Gibbs elasticity parameter for
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle o}=0.01
. It also indicates that there exists certain ranges of parameters that can make the surface tension become negative; in this work, we avoid choosing the range of parameters that result in negative values of the local surface tension.

Figure 2
:
Illustration of the Langmuir Isotherm surface indicating that the local surface tension decreases for any given values of Gibbs elasticity parameter and surfactant concentration when compared to the clean interface case.

3
Central moment LBM for Transport of Interface-Bound Scalar Field

The governing equations given in the previous section represent a coupled system for 1) the transport of the interface-confined scalar field, 2) the tracking of interfaces, and 3) computation of multiphase flows with variable surface tension forces. In this paper, we develop novel central moments-based LB schemes using the D2Q9 lattice for computing each of these three components. They each involve computing a separate LB equation whose collision is modeled by relaxation of nine central moments of the respective distribution functions to their carefully constructed equilibria and followed by the streaming of the resulting post-collision distribution functions; and from the latter, the macroscopic variable of interest, viz., the concentration field
c
, order parameter
\phi
, or the hydrodynamic velocity field
\bm{u}
, are updated. Since the computation of
c
depends on
\phi
and
\bm{u}
, and the latter, in turn, is modulated by
c
via the Langmuir isotherm for the local surface tension, their solutions are fully coupled. Among these, the focus of this paper is on the development of a new central moment LB scheme for the interface-confined scalar field for simulating the transport of the insoluble surfactants, which is discussed in this section, while the other two LB schemes are extensions of our recent work
[
29
,
31
,
32
]
and hence they are presented in various appendices for completeness.

The discrete distribution functions for the evolution of the interface-restricted scalar field
c
is given by
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, where
\alpha=0,1,2\dots,8
are the nine discrete particle velocity directions
\bm{e}_{\alpha}
associated with the D2Q9 lattice (see Fig.
3
). In general, the simplest model for collision represents the relaxation of
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
towards their respective equilibrium distributions
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}
. Instead, in this work, we introduce an advanced formulation that relaxes the central moments of the distribution functions to their appropriate equilibria so that the transport of
c
as prescribed by Eq. (
9
) is recovered in simulations.

Figure 3
:
A schematic of the two-dimensional, nine particle velocity (D2Q9) lattice used to develop the LB algorithms in this manuscript.

The Cartesian components of the velocity directions associated with this lattice can be expressed as follows:

\qquad\left|\bm{e}_{x}\right>=(0,1,0,-1,0,1,-1,-1,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top},

(17a)

\qquad\left|\bm{e}_{y}\right>=(0,0,1,0,-1,1,1,-1,-1)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(17b)

Here and henceforth, the superscript
\top
refers to the transpose operator. We use the standard Dirac’s bra-ket notation, that is
\left<\cdot\right|
and
\left|\cdot\right>
, represent the row and column vectors, respectively. Then, the nine distribution functions can be collected as the vector
\left|\mathbf{h}\right>=(h_{\scriptscriptstyle 0},h_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ldots h_{\scriptscriptstyle 8})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
. For constructing the LB scheme, we also need the following 9-component vector containing each element equal to unity given by

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\mathbf{1}\right>=(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

so that its inner product with the vector of distribution functions
\mathbf{h}
, i.e.,
\left<\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{1}\right>
will yield the local interfacial concentration field:
c=\left<\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{1}\right>
. Then, the nine independent non-orthogonal basis vectors used to construct a central moment LB formulation in what follows are given by

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\mathbf{p}_{0}\right>=\left|\mathbf{1}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{1}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{2}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{y}\right>,

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\mathbf{p}_{3}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}^{2}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{4}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{y}^{2}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{5}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{y}\right>,

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\mathbf{p}_{6}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}^{2}\bm{e}_{y}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{7}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{y}^{2}\right>,\quad\left|\mathbf{p}_{8}\right>=\left|\bm{e}_{x}^{2}\bm{e}_{y}^{2}\right>.

(18)

These can be utilized for mapping the distribution functions to the respective nine raw moments via taking the inner products of
\left<\mathbf{h}\right|
with
\left|\mathbf{p}_{j}\right>
, where
j=0,1,\ldots,8
. Such operations can be compactly represented by grouping together all the basis vectors as the following transformation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{P}}
:

\qquad\bm{\mathsf{p}}=\left[\left|\mathbf{p}_{0}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{1}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{2}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{3}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{4}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{5}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{6}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{7}\right>,\left|\mathbf{p}_{8}\right>\;\right]^{\scriptscriptstyle\top},

(19)

For convenience, the elements of this transformation matrix and its inverse are given in Appendix
A
. Then, the discrete raw moments of the distribution functions
\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}
and the equilibrium distribution functions
\chi^{\;\scriptscriptstyle{eq^{\prime}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}
of order (
m+n
) can be defined in their component forms as follows:

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\chi^{\;\scriptscriptstyle{eq^{\prime}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}^{\scriptscriptstyle m}e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}^{\scriptscriptstyle n}.

(20)

Since our goal is to actually construct a central moments-based LB scheme, next, we define the discrete central moments of
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
and its equilibria of order (
m+n
), which read as

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle x})^{\scriptscriptstyle m}(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle y})^{\scriptscriptstyle n}.

(21)

Then, for convenience, we represent all of the independent discrete raw moments and central moments of various orders, associated with the D2Q9 lattice using the vectors
\bm{\chi^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\chi}
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\chi^{\prime}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\chi^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}),

(22a)

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\chi}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}).

(22b)

Based on the above considerations, we now note that the distribution functions can be mapped into raw moments by
\bm{\chi^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\mathbf{h}
, and can then be mapped further into central moments using
\bm{\chi}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}\bm{\chi^{\prime}}
, where the frame transformation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{F}}
is defined by taking binomial expansions of Eq. (
21
) (see e.g.,
[
33
]
). For convenience, the latter as well as its inverse are presented in Appendix
B
. Conversely, one can perform the reverse mappings via applying, in turn, the inverse matrices of
\bm{\mathsf{F}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{P}}
on central moments
\bm{\chi}
to obtain the distribution functions
\mathbf{h}
.

Next, to construct the collision step, we define the discrete central moment equilibria by means of a matching principle whereby we match the independent discrete central moments supported by D2Q9 lattice with the corresponding continuous central moments of the Maxwell distribution, after replacing the density
\rho
with the insoluble surfactant concentration
c
. Moreover, the flux term in Eq. (
9
) involving
D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}\bm{n}
is accounted for using the method of extended moment equilibria, where the first order equilibrium central moments are augmented to include these terms. A Chapman-Enskog analysis, not presented here for brevity, confirms that Eq. (
9
) is recovered. The resulting central moment equilibria for the D2Q9 lattice then read as

\displaystyle\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c,\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}n_{\scriptscriptstyle x},\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle c}n_{\scriptscriptstyle y},

\displaystyle\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\cdot c,\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\cdot c,\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 4}\cdot c,

(23)

where
c_{s}^{2}=1/3
, with
c_{s}
being the lattice speed of sound. From the above considerations, we can then summarize the central moment LB scheme to solve the interface-bound surfactant concentration field as follows:

h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x}+\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-h_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\left\{\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi}}\left[\bm{\chi}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}-\bm{\chi}\right]\right\},

(24)

where
\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi}}=\mbox{diag}(1,\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi},\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi},1,1,1,1,1,1)
is the diagonal relaxation rate matrix in which the relaxation rate
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi}
for the first order moments is related to the diffusion coefficient
D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
by

D_{\scriptscriptstyle s}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\left(1/\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi}-1/2\right)\Delta t.

(25)

4
Results and Discussion

We will present a series of benchmark test cases that demonstrate the efficacy of our new central moment LB formulation used to obtain the evolution of the interface-bound surfactant concentration field as modeled by Eq. (
9
). The first two cases utilize this LB solver independently from the central moment LB methods for interface tracking and two-fluid motions presented in appendices. The first case is that of a static drop inside a periodic box that has a prescribed surfactant gradient along the interface. After some time, the interfacial diffusion process causes the concentration to become more uniformly distributed across the interface. We will compare the spatial distributions and time variations of the concentration field to that of an analytical solution for this case at a variety of dimensionless times and interfacial diffusion coefficients. The second case builds on the first case in that it also includes a prescribed constant background flow field. The domain is extended by double in the direction of the flow, and the drop becomes advected while undergoing the same diffusion process along the interface. Again, we compare the final results with an analytical solution.

Finally, we couple the three central moment-based LB solvers together for the transport of interfacial surfactant concentration, tracking of interfaces, and two-fluid motions in a case of a surfactant-laden drop in surfactant-laden shear flow. The top and bottom horizontal walls of a channel move with constant velocity in opposite directions causing the continuous phase to shear, while the two vertical boundaries are made periodic. Then a drop is placed in the center of the domain and is made to deform by the flow in the bulk region. The surface tension forces tend to cause the drop to revert back to a circular shape while the viscous forces tend to deform the drop further. We modulate this behavior via varying the dimensionless parameter known as the capillary number and compare the deformation parameter to that of an analytical solution in the creeping flow regime with and without the presence of surfactants. Lastly, we demonstrate the fidelity of our approach by simulating the break up of the drop involving inertial effects at higher Reynolds numbers and capillary numbers for cases with and without surfactants. This also serves to demonstrate how the presence of insoluble surfactants on the interface modulate the breakup process of drops under shear.

4.3
Deformation and break-up processes of insoluble surfactant-laden drop in shear flow

This final case study consists of an insoluble surfactant-laden drop placed in a shear flow within a channel whereby the upper and lower plates move in opposite directions with the same speed,
U_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
, while the two vertical boundaries on each side are considered periodic. The flow domain is resolved with
3H\times H

R=H/8

Figure 8

Schematic of the insoluble surfactant-laden drop in shear flow problem set up.

The flow field without a drop would reach a steady state solution of the following form, and here we use this as an initial condition

u(y)=U_{\scriptscriptstyle o}\left(2\frac{y}{H}-1\right).

Then, a drop is placed at the center of the domain filled with a neutrally buoyant fluid (i.e., with a density ratio of unity) and experiences shear from the upper and lower parts of the flow field in opposing directions, and thus deforms anti-symmetrically across the domain half channel. Then, the magnitude of the shear rate experienced by the fuids is
\dot{\gamma}=2U_{\scriptscriptstyle o}/H
. The primary dimensionless number that characterizes the drop deformations is the capillary number
\mbox{Ca}=\mu\dot{\gamma}r/\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle o}

These forces tend to compete in the drop deformation process as the viscous drag causes drop deformation while the surface tension tends to cause the deformed drop to revert back into a circular shape. Moreover, the Reynolds number is also used here to modulate the inertial forces relative to the viscous forces and is defined as
\mbox{Re}=\dot{\gamma}R^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}/\nu

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle a}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle b}=1

\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle a}=\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle b}=0.1

5
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we present a new LB method using a robust central moment-based collision model to solve a recently derived formulation for the transport of an interface-confined scalar field. The latter is modeled as a regularized advection-diffusion equation on a diffuse interface, which has applications related to insoluble surfactant-laden multiphase flows. We then demonstrated the fidelity of this novel LB approach in a series of benchmark test cases. The first case demonstrated the diffusion of the surfactant concentration with a nonuniform initial distribution along the interface of a static drop is accurate in that quantitative comparisons of the computed concentration profiles at various times with an analytical solution were found to match quite well. The second test case involved an imposed background flow field that does not modify the shape of the same drop but causes the drop to become advected and the temporal variation of surfactant diffusion on the interface in such a case was again consistent with the analytical solution.

Lastly, the third case considered the simulations of surfactant-laden drop in shear flow, which required coupling the central moment-based LB scheme for interface-confined surfactant field with two additional LB solvers, which are again based on central moments, for interface and two-fluid motions in a triple distribution functions-based approach. In this regard, we use the Langmuir isotherm as the interface equation of state to couple the surfactant concentration gradients to the fluid motions via normal and tangential surface tension forces. At low Reynolds numbers, i.e., in the creeping flow regime, the computed Taylor deformation parameter of the drop at steady state at various capillary numbers was found to be in good quantitative agreement with the theory due to Stone and Leal for both clean and contaminated interface cases, with the latter exhibiting larger deformation than the former. Lastly, we showed that with larger Reynolds numbers and capillary numbers, the drop breaks up under higher inertial and viscous forces and the onset of this fragmentation was found to be consistent with those found in literature for the same parameters. Moreover, the presence of surfactants was found to facilitate the break up process. The central moment LBM for interface-confined transport of concentration field is thus accurate, robust and free of any singularity issues in phase field modeling and simulation of surfactant-laden multiphase flows and its extension to three-dimensions is straightforward. Moreover, the approach can be further adapted for soluble surfactants which is a subject for future investigations.

\end{bmatrix}

(34)

Appendix C
Central moment LBM for Interface Tracking Based on Conservative Allen-Cahn Equation

The discrete distribution functions
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, where
\alpha=0,1,\ldots,8
are evolved to compute the order parameter used in the conservative Allen-Cahn equation (see Eq. (
5
)) for the tracking of interfaces. In order to construct a central moment LBM for
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, we first define their discrete raw moments and central moments, respectively, of order (
m+n
)as

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\kappa^{\prime\;\scriptscriptstyle{eq}}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}^{\scriptscriptstyle m}e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}^{\scriptscriptstyle n},

(35a)

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\end{array}\right)(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle x})^{\scriptscriptstyle m}(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle y})^{\scriptscriptstyle n}.

(35b)

Their nine independent components for the D2Q9 lattice are represented by the vectors
\bm{\kappa^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\kappa}
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\kappa^{\prime}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\kappa^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top},

(36a)

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\kappa}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(36b)

Then, to construct the collision step, following
[
29
]
, we determine the discrete central moment equilibria via a matching principle by matching the discrete equilibrium central moments to the corresponding continuous central moments of the Maxwell distribution by replacing the density
\rho
with the order parameter
\phi
. Moreover, the interfacial sharpening flux term
M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}\theta\bm{n}
in Eq. (
5
) are accounted for via the extended moment equilibria of the first order moments so as to recover the conservative Allen-Cahn equation.

\displaystyle\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\phi,\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}\theta n_{\scriptscriptstyle x},\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}\theta n_{\scriptscriptstyle y},

\displaystyle\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\cdot\phi,\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\cdot\phi,\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=0,\qquad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 4}\cdot\phi,

(37)

Based on these considerations, we can now write central moment LB equation for interface tracking as

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x}+\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\left\{\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\left[\bm{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}-\bm{\kappa}\right]\right\},

(38)

where
\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
is a diagonal relaxation rate matrix is given by
\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=\mbox{diag}(1,\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa},\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa},1,1,1,1,1,1)
. Note that the first order moments are related to the mobility coefficient
M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}
by

M_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\left(1/\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}-1/2\right)\Delta t,

(39)

and typically we let all other relaxation rates be equal to unity. Finally, what follows are the algorithmic steps taken to solve the LBE across one time step. This process is then marched in time for dynamic tracking of interfaces.

•

Compute pre-collision raw moments from distribution functions through the transformation

\bm{\kappa^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\bm{f}

•

Compute pre-collision central moments from raw moments using

\bm{\kappa}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}\bm{\kappa^{\prime}}

•

Perform collision by relaxing the central moments towards equilibrium central moments

\bm{\tilde{\kappa}}=\bm{\kappa}-\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\left(\bm{\kappa}-\bm{\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}}\right)

•

Compute post-collision raw moments from central moments via

\bm{\tilde{\kappa}^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\bm{\tilde{\kappa}}

•

Compute post-collision distribution functions from raw moments through the mapping

\bm{\tilde{f}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\bm{\tilde{\kappa}^{\prime}}

•

Perform streaming via

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)=\tilde{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x}-\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\Delta t,t)

where
\alpha=0,1,2\dots 8
.

•

Update the order parameter
\phi
by computing the zeroth moment of
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
as

\phi=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}.

(40)

Appendix D
Central moment LBM for Two-Fluid Hydrodynamics Based on Navier-Stokes Equations with Variable Surface Tension

For handling general cases in multiphase flows with high density ratios in LBM, a pressure-based formulation achieved via a transformation of the distribution functions
[
37
]
, which is further extended for a central moment-based collision model
[
29
]
is adopted to simulate two-fluid hydrodynamics with variable surface tension effects. Then, the discrete distribution functions
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, where
\alpha=0,1,\ldots,8
are solved to compute the velocity field
\bm{u}
and pressure
P
(rather than the density
\rho
, which evolves according to the order parameter
\phi
in an affine way as noted as the end of this section). In order to develop a central moment LBM for
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, we first define the discrete raw moments and central moments, respectively, of
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, its equilibria
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}
, and the source terms
S_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
, which account for surface tension and other forces, of order (
m+n
) as

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\eta^{\prime\;\scriptscriptstyle{eq}}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\sigma^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\\[5.69054pt]
S_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\end{array}\right)e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}^{\scriptscriptstyle m}e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}^{\scriptscriptstyle n},

(41)

and

\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\\[5.69054pt]
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\\[5.69054pt]
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle mn}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\\[5.69054pt]
S_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\end{array}\right)(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha x}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle x})^{\scriptscriptstyle m}(e_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha y}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle y})^{\scriptscriptstyle n}.

Then, we list the nine independent discrete raw moments and central moments supported by the D2Q9 lattice as the vectors
\bm{\eta^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\eta}
as

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\eta^{\prime}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\eta^{\prime}_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\eta}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

Similarly, we can group all the central moments of the equilibria and the source terms as the following vectors

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\eta}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\sigma}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 20},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

Next, to construct the collision step based on central moments, we match the discrete central moments of equilibria with the continuous central moments of the modified continuous Maxwell distribution that accounts for the pressure-based transformation indicated above. Then, we get
[
29
]

\displaystyle\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=P,\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\psi(\rho)u_{\scriptscriptstyle x},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\psi(\rho)u_{\scriptscriptstyle y},

\displaystyle\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}P+\psi(\rho)u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}P+\psi(\rho)u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\psi(\rho)u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}u_{\scriptscriptstyle y},

\displaystyle\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=-\psi(\rho)(u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2})u_{\scriptscriptstyle y},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=-\psi(\rho)(u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2})u_{\scriptscriptstyle x},

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 6}\rho+\psi(\rho)(u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2})(u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}),

where
\psi(\rho)=P-\rho c_{s}^{2}
.
Similarly, the discrete central moments of source terms obtained by an analogous matching principle read as
[
29
]

\displaystyle\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}=\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}F_{\scriptscriptstyle tx}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}F_{\scriptscriptstyle ty}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},

\displaystyle\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}=2c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}F_{\scriptscriptstyle px}u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}+(u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2})\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}=2c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}F_{\scriptscriptstyle py}u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}+(u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2})\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}(F_{\scriptscriptstyle px}u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}+F_{\scriptscriptstyle py}u_{\scriptscriptstyle x})+u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle p},

\displaystyle\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}=0,\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}=0,\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}=0,

where
F_{\scriptscriptstyle px}=-\partial_{x}\psi(\rho)
,
F_{\scriptscriptstyle py}=-\partial_{y}\psi(\rho)
, and
\Gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle p}_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}=F_{\scriptscriptstyle px}u_{\scriptscriptstyle x}+F_{\scriptscriptstyle py}u_{\scriptscriptstyle y}
. In addition, the total force components are
F_{\scriptscriptstyle tx}=F_{\scriptscriptstyle sx}+F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext,x}
and
F_{\scriptscriptstyle ty}=F_{\scriptscriptstyle sy}+F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext,y}
, where
\bm{F}_{s}=(F_{\scriptscriptstyle sx},F_{\scriptscriptstyle sy})
and
\bm{F}_{ext}=(F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext,x},F_{\scriptscriptstyle ext,y})
are the local surface tension force (see Eq. (
15
) which accounts for its capillary and Marangoni components that vary locally based on the interfacial surfactant concentration field) and any external force, respectively.

In order to construct the collision step based on central moments for hydrodynamics, for generality and improved stability, we need to segregate the emergent shear viscosity effects from that of bulk viscosity, where the latter is related to the relaxation of the trace of the second order moments under collision. In other words, the collision model should relax
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}
and
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}
at different rates, where the former is related to the bulk viscosity and the latter to the shear viscosity, rather than relaxing
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}
and
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}
separately. To accomplish this, we introduce the combined second order moment variables

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}=\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}+\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},\qquad\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\qquad\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d}=\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}-\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 02},

(44)

and define the following vectors each containing nine independent central moments where the second order diagonal components are replaced with the above combinations:

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\hat{\eta}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 10}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 01}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 11}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 21}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle 12}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle 22}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\hat{\sigma}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!(\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 00},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 10},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 01},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 11},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 21},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 12},\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 22})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

Then, the vectors that were defined earlier (which involved bare forms of diagonal second order moments) with these vectors (which involve combined forms of second order moments and identified with the ‘hat’ over their notations) can be related formally as

\bm{\hat{\eta}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\eta},\qquad\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\eta}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\qquad\bm{\hat{\sigma}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\sigma},

(46)

where
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
is a block diagonal matrix that transforms such bare moments into combined moments. Based on these considerations, we can then write the central moment LBM for two-fluid hydrodynamics with variable surface tension effects as

g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x}+\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}\left\{\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}\left[\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}-\bm{\hat{\eta}}\right]+\left(1-\frac{\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}}{2}\right)\bm{\hat{\sigma}}\Delta t\right\},

(47)

where
\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}
is the relaxation rate matrix given by
\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}=\mbox{diag}(1,1,1,\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\zeta},\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\nu},\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\nu},1,1,1)
. Note that the shear viscosity
\nu
and bulk viscosity
\zeta
are related to the respective relaxation rates via

\nu=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\left(1/\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\nu}-1/2\right)\Delta t,\qquad\zeta=c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\left(1/\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\zeta}-1/2\right)\Delta t.

(48)

Here, typically we let all other relaxation rates be equal to unity, based on simplicity and stability considerations. Note that while Eq. (
46
) involves a formal transformation involving multiplication of vectors with a block matrix, in practical implementations, no such multiplications are necessary as these mappings simply imply that we replace component-wise the two second order diagonal bare moments with the combined forms as shown in Eq. (
44
) before collision; then following collision, the post-collision components (referred with ‘tilde’ over symbols in what follows) in their bare forms can be retrieved from their combined forms via
\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 20}=(\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}+\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d})/2
and
\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 02}=(\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2s}-\tilde{\eta}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2d})/2
, which amount to applying the inverse operator
\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{-1}
in Eq. (
47
).

The algorithmic steps for implementing Eq. (
47
) are outlined in what follows, which are marched in time for computing the flow fields, viz., fluid velocity and pressure.

•

Compute pre-collision raw moments from distribution functions via

\bm{\eta^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\bm{g}

•

Compute pre-collision central moments from raw moments through

\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}\bm{\eta^{\prime}}

•

Perform collision by relaxing the central moments towards equilibrium central moments
First, combine second order diagonal central moments using

\bm{\hat{\eta}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\eta},\qquad\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\eta}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq},\qquad\bm{\hat{\sigma}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\sigma},

and then relax them under collision with updates from the source terms as

\bm{\tilde{\hat{\eta}}}=\bm{\eta}+\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}\left(\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle eq}-\bm{\hat{\eta}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\bm{\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\eta}}}{2}\right)\bm{\hat{\sigma}}\Delta t,

which is followed by retrieving the post-collision central moments in their bare forms using

\bm{\tilde{\eta}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\tilde{\hat{\eta}}}

•

Compute post-collision raw moments from central moments through

\bm{\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\bm{\tilde{\eta}}

•

Compute post-collision distribution functions from raw moments via

\bm{\tilde{g}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\bm{\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}}

•

Perform streaming via

g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)=\tilde{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}(\bm{x}-\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\Delta t,t),

where
\alpha=0,1,2\dots 8
.

•

Update the pressure and fluid velocity by computing the zeroth moment and first order moments, respectively, of
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
as

P=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle 00}\Delta t,\qquad c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\rho\bm{u}=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha=0}^{\scriptscriptstyle 8}\bm{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}c_{\scriptscriptstyle s}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bm{F_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\Delta t,

(49)

and the local density
\rho
and viscosity
\nu
are obtained from the order parameter
\phi
as follows so that they vary smoothly across the interfaces:

\rho=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle b}+\left(\frac{\phi-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}}{\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}}\right)(\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle b}),\qquad\nu=\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle b}+\left(\frac{\phi-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}}{\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle b}}\right)(\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle b})

(50)
