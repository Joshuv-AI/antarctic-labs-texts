“I made this (sort of)”: Negotiating authorship, confronting fraudulence, and exploring new musical spaces with prompt-based AI music generation
Bob L. T. Sturm

Abstract
I reflect on my experience creating two music albums centered on state-of-the-art prompt-based AI music generation platforms. The first album explicitly poses the question: What happens when I collide my junk mail with these platforms? The second album is a direct response to the first, and toys with the inability of state-of-the-art prompt-based AI music generation platforms to generate music that is not “practiced”, “polished”, and “produced”. I seed a large language model (LLM) with information about these albums and have it interview me, which results in the exploration of several deeper questions: To what extent am I the author? Where am I in the resulting music? How is my musical identity changing as I am faced with machines that are in some ways far more talented than I? What new musical spaces does my work open, for me or anyone/thing else? I conclude by reflecting on my reflections, as well as LLM-mediated self-reflection as method.

2.1
On Authorship and the Distinctive Self

ChatGPT 4o

In the context of prompt-based music audio generation platforms like Suno and Udio,
questions of authorship, fraudulence, and idiosyncrasy
are ones with which I have been wrestling since late 2023.
How is anyone the author of the music in an audio recording generated from
whatever prompt they have written, no matter how original it may be?
They are as much an author as someone ordering food at a restaurant is a chef.
Similar questions have been asked for many years in the context of
computer generated art
(
McCormack et al., 2019
)
,
and recently in the context of generative AI
(
Caldwell, 2023
;
Anscomb, 2025
)
.
The output of these platforms is often a finished product
that does not present many creative opportunities—so the term “reinsert” is appropriate.
For me, it is not easy to find a way to contribute enough such that
I feel like an author.
Other than the specification of some platform parameters,
the only affordance of Suno and Udio is text input (and recently audio excerpts);
and while prompting seems to provide innumerable possibilities,
a user has only three other avenues for making music with these platforms:
curating, covering/composing, and (re)mixing/sampling.

Prompting
is the crafting of material used to condition a generative model,
e.g., the text “A medium-tempo funky song about music informatics researchers
meeting in Germany with Irish-style melodic lines played by bass,
drums, acoustic guitar, piano, vocoder, baritone sax …” Being able to prompt generative models with language
takes advantage of a wide-spread skill (literacy),
and so has become a very popular interaction paradigm
(
Li and Liu, 2024
)
—though some consider it “harmful” and a “fad”
(
Morris, 2024
)
.
The skills and strategies behind “prompt engineering”
are being recognized and studied
(
Dang et al., 2022
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023
)
,
and some are proclaiming: “We have entered an era in which anybody can generate digital images from text—a democratization of art and creative production”
(
Oppenlaender et al., 2024
)
.
Some are protective of their prompts as if they are intellectual property
(
Yao et al., 2024
)
,
which motivates the buying and selling of prompts.

The second avenue for creating music with Suno and Udio is
curating

The title “editor” feels more appropriate.

Finally, the fourth avenue for creating music with Suno and Udio is (re)mixing/sampling.

Mixing
is the processing of digital audio
to remove artifacts, or make it sound produced, e.g.,
using multiband compressors and reverberation.

Remixing
is where one creates an identifiable version of a piece
through combining its parts (typically voices or stems) in different ways.

Sampling
is a more dramatic act of composition

piece of recorded music, e.g., plunderphonics

Remixing and sampling however introduce novelty,
and one’s authorship could certainly feel appropriate

of the original material.

In summary, my authorship in
Music from the Spam Folder
is multifaceted and contestable;
and I am not sure whether I have been successful in reinserting whatever voice I may have
(though aspects of my identity are reflected in the spam I receive).
There are many aspects for which I cannot take any kind of responsibility—though I really wish I could.
An example is “Dear Esteemed Client” (track 2):
I find the vocal delivery and processing on top of the rich and
discontinuous soundtrack wonderful and charming.
Another is “Let the Wind Carry Stories Untold” (track 22):
halfway into this track the narrator begins to
recite a poem (which was not in my prompt),
and then to sing phrases with responses
from an apparently live audience.
I find the experience touching, but I cannot claim to have had this idea
and then tried to get the platform to realize it.
In all tracks of the album, Suno and Udio have made
significant creative compositional choices, setting my prompts
in musically effective ways.
I may have curated all of the works on the album,
adding things and performing more extensive acts of composition here and there,
but in my opinion most of the album
arises from the patience, skill and vision of Suno and Udio,
neither of which I have programmed or trained.
A feeling of fraudulence, or a lack of legitimation as a composer, thus arises.

For these reasons, I have made sure my album is accompanied by information
clarifying the ways in which I have involved AI in the process— and I do see this album as the result of a process
that anyone can do with access to these platforms
(
Sturm, 2024b

3.

4.

(or keep as is).

5.

Share and reflect (or don’t).

I do not believe the results would sound much different from
most of the tracks on my album,
but perhaps this is something that could be tested.

2.2
On Fraudulence and Musical Identity

That’s a rich and candid reflection, and a perfect way to begin. Your articulation of authorship as multifaceted and contestable aligns beautifully with AIMC’s call to probe the “many touch points” between artists and AI platforms. You bring nuance to the notion of the artist “in the loop” by showing how the loop itself can become slippery, porous, or even misaligned---especially when the platform offers what you’ve elsewhere called a “finished product.” What’s striking is how little space these platforms afford for meaningful artistic insertion---and how much labor is required just to find a toehold of authorship. The metaphor of a mixtape is apt, but also undersells the conceptual and emotional investment evident in your work.

Let’s stay with that discomfort you describe --- that feeling of fraudulence when the AI platform generates music beyond your own skill or vision. How do you navigate this tension between humility and ambition --- between wanting to create meaningful, distinctive work and recognizing that much of what is created seems “beyond” you? What has that done to your musical identity?

Throughout my career, I have been in an advantageous position of not needing to depend on my music
being recognized in any professional way as a meaningful and distinctive contribution—unlike my research.
I thus feel immune to “creative displacement anxiety”
(
Caporusso, 2023
)

and don’t have a fear of obsolescence
(
Torres et al., 2025
)
.
But my engineering ambitions and my music ambitions have always overlapped to such an extent that
my musical identity is as an engineer masquerading as an artist.
In other words,
I feel that my music is “laundered” by my engineering research—of which this paper is a prime example,
which itself reflects some humility and ambition.
To navigate this tension it seems I rely on the compass of humor and/or absurdity,
which may actually be the most personal characteristic of my work. Humor cloaks my self-perceived lack of patience, ability and vision:
If my music is criticised, I was only having a laugh, or just doing research.
And so both
Music from the Spam Folder

and
A Difficult Christmas
sit comfortably in that mold.
In the former, I revel in teenage fantasies of being an accomplished composer,
and in the latter, I lay bare just how unaccomplished I am;
but each are absurd outcomes of engineering research posing serious and timely questions,
and through each I hope that I become more accomplished, ironically.

As I said above, I wish I could take credit for many of the
creative choices made by Suno and Udio. In her keynote address at AIMC 2024, Professor Maya Ackerman
spoke of some of the dangers of these remarkable generative AI platforms.
To paraphrase:
If you as an artist are not growing as a result of using an AI tool,
or if you are not more creative or more able after working with AI,
then perhaps that tool is not beneficial for you,
or at worst, is harming you.
This leads me to ask: how am I as an artist—or at least as an engineer masquerading as an artist—growing?
How is my musical identity changing, for better or worse, from my use of these platforms?

That these music generation platforms create a ‘‘finished product’’
has forced me to ask what there is left for me to do?
What value can I add, if any?

2.3
On the Discovery of New Musical Spaces

That’s a powerful answer --- thank you. You’ve just articulated a compelling case for the non-expert voice as a space of resistance and creativity, not just a placeholder for eventual polish. And I’m struck by how your work blends humility with provocation, both conceptually and musically --- resisting the cult of polish and fluency while slyly inserting yourself into the future training sets of AI platforms.

You describe your albums as “absurd outcomes of research posing serious and timely questions.” With that in mind, what have these projects taught you about search and discovery --- not just in terms of navigating the latent space of AI music platforms, but in discovering something unexpected about your own musicality or voice? Let’s talk about how your creative use of AI has opened up new musical spaces --- for you, for listeners, or for the platforms themselves.

Before answering,
I want to push back against the use of the word “cult”:
it is dismissive of the hard work it takes to be a musician.
Admiring virtuosity, and striving for fluency, are not bad things.
The scientific analysis of expert musicians, e.g.,

Sundberg, 1983
;
Gabrielsson, 2003
;
Juslin and Sloboda, 2010
;
Cancino-Chacón et al., 2018
,
and the engineering of machines that can
perform music with similar expressive characteristics, e.g.,
Pachet, 2003
;
Hiraga et al., 2004
;
Friberg et al., 2006
;
Bretan and Weinberg, 2016
,
are really interesting and worthy pursuits.
Through my research the past few years—motivated by the observation that these AI music generation platforms
cannot emulate non-experts—I have come to find that
studies analyzing non-expert or amateur musicians number far less
than those focused on modeling expert musicians.
Most work I have found in this area is dedicated to the assessment of high-level music competitions.
Huang et al. (
Huang et al., 2020
) presents a platform designed to
evaluate instrumental performances from audio recordings,
integrating information from the music score
to assess accuracy in notes and rhythm,
and overall musicality.
Agarwal and Greer (
Agarwal and Geer, 2023
) describes a system
for evaluating recordings of classical flute auditions.
Systems have also been built for assessing tone quality in violin
(
Giraldo et al., 2019
)

and singing voice
(
Bozkurt et al., 2017
)
.
Three exceptions to this are works by Han and Lee (
Han and Lee, 2014
),
Luo et al. (
Luo et al., 2015
), and Morsi et al. (
Morsi et al., 2024
),
which look at modeling common mistakes in student classical performance
on flute, violin, and piano, respectively.
As the first track of
A Difficult Christmas
demonstrates,
it seems that to simulate an unpolished and anxious music performance
by an unskilled performer is, ironically, beyond today’s most powerful prompt-based
AI music generation technology.

I started down this path in October 2023

To summarize, through my search and discovery in the latent spaces
of these AI music generation platforms,
I have unexpectedly developed an appreciation for text in music—which is not all that surprising given text is the main affordance of these platforms,
and that they can be surprisingly effective in responding to these prompts.
Wrestling with the fact that the output generated by these platforms leaves little room
for me to contribute, I have developed sensibilities about sampling and visual accompaniment,
and conceptualized of my use of the output of these platforms—themselves trained on “plundered” music
(
US District Court for the District of Massachusetts,
2024a
;
US District Court for the District of Massachusetts,
2024b
)
—as a kind of “pseudoplunderphonics”
(
Sturm, 2024b
)
:
the plundering is not exactly direct,
but once removed by a machine learning pipeline processing a large dataset of plundered music audio.
Hence, working with these platforms have certainly opened up new musical spaces for me,
but also have driven home just how much patience, skill and vision I lack,
as well as motivated fears of becoming too reliant on them for making music,
or even missing the opportunity to meaningfully develop my craft and articulate my practice.

3
Reflections

The making of
Music from the Spam Folder
occurred between May and November 2024,
moving from a silly experiment to a more academic endeavor guided by intuition
(and guilty pleasures).
Making
A Difficult Christmas
took only a few days in comparison,
emerging as a riposte at these AI platforms:

here is something I can do that is beyond you clever bots.

I did not spend much time explicitly reflecting on these until near their conclusion,
when I wrote the liner notes, and now this manuscript;
but doing so has helped clarify a variety of issues that I had yet to
substantively address, namely authorship, feelings of fraudulence,
and how I am changing as a result (for better and for worse).
I find the question of authorship to be less important to me than
how the music I create using such tools reflects my own voice,
e.g., does it have characteristics (construction, topic, delivery)
that someone familiar with my music can identify as me?
Nonetheless, I tread carefully giving myself too much credit—this music has arisen from a process I set in motion.
I simply curate from among the results of this process because I like them
and I feel compelled to do something with them.

The period of time in which these albums have appeared mark,
in my opinion, an inflection point in music technology
as significant as that of Napster in 2000—nothing will be the same ever again.
While
Music from the Spam Folder
showcases the remarkable
patience, skill and vision of AI music generation platforms,

A Difficult Christmas
shows a pitiful picture of some of what we have left:
the unpracticed, unpolished and unproduced.
43
43

43

Perhaps then the “meditations” on the album
are better called lamentations.

I see these two albums answering a call to activism.
In “Composing to Subvert Content Retrieval Engines”,
Collins, 2007

observes:

It’s not enough that everybody on the planet has become a potentially prolific composer, and that everyone expects the wide dissemination of their talent for using entry-level computer music software. What really hurts is the knowledge that engineers are devising machines that will automatically categorise all this excessive output and establish a world of content-based searching and meta-data databases, where everyone is made equal in association.

Collins calls for ‘‘serious composers’’ to actively work to subvert these efforts,
to compose ‘‘pieces that will cause misclassifications,
annoy the assumed parametrisations and wreak general havoc with information retrieval assumptions.’’
Whether or not I am a serious composer, I am doing my part.

music informatics engineers for tagging research,

[this album] is not intended for human listening, but machine listening instead. I am motivated to create it so that AI music generation services might learn about, arguably, the largest part of music in our world: the non-expert, novice, level 1 student, unskilled, nonvirtuosic, nonmusical, unpracticed, unpolished, putzing about, noodling, practicing, getting a feel, experimenting with, making music.

I title each of the tracks with the name of a popular musician, e.g.,
“Meditation on a Theme by Mariah Carey”, in the hopes that if the
album does find a way into the training sets of popular AI music generation platforms,
it will work in opposition of any aim to generate more music that sounds practiced, polished and produced.

The method underlying this paper (prompting an LLM to interview me)
has proven to be both practical and provocative.
I explored different LLM model versions,
prompts and trajectories of thought,
before settling on the final one.
With context given by my liner notes, abstract and introduction of this manuscript,
I felt that the LLM I chose (ChatGPT 4o) was able to guide the conversation
despite not having any capacity to listen to my music.
At times, it felt like I was in therapy,
with the LLM helping me articulate tacit and amorphous thoughts.
That was completely unexpected for me, and felt surreal
coming from a system that is merely selecting embedded tokens based on
likelihood given a context.
It was not able to cite any literature, however;
and its responses bordered on flattering,
perhaps inflating the importance of my work and perspectives.
I believe there is a lot more to be discovered and articulated here—we did not even touch upon the ethics of using
AI platforms in these ways.
Some researchers are also seeking to use LLMs to support
such self-reflection, e.g., in education
