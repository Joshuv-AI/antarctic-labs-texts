Sox9 regulation of hexokinase 1 controls neuroinflammatory astrocyte subtypes in a rat model of neuropathic pain
Authors: Yonglong Chen, Yu Liao, Huazhang Zhu, Zhaoming Wu, Yutong Wan, Xianglan Feng, Chaoyang Fan, Dengcheng Zhan, Haoyang Cai, Martin Cheung, Chi Wai Cheung, Jessica Aijia Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain, caused by nerve damage, lacks effective treatments. Astrocytes drive chronic neuroinflammation in the dorsal horn, but directly targeting them is challenging due to their heterogeneity and beneficial roles. The regulation of deleterious astrocyte subset emergence in pain is also unknown. Here, we identify distinct astrocyte clusters and elucidate mechanisms by which metabolic regulation of neuroinflammatory astrocyte subsets during pain pathogenesis. We show that the astrocyte specifier Sox9 transcriptionally regulates hexokinase 1 (Hk1), the enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting first step of glycolysis. Nerve damage induces abnormal Sox9 phosphorylation, triggering aberrant Hk1 activation for high-rate astrocytic glycolysis. Moreover, the excessive lactate production from heightened glycolysis remodels histones of gene promoters via lactylation, H3K9la, promoting transcriptional modules of pro-inflammatory and neurotoxic genes, which induce pathogenic astrocyte properties while reducing beneficial populations, ultimately causing NeP. Importantly, targeted modulation of the Sox9-Hk1-H3K9la axis specifically dampens neuroinflammatory astrocyte subsets, promoting long-lasting pain relief. Collectively, our findings unveil an important immunometabolic mechanism and identify potential targets for neuropathic pain treatment.Subject terms: Astrocyte, Neuroimmunology

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NeP) is a debilitating condition caused by a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory system, which is characterized by long-term allodynia and hyperalgesia1. As a prevalent chronic disease affecting around 7–10% of the global population, NeP treatment poses significant challenges due to the lack of effective therapies, the presence of refractory pain, drug tolerance and addiction1. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the etiology and pathogenesis of NeP is essential to develop targeted and efficient therapies.

The spinal cord serves as a relay station for noxious signals, where dorsal horn neurons modulate and ‘gate’ painful signals before transmitting them to higher brain centers. Prolonged neuroinflammation is a key mechanism driving NeP development, which promotes hyperexcitability of dorsal neurons for central sensitization and chronic pain states. Astrocytes are major glial cells in the central nervous system (CNS), performing crucial homeostatic functions to support neurons and detoxify metabolites and peripheral insults. In response to initial nerve damage or noxious stimuli, dorsal spinal astrocytes can undergo “reactive” transition, exhibiting pro-inflammatory and neurotoxic functions that sustain neuroinflammation even after the peripheral injury sites have healed, leading to pathological pain conditions2,3. Spinal astrocytes also possess beneficial effects in modulating immune responses, nerve repairing, and pain resolving/gating3–5, posing challenges for directly targeting astrocytes. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies revealed astrocyte heterogeneity in other neurological disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and multiple sclerosis (MS), offering new molecular insights into therapeutic development6,7. Given the distinct actions of astrocytes in neuropathic pain, an in-depth understanding of astrocyte heterogeneity under physiological and pathological pain conditions, along with the mechanisms controlling deleterious astrocyte populations for NeP development, is therefore critical, and such knowledge is currently lacking.

Here, through a multimodal approach involving metabolomics, single-cell transcriptomics, and epigenetic profiling, combined with functional perturbation studies in vitro and in vivo, we uncovered mechanistic insights into the heterogeneity of dorsal horn astrocytes in NeP. We have identified that the emergence of detrimental astrocyte clusters that promote neuroinflammatory states for pathological pain conditions is induced by heightened glycolysis, which is predominantly driven by aberrant activation of hexokinase 1(Hk1), a critical enzyme catalyzing the first step of glycolysis irreversibly. Notably, we have found that levels of astrocytic glycolysis mediated by Hk1 are intricately linked to altered transcriptional activities of Sox9, a member of the SRY-like HMG-box family crucial for glial differentiation during development8,9. Nerve injury or noxious stimuli trigger phosphorylation of Sox9 at site 181, leading to increased nuclei translocations with abnormally high transcriptional activation of Hk1. Excessive lactate production resulting from high-rate glycolysis remodeled histones of key genes controlling neuroinflammatory astrocyte subtypes through lactylation, ultimately causing profound NeP induction and development. Taken together, our study identified heterogeneous responses of astrocytes during NeP pathogenesis and unraveled the molecular mechanisms that control deleterious astrocytic properties, providing targets for therapeutic intervention for NeP.

Results

ScRNA-seq analysis of dorsal spinal astrocytes in Neuropathic pain

The analysis of heterogeneous immune cells, such as microglia and macrophages, in the spinal cord has provided important insights into pain pathogenesis10–12. However, little is known about astrocyte heterogeneity and its regulation during NeP development. Thus, we used a pre-clinical model to induce NeP condition by spared nerve injury (SNI) in SD rats, which produces NeP behaviors, including mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and cold allodynia, long-lasting over 21 days post-injury (dpi)13 (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig.1a-c). In line with previous findings14,15, our time course analysis demonstrated a gradual increase of reactive astrocytes, marked by Gfap, in the ipsilateral side of the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1d). Examination of astrocyte populations at key transitional timepoints (7 dpi for acute-to-chronic transition and 14 dpi for chronic phase) revealed hypertrophic morphological changes in astrocytes, accompanied by upregulated proinflammatory and neurotoxic genes that promote nociceptive signaling14,15 (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig 1.e). Despite the subtle elevation of a few anti-inflammatory effectors, such as S100a10 and Tgf-β, neuropathic pain cannot be sufficiently resolved (Fig. 1e). Collectively, these results confirmed that dorsal spinal astrocytes acquire reactive phenotypes and deleterious activities during NeP pathogenesis.

Subsequently, we performed ScRNA-seq in the ipsilateral side of lumbar SDH in SNI and Sham on 14 dpi, a critical time point establishing chronification of NeP with stable expression of neuroinflammatory effectors and pain syndromes (Fig. 1f). Based on unsupervised cell clustering, we hierarchically categorized cells into nine principal cell types according to their unique marker expression, visualized by tSNE (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig.2a, b). The cluster frequency analysis revealed an expansion of immune cells in the SNI group, including microglia and astrocytes, reflecting ongoing neuroinflammation in the SDH that induces central sensitization for NeP pathogenesis (Supplementary Fig.2c). By focusing on astrocytes, we reclustered astrocytes following muscat and seurat pipelines16, which identified 5 major subpopulations Astro1-5, indicating multiple transcriptional states of astrocytes during the chronic transition of pain stage (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig.2d). We identified astrocyte clusters 1, 2, and 5 expanded while Astro 3 and 4 reduced during pain development. Astro1 is the most expanded subpopulation during NeP development, exhibiting gene expression patterns associated with pathogenic astrocyte activities in promoting pain, including pro-inflammatory signaling(e.g., NF-κB signaling, Gstm1)17,18, and neurotoxic genes(C3, Cfb)15,19,20(Fig.1i, Supplementary Dataset 1). Astro 2 expressed genes involved in neurodegenerative progression (e.g., Aplp, Qdpr, Edil3, and Hapln2)21–23 and demyelination (e.g., Ptgds and Cryab)21,24–27. Additionally, it also presents as an astrocyte-like NG2 glia subpopulation, potentially exerting neuroprotective functions in response to the injury28. Astro 5, which represents a small population and is defined as early transiting astrocytes, showed a subtle expansion after nerve injury and was enriched with reactive markers (e.g., Gfap, Apoe, Aqp4, and Atp1b2) and glutamate transporters, particularly Slc1a2 (Glt1) (Fig.1i, Supplementary Dataset 1). These observations align with established literature demonstrating that nerve injury induces a dynamic regulation of glutamate transporters in spinal astrocytes - an initial upregulation followed by sustained downregulation2,3. Astro 3 represents a functionally distinct astrocyte subtype that is markedly diminished following nerve injury. Unlike injury-responsive/induced astrocyte populations, Astro 3 exhibits minimal association with pathogenic activities, but is highly enriched for genes involved in tissue homeostasis and repair. They expressed genes for G-protein signaling (e.g., Gng11, Rgs5, and Pth1r)29 and ion channel (Kcnj8)27. Additionally, we also found Astro 3 is highly enriched in genes involved in resolving inflammatory responses (e.g., Igfbp7, TGF-β–dependent IGF-binding protein 7; and Ptn)30 and regulating astrocytes’ plasticity (Gal1 and Pdgfrb), indicating their beneficial roles in modulating the injury niche31–33. Astro 4 is also a small population, expressing proliferative markers(Ccnd1 and Serpine2) that are typically associated with early glial responses to injury, as well as showing enrichment of neurotoxic factor C334–36. This population may represent a transitional population following nerve injury, and its reduction likely reflects the progression of NeP. Taken together, these data revealed the heterogeneity of astrocytes with distinct properties in the dorsal spinal cord during the chronic phase of pain development. Importantly, we also identified detrimental subpopulations, especially Astro 1, which may drive prolonged neuroinflammatory states for central sensitization during NeP pathogenesis.

Pathogenic astrocyte states are highly associated with aberrant glycolytic activation

To investigate the potential mechanisms involved in regulating heterogeneity of astrocytes in pain, in particular deleterious astrocyte clusters, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes(KEGG) analysis. We find a common signaling enrichment, gluconeogenesis/glycolysis, shared among neuroinflammatory astrocyte subclusters Astro 1, early activating Astro 4, and proliferating Astro 5, suggesting a metabolic remodeling or impairment in these subclusters during pain pathogenesis (Fig. 1i). Notably, Astro 1 and 5 subclusters exhibited significantly higher enrichment of both glucose transporter Slc2a1 and Hk1, the first enzyme in catalyzing the irreversible conversion of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, playing a crucial role in controlling glycolytic flux irreversibly (Fig.2a, b). Other glycolytic enzymes were either differentially enriched in Astro5 (Gpi and Pfkp), or uniformly present across subclusters but more prominently in Astro1, 4, or/and 5 (Gapdh, Eno1, and Pkm). Our gene module analysis comparing astrocyte subclusters in SNI versus Sham conditions revealed significant enrichment of Hk1 and neuroinflammatory signaling molecules in the markedly expanded Astro1 subcluster, along with the smaller transitional subclusters Astro 4 and 5 in SNI (Fig. 2c). Immunostaining confirmed elevated expression of Hk1 in dorsal spinal Gfap+ astrocytes (perinuclear) with hypertrophic morphology following nerve injury as compared to Sham (Fig. 2d). Importantly, we detected increased astrocytic Hk1 enzyme activity and glycolytic metabolites by metabolomics in the SNI group. compared to Sham group. In particular, lactate production was dramatically elevated, confirming metabolic abnormality toward heightened astrocytic glycolysis (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig.2). Our time-course analysis of dorsal spinal astrocytes further revealed a positive correlation between gradually elevated glycolytic genes and the activation of pro-inflammatory genes in astrocytes during NeP development from 3 to 21 dpi (Fig. 2g). Collectively, these findings suggest that enhanced glycolytic flux, potentially driven by aberrantly activated Hk1, is highly associated with deleterious astrocyte subclusters that induce neuroinflammation for NeP pathogenesis.

Potentiated Hk1 drives the emergence of astrocyte deleterious clusters in promoting NeP Progression

To investigate the metabolic effects mediated by Hk1 on astrocyte phenotypes and functions during pain development in vivo, we specifically knock down Hk1 in dorsal spinal astrocytes ipsilaterally by intraspinal injection of AAV expressing small hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Hk1 or Scramble driven by the astrocytic-specific GFAP short-promoter (AAV-gfaABC1D-mCherry) (Fig. 3a)37–39. Given the key role of Hk1 in regulating homeostatic glycolysis, an optimized AAV amount was applied to reduce Hk1 to physiological levels in SNI. The successful transduction in the dorsal astrocyte population is confirmed by dorsal expression of mCherry in the ipsilateral side(along L4-L6) co-localized with Gfap+ cells 14 days post-injection. Reduced Hk1 expression ameliorated astrocyte reactivity, as shown by reduced astrocytic hypertrophic morphology and Gfap expression (Fig.3b, c). Hexokinase activity contributes to lower glycolytic gene expression through a feedback mechanism40. Indeed, decreased Hk1 enzyme activity upon knocking down Hk1 in astrocytes resulted in a notable downregulation of glycolytic enzymes and the depletion of glycolytic metabolites, lactate, in astrocytes(Fig.3d, e). Importantly, inhibition of astrocytic Hk1 dramatically reduced expression of pro-inflammatory/neurotoxic genes and reactive markers of astrocytes compared to the scramble control (Fig. 3f). Additionally, we detected an elevation of Tgf-β, which typically modulates neuroinflammation for pain resolution (Fig. 3f)41. Importantly, Hk1 knockdown significantly attenuated NeP symptoms in the ipsilateral hind paw from 14 days post-injection, with long-lasting analgesic effects (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig.3a, b).

We next asked whether ectopically increasing endogenous levels of Hk1 in astrocytes could promote neuroinflammatory responses and induce NeP. To address this issue, we conducted astrocyte-specific overexpression of Hk1 in the ipsilateral side of the SDH in the Sham group, in which AAV particles of Cre recombinase driven by the short GFAP promoter were co-injected with DIO-Hk1-mCherry intraspinally39(Fig.3h). Consistently, overexpression of Hk1 enhanced Hk1 enzyme activities, and increased expression of downstream glycolytic genes and lactate production (Fig. 3j–i). This aberrant activation of Hk1 leads to the induction of reactive astrocyte phenotype in Sham, as evidenced by an increased Gfap population with hypertrophic morphology (Fig. 3l). Further analysis revealed increased neuroinflammatory profiles of FACS-enriched dorsal astrocytes after Hk1 overexpression (Fig. 3m). Despite detecting elevated anti-inflammatory genes (e.g., Il-4) upon Hk1 overexpression, these rats still developed NeP-like behaviors from 10 days post injections, with mechanical allodynia lasting over 21 dpi, indicating the presence of chronic pain features (Fig. 3m,n and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). These data suggest that aberrant activation of Hk1 is required and sufficient to induce NeP through promoting astrocytes’ pathogenic activities.

To establish the pivotal role of Hk1 in regulating astrocyte subpopulations, we performed scRNA-Seq analysis of the ipsilateral SDH with genetic manipulation of Hk1 expression during early chronification stages (14 dpi) in astrocytes. Consistently, the bulk analysis of transcriptome changes in dorsal astrocytes confirmed altered immunoregulatory molecules upon Hk1 knockdown and overexpression, in which Hk1 expression is positively correlated with signaling for cytokines, chemokines and reactivity of astrocytes (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Dataset 2). By combining the datasets from Sham and SNI, we consistently identified 5 subclusters of astrocytes, with Astro 1, 2 and 3 being the prominent clusters. Similarly, Astro 1 emerged progressively in SNI characterized by deleterious populations in promoting neuroinflammation for NeP, expressing a wide range of neurotoxic and pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., S100b, C3, and Cfb) with Hk1 enrichment (Fig. 4c–e, Supplementary Dataset 2). This cluster was also induced upon Hk1 overexpression in Sham but dramatically diminished after Hk1 knockdown, correlating with pain resolution. Additionally, both SNI and Hk1 overexpression have dramatic negative impacts on the Astro 3 population, which is defined as the homeostatic or/and beneficial subgroup. Reduced Hk1 levels in the SNI group partially restored Astro 3 subcluster. Notably, differential Hk1 expression had less influence on the Astro 2 subgroup, which was significantly induced by SNI and highly associated with neurodegenerative signaling, indicating that Hk1 activities have specific effects on distinct astrocyte subgroups32. Collectively, these results revealed the important role of aberrantly high Hk1 activities in driving deleterious subgroups of astrocytes that sustain neuroinflammatory responses in the dorsal horn, contributing to NeP induction and development.

Sox9 transcriptionally controls Hk1 activities that determine pain responses

To elucidate the molecular mechanism driving pathological Hk1 activation specifically in astrocytes, we analyzed differential Hk1 expression alongside heterogeneous astrocytic subclusters from different treatments and identified Sox9 as a prime candidate regulator. SOX9 is a transcriptional factor, playing crucial roles in regulating astrocyte differentiation during development and glial scar formation following injury8. Previous studies have established a potential link between SOX9 and glucose metabolism, revealing its functional overlap with glycolytic enzymes (e.g., HK2, LDHA) in promoting astrocyte specification and glioma/glioblastoma progression through enhanced glycolysis42,43. The transcriptional activity of SOX9 can vary, influencing its functional outcomes, which are often associated with different pathological states44–46. Notably, Sox9 can undergo phosphorylation, in particular at site 181, leading to enhanced transactivation to regulate distinct cellular behaviors and processes47–49. Interestingly, the requirement of phosphorylated Sox947 and enhanced glycolysis for neural crest delamination50 provides a strong association between Sox9 phosphorylation and the metabolic shift to heightened glycolysis. We found that Sox9 exhibited relatively high enrichment in neuroinflammatory clusters Astro 1 and 5, aligning with the expression patterns of Hk1 and other top-enriched deleterious effectors for pain, such as C3, Mt3, Clu, and Cfb (Fig. 2c and Fig. 5a). Despite time course analysis identifying the progressive increase of Hk1 activities during NeP induction and pathogenesis, we did not detect a significant elevation of Sox9 at protein levels (Fig. 5b). However, we observed a gradual increase in phosphorylated Sox9 (p-Sox9) levels in the SDH from 7 dpi, persisting over 21 dpi, which coincided with elevated protein levels of Hk1, neuroinflammatory effectors and NeP responses (Figs. 2g, 5b). Immunofluorescence showed that p-Sox9 was barely detected in astrocytes from the Sham group, whereas it was dramatically induced in the dorsal spinal astrocytes after nerve injury (Fig. 5c, d). Based on this observation, we hypothesized that astrocytic Sox9 is required for the basal level of Hk1 expression, while p-Sox9, which exerts enhanced transcriptional activation, will lead to aberrant activation of Hk1, contributing to pain induction and development. To test this hypothesis, we conducted intraspinal delivery of AAV9-GFAP-shRNA to target Sox9 in the ipsilateral side of the SDH during pain development (Fig. 5e), which effectively reduced aberrantly activated p-Sox9 to levels comparable to those observed in the sham group. The decrease in p-Sox9 in astrocytes from the SNI group resulted in downregulations of both Hk1 and GFAP, leading to diminished Hk1 enzyme activities and reduced production of lactate from glycolysis (Fig. 5f-h). Importantly, Sox9 knockdown in dorsal spinal astrocytes attenuated mechanical allodynia and thermal hypersensitivity in SNI (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Additionally, knocking down Sox9 in naïve rats also diminished Hk1 expression, indicating that Sox9 is required for the basal level of Hk1 expression (Supplementary Fig.5b, c). To investigate whether p-Sox9 contributes to higher Hk1 activities in astrocytes that cause pain pathology, we generated GFAP-AAV-GFP-Sox9 (parental form), GFAP-AAV-GFP-Sox9S181D (constitutively phosphorylated form) and GFAP-AAV-GFP-Sox9S181A (non-phosphorylated form)47 to specifically induce their expression in astrocytes37–39 (Fig. 5j). Constitutive activation of phosphorylated Sox9(Sox9S181D) in Sham dramatically induced Hk1 expression in the SDH, whereas Sox9 overexpression had less impact on Hk1 expression and enzyme activities (Fig. 5k-n). On the other hand, non-phosphorylated Sox9 (Sox9S181A) was insufficient in enhancing the expression levels of Hk1 (Fig. 5k-n). Moreover, both Sox9 and Sox9S181D could increase Gfap astrocyte populations with hypertrophy morphology, with Sox9S181D showing much stronger effects (Fig. 5m, n). Despite both Sox9 and Sox9S181D induced NeP-like behaviors, including mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in Sham, only constitutive activation of p-Sox9 in the dorsal astrocytes led to severe and sustained NeP(Fig. 5o). In contrast, Sox9S181A and the vehicle did not affect pain sensitivity (Fig. 5o). These data suggest the upstream role of Sox9 and its phosphorylation in regulating differential Hk1 expression and activities in astrocytes, which is highly associated with NeP pathogenesis.

Subsequently, we explored the molecular mechanisms by which p-Sox9 drives elevated Hk1 activities in astrocytes during pain development. To further elucidate the effects of pSox9-Hk1-mediated astrocyte activity on neuronal excitability related to pain behaviors, we conducted two-photon Ca²⁺ imaging (EGFP) in live animals in response to altered astrocyte activity51,52. Our results consistently showed that both the SNI group and pSox9 overexpression in the sham group significantly increased neuronal activity, as evidenced by elevated ΔF/F Ca²⁺ traces and high-frequency spike events, which correlated with NeP behaviors (Fig. 6k and l). Conversely, knocking down Sox9 in the SNI group or inhibiting Hk1 in sham animals with pSox9 overexpression dramatically reduced spontaneous Ca²⁺ activity, leading to decreased neuronal excitability and mitigating central sensitization, including spontaneous pain(Fig. 6i–k). These findings confirmed astrocytic Sox9-Hk1 signaling modulating neuronal activity during NeP pathogenesis.

Together, these results illustrate the pivotal role of Sox9 in the direct regulation of Hk1 activities. Phosphorylated Sox9, which exhibits enhanced transcriptional activity, leads to sustained high Hk1 activation under pathological conditions, enhancing glycolytic pathway in astrocytes and contributing to NeP induction and pathogenesis.

p-Sox9 drives pathogenic astrocyte clusters

To further explore the influence of Sox9 on the emergence of detrimental astrocyte subclusters during NeP pathogenesis, we analyzed the heterogeneous astrocyte properties in response to increased Sox9S181D in Sham and Sox9 knockdown in SNI, linking to the Hk1-mediated glycolytic pathway. First, we found that reduced p-Sox9 levels significantly alleviate the hypertrophic Gfap+ populations in SNI and decrease the gene expressions associated with pro-inflammation and neurotoxic and astrocyte reactivity (Figs. 5f, g, 7a). We also detected a significant elevation of an anti-inflammatory gene, TGF-β. In contrast, overexpression of Sox9S181D in Sham induced reactive astrocytes in the dorsal spinal cord along with a set of genes encoding for pathogenic activities during pain pathogenesis (Figs. 5l, m, 7b). By combining the ScRNA datasets from Sham and SNI, we revealed five astrocyte subclusters similar to those reported above (Fig. 7c). Similarly to SNI or Sham treated with Hk1 overexpression (Sham+Hk1OE), increased Sox9S181D reduced homeostatic and inflammatory resolving astrocytes subcluster, Astro 3, while induced neuroinflammatory astrocytes subset (Astro1) with Hk1 enrichment, which express genes associated with pain signaling (e.g., C3, Cfb, Clu, CxCl2). Notably, Sox9 activities also influenced Astro 2, defined as a neurodegenerative-associated subcluster. Increased p-Sox9 significantly expanded Astro 2 populations, indicating the border detrimental effects of p-Sox9 in promoting injury-responsive astrocytes subclusters than Hk1. Conversely, Sox9 knockdown caused a major contraction of Astro 1 and the depletion of Astro 5, confirming a suspended reactivating process and formation of neuroinflammatory properties (Fig. 7c,d). Subsequent trajectory inference analysis clearly shows that the hierarchy of astrocytic phenotypes resulted from homeostatic/inflammatory resolving cluster (Astro 3, expressing Kcni8 and Cox4i2) to intermediate stage including transitioning Astro 4 (Proliferating) or early reactivating Astro 5 (transiently upregulated glutamate transmitter in pain), then to more terminally neuroinflammatory Astro1 or neurodegenerative/demyelinated Astro 2 (e.g., S100b, Cryab, and Aplp1) (Fig. 7e,f), indicating the differential pathway in forming deleterious astrocytes clusters during pain development. Maximal differences in Sox9, glycolytic genes, and genes for pathogenic astrocyte activities in promoting nociceptive signaling were observed mostly in Astro 1 (Fig. 7f). Collectively, these data indicated that phosphorylation of Sox9 drives the formation of detrimental astrocyte subclusters and is important for Hk1-mediated pathogenic astrocytes in pain.

The catalytic activity of Hk1 induced by p-Sox9 drives histone lactylation for genes controlling neuroinflammatory astrocyte properties

Next, we asked how enhanced glycolysis driven by p-Sox9-Hk1 promotes detrimental astrocyte properties. Histone lactylation was recently identified as an epigenetic modification that is controlled by the amount of lactate content in cells53. Similar to histone acetylation, histone lactylation can directly stimulate gene transcriptions and alter the cellular status and functions, such as controlling the pro- or anti-inflammatory status of macrophages/microglia54,55. The increased production of lactate in detrimental astrocytes prompted us to examine whether lactylation could affect gene expression inducing astrocyte pathogenic activities under pain conditions. Indeed, western blotting analysis of acid-extracted histones showed an increase in the levels of Pan-lysine lactylation (Pan Kla) after injury, which is abolished by glycolytic inhibitor, 2-DG, delivered via intrathecal injection (Fig. 8a). To examine specific changes in histone lactylation in astrocytes that are associated with altered glycolysis, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining of H3K18la, H3K9la and H3K14la along with Gfap53. All of them can be detected in spinal astrocytes homeostatically and were significantly activated in astrocytes upon injury, with C3 elevation and responsive to glycolytic inhibition (Fig. 8b,c and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Further investigation into histone lactylation revealed that Hk1 or p-Sox9 activation predominantly contributes to H3K9la expression, with minor effects on H3K9la or H3K14la in astrocytes. Moreover, knocking down Hk1 in Sox9S181D expressed astrocytes abolished H3K9la elevation (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Fig. 6c,d). These data suggest that H3K9la is the most prevalent differentially affected histone lactylation modification in the dorsal astrocytes, controlled by the p-Sox9-Hk1-lactate axis.

To investigate the functional link of H3K9la with detrimental astrocyte properties in promoting NeP, we performed genome-wide CUT&Tag analysis using antibodies against H3K9la to identify candidate genes regulated by H3K9la in Sham and SNI. Analysis of k-means clustering partitioned the dataset into four distinct clusters, revealing obvious enrichment of H3K9la peaks in clusters 1 and 2, which predominantly bind within 1.5 kb of transcription start sites (TSS), spanning 17.06% promoter regions (Fig. 8e,f). Gene ontology biological process (GOBP) terms and KEGG pathways of higher H3K9la binding peaks at the gene promoter showed that these genes are enriched in cytokine/chemokines and signaling pathways for NF-kB, JAK-STAT and PI3K-AKT, which are involved in regulating pro-inflammation and glial activation (Fig. 8g and Supplementary Fig.7a). Specifically, we found that increased H3K9la peaks at the promoters of key genes that promote astrocytes’ deleterious activities and neuroinflammatory phenotypes in SNI (Fig. 8h and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Consistently, a quantitative chromatin immunoprecipitation analysis indicated that the H3K9la levels on Gfap, C3, and Cfb promoters are significantly elevated in astrocytes from SNI and overexpressions of Hk1 and Sox9S181D rats compared with Vector control or Sham. Furthermore, H3K9la enrichment in the promoter regions can be abolished upon Hk1 knockdown in astrocytes (Fig. 8i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that p-Sox9-Hk1 drives astrocytic histone lactylation, which activates key genes regulating deleterious astrocyte properties through H3K9la modifications, contributing to NeP pathology.

Discussion

Astrocytes are central players in a myriad of processes in the healthy and diseased CNS, ranging from metabolism to immunity and degeneration. The analysis of the heterogeneity of microglia and macrophages has provided important insights into immune responses in pain pathogenesis. Despite astrocytes having been identified as a key driver for pro-longed neuroinflammation in the dorsal spinal cord, little is known about astrocyte heterogeneity and its regulation in NeP development. Our study identified differential astrocyte subclusters under physiological and pathological pain conditions and uncovered a key phosphorylated Sox9-HK1-H3K19la signaling axis that controls the emergence of deleterious astrocyte subsets, which promote neuroinflammatory responses, leading to pain induction and development.

Identifying astrocyte subtypes with distinct functions in pain pathologies is a critical step toward better therapeutic strategies, which involves targeting detrimental clusters while preserving properties in the pro-resolution and neural repair phase for treating NeP. Our findings revealed that glycolysis as a common GO term enriched in the detrimental astrocyte cluster Astro 1, as well as in two transiting/proliferating subclusters, Astro 4 and 5. The large glycolytic capacity of the CNS is primarily attributed to astrocytes, crucial for supporting neuronal activities and metabolism under homeostatic conditions. In response to inflammatory stimuli, astrocytes could increase their rate of glycolysis to prevent ATP depletion and cell death in vitro56,57. Enhanced astrocytic glycolysis, beneficial in certain neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease, can improve cognition by supporting neuronal survival and axon growth in the brain58,59. In multiple sclerosis, decreased HK enzymatic activity in astrocytes impaired glycolysis, leading to reduced production of lactate, which promotes neuronal degeneration60. However, prolonged high astrocytic glycolysis induced by increased HK enzymatic activity is harmful in pain pathology. Notably, we found that Hk1 is enriched in Astro1 and aberrantly activated from the early stage of pain induction, persisting over chronic phases. The heightened glycolysis induced by Hk1 exerts specific effects in controlling the emergence of the detrimental Astro 1 cluster, driving immunopathogenic activities in the dorsal horn and ultimately contributing to the development of NeP. The finding aligns with a recent study demonstrating PTG−/− mouse inflammatory pain model reduces glycogen accumulation in astrocytes, leading to decreased pain-related behaviors and facilitating a quicker recovery. Additionally, this model showcases a reduced glycolytic capacity as compared with the WT dorsal spinal cord network61.

Our studies also identified an important regulatory link between the astrocyte specifier, Sox9 and glycolysis, potentially explaining differential metabolic programs adopted by astrocytes and neurons during development and homeostasis. Sox9 belongs to a member of the SRY-like HMG-box family, which functions as a key transcriptional regulator for the specification of astrocytes, neural crest and chondrocytes during development9,62. In adults, Sox9 is specifically expressed in naïve astrocytes and upregulated in reactive astrocytes following brain and spinal cord injury8,63. Previously, we and others found that Sox9 can undergo phosphorylation, leading to distinct effects on cellular behavior and activities47. Here, we found that Sox9 maintains basal Hk1 levels in naïve animals, while nerve injury triggers Sox9 phosphorylation that enhances its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity. This post-translational modification leads to pathological Hk1 upregulation, promoting aberrant glycolytic flux in astrocytes. Phosphorylated Sox9 not only contributes to the emergence of the Hk1-mediated neuroinflammatory astrocyte cluster 1, but also induces Astro 2, which represents a property with neurodegenerative and demyelinating nature. It is plausible that the phosphorylation of Sox9 may trigger different downstream signaling pathways depending on the cellular context, resulting in the development of distinct astrocyte subtypes. Notably, the intimate link between transcriptional regulation of Sox9 and glycolytic changes has also been implicated in a few studies. For example, Sox9-haploinsufficient mice exhibit metabolic abnormalities with impaired glucose tolerance64. The ectopic expression of two key glycolytic enzymes, HK2 and LDHA, has been shown to promote astrocyte differentiation from human neural stem cells via enhanced aerobic glycolysis42, resembling the functions of Sox9. It is interesting to note that phosphorylated Sox9 is required to regulate neural crest delamination47, with recent research from Bhattacharya et al indicating that delaminating neural crest cells exhibit heightened glycolytic activities to facilitate cell migration50, revealing a strong link between phosphorylation of Sox9 and increased cellular glycolytic activity. The interaction between Sox9 and hexokinase mediated glycolytic activities may represent a common regulatory axis in diverse cellular contexts in health or disease. The upstream regulation of Sox9 phosphorylation during pain pathogenesis may involve the activity of one or more protein kinases. For example, the cyclic adenosine monophosphate-protein kinase A (cAMP-PKA), highly involved in maintaining neuropathic pain65, has previously been shown to phosphorylate Sox9 during neural crest development. Other kinases, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and PI3K/Akt pathways, which are known to regulate reactive astrocytes in chronic pain2, have also been associated with Sox9 phosphorylation or expression in cancer66. Thus, involvement of posttranslational modification in pain pathogenesis also outlines the potential of targeting specific kinases as a possible therapeutic in the future.

Finally, we uncovered the mechanism underlying altered energy metabolism and epigenetic regulation in pain pathogenesis, which through H3K9la induction at the promoter regions of neuroinflammatory-regulatory genes for inducing specific types of deleterious astrocytes in NeP. Differential levels of metabolites, such as acetyl-CoA, ATP, and lactate can serve as substrates for histone modifications that directly stimulate gene transcription from chromatin. Recently, histone lactylation has emerged as an essential epigenetic alteration mediated by cellular lactate levels53. A few studies have highlighted how accumulating such substrates significantly impacts immune cell functions, such as microglia and macrophages, potentially exacerbating neurodegenerative progression54,55. Despite pain status being highly associated with metabolic diseases, diabetes, excess glucose intake, and increased lactate67–69, the link and molecular understanding of such knowledge are less documented. In our study, we found that heightened glycolysis in SNI facilitated lactate production with less influence on TCA cycles, resulting in elevated histone lactylations, including H3K18la, H3K9la, and H3K14la modifications, in astrocytes. The p-Sox9-Hk1-lactate axis mainly contributes to H3K9la inductions in dorsal spinal astrocytes, with less effect on H3K18la and H3K14la. Analysis using Cut& Tag further indicates that H3K9la is highly enriched at the promoters of key genes responsible for inducing detrimental astrocyte phenotypes and regulating pathogenic activities, thereby leading to a sustained neuroinflammatory state in NeP. Additionally, our results imply that the complexity of histone lactylation may exist in astrocytes, possibly involving other increased histone lactylation marks, such as H3K18la and H3K14la, which may implicate distinct regulators or different subtypes of astrocytes, warranting further investigation. Our study offers valuable insights to aid future epigenomic studies on pain pathogenesis.

In summary, we have revealed that phosphorylated Sox9 induces aberrantly high Hk1 activities that affect gene expression via histone lactylation in astrocytes. This regulatory mechanism promotes detrimental astrocyte properties in the dorsal horn, thereby contributing to NeP development. These findings may guide new therapeutic approaches for the modulation of astrocyte pathogenic activities in other neurologic disorders.

While our research uncovers the emergence of neuroinflammatory astrocyte subtypes mediated by p-Sox9-HK1, we have also observed the development of another potentially detrimental astrocyte subset, Astro 2, during NeP progression. Further investigations are warranted to explore the functional roles of this subset, which will provide deeper insights into the mechanisms of astrocyte heterogeneity in the initiation and maintenance of NeP. Another limitation is that we found SNI-induced other forms of lactylation, specifically H3K18la and H3K14la, in astrocytes. Further research efforts may reveal previously unidentified genes or mechanisms associated with the regulation of these histone lactylations.

Methods

Animal

Male and female adult Sprague-Dawley rats (Rattus norvegicus) aged between 4 to 6 weeks and weighed approximately 230 g were sourced from the Centre for Comparative Medicine Research(CCMR) at the University of Hong Kong(accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International). All experimental and handling protocols were conducted with the prior approval of the Faculty Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research (CULATR), with reference numbers 5457-20 and 5859-21. The rats were maintained in a temperature-controlled environment at 25 °C with a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle, housed and used following the guidance of CCMR. They are free to access the food and water. Before any procedures, all rats were randomly assigned to their respective groups and allowed to acclimate in new cages for 2 to 3 days.

Establishment of nerve injury model

The injury model of spared nerve injury (SNI), originally described by Decosterd and Woolf in 200013, is utilized to mimic human NeP associated with peripheral nerve injury. This model can induce early ( < 24 h) and long-lasting ( > 6 months) neuropathic pain-like behaviours (e.g., allodynia and hyperalgesia)13. In this laboratory animal model, animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of Ketamine (75–100 mg/kg) and Xylazine (10 mg/kg) prior to surgery. An incision was made in the skin on the lateral surface of the left thigh of the rat, exposing the terminal branches of the sciatic nerves—specifically, the sural, common peroneal, and tibial nerves-by dissecting the biceps femoris muscle. The sural, peroneal and tibial nerves were tightly ligated with 4-0 silk at the trifurcation point, then severed distally to the knot, with the distal nerve ends trimmed ~3–5 mm. The sural nerve remained untouched during the surgical procedure. In the SHAM-operated rats, the sciatic nerve was neither ligated nor cut.

Intraspinal virus injections

The virus was delivered into the dorsal spinal cord as previously described70,71. Rats are subjected to pre-operative care, including subcutaneous administration of Meloxicam (1 mg/kg) and Buprenorphine (0.03 to 0.05 mg/kg) a day before the surgery. All surgical procedures are performed under anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine(80 mg/kg) and xylazine(10 mg/kg), supplemented with local application of lidocaine hydrochloride (dilute to 0.5%, 5 mg/kg) to reduce the pain. The surgical site was prepared by first removing lumbar spine hair using depilatory cream and shavers, followed by sequential scrubs with iodophor and alcohol. The area was allowed to air dry for 30 s before proceeding. A midline skin incision exposed the T12-L1 vertebrae, after which a precise intervertebral approach was performed. Muscles were carefully dissected along the left lumbar vertebrae to visualize the spinal cord between the T12-T13 intervertebral spaces. A KDS Legato 130 Syringe Pump (RWD) with a 10 μL Hamilton microinjection syringe(33-gauge), equipped on the stereotaxic apparatus, is used to deliver the AAV2/9 virus(Supplementary Dataset 3). The viral titer ( ~ 2.5 × 10¹² vg/mL) was optimized into high-dose (5 μL) and low-dose (2.5 μL) groups, based on previous studies70,72, and the low dose was applied to achieve moderate gene manipulations within the dorsal spinal cord along L4-L6. The injection rate of 0.2 μL/min, and the microinjection syringe was left in place for 5 min before being gently withdrawn. After injection, the muscles on the spine were sutured with 4-0 absorbable suture. Baytril (Enrofloxaim, 6.6 mg/kg, intramuscular) was administered immediately post-surgery and continued daily for 3 days. The analgesic, buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) is delivered post-surgery every 12 h for 3 days, followed by daily injection of Meloxicam (1 mg/kg) for 7 days.

Intrathecal drug delivery

The intrathecal drug delivery protocol was adapted from established methods70,71. Surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia induced with 5% isoflurane and maintained at 2% throughout the operation. Following shaving and sterilization of the surgical site with povidone-iodine, injections were targeted to the lumbar enlargement (approximating L3-L4 spinal segments) using a 25-gauge beveled needle inserted at a 30° angle to the vertebral column. Successful entry into the intrathecal space was confirmed by observation of a characteristic tail-flick reflex at either the tail base or tip. Saline vehicle or 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; Sigma-Aldrich, D8375) was administered every three days.

In vivo calcium imaging for dorsal horn neurons

For imaging, rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine(10 mg/kg), with depth of anesthesia monitored via pedal reflex. A laminectomy was performed to remove the vertebral bone overlying the AAV injection site, exposing a ~ 10 × 5 mm region of the dorsal spinal cord. The dura mater was kept intact to minimize tissue trauma. A custom-cut rectangular quartz coverslip (thickness: 0.17 mm) was positioned over the exposed spinal cord, with a thin layer of iodixanol (60% w/v, OptiPrep™) applied as an optical immersion medium to match refractive indices. The coverslip was secured using dental cement (Metabond, Parkell) reinforced with cyanoacrylate adhesive, ensuring a stable optical window while preventing cerebrospinal fluid leakage.GCaMP6f fluorescence was acquired using a Bergamo II multiphoton microscope (Thorlabs) equipped with a 16× water-immersion objective (Nikon, NA 0.80), as well as Ti:Sapphire laser (Mai Tai DeepSee, Spectra-Physics) tuned to 920 nm for excitation. Image acquisition (512 × 512 pixels, 2 min duration) was controlled by ThorImage®LS 4.0 software, emitted light was filtered through a 525/50 nm bandpass filter and captured with a GaAsP photomultiplier tube (PMT). Following motion correction (TurboReg) and background subtraction, neuronal ROIs were identified via CNMF-E or manual tracing in ImageJ. Calcium transients were detected when ΔF/F₀ (F₀: 10th percentile baseline) exceeded 3×SD of noise, with kinetics (amplitude, frequency, rise/decay tau) extracted using Savitzky-Golay filtering. Data were z-scored per animal and compared via ANOVA/Tukey’s tests.

Von Frey test for mechanical allodynia

Mechanical pain sensitivity was evaluated using blinded behavioral testing as previously described73. Animals were housed under standardized conditions and randomly assigned to treatment groups before testing. The testing apparatus consisted of a transparent plastic containment chamber (70 × 24 × 15 cm) placed on an elevated wire mesh platform (70 × 36.5 × 42 cm), using calibrated Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments (RWD Aesthesio series) for stimulation. Rats were first acclimated to the testing environment for 30 min before assessment. Throughout the examination, plastic filaments are applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the hind paw with constant pressure. Testing was conducted employing a set of calibrated Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments (commonly called Von-Frey Hairs) using the Up-Down method73, beginning with a 4.0 g filament (RWD, Aesthesio). The 50% paw withdraw threshold (PWT) was assessed for each rat on the left hind paw. Every filament was gently placed on the hind paw’s plantar surface for 5 s or until a reaction like abrupt withdrawal, shaking, or limb licking occurred. Instances of rearing or regular ambulation during filament application were disregarded. Filaments were used at intervals of five min until thresholds were established. Each measurement was conducted twice per rat, and the average value was considered the final PWT value.

Plantar heat test (Hargreaves)

To assess heat-thermal hyperalgesia, the ipsilateral hind paw of the animal was measured using a thermal stimulus apparatus (Ugo Basile, Varese, Italy). The beam intensity was calibrated and set to a baseline latency of 16 s before the test. For each paw, the withdrawal latency was determined by averaging five separate measurements. The hind paws were tested alternately, with a minimum 5 min interval between stimuli applied to the same paw to prevent sensitization. Responses were only be considered a valid withdrawal if it consisted of a swift, distinct movement of the hind paw away from the stimulus, which was sometimes accompanied by licking. Movements related to locomotion or weight shifting were not classified as a response, and the trial was repeated.

Two-temperature preference (TTP) analysis

Thermal preference behavior was assessed using a dual-plate system (BIO-CHP-ER, RWD Limited) consisting of two adjacent 165 × 165 mm thermal plates enclosed within a unified chamber. Plate temperatures were precisely maintained ( ± 0.1 °C) using SMART 3.0 software (PanLab), with thermal preference quantified over 6-minute test sessions via video tracking (Bioseb automated system) as previously described74. In brief, the reference plate was maintained at 30 °C (thermoneutral zone for rats) for all experiments. For heat sensitivity assessment, the test plate was heated to 45 °C, while cold sensitivity was evaluated with the test plate cooled to 20 °C. Temperature assignments were counterbalanced across trials to control for positional bias. Animals were gently positioned straddling the plate junction at session initiation, allowing free movement between plates. Each session was video-recorded for offline analysis of: (1) total time spent on each plate, (2) number of plate transitions, and (3) latency to first transition. The apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate odor cues. To minimize novelty stress, rats underwent habituation via two 5-minute daily sessions on equilibrated plates (both 30 °C). After this familiarization period, rats were expected to spend a similar duration on each plate during a 6-minute session, ideally between 300 and 360 s. Rats deviating significantly in time spent on either plate, making only one or no transitions, were excluded. To mitigate learning effects, the reference and test plate temperatures were switched for subsequent trials. Each rat was tested twice per parameter set, with re-habituation between trials. Video analysis via an automated tracking system (Bioseb) quantified the percentage of time spent on each plate75.

Acetone test

Cold allodynia was evaluated using the established acetone evaporation test76. Rats were individually placed on an elevated metal mesh floor within a transparent testing chamber and allowed to acclimate for 30 min. A 50 μL drop of HPLC-grade acetone was then gently applied to the plantar surface of the left hind paw using a blunt syringe, ensuring no mechanical stimulation. Behavioral responses were recorded and scored using a validated scale: 0 indicated no response; 1 represented a brief paw flick ( < 1 s); 2 denoted sustained paw elevation ( > 1 s); and 3 reflected vigorous responses such as licking, biting, or prolonged flicking. Each rat underwent three trials at 15 min intervals, with the highest score per trial recorded. The final score was calculated as the average of the three trials.

Spontaneous nociceptive responses

Prior to behavioral testing, each rat was acclimated to the experimenter through 10-minute daily handling sessions over three consecutive days. The experimenters were unaware of the animals’ group assignments. Spontaneous nociceptive responses were assessed at 14 days following surgery or AAV injection. The rats were placed in a clear plastic enclosure (60 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) and filmed for 60 min. The recorded videos were analyzed in slow motion to tally episodes of licking and flinching/shaking/lifting of the affected hind paw. Movements unrelated to nociception, such as exploration, locomotion, repositioning, and grooming, were disregarded. Each licking bout was scored as 2 points, while each flinching/shaking/lifting bout was assigned 1 point. The cumulative score from the 1 h recording period served as the spontaneous pain score75.

BBB test for locomotor activity

Rats that underwent SNI or AAV injection were randomized to perform the behavioral studies at 14 days following surgery or AAV injection. The BBB(Basso, Beattie, Bresnahan) open-field 21-point locomotion rating scale was used in the assessments of rats conducted by two independent observers77.

Protein extracts, SDS-PAGE, and western blotting

Tissue samples from the L4–L6 left dorsal spinal cord were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #87788) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #78440) using a glass homogenizer (200 μL buffer per sample). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. For SDS-PAGE, protein samples (30 μg) were mixed with 2× Laemmli buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 8 min, and resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels alongside prestained molecular weight markers (Bio-Rad, #161-0374). Electrophoresis was performed at 100 V for 20 min in running buffer (25 mM Tris, 190 mM glycine, 0.1% SDS, pH 8.3), followed by 120 V for 1 h until complete separation. For protein transfer, PVDF membranes were activated in methanol for 2 min and equilibrated in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 190 mM glycine, 20% methanol, pH 8.3). Proteins were transferred at 100 V for 1.5 h at 4 °C. For immunoblotting, membranes were blocked in 4% nonfat milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies(Supplementary Dataset 3) diluted in blocking buffer. After three 10 min washes with TBST (TBS + 0.1% Tween-20), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad, #1705062) and imaged using a ChemiDoc imaging system.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following euthanasia via isoflurane overdose (5%) and CO₂ asphyxiation, rats were transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.9% saline (100-200 mL) to clear blood, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4, 100–200 mL) for fixation. The L4–L6 spinal cord segments were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (in 0.1 M PB) until tissue sank. Samples were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned at 20 µm thickness using a cryostat. For immunostaining, sections were first. permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS (30 min), blocked in Blocking Buffer (1% BSA into PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500–1:1000) for 2 h at RT and protected from light, counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, 5 min), and mounted with Mounting medium. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800 or Nikon AXR), and quantification was conducted in ImageJ/Fiji with background subtraction, thresholding, and particle analysis for the objective.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from sorted GFAP-positive cells using an RNA extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM1931). Briefly, cells were centrifuged at 750 g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain a pellet following FACS and resuspended by vigorous vortexing in 100 μL of Lysis Solution and then incubated on ice for 5 min. Next, 50 μL of 100% ethanol was added to the lysate and mixed by brief vortexing. The lysate/ethanol mixture was loaded onto a Micro Filter Cartridge Assembly, and RNA was bound to the filter by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 s. After a series of washing steps, RNA was eluted with 10 μL of Elution Buffer preheated to 75 °C. The extracted total RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with a RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, RR036). RT-qPCR was subsequently performed using SYBR-Green qPCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, RR420) on a LightCycler 480 System. Gene expression was normalized to reference genes and is presented as the relative fold change compared to control samples. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate. All RT-qPCR primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Dataset 3.

Plasmid construction

The plasmids constructed for the luciferase assay were referred to the previous protocol78. Briefly, the segments of the HK1 promoter region were amplified from rat genomic DNA. The HK1 promoter segments were cloned into the PGL3_basic plasmid (Promega, Cat# E1751) using restriction enzymes Xho I and Hind III. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Dataset 3.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for GFAP+ astrocyte isolation

Following transcardial perfusion with 0.9% sodium chloride (Sigma, S7653), L4-L6 dorsal spinal cord tissue was rapidly dissected on ice and minced into <1 mm³ pieces using a sterile scalpel. Tissue fragments were transferred to 15 ml Falcon tubes containing 3 ml of 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Sigma, T4049) supplemented with 30 U DNase I (Roche, 10104159001) and incubated at 37 °C for 15–20 min with gentle mechanical dissociation every 5 min using a 200 μl pipette. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed with 5 ml ice-cold HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 24020117). The filtrate was centrifuged at 450 g (4 °C, 5 min), and the pellet was washed once with 2 ml HBSS (750 g, 4 °C, 5 min). Cells were then resuspended in 1 ml 30% Percoll solution (GE Healthcare, 45-001-748; diluted in 10× HBSS) and centrifuged at 800 g (4 °C, 15 min) for myelin removal. The pellet was collected and washed in FACS buffer (1× PBS with 0.4% BSA or PBS/0.5% BSA/2 mM EDTA/20 mM glucose). Cells were incubated with APC-conjugated anti-GFAP antibody (1:50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 50-9892-82) in 300 μl FACS buffer for 1 h on ice with gentle rotation. After washing, cells were stained with DAPI (1 μg/ml final concentration; Sigma, D9542) for 8 min on ice for live/dead discrimination. GFAP+ cells were sorted using a BD FACSAria Fusion cell sorter (100 μm nozzle, 20 psi sheath pressure) into FACS tubes containing 0.5 ml collection buffer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Cell fraction extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractions were isolated from L4-L6 spinal dorsal horn in the SNI or sham groups using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #78833) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. Briefly, freshly dissected SDH tissues were homogenized in ice-cold CER I buffer (100 mg tissue/ml buffer) using a Dounce homogenizer (15 strokes with tight pestle). The homogenate was vortexed vigorously for 15 s and incubated on ice for 10 min to ensure complete cell membrane lysis. Following addition of CER II buffer (55 μl per 1 ml CER I used), samples were vortexed briefly and incubated on ice for 1 minute to solubilize cytoplasmic components. The lysate was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to separate the cytoplasmic fraction (supernatant) from membrane/organelle components (pellet). The insoluble pellet was then resuspended in ice-cold NER buffer (50 μl per 100 mg original tissue weight) and vortexed every 10 min during a 40 min incubation on ice to extract nuclear proteins. After final centrifugation (16,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), the nuclear fraction (supernatant) was collected.

Dual luciferase reporter gene assay

The luciferase assay was performed as previously described79. Briefly, treated hiPSC-derived astrocytes were harvested and lysed using the kit’s cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, RG027). The lysate supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. After all buffers were equilibrated to room temperature, samples were combined with the firefly luciferase assay reagent, and relative light units (RLU) were measured at 560 nm using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG LABTECH). Subsequently, the Renilla luciferase assay working buffer was added; this quenches the firefly luminescence and simultaneously activates the Renilla reaction, the signal of which was measured at 465 nm. The ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity was calculated to normalize for variations in cell number or transfection efficiency.

Targeted metabolomics

L4–6 spinal dorsal horn (SDH) tissue was harvested on day 7 following spared nerve injury (SNI), with sham-operated animals serving as controls. Tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were then ground into a fine powder using a Dounce homogenizer under liquid nitrogen to maintain frozen conditions. For each treatment condition, 35 mg of tissue powder was submitted to the HKU Proteomics and Metabolomics Core for targeted analysis of metabolites involved in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). A minimum of three biological replicates were performed per treatment group, and error bars represent the standard deviation or standard error of the mean.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (CHIP-qPCR)

L4-L6 spinal dorsal horn (SDH) tissues were dissociated into single cells using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-092-628) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min with gentle agitation, followed by quenching with 125 mM glycine for 5 min. After two washes with ice-cold PBS containing protease inhibitors, nuclear fractions were isolated using membrane lysis buffer (Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #26157). Chromatin was sheared to 200–700 bp fragments using a Bioruptor Pico Ultrasonication System (Diagenode) with 30 s pulses at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, 10% of the supernatant was reserved as input control, while the remaining 90% was subjected to immunoprecipitation overnight at 4 °C with 5 μg of either anti-SOX9 (Merck Millipore, AB5535), anti-phospho-SOX9 (Abcam, ab59252), or normal rabbit IgG (negative control) antibodies. Protein-DNA complexes were captured using 20 μL of magnetic beads. Precipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green master mix and specific primers targeting the HK1 promoter regions containing predicted SOX9 binding motifs. Enrichment was calculated relative to input controls using the ΔΔCt method, with IgG controls establishing baseline signals.

Dead cell removal and cell preparation for single-cell RNA sequencing

Sample preparation for scRNA-seq followed a previously established protocol16. Animals were perfused with 0.9% saline, and the left L4–6 spinal dorsal horn was dissected and placed in ice-cold Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #14175095). Tissue was minced into small fragments using a sterile scalpel. modified papain-based dissociation protocol was employed to generate a single-cell suspension (Miltenyi Biotec Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit, #130-092-628). Specifically, tissue was transferred to a 15 mL Falcon tube containing 2 mL digestion solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10–15 min. The suspension was triturated gently every 5 min using a 200 µL pipette. The dissociated cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer and washed with 5 mL HBSS (with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #24020117). The filtrate was centrifuged at 450 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of 30% Percoll solution (GE Healthcare, #45-001-748) to remove debris and dead cells. The 30% Percoll solution was prepared as follows: 100% Percoll was made by combining 9 parts Percoll with 1 part 10× HBSS (Sigma-Aldrich, #H4385); this was then diluted to 30% with 1× HBSS. After centrifugation at 800 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the pellet was collected and resuspended in 0.5 mL HBSS buffer (1× HBSS + 0.4% BSA). Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, #420301) for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed and resuspended in HBSS buffer. All subsequent steps were performed on ice. Following dead cell removal via FACS (DAPI-negative cells retained), cell concentration and viability were assessed using an automated cell counter (Countess, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #AMQAX1000) and trypan blue staining (Gibco, #15250061). Only samples with >70% viability were used for scRNA-seq. Libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Gene Expression Kit (v3, 10X Genomics) targeting ~10,000 viable cells per sample. Library construction, quality control, and sequencing were performed by the Centre for Panoromic Sciences (CPOS, HKU). Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (count and multi pipelines). Downstream analyses—including graph-based clustering, differential expression, and visualization—were conducted in R using the Seurat package.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and quality-control filtering

Single cells in each sample were encapsulated into droplet emulsions and converted to barcoded single-cell cDNA libraries using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ V2 Library, Gel Bead, Chip, and Multiplex Kit (10x Genomics), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The isolated single cells were then loaded in each channel, targeting for a total of 10,000 cells per library. Single-cell libraries were then sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina). The sequenced reads were then mapped to the rat genome (NCBI Rnor6.0) using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger (v2.1.1) pipeline to generate the filtered gene-barcode matrix containing valid cell barcodes and transcript unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts. For each gene and each cell barcode, UMIs were counted to construct digital expression matrices, which were filtered a second time using Seurat software. Genes found in fewer than three cells and cells with <200 genes having nonzero counts were excluded from the analysis. We also removed cells with total UMI counts >7,500 or the number of detected genes was >2,500 in order to exclude possible multiple captures, a key problem in microdroplet-based tests. We further eliminated low-quality cells where more than 4% of the counts belonged to mitochondrial genes after visually examining the distribution of cells by the fraction of expressed mitochondrial genes. These QC standards were used, and 49,571 single cells were used in the subsequent investigations. To get the normalized count, the filtered matrix was subjected to library size normalization in Seurat.

Multiple scRNA-seq dataset integration and t-SNE analysis

Remaining single cells from different scRNA-seq datasets across different experimental conditions were combined into an integrated, unbatched dataset using the Seurat software (v.4.1.0) following established protocols75, with 2000 integration features. Use of the FindIntegrationAnchors function allowed for the identification of anchors between individual datasets. By entering these anchors into the IntegrateData function, all cells’ batch-corrected expression matrices were produced, enabling the integration and analysis of cells from various datasets. Cell Ranger employs Principal Components Analysis (PCA) to alter the dimensionality of the dataset from cells x genes to cells x M, where M is a user-selectable number of principle components, in order to reduce the gene expression matrix to its most significant properties. The PCA-reduced data were subsequently visualized in two dimensions using t-Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). This nonlinear dimensionality reduction approach clustered cells based on shared transcriptional profiles while maintaining local data structure. Cell type annotation was performed by identifying cluster-specific marker genes using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. The expression of the canonical markers of specific cell types was used to classify and annotate the clusters based on published references16,80. For sub-clustering analysis, clusters of interest were subset by sample, then reprocessed through normalization, variance stabilization, and re-integration using identical parameters. The subset data underwent subsequent PCA, t-SNE, and Louvain clustering to resolve distinct subpopulations.

Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis

With the following parameters, we used Seurat’s “FindAllMarkers” tool to find genes that express themselves differently between clusters: min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.25, pseudocount.use = 0.1, only.pos = T. The p-values for comparisons and the adjusted p-values, depending on Bonferroni correction, for each gene in the dataset were obtained using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The log-transformed and scaled DEGs based on gene expression were shown using a heatmap.

GO biological progress (GOBP) analysis

After the DEGs were identified between the treatments, the Enricher database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) was applied to GO enrichment analysis for the biological progress. GO terms with a p value < 0.05 were significant the highly associated GO terms of biological process were shown.

KEGG pathway analysis

Similar to the GOBP analysis, DEGs identified in the compared treatments was applied to KEGG enrichment analysis using the KOBAS database (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/). KEGG with a p value < 0.05 were significant the highly associated pathways were shown.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Single-cell pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed with monocle381 (https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle3/docs/trajectories/). The developmental processes of cell differentiation were inferred using Monocle 3. These algorithms use an individual cell’s asynchronous evolution within an unsupervised framework to position the cells along a trajectory matching to a biological process. This increases the temporal resolution of transcriptome dynamics of key regulatory factors.

CUT&Tag analysis

In this study, we employed the CUT&Tag (Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation) technology to profile genome-wide protein-DNA interactions and histone modifications in native chromatin. Briefly, the procedure began with cell preparation and fixation to preserve chromatin integrity on ice. Cells were incubated with specific antibodies(H3K9la) and anti-IgG negative control, followed by the addition of a transposase adaptor complex, which performs targeted tagmentation. This process cleaves the DNA at antibody-bound sites and inserts sequencing adapters simultaneously. The resulting DNA fragments are then extracted, amplified, and purified to create a sequencing library by the PTM BIO company.

The paired-end sequencing reads were aligned to the rat genome Rnor6.0 using bowtie2 with options--end-to-end--very-sensitive--no-mixednan--phred33 -I 10 -X 700. To generate heatmap, deeptools 3.5.5 was used. First, scale factors were computed using multiBamSummary in bins mode. BIGWIG signal tracks were then generated using bamCoverage with the scale factors obtained from the previous step. The matrix for heatmap covering 3 kb up and downstream of all transcripts starting sites was computed using computeMatrix and the heatmap was created using plotHeatmap with option --kmeans 3. For visual representation of selected genomic regions, BIGWIG files were loaded into Integrated Genomics Viewer 2.17. To perform differential peak analysis, peak calling was performed using MACS2 in callpeak mode with options -f BAMPE -g 2285452802 -q 0.01 --keep-dup 1 --nomodel. The resulting narrowPeak files were combined into one BED file containing all the peaks from every sample. Quantification of each peak was performed using featureCounts 2.2.1, and the R package DESeq2 was employed for differential peak test. We used an adjust P-value cutoff of 0.3 for differential peaks to be included in downstream enrichment analyses. The differential peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker based on genomic annotation TxDb.Rnorvegicus.UCSC.rn6.refGene. Gene Ontology enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler. All the R packages were run under R 4.4.1.

Hexokinase activity assay

Hexokinase enzymatic activity was quantified using a commercial assay kit (Abcam, ab136957) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, FACS-sorted GFAP⁺ rat spinal cord astrocytes (minimum 1 × 10⁶ cells, quantified using CyQUANT Cell Proliferation Assay Kit [Invitrogen, C7026]) were lysed in ice-cold assay buffer. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and the supernatant was immediately aliquoted for analysis. Reaction mixtures containing protein lysate were incubated in 96-well plates at room temperature for 20–60 min (optimized for linear detection range). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Model), with activity calculated relative to a concurrently run standard curve.

Lactate measurement

L-lactate assay kit (Sigma, MAK329) was utilized to detect lactate levels of Gfap+ astrocyte cells from rat dorsal spinal cord with different treatments according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed in ice-cold assay buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and the supernatant was immediately aliquoted for analysis. Reaction mixtures containing protein lysate were incubated in 96-well plates at room temperature for 20 min (optimized for linear detection range). Absorbance at 565 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Model), with activity calculated relative to a concurrently run standard curve.

Statistics analysis

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but sample sizes in the current study were similar to previous reports11,70. Rats were randomly allocated to groups in all experiments. Data collection and analyses were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. All results are expressed as mean ± SEM. For comparison between two groups, two-tailed unpaired t-test was used. The measurements taken at different groups or time points were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons, Dunnett’s or Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, or two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons. For all tests, values were considered significantly different at p < 0.05. * p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. For Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, Fisher’s exact test with an adjusted p-value was applied. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0. The statistical details of each experiment can be found in the Figure legends.
