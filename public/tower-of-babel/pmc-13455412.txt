Risk factors for residual cancer and lymph node metastasis after non-curative endoscopic resection of colorectal cancer: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Soo Young Oh, Hyung-Hoon Oh, Young Il Kim, Dong-Hyun Kim, Young-Eun Joo, Hyun-Soo Kim, Won-Kyung Kang, Jong Lyul Lee, Yong Sik Yoon, Chan Wook Kim, In Ja Park, Seok-Byung Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsFollowing a non-curative endoscopic resection of T1 colorectal cancer, current guidelines recommend additional surgery based on high-risk pathological features. However, the actual rates of residual disease, such as residual cancer and lymph node metastasis (LNM), remain unclear and risk factors vary significantly across studies. We aimed to determine the true incidence of residual cancer and LNM and identify independent risk factors to guide surgical decision-making.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included patients who underwent radical surgery after non-curative endoscopic resection of T1 colorectal cancer at four hospitals between 2015 and 2020. Non-curative resection was defined by positive margins, deep submucosal invasion (≥ 1000 μm), poor differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, or high-grade tumor budding in accordance with current guidelines.ResultsAmong 517 patients, residual cancer was found in 3.7% and LNM in 7.7% of the cases. Independent predictors of residual cancer were ESD procedure (OR 9.87, P < 0.001), piecemeal resection (OR 5.51, P = 0.017), positive lateral margin (OR 5.88, P = 0.004), and positive deep margin (OR 10.30, P = 0.003). For LNM, submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm (OR 4.66, P = 0.015), poor differentiation (OR 4.30, P = 0.026), lymphovascular invasion (OR 2.42, P = 0.016), and positive deep margin (OR 2.26, P = 0.018) were significant risk factors. Risk increased substantially with cumulative factors: patients with 0–1 risk factors had minimal residual cancer rates (< 1%), while those with ≥ 2 factors showed exponentially higher risks.ConclusionsThe actual incidence of residual cancer and LNM after non-curative endoscopic resection is relatively low. A stepwise framework that evaluates local completeness and LNM risk independently and then integrates the two assessments may support individualized decision-making, potentially sparing low-risk patients from radical surgery while ensuring appropriate intervention for high-risk patients.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Endoscopic resection, Residual cancer, Lymph node metastasis, Risk stratification

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with early detection and effective management being critical for favorable patient outcomes. Advances in screening colonoscopy have significantly increased the detection of early-stage CRC and reduced mortality, enhancing the potential for curative endoscopic treatments [1, 2]. Endoscopic techniques including endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) offer a minimally invasive alternative to surgery, thereby preserving bowel integrity and reducing morbidity, particularly for tumors confined to the mucosal layer or exhibiting superficial submucosal invasion.

International guidelines, including those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE), and the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR), recommend additional surgery when high-risk pathological features are present, due to the potential risk for residual cancer or lymph node metastasis (LNM) [3–6]. These features typically include deep submucosal (SM) invasion (≥ 1000 μm), poor differentiation, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), high tumor budding (grade ≥ 2), and positive or unclear resection margins [4–6]. However, most of these recommendations are derived from surgical series, which may not accurately reflect outcomes following initial endoscopic resection. In practice, the reported incidence of residual cancer or LNM after endoscopic resection of T1 cancers ranges from approximately 6% to 14%, and the associated risk factors vary significantly across studies [7–10]. This variability presents a major clinical challenge in balancing the risk of overtreatment against the need for additional surgery to prevent recurrence or metastasis.

To address this clinical dilemma, the study aims to more accurately determine the true incidence of residual cancer and LNM through a large multicenter cohort of patients who underwent surgery following non-curative endoscopic resection. Furthermore, it aims to identify independent risk factors associated with residual cancer and LNM, providing further insight into clinical decision-making regarding the need for additional surgery and supporting more optimized patient management.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective cohort study involving three tertiary hospitals and one secondary hospital in Korea. Patients diagnosed with T1 CRC after radical surgery between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020, who initially underwent endoscopic resection, were included. We retrospectively collected patient data from electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, tumor location, and histopathological findings from both endoscopic and surgical resection specimens.

Eligibility criteria included histopathologically confirmed T1 CRC cases initially treated by endoscopic resection, which were subsequently determined to be pathologically non-curative, and thus required additional surgical resection with curative intent. Patients with synchronous CRCs, metastatic disease at diagnosis, inflammatory bowel disease, or familial adenomatous polyposis were excluded from the analysis. None of the included patients received preoperative chemoradiotherapy.

Endoscopic resection methods included both EMR and ESD, including procedures performed at all healthcare levels from primary care clinics to tertiary referral centers. Detailed information on the initial histopathological characteristics was recorded at the participating healthcare centers. All cases included in the final analysis were subsequently reviewed by expert gastrointestinal pathologists following referral to tertiary-level hospitals.

This multicenter retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all four participating hospitals: Asan Medical Center (approval No. 2024 − 0237; February 16, 2024), Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (approval No. 2024 − 133; July 11, 2024), Chonnam National University Hospital (approval No. 2024 − 307; November 4, 2024), and Yeouido St. Mary’s Hospital (approval No. SC24RIDI0056; May 21, 2024). Given the retrospective design using de-identified patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the respective ethics committees.

Definitions and outcomes

A non-curative endoscopic resection was defined in accordance with international guidelines (NCCN, ESGE, JSCCR) as the presence of one or more high-risk pathological features on the resected specimen, including: (1) positive or indeterminate resection margins (lateral or deep margins; R1/Rx); (2) deep submucosal invasion (≥ 1000 μm from the muscularis mucosa); (3) poor histological differentiation; (4) LVI; or (5) high tumor budding (grade ≥ 2). Resection margin involvement was defined as the presence of microscopic tumor cells at the lateral (mucosal) or deep (submucosal) margins. The depth of submucosal invasion was measured from the lower border of the muscularis mucosae to the deepest invasive front of the tumor. For pedunculated lesions with distorted muscularis mucosae, invasion depth was measured from the deepest invasive point to the boundary between the tumor head and stalk (Haggitt level 2). Lesions confined to the tumor head (Haggitt level 1) were classified as having an invasion depth of 0 mm. Histologic differentiation was determined based on the predominant tumor morphology, as according to the Japanese Classification of Cancer of the Colon and Rectum. LVI was assessed using elastic fiber and immunohistochemical staining as needed. Indeterminate margins, LVI, and perineural invasion were all considered equivalent to positive status for risk stratification, as the inability to confirm negativity and high interobserver variability in these features necessitate a conservative approach [10–13]. Tumor budding was defined as small clusters of undifferentiated cancer cells or microtubular cancer nests located at the invasive margin, irrespective of histologic type [14].

The primary outcomes assessed in this study were the incidence rates of residual cancer in the bowel wall and LNM identified in surgical specimens following curative-intent surgical resection. Residual cancer was defined as the presence of malignant cells within the bowel wall on subsequent surgical histopathological examination. LNM was defined as the histologically confirmed presence of metastatic carcinoma cells in regional lymph nodes retrieved during surgical resection. Adequacy of lymph node evaluation was assessed using the National Comprehensive Cancer Network–recommended threshold of ≥ 12 retrieved nodes. Secondary outcomes included independent risk factors associated with residual cancer and LNM.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as number and percentage. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables with the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. To identify risk factors associated with residual cancer, a univariate analysis of possible factors was performed, and variables with values of p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The analytical results are expressed as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance. To verify the robustness of the LNM analysis with respect to sampling adequacy, a sensitivity analysis was performed by repeating the multivariable model in patients with ≥ 12 retrieved lymph nodes. Analyses were carried out with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 517 patients who had undergone initially non-curative endoscopic resection followed by additional surgery were enrolled in the study. The characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 59.5 ± 10.7 years, and 59.6% were male. The mean preoperative CEA was 1.8 ± 1.3 ng/mL. Lesions were more frequently located in the colon (70.0%) compared to the rectum (30.0%). EMR was performed in 76.0% of cases, while ESD was conducted in 24.0%. Most resections were performed en bloc (96.5%), with piecemeal resections comprising 3.5%.

Histological evaluations revealed submucosal invasion depth of ≥ 1000 μm in 78.1% of lesions, whereas 21.9% of the cases showed submucosal invasion depth of < 1000 μm. Poor differentiation was observed in 3.1% of cases. LVI was present in 23.6% of the patients, and perineural invasion occurred infrequently (2.1%). Positive lateral resection margins were noted in 15.1%, and positive deep resection margins were found in 33.7%. High-grade tumor budding (grade ≥ 2) was documented in 15.1% of evaluable specimens. Histological assessment of surgical specimens identified residual cancer within the bowel wall in 3.7% of cases and LNM in 7.7%. A median of 19 lymph nodes (IQR, 15–26) were retrieved at surgery, with 463 patients (89.6%) meeting the NCCN-recommended threshold of ≥ 12 nodes.

Factors associated with residual cancer

Comparisons of clinicopathologic factors associated with residual cancer are presented in Table 2. Elevated preoperative CEA levels (≥ 5 ng/mL), ESD procedure, piecemeal resection, positive lateral resection margin, and positive deep resection margin showed significant associations with residual cancer. Specifically, patients who underwent piecemeal resection had a residual cancer rate of 38.9%, compared to only 2.4% after en bloc resection (P < 0.001). Patients who underwent ESD had a higher residual cancer rate (11.3%) compared to EMR (1.3%; P < 0 0.001). Regarding resection margins, positive lateral margins were associated with significantly higher residual cancer rates (12.8%) compared to negative margins (2.1%; P < 0.001). Similarly, positive deep margins demonstrated notably higher residual cancer rates (9.8%) compared to both negative (0.6%; P < 0.001). On multivariate logistic regression analysis, ESD procedure (OR 9.87; 95% CI, 2.98–32.68; P < 0.001), piecemeal resection (OR 5.51; 95% CI, 1.36–22.37; P = 0.017), positive lateral margin (OR 5.88; 95% CI, 1.75–19.8; P = 0.004), and positive deep margin (OR 10.30; 95% CI, 2.16–49.06; P = 0.003) were identified as independent predictors of residual cancer (Table 3; Fig. 3). Elevated preoperative CEA levels (≥ 5 ng/mL) showed a trend in this regard but did not reach statistical significance on multivariate analysis (OR 6.05; 95% CI, 0.98–37.31; P = 0.053). Moreover, as the cumulative number of these risk factors increased, the risk of residual cancer rose significantly (Fig. 1). Patients with zero or only one risk factor had minimal residual cancer rates (0% and 0%, respectively). In contrast, the residual cancer rate substantially increased to 15.4% (95% CI, 9.0-25.1) when with two risk factors, further rising to 33.3% (95% CI, 13.8–60.9) with three factors, and reaching up to 60% (95% CI, 23.1–88.2) in the five patients who presented with all four risk factors. A subgroup analysis of patients with positive or indeterminate deep margins is provided in Table 4. A similar pattern was observed for lateral margin status: residual cancer was identified in 12.8% (10/78) of patients with positive or indeterminate lateral margins, compared with 2.1% (9/439) in those with negative lateral margins, with the rate being higher in true positive (16.4%) than in indeterminate (4.3%) margins (Table 4).

Factors associated with lymph node metastasis

Clinicopathologic factors related to LNM are shown in Table 5. Submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm, poor differentiation, LVI, and positive deep resection margin showed significant associations with LNM. Specifically, patients with submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm exhibited a significantly higher LNM rate (9.2%) compared to those with invasion depth < 1000 μm (2.7%; P = 0.022). Poorly differentiated tumors were associated with a notably higher rate of LNM (25.0%) compared to well-to-moderately differentiated tumors (7.2%; P = 0.009). Patients with LVI had significantly higher LNM rates (12.4%) compared to those without LVI (6.3%; P = 0.028). Furthermore, positive deep resection margins were associated with higher LNM rates (11.5%) compared to negative margins (5.8%; P = 0.023).

In multivariate logistic regression model (Table 6), submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm (OR 4.66; 95% CI, 1.34–16.18; P = 0.015), poor differentiation (OR 4.30; 95% CI, 1.20–15.46; P = 0.026), LVI (OR 2.42; 95% CI, 1.18–4.94; P = 0.016), and positive deep margin (OR 2.26; 95% CI, 1.15–4.44; P = 0.018), remained independent predictors of LNM (Table 6; Fig. 3). Moreover, the risk of LNM substantially increased with the cumulative presence of these risk factors (Fig. 2). Patients with no risk factors had minimal risk (2.2%; 95% CI, 0.4–11.3), and the rate gradually increased to 4.6% (95% CI, 2.7–7.9) with one risk factor, and further increased to 9.9% (95% CI, 6.4–15.2) with two risk factors. The risk markedly escalated to 26.7% (95% CI, 14.2–44.4) in patients who presented three risk factors and reached 100% in the single patient with all four risk factors. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 463 patients with ≥ 12 retrieved lymph nodes, SM invasion ≥ 1000 μm (OR 4.62, 95% CI 1.34–15.95; P = 0.016), LVI (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.07–4.77; P = 0.033), and positive deep margin (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.28–5.11; P = 0.008) remained independent predictors of LNM. Poor differentiation showed a similar effect estimate (OR 3.09, 95% CI 0.73–13.00; P = 0.124) but did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the small number of poorly differentiated cases (n = 14) in the restricted cohort. The LNM rate did not differ significantly between patients with ≥ 12 versus < 12 retrieved nodes (8.2% vs. 3.7%; P = 0.415).

Stepwise risk stratification and treatment algorithm

Recognizing that residual cancer and LNM represent distinct clinical entities, we developed a stepwise framework that integrates both (Fig. 3). Step 1 evaluates local completeness using the four residual cancer risk factors, and Step 2 evaluates LNM risk using the four LNM-related factors. The joint assessment classifies patients into four groups: Group A (low + low; n = 281, 54.4%; observed RC 0.0% [95% CI, 0.0–1.3], LNM 4.6% [95% CI, 2.7–7.7]), for whom surveillance may be considered; Group B (low RC + high LNM; n = 141, 27.3%; RC 0.0% [95% CI, 0.0–2.7], LNM 9.2% [95% CI, 5.5–15.1]), for whom oncologic resection with lymph node dissection is warranted; Group C (high RC + low LNM; n = 23, 4.4%; RC 17.4% [95% CI, 6.9–37.1], LNM 0.0% [95% CI, 0.0–14.3]), for whom local re-treatment or limited resection may be appropriate, with a conservative endoscopic approach considered for the subset with only indeterminate margins; and Group D (high + high; n = 72, 13.9%; RC 20.8% [95% CI, 13.0–31.7], LNM 19.4% [95% CI, 12.0–30.0]), for whom radical oncologic surgery should be considered without delay.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study involving 517 patients, we found residual cancer in 3.7% and LNM in 7.7% of patients who had undergone non-curative endoscopic resection followed by curative-intent surgery. The independent risk factors significantly associated with residual cancer included piecemeal resection, ESD, and positive resection margins (lateral and deep). Likewise, submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm, positive deep margin, poor histological differentiation, and LVI were independent predictors of LNM. Notably, the cumulative presence of these risk factors markedly increased the risk of both residual cancer and LNM. These findings highlight the need for individualized risk stratification to guide clinical decisions regarding the necessity of additional surgical intervention after initial endoscopic resection, and thereby optimizing patient outcomes and minimizing radical treatments.

Compared to our findings, previous studies have reported higher rates of residual cancer and LNM following non-curative endoscopic resection. However, most previous studies were limited to ESD populations, which may describe higher rates of residual cancer and LNM. In a previous Korean single-center study of 85 patients, Kim et al. [7]. found residual tumor in 7.1% and LNM in 8.2% of the surgical specimens after non-curative endoscopic resection. Importantly, 12.9% of their cases showed a non-lifting sign at the index endoscopy, suggesting technically difficult lesions and deeper fibrosis that may have contributed to the higher residual tumor rate. Western studies on non-curative ESD have described even higher nodal risk. Spadaccini et al. [15] reported residual tumor rate of 7.1% and LNM rate of 15.1% in 207 patients across 13 centers, and Santos-Antunes et al. [16] reported residual tumor in 18% and LNM in 14% of 135 colorectal ESDs. As ESD is preferentially selected for larger, non-lifting, or morphologically complex lesions these factors may contribute to different outcomes. Our study, which included both EMR and ESD, provides a broader real-world perspective of endoscopic resection outcomes. The finding that ESD emerged as an independent predictor of residual cancer in our cohort should nonetheless be interpreted with caution. ESD generally achieves higher en bloc and R0 resection rates than EMR [5], and is typically reserved for technically more challenging lesions—including larger, non-lifting, or fibrotic tumors—which inherently carry a higher risk of incomplete resection regardless of the resection technique applied. The absence of uniform data on lesion size and endoscopic morphology in our multicenter cohort prevented adjustment for these baseline differences; thus, the observed association between ESD and residual cancer likely reflects the underlying characteristics of lesions selected for ESD rather than a deficiency of the ESD procedure itself.

Interestingly, our findings revealed a distinction between the risk factors associated with residual cancer and those related to LNM, presented with treatment algorithm. This may reflect the different pathophysiological mechanisms underlying local tumor persistence versus tumor spread to lymph nodes. Risk factors for residual cancer, including piecemeal resection, ESD procedure, and positive resection margins, are primarily related to technical aspects of the initial endoscopic procedure and completeness of local tumor removal. These factors weigh more toward inadequate local control rather than inherent tumor aggressiveness. In contrast, risk factors for LNM, i.e. submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 μm, poor differentiation, and LVI, represent markers of aggressive tumor biology and metastatic potential. This distinction may have important clinical implications for treatment planning. Patients at high risk for residual cancer but low risk for LNM might benefit from more extensive local treatments, such as deeper segmental resection or repeated endoscopic intervention when technically feasible. Conversely, patients with high-risk features for LNM should be more strongly considered for radical surgery with lymph node dissection, as these features suggest systemic disease that cannot be adequately addressed through local measures alone [17]. This conceptual separation underlies our stepwise framework (Fig. 3), in which local completeness and LNM risk are evaluated independently before being integrated into a final treatment decision.

Positive or indeterminate vertical margin should be interpreted not only as a risk factor but also as a marker of pathological uncertainty. Although a positive deep margin most directly suggests residual local disease, it simultaneously limits pathological assessment of submucosal invasion depth, LVI, and tumor budding at the deepest invasive front, and diathermic artifacts may further compromise interpretation [9]. Accordingly, ESGE and JSCCR guidelines classify “positive” and “non-evaluable” vertical margins together as a single high-risk category [5, 6]. In our cohort, deep margin positivity was an independent predictor of both residual cancer (OR 10.30) and LNM (OR 2.26). The latter association may not solely reflect aggressive tumor biology, but could partly arise from under-recognition of other LNM-related features when these cannot be fully evaluated. This is why deep margin positivity is treated as both a residual cancer risk factor (Step 1) and an LNM risk factor (Step 2) in our algorithm (Fig. 3). Notably, however, the actual presence of residual cancer among patients with positive or indeterminate deep margins (n = 174) was strongly modulated by concurrent procedural and pathological features. The residual cancer rate differed substantially between true positive (15.0%) and indeterminate (1.5%) margins, and reached 20.0% in patients with deep margin positivity plus at least one additional residual cancer risk factor (ESD procedure, piecemeal resection, or positive lateral margin), but was 0% in 89 patients with an isolated positive or indeterminate deep margin and no other risk factors (Table 4). These findings suggest that, while deep margin positivity warrants cautious clinical management as a marker of pathological uncertainty, an isolated margin abnormality—particularly when indeterminate—may not by itself necessitate immediate surgery, and supports a more nuanced, multifactorial interpretation in clinical decision-making. A similar pattern was observed for lateral margin involvement: among the 78 patients with positive or indeterminate lateral margins, residual cancer was found in 12.8% overall (16.4% in true positive vs. 4.3% in indeterminate), but in none of the 20 patients with an isolated lateral margin abnormality. Because lateral margin involvement is potentially manageable by endoscopic re-resection rather than surgery, this subgroup may represent a particularly suitable scenario for repeat endoscopic management or close surveillance, although prospective studies with detailed endoscopic data are needed to confirm this approach. For practical clinical application, our stepwise algorithm (Fig. 3) treats “true positive” and “indeterminate” margins together within the same high-risk category, in line with current ESGE and JSCCR guidelines. However, given the substantial differences in residual cancer rates observed between these subcategories, clinicians should consider this qualitative distinction when applying the algorithm. This consideration is particularly relevant in Group C (High RC + Low LNM risk), in which the subset of patients with only indeterminate margins (no true positive margin) showed no residual cancer (0/6) in our cohort, in contrast to 23.5% (4/17) in those with any true positive margin (Table 4). For such patients with isolated indeterminate margins, a conservative endoscopic re-treatment or close surveillance strategy may be a reasonable alternative to immediate radical surgery, as also reflected in the Group C recommendation in Fig. 3.

In our multivariable analysis, SM ≥ 1000 μm remained an independent predictor of LNM (OR 4.66). However, recent evidence has questioned its standalone significance: a meta-analysis by Zwager et al. found that DSI was not an independent predictor (pooled OR 1.73, 95% CI 0.96–3.12), with an absolute LNM risk of only 2.6% when DSI was the sole risk factor [18]. In our cohort, the LNM rate among 200 patients with isolated SM ≥ 1000 μm was 5.5%, substantially lower than the 12.7% observed when other risk factors coexisted—reinforcing our broader finding that cumulative risk, rather than any single factor, should guide clinical decision-making.

While clinical guidelines outline several high-risk pathological features, few studies have demonstrated statistical significance for all these factors simultaneously, and individual reports have identified different significant predictors depending on patient population and methodology [7, 15, 16, 19–22]. Our study not only confirmed multiple established risk factors as independent predictors of both residual cancer and LNM in a single multivariable analysis, but also examined how their joint presence affects clinical risk. However, residual cancer and LNM represent fundamentally different clinical entities, reflecting local control and tumor biology, respectively, and their corresponding therapeutic objectives—addressing local residual disease versus addressing nodal metastatic risk—also differ. Combining them within a single cumulative score may therefore obscure rather than clarify clinical decision-making. We instead propose a stepwise framework in which local completeness (Step 1) and LNM risk (Step 2) are evaluated independently and then integrated into a final treatment decision (Step 3) (Fig. 3). Within our cohort, the residual cancer rate rose from < 1% with 0–1 risk factors to 15.4% with two and 33.3% with three factors, while the LNM rate rose from 2.2% to 26.7% over the same range. Stratifying patients into four clinically distinct groups based on this joint assessment provides a more transparent rationale for individualized surgical decision-making than a single composite score.

Balancing the potential oncological benefits of additional surgery against its inherent risks remains a critical challenge in managing patients with early CRC following non-curative endoscopic resection. A recent meta-analysis of 3,508 patients demonstrated that additional surgery was associated with significantly improved overall survival (OR = 2.95), enhanced recurrence-free survival (OR = 2.53), and lower recurrence rates, including local recurrence (OR = 2.35), compared to surveillance alone [23]. These findings suggest a clear oncologic benefit from surgical resection, but more recent studies challenge this paradigm. For instance, among 207 patients undergoing non-curative colorectal ESD, additional surgery resulted in significantly better overall survival compared to follow-up alone (HR = 3.95; 95% CI, 1.21–12.86; P = 0.014); yet notably, this survival difference was entirely attributed to non-CRC-related causes, and disease-specific survival did not significantly differ between surgical and non-surgical groups [15]. Similarly, another study of 197 patients found no significant differences in cancer recurrence or mortality between those who underwent surgery and those who did not, despite the presence of residual cancer in up to 19.8% of cases [24]. Importantly, the potential oncologic benefit of surgery must be weighed against its procedural risks. In another previous large cohort of over 6,000 CRC patients, surgery was associated with a 3.3% mortality rate and a 14.4% incidence of major complications, including Clavien-Dindo grade III to V events [25]. These risks were particularly elevated among elderly patients (≥ 70 years), those with chronic comorbidities, low preoperative serum albumin, or those requiring anticoagulant therapy. These risks further highlight the importance of individualized decision-making, identifying patients who may or may not benefit from additional surgery.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and variability in clinical decision-making across participating centers. In particular, our cohort included only patients who underwent additional surgery after non-curative endoscopic resection, while those managed with surveillance alone were not analyzed. As a result, the observed incidences of residual cancer and LNM reflect outcomes among patients selected for surgical intervention, and may not be directly generalizable to all patients with non-curative endoscopic resection. Second, several procedural and lesion-related variables were not consistently recorded across the participating centers, which prevented adjustment for potential confounders. These included detailed endoscopic morphology, standardized lesion size, lesion classification systems (Paris, NICE, or JNET), lifting sign, the number of fragments in piecemeal resections, the specific criteria guiding EMR versus ESD selection, and standardized methods of intraprocedural margin assessment. In addition, a substantial proportion of patients underwent the initial endoscopic resection at outside institutions before referral, further limiting uniform retrospective collection of these procedural details. This residual confounding may have partially accounted for the higher residual cancer rate observed in the ESD subgroup, as larger or more complex lesions are typically managed with ESD. Third, initial histopathological assessments of endoscopic resection specimens lacked complete standardization at diagnosis, although this issue was partially mitigated through centralized pathology review by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists at each participating center. Nevertheless, this real-world heterogeneity may better reflect actual clinical practice across various healthcare settings. In addition, although significant associations between risk factors and residual cancer or LNM were identified, the retrospective nature of the study precludes definitive conclusions about causality. Although 89.6% of patients met the NCCN-recommended threshold of ≥ 12 retrieved lymph nodes and a sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of our LNM predictors, the possibility that inadequate sampling in the remaining 10.4% of patients may have contributed to a modest underestimation of the true LNM rate cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the relatively small number of residual cancer (n = 19) and LNM (n = 40) events may have introduced overfitting and wide confidence intervals in the multivariable analyses. Our risk stratification also counts the number of risk factors equally rather than weighting them by effect size; this simplified, count-based approach was chosen to facilitate clinical applicability, but the cumulative risk estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, the relatively short median follow-up period limited the evaluation of long-term outcomes such as recurrence and disease-specific mortality. Future prospective studies with standardized documentation protocols and multicenter registries would help validate our findings and enable more precise, lesion-specific risk stratification.

Despite the above limitations, the study has several notable strengths. It involves one of the largest multicenter cohorts to date specifically examining outcomes following non-curative endoscopic resection for T1 CRC, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, pathological reassessment of both endoscopic and surgical specimens by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists ensured high diagnostic accuracy. Lastly, the study proposes a stepwise risk stratification algorithm that integrates local completeness and LNM risk into four clinically distinct groups, offering a transparent framework for individualized surgical decision-making.

Conclusions

This multicenter study found a relatively low incidence of residual cancer and LNM following non-curative endoscopic resection of T1 CRC, at 3.7% and 7.7%, respectively. These findings revealed distinct risk factor profiles, with technical factors (piecemeal resection, ESD, positive margins) primarily predicting residual cancer, while tumor biological factors (deep submucosal invasion, poor differentiation, LVI) predict LNM. Moreover, our cumulative risk assessment model demonstrated that patients with zero or only one risk factor had minimal risk (< 1% for residual cancer), whereas those with two or more risk factors exhibited exponentially increased risk for both outcomes. These findings support a more individualized approach to risk stratification, aiding in the balance between overtreatment and oncological safety. A stepwise framework that separately assesses local completeness and LNM risk before integrating both may better support individualized decision-making, sparing low-risk patients from radical surgery while ensuring timely intervention for those at higher risk.
