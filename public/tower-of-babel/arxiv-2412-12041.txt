Conjecture: the set of prime numbers is supernatural
Arnaud Mayeux

Abstract
Prime numbers are fascinating by the way they appear in the set of natural numbers. Despite several results enlighting us about their repartition, the set of prime numbers is often informally qualified as misterious. In the present paper, we introduce a formalism allowing to state a formal conjecture: the set of prime numbers is supernatural. Our conjecture has no analog in the existing literature. We explain that this conjecture is expected to be a hard challenge for any kind of intelligence. However it is really natural and even seems very close to be canonical.

Prologue

Mathematics establishes theorems from axioms. This was developed by humans in strong correlation with parts of full nature.
Two facets of nature are very important for the inspiration behind mathematics: abstracts natural numbers and the physical world as perceived by humans. Some mathematical developments of the XX-th century tend to combine these two inspirations (e.g. the Langlands Program, arithmetic geometries). Many conjectures are produced and proved each year, often as generalizations or combinations of existing previous conjectures. Difficulties sometimes come from technicality and, following Grothendieck’s principle of ”Rising Sea”, one can imagine that combinations of several facts and generalizations will culminate in proofs of some of these generalized conjectures. Recently, mathematics received an other inspiration from nature: the hard concept of human intelligence. A combination of machine artificial intelligence (with formal proof assistants) and human supervision will probably participate in solving some of these generalized conjectures and produce connected ideas. We believe this does not describe all the future of mathematics.
Indeed, long times ago, machines, created and directed by humans, surpassed humans at carrying heavy objects. Later, machines, created and directed by humans, surpassed humans at computing arithmetical operations on everyday life numbers. Now, machines, created and directed by humans, are surpassing humans at checking mathematical proofs. Possibly, soon, machine will surpass humans at generalizing and combining some proofs and resolve some ”generalized” conjectures, trained on existing human ideas. All these beautiful machines were created by humans, involving mathematicians, with the help of previous machines. However, since our understanding of ourselves is still very limited today, it is hard to create a (non-human) machine working exactly as humans and surpassing them for all tasks.
We saw that machines will continue to surpass (eventually with assistance) humans for some tasks previously done by them.
Today, we believe it is still our job to produce radically new mathematical ideas. Possibly, producing radically new mathematical ideas could then be among the next challenges for some next generations of machines (all of the above seems well known, standard and in any case does not claim originality).

The aim of this paper is to introduce such a new idea in number theory. This idea is, to us, beautiful, unique, extremely deep and completely natural with no technicalities. It is not similar to related ideas in the existing fields of mathematics, more precisely, the only inspiration goes back to an error of Fermat many centuries ago. This idea is about natural and prime numbers but does not involve a particular equation or number, it is about the whole structure of natural arithmetic. Though this conjecture seems obviously true, it seems so hard that we do not find any way of solving this conjecture. It seems very hard to ”deform” or ”weaken” a bit the conjecture to prove it there and find non-trivial inspiration or hope to prove it in general (a weaker form is given in Corollary
3.1
, but it still seems very hard). The only way to prove or refute this conjecture seems to produce radically new ideas.

Introduction

We now introduce our conjecture.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set of prime numbers.
Fermat
[
Fermat
]
conjectured that
\{2^{2^{n}}+1\mid n\text{ positive integers}\}\subset\mathbb{P}.
Euler
[
Euler1
,
Euler2
]
proved that this is wrong, i.e.
\{2^{2^{n}}+1\mid n\text{ positive integers}\}\not\subset\mathbb{P}
. Let
\mathbb{I}
be the set of positive non-zero integers. We say that a function
f:\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{I}
with variable
n
is a natural function if it is built using formal (and coherent) combinations of the symbols
n,\{a\in\mathbb{I}\},+,\times,\wedge
. Here
\wedge
means elevation to the power. We prove that every natural function is constant or strictly increasing (Proposition
1.6
). We then conjecture that for every non-constant natural function
f
, we have
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
. This is our conjecture. ”Evidences” for this conjecture are intuition about the ”irregularity” of the set of prime numbers, and that it is verified for all ”small natural functions” that we have tested, cf. e.g. Proposition
2
. However, testing it for ”higher natural functions” is difficult because the numbers involved are too big after few initial computations.

In this text we formally introduce notation and definitions. Then we state our conjecture. Our conjecture was formulated in 2013. We explain that this conjecture can be reformulated as in the title of the article:
\mathbb{P}
is supernatural. In some very easy cases which seem not inspiring for the general case, we prove this conjecture. Proposition
3
shows that if the conjecture is true then
f(\mathbb{I})\cap(\mathbb{I}\setminus\mathbb{P})
is an infinite set for all non-constant natural function
f
. In particular, this ”canonical” conjecture implies the existence of infinite composite Fermat numbers (an open question today), cf. Corollary
3.1
. Proposition
4
explains that if the conjecture is wrong, this would provide a natural function so incredible that we can not believe it.

Definition 1.1

An elevation structure is a
4
-uple
(E,+,\times,\wedge)
where
E
is a set and

1.

+

E\times E\to E

a,b

2.

\times

E\times E\to E

a,b

a\times b

3.

\wedge
is a map
E\times E\to E
sending
a,b
to
a\wedge b

such that
\forall~a,b,c\in E

1.

a+b=b+a

2.

a+(b+c)=(a+b)+c

3.

b\times a=a\times b

4.

a\times(b\times c)=(a\times b)\times c

5.

a\times(b+c)=a\times b+a\times c

6.

(a\wedge b)\times(a\wedge c)=a\wedge({b+c})

7.

(a\wedge b)\wedge c=a\wedge(b\times c)
.

We write
a_{1}\wedge a_{2}\wedge\ldots\wedge a_{n}

a_{1}\wedge(a_{2}\wedge(\ldots\wedge a_{n}))
, be careful that

a_{1}^{a_{2}^{\udots^{a_{n}}}}

a_{1}\wedge a_{2}\wedge\ldots\wedge a_{n}

A morphism of elevation structure from

to
(F,+,\times,\wedge)

M:E\to F
such that
\forall~a,b\in E

M(a+b)=M(a)+M(b)
,
M(a\times b)=M(a)\times M(b)
and
M(a\wedge b)=M(a)\wedge M(b)
. We obtain a category.

2
Statement of the conjecture

2.1
Statement

We now state our conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}_{Natural}
be a non-constant natural function.
Then

f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}.

Proposition 2
.

Conjecture
1
is true in the following cases.

1.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}_{Polynomial}
, assume
f
is non-constant, then
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

2.

Let
a\in\mathbb{I}_{>1}
,
b\in\mathbb{N}
and
f
be
n\mapsto a^{n}+b
, then
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

3.

Let
f
be
n\mapsto 2^{2^{n}}+1
, the Fermat function. Then
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

Proof 2.1
.

1.

Since
f
is non-constant, it is strictly increasing by
1.6
, so there exits
n\in\mathbb{I}
such that
f(n)>1
. Let
p\in\mathbb{P}
such that
p\mid f(n)
. Then
f(n)=0\mod p
. Since
f
is polynomial, we have

f(n+p)=f(n)\mod p.

So we have

p\mid f(n)~~,~~~p\mid f(n+p)~~,~~~1<f(n)<f(n+p).

This implies that
f(n+p)
is not a prime number. So
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

2.

Let
n\in\mathbb{I}_{>1}
. Then
f(n)=a^{n}+b>1
. Let
p\in\mathbb{P}
such that
p\mid f(n)
. Then
a^{n}+b=0~\mod p.
Assume first that
p\nmid a
, then by Fermat’s little theorem

a^{n+(p-1)}+b=a^{n}+b=0\mod p.

So we have

p\mid f(n)~,~~~~p\mid f(n+(p-1))~,~~~~1<f(n)<f(n+(p-1)).

This implies that
f(n+p-1)
is not a prime number. Now if
p\mid a
, then
p<f(n)
and
f(n)
is not a prime number. So we have proved that
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

3.

We have
f(5)=2^{2^{5}}+1=4294967297=641\times 6700417
, as Euler
[
Euler1
,
Euler2
]
computed. So
f(\mathbb{I})\not\subset\mathbb{P}
.

2.2
Reformulation:
\mathbb{P}
is supernatural

An infinite subset of
\mathbb{I}
is said to be natural if it is of the form
f(\mathbb{I})
where
f\in\mathcal{F}_{Natural}
is non-constant. An infinite subset of
\mathbb{I}
is said to be supernatural if it does not contain any natural subset of
\mathbb{I}
. Our conjecture is now equivalent to the following statement:
\mathbb{P}
is supernatural.

3
Implications and remarks

In this section we discuss implications of our conjecture and comment it.

Proposition 3
.

Assume Conjecture
1
is true. Let
f\in\mathcal{F}_{Natural}
non-constant, then
\{x\in f(\mathbb{I})\mid x\text{ is not a prime number }\}
is infinite.

Proof 3.1
.

By
1.6
,
f
is strictly increasing. It is enough to prove the following statement. For all
k\in\mathbb{I}
, there exists
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>k}
, such that
f(q)
is not a prime number.
So let
k\in\mathbb{I}
. Let us consider the function
g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{I},\mathbb{I})
defined by
g(n)=f(n+k)
. The function
g
is natural. So there exists
d\in\mathbb{I}
such that
g(d)
is not a prime number. So
q:=d+k
is such that
q>k
and
f(q)
is not a prime number. This finishes the proof.

Corollary 3.1

Assume Conjecture
1
is true. Then there are infinitely many composite (i.e. not prime) Fermat’s numbers (this can be seen as a weaker form of our conjecture, but still seems very hard).

Proof 3.2
.

Apply
3
to Fermat’s natural function
n\mapsto 2^{2^{n}}+1
.

Proposition 4
.

Assume Conjecture
1
is wrong. Then there exists an ”arithmetical” (relying only on
+,\times,\wedge
) formula giving arbitrary big prime numbers.

Proof 3.3
.

If Conjecture
1
is wrong. There exists a non-constant (and thus strictly increasing) natural function giving only prime numbers. It is ”arithmetical”.

Remark 3.2

In order to define natural functions in Definition
1.4
, we have used
+,\times,\wedge
. One can define with the same formalism a larger class of function using moreover Knuth’s up arrow
\{\uparrow^{n}\mid n\geqslant 1\}
notation for hyperoperation
(\uparrow^{1}=\wedge)
. Then one can extend Conjecture
1
to this larger class of functions.
