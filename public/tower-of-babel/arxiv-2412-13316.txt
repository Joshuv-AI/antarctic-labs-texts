Endogenies and Linearisation
Adrien Deloro and Frank Wagner

Abstract
. We show that the action of two infinite commuting invariant rings of endomorphisms of a finite-dimensional virtually connected irreducible bi-module linearizes into a vector space over a definable field. The same holds if the action is merely by strongly commuting endogenies, modulo some finite katakernel.

Introduction

The present work studies—under the model-theoretic hypothesis of finite-dimensionality—irreducible bi-modules, i.e. abelian groups together with two commuting subrings of endomorphisms, and which are irreducible for the bi-action. This enhances the result in
[
2
]
, which itself stems from our initial investigations.

In fact, we are working with a more general notion than endomorphism, namely
endogenies
, which are endomorphisms
blurred
by a finite subgroup. We work in finite-dimensional theories in the sense of
[
6
]
(see Definition
1.25
), and contrary to customary practice, in the category of
virtually
connected groups (i.e., connected-by-finite). We first show that in a finite-dimensional theory, an irreducible virtually connected
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-bi-module
A
admits a quotient by a finite subgroup
F
such that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
act on
A/F
as actual endomorphisms rather than endogenies (Theorem A), and then characterise this action in Theorem B.

Remark
.

Irreducibility is with respect to the bi-action, and in the category of virtually connected bi-modules. In line with common model-theoretic teminology, we shall call this
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimality.
In case
\Gamma
and
\Delta
consist of
endogenies
as discussed in Section
1
,

invariance
splits into a weak and a full form; the former is more important, and minimality is with respect to it (see Section
1.5
). Commutation is in the sharp sense of Section
1.4

We shall show:

Theorem A
.

In a finite-dimensional theory, let
A
be a definable, virtually connected, abelian group. Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be two invariant prerings of definable endogenies of
A
such that
A
is
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal. Suppose
\Gamma
and
\Delta
commute sharply, both are essentially infinite, and one of them is essentially unbounded.
Then there is a finite fully
\Gamma
- and
\Delta
-invariant subgroup
F
such that the induced action of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
on
A/F
is by endomorphisms.

In fact,
F
is given explicitly.

Theorem B
.

In a finite-dimensional theory, let
A
be a definable, connected, abelian group. Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be two invariant rings of definable endomorphisms of
A
such that
A
is
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal. Suppose that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
commute, both are infinite and one of them is unbounded. Then there is a definable field
\mathbb{K}
such that
A
is definably a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space of finite linear dimension, and both
\Gamma
and
\Delta

\mathbb{K}

Endogenies are discussed at length in Section
1

1.
Endogenies

This section describes a variation on endomorphisms called
endogenies
, which are like morphisms but ‘blurred’ by finite subgroups.
Some of their properties can disconcert at first and one may wish to spend a little time computing and checking statements in order to become familiar with them. We shall add some definability and connectedness in Sections
1.7
and
1.8
; the beginning is purely algebraic.

Endogenies abound in the theory of elliptic curves, with which we assume no familiarity.
A simpler example arises as follows. Let
\mathbb{K}\models\mathrm{ACF}_{p}
and
A=(\mathbb{K},+)
. The Artin-Schreier endomorphism of
A
, viz.,
f(x)=x^{p}-x
, is onto with a finite kernel. Its inverse relation
f^{-1}
has domain
A
and is additive (viz., is a subgroup of
A\times A
), but behaves like a
p
-valued polymorphism. For instance it ‘takes’
0
to the subgroup
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
To gain intuition through practice, one should compose
f^{-1}
with itself, and then with
f
on both sides.

To our knowledge endogenies made their appearance in model theory (under the name of
quasi-endomorphism
) in the beautiful paper
[
1
]
which proved that
\aleph_{0}
-categorical
\aleph_{0}
-stable connected groups are abelian. The arise quite naturally: If an abelian group
A
decomposes as almost linearly independent sum of definable minimal groups, then any definable group gives rise to endogenies between the components, and is itself characterized by these endogenies. Ever since this has been a major ingredient in the study of
\aleph_{0}
-categorical tame groups
[
5
,
4
,
3
]
.

1.1.
The definition

The general definition could be formulated with non-commutative groups, but we shall quickly require abelianity. We therefore assume it from the outset.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be abelian groups.

Definition 1.1
.

An
endogeny
from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
is a
subgroup
\gamma\leq A_{1}\times A_{2}
with the following properties:

•

\gamma
is global, viz.,
\pi_{1}(\gamma)=A_{1}
(where
\pi_{i}\colon A_{1}\times A_{2}\to A_{i}
is the canonical projection);

•

the
katakernel

\operatorname{kat}\gamma=\{a\in A_{2}:(0,a)\in\gamma\}

is finite.

If
A_{2}=A_{1}=A
, we simply call
\gamma
an endogeny
of

A
.

Remarks 1.2
.

•

In Section
1.3
we shall introduce a natural equivalence relation on endogenies.

•

(The graph of) a morphism from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
is also an endogeny; the converse does not hold.

•

An endogeny from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
may also be viewed:

–

as an additive relation
\gamma
on
A_{1}\times A_{2}
with
\pi_{1}\gamma=A_{1}
and finite fibre over
0\in A_{1}
;

–

as a multi-but-finite-valued morphism
A_{1}\to A_{2}
;

–

or as a (classical) morphism
g\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}/\operatorname{kat}\gamma
.

Be careful that the image of an element
a_{1}\in A_{1}
under
\gamma
is a
set
, namely a coset of
\operatorname{kat}\gamma
. To avoid confusion we write
\gamma[a_{1}]
, with square brackets. Hence
\operatorname{kat}\gamma=\gamma[0]
and
\operatorname{im}\gamma=\gamma[A]
.
Before going any further, one should check under all interpretations above that
\gamma^{-1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]=\ker\gamma
.

1.2.
The prering of endogenies

Definition 1.3
.

Let
A
be an abelian group, and
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
the set of endogenies of
A
. We equip
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
with the following two operations:

•

pointwise addition/subtraction, viz.:

(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}):=\{(a,b_{1}+b_{2}):b_{1}\in\gamma_{1}[a]\wedge b_{2}\in\gamma_{2}[a]\};

•

product as composition of relations, viz.:

(\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}):=\{(a,c):\exists b\,(b\in\gamma_{2}[a])\wedge(c\in\gamma_{1}[b])\}.

One should quickly check that
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
is closed under these operations. We omit
\circ
in practice. One sees easily that
\operatorname{kat}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})=\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}
and
\operatorname{kat}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})=\gamma_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}]
.

Remark 1.4
.

Functional interpretation:

•

Each
\gamma_{i}
corresponds to a morphism
g_{i}\colon A\to A/\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{i}
, which composes to
h_{i}\colon A\to A/(\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2})
. The sum
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
corresponds to
h_{1}+h_{2}:A\to A/(\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2})
.

•

Also,
\gamma_{1}
induces
g^{\prime}_{1}\colon A/\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}\to A/\gamma_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}]
, and
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
corresponds to
g^{\prime}_{1}g_{2}:A\to A/\gamma_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}]
.

In the sequel, we shall avoid these alternative points of view.

Lemma 1.5

(Prering Lemma)
.

(\operatorname{Endog}(A);+,\circ)
is a
prering
, in the following sense:

•

(\operatorname{Endog}(A);+)
is associative and commutative; the zero morphism is neutral;

•

(\operatorname{Endog}(A);\circ)
is associative; the identity morphism is neutral;

•

right-distributivity holds, viz.,
\gamma(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})=\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2}
;

•

left-distributivity almost holds. More precisely, one has
(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\delta\leq\gamma_{1}\delta+\gamma_{2}\delta
but only
\gamma_{1}\delta+\gamma_{2}\delta\leq(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\delta+\gamma_{2}[\operatorname{kat}\delta]=(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\delta+\gamma_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\delta]
.

Proof.

The first claims are clear and we focus on (partial) distributivity.

•

Let
b\in\gamma(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})[a]
. By definition, there are
x_{1},x_{2}
such that
x_{i}\in\delta_{i}[a]
and
b\in\gamma[x_{1}+x_{2}]
. Since endogenies are global, we take
b_{i}\in\gamma[x_{i}]
. Then
b_{i}\in\gamma\delta_{i}[a]
and
b_{1}+b_{2}\in(\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2})[a]
.

By definition,
b_{1}+b_{2}\in\gamma[x_{1}+x_{2}]
; so
b-b_{1}-b_{2}\in\operatorname{kat}\gamma\leq\operatorname{kat}(\gamma\delta_{1})\leq\operatorname{kat}(\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2})
. Hence
b\in(\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2})[a]
.

•

Let
b\in(\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2})[a]
. By definition, there are
b_{i}\in\gamma\delta_{i}[a]
with
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
; this is witnessed by
x_{i}\in\delta_{i}[a]
and
b_{i}\in\gamma[x_{i}]
. Then
x_{1}+x_{2}\in(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})[a]
and
b=b_{1}+b_{2}\in\gamma[x_{1}+x_{2}]\leq\gamma(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})[a]
, as claimed.

•

Let
b\in(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\delta[a]
. Then there are
b_{1},b_{2},x
with
x\in\delta[a]
,
b_{i}\in\gamma_{i}[x]
, and
b=b_{1}+b_{2}\in\gamma_{1}\delta[a]+\gamma_{2}\delta[a]=(\gamma_{1}\delta+\gamma_{2}\delta)[a]
.

•

Finally let
b\in(\gamma_{1}\delta+\gamma_{2}\delta)[a]
. By definition there are
b_{i}
and
x_{i}
with
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
,
x_{i}\in\delta[a]
and
b_{i}\in\gamma_{i}[x_{i}]
. Since endogenies are global, there is
y\in\gamma_{2}[x_{1}]
. Then
b_{1}+y\in(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})[x_{1}]\leq(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\delta[a]
.

We now estimate
b-b_{1}-y
. By construction,
x_{1}-x_{2}\in\operatorname{kat}\delta
. Moreover
y\in\gamma_{2}[x_{1}]
and
b_{2}\in\gamma_{2}[x_{2}]
so
b-b_{1}-y=b_{2}-y\in\gamma_{2}[x_{2}-x_{1}]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}[\delta]
, as claimed.
∎

We shall accordingly use the phrase
sub-prering
for a subset of
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
which is closed under
+
,
-
and
\cdot
.

1.3.
Equivalence of endogenies

Of course
\gamma-\gamma=z_{\operatorname{kat}\gamma}
, the endogeny taking all
A
to
\operatorname{kat}\gamma
. As a relation, this is simply
A\times\operatorname{kat}\gamma
. This begs for an equivalence relation.

Definition 1.6
.

Two endogenies
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
equivalent
if there is a finite
F\leq A
such that for all
a\in A
, one has
\gamma_{1}[a]+F=\gamma_{2}[a]+F
. We shall denote equivalence by
\gamma_{1}\sim\gamma_{2}
.

This clearly is an equivalence relation.

Remark 1.7
.

Alternative ways to rephrase this condition are:

•

there is a finite subgroup
F\leq A
such that

\gamma_{1}+z_{F}=\gamma_{2}+z_{F}
;

•

there is a finite subgroup
F\leq A
such that

\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\leq A\times F
as subgroups of
A^{2}
;

•

there is a finite subgroup
F\leq A
such that

\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}\leq F
and
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
induce the same morphism
A\to A/F
. (Note that
F=\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma_{2}
is minimal with this property.)

Remarks 1.8
.

•

There even is a preordering
\gamma_{1}\preceq\gamma_{2}
whose associated equivalence is the above. Morphisms are minimal endogenies in this preordering, but there are other minimal endogenies.

•

Not every endogeny is equivalent to a morphism (e.g., the inverse of the Artin-Schreier morphism).

Corollary 1.9

(to Lemma
1.5
)
.

Equivalence of endogenies is preserved under
+
and
\circ
. Moreover
\operatorname{Endog}(A)/{\sim}
is a ring.

Proof.

The first is tedious and easy. Then by Lemma
1.5
, distributivity holds modulo equivalence.
∎

In practice the prering
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
and its quotient ring
\operatorname{Endog}(A)/\!\sim
compete with each other. Model theory tends to favour the former as closer to definability issues; algebraic arguments however take place in the latter.

Definition 1.10
.

A set
X\subset\operatorname{Endog}(A)
is
essentially infinite
if its image is infinite in
\operatorname{Endog}(A)/{\sim}
.

Later, when dealing with model-theoretic unboundedness, we shall use the phrase ‘essentially unbounded’.

1.4.
Sharp commutation of endogenies

The usual notion of commutation (as relations) turns out to be too restrictive for our purposes.
On the other hand, commutation modulo equivalence is too weak.
We strike the balance as follows.

Definition 1.11
.

Two endogenies
\gamma
and
\delta

commute sharply
if

\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta-\delta\gamma)\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta

(equality then follows).
We write
C^{\sharp}(\gamma)
for the set of endogenies commuting sharply with
\gamma
.

Remark 1.12
.

The condition is more restrictive than finiteness of
\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta-\delta\gamma)
. In particular, not every endogeny commutes with itself; for instance the inverse endogeny of
(x\mapsto x^{p}-x)
does not. (A morphism of course does.)

Lemma 1.13

(Sharp Commutation Lemma)
.

(1)

If
\gamma
and
\delta
commute sharply, then
\delta[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta
.

(2)

C^{\sharp}(\gamma)\subseteq\operatorname{Endog}(A)
is a sub-prering of
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
, viz., is closed under
+
,
-
and
\circ
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
a\in\operatorname{kat}\gamma=\gamma[0]
, and
b\in\delta[a]
. Then
b\in\delta\gamma[0]
, and
0\in\gamma\delta[0]
. By sharp commutation,

b=b-0\in\operatorname{im}(\delta\gamma-\gamma\delta)\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta.

Hence
\delta[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta
.

(2)

Suppose
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in C^{\sharp}(\gamma)
.

•

By Lemma
1.5
and sharp commutation:

\displaystyle\operatorname{im}(\gamma(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})-(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{1}+\gamma\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}\gamma-\delta_{2}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{1}-\delta_{1}\gamma)+\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{2}-\delta_{2}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{1}+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}).

•

Note that
\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{1}
and
\gamma[\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})
by 1. Moreover
\operatorname{kat}\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\delta_{2}]=\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})
.
Thus:

\displaystyle\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{1}\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{1}\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}\gamma\delta_{2}+\delta_{1}\gamma\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{1}-\delta_{1}\gamma)+\gamma\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\delta_{2}]+\delta_{1}[\operatorname{im}(\gamma\delta_{2}-\delta_{2}\gamma)]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{1}+\gamma[\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})]+\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta_{1}+\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})+\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]+\delta_{1}[\operatorname{kat}\delta_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2}).\qed

1.5.
Invariance

As one expects, invariance downgrades to a weak form, allowing for a katakernel-error term. (Irreducibility consequently upgrades to a stronger form; see the definition of minimality in Section
1.8
.)

Definition 1.14
.

Let
A
be an abelian group,
B\leq A
a subgroup, and
\gamma
an endogeny of
A
.

•

B
is

weakly
\gamma
-invariant
if
\gamma[B]\leq B+\operatorname{kat}\gamma
;

•

B
is

fully
\gamma
-invariant
if
\gamma[B]\leq B
.

Remark 1.15
.

The sum of two (weakly/fully)
\gamma
-invariant subgroups is still (weakly/fully)
\gamma
-invariant, and the intersection of two fully
\gamma
-invariant subgroups is fully
\gamma
-invariant.
However,
the intersection of two weakly
\gamma
-invariant subgroups need not be weakly
\gamma
-invariant
.

In general, weak invariance is the more useful notion of the two notions, which will be used in most instances. (If
\gamma
is a morphism, then full
\gamma
-invariance and weak
\gamma
-invariance are the same.)

Lemma 1.16

(Invariance Lemma)
.

Suppose
\gamma
and
\delta
are two sharply commuting endogenies of
A
.
Then:

(1)

\operatorname{kat}\gamma
is weakly
\delta
-invariant;

(2)

if
B\leq A
is weakly
\gamma
-invariant, then so is
\delta[B]
.

Proof.

(1)

This is a reformulation of Lemma
1.13
.
1
.

(2)

By sharp commutation,
\gamma\delta[B]\leq\delta\gamma[B]+\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta
. By assumption
\gamma[B]\leq B+\operatorname{kat}\gamma
. Since
\delta[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta
by part
1
. and
\operatorname{kat}\delta\leq\delta[B]
, we are done.
∎

Remarks 1.17
.

•

Nothing like
2
. holds with inverse images. This has annoying consequences:

–

\ker\gamma
need not be weakly
\delta
-invariant (however see Remarks
1.28
);

–

even if
\delta
is invertible,
\delta^{-1}
need not commute sharply with
\gamma
.

•

However,
if
\gamma
is a morphism
, then
\ker\delta
is fully
\gamma
-invariant.

Indeed if
a\in\ker\delta
, then

\delta[\gamma(a)]\leq\gamma\delta[a]+\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta\leq\gamma[\operatorname{kat}\delta]+\operatorname{kat}\delta=\operatorname{kat}\delta,

so
\gamma(a)\in\delta^{-1}[\operatorname{kat}\delta]=\ker\delta
.

Lemma 1.18

(Restriction Lemma)
.

If
B\leq A
is weakly
\gamma
-invariant, then the restriction-corestriction
\rho_{B}(\gamma)=\gamma\cap B^{2}\leq B^{2}
is an endogeny of
B
.

Of course one then has
\operatorname{kat}\rho_{B}(\gamma)\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma\cap B
. If
B
is clear from the context, we also write
\rho_{B}(\gamma)=\check{\gamma}
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma[B]\leq B+\operatorname{kat}\gamma
, for every
b\in B
there is
b^{\prime}\in B\cap\gamma[b]
; so
\check{\gamma}
is global on
B
. It clearly is a subgroup of
B^{2}
, and the katakernel is finite.
∎

The map
\gamma\mapsto\check{\gamma}
need not be additive nor multiplicative, except modulo equivalence.

Remark 1.19
.

If
\gamma
and
\delta
are two sharply commuting endogenies of
A
and
B\leq A
is weakly
\gamma
- and
\delta
-invariant, there is no reason why
\rho_{B}(\gamma)
and
\rho_{B}(\delta)
should commute sharply. They do, however, commute modulo equivalence.

1.6.
The global katakernel

Definition 1.20
.

Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be two sharply commuting prerings of endogenies of some abelian group.

•

The
katakernel
of
\Gamma
is
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\operatorname{kat}\gamma
.

•

The
bi-katakernel
of
(\Gamma,\Delta)
is
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)=\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)+\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
.

Lemma 1.21

(Katakernel Lemma)
.

Let
A
be an abelian group and
\Gamma,\Delta
be two sharply commuting prerings of endogenies.

(1)

The katakernel of
\Gamma
is fully
\Gamma
-invariant.
In particular
\Gamma
acts on
A/\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)
by endomorphisms.

(2)

The bi-katakernel of
(\Gamma,\Delta)
is fully
\Gamma
- and
\Delta
-invariant.
Hence
\Gamma
and
\Delta
act on
A/\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
by commuting endomorphisms.

(3)

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma
the subgroup
\gamma^{-1}[\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)]
is fully
\Delta
-invariant.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
. Then
\gamma[\operatorname{kat}\gamma^{\prime}]=\gamma\gamma^{\prime}[0]=\operatorname{kat}(\gamma\gamma^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)
. This shows full
\Gamma
-invariance. Clearly, factoring out the katakernels transforms the endogenies into endomorphisms (which would not hold under
weak
invariance).

(2)

For
\gamma\in\Gamma
its katakernel
\operatorname{kat}\gamma
is
weakly

\Delta
-invariant by sharp commutation and Lemma
1.16
.
1
. So
\delta[\operatorname{kat}\gamma]\leq\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta\leq\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
. This proves
full
invariance.

(3)

Put
K=\gamma^{-1}[\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)]
, and note that
K\geq\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
. Consider any
\delta\in\Delta
. Then

\gamma\delta[K]\leq\delta\gamma[K]+\operatorname{kat}\gamma+\operatorname{kat}\delta\leq\delta[\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)]+\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)=\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta),

so
\delta[K]\leq\gamma^{-1}[\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)]=K
and
K
is fully
\Delta
-invariant.
∎

Remark 1.22
.

The proof of
3
. even shows that for any fully
\Delta
-invariant subgroup
B
containing
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
and
\operatorname{kat}\gamma
, the inverse image
\gamma^{-1}[B]
is fully
\Delta
-invariant.

1.7.
Definability and Dimensionality

We now add some definability on
A
; an endogeny is said to be
definable
if it is, as a relation.
The set of definable endogenies
\operatorname{DefEndog}(A)
of
A
is a sub-prering of
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
(see Section
1.2
), viz., it is closed under
+
,
-
and
\circ
.

Our endogeny prerings will in general not be definable, but merely invariant in the following sense.

Definition 1.23
.

Let
A
be a definable abelian group, and
\Gamma
a ring of definable endogenies of
A
. We say that
\Gamma
is
invariant
if there is a set
C
of parameters such that (in a big
\kappa
-saturated monster model)
\Gamma
is stabilized by all automorphisms which fix
C
.

Remark 1.24
.

Thus
\Gamma
is invariant if the set of parameters for the endogenies in
\Gamma
is a union of types over
C
.

There is an unfortunate clash of terminology between the model-theoretic and the algebraic meaning of invariance, but both are well-established. This should not lead to confusion.

We shall now define finite-dimensionality, introduced in
[
6
]
.

Definition 1.25
.

A theory
T
is
finite-dimensional
if there is a dimension function
\dim
from the collection of all interpretable sets in models of
T
to
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
,
satisfying for a formula
\varphi(x,y)
and interpretable sets
X
and
Y
:

•

Invariance:
If
a\equiv a^{\prime}
then
\dim(\varphi(x,a))=\dim(\varphi(x,a^{\prime}))
.

•

Algebraicity:

X
is finite non-empty if and only if
\dim(X)=0
, and
\dim(\emptyset)=-\infty
.

•

Union:

\dim(X\cup Y)=\max\{\dim(X),\dim(Y)\}
.

•

Fibration:
If
f:X\to Y
is a interpretable map such that
\dim(f^{-1}(y))=d
for all
y\in Y
, then
\dim(X)=\dim(Y)+d
.

Examples of finite-dimensional theories include groups of finite Morley rank (where dimension is Morley rank), groups of finite SU-rank (where dimension is SU-rank), and groups definable in
o
-minimal theories (where dimension is
o
-minimal dimension).

The following will be used without mention and follows immediately from the fibration axiom.

Lemma 1.26

(Dimension Lemma)
.

In a finite-dimensional theory, let
A
be a definable group and
\gamma
be a definable endogeny of
A
. Then
\dim\ker\gamma+\dim\operatorname{im}\gamma=\dim A
.

Proof.

Treat
\gamma
as a subgroup of
A^{2}
. The first projection has image
A
and kernel isomorphic to
\operatorname{kat}\gamma
, so
\dim\gamma=\dim A
. Now the second projection has image
\operatorname{im}\gamma
and kernel
\ker\gamma
, so
\dim\gamma=\dim\ker\gamma+\dim\operatorname{im}\gamma
.
∎

In particular,
|A:\operatorname{im}\gamma|
is finite if and only if
\ker\gamma
is finite.

1.8.
Connectedness and minimality

The notion of connectedness is the usual one. Having a connected component is equivalent to being virtually connected.

Lemma 1.27

(Connectedness Lemma)
.

Let
B\leq A
be a virtually connected subgroup, and
\gamma
an endogeny of
A
. Then
\gamma[B]
is virtually connected. Moreover,
\gamma[B^{\circ}]=\gamma[B]^{\circ}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma
.

Proof.

Let
C\leq\gamma[B]
have finite index in
\gamma[B]
. Then
\gamma^{-1}[C]
has finite index in
B
and contains
B^{\circ}
. Considering the induced endomorphism
\bar{\gamma}:B\to\gamma[B]/\operatorname{kat}\gamma
, we see that

\displaystyle|\gamma[B]:C+\operatorname{kat}\gamma|

\displaystyle=|\gamma[B]/\operatorname{kat}\gamma:(C+\operatorname{kat}\gamma)/\operatorname{kat}\gamma|

\displaystyle\leq|B:\gamma^{-1}[C+\operatorname{kat}\gamma]|=|B:\gamma^{-1}[C]|\leq|B:B^{\circ}|.

So the index of any definable subgroup of finite index in
\gamma[B]
is bounded, and
\gamma[B]
is virtually connected.

Clearly
\operatorname{kat}\gamma\leq\gamma[B^{\circ}]
, and
\gamma[B^{\circ}]
has finite index in
\gamma[B]
. Thus
\gamma[B]^{\circ}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma\leq\gamma[B^{\circ}]
. Conversely,
\gamma^{-1}[\gamma[B]^{\circ}]
has finite index in
B
and contains
B^{\circ}
. Hence
\gamma[B^{\circ}]\leq\gamma[B]^{\circ}+\operatorname{kat}\gamma
, and equality holds.
∎

Remarks 1.28
.

•

In general
\ker\gamma
need not have a connected component. Moreover, if
\delta
commutes sharply with
\gamma
, then
\ker\gamma
need not be weakly
\delta
-invariant (see Remark
1.17
.). But one can prove that
if

\ker\gamma
is virtually connected, then
(\ker\gamma)^{\circ}
is weakly
\delta
-invariant.

•

By methods similar to the proof of Lemma
1.27
, one can show that if
A
is virtually connected, then so is every definable endogeny of
A
, as a subgroup of
A^{2}
. Thus if
A
is connected, every endogeny is equivalent to a
connected endogeny
. However these do not form a sub-prering of
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
, and
C^{\sharp}(\delta)
is not closed under taking connected components. We therefore do not pursue the matter any further.

We can now define the relevant notion of minimality.

Definition 1.29
.

Let
A
be a virtually connected definable abelian group in a finite-dimensional theory, and
\Gamma
,
\Delta
two invariant rings of definable endogenies of
A
. We say that
A
is
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal if there is no infinite, definable, virtually connected, weakly
\Gamma
- and
\Delta
-invariant subgroup of infinite index.

Remark 1.30
.

As noted in Remark
1.19
, sharp commutation is not hereditary on weakly
\Gamma
- and
\Delta
-invariant subgroups. Thus, if
A
is a virtually connected definable abelian group in a finite-dimensional theory, and
\Gamma
,
\Delta
two sharply commuting invariant rings of definable endogenies of
A
, then
A
contains a
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal virtually connected subgroup
B
by finite-dimensionality, but need not contain one where the restriction-corestrictions
\rho_{B}(\Gamma)
and
\rho_{B}(\Delta)
commute sharply.

In particular, since
A
is virtually connected, its connected component
A^{\circ}
will be weakly
\Gamma
- and
\Delta
-invariant, but again
\rho_{A^{\circ}}(\Gamma)
and
\rho_{A^{\circ}}(\Delta)
might not commute sharply. For this reason we work in the virtually connected category rather than the more usual connected one.

2.
Proof of Theorem A

In this section we shall prove our first main theorem. Full familiarity with the notions of Section
1
is required. We can now give the precise statement.

Theorem A
.

In a finite-dimensional theory, let
A
be a definable, virtually connected, abelian group. Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be two invariant prerings of definable endogenies of

such that

is
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal. Suppose
\Gamma
and
\Delta
commute sharply, both are essentially infinite, and one of them is essentially unbounded.
Then
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is finite, and the induced action of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
on
A/\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is by commuting endomorphisms.

Of course, the finite group
F
from the introduction equals
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
. The proof is by induction on dimension, reducing to a local form given in Section
2.1
. The reduction itself involves subgroups of

called

and introduced in Section
2.2
.
One needs to reduce
\Gamma
and
\Delta
to the relevant variants
\Gamma_{L},\Delta_{L}
in Section

.
Returning from lines to
A
takes place in Section

. Then the inductive proof of Theorem A quickly follows in Section
2.5
.

One general notation is used throughout this section: when

are (abelian) groups, we write

if

is infinite. In particular,

means that

is infinite.

2.1.
Local version (finite kernels)

Proposition 2.1

(Local version of Theorem A)
.

Under the same hypotheses as Theorem A, instead of assuming
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimality, suppose that every endogeny in
\Gamma\cup\Delta
inequivalent to
0
has finite kernel. Then
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
is finite.

Proof.

First suppose that
\Gamma
is essentially unbounded. If the sum giving
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
is infinite, we can take a subsum
B
which is countably infinite. Note that it is weakly
\Gamma
-invariant.
Of course
\operatorname{Endog}(B)
is bounded.
By essential unboundedness, there are inequivalent
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
restricting to the same
\rho_{B}(\gamma_{i})
, meaning that
B\leq\ker(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})
, a contradiction to kernel finiteness. Therefore
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
is finite.

Now suppose that
\Gamma
is essentially bounded. By assumption,
\Delta
is not; so by the above,
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)
is finite. Now
\operatorname{kat}\gamma\leq\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)
for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, so there are only finitely many possible katakernels for elements of
\Gamma
. In particular, there are finitely many
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
equivalent to each
\gamma
.
Thus equivalence classes are finite, and there are only boundedly many of them by essential boundedness.
This proves that
\Gamma
is bounded, as a subset of
\operatorname{Endog}(A)
.

It is however essentially infinite. So for every
\delta\in\Delta
there is
\gamma\in\Gamma
inequivalent to
0
which annihilates the finite weakly
\Gamma
-invariant group
\operatorname{kat}\delta
, viz.,
\operatorname{kat}\delta\leq\ker\gamma
. This proves that
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)\leq\sum\{\ker\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\wedge\gamma\not\sim 0\}
. But the latter is bounded, and so is
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
, which is fully
\Delta
-invariant. Since
\Delta
is essentially unbounded, the argument from the first case implies finiteness of
\operatorname{Kat}(\Delta)
.

By symmetry,
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma)
is finite, as is
\operatorname{Kat}(\Gamma,\Delta)
.
∎

2.2.
Lines

The proof of Theorem A starts here;

,
\Gamma
and

Proposition 2.3
.

(1)

Any line is weakly

-invariant and virtually connected.

(2)

If
\gamma[A]\gg 0

.

(3)

Let

is contained in a finite sum of
\Gamma

(4)

If

\gamma^{*}\in\Gamma

(5)

All lines have the same dimension. If

(6)

If

\gamma\in\Gamma

Items
1
. and

. do

(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimality of

.

Proof.

(1)

This holds for all
\Gamma

and

Note that the connected component of a line might not be a line itself since it need not be a
\Gamma
-image of

(2)

Since
\Gamma

\Gamma

(3)

By Lemma
1.27

\gamma\in\Gamma

\gamma[L]
is virtually connected, and
\gamma[L]^{\circ}
is weakly
\Delta

-invariant; moreover it is a finite sum

We contend that

is weakly

Consider

\gamma\gamma_{i}[L]^{\circ}\leq\gamma[\gamma_{i}[L]^{\circ}]\leq\gamma\gamma_{i}[L],

Summing over

By

-minimality of

(4)

Let

be lines, with

and
L^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}[A]
, and consider
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
for

with

as in

.

(5)

If

(6)

We apply

Thus equality holds. The rest follows from

∎

2.3.
Local endogenies

Reduction to lines involves finding the suitable local analogues of

Let

(1)

Any

\check{\gamma}

\varphi:L\to L

\Delta_{L}
-covariant, then define

\gamma=\varphi\pi_{L}

.

Now let
\varphi:L\to L
be
\Gamma_{L}
-covariant. We need to find
\delta\in C(\Gamma)=\Delta
with
\check{\delta}=\varphi
.

For any line
L^{\prime}
choose
\gamma\in\Gamma

\gamma[L]=L^{\prime}
.
Then
\gamma

L^{\prime}\cong L
by

2
. we may assume that
\gamma\in\Gamma
is invertible. Now
\gamma\varphi\gamma^{-1}
leaves

invariant; let

be the induced endomorphism of
L^{\prime}
. If
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma

, then
\rho_{L}(\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{L}
commutes with
\varphi
. Thus

\gamma^{\prime}\varphi\gamma^{\prime-1}=\gamma(\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})\varphi\gamma^{\prime-1}=\gamma\varphi(\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime-1}=\gamma\varphi\gamma^{-1},

so
\varphi_{L^{\prime}}
does not depend on the choice of
\gamma
.

Consider non-zero
\check{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma_{L^{\prime}}
. By
2
we may assume that it is induced by an invertible
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma
stabilising
L^{\prime}
. Then
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\gamma
is another invertible element of
\Gamma

\gamma_{1}

Finally, let

3.4.
The induction

We can now prove Theorem B. We recall its statement.

Theorem B
.

In a finite-dimensional theory, let
A
be a definable, connected, abelian group. Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be two invariant rings of definable endomorphisms of
A
such that
A
is
(\Gamma,\Delta)
-minimal. Suppose that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
commute, both are infinite and one of them is unbounded. Then there is a definable field
\mathbb{K}
such that
A
is definably a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space of finite linear dimension, and both
\Gamma
and
\Delta
act
\mathbb{K}
-linearly.

Proof.

By Remark
3.2
.
2
. we may assume that
\Gamma=C(\Delta)
and
\Delta=C(\Gamma)
.
We shall use induction on the dimension
\dim A
.
If
A
has no proper
\Gamma
- or
\Delta
-lines, all endomorphisms in
\Gamma\cup\Delta
have finite kernel by dimensionality, and we finish by Proposition
3.3
.

Otherwise, we can apply the inductive hypothesis to any line
L
, with
C(\Delta_{L})\geq\Gamma_{L}
and
C(C(\Delta_{L}))\geq\Delta_{L}
acting on it. So there is a field
\mathbb{K}_{L}\leq C(\Delta_{L})\cap C(C(\Delta_{L}))
such that
L
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{K}_{L}
-vector space, on which both
C(\Delta_{L})
and
C(C(\Delta_{L}))
act
\mathbb{K}_{L}
-linearly. In particular all surjective endomorphisms in
C(\Delta_{L})\cup C(C(\Delta_{L}))
have trivial kernel. By Proposition
3.9
we have
C(\Delta_{L})=\Gamma_{L}
and
C(C(\Delta_{L}))=\Delta_{L}
, so
\mathbb{K}_{L}\leq\Gamma_{L}\cap\Delta_{L}
.

By Proposition
3.9
.
4
., for every
\varphi\in\Gamma_{L}\cap\Delta_{L}
there is
\hat{\varphi}\in\Gamma\cap\Delta
restricting to
\varphi
on

\mathbb{K}=\langle\hat{\varphi}:\varphi\in\mathbb{K}_{L}\rangle\subseteq\Gamma\cap\Delta
, a commutative subring. But the restriction-corestriction map
\rho_{L}:\Delta\to\Delta_{L}
is a bijection. It follows that
\mathbb{K}
is a definable field,
A
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{K}
-vector space, and both
\Gamma
and
\Delta
act
\mathbb{K}
-linearly.∎
