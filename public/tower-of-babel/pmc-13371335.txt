Traumatic stress and eating-related behaviours after the 6 February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Hakan Toğuç, Büşra Hökelek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLarge earthquakes may be associated with traumatic stress and eating-related outcomes, but evidence from Turkey remains limited. This study examined the associations between direct earthquake exposure, post-earthquake traumatic stress, and eating-related outcomes approximately two years after the 6 February 2023 Kahramanmaraş-centred earthquakes.MethodThis cross-sectional comparative online survey was conducted in Turkey between March and June 2025. Adults were classified as directly exposed (n = 602) or comparison (n = 615) based on residence in the 11 affected provinces and self-reported earthquake experience. Participants completed the Post-Earthquake Trauma Level Determination Scale (PETLDS), Salzburg Emotional Eating Scale (SEES), Mindful Eating Questionnaire (MEQ), and SCOFF eating disorder screening tool, together with sociodemographic and self-reported anthropometric measures. Adjusted group differences were estimated using multivariable regression models controlling for age, BMI, gender, marital status, education, employment, income status, and earthquake-related loss. Additional adjusted models examined whether PETLDS total score was independently associated with eating-related outcomes.ResultsDirect exposure was strongly associated with higher traumatic stress (PETLDS total: B = 12.30, 95%CI 10.66 to 13.94; p < 0.001) and with higher scores on most PETLDS subscales, whereas the PETLDS emotional subscale was higher in the comparison group (B=-1.09, 95%CI -1.37 to -0.81; p < 0.001). Direct exposure was not independently associated with SEES total score (p = 0.153), MEQ total score (p = 0.584), or a positive SCOFF screen (OR = 1.286, 95%CI 0.969 to 1.706; p = 0.081), although small exploratory subscale differences were observed. In additional adjusted models, higher PETLDS total score was associated with lower MEQ total score and greater odds of a positive SCOFF screen (both p < 0.001), but not with SEES total score.ConclusionDirect exposure was strongly associated with traumatic stress, whereas eating-related associations were limited. Traumatic stress severity was more clearly associated with lower mindful eating and higher eating disorder risk screening positivity, supporting trauma-informed post-disaster care and nutritional screening in highly stressed individuals.Keywords: Earthquake, Traumatic stress, Eating behaviours, Emotional eating, Eating disorder, Mindful eating

Introduction

Earthquakes are disasters that not only cause physical destruction but also have profound psychosocial consequences that can substantially alter individuals’ lives. Turkey is a country with a high risk of earthquakes due to its geographical location and has experienced major events throughout history resulting in significant loss of life and socioeconomic disruption [1]. On 6 February 2023, two major earthquakes (Mw 7.7 and Mw 7.6) occurred in succession in southeastern Turkey, centred in Kahramanmaraş, causing extensive loss of life and property across a wide geographical area covering 11 provinces, four of which were severely affected. This event has been recorded as one of the most destructive disasters in Turkish history. According to official data, over 50,000 people lost their lives, hundreds of thousands were injured, and millions faced severe hardships related to shelter, safety, and basic living needs [2]. Evidence indicates that such large-scale disasters may be associated with both acute and persistent psychological consequences in affected populations [3].

Trauma refers to the psychological response that can arise following exposure to an event involving serious threat and severe distress [4]. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is among the most common psychiatric conditions reported after natural disasters [5]. A range of disaster-related circumstances—including disruptions in living conditions and access to basic needs, property loss, bereavement, injury, entrapment under debris, low income, inadequate social support, and lower educational level—have been described as factors associated with PTSD risk [6]. Importantly, PTSD-related symptoms and post-traumatic stress may also be linked to changes in eating-related behaviours, particularly among women and individuals prone to emotional eating [5]. Stress responses following trauma can influence physiological and psychological aspects of eating, contributing to patterns such as overeating, appetite loss, irregular meal timing, and body-weight changes [7]. In a study conducted in Turkey, traumatic stress levels were significantly associated with emotional eating, uncontrolled eating, and insomnia among individuals who were directly or indirectly affected by an earthquake [8].

Eating behaviour is shaped not only by physiological hunger but also by psychological processes. Emotional eating, in particular, is described as eating in response to negative emotions and is often characterised by preference for energy-dense foods [9]. In post-trauma contexts, increases in such behaviours may co-occur with poorer mental well-being and may contribute over time to adverse physical health outcomes such as obesity [10]. Studies examining earthquake-related stress have similarly reported that higher stress levels are associated with greater tendency toward emotional eating [5, 8]. Beyond emotional eating, other eating-related patterns—including mindful eating behaviours and eating disorder risk screening indicators—may also vary in relation to traumatic stress and disaster exposure, warranting systematic evaluation.

The relationship between traumatic stress and eating behaviours may also be considered from a neurobiological perspective. Activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and stress-related increases in cortisol can influence appetite regulation mechanisms [11]. In addition, dysregulation in neurotransmitter systems such as serotonin and dopamine has been observed in individuals experiencing post-traumatic stress, and this dysregulation has been linked to alterations in eating-related behaviour [12].

In this context, it is important to examine how earthquake exposure and post-earthquake traumatic stress relate to eating behaviours following the 6 February Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. Research on the psychological consequences of large-scale disasters in Turkey has commonly focused on anxiety and depression, whereas changes in eating-related behaviours have been comparatively less studied [13]. Nevertheless, disruption of eating patterns after disasters constitutes an important public health issue, with implications for both physical and mental health [14]. Therefore, the aim of this study is to examine the association between earthquake exposure and post-earthquake traumatic stress levels and multiple eating-related outcomes, including emotional eating, mindful eating, and eating disorder risk screening indicators, and to provide evidence that may inform post-disaster nutrition-oriented and psychosocial support strategies.

Methods

Study setting and ethics

This cross-sectional online survey study was conducted between March and June 2025 in multiple cities across Turkey to examine associations between earthquake exposure, post-earthquake traumatic stress, and eating-related behaviours following the 6 February 2023 Kahramanmaraş-centred earthquakes. Ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically before respondents accessed the questionnaire.

Participants

Eligibility criteria included: (i) being ≥ 18 years of age, (ii) residing in Turkey, (iii) being a citizen of the Republic of Turkey, and (iv) providing informed consent. Individuals who did not meet the age criterion or declined participation were excluded.

Recruitment and data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire created in Google Forms. Participants were recruited through online convenience sampling via social media and messaging platforms (WhatsApp, Telegram, X, Instagram). The survey link was disseminated to 1,633 individuals, and 1,217 participants completed the questionnaire and were included in the analyses.

Measures

Sociodemographic information

Participants reported age, gender, city of residence, marital status, education level, employment status, monthly income status, smoking, and alcohol use.

Earthquake exposure and bereavement

Participants were asked whether they experienced the 6 February 2023 earthquakes and whether they lost a close relative due to the earthquakes. For the primary analyses, exposure was operationalised as described below (Study design and exposure definition). Loss of loved ones due to the earthquake was treated as an indicator of exposure severity and included in adjusted models.

Anthropometrics

Height and weight were self-reported. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2.

Post-earthquake trauma level determination scale (PETLDS)

PETLDS was developed by Tanhan and Kayri (2013) and comprises 20 items across five subdimensions: Sleep Problems, Cognitive Organisation, Emotional, Affective Limitations, and Behavioural Problems. Total scores range from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater earthquake-related distress/traumatic stress [15].

Salzburg emotional eating scale (SEES)

SEES was developed by Meule et al. (2018), and the Turkish validity and reliability were established by Gültekin and Bozkir (2025). The instrument includes 20 items across four subdimensions (happiness, sadness, anger, anxiety) [16, 17]. In this study, SEES outcomes were expressed as mean item scores (subscale totals divided by 5; total score divided by 20) to facilitate interpretability on the 1–5 response metric.

SCOFF (Eating disorder risk screening)

The SCOFF was developed by Morgan et al. (1999) and adapted to Turkish by Aydemir et al. (2015) [18, 19]. The SCOFF consists of five yes/no items scored 0–5 and is used as a screening tool for eating disorder (ED) risk, not as a diagnostic measure. Higher scores indicate higher ED risk screening levels.

Mindful eating questionnaire (MEQ)

The MEQ was developed by Framson et al. (2009) and adapted to Turkish by Köse et al. [20, 21]. The MEQ includes subscales for disinhibition, emotional eating, eating control, mindfulness, eating discipline, conscious nutrition, and interference (being influenced by external factors), and yields a total mindful eating score. Higher scores indicate higher mindful eating.

Study design and exposure definition

This study was designed as a cross-sectional comparative observational study. Participants were classified into two groups based on earthquake exposure. The directly exposed group comprised individuals who reported residing in one of the 11 provinces officially affected by the earthquakes (Adana, Adıyaman, Diyarbakır, Elazığ, Gaziantep, Hatay, Kahramanmaraş, Kilis, Malatya, Osmaniye, and Şanlıurfa) at the time of the event and who reported experiencing the earthquakes. The comparison group comprised individuals residing outside these affected provinces (not directly exposed). Given the potential for indirect exposure through nationwide disruption and media coverage, the comparison group was conceptualised as not directly exposed rather than unexposed.

A total of 1,217 participants were included: 602 in the directly exposed group and 615 in the comparison group. The distribution of participants by city is presented in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as n (%). For unadjusted comparisons (Tables 1, Table 2), categorical variables were analysed using the χ² test, and continuous variables using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Unadjusted associations between traumatic stress and eating-related measures were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients (Table 3). Correlation and subscale analyses were considered exploratory. Adjusted group differences by earthquake exposure (Table 2) were estimated using separate multivariable linear regression models for each PETLDS, SEES, MEQ, and SCOFF score outcome. Models were adjusted for age, BMI, gender, marital status, education, employment, income status, and earthquake-related loss. SEES outcomes were analysed as mean item scores (Table 4).

Results

Table 1 presents unadjusted participant characteristics by earthquake exposure. The directly exposed group included a higher proportion of females (78.24% vs. 49.76%) and was younger than the comparison group (22.40 ± 6.26 vs. 25.60 ± 8.98 years; both p < 0.001). A greater proportion of directly exposed participants were single (90.53% vs. 77.40%) and students (71.59% vs. 57.40%), whereas employment was more common in the comparison group (37.07% vs. 11.63%; p < 0.001). Mean BMI was lower in the directly exposed group (22.93 ± 4.50 vs. 24.24 ± 3.67 kg/m²; p < 0.001), and bereavement related to the earthquake was more frequently reported among directly exposed participants (21.10% vs. 3.90%; p < 0.001). Given these fundamental differences, unadjusted group comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive.

Table 2 presents observed means and revised adjusted group differences by earthquake exposure. After adjustment, direct exposure was strongly associated with higher post-earthquake traumatic stress, including PETLDS total score (B = 12.30, 95% CI 10.66 to 13.94; p < 0.001), as well as higher sleep problems, cognitive organisation difficulties, affective limitations, and behavioural problems (all p < 0.001). In contrast, the PETLDS emotional subscale was higher in the comparison group (B = -1.09, 95% CI -1.37 to -0.81; p < 0.001). For emotional eating, SEES total score was not associated with exposure after adjustment (B = -0.08, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.03; p = 0.153), although small exploratory differences were observed for anger (B = -0.16, p = 0.015) and anxiety (B = -0.14, p = 0.049), with slightly higher scores in the comparison group. MEQ total score was not associated with exposure (B = 0.02, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.07; p = 0.584), whereas small exploratory subscale differences were observed, including higher eating control and interference and lower mindfulness and conscious nutrition in the directly exposed group. For SCOFF score, a small adjusted difference was observed in the linear model (B = 0.16, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.30; p = 0.030). Across eating-related outcomes, explained variance was modest, indicating that these associations were generally small in magnitude.

Table 3 shows unadjusted bivariate correlations between PETLDS and eating-related measures and should be interpreted as exploratory. Overall, correlations were small. PETLDS total score was not significantly correlated with SEES total score (r = 0.017, p = 0.546), although a trivial positive correlation was observed with SEES happiness (r = 0.056, p = 0.049). In contrast, PETLDS total score was inversely correlated with MEQ total score (r = -0.153, p < 0.001) and several MEQ subscales, including disinhibition, emotional eating, conscious nutrition, and eating control. PETLDS total score was also positively correlated with SCOFF score (r = 0.246, p < 0.001).

Table 4 presents unadjusted descriptive comparisons by gender. Females had higher PETLDS total scores than males (43.95 ± 15.30 vs. 36.92 ± 12.29; p < 0.001) and higher scores on sleep problems, cognitive organisation, affective limitations, and behavioural problems (all p < 0.001), whereas the PETLDS emotional subscale was slightly higher in males (10.88 ± 2.17 vs. 10.45 ± 2.20; p = 0.001). For SEES, females scored higher on sadness (2.65 ± 1.14 vs. 2.47 ± 0.98; p = 0.004), while SEES total score did not differ significantly by gender. For MEQ, males scored higher on the emotional eating subscale (3.69 ± 0.91 vs. 3.37 ± 1.00; p < 0.001), whereas females scored higher on eating control, conscious nutrition, and interference. MEQ total score did not differ by gender (p = 0.891). Females also had higher SCOFF scores than males (1.27 ± 1.19 vs. 0.89 ± 1.08; p < 0.001). These comparisons were unadjusted and are presented descriptively.

Table 5 presents multivariable associations of earthquake exposure and sociodemographic variables with the main study outcomes. In Model 1, direct earthquake exposure was strongly associated with higher PETLDS total score (B = 12.248, 95% CI 10.659 to 13.837; p < 0.001). Male gender was associated with lower PETLDS scores (B = -2.976, p < 0.001), whereas higher educational status (B = 1.985, p = 0.014) and loss of loved ones due to the earthquake (B = 4.675, p < 0.001) were associated with higher PETLDS scores; higher income was associated with lower PETLDS scores (B = -1.801, p < 0.001). In Model 2, direct exposure was not independently associated with SEES total score (B = -0.098, 95% CI -0.202 to 0.005; p = 0.063). Lower age, female gender, higher income, and higher BMI classification were associated with higher SEES scores. In Model 3, direct exposure was not associated with MEQ total score (B = 0.016, 95% CI -0.036 to 0.067; p = 0.547). Lower BMI classification and being married were associated with higher MEQ total scores. In Model 4, which examined a positive SCOFF screen (score ≥ 2) using logistic regression, direct earthquake exposure was not independently associated with screening positivity (OR = 1.286, 95% CI 0.969 to 1.706; p = 0.081). Higher BMI was associated with greater odds of a positive SCOFF screen (OR = 1.200, 95% CI 1.155 to 1.246; p < 0.001), while male gender was associated with lower odds (OR = 0.382, 95% CI 0.282 to 0.516; p < 0.001). Overall, the PETLDS model explained a substantially larger proportion of variance than the eating-related models, whose explanatory power remained modest.

To better align the analyses with the study aim, additional adjusted models were fitted using PETLDS total score as the main predictor of eating-related outcomes (Table 6). In these models, PETLDS total score was not independently associated with SEES total score after adjustment (B = 0.0036, 95% CI -0.0007 to 0.0080; p = 0.101). However, higher PETLDS total score was associated with lower MEQ total score (B = -0.0056, 95% CI -0.0076 to -0.0036; p < 0.001) and with higher odds of a positive SCOFF screen (OR = 1.0329, 95% CI 1.0224 to 1.0436; p < 0.001). These additional models suggest that, after covariate adjustment, traumatic stress showed clearer associations with mindful eating and eating disorder risk screening than with overall emotional eating.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between direct exposure to the earthquake, post-earthquake traumatic stress and various eating-related outcomes approximately two years after the earthquakes centred in Kahramanmaraş on 6 February 2023. Overall, the clearest and most consistent finding was that direct exposure to the earthquake was strongly associated with higher levels of traumatic stress. Furthermore, similar associations were found between direct exposure and eating-related outcomes. Furthermore, in adjusted models, post-earthquake traumatic stress itself demonstrated clearer associations with mindful eating and eating disorder risk screening compared to general emotional eating. These findings support the interpretation that traumatic stress represents the strongest post-disaster correlation in the current dataset, whilst eating-related outcomes, though limited, are also significant.

Traumatic stress and earthquake exposure

After adjustment for sociodemographic factors, BMI, employment, income, and earthquake-related loss, directly exposed participants had substantially higher PETLDS total scores and higher scores on sleep problems, cognitive organisation, affective limitations, and behavioural problems. This pattern is consistent with previous literature showing that major disasters are associated with elevated post-traumatic stress symptoms, including sleep disruption, impaired functioning, and broader behavioural distress [3, 5, 22]. It is also compatible with post-earthquake findings from Turkey indicating that traumatic stress remains associated with sleep-related and behavioural difficulties beyond the acute phase [8, 22].

A noteworthy finding was that the PETLDS emotional subscale differed in direction from the other PETLDS dimensions, with higher scores observed in the comparison group. Because this finding diverged from the remaining PETLDS dimensions, the scoring direction of the emotional subscale was rechecked before interpretation. Because this pattern contrasts with the total scale and the remaining subscales, it should be interpreted cautiously. One possible explanation is that emotional reactions may have remained elevated in indirectly affected individuals through prolonged nationwide media exposure, social disruption, ongoing uncertainty, and broader psychological strain, even in the absence of direct residence in the affected region [23]. Another possibility is that this subscale may capture a somewhat different dimension of emotional appraisal or expression than the other PETLDS components. For this reason, the emotional subscale result should not be overinterpreted in isolation, but rather viewed as an unexpected pattern that warrants further psychometric and contextual examination in future studies.

Emotional eating (SEES)

In the adjusted exposure models, direct earthquake exposure was not independently associated with overall emotional eating. Although small exploratory differences were observed for anger and anxiety subscales, the SEES total score did not differ significantly between groups. Moreover, in the additional adjusted models using PETLDS total score as the main predictor, traumatic stress was also not independently associated with SEES total score. Taken together, these findings suggest that neither direct exposure status nor traumatic stress severity showed a robust relationship with overall emotional eating in this sample.

This pattern may indicate that emotional eating in the post-disaster context is shaped by a broader constellation of factors beyond disaster exposure alone, including general psychological distress, habitual coping style, depressive symptoms, perceived stress, food environment, and individual predisposition to emotional eating [7, 23]. Kuijer and Boyce reported that increased food intake after disaster exposure was more evident among individuals already prone to emotional eating [7], which is consistent with the possibility that disaster-related stress may amplify pre-existing tendencies rather than independently determine them. Similarly, Turkish studies conducted after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes have reported links between trauma-related distress and dysregulated eating-related patterns [8, 22]. However, in the present study, the adjusted associations for SEES were small, the variance explained was modest, and the overall emotional eating score remained non-significant. Accordingly, the practical significance of the emotional eating findings should be considered limited.

Mindful eating (MEQ)

Direct earthquake exposure was not independently associated with MEQ total score, although exploratory differences emerged in several subscales. Specifically, directly exposed participants showed slightly lower mindfulness and conscious nutrition scores and slightly higher eating control and interference scores. These subscale-level findings may reflect subtle alterations in eating awareness, attention, or external influence on eating under conditions of post-traumatic strain and disrupted routines. Nevertheless, because these effects were small and emerged mainly in exploratory subscale analyses, they should be interpreted cautiously rather than as strong indicators of broad impairment in mindful eating.

Importantly, the additional adjusted models provided clearer evidence at the traumatic stress level than at the exposure level: higher PETLDS total score was independently associated with lower MEQ total score. This finding suggests that traumatic stress severity itself may be more relevant to mindful eating than simple direct-versus-comparison group classification. This interpretation is consistent with prior work suggesting that trauma-related symptoms may be linked to reduced self-regulation, impaired attentional control, and more automatic or less reflective eating behaviours [8, 24, 25]. Even so, the magnitude of the association was modest, and the findings are more supportive of a limited statistical relationship than of a strong clinical determinant.

ED risk screening (SCOFF)

In the revised exposure analyses using binary logistic regression with the conventional SCOFF threshold, direct earthquake exposure was not independently associated with a positive SCOFF screen after adjustment. Thus, although the linear SCOFF score in Table 3 showed a small adjusted difference, this was not supported in the more clinically interpretable binary screening model. Accordingly, the present data do not provide strong evidence that direct earthquake exposure alone was independently associated with a positive ED risk screen approximately two years after the disaster.

At the same time, the additional adjusted models showed that higher PETLDS total score was associated with greater odds of a positive SCOFF screen. This suggests that traumatic stress severity, rather than exposure status per se, may be more closely related to eating disorder risk screening indicators in this sample. Such a pattern is plausible in light of literature indicating that trauma-related psychopathology may be associated with restrictive eating, avoidance, loss of appetite, or other maladaptive eating-related responses [5, 25, 26]. However, the present findings remain observational, screening-based, and cross-sectional. Therefore, they should not be interpreted as evidence that earthquake exposure causes eating disorder pathology, but rather as indicating that elevated traumatic stress may co-occur with higher ED risk screening positivity in some individuals.

Gender and sociodemographic patterns

Unadjusted descriptive comparisons revealed higher traumatic stress scores among women; this finding is consistent with previous disaster literature indicating that post-traumatic stress symptoms are more severe in women [27, 28]. Whilst women also had higher SCOFF scores and emotional eating behaviours associated with sadness, men scored higher on the MEQ emotional eating subscale. In multivariate models, several sociodemographic correlations were observed; however, the explanatory power of the models relating to eating remained modest. A higher BMI was consistently associated with an increased likelihood of greater emotional eating and a positive SCOFF screening result, whilst a lower BMI classification was associated with greater mindful eating. Male gender was associated with lower levels of traumatic stress and a lower likelihood of a positive SCOFF screening result. These patterns are generally consistent with the literature linking weight status, self-regulatory eating processes and stress-related vulnerability [5].

Implications

Current findings strongly support the importance of trauma-informed psychosocial approaches in post-disaster settings. Given the robust and consistent link between direct exposure and traumatic stress, routine screening for post-traumatic stress symptoms and timely referral pathways for psychosocial support appear to be well-founded. From a nutritional perspective, the findings suggest that post-disaster care should not be limited to general food access and basic nutritional adequacy, but should also take into account trauma-related changes in eating behaviour and dietary patterns. Although direct exposure to the earthquake was not independently associated with widespread disturbances in all eating-related outcomes, higher traumatic stress was linked to less mindful eating and an increased likelihood of screening for positive eating disorder (ED) risk. This pattern suggests that nutritional monitoring may be particularly important for individuals with high traumatic stress, even if overt eating-related problems are not immediately apparent. In this study, dietary monitoring may be particularly important for subgroups that appear more vulnerable, such as those with a high BMI and a high burden of traumatic stress. However, as the observed associations with eating were generally modest, these results should be interpreted as supporting targeted and integrated screening rather than broad assumptions that earthquake exposure alone leads to significant eating-related pathologies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference about causality, and no pre-earthquake baseline data on traumatic stress or eating-related behaviours were available. Secondly, participants were recruited through online convenience sampling, resulting in a relatively young and student-dominated sample, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Thirdly, earthquake exposure was determined on the basis of self-reported experience and place of residence at the time of the disaster; accordingly, the comparison group should be regarded as not directly exposed rather than wholly unexposed, as indirect exposure through nationwide disruption, media coverage, and broader social consequences remained possible. Fourthly, height and weight were self-reported and may therefore be subject to reporting bias. Finally, the modest explained variance observed for eating-related outcomes suggests that other unmeasured psychological and contextual factors, including depression, coping style, social support, food insecurity, and ongoing stressors, may also have contributed to the observed associations.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations between direct earthquake exposure, post-earthquake traumatic stress, and eating-related outcomes approximately two years after the 6 February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. The most robust finding was that direct exposure was strongly associated with higher traumatic stress across the PETLDS total score and most subscales. By contrast, direct exposure showed limited independent associations with overall eating-related outcomes after adjustment. In particular, direct exposure was not independently associated with overall emotional eating, overall mindful eating, or a positive SCOFF screen in the revised main models. Additional adjusted analyses suggested that traumatic stress severity may be more informative than exposure status alone for selected eating-related outcomes. Higher PETLDS total score was associated with lower mindful eating and greater odds of a positive SCOFF screen, whereas no independent association was observed with overall emotional eating. Taken together, these findings suggest that the clearest post-disaster signal in the present study is the burden of traumatic stress, while eating-related outcomes appear more heterogeneous, modest in magnitude, and likely influenced by additional psychological and contextual factors not captured here.

Overall, the findings support trauma-informed post-disaster care in which psychosocial assessment remains central, while nutrition-related screening and support may be particularly relevant for individuals with elevated traumatic stress or other vulnerability indicators. Rather than suggesting that earthquake exposure alone leads to broad eating-related pathology, the present results support a more targeted interpretation: post-disaster traumatic stress may identify subgroups in whom mindful eating difficulties and eating disorder risk screening positivity warrant closer attention. Longitudinal studies with more comprehensive psychological and contextual measurement are needed to clarify temporal relationships, mechanisms, and clinical relevance.
