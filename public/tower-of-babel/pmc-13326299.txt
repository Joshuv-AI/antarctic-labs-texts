Development of ‘Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life’, a service delivery intervention to eliminate unwarranted variation in home dialysis uptake between kidney services and to optimise home dialysis use
Authors: Louise Weight, Iestyn Williams, Hilary L. Bekker, David Coyle, James Fotheringham, Harry Hill, Mark Lambie, Sarah Damery, Kerry Allen, Ivonne Solis-Trapala, Lisa Dikomitis, Simon J. Davies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo introduce a new service delivery intervention to improve equity of access to home dialysis therapy and describe the process of intervention development.BackgroundDespite strong evidence in favour of home dialysis, its uptake remains stubbornly low in England and elsewhere. Furthermore, uptake levels vary between kidney services and between population groups. The Inter-CEPt study identified several enablers of access to home dialysis, including: receptive organisational cultures; adoption of reflective practice; engagement in quality improvement, and shared belief in the benefit of home dialysis.MethodsDrawing on the Inter-CEPt study, and using established intervention development frameworks, we developed ‘Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life’ as a targeted intervention for improving the uptake of home dialysis. In this paper, the intervention and the processes involved in its design are described. This involved co-design workshops with professional stakeholders and people with lived experience.Proposed service interventionThe Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life intervention consists of two principal components: (1) quality improvement activities focussed on supporting dialysis care in the home, and (2) dedicated home therapies leadership roles and activities in kidney services. The rationale of the intervention is to develop and sustain a culture that improves equitable access to home dialysis.ConclusionWe report a staged process of intervention development that combines the principles of evidence-based intervention development and user co-design. The resulting intervention proposal can be adapted by kidney services to meet their specific needs and challenges. The study identifies organisational culture as one of the biggest determinants of uptake in home dialysis. No other study to our knowledge has fully investigated this as a barrier to home dialysis, nor indeed how to begin to change it. The Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life intervention requires future piloting and evaluation.

Background

In the United Kingdom people with kidney failure are offered three types of treatment to manage their long-term condition: transplantation, dialysis and conservative management [1]. Most choose a dialysis treatment, which can include (a) haemodialysis, managed either in a health care facility (‘in-centre’) or at home (‘home haemodialysis’) or (b) peritoneal dialysis which is always managed at home [2]. In 2021 the Renal Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) report highlighted the benefits of access to home-based forms of dialysis, proposing a minimum target of 20% of dialysis patients [3]. An economic evaluation conducted by the authors confirmed that home dialysis is associated with health gains (quality adjusted life years) and cost savings [4]. Despite this, rates in England remain low, and there is considerable variation between services and by population subgroups [5]. People living in more socio-economically deprived areas and those from black, Asian, mixed race and minority ethnic (‘ethnic minority’) populations are less likely to have home dialysis [6, 7].

The ‘Intervening to eliminate the centre-effect variation in home dialysis use’ study (or ‘Inter-CEPt’) [8] was designed to address the twin problems of variable levels of home dialysis uptake, and inequity across population groups. It aimed to understand the reasons for current uptake levels focussing on kidney service provider organisations (or ‘centres’), with the purpose of developing a service intervention[9] to improve access to home dialysis.

The first stage of Inter-CEPt was an ethnography of kidney services which sought to understand approaches to home dialysis, exploring the behaviours of staff, interactions with service users, and cultures within services. This involved semi-structured interviews with staff and people with kidney failure exploring experiences, treatment choices and organisational practices and cultures [8]. Following this, we conducted a survey of all 52 dialysis centres in England[10] to identify and explore correlations between home dialysis uptake and characteristics of kidney services identified in the prior ethnography. Statistical modelling of the associations between these characteristics and home dialysis uptake among people with kidney failure was then undertaken using linked survey data and UK Renal Registry (UKRR) patient data [11]. These multistate models were then used within a health economic model to enable appraisal of the proposed intervention in terms of health benefits (quality-adjusted life years) and costs, and therefore the likely cost-effectiveness over the lifetime of people with kidney failure.

This paper describes the methods used to develop a service delivery intervention to improve uptake of home dialysis, based on findings from the Inter-CEPt study and a process of intervention co-development. The resulting ‘Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life’ (LOCAL) intervention is then described.

Methods

The complex nature of dialysis and its impact on the people’s lives mean any intervention requires a thorough understanding of the specific stressors on people with kidney failure, their carers and those providing services. Rather than being ‘one size fits all’, interventions should be designed to enable flexibility of application and adaptation to each service’s needs and circumstances. The development of the intervention was informed by the MRC framework guidance for how to develop complex interventions [12]. We devised a series of iterative activities which enabled us to draw on principles of user-centred design [13] and behaviour change theory, as well as the unique insights provided by the Inter-CEPt study results. Over 12 months the team planned the steps required to move from evidence generation to intervention prototype. There were five stages in the development of the intervention. These involved:

Stage 1. Synthesising Inter-CEPt findings and mapping these to a behaviour change framework.

Stage 2. First co-design workshop to develop intervention components.

Stage 3. Drafting the initial intervention.

Stage 4. Discussion and refinement of the intervention at a second co-design workshop.

Stage 5. Finalising the intervention for subsequent piloting and evaluation.

Each of these are described below, with the intervention and its components then fully described.

Stage one: applying research findings

The Inter-CEPt study identified some consistent findings with regards to the organisational factors that are most important in influencing uptake of home dialysis, and these have been extensively reported elsewhere [8, 10, 11]. A summary of these is factors provided here and represented in Fig. 1.

Taking a person-centred approach: the ethnography [8] suggested that kidney services with good overall performance in home dialysis uptake tended to focus on ‘the right dialysis for the right people’ rather than on increasing home therapy uptake per se. This was combined with a shared belief in the value of home dialysis for people with kidney failure and a presumption that it would be possible to deliver care at home if this was the preference. Findings from the survey of kidney services and statistical modelling at the person level confirmed that clinical leadership and staff attitudes to home dialysis were associated with uptake levels [10, 11]. The ethnography also underlined the importance of taking a flexible approach, for example, regularly reviewing decisions about where to undertake dialysis to ensure that people with kidney failure were using the approach best suited to them at that time.

Innovation and improvement: the ethnography found that high performing services sought support from a range of sources including specialists, families of people with kidney failure, other services, and community resources. They also recognised and attempted to address barriers faced by under-represented groups, and to work with relevant stakeholder organisations on behalf of these. The high-performing case study centres all demonstrated active engagement in service improvement initiatives (hereafter called ‘quality improvement’ or ‘QI’). This finding was echoed in the survey and statistical modelling which revealed that patients treated in centres that had engaged in a home dialysis QI initiatives in the previous five years had twice the odds of receiving home dialysis [10, 11].

Other factors associated with home dialysis uptake: this ability to take a flexible, person-centred, problem-solving approach was dependent to some extent on time and resources. For example, our statistical modelling showed that people treated in centres reporting stress associated with a lack of home dialysis staff capacity had almost half the odds of getting dialysis at home [11]. Centres that offered Assisted Peritoneal Dialysis, in which a paid assistant undertakes a supportive role in enabling the treatment, doubled the odds of their patients getting home dialysis. People with kidney failure had greater odds of getting home dialysis when treated by services that hosted ‘home dialysis roadshows’ in the community. These are events in which staff and people with lived experience of dialysis show-case home dialysis to those considering which dialysis modality to choose or to those considering a change in the location of their treatment.

Overall, Inter-CEPt study results suggest a key role played by organisational culture in providing a supportive environment in which home dialysis therapies are promoted, valued and adequately resourced. This, in turn, is linked to supportive and effective leadership at the centre level. We sought to apply these principles and insights to the intervention development process.

Stage two: first co-design workshop

We then held the first of two co-design workshop with stakeholders including people and carers with lived experience of dialysis (n = 6), kidney doctors (n = 5), nurses (n = 4), other staff (n = 2) and representatives from industry (n = 2) [13]. The aim was to discuss the Inter-CEPt study results and identify other potentially important factors to consider in any intervention. We also used the discussion to focus on how the needs of populations from ethnic minority and/or lower socio-economic populations could be addressed.

To support the discussions, a plain language summary of Inter-CEPt study findings was shared in the weeks before the workshop and an online pre-session briefing meeting was held with attending people with lived experience of dialysis. The workshop was held over three hours at a venue in central Birmingham in March 2023. At the beginning of the workshop, participants received a short verbal and visual presentation of Inter-CEPt study findings and were then asked to discuss possible ways to tackle the main factors found to affect levels of home dialysis uptake. Data from the workshop were gathered in the form of notes by the research team, audio recordings and notes from the participants. A written summary of these notes and points of agreement was shared with participants ahead of the second workshop (summarised in Table 2).

Summary points of discussion from stakeholder workshop 1

The primary topic of discussion centred around how services might seek to change organisational culture to become more receptive to supporting home dialysis uptake. It was noted that culture change couldn’t be imposed but rather required intervention at multiple levels within organisations. To facilitate organisational change, it was agreed that teams require individuals with leadership skills and that this leadership needed to be shared and supported across wider kidney services and networks, with open discussions on areas for change and improvement, and with teams working towards a blame-free culture. It was also considered essential that staff working in kidney services felt properly supported to make changes and improvements.

Stage three: intervention drafting

The Inter-CEPt research team held a series of working meetings designed to further connect the various influencing factors with potential behaviour change strategies. To help scaffold the findings and identify factors in the kidney care context that may bias or facilitate reasoning, we drew on the Making Informed Decisions Individually and Together (MIND-IT) framework [15]. This conceptual framework is informed by decision science approaches to analysing the decision context, recognising the agency of each person involved in making the same decision, and that they have different goals, values and needs to address the problem from their differing perspectives. This exercise enabled us to define the roles and practices of multiple stakeholders in making dialysis decisions across kidney care and daily life, to consider the factors impacting on their decision making, and to identify active ingredients or mechanism of change at micro, meso and macro levels (Fig. 2) [15].

Based on Inter-CEPt analysis of where most gains could be made [11], combined with the themes from the workshops, we developed an initial description of an intervention involving principal elements of home dialysis quality improvement and dedicated home dialysis leadership roles. Rapid evidence reviews were then conducted using PubMed and Embase to identify existing evidence on the effectiveness, costs and implementation of such interventions in similar health care settings. Results were restricted to the UK NHS within the period 2015–2023 and were used to help define core elements of the intervention, for example the characteristics and activities of ‘leaders’ and ‘champions’ involved in quality improvement [16–19].

Stage four: second co-design workshop

A plain language description of the proposed intervention was then shared with participants ahead of a second workshop. As before, a pre-briefing session was held for participants with lived experience of dialysis to facilitate their subsequent involvement in the workshop. Pre-workshop information included an explanation of the underpinning logic model and how the draft intervention was intended to address each of the elements identified as important in the first workshop. The second workshop was held in June 2023, with the same participants who attended the first workshop, this time at the University of Birmingham. The aims were to introduce and discuss the proposed intervention elements (see Table 3) with a view to either rejecting them and identifying alternative approaches or approving and refining them. Participants received a verbal and visual presentation on the outline proposal for the intervention components as well as early findings from the health economic modelling. The workshop discussions generated insights into how the intervention might be adapted for implementation in different organisations and helped to set the proposed interventions in the context of wider lived experience and community networks, as well as funding and incentive structures.

Stage five: refining the service delivery intervention

Following the second workshop the research team met to create a refined and more detailed description of the overall intervention (see Fig. 4). A plain language report was also drafted explaining the intervention development process and the intervention itself, which was given the name ‘Location of Dialysis Care in Kidney Life’ (LOCAL). A further Patient Advisory Group meeting was convened to discuss the intervention, resulting in further additions and amendments. It was then presented at a national meeting attended by approximately 200 kidney services staff, people with lived experience and carers and further and final adjustments made, in particular to clarify the importance of lived experience input in the development and delivery of the dedicated role(s).

Proposed service intervention

This intervention design process resulted in the development of the LOCAL service delivery intervention. LOCAL is designed to recognise the various roles, groups and resources that can be mobilised to make the best use of home dialysis. Principal Component 1: service improvement

The first component of the LOCAL intervention involves the implementation of a bespoke QI programme drawing on established improvement methodologies to target enhanced and equitable uptake of home dialysis within kidney services. It was agreed that kidney services could utilise existing QI skills and resources within their organisations and/or networks to create a bespoke package of change to fit their development needs. Our stakeholder discussions found that prescribing a specific improvement methodology was not appropriate, but rather that teams should engage with existing, established methods and resources [19–23] including decision aids, training in shared decision making, and peer group support [24, 25].

Enablers required for the successful adoption of this component include: engaging with all staff both within the centre and the wider stakeholders such as commissioners; knowledge of uptake rates and local factors and barriers influencing this; funding/capacity to allow staff time to engage with improvement, and; suitable training in improvement methodologies. Although individual kidney services should identify their own specific areas for service development, evidence from the Inter-CEPt study suggests the following areas should be considered:

Empowering patients, their families and carers: incorporation within the care pathway of tools that support empowerment, e.g. shared haemodialysis care, shared decision making, decision aids, peer support, incremental approaches to taking responsibility or supporting dialysis at home (Assisted Peritoneal Dialysis), as well as ensuring that immediate socio-economic barriers are removed (e.g. utility bills).Embedding a supportive culture for home dialysis: including via training in shared decision making and identification of unconscious bias, and training of non-specialist staff in the benefits of home dialysis.Optimising the care pathway: service improvements that focus on creating a smooth transition from chronic kidney disease care to dialysis initiation that includes specific pathway modifications for unplanned or urgent start: e.g. non-surgical PD catheter insertion, urgent start PD, audit of pathway using established tools [26]. Audit of treatment complications to minimise treatment failure (e.g. PD related infections, and psychological support to help with treatment burnout).Focussing on lived experience and community engagement: engaging with community organisations, and recognition that attitudes and concerns related to having kidney failure will vary.

Principal Component 2: dedicated roles

The development of staff roles dedicated to enhancing home dialysis usage was supported by the study and co-design workshops. These roles, designed to promote distributed leadership within the multidisciplinary team, would aim to bring about change by focusing on factors such as increasing staff knowledge across the centre, growing awareness of value and expertise in home dialysis, improving ways for staff to engage with patients in dialysis modality choice, engaging with staff in QI and coordinating services to support improved home dialysis uptake (see Fig. 6).

The roles would involve advising, supporting, connecting and advocacy with regards to home dialysis, as well as addressing priorities highlighted in the QI process. Workshop participants agreed that these roles could either be shared amongst the multi-disciplinary team or assigned to one individual. It was also agreed that they should be predominantly assigned to people within the organisation or existing multidisciplinary teams, representing a ring-fenced part of their existing job plan, rather than requiring an external appointment, although some components could be shared across multiple centres via local networks.

Co-design partners felt strongly that the roles should focus on centre culture and how this facilitates choices over location of care and that the roles would need to be attractive to incentivise staff involvement, for example by ensuring career development within the role. Another key recommendation was ensuring skill development and training was provided for the role to be effective. Finally, the roles should embrace co-production with people with lived experience of dialysis at every point of service provision as well as linking with charities and networks, with an ability to work across organisational silos. Each kidney service would need to identify the extent of change required to decide the number of people employed in these roles, recognising that they might be part-time or temporary. Stakeholders also felt that some roles could be shared across centres if needed, e.g. via regional networks.

Enablers of successful adoption of this element of the intervention include making resources and staff available to fund the roles, a supportive leadership, buy-in from wider kidney networks and availability of training resources. Key activities that this role should perform were identified in the stakeholder workshops and have been mapped to behaviour change techniques (see Fig. 6). The role(s) should signpost to the multidisciplinary team, available resources, education, renal networks, and decision aids.

Other intervention elements

These two components – active quality improvement and dedicated roles – are the principal LOCAL intervention mechanisms of change. However, Inter-CEPt study findings and our engagement with lived experience and professional stakeholders indicate that the following are important additional elements of a successful home dialysis programme:

Assisted Peritoneal Dialysis: although this is now delivered by most centres in England it is included here as it remains absent from a small number of centres, despite its association with higher home dialysis uptake [11]. This modality has the clear benefit of enabling people from an older demographic with more comorbidity and other healthcare challenges to manage at home. It is also likely that centres who integrated this modality within their services (a) provide a workflow supporting its discussion and management within current service provision and (b) have identified additional resources within their service to support respite care, changing family circumstances or achieving urgent start or helping patients gradually cover their independence. Clearly there are costs to ensuring home dialysis options are supported, equitable and used effectively by services.Home dialysis roadshows: as noted, a roadshow is an event designed to showcase different treatment options for patients and their families who are considering dialysis. It provides an opportunity to meet others with lived experience of dialysis using different modalities and see what home dialysis entails, including the dialysis technology. Services and organisations providing these roadshows ensure a multidisciplinary team provides information, skills training and guidance enabling patients and their families to explore kidney care management at home, including dialysis. The Inter-CEPt patient advisory group and collaborators endorsed the benefits of demonstrating kidney care options outside of the hospital context and in the community. Services and organisations providing these roadshows ensure a multidisciplinary team provides information, skills training and guidance enabling patients and their families to explore kidney care management at home, including dialysis. The Inter-CEPt patient advisory group and collaborators endorsed the benefits of demonstrating kidney care options outside of the hospital context and in the community. There is further evidence from Inter-CEPt that these events are associated with a positive effect on home-therapy uptake [11].

Networks and infrastructure: implementing the LOCAL intervention can be supported by wider networks within the hospital, region and nationally. For example, innovations in service can be linked to hospital quality improvement processes and regional kidney networks can play an important role in monitoring outcomes of quality improvement, facilitating exchange of good practice, and helping to mobilise resources alongside commissioning bodies.

Discussion

Inter-CEPt study findings suggest that there is no single mechanism or approach that can be replicated across multiple kidney services to increase home dialysis usage because of the variation between them and the challenges they face. Kidney services clearly vary in structure, relationships, funding and staff make-up which means that interventions need to be multifaceted, flexible and continually evaluated to have a long-term effect. The evidence supports a focus on actions that facilitate a culture that enables patient-centred practice and quality improvement, and that achieving this requires the time and resource to ‘step back’ from the day to day running of the service. In this regard, Inter-CEPt and the proposed LOCAL intervention align with recent research emphasizing the importance of co-production [27] but differ in that they focus on developing a culture that enables the use of existing tools, such as quality improvement, co-production, peer support, adequate staffing to name but some. Although these intervention components have clear resource implications, our economic analysis indicates that because of the cost-effectiveness of peritoneal dialysis [4] the interventions could have modest costs and still be considered cost-effective.

The LOCAL intervention is designed to be useful in a range of settings, recognising that kidney teams and services vary in the way they are organised and have different resources to draw on, facing a variety of challenges when considering how to improve use of home dialysis. It is important to recognise that each kidney service is distinctive, working with a team made up of different specialists and expertise, a patient population with varied needs and resources, and a service infrastructure with, for example, different quality improvement structures. LOCAL is intended to empower teams, irrespective of these differences, to present patients with the full range of treatment options, and support services to meet improvement priorities. An important underlying principle is that the focus during treatment modality selection should be on the optimal location of care, with all options available to every group, rather than advocating for specific treatment modalities. Not all patients will wish to undergo home dialysis, but they should be given equitable opportunities and support to choose the best option for them, recognising the need to revisit management choices in light of any changes in their clinical and life circumstances. Success would be measured by a reduction in the large variation in home dialysis uptake currently observed. The LOCAL intervention is intended to enable kidney services to achieve this change. It requires teams to identify where the local barriers and good practices might be within their services, and empower staff, patients and relatives to do things differently where necessary.

The Inter-CEPt study findings highlight the need to focus on organisational culture. In this, we have sought to address not only the specific barriers and behaviours observed in our study, but also those documented in previous research. For example, a mixed methods case study by Combes et al. [28] identified four factors associated with increased uptake of home dialysis: a commissioning target linked with financial penalties; additional funding and equipment; visible clinical champions; and good systems of training and ongoing patient support. They concluded that further improvements in individualised patient education and emotional support were required. Similarly, in Canada, the Ontario Renal Network identified four key enablers of home dialysis, and 13 specific interventions based on evidence from literature reviews, expert panels and collaboration with staff and people with lived experience of dialysis [29]. The LOCAL intervention reflects the need for local ownership, willingness to innovate and, where necessary, change infrastructure and processes. It is also clear that the intervention will require leadership and management, as well as the dedication of some resources to have a significant impact. Our health economics programme of work first sought to determine if home dialysis is cost-effective, which was found to be the case in a range of scenarios [4]. Therefore, the intervention can incur costs while increasing home dialysis use and still be cost-neutral to the NHS or represent good value for money.

The LOCAL intervention has yet to be implemented and therefore should be subject to pilot and evaluation prior to being considered for wider adoption [14]. Our initial cost analysis suggests that implementation will require either additional resources or some reallocation of current resources. The LOCAL intervention is based on practices ethnographically observed in current kidney services, co-designed with practitioners, and is designed to be adaptable to local contexts and resources. Our quantitative analysis suggests that if implemented successfully, LOCAL has the potential to be both effective and cost effective in improving the use of home dialysis. Ensuring acceptability to those implementing the intervention has been the aim of multiple rounds of engagement and deliberation with people with lived experience, carers and kidney services. However, we recognise that this can only be gauged fully in subsequent evaluation. It is not possible, at this pre-implementation stage, to anticipate all potential unintended effects of introducing this service delivery intervention. However, the underpinning logic of LOCAL is to build on current strengths and resources, rather than to risk displacing these through over-prescription. Future evaluation should generate data on implementation and impacts as these become available.

Our proposal has several limitations. Although the LOCAL intervention seeks to be evidence based, drawing on existing literature and the findings of the Inter-CEPt study, it should be recognised that this evidence is observational in nature, so some of the associations may not be causal. For example, the association between centres reporting stress on staffing capacity with an almost 50% reduction in the odds of their patients using home dialysis is likely to mask some complex factors- albeit through our graphical model which includes several centre- and patient-level factors we have tried to disentangle some of this complexity [11]. The assumption here is that centres with stretched staffing capacity do not have the time and headspace to think about how their services could be improved, let alone engage in quality improvement in the ways observed in our ethnography [8] and advocated here. Our proposal is that the affordable resourcing of individuals to undertake specific roles might break this ‘vicious cycle’. The early piloting and evaluation of the proposed intervention in such services would therefore be of value.

Not all components of the proposed intervention can be expected to have the same impact in all centres and centres may wish to evaluate their barriers against the definitions used in the Inter-CEPt research to inform prioritisation of aspects of the LOCAL bundle. Furthermore, it should be recognised that the proposal focusses on making home dialysis available equitably to everyone, and in so doing should have the secondary benefit of increasing the overall numbers treated with home dialysis by providing strategies for services with low numbers of patients to reach the current 20% target for the NHS in England [3]. However, maintaining prevalent populations at this level also means services need to focus on preventing unnecessary transfer to in-centre haemodialysis. Again, there is substantial evidence that these modality transfer rates are more dependent on service factors than patient-level factors, even when considering the most common such as dialysis related infection. However, it is encouraging to note that the services with the lowest modality transfer rates tend to be those who do the most home dialysis, which likely reflects the fact that these have more expertise and that their culture is favourable to home dialysis [30].

Conclusions

LOCAL is a service delivery intervention designed to equitably improve access to, and uptake of, home dialysis. It draws on new research evidence, theory and user co-design processes to enable those wishing to explore home dialysis to be able to do so, regardless of existing prejudices and barriers. The study identifies organisational culture as one of the biggest determinants of uptake in home dialysis. No other study to our knowledge has fully investigated this as a barrier to home dialysis, nor indeed how to begin to change it. Our intervention package tackles this from two angles: first through quality improvement programmes targeting problem areas of the kidney services identified by staff and people with lived experience, and second through dedicated home dialysis roles, driving positive change, educating staff and patient groups and sharing knowledge and learning across wider networks. LOCAL is based on extensive ethnographic research and modelled to behaviour change techniques. We have shown the evidence base for our model but recognise that this may be challenging for clinical teams to implement in the NHS at a time where resources are stretched. The next steps would be to undertake pilots and evaluate the intervention in UK kidney services.
