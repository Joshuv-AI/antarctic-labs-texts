Superficial siderosis (SS) is a rare condition caused by chronic subarachnoid bleeding, resulting in hemosiderin deposition on the surface of the cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem, and spine, leading to sequelae such as late-onset cerebellar ataxia, hearing loss, and dementia.1 Recently, some idiopathic cases have been attributed to chronic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) around a dural defect, requiring direct repair to prevent the progression of neural damage.2 However, precise preoperative localization may be difficult when the dural defect is very small. Here, we report a case of SS with a tiny dural defect, which was identified by analyzing differences in contrast attenuation between the intradural (true lumen) and extradural (false lumen) spaces on CT myelography.

Illustrative Case

A 47-year-old male was referred to our institution due to hemosiderin deposition on the surface of the cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem, and spine, revealed by a recent MRI examination (Fig. 1A and B). He had no symptoms, but progressive hemosiderin deposition was observed on follow-up MRI, and spinal MRI further revealed ­double-layered ventral dura mater between vertebral levels C7 and T8 (Fig. 1C), suggesting that CSF leakage from a dural defect induced a chronic SAH, eventually resulting in SS. To prevent further neurological damage associated with SS progression, we attempted to localize the defect accurately for surgical repair. Although heavily T2-weighted MRI could not precisely locate the defect (Fig. 1D– F), subsequent CT myelography showed differences in contrast enhancement between the true lumen (intradural space) and false lumen (extradural CSF collection) (Fig. 2A and B). By calculating the difference in contrast enhancement at each spinal level (Fig. 2C), the isodense crossing point was identified at the C7–T1 level (Fig. 3). Thus, we surmised that the contrast agent leaked through a dural defect near the C7–T1 level and subsequently spread to the extradural space of other spinal levels.

For surgical repair, hemilaminectomy was conducted from C7 to T1 with the patient in the prone position under general anesthesia. Bloody CSF was observed within the intradural subarachnoid space, and a small defect of the ventral dura, about 1 × 2 mm in size, was identified at the C7–T1 spinal level, precisely matching the preoperative localization based on CT myelography (Fig. 4A and B).

Intraoperative microscopic images. A: A small defect of the ventral dura was detected at the C7–T1 spinal level (arrow). B: The size of the defect was 1 × 2 mm.

Because of limited working space anterior to the spinal cord, primary suturing was considered technically challenging. Therefore, the defect was repaired using fragmented artificial dura (DuraGen) packed into the extradural space without sutures, based on our previously reported sutureless repair technique for ventral dural defects.3 The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up spinal MRI at 1 year postsurgery revealed that the false lumen between C7 to T8 had completely disappeared.

Discussion

Recent studies have suggested that spinal dural defects causing chronic CSF leakage, collectively referred to as duropathy, account for a subset of SS previously considered idiopathic. Continuous CSF leakage is thought to increase epidural pressure and induce rupture of fragile microvessels, resulting in recurrent hemorrhage and progressive hemosiderin deposition.4,5 In such cases, accurate localization by MRI of the spine6 and surgical closure of the dural defect are essential to prevent further neurological deterioration.4 However, precise localization remains challenging when the dural defect is extremely small. Previously, it was reported that other MRI sequences or modalities may reveal dural discontinuity,7 such as high-resolution constructive interference in steady-state MRI,8 CT myelography, or digital subtraction myelography.4,9

Observations

In our case, a subtle abnormality suggestive of dural discontinuity was suspected at the ventral dura at C7–T1 on heavily T2-weighted MRI; however, it could not be definitively identified as the surgical target. Takai et al. proposed that the site of maximal epidural contrast enhancement on CT myelography corresponds to the location of dural leakage, reflecting early contrast extravasation.9 This approach, however, may be unreliable when imaging is performed in a delayed phase, as epidural contrast distribution tends to become homogeneous over time. Furthermore, although invasive techniques such as spinal endoscopy combined with selective CT myelography have been reported for direct visualization of dural defects,10 these approaches increase procedural complexity and invasiveness.

In contrast, the method proposed in the present case exploits dissipation of the attenuation gradient between the intradural (true lumen) and extradural (false lumen) spaces. This observation suggests that the attenuation difference would become smallest near the leakage point, where the intradural contrast agent started to flow and spread. Consistent with our hypothesis, the location of the dural defect accurately corresponded to this isodense crossing point. Furthermore, unlike prior reports relying on peak epidural contrast accumulation during early-phase CT myelography, our method utilized attenuation gradient dissipation between intradural and extradural compartments, allowing localization even in delayed-phase imaging. An advantage of this approach is that it widens the diagnostic time window and reduces dependence on precise imaging timing. Thus, the present technique requires only quantitative analysis of conventional CT myelograms and can be readily integrated into standard diagnostic workflows in routine clinical settings. This observation is based on a single case, and further validation is needed to determine reproducibility.

Lessons

In patients with SS and suspected spinal CSF leakage, identifying an isodense crossing point between intradural and extradural spaces on CT myelography may help localize tiny dural defects. This approach may facilitate targeted surgical exposure while reducing unnecessary multilevel exploration and associated morbidity.
