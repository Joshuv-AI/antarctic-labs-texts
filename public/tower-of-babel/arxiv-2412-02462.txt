Introduction and the main results

The deformed exponential function is defined for
x,q\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|q|\leq 1
as

f(x)=f(x;q):=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}q^{n(n-1)/2}.

(1)

It is the unique differentiable solution to the functional-differential equation

f^{\prime}(x)=f(qx),\;\;\;f(0)=1.

The deformed exponential function has significant applications in combinatorics
[
4
,
9
,
13
]
and statistical physics
[
12
]
.
When
|q|=1
, the function
f(x)
is entire of order one and type one and its properties have been extensively studied (see
[
3
]
and references therein). In this paper, we will focus on the case
|q|<1
, where
f
is entire of order zero and admits the Hadamard product representation

f(x;q)=\prod\limits_{k\geq 1}\Big(1-\frac{x}{x_{k}(q)}\Big).

(2)

For
q\in(0,1)
, the zeros
x_{k}=x_{k}(q)
of the deformed exponential function are known to be simple and negative, see
[
5
,
8
,
10
]
. We assume that these zeros are arranged in the decreasing order, that is

\dots<x_{k+1}<x_{k}<\dots<x_{1}<0
.
Note that we follow Wang and Zhang
[
18
]
and index the zeros by
k=1,2,3,\dots
, whereas Langley
[
7
]
, Liu
[
8
]
and Sokal
[
15
]
start indexing at
k=0
. The implications of this choice are discussed in Section
3
.

The logarithm of the deformed exponential function is related to the generating polynomials of complete graphs
C_{n}(v)
(see
[
13
,
15
]
) via the identity

\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}C_{n}(v)=\ln\big(f(x;1+v)\big),\;\;\;x\in{\mathbb{C}},\;|1+v|<1.

(3)

Using (
3
) and the Hadamard product representation (
2
), Sokal
[
15
]
derived an identity

\overline{C}_{n}(q):=C_{n}(q-1)=-(n-1)!\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}x_{k}(q)^{-n},\;\;\;n\geq 1,\;q\in(0,1).

(4)

The polynomials
\overline{C}_{n}(q)
are connected to Tutte polynomials
[
13
,
4
]
and enumerations of trees
[
9
]
. They are known to satisfy the recurrence relation

\overline{C}_{n+1}(q)=q^{n(n+1)/2}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\overline{C}_{n+1-j}(q)q^{j(j-1)/2},

(5)

which can be established from (
3
) using logarithmic differentiation.
The first three polynomials are given explicitly as

\overline{C}_{1}(q)=1,\;\;\overline{C}_{2}(q)=q-1,\;\;\overline{C}_{3}(q)=(q-1)^{2}(q+2).

(6)

Since the zeros of
f(x;q)
are simple for
q\in(0,1)
, the functions
x_{k}(q)
are analytic in an open domain that contains
(0,1)
(this follows from the Implicit Function Theorem). Sokal
[
15
,
16
]
studied the analyticity of
x_{k}(q)
in the disk
|q|<1
and made several conjectures regarding their series expansions in powers of
q
. Throughout this paper, we denote
D_{t}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;|z|<t\}
.

Theorem 1

(Sokal,
[
16
]
)
.

(a)

x_{1}(q)
is analytic in
D_{t_{1}}
with
t_{1}=0.44175

(c)

is analytic in

For

Our first result demonstrates that, in addition to the results stated in (

Theorem 2
.

For
k\geq 1
and
q\in(0,1)
the following three identities hold:

\displaystyle\frac{{\textnormal{d}}x_{k}}{{\textnormal{d}}q}=\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{2}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{x_{j}-qx_{k}},

(11)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{x_{k}-qx_{j}}=0,

(12)

\displaystyle(q-1)x_{k}=\prod\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\geq 1}}{{j\neq k}}}\frac{x_{j}-x_{k}}{x_{j}-qx_{k}}.

(13)

To state our second result in this paper, we define

\gamma_{n,l}:=\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{l(l+1)}\Big\rfloor,\;\;\;\hskip 20.00003ptQ_{n}(x):=x^{n}\prod\limits_{l\geq 1}(x+l)^{\gamma_{n,l}},\;\;\;\hskip 20.00003pt\mu_{j}(n):=\sum\limits_{l\geq 1}l^{j}\gamma_{n,l}.

(14)

Note that
\gamma_{n,l}=0
for
l\geq\sqrt{2n}
and
Q_{n}(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
is a polynomial of degree

M_{n}:={\textnormal{deg}}(Q_{n})=\mu_{0}(n)+n.

(15)

Theorem 3
.

(i)

There exist polynomials
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{\widehat{P}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with integer coefficients such that for all

k,n\geq 1

a_{k,n}=\frac{P_{n}(k)}{Q_{n}(k)},\;\;\;\hat{a}_{k,n}=\frac{\widehat{P}_{n}(k)}{Q_{n}(k)}.

(16)

(ii)

We have

and

as

and

(17)

The second coefficient in the above expression is zero for

The third, fourth, and other coefficients of

The following proof of Theorem
1

is a rephrasing of the original proof presented in
[
16
]
. Recall that
D_{t}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;|z|<t\}
. We begin by establishing the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 1
.

Assume that for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
,
u>0
and
t\in(0,1)

G_{k}(u;t):=k!\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\frac{u^{n-k}}{n!}t^{(n-k)^{2}/2}\leq 2.

(19)

Then for any

the function
x\mapsto f(x;q)
has exactly
k
zeros inside the disk
D_{r}
with
r=u|q|^{1/2-k}
and no zeros on its boundary.

Proof.

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Assume that for some
q\in D_{1}\setminus\{0\}
and for all
x
on the circle
|x|=\rho>0
, we have

\Big|f(x;q)-2\frac{x^{k}}{k!}q^{k(k-1)/2}\Big|<2\frac{\rho^{k}}{k!}|q|^{k(k-1)/2}.

Then, by Rouche’s Theorem, the function
f(x;q)
has exactly
k
zeros inside
D_{\rho}
, and none on the circle
|x|=\rho
.
Next, we derive an upper bound

\Big|f(x;q)-2\frac{x^{k}}{k!}q^{k(k-1)/2}\Big|=\Big|\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{\delta_{n,k}}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}q^{n(n-1)/2}\Big|\leq\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\frac{|x|^{n}}{n!}|q|^{n(n-1)/2}.

Here
\delta_{n,k}=1
if
n=k
and
\delta_{n,k}=0
otherwise.
Combining these results, we see that if for some
\rho>0
and
q\in D_{1}\setminus\{0\}

\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\frac{\rho^{n}}{n!}|q|^{n(n-1)/2}<2\frac{\rho^{k}}{k!}|q|^{k(k-1)/2},

(20)

then the function
f(x;q)
has
k
roots inside
D_{\rho}
.
Now we set
|q|=t
,
\rho=ut^{1/2-k}
, divide both sides of (
20
) by the right-hand side and, after simplifying the resulting left-hand side, we obtain the following result: the inequality (
20
) (with
|q|=t
and
\rho=ut^{1/2-k}
) is equivalent to
G_{k}(u;t)<2
. The function
G_{k}(u,t)

is manifestly increasing in
t
. Thus, if
G_{k}(u,t)\leq 2
, then also

G_{k}(u,|q|)<2
whenever
|q|<t
. This completes the proof of Lemma 1.

w_{k}(q)\times(1/w_{k}(q))=1
.
Now, applying (
26
) with
w_{k}^{(0)}(q)=1
we compute

w_{k}^{(1)}(q)=1+\frac{1}{k(k+1)}q+O(q^{2}),\;\;\;q\to 0,

which implies
P_{1}(k)=1
. Formula (
44
) then gives us
\widehat{P}_{1}(k)=1
. Applying iteration (
26
) once again, we compute

w_{k}^{(2)}(q)=1+\frac{1}{k(k+1)}q+\frac{3k^{2}-1}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}q^{2}+O(q^{3}),

yielding
P_{2}(k)=3k^{2}-1
, and from (
44
) we obtain
\widehat{P}_{2}(k)=3k^{2}-2
. In the next iteration, we have

w_{k}^{(3)}(q)=1+\frac{1}{k(k+1)}q+\frac{3k^{2}-1}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}q^{2}+\frac{4k^{5}+8k^{4}-7k^{3}-4k^{2}+6k+4}{k^{3}(k+1)^{3}(k+2)}q^{3}+O(q^{4}),

which gives
P_{3}(k)
and then
\widehat{P}_{3}(k)
via (
44
). In general,
m
-th iteration of (
26
) yields the polynomial
P_{m}
, which is then used to find
\widehat{P}_{m}
via
(
44
).
The first ten polynomials

and
\widehat{P}_{n}
can be easily computed using any symbolic computation software. We present them in Appendix

Remark 2
.

An alternative recursive method for computing polynomials

and

can be formulated, which may offer advantages for theoretical analysis. We denote
{\mathbf{n}}=\{n_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}

(

The next result provides algernative bounds on the largest real root of a polynomial. These bounds are particularly useful for reducing the computational effort required for verifying the non-negativity of
\nu(P_{n})

\nu(\widehat{P}_{n})
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P(x)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}x^{j}
be a real polynomial of degree
n
such that
a_{j}\geq 0
for
m\leq j\leq n
. For
r>0
denote
\nu_{r}(P):=\max\{r,2c(r)\}
, where

c(r):=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}\sqrt[i]{\frac{|a_{m-i}|}{b(r)}}\;\;{\textnormal{ and }}\;b(r):=\sum\limits_{j=m}^{n}a_{j}r^{j-m}.

Then
P(x)>0
for all
x>\nu_{r}(P)
.

Proof.

Assume that
z\in[r,\infty)
and
P(z)=0
. Then

\sum\limits_{j=m}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}=-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{j}z^{j}.

Since
z\geq r
and
a_{j}\geq 0
for
m\leq j\leq n
, we have

z^{m}b(r)=z^{m}\sum\limits_{j=m}^{n}a_{j}r^{j-m}\leq\sum\limits_{j=m}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}=\Big|-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{j}z^{j}\Big|\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}|a_{j}|z^{j},

which implies

1\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{|a_{j}|}{b(r)}z^{j-m}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{|a_{m-i}|}{b(r)}z^{-i}\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}c(r)^{i}z^{-i}\leq\frac{c(r)z^{-1}}{1-c(r)z^{-1}}.

From this last inequality it follows that
c(r)z^{-1}\geq 1/2
, so that
z\leq 2c(r)
. Thus we proved that every root
z
of the polynomial
P
in the interval
[r,\infty)
must satisfy
z\leq 2c(r)
, which is equivalent to saying that
P
has no roots
z
in the interval
z>\max\{r,2c(r)\}
. This fact, together with the observation that
P(x)\to+\infty
as
x\to+\infty
, implies the statement of this result.
