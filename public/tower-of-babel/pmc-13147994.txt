Impact of establishing a new emergency and critical care center on regional out-of-hospital cardiac arrest transport patterns and outcomes
Authors: Ichiro Hirayama, Minaho Nonaka, Hiromu Naraba, Tetsuhiro Yano, Mitsuru Ishii, Yoshiteru Tominaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The impact of establishing a new ECCC on destination selection remains unclear.•Establishing a new ECCC is associated with redistribution of OHCA transport.•Improved ROSC in older patients does not translate into better long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Background and context

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a major global health issue with high mortality.1, 2 Survival varies widely across regions,3 indicating a strong influence of system-level factors. Access to specialized centers providing comprehensive post–cardiac arrest care is associated with improved survival and neurological outcomes.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Notably, transport to such centers is linked to better outcomes even when transport times are longer.9 These observations underscore the importance of integrated systems connecting prehospital and in-hospital care.

Rationale and summary of existing evidence

In Japan, emergency care is structured into three tiers, with tertiary institutions designated as emergency and critical care centers (ECCCs). Guidelines recommend transport of OHCA patients to ECCCs when feasible.10 However, access to ECCCs differs across regions.11 In areas without nearby ECCCs, patients are often taken to secondary emergency hospitals, which may have limited resources for advanced care,12 and generally remain there rather than being transferred to an ECCC, except in selected cases. While prior studies have explored associations between transport time or distance and outcomes,13, 14, 15, 16 the effect of establishing a new ECCC on transport patterns has not been well characterized.

Research question and study aim

In May 2021, our hospital was upgraded from a secondary emergency facility to an ECCC in a region that previously lacked one. This change provided an opportunity to assess its impact on EMS transport patterns. We therefore examined the association between transport distance and hospital destination in OHCA patients and evaluated how the establishment of a new ECCC influenced regional transport patterns and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective observational study using a single regional EMS system. A new ECCC began operating on May 1, 2021. Between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, 1916 OHCA cases were recorded. Patients aged <20 years (n = 18) and those with missing data (n = 14) were excluded. Cases occurring in 2021 (n = 387) were also excluded to avoid potential transitional effects during the establishment year. The final analytical cohort consisted of 1497 patients (Fig. 1). Patients treated between 2019 and 2020 comprised the pre-establishment group, whereas those treated between 2022 and 2023 comprised the post-establishment group. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval number: R2023-15; December 7, 2023). In our region, EMS transport decisions for OHCA are based on proximity and real-time hospital acceptance rather than a formal triage protocol, and no guideline changes occurred with the establishment of the ECCC.Fig. 1Flowchart of the study population.OHCA, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

Data collection

De-identified prehospital transport data were obtained from the regional fire department. Variables included age, sex, witnessed status, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), public-access automated external defibrillator (AED) use, EMS defibrillation, etiology (cardiac vs. noncardiac, traumatic vs. nontraumatic), initial rhythm (shockable vs. non-shockable), transport distance and time, year of occurrence, destination hospital (our hospital, a secondary emergency hospital, or a nearby ECCC), return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at hospital arrival, 1-month survival, and neurological status at 1 month assessed using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale. Transport distance was defined as the straight-line distance between the scene location and the receiving hospital (“as the crow flies”). Transport time had been defined as the interval between scene departure and hospital arrival.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the hospital destination. To assess whether the association between transport distance and hospital selection differed before and after the ECCC establishment, we applied multinomial logistic regression, including an interaction term between transport distance and study period.

Secondary outcomes and subgroup analyses

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify patient factors independently associated with transport to our hospital (the newly established ECCC). The results were visualized using a forest plot. Based on these findings, subgroup analyses were performed among patients aged ≥65 years to compare ROSC at hospital arrival, 1-month survival, and favorable neurological outcomes (CPC 1–2) between the study periods.

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, we included cases from 2021, which had been excluded in the primary analysis as a transitional period. The study period was redefined as pre-ECCC (January 1, 2019–April 30, 2021) and post-ECCC (May 1, 2021–December 31, 2023), and the analyses for the primary outcome were repeated using the same model specifications.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For the primary analysis, multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed with an interaction term between transport distance and study period. The outcome variable comprised three categories: transport to a nearby pre-existing ECCC, the newly established ECCC, and secondary emergency hospitals. The model estimated the likelihood of transport to each category versus all others (i.e., one-vs-rest comparisons). For secondary analyses, logistic regression models included age ≥65 years, sex, witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, AED use, shockable rhythm at EMS contact, traumatic cause, transport distance (categorized as short, medium, or long with each group representing one-third of the overall distribution), and ECCC establishment period. Because no patients transported to the existing nearby ECCC were classified as short-distance cases, comparisons were limited to medium- and long-distance categories. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were low (range, 1.05–1.19), indicating no significant multicollinearity. Analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.2).

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, witnessed status, and bystander CPR, did not significantly differ between the two periods. Public-access AED use significantly increased after ECCC establishment (1.7% vs. 14.3%, p < 0.001). The proportion of patients with a cardiac etiology decreased in the post-establishment period (62.3% vs. 46.5%, p < 0.001), and the distribution of initial rhythms differed between the periods (p = 0.04).

Regarding EMS interventions, adrenaline administration increased after ECCC establishment (56.9% vs. 67.9%, p < 0.001), whereas defibrillation rates were comparable between groups. Advanced airway management was performed less frequently during the post-establishment period than in the pre-establishment period (100% vs. 93.9%, p < 0.001).

Median transport distance increased from 3.7 km (interquartile range [IQR], 2.2–6.3) to 6.5 km (IQR, 3.6–8.9) (p < 0.001), and median transport time increased from 9.5 min (IQR, 6.4–14.3) to 13.8 min (IQR, 8.9–18.2) (p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis including cases from 2021, the transport proportions to the nearby pre-existing ECCC and the newly established ECCC were similar to those in the primary analysis. However, the interaction term for transport to secondary emergency hospitals was not statistically significant. Detailed results are presented in Fig. S2.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this pre–post analysis, the establishment of a new ECCC was associated with changes in regional OHCA transport patterns. Transfers to distant pre-existing ECCCs declined, and short-distance transport to secondary hospitals was reduced, resulting in increased direct transport to the newly established ECCC. Patients aged ≥65 years were more likely to be transported to the new ECCC, whereas trauma-related cases continued to be preferentially directed to pre-existing ECCCs (including trauma centers). Although ROSC improved in older patients, this did not translate into improved long-term outcomes.

Comparison with previous studies

EMS transport patterns in patients with OHCA are influenced by the balance between proximity and hospital capability. In many regions, patients are transported either to the nearest available hospital or preferentially to an ECCC capable of providing comprehensive post-cardiac arrest care, depending on local protocols and resource availability.8, 17 Our findings align with these reports, demonstrating that the availability of a local ECCC was associated with changes regional transport patterns, reductions in long-distance transfers, and decreases reliance on secondary emergency hospitals.

Advanced age has consistently been associated with poorer outcomes after OHCA. Although ROSC may be achieved in elderly patients, long-term survival and favorable neurological recovery remain limited.18, 19 In our cohort, patients aged ≥65 years were more frequently transported to the newly established ECCC, and ROSC rates improved after its opening. However, this did not translate into significant improvements in 1-month survival or neurological outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous literature and highlight the ongoing challenge of improving meaningful recovery in aging populations.

Regarding traumatic OHCA, regionalized trauma systems and transport to dedicated trauma centers are associated with improved survival.20, 21 In our study, traumatic cases were less likely to be transported to the newly established ECCC and continued to be directed to pre-existing ECCC, including trauma centers. This suggests that the established trauma referral pathways remain intact and that EMS triage appropriately differentiates between medical and trauma-related cardiac arrests.

Study strengths

By evaluating transport patterns immediately before and after the establishment of a new ECCC, we demonstrated that EMS destination patterns changed temporally following system restructuring. In the sensitivity analysis including 2021, transport to ECCCs showed similar patterns to the primary analysis, whereas changes in transport to secondary emergency hospitals were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. This temporal pattern may suggest that the impact of the new ECCC establishment on transport to ECCCs was immediate, while changes in transport to secondary emergency hospitals occurred more gradually. This shift may reflect that the new ECCC was associated with addressing pre-existing operational challenges within the regional EMS system, likely reducing uncertainty in destination decisions and facilitating smoother transport processes for emergency personnel.

Notably, even after introducing the new ECCC, the patient distribution remained selective rather than uniform. Older patients (≥65 years) were more likely to be transported to the new ECCC, whereas trauma-related OHCA cases continued to be preferentially directed to established ECCC, including trauma centers. This indicates that EMS triage decisions reflect patient characteristics and regional specialization, rather than simply defaulting on the newly available facility.

Therefore, future ECCCs should focus on both increasing capacity and improving resuscitation approaches for older adults and supporting appropriate triage and transport decisions. Our findings suggest that establishing a new ECCC may be associated with alterations in regional transport patterns and may improve access to specialized care, providing insights for planning emergency medical systems, while future studies should evaluate its impact on patient outcomes and resource allocation.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, this was a retrospective study, and causality could not be definitively established. Although the observed changes occurred shortly after ECCC establishment, unmeasured secular trends or concurrent system modifications may have influenced transport patterns and outcomes. Second, the study period overlapped with the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, which may have affected EMS transport decisions and hospital acceptance policies owing to crowding, infection control measures, or temporary admission restrictions.22 During the COVID-19 pandemic, infection control measures within hospitals substantially reduced hospital capacity, which occasionally made hospital acceptance more difficult and increased the workload of EMS services. These time-related factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, may have acted as important confounders influencing both transport patterns and outcomes during the study period. Third, we analyzed the transport distance as a structural measure of geographic accessibility. Distance was selected because it reflects a relatively stable regional resource distribution; however, transport time may substantially vary depending on traffic conditions, weather, and operational factors. In addition, factors such as EMS crew availability, real-time hospital bed capacity, and individual paramedic judgment were not captured and may have influenced destination decisions. Fourth, detailed in-hospital treatment data were not available. Important post-cardiac arrest interventions, such as targeted temperature management, coronary angiography, mechanical circulatory support, and decisions regarding the withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment, were not assessed.23, 24 The newly established ECCC is equipped to provide advanced post–cardiac arrest care, including 24-h PCI, TTM, and ECMO, whereas secondary emergency hospitals generally do not offer these therapies. These unmeasured factors may have contributed to the lack of improvement in the long-term outcomes among older patients. Fifth, the post-establishment observation period was relatively short, and long-term system maturation effects may not have been realized. Finally, this study was conducted using a single-region EMS system in Japan. Differences in emergency medical infrastructure, geography, and population characteristics may limit the generalizability to other settings.

Conclusion

The establishment of a new ECCC is associated with a substantial redistribution of OHCA transport within the region, increasing direct access to ECCC care. However, improved ROSC among older patients does not translate into better long-term outcomes, underscoring the need for optimized resuscitation and post-cardiac arrest strategies in aging societies.
