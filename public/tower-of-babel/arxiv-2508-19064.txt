Explicit Inversion of the Attenuated Photoacoustic Operator in General Observation Geometries
Cong Shi cong.shi@univie.ac.at

Abstract
In this paper, we derive explicit reconstruction formulas for two common measurement geometries: a plane and a sphere. The problem is formulated as inverting the forward operator R a R^{a} , which maps the initial source to the measured wave data. Our first result pertains to planar observation surfaces. By extending the domain of R a R^{a} to tempered distributions, we provide a complete characterization of its range and establish that the inverse operator ( R a ) − 1 (R^{a})^{-1} is uniquely defined and ”almost” continuous in the distributional topology. Our second result addresses the case of a spherical observation geometry. Here, with the operator acting on L 2 L^{2} spaces, we derive a stable reconstruction formula of the filtered backprojection type.

1. Introduction

In photoacoustic tomography (PAT), an initial pressure distribution — induced by the absorption of a short electromagnetic pulse in a biological medium — is recovered from acoustic measurements taken at its boundary. A significant challenge arises from acoustic attenuation, where viscous effects in biological tissues cause frequency-dependent energy loss, complicating accurate reconstruction. While exact inversion formulas are well-established for non-attenuating media, none are available for the key geometries of a plane or a sphere in attenuating media. Current approaches for these cases, such as time-reversal
[
1
]
or corrected filtered backprojection methods
[
20
]
, offer only approximate solutions. This paper addresses this gap by deriving the first exact, analytical reconstruction formulas in the frequency domain for these two geometries, thereby providing a fundamental solution to the attenuated PAT problem.

Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) in non-attenuating media is a well-established field, where the forward model reduces to the classical spherical mean operator
R
. For planar geometries, a rich body of work provides exact reconstruction formulas, broadly categorized into temporal back-projection
[
3
,
8
,
4
,
24
]
and highly efficient Fourier-domain methods
[
8
,
3
,
10
,
25
,
26
,
2
]
. The latter utilize the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for computational efficiency. Similarly, for closed surfaces such as a sphere, filtered backprojection (FBP) algorithms offer a powerful framework that combines theoretical elegance with practical utility
[
19
]
.

Current reconstruction methods in PAT are increasingly leaning towards data-driven and computationally intensive model-based approaches, in order to overcome ill-posedness and model imperfections. Deep learning continues to be a dominant force in this area, with innovations like the pattern framework demonstrating powerful cross-modal analysis for complex structures like the whole brain
[
5
]
. Meanwhile, significant advancements are also being made in model-based techniques. A main research direction is given by simultaneous reconstruction of multiple biological and acoustic parameters, such as initial pressure and spatially varying speed of sound, to correct for tissue heterogeneity and improve quantitative accuracy, as seen in the work of Suhonen et al.
[
22
]
and
[
13
]
. Other innovations focus on optimizing the imaging process itself, from enhancing image quality via novel sampling strategies
[
9
]
to determining the optimal illumination function
[
12
]
. While these methods show impressive empirical performance, a key criticism of the deep learning approaches is their lack of interpretability; they often function as ”black boxes” without providing insight into the underlying physics. Similarly, advanced joint-reconstruction methods, though physics-based, are often computationally prohibitive, iterative, and lack closed-form solutions (see, e.g.,
[
17
]
). This situation calls for explicit, model-based reconstruction formulas that are both analytically rigorous and computationally efficient, particularly for attenuating media where researches are still in an early phase.

However, these advancements share a critical limitation: they rely on the assumption of a lossless medium. In biological tissues, acoustic attenuation – the frequency-dependent dissipation of wave energy – fundamentally alters the physics of wave propagation. This deviation from the standard model introduces significant blurring and artifacts, making classical inversion formulas inadequate. While current state-of-the-art approaches for attenuating media, such as time-reversal
[
1
]
or approximate FBP corrections
[
20
]
, offer pragmatic solutions, they are inherently iterative or approximate. A fundamental question remains unanswered: does an exact, analytical inversion formula exist for the attenuated operator
R^{a}
?

This paper answers this question definitively by deriving the first exact reconstruction formulas for the attenuated PAT problem in two canonical geometries: a plane and a sphere. Our work moves beyond numerical compensation and establishes a new analytical foundation for quantitative imaging in attenuating media.

Our approach is twofold:

For Planar Geometry:
We introduce a novel factorization of the attenuated operator
R^{a}=A\circ R
within the space of tempered distributions. We then systematically construct the inverses of both components: the classical
R
and the attenuating operator
A
. This composition of inverses yields a new, exact closed-form solution, for which we prove uniqueness and stability. The conceptual foundation for this distributional framework builds upon the pioneering work of
[
3
]
.

For Spherical Geometry:
We reformulate the inverse problem by constructing a compact operator
T
that precisely encodes the attenuation. This allows us to express the inverse of
R^{a}
as a Neumann series expansion,
(I+T)^{-1}
, applied to a modified FBP operator. This result elegantly generalizes the classical FBP formula
[
19
]
to account for attenuation exactly, providing a direct and computationally structured reconstruction method.

In essence, we provide exact inversion formulas (shown in
section 3
) to solve the inverse problem, moving beyond the approximate reconstructions prevalent in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, these constitute the first such analytical solutions for these specific geometries. They offer not only practical algorithms but also a deeper theoretical understanding of the attenuated PAT problem. Furthermore, we derive two explicit analytic reconstruction formulas: one for a planar observation surface
section 3
and another for a spherical surface
section 3
. Finally, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solutions achievable with these formulas in
section 4
and
section 5
, respectively.

The paper is structured as follows:
section 2
formalizes the attenuated PAT model.
section 3
provides a concise overview of our two main explicit reconstruction formulas.
section 4
details the derivation, implementation, and uniqueness proof for the planar case.
section 5
presents the derivation of the novel FBP-based algorithm for the spherical geometry. We conclude with a discussion on the implications of our findings.

2. photoacoustic tomography with considering attenuation

In standard photoacoustic imaging, see e.g.
[
23
]
, it is assumed that the medium is
non-attenuating
, and the imaging problem consists in visualising the spatially, compactly supported
absorption density function

h:\mathds{R}^{3}\to\mathds{R}
, appearing as a source term in the wave equation

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}p(t,\mathbf{x})-\Delta p(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}(t)h(\mathbf{x}),\quad

\displaystyle t\in\mathds{R},\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3},

((2.1))

\displaystyle p(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle t<0,\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3},

from the measurements.

Define the
n-
dimensional Fourier transform of a function
f(\mathbf{x})
through

(\mathcal{F}f)(\mathbf{\eta})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\eta\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

((2.2))

and we will use
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\dots
to denote Fourier transforms with respect to the
1st
,
2nd

The inverse Fourier transform will be similarly defined as

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}f)(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{\eta})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{\eta}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

((2.3))

and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{2},\dots
denote inverse Fourier transforms with respect to the
1st
,
2nd

In the case where the function being transformed is supported on the positive real axis, we define the

In this paper, we consider photoacoustic imaging in attenuating media. if we denote

as the notation in attenuation media for distinguishing the pressure

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}p^{a}(t,\mathbf{x})-\Delta p^{a}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}(t)h(\mathbf{x}),\quad

\displaystyle t\in\mathds{R},\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3},

((2.4))

\displaystyle p^{a}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle t<0,\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3},

where

is the pseudo-differential operator defined in frequency domain by:

\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{(}A_{\kappa}p^{a})(\omega,\mathbf{x})=-\kappa^{2}(\omega)\mathcal{F}_{1}p^{a}(\omega,\mathbf{x}),\quad\omega\in\mathds{R},\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3},

((2.5))

for some attenuation coefficient
\kappa:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{C}
which admits a solution
(2.4)
.

The formal difference between
(2.1)
and
(2.4)

\partial_{tt}

\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
.
We emphasise that standard photoacoustic imaging corresponds to
\kappa^{2}(\omega)=\omega^{2}
.

The
attenuated photoacoustic imaging

in the object

Definition 2.1
.

We call a non-zero function
\kappa\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R};\overline{\mathds{H}})

(i)

\kappa
are polynomially bounded. That is, for every

((2.6))

(ii)

there exists a holomorphic continuation

on the upper half plane, that is,
\tilde{\kappa}\in C(\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}};\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}})

\tilde{\kappa}|_{\mathds{R}}=\kappa

\tilde{\kappa}:\mathds{H}^{+}\to\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}

|\tilde{\kappa}(z)|\leq\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(1+|z|)^{\tilde{N}}\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}

\tilde{N}\in\mathds{N}

(iii)

we have the symmetry

The meaning of these three conditions are mentioned in

belongs to the Schwartz space when the observation surface is a plane. The second condition is to prove the continuity of the operator which relates

Definition 2.2
.

We call an attenuation coefficient
\kappa\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R};\overline{\mathds{H}})

\kappa(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}+\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\infty}+\kappa_{*}(\omega),\quad\omega\in\mathds{R},

for some constants

\kappa_{\infty}\geq 0
and a bounded function
\kappa_{*}\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathds{R})

For the plane case, the boundedness of

\kappa_{*}\in L^{2}(\mathds{R})
is used for the doing the taylor expansion.

Theorem 3.1

(i)

Suppose that

(ii)

There exists an analytic function
\kappa^{-1}:\kappa(\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}})\to\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}
serving as the inverse function of
\tilde{\kappa}
.

(iii)

Let
\kappa
be as above, and suppose further that
\kappa^{-1}
can be analytically continued to
\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}
(note that the values of
\kappa^{-1}
on
\mathds{H}^{+}\setminus\kappa(\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}})
belongs to the lower half-plane). Let
p(t,x)
be the unique solution of the (un-attenuated) wave equation
(2.1)
,

.
Then

\mathbb{H}^{+}

(iv)

Moreover, the following relationships between

holds for all

:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}p^{a})(\omega,x)=\frac{\omega}{\kappa(\omega)}(\mathcal{F}p)(\kappa(\omega),x),

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{H}^{+}}.

((3.1))

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}p)(\omega,x)=\frac{\omega}{\kappa^{-1}(\omega)}(\mathcal{F}p^{a})(\kappa^{-1}(\omega),x),

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{H}^{+}}.

((3.2))

Proof
:

(i)

This is a proof that
\tilde{\kappa}
is injective on
\mathds{R}
. We use the Herglotz Representation Theorem to write
\tilde{\kappa}
as

\tilde{\kappa}(z)=Az+B+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1+z\nu}{\nu-z}d\sigma(\nu),

for some
A>0,B\in\mathbb{R}
, and some bounded increasing function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then we have

\operatorname{Re}\kappa^{\prime}(z)=A+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(1+\nu^{2})\left((\nu-\operatorname{Re}z)^{2}-(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}\right)}{|\nu-z|^{4}}d\sigma(\nu).

For

we have
\operatorname{Im}z=0
, therefore the integrand is non-negative, and therefore
\operatorname{Re}\kappa^{\prime}(z)\geq A>0
. This means
\operatorname{Re}\kappa(z)
is monotonic increasing on
\mathbb{R}
, and therefore
\kappa
is injective on
\mathds{R}
. To prove that
\tilde{\kappa}
is injective on
\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}

and invoke the Noshiro–Warschawski theorem
[
6
]

(ii)

This is an direct consequence of (i).

(iii)

This can be inferred from (ii) and the Paley-Wiener Theorem.

(iv)

This can be proved by comparing the equations
(2.1)
and
(2.4)
and using (iii).

\square

4. For a plane observation surface

In this section, we will demonstrate the viability of the reconstruction formula for a planar observation surface. Let
\Omega=\mathds{R}^{2}\times\mathds{R}^{+}\subset\mathds{R}^{3}
be a half-space, and then
\partial\Omega=\mathds{R}^{2}\times\{0\}
is the plane of observation.

4.1. Preliminaries and notations

Definition 4.1
.

The
Schwartz space
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
is the space consisting of smooth functions all of whose derivatives are rapidly decreasing, that is,

\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{n}):\|f\|_{\alpha,\beta}<\infty,\forall\alpha,\beta\in Z_{+}^{n}\},

where
\alpha,\beta
are multi-indices,
C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is the set of smooth functions from
\mathds{R}^{n}
to
\mathds{C}
where
\mathds{C}
is the complex plane, and

\|f\|_{\alpha,\beta}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}}|\mathbf{x}^{\beta}D^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x})|

are a family of semi-norms on the space.

Definition 4.2
.

The
space of all tempered distribution
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, is defined as the dual space of the Schwarz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. That is, it is the set of all continuous linear functionals

f:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to\mathds{C},

where
\mathds{C}
denotes the complex plane.

For a closed set
D\subset\mathds{R}^{n}
, the spaces
\mathcal{S}(D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(D)
denotes the Schwartz functions and the tempered distributions with
\emph{support}
inside
D
, respectively. We emphasize that any subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is able to act on any subspace of
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
.

It is conventional to write
\langle f,\varphi\rangle
for the result of the distribution
f\in S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
acting on
\varphi\in S(\mathds{R}^{n})
. In this notation,

•

f
is
linear
means that
\langle f,a\varphi+b\psi\rangle=a\langle f,\varphi\rangle+b\langle f,\psi\rangle
for all
\varphi,\psi\in S(\mathds{R}^{n})
and all
a,b\in\mathds{C}
.

•

f
is
continuous
means that if
\varphi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}
in
S(\mathds{R})
, then
\langle f,\varphi\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle f,\varphi_{n}\rangle
.

•

In this paper, we use the
weak topology
on the space of tempered distributions. That is,
f_{n}\to f
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R})
means that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle f_{n},\varphi\rangle=\langle f,\varphi\rangle
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R})

•

Operator
A:S\to S^{\prime}
is continuous means that if
\psi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}
in
S(\mathds{R})
, then

\langle A\psi,\varphi\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle A\psi_{n},\varphi\rangle

((4.1))

•

A locally integrable function
g\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathds{R}^{n})
can be identified with a distribution in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
by considering the inner product

\langle g,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}g(\mathbf{x})\bar{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

•

A distribution
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is
regular
in an open region
U
if there exists a locally integrable function
f_{0}(\mathbf{x})
on
U
, such that

\langle f,\varphi\rangle=\int_{U}f_{0}(\mathbf{x})\bar{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

holds for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
with support inside
U
.

The Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
is an isomorphism of
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, which means
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. This definition can be extended to
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}(\mathds{R}^{n})
by defining
\langle\mathcal{F}f,\varphi\rangle=\langle f,\mathcal{F}\varphi\rangle
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. Under this definition, the Fourier transform is also an isomorphism of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, which means
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{n})
.

Since when the observation surface is a plane (and therefore not compact), the measurements will generally does not belong to spaces such as
L^{2}
. Therefore, we will define the relevant operators on the space of tempered distributions.

Definition 4.3
.

The
Hankel transform
of order
\nu
of a function
f(r)
is given by

F_{\nu}(k)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)J_{\nu}(kr)r\,\mathrm{d}r

where
J_{\nu}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
\nu
with
\nu\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}
.

There is an important relation between Hankel transform and Fourier transform: If the function
f(r)
is radially symmetric with respect to
r
, then its Fourier transform is also radially symmetric, which is given by

k^{n/2-1}(\mathcal{F}f)(k)=(2\pi)^{n/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n/2-1}f(r)J_{n/2-1}(kr)r\,\mathrm{d}r,

((4.2))

where
n
is the dimension of space.

4.2. Direct problem

For a vector in
\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{2}\times\mathds{R}^{+}
, we will write it as
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{12},\mathbf{x}_{3})
where
\mathbf{x}_{12}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
and
\mathbf{x}_{3}\in\mathds{R}^{+}
. Since
\partial\Omega
is a plane
\mathds{R}^{2}\times\{0\}
, then for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\partial\Omega
, we slightly abuse the notation to use
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to denote its first two components for convenience.

Define the
integrated photoacoustic operator

R
by

R:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}),\;(Rh)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

((4.3))

where
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is defined in
(3.4)
. It is well-known that the operator
R
is equal to the spherical mean operator in
\mathds{R}^{3}
(see, for example,
[
16
]
).

We also define the
attenuated integrated photoacoustic operator
by

R^{a}:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}),\;(R^{a}h)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

((4.4))

where
q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is defined in
(3.3)
.

In this section, we will first give expressions of the operators
R
and
R^{a}
which act on the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
. Then we extend their domain to the space of tempered distribution
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
by considering their adjoint operators
R^{*}
and
(R^{a})^{*}
. After this, we will give a formula for the inverse of the adjoint operators, and use that to construct an inverse of the operator
R^{a}
.

Using the formula for the solution of standard wave equation
(2.10)
,
R
is written by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

For any
h(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{3})
,
R
is defined in
(4.3)
, then

(Rh)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{S^{2}}th(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\eta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\eta)

((4.5))

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\partial\Omega
and
\eta\in S^{2}
which denotes the unit sphere.

Proof
:

The solution of the standard wave equation
(2.1)
is given in
(2.10)
, by doing the inverse Fourier transform with respect to
\omega
,

p(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathcal{F}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{1}p(\omega,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega e^{\mathrm{i}\omega|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}\omega

Therefore, the integrated measurement is written by
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathds{R}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{4\pi}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\omega|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}\omega
. We consider the integral with respect to
\omega
first,

\displaystyle q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}\int_{\mathds{R}}e^{i\omega(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|-t)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|-t)}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi t}\int_{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|=t}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Since
y
is on the sphere surface
|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|=t
, we change the variables
y=\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\eta
where
\eta
belongs to the unit sphere
S^{2}
, with area element
\,\mathrm{d}S(y)=t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S(\eta)
, and then
Rh
has the following representation in the time domain,

\displaystyle(Rh)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{S^{2}}th(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\eta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\eta).

From this integral representation, we can immediately see that for any
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{3})
,
(Rh)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is a smooth function bounded above by
t\|h\|_{C^{\infty}}
, and therefore belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
.

\square

Sadly, we do not have such elegant representation for the operator
R^{a}
. By comparing the formulas
(2.9)
and
(2.10)
, the distribution
q^{a}
can be obtained from
q
by, informally speaking, ”replacing
\omega
by
\kappa(\omega)
in the frequency domain”. However, since
\kappa(\omega)\in\mathds{C}
, we need to define the meaning of having a complex value in the frequency domain, where we use the
Fourier-Laplace transform
, as defined in the appendix, to arrive at the following theorems. We will use the same notation for the Fourier transform and the Fourier-Laplace transform without confusion.

Theorem 4.5
.

For any
g(t,\xi)\in S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
,
\varphi(t,\xi)\in S(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
, there exists a continuous operator
A:S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})\to S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
such that

\langle\mathcal{F}(Ag)(z,\sigma),\mathcal{F}\varphi(z,\sigma)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma),\mathcal{F}\varphi(z,\sigma)\rangle,

((4.6))

where
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
and
\sigma
are the variables corresponding to
t
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
respectively after the Fourier-Laplace transform.

Proof
:

We will first prove
Ag\in S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
for any
g\in S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
. According to
Appendix
, there exist constants
N^{\prime}\in\mathds{N}
and
C>0
, such that for every
\eta
with
\Im z\geq\eta
and
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
,

|\mathcal{F}g(z,\sigma)|\leq C(1+\sqrt{|z|^{2}+|\sigma|^{2}})^{N^{\prime}}.

Therefore,
\mathcal{F}g(z,\sigma)
is holomorphic with respect to
z
in the region
\mathds{H}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}
. Since
\kappa(z)
is holomorphic from
section 2
(ii),
\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma)
is also holomorphic with respect to
z
.

Also, using
section 2
(ii),
\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma)
could be bounded by,

\begin{split}|\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma)|&\leq C(1+\sqrt{|\kappa(z)|^{2}+|\sigma|^{2}})^{N^{\prime}}\\
&\leq C(1+\sqrt{|(1+|z|)^{N}|^{2}+|\sigma|^{2}})^{N^{\prime}}\\
&\leq C(1+\sqrt{|z|^{2}+|\sigma|^{2}})^{NN^{\prime}}\end{split}

((4.7))

for all
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
.

Therefore, applying
Appendix
on
(4.7)
implies that
\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma)
is the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution supported on
\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}
, so the operator
A
is well-defined.

To prove the continuity of
A
, let
g_{n}\to g
in
S^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
, which means that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\langle g_{n},\phi\rangle=\langle g,\phi\rangle
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
. For any
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{2})
, in the inner product
\langle g,\phi\rangle
by taking
\phi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\tilde{e}_{z}(t)\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})
, where
\tilde{e_{z}}
is defined in the Appendix, we have
\mathcal{F}g_{n}(z,\sigma)=\langle g_{n}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi}),\tilde{e}_{z}(t)\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\rangle
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}g_{n}(z,\sigma)
converges pointwise to
\mathcal{F}g(z,\sigma)
in
\mathds{H}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}
. For every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
, we use the dominated convergence theorem to get

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\langle Ag_{n},\phi\rangle&=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{F}g_{n}(\kappa(z),\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}\phi(z,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathcal{F}g_{n}(\kappa(z),\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}\phi(z,\sigma)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{F}g(\kappa(z),\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}\phi(z,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\langle Ag,\phi\rangle.\end{split}

This shows that
Ag_{n}\to Ag
whenever
g_{n}\to g
, so
A
is continuous.

Using the operator
A
, the relationship between the operators
R
and
R^{a}
will be given in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

For
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
defined in
(3.4)
and
(3.3)
respectively, operator
A
defined in
(4.6)
satisfies

Aq(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Furthermore, for all
h(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{3})
,

R^{a}h(\mathbf{x})=ARh(\mathbf{x}).

((4.8))

Proof
:

Combining the definition of
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
in
(3.4)
and the representation of
\mathcal{F}_{1}p(\omega,\boldsymbol{\xi})
in
(2.10)
yields

\mathcal{F}_{1}q(\omega,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

((4.9))

for all
\omega\in\mathds{R}
.

We will prove that
(4.9)
also holds when
\omega
is replaced by
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
, by showing that both sides are holomorphic functions with respect to
z
.

On one hand, since
\mathcal{F}_{1}q(z,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is the Fourier-Laplace transform of
q
,
Appendix
shows that
\mathcal{F}_{1}q(z,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is holomorphic with respect to
z
. On the other hand, to prove the right hand of
(4.9)
is also holomorphic with respect to
z
, we verify the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\left(-\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\\
&=-\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\partial\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{\partial\bar{z}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=0.\end{split}

where the last equality holds by using the fact that
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}
is holomorphic with respect to
z
, so
\frac{\partial\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{\partial\bar{z}}=0
.

Since the both sides of
(4.9)
agree on the real axis
\mathds{R}
, by the identity theorem for holomorphic functions, they must agree everywhere in
\overline{\mathds{H}^{+}}
. Based on this, we can substitute
\kappa(z)
for
z
in
(4.9)
. Using the definition of the operator
A
, for any
\varphi(t,\xi)\in S(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
the left hand becomes

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{F}_{1}q)(\kappa(z),\boldsymbol{\xi}),(\mathcal{F}_{1}\varphi)(z,\boldsymbol{\xi})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\mathcal{F}q)(\kappa(z),\sigma),(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(z,\sigma)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\mathcal{F}Aq)(z,\sigma),(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(z,\sigma)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\mathcal{F}_{1}Aq)(z,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(\mathcal{F}_{1}\varphi)(z,\boldsymbol{\xi})\rangle.

The right hand side becomes

-\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\kappa(z)|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\mathcal{F}_{1}q^{a}(z,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

This shows
Aq=q^{a}
since Fourier transform is a bijection. Consequently, for any
h(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{2}\times\mathds{R}^{+})
,

ARh=Aq=q^{a}=R^{a}h,

which impiles
R^{a}=AR
.

\square

4.3. Inverse problem

To invert the operator
R^{a}
,
subsection 4.2
shows it is necessary to know how to invert operators
R
and
A
. It turns out both inversions can be done in frequency space, and the inverse operators can be composed naturally. We will first discuss how to invert the operator
R
using its adjoint, and then directly construct the inverse operator of
A
.

4.3.1

The adjoint of the operator
R

In this part, we will prove that
R
is a continuous operator by constructing its adjoint operator
R^{*}
, and extend its domain to the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
. This allows us to invert
R
via the inverse of its adjoint.

Define

\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2})=\{g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}):g(\omega,\sigma)=g(-\omega,\sigma)\;\;\forall\;\omega\in\mathds{R},\;\sigma\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}.

((4.10))

Theorem 4.7
.

There exists a continuous linear operator
R^{*}:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}^{3})
such that

\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle=\langle h,R^{*}\varphi\rangle
for all
h(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{2}\times\mathds{R}^{+})
and
\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
. Furthermore, the operator
R^{a}
in the time domain is given by

(R^{*}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}\varphi(|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi},

((4.11))

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\partial\Omega
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{3}
. In frequency domain
R^{*}
is given by

(\mathcal{F}R^{*}\varphi)(\sigma,\varrho)=\frac{1}{8\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{(\mathcal{F}\varphi(\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma)-\mathcal{F}\varphi(-\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma))}{\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}},

((4.12))

where
\sigma
and
\varrho
are the variables corresponding to
\mathbf{x}_{12}
and
\mathbf{x}_{3}
after the Fourier transform.

Proof
:

For any
\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\in S(\mathds{R}^{+},\mathds{R}^{2})
,

\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}(Rh)(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\overline{\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Plugging
(4.5)
into it yields
\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\int_{S^{2}}th(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\eta)\overline{\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\,\mathrm{d}S(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t
. We make a substitution
\mathbf{x}=t\eta
for
t\in\mathds{R}
and
\eta\in\mathds{R}^{3}
so that
\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\eta)
. Plugging this relation to the integral and then do a translation, the inner product becomes

\displaystyle\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}|\mathbf{x}|^{-1}h(\boldsymbol{\xi}+\mathbf{x})\overline{\varphi(|\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}h(\mathbf{x})\overline{\varphi(|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})}|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Therefore, the adjoint operator
R^{*}
is given by

(R^{*}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}\varphi(|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

so that
\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle=\langle h,R^{*}\varphi\rangle
always holds.

The computation is much faster if the formula is in the frequency domain as mentioned
[
10
]
, the representation of
R^{*}
in the frequency domain is needed.

Applying Fourier transform with respect to
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{12},\mathbf{x}_{3})
in
(4.11)
, and let
\sigma\in\mathds{R}^{2}
and
\varrho\in\mathds{R}
be the variables after the Fourier transform corresponding to
\mathbf{x}_{12}
and
\mathbf{x}_{3}
respectively,

(\mathcal{F}{R^{*}\varphi})(\sigma,\varrho)=\frac{1}{4\pi(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\sigma,\mathbf{x}_{12}\rangle+\mathrm{i}\varrho\mathbf{x}_{3}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}\frac{\varphi(\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}_{12}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}+\mathbf{x}_{3}^{2}},\boldsymbol{\xi})}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}_{12}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}+\mathbf{x}_{3}^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{12}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{3}.

By doing a translation
\mathbf{x}_{12}\to\mathbf{x}_{12}+\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and first consider the integral with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}{R^{*}\varphi})(\sigma,\varrho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\sigma,\mathbf{\mathbf{x}_{12}}\rangle}e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle}e^{\mathrm{i}\varrho\mathbf{x}_{3}}\frac{\varphi(|\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})}{|\mathbf{x}|}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\sigma,\mathbf{\mathbf{x}_{12}}\rangle+\mathrm{i}\varrho\mathbf{x}_{3}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(|\mathbf{x}|,\sigma)}{|\mathbf{x}|}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\langle(\sigma,\varrho),\mathbf{x}\rangle}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(|\mathbf{x}|,\sigma)}{|\mathbf{x}|}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

which could be seen as Fourier transform with respect to
\mathbf{x}
. By taking
|\mathbf{x}|=\nu
,
\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)}{\nu}
is a radially symmetric function with respect to
\nu
. Inserting the relation between Hankel transform and Fourier transform
(4.2)
into above representation of
(\mathcal{F}{R^{*}\varphi})(\sigma,\varrho)
yields

\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)(\sigma,\varrho)=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|^{1/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}}\nu^{3/2}J_{1/2}(\nu|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|)\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)}{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\nu.

((4.13))

Bessel function
J_{1/2}
has elementary representation, which is
J_{1/2}(z)=\frac{\sin z}{\sqrt{z}}
. Inserting it into
(4.13)
yields

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi))(\sigma,\varrho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|^{1/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}}\nu^{3/2}\frac{\sin(\nu|(\sigma,\varrho)|)}{\nu^{1/2}|(\sigma,\varrho)^{1/2}|}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)}{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2\pi}|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|}\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}}\sin(\nu|(\sigma,\varrho)|)\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\nu

Since
|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|=\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}
, and using Euler’s formula for
\sin
function,

(\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi))(\sigma,\varrho)=\frac{1}{8\pi i\sqrt{2\pi}|\langle\sigma,\varrho\rangle|}(\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}}e^{\mathrm{i}\nu\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\nu\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{2}\varphi(\nu,\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\nu).

Using the definition of Fourier transform, in the frequency domain
R^{*}
is written by,

\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)(\sigma,\varrho)=\frac{1}{8\pi i}\frac{(\mathcal{F}\varphi(\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma)-\mathcal{F}\varphi(-\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma))}{\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}}.

((4.14))

Since
(4.3)
shows
R:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, naturally,
R^{*}
is an operator mapping
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. We will now prove that the range of
R^{*}
actually lies in
\mathcal{S}
. For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
, we will prove
R^{*}\varphi\in S
by showing all its semi-norms
\|\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)\|_{\alpha,\beta}
are finite.

First of all, to get rid of the denominator
\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}
, we write

\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)=\int_{-1}^{1}D^{(1,0)}\left(\mathcal{F}\varphi(\zeta\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

where
D^{(1,0)}
denoted the defferential operator with respect to the first variable with the order
1
.

Using the chain rule repeatedly, by induction there exists a family of polynomials
P_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
for all multi-indices
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
with
|\alpha^{\prime}|\leq|\alpha|
, such that the derivative with order
\alpha

D^{\alpha}_{\sigma,\varrho}\left(\mathcal{F}\varphi(\zeta\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma)\right)=\sum_{|\alpha^{\prime}|\leq|\alpha|}P_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\zeta\varrho}{\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}},\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}}}\right)D^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{F}\varphi(\zeta\sqrt{|\sigma|^{2}+\varrho^{2}},\sigma)\right),

where
D^{\alpha}_{\sigma,\varrho}
denotes the
\alpha
-th derivative with respect to
\sigma
and
\varrho
. Therefore, the semi-norms
\|\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)\|_{\alpha,\beta}
can be controlled by the semi-norms
\|\mathcal{F}\varphi\|_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta}
for all
|\alpha^{\prime}|\leq|\alpha|
. Consequently,
R^{*}\varphi
does indeed belong to
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
.

The above argument also shows that the operator
R^{*}
is continuous, since when
\varphi\to 0
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
, all its semi-norms tends to zero, which means that
\mathds{R}^{*}\varphi
also tends to zero.

\square

Lemma 4.8
.

The mapping
R:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
could be extended to a continuous linear operator
R:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Proof
:

subsubsection 4.3.1
shows that
R^{*}
is an operator which maps
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}
, we now extend operator
R
such that
Rh\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
for all
h\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
by

\langle Rh,\varphi\rangle=\langle h,R^{*}\varphi\rangle\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}).

For any
h\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
Rh
is a well-defined linear functional, and satisfies

. Since
h
is a continuous linear functional and

Rh
belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})

The continuity of the extended operator

can be proved by the following. For any sequence

satisfying

\square

Lemma 4.9

Lemma 4.10
.

There exists a continuous operator
E_{1}:\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{3})\to\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}^{3})
such that

holds for all

Proof
:

Since the partial derivatives of
E_{1}\psi(\omega,\sigma)=\psi(\omega^{2}-\sigma^{2},\sigma)
are combinations of polynomials of
\omega
,
\sigma
as well as the derivatives of

E_{1}\psi

Lemma 4.11
.

There exists a continuous operator
E_{2}:\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}^{3})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{3})
such that, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}^{3})
,

E_{2}\psi(\omega,\sigma)=\omega\psi(\sqrt{\omega},\sigma)

((4.18))

holds for all
\omega\geq 0
.

Proof
:

Define

\chi(\omega,\sigma)=\psi(\sqrt{\omega},\sigma)

for all
\omega\geq 0
. Since
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{e}(\mathds{R}^{3})
, it is obvious that
\chi
is smooth when
\omega>0
and rapidly decreasing. We will first prove that
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial\omega^{n}}\chi(\omega,\sigma)
are smooth functions when
\omega=0
. (directly using the chain rule could not help since the derivatives of
\sqrt{\omega}
are not bounded when
\omega
is small.)

Since
\psi
is a smooth, by doing a Taylor expansion for
\psi(\omega,\sigma)
at
\omega=0
, and noticing that
\psi
is even with respect to
\omega
, so
\psi
could be written by

\psi(\omega,\sigma)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\psi_{2j}(\sigma)\frac{\omega^{2j}}{(2j)!}+o(|\omega|^{2k}),

((4.19))

where
\psi_{2j}(\sigma)
is smooth functions on
\mathds{R}^{2}
. This implies the function
\chi
also has the Taylor expansion when
\omega\to 0^{+}
,

\chi(\omega,\sigma)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\psi_{2j}(\sigma)\frac{\omega^{j}}{(2j)!}+o(|\omega|^{n}).

((4.20))

for all
\omega\geq 0
. This shows that
\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\omega^{j}}\chi(0,\sigma)=\frac{j!}{(2j)!}\psi_{2j}(\sigma)
is indeed a smooth function.

The next step is to do the extension of
\chi
to
\omega<0
. Following Seelay in
[
21
]
, we choose a sequence of real numbers
\{a_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
such that
\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}2^{mj}=(-1)^{j}
, and a bump function
\theta(\omega)\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathds{R})
such that
\theta(\omega)=1
when
|\omega|<1
. Define

\tilde{\chi}(\omega,\sigma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\chi(\omega,\sigma),&\omega\geq 0\\
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}\chi(-2^{m}\omega,\sigma)\theta(-2^{m}\omega),&\omega<0.\end{array}\right.

((4.21))

It is obvious that
\tilde{\chi}(\omega,\sigma)
is a smooth function when
\omega<0
and rapid-decreasing since the construction of
\theta
deduces
\tilde{\chi}=0
when
\omega\rightarrow-\infty
. what remains is to prove the smoothness of
\tilde{\chi}(\omega,\sigma)
at
\omega=0
. This will be done by computing its Taylor expansion when
\omega\to 0^{-}
.

The definition of
\theta(\omega)
deduces that
\theta(-2^{m}\omega)=1+o(|\omega|^{n})
for all
n\in Z^{+}
, and using the definition of sequence
a_{m}
yields

\begin{split}\tilde{\chi}(\omega,\sigma)&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}\chi(-2^{m}\omega,\sigma)\theta(-2^{m}\omega)\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\psi_{2j}(\sigma)\frac{(-2^{m}\omega)^{j}}{(2j)!}+o(|\omega|^{n})\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}a_{m}2^{mj}\right)\psi_{2j}(\sigma)\frac{\omega^{j}}{(2j)!}+o(|\omega|^{n})\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\psi_{2j}(\sigma)\frac{\omega^{j}}{(2j)!}+o(|\omega|^{n}),\end{split}

((4.22))

which agrees with the Taylor expansion when
\omega\to 0^{+}
. This shows that
\tilde{\chi}
is smooth at
\omega=0
, and therefore belongs to
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
.

We now proceed to estimate the semi-norms of
\tilde{\chi}
to prove that the operator mapping
\psi
to
\tilde{\chi}
is continuous. This will be broken up into two parts: first we estimate the derivatives of
\chi
by the derivatives of
\psi
, then we control the derivatives of
\tilde{\chi}
by the derivatives of
\chi
.

For the first part, a representation for the derivatives of
\chi
is needed. The following integral representation can be proved by induction,

\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\omega^{j}}\chi(\omega,\sigma)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2^{2j-1}(j-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\zeta^{2})^{j-1}\left((\frac{\partial^{2j}}{\partial\omega^{2j}}\psi)(\zeta\sqrt{\omega},\sigma)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

((4.23))

holds for all
\omega>0
and
j\geq 1
.

Therefore, the semi-norms of
\chi
could be bounded by,

\begin{split}&\sup_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}|\omega^{\alpha_{1}}\sigma^{\alpha_{2}}D_{\omega}^{\beta_{1}}D_{\sigma}^{\beta_{2}}\chi(\omega,\sigma)|\\
&\leq\sup_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}C\left|\int^{1}_{0}(1-\zeta^{2})^{\beta_{1}-1}\left(\omega^{\alpha_{1}}\sigma^{\alpha_{2}}(\frac{\partial^{\beta_{2}}}{\partial\sigma^{\beta_{2}}}\frac{\partial^{2\beta_{1}}}{\partial\omega^{2\beta_{1}}}\psi)(\zeta\sqrt{\omega},\sigma)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\right|\\
&\leq C\left(\int^{1}_{0}(1-\zeta^{2})^{\beta_{1}-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\right)\|\psi\|_{(2\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(2\beta_{1},\beta_{2})}\\
&\leq C\|\psi\|_{(2\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(2\beta_{1},\beta_{2})}\\
\end{split}

((4.24))

where
C
is a constant that only depends on
\beta_{1}
.

This means that the semi-norms of
\chi
can be controlled by the corresponding semi-norms of
\psi
.

Again using the arguments from
[
21
]
, the semi-norms of
\tilde{\chi}
can be controlled by the semi-norms of
\chi
. This finally shows that the operator
E_{2}
is continuous.

\square

It follows that

E=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\circ E_{1}\circ E_{2}\circ\mathcal{F}

((4.25))

is a continuous operator.

\square

((4.26))

Lemma 4.12
.

For the operator
E
satisfying
Lemma 4.9
and the operator
R^{*}
satisfying
subsubsection 4.3.1
, the following relation holds,

\langle g,ER^{*}\varphi\rangle=\langle g,\varphi\rangle

((4.27))

for all
g\in S^{\prime}_{\text{cone}}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
.

Proof
:

Let
g\in S^{\prime}_{\text{cone}}
, by the Parseval equality,

\begin{split}\langle g,ER^{*}\varphi\rangle&=\langle\mathcal{F}g,\mathcal{F}(ER^{*}\varphi)\rangle\\
&=\int_{\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(ER^{*}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.\end{split}

((4.28))

By the definition of the space
S^{\prime}_{\text{cone}}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2})
, the distribution
\mathcal{F}g
is supported in the set
\{(\omega,\sigma)\in\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}\mid|\omega|\geq|\sigma|\}
. Therefore, we could restrict the integral
\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}
to
|\omega|\geq|\sigma|
, and then plug
(4.16)
into
(4.28)
yields,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(ER^{*}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(ER^{*}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=8\pi\mathrm{i}\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\omega\overline{\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)(\sgn(\omega)\sqrt{|\omega|^{2}-|\sigma|^{2}},\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

Then inserting the expression
(4.12)
into above, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}8\pi\mathrm{i}\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\omega\overline{\mathcal{F}(R^{*}\varphi)(\sgn(\omega)\sqrt{|\omega|^{2}-|\sigma|^{2}},\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\sgn\omega\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(|\omega|,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(-|\omega|,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

To remove
\sgn\omega
, we divide the integral with respect to
\omega
on
\mathds{R}
into two parts,
\omega\geq|\sigma|
and
\omega\leq-|\sigma|
, and then

\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\sgn\omega\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(|\omega|,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(-|\omega|,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=P+Q,

where

P=\int_{\omega\geq|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(-\omega;\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

and

\begin{split}Q&=-\int_{\omega\leq-|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(-\omega,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\int_{\omega\leq-|\sigma|}\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(-\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\end{split}

which can be recombined to become

\displaystyle P+Q

\displaystyle=\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}(\mathcal{F}g)(\omega,\sigma)\overline{(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)-(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(-\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

By a change of variable
\omega\mapsto-\omega
, we have

\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}(\mathcal{F}g)(\omega,\sigma)\overline{(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(-\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}(\mathcal{F}g)(-\omega,\sigma)\overline{(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

and therefore

P+Q=\int_{|\omega|\geq|\sigma|}((\mathcal{F}g)(\omega,\sigma)-(\mathcal{F}g)(-\omega,\sigma))\overline{(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Since Fourier transform has the property that
\mathcal{F}(g(-t,\sigma))=(\mathcal{F}g)(-\omega,\sigma)
, combining this property with Parseval identity,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}(\mathcal{F}g(\omega,\sigma)-\mathcal{F}g(-\omega,\sigma))\overline{\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(\omega,\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}(g(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})-g(-t,\xi))\overline{\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{+}\times\mathds{R}^{2}}g(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\overline{\varphi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\langle g,\varphi\rangle,

which finishes the proof.

\square

. On the other hand the operator
A
is also a bijection, so the composition
R^{a}=AR
is also bijective. This gives us the existence and uniqueness of source term for the inverse problem.

Lemma 4.18
.

For every

,

.

Proof
:

Let

be an arbitrary function in

for any

. Then directly,

((4.31))

Recall the representation of

in

. It is obvious that

\square

Lemma 4.19

Lemma 4.20

5. For a sphere observation surface

In this section, we will demonstrate the viability of the reconstruction formula for a sphere observation surface.

5.1. Filtered backprojection

In this part, we consider the observation surface
\partial\Omega
is a sphere
\partial B(0,\varpi)
.

The favourite way of inverting operator
R
is the filtered backprojection formulas. Several different variations of such formulas were developed and various versions of the 3D inversion formulas could be found in
[
16
,
15
]
. The classical backprojection formula is given by

h(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{q(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

((5.1))

where
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is defined in
(3.4)
.

However, since there is attenuation in the media, the measurement is actually
q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
instead of
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
. If the formula
(5.1)
is directly used on the real data
q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, the reconstructed image will not be the real
h(\mathbf{x})
.

5.2. reconstruction formula

Recall in the frequency domain the solution of standard wave equation and attenuated wave equation in
(2.10)
and
(2.9)
, doing the inverse Fourier transform for them with respect to
\omega
, we get the expression of
q
and
q^{a}
in the time domain,

q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)}{t}h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

((5.2))

and

q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}(e^{\mathrm{i}\omega|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}e^{\kappa_{*}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|})h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

((5.3))

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}
denotes the inverse Fourier transform with respect to the first variable, which in this section is
\omega
. Inserting
\kappa(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}+\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\infty}+\kappa_{*}(\omega)
into
(5.3)
, we get

q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}(e^{\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega)|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|})(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

((5.4))

Since
\kappa_{*}
is bounded from
section 2
, the Taylor expansion

e^{\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega)|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega)|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)^{j}}{j!}

with respect to
\kappa_{*}(\omega)
is uniformly convergent. Since
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}(e^{\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega)|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|})
is the inverse Fourier transform only with respect to
\omega
, so it is equal to
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{-1}((\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega))^{j})
. Plugging this into
(5.4)
, and then

q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}((\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega))^{j})(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

((5.5))

If we denote

r_{j}(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}((\mathrm{i}\kappa_{*}(\omega))^{j}))(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|),

((5.6))

it is easy to see
r_{j}(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)=\int_{\mathds{R}}\delta(\tau-(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|))r_{j}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
, and then plugging this formula and
(5.6)
into
(5.5)
yields

\displaystyle q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{-\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|^{j-1}}{j!}r_{j}(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|)h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{-\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|^{j-1}\int_{\mathds{R}}\delta(\tau-(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|))r_{j}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Noticing that
\delta
is supported on
|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|=t-\tau
, so

q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}e^{-\kappa_{\infty}(t-\tau)}(t-\tau)^{j-1}\int_{\mathds{R}}\delta(\tau-(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|))r_{j}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Apply the multiplication operator
M=e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}
on
q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, we have

\begin{split}e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}}(t-\tau)^{j-1}r_{j}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\delta(\tau-(t-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|))h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}}(t-\tau)^{j}r_{j}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}q(t-\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\end{split}

where the last equality holds by noticing the expression of
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
in
(5.2)
.

We note that the summand in the above equation when
j=0
is equal to

\int_{\mathds{R}}r_{0}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}q(t-\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{\mathds{R}}\delta(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}q(t-\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\tau=q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Therefore, we could get the expression of
e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})-q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
,

e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})-q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}}(t-\tau)^{j}r_{j}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}q(t-\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

((5.7))

Denote

h^{a}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\frac{q^{a}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

((5.8))

From
(5.8)
and
(5.1)
, we know

h^{a}(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\frac{e^{\kappa_{\infty}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|}q^{a}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi})-q(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|,\boldsymbol{\xi}))\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

((5.9))

By plugging
(5.7)
into
(5.9)
, we could compute the relation between
h^{a}(\mathbf{x})
and
h(\mathbf{x})
.

\displaystyle h^{a}(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-\tau)^{j-1}r_{j}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}q(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Inserting
(5.2)
into above yields

\displaystyle h^{a}(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathds{R}}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-\tau)^{j-1}r_{j}(\tau)e^{\kappa_{\infty}\tau}h(y)\delta(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j-1}r_{j}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)e^{\kappa_{\infty}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Therefore, we can write
h^{a}(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{x})
as
Th(\mathbf{x})
, where
T
is an integral operator with kernel

F_{T}(\mathbf{x},y)=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j-1}r_{j}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)e^{\kappa_{\infty}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

((5.10))

Finally, let us sum up our reconstruction formula for the sphere observation surface.

h=(I+T)^{-1}B_{p}Mq^{a},

((5.11))

where

•

M
is a multiplication operator satisfying
Mq^{a}=e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
;

•

B_{p}
is the Filtered backprojection formula defined in
(5.8)
;

•

T
is an integral operator with kernel given by
(5.10)
.

We will prove later that under certain additional assumptions on
\kappa_{*}(\omega)
, the operator
T
will be compact, and therefore the singular values of
(I+T)
have a positive lower bound. Thus
(I+T)^{-1}
could be calculated by a matrix inversion numerically without ill-conditioning. Also, in the next subsection we will prove this decomposition of the operator
(R^{a})^{-1}
is feasible.

5.3. Space clarification

Assume
h(\mathbf{x})\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\epsilon})
, to clarify the range and domain of the operators

,
B_{p}
and

could coincide, we will introduce their domain and range respectively.

Lemma 5.1
(
[

]

Suppose that the observation surface

is a smooth convex surface. Then the operator

defined in

is a bounded linear operator from

Theorem 5.2
.

For every

, let

be the multiplication operator satisfying
Mq^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}q^{a}(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})

.

Proof
:

Lemma 5.1
deduces
q(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathds{H}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{+}\times\partial\Omega)
. From the assumption that
\kappa_{*}\in L^{2}
in

, the representation of
r_{j}(\tau)
in
(5.6)
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathds{R})
.
(5.7)
deduces that

is the result of an integral operator with

kernel acting on

, and therefore belongs to

with respect to

\square

Again using
Lemma 5.1
, the adjoint operator

is a bounded linear operator from

to

. Recall the expression of the operator

.

To identify the domain and range of operator

, noticing

is an integral operator with kernel

, we first give the boundedness of

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
r_{j}(t)
be defined in
(5.6)
for every
j\in Z^{+}
, if
(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)
is bounded and there exist some constants
b_{m}
such that
(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)
has an asymptotic expansion

(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}\omega^{-m}

((5.12))

at
\omega\to\infty
, then
r_{j}(t)
is bounded in
\mathds{R}^{+}
.

Proof
:

Using the asymptotic expansion
(5.12)
, when
\omega\to\infty
,

\begin{split}(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)-\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}\omega^{-m}-\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}\\
&=\frac{b_{1}}{\omega}-\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}b_{m}\omega^{-m}\\
&=\frac{\mathrm{i}b_{1}}{\omega(\omega+i)}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}b_{m}\omega^{-m}.\end{split}

Since
\frac{\mathrm{i}b_{1}}{\omega(\omega+i)}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}b_{m}\omega^{-m}=O(|\omega|^{-2})
when
|\omega|\to\infty
, we conclude that
(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)-\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}
is an
L^{1}
function. It is obvious that the inverse Fourier transform of an
L^{1}
function is an
L^{\infty}
function. Also, the Inverse Fourier transform of
\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}
is
-b_{1}i\sqrt{2\pi}H(t)e^{-t}
where
H
is the Heaviside function, as proved below.

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}}e^{i\omega t}(-ib_{1}\sqrt{2\pi}H(t)e^{-t})\,\mathrm{d}t&=-b_{1}i\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(i\omega-1)t}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-b_{1}i\left.\frac{e^{(i\omega-1)t}}{i\omega-1}\right|^{\infty}_{0}\\
&=\frac{b_{1}i}{i\omega-1}\\
&=\frac{b_{1}}{\omega+i}.\end{split}

Therefore,
r_{j}(t)
is equal to the sum of
-b_{1}i\sqrt{2\pi}H(t)e^{-t}
and an
L^{\infty}
function, so it is bounded.

\square

Theorem 5.4
.

If
\kappa_{*}(\omega)
has an asymptotic expansion at
\omega\to\infty

\kappa_{*}(\omega)\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{-m}

((5.13))

Then the integral operator
T
with kernel defined by
(5.10)
is a bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
to
L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
.

Proof
:

Let

F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\varpi}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j}r_{j}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)e^{\kappa_{\infty}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

((5.14))

so that
F_{T}(\mathbf{x},y)=\Delta_{x}F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)
.

The aim is to prove that
|F_{T}(\mathbf{x},y)|
can be bounded above by
C|\mathbf{x}-y|^{-2}
for some constant
C
. To do this, we will prove that when
\mathbf{x}\to y
, the kernel
F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)
has an asymptotic expansion

F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}f_{m}(y,\frac{\mathbf{x}-y}{|\mathbf{x}-y|})|\mathbf{x}-y|^{m}

((5.15))

where
f_{m}
are smooth functions on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times S^{2}
. Here
S^{2}\subset\mathds{R}^{3}
is the unit sphere. We will focus on the
j=1
term in
(5.14)
. The other terms with

will be processed by essentially the same argument.

Using
section 2
(ii) and
Appendix
originally defined in
[
11
]
, the functions
r_{j}(t)=0
when
t<0
. Therefore, we will investigate the properties of
r_{j}(t)
when
t>0
.

First, we will prove that all derivatives of the function
r_{1}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(i\kappa_{*}(\omega))
are bounded in
\mathds{R}^{+}
. By using the asymptotic expansion
(5.13)
,

\begin{split}(\mathcal{F}r_{1}^{(l)})(\omega)&=i(-i\omega)^{l}\kappa_{*}(\omega)\\
&\sim i(-i)^{l}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}\\
&=i(-i)^{l}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{l}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}+\sum_{m=l+1}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}\right).\end{split}

Noticing that
\sum_{m=0}^{l}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}
is a polynomial in
\omega
, and
\sum_{m=l+1}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}
is a function satisfying the assumptions of

. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{l}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}\right)
is a distribution supported at
\{0\}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\sum_{m=l+1}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{l-m}\right)
is a bounded function supported in
\mathds{R}^{+}
. Thus every derivative of
r_{1}
are bounded in
\mathds{R}^{+}
, as claimed.

For the terms
r_{j}^{(l)}(t)
with
j\geq 2
, by using the asymptotic expansion
(5.13)
,

\begin{split}(\mathcal{F}r_{j})(\omega)&=(i\kappa_{*}(\omega))^{j}\\
&\sim i^{j}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\omega^{-m}\right)^{j}\\
&\sim i^{j}\sum_{m=j}^{\infty}P_{j,m}(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots)\omega^{-m},\end{split}

where
P_{j,m}
are polynomials of degree

. Therefore, for any

,
r_{j}^{(l)}(t)
is bounded in
\mathds{R}^{+}
.

Let
r_{j}^{*}(t)=e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}r_{j}(t)
. Based on the boundedness of

,
r_{j}(t)
is amooth in
\mathds{R}^{+}
, so

is also smooth in
\mathds{R}^{+}
because
e^{\kappa_{\infty}t}
is smooth. Thus the following asymptotic expansion holds when
t\to 0^{+}
,

r_{j}^{*}(t)\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{j,m}t^{m}

for all
j\geq 1
, where
d_{j,m}\in\mathds{C}
. For any fixed
m\geq 0
, the numbers
|d_{j,m}|
can be bounded above by
(C_{m})^{j}
for some constants
C_{m}
.

Based on the smoothness of the observation surface
\partial\Omega
and the smoothness of the functions
r_{j}^{*}(t)
in
\mathds{R}^{+}
, from
(5.14)
,
F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)
is smooth outside of the main diagonal
\mathbf{x}=y
. We will now prove the asymptotic expansion
(5.15)
holds.

Let
\mathbf{x}=y+\rho\eta
with
\rho>0
and
\eta\in S^{2}
. For any
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, the difference
|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y-\rho\eta|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|
is a smooth function of
\rho
, and can be written as
\frac{-\langle\eta,\boldsymbol{\xi}-y\rangle}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|}\rho+\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{2}-\langle\eta,\boldsymbol{\xi}-y\rangle^{2}}{2|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{3}}\rho^{2}+O(\rho^{3})
. The coefficients for the linear term and the quadratic term cannot both be zero, so one of these two terms dominates when
\rho\to 0^{+}
. Therefore, when
\rho\to 0^{+}
,
|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y-\rho\eta|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|
goes to either
0^{+}
or
0^{-}
.

Using the smoothness and expansion of the function
r_{j}^{*}
, we can conclude that the function
|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j}r_{j}^{*}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y-\rho\eta|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)
has an asymptotic expansion with respect to
\rho
when
\rho\to 0^{+}
.

Since all the functions involved are bounded above by
C^{j}
for some
C
, we can conclude the series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j}r_{j}^{*}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y-\rho\eta|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)

converges uniformly, and therefore can also be written as
\sum_{m\geq 0}f_{m}^{*}(y,\boldsymbol{\xi},\eta)\rho^{m}
for some smooth functions
f_{m}^{*}
.

\begin{split}F_{0}(y+\rho\eta,y)&=C\int_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|^{j}r_{j}^{*}(|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y-\rho\eta|-|\boldsymbol{\xi}-y|)\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
&=C\int_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{m\geq 0}f_{m}^{*}(y,\xi,\eta)\rho^{m}\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
&=C\sum_{m\geq 0}f_{m}(y,\eta)\rho^{m}\end{split}

((5.16))

where
f_{m}(y,\eta)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f_{m}^{*}(y,\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.

Having established the expansion
(5.16)
, we can apply the Laplace operator with respect to
\mathbf{x}
on it to get

\begin{split}F_{T}(\mathbf{x},y)&=\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}F_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}f_{m}(y,\frac{\mathbf{x}-y}{|\mathbf{x}-y|})|\mathbf{x}-y|^{m}\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}+\frac{2}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Delta_{\eta}\right)f_{m}(y,\eta)\rho^{m}\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(m(m+1)f_{m}(y,\eta)+\Delta_{\eta}f_{m}(y,\eta))\rho^{m-2},\end{split}

which means that
|F_{T}(\mathbf{x},y)|\leq C|\mathbf{x}-y|^{-2}
, so
T
is an integral operator with a weakly singular kernel. Using Theorem 1 of
[
14
]
,
T
is a compact operator from
L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
to
L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
.

\square

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
T
be the integral operator with kernel
(5.10)
and
\kappa_{*}(\omega)
has an asymptotic expansion
(5.13)
, then
(I+T)^{-1}
is a bounded operator from

Appendix

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(R^{+})
is a tempered distribution. We consider the function
e_{z}(t)=e^{izt}
for
t>0
. If
\Im z>0
, the function is exponentially decreasing when
t\to+\infty
, so we can extend it to a Schwartz function
\tilde{e}_{z}(t)
on
\mathds{R}
. The Fourier-Laplace transform of
f
is defined as

\mathcal{F}f(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\langle f,\tilde{e}_{z}\rangle.

((5.17))

Since
f
is supported on
\mathds{R}^{+}
, this inner product does not depend on the extension.

The following theorem shows that the function
\mathcal{F}f(z)
is holomorphic on the upper half-plane
\mathds{H}^{+}
(for example, by verifying the Cauchy-Riemann equations). Conversely, every holomorphic function on
\mathds{H}^{+}
satisfying a growth condition is the Fourier-Laplace transform of a distribution supported in
\mathds{R}^{+}
.

Theorem 5.6
(
[
11
]
).

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{+})
be a tempered distribution. Then its Fourier-Laplace transform
\mathcal{F}f(z)
is holomorphic for
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
. Furthermore, for every
\eta>0
, there exists constants
C>0
and
N\in\mathds{N}
such that

|\mathcal{F}f(z)|\leq C(1+|z|)^{N}

((5.18))

for every
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
with
\Im z\geq\eta
.

Conversely, If there is a holomorphic function
u:\mathds{H}\to\mathds{C}
, and there exist constants constants
C>0
,
\eta>0
and
N\in\mathds{N}
such that

|u(z)|\leq C(1+|z|)^{N}

((5.19))

for every
z\in\mathds{H}^{+}
with
\Im z\geq\eta
, Then there exists a tempered distribution
f
supported on
\mathds{R}^{+}
such that
u=\mathcal{F}f
.
