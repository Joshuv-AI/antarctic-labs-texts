Risk factors for short-term survival in EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer with brain metastasis and prognosis of the leptomeningeal metastasis subgroup: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jinghaozheng Bo, Zhuoxuan Wu, Yanyan Yang, Weidong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrognostic stratification for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with brain metastases (BM) varies widely, and identifying drivers of early mortality remains challenging. This study systematically evaluated the impact of clinical and molecular factors on short-term survival (≤6 months) and treatment-related outcomes using a comprehensive real-world dataset, and characterized the leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) subgroup with its distinct clinical features and exceptionally poor prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 261 consecutive EGFR‑mutated NSCLC patients with BM diagnosed at a single center between 2014 and 2024. Data on Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, timing of BM (synchronous initial vs. metachronous), presence of LM, generation of EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) administered, and other relevant covariates were extracted. Overall survival (OS) was defined from the date of BM diagnosis to death or last follow‑up; short‑term survival was defined as OS ≤6 months. Independent risk factors were identified by multivariable logistic regression with backward selection. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log‑rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOf the 261 patients, 79 (30.3%) had short‑term survival. Independent risk factors were ECOG ≥1 [odds ratio (OR) =4.11, 95% CI: 1.82–9.30, P<0.001], synchronous initial BM (OR =5.57, 95% CI: 2.56–12.13, P<0.001), and first‑generation EGFR‑TKI vs. third‑generation (OR =5.88, 95% CI: 2.19–15.81, P<0.001). Third‑generation TKIs (OR =0.17, 95% CI: 0.06–0.46) and ECOG 0 (OR =0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.50) were independently protective (both P<0.001). Median OS was longer with third‑generation vs. first‑generation TKIs (34.0 vs. 17.0 months; HR =0.424, 95% CI: 0.31–0.58, P<0.001). In the LM subgroup (n=37), third‑generation TKIs showed a trend toward improved survival (HR =0.464, 95% CI: 0.211–1.017, P=0.04).ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort of EGFR‑mutated NSCLC patients with BM, poor performance status, synchronous initial BM, and first‑generation EGFR‑TKIs independently predicted short‑term survival. Third‑generation EGFR‑TKIs demonstrated significantly longer OS, suggesting a potential survival benefit that warrants consideration in clinical decision‑making, including for patients with LM. However, given the retrospective design and single‑center setting, these findings should be validated in prospective studies. Our results reinforce the importance of early molecular testing and upfront central nervous system (CNS)‑penetrant therapy in this high‑risk population.Keywords: Lung cancer, brain metastasis (BM), EGFR, short-term survival, leptomeningeal metastasis (LM)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Independent risk factors for short-term survival (≤6 months) in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with brain metastasis (BM): Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) ≥1 [odds ratio (OR) =4.11], BM at initial diagnosis (OR =5.57), and first-generation EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy (OR =5.88).

• Third-generation EGFR-TKIs significantly improved median overall survival vs. first-generation [34.0 vs. 17.0 months; hazard ratio (HR) =0.424, P<0.001].

• In the leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) subgroup, third-generation EGFR-TKIs showed a trend toward better survival (HR =0.464, P=0.04).

What is known and what is new?

• EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM have heterogeneous survival outcomes; third-generation TKIs offer better central nervous system penetration.

• This study identifies specific, easily assessable risk factors for short-term (≤6 months) mortality, quantifies the survival advantage of third-generation TKIs in a real-world cohort, and provides the first evidence of a protective trend for third-generation TKIs even in the poor‑prognosis LM subgroup.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should prioritize third-generation EGFR-TKIs for EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM, including those with LM.

• Early risk stratification using ECOG, timing of BM, and TKI generation can identify ultra-high-risk patients who may need more intensive supportive care and monitoring.

• The differential benefit of combining radiotherapy with first- vs. third-generation TKIs informs individualized local treatment decisions.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy globally, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for 80–85% of cases. Due to its high incidence and mortality rates, lung cancer represents a significant global public health challenge (1). In 2022, approximately 1.06 million new lung cancer cases were diagnosed in China, representing 37.6% of the global lung cancer burden (2). The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor and a major oncogenic driver in NSCLC. Its mutant subtypes (e.g., exon 19 deletion, exon 21 L858R point mutation) can activate downstream signaling pathways by altering receptor conformation, and these mutations are particularly prevalent in Asian populations, females, non-smokers, and patients with adenocarcinoma (3).

Approximately 10–30% of NSCLC patients develop brain metastasis (BM). BM not only significantly reduces quality of life but can also lead to fatal complications (e.g., increased intracranial pressure, brain herniation), with untreated patients having a median survival of only 1–2 months (4,5). It is noteworthy that among all NSCLC patients, 40–60% of BM cases are associated with alterations in the EGFR kinase domain, which may influence the response to targeted therapy. Furthermore, the incidence of leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) in advanced NSCLC is approximately 3–5%, but this can rise to 9% in EGFR-mutated patients. Overall, the incidence of both BM and LM in EGFR-mutated patients continues to show an increasing trend (6).

Traditional treatment modalities for EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM patients include whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), chemotherapy, and surgical resection. However, current clinical practice increasingly favors prioritizing EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs). The AURA3 trial demonstrated that the EGFR-TKI osimertinib was significantly superior to platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy in patients with EGFR T790M-positive advanced NSCLC and central nervous system (CNS) metastasis. Osimertinib resulted in a higher intracranial objective response rate (iORR) (70% vs. 31%) and a longer median intracranial progression-free survival (iPFS) (11.7 vs. 5.6 months) compared to chemotherapy (7). First-generation EGFR-TKIs (e.g., gefitinib, erlotinib) were proven superior to cytotoxic chemotherapy in the early 2000s; the efficacy has been further enhanced with third-generation agents [e.g., osimertinib, which, when used as first-line therapy for EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM, can achieve a median overall survival (OS) of up to 38.6 months] due to their superior blood-brain barrier penetration (8,9).

The efficacy of systemic therapies is often hampered by the low permeability of the blood-brain barrier, which severely limits the concentration of therapeutic agents reaching intracranial lesions. While modalities like traditional WBRT can provide short-term local control, their long-term control rates are low (1-year intracranial recurrence rate >50%) due to tumor heterogeneity, radioresistance, and the need to protect normal brain tissue (e.g., hippocampal dose constraints) (10,11). Furthermore, approximately 30% of BM exhibit genetic heterogeneity compared to the primary tumor (e.g., the emergence of the EGFR T790M resistance mutation), leading to reduced sensitivity to targeted therapy. Collectively, these factors render BM a leading cause of death in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients (accounting for about 60–70%), with significant survival heterogeneity observed among patients (median OS ranging from 12 to 48 months). This survival heterogeneity stems not only from the dynamic evolution of the tumor microenvironment (TME) (e.g., an immunosuppressive TME) but also from the spatiotemporal heterogeneous distribution of resistance mutations (e.g., MET amplification, PIK3CA co-mutations), further highlighting the complexity of systemic therapy for NSCLC-BM (12,13). In response to these challenges, comprehensive therapy combining EGFR-TKIs has been proven to synergistically improve patient survival: radiotherapy helps enhance local lesion control, while chemotherapy or anti-VEGF therapy (e.g., bevacizumab) can suppress extracranial metastasis, thereby overcoming the limitations of monotherapy.

In recent years, significant survival heterogeneity has been observed among EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM patients, underpinned by molecular differences and varying responses to treatment. Studies have confirmed that EGFR gene amplification, exon 19 deletion mutations, and the dynamic evolution of resistance genes (e.g., emergence of T790M mutation) are major drivers of this survival disparity. Regarding treatment optimization, research indicates that combining third-generation TKIs (e.g., osimertinib) with radiotherapy can extend iPFS to approximately 10.1 months (AURA-LM trial) (14,15). Currently, although preliminary prognostic stratification strategies exist in clinical practice (e.g., OS decreases by approximately 40% when the number of BM is ≥3), research on the characteristics of short-term survivors remains scarce, and their potential high-risk factors require further validation (15,16).

Although research on the survival heterogeneity of EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM patients has commenced, the characteristics of the short-term survival population remain unclear. Existing studies have predominantly focused on molecular mechanisms or treatment optimization, lacking a systematic analysis of high-risk populations. Retrospective data from the updated Diagnosis-Specific Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA) indicate that patients in the poorest prognostic group have a median survival of approximately 6 months after BM diagnosis (17), highlighting that a substantial subset of patients may face short-term mortality. However, the potential risk factors for such short-term survival [e.g., baseline Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) ≥1, specific mutation subtypes] have not been fully validated. This retrospective single-center cohort study aims to identify the independent prognostic factors in EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM patients, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for early risk stratification and individualized intervention. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0469/rc).

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital in Zhejiang Province, China, between January 2014 and January 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (I) age >18 years; (II) radiographic evidence confirming BM [magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT)]; (III) histological diagnosis of NSCLC with confirmed EGFR mutation by genetic testing. Exclusion criteria included: (I) incomplete clinical and pathological data; (II) loss to follow-up within 90 days of diagnosis or lack of follow-up information. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (No. SRRSH-EC-2024-1156), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

We extracted patient clinical data from electronic medical records, follow-up systems, and telephone follow-up records. Data collection and follow-up were conducted until December 2024, or the date of death, whichever occurred first. The extracted data included: demographic information (age, sex), clinical parameters (ECOG score, gene mutation profile), BM characteristics (timing of BM diagnosis, number of BM lesions, presence of LM), treatment details (treatment sequence), and outcome measures (patient OS). “BM at initial diagnosis” was defined as the presence of BM confirmed by imaging (MRI or contrast-enhanced CT) at the time of initial lung cancer diagnosis. Treatment regimens were categorized into targeted monotherapy groups (EGFR-TKI monotherapy) and combination therapy groups (radiotherapy ± chemotherapy ± targeted therapy, where an interval ≤2 weeks between different treatments was considered concurrent combination). EGFR-TKI generation was defined as follows: first-generation TKIs referred to the use of erlotinib, gefitinib, or icotinib after BM diagnosis; third-generation TKIs referred to the use of osimertinib, furmonertinib, or aumolertinib after BM diagnosis (sequential use of different generation agents was allowed). Combination therapy was defined as targeted therapy conducted synchronously with radiotherapy/chemotherapy (time window ±14 days). Study endpoints: OS was defined as the time from BM diagnosis to death or the last follow-up; OS ≤6 months was defined as short-term survival. Some variables had missing data due to incomplete medical records. Specifically, ECOG was missing for 21 patients (8.0%), EGFR mutation subtype was missing for 26 patients (10.0%), and information on BM at initial diagnosis was missing for 22 patients (8.4%). In all statistical analyses, cases with missing data for a particular variable were excluded from the analyses involving that variable (complete-case analysis).

To minimize selection bias, we consecutively screened and enrolled all eligible patients diagnosed with EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM between January 2014 and January 2024, based on the predefined stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., age >18 years, radiologically confirmed BM, histologically confirmed EGFR mutation). This approach avoided arbitrary sampling and ensured the representativeness of the study cohort. The sample size of this retrospective study was determined by the availability of eligible patients. We consecutively included all patients diagnosed with EGFR-mutated NSCLC and BM at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital between January 2014 and January 2024 who met the inclusion criteria. No a priori sample size calculation was performed. Quantitative variables were handled as follows: age was first summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for descriptive purposes. For regression analyses, age was dichotomized at 75 years, a clinically meaningful cutoff commonly used to identify elderly patients who may have poorer treatment tolerance and prognosis. ECOG performance status (PS) was categorized into 0 vs. ≥1, based on the standard distinction between patients with no functional impairment and those with any degree of impairment, which is a well-established prognostic factor in advanced cancer. OS was analyzed both as a continuous time-to-event variable in Cox regression and as a binary outcome (≤6 vs. >6 months) in logistic regression. The 6-month cutoff was chosen based on previous reports indicating that patients in the poorest prognostic group have a median survival of approximately 6 months after BM diagnosis, making it a clinically relevant threshold for identifying ultra-high-risk patients. Other variables, such as EGFR mutation subtype and treatment modalities, were analyzed as categorical variables. All grouping thresholds were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and prior literature.

Subgroup analysis was performed for patients with LM, as this population was prespecified as a high-risk subgroup of interest. Interaction tests were not conducted, as the study was not powered for such analyses and the subgroup analysis was intended to be descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± SD (compared using Student’s t-test), and non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (range) (compared using the Mann-Whitney U test). Categorical variables were described as frequencies (%), and compared using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. For survival analysis, OS was defined as the time from BM diagnosis to death or the last follow-up (≤6 months for short-term survival). Kaplan-Meier method was used to generate survival curves, and differences between groups were assessed by the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables including age, sex, ECOG score, EGFR mutation subtype, BM characteristics (presence at diagnosis, leptomeningeal involvement), and treatment modalities (TKI generation, combination with radiotherapy) were included in the univariate logistic regression analysis, with a screening criterion of P<0.05; multivariate analysis used backward stepwise selection (retention threshold P<0.05) to derive adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI. For survival analyses, the Kaplan-Meier method was used for visualization, and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to compare survival outcomes between groups. Sensitivity analyses were not conducted, as this was an exploratory retrospective study with a predefined analysis plan focused on primary and subgroup analyses.

Results

A total of 1,164 patients with BM were initially identified by searching the case database of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (Hangzhou, China) from January 2014 to January 2024 (Figure 1). After excluding 137 BM cases with primary tumors other than lung cancer (e.g., breast cancer, n=38; gastric cancer, n=12; colorectal cancer, n=10), 1,027 lung cancer BM patients were ultimately enrolled, comprising 674 NSCLC-BM patients and 353 BM patients with other types of lung cancer. Among the 674 NSCLC-BM patients, 261 (38.7%) were EGFR mutation-positive. Of these 261 patients, 79 (30.3%) were short-term survivors (OS ≤6 months) and 182 (69.7%) were long-term survivors (OS >6 months). Comparison of baseline characteristics showed no statistically significant difference in mean age between the short-term and long-term survival groups (67.35±12.14 vs. 65.18±10.15 years, P=0.17). However, when further stratified by age group, the proportion of patients older than 75 years was significantly higher in the short-term survival group compared to the long-term survival group (24.36% vs. 13.74%, P=0.04). However, when stratified by age (≤75 vs. >75 years), patients older than 75 years constituted a significantly larger proportion of the short-term survival group compared to the long-term survival group (24.36% vs. 13.74%, P=0.04). Patients with poor PS (ECOG ≥1) were significantly more common in the short-term survival group (OS ≤6 months) compared to the long-term survival group (OS >6 months) (88.16% vs. 65.24%, P<0.001). Regarding molecular characteristics, the proportion of EGFR exon 21 L858R mutation was 57.14% in the short-term survival group and 48.48% in the long-term survival group; this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.23). Regarding treatment, the proportions of patients who had received chemotherapy (50.63% vs. 71.43%, P=0.001), previous curative lung tumor resection (11.39% vs. 23.63%, P=0.02), and third-generation EGFR-TKI therapy (7.32% vs. 37.11%, P=0.002) were significantly lower in the short-term survival group than in the long-term survival group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in variables such as gender, EGFR mutation type, or history of cranial radiotherapy (P>0.05) (Table 1). The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 20 months (range: 2.0–111.0 months).

Univariate analysis preliminarily identified several clinicopathological and treatment factors closely associated with short-term survival (≤6 months) in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM. When patients were stratified by age, a significantly higher proportion of patients older than 75 years was observed in the short-term survival group compared to the long-term survival group (24.36% vs. 13.74%, P=0.04). This finding suggests that very elderly patients (>75 years) may face an increased risk of early mortality, potentially due to a higher burden of comorbidities and reduced tolerance to intensive antitumor therapies. Furthermore, an ECOG score ≥1 was a significant predictor of short-term survival, further confirming the central role of baseline performance status in prognostic assessment for advanced cancer. In the univariate analysis, the exon 21 L858R point mutation was numerically more frequent in the short-term survival group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. While this observation hints at a potential association between this mutation subtype and aggressive disease biology or differential treatment response, the lack of statistical significance, particularly after multivariable adjustment, precludes definitive conclusions. Further research incorporating larger cohorts and detailed molecular characterization is warranted to clarify the prognostic role of specific EGFR mutation subtypes in this high-risk population. Among treatment-related factors, a history of chemotherapy, previous curative lung surgery, and third-generation EGFR-TKI therapy all showed a positive correlation with long-term survival. In particular, the use of third-generation EGFR-TKIs demonstrated a strong protective effect in the univariate analysis, consistent with their superior blood-brain barrier penetration and efficient intracranial lesion control. Additionally, the presence of BM at diagnosis was also significantly associated with poor prognosis, highlighting the importance of early identification and intervention of BM for improving OS (Figure 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified treatment with third-generation EGFR-TKIs as a strong protective factor against short-term survival (OR =0.17, 95% CI: 0.06–0.46, P<0.001), which is consistent with findings from clinical trials such as FLAURA and AURA-LM. This confirms that third-generation EGFR-TKIs like osimertinib, by virtue of their enhanced blood-brain barrier penetration and superior intracranial activity, can more effectively control intracranial lesions, thereby significantly prolonging patient survival. Conversely, the presence of BM at diagnosis (OR =5.57, 95% CI: 2.56–12.13, P<0.001) and co-existing LM (LM vs. BM only, OR =4.05, 95% CI: 1.40–11.72, P=0.01) were independent risk factors for short-term survival, reflecting, to some extent, the treatment challenges and high aggressiveness of the disease associated with impaired blood-tumor barrier function. On the other hand, good PS (ECOG =0) showed a protective effect (OR =0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.50, P<0.001), emphasizing the importance of baseline PS assessment in prognostic stratification (Figure 2). Notably, a history of chemotherapy and prior curative lung surgery, which were significant in the univariate analysis (chemotherapy: OR =0.41, 95% CI: 0.24–0.71; curative lung surgery: OR =0.42, 95% CI: 0.19–0.90), did not retain their status as independent factors in the multivariate model, possibly due to their interactions with TKI therapy and disease stage. Therefore, clinical decision-making should focus more on the generation of EGFR-TKI selected and the comprehensive management of the type of BM.

In this retrospective cohort, history of chemotherapy, ECOG score, baseline BM status, and the generation of EGFR-TKI all impacted patient OS. Specifically, the median OS for patients who did not receive chemotherapy was 16.0 months (95% CI: 12.0–21.0), compared to 23.0 months (95% CI: 20.0–27.0) for those who did, with the difference approaching statistical significance (HR =0.792, 95% CI: 0.607–1.034, P=0.08) (Figure 3A, Table 2). Regarding PS, the median OS for patients with ECOG ≥1 was 18.0 months (95% CI: 15.0–24.0), significantly shorter than that of patients with ECOG =0 (24 months, 95% CI: 21.0–31.0) (HR =0.645, 95% CI: 0.477–0.870, P=0.03) (Figure 3B, Table 2). For baseline BM, patients without BM at diagnosis had a median OS of 24.0 months (95% CI: 22.0–28.0), significantly longer than that of patients who had BM at diagnosis (median OS 12 months, 95% CI: 10.0–16.0) (HR =1.408, 95% CI: 1.050–1.889, P=0.02) (Figure 3C, Table 2). Regarding the generation of EGFR-TKI therapy: the median OS for patients receiving third-generation EGFR-TKIs was 34.0 months (95% CI: 30.0–48.0), significantly superior to the median OS of 17.0 months (95% CI: 14.0–19.0) in patients treated with first-generation EGFR-TKIs (HR =0.424, 95% CI: 0.313–0.575, P<0.001) (Figure 3D, Table 2).

Further comparison of survival outcomes across different treatment strategies revealed that third-generation EGFR-TKI monotherapy significantly reduced the risk of death compared to first-generation EGFR-TKI monotherapy (HR =0.424, P<0.001), increasing the median OS from 17.0 to 34.0 months. For patients receiving first-generation EGFR-TKIs, combination with radiotherapy provided a survival benefit compared to monotherapy (HR =0.596, P=0.02), extending the median OS from 15.0 to 18.0 months. However, for patients receiving third-generation EGFR-TKIs, the addition of radiotherapy did not confer an additional survival benefit (HR =0.701, P=0.34), potentially attributable to the superior intrinsic intracranial penetration capability of third-generation TKIs (Table 3).

Discussion

Through systematic retrospective analysis, this study identified ECOG ≥1, presence of BM at initial diagnosis, and first-generation EGFR-TKI therapy as independent risk factors for short-term survival (≤6 months) in EGFR-mutated NSCLC-BM patients. A history of chemotherapy was associated with improved survival in univariate analysis, but this effect was not independently significant after multivariate adjustment, underscoring the dominant role of PS and targeted therapy selection. These findings are largely consistent with the current understanding of lung cancer BM, providing evidence to support more accurate prognostic risk stratification. Generally, the emergence of BM often marks a turning point in NSCLC disease—frequently accompanied by blood-brain barrier dysfunction and an immunosuppressive TME, leading to decreased treatment responsiveness and significantly poorer prognosis. This study further quantified this impact: we found that the median OS of patients with BM at diagnosis was only 12 months, significantly shorter than the 24 months in patients without BM at diagnosis (HR =1.41, P=0.02), a difference that also aligns with data from several large clinical studies in recent years (18,19).

It is particularly noteworthy that this study confirmed the significant survival advantage of third-generation EGFR-TKIs compared to first-generation EGFR-TKIs in patients with BM: the median OS was 34.0 vs. 17.0 months, respectively (HR =0.424, P<0.001). Studies have shown that osimertinib combined with local consolidative therapy can significantly prolong progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC (20). The superior efficacy of third-generation TKIs is primarily attributed to their excellent blood-brain barrier penetration capability, enabling more effective suppression of intracranial lesion progression. Recent data from the FLAURA2 trial further demonstrated that first-line intensification with osimertinib plus platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy yields significant OS benefit over osimertinib monotherapy in patients with baseline CNS metastases, with a median OS of 47.5 vs. 37.6 months, respectively (21). Meanwhile, recent data from the FLAURA2 study demonstrated that even in patients with baseline CNS metastasis, osimertinib monotherapy still exhibited significant intracranial control advantages, further consolidating its position as a core treatment for patients with BM (22,23).

Furthermore, the emergence of therapeutic resistance, which ultimately limits the long-term efficacy of even the most potent TKIs, is frequently driven by intratumoral heterogeneity and the pre-existence or evolution of resistant subclones. For instance, co-occurring genetic alterations such as MET amplification or PIK3CA mutations can confer de novo or acquired resistance to EGFR-TKI monotherapy. This biological complexity underscores the rationale for a multi-drug approach in managing metastatic EGFR-mutated NSCLC. Landmark clinical trials such as FLAURA2 have recently demonstrated that a first-line combination of osimertinib plus cytotoxic chemotherapy yields a significant PFS and OS advantage over osimertinib monotherapy, including in patients with baseline CNS metastases (24). These findings support a paradigm shift toward more intensive upfront combination regimens to achieve deeper and more durable systemic and intracranial disease control.

In the univariate analysis, carriers of the exon 21 L858R point mutation exhibited a higher proportion in the short-term survival group (57.14% vs. 48.48%, P=0.23), and the OR for short-term survival was 2.03 (P=0.04) in the unadjusted model. However, after adjusting for other clinicopathological factors in multivariate logistic regression, EGFR mutation subtype did not retain statistical significance as an independent predictor (P>0.05). This suggests that the apparent association might be confounded by other variables, such as treatment selection or baseline characteristics, and highlights the need for further investigation into the biological and therapeutic implications of different EGFR mutations in the context of BM (24,25). Notably, a recent large retrospective study of 324 patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC demonstrated that the EGFR L858R mutation was independently associated with significantly shorter median iPFS compared to EGFR exon 19 deletion (13.9 vs. 23.4 months; HR =1.60, P<0.001), and lower iORRs (31.2% vs. 58.8%), highlighting the distinct prognostic and predictive implications of specific EGFR mutation subtypes in the context of brain metastases (26).

An ECOG score ≥1 was a strong predictor of short-term survival (OR =4.11), aligning with the general consensus in advanced cancer that “PS determines treatment tolerance and survival”. For patients with BM, complications such as increased intracranial pressure and impaired neurological function often lead to declined PS, thereby affecting tolerance to intensive therapy and influencing treatment decisions. Consequently, for BM patients with a poor ECOG score (≥1), in addition to actively controlling intracranial lesions, it is essential to enhance supportive care and symptom management to create conditions for subsequent antitumor therapy (27,28). Regarding the value of local therapy for BM patients, our study indicated that combining radiotherapy provided a survival benefit in patients receiving first-generation EGFR-TKIs (median OS: 18 vs. 15 months); however, a similar additional survival benefit was not observed in patients receiving third-generation EGFR-TKIs. This finding is further supported by a recent multi-center retrospective analysis of 193 patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC and synchronous BM, which demonstrated that early brain radiotherapy combined with first-line third-generation EGFR-TKIs significantly improved both iPFS (median 22.4 vs. 15.7 months; HR =0.628, P=0.002) and OS (median 37.5 vs. 31.8 months; HR =0.637, P=0.003) compared to deferring radiotherapy until intracranial progression (29). Notably, this survival benefit was most pronounced in patients with 1–3 brain metastases and exon 19 deletions, while our study similarly observed that the addition of radiotherapy did not confer a significant survival benefit in the third-generation EGFR-TKI subgroup, potentially due to the superior intrinsic intracranial penetration of these agents. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 retrospective studies involving 2,119 patients demonstrated that early brain radiotherapy combined with EGFR-TKI significantly improved OS (HR =0.87, 95% CI: 0.76–0.99) and iPFS (HR =0.77, 95% CI: 0.61–0.97) compared to TKI alone in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC and brain metastases (30). Subgroup analysis indicated that patients receiving osimertinib monotherapy showed a trend toward improved OS and iPFS without statistical significance, whereas first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs combined with early brain radiotherapy significantly prolonged both OS and iPFS. Notably, the combination therapy group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of neurological adverse events [relative risk (RR) =15.82, 95% CI: 2.31–108.54], underscoring the need to carefully balance efficacy and safety in clinical decision-making (30). This finding coincides with the implication of the North Star study—which emphasized that the “adequate implementation of consolidative therapy” is key to enhancing the efficacy of local therapy, potentially even outweighing the importance of the patient’s initial metastatic burden (31).

In univariate analysis, a history of chemotherapy was associated with a reduced risk of short-term survival (OR =0.37, P=0.02), suggesting a potential protective effect. However, this association did not remain significant in the multivariate model after adjusting for key confounders such as ECOG, BM at diagnosis, and EGFR-TKI generation. This indicates that the benefit of chemotherapy may be mediated through its interaction with other prognostic factors or that the effect is not independent in this cohort. Therefore, while our findings are consistent with the emerging paradigm of combining targeted therapy with chemotherapy, the interpretation should be tempered by the lack of independent significance in the adjusted analysis. Future studies with detailed data on chemotherapy timing, regimen, and line of therapy are needed to clarify its role. Chemotherapy exerts broad-spectrum cytotoxic effects to improve tumor control, while targeted therapy can precisely act on specific oncogenic driver mutations, such as EGFR mutations, inhibiting tumor signaling pathway activity at the molecular level. This synergy not only enhances antitumor efficacy but may also reduce the burden of BM and even the risk of LM by improving drug penetration across the blood-brain barrier. Existing literature further supports this conclusion. For instance, a study by Han et al. showed that osimertinib plus anti-angiogenic therapy prolonged survival in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients, demonstrating significant therapeutic advantages particularly in those with BM (32). Furthermore, a retrospective study by Yu et al. on EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM revealed that the combination of TKIs and cranial radiotherapy significantly improved median OS (33). In the LM subgroup, this combination strategy was associated with higher disease control rates and improved quality of life (34). Other studies have also indicated that EGFR-TKI-based strategies, when combined with local therapy, can significantly improve patient outcomes, regardless of whether presenting with extensive BM or limited CNS oligoprogression (35).

A key contribution of this study is its specific focus on “short-term survival”, a critical clinical challenge requiring optimization, in the prognostic analysis of EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM. Our risk model confirms that baseline ECOG, BM status at diagnosis, and the generation of TKI are key intervenable factors driving short-term prognosis. This finding goes beyond traditional generalized analyses of survival time, providing a direct and practical decision-making tool for accurately identifying high-risk populations early in the disease course and implementing aggressive interventions. Most importantly, the trend towards survival benefit demonstrated by third-generation EGFR-TKIs in the subgroup with LM, which has an extremely poor prognosis, strongly argues that even for such refractory patients, targeted therapy based on potent brain-penetrant drugs remains an indispensable cornerstone strategy. This is further corroborated by a recent study analyzing the genomic profiling and prognostic factors of 116 EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with LM after resistance to third-generation EGFR-TKIs, which identified good ECOG PS, furmonertinib combined with bevacizumab, and high-dose intraventricular pemetrexed as independent favorable predictors of survival, with a median OS of 14.4 months after LM diagnosis (30). This provides crucial confidence and evidence-based support for clinicians facing this therapeutic dilemma. The results of this study, spanning from risk to strategy optimization, contribute novel insights with direct translational significance for advancing the practice of individualized precision therapy in this field.

This study also has several limitations. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, the nearly 10-year study time span witnessed continuous evolution in diagnostic techniques and treatment strategies, and these changes might have influenced the study results. Furthermore, as a single-center study, the generalizability of its conclusions needs to be validated in broader populations. Future research should focus on conducting multicenter, prospective cohort studies and incorporating liquid biopsy techniques (e.g., dynamic ctDNA monitoring) to more accurately assess treatment response and the evolution of resistance. Simultaneously, in-depth exploration of the role of immune microenvironment characteristics in the occurrence, development, and treatment response of BM is warranted to provide a theoretical basis for combination immunotherapy strategies. Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias, as the study population may not fully represent the broader population of EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with BM. This could potentially overestimate or underestimate the true effect sizes, depending on the direction of selection. Second, the long study period [2014–2024] encompassed significant advances in diagnostic techniques and treatment strategies, particularly the introduction of third-generation EGFR-TKIs. This temporal heterogeneity may introduce confounding by indication, where patients treated in later years may have received more effective therapies, potentially biasing the results in favor of newer treatments. Third, missing data for some variables (e.g., ECOG, EGFR mutation subtype) may have introduced information bias, although the proportion of missing data was relatively low (<10%). Finally, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., detailed molecular profiles, socioeconomic status) cannot be ruled out, which may have influenced the observed associations. Furthermore, due to the retrospective nature of the study, more granular clinical data such as specific location and size of brain metastases, patient-reported outcomes (e.g., pain scores, mood), weight changes, and socioeconomic factors were not consistently available for analysis. Future prospective studies incorporating these detailed variables are warranted to further refine prognostic models. Similarly, while advanced age (>75 years) was associated with short-term survival in univariate analysis, the lack of detailed data on comorbidity burden, polypharmacy, geriatric assessment metrics (e.g., frailty index), and specific organ function reserve (e.g., renal function) prevented us from further dissecting the specific mechanisms driving this association in the multivariate model. Age in this context may serve as a surrogate for unmeasured factors such as frailty and decreased physiological reserve.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort of patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC and brain metastases, poor PS (ECOG ≥1), presence of BM at initial diagnosis, and treatment with first-generation EGFR-TKIs were identified as independent risk factors for short-term survival. The use of third-generation EGFR-TKIs was associated with improved OS, suggesting a potential survival benefit in this high-risk population, including those with leptomeningeal disease. These findings reinforce the critical importance of early molecular testing and upfront use of potent, CNS-penetrant targeted therapies to optimize patient outcomes.
