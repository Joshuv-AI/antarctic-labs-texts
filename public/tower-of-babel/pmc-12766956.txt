Targeting autophagy and programmed cell death in neurological diseases via probiotic intervention
Authors: Lei Chen, Sima-sadat Sabihi, Chaoqun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe progressive degeneration in neuroprotection and cellular homeostasis related to aging significantly contributes to the beginning and development of neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and Huntington’s disease. Autophagy as well as programmed cell death (PCD)—such as pyroptosis, apoptosis, and ferroptosis—play central roles in maintaining neuronal integrity and regulating brain aging. Recent discoveries underscore the essential function of the gut microbiota in modulating these pathways, with probiotic interventions emerging as promising modulators of autophagic flux and cell death signaling. This review explores the intricate crosstalk between probiotics, autophagy, and PCD mechanisms in the context of neurodegenerative diseases and aging. We highlight the capability of specific probiotics to influence molecular regulators such as Bcl-2 family proteins, caspases, and oxidative stress mediators, thereby restoring cellular balance and promoting neuronal survival. Additionally, we examine how probiotics metabolites act as signaling molecules that influence neuroinflammation, mitochondrial function, and epigenetic modifications. Emerging evidence supports the concept that probiotics may serve as non-invasive, adjunctive therapeutic agents capable of mitigating neurodegeneration by modulating autophagic and apoptotic responses. Understanding these mechanistic underpinnings paves the way for innovative microbiota-based involvements aimed at stimulating healthy brain aging and delaying the development of NDs.Keywords: Probiotics, Autophagy, Programmed cell death, Neurodegenerative diseases, Aging

Introduction

Aging is the strongest risk factor for major neurodegenerative diseases (NDs), including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease. As individuals age, progressive declines in proteostasis, mitochondrial function, and immune regulation increase neuronal vulnerability and promote the accumulation of toxic proteins and damaged organelles. These age-related alterations create a cellular environment in which the mechanisms maintaining neuronal homeostasis become increasingly compromised, ultimately contributing to the onset and progression of NDs. Aging is not a disease but is the main risk factor for major neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which are highly prevalent in older adults [1–4]. Among the pathways most affected during aging are autophagy and programmed cell death (PCD), two tightly coordinated processes that determine cellular survival or elimination. Autophagic flux, which ensures the timely degradation of damaged cellular components, declines with age, leading to impaired clearance of misfolded proteins such as Aβ, tau, and α-synuclein. Simultaneously, dysregulation of apoptotic, pyroptotic, necroptotic, and ferroptotic pathways contributes to chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal loss. Together, these defects in cellular quality-control systems represent a core pathological axis linking aging to neurodegeneration [5].

Growing evidence indicates that the gut–brain axis plays a central role in shaping these cellular processes. Aging and NDs are both associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, increased gut permeability, and altered microbial metabolite production [6]. These changes can exacerbate neuroinflammation, disrupt mitochondrial function, and modulate autophagy and PCD signaling in the central nervous system. Thus, the microbiome has emerged as a key external regulator of molecular pathways that determine neuronal health and disease progression [7].

The utilization of probiotics manifests advantageous effects such as the modest exclusion of pathogenic microorganisms, the excretion of antimicrobial compounds, the improvement of epithelial barrier integrity, the biosynthesis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) as well as other metabolites, the reestablishment of intestinal microbiota homeostasis, and the modulation of immune responses [8, 9]. Numerous in vivo studies substantiate the beneficial influence of probiotics on age-related procedures, particularly in relation to central nervous system (CNS) functionality. This establishes a basis for the exploration of the health-promoting possible of macromolecular structures and their metabolites at both molecular and cellular dimensions within a host organism [10–12].

Autophagy is a conserved catabolic process that removes misfolded proteins and damaged organelles, thereby maintaining neuronal integrity and cellular homeostasis. Dysfunctional autophagy is a hallmark of numerous NDs, including Huntington’s disease (HD), AD, and PD, where impaired clearance of protein aggregates contributes to progressive neuronal loss. Recent advances in gut-brain axis research have unveiled the capability of probiotics to control autophagy-related signaling pathways [13, 14], offering promising therapeutic insights for neurodegeneration and healthy aging. The microbiota is capable of modulating apoptosis through signaling mechanisms related to the gut-brain axis [15]. In the state of dysbiosis, deleterious signals are disseminated, resulting in elevated production of inflammation and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which subsequently causes mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby facilitating the freedom of cytochrome c, which in order stimulates caspase-3 and promotes apoptosis along with neuronal cell death [16].

Despite expanding research across these domains, an integrated understanding of how probiotics influence autophagy and PCD in the context of aging and neurodegeneration remains limited. The present review aims to synthesize current knowledge connecting these fields, highlight existing mechanistic gaps, and outline future directions for therapeutic development. By bringing together evidence from microbiology, neurobiology, and aging research, this article underscores the importance and potential of probiotic intervention in modulating key cellular pathways implicated in NDs.

Mechanistic foundations

Step-by-step overview of autophagic flux and programmed cell death pathways

Autophagic flux refers to the complete progression of autophagy from its initiation to the final lysosomal degradation of cellular cargo, and understanding this sequential flow is essential for interpreting how probiotics influence neuronal homeostasis. Autophagy begins when cellular stressors such as nutrient deprivation, oxidative imbalance, or organelle damage activate AMPK and inhibit mTOR, thereby enabling the ULK1–Beclin-1 complex to initiate phagophore formation [17, 18]. The phagophore gradually expands and sequesters misfolded proteins, dysfunctional mitochondria, or other damaged components through selective receptors, including p62/SQSTM1, which link ubiquitinated cargo to LC3-II on the growing membrane [18]. As the membrane closes, a double-membrane autophagosome is formed and subsequently traffics toward lysosomes, where it fuses to generate an autolysosome. Within this compartment, lysosomal hydrolases degrade the enclosed cargo, allowing the resulting metabolites to be recycled [19, 20]. Autophagic flux is therefore defined not by the number of autophagosomes present but by the successful completion of this entire degradative sequence. In neurodegenerative diseases, the flux often stalls at the maturation or lysosomal stage, leading to the pathological accumulation of autophagic vacuoles and toxic protein aggregates that further exacerbate cellular stress [17, 18].

Programmed cell death (PCD) pathways operate alongside autophagy to determine neuronal survival or loss. In apoptosis, intrinsic stress signals activate BH3-only proteins, which neutralize anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members and allow BAX and BAK to permeabilize the mitochondrial outer membrane. This event releases cytochrome c, triggers apoptosome assembly, and activates caspase-9 and downstream executioner caspases, culminating in controlled cellular dismantling [21, 22]. The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is initiated instead by the binding of death ligands to their receptors, leading to formation of the death-inducing signaling complex and subsequent caspase-8 activation [23]. Other regulated cell death modalities also contribute to neurodegeneration. Pyroptosis is driven by inflammasome-mediated activation of caspase-1 or caspase-4/5/11, which cleave gasdermin D and induce a pro-inflammatory form of membrane perforation and cytokine release [24, 25]. Necroptosis emerges when death receptor signaling proceeds in the absence of caspase-8 activity, enabling RIPK1 and RIPK3 to phosphorylate MLKL and cause membrane rupture with release of inflammatory mediators. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent oxidative death pathway, results from uncontrolled lipid peroxidation associated with reduced GPX4 activity and increased ACSL4 expression, and plays a role in dopaminergic vulnerability in Parkinson’s disease [26].

Although these pathways are distinct, they are tightly interconnected, with mitochondria serving as a central regulatory hub. Efficient autophagic flux typically acts as a protective mechanism by removing damaged organelles and limiting inflammation. When autophagy is impaired, however, cells become more susceptible to apoptotic, pyroptotic, necroptotic, or ferroptotic signaling. This shift toward cell death is a prominent feature of aging and neurodegeneration, and it provides a mechanistic rationale for the therapeutic potential of probiotics, which have been shown to enhance autophagic activity and thereby attenuate maladaptive PCD responses [17, 27].

Autophagy in neurodegeneration

In order to sustain homeostasis and endure periods of inflammation, and oxidative stress—circumstances instigated by damaged organelles or misfolded proteins—cells possess the capacity to initiate an autophagy program. Also, autophagy is fundamental for the optimal operation of the CNS. Within neurons, it facilitates the removal of extensive and insoluble protein aggregates as well as dysfunctional organelles, and it is also critical for synaptic functionality, rendering neuronal cells mainly susceptible to autophagic dysfunction. Accumulating research designates that compromised autophagy serves a significant function in the pathogenesis of NDs through various interconnected mechanisms. While an increase in autophagy is noted during the early stages of AD progression, this enhancement is ultimately overshadowed by disruptions in autophagic flux. Therefore, both selective autophagy and macroautophagy pathways are implicated in the metabolism of tau and Aβ proteins, which are crucial for averting protein aggregation. Moreover, the accretion of Aβ induces oxidative stress within mitochondria. Nonetheless, the dysfunctional organelles are not sufficiently eliminated, attributable to the diminished mitophagy detected in subjects diagnosed with AD [17, 18].

In a manner analogous to AD, investigations into the pathogenesis of PD suggest that the accumulation of tau and α-synuclein is attributable to compromised autophagic–lysosomal degradation, which in turn disrupts mitochondrial functions. On the other hand, dopaminergic neurons, characterized by heightened levels of metabolic activity, exhibit a particular susceptibility to discrepancies in oxidative imbalances and mitochondrial energy arising from the accretion of dysfunctional mitochondria. Mutations associated with the disease can hinder autophagic flux, whereas deficiencies in autophagy can lead to downstream signaling pathways and dysregulation of gene expression. This phenomenon engenders a pathological feedforward loop that intensifies autophagy dysfunction and influences cellular processes that are critical for the progression of the disease [28, 29]. According to an expanding corpus of research, impaired autophagy seems to facilitate the development of proteinopathies linked to NDs, thereby compromising neuronal functionality. Consequently, the enhancement of autophagic flux is advantageous for alleviating the advancement of these diseases. Numerous autophagic pathways are regarded as potential therapeutic targets for both PD and AD. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that autophagic processes diminish in the brain as part of the normal aging process; therefore, interventions aimed at counteracting this decline could potentially lower the risk of age-associated NDs [19, 20].

Probiotics exert neuroprotective effects in NDs by modulating autophagy through multiple interlinked mechanisms. These pathways influence protein aggregate clearance, oxidative stress response, neuronal survival, mitochondrial health, and inflammation—all of which are tightly connected to autophagy. Probiotics can regulate autophagy via the mTOR and AMPK pathways, which are key upstream regulators of autophagy initiation [27]. Also, mitochondrial dysfunction is central in neurodegeneration, particularly in PD. Probiotics enhance PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria [30]. Probiotics influence autophagy through the creation of SCFAs such as propionate and butyrate [31].

Programmed cell death pathways

PCD, which is pivotal for both developmental processes and pathological states, is instrumental in the preservation of tissue homeostasis [21]. Numerous PCD pathways exist, such as necroptosis, pyroptosis, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy, each exhibiting distinct morphological and biochemical attributes [24, 25]. Contemporary research indicates that PCD is implicated in a variety of NDs, encompassing AD, PD, cerebrovascular accidents, and other NDs [32, 33]. The dysregulation of PCD paths can be discerned in the pathophysiology of multiple NDs [21]. Apoptosis can be originated through two distinct mechanisms: the extrinsic pathway and the intrinsic pathway involving death receptors [23, 34]. The intrinsic pathway is modulated by the anti-apoptotic and pro-apoptotic components of the BCL-2 protein family. In normal cellular contexts, the anti-apoptotic proteins MCL-1, BCL-W, BCL-XL, BCL-2, and A1/BFL1 serve a serious function in maintaining cellular viability by inhibiting the pivotal mediators of BAX, apoptosis, and BAK [22]. Also, in response to intracellular perturbations, the BH3-only proteins, which serve as essential originators of apoptosis, are transcriptionally or post-transcriptionally raised [35]. The BH3-only proteins display a high attraction for binding to anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins, consequently releasing BAK and BAX. Certain BH3-only proteins have been recognized to directly stimulate BAK and BAX [36]. Following their activation, BAX and BAK oligomerize, leading to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and the subsequent freedom of Smac/DIABLO and cytochrome c from the mitochondria [37, 38]. The aforementioned apoptogenic factors facilitate the initiation of the caspase cascade, culminating in the proteolytic split of numerous proteins which ultimately contributes to cellular disintegration. The death receptor path is instigated through the ligation of specific members of the tumour necrosis factor receptor superfamily possessing an intracellular death domain by their corresponding ligands [39]. This event fosters the assembly of an intracellular death-inducing signaling complex, which leads to the stimulation of caspase-8 alongside the subsequent effector caspases.

Probiotics, whether through direct action or via the secretion of metabolic byproducts and the modulation of host pathways like caspase cascades, Bcl-2/Bax, and pivotal anticancer paths, have the capacity to impede excessive apoptosis or assist apoptosis in aberrant cells, all the while safeguarding normal cells. These mechanisms serve a considerable role in mitigating pathological circumstances, as exemplified by Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus plantarum among others [40, 41]. Postbiotics (a term that includes the metabolic ingredients of probiotics after processing) serve as practical metabolites of probiotics and may directly or indirectly engage critical nodes within the apoptosis pathway, like heat-inactivated probiotics, SCFAs, etc [42]. Few studies have described the helpful effects of probiotics on PCD in NDs [43, 44]. Despite these promising findings, most studies remain at the preclinical stage, and heterogeneity in probiotic strains, dosages, and study models limits translation to human application. Future research should prioritize well-designed clinical trials, mechanistic investigations, and the development of strain-specific probiotic formulations tailored to the pathophysiological features of individual NDs. Targeting PCD with probiotics may offer a safe adjunctive strategy to slow neurodegeneration and support healthy brain aging.

Implication of autophagy and PCD in pathogenesis of ND

Aging is recognized by an advanced reduction in physiological function and cellular homeostasis, underpinned by disruptions in critical pathways such as autophagy and PCD. Emerging research reveals that the gut bacteria and its modulation through probiotics serve a central function in regulating these processes, influencing systemic inflammation, mitochondrial health, genomic stability, and metabolic integrity. However, despite an increasing understanding of the gut–brain–immune axis and its consequences for healthy aging, the intersection between probiotic intervention and autophagy/PCD signaling remains underexplored (Fig. 1).

Autophagy

Proteostasis represents a pivotal hallmark of aging, primarily tasked with the elimination of misfolded proteins or damaged and their aggregates, achieved through the enhancement of proteasomal split through the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP). The investigation into the effects of heat-killed probiotic and their structural ingredients on innate immune responses and aging hallmarks remains limited; nevertheless, the amplification of these effects may have the potential to mitigate the progression of age-associated diseases. A recent study presents a novel strain of heat-killed Levilactobacillus brevis, designated MKAK9 (HK MKAK9), alongside its EPS, illustrating their capacity to prolong lifespan via augmenting immune responses and proteostasis. From a mechanistic standpoint, both EPS and HK MKAK9 led to the downregulation of the insulin-like signaling pathway in a DAF-16-dependent manner, thereby elevating protein ubiquitination and in turn proteasomal degradation via the stimulation of the ALP path, which is partly mediated by the microRNA-243. Significantly, autophagosomes incorporate ubiquitinylated proteins, as demonstrated by the upregulation of the autophagy receptor sqst-3, and in turn merge with lysosomes, a process augmented by elevated concentrations of the lysosome-associated membrane protein lmp-1, indicating the establishment of autolysosomes for the degradation of the designated cargo. Furthermore, HK MKAK9 and its extracellular polysaccharides stimulated the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and its downstream effector, the SKN-1 transcription factor, which are recognized for their regulatory roles in genes associated with the innate immune response (ilys-1, thn-1, spp-21, cnc-2, spp-9, clec-266 and clec-47) as well as antioxidative mechanisms (gst-44 and sod-3), thereby mitigating the addition of ROS at both mitochondrial and cellular levels. Importantly, SOD-3 has been identified as a transcriptional target of both SKN-1 and DAF-16 transcription factors [45] (Table 1). Aging is distinguished by the emergence of chronic low-grade inflammation, which has been documented as an essential factor contributing to sarcopenia by impairing postprandial skeletal muscle anabolism. The etiology of this phenomenon referred to as “inflammageing” remains inadequately elucidated. An enhanced intestinal permeability, a dysbiosis of the microbiota, and the resultant initiation of localized, followed by systemic inflammation, have been proposed as potential mechanisms. The aim of a separate investigation was to empirically examine during the aging process whether (1) a sustained, low-grade intestinal inflammation can precipitate anabolic resistance and subsequent muscle atrophy, and (2) if a bacterial strain exhibiting anti-inflammatory characteristics could mitigate these detrimental outcomes. Young adult and senescent rats were administered Dextran Sodium Sulfate (DSS) for a duration of 28 days to induce low-grade inflammation, and were afterwards assigned to receive either (PB2 or PB1 groups) or not (DSS group) one of the two strains of S. Thermophilus (5 × 109 CFU/day) that had previously demonstrated anti-inflammatory efficacy in vitro. They were compared with pair-fed controls (PF). The findings demonstrated that in both aged and adult rats, DSS diminished absolute protein synthesis (ASR) in the gastrocnemius muscle while augmenting ASR in the colon. The PB1 (CNRZ160 strain) treatment, in contrast to PB2, led to an enhanced muscle ASR relative to DSS in adult specimens, a pattern similarly noted for PB1 in older subjects. This phenomenon was correlated with a mitigated elevation in colon ASR. In aged rats, PB1 obviously reduced the gene expression of indicators of ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy pathways when compared with the DSS groups, while simultaneously enhancing gut permeability and mitigating systemic inflammation [46].

PCD

In a research investigation, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum X7022 was utilized to mitigate memory deficits associated with aging in mice that were subjected to D-galactose administration. The strain demonstrated a distinctive capability for choloylglycine hydrolysis as evidenced by in vitro analyses. The results specified that the intake of L. plantarum X7022 significantly enhanced spatial memory and cognitive learning in aging mice. The gavage of L. plantarum X7022 exhibited a noteworthy potential to alleviate cerebral oxidative stress and inflammatory conditions within the hippocampus, as indicated by the elevation of GSH levels and SOD activity, alongside reductions in malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 concentrations. The intervention with this probiotic may confer neuroprotective effects by modulating cellular apoptosis and AChE overexpression while also inhibiting the accumulation of Aβ, in addition to influencing neuronal functions through the regulation of iNOS expression and CREB-BDNF signaling. Furthermore, this probiotic was found to enhance fecal SCFAs levels and elevate the prevalence of genera associated with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, such as Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Adlercreutzia. The findings indicated that L. plantarum X7022 may represent a promising therapeutic intervention for the enhancement of cognitive deficits in the geriatric population [47]. The antioxidative and anti-aging properties of probiotic fermented ginseng (PG) were rigorously assessed in the model organism Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans). The lifespan, as well as the responses to acute oxidative stress and thermal stress in C. elegans, were meaningfully improved by the administration of PG. The expression levels of antioxidative enzymes, including GSH-PX, T-SOD, and CAT, demonstrated a considerable upregulation, while ROS, MDA, and apoptosis exhibited a marked downregulation. Concurrently, PG manifested its anti-aging and antioxidant effects by diminishing DAF-2 mRNA expression, while simultaneously elevating the expression of SOD-3 and SKN-1 mRNA in C. elegans. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms through which PG exerts its antioxidative and anti-aging effects were investigated utilizing gut microbiota sequencing and untargeted metabolomics approaches. The findings from gut bacteria analyses revealed that PG significantly enhanced both the ingredient and structural diversity of microbial communities within the gut of C. elegans, accompanied by an upregulation in the relative abundance of beneficial bacterial taxa. Untargeted metabolomic analyses revealed that PG influenced anti-aging and antioxidant functionalities by means of neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, the citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), pyruvate metabolic pathways, as well as the metabolism of ascorbate and aldarate, in addition to D-ornithine and D-arginine metabolic processes in C. elegans [48].

Another investigation utilized Caenorhabditis elegans as a model organism to elucidate the possible properties of Lactobacillus plantarum JBC5 (LPJBC5), which was isolated from a fermented food sample, on lifespan extension and aging, as well as the underlying biological mechanisms. Initially, LPJBC5 was found to augment the mean lifespan of C. elegans until 27.81% in comparison to the control group (untreated). The lifespan extension induced by LPJBC5 was concomitant with the enhancement of aging-associated markers, including fat accumulation, physical performance, and lipofuscin deposition. Lifespan assays conducted on mutant strains of the worms and subsequent gene expression analyses suggested that the longevity mediated by LPJBC5 was attributable to the increase of the skinhead-1 (skn-1) gene, which is stimulated via the p38 MAPK signaling. Furthermore, the stimulated transcription factor SKN-1 was observed to induce the gene expression associated with thermotolerance, antioxidative properties, and pathogen resistance. In corroboration, LPJBC5 conferred enhanced resistance to both biotic and abiotic stressors, including heat, oxidative stress, and pathogenic organisms. Additionally, LPJBC5 was shown to upregulate the intestinal tight junction protein ZO-1, thereby improving gut integrity. Thirdly, LPJBC5 significantly enhanced the memory and learning capabilities of nematodes subjected to LPJBC5 training when juxtaposed with their naive counterparts. The findings indicated an increase of genes associated with serotonin signaling in the LPJBC5-treated nematodes relative to the control group, thereby implying that serotonin signaling is crucial for the cognitive improvements mediated by LPJBC5. Fourthly, LPJBC5 mitigated lipid accretion in nematodes by the reducing expression of genes that encode pivotal enzymes and substrates involved in lipid metabolism. Lastly, LPJBC5 diminished the generation of ROS and augmented mitochondrial functionality, consequently leading to a reduction in apoptosis among the nematodes. The singular capability of this bacterium to promote longevity and its attributes of fostering healthy aging, which encompass the improvement of cognitive functions and gut integrity, render it an exemplary candidate for advancement as a next-generation probiotic [49]. The objective of another work was to examine the potential benefits of Lactobacillus plantarum DR7 on neurological health, particularly concerning memory and cognitive functions in the context of aging, as well as the impacts of a high-fat diet (HFD) over a duration of 12 weeks. Rats were categorized into six distinct groups: (1) young subjects maintained on a normal diet (ND), (2) young subjects subjected to a HFD, (3) aged subjects adhering to ND, (4) aged subjects on HFD, (5) aged subjects receiving HFD in conjunction with L. plantarum DR7 (109 cfu/day), and (6) aged subjects administered HFD alongside lovastatin. To simulate the aging process, all rats in groups 3 through 6 were administered with D-galactose at a dosage of 600 mg/kg/day. The administration of DR7 has been shown to alleviate anxiety while concurrently enhancing memory performance during behavioral evaluations in aged-HFD rats. All 3 pro-inflammatory cytokines within the hippocampus exhibited an increase with aging; however, this increase was mitigated following the intake of both DR7 and statin. The gene expression levels of apoptosis-related and hippocampal neurotransmitters demonstrated a reduction in the gene expression of P53 [50] and indoleamine dioxygenase, alongside an elevation in TPH1 expression in aged-HFD rats treated with DR7, suggesting the possible influence of DR7 on the serotonin synthesis pathway and the mechanisms of oxidative senescence. The investigation proposed significant insights into the potential of L. plantarum DR7 as a promising dietary intervention aimed at enhancing cognitive functions in the context of aging [51].

The objective of an additional investigation was to assess the anti-aging effects of a probiotic formulation utilizing an in vivo murine model in which senescence was induced via d-galactose administration. The findings demonstrated that prolonged intake of the probiotic formulation enhanced cognitive functions, specifically memory and learning capabilities, while also mitigating the apoptotic patterns observed in the hippocampal region of aging mice subjected to d-galactose. An analysis focused on antioxidative properties revealed that the administration of the probiotic formulation effectively reinstated the activities of SOD and CAT, concurrently inhibiting the synthesis of MDA. The antioxidant-associated proteins, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) exhibited upregulation in the hepatic tissue following the treatment of aging mice, previously exposed to d-galactose, with probiotics. Ultimately, the probiotic intervention significantly influenced the synthesis of SCFA in aging mice treated with d-galactose [52]. The contributions of the dietary fiber complex (DFC) and probiotics to the functional dynamics of both the gastrointestinal tract and the brain in aged murine models were systematically examined in a separate investigation. Lactobacillus casei LTL1361 and DFC were administered via oral route for a duration of 12 weeks, during which the parameters of learning, memory capabilities, oxidative stress indicators, inflammatory biomarkers, gut barrier integrity, and the concentrations of microbial metabolites, specifically SCFAs, were meticulously evaluated. The supplementation with LTL1361 and DFC resulted in a notable enhancement of cognitive functionalities, a reduction in oxidative stress within the cerebral context and inflammation within the serum and colonic regions, an improvement in gut barrier integrity, alongside an elevation in SCFA concentrations and the expression of genes associated with SCFA receptors. These findings may be correlated with the preservation of pyramidal neuronal morphology within the hippocampal region of the murine brain, as well as the reduction of caspase-3 in the brain and the increase of tight junction proteins within both the colon and small intestine [53]. Accumulating evidence underscores the potential of probiotics as modulators of autophagy and PCD, offering promising avenues to mitigate age-related functional decline and disease risk. Multiple in vivo, in vitro, and clinical studies propose that specific probiotic strains—like Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species—can rise autophagic flux, modulate apoptosis, and reduce inflammatory forms of cell death like necroptosis and pyroptosis. In the context of aging, probiotic supplementation has demonstrated beneficial effects such as enhanced cognitive function, reduced oxidative stress, improved intestinal barrier integrity, and attenuation of chronic inflammation—factors all closely linked to longevity and reduced disease burden. Notably, the ability of probiotics to restore autophagic balance and fine-tune cell death mechanisms may also donate to the prevention or delay of age-related diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, and metabolic syndromes.

Contribution of the gut–brain axis to neurodegenerative disease development

The gut–brain axis is an integrated bidirectional communication system connecting the gastrointestinal tract with the central nervous system through neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic pathways [54]. Disruptions in this axis have emerged as important contributors to the onset and progression of major neurodegenerative diseases (NDs), including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease. Increasing evidence shows that alterations in gut microbiota composition, often referred to as dysbiosis, can initiate or amplify pathological processes that ultimately affect brain function [55, 56]. In aging and NDs, dysbiosis typically involves the loss of beneficial commensal species and the expansion of pro-inflammatory or metabolically harmful microbes. These shifts weaken intestinal barrier integrity, allowing microbial components such as lipopolysaccharides to enter the systemic circulation and trigger chronic peripheral inflammation [57]. Because inflammatory mediators readily influence microglial activation and neuronal signaling, disturbances originating in the gut can propagate to the brain and promote neuroinflammatory states that accelerate neuronal dysfunction [57].

Microbial metabolites play a central role in this process. Short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan derivatives, bile acid metabolites, and other microbial products regulate processes such as mitochondrial homeostasis, oxidative stress responses, autophagy, and programmed cell death. When metabolite production becomes imbalanced, these regulatory mechanisms are impaired, increasing susceptibility to protein misfolding, synaptic dysfunction, and progressive neuronal loss. Moreover, gut microbiota can modulate vagal nerve activity, stress hormone release, and neurotransmitter synthesis, creating additional routes through which gut disturbances can influence central nervous system physiology [58]. Collectively, these interactions illustrate how gut-derived inflammatory signals, metabolic disturbances, and neural communication converge to affect key molecular pathways implicated in neurodegeneration. The gut–brain axis therefore represents not only a source of pathogenic signals in NDs but also a promising target for therapeutic intervention aimed at restoring microbial balance, reducing inflammation, and supporting neuronal resilience.

Probiotics in alzheimer’s disease

AD is a progressive NDs considered by neuroinflammation, cognitive decline, synaptic dysfunction, and the pathological accumulation of tau tangles and Aβ plaques. Despite extensive research, current therapeutic options offer only limited symptomatic relief and do not halt or reverse disease progression. There is a critical need for novel, multi-targeted approaches that address the underlying molecular mechanisms driving AD pathology. Emerging evidence implicates dysregulated autophagy and aberrant PCD as key contributors to AD pathogenesis. Impaired autophagic flux leads to the addition of damaged organelles and toxic protein aggregates, while excessive or misregulated cell death accelerates neuronal loss. Modulating these pathways characterizes a hopeful therapeutic strategy to restore proteostasis, reduce neuroinflammation, and preserve neuronal integrity. Recent studies have identified probiotics as potential modulators of both autophagy and PCD in the central nervous system. Specific probiotic strains can influence gut-brain axis signaling, enhance autophagic clearance of Aβ, suppress neuroinflammation, and regulate apoptosis through modulation of key signaling cascades. Furthermore, microbial-derived metabolites like SCFAs have been shown to influence epigenetic regulation and neuroprotective pathways (Fig. 2).

Human senescence is characterized by a progressive deterioration in organ functionality, culminating in organ-specific age-associated chronic pathologies such as AD. Despite notable advancements in the formulation of novel pharmacological agents, innovative surgical techniques, and enhancements in dietary and lifestyle practices that have led to a replication of human lifespan, the quality of life (QoL) frequently residues suboptimal due to the heightened prevalence of age-related chronic ailments. Studies using accelerated-aging models have helped identify key cellular changes shared between aging and Alzheimer’s disease. These include increased oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, impaired autophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of the gut microbiome, where beneficial species decline and harmful ones expand. Many of these changes contribute to the progression of neurodegeneration [59], dysfunction of mitochondria, and shifts in the ingredients of gut bacteria favoring pathogenic bacteria over helpful strains are prevalent in AD, whereas anomalies like depletion of collagen, elastin, telomere shortening, and hyaluronic acid, breakdown of the DNA repair mechanisms, and compromised immune functionality are distinctively associated with the aging process; additionally, certain features such as heightened synthesis of beta-amyloids and atypical behavioral manifestations are specific to AD [60]. In an investigative study, the application of engineered probiotics as treatment modalities for the targeted delivery of biomolecules to eukaryotic cells has been identified in the context of various disorders. A work elucidated a new method for the treatment of AD predicated on the utilization of a Lactobacillus lactis strain harboring a plasmid (pExu) that encompasses a eukaryotic expression cassette encoding the human p62 protein. In the study, 3xTg-AD mice that were ordered these engineered bacteria via oral route for a duration of 2 months exhibited a significant upregulation of endogenous p62 expression within the cerebral region, facilitated by a protein delivery mechanism that engaged both neural terminations and lymphatic vessels, alongside beneficial impacts on the principal hallmarks of AD. The data demonstrated enhancements in memory performance, the modulation of autophagic and UPS processes, reductions in amyloid peptide levels, and a decrease in neuronal oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [61] (Table 2). In a separate investigation, 3xTg-AD murine models in the preliminary stages of AD were subjected to treatment with the SLAB51 probiotic formulation, which subsequently altered the ingredients of the gut bacteria and its metabolic byproducts. This modification had a significant impact on the plasma levels of inflammatory markers and essential metabolic hormones regarded as therapeutic targets in the context of neurodegeneration. The mice that underwent treatment exhibited a partial restoration of two compromised neuronal proteolytic paths, namely the autophagy and UPS. Their cognitive deterioration was mitigated in comparison to the control group, attributable to a decrease in cerebral damage and a reduced addition of Aβ aggregates [62].

In Alzheimer’s disease, the accumulation of Aβ in the brain increases oxidative stress and contributes to neuronal damage and cell death. Growing evidence also shows that the gut microbiota plays an important role in brain health, prompting increasing interest in how microbial changes may influence neurodegeneration [63]. The administration of heat-killed Ruminococcus albus (hkRA) has been documented to confer neuroprotection against oxidative stress-related neuronal injury. In a particular investigation, the purpose was to evaluate the neuroprotective efficacy of hkRA against apoptosis induced by Aβ in the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y. Treatment with hkRA (108 cells/ml) resulted in a marked reduction of Aβ-induced genetic damage and cytotoxicity in SH-SY5Y cells. Furthermore, there was a notable enhancement in the bax/bcl-2 expression ratio among the Aβ-exposed SH-SY5Y cells. In addition, hkRA treatment motivated the upregulation of genes associated with antioxidative processes, such as Nrf2, HO-1, and PKC-δ, alongside an augmentation in the expression levels of BDNF. Moreover, the protein concentrations of mitochondrial cytochrome c exhibited an increase, while those of cytosolic cytochrome c demonstrated a decrease in the treated cells [43]. The objective of a subsequent investigation was to elucidate the impact of probiotic utilizing a combination of Bifidobacterium longum R0175 and Lactobacillus helveticus R0052 on the gene expression of proteins integral to neuronal apoptosis within the hippocampus of rats subjected to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure. 4 distinct groups of subjects (LPS, Probiotic, Control and Probiotic + LPS) were administered either probiotic (109 CFU/ml/rat) or maltodextrin (as a placebo) via gavage for a duration of 2 weeks. On the fifteenth day, a singular intraperitoneal administration of either saline or LPS (1 mg/kg) was conducted, followed by a protein assessment executed through western blotting on hippocampal tissues four hours’ post-injection. LPS administration markedly elevated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and Bax, and cleaved caspase-3, concurrently diminishing procaspase-3 and Bcl-2 proteins [64]. The prophylactic intervention with these probiotic strains also mitigated LPS-induced caspase-3 activation by elevating the expression of procaspase-3 while concurrently decreasing the levels of cleaved caspase-3 within the targeted tissues [65].

Targeting autophagy and PCD represents a promising therapeutic approach in AD, given the essential function of impaired proteostasis, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss in disease development. Probiotics have emerged as potential modulators of these cellular processes through their ability to influence gut-brain axis communication, restore microbial homeostasis, and regulate key molecular pathways. Preclinical studies demonstrate that specific probiotic strains can enhance autophagic flux, promote the clearance of amyloid-beta and damaged organelles, and attenuate neuroinflammatory signaling. Additionally, probiotics have been shown to modulate various forms of PCD, thereby contributing to neuronal survival and reduced cognitive decline in animal models of AD. Microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and tryptophan derivatives further support neuroprotection by influencing epigenetic regulation and maintaining BBB integrity. In conclusion, probiotics represent a safe, accessible, and multifaceted strategy to modulate autophagy and PCD in AD [66]. Integrating probiotic-based therapies with existing pharmacological and lifestyle interventions could open new avenues for personalized, systems-based approaches in the treatment and prevention of AD.

Gram-negative bacterial dysbiosis and the resulting elevation of circulating lipopolysaccharides (LPS) have gained increasing attention as contributors to Alzheimer’s disease [67]. LPS can cross a compromised intestinal or blood–brain barrier and activate TLR4-dependent inflammatory pathways, promoting microglial activation, oxidative stress, and enhanced aggregation of amyloid-beta peptides [67]. Chronic LPS exposure has also been shown to impair autophagic flux, thereby reducing the clearance of misfolded proteins in the brain. Probiotic supplementation may counteract these harmful effects through several mechanisms: (1) reducing the abundance of LPS-producing Gram-negative bacteria, (2) strengthening intestinal barrier integrity and lowering systemic endotoxin load, (3) producing anti-inflammatory metabolites such as SCFAs that suppress microglial activation, and (4) restoring autophagy-related pathways involved in Aβ degradation [67]. Notably, strains such as Lactobacillus plantarum, Bifidobacterium longum, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus have been shown to reduce LPS-induced neuroinflammation and attenuate Aβ accumulation in preclinical models [67]. These findings suggest that probiotics may indirectly limit LPS-mediated neurotoxicity and represent a promising adjunctive strategy to mitigate amyloid pathology in Alzheimer’s disease. Loffredo and colleagues [68] conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to investigate whether the probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) can reduce systemic inflammation and gut-derived endotoxin burden in older adults with mild AD. The underlying rationale is that Gram-negative bacteria in the gut release lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which may translocate across a leaky gut barrier and promote neuroinflammation and oxidative stress via activation of NADPH oxidase-2 (NOX2). In AD, increased gut permeability (“leaky gut”) and chronic inflammation may exacerbate disease progression [68].

In this study, 40 participants with mild AD were randomized to receive either EcN (2.5–25 × 10^9 CFU per capsule) or placebo daily for six weeks. Serum biomarkers measured before and after the intervention included soluble NOX2-derived peptide (sNOX2-dp), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) production, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), LPS, and zonulin (a marker of gut permeability). Cognitive function was assessed via the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [68]. After six weeks, the EcN-treated group showed a significant reduction in sNOX2-dp (–21%), H₂O₂ (–27%), TNF-α (–18%), LPS (–15%), and zonulin (–35%) compared to baseline, whereas the placebo group showed no meaningful changes. Importantly, improvements in gut barrier integrity (as indicated by zonulin) correlated with reductions in circulating LPS (Spearman’s ρ = 0.408, p = 0.011). Moreover, EcN supplementation was associated with improvements in MMSE scores over the study period. The findings suggest that E. coli Nissle 1917 may help reinforce intestinal barrier function, reduce endotoxemia, and attenuate oxidative stress and systemic inflammation in AD patients. These effects may offer a novel, gut-targeted strategy to mitigate inflammatory and oxidative mechanisms implicated in Alzheimer’s pathology. The authors note, however, that larger and longer trials are needed to confirm these preliminary benefits and to determine whether such improvements translate into sustained cognitive protection [68].

Probiotics in parkinson’s disease

PD is a progressive NDs considered by the selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the addition of α-synuclein aggregates, ultimately leading to non-motor and motor symptoms that meaningfully impair QoL. Emerging evidence implicates disrupted autophagy and dysregulated PCD pathways in the pathogenesis of PD. At the same time, growing interest in the gut–brain axis has discovered that gut microbiota dysbiosis may serve a critical role in controlling neurodegeneration through metabolic, immune, and neurochemical pathways. Probiotics suggestion a non-invasive and promising approach to restore microbial balance, reduce neuroinflammation, enhance neurotrophic signaling, and potentially regulate autophagy and PCD mechanisms.

In an investigation, an engineered strain of Clostridium butyricum-GLP-1 was developed, representing a probiotic variant of C. butyricum that reliably synthesizes glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) with the objective of exploring its therapeutic implications in the context of PD treatment. The findings revealed that C. butyricum-GLP-1 has the capacity to enhance motor function and mitigate neuropathological alterations by elevating tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression and diminishing alpha-synuclein (α-syn) levels. Additionally, it was substantiated that C. butyricum-GLP-1 rectified the dysbiosis observed in PD mice by decreasing the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium, reinforcing gut integrity, and upregulating the expression of GPR41/43. Intriguingly, it was discovered that the neuroprotective properties could be mediated via the promotion of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and the attenuation of oxidative damage [30] (Table 3).

A research investigation was conducted to explore the prospective neuroprotective properties and the underlying mechanisms of Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) and polymannuronic acid (PM), as well as their synergistic effects in a chronic PD murine model. The findings indicated that the oral intake of prebiotic LGG or PM, either individually or as combined, over a duration of five weeks could mitigate dopaminergic neuronal degeneration by enhancing diminished ambulation and activity levels, alongside ameliorating compromised muscle strength in behavioral assessments through the upregulation of TH gene and/or protein expression in the striatum or midbrain of PD mice. Remarkably, combined LGG and PM exhibited significantly superior neuroprotective influences compared with the administration of LGG or PM alone. The neuroprotective action of PM was attributed to a mechanism involving SCFAs, which exerted anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects. The neuroprotective impacts of LGG may be correlated with its capacity to elevation glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) expression in the striatum and to augment the abundance of Clostridiales bacteria. In experiments involving PD mice that received an administration of PM + LGG, the presence of PM as a prebiotic facilitated the proliferation of specific bacterial populations (notably from the Bacilli family to the Lactobacillus genus) within the colon, which subsequently contributed to the enhancement of BBB integrity and the upregulation of BDNF and GDNF expression levels, consequently leading to the inhibition of apoptosis within the striatum [44]. In a subsequent investigation, the impacts of the innovative formulation SLAB51 on PD were examined. The results demonstrated that this probiotic formulation possesses the capacity to mitigate the adverse impacts of 6-OHDA in both in vivo and in vitro models of PD. Apoptosis was markedly elevated following 6-OHDA treatment, whereas the introduction of the probiotic mixture significantly diminished the apoptotic nuclei index until nearly 10%, thereby suggesting that the probiotic formulation provides a protective impact against 6-OHDA-induced apoptosis [69].

A subsequent investigation surveyed the neurotrophic role of the novel probiotic strain L. lactis MG1363-pMG36e-GLP-1 in murine models of PD induced by 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP). The findings indicated that L. lactis significantly ameliorated motor impairments resulting from MPTP through the rescue of dopaminergic (DAergic) neuronal degeneration within the substantia nigra (SN). It was observed that L. lactis exerts neurotrophic effects by activating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling cascade, which led to the down-regulation of ACSL4 and the up-regulation of FSP1 expression, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. Furthermore, the reduction in oxidative stress levels, achieved through the suppression of ROS and MDA production, corroborated these findings. Finally, it was ascertained that the administration of L. lactis rectified dysbiosis in PD mice by enhancing the abundance of Akkermansia, Oscillospira, and Sutterella at the genus classification level [26]. Another investigation was conducted to assess the neuroprotective properties of Lactobacillus plantarum DP189 on the apoptosis of dopaminergic neurons located in the substantia nigra (SN) of a PD model in murine subjects. Male C57BL/6 mice underwent induction of a PD model and received treatment via oral intake of L. plantarum DP189 for a duration of 14 days. The findings demonstrated that L. plantarum DP189 enhanced behavioral performance and elevated dopamine and 5-hydroxytryptamine concentrations in the PD-affected mice. L. plantarum DP189 significantly augmented the incidence of TH-positive cells. Furthermore, L. plantarum DP189 activated the AKT/mTOR and ERK2 signaling pathways, facilitated the upregulation of Bcl-2 expression, and suppressed the activities of Bax and Caspase 3 [70]. Overall, probiotics represent a promising therapeutic approach in PD by modulating autophagy and various forms of PCD. Current evidence suggests that specific probiotic strains can improve dopaminergic neuron survival, reduce neuroinflammation, and restore gut–brain axis balance through pathways such as PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, apoptosis inhibition, and ferroptosis suppression. These data highlight the possible of microbiome-based strategies as adjunctive treatments in neurodegenerative disease management.

Future research directions and clinical implications

Future investigation should focus on recognizing the specific probiotic strains that can modulate autophagy and various forms of PCD, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, in neural cells. Detailed mechanistic studies are required to elucidate how microbial metabolites, like SCFAs and neurotransmitter-like composites, interact with cellular signaling pathways such as mTOR, AMPK, SIRT1, and Nrf2, which are central to neuronal survival and aging. Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) is recognized as a pivotal anti-aging regulator that modulates autophagy and multiple pathways of programmed cell death, including pyroptosis, apoptosis, and ferroptosis, thereby contributing to neuronal survival and the prevention of neurodegeneration [50]. Although the central role of SIRT1 in maintaining cellular homeostasis and brain aging is well established, current evidence directly linking probiotic interventions to SIRT1 activation remains limited. One available study suggests that probiotics may influence upstream metabolic and inflammatory signals capable of modulating SIRT1 activity, thereby offering a potential mechanism through which probiotic therapy could contribute to the attenuation or reversal of brain aging. Further research is required to clarify the extent to which specific probiotic strains can stimulate SIRT1-dependent pathways and whether such effects translate into meaningful neuroprotective outcomes in aging populations [50].

Integrating multi-omics methods—such as transcriptomics, metbolomics, metagenomics, and proteomics—will be instrumental in uncovering host–microbe interactions that regulate autophagic flux and cell death in the aging brain, potentially revealing novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Another critical avenue involves investigating how probiotics remodel the gut microbiota to influence neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and protein aggregation, all of which are key pathological features of neurodegenerative diseases. This line of research should include examining their effects on microglial activation and BBB integrity via the microbiota–gut–brain axis. The improvement of next-generation probiotics that can be engineered to deliver therapeutic molecules or genes targeting autophagy and PCD pathways directly to the central nervous system represents a promising strategy that warrants further exploration. Longitudinal studies using age-appropriate animal models—like primates, aged rodents, Drosophila and C. elegans—will be necessary to estimate the long-term impacts of probiotic intake on brain aging and neurodegeneration. Additionally, combining probiotics with established autophagy-inducing agents like resveratrol, rapamycin, or metformin, as well as lifestyle interventions such as exercise, may reveal synergistic neuroprotective effects. Personalized probiotic therapies tailored to genetic backgrounds, individual microbiome profiles, and disease phenotypes are likely to be more effective and should be a central goal in future translational research.

Next-generation probiotics (NGPs) refer to newly identified commensal microbial strains—such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Bacteroides species—that exert well-defined mechanistic effects beyond those of traditional probiotic genera. These strains possess enhanced immunoregulatory, metabolic, and epithelial-supporting functions that make them promising candidates for targeted microbiome-based therapies. Their relevance to neurodegenerative diseases lies in their ability to modulate neuroinflammation, restore impaired autophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis, strengthen gut barrier integrity, and generate neuroprotective metabolites with epigenetic and anti-inflammatory activity. By influencing multiple pathogenic nodes simultaneously, NGPs represent a potential next step in precision probiotic interventions aimed at slowing or modifying the progression of NDs.

Clinically, probiotics capable of modulating autophagy and PCD may emerge as valuable adjuncts to conventional treatments for AD, PD, and other neurodegenerative disorders, offering a non-invasive and safe approach to slow disease progression. In the context of aging, these interventions may help preserve cognitive function, delay frailty, and promote healthy brain aging. The identification of gut microbiota signatures and microbial metabolites related to autophagy could also lead to novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for early detection of neurodegenerative conditions. Furthermore, neuroprotective probiotic formulations could be integrated into clinical nutrition strategies, supporting brain health maintenance in elderly populations. Nevertheless, for successful clinical translation, challenges such as standardizing probiotic formulations, optimizing dosages, ensuring CNS bioavailability, and monitoring therapeutic responses need to be addressed. Regulatory frameworks must adapt to accommodate engineered probiotic therapeutics, and rigorous clinical trials are essential to confirm safety and efficacy. In the broader public health context, probiotic-based approaches may contribute significantly to preventive strategies against cognitive decline and age-related NDs, particularly in aging societies worldwide.

Conclusions

Mounting evidence highlights the pivotal role of autophagy and PCD pathways in maintaining neuronal homeostasis and counteracting the development of NDs and aging-related cognitive decline. Dysregulation of these cellular processes is a common pathological hallmark in disorders such as AD, PD, and other age-associated neurodegenerative conditions. The modulation of autophagy and PCD via gut–brain axis mechanisms presents a compelling therapeutic strategy, and probiotics have emerged as potent modulators in this context. This review consolidates data from various experimental models demonstrating that specific probiotic strains and formulations can exert neuroprotective effects by modulating autophagy, apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial integrity, and synaptic plasticity. Probiotics influence key molecular targets such as Beclin-1, LC3, Bcl-2, Bax, caspase-3, Nrf2, and p62, while also restoring gut microbial balance and enhancing the production of SCFAs that have systemic neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. In particular, engineered probiotics and multi-strain formulations have shown promise in enhancing cognitive functions, reducing amyloid-beta burden, and preserving neuronal integrity in animal models of neurodegeneration. While these findings are encouraging, clinical translation remains in its infancy. The complexity of host–microbiota interactions, strain-specific effects, dosage optimization, and long-term safety profiles must be thoroughly investigated in human populations. Furthermore, integrating probiotic therapy with existing pharmacological and lifestyle interventions could offer synergistic benefits, particularly in preventive strategies for at-risk aging individuals. In summary, probiotics represent a novel and promising class of biotherapeutics that can target autophagy and PCD, providing new avenues for the management of neurodegenerative diseases and healthy aging. Continued multidisciplinary research and rigorously designed clinical trials are essential to unlock their full therapeutic potential and establish their role in personalized neuroprotective strategies.
