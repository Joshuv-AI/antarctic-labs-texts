Synergistic Ce–Ce dimerization regulates electronic hybridization for enhanced oxygen reduction
Authors: Xue Bai, Jingru Sun, Fuquan Bai, Lina Li, Jingqi Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Compared with 3d transition metals, rare-earth elements, particularly cerium, exhibit more markedly sluggish Fenton-like reaction kinetics, making them more stable under oxidative oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) conditions. Here, a dual-atom cerium catalyst is fabricated on nitrogen-doped graphene (Ce2-NG) through an ultrafast Joule-heating strategy. This rapid synthesis enables the construction of Ce–Ce dimers within milliseconds, effectively suppressing aggregation and ensuring the atomic-level stabilization. Ce2-NG demonstrates remarkable ORR performance, reaching a half-wave potential of 0.906 V, along with superior durability and strong resistance to methanol crossover. When employed as the cathode catalyst in a zinc–air battery (ZAB), the device exhibits an elevated open-circuit voltage and excellent long-term operational stability, clearly surpassing those of Pt/C + RuO2 counterparts. Operando spectroscopy combined with density functional theory supports the Ce2–N6 moiety as the active center, where dual-atom cerium cooperation drives charge redistribution, enhances *OH binding, and lowers the free energy change of the potential-determining step. This study introduces a controllable synthesis method for constructing rare-earth dual-atom catalysts, broadening the compositional landscape of atomically dispersed electrocatalysts for sustainable energy conversion.

Introduction

The ORR plays a crucial function in driving electrochemical energy conversion systems including fuel cells and ZABs.1,2 Yet, the inherently sluggish multi-electron transfer kinetics of the ORR greatly constrains the overall power efficiency of these energy devices.3,4 At present, platinum-based materials are considered the state-of-the-art ORR electrocatalysts due to their excellent intrinsic activity. Nevertheless, their high cost, low durability, and vulnerability to poisoning significantly impede large-scale commercialization. Thus, it is of urgent importance to develop alternative catalysts that are efficient, stable, and economically viable.5,6

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) have attracted extensive interest due to their nearly complete metal utilization, precisely defined coordination structures, and unique electronic properties. In particular, transition-metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C) SACs have demonstrated promising activity toward the ORR.7,8 Yet, their isolated nature inherently limits the number of accessible active sites and constrains their ability to simultaneously adsorb and convert multiple reaction intermediates simultaneously. In addition, the lack of inter-site cooperativity hinders electron redistribution and multi-pathway selectivity, resulting in suboptimal kinetics in complex multi-electron processes such as the ORR.

To overcome these limitations, dual-atom catalysts (DACs) have arisen as a next-generation class of materials bridging the advantages of single-atom and nanoparticle catalysts. By anchoring two neighboring metal centers in well-defined coordination environments, DACs preserve the atomic dispersion of SACs while enabling strong metal–metal cooperative interactions, which can fundamentally tune the electronic structure and catalytic behavior of the active sites.9 Unlike isolated single atoms, which typically follow end-on *O2 adsorption pathways and suffer from limited intermediate binding versatility, dual-atom sites offer richer structural motifs and multiple adsorption configurations—including side-on and bridge modes.10 In DACs, the presence of a second neighboring atom introduces local asymmetry and electronic polarization, which can be leveraged to tune adsorption energies and accelerate rate-determining steps such as *OOH to *O conversion.9 Moreover, cooperative effects between metal atoms enable delocalized charge distribution, leading to enhanced orbital overlap with reaction intermediates. It results in improved activation of the O–O bond and lowered energy barriers for four-electron pathways. DACs also offer more flexibility in optimizing metal–ligand coordination geometry, allowing for fine-tuning of reactivity and selectivity across different pH environments.11 However, these transition-metal-based DACs frequently suffer from poor long-term durability. This vulnerability mainly arises from the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide, which readily engage in Fenton-like reactions that attack the metal centers, leading to detachment or aggregation of active sites and severe performance degradation over time. This critical drawback highlights a fundamental challenge in the field: how to design DACs that are not only catalytically active and electronically tunable but also inherently resistant to oxidative decomposition under harsh electrochemical conditions.12

Addressing this challenge calls for exploring beyond the conventional 3d transition metals to alternative elements that can mitigate ROS-induced degradation while still enabling synergistic catalytic mechanisms. Rare-earth elements, particularly cerium (Ce), present a promising yet underexplored avenue for DAC development. Ce possesses several unique properties: a flexible redox pair (Ce3+/Ce4+), a partially filled 4f shell that can influence nearby d orbitals via spin–orbit coupling, and strong metal–ligand covalency that aids in anchoring atomic sites.13 Most notably, Ce exhibits a markedly sluggish Fenton-like reaction, making it far more stable under oxidative ORR conditions compared to 3d transition metals.14 These attributes collectively suggest that Ce-based DACs could overcome the instability issues plaguing conventional systems, providing a pathway toward designing DACs that integrate high activity with exceptional electrochemical robustness.

Despite these advantages, Ce has largely been studied in the form of oxides or as single-atom Ce–N–C sites, with limited success in maximizing ORR kinetics due to low conductivity or insufficient intermediate binding strength. To date, there is no experimental report on Ce-based DACs with atomic-level Ce–Ce coupling in the context of the ORR. The question of how two closely positioned Ce atoms jointly modulate the electronic environment, influence intermediate adsorption, and cooperate during the catalytic cycle remains entirely open. This gap provides a timely opportunity to explore rare-earth DACs, particularly Ce2-based architectures, as a new frontier in electrocatalyst design. Recent advances in oxygen electrocatalysis and zinc–air batteries have provided valuable insights for the rational design of advanced non-precious metal catalysts.15–18

In this study, we present the fabrication of a dual-atom cerium catalyst (Ce2-NG) using a structurally defined molecular precursor, [Ce2(BA)4(NO3)2(phen)2] (Ce2L), by rapid pyrolysis under an ammonia atmosphere. This strategy allows for accurate regulation of the spatial arrangement and coordination of Ce–Ce dimers, yielding atomically isolated Ce2 centers anchored onto nitrogen-doped graphene. Structural analyses suggest the formation of Ce–Ce pairs with strong Ce–N bonding. Ce2-NG exhibits outstanding ORR activity (E1/2 = 0.906 V), outperforming Pt/C and Ce SACs, with excellent stability and a four-electron pathway. DFT simulations demonstrate that Ce–Ce synergy optimizes intermediate binding and electron transfer. When applied in ZABs, Ce2-NG achieves high power density and specific capacity, showcasing its promise as a rare-earth DAC for efficient and durable electrocatalysis.

Experimental methods

Synthesis of the [Ce2(BA)4(NO3)2(phen)2] ([Ce2L]) precursor

0.2606 g (0.6 mmol) of Ce(NO3)3·6H2O was dissolved in 4 mL H2O and 10 mL ethanol. Separately, 0.1388 g (0.7 mmol) of phen·H2O, 0.1452 g (1.0 mmol) of HQ, and 0.7327 g (6.0 mmol) of HBA were dissolved in 10 mL ethanol. They were mixed and left to stand for 24 h under ambient conditions to allow crystallization. Subsequently, the obtained colorless crystals were separated through vacuum filtration, rinsed repeatedly with ethanol three times, and dried at 60 °C.

Synthesis of Ce2-NG, Ce1-NG and Ce2-G

100 mg of graphene oxide and 2.23 mg of [Ce2(BA)4(NO3)2(phen)2] were dispersed in 50 mL deionized water to obtain a uniform suspension via ultrasonication for 30 min.19 After removing water by rotary evaporation, [Ce2(BA)4(NO3)2(phen)2] was anchored on GO through electrostatic interactions with the oxygen-containing functional groups on GO. The composite was subsequently exposed to flash pyrolysis in an NH3 environment using a Joule ultrafast heating device (U = 30 V; I = 150 A) to afford Ce2-NG. For comparison, Ce2-G was synthesized by performing the same procedure under a pure N2 atmosphere without NH3, whereas Ce1-NG was obtained by replacing the binuclear precursor with a mononuclear Ce complex, followed by pyrolysis under identical NH3 conditions.

Results and discussion

Materials characterization

The Ce2-NG catalyst was synthesized via a ligand-directed strategy followed by an ammonia-assisted thermal treatment (Fig. 1a). Initially, a binuclear cerium precursor, Ce2L (Fig. S1 and S2), was prepared by coordinating Ce3+ ions with benzoate, 8-hydroxyquinoline and 1,10-phenanthroline ligands. The obtained crystals were adsorbed onto graphene oxide (GO) by ultrasonic dispersion. Finally, the composite was subjected to rapid pyrolysis under an NH3 atmosphere, leading to the formation of nitrogen-doped graphene-supported dual-atom Ce sites (Ce2-NG). For comparison, a single-atom Ce catalyst (Ce1-NG) was prepared by replacing the binuclear precursor with a mononuclear Ce complex, while a nitrogen-free dual-atom Ce catalyst (Ce2-G) was synthesized under an inert N2 atmosphere without ammonia introduction.

The high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) image of Ce2-NG (Fig. 1b) displays typical crumpled nanosheet morphology, which is characteristic of the reduced graphene oxide framework. Such structure can effectively expose abundant surface sites and facilitate mass transport. Elemental mapping confirms that C, Ce, and N are homogeneously distributed across the entire graphene matrix (Fig. 1c). This uniform dispersion suggests successful integration of Ce species with the carbon support. HAADF-STEM imaging reveals numerous adjacent bright spots, highlighted by red boxes, indicating the presence of closely paired Ce atoms (Fig. 1d and e). The measured Ce–Ce distance is approximately 0.35 nm in both region 1 and region 2 (Fig. 1f and g), consistent with the expected separation for dual-atom configurations. Statistical analysis of HAADF-STEM images from three different regions (Fig. S3 and Table S1) reveals that paired Ce atoms constitute the majority (80.3%) of the observed features, with only a minor fraction of isolated atoms and negligible larger aggregates. On the basis of this analysis, the Ce2 configuration is identified as the most likely structural model for the dominant active sites in Ce2-NG.

XRD analysis was performed to probe the phase composition of Ce2-NG. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, all samples exhibit two pronounced XRD peaks at about 25° and 44°, corresponding to the (0 0 2) and (1 0 1) planes of graphitic carbon, respectively.20 This result indicates a highly graphitized nature, which is beneficial for achieving high electrical conductivity. Notably, there are no detectable peaks associated with metallic Ce, Ce-based oxides, or Ce-based carbides, implying that Ce species remain highly dispersed at the atomic level. XPS measurements further verify the composition and chemical speciation of the synthesized catalyst. The full-range spectrum demonstrates the presence of C, O, N, and Ce elements in Ce2-NG (Fig. S4). High-resolution Ce 3d spectra can be deconvoluted into five characteristic peaks. The binding energies of 878.1 and 887.6 eV are characteristic of Ce 3d5/2, while those at 899.6 and 908.1 eV correspond to Ce 3d3/2 (Fig. 2b).21 Meanwhile, the high-resolution N 1s spectrum reveals five types of nitrogen functionalities. Peaks located at 398.4, 399.0, 400.5, 401.3, and 404.2 eV are assigned to pyridinic N, Ce–N coordination, pyrrolic N, graphitic N, and oxidized N, respectively (Fig. 2c).22 This result demonstrates that Ce atoms are predominantly coordinated with nitrogen, forming stable Ce–N bonds, which is critical for anchoring the dual-atom sites and modulating their electronic environment.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was further employed to characterize the chemical state and surrounding coordination environment of Ce atoms in Ce2-NG. Fig. 2d shows the Ce L3-edge XANES spectra of Ce2-NG, Ce1-NG, Ce2O3, and CeO2. The white-line intensity of CeO2 is the strongest. Ce1-NG and Ce2-NG show a similarly strong white-line feature to that of Ce2O3, indicating that Ce mainly exists in a valence state of +3, which is consistent with the XPS results. The k3-weighted EXAFS spectra of Ce2-NG, Ce1-NG, and Ce2O3 are presented in Fig. 2e. The main peak corresponding to the Ce–N coordination in Ce2-NG appears at 1.66 Å, which is slightly shorter than that in Ce–N (1.75 Å), indicating a more contracted Ce–N bond length in the dual-atom configuration and thus a modified local coordination environment around Ce.23 In addition, a weak peak is observed around ∼3 Å, which is absent in Ce1-NG and can be assigned to the Ce–Ce scattering path, indicating that two neighboring Ce atoms form a dual-atom configuration. Further quantitative fitting of the FT-EXAFS curves in both R and K spaces (Fig. 2f and Table S2) yields bond lengths of approximately 2.26 ± 0.023 Å for Ce–N and 3.52 ± 0.012 Å for Ce–Ce. These results are in agreement with the HAADF-STEM observations and provide strong validation for the creation of Ce dual sites. Wavelet transform (WT) EXAFS analysis was carried out to distinguish different scattering paths. For Ce2-NG, a strong intensity maximum is observed at (1.7 Å, 4.9 Å−1) (Fig. 2g), which differs from the first-shell signal of Ce1-NG at (2.1 Å, 6.9 Å−1) (Fig. 2h) and Ce2O3 at (1.9 Å, 5.3 Å−1) (Fig. 2i), further confirming Ce–N coordination.24 Additionally, a second intensity maximum is detected at (2.3 Å, 1.7 Å−1), corresponding to the Ce–Ce path, which indicates the coexistence of dual Ce atoms in the catalyst. Together, the collective evidence verifies the successful creation of atomically dispersed Ce2–N6 sites, which are expected to serve as highly active centres for oxygen reduction.

ORR performance

The ORR activity of Ce2-NG was thoroughly assessed in O2-saturated 0.1 M KOH using a standard three-electrode configuration equipped with a rotating disk electrode (RDE). To distinguish the Ce–Ce dimer effect from support and N-coordination contributions, we compared Ce2-NG with control samples including NG (Ce-free), Ce2-G (without N-doping), and Ce1-NG (single-atom Ce). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were first conducted in O2- and N2-saturated electrolyte without electrode rotation (Fig. 3a). Under N2-saturated conditions, all catalysts exhibit featureless CV curves with no observable reduction peaks, confirming the absence of oxygen reduction. In contrast, clear cathodic ORR peaks emerge in O2-saturated electrolyte, indicating that the ORR has occurred. Ce2-NG exhibits the most positive cathodic peak potential of 0.921 V, which is significantly higher than those of G (0.767 V), NG (0.736 V), Ce2-G (0.817 V), Ce1-NG (0.873 V), and even Pt/C (0.866 V). This finding further underscores the superior ORR catalytic activity of Ce2-NG under practical static conditions. To gain deeper insights, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were then collected with RDE rotation (Fig. 3b). Ce2-NG exhibits an impressive onset potential of 0.989 V and a half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.906 V vs. RHE. These values are notably more positive than those of G (Eonset = 0.822 V, E1/2 = 0.741 V), NG (0.855 V, 0.743 V), Ce2-G (0.889 V, 0.795 V), Ce1-NG (0.959 V, 0.861 V), and even commercial Pt/C (0.896 V, 0.844 V), underscoring the exceptional ORR activity of Ce2-NG. The high limiting current density indicates efficient mass transport of O2 to the catalyst surface.25 The catalytic kinetics was further examined by constructing Tafel plots from the LSV data (Fig. 3c). Ce2-NG displays a remarkably low Tafel slope of 32 mV dec−1, considerably smaller than those measured for G (84 mV dec−1), NG (107 mV dec−1), Ce2-G (83 mV dec−1), Ce1-NG (62 mV dec−1), and Pt/C (55 mV dec−1). Such a small Tafel slope indicates that Ce2-NG facilitates rapid charge transfer and can drive large current densities even at low overpotentials, highlighting its superior intrinsic ORR kinetics.

To probe the catalytic kinetics and mechanistic origin of the enhanced ORR activity of Ce2-NG, double-layer capacitance (Cdl) measurements were performed by recording CV curves at scan rates ranging from 10 to 100 mV s−1 in the non-faradaic region (Fig. 3d, S5 and S6). The calculated Cdl values were then used to estimate the electrochemical active surface area (ECSA). A higher Cdl generally indicates a larger ECSA, suggesting more exposed active sites available for the adsorption of oxygenated intermediates. As clearly illustrated in Fig. 3d, S5 and S6, Ce2-NG exhibits Cdl and ECSA values of 17.07 mF cm−2 and 426.8 cm2, respectively, which are substantially greater than those of G (5.08 mF cm−2, 127 cm2), NG (10.70 mF cm−2, 267.5 cm2), Ce2-G (7.69 mF cm−2, 192.1 cm2), Ce1-NG (12.56 mF cm−2, 313.9 cm2), and even Pt/C (15.82 mF cm−2, 395.5 cm2). Such a large ECSA provides more accessible catalytic sites, which likely contributes to the outstanding ORR performance of Ce2-NG.26

The effect of annealing temperature on the electrocatalytic performance of Ce2-NG was systematically explored. As illustrated by the LSV curves in Fig. S7, the catalyst treated at 1300 °C exhibits the most outstanding activity among all samples. Compared to those annealed at other temperatures, this sample demonstrates clearly enhanced onset and half-wave potentials, highlighting the significance of an optimized thermal process in promoting catalytic performance. This enhancement likely stems from the formation of dual-atom Ce sites with favourable nitrogen coordination at this temperature, which stabilizes the active centers and fine-tunes their electronic environment. The Tafel plots further reinforce this conclusion, as the sample annealed at 1300 °C shows the smallest slope, indicative of accelerated charge transfer and superior ORR kinetics. Together, these results underscore the critical role of precise thermal regulation in tailoring the local structure and maximizing the intrinsic activity of Ce2-NG. To further distinguish the intrinsic contribution of the Ce–Ce dimer from other factors, we compared the Ce-mass-normalized activities and estimated TOF values of Ce2-NG and Ce1-NG (Table S3). At 0.85 V, Ce2-NG exhibits a mass-normalized kinetic current of approximately 0.70 A gCe−1 and a TOF of 0.052 s−1, approximately 1.6–1.7 times higher than those of Ce1-NG (0.42 A gCe−1 and 0.032 s−1). Additionally, Ce2-NG possesses a larger ECSA (426.8 cm2) than Ce1-NG (313.9 cm2), which likely contributes to the observed activity enhancement. Therefore, while the Ce–Ce synergy plays a role in the improved performance, the activity enhancement is best described as arising from a combination of factors, including electronic effects from the dimeric configuration and larger ECSA. Ce2-NG exhibits superior ORR activity, surpassing the vast majority of dual-atom electrocatalysts reported previously (Fig. 3e and Table S4).

To further elucidate the ORR mechanism of Ce2-NG, LSV measurements were carried out at rotation speeds ranging from 400 to 2500 rpm (Fig. 3f). As expected, the current density progressively increased with increasing rotation rate, indicating enhanced mass transport of oxygen to the electrode surface. The corresponding Koutecky–Levich (K–L) plots, shown as insets, reveal nearly parallel and linear relationships, suggesting first-order kinetics with respect to dissolved oxygen. Quantitative analysis of the slopes indicates that Ce2-NG follows an average electron transfer number of approximately 3.93, closely matching that of Pt/C (3.97), which signifies a predominant four-electron ORR pathway that directly reduces O2 to H2O, effectively suppressing the undesirable two-electron route leading to peroxide formation.27 This conclusion was further validated by rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) tests (Fig. S8 and 3g). Ce2-NG exhibited a peroxide yield consistently below 5%, with calculated electron transfer numbers exceeding 3.9, comparable with those of Pt/C. These results conclusively demonstrate that Ce2-NG catalyses the ORR via an efficient four-electron pathway in alkaline media, which is critical for maximizing the energy efficiency of zinc–air batteries.

Long-term operational stability is pivotal for evaluating the practical feasibility of ORR catalysts.28 To this end, we monitored the chronoamperometric performance of Ce2-NG at 0.6 V vs. RHE in O2-saturated 0.1 M KOH electrolyte over a duration of 12 hours (Fig. 3h). Notably, Ce2-NG sustained approximately 86% of its initial current, markedly outperforming Pt/C, which retained only about 60% under identical conditions, underscoring its superior electrochemical robustness. To further verify the structural stability after prolonged operation, we performed TEM characterization on the Ce2-NG catalyst after the 12 h durability test. As shown in Fig. S9, the post-ORR TEM image reveals that the catalyst retains its typical crumpled nanosheet morphology, with no obvious aggregation or nanoparticle formation observed. The catalyst's tolerance to methanol crossover was further examined by introducing 3 M methanol into the alkaline solution and tracking the i–t response (Fig. 3i). While Pt/C exhibited a sharp decline in current due to methanol adsorption and subsequent site blockage, Ce2-NG displayed minimal current change, indicating high resilience against methanol-induced deactivation. This highlights its promise for applications prone to the fuel crossover issue.

Additionally, thiocyanate poisoning tests were carried out by adding 10 mM KSCN to the electrolyte to interrogate the nature of the active sites (Fig. S10). Following SCN− addition, Ce-based catalysts, including Ce2-NG, Ce1-NG, and Ce2-G, experienced pronounced decreases in the onset potential, E1/2, and limiting current density, analogous to Pt/C. This behaviour suggests a preferential binding of SCN− to Ce centres, effectively quenching their catalytic activity and indicating that Ce species are involved in the ORR. Meanwhile, the assignment of the Ce–Ce dimer as the primary active site is supported by the combined evidence from HAADF-STEM, EXAFS, operando XAS, and DFT calculations.

Mechanistic understanding of the ORR on Ce2-NG

To probe the ORR mechanism over Ce2-NG, in situ electrochemical Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor the evolution of surface species under varying potentials in 0.1 M KOH (Fig. S11). The Raman spectra reveal characteristic D and G bands of carbon at 1350 cm−1 and 1630 cm−1, respectively,29 confirming the graphitic structure of the catalyst support. Notably, as the applied potential decreased from 0.8 to 0.2 V, a broad new band emerged around 1147 cm−1, which can be tentatively attributed to the stretching vibration of O–O bonds in adsorbed superoxide (*O2−) species. This indicates that the initial step of O2 reduction likely involves a one-electron transfer to generate *O2− intermediates.30 Additionally, a distinct band appeared near 1550 cm−1, tentatively assignable to the O–OH stretching mode in *OOH intermediates, suggesting further proton-coupled reduction of *O2− to *OOH on the catalyst surface. These tentative assignments are consistent with previous isotope-labelled in situ Raman studies on carbon-based ORR catalysts, where similar bands were assigned to *O2− and *OOH intermediates.31–33

To complement these findings, XPS analyses were conducted to probe changes in the oxidation states of Ce and its coordination with nitrogen after ORR operation (Fig. S12). Both the pristine and post-reaction samples exhibit characteristic Ce 3d features corresponding to the +3 valence state, and the binding energies show no noticeable shift after the ORR, suggesting that the overall electronic state of Ce remains stable during catalysis.34 Moreover, the persistence of a clear Ce–N contribution in the N 1s region demonstrates that the coordination framework of the dual-atom sites remains intact, confirming their robustness under electrochemical conditions.

Operando XAS measurements were carried out to unravel the dynamic structural and electronic evolution of Ce2-NG during the ORR. To capture the key stages of the ORR process, measurements were performed at three representative potentials: OCP (resting state), 0.9 V (ORR onset region), and 0.7 V (near the half-wave potential, where the ORR proceeds actively) (Fig. 4a). The white line intensity of Ce2-NG at 0.9 V and 0.7 V is slightly lower than that at OCP, indicating a gradual reduction of Ce sites under cathodic polarization. To quantitatively evaluate the valence evolution, the average Ce oxidation state at each potential was determined by linear combination fitting (LCF) of the XANES spectra using Ce3+ and Ce4+ standard references (Fig. S13). The calculated average oxidation states were +3.17 (OCP), +3.11 (0.9 V), and +3.09 (0.7 V), confirming that more Ce sites are reduced to lower oxidation states as the potential decreases. This reduction weakens OH/O adsorption on Ce sites, promoting intermediate desorption and consequently lowering the ORR overpotential, consistent with the DFT calculations. Consistent with this redox adaptation, FT-EXAFS spectra (Fig. 4b) and their corresponding fitting results (Fig. 4c, d, S14 and Table S5) resolve persistent Ce–N scattering at all potentials (N = 2.6–2.7), evidencing that the di-Ce motif remains robustly anchored to the N-doped graphene support.35 At 0.9 V, an additional Ce–O contribution emerges (N ≈ 1.41; R ≈ 1.85 Å), consistent with the adsorption of oxygenated intermediates such as *OOH. Upon further polarization to 0.7 V, the Ce–O coordination number decreases (N = 0.69) and the Ce–O distance (1.73 Å) shortens, indicative of the *OOH–*O/*OH conversion.36 Concomitantly, the Ce–N bond length slightly expands (2.29–2.35 Å), whereas the Ce–Ce distance contracts at 0.9 V and partially rebounds at 0.7 V (3.63–3.39–3.42 Å), reflecting local geometric relaxation coupled to charge redistribution rather than structural degradation.

Wavelet-transform EXAFS (Fig. 4e–g) consistently resolves strong Ce–N/O and Ce–Ce signatures across potentials, further confirming the structural integrity of the dual-atom Ce site under reaction conditions. Taken together, the combination of redox flexibility, intermediate-responsive coordination, and preserved metal–support anchoring rationalizes the fast ORR kinetics of Ce2-NG: reversible Ce4+/Ce3+ cycling modulates OH/O binding and accelerates product desorption, thereby lowering the overall reaction barrier. The potential-dependent evolution of the Ce oxidation state and coordination environment observed by operando XAS can be rationalized by the DFT-calculated free-energy diagram. The decrease in the Ce oxidation state from OCP to 0.7 V correlates with the sequential formation of *OOH, *O, and *OH intermediates along the four-electron pathway, while the emergence and transformation of the Ce–O scattering path at 0.9 V and 0.7 V correspond to the DFT-predicted *OOH adsorption and subsequent *O/*OH conversion, respectively. The observed contraction and partial rebound of the Ce–Ce distance are also consistent with the DFT-optimized structural relaxations during intermediate adsorption and desorption (Table S6). This correlation reinforces the mechanistic picture derived from the combination of experimental and theoretical approaches.

Theoretical calculations were further carried out to provide insight into the intrinsic origin of the high ORR activity of Ce2-NG. As depicted in Fig. 5a and S15–S18, the catalytic cycle proceeds through a classical four-electron pathway, in which the adsorbed intermediates (*OOH, *O, and *OH) are sequentially formed and converted on the dual-cerium sites. The corresponding free-energy profiles of Ce2-NG, Ce2-G, Ce1-NG, Ce2-N2O4G, and Ce2-N4O2G are illustrated in Fig. 5b. Across all evaluated catalysts, the formation and conversion of *OOH and *O intermediates are exergonic, while the final proton-coupled electron transfer (*OH → H2O) dictates the reaction overpotential as the true potential-determining step. Notably, Ce2-NG exhibits the lowest free energy change (0.91 eV), which is substantially smaller than that of Ce1-NG (1.99 eV), Ce2-N2O4G (1.93 eV), Ce2-G (1.53 eV), and Ce2-N4O2G (1.04 eV), highlighting the unique catalytic advantage of the dual-atom configuration. The electronic redistribution underlying this performance enhancement was analyzed by differential charge density calculations (Fig. 5c–e). Pronounced charge accumulation can be observed around the N-doped carbon scaffold, with electrons transferred predominantly from the Ce centers to the adjacent nitrogen-coordinated carbon atoms. Bader charge analysis (Table S6) further quantifies this effect, showing a relatively larger charge transfer (6.43e) in Ce2-NG compared to Ce1-NG (6.13e) and Ce2-G (6.37e), consistent with the enriched electron density near the dual Ce sites. This enhanced electron density influences the adsorption of oxygenated intermediates, though the relationship between charge transfer and adsorption strength should be interpreted with caution. Indeed, adsorption charge-density maps for *OH (Fig. 5f–g) show that both Ce2-NG and Ce1-NG exhibit substantial charge donation to the adsorbate (0.67e and 0.66e, respectively), indicating effective electron transfer from the Ce sites.37 The slightly higher value for Ce2-NG is consistent with its optimized OH binding energetics; however, the difference is modest and should not be overinterpreted. More importantly, the key advantage of Ce2-NG lies not in maximizing charge transfer or simply strengthening OH adsorption but in achieving a near-optimal OH binding energy that avoids the overly strong adsorption observed for Ce2-G and Ce1-NG (as evidenced by their more negative ΔGOH values and higher overpotentials in Fig. S19). This optimized binding reduces the thermodynamic overpotential of the potential-determining step (*OH → H2O), thereby enhancing the overall ORR activity. The density of states (DOS) analysis (Fig. 5i and S20) provides further evidence. Ce2-NG displays the d-band center (−1.40 eV) closer to the Fermi level than either Ce1-NG or Ce2-G, which facilitates stronger orbital overlap with adsorbed species.38 Collectively, these theoretical insights indicate that the dual-atom Ce sites in Ce2-NG not only lower the reaction free energy change but also induce favorable charge redistribution and intermediate stabilization, which is consistent with the superior ORR activity.

ZAB performance

To explore the viability of the synthesized Ce2-NG catalyst, it was incorporated as the air cathode in a rechargeable ZAB (Fig. 6a). For comparison, a ZAB assembled with the commercial Pt/C + RuO2 cathode was also fabricated and evaluated under identical conditions. As shown in Fig. 6b, the Ce2-NG-based ZAB exhibited an open-circuit voltage (OCV) of 1.54 V, which is higher than the 1.49 V observed for the Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB, indicating higher intrinsic electrochemical potential. Polarization and power density curves (Fig. 6c) reveal that the ZAB equipped with Ce2-NG delivers a maximum power density of 162.2 mW cm−2, outperforming the Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB (147.5 mW cm−2). Specific capacity measurements (Fig. 6d) show that the Ce2-NG-based ZAB achieves 807.3 mA h gZn−1, approaching the theoretical limit (820 mA h gZn−1) and significantly surpassing the 734.5 mA h gZn−1 obtained with the Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB. The specific capacity was calculated based on the mass of Zn consumed during discharge. Based on this value, Zn utilization was estimated to be approximately 98.5%, assuming a theoretical capacity of 820 mA h gZn−1 for Zn. Furthermore, within a current density range of 5 to 50 mA cm−2, the Ce2-NG ZAB displays stable discharge voltage plateaus, consistently higher than those of the benchmark catalyst assembled ZAB (Fig. 6e). The galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles also exhibit smooth, nearly symmetric transitions across increasing and decreasing current steps, highlighting excellent reversibility and high-rate capability. Long-term cycling stability was evaluated at a constant current density of 5 mA cm−2 over 200 hours (Fig. 6f). The Ce2-NG ZAB maintained a robust and stable round-trip efficiency (ratio of discharge to charge voltage) between 61.7% and 59.5% (Fig. 6g), substantially exceeding the performance of the Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB under the same conditions.39 These results collectively demonstrate that Ce2-NG is a highly promising cathode catalyst for rechargeable ZABs, offering outstanding discharge capacity, high energy conversion efficiency and excellent cyclability. A benchmarking comparison of the Zn-air battery performance of Ce2-NG with recently reported non-precious metal ORR catalysts is provided in Table S7, confirming that the performance of Ce2-NG is competitive with or superior to many state-of-the-art non-precious metal catalysts.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report a novel dual-atom cerium catalyst through an ultrafast Joule-heating strategy. This synthetic approach enables atomic-level control within only a few milliseconds, ensuring the stable formation of Ce–Ce dimers without aggregation. Distinct from the intensively studied transition-metal dimers, this work represents the first demonstration of rare-earth cerium as a dual-atom active site, thereby broadening the compositional space of atomically dispersed catalysts. Ce2-NG not only delivers outstanding ORR activity, durability, and crossover tolerance but also drives ZABs with high power output and long-term rechargeability. Mechanistic investigations combining in situ spectroscopy and theoretical calculations revealed that the dual-cerium centers, in synergy with nitrogen coordination, induce pronounced electronic redistribution and optimize *OH binding, thus minimizing the critical free energy change of the rate-determining step. This study establishes a new paradigm for constructing rare-earth-based DACs and provides generalizable guidance for the rational design of high-performance electrocatalysts in sustainable energy applications.
