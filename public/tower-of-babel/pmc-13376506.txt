MXene membrane with directionally functionalized channel entrances for enhanced ion selectivity and permeability
Authors: Hang Yu, Rongming Xu, Jiachun Ren, Ling Yuan, Zheng Cui, Weiming Zhang, Zhuyuan Wang, Lu Lv, Xiwang Zhang, Bingcai Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ion-separation membranes that combine high selectivity with high permeability are important for various practical applications. Molecular functionalization of sub-nanochannel membranes is widely used to improve ion separation selectivity; however, it often reduces ion permeation. Herein, inspired by the structural features of biological ion channels, we introduce a channel-entrance-directional functionalization strategy that integrates selective recognition with rapid ion transport. By directionally anchoring α-cyclodextrin molecules at the entrances of sub-nanochannel in two-dimensional MXene (Ti3C2Tx) lamellar membranes, we construct biomimetic ion channels in which entrance-localized recognition sites guide ion separation while unobstructed pathways enable rapid monovalent cation transport. The resulting membranes exhibit mono-/divalent cation selectivity exceeding 103 while preserving high permeability for monovalent ion, thereby surpassing the selectivity-permeability trade-off limit of reported ion-separation membranes. Combined experiments and simulations reveal that alterations in ion transport pathways caused by differences in ion hydration structures within sub-nanochannels are the primary mechanism for ion separation. This mechanism contrasts with conventional interpretation that primarily attributes ion separation to differences in ion-molecule binding energies. This work establishes channel-entrance engineering as a useful strategy for the design of ion-separation membranes for mono-/divalent cation separation in brine upgrading and resource-recovery.

Introduction

Ion-separation membranes are important for sustainability-related technologies, including water purification1–3, critical resource extraction4,5, and renewable energy harvesting6–9. A central scientific challenge across these applications is the precise discrimination of ions with subtle differences in hydration radius and charge density10,11. Sub-nanochannel membranes, with channel dimensions comparable to hydrated ions, can enhance ion-membrane interactions and therefore provide a route for ion separation5,12,13. Recent progress in two-dimensional (2D) lamellar membranes offers additional opportunities for such designs14–18. Their tunable interlayer spacing, inherent surface charge, and versatile chemistry make them attractive candidates for ion separation19–21. However, previous studies have demonstrated that the intrinsic sub-nanometer channels of 2D membranes alone are insufficient to achieve high selectivity22–27. To address this, channel-internal functionalization with ion-recognition molecules such as poly(sodium 4-styrenesulfonate), crown ethers, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, or poly(γ-glutamic acid) has been widely employed28–31. While such functionalization enhances selectivity, it inevitably increases transport resistance, leading to an obvious loss of ion permeability28,32–35. Consequently, membranes prepared by this approach are constrained by the long-standing trade-off between selectivity and permeability19,36. Achieving a significant enhancement in selectivity without substantial permeability loss therefore remains challenging.

Biological ion channels, exemplified by KcsA, exhibit K+/Na+ selectivity exceeding 104 together with rapid ion transport (~108 ions per second). This remarkable performance arises from positioning a short (~12 Å) selectivity filter at the entrance of the wider protein channel37, where aligned carbonyl oxygens enforce sub-nanometer ion coordination38. Based on this entrance-functionalized architecture, we designed 2D Ti3C2Tx membranes in which α-cyclodextrin (α-CD), a macrocyclic oligosaccharide with hydroxy oxygen spacing (~6 Å) comparable to that of biological filters and an axial length of a few angstroms, is directionally anchored at channel entrances (Fig. 1a). This channel-entrance-directional functionalization strategy leverages the intrinsic charge asymmetry of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets, where negatively charged basal planes contrast with positively charged edges arising from unsaturated titanium (Ti) atoms39,40. During membrane assembly, this asymmetry drives the preferential anchoring of α-CD at Ti3C2Tx nanosheet edges, thereby generating functionalized channel entrances. These engineered entrances selectively impose larger energetic barriers against divalent cations, while the pristine interlayer channels preserve low-resistance transport pathways for monovalent cations. The resulting membranes, termed MLM-CD, exhibit mono-/bivalent cation selectivity of up to 1247 while maintaining high monovalent cation permeability. This performance stems from the channel-entrance-directional functionalization strategy, as confirmed by structural characterization and stability tests, establishing a broadly applicable route to overcome the selectivity-permeability trade-off in ion-separation membranes. Computational analysis reveals that α-CD at channel entrances specifically recognizes monovalent cations, compensating for the energy penalty associated with subsequent passage through the α-CD cavity. Consequently, mono- and divalent cations adopt distinct transport pathways, which accounts for the observed selectivity. At the same time, the unobstructed interlayer channels preserve rapid transport of monovalent cations, ensuring high permeability. Overall, our findings demonstrate that channel-entrance-directional functionalization via edge-specific anchoring offers a strategy for designing ion-separation membranes for brine upgrading and resource-recovery applications.

Results and Discussion

Directional edge anchoring of α-CD on Ti3C2Tx nanosheets

To construct channel-entrance-directionally functionalized membranes, α-CD molecules were anchored specifically to the edges of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets prior to assembly. Monolayered Ti3C2Tx nanosheets were synthesized via an improved minimally intensive layer delamination (MILD) method42 (Supplementary Fig. 1), yielding nanosheets with a thickness of ~1.5 nm, slightly exceeding the theoretical value (1.0 nm) due to the adsorption of water molecules43,44 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subsequently, the Ti3C2Tx nanosheet suspension was mixed with α-CD solution. Due to the electron-rich primary hydroxyl groups at the narrow rim45,46 (Supplementary Fig. 3), α-CD molecules exhibit preferential binding affinity towards the under-coordinated, positively charged Ti atoms exposed at nanosheet edges39,40, primarily driven by electrostatic attraction. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations supported this binding preference. We evaluated multiple plausible adsorption configurations, including basal-plane adsorption and several edge-adsorption modes (e.g., rim-mediated and sidewall-mediated attachment with different orientations). In all cases, α-CD adsorption at Ti3C2Tx edges exhibits more negative binding energies (Eads = −2.86 to −4.75 eV) than basal-plane adsorption (Eads = −1.42 to −1.84 eV), supporting preferential binding at the edges (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis confirmed that α-CD modified Ti3C2Tx (α-CD@Ti3C2Tx) nanosheets preserved their basal plane morphology and thickness (~1.5 nm), while exhibiting distinct edge-localized bulges (Fig. 2b, c). AFM-based infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR) mapping at 1060 cm-1, corresponding to the C-O-C stretching vibration of α-CD by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)47 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5), further revealed distinct spatial differences between pristine and modified nanosheets. Pristine Ti3C2Tx nanosheets exhibited a uniform low-intensity IR signal across the entire basal plane (Fig. 2e), whereas α-CD@Ti3C2Tx nanosheets showed enhanced IR intensity localized specifically at the edges (Fig. 2f), indicating successful edge-specific anchoring of α-CD molecules48. Assembly and Structure of Ti3C2Tx Membranes with Entrance-Directional Functionalization

Self-assembly of α-CD@Ti3C2Tx nanosheets yielded lamellar membranes (designated MLM-CD-X, where X denotes the α-CD loading in μmol ranging 0–30). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses confirmed continuous, defect-free surfaces and well-aligned layered cross-sections across all membranes (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 6). Spectroscopic analyses were used to investigate the chemistry and spatial localization of α-CD within the membranes. FT-IR spectroscopy of MLM-CD membranes detected the characteristic C-O-C stretching vibration of α-CD47, confirming successful incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The -OH stretching peak at 3460 cm−1 remained largely unchanged, differing from previous reports29,34 where a red shift was observed, typically attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions49. This observation suggests that the interaction mechanism in the present system differs from those previously reported. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed Ti-O bonding features attributable to interactions between α-CD hydroxyl groups and under-coordinated Ti atoms exposed at nanosheet edges50,51 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The nature of this interaction between α-CD and Ti3C2Tx dictated the spatial distribution of α-CD during membrane assembly. High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) revealed that the free interlayer spacing of MLM-CD-0 and MLM-CD-10 remained unchanged at ~3.2 Å (Fig. 3a, b), indicating that α-CD did not intercalate into the interlayer channels. AFM further showed that the main morphological difference between MLM-CD-0 and MLM-CD-10 was the appearance of edge-localized bulges (Fig. 3c). Consistently, XRD patterns collected under both dry and hydrated states corroborated preservation of the pristine interlayer spacing (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 8–10). Together with FT-IR evidence of α-CD incorporation, these findings demonstrate that in MLM-CD-10, α-CD molecules localized exclusively at channel entrances (Fig. 3b). By contrast, higher loading (e.g., MLM-CD-30) induced markable interlayer expansion, as evidenced by the emergence of a new diffraction peak in XRD patterns (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Figs. 8 and 10). Macroscopic thickness measurements further confirmed overall membrane swelling (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12), collectively demonstrating that excess α-CD molecules intercalated into the interlayer channels, generating a structure distinct from that at lower loadings. Hydrophilicity measurements provided additional insights into α-CD spatial distribution. Water contact angle of MLM-CD-10 closely matched that of MLM-CD-0, suggesting negligible surface deposition of α-CD (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). In contrast, a control membrane (MLM-CD-P), fabricated by depositing of α-CD onto a pre-formed MLM-CD-0, exhibited a significantly reduced contact angle (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14), consistent with surface accumulation of α-CD confirmed by AFM imaging (Supplementary Fig. 15). Taken together, these results show that pre-modification of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets with controlled α-CD loadings enables precise engineering of Ti3C2Tx membranes. At the optimal loading of 10 μmol, α-CD molecules are directionally anchored at channel entrances while preserving the pristine interlayer structure and chemistry. This targeted nano-architectonics approach may mitigate the detrimental permeability losses typically associated with conventional channel-internal modification strategies29,31,34,35,54–57.

MLM-CD Membranes Achieve Both High Mono-/Divalent Cation Selectivity and Permeability

The ion separation performance of pristine and MLM-CD membranes was evaluated using a custom U-shaped device to measure the permeation rates of mixed cations (Li+, Na+, K+, Mg2+ and Ca2+) (Supplementary Fig. 16). The pristine Ti3C2Tx membrane exhibited poor mono-/divalent cation discrimination, with a K+/Mg2+ selectivity of 28.6 (Fig. 4a). By contrast, directional incorporation of α-CD at channel entrances markedly enhanced separation. With increasing α-CD loading up to 10 μmol, progressively more entrances were functionalized with α-CD filters that selectively impeded divalent cations, leading to a dramatic rise in K+/Mg2+ selectivity (Fig. 4b). At a loading of 10 μmol (MLM-CD-10), the channel entrances were nearly fully decorated with α-CD (Fig. 4c), effectively eliminating leakage pathways, and yielding a K+/Mg2+ selectivity of 1247.0. Since α-CD was localized exclusively at entrances rather than intercalating into interlayer channels, the low-resistance transport pathways for monovalent transport were preserved, maintaining high K+ permeation rate comparable to that of MLM-CD-0 (Fig. 4a). At loadings above 10 μmol, α-CD intercalated into the interlayer channels, causing channel expansion and structural disorder (Fig. 4c). These changes introduced bypass routes for divalent cations and diminished selectivity (Fig. 4b). The entrance-functionalized architecture enabled MLM-CD-10 with strong ion-separation performance and suggests potential for practical application. Compared with MLM-CD-0, Li+/Mg2+, Na+/Mg2+, K+/Mg2+ selectivity increased by 52-fold, 40-fold, and 43-fold, respectively (Fig. 4d). Such performance surpasses previously reported ion-selective membranes under comparable conditions and overcomes the long-standing selectivity-permeability trade-off (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 3). The robustness of MLM-CD-10 was further validated under varying operating conditions. The membrane retains its exceptional separation performance over a range of salt concentrations (0.01–1.0 M) and pH values (4–8.6) (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). When tested with natural brine from Baqiancuo Salt Lake (Tibet, China; Supplementary Table 2), MLM-CD-10 achieved a 1356-fold increase in the permeate Li+/Mg2+ ratio after 72 hours of continuous operation (Fig. 4f). Mg2+ was below the detection limit in the permeate during the first 48 hours, while Li+ permeated at a stable, high rate (Supplementary Fig. 19). Post-operation characterization by XRD and SEM confirmed that the membrane structure remained intact, with no evidence of delamination or α-CD leaching (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). This combination of high performance, durability, and applicability under realistic brine conditions demonstrates that MLM-CD-10 as a robust membrane platform for mono-/divalent cation discrimination, enabling brine upgrading via divalent-ion suppression and thereby facilitating downstream lithium recovery.

Ion Separation Mechanism of MLM-CD

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the ion selectivity of MLM-CD membranes, we combined molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and DFT calculations to probe the interactions between hydrated ions and α-CD at channel entrances. MD simulations revealed faster transport of K+ than Mg2+ (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 22), consistent with experimentally determined lower energy barrier for K+ permeation (3.36 kcal mol−1) versus Mg2+ (12.67 kcal mol−1) (Supplementary Fig. 23). We therefore examined ion-specific behavior near the α-CD functionalized channel entrances in more detail. Analysis of ion density distributions around α-CD revealed distinct spatial preferences: K+ ions distributed both at cavity center and close to the oxygen atoms at the narrow rim of α-CD (Fig. 5b), whereas Mg2+ ions predominantly accumulated near the center (Fig. 5c). Radical distribution function (RDF) analysis further quantified this difference, showing a closer approach of K+ to α-CD oxygen atoms (α-CD-O) at a distance of ~2.5 Å, while Mg2+ ions remained farther away at ~3.7 Å (Fig. 5d). Hydration analysis suggested that the first solvation shell of K+ was partially replaced by α-CD-O (Supplementary Fig. 24), reflecting favorable interactions between K+ and α-CD. DFT calculations corroborated these findings. Geometry optimization of hydrated cations near the α-CD narrow rim revealed that hydrated K+ readily shed three water molecules and formed coordination bonds with α-CD-O, exhibiting a favorable binding energy of −3.67 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 25). In contrast, hydrated Mg2+ maintained its intact solvation shell, showing negligible binding with α-CD. These distinct interaction behaviors were reflected in ion transport pathways. Trajectory analysis showed that K+ migrated along paths involving coordination with α-CD-O, while Mg2+ traversed primarily along the central axis (Fig. 5f), consistent with MD-derived density distributions (Fig. 5b, c). To further quantify the energy barrier for ion transit at channel entrances, we employed a constant-speed dragging technology within MD simulations to measure the force required to pull ions through specific regions. When dragged near the narrow rim of α-CD, Mg2+ experienced a sharp increase in dragging force, whereas the force required for K+ remained nearly constant (Supplementary Fig. 26). These observations align with DFT findings: the favorable coordination of K+ with α-CD-O effectively offsets steric hindrance at channel entrances, providing energetic compensation for the subsequent translocation through the cavity58,59. By contrast, Mg2+, with its stable hydration shell and lack of specific binding, faces uncompensated steric hindrance, resulting in higher passage resistance. This difference was further amplified as ions moved deeper into the α-CD cavity. We further examined ionic-strength effects by increasing the feed concentration from 0.05 to 1.0 M (Supplementary Fig. 18b), which led to a more pronounced increase in Mg2+ permeation than in K+ permeation. This trend is consistent with hydration-shell compression of Mg2+ at high ionic strength (e.g., Mg2+ hydration number decreasing from ~21 at 0.1 M to ~13 at 1.0 M)60,61, enabling closer approach to α-CD and more frequent transient rim-coordination. This concentration dependence provides additional experimental support for the proposed hydration-structure-governed pathway differentiation mechanism. In addition, experimental measurements revealed that K+ permeation rates exhibited scaled quadratically with concentration rather than linearly (Supplementary Fig. 27). This nonlinearity is consistent with the predictions of classical collision theory62,63 and suggests a multi-ion “knock-on” transport mechanism, reminiscent of biological K+ channels64,65. In summary, using the entrance-functionalized architecture of biological K+ channels as a design reference, we designed MLM-CD membranes by directional anchoring α-CD molecules at the edges of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets. By tuning the α-CD loading, we obtained a membrane architecture in which α-CD selectively decorates channel entrances while the pristine interlayer channels remain unmodified, thereby spatially decoupling selective recognition from ion transport. This channel-entrance-directional functionalization, in contrast to conventional channel-internal modifications that frequently obstruct ion transport pathways, effectively overcomes the long-standing selectivity-permeability trade-off that commonly limits ion-separation membranes. Integrated experimental and computational analyses reveal that α-CD molecules at channel entrances act as a short selectivity filter that induces pathways differentiation. Monovalent cations preferentially follow a rim-guided, curved trajectory along the oxygen-rich edge of the α-CD cavity, where transient multi-point coordination partially compensates the dehydration penalty and facilitates entry. By contrast, divalent cations retain more persistent hydration shells and tend to pass closer to the central axis, where they receive less energy compensation and encounter a higher entry barrier. The different pathways for mono- and divalent ions governs the observed ion separation performance. Overall, our results demonstrate that localizing recognition sites at channel entrances enables strong ion discrimination while keeping the main transport pathway largely unobstructed. This entrance-engineering concept provides a nano-architectonics framework for designing sub-nanochannel and porous membranes for selective mono-/divalent cation separation in processes such as lithium extraction from salt lakes, resource recovery from battery-recycling leachate, and industrial brine treatment for removal of multivalent cations.

Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

The bulk material MAX (Ti3AlC2, 400 mesh) was purchased from Kaixi Tech, China. LiF (≥ 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd, China. α-CD was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany. Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) substrate (0.22 μm, 47 mm) and Polyethersulfone (PES) substrate (0.22 μm, 47 mm) were purchased from Hangzhou Cobetter Filtration Equipment Co., Ltd. HCl (GR), CaCl2 were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, China. KCl, NaCl, LiCl, and MgCl2 were purchased from Heowns Biochem Technologies, LLC, Tianjin, China. All the chemicals were used as received without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was produced by an ULUPURE ultrapure water system (ULUPURE, China).

Preparation of the MXene (Ti3C2Tx) Nanosheets

Ti3C2Tx nanosheets were prepared using the MILD method42. Typically, 1.0 g of LiF was added to 20 mL of 9 M HCl solution in a 100 mL Teflon vessel and stirred for 5 minutes. Subsequently, 1.0 g of Ti3AlC2 powder was slowly added to the above etching solution, followed by continuous stirring for 24 h at 37 °C to ensure efficient etching. The etched product was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1685×g for 5 minutes (Rotator FA-45-6-30, Centrifuge 5810 R, Eppendorf SE, Germany). Afterward, the supernatant was discarded. Next, 30 mL of DI water was added to the slurry and mixed using a vortex mixer (MX-S, SCILOGEX, LLC, USA). The resulting mixture was washed with DI water by repeated centrifugation at 1685 × g for 5 min per circle until the pH reached 5-6. The sediment was re-dispersed and mildly sonicated (112 W, CPX5800H, Emerson Electric Co., USA) for 30 minutes in an ice water bath under argon flow to further delaminate the few-layered Ti3C2Tx stacks. The sonicated product was further washed with DI water by centrifugation at 19802 × g for 10 minutes, twice, to remove impurities. Finally, the monolayer Ti3C2Tx nanosheets dispersion was obtained by centrifuging at 1685 × g for 60 min. The as-obtained Ti3C2Tx dispersion typically has a concentration around 3.0 mg mL−1. Argon was pumped into the dispersion, which was then stored at low temperature (~4 °C).

The concentration of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets was measured by membrane mass difference subtraction. Briefly, Ti3C2Tx nanosheets were loaded onto a PES substrate by vacuum and the mass of the substrate was recorded in advance. The Ti3C2Tx-PES membrane was vacuum dried at 80 °C for at least 2 h. After drying, the mass was recorded again. Fabrication of MLM-CD Membranes

The Ti3C2Tx dispersion (3.0 mg mL−1) was added to 30 mL of α-CD solution containing 0, 2, 5, 10, 20, and 30 μmol of α-CD, respectively, followed by rotary mixing at room temperature for 4 hours. The Ti3C2Tx / α-CD mixture solution was poured into a vacuum filter device fitted with a PVDF substrate. After standing for 8 minutes, the mixture solution was filtered under a pressure of 0.3 bar until drained. The pressure was then increased to 1 bar, and filtration was continued for 5 min to remove the residual solution from the interlayer spacing. The obtained membrane was vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 6 h. Based on the molar amount of α-CD, the synthesized membranes were labeled as MLM-CD-0, MLM-CD-2, MLM-CD-5, MLM-CD-10, MLM-CD-20, MLM-CD-30, respectively.

The morphology and height profile of the Ti3C2Tx nanosheets before and after α-CD modification were acquired by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Multimode 8, Bruker, Germany) in ScanAsyst mode. Ti3C2Tx nanosheets dispersion was drop-cast onto a mica disc (12 mm, Ted Pella, USA). To further investigate the reaction site between Ti3C2Tx and α-CD, atomic force microscopy-based infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR, Anasys NanoIR3, Bruker, USA) was conducted at a wavenumber of 1060 cm-1, corresponding to the characteristic peak of α-CD in FT-IR. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI5000 VersaProbeIII, ULVAC-PHI, Japan) was employed to examine the chemical constitution and chemical state. An Al Kα (150 W) X-ray source was used to excite photoelectrons in the analysis. The obtained XPS spectra was processed using MultiPak software for peak fitting. The zeta potential of Ti3C2Tx nanosheets dispersions was measured in aqueous solutions using a Laser Particle Size and Zeta Potential Analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK) with Ti3C2Tx concentration set to 50 mg/L. The pH was adjusted with 0.1 M and 0.05 M HCl and NaOH. The surface contact angle of the membranes was measured using an optical contact angle measuring goniometer (DSA100, Kruss, Germany) to evaluate their surface hydrophilicity. To analyze the crystallographic structure of the restacked Ti3C2Tx membranes, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu sealed tube (λ = 1.54178 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA at room temperature. All samples were gold-coated before testing. For detailed examination of the membranes cross-sections, the membrane was first cut into 1 × 8 mm rectangle using a blade. The cut samples were then embedded in Epon812 resin. The embedded membrane samples were sectioned using an ultrathin microtome (EM UC 7, Leica, Germany) to produce ultrathin resin slices (~50 nm) exposing the cross-section of the membranes. The ultrathin resin slices were carefully transferred to an ultrathin carbon film supported on a copper grid and analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai F20, FEI, USA), operating at voltage of 200 kV.

Ion Permeation Measurement

Ion permeation across the membranes was measured using a custom-made U-shaped diffusion cell with two 30 mL chambers. The membrane was first sealed between two pieces of aluminum tape, each with a hole (diameter of 1.6 cm), using high vacuum grease. The sealed membrane sample was then placed between the two chambers, with the MXene layer facing the feed side and the PVDF substrate facing the permeate side. The feed side solution contained a mixture of five salts (LiCl, NaCl, KCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2), each at 0.2 M and adjusted to pH 8.6, while the permeate side contained DI water, unless otherwise specified. The device was magnetically stirred throughout the diffusion process to prevent concentration polarization. The system was allowed to stabilize for at least 1 h before sampling. After the measurement period, the solutions were drained, and the device was washed several times with DI water until the conductance matched that of pure DI water. Following washing, the membrane was stored in DI water until the next measurement period. Ion concentration on the permeate side was determined using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (iCAP7400 Duo MFC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Density Functional Theory Calculation

The calculations of the binding energy between Ti3C2Tx nanosheets and the α-CD molecule were performed in the framework of the spin-polarized density functional theory with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)67,68. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) was selected for the exchange-correlation potential69,70. The long-range van der Waals interaction was described by the DFT-D3 approach71. The cut-off energy for plane wave was set to 480 eV. The energy criterion was set to 10−4 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn-Sham equation. All the structures were relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms had declined to less than 0.05 eV/Å. Data analysis and visualization were carried out with the help of VASPKIT72 code and VESTA73.

MD Simulations

In this study, all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were employed to construct a MXene-cyclodextrin composite nanochannel system for investigating the atomic-scale mechanisms of K+ and Mg2+ ion transmembrane transport. The simulation system consisted of two single-layer MXene nanosheets (21.5 × 37 Å2) arranged parallel along the z-axis, with a precisely controlled interlayer spacing of 6.8 Å to form a quasi-two-dimensional confined nano space. At the positive end of the y-axis of the channel, cyclodextrin molecules were precisely anchored to the MXene edge, with their macrocyclic planes oriented at a 45° angle relative to the MXene layers, creating an asymmetric composite transport channel. The system was constructed along the y-axis with a three-dimensional simulation box (21.5 × 131.1 × 28.58 Å3) comprising a feed region (0 < y < 42 Å), a nanochannel region (42 ≤ y ≤ 89.1 Å), and a permeation region (89.1 < y ≤ 131.1 Å), where a vacuum layer was introduced to effectively eliminate image effects from periodic boundary conditions.

In the initial configuration, the feed region contained randomly distributed 12 Mg2+, 12 K+, and 36 Cl− ions (corresponding to 0.25 M MgCl2 / KCl mixed electrolyte), while 1821 water molecules were filled across all three regions, with the system density strictly calibrated to experimental values at 1 atm. Critical interactions were described using a multiscale force field combination: the Ti-O-C bonding network of the MXene layers was characterized by the UFF force field combined with the QEq charge equilibration method77,78, with parameters validated in previous work to accurately reproduce solution interface behaviors; cyclodextrin and ions were parameterized using the GAFF force field79, with cyclodextrin charge distributions optimized through the RESP method80; water molecules were represented by the rigid SPC/E model81. System optimization employed a hierarchical energy minimization strategy: first, the MXene framework underwent 5000 steps of steepest descent optimization, followed by secondary optimization with released solvent and ion degrees of freedom. The equilibration phase was conducted under the NVT ensemble for 5 ns, with temperature maintained at 298.15 K using the Nose-Hoover thermostat82. Notably, MXene atomic positions were fixed through position constraints, while oxygen atoms in cyclodextrin molecules in contact with the MXene interface (distance < 3 Å) were subjected to harmonic potential constraints of 1000 kJ/mol/nm, maintaining molecular orientation while allowing local conformational relaxation. Long-range electrostatic interactions were processed using the PME method (cutoff radius 12 Å)83.

The diffusion behavior was investigated using two complementary approaches: (1) In periodic flux simulations, ions adopted periodic boundary conditions in the y-direction, with directional pulling forces (5000 kJ/mol/nm along the +y-axis) driving a 200 ns steady-state transport process, equivalent to a non-equilibrium simulation with an infinitely large concentration gradient; (2) Single penetration simulations prevented ion backflow by constraining water molecules in the permeation region (position restraints), conducting 600 ns long-term simulations to obtain comprehensive penetration pathway statistics. Both schemes utilized a 2 fs integration time step, with the LINCS algorithm constraining bonds involving hydrogen atoms. To quantitatively analyze the spatial distribution of transport barriers, we employed constant velocity dragging techniques: individual ions were initially positioned at specific locations in the feed region (inside/outside the axial projection area of the cyclodextrin channel), pulled along the +y-axis at a constant velocity of 0.001 nm/ps while simultaneously recording the resistive forces experienced by the ions. Each configuration was simulated 10 times to eliminate thermal fluctuation effects, with data collection processed using a moving window averaging method. All simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022.584, trajectory analysis was conducted with built-in GROMACS tools, and three -dimensional visualization was implemented through OVITO 3.8.485.
