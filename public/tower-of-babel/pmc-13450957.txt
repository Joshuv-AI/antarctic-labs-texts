High Prevalence of Secondary Hyperparathyroidism Before Kidney Transplantation in Brazil
Authors: Lorena C. Mainardes, Eduardo J. Duque, Elias David-Neto, Vanda Jorgetti, Thomas L. Nickolas, Rosilene M. Elias, Rosa M.A. Moysés
Published: 2026

Kidney transplantation (KTX) is considered the optimal treatment for patients with CKD, as it significantly improves both quality of life and overall survival.1 In most cases, successful transplantation corrects many of the metabolic disturbances associated with declining kidney function, including CKD-mineral and bone disorder. However, in patients with severe preexisting secondary hyperparathyroidism (HPT), restoration of kidney function is often insufficient to fully reverse these abnormalities. Consequently, persistent HPT can develop following KTX. Persistent HPT has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including graft dysfunction, bone loss, increased fracture risk, and cardiovascular complications. Moreover, the optimal post-transplant parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels remain poorly defined.2 In Brazil, severe HPT is highly prevalent,3 and we hypothesized that many patients on the KTX waiting list in our country should have previously undergone parathyroidectomy (PTX). In addition, we suspected that these patients might have limited access to KTX and, if they did undergo KTX, would face a higher risk of graft loss.

We conducted a 10-year retrospective study at a tertiary hospital to evaluate the prevalence of secondary HPT among transplant candidates and to investigate the relationship between persistent HPT and graft outcomes following KTX., the cohort study involved 3492 patients listed for KTX between January 2011 and December 2020. PTH values were available for 2910 of these individuals. Among them, 870 patients underwent KTX during the study period and had PTH levels measured between 6 months and 1 year postoperatively. Persistent HPT was defined as serum calcium >10.2 mg/dl and/or PTH >100 pg/ml—equivalent to 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN, 15–63 pg/ml). Poor graft function was defined as an eGFR, calculated using the MDRD formula, <30 ml/min per 1.73 m2 1 year after KTX.

As shown in Figure 1A, although the median PTH was within the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes–recommended range for patients with ESKD on dialysis, 1396 patients (48%) had PTH levels above 300 pg/ml, and 654 patients (22%) had PTH levels exceeding nine times the ULN. Notably, 399 patients (14%) had PTH levels above 800 pg/ml. One year after surgery, as shown in Figure 1B, persistent HPT was independently associated with pre-KTX serum calcium levels, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.354 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.114 to 1.647; P < 0.001) for each 1 mg/dl increase, and PTH levels, with an OR of 1.134 (95% CI, 1.076 to 1.196; P < 0.001) for each 100 pg/ml increase. It was also associated with graft function, with an OR of 0.817 (95% CI, 0.971 to 0.888; P < 0.001) for each 10 ml/min per 1.73 m2 increase. In multivariate analysis (Figure 1C), persistent HPT was linked to a 2.3-fold increased risk of poor graft function after adjusting for age, sex, race, and donor type. Several studies have compared different treatment strategies for persistent HPT, including the use of calcimimetics and PTX.4 While calcimimetic therapy has represented a paradigm shift in the management of HPT,5 evidence suggests that PTX remains the most effective option for controlling mineral and bone disorders in patients with severe persistent HPT.1 Despite this, in our cohort, PTX was performed in only 37 patients (4%) within the first year after KTX. The limited number of procedures is likely because PTX is available in only a few specialized centers in Brazil. The severity of persistent HPT has been adversely associated with bone quality impairment,6 and there is general agreement that secondary HPT should be controlled prior to KTX. In this study, we investigated patients on the waiting list and evaluated the impact of pre-KTX PTH levels on KTX access and post-KTX graft function. While current Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines recommend maintaining PTH levels within two to nine times the ULN during dialysis, emerging data—including our own—suggest that maintaining PTH levels within this range may partially help to avoid bone outcomes in patients on dialysis, but not persistent HPT after KTX.7 These findings support the need for more stringent PTH control before transplantation. Overall, our data reinforce the importance of prioritizing CKD-mineral and bone disorder management in all patients with CKD, particularly those awaiting transplantation, to reduce the risk of persistent HPT and graft impairment. Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective cohort design may introduce inherent biases. In addition, rejection episodes and delayed graft function were not analyzed. Both the exposure (persistent HPT) and the outcome (poor graft function) were assessed within the first post-transplant year rather than examined using a time-to-event framework. Finally, mortality was not included as a competing risk. However, the strengths include a large sample size, no post-KTX follow-up losses, and a 10-year follow-up period. In conclusion, our data highlight that KTX should not be viewed as a definitive treatment for severe secondary HPT, as the condition often persists post-transplant and may adversely affect graft survival.
