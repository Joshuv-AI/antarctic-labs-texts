A Study on the Correlation between Noise and Sleep Disorders in the Emergency Observation Ward for Elderly Patients
Authors: LiFen Tang, HuaQin Shao, Hui Ma, XiaoDong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate the relationship between objective nighttime noise exposure and sleep quality in elderly patients under emergency observation and to verify whether higher nighttime noise levels are an independent factor contributing to poorer sleep quality after controlling for multiple confounding factors.Methods:The single-center retrospective observational study consecutively enrolled 150 elderly patients (age ≥ 65 years) who were under observation in the emergency department of Zhejiang Sian International Hospital for ≥ 24 h between 31 December 2024 and 31 December 2025. Noise data for the first full night (22:00 to 06:00 the next day) during the observation period were obtained using a fixed automatic environmental noise monitor, with nighttime equivalent sound level (LAeq,8h) as the core exposure indicator. Sleep quality was measured with the Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ), and an RCSQ score < 50 was defined as having a sleep disorder. Covariates included age, sex, disease severity (Modified Early Warning Score, MEWS), pain intensity (Numerical Rating Scale, NRS), anxiety status (Self-Rating Anxiety Scale, SAS), observation duration, and sedative-hypnotic drug use. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between noise and sleep quality, and multiple linear regression analysis was used to adjust for confounding factors and test the independent effect of LAeq,8h on RCSQ score.Results:Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between LAeq,8h and RCSQ score (r = −0.431, P < 0.001). After adjusting for all covariates, multiple linear regression confirmed that LAeq,8h remained an independent predictor of a lower RCSQ score (unstandardized B = −0.313, 95% CI: −0.434 to −0.193, standardized β = −0.404, t = −5.130, P < 0.001). In logistic regression, each 1 dB increase in LAeq,8h was associated with 8% higher odds of sleep disorder (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.13, P = 0.002).Conclusion:In elderly patients under emergency observation, higher LAeq,8h is independently associated with poorer sleep quality.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep disorders are common among elderly patients in emergency observation wards and adversely affect recovery.[12] With the aging population, the proportion of older emergency patients is increasing, and this group is particularly sensitive to environmental stressors.[34] Ward noise, generated by equipment alarms and staff activities, is a major sleep disruptor. However, most previous studies relied on subjective noise reports and did not adequately control for confounders such as anxiety, pain, or disease severity.[56] Anxiety itself is negatively correlated with sleep quality,[7] and a study in intensive care settings has shown that noise remains an independent predictor of sleep after adjusting for anxiety.[8] Therefore, objective noise measurement and rigorous adjustment for multiple covariates are needed to clarify the independent relationship between nighttime noise and sleep quality in elderly patients under emergency observation. This retrospective study aimed to investigate the relationship between objectively measured nighttime noise levels (LAeq,8h) and sleep quality (measured by the Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire, RCSQ) in elderly patients under emergency observation while controlling for potential confounders, thereby providing evidence for environmental interventions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Research Design and Research Subjects

This was a single-center retrospective observational study. We consecutively enrolled elderly patients (≥65 years old) who stayed ≥24 h in the emergency department between 31 December 2024 and 31 December 2025 through the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Based on predefined sample size estimates and data accessibility, a total of 150 patients were ultimately included. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Sian International Hospital (Approval Number: 2026–001). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025).[9]

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A total of 210 elderly patients who stayed ≥24 h during the study period were initially screened. After applying the exclusion criteria, 60 patients were excluded: 22 had severe cognitive impairment or could not complete the assessment, 18 had incomplete key data (e.g., missing noise or RCSQ records), 12 were transferred to other departments before completing the first-night assessment, and 8 died during the observation period. Ultimately, 150 patients were included in the final analysis. Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) based on Cohen’s guidelines for multiple regression,[10] with 13 predictors, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 92. Thus, 150 patients were included, providing adequate statistical power.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥65 years; (2) unstable condition requiring emergency observation and hospitalization time ≥24 h; (3) complete clinical data and informed consent; and (4) routine environmental noise monitoring was conducted in the ward during the observation period.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe cognitive impairment, impaired consciousness, or a history of mental illness that prevented completion of the scale assessment; (2) transfer, withdrawal, or death during the study; (3) incomplete clinical data and missing key variables (e.g., pain scores and anxiety assessments); or (4) sleep assessments were severely distorted during the observation period, mainly due to medical procedures (e.g., continuous mechanical ventilation).

Data Collection and Variable Definition

All data were collected retrospectively from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and related environmental monitoring system databases. To ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data, two researchers who had received standardized training independently extracted and entered the data and verified and adjudicated any discrepancies.

Key Outcome Indicators

Nighttime Noise Exposure Level

Noise data were obtained from a fixed, standard-compliant automatic monitor installed in the emergency observation ward. The instrument was fixed in the central area of the ward, approximately 1.5 m above the ground, conforming to acoustic measurement specifications. Data were recorded automatically and continuously by the system. Noise monitoring data for the first full night (usually defined as 22:00 to 06:00 the following day) of each patient’s observation period were retrospectively extracted. If the first-night data were missing, the next full night data were extracted. However, no first‑night noise data were missing; thus, no substitution was applied. The extracted raw data were the equivalent continuous A-weighted sound levels per minute (LAeq, 1 min). The evaluation criteria were as follows: core exposure indicators were calculated during the 8-h monitoring period: (1) LAeq,8h, representing the average noise level during this period. LAeq,8h was included as a continuous variable in the model for comparison during data analysis; (2) maximum sound level (LAmax), representing the instantaneous peak noise level during the monitoring period. LAeq,8h and LAmax were included as continuous variables in the model for comparison during data analysis. For each patient, an RCSQ assessment was performed on the morning following the first full night (22:00–06:00) of the observation period. Thus, the exposure window (nighttime noise) and the outcome assessment (sleep quality) referred to the identical night. Each patient contributed only one observation to the analysis.

Sleep Quality

Sleep quality was assessed using the Richards–Campbell sleep questionnaire (RCSQ).[11] The main observation indicators included five items: nighttime sleep depth, time to fall asleep at night, number of awakenings at night, time to fall back asleep after awakening at night, and overall nighttime sleep quality. Each item was scored using a visual analog scale of 0 to 100 mm (1 mm = 1 point), with scores ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicated better sleep quality.[12] The Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) has been shown to have good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89 in the original validation study). Due to the retrospective design, individual item scores were unavailable to recalculate Cronbach’s α in our sample; however, the scale has been well validated in similar populations, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.86 to 0.91.[13]

Control Variables (Covariates)

(1) Demographic and clinical basic characteristics: Basic information such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), history of sleep disorders, smoking history, and alcohol consumption was directly extracted from the demographic information of the electronic medical record system.

(2) Disease severity: The Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) was used for assessment.[14] The highest MEWS score recorded within 24 h of the patient’s admission to the emergency observation area was collected. The MEWS score was calculated based on heart rate, systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, body temperature, and level of consciousness, with a total score ranging from 0 to 14 points. A higher score indicates a more severe condition.

(3) Pain intensity: The Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) was used for assessment. The most severe pain score reported by the patient within 24 h prior to the RCSQ assessment was collected. The NRS score ranged from 0 to 10 (0 for no pain, 10 for the most severe pain imaginable). The NRS has good test–retest reliability (r = 0.96) and convergent validity with the Visual Analog Scale (r = 0.95) in elderly populations.[15]

(4) Anxiety status: The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) was used for assessment. The SAS has satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.82–0.85) and construct validity in geriatric medical settings.[1617] SAS assessment records closest to the RCSQ assessment time (usually within the same day or adjacent 24-h period) were retrospectively collected. The SAS contains 20 items and uses a 4-point rating scale. After calculating the raw score, it was multiplied by 1.25, and the integer part was rounded to obtain the standard score. The assessment criteria were as follows: A standard SAS score ≥50 was defined as anxiety symptoms. In the analysis, the standard SAS score was included as a continuous variable to adjust for the effect of anxiety severity on sleep.

(5) Observation duration: Calculate the time (in hours) from emergency observation entry to transfer out or completion of RCSQ assessment. Based on clinical routines and data distribution, we converted it into a binary variable: <72 h and ≥72 h.

(6) Sedative-hypnotic drug use: Review the patient’s medication records during observation. “Use” was defined as any prescription sedative, hypnotic, or significantly sedative medication (e.g., benzodiazepines, non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic drugs, some antihistamines with sedative effects, or antipsychotics) that was administered during or before bedtime during the sleep period reviewed in the RCSQ assessment (usually the previous night). This was a binary variable (yes/no).

All assessments of the scales (RCSQ, SAS) were assumed to be completed by trained healthcare professionals following standard instructions. For individual missing items in retrospective data, if the missing percentage was <20% of the total number of scale items, the average score of the patient’s remaining items was used for imputation. If the missing percentage was high, the case was considered to have a missing key variable and was handled according to the exclusion criteria.

Statistical Methods

Age was dichotomized at 70 years based on clinical relevance (commonly used to define the “oldest-old” in emergency settings), and BMI was dichotomized at 24 kg/m2 according to the Working Group on Obesity in China criteria for overweight.[1819]

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD); comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent-samples t test, while those not conforming were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (IQR)]. Categorical variables were expressed as the number of cases (percentage) [n (%)]. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between nighttime noise decibels and RCSQ scores. Additionally, the relationship between each control variable and RCSQ scores was analyzed to provide a basis for subsequent regression model construction. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to verify the independent association of nighttime noise exposure levels with sleep quality. The RCSQ score was used as the dependent variable, and the core nighttime noise indicators LAeq,8h and LAmax were used as independent variables. Sex, age (categorical variable), BMI (categorical variable), history of sleep disorders, smoking history, alcohol consumption, observation duration (categorical variable), sedative-hypnotic drug use, SAS standard score, NRS score, and MEWS score were included as control variables in the model. Input regression method was used to screen statistically significant variables and clarify the independent effect of LAeq,8h on RCSQ score [regression coefficient β and 95% confidence interval (CI)]. For the binary outcome of sleep disorder (RCSQ < 50), multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the same set of covariates as in the linear regression model. Results were presented as odds ratios with 95% CI.

Correlation Between Noise and Sleep Quality

The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that RCSQ scores were normally distributed (W = 0.992, P = 0.474). The mean ± SD was 53.58 ± 7.44. A total of 50 patients (33.3%) had RCSQ < 50, indicating the presence of a sleep disorder.

Correlation analysis revealed that the SAS score (43.37 ± 7.47), NRS score (1.53 ± 0.88), MEWS score (5.05 ± 1.32), LAmax (84.11 ± 4.24 dB), and RCSQ score of elderly patients undergoing emergency observation were not related; LAeq,8h (49.61 ± 9.94 dB) was negatively correlated with the RCSQ score (r = −0.431, P < 0.05), meaning that the higher average nighttime noise levels were associated with the worse sleep quality, suggesting a potential role of noise in disrupting sleep continuity and onset, although item-level analysis was not performed in this study [Table 2].

Core Results of Multiple Regression

After adjusting for sex, age, BMI, history of sleep disorders, smoking history, alcohol consumption, observation duration, and sedative-hypnotic drug use, multiple linear regression analysis showed that LAmax did not significantly affect RCSQ score (P = 0.137), while LAeq,8h was significantly negatively associated with the RCSQ score (standardized β = −0.404, t = −5.130, P < 0.001). This suggests that even after excluding other confounding factors, increased LAeq,8h was still associated with a significant decrease in sleep quality in elderly patients [34]. To further evaluate the association of nighttime noise with the binary outcome of sleep disorder (RCSQ 50), we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for the same covariates. The results showed that LAeq,8h was significantly associated with increased odds of sleep disorder (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.03–1.13, P = 0.002), indicating that each 1 dB increase in LAeq,8h was associated with an 8% higher risk of sleep disorder [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

This single-center retrospective study explored the correlation between nighttime objective noise exposure and sleep quality in elderly patients in the emergency department. After rigorously adjusting for multiple confounding factors (e.g., sex, age, BMI, history of sleep disorders, smoking history, alcohol consumption, observation duration, and sedative-hypnotic drug use), the study confirmed that LAeq,8h was significantly negatively associated with RCSQ scores in elderly patients. These results echo the presupposed hypotheses of this study and address the lack of objective measurements and adequate confounding correction in previous studies on the association of noise with sleep in elderly patients in the emergency department. These findings provide evidence-based support for optimizing the emergency observation environment and improving sleep outcomes in elderly patients.

Baseline characteristics showed that among the 150 elderly patients, 75% were aged ≥70 years, and the proportion with sleep disorders was 33.3%, which is generally consistent with previous findings that elderly emergency patients tend to be older and that sleep disturbances are prevalent in older adults.[2021] Due to physiological decline, elderly patients have fragmented sleep structures and reduced deep sleep, and their tolerance for environmental stimuli is significantly reduced.[22] The environmental pressure of the emergency observation area, which is characterized by critical care, further increases the risk of sleep disorders, which also provided a physiological basis for the more significant noise interference effect on the sleep of this group.

Correlation analysis showed that LAeq,8h was negatively correlated with RCSQ scores. Although item-level analysis was not performed, previous research suggests that noise may primarily interfere with sleep continuity and sleep onset. Future studies should examine these specific domains using RCSQ subscale scores. This result aligns with previous research conclusions. The noise in the emergency observation area mainly comes from equipment alarms, medical staff operations, and patient and family activities. Such noise is often sudden and high-frequency, which can easily disrupt the sleep cycle, leading to an increase in the number of nighttime awakenings and prolonged sleep latency.[2324] Compared with younger patients, elderly patients have weaker sleep regulation ability, lower arousal thresholds for sudden noise, and are more prone to sleep structure disorders, which also explains why noise has a more prominent impact on their sleep quality.[22]

A previous study on ward noise and sleep quality has mostly focused on general wards or intensive care units and has relied heavily on patients’ subjective noise assessments, lacking objective acoustic measurements.[25] For example, an interventional study conducted in older patients following gastrointestinal tumor surgery found that the implementation of a ward noise-reduction strategy significantly lowered nocturnal noise levels and markedly improved sleep depth, sleep onset latency, nocturnal awakening frequency, and overall sleep quality scores; however, as this was an interventional design, it could not establish an independent association between noise and sleep.[26] This study used a fixed environmental noise automatic monitoring instrument to objectively record 8 h of noise data at night, using LAeq,8h as the core indicator, and found that even after excluding psychological and clinical factors, noise was still an independent influencing factor on sleep quality. Contrary to some previous studies, in the present cohort, no significant correlations were observed between anxiety (SAS), pain (NRS), or disease severity (MEWS) and sleep quality (RCSQ) (all P > 0.05) [Table 2], suggesting that the impact of these factors may be attenuated in this specific elderly emergency observation population.[27282930] This may be due to the fact that this study focused on the special population of the elderly who are hospitalized in the emergency room for observation. However, as an environmental factor that can be modified, the independent role of noise deserves clinical attention, providing a reference for clinical noise control. In a targeted study on the elderly hospitalized population, some scholars have found that elevated ward noise levels are significantly associated with poorer sleep quality and an increased incidence of sleep disorders.[31] However, most of these studies have not focused on the emergency observation scenario. The noise characteristics of the emergency observation area differ from those of general wards and intensive care units. The sources of noise are more complex and more variable, and patients’ conditions are often unstable. These factors collectively make sleep more complex. This study analyzed the association of LAeq,8h with sleep quality through multiple linear regression. This indicates that this study, focusing on the specific scenario of emergency observation and targeting elderly patients aged ≥65 years, confirmed the independent association of noise with sleep quality in this group. LAeq,8h and LAmax were included in the model at the same time and showed different effects, further suggesting that the average nighttime noise level, rather than a single peak, is the dominant factor affecting the sleep quality of elderly patients. These findings enriched the research evidence on sleep management of elderly patients and provided differentiated references for noise intervention in different medical scenarios.

The advantages of this study are mainly reflected in three aspects: First, the use of objective noise monitoring data avoided the bias of subjective evaluation and improved the reliability of the research results; second, it focused on the specific population of elderly patients in emergency observation, making it highly targeted, and the research conclusions have clear clinical application scenarios. This study also has several limitations: First, as a single-center retrospective study with a sample size of 150 cases from one hospital, selection bias may exist, and the generalizability of the findings requires validation through multicenter, large-sample studies. Second, missing first-night noise data were handled by substituting with data from the next full night; however, the missing rate and reasons were not reported, and sensitivity analyses were not performed to assess the robustness of this substitution. Third, the retrospective design cannot avoid missing data and measurement bias; although reasonable missing value handling methods were used, some residual bias may remain. Fourth, we did not distinguish the interference effects of different types of noise (e.g., equipment alarms versus personnel activities), making it impossible to determine which type had a greater impact on elderly patients’ sleep. Fifth, no item-level analysis of RCSQ was conducted; therefore, our statements regarding noise primarily interfering with sleep continuity and sleep onset are speculative and should be verified in future studies using domain-specific assessments. Sixth, we did not follow up on patients’ sleep recovery after discharge, limiting our ability to assess the long-term impact of noise on sleep and rehabilitation prognosis. Based on the findings of this study, the clinical environment of the emergency observation area can be optimized in three ways to improve the sleep quality of elderly patients: First, establish a noise control mechanism, which can be achieved by optimizing equipment layout, reducing equipment alarm volume, standardizing medical staff operating procedures (e.g., communicating quietly at night and reducing unnecessary nighttime operations), and rationally dividing patient areas to reduce noise generation and transmission. Second, conduct targeted sleep interventions by using simple protective tools such as earplugs and eye masks for patients with high levels of nighttime noise exposure while strengthening psychological counseling to alleviate anxiety and improve sleep quality. Third, improve the sleep assessment system for elderly patients, regularly monitor patients’ sleep quality and noise exposure levels, and adjust intervention measures in a timely manner to form a closed-loop management of “monitoring–assessment–intervention.”

Future research can focus on the following directions: First, conduct multicenter, prospective studies to expand the sample size, verify the generalizability of the findings, and employ more precise noise monitoring methods to differentiate the interference effects of different types of noise; second, incorporate more potential confounding factors, such as patients’ hearing status and sleep habits, to further refine the correlation analysis between noise and sleep quality; third, conduct interventional studies to verify the association between noise control measures and improvements in sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes among elderly patients under the emergency observation ward; and fourth, conduct long-term follow-ups to explore the impact of noise exposure on sleep quality and the long-term prognosis of elderly patients after discharge, providing support for building a more comprehensive medical environment management system for elderly patients.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, higher LAeq,8h is significantly associated with poorer sleep quality and an increased risk of sleep disorders in elderly emergency observation patients. As a modifiable environmental factor, noise deserves clinical attention for its independent impact on sleep in this population.
