Global, regional, and national bladder cancer burden from 1990 to 2021 and forecasts to 2050: a systematic analysis of the global disease burden study in 2021
Authors: Weici Feng, Yanjun Wang, Kangbu Wu, Jiamin Zeng, Qianghua Zhou, Xingliang Tan, Xinpei Deng, Runhao Zheng, Long Huang, Xingjian Li, Kai Yao, Zhiming Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer (BCa) remains a global health challenge, marked by geographic variations and evolving epidemiology. Despite new therapies, comprehensive assessments of long-term trends, risk factors, and predictive modeling are scarce. This study aimed to quantify the global, regional, and national burden of BCa in 2021, with stratification by age, sex, and geography. We further assessed temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 and projected future prevalence and mortality up to 2050. Finally, we analyzed demographic and regional disparities to identify high-risk populations and inform targeted prevention and resource allocation.MethodsWe utilized sex/age-stratified global registry data (29,377 sources) to model mortality via Bayesian spatiotemporal smoothing and incidence through hierarchical regression. DALYs were calculated by integrating mortality and disability using standardized life tables. Multilevel Bayesian age-period-cohort models, incorporating UN population projections and metabolic risk trajectories, generated 2050 estimates, with uncertainty quantified through 1,000 bootstrap replicates.ResultsIn 2021, global BCa prevalence was 540,310 cases (95% UI: 494,721–582,579), with an age-standardized prevalence of 0.00635%. Geographic variations were marked: Western Europe reached 0.078% (95% CI: 0.073–0.083%), with six countries exceeding 0.08%, while the United States had the highest at 0.101% (95% CI: 0.095–0.105%), with 31 states surpassing 0.1%. Males bore a substantially higher burden (sex ratio 4.2), with prevalence peaking in ages 85–89 (0.308%, 95% UI: 0.264–0.334%). From 1990 to 2021, the global age-standardized prevalence increased by 4% (95% UI: 2–14%).ConclusionBCa continues to exert a rising and uneven global burden. Temporal analyses revealed heterogeneous trends across regions, while projections indicate sustained increases by 2050, particularly in aging populations and resource-limited settings. Marked disparities by sex, age, and geography highlight high-risk groups requiring targeted interventions. These findings provide critical evidence to inform global health planning and prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25611-x.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Global burden of disease, Age-standardized prevalence, Gender disparity, Epidemiology

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is a prevalent malignancy, predominantly arising from the epithelial cells of the bladder’s inner lining, a histological subtype known as urothelial carcinoma [1]. This cancer is the seventh most common cancer in men worldwide. In China, the incidence of BCa is on the list of genitourinary and reproductive tumors and shows an upward trend every year [2]. Data from the American Cancer Society indicate that in 2022, BCa was the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer and ranked eighth as a cause of cancer-related mortality among males in the United States. BCa not only diminishes patients’ quality of life but also contributes to a reduction in their life expectancy [3]. The management of BCa necessitates considerable financial resources, invariably imposing substantial economic burdens on patients, their families, and society at large [4]. With the expansion of the global population, the prevalence of BCa is projected to continue its upward trend [5]. Nevertheless, the burden of BCa exhibits regional variability [6]. It is imperative to conduct a thorough and precise evaluation of the global disease burden imposed by urological pathologies.

BCa is a multifactorial disorder influenced by a spectrum of genetic and environmental determinants that collectively contribute to its pathogenesis. Numerous studies have corroborated smoking as a predominant risk factor for the development of BCa [7]. Chemicals such as aromatic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco can cause cancer after kidney metabolism. Quitting smoking can reduce the risk of BCa development [8]. In addition to smoking, prolonged occupational exposure to chemicals such as aromatic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons is closely associated with the incidence of BCa. Gender and age represent two pivotal epidemiological factors associated with the pathogenesis of BCa. Males are more frequently afflicted by this malignancy, exhibiting a relatively consistent gender distribution with a male-to-female incidence ratio of approximately 4:1 [9].

The highest estimated incidence rates for both genders are predominantly observed in most European regions, North America, Oceania, and North Africa. Conversely, the lowest incidence rates are generally found among both men and women in sub-Saharan Africa, southern Asia, and South America [10]. This discrepancy is primarily attributable to variations in the prevalence of risk factors across these regions. Prominent risk factors for the development of BCa encompass tobacco consumption, infection with Schistosoma species, and occupational exposure to aromatic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). Additional putative risk factors for BCa encompass specific dietary habits, exposure to environmental contaminants, and genetic susceptibilities [11]. Given these regional disparities, accurately refined data are urgently needed to support the development of targeted public health strategies.

Building on previous research, reducing the incidence and mortality of BCa requires a multidimensional strategy. Such approaches include smoking cessation, stringent regulation of carcinogenic substances in the environment—such as aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, arsenic, and nitrates—modification of unhealthy personal behaviors, and personalized prevention for genetically susceptible populations. Parallel efforts involve sustained investment in research to elucidate risk factors and gene–environment interactions, as well as the establishment of standardized registries to ensure the accuracy of epidemiological data. Although no explicit global or regional reduction targets have been set specifically for BCa, international efforts in cancer prevention and control provide indirect guidance. For instance, the World Health Organization’s “Sustainable Development Goals 2030” include reducing the incidence and mortality of cancer as a whole, which implicitly encompasses BCa. At the national level, strategies to reduce the burden of BCa primarily emphasize early screening, standardized treatment, and scientific innovation. However, a unified, quantifiable reduction goal has yet to be formulated [12, 13].

To meet this need, our study utilizes the updated Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework to estimate the global prevalence and burden of BCa from 1990 to 2021. This approach allows for high-resolution analysis by location, age, and sex, offering a detailed epidemiological profile of the disease. We also examine associated risk factors and their trends over time, and project future incidence rates at global and regional levels through 2050.

Methods

Date source

To source the data employed in the model, a systematic review was conducted utilizing information shared by national collaborators and the World Health Organization (WHO). To identify a novel data source, an iterative and ongoing data search process is employed. All raw datasets and visualization tools used in this analysis are available at GBD Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The original data can be found in the Supplementary materials. Details of the iterative data search efforts conducted for the GBD study in 2019 have been published [14–17]. In this study, we identified 29,377 data sources and applied the methodology and evidence framework provided by the GBD study.

This report primarily focuses on BCa incidence and burden, as these metrics are especially salient in delineating the disease’s characteristics and encapsulating the multifaceted aspects of its global surge; additionally, mortality estimates are included. Additionally, mortality data were incorporated into the computation of our key metrics: prevalence (utilizing cause-specific mortality within the context of international disease modeling) and years of life lost (YLLs), quantified by the age at expected mortality. We also calculated disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), which is the sum of YLLs and years lived with disability (YLDs).

We present a range of estimates derived from standardized outcomes, specifically those based on hypothetical populations with uniform age distributions, to facilitate the comparison of estimates across populations with varying age structures. The standard population was derived using the weighted mean of the 2019 GBD data, reflecting the proportional distribution of age-specific populations across countries with populations exceeding 5 million in 2019.

Statistical analysis

Reported geographic location

Estimates of BCa incidence were generated for 204 countries and regions, categorized into seven super-regions based on epidemiological patterns. These super-regions were subsequently subdivided into 27 regions, reflecting both geographical and epidemiological similarities.

Mortality

The mortality model incorporates data from 29,377 person-years of death. We utilized vital registration data and anatomical findings from oral autopsies coded as BCa from 1990 onwards [18]. We developed a mortality model specific to BCa to estimate cancer-related deaths. This study employs a Bayesian Hierarchical Model for several key reasons [5]: the data exhibit a hierarchical structure (e.g., individuals nested within regions); the Bayesian approach facilitates the integration of prior knowledge with observational data, thereby offering uncertainty estimates for the parameters; it is well-suited for analyzing sparse data and enables the borrowing of information across hierarchical levels. This study utilizes a highly automated analytical tool that selects the mortality rate from a log-normal distribution regression model or adjusts the rate based on various predictive covariates through alternative combinations. Combinations are selected based on their out-of-sample predictive validity as assessed by a validation test. We selected three covariates—gender, age, and region—based on established or hypothesized associations with the development and management of BCa [19].

Forecasting

Utilizing the predicted Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) as a predictor variable, we incorporated this measure into our regression model to estimate the incidence rates of BCa [20]. These indicators are projected by age, gender, year, and location through to the year 2050. We developed a prediction dataset for 2050, incorporating demographic variables such as age, gender, and region, and employed models to forecast and compute the posterior distribution of morbidity rates.

Results

Total prevalence of BCa

Between 1990 and 2021, the global age-standardized prevalence of BCa showed a modest but notable upward trend. Overall, prevalence increased from 0.033% in 1990 to 0.035% in 2021, representing a relative rise of 4.5% (95% UI: 2–14). However, substantial regional heterogeneity was observed, with the Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania super-region rising by 65% and North Africa and the Middle East increasing by over 50%, while high-income regions experienced only marginal change (1.1%) and some areas even reported declines. These long-term shifts highlight the growing burden of BCa in many low- and middle-income regions, underscoring the need for targeted prevention and healthcare strategies (Table 1).

Bladder cancer prevalence (1990 vs. 2021) and percentage changes globally and in 27 super regions

In 2021, the global prevalence of BCa across all age groups was estimated at 540,310 cases (95% UI: 494,721–582,579), corresponding to a worldwide age-standardized prevalence rate of 0.00635%. Prevalence varied substantially across regions: it was highest in high-income areas such as Western Europe (0.078%) and the United States (0.101%), where several countries or states exceeded 0.1%, and lowest in sub-Saharan West Africa (0.007%) (Fig. 1).

Global age-standardized prevalence rate (ASR) of bladder cancer in 2021

Total prevalence of gender specificity

In 2021, the global age-standardized prevalence of BCa was higher among males than females, with rates of 0.059% (95% UI: 0.055–0.063) for males and 0.014% (95% UI: 0.013–0.015) for females, resulting in a sex ratio of 4.2:1 favoring males. The sex ratio for BCa prevalence varies geographically, ranging from 11.88 in the Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia super-regions to 2.05 in the Sub-Saharan Africa super-region. At the regional level, the prevalence of age-standardized BCa is higher in men than in women. Specifically, in Lebanon, Greece, Spain, and Italy, the male prevalence exceeds the female prevalence by more than 0.15%.

Total prevalence of BCa in different age groups

Globally, the prevalence of BCa exceeds 0.1% within each age cohort from 60 to 95 years, whereas it is less than 0.001% among individuals under 24 years of age. The global prevalence of BCa reached its peak within the age bracket of 85 to 89 years, at a rate of 0.308% (95% UI: 0.264–0.334). Within this age cohort, the highest prevalence rate of BCa was observed at 0.612% (95% UI: 0.515–0.668), whereas the lowest prevalence was recorded in South Asia, at 0.031% (95% UI: 0.027–0.038). At the regional level, the highest prevalence of age-specific BCa was observed in high-income North America among individuals aged 85–89 years, reaching 0.777% (95% UI: 0.645–0.849). The highest national-level prevalence of BCa was identified in Italy, where it reached 1.056% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.873–1.213) among individuals aged 85–89 years (Fig. 2).

Total BCa burden: YLLs, YLDs, and DALYs

Globally, the YLLs attributed to BCa amounted to 4.12 million (95% UI [UI]: 3.80–4.50) and the YLDs (Years Lived with Disability) were 0.28 million (95% UI: 0.21–0.37) in 2021, resulting in a total of 4.40 million (95% UI: 4.03–4.81) DALYs (Disability-Adjusted Life Years) for that year. (Table S1) The global age-standardized Disability-Adjusted Life Year (DALY) rate for BCa is 51.58 per 100,000 population (95% UI [UI]: 47.56–56.42), with the Years of Life Lost (YLL) rate at 3.27 per 100,000 (95% UI: 2.39–4.30), and the Years Lived with Disability (YLD) rate at 48.31 per 100,000 (95% UI: 44.43–52.68).

Changes in the prevalence of BCa: 1990 to 2021 and forecast by 2050

From 1990 to 2021, the global age-standardized prevalence of BCa increased modestly by 4% (95% UI: –2% to 14%), rising from 0.033% (95% UI: 0.031–0.035) to 0.035% (95% UI: 0.033–0.037) (Table 1). Substantial regional increases were observed in Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania (+ 65%; 95% UI: 27–130%) and in high-income regions (+ 114.8%; 95% UI: 109.6–119.7%). Prevalence rose by over 50% in North Africa/Middle East and by > 40% across Central and Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and South Asia. At the national level, prevalence doubled in six countries, most notably Cape Verde (+ 781%; 95% UI: 151–1379%) and Vietnam (+ 128%; 95% UI: 63–213%). Conversely, 29 countries experienced declining prevalence, including San Marino (–33%; 95% UI: –59 to –4%) and the United Kingdom (–26%; 95% UI: –29 to –24%). Some high-income countries such as the United Kingdom and San Marino achieved substantial reductions over the past three decades, while other regions, particularly the Nordic and high-income super-regions, exhibited only slow or minimal change. In contrast, several low- and middle-income settings—including Vietnam, Poland, and Romania—experienced sharp increases, underscoring widening global disparities in the epidemiological trajectory of BCa.

Globally, BCa resulted in 4.40 million DALYs in 2021, with premature mortality (YLLs) accounting for 94% of the burden, highlighting that the disease is primarily driven by fatal outcomes rather than disability. Significant regional disparities were observed: high-income and European nations showed stable or declining trends, whereas South and East Asia, North Africa, and the Middle experienced substantial increases, underscoring the influence of healthcare capacity on disease burden.

By 2050, the global age-standardized prevalence is projected to increase slightly to 0.037%, with the highest rates expected in individuals aged 90–94 years. Regional trajectories diverge: prevalence is projected to rise in the African Union (0.078% to 0.098%), Central/Eastern Europe and Central Asia (0.133% to 0.157%), and North Africa/Middle East (0.123% to 0.145%). In contrast, declines are anticipated in high-income regions (0.331% to 0.253%), the high SDI super region (0.247% to 0.186%), and the European Union (0.356% to 0.250%). These projections indicate that while some resource-rich regions may stabilize or reduce their future prevalence burden, many developing regions—particularly in Africa, Central Asia, and the Middle East—are likely to face sustained growth, reflecting disparities in demographic change, prevention, and healthcare access (Table 2).

Discussion

To contribute to this effort within the GBD study, we stratified the prevalence of BCa by geographic and other relevant factors and projected the incidence of the disease in select regions by the year 2050. In 2021, an estimated 540,310 individuals were afflicted with BCa. The global prevalence of age-standardized diabetes was 0.00635%, whereas the prevalence of age-standardized BCa in Western Europe was 0.078% (95% CI: 0.073%−0.083%). Globally, the prevalence of BCa exceeds 0.1% among individuals aged 60 to 95 years, whereas it is less than 0.001% in the population under 24 years of age. The global prevalence of BCa reaches its peak within the age range of 85 to 89 years, with a rate of 0.308% (95% UI: 0.264–0.334). Utilizing a Bayesian stratification model, we predicted that the incidence of BCa will peak among individuals aged 90 to 94 years in 2050. Specifically, the incidence in the African Union super region is projected to increase from 0.078% to 0.098%, representing a 26% rise; in the Southeast National Association super region, it is expected to increase from 0.042% to 0.062%, a 31% increase; and in the Eastern Europe and Central Asia super region, the incidence is projected to rise from 0.147% to 0.158%, indicating a 7% increase. We predict that the mortality rate due to BCa will peak among individuals aged 90 to 94 years in 2050. Specifically, the projected increases are as follows: in the African Union super region, from 0.100% to 0.149%; in the Southeast National Association super region, from 0.057% to 0.077%; and in the combined super regions of Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia, from 0.191% to 0.165% (Fig. 4). To ensure the accuracy of our long-term forecasts, we used a multilevel Bayesian age–period–cohort model alongside UN population projections. We assessed robustness using 1,000 bootstrap replicates and provided 95% UI for all estimates. Although uncertainty remains—especially over a 30-year period—this approach produces projections that are both scientifically rigorous and consistent with global burden forecasting standards. Our estimates should therefore be seen as reflecting long-term trends rather than exact predictions.

This study examines the most recent patterns and trends in BCa on a global scale, identifying the highest incidence rates in countries with high human development indices in Europe and North America. Additionally, smoking continues to be the most prevalent risk factor for BCa, notwithstanding the multifactorial etiology of the disease. We estimate that this factor accounts for approximately 50% of all BCa cases [21]. The WHO has issued a warning regarding the high prevalence of smoking in European countries and has outlined critical interventions that policymakers are encouraged to implement to mitigate exposure [22]. Some studies have shown that quitting smoking not only prevents recurrence and progression [23, 24] but also affects survival [25]. The secondary objective is to diminish exposure to both occupational and environmental carcinogens. The significance of primary prevention cannot be overstated, and it is imperative that urologists actively engage in the promotion and facilitation of these preventive strategies [12].

The global age-standardized prevalence of BCa was higher in men than in women in 2021, with rates of 0.059% (95% UI, 0.055–0.063) for men and 0.014% (95% UI, 0.013–0.015) for women, resulting in a sex ratio of 4.2:1 favoring males. In certain regions of North Africa and West Asia, men bear a greater burden of BCa. However, variations in gender-specific incidence trends have been noted across some countries, with a decline observed in the male incidence rate and an increase in the female rate. The incidence and severity of diseases between genders may be related to differences in carcinogenic exposure, access pathways, enzymatic processing of environmental substances, and cellular and physiological responses [26]. In recent years, there has been a gradual rise in the incidence of BCa among women, corresponding with the increasing prevalence of smoking and chemical exposure in this demographic [27]. Overall, the mortality rate for BCa has exhibited a downward trend, with the exception of countries experiencing rapid economic growth in Central, Southern, and Eastern Europe, as well as in the Baltic region [28].

This study identified age-related differences in the prevalence of BCa, with the global incidence reaching a peak among individuals aged 85 to 89 years at a rate of 0.308% (95% UI: 0.264–0.334). Within this age cohort, the highest prevalence rate of BCa is observed at 0.612% (95% CI: 0.515–0.668), whereas the lowest prevalence is documented in South Asia, at 0.031% (95% CI: 0.027–0.038). The age group is less than 0.001%. Given the aging demographic and the rising life expectancy, there is an increased imperative to focus on the incidence and management of BCa among the geriatric population. Drawing on the age-related changes in cancer prevalence, recent studies have advanced several hypotheses to elucidate the interplay between carcinogenic factors and the aging mechanism [27]. Initially, with advancing age, individuals incur increased environmental exposure to carcinogens, particularly from tobacco smoke and occupational or residential settings with high levels of contamination. Secondly, aging may be associated with impaired bladder emptying, potentially extending the duration of exposure to urinary carcinogens. Over time, this may result in an elevated concentration of urinary carcinogens among the elderly. An additional factor that may contribute to rising urinary carcinogen concentrations is the diminished capacity of the aging organ system to detoxify potential carcinogens.

Precision in the data pertaining to the epidemiology and societal impact of BCa is essential for the equitable distribution of scarce resources across global, regional, and national platforms. Building upon prior research endeavors, this study offers a thorough evaluation of the global burden of BCa, thereby establishing a robust analytical framework to underpin healthcare policies and intervention strategies. Variations in healthcare systems, treatment modalities, and access to diagnostic and therapeutic services, particularly across countries with disparate resource levels, contribute in part to the observed disparities in BCa survival and mortality rates internationally [28]. Improvements in treatment may increase survival and reduce mortality [29].

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous global studies on BCa. For example, GLOBOCAN 2020 data reported approximately 573,000 new BCa cases and 213,000 deaths worldwide in 2020, with the highest incidence rates observed in Southern and Western Europe and the highest mortality in Northern Africa [30]. Similarly, past GBD analyses have noted that while absolute case numbers have risen due to population aging, global age-standardized incidence and mortality rates have gradually declined since 1990 [31]. Notably, trends vary by region: over the last decade, BCa incidence in much of Europe increased even as mortality declined – likely reflecting improved early detection and treatment – whereas parts of Asia saw incidence rates decrease but male mortality rise, indicating persistent gaps in access to care. In terms of risk factors, our data accord with prior research showing tobacco as the leading modifiable cause of BCa (50% of cases) [30]. This reinforces WHO warnings about high smoking prevalence in Europe and the critical need for tobacco control. Indeed, countries that implemented aggressive smoking cessation policies (e.g. taxation, advertising bans, public smoking restrictions) have started to see reductions in smoking-related BCa burden [31]. Concurrently, strengthened occupational safety regulations in high‐HDI nations have reduced exposure to industrial carcinogens (aromatic amines, etc.), which may contribute to the stabilizing or declining incidence observed in those countries. By contrast, several low- and middle-HDI countries with rising BCa rates are undergoing rapid industrialization and have experienced increasing tobacco use and persistent occupational exposures. In these regions, limited healthcare resources and lack of routine urologic screening (e.g. timely evaluation of hematuria) have historically led to under-diagnosis or late diagnosis. Technological advances are increasingly shaping BCa management. For example, on the prevention side, Internet-of-Things ecosystems—remote monitors, wearables and real-time symptom apps—generate continuous data that detect recurrence or toxicity earlier, drive adherence to fluids/medications and trigger personalized interventions without extra clinic visits [32]. At the curative end, 18F-FDG PET-CT now surpasses conventional imaging for radiotherapy planning, delineating tumors with sub-centimetre precision so radiation can be sculpted to the malignancy while sparing adjacent bowel, bladder wall and bone [33]. These innovations partly explain why mortality and DALYs decline in some high-income countries despite persistently high prevalence, as patients are living longer with BCa as a chronic condition. In sum, the observed international differences in BCa trends likely reflect each country’s implementation of public health measures and clinical strategies. Declining trends in certain nations correspond with effective primary prevention (tobacco and occupational exposure control), improved diagnostics, and better access to care, while rising trends elsewhere underscore the need to strengthen these interventions. Continued investment in preventive strategies as well as enhancing early detection and treatment infrastructure, will be crucial to curb the global burden of BCa [30, 31].

Conclusion

Between 1990 and 2021, the global age-standardized prevalence of BCa rose slightly from 0.033% to 0.035%, but sharp increases occurred in Southeast/East Asia and North Africa/Middle East, while high-income regions remained stable. Men carried a far greater burden than women (sex ratio 4.2:1), and prevalence peaked at 0.308% in those aged 85–89 years. By 2050, the burden is projected to rise further in many low- and middle-SDI regions. These findings emphasize the urgent need for region-tailored strategies—tobacco control, carcinogen regulation, early detection, and equitable access to effective treatment—to curb the future global burden of BCa.
