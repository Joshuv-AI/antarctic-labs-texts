Undergraduate health sciences students’ perceptions and practices of evidence-based practice: a cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Basmah Alharbi, Razan Almohimid, Mawahib Ahmed, Amal Mackawy, Amal Mohammed, Afshan Wasti, Thamer Alslamah, Abdulrahman Almujaidel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence-based practice (EBP) is vital for clinical professionals, including physicians, physiotherapists, nurses, dentists, and psychiatrists from diverse cultural backgrounds. EBP ensures the implementation of systematic, standardized, safe, and cost-effective interventions. While existing Saudi literature has primarily focused on current practitioners, exploring the perspectives of local healthcare students regarding EBP is essential.MethodsA descriptive survey with cross-sectional data collection was conducted online using the Arabic version of the Student Evidence-Based Practice Questionnaire (S-EBPQ) with 132 Saudi undergraduate students in the health sciences. Descriptive statistics, t-test, ANOVA, and linear regression were used for data analysis.ResultsThe results indicate that undergraduate health sciences students viewed EBP positively, with the attitude subscale being the highest (mean = 5.32), and the practice subscale being the lowest (mean = 4.89). The multiple linear regression analysis revealed no significant differences due to demographic characteristics (gender, age, study year, and marital status). Retrieving and reviewing the evidence on the EBP practice revealed a significant positive effect (t = 3.537, P < 0.001, along with a significant positive effect of sharing and applying EBP (t = 2.255, P = 0.026) on the EBP practice. However, there was no significant effect of attitude toward EBP on the EBP practice (P = 0.921).ConclusionThis study revealed that significant levels of EBP exist among health sciences undergraduates; however, the outcomes showed that practice scores were less than attitude scores. While theoretical knowledge is strong, opportunities exist for improvement in critical analysis and application to specific cases. Moreover, educators can enhance proficiency through interactive learning experiences and practical workshops.Keywords: Evidence-based practice, Health sciences, Undergraduate students, Saudi Arabia, Epidemiology

Introduction

Evidence-based practice (EBP) has gained importance in global clinical care over the past two decades, becoming recognized as a gold standard for high-quality patient care [1, 2]. Studies have generally defined EBP as a problem-solving approach that applies prevailing research evidence and clinical expertise, respects patient values and experiences in clinical practice [2], and adheres to local health laws and regulations [3]. Evidence-based practice is a critical component for professionals from diverse cultural backgrounds, including physicians, physiotherapists, nurses, dentists, and psychiatrists. Consequently, it can result in an effective, safe, cost-effective intervention [2]. For its effective implementation, specific skills are necessary, including conducting literature searches and critically appraising the validity, clinical relevance, and applicability of findings, as well as accessing databases and full-text articles [3, 4]. Nonetheless, challenges persist in developing and implementing professional education for EBP, including inadequate training, time constraints, and difficulties in applying research findings in practice [2]. As outlined in the 1990 s, the EBP model comprises five steps that provide a concise overview of the process [4]. These steps involve asking answerable clinical questions, conducting a systematic search for optimal evidence, critically evaluating the retrieved evidence for relevance, validity, and applicability, implementing an appropriate plan, and assessing clinical outcomes and performance [5].

Evidence-based practice evolved from evidence-based medicine (EBM) [5], which is also known as evidence-based care (EBC), evidence-based health (EBH), and evidence-based nursing (EBN) [6]. EBM was initially presented in the 1990 s, described as “the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients” [7]. Evidence-based medicine principles involve formulating the appropriate clinical question, seeking pertinent clinical data, evaluating it, applying the data to the proper clinical context, and assessing the results [8]. Scientific evidence initially focused on medicine; however, EBM has been integrated into various fields in subsequent years. Many health professionals follow an EBP approach [9, 10].

Incorporating EBP into healthcare has been increasingly emphasized to enhance outcomes and address the rising demand for healthcare services [11]. Studies indicate that healthcare workers following targeted training programs have significantly improved their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding EBP, as indicated by higher scores on knowledge and attitude assessments following training [9, 10, 12]. Wang et al. [13] and Jia et al. [14] reported that the development and validation of assessment tools, such as the EBP Profile Questionnaire (EBP2), have provided reliable means of assessing EBP knowledge among nurses and clinical postgraduates. Furthermore, various European countries collaborated on the Strategic Partnership in Innovation and Development of Evidence-Based Healthcare (SPIDER) project to improve the translation and application of EBP in clinical practice [15]. Moreover, health and non-health sectors have recognized the importance of integrating EBP into decision-making, as it enhances critical thinking skills, facilitates informed decision-making, and assists individuals in practice based on a variety of trustworthy evidence from various reliable sources [14, 16].

Evidence-based practice has become increasingly important in the Middle East’s healthcare sector. Studies have verified that the English version of the S-EBPQ can be used as a gold-standard questionnaire to collect valid data on healthcare professionals’ attitudes, knowledge, and proficiency in making evidence-based clinical decisions among Arabic-speaking undergraduate students in the health sciences [5, 6]. Furthermore, a study conducted in the United Arab Emirates found that structured EBP training significantly improved nurses’ confidence and promoted a culture of EBP in clinical settings [17]. Moreover, a study in Saudi Arabia validated the Arabic version of the S-EBPQ, demonstrating its reliability in assessing medical students’ knowledge and skills in EBP [7]. The Ministry of Health’s clinical audit program demonstrated that the EBP can enhance compliance with clinical standards across hospitals, thereby significantly improving the quality of care [18]. Additionally, there are positive indications of a rise in organizational culture and readiness for EBP in Saudi healthcare systems, indicating that proper support and training can improve the integration of EBP [19]. Efforts to enhance EBP at King Saud University are exemplified by initiatives such as the implementation of clinical practice guidelines, accompanied by tailored training and adaptation to the local context [12]. The Saudi government’s Vision 2030 initiative aims to promote EBP by creating national guidelines and a structured clinical care approach, which is essential for reducing care disparities and improving patient outcomes [20]. Several factors influence the introduction of EBP in the Middle East, including healthcare infrastructure, education systems, cultural aspects, and resource availability [17, 20]. Some Middle Eastern countries have achieved significant progress in implementing EBP; however, others face challenges with training, resources, and organizational support.

Although EBP has its advantages, numerous barriers prevent its adoption and utilization, with ongoing research uncovering disparities in its implementation in clinical settings [21]. While several studies have examined how medical professionals and students perceive EBP, few studies have been conducted in Saudi Arabia [2]. Local data in Saudi Arabia are vital due to socio-cultural diversity, and locally trained healthcare providers can standardize EBP implementation [1, 4]. Generally, healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia might not have formal EBP training, resulting in a knowledge gap regarding how research results are incorporated into clinical practice [6]. The main barrier identified is the limited time, as more than 50% of physiotherapists and primary care physicians cite patient workload and time restrictions as key hindrances to EBP implementation [22, 23]. According Youssef & Alharbi [4], a notable disparity exists in understanding, beliefs, and implementation of EBP in Saudi Arabia’s clinical practice. Many healthcare professionals have also expressed a lack of confidence in their ability to assess literature and apply research findings, highlighting the need for improved training and educational measures [2, 22]. Furthermore, language is often reported as a barrier; however, it has only been explored in certain studies conducted in non-English-speaking countries, and it is perceived as a barrier by more than 50% of responders [2, 23]. Language issues and the lack of management support for EBP training are significant barriers to full-text access to research papers [6, 24]. The fact that most questionnaires originate from English-speaking countries poses a challenge for Saudi Arabian healthcare professionals, particularly those with limited proficiency in the English language.

Teaching the basics of EBP is fundamental to delivering high-quality care, emphasizing the need to pass on knowledge and cultivate skills and attitudes from the beginning of university education [25]. Studies have shown that a properly designed EBP course can have a positive impact on undergraduate students’ attitudes towards EBP after graduation. EBP courses prepare undergraduates to utilize evidence-based decision-making in their future practice by providing them with essential knowledge, skills, and confidence [3]. Such courses must incorporate interactive methods, such as lectures, small-group work, journal clubs, reading quizzes, clinical presentations, workshops, and problem-based learning [26]. Several countries, including Australia, Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States, have already integrated EBP subjects into their curricula for undergraduate and graduate students. In contrast, numerous developing nations, such as Saudi Arabia, remain in the early stages of integrating the crucial subject of EBP into their undergraduate and graduate curricula [21]. Likewise, research involving Saudi nationals who obtained their undergraduate education and initial training in Saudi Arabia also reveals a lack of emphasis on EBP in undergraduate students’ clinical placements [2, 6, 25]. A significant deficiency also exists in comprehending and implementing the EBP concept in Saudi Arabia; therefore, the focus of teaching EBP in undergraduate and graduate programs should be on meeting the needs of healthcare professionals, rather than focusing on the skills of EBP [27].

Several approved instruments are available and have been extensively utilized for evaluating competence. The most common questionnaires in this field include the EBP questionnaire (EBPQ), which consists of 24 items [25], and the student EBP questionnaire (S-EBPQ), comprising 21 items, developed by Upton et al. [28]. Additional questionnaires include the health sciences EBP questionnaire (HS-EBP) (with 60 items by Fernández-Domínguez et al. [29]), the EBP competence questionnaire (EBP-COQ) (with 25 items by Ruzafa-Martinez et al. [30]) for undergraduate nursing students, the McEvoy trans-professional instrument of EBP profile (EBP2) (with 49 items [31]), and the competencies, beliefs, facilitators, barriers, and implementation of EBP (EBP-CBFRI) (with 55 items [32]).

The S-EBPQ comprises four subscales and a total of 21 items, including the frequency of practice subscale (6 questions), the attitudes subscale (3 questions), the retrieving and reviewing evidence subscale (7 questions), and the sharing and applying EBP subscale (5 questions) [31]. Every item is assessed using a 7-point Likert scale. More outstanding scores reflect greater competency in evidence-based practice [32]. The S-EBPQ is a revised form of the EBPQ, created by Dominic and Penney Upton in 2006, to assess nurses’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward EBP [26]. Upton et al. [28] discovered that the retrieving and reviewing evidence subscale and the sharing and applying EBP subscale were based on the knowledge and skills subscale of the EBPQ. The tool’s reliability was assessed using item response theory, and its construct validity was evaluated through exploratory factor analysis [30]. Due to its brevity, speed, ease of administration, and psychometric properties, the S-EBPQ is widely used among undergraduate students [4]. It has been validated by nursing students in the United Kingdom, Australia, and China [30]. The S-EBPQ was specifically created for nursing students; nonetheless, students from other healthcare fields can also apply this method to assess their attitudes, knowledge, and skills concerning EBP—this idea is recognized as interdisciplinary. Although EBP has been explored in Saudi Arabia, there are limited studies that include a multi-disciplinary cohort of health sciences undergraduates using an instrument like the S-EBPQ.

Currently, no studies have used the Arabic version of the S-EBPQ to examine the knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and experiences of undergraduate health science students. Consequently, this study is the first to investigate these aspects in this demographic in Saudi Arabia. This study aims to evaluate the perceptions and implementation of evidence-based practice among undergraduate health sciences students at Qassim University during their clinical placements. Therefore, this research explores the implementation of the student evidence-based practice questionnaire among health sciences undergraduate students. Assessing the views of healthcare students at Qassim University on EBP is essential for identifying gaps in practice and promoting professional development to maintain future care quality.

Methodology

Study design & data collection

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design using an online, self-administered survey to examine the practice, attitudes, proficiency, and effectiveness of undergraduate health sciences students in EBP. The target population consisted of male and female students enrolled in the College of Applied Medical Sciences at Al-Qassim University, representing various disciplines, including radiology, medical laboratory sciences, physiotherapy, public health, health informatics, and optometry. These students are typically under 25 years of age, which was anticipated. A minimum sample size of 129 participants was calculated using G*Power software to achieve a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 95%.

A convenience sampling approach was employed, and invitations were primarily sent via students’ official university email accounts to ensure accessibility. The purpose of the study was explained, and participation was voluntary. Data were collected using the Arabic version of the S-EBPQ, which had previously been adapted and validated among Saudi students [4, 6]. The adaptation involved forward–backward translation, expert panel review, and pilot testing to ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness. Reliability and validity had been established in earlier research; in this study, the Arabic S-EBPQ again demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.859. Accordingly, a validated and culturally adapted tool was used to ensure methodological rigor.

The survey was conducted using Google Forms and shared through the official university email. It included demographic questions (age, gender, and year of study), items assessing students’ perceptions and use of EBP, and a series of Likert-scale statements to evaluate their attitudes, practices, and proficiency. Additionally, a small set of multiple-choice questions was added to support the perception measures. To ensure data integrity, access to the Google Form was restricted to Qassim University email accounts, and the form allowed only one submission per account. This approach ensured that only eligible students could participate and prevented duplicate responses. On average, participants required 10 to 15 min to complete the questionnaire.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; approval was granted by the Deanship of Scientific Research and the Ethical Committee at Qassim University, Saudi Arabia (Reference No: 25-21-06). Informed consent was secured from all participants, ensuring they were fully aware of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential benefits. All data collected in this study were kept confidential and used solely for research purposes. The information gathered was anonymous and could not be linked to any individual participant.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to provide a summary of student characteristics, including age, gender, study year, and marital status. Feedback on EBP practice and attitudes was summarized, the evidence was retrieved and reviewed, and EBP was shared and applied using means and standard deviations for Likert scale items. Independent-sample t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to analyze variations in EBP practice and attitude, retrieve and review the evidence, and share and apply EBP across different groups (gender, age, study year, and marital status). A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to predict students’ practice of EBP, considering their attitudes, retrieval and review of evidence, and sharing and application.

Discussion

The importance of EBP in healthcare for ensuring patient safety has increased. Worldwide, the implementation of EBP remains a persistent difficulty that the KSA has also faced. Implementing EBP in Saudi Arabia is crucial for enhancing healthcare quality [19]. It can provide high-quality healthcare, reduce the occurrence of complications, and lower costs and resource utilization in healthcare [21]. This study is the first to evaluate undergraduate health sciences students’ perceptions and implementation of the Arabic version of the S-EBPQ in Saudi Arabia. The results of this research indicate that undergraduate health sciences students had a positive perception of EBP across all four subscales. The mean scores were as follows: EBP practice (4.89), attitude toward EBP (5.32), retrieving and reviewing evidence (4.92), and sharing and applying EBP (5.20). These results align with the increasing recognition of EBP among healthcare students and professionals in the region [7, 9, 33, 34]. Although the four subscales received positive scores, they were comparable to those found in research with undergraduate nursing students. This discrepancy between strong attitudes and lower practice scores reflects a known challenge in translating EBP beliefs into behaviors. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [35], attitudes alone are insufficient to predict behavioral performance unless accompanied by perceived behavioral control and supportive social norms. This suggests that while students value EBP, they may lack the confidence, mentoring, and clinical autonomy necessary to apply evidence in real practice settings. This interpretation is further supported by previous research showing variations in EBP-related competencies across different health disciplines and educational institutions [22]. One major challenge reported by health sciences undergraduates is the limited exposure to clinical practice compared with nursing students, who typically begin clinical placements earlier but may still face barriers such as difficulty collaborating with other healthcare workers [6, 29]. In contrast, nursing students in countries such as Thailand appear to benefit from both early practice opportunities and stronger clinical support systems, which may contribute to higher levels of EBP engagement [30].

The item “awareness of major information types and sources” received the highest score, with a mean of 5.62, indicating that students are well-versed in recognizing different evidence sources. Researching literature is an essential skill, similar to taking patient histories and conducting physical examinations. This type of practical training will instill a positive attitude towards utilizing EBP in everyday practice [23]. This finding is encouraging because knowing a variety of information sources is essential in EBP, enabling professionals to obtain and apply extensive evidence in making clinical decisions. Conversely, the item “ability to analyze critically evidence against set standards” had the lowest score, with a mean of 4.38, suggesting a potential gap in students’ skills related to critical appraisal. This disparity could be attributed to various reasons, including the absence of discussion in Saudi Arabian literature. As critical appraisal is fundamental to EBP, this finding indicates a need for improved training or curriculum modifications to enhance students’ analytical skills. This approach can help students evaluate the validity and relevance of evidence more thoroughly. Additionally, Abu-Baker et al. [27] recommend that academic administrators and teachers design their courses to incorporate EBP principles. Utilizing various teaching methods, such as lectures, computer sessions, small-group discussions, journal clubs, and assignments, is considered advantageous in evidence-based teaching compared with a single educational intervention [33]. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of improved educational strategies that foster higher-order critical thinking and the practical application of evidence in real healthcare settings.

The demographic results of the research revealed no significant differences in the mean S-EBPQ scores for practice, attitude, retrieving and reviewing evidence, and sharing and applying EBP based on gender, year of study, or marital status. The study’s findings support previous research by Nantsupawat et al. [22], which found that factors such as academic year, gender, and school area were not predictive of EBP competency. Hasani et al. [8] also revealed that attitudes towards EBP were similar among younger and older physiotherapists. This further reinforces the key observation that attitude alone does not predict EBP implementation, aligning with TPB assumptions that behavior depends on perceived behavioral control and context support. This finding contrasts with the findings of [16], which indicated a strong link between physiotherapists’ attitudes and their level of education, suggesting that those with PhD and MSc degrees were more inclined towards implementing EBP compared to those with BSc and diploma degrees. This result indicates that EBP skills may not be fully evident in undergraduates but exhibit a connection with higher academic levels, such as master’s and PhD programs. This outcome may occur due to the more advanced courses, as the content covered in the field of EBP increases as students progress through their studies [10], as well as the increased clinical experience compared to undergraduate students.

Multiple regression analysis revealed that “retrieving and reviewing evidence” and “sharing and applying EBP” both have a significant and positive influence on EBP practice. Specifically, a higher level of retrieving and reviewing evidence is linked to a 0.370 increase in EBP practice (p < 0.001), while a higher level of sharing and applying EBP results in a 0.223 increase (p = 0.026). Conversely, the students’ perception of EBP did not directly influence their practical application, suggesting that merely holding a positive view of EBP is insufficient to ensure its implementation in clinical settings. A study with senior undergraduate students found that while they understand EBP, they are not fully incorporating it into their clinical practice [6]. Furthermore, supporting our emphasis on critical thinking with a 2025 study [36] that examines its connection to emotional intelligence among nursing students underscores our dedication to developing the skills that empower future healthcare professionals. This outcome highlights the importance of practical experience and skill development in integrating EBP principles into daily decision-making, rather than relying solely on a positive attitude towards EBP. This further emphasizes that practical skill development, reinforcement through clinical exposure, and organizational support systems are crucial for effectively translating EBP into practice. Therefore, enhancing case-based learning, simulation activities, and mentorship from experienced clinicians, as supported by regional evidence [9, 19], may boost students’ confidence and their ability to apply evidence effectively in real-world practice environments.

Limitations

The research was primarily descriptive, and participants were recruited from the College of Applied Medical Sciences at Al-Qassim University using convenience sampling, which may have limited the study’s generalizability. Additionally, the survey data were based on self-reports. Therefore, biases associated with self-reported surveys, including subjectivity, exaggeration of reporting, and recall bias, should not be ignored. Finally, applied medical sciences students have limited access to clinical placements, which may have influenced their views on EBP.

Future research can build on the findings of this study to improve evidence-based medical education. Future studies should prioritize confirmatory factor analysis to validate the scale across various cultures. Furthermore, investigating test-retest reliability and interrater agreement can provide a better understanding of the scale’s stability and consistency.

Conclusion

This research offers important insights into evidence-based practice (EBP) among undergraduate health sciences students, showing that they exhibit strong levels of EBP practice, attitude, and skills in retrieving and reviewing evidence, as well as sharing and applying EBP. While their theoretical understanding of EBP is strong, there is potential for growth, especially in critical analysis and its application to specific situations. Educators can improve students’ skills in EBP by integrating interactive learning methods, such as case-based learning and hands-on workshops that emphasize skill application in real-life contexts. This strategy fosters high-quality, evidence-based care in healthcare environments.
