Geometry-Aware Predictive Safety Filters on Humanoids: From Poisson Safety Functions to CBF Constrained MPC
Ryan M. Bena, Gilbert Bahati, Blake Werner, Ryan K. Cosner, Lizhi Yang, Aaron D. Ames

Abstract
Autonomous navigation through unstructured and dynamically-changing environments is a complex task that continues to present many challenges for modern roboticists. In particular, legged robots typically possess manipulable asymmetric geometries which must be considered during safety-critical trajectory planning. This work proposes a predictive safety filter : a nonlinear model predictive control (MPC) algorithm for online trajectory generation with geometry-aware safety constraints based on control barrier functions (CBFs). Critically, our method leverages Poisson safety functions to numerically synthesize CBF constraints directly from perception data. We extend the theoretical framework for Poisson safety functions to incorporate temporal changes in the domain by reformulating the static Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation as a parameterized moving boundary value problem. Furthermore, we employ Minkowski set operations to lift the domain into a configuration space that accounts for robot geometry. Finally, we implement our real-time predictive safety filter on humanoid and quadruped robots in various safety-critical scenarios. The results highlight the versatility of Poisson safety functions, as well as the benefit of CBF constrained model predictive safety-critical controllers.



I
INTRODUCTION

Robotic technology is evolving at an exciting rate; with this rapid evolution comes the increasing need for safe robotic autonomy. Whether one considers a quadrupedal robot performing an industrial inspection, an autonomous vehicle transporting passengers, or the Ingenuity helicopter flying above the Martian surface, collisions with the environment can often be irreversible and catastrophic. Humanoid robots in particular are designed to operate in complex and dynamically-changing human-occupied environments. Thus, it is critical to ensure that autonomous robots interact with their environments in a safe, predictable, and robust manner.

The field of safety-critical control aims to tackle this challenge. Over the past several decades, various approaches have been proposed to address the notion of safe autonomy for dynamical systems. A few popular examples include Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability methods

MPC+CBF methods seek to perform some nominal autonomous task while keeping the state of the robotic system within a safe region. This is often formalized as enforcing forward invariance of a
safe set
with respect to the closed-loop dynamics. As the first step in achieving forward invariance, the safe set is frequently characterized by a continuous
safety function
, the zero superlevel set of which precisely defines the safe set. In most published work, the safety function is specified via two methods: 1) an
ad hoc
analytical expression — this is the case in most CBF research, where “obstacles” in the environment are described by implicit geometric shapes, e.g., circles, ellipses, and rectangles
[
14
,
15
]

[
16
,
17
]
. We explore a more recent result: the
Poisson safety function

[
18
]
, a numerical method which uses partial differential equations (PDEs) to characterize safe sets by solving Poisson’s equation in the spatial domain. Poisson safety functions have demonstrated benefits over
ad hoc
and SDF methods.

In this work, we present a geometry-aware
predictive safety filter
for robotic systems, based on nonlinear MPC+CBF, that utilizes Poisson safety functions in higher-dimensional configuration space to enforce safe set forward invariance via translational and rotational degrees-of-freedom (DOFs). The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

Recasting the static Dirichlet problem in
[
18
]
as a moving boundary problem, we generate a time-varying safety function that extrapolates the evolution of the safe set over a finite future time interval.

•

Using Minkowski set operations based on robot occupancy, we lift the domain for Poisson’s equation into higher-dimensional configuration space, thus producing a Poisson safety function that has additional DOFs.

•

Employing our extended Poisson safety function as a CBF for a single-integrator, we formulate a novel MPC+CBF predictive safety filter, which enables safe geometry-aware trajectory generation in real-time.

•

We experimentally demonstrate the advantages of our online predictive safety filtering method on quadruped and humanoid robots in multiple challenging safety-critical scenarios.

PRELIMINARIES

II-A

Safety-Critical Control: Control Barrier Functions

In this work, we discuss safety for control affine dynamical systems, i.e., systems whose state
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
evolves according to the continuous-time dynamics:

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, assumed to be Lipschitz continuous, characterize the autonomous and non-autonomous behavior of the system, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input. Given some Lipschitz continuous feedback law
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
, the closed-loop dynamics are:

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2)

We characterize the safety of the system (
1
) with respect to a set
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,h(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0\right\},

(3)

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,h(\boldsymbol{x})=0\right\},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{int}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,h(\boldsymbol{x})>0\right\},

(5)

where the zero superlevel set of the function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which the system state is considered safe.
The aim of safety-critical control is to design a feedback control law
\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
that renders
\mathcal{S}
forward invariant.

Definition 1
.

(Forward Invariance) A set

remains in

for all

.

For control affine systems as in (

can be enforced using CBFs.

Definition 2
.

(Control Barrier Function (CBF)
[

]

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\dot{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=L_{\boldsymbol{f}}h(\boldsymbol{x})+{L_{\boldsymbol{g}}h(\boldsymbol{x})}\boldsymbol{u}>-\gamma(h(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\}.

Given a CBF

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CBF}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\big|\,\dot{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\geq-\gamma(h(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\},

(7)

such that any locally Lipschitz controller for which
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CBF}}(\boldsymbol{x})
results in the forward invariance of
\mathcal{S}

[
19
]
.

2

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}),

\displaystyle\forall k\in\mathbb{N},

(8)

where
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the discretization of (
1
) over a finite time interval
\delta_{t}>0
for a constant input,
\boldsymbol{u}
.
Given this version of the system, we focus on the forward invariance of the discrete-time system at sample times

Let
\mathcal{S}

(10)

DCBFs can be used to generate safe control actions in the form of a safety filter, where performance goals are encoded via a nominal, but potentially unsafe, controller
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\textup{nom}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
which is minimally modified in a point-wise fashion to produce safe control inputs:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\textup{cbf}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\underset{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\textup{arg min}}

\displaystyle\quad\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\textup{nom}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}

(11)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad h(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}))\geq\rho h(\boldsymbol{x}).

(12)

In practice, the

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\,\big|\,h_{0}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0\right\},

(13)

where the zero superlevel set of the
safety function

II-B

Model Predictive Control

Despite the theoretical utility of CBFs in guaranteeing safety, they can often result in undesirable closed-loop properties in their practical implementation when the performance goals, encoded in the nominal controller, conflict with the safety constraints. This can result in the emergence of undesirable stable equilibrium points which keeps the system safe, but prevents it from achieving its goal
[
26
]
.
To simultaneously achieve both safety and performance goals, it is often useful to incorporate horizon-based optimization into the controller. To do this we adopt an MPC framework that incorporates the DCBF constraint as in
[
10
,
13
]
, enforcing safety over a time horizon.

The
predictive safety filter
is defined using the following finite-time optimal control problem (FTOCP) with a DCBF contraint along a horizon of length
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0:N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\underset{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\min}}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}c(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i})+V(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})

(14)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i+1}=\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}),\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}

\displaystyle\quad h(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i+1})\geq\rho h(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}),\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

where
c:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is the stage cost,
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the terminal cost used to approximate the infinite-horizon optimal control problem, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represent the planned state and inputs at time
k+i
. The first constraint ensures that the planned trajectory is dynamically feasible, the second constraint is the DCBF condition that enforces safety along the trajectory, and the last constraint ensures that the plan aligns with the current state.

To generate an input using this FTOCP, the optimal plan of inputs is computed as
[\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),\dots,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{N-1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})]
, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
is applied to the system, defining the MPC+CBF controller:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\textup{MPC+CBF}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}).

(15)

By enforcing the safety constraint in the FTOCP and optimizing across the planning horizon
N
, the
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\textup{MPC+CBF}}
controller displays favorable performance and robustness properties when compared to standard MPC or CBF controllers
[
13
]
.

III
EXTENDING POISSON SAFETY FUNCTIONS

III-A

Spatial Poisson Safety Functions

In robotic collision avoidance, the safety function
h_{0}
used to characterize
\mathcal{C}
, as in (
13
), is traditionally defined as a function of the spatial output
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, the nature of the physical spatial environment is frequently unknown
a priori
, and thus it can be difficult to predetermine an analytical expression for
h_{0}
. More often, general occupancy information for the local environment is collected in real-time, either via onboard sensors or global perception systems. This occupancy information can be used to estimate membership of the safe set
\mathcal{C}
, without an explicit functional characterization. In such cases, the estimated boundary of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.
\partial\mathcal{C}
, can be used to define the zero level set of the function
h_{0}
. Subsequently, this level set can be used to determine the entire function over some bounded domain via a Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation
[
18
]
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
define a domain:

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega}\coloneqq\mathcal{C},\quad\partial\Omega\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{C},\;\text{and}\quad\Omega\coloneqq\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{C}),

(16)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the smooth, open, bounded, and connected set over which Poisson’s equation will be solved;
\partial\Omega
is the boundary of that set; and
\overline{\Omega}
represents the closure of
\Omega
, i.e.
\overline{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\partial\Omega
. On this domain, we can establish the following Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta h_{0}(\boldsymbol{y})&=f(\boldsymbol{y})\quad&\forall\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega,\\
h_{0}(\boldsymbol{y})&=0\quad&\forall\boldsymbol{y}\in\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

where
\Delta=\nabla\cdot\nabla
is the
Laplace
operator, which computes the divergence of the gradient of
h_{0}
; the forcing term
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{<0}
is a user-defined function that forces
h_{0}
to be superharmonic. In
[
18
]
, it was shown that, if the forcing function
f
is smooth on the domain, i.e.,
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, then the resultant Poisson safety function is also smooth on that domain, i.e.,
h_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. It follows that
h_{0}
will be a CBF for systems with single-integrator dynamics; furthermore,
h_{0}
is compatible with the aforementioned CBF dynamic extension methods.

In previous research, discussions of safe sets and Poisson safety functions leveraged the assumption that safe sets do not change explicitly with respect to time. In dynamic environments however, the set itself can change over time, and thus its corresponding safety function will have an explicit time dependence. To account for these temporally-evolving environments, we can modify the set
\mathcal{C}
, leading to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
t\in[0,T]
is the value of time, defined over some bounded interval.

Determining the time dependence of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
(and the corresponding membership of the temporally lifted set
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
) requires knowledge of the behavior of the safe set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
and its boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
for
t\geq 0
. In practice, robotic systems often observe their environments in real-time; thus, the evolution of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
may not be known
a priori
. In such cases, it becomes necessary to predict the time evolution of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Level set methods
[
27
]
are widely utilized techniques for propagating boundaries by representing them implicitly and modeling their evolution using appropriate partial differential equations. The boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
is represented as the zero level set of an implicit function as follows:

\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\phi(\boldsymbol{y},t)=0\}.

(18)

Since
\phi(\boldsymbol{y},t)=0
on the evolving front, by the chain rule, we arrive at the transport equation:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(\boldsymbol{y},t)+\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)\cdot\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{y},t)=0,

(19)

with the initial condition
\phi(\boldsymbol{y},0)=\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{y})
, where
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}(t)=\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)
for each point
\boldsymbol{y}(t)\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
. The values of the solution to (
19
) remain constant along characteristic curves
[
28
]
, defined as solutions to the ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}(t)=\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)
with
\boldsymbol{y}(0)\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(0)
. This Lagrangian formulation allows us to track the boundary’s evolution over time
[
29
]
, enabling us to determine
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
for each
t\in[0,T]
by rewriting
\phi(\boldsymbol{y},t)
as:

\phi(\boldsymbol{y}(t),t)=\phi_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}(\tau)d\tau\right),

(20)

where
\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}
is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous in its arguments. This leads to the prediction model:

\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=\left\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\phi_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{y}-\int_{0}^{t}\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}(\tau)d\tau\right)=0\right\},

(21)

where we assume the variation of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot)
is continuous in time, following from the appropriate regularity of
\phi
.

Finally, we can construct the following non-cylindrical space-time domain:

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{T}}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)\times\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T],

(22)

which represents a temporally-lifted set corresponding to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)
, allowing us to formulate the moving boundary Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}h_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{y},t)&=f(\boldsymbol{y},t)\quad&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{T}},\\
h_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{y},t)&=0\quad&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},t)\in\partial\Omega_{\mathcal{T}},\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

where
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}
represents the
Laplacian
operator computed strictly with respect to the spatial output
\boldsymbol{y}
, and the Poisson safety function
h_{\mathcal{T}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
characterizes the safe set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Given a constant velocity field
\vec{\boldsymbol{v}}

III-C

Geometric Parameterization — Safe Set Buffering

Given the safe set
\mathcal{C}
in (
13
), a point is considered safe as long as its spatial output
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is in
\mathcal{C}
. Consequently, physical robotic platforms are often controlled as a point mass via their centroidal motion, and thus the techniques described in Section
II
are applied with respect to some arbitrary point on the robot. However, real-world robots have asymmetric geometries which must be suitably considered during collision avoidance. A common naive approach to handling robot geometry is to define a single robot
radius
and reduce the safe set
\mathcal{C}
by this radius equally in all spatial dimensions. Although this method is often computationally efficient, it lacks awareness of the true shape of the robot, which can result in significant conservatism, especially when dealing with highly asymmetric robot geometries.

Instead, we introduce the concept of
safe set buffering
, where the safe set (representing free space in the environment) is modified to account for the potentially-complex geometric shape of the robotic system in a particular orientation. First, we define the set
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{q})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
of all points occupied by the robot, relative to its centroid, where
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
is a quaternion representing the attitude of the system with respect to some global reference frame. In this work, we model the robot as a rigid-body, but in general the robot geometry might be further manipulable via additional DOFs in some high-order configuration space.

Next, we construct the reduced safe set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})\subset\mathcal{C}
. This is accomplished via a
Minkowski difference
2
2

2

This is equivalent to the
Minkowski sum
:
\mathrm{ext}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)=\mathrm{ext}\left(\mathcal{C}\right)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{q})
. In practice, the Minkowski difference can be computed as a convolution of the occupancy map with a kernel representing the robot occupancy.
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})=\mathcal{C}\ominus\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{q}).

(24)

The resultant safe set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})
is the minimal pointwise reduction of
\mathcal{C}
required to keep the entire robotic platform collision-free. An illustration of the Minkowski difference operation is presented in Fig.
2
.

Re-examining the PDE formulation in (
17
), the new attitude-dependent safe-set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})
can be lifted to a higher dimensional space to create the new non-cylindrical domain:

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{q})\times\{\boldsymbol{q}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{3},

(25)

which is extended to incorporate robot orientation. We can solve for the rotationally-dependent Poisson safety function:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}h_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q})&=f(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q})\quad&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q})\in\Omega_{\mathcal{Q}},\\
h_{\mathcal{Q}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q})&=0\quad&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q})\in\partial\Omega_{\mathcal{Q}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(26)

Because the original safe set
\mathcal{C}
only defines safety with respect to the translational output
\boldsymbol{y}
, the rotational output vector
\boldsymbol{q}
does not need to satisfy a boundary condition along
\boldsymbol{q}
and thus does not need to appear in the Laplacian.

IV
CONTROL ARCHITECTURE

Using the methods in Sections
III-B
and
III-C
, let
\Omega_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
be a non-cylindrical domain in configuration space and time:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}\!=\!\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{q},\,t}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{q},t)\times\{\boldsymbol{q}\}\times\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}\times[0,T],

(27)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
is a safe set parameterized in orientation and time. This allows us to formulate the final parameterized Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q},t)&=f(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q},t)\quad\!\!\!\!&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q},t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}},\\
h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q},t)&=0\quad\!\!\!\!&\forall(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q},t)\in\partial\Omega_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(28)

Solving this PDE produces the Poisson safety function
h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
corresponding to the safe set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
. In
[
18
]
, it was proven that the static Poisson safety function
h_{0}
in (
17
) is a CBF for robotic systems with single-integrator dynamics. While safety cannot be formally guaranteed in general time-varying environments, due to the inherently unknown nature of the future temporal behavior of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
, we can still harness
h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
to perform safety-critical control using an ROM-based hierarchical framework
[
32
,
33
]
, as depicted in Fig.
3
.

We employ a ROM for predictive safety filtering; specifically, we represent the system as a fully-actuated single-integrator with two translational DOFs and a single rotation DOF. The resultant 3-DOF continuous-time system model is
\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, where the reduced-order state
\boldsymbol{\chi}=[x,y,\theta]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
is directly controlled via the input
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[v_{x},v_{y},\omega]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

With this model, we design an MPC-based feedback control policy based on discrete-time single-integrator dynamics, which nominally drives the system state
\boldsymbol{\chi}
to the goal state
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathrm{d}}
while enforcing the forward invariance of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
using DCBF constraints:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\;\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathrm{d}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathrm{d}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(29)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\delta_{t},\quad\forall i\in[0,N-1]

\displaystyle\quad h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i+1},t_{i+1})\geq\rho h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},t_{i}),\quad\forall i\in[0,N-1]

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\chi}_{k}.

In our MPC formulation, the quadratic costs
\boldsymbol{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{R}
are chosen to elicit the desired system behavior; the time step
\delta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is chosen in conjunction with
N
to yield a suitable prediction horizon
T
; the CBF parameter
\rho\in\left(0,1\right)
is selected to produce appropriate dynamic robustness; and the initial
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
is constrained to match the current state
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{k}
.

V-B

Scenario 1: Dynamic Collision Avoidance

In the first scenario, we commanded both robots (Go2 quadruped and G1 humanoid) to maintain a fixed goal state while simultaneously avoiding an incoming dynamic object, i.e. a red dodgeball. This distills the practical scenario in which legged robots must operate safely in unknown dynamic environments. The outcome of a representative experimental trial for each legged robot are depicted in Fig.
4
, and the corresponding data is displayed in Fig.
5

In the figures, it can be observed that, by considering orientation in the predictive safety filter, both robots efficiently avoid the dynamic obstacle via simultaneous translation and rotation. Specifically, the quadruped initiates its avoidance maneuver by aligning its longitudinal axis (major axis) with the predicted trajectory of the incoming obstacle. Similarly, the humanoid aligns its lateral axis (major axis) with the predicted obstacle trajectory. These twisting actions are pivotal in minimizing the amount of translational motion required to maintain safety. This can be confirmed by examining the evaluated value of
h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
, which remained positive throughout the avoidance maneuver. This implies that the centroid of the robot remained in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}
and that all points on the robot remained within the original
\mathcal{C}
. These results were consistent and repeatable, as demonstrated in the video.

V-C

Scenario 2: Environmental Navigation

In the second scenario, we tele-operated the G1 humanoid through an unstructured static environment for 100 seconds. The user-controlled goal point, which was prescribed without inherent safety consideration, was passed to our MPC+CBF predictive safety filter to produce safe velocity commands. These commands were in turn tracked by our RL locomotion controller. The results are shown in Fig.

By examining Fig.

Without our geometry-aware safe set buffering method, the robot would not attempt to pass through this corridor, resulting in a navigational “deadlock”. However, the geometry-aware safety-critical controller proceeds to open this gap by planning a trajectory that reorients the humanoid (right image), aligning its lateral axis (major axis) with the corridor traversal direction and allowing the robot to effectively reach its goal position. This point can be further elucidated by examining the Poisson safety function manifolds, and corresponding measured heading angles, in Fig.

h_{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{T}}

\mathcal{C}

VI
CONCLUSIONS

To summarize, we proposed and demonstrated a simple yet effective means of extending Poisson safety functions into higher-dimensional, non-spatial domains, thus enabling the synthesis of a predictive safety filter. We first established a parameterized moving boundary Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation to generate a time-varying predictive Poisson safety function. Next, we employed the Minkowski difference operation to lift the domain for Poisson’s equation into rotational configuration space. Following these domain extensions, we incorporated our Poisson safety function into an MPC+CBF predictive safety filter algorithm for safe navigation and dynamic collision avoidance. We emphasized the real-time utility of our architecture by implementing it on several legged robots across a breadth of navigational tasks.
