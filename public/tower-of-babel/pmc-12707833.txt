Measuring and Forecasting the Healthy Eating Index in Iran: 1991–2027
Authors: Omid Emami, Amirhossein Takian, Ahmadreza Dorosty Motlagh, Nayereh Esmaeilzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Compliance with food-based dietary guidelines, as measured by the Healthy Eating Index (HEI-2015), and an enhancement in its score have been linked to a reduction in mortality risk and the prevalence of chronic diseases. The objective of this study was to compute and scrutinize the HEI for Iran over the preceding 30 years and to project the anticipated index for the forthcoming 7 years.Material and Methods:This research is a repeated cross-sectional study on 665254 Household. Through the application of an array of statistical and nutritional methodologies, we have successfully transformed the Household Income-Expenditure Survey (HIES) questionnaire data, spanning from 1991 to 2020 for the Iranian population, into meaningful household food consumption data. The data analysis was executed using STATA v.17.Results:We found a growing trend in HEIs until 2017, which dropped sharply in the following years. The average HEI at the national level was estimated to be 48.39 ± 10.25 over 30 years. The lowest and highest indices were in Sistan and Baluchistan Province (41.23 ± 7.56) and Qom Province (57.73 ± 11.27). The highest average energy intake was in the year 2005 (2695.33 ± 808.31 Kcal).Conclusion:The utilization of HIES data in this study serves as a proxy for household consumption values. We observed a commendable trend in the HEI up until 2017. However, post 2018, the trend exhibited a decline, potentially attributable to multiple factors. We strongly recommend a regular and strategic review of food and nutrition policies, particularly during crises, to circumvent undesirable outcomes.

Introduction

One out of five deaths are attributed to nutritional risk factors worldwide.[1] Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) account for 53% of the global disease burden, which are projected to increase to 60% in the years to come. Of the five major NCDs’ risk factors (alcohol use, inactivity, smoking, tobacco smoke, and poor diet), nutritional risk factors are responsible for the most Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs).[2] Furthermore, 73% of all deaths are related to NCDs, at the top of which are cardiovascular diseases,[3] to which nutritional factors account for over 50% of related deaths.[4] In fact, those who do not follow food-based dietary recommendations have 20–30% higher death rate.[5]

Diets high in sodium and low in whole grains, fruit, nuts and seeds, vegetables, and omega-3 fatty acids are the main dietary risk factors for mortality, each accounting for about 2% of global deaths.[1] In line with the global action plan for prevention and control of NCDs, the World Health Organization (WHO) has published a series of cost-effective interventions, so-called “Best Buys” to reduce the burden of NCDs.[6] Iran’s national action plan for prevention and control of NCDs and related risk factors has 13 goals, five of which are directly related to nutrition.[237] The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) regularly measures Healthy Eating Index (HEI) to estimate adherence to the dietary guidelines and update them every 5 years.[8] In other words, the goal of nutritional policies in any country is to provide comprehensive food-based dietary guidelines for different population groups to promote health and prevent diseases. Dietary guidelines are developed for policymakers and various health-related professional groups to help all individuals and their families achieve a healthy and adequate dietary intake. Adherence to HEI results in a 22% decrease of total deaths, 22% decrease of cardiovascular diseases, 16% decrease of cancers, 18.2% decrease of diabetes, and 15% decrease of neurodegenerative diseases.[91011] HEI is one of the most important indices for measuring food quality, especially for the level of compliance with food-based dietary guidelines (the Food Pyramid in Iran).[12]

The benefits of improving HEI are not limited to the most prevalent NCDs (cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes, etc.), but it can also contribute to improving other diseases, that is, oral and dental caries as well as depression.[13] Studies have shown that children with a higher HEI have 44% less dental caries than other children. Furthermore, adherence to depression-specific dietary guidelines can reduce depression by 20–40%.[14] A study of military forces in Iran revealed that those who better followed the food pyramid had an 80% lower chance of suffering from depression.[15] HEI is one of the most important evaluation tools for food quality control, which is in compliance with food-based dietary guidelines.[16] It is also used to examine the relationship between adherence to food-based dietary guidelines and the reduction of disease and mortality.[17]

In Iran, many studies have been conducted to relate the HEI to different health conditions, but there is no comprehensive study that shows the state of diet quality in Iran every year. In the National Comprehensive Study on Household Food Consumption Patterns and Nutritional Status 2001–2003, on average, the HEI was estimated as 34 out of 100.[18]

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to measure and analyze the HEI-2015 for the period of past 30 years in Iran. It aims to determine the rate of compliance with the dietary guidelines, which can be used to change or modify the nutritional guidelines.

In this study, we utilized the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) method to forecast the HEI over the next 7 years. We used univariate time series analysis to measure and forecast the HEI in Iran from 1991 to 2020, with projections to 2027. In order to obtain a more accurate assessment of HEI changes over time, we used time series analysis to identify trends, patterns, and seasonality.

By leveraging this predictive analysis, Iranian policymakers and stakeholders can make evidence-based decisions to enhance public health. Specifically, this knowledge can inform the identification of dietary gaps and strengths, the prioritization of budget allocation for nutrition-related programs, the facilitation of intersectoral cooperation, and the development of comprehensive intervention strategies.

Material and Methods

Subjects and sampling

This is a repeated cross-sectional study. We analyzed the data collected by Statistical Center of Iran (SCI) for the Household Income-Expenditure Survey (HIES) for the period of 1991–2020 among Iranian households to extract the HEI-2015. Household Income and Expenditure Surveys (HIESs) provide information on people’s living conditions and income/expenditure patterns. Data from HIESs are used for calculating Consumer Price Index (CPI) and to analyze the socioeconomic and wellbeing statuses.[19] The SCI uses a multistage sample design for this survey, and its target population includes all private and collective settled households in urban and rural areas. HIES is used by many countries. The United Nations (UN), World Bank (WB), International Labor Organization (ILO), and Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) use them for economic, social, and food security analyses.[2021] This paper is a part of doctoral thesis with an ethics license from Tehran University of Medical Sciences with code IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.788 – 2021/10/18.

Using the sample size of the extracted data [Table 1], out of several sections of the survey, two sections were mainly analyzed: Food purchase (food expenses) and food consumption outside home. The number of households entitled to entering this study was determined after the first stage of data cleaning. In general, after applying the exclusion criteria, 665,254 were included in the study. We considered heads of household as representatives of all household members.

HEI calculation

The HEI is a valuable tool in the field of health and nutrition. It provides a measure of diet quality that aligns with the key recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines. The HEI-2015, which is the most current version, can be used to assess dietary intakes of populations ages 2 years and older. The HEI-2015 consists of 13 components that reflect the different food groups and key recommendations in the Dietary Guidelines.[25] These components include aspects related to adequacy (foods to eat more of for good health) and moderation (foods to limit for good health) [Table 2].

HEI–2015 components and scoring standards

To convert HIES data into food consumption data, we needed to make adjustments to the data. The scope and focus of data collected in the HIES pertain primarily to economic analysis and purchased food items, whereas the Household Food Consumption Survey (HFCS) centers on food consumed within the household. Upon applying the appropriate coefficients, these data are utilized as a proxy for consumption.

To convert price (Iranian Rial) to weight (gram) before 2019, we calculated the price of each product in every state back until 2010 using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) of 2019.[26] For some food items, it was necessary to apply more conversion factors to convert them into pure meat for consumption. The food items related to the purchased live livestock and poultry had to be converted into meat without skin, intestines, or viscera, which was used in the book “Food Yields summarized by different stages of preparation” published by the USDA.[27] For example, when a household bought a live sheep weighing 96 kg for consumption for a month, the whole sheep, along with the skin, was not cooked, but the carcass meat was separated and then cooked and consumed. Accordingly, the conversion factor of live livestock to intestines and carcasses for sheep is 0.49; that is, a 96 kg live sheep has about half the weight of a 47 kg meat carcass. This coefficient was extracted and applied to live livestock and poultry. The important point is that we removed the null codes from our data, such as wages.

To allocate the amount of food weight to each family member, based on age and gender, we used the Adult Male Equivalent (AME) method developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations (FAO).[28] Since most fruits with thick skin, such as pineapple, oranges, watermelon, and melons, are not consumed with the skin, the “edible portion” coefficient was applied. This coefficient was applied to poultry and light livestock meat, which are not consumed with bones but cooked lean meat separated from the bones and consumed on the table. The coefficients of edible portions were extracted from the guidebook for calculating the nutritional value of Iranian diets[29] and USDA food yields summarized by different stages of preparation based on different stages of preparation. It is necessary to emphasize that since these data may be different from the actual consumption data, we refer to it as “proxy intake”. In the next step, the cooking coefficient was applied to the values during the data extraction process. To estimate the food loss coefficient, the food conversion coefficient was applied to the value of each food item code based on reliable sources. FAO has determined coefficients for the countries of South and Southeast Asia in food production and consumption stages; these coefficients were applied to the analysis until reaching the net consumption amount.[30]

Before applying the micronutrient coefficients, we used the second part of the questionnaire which contains information about food consumption of households outside home during the past month, such as grilled cabbage. We extracted the price of each food press using data from the SCI for each state, the guidebook for calculating the nutritional value of Iranian diets, and other reliable sources. The components and the amount of each food press were converted into grams; first, the food cost was collected using the department data.

Using NUTRITIONIST IV and DFP1 v.1 software adapted to analyze of Iranian food compositions, the average amount of energy, PUFA2, MUFA3, Na, sugar, and saturated fatty acids were extracted for each food item code per 100 g; these micronutrients were calculated in the ratio of the final amount consumed. As mentioned earlier, some food codes were combined, and several food items were included in one code, for which we averaged the micronutrient and energy values as well as the serving amount. HEI-2015 is calculated for ages 24 and older.[3132] In the next step, each food item code belonging to the 13 food groups was determined; in other words, 13 components were determined by coding them in SQL Access.[25] Finally, the HEI was classified into three categories (<45: Poor, 45–72: Needs improvement, and >72: Good) for each state.[33]

Socioeconomic characteristics 1991–2020

9.44% in 1991 and 13.01% in 2020 were allocated by female heads of households, and an increasing trend was observed in the percentage of female householders from 1991 to 2020. The results revealed a decreasing trend in family size from 1991 to 2020. Its size decreased from 5.48 in 1991 to 3.52 in 2020. On average, the Iranian households lived in a house with an area of 51.67 ± 86.97 square meters. Energy is one of the most important factors in determining HEI, whose average per capita income over the past 30 years is shown in Figure 1. The highest average energy intake is related to 2005 (2695.33 ± 808.31), and the lowest is related to 2020 (2286.53 ± 748.25).

Trend of average of energy intake 1991–2020

The data analysis showed that the trend of energy intake reduction during the years 2005 to 2020 occurred with a high slope. On the other hand, the HEI illustrated that this energy was obtained from unhealthy food sources, especially in recent years. The same trend of energy consumption reduction is observed among different states, but it is more intense in Sistan and Baluchistan State.

Using the ARIMA model, we predict the HEI will decline until 2027 [Figure 2]. It suggests a concerning trend in dietary patterns, which may negatively affect health.

According to our analysis, energy intake decreased significantly between 2005 and 2020. In contrast, this decrease was primarily caused by a decrease in consumption of healthy foods, as evidenced by the declining HEI.

We used an ARIMA (2,0,1) model to forecast future trends in the HEI. In the long run, the model suggests that the downward trend in the HEI will continue, with only a slight recovery possible.

ARIMA’s autoregressive component indicates that the HEI’s past values significantly influence its future values. An increase of one unit in the HEI 2 years ago leads to a 1.96-unit increase in the current year, whereas a decrease of one unit in the HEI 2 years ago results in a 0.98-unit decrease.

However, it is important to note that the COVID-19 pandemic and economic shocks may have influenced the recent trends in the HEI. In future research, policy interventions could mitigate negative trends in dietary patterns by considering these factors.

Discussion

HEI is one of the methods for assessing the quality of a diet that indicates adherence to a healthy dietary pattern (in Iran, the food pyramid). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis of 30-year data to assess diet quality using the HEI in Iran. As presented in Table 3, some economic and social data demonstrate that the size of households was decreasing from 1991 to 2020; instead, households with a female head have been increasing during these years. We calculated the average energy intake per capita, 2482.24 ± 818.60 Kcal [Figure 1]. Another Iranian study estimated this average at 3212.08 ± 829.02 Kcal,[37] while the study of food consumption patterns of household calculated this at 2361 Kcal for the years 2000 to 2002. This difference can be in the method of extraction and method of analysis. The Iranian Parliament’s analysis of the SCI data for 2018, based on the criteria of the WHO and the MoHME4, reported this average 2097 and 2367 Kcal, respectively.[38] WHO guideline considers the needed per capita energy 2100 Kcal per day, while the MoHME recommendation is 2381 Kcal. The desirable estimated daily calorie in the Iranian Food Basket is 2573 Kcal.[39] The values obtained in our analysis are very close to the report of the parliament, the optimal food consumption basket, and the MoHME criteria. The current data and other country studies show that the trend of receiving energy has decreased in recent years. The effects of economic sanctions, climate change, and access to healthy food appear to influence the energy intake process.

The US Nutrition Guide of 2015 to 2020 estimated the HEI trend of the American population over 2 years of age for the years 2005 to 2016 at 56 points from 2005 to 2006 and 59 points from 2015 to 2016.[40] Our calculation revealed 47.05 and 51.86 points for 2005 and 2016, respectively, in Iran [Figures 2 and 3]. One possible reason for the higher HEI score in the US compared to Iran might be the implementation of the food facts label policy and economic condition,[41] which became mandatory in Iran since 2009. However, all US companies have been mandated to include nutritional facts since 1990.

The Golestan study, an ongoing longitudinal cohort study in Golestan state, north of Iran, calculated the HEI-2015 and AHEI5-2010 as 33 out of 100 and 37 out of 110 from 2003 to 2008, respectively.[42] The study of the food consumption pattern of Iranian households, 2001–2003, calculated the HEI-2015 at approximately 34 points out of 100.[18] We estimated the score of these years at 44 to 46 out of 100, which is close to the global values. A global study reported the AHEI score at 62 in Iran, which is above the global average (50 on average, 49.5 for men and 50.5 for women), as well as the scores in countries such as Turkey, 58 to 62; Egypt, 56 to 58; Saudi Arabia, 42 to 46; and Pakistan, 36 to 38. From 1991 to 2017, the average global AHEI score increased from 45.4 to 50.0 out of 100. According to this global study, diet quality varied significantly around the world. Mediterranean coastal countries, the Caribbean region, and East Asia (except China and Mongolia) had higher AHEI scores, while Central Asia, South Pacific, and Eastern and Northern Europe showed lower scores.[11] We calculated HEI at 44.90 and 52.36 points out of 100 for 1991 and 2017, respectively, which is very close to the global number. It is expected that active athletes who follow a nutritional program will have higher HEI. Nevertheless, a study conducted on Iranian athletes reported the average of this score at 65.04,[43] which is not satisfactory.

The HEI trend has been growing from 2000 to 2017 in Iran. The results of the time series analysis based on the ARIMA model show [Figure 2] that in the coming years, we will face a decrease in the HEI score. The HEI decline after 2017 might be due to various domestic and foreign events, that is, inflation, economic shocks caused by sanctions,[44] the COVID-19 pandemic,[45] and the Russia–Ukraine war.[46]

At the subnational level, Sistan and Baluchistan, Khorasan Razavi, Kermanshah, and Ilam were the states with a low HEI score, whereas Qom, Alborz, Bushehr, and Tehran states showed the highest HEI score in Iran, while most of bordering states showed unsuitable situation. Table 4 shows that only 1.6% of people are in a good HEI situation in Iran, while 38.69% are in unsatisfactory situation. According to a study conducted in 2013 and 2014 in Turkey, less than 2% of the population benefits from healthy nutrition.[47] We advocate enhancing efforts in all areas for HEI promotion, including healthy schools and improving nutritional literacy.

Economic inflation can lead to an increase in prices and a decrease in people’s purchasing power, which ultimately leads people to cheaper and lower-quality options with limited access. On the other hand, economic sanctions may lead to a lack of resources and a change in consumption patterns, and households turn to using alternative and less nutritious foods. It can be said that one of the most important effects of the COVID-19 pandemic was the widespread disruption in the food supply chain. The closure of places of sale due to the fear of the epidemic and quarantine restrictions may lead to a change in the consumption pattern of households and an increase in the consumption of processed foods.

Iran appears to be aligning its strategies for improving public health with global trends. The country has implemented a range of measures, including an operational plan for noncommunicable disease control, taxes on unhealthy foods (added sugars, trans fats), tax incentives for healthy foods (low-fat dairy), food labeling, a national knowledge-based food security document, imports of healthy foods, health education for various age groups, particularly students, restrictions on unhealthy food access in schools, and household subsidies. However, improvements in the economic situation and household livelihoods could have a significant impact on overall health outcomes.[34849]

In general, a predictive model should meet the following criteria: Theoretical plausibility, reliable parameter estimates, good fit to the data, accurate predictions, and simplicity (i.e., the model should have the fewest variables and the simplest equations possible). Fortunately, our study successfully met these criteria.

Limitations and strengths

The main limitation was that household income and expenditure data do not necessarily indicate actual consumption, which are considered as a proxy for dietary patterns.

Strengths: Since annual dietary pattern surveys are not feasible in low- and middle-income countries, household income and expenditure data can assist in identifying household consumption patterns using data transformation methods, extracting HEI from the country’s routine data and displaying its annual trend for the first time elaborate on the past, present, and future actions of food policies in Iran.

Policy implications

The degree of adherence to a healthy food pattern plays a significant role in advancing disease control and prevention policies, especially NCDs. Our findings can help policy makers, we envisage, make decisions in changing or modifying food recommendations and guidelines.

The results of this study and using HEI information enable policymakers to identify which food groups require enhancement and which should be diminished. This insight facilitates more informed decisions aimed at promoting healthy food consumption and curbing unhealthy dietary habits.

Conclusions

HEI is a useful tool for determining food quality using the degree of adherence to the food-based dietary guidelines (food pyramid). Many studies have investigated the relationship between HEI and the occurrence of diseases. Past evidence shows that improving HEI can be very effective in preventing premature deaths. In the context of Iran, the diet overall quality may decrease in the coming years. Our subnational analysis of HEI showed serious interprovincial variations, in favor of well-off states. To address the inequalities, we propose the following interventions to increase the HEI score: Consumption of healthier food items, for example, whole grains, vegetables, whole fruits, dark green vegetables, dairy products, legumes, seafood, and vegetable proteins; reducing salt consumption; and consuming less saturated fatty acids and sugar. In addition to socioeconomic status and income, food and nutritional literacy play a significant role in promoting healthy eating. We encourage relevant authorities to enhance adoption of appropriate educational policies, facilitating consumption of fruits and vegetables, and tailored financial policies, that is, sin taxes on added sugar and salt, to improve HEI in Iran.

At the subnational level, the marginal areas and bordering states showed a worse situation compared to the central states. Worse still, in states with nomad populations, vegetable intake was low and saturated fat intake was high, rendering more education, for example, appropriate food labeling with information related to nutritional facts. We did not measure physical activity in this study, which is often proposed as the main supplement to health along with nutritional recommendations to have a healthy body and a healthy society. Finally, although the household income-expenditure information may not accurately represent the actual consumption of the household, our findings, as a representative (Proxy) of the intake, can be used as a tool to foster agenda setting for healthy eating policies in Iran, and perhaps beyond.
