The non-linear steepest descent approach to the singular asymptotics of the sinh-Gordon reduction of the Painlevé III equation
Alexander R. Its, Kenta Miyahara, Maxim L. Yattselev

Abstract
. Motivated by the simplest case of tt*-Toda equations, we study the large and small x x asymptotics for x > 0 x>0 of real solutions of the sinh-Godron Painlevé III( D 6 D_{6} ) equation. These solutions are parametrized through the monodromy data of the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem. This unified approach provides connection formulae between the behavior at the origin and infinity of the considered solutions. Keywords : Painlevé III equation; sinh-Gordon equation; isomonodromic deformation; Riemann-Hilbert problem; steepest-descent method



1 Introduction

The tt* (topological—anti-topological fusion) equations were introduced by Cecotti and Vafa to describe certain deformations of super-symmetric quantum field theories
[
CV91
,
CV92
,
CV93
]
. Our interest lies in the special case known as tt*-Toda equations subject to the anti-symmetry and radial symmetry conditions
[
GL14
,
GIL15a
,
GIL15b
,
GIL20
]
. In the simplest case (tt*-Toda equation of type
A_{1}
) it becomes

(1.0.1)

\displaystyle(w_{0})_{rr}+\frac{(w_{0})_{r}}{r}=4\sinh(4w_{0}),

where
w_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
,
r\mapsto w_{0}(r)
, and
\mathbb{R}_{>0}:=(0,\infty)
. It is well known that the above equation is a special reduction of the Painlevé III(
D_{6}
) equation, given, in the canonical form, by

(1.0.2)

\displaystyle w_{xx}=\frac{w_{x}^{2}}{w}-\frac{w_{x}}{x}+\frac{\alpha w^{2}+\beta}{x}+\gamma w^{3}+\frac{\delta}{w},

where
w:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
,
x\mapsto w(x)
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
are some complex parameters such that
\gamma\delta\neq 0
. If we choose
\alpha=\beta=0
and
\gamma=-\delta=1/4
, then any of the four functions
w(x)=\pm e^{\pm u(x)/2}
solves (
1.0.2
) if
u(x)
is a solution of the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation

(1.0.3)

\displaystyle u_{xx}+\frac{u_{x}}{x}=\sinh u,

which is identical to (
1.0.1
) subject to a minor transformation
4w_{0}(r)=u(4r)
. A further transformation
u(x)=i\pi-iv(x)
brings (
1.0.3
) into the radial symmetric reduction of the elliptic sine-Gordon equation:

(1.0.4)

\displaystyle v_{xx}+\frac{v_{x}}{x}+\sin v=0,

which is usually called the sine-Gordon Painlevé III equation and plays an important role in the study of the 2D Ising model
[
BMW73
,
MTW77
]
.

Due to our interest in tt*-Toda equations, we study asymptotics (as
x\to+\infty
and
x\to 0^{+}
) of
real
solutions of (
1.0.3
). More precisely, recall that solutions of (
1.0.2
) can have non-polar singularities only at
0
and
\infty
while polar singularities in
\mathbb{C}^{*}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
do depend on the considered solution (this is the so-called Painlevé property). Hence, solutions of the sinh-Gordon reduction (
1.0.3
) of (
1.0.2
) can have movable logarithmic singularities. It also can be readily verified that solutions of (
1.0.2
) can only have simple poles. Thus, going around any logarithmic singularity (via the complex plane) of a solution of (
1.0.3
) will result in the addition of
2\pi i
to this solution. So, when we speak of real solutions, we ignore all the acquired additions of
2\pi i
(since (
1.0.3
) does not change when
u(x)
is replaced by
u(x)+2\pi in
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, thus obtained functions are still local solutions of (
1.0.3
)). Clearly, real solutions of (
1.0.3
) correspond to the real (on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
) solutions of (
1.0.2
).

Investigations of this type are not new. A lot of information on the history of the sine/sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation can be found in the monograph
[
FIKN06
]
. In particular, McCoy, Tracy, and Wu
[
MTW77
]
constructed a one-parameter family of solutions of (
1.0.2
) with
\alpha(-\delta)^{1/2}+\beta\gamma^{1/2}=0
that remain bounded for large
x
and described their behavior as
x\to+\infty
and
x\to 0^{+}
. These solutions are in fact smooth on the whole
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and are called
global solutions
, see
[
GIL15b
]
. Later, Novokshënov
[
Nov86
]
asymptotically identified the locations of singularities of non-smooth real solutions of (
1.0.3
) parametrized by their behavior at the origin:
u(x)=r\ln x+s+\mathcal{O}(x^{2-|r|})
for some
s,|r|<2
.

A substantial amount of work has been dedicated to describing asymptotics of the solutions of (
1.0.4
) similarly parametrized by their behavior at the origin via

(1.0.5)

v(x)=r\ln x+s+\mathcal{O}\big(x^{2-|\operatorname{Im}(r)|}\big),\quad x\to 0^{+}.

In
[
Nov84
,
Nov85
]
, Novokshënov studied asymptotics as
x\to+\infty
of the real solutions, which correspond to purely imaginary solutions of (
1.0.3
). In
[
Nov07
,
Nov08
]
, assuming
r,s\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\operatorname{Im}(r)|<2
, he described asymptotics of (
1.0.5
) for large
|x|
,
0<\arg(x)<\frac{\pi}{2}
, in terms of the Jacobi
\mathrm{sn}
function (when
r,s
are purely imaginary, this provides asymptotics of the real solutions of (
1.0.3
) in the first quadrant). Asymptotics as
x\to+\infty
of (
1.0.5
) for a range of parameters
r,s
including purely imaginary ones with
|\operatorname{Im}(r)|<2
, can be found in the introduction of
[
IP16
]
. Even though these asymptotics can be obtained from the work of Kitaev
[
Kit87
]
outlined in the next paragraph, they were re-derived by the authors using the Riemann-Hilbert approach employed in this paper. Let us point out that the small
x
asymptotics (
1.0.5
) does not describe all the behavior of solutions near
0^{+}
, see Theorem
2.2
further below, which is based on the work of Niles
[
Nil09
]
.

In
[
Kit87
]
, Kitaev used the isomonodromy theory and the WKB method to find the connection formulae for the small and large parameter asymptotics of the solutions of the degenerate Painlevé V equation, which bijectively corresponds to Painlevé III(
D_{6}
). These results then in principle allow one to extract the large and small
x
asymptotics of the solutions of (
1.0.3
) via certain non-trivial changes of variables. However, these transformations are rather complicated. More importantly, the approach taken in
[
Kit87
]
does not readily generalize to tackling the asymptotics of tt*-Toda equations of type
A_{n}
for general
n>1
, which is our overarching goal. On the other hand, the approach we take here of parametrizing solutions via the monodromy data of the associated linear system and then applying Deift-Zhou steepest descent techniques
[
DZ93
]
as
x\to+\infty
and techniques of Niles
[
Nil09
]
as
x\to 0^{+}
to the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem seem to be much more promising in this regard, see
[
GIK+23
]
.

2 Main Results

As standard in the theory of Painlevé transcendents, we parameterize the solutions by the monodromy data of the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem. Section
3
contains a detailed description of how this parameterization is achieved. At this point, it suffices to say that the monodromy data can be represented by a single parameter
p
that belongs to
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, where
\mathbb{D}
is the open unit disk centered at the origin. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

To each finite
p
,
|p|>1
, there corresponds one real solution of the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation (
1.0.3
) on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and

(2.0.1)

\displaystyle u(x)=2\,\ln\left(\frac{1-\sin\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1})}{\left|\cos\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1})\right|}\right),

as
x\to+\infty
, where the error term is uniform in
x
,

(2.0.2)

\kappa:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\big(|p|^{2}-1\big),\quad\text{and}\quad\phi:=-\kappa\ln 4+\arg\left(p\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\kappa\right)\right).

To
p=\infty
there corresponds a one-parameter family of real solutions of the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation (
1.0.3
) on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

(2.0.3)

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{2s^{\mathbb{R}}}{\pi}K_{0}(x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{x}}\right),

as
x\to+\infty
, where
s^{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathbb{R}
is the parametrizing (Stokes) parameter and
K_{0}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.

Remark
.

Recall, see
[
DLMF
, Equation (10.40.2)]
, that for large
x
it holds that

K_{0}(x)\sim\frac{e^{-x}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}}\frac{\Gamma^{2}(n+1/2)}{\Gamma(n+1)}\frac{1}{x^{n+1/2}}.

Remark
.

Formula (
2.0.1
) implies that if we label the singularities of
u(x)
by
x_{n}
in the increasing order, then for all natural numbers
n
large enough it holds that

(2.0.4)

x_{n}=\pi\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\kappa\ln(4\pi n)-\arg\left(p\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\kappa\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln n}{n}\right).

Remark
.

Denote by
\Xi_{\epsilon}
the complement of
\cup_{n}(x_{n}-\epsilon,x_{n}+\epsilon)
in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Then,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=2\,\ln\left(\frac{1-\sin\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)}{\left|\cos\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)\right|}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}(x^{-1})

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\frac{1-\sin\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)}{1+\sin\left(x-\kappa\ln x+\phi\right)}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}(x^{-1}),

where the error term is uniform on
\Xi_{\epsilon}
for all
x
large (i.e., locally uniformly in
\Xi_{0}
).

Remark
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the location of poles of
w
, the corresponding solution of (
1.0.2
), has been investigated in
[
Nov86
]
when
|\operatorname{Im}(p)|<1
. Novokshënov considered (
1.0.3
) with
\sinh u
replaced with
4\sinh u
, in which case the solution is given by
u(2x)
. Moreover, he was only interested in the singularities if
u
that lead to the poles of
w
(and not zeros), i.e., in
x_{2n}
. These points were denote by
a_{n}
in
[
Nov86
, Equation (0.9)]
(this formula has a typo,
\pi(n-\frac{1}{2})
needs to be replaced by
\pi(n+\frac{1}{4})
as evident from
[
Nov86
, Equations (1.1) and (4.9)]
). Then one can readily verify that
a_{n}
and
\frac{1}{2}x_{2n}
have exactly the same asymptotic behavior upon noticing that
r,s
in
[
Nov86
, Equation (0.2)]
are equal to
\gamma,\rho_{p}+\gamma\ln 2
, see Theorem
2.2
further below, and
A,B
from
[
Nov86
, Equation (0.8)]
can be expressed as

A=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1-\operatorname{Im}(p)^{2}}}\frac{\sqrt{|p^{2}|-1}}{\operatorname{Re}(p)+\sqrt{1-\operatorname{Im}(p)^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad AB=\frac{\pi^{2}}{1-\operatorname{Im}(p)^{2}}.

Due to
[
Nil09
]
, one can describe the asymptotic behavior near
0
of the real solutions of (
1.0.3
) using the above parameter
p
, which provides the connection formulae between asymptotics near infinity and the origin for each real solution.

Theorem 2.2
.

When
p=\infty
, the corresponding real solutions of the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation (
1.0.3
) parametrized by the Stokes parameter
s^{\mathbb{R}}
as in Theorem
2.1
admit the following asymptotic behavior on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
near the origin:

(2.0.5)

u(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\gamma\ln x+\rho_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\big(x^{2-|\gamma|}\big),&|s^{\mathbb{R}}|<2,\\
\displaystyle 2\sigma\ln\left[-\frac{x}{2}\left(\ln\frac{x}{8}+\delta_{\infty}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x}{\ln x}\right),&|s^{\mathbb{R}}|=2,\\
\displaystyle 2\sigma\ln\left|\frac{x}{4t}\sin\left(2t\ln\frac{x}{8}+\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right|+\mathcal{O}(x^{2}),&|s^{\mathbb{R}}|>2,\end{cases}

where the error terms hold as
x\to 0^{+}
and, in the third case, it is locally uniform in the complement of
\cup_{n\geq 0}8e^{-\frac{\pi n+\Delta_{\infty}}{2t}}
(asymptotic locations of the singularities),

\gamma:=-\frac{4}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\frac{s^{\mathbb{R}}}{2}\right),\quad t:=\frac{1}{\pi}\arccosh\left(\frac{|s^{\mathbb{R}}|}{2}\right),\quad\sigma:=-\mathrm{sgn}(s^{\mathbb{R}}),

\delta_{\infty}
is the Euler’s constant, and

\rho_{\infty}:=-3\gamma\ln 2+\ln\left[\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)}\right],\quad\Delta_{\infty}:=-2\arg\Gamma(1+it).

For finite
p
,
|p|>1
, the corresponding real solution of the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation (
1.0.3
) admits asymptotic behavior on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
near the origin as in (
2.0.5
), this time depending on the value of
s^{\mathbb{R}}:=-2\operatorname{Im}(p)
and with constants
\rho_{\infty},\delta_{\infty},\Delta_{\infty}
replaced by

\rho_{p}:=\rho_{\infty}+\ln\left[\frac{|p|^{2}-1}{q_{p}^{2}}\right],\quad\delta_{p}:=\delta_{\infty}+\frac{\sigma\pi}{2q_{p}},\quad\Delta_{p}:=\Delta_{\infty}+\sigma\arg(q_{p}),

where
\displaystyle q_{p}:=\operatorname{Re}(p)+\sqrt{1-\operatorname{Im}(p)^{2}}\in\{z:\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 0\}
.

Remark
.

As shown by
[
GIL15b
,
GIL23
]
, the case
p=\infty
and
s^{\mathbb{R}}\in[-2,2]
corresponds to the globally smooth solutions on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
show that these solutions admit exponentially decaying behavior (
2.0.3
) near
x=\infty
and logarithmic behavior near
x=0
as in the first two lines of (
2.0.5
).

Remark
.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
provide a connection between the behavior at infinity and around the origin along
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of the corresponding solutions Painlevé III equation (
1.0.2
), but only up to a sign since
w(x)=\pm e^{\pm u(x)/2}
and
u(x)/2
is defined up to addition of integer multiples of
\pi i
. However, the proofs of these theorems allow us to determine the sign uniquely, which we do in Appendix
A
.

Remark
.

Recent paper
[
Li24
, Section 4.1]
conjectures, based on numerical analysis, singular asymptotics near the origin of exponentially decaying near infinity solutions
w_{0}(r),w_{1}(r)
of the tt*-Toda equation of type
A_{3}
:

(2.0.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(w_{0})_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}(w_{0})_{r}=-2e^{2(w_{1}-w_{0})}+2e^{4w_{0}},\\
(w_{1})_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}(w_{1})_{r}=-2e^{-4w_{1}}+2e^{2(w_{1}-w_{0})},\end{cases}

where
w_{0},w_{1}
are real-valued functions of
r
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Clearly, a further assumption
w_{0}+w_{1}=0
reduces (
2.0.6

1.0.1

2.2

As standard in the theory of Painlevé transcendents, we view (
1.0.3
) as a compatibility condition of an overdetermined system of linear differential equations, a Lax pair. A Lax pair for the sinh-Gordon equation was first found by Flaschka-Newell
[
FN80
]
, see also
[
FIKN06
, Chpater 15]
(the Lax pairs in these references lead to the one used below after minor changes of variables). Let a matrix function
\Psi:\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
,
(\lambda,x)\mapsto\Psi(\lambda,x)
, be such that

(2.0.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{d\Psi}{d\lambda}=\left(\frac{ix^{2}}{16}\sigma_{3}+\frac{xu_{x}}{4\lambda}\sigma_{1}+\frac{i}{\lambda^{2}}Q\right)\Psi,\\
\frac{d\Psi}{dx}=\left(\frac{u_{x}}{2}\sigma_{1}+\frac{ix\lambda}{8}\sigma_{3}\right)\Psi,\end{cases}

where

(2.0.8)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh u&-\sinh u\\
\sinh u&-\cosh u\end{pmatrix}.

Then one can readily verify that the compatibility condition
\Psi_{\lambda x}=\Psi_{x\lambda}
is equivalent to the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation.

Remark
.

As pointed out in the introduction, when
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, equation (
1.0.3
) could be seen as the tt*-Toda equation of type
A_{1}
, which admits a Lax pair representation originally discovered by Mikhailov in
[
Mik81
]
, see also
[
GIL15b
, Equations (1.3)-(1.4)]
,

(2.0.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\Psi}_{\zeta}=\left(-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}W-\frac{r(w_{0})_{r}}{\zeta}\sigma_{3}+r^{2}W^{T}\right)\tilde{\Psi},\\
\tilde{\Psi}_{r}=\left(-(w_{0})_{r}\,\sigma_{3}+2r\zeta W^{T}\right)\tilde{\Psi},\end{cases}

where
\tilde{\Psi}
maps
(\zeta,r)\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to
\tilde{\Psi}(\zeta,r)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and

(2.0.10)

\displaystyle W=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-2w_{0}}\\
e^{2w_{0}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Under a change of variables

(2.0.11)

\displaystyle w_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{4}u(x),\quad r=\frac{1}{4}x,\quad\zeta=i\lambda,

the gauge transformation

(2.0.12)

\displaystyle\Psi(\lambda,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-w_{0}}&e^{w_{0}}\\
-e^{-w_{0}}&e^{w_{0}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\tilde{\Psi}(\zeta,\tilde{x})

defines a bijection between solutions of the Lax pair (
2.0.9
) for the tt*-Toda equation of type
A_{1}
and solutions of (
2.0.7
), the Lax pair for the sinh-Gordon equation.

Remark
.

Another important Lax pair for us is the Lax pair for the sine-Gordon equation (
1.0.4
) that can be found in
[
FIKN06
, Chapter 13]
and
[
Nil09
]
:

(2.0.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{d\Phi}{d\lambda}=\left(-\frac{ix^{2}}{16}\sigma_{3}-\frac{ixv_{x}}{4\lambda}\sigma_{1}+\frac{i}{\lambda^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos v&i\sin v\\
-i\sin v&-\cos v\end{pmatrix}\right)\Phi,\\
\frac{d\Phi}{dx}=\left(-\frac{ix\lambda}{8}\sigma_{3}-\frac{iv_{x}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)\Phi.\end{cases}

One can readily check that the change of variables
u(x)=i\pi-iv(x)
and gauge transformations
\Psi(\lambda)=i\sigma_{1}\Phi(\lambda)
or
\Psi(\lambda)=\sigma_{1}\Phi(\lambda)\sigma_{1}
provide correspondence between (
2.0.13
) and (
2.0.7
).

3 Proof of Theorem
2.2

In this section, we show how to formulate a Riemann-Hilbert problem for a solution of (
2.0.7
). Then we state an analogous Riemann-Hilbert problem for a solution of (
2.0.13
) and show how to connect the monodromy data between these two problems. Theorem
2.2
is then automatically obtained from
[
Nil09
, Theorems 7–9]
.

3.1  Direct Monodromy Problem for sinh-Gordon Equation

Consider the first equation of the Lax pair (
2.0.7
), that is,

(3.1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d\Psi}{d\lambda}=A(\lambda,x)\Psi,\quad A(\lambda,x):=\frac{ix^{2}}{16}\sigma_{3}+\frac{xu_{x}}{4\lambda}\sigma_{1}+\frac{i}{\lambda^{2}}Q,

where
Q
is given in (
2.0.8
). For future use observe that the coefficient matrix
A(\lambda,x)
satisfies the following relations:

•

anti-symmetry:
\sigma_{3}A(\lambda,x)\sigma_{3}=A^{T}(-\lambda,x)
;

•

inversion symmetry:
P_{0}\sigma_{3}A\big(-\frac{16}{\lambda x^{2}},x\big)\sigma_{3}P_{0}^{-1}=\frac{\lambda^{2}x^{2}}{16}A(\lambda,x)
;

•

cyclic symmetry:
\sigma_{1}A(\lambda,x)\sigma_{1}=-A(-\lambda,x)
;

•

reality:
\overline{A(\overline{\lambda},x)}=\sigma_{1}A(\lambda,x)\sigma_{1}
.

We note that equation (
3.1.1
) has two singular points
\lambda=0,\infty
, which are both irregular, and that the coefficients of
A(\lambda,x)
near the leading order singularities are diagonalizable. Indeed, near infinity (after changing to a local variable
\xi
, where
\lambda=1/\xi
) it is
-(ix^{2}/16)\sigma_{3}
, which is already diagonal, and near zero it is

(3.1.2)

\displaystyle iQ=P_{0}\,i\sigma_{3}\,P_{0}^{-1},\quad P_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\frac{u}{2}&\sinh\frac{u}{2}\\
\sinh\frac{u}{2}&\cosh\frac{u}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, see
[
FIKN06
, Prop. 1.1]
,
formal fundamental solutions
of (
3.1.1
) at
\lambda=0,\infty
must be of the form

(3.1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Psi_{f}^{(0)}(\lambda)=P_{0}(I+\mathcal{O}(\lambda))e^{-\frac{i}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}},\\
&\Psi_{f}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=(I+\mathcal{O}(1/\lambda))e^{\frac{ix^{2}\lambda}{16}\sigma_{3}}.\end{aligned}

Formal fundamental solutions (
3.1.3
) determine
canonical solutions
(genuine solutions that possess asymptotic behavior (
3.1.3
) in the corresponding Stokes sectors) near
\lambda=0,\infty
. This principle is explained in detail in Chapter 2 of
[
FIKN06
]
. We give only a brief list of facts here.

•

Stokes sectors at
\zeta=0
:

(3.1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Omega_{2k+1}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\,|\,-(2k+1)\pi<\arg\lambda<-(2k-1)\pi\right\},\\
&\Omega_{2k}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\,|\,-2k\pi<\arg\lambda<-2(k-1)\pi\right\}.\end{aligned}

•

Stokes sectors at
\zeta=\infty
:

(3.1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Omega_{2k+1}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\,|\,(2k-1)\pi<\arg\lambda<(2k+1)\pi\right\},\\
&\Omega_{2k}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\,|\,2(k-1)\pi<\arg\lambda<2k\pi\right\}.\end{aligned}

It is important for us to keep track of the argument of
\lambda
as we think of Stokes sectors as subsets of the universal covering of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Notice that
\Omega_{n}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\Omega_{n}^{(\infty)}(\lambda^{-1})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In each Stokes sector, there is a unique (canonical) solution that satisfies

(3.1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Psi_{n}^{(0)}(\lambda)\sim\Psi_{f}^{(0)}(\lambda),\quad\lambda\to 0,\quad\lambda\in\Omega_{n}^{(0)},\\
&\Psi_{n}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)\sim\Psi_{f}^{(\infty)}(\lambda),\quad\lambda\to\infty,\quad\lambda\in\Omega_{n}^{(\infty)}.\end{aligned}

Every solution of (
3.1.1
) and particularly all of the canonical solutions are analytically continued on the whole universal covering of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Hence, it holds that

(3.1.7)

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\Psi_{n-2}^{(0)}(\lambda e^{2\pi i}),\quad\Psi_{n-2}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\Psi_{n}^{(\infty)}(\lambda e^{2\pi i}),

where
\Psi(\lambda e^{2\pi i})
is the short-hand notation for the analytic continuation of
\Psi(\lambda)
to the same point
\lambda
along the path that winds once around the origin in the counter-clockwise direction.

Because any two solutions are connected via multiplication by a constant matrix on the right, there exists a connection matrix
E
such that

(3.1.8)

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\Psi_{1}^{(0)}(\lambda)E.

Using cyclic symmetry of
A(\lambda,x)
one can readily verify that
\sigma_{1}\Psi(-\lambda)\sigma_{1}
is a canonical solution if
\Psi(\lambda)
is a canonical solution. In particular, it holds that

(3.1.9)

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}^{(0)}(\lambda)=\sigma_{1}\Psi_{2}^{(0)}(\lambda e^{-i\pi})\sigma_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{1}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\sigma_{1}\Psi_{2}^{(\infty)}(\lambda e^{i\pi})\sigma_{1}.

Combining the above observations with (
3.1.8
), we get that

(3.1.10)

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}^{(\infty)}(\lambda)=\Psi_{2}^{(0)}(\lambda e^{-2\pi i})\sigma_{1}E\sigma_{1}.

We define Stokes matrices as the ones connecting adjacent canonical solutions:

(3.1.11)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(0,\infty)}=\left[\Psi_{n}^{(0,\infty)}(\lambda)\right]^{-1}\Psi_{n+1}^{(0,\infty)}(\lambda),

where
\lambda
belongs to the upper half-plane if
n
is odd and to the lower half-plane if
n
is even (again, these matrices are necessarily constant). Due to the analytic continuation property, it holds that

S_{2k+1}^{(0,\infty)}=S_{1}^{(0,\infty)}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2k}^{(0,\infty)}=S_{2}^{(0,\infty)}.

Taking
\lambda\to 0,\infty
in (
3.1.11
) for
\lambda
in the appropriate Stokes sector and using the series expression of each canonical solution (
3.1.3
) at those singularities, one can readily establish triangularity of the Stokes matrices and the fact that they have 1’s on the main diagonal. Moreover, by using the symmetries of
A(\lambda,x)
as we did before (
3.1.10
), one gets that Stokes matrices must satisfy

•

anti-symmetry:
S_{1}^{(\infty)}=\sigma_{3}\left[S_{2}^{(\infty)}\right]^{T-1}\sigma_{3}
;

•

inversion symmetry:
S_{n}^{(0)}=\sigma_{3}S_{n+1}^{(\infty)}\sigma_{3},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}
;

•

cyclic symmetry:
S_{1}^{(\infty)}=\sigma_{1}S_{2}^{(\infty)}\sigma_{1}
;

•

reality:
\overline{S_{1}^{(\infty)}}=\sigma_{1}\left[S_{2}^{(\infty)}\right]^{-1}\sigma_{1}
;

and therefore they depend on a single real parameter (Stokes parameter)
s^{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathbb{R}
:

(3.1.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &S^{(\infty)}_{1}=\left[S_{2}^{(0)}\right]^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad S^{(\infty)}_{2}=\left[S_{1}^{(0)}\right]^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
a&1\end{pmatrix},\\
&a=is^{\mathbb{R}}\in i\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}

Using the same symmetries once more, one also gets that

(3.1.13)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}E\sigma_{1}=S_{2}^{(0)}ES_{1}^{(\infty)}=\overline{E}.

Because
\det E=1
, relations (
3.1.13
) show that

(3.1.14)

\displaystyle E=\begin{pmatrix}A&B^{\mathbb{R}}\\
B^{\mathbb{R}}&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix},\quad|A|^{2}=1+(B^{\mathbb{R}})^{2},\quad A-\overline{A}=aB^{\mathbb{R}},\quad B^{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathbb{R}.

If we write
a=is^{\mathbb{R}}
and
A=x^{\mathbb{R}}+iy^{\mathbb{R}}
where
x^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathbb{R}
, expression (
3.1.14
) implies that
E
is parametrized by triples
(x^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},B^{\mathbb{R}})
such that

(3.1.15)

\displaystyle(x^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}+\left(\frac{(s^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}}{4}-1\right)(B^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}=1,

see Figure
1
. We shall call the set of the above triples the monodromy surface

(3.1.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\left\{m\equiv(x^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},B^{\mathbb{R}})\,|\,\text{$m$ satisfies }\eqref{monodromy surface}\right\}.

We refer those who are interested in the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
in detail to the monograph
[
GH17
]
.

Figure 1
.
Monodromy surface
\mathcal{M}
for the sinh-Gordon Painlevé III equation.

What we have shown is that one can always associate the monodromy data
m\in\mathcal{M}
to each solution
u(x)
of (
1.0.3
).

Another way to parameterize the monodromy data is as follows. Let
p\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
\iota\in\{\pm 1\}
. Set

(3.1.17)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle s^{\mathbb{R}}=-2\operatorname{Im}(p),\quad B^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{\iota}{\sqrt{|p|^{2}-1}},\quad A=\overline{p}B^{\mathbb{R}},&p\neq\infty,\\
s^{\mathbb{R}}\text{ is any},\quad B^{\mathbb{R}}=0,\quad A=\iota,&p=\infty.\end{cases}

It can be readily verified that conditions (
3.1.14
) and (
3.1.15
) are indeed fulfilled. Conversely, given a triple
(x^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},B^{\mathbb{R}})
, we can set
p=\overline{A}/B^{\mathbb{R}}
,
\iota=\sign(B^{\mathbb{R}})
when
B^{\mathbb{R}}
is non-zero and
p=\infty
,
\iota=A
otherwise.

3.2  Riemann-Hilbert Problem for sinh-Gordon Equation

Riemann-Hilbert problems arise when one considers the inverse monodromy map

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\ni m\mapsto u(x,m).

Given any
\rho>0
, define a sectionally holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
matrix function

(3.2.1)

\begin{cases}\Psi_{1}^{(0)}(\lambda),&\arg(\lambda)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}),~~0<|\lambda|<\rho,\\
\Psi_{2}^{(0)}(\lambda),&\arg(\lambda)\in(-\frac{3\pi}{2},-\frac{\pi}{2}),~~0<|\lambda|<\rho,\\
\Psi_{1}^{(\infty)}(\lambda),&\arg(\lambda)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}),~~|\lambda|>\rho,\\
\Psi_{2}^{(\infty)}(\lambda),&\arg(\lambda)\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}),~~|\lambda|>\rho.\end{cases}

It readily follows from (
3.1.8
)–(
3.1.11
) that (
3.2.1
) has constant jumps on the circle

and the imaginary axis as specified on Figure

(here, one needs to recall (
3.1.7
) to find the jumps on
(0,i\rho)
and
(-i\infty,-i\rho)
) as well as asymptotic behavior at

given by the formal solutions
\Psi_{f}^{(0,\infty)}
. By construction, the jump matrices depend only on the monodromy data.

Conversely, let
m\in\mathcal{M}
be monodromy data and
S_{1,2}^{(0,\infty)}
and
E
be as in (
3.1.12
) and (

) corresponding to

. Consider the following Riemann-Hilbert problem.

Riemann-Hilbert Problem 1
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix function
\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)
such that

(1)

sectional analyticity
:
\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)
is analytic for

, where the oriented contour
\Gamma
is depicted in Figure
2
;

(2)

jump condition
: one-sides traces
\hat{\Psi}_{\pm}(\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{\pm}(\lambda):=\lim_{\{\pm\text{ side of }\Gamma\}\ni\lambda^{\prime}\to\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\Psi}(\lambda^{\prime}),

where a subscript

(resp.

) refers to a limit to the oriented contour from its left (resp. right) side,
exists a.e. on
\Gamma
, belong to

, and satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{+}(\lambda)=\hat{\Psi}_{-}(\lambda)G_{\hat{\Psi}},\quad\lambda\in\Gamma,

where the jump matrices

on
\Gamma
are as on Figure

;

(3)

normalization conditions
: it holds that

(3.2.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}(I+\mathcal{O}(1/\lambda))e^{\frac{ix^{2}\lambda}{16}\sigma_{3}}&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to\infty,\\
P_{0}(I+\mathcal{O}(\lambda))e^{-\frac{i}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0.\end{cases}

.

and the jump matrices

for RHP

.

It is a non-trivial but standard result in integrable systems, see for example
[

, Theorem 5.4]
and
[

]
, that the solution of the above problem exists and is meromorphic in

, determines solutions of (

) via (

), and allows one to recover the corresponding solution of (

) via asymptotics around the origin. The proof of Theorem
2.1
proceeds by asymptotically analyzing RHP

for large

.

Remark
.

The choice of
\iota
, see (

), does not affect real solutions of (

). Indeed, let

be the solution of (

) corresponding to some

and

and
\hat{\Psi}
be the corresponding solution of RHP

. Define

One can readily see that

satisfies RHP

3.3  Riemann-Hilbert Problem for sine-Gordon Equation

The direct monodromy problem for the sine-Gordon equation is well known, see for example
[
FIKN06
, Chapter 13]
or
[
Nil09
]
, and can be deduced in the same way as above. Following
[

]
, the monodromy data for all (not necessarily real) solutions of (

\varsigma&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2,\sin}^{(\infty)}=S_{1,\sin}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\varsigma\\

as well as the connection matrix

E_{\sin}=\pm i\sigma_{1}\quad\text{or}\quad E_{\sin}=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{1+p_{\sin}q_{\sin}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&p_{\sin}\\
-q_{\sin}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{cases}1+p_{\sin}q_{\sin}\neq 0,\\
\varsigma=p_{\sin}+q_{\sin},\end{cases}

where the first case is known as
special case
and the second one as the
general case
. Again, exactly as in the case of the sinh-Gordon equation, the inverse monodromy problem relies on the following Riemann-Hilbert problem.

Riemann-Hilbert Problem 2
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix function
\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)
such that

(1)

\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)
is analytic for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is the same contour as in RHP
1
;

(2)

one-sides traces
\hat{\Phi}_{\pm}(\lambda)
exists a.e. on
\Gamma
, belong to
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, and satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{+}(\lambda)=\hat{\Phi}_{-}(\lambda)G_{\hat{\Phi}},\quad\lambda\in\Gamma,

where
G_{\hat{\Phi}}
on
\Gamma
is as on Figure
3
and in (
3.3.1
) and (
3.3.2
);

(3)

it holds that

(3.3.3)

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}(I+\mathcal{O}(1/\lambda))e^{\frac{-ix^{2}\lambda}{16}\sigma_{3}}&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to\infty,\\
P_{\sin}(I+\mathcal{O}(\lambda))e^{-\frac{i}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0,\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle P_{\sin}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{v}{2}&-i\sin\frac{v}{2}\\
-i\sin\frac{v}{2}&\cos\frac{v}{2}\end{pmatrix}
.

As usual, (
3.3.3
) describes asymptotics of the formal fundamental solutions of the first equation in (
2.0.13
) at
\lambda=0,\infty
.

Figure 3
.
The jump matrices
G_{\hat{\Phi}}
for RHP

Recall that Lax pair (
2.0.13
) can be transformed into (
2.0.7
) by setting either
\Psi(\lambda)=i\sigma_{1}\Phi(\lambda)
or
\Psi(\lambda)=\sigma_{1}\Phi(\lambda)\sigma_{1}
and making a change of variables

. Notice that the first gauge transformation takes the formal fundamental solution of (

) around

into the formal fundamental solutions of (

) around

while the second one provides correspondence between the formal fundamental solutions at

. Therefore, RHP
2
transforms into RHP
1
upon setting

(3.3.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{1}\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)\sigma_{1},&|\lambda|>\rho,\\
i\sigma_{1}\hat{\Phi}(\lambda),&|\lambda|<\rho.\end{cases}

The above transformation induces the following relation between monodromy data:

\begin{cases}S_{1}^{(\infty)}=\sigma_{1}S_{1,\sin}^{(\infty)}\sigma_{1},\quad S_{2}^{(\infty)}=\sigma_{1}S_{2,\sin}^{(\infty)}\sigma_{1},\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
S_{2}^{(0)}=S_{1,\sin}^{(0)-1},\quad S_{1}^{(0)}=S_{2,\sin}^{(0)-1},\quad E=-iE_{\sin}\sigma_{1}.\end{cases}

Using
(\iota,p)
parametrization (
3.1.17
) of the monodromy data for sinh-Gordon equation, we get that

corresponds to the special case with

and
1<|p|<\infty
corresponds to the general case with

and

. In both cases

corresponds to the choice of sign in (

) and we interpret

as

that cannot be parametrized by

of sinh-Gordon equations.

The results of Theorem
2.2
now follow from
[
Nil09
, Theorems 7-9]
and the above correspondence for the monodromy data, see also Appendix
A
.

4 Proof of Theorem
2.1

As indicated in the introduction, we shall use the nonlinear steepest descent method of Deift and Zhou
[
DZ93
]
to prove the desired asymptotics. In Section
4.1
we review the case
B^{\mathbb{R}}=0
that includes the McCoy-Tracy-Wu one-parameter family of smooth solutions (this proof has already been outlined in Niles’ thesis
[
Nil09
, Apendix D]
). Then, in Section
4.2
, we consider the case
B^{\mathbb{R}}\neq 0
.

4.1  Case 1:
B^{\mathbb{R}}=0

When
B^{\mathbb{R}}=0
, it readily follows from (
3.1.15
) that
A=\pm 1
. As we pointed out in the remark of Section
3.2
, it is enough to consider the case
A=1
only. Then, the jump matrices on the arcs of
S^{1}_{\rho}
become equal to the identity matrix. That is,
\hat{\Psi}
has the discontinuity only along the imaginary line. Let

(4.1.1)

\displaystyle Y(\lambda):=\hat{\Psi}\left(\frac{4\lambda}{x}\right)e^{-x\theta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}},\quad\theta(\lambda):=\frac{i}{4}\left(\lambda-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right).

Then
Y(\lambda)
is the solution of the following Riemann-Hilbert problem:

Riemann-Hilbert Problem 3
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix function
Y(\lambda)
such that

(1)

Y(\lambda)
is analytic for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma_{Y}
, where
\Gamma_{Y}
is the imaginary line oriented as on Figure
4
;

(2)

one-sides traces
Y_{\pm}(\lambda)
exists a.e. on
\Gamma_{Y}
, belong to
L^{2}(\Gamma_{Y})
, and satisfy

\displaystyle Y_{+}(\lambda)=Y_{-}(\lambda)G_{Y},\quad\lambda\in\Gamma_{Y},

where the jump matrices
G_{Y}
are given by

(4.1.2)

\displaystyle G_{Y}=e^{x\theta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}}\,G_{\hat{\Psi}}\,e^{-x\theta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}},\quad\lambda\in\Gamma_{Y};

(3)

it holds that

\displaystyle Y(\lambda)=\begin{cases}I+\mathcal{O}(1/\lambda)&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to\infty,\\
P_{0}(I+\mathcal{O}(\lambda))&\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0,\end{cases}

where
P_{0}
was defined in (
3.1.2
).

Figure 4
.
The jump of
Y
on the imaginary line
\Gamma_{Y}
.

Note that
2\theta(\lambda)<-1
when
\lambda\in\Gamma_{Y}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)>0
, and
-2\theta(\lambda)<-1
when
\lambda\in\Gamma_{Y}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)<0
. Thus, both jump matrices comprising
G_{Y}
exponentially decay to the identity matrix
I
. In fact, one can show that

(4.1.3)

\displaystyle\|G_{Y}-I\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{Y})\cap L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{Y})}\leq C\,\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{1/4}},

where
C
is some constant. Hence, by the small norm theorem, see for example
[
FIKN06
, Theorem 8.1]
, there exists a unique solution of RHP
3
for all
x
large and

(4.1.4)

\displaystyle Y(\lambda)=I+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{Y}}\frac{\rho(\lambda^{\prime})(G_{Y}(\lambda^{\prime})-I)}{\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime},\quad\lambda\notin\Gamma_{Y},

where
\displaystyle\rho(\lambda^{\prime})=Y_{-}(\lambda^{\prime})
for
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{Y}
(hence,
\rho(\lambda^{\prime})
is the solution of the singular integral equation obtained by taking the traces of both sides of (

) on the left-hand side of

). The small norm theorem further implies that

\displaystyle\|\rho-I\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{Y})}\leq\tilde{C}\,\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{1/4}}

for some constant

. Observe that

vanishes exponentially at the origin. Hence,
Y_{+}(0)=Y_{-}(0)=:Y(0)
and one has that

(4.1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Y(0)&=I+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{Y}}\frac{G_{Y}(\lambda^{\prime})-I}{\lambda^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{Y}}\frac{(\rho(\lambda^{\prime})-I)(G_{Y}(\lambda^{\prime})-I)}{\lambda^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime}\\
&=I+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{Y}}\frac{G_{Y}(\lambda^{\prime})-I}{\lambda^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{x}}\right),\end{aligned}

by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
4.1.3
), and (
4.1.5
). Recall that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x}{2}(y+\frac{1}{y})}\frac{dy}{y}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x\cosh s}ds=\frac{1}{\pi}K_{0}(x),

see
[
DLMF
, Equation (10.32.9)]
. Then, (
4.1.6
) becomes

(4.1.7)

\displaystyle Y(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{\pi}s^{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(x)\\
\frac{1}{\pi}s^{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(x)&1\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{x}}\right).

On the other hand, it immediately follows from RHP
3
(3) and (
3.1.2
) that

(4.1.8)

\displaystyle Y(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\frac{u}{2}&\sinh\frac{u}{2}\\
\sinh\frac{u}{2}&\cosh\frac{u}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

By comparing (
4.1.7
) with (
4.1.8
), we get that

as

to deduce the leading order behavior of the logarithm.
This finishes the proof of Theorem
