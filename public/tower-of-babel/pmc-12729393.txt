Typical atrial flutter with atypical electrocardiogram morphology: electrophysiology and atrial anatomic characteristics
Authors: Jian-du Yang, Qi Sun, Gong-bu Zhou, Xu Liu, Bin Luo, Hai-yang Xie, Xiao-gang Guo, Jian Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of this study is to analyze the relationship between atypical ECG patterns in typical atrial flutter (AFL) and cardiac structure, as well as sinus rhythm P-wave parameters.Materials and methods389 consecutive patients diagnosed with typical AFL who were treated at our center were included. The morphology of the flutter wave was assessed in each lead. Counterclockwise AFL could be classified into three typical groups and two kinds of atypical groups based on the flutter ECG patterns. Sinus rhythm P-wave parameters, including PtfV1 ( P Wave Terminal Force of V1 ), P-wave area, axis, and interatrial block (IAB), etc. were measured manually. The analysis focused on examining the correlation between flutter wave and atrial structure characteristics, as well as the influence of prior cardiac surgery. Furthermore, an analysis was conducted on the correlation between P-wave parameters and F-wave morphology.ResultsThe size of the atrium and previous cardiac surgery have an impact on the AFL ECG pattern. P-wave amplitude in lead II (p < 0.0001), lead III (p < 0.0001), V1 terminal interval (p = 0.01), and P-wave area (p < 0.0001) exhibited significant differences between the typical and atypical groups. Furthermore, the SR P-wave axis and the presence of IAB may also be associated with an atypical AFL pattern.ConclusionTypical AFL may exhibit various ECG patterns and two new patterns were found. SR P-wave, such as IAB and P wave amplitude, duration, etc. has the potential to predict atypical ECG patterns in typical AFL. The size of the right atrium significantly influence the characteristic ECG patterns observed in typical AFL.Keywords: Atrial flutter, Atypical ECG pattern, Anatomic characteristics, P wave parameters

Background

Cavotricuspid isthmus dependent right atrial flutter (AFL) has been well described and are the most common macro-reentrant atrial arrhythmias [1]. Reentry may occur in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction around the tricuspid annulus. “Typical” flutter wave (F-wave) morphologies on 12-lead ECG have been described for counterclockwise (CCW) cavotricuspid isthmus-dependent AFL: CCW F-waves are described as having a “sawtooth” pattern with predominantly negative deflections in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF) and in lead V6 and positive deflections in lead V1 [2]. Although there are certain variances in F-wave morphology among CCW flutters, some specific patterns were not well described. The relationship between the ECG patterns of CCW AFL and the characteristics of the cardiac structure is still not well understood. Additionally, there isn’t much information on the correlation between sinus rhythm P wave characteristics and flutter ECG.

Methods

Study population

We screened 389 consecutive patients with cavo-tricuspid isthmus-dependent atrial flutter who were treated with radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) in our center from May 2013 to January 2020. All patients underwent EP study because of symptomatic spontaneous tachycardias.

Among all included patients, 89 had undergone prior heart surgery. Before the procedure, each patient underwent a clinical evaluation including an electrocardiogram (ECG), an echocardiogram, and laboratory testing. All patients provided written informed consent before the procedure. The research protocol used in this study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Fuwai Hospital.

Class I and III AADs were withheld for at least five half-lives, except for amiodarone, which was discontinued at least two weeks before the procedure.

Electrocardiogram study

All patients had 12-lead ECG recorded during AFL tachycardia and sinus rhythm in the electrophysiology laboratory. The sinus rhythm electrocardiogram (ECG) was acquired after successful ablation when patients had converted to stable sinus rhythm. Electrocardiograms were recorded at a standard paper speed of 25 mm/s and a gain of 10 mm/mV. When 2:1 atrioventricular conduction was present at baseline, ECGs during AFL and sinus rhythm were examined by 2 independent observers. Any discrepancy was resolved by consensus with the third observer. P-wave parameters were measured manually on the digital multichannel system. P-wave duration was measured from its earliest onset to the latest offset in any of the recorded leads. PtfV1 (P Wave Terminal Force of V1) was calculated by multiplying the duration of the terminal force. A ptfV1 of >0.04 mm×s was considered abnormal [2]. P-wave area was calculated in lead II using this formula: P-wave area = 1⁄2 P-wave duration × P-wave voltage [3]. The P-wave axis was measured in the frontal plane. A normal P-wave axis was between 0°and + 75°. If the P-wave axis cannot be calculated due to the biphsic morphology of the P wave in leads II, III, and aVF, the axis is considered abnormal.

Flutter-wave morphology in each lead was evaluated. The “isoelectric” interval between F-waves was defined as the interval with a slope of 30° in relation to the horizontal plane. Monophasic F-waves were classified as either “F (+)” or “F (-)” based on their polarity. Biphasic F-waves were categorized according to their initial and terminal components. The major component was termed “F” and the minor component “f” with “+” or “-” assigned to each constituent according to their respective polarities. When the positive and negative components of the F-wave were of equal magnitude, the F-wave was referred to as isoelectric.

In individuals presenting with counter-clockwise typical AFL, the flutter waves observed in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF) exhibit a “sawtooth” pattern and are upright in lead V1. Counterclockwise isthmus-dependent flutters can be categorized into three typical groups based on the morphology of F-waves in these leads [4]: (1) F(-) inferiorly, F(+) in V1; (2) F(-)/f(+) inferiorly, F(+) in V1; (3) f(-)/F(+) inferiorly, F(+) in V1 (Fig. 1). Any different ECG morphology was classified as atypical ECG morphology. This included two specific patterns: (1) F(-) inferiorly with an isoelectric interval between the negative component and F(+) in V1 (Atypical pattern 1, Fig. 1); (2) f(-)/F(+) in V1, with typical patterns observed in the inferior leads (Atypical pattern 2, Fig. 1). Interatrial conduction block, as defined in the consensus report sponsored by the International Society for Holter and Noninvasive Electrocardiology [5]: Partial Advanced interatrial block (Partial-IAB), manifests as a P-wave duration >120 ms on the surface electrocardiogram. Advanced-IAB is defined as a P-wave duration >120 ms with biphasic P-wave configuration in the inferior limb leads (II, III, and aVF).

Echocardiography study

Before the procedure, all patients underwent both transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). On TTE, the measurement of left or right atrial diameter was conducted using the four-chamber apical view during the systolic phase. On TEE, the presence of thrombus in the LA and left atrial appendage (LAA) was excluded. Our echocardiography examination protocol adhered to standard techniques, which involved averaging measurements from a minimum of three cardiac cycles. Subsequently, these measurements were reviewed by two echocardiologists. The normal value of echocardiography measurements in the Chinese population refers to the consensus statements endorsed by the Beijing Task Force on Echocardiography. Enlargement of the LA/RA was qualitatively measured using TTE [6]: LA dimension >3.5 cm in the long-axis view, while RA enlargement was defined as a vertical dimension >4.9 cm and/or a transverse diameter >4.4 cm in the four-chamber view, and/or RA area >16.7cm2.

Electrophysiology study

A decapolar catheter was positioned in the coronary sinus (CS) via the right internal jugular vein. Data was recorded simultaneously by a digital multichannel system (LabSystem PRO, Bard Electrophysiology, Lowell, MA, USA). Bipolar signals were filtered at 30 to 500 Hz, and unipolar signals were filtered at 0.05 to 500 Hz.

Electroanatomic mapping was performed using the Carto 3 systems (Carto, Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA). Entrainment mapping from multiple sites was performed to delineate the mechanisms and the critical isthmuses for AFL. In all cases, isthmus dependence was proven by the demonstration of concealed entrainment while pacing from the cavotricuspid isthmus during flutter with a post-pacing interval within 20 ms of the flutter cycle length. If AFL was interrupted during entrainment, programmed stimulation was performed to re-induce the AFL.

Radiofrequency ablation

Ablation lesions were performed with radiofrequency current in a power control mode (30–40 W at the CTI line, 45˚C maximum temperature with irrigation rate of 17–30 mL/min) using a Navistar/Smart Touch catheter (Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA, USA). RFCA at each site was continued for a minimum of 30 s and a maximum of 1 min unless a sharp impedance drop was observed. If AFL terminated during RF application, validation of the line of block was performed during atrial pacing and additional RFCA was performed in any identified gaps. Subsequently, bidirectional block was confirmed before and after a 10–15 min observation period.

Ablation was performed in the region of the cavotricuspid isthmus and was considered successful when flutter terminated and was not reinitiated and/or when bidirectional block was demonstrated.

Reinduction was then attempted using atrial pacing maneuvers. Acute procedural success was defined as noninducibility at the end of the case with the confirmation of a bidirectional block across all lines. The CTI block was demonstrated by mapping the atrial activation during pacing. If the activation of the right atrial inferolateral free wall was in a cranial to caudal direction with CS proximal pacing and the activation of the right atrial septum also occurred in a cranial to caudal direction during lateral right atrial pacing, it was considered a bidirectional block. After the successful acute procedures, AADs were typically discontinued.

Follow-up

After successful ablation, all patients underwent clinical follow-up with 24 h Holter monitoring at 3 and 12 months, and annually thereafter. Patients were also instructed to undergo ECG tests upon symptoms. Additionally, telephone interviews were conducted in some patients.

Statistical analysis

Values are given as mean ± SD. All continuous parameters were assessed using Student’s t-study or Mann-Whitney test and all categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analyzed using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered significance differences. SPSS, version 22.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Patients characteristics

The entire study cohort consisted of 389 patients (196 (50.38%) male, mean age 54.61 ± 13.1 yo). Analysis of the ECGs revealed that differentiation between typical and atypical ECG patterns could be made based on F-wave morphology in leads II, III, aVF and V1 in 100% of cases (confirmed by review of intracardiac tracings). The most common reason for open-heart surgery was ASD in congenital heart disease and valve disease in acquired heart disease (AHD). Prevalence and classification of typical and atypical AFL ECG patterns in typical AFL

As normally observed, typical ECG patterns were the most common ones in this cohort (317 of 389, 81.49%). Among the 317 typical ECG pattern, 142 (44.80%) were categorized as type 1, 118 (37.22%) as type 2, and the remaining 57 (17.98%) as type 3. Atypical AFL ECG patterns in typical AFL was not rare in this cohort, which was presented in 71 (18.41%) out of the total 389 patients. Forty-two patients showed atypical type 1, accounting for 10.74% of the total population; the remaining 27 showed atypical type 2 with biphasic in lead V1, accounting for 6.90% of the total number (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Structural characteristics and ECG patterns

All the patients performed TTE before EP procedures, and RA and LA parameters were measured by an experienced radiologist. Fig. 4 shows the proportion of RA and LA enlargement in the typical ECG patterns and atypical ECG patterns in typical atrial flutter. There was no obvious difference in LA enlargement between the typical subgroups (38.8%) and the atypical subgroups (atypical type 1 with 48.94%, p = 0.19; atypical type 2 with 52.00%, p = 0.19). Interesting but not unexpected, there was a statistical difference in RA size between the typical pattern group and the atypical ones, among which 16 patients (34.04%) in the type 1 atypical atrial flutter group had enlargement of the RA vs. 18.61% in typical group (p = 0.02). Similarly, the proportion of RA enlargement was higher in the atypical type 2 group than in the typical groups(44.0% vs. 18.61%, p = 0.0025).

Comparisons between the typical groups and the atypical groups in terms of right atrial (RA) and left atrial (LA) size

Not only atrium size could affect the ECG pattern, prior cardiac surgery also had great impact on the ECG morphology. Among the patients diagnosed with atypical type 1, a total of 17 patients (36.17%), had received cardiac surgery, similarly, a total of 18 patients (72%) had received cardiac surgery in atypical type 2 group. The proportion of cardiac surgery in the typical group (54 out of 317, 17.03%) was found to be significantly lower than atypical type 1 (p = 0.002) and atypical type 2 (p < 0.0001).

Different AFL ECG patterns

Similar to previous studies, we have identified four different types of AFL ECG morphology in typical AFL [4, 7]. In addition to these, an additional type of AFL morphology was observed. This particular morphology exhibited a small positive but predominant negative F-wave in the V1 lead. This ECG finding can assist clinicians in preoperative evaluation and provide guidance for electrophysiological mapping and ablation procedures.

P-wave parameters and AFL ECG patterns

As the best of our knowledge, our study was the first one to investigate the correlation between SR P-wave parameters and typical AFL patterns. P-wave parameters are to a large extent defined by the trajectory of the atrial depolarization wave takes after leaving the sinus node. It has been demonstrated the changes in surface P wave convey significant information about the cardiovascular disease. The parameters, such as P-wave duration, interatrial block (IAB), P-wave terminal force in V1, P-wave axis, P-wave voltage, and P-wave area reflect the anatomic changes, including interatrial pathway anatomy [8] and left atrial enlargement [9], etc.

As previous studies, prolonged total atrial activation time serves as an indirect indicator of atrial fibrosis [10]. Fibrosis has been identified as a factor that reduces conduction [11], resulting in a smaller and prolonged signal on the ECG [12]. A small P-area index may, therefore, be regarded as a potential indicator of atrial fibrosis. The amplitude of lead II/III was found to be lower and the P-area was smaller in patients with atypical type 1 group compared to those in the typical group. This difference in amplitude and P-area may be caused by atrial fibrosis, which can alter the conduction sequence of atrial flutter excitation and subsequently result in distinct changes in the electrocardiogram of atrial flutter.

Similarly, the axis of the P-wave and IAB can serve as valuable indicators of abnormal atrial conduction caused by structural alterations. These abnormalities can potentially result in variant ECG patterns during AFL. This could explain the potential rationale behind the disparity in IAB and P-wave axis between typical and atypical ECG patterns.

The relationship between SR P-wave parameters and AFL ECG changes could also be demonstrated by 3D activation mapping and endocardial recordings. The isoelectric F-wave in the inferior leads was mainly generated by the posterior inferior wall of the right atrium (Fig. 5A-C). And the low voltage area in the inferior wall would produce isoelectric interval between F wave (Fig. 5D). As it shown, the extensive scar on the right atrium would result in a low amplitude in inferior leads. Moreover, the patients with IAB during SR and presented with atypical pattern 2 could also be demonstrated by activation map. As shown in Fig. 5E, the negative component of V1 during AFL was generated by the RA lateral wall in an anterior-posterior direction. In the voltage map, it is clearly shown the surgery has resulted in the creation of a large low voltage area in the lateral wall (Fig. 5F). This phenomenon is likely attributed to the abnormal axis during SR.

Above all, the ECG during sinus rhythm has the potential to provide insights into the alterations in the cardiac substrate. These changes may be attributed to either the presence of cardiomyopathy or the patient’s history of prior surgery. We can make preoperative anticipation of AFL mechanism even if it presents with different ECG patterns based on the clinical characteristics of the patients.

Anatomic considerations and the AFL ECGs

Previous study has shown that the impulse conduction to the left atrial free wall through either lower or upper interatrial connection is a major determinant of ECG morphology in AFL [13]. In other words, flutter wave morphology of CTI dependent AFL is mainly determined by left atrial activation. Different from the previous studies, even LA enlargement with a consequent functional “remodeling” that in turn promotes electrical remodeling [14], it seems LA enlargement has no impact on the typical ECG morphology. On the contrary, enlargement of the right atrium (RA) has an impact on the ECG characteristics of typical AFL and can result in the manifestation of atypical patterns. As demonstrated by previous study, the RA substrate remodeling [15] would determine the outcome of atrial fibrillation ablation. A potential explanation for this phenomenon is that RA may play a role in the development of atrial fibrillation (AF) during its initial stages. Under various circumstances, pathological alterations, such as cellular hypertrophy, inflammation, dedifferentiation, fibrosis, and apoptosis, can impact the myocardium of both the left and right atrium. These changes are often observed as a result of systemic diseases, such as diabetes. All pathological alterations have the potential to result in atrial enlargement and conduction abnormalities, subsequently leading to various ECG patterns. Additionally, a significant number of patients in the atypical groups had previously undergone cardiac surgery involving a RA incision, which could potentially impact the conduction of activation due to the presence of an atrial scar. Hence, it is reasonable to hypothesize that enlargement of the right atrium could result in structural remodeling and alterations in interatrial connections, thereby potentially contributing to the development of various ECG patterns associated with AFL.

Limitation

This is a single center retrospective study. We only performed activation mapping of right atrium in most of the patients. It was necessary to study the left atrium role in the patients with atypical ECG patterns. Our SR P-wave analysis being performed immediately post-ablation during initial sinus rhythm restoration. Long-term AFL-induced remodeling may affect the SR ECG morphology. Therefore, further study conducted weeks to months post-ablation to account for atrial reverse remodeling, thereby minimizing the confounding effects of long-standing AFL on sinus rhythm P-wave morphology.

Conclusion

Typical atrial flutter may manifest with various electrocardiogram patterns, and two atypical patterns were found: (1) F wave with an isoelectric interval between the negative component in inferior leads and positive in lead V1; (2) F wave manifests as -/+ in lead V1, with typical patterns observed in the inferior leads. This study demonstrates that SR P-wave parameters, such as lower inferior leads amplitude, smaller P wave area, abnormal axis, IAB, could predict atypical ECG patterns in typical AFL, reflecting underlying atrial fibrosis/scarring. Additionally, the ECG patterns of typical atrial flutter can be influenced by the presence of RA enlargement and prior cardiac surgery, which suggests that these parameters may have the potential to predict the underlying mechanism.
