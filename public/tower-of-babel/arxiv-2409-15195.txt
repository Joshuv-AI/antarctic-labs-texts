Control of Conditional Processes and Fleming–Viot Dynamics


Abstract
We discuss equivalent formulations of the control of conditional processes introduced by Lions. In this problem, a controlled diffusion process is killed once it hits the boundary of a bounded domain and the controller’s reward is computed based on the conditional distribution given the process’s survival. So far there is no clarity regarding the relationship between the open- and closed-loop formulation of this nonstandard control problem. We provide a short proof of their equivalence using measurable selection and mimicking arguments. In addition, we link the closed-loop formulation to Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type, where upon being killed the diffusion process is reinserted into the domain according to the current law of the process itself. This connection offers a new interpretation of the control problem and opens it up to applications that feature costs caused by reinsertion.

In this article, we consider the control of conditional processes, originally introduced by Lions in a series of lectures at the Collège de France

1.1
Open- Versus Closed-Loop Formulation

As it stands, there is a lack of clarity about whether the open- and closed-loop formulation of the control problem are equivalent, that is, whether
V=V_{\textup{closed}}
. In his lectures at the Collège de France, Lions demonstrated that the control of conditional processes differs from standard control problems in that an optimal closed-loop control does not only depend on the current state
X_{t}
but also the conditional distribution
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
2
2

2

Cf. Lecture
9
of the course by Lions
[
16
]
.
. This resembles the situation for the control of McKean–Vlasov SDEs, where optimal closed-loop controls vary with the law of the solution to the McKean–Vlasov SDE.
In both situations, this additional dependence can be subsumed in the time variable of the feedback function
a
associated with an optimal closed-loop control, suggesting that the supremum of
J
over all open- and closed-loop controls may coincide, i.e.
V=V_{\textup{closed}}
.
As is now well-known, the latter is not only the case for standard control problems, but also typically holds for McKean–Vlasov control problems
[
14
,
15
]
. Thus, it is natural to suspect the same for the control of conditional processes.

The first objective of the present article is to confirm this hypothesis. To this end, we build on a strategy used by Lacker
[
14
]
to prove the equivalence between open- and closed-loop controls for standard McKean–Vlasov control problems. This approach was suggested in a recent article
[
10
, Appendix A.4]
by the author and B. Hambly, but requires further theoretical justification, which we provide here while simplifying the arguments. Starting from any open-loop control
(\alpha,\mu)\in\mathcal{A}
with corresponding solution
X
to SDE (
1.1
), we employ a measurable selection theorem by Haussmann & Lepeltier
[
12
, Theorem A.9]
together with the mimicking theorem by Brunick & Shreve
[
2
]
to construct a feedback function
a
, derived from the control
\alpha
, as well as a solution
\tilde{X}
to the SDE

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=b\bigl(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,\tilde{X}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(1.3)

such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
. If SDE (
1.1
) were a classical McKean–Vlasov SDE, meaning that
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})
, then it would follow that
\tilde{X}
is a solution to this McKean–Vlasov SDE and the selection of
a
through
[
12
, Theorem A.9]
would guarantee that
J(\tilde{\alpha},\mu)\geq J(\alpha,\mu)
, where
\tilde{\alpha}_{t}=a(t,\tilde{X}_{t})
. However, in order for
\tilde{X}
to solve the
conditional
McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
), we must have
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tilde{\tau}>t)=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)=\mu_{t}
, with
\tilde{\tau}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\tilde{X}_{t}\notin D\}
, instead of
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=\mu_{t}
. To achieve this, the key insight is that, under mild regularity conditions on the domain
D
, we can express the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) equivalently as the system

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}\notin D}\,\mathrm{d}t

with initial condition
X_{0}=\xi
,
\Lambda_{0}=0
, and
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\Lambda_{t}=0)
, using that a.s.
@
\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\notin D\}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\Lambda_{0}>0\}
. Then, we can conclude by applying the mimicking theorem to
(X,\Lambda)
.

It should be stressed that a closely related result has been obtained by Campi, Ghio & Livieri
[
6
]
who study mean-field games in which the players interact through their subprobability distribution
\nu_{t}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau>t)
instead of the conditional distribution
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
. The computation of the reward functional in this model is also based on the subprobability distribution, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})|\tau>t]
and
g(\mu_{T})
in (
1.2
) are replaced by
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\tau>t}f(t,X_{t},\nu_{t},\alpha_{t})]
and
g(\nu_{T})
. Using ideas that resemble the approach we take here, they construct a closed-loop Nash equilibrium in
[
6
, Proposition 3.6]
. Their proof skips certain details associated with the removal of players at the exit time
\tau
. Indeed, instead of relying on the pair
(X,\Lambda)
as outlined above, they mimic the process
Y=(Y^{1},Y^{2})
given by
Y_{t}=(t\land\tau,X_{t\land\tau})
. However, it is not immediately clear whether the mimicking process
\tilde{Y}=(\tilde{Y}^{1},\tilde{Y}^{2})
takes the desired form
(t\land\tilde{\tau},\tilde{X}_{t\land\tilde{\tau}})
, where
\tilde{X}
solves SDE (
1.3
) and
\tilde{\tau}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\tilde{X}_{t}\notin D\}
. It is possible to resolve this by applying the extended mimicking theorem by Brunick & Shreve
[
2
, Theorem 3.6]
with an appropriate choice of
updating function
(a concept introduced in
[
2
, Definition 3.1]
), but this was not discussed in
[
6
]
. Private communication with the article’s authors revealed that they had considered the use of a suitable updating function, but ultimately this was not included in the article. Our approach circumvents the usage of updating functions altogether.

Let us also mention that in an earlier paper, Campi & Fischer
[
5
]
considered mean-field games in which players interact through their conditional distribution
\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
while the computation of the reward
J
is based on the subprobability distribution
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau>t)
. They, too, show the existence of a closed-loop Nash equilibrium, but following an entirely different line of argument. They characterise Nash equilibria through a forward-backward SDE and, subsequently, express the solution to the backward SDE, and therefore the optimal control of the representative player, as a function of the state process
X_{t}
(and the current time
t
). These arguments, however, only work in the mean-field game setting and, as a result, cannot be transferred to the control of conditional processes.

In a recent work, aimed at making progress towards confirming the equivalence of the open- and closed-loop formulation for the control of conditional processes, Carmona, Laurière & Lions
[
7
]
studied a relaxation of this problem with “soft” as opposed to “hard” killing. That is, instead of killing the diffusion once the state
X_{t}
hits the boundary of
D
, the process
X
can exit the domain and is killed at a positive rate
\lambda(X_{t})>0
whenever it is located outside of
\bar{D}
. More precisely, the diffusion is killed once
\Lambda_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s
exceeds a standard exponential random variable. Accordingly,
\mu_{t}
is replaced by
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Lambda_{t}}\delta_{X_{t}}]/\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Lambda_{t}}]
, where the fraction
e^{-\Lambda_{t}}
measures the probability that the diffusion is still alive. An analogous replacement is made in the conditioning appearing in the definition of the reward functional
J
in (
1.2
). Note that for the specific choice
\lambda(x)=\infty\mathbf{1}_{\{x\notin\bar{D}\}}
, not covered in
[
7
]
, one recovers Lions’ original problem, since then
e^{-\Lambda_{t}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}\in\bar{D},\,0\leq s\leq t\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}
outside the nullset
\{\tau=t\}
. In the case of soft killing, the main difficulty in establishing the equivalence between the open- and closed-loop formulation lies in proving that one does not need to keep track of
\Lambda_{t}
in order to optimally control the system. In other words, it suffices to consider feedback functions
a
that depend on time and the state
X_{t}
but not the cumulative intensity
\Lambda_{t}
. This fact is closely related to the memoryless property of the exponential distribution. Carmona, Laurière & Lions achieve this by studying two nonlocal PDEs of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman type that characterise optimal closed-loop controls with and without dependence on
\Lambda_{t}
. They show that both PDEs have the same unique solution, thereby guaranteeing that both types of closed-loop controls yield the same value. A similar equivalence result was obtained by Hambly & Jettkant
[
11
]
in the context of McKean–Vlasov control with soft killing and common noise. Its proof employs a straightforward approach based on measurable selection arguments and a generalisation of the well-known mimicking theorem of Gyöngy
[
9
]
to McKean–Vlasov SDEs with common noise by Lacker, Shkolnikov & Zhang
[
15
]
, thus circumventing the study of nonlocal PDEs. A key contribution of this paper is to address the equivalence hypothesis directly for Lion’s original model with hard killing, without introducing a relaxation of the control problem.

1.2
Fleming–Viot Dynamics of McKean–Vlasov Type

The second contribution of this article is to link the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) to Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type. The connection between (
1.1
) and a Fleming–Viot type particle system was already made by Burdzy et al.
[
4
,
3
]
. The particle system consists of
N\geq 1
Brownian particles
X^{i}_{t}
in a domain
D
. Whenever one of the particles hits the boundary, it is reinserted at a position that is chosen uniformly at random amongst the current locations of the remaining particles. Burdzy, Hołyst & March
[
3
]
show that as
N\to\infty
, for each
t\geq 0
, the empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
of the particles converges to the marginal distribution of a Brownian motion killed upon hitting
\partial D
, conditioned on survival. This dynamic is a special case of the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
), where
b
vanishes and
\sigma
is the identity matrix. A uniform-in-time version of this result was later proven by Grigorescu and Kang
[
8
]
. Subsequently, Tough and Nolen
[
18
]
generalised the theory to systems, where the dynamics of each particle can feature a drift that may depend on the particle’s location as well as the empirical measure similar to (
1.1
). Note that in each of these cases, only the single marginal empirical distributions
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
of the particle system converge to the time marginals
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
of the conditional process, while the trajectories
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{\cdot\land t}}
and
\mathcal{L}(X_{\cdot\land t}|\tau>t)
of the two systems are rather different. In this work, we introduce a mean-field analogue of the Fleming–Viot type particle system, that may be thought of as also capturing the trajectorial behaviour. We refer to this mean-field system as Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type. The state
Y
of the representative particle of this system follows the McKean–Vlasov SDE

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=b\bigl(t,Y_{t},\mathcal{L}(Y_{t}),a(t,Y_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(1.4)

for a feedback function
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
. In addition, if the particle hits the boundary
\partial D
at some time
t\geq 0
, it is reinserted according to the current law
\mathcal{L}(Y_{t})
. This process is repeated whenever the particle exits
D
. The expected number of reinsertions up to time
t
is denoted by
F_{t}
. We show that the above dynamics are well-posed, that the marginal law
\mathcal{L}(Y_{t})
coincides with the conditional distribution
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
of the solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) with closed-loop control
\alpha_{t}=a(t,X_{t})
, and that
F_{t}=-\log(\tau>t)
. In particular, controlling the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) over feedback functions
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
is equivalent to controlling the Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type (
1.4
) over such
a
.

The dynamics (
1.4
) open up the control problem for conditional processes to a larger class of applications, where reinsertion is penalised by adding the cost
-cF_{T}
for
c>0
to the reward functional. Note that this term is already present in the work by Achdou, Laurière & Lions
[
1
, Equation (6)]
to facilitate the passage to the limit as
T\to\infty
, but is not interpreted as a reinsertion cost. A concrete example of a model with reinsertion is the following:
Y_{t}
represents the workload of a machine of a large manufacturing company. The workload is directed by the company’s employees through the control
a(t,Y_{t})
, based on the current workload, but is also subject to random shocks, modelled by a Brownian motion, i.e.
\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=a(t,Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
. Increasing the machine’s workload raises the company’s revenues, but also makes the machine more susceptible to defects. If the workload becomes too high, the machine breaks down and has to be replaced at the cost
c
. The company’s objective is to find the right balance between revenue generation and minimisation of operating costs. The analysis of the control problem for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.4
) in the presence of reinsertion costs is left for future work.

2
Equivalence between the Open- and Closed-Loop Formulation

In this section, we establish the equivalence between the open- and closed-loop formulation of the control of conditional processes. We begin by stating the assumptions under which we will be working in the remainder of this section. Since we restrict our attention to the compact interval
[0,T]
throughout this article, we assume for simplicity that the coefficient
b
vanishes for
t\in(T,\infty)
. Consequently, if we apply a change of measure à la Girsanov to remove or adjust the drift, it suffices to evaluate the stochastic exponential, which provides the density of the new measure, at time
T
.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume that
b
,
f
, and
g
are measurable and bounded functions and that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(i)

for all
(t,x,a)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A
and
m
,
m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(D)
, we have

\bigl\lvert b(t,x,m,a)-b(t,x,m^{\prime},a)\rvert\leq C\lVert m-m^{\prime}\rVert_{\textup{TV}};

(ii)

\sigma
is invertible.

Here
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\textup{TV}}
denotes the total variation norm on the space of finite signed measures on
D
.

The following convexity assumption is required to apply a measurable selection theorem from Haussmann & Lepeltier
[
12
, Theorem A.9]
. It allows us to construct a closed-loop from an open-loop control.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that for any
(t,x,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times D
, the set

\bigl\{\bigl(b(t,x,m,a),z\bigr)\mathpunct{:}z\in\mathbb{R},\,a\in A,\,z\leq f(t,x,m,a)\bigr\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

is closed and convex.

Our goal is to show that
V_{\textup{closed}}=V
. We achieve this by obtaining for any given admissible control
(\alpha,\mu)
a closed-loop control that achieves the same or a larger reward. In the remainder of this section, we will denote the solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) associated to an admissible control
(\alpha,\mu)
by
X^{\alpha,\mu}
. We write
\tau_{\alpha,\mu}
for the corresponding hitting time on
D^{c}
. We start with a simple uniform lower bound on the probability
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\alpha,\mu}>t)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied. Then
\inf_{(\alpha,\mu)\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\alpha,\mu}>t)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let us fix
t\geq 0
and a sequence
(\alpha^{n},\mu^{n})_{n\geq 1}
of admissible controls such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}>t)=\inf_{(\alpha,\mu)}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\alpha,\mu}>t)
, where
\tau_{n}=\tau_{\alpha^{n},\mu^{n}}
and
X^{n}=X^{\alpha^{n},\mu^{n}}
. Since
b
is bounded, the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem guarantees the tightness of the sequence
(\beta^{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
\beta^{n}=\int_{0}^{\cdot}b(s,X^{n}_{s},\mu^{n}_{s},\alpha^{n}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}t
. Hence, selecting a subsequence and enlarging the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
if necessary, we may assume there exists a random variable
\beta
with values in
C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
(\xi,\beta^{n},W)_{n\geq 1}
converges weakly on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
(\xi,\beta,W)
. In particular, it holds that
X^{n}\Rightarrow X=\xi+\beta+W
on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We set
\tau=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\notin D\}
. Now, we want to establish two facts: firstly,
\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)>0
and, secondly,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}>t)\geq\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)
. The first statement can be easily proven by a change of measure argument through which we remove the drift of
X
. This relies on the boundedness of
b
, guaranteed by Assumption
2.1
, whereby
\lvert\beta_{s}-\beta_{u}\rvert\leq C_{b}\lvert s-u\rvert
, so that
\beta_{s}=\int_{0}^{s}f_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u
for some
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued process
f
with
\lvert f_{s}\rvert\leq C_{b}
for
s\geq 0
. It also uses the invertibility of
\sigma
from Assumption
2.1

(ii)
. We will skip the details here. For the second fact, let us note that
\tau_{n}\leq t
if and only if
\inf_{s\in[0,t]}d(X^{n}_{s},\partial D)=0
. But the expression
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni x\mapsto\inf_{s\in[0,t]}d(x,\partial D)
is continuous, meaning that the set of
x\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
for which
\inf_{s\in[0,t]}d(x,\partial D)=0
is closed. Consequently, the Portmanteau theorem implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq t)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\inf_{s\in[0,t]}d(X^{n}_{s},\partial D)=0\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\inf_{s\in[0,t]}d(X_{s},\partial D)=0\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq t).

From this we deduce
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}>t)\geq\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)>0
.
∎

As mentioned in Section
1
, the uniqueness of the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) for an arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable
A
-valued process
\alpha
is not obvious, due to the comparably irregular behaviour of the hitting time
\tau_{\alpha,\mu}
. This is why our definition of an admissible control includes the flow
\mu
of the conditional law of the solution. However, as we show next, starting from a feedback function
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
, we can construct a unique strong solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,X_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(2.1)

with
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>t)
and
\tau=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\notin D\}
. Then the pair
(\alpha,\mu)
given by
\alpha_{t}=a(t,X_{t})
and
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
yields a closed-loop control. In particular, the set of admissible controls is not empty. Since the feedback function
a
completely determines the associated closed-loop control
(\alpha,\mu)
, we will sometimes refer to
a
itself as a closed-loop control. Moreover, we define the closed-loop reward functional
J_{\textup{CP}}
by
J_{\textup{CP}}(a)=J(\alpha,\mu)
.

Before we state the existence and uniqueness result for the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
2.1
), we need to make a couple of definitions. We equip the space
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the metric

(x,y)\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\bigl(\lVert x_{\cdot\land k}-y_{\cdot\land k}\rVert_{\infty}\land 1\bigr).

The total variation norm on the space of finite signed measures on
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to this metric is denoted by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\textup{TV}}
. Next, for a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
t\geq 0
, we denote the
\nu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d}))
the pushforward of
\nu
under the map
\pi_{t}\mathpunct{:}C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
x\mapsto x_{\cdot\land t}
. Finally, we let
C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be the space of flows of measures on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that are continuous with respect to the bounded Lipschitz distance on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied and assume that
\partial D
has zero Lebesgue measure. Then for any measurable function
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
, the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
2.1
) has a unique strong solution. In particular, the set
\mathcal{A}
of admissible controls is nonempty.

Proof.

We introduce a map
\Phi\mathpunct{:}C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
defined as follows: for
\mu\in C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, let
\nu^{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d}))
denote the distribution of the unique strong solution of the SDE

\mathrm{d}X^{\mu}_{t}=b\bigl(t,X^{\mu}_{t},\mu_{t\land T},a(t,X^{\mu}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(2.2)

with initial condition
X^{\mu}_{0}=\xi
and set
\tau_{\mu}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X^{\mu}_{t}\notin D\}
. The existence of a unique strong solution to SDE (
2.2
) is guaranteed by a well-known result of Veretennikov
[
19
, Theorem 1]
. Note that since
b
vanishes after
T
, stopping
\mu
at
T
does not pose any issues. Then
\Phi(\mu)
is given by
\Phi(\mu)_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X^{\mu}_{t}|\tau_{\mu}>t)
for
t\in[0,T]
. It is easy to show that
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(X^{\mu}_{t}|\tau_{\mu}>t)
is continuous with respect to the bounded Lipschitz distance on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so
\Phi
is well-defined. The former statement exploits the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\mu}=t)\leq\mathbb{P}(X^{\mu}_{t}\in\partial D)=0
because
\partial D
has zero Lebesgue measure by assumption and
\mathcal{L}(X^{\mu}_{t})
has a density for
t>0
.

We will show that the map
\Phi
is a contraction on
C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with respect to an appropriately chosen metric. We follow the arguments from the proof of
[
5
, Proposition C.1]
, see also
[
18
, Section 8.3]
, designed to show uniqueness of equations of the same type as McKean–Vlasov SDE (
2.1
). Let
\mu^{1}
,
\mu^{2}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, set
\nu^{i}=\nu^{\mu_{i}}
,
i=1
,
2
, and let
X=X^{\mu^{1}}
.
Next, we introduce the measure
\mathbb{Q}
defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg\rvert_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{E}\biggl(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma^{-1}\bigl(b_{2}(t,X_{t})-b_{1}(t,X_{t})\bigr)\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\biggr)_{T},

where
b_{i}(t,x)=b\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu^{i}_{t\land T},a(t,X_{t})\bigr)
for
i=1
,
2
. Note that since
b
is bounded by Assumption
2.1
and
\sigma
is invertible by Item
(ii)
of the same assumption, the stochastic exponential is a martingale by Novikov’s condition, so that the measure
\mathbb{Q}
is indeed well-defined and equivalent to
\mathbb{P}
. Hence, by Girsanov’s theorem, the process
B
defined by
B_{t}=W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}(b_{2}(s,X_{s})-b_{1}(s,X_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s
is a Brownian motion under
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus,
X
solves the SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu^{2}_{t\land T},a(t,X_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}

under
\mathbb{Q}
. This SDE exhibits uniqueness in law, whence
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}(X)=\nu^{2}
. Consequently, for any
t\in[0,T]
and all Borel subset
A\subset C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\nu^{1}_{t}(A)-\nu^{2}_{t}(A)\bigr\rvert=\bigl\lvert\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{\cdot\land t}\in A\}}(1-Z_{t})\bigr]\bigr\rvert\leq\sqrt{\nu^{1}_{t}(A)}\lVert Z_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert Z_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{2}},

where
Z_{t}=\mathcal{E}\bigl(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma^{-1}(b_{2}(s,X_{s})-b_{1}(s,X_{s}))\cdot\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\bigr)_{t}
. Taking the supremum over all Borel sets
A
implies that
\lVert\nu^{1}_{t}-\nu^{2}_{t}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}\leq\lVert Z_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{2}}
. Recalling that
Z
is a martingale started from
1
, we find

\displaystyle\lVert\nu^{1}_{t}-\nu^{2}_{t}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Z_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{t}\rvert^{2}-2\mathbb{E}Z_{t}+1=\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{t}\rvert^{2}-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\lvert\sigma^{-1}\bigl(b_{2}(s,X_{s})-b_{1}(s,X_{s})\bigr)Z_{s}\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c_{\sigma}^{2}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\lvert b_{2}(s,X_{s})-b_{1}(s,X_{s})\bigr\rvert^{2}\lvert Z_{s}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c_{\sigma}^{2}C_{b}^{2}\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{T}\rvert^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\mu^{1}_{s}-\mu^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(2.3)

where we applied Itô’s isometry in the second line and used in the last inequality that
\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{s}\rvert^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{T}\rvert^{2}
for
s\in[0,T]
. In addition, we used the Lipschitz continuity of
b
in the measure argument and the invertibility of
\sigma
guaranteed by Assumption
2.1

(i)
and
(ii)
. By Lemma
2.3
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\tau>T)
and
\mathbb{Q}(\tau>T)
are bounded from below by some
\delta>0
. Consequently,

\bigl\lVert\Phi(\mu^{1})_{t}-\Phi(\mu^{2})_{t}\bigr\rVert_{\textup{TV}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\bigl\lVert\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau>t)-\mathbb{Q}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau>t)\bigr\rVert_{\textup{TV}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\lVert\nu^{1}_{t}-\nu^{2}_{t}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}.

(2.4)

Inserting this into (
2
) yields

\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigl\lVert\Phi(\mu^{1})_{s}-\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}\bigr\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\delta^{2}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lVert\nu^{1}_{s}-\nu^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq u\leq s}\lVert\mu^{1}_{u}-\mu^{2}_{u}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
C=\frac{4c_{\sigma}^{2}C_{b}^{2}\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{T}\rvert^{2}}{\delta^{2}}
. Multiplying both sides by
e^{-2Ct}
, integrating over
t\in[0,t^{\prime}]
, and applying integration by parts implies that

\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}e^{-2Ct}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\land T}\bigl\lVert\Phi(\mu^{1})_{s}-\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}\bigr\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}e^{-2Ct}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\land T}\lVert\mu^{1}_{s}-\mu^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, equipping
C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with the metric

(m^{1},m^{2})\mapsto\biggl(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2Ct}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\land T}\lVert m^{1}_{s}-m^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\textup{TV}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\biggr)^{1/2},

we find that
\Phi
is a contraction. Consequently, there exists a unique fixed point
\mu
. It follows that the unique strong solution of SDE (
2.2
) with this choice of
\mu
, is a strong solution to SDE (
2.1
). Since every solution to the latter equation yields a fixed point of
\Phi
, the contraction property of
\Phi
implies weak uniqueness for the conditional McKean–Vlasov (
2.1
). Strong uniqueness then is a consequence of the result by Veretennikov
[
19
, Theorem 1]
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we show how from any admissible control we can construct a closed-loop control with an equal or larger reward. The construction is based on a measurable selection argument, for which we require Assumption
2.2
to be in place. Further, we have to assume that the domain
D
satisfies the
Poincaré–Zaremba cone condition
. That is, for any point
x\in\partial D
, there exists an open cone
C
with apex
x
and an
\epsilon>0
such that
C\cap B_{\epsilon}(x)\subset\bar{D}^{c}
. This condition translates into a regularity property for the hitting time
\tau_{\alpha,\mu}
of
X^{\alpha,\mu}
on
\partial D
and is relevant for the mimicking argument outlined in Section
1
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
be satisfied and suppose that
D
possesses the Poincaré–Zaremba cone condition. Then for any admissible control
(\alpha,\mu)
, there exists a closed-loop control
(\tilde{\alpha},\mu)
such that
J(\tilde{\alpha},\mu)\geq J(\alpha,\mu)
. In particular,
V_{\textup{closed}}=V
.

Proof.

Let
(\alpha,\mu)
be a weak control, set
X=X^{\alpha,\mu}
, and define
\Lambda_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{t}\notin\bar{D}\}}
. Now, proceeding as in the proof of Theorem 3.7 in
[
13
]
, using Assumption
2.2
, we can find a measurable function
\tilde{a}\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to A
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[b(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})|X_{t},\Lambda_{t}]&=b\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,X_{t},\Lambda_{t})\bigr)\\
\mathbb{E}[f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})|X_{t},\Lambda_{t}]&\leq f\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,X_{t},\Lambda_{t})\bigr)\end{split}

(2.5)

for
t\in[0,\infty)
. Note that the arguments in
[
13
]
are based on the measurable selection theorem
[
12
, Theorem A.9]
. Now, we apply the mimicking theorem by Brunick & Shreve
[
2
, Corollary 3.7]
to obtain a weak solution
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\Lambda})
to the SDE

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=b\bigl(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{\Lambda}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t},\quad\mathrm{d}\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{X}_{t}\notin\bar{D}\}}\,\mathrm{d}t

(2.6)

on some probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
carrying a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
\tilde{W}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X},\tilde{\Lambda})=\mathcal{L}(X,\Lambda)
.
Next, we claim that
\tau=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\Lambda_{t}>0\}
holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and, similarly,
\tilde{\tau}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}>0\}
holds
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., where
\tilde{\tau}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\tilde{X}_{t}\notin D\}
. We will only show this for the process
(X,\Lambda)
, the proof for
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\Lambda})
is analogous. Since
X_{t}\in\bar{D}
on
[0,\tau]
, it holds that
\Lambda_{t}=0
on
[0,\tau]
, whence
\tau\leq\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}>0\}
. Let us now establish the converse. By Proposition
A.1
, a.s. for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
t\in(0,\epsilon]
such that
X_{\tau+t}\notin\bar{D}
. By continuity of
X
, this means that
\Lambda_{\tau+\epsilon}>0
. Hence, it follows that
\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\Lambda_{t}>0\}\leq\tau+\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\to 0
yields the desired inequality, so that a.s.
\tau=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\Lambda_{t}>0\}
. This implies that
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\tau>0)=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|\Lambda_{t}=0)=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}=0)=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tilde{\tau}>t)
, so that
\tilde{X}
is a weak solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
), where
\alpha_{t}
is replaced by
\tilde{a}(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{\Lambda}_{t})
. Moreover, in view of Equation (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle J(\alpha,\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})\big|\tau>t\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,X_{t},\Lambda_{t})\bigr)\big|\Lambda_{t}=0\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigl[f\bigl(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{\Lambda}_{t})\bigr)\big|\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}=0\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigl[f\bigl(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,\tilde{X}_{t},0)\bigr)\big|\tilde{\tau}>t\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T}).

(2.7)

Let us now define
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
by
a(t,x)=\tilde{a}(t,x,0)
and let
X^{\prime}
be the unique strong solution to SDE

\mathrm{d}X^{\prime}_{t}=b\bigl(t,X^{\prime}_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,X^{\prime}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

with initial condition
X^{\prime}_{0}=\xi
. Note that
X^{\prime}
and
\tilde{X}
satisfy the same SDE up to their respective first hitting time on
D^{c}
, so uniqueness in law for this SDE implies that
\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{\cdot\land\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}},\tilde{\tau})
, where
\tau^{\prime}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X^{\prime}_{t}\notin D\}
. Consequently,
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tilde{\tau}>t)=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
, so that
X^{\prime}
is a strong solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) with
\alpha
replaced by
\tilde{\alpha}
given by
\tilde{\alpha}_{t}=a(t,X^{\prime}_{t})
. Thus,
(\tilde{\alpha},\mu)
is a closed-loop control and by (
2
) it holds that

\displaystyle J(\alpha,\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigl[f\bigl(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{a}(t,\tilde{X}_{t},0)\bigr)\big|\tilde{\tau}>t\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f\bigl(t,X^{\prime}_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,X^{\prime}_{t})\bigr)\big|\tau^{\prime}>t\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\mu_{T})

\displaystyle=J(\tilde{\alpha},\mu).

∎

Remark 2.6
.

Note that the results in this section also hold if we no longer require that
b
is bounded but instead assume that
b(t,x,m,a)=a
and
\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\alpha_{t}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
. This is one of the settings considered by Lions
[
16
]
. We may achieve this by first considering the case of bounded controls and then passing to the limit. In principle, we could also follow this procedure in the case of a general unbounded
b
that satisfies suitable growth assumptions, but it becomes more involved if
b
can depend on the conditional law. We impose the boundedness assumptions on
b
to avoid these technical issues.

3
Reformulation through Fleming–Viot Dynamics

In this section, we reformulate the closed-loop variant of the control problem for the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) in terms of Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type. This will in particular allow us to consider more general reward functionals that open up the model to a wider range of applications. We consider an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that follows the dynamics

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,X_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}J_{t}

(3.1)

with initial condition
X_{0}=\xi
and
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
. The jump process
J=(J_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is given by
J_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(\xi_{k}-X_{s-})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq s\}}
, where the stopping times
(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are defined by
\tau_{0}=0
and, for
k\geq 1
,
\tau_{k}=\inf\{t>\tau_{k-1}\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\notin D\}
, and the
D
-valued random variables
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are independent with
\mathcal{L}(\xi_{k}|\tau_{k}=t)=\mu_{t}
for
t\geq 0
. Note that there is a slight subtlety here, namely that it not immediately clear whether
\tau_{\infty}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}=\infty
almost surely. To avoid any ambiguities, when we speak of a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
), we shall always require that
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
almost surely.

The process
X
is a mean-field analogue of a Fleming–Viot type particle system introduced by Burdzy et al.
[
4
]
. Whenever the representative particle hits the boundary, it is reinserted at a location that is distributed according to the current law
\mu_{t}
of the system. As we will show next, the law of the solution of (
3.1
) coincides with the conditional law of the killed process discussed in Section
2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied and assume that
\partial D
has zero Lebesgue measure. Then the McKean–Vlasov SDEs (
3.1
) and (
2.1
) have unique in law weak solutions
X
and
X^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
for all
t\geq 0
, where
\tau^{\prime}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X^{\prime}_{t}\notin D
}
.

Proof.

We already established the existence of a unique strong solution for the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
2.1
) in Proposition
2.4
. Let
X^{\prime}
be this unique strong solution and set
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
, where
\tau^{\prime}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}X^{\prime}_{t}\notin D
}. We will use the flow
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to construct a weak solution
X
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
). Indeed, we simply let
X
be the unique in law weak solution of the SDE (
3.1
), where we view the appearance of
\mu_{t}
both in the coefficient
b
and to describe the conditional law
\mathcal{L}(\xi_{k}|\tau_{k}=t)
of the random variables
\xi_{k}
as a fixed input rather than being determined by the SDE. Note that the solution to this SDE can be obtained in an iterative fashion, but it is a priori only defined up to the time
\tau_{\infty}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}\in[0,\infty]
. Our first goal will be to show that
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
almost surly. To that end, let us define the function
F^{n}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq t)
for
n\geq 1
as well as
F_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq t)
. It holds for
k\geq 2
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq t|\tau_{k-1})]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}\leq t-s|\tau_{k-1}=s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k-1}\leq s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k-1}\leq s).

(3.2)

Here for
s\geq 0
and
m\in\mathcal{P}(D)
,
X^{s,m}
denotes the unique strong solution to

\mathrm{d}X^{s,m}_{u}=b\bigl(s+u,X^{s,m}_{u},\mu_{s+u},a(s+u,X^{s,m}_{u})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}(W_{s+u}-W_{s})

with initial condition
X^{s,m}_{0}\sim m
and
\tau_{s,m}=\inf\{u>0\mathpunct{:}X^{s,m}_{u}\notin D\}
. From (
3
), we deduce

\displaystyle F^{n}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k-1}\leq s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}F^{n-1}_{s}

(3.3)

for
n\geq 2
. Next, we claim that for any
t\geq 0
there exists
p_{t}\in[0,1)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\leq p_{t}
. If this is indeed the case, then it follows from the above equality that
F^{n}_{t}\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq 1)+p_{t}F^{n-1}_{t}\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq 1)+p_{t}F^{n}_{t}
. Rearranging gives
F^{n}_{t}\leq\frac{1}{1-p_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)
, so that
F_{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{n}_{t}\leq\frac{1}{1-p_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, taking the limit
n\to\infty
in (
3
) yields

F_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}F_{s}.

(3.4)

Let us prove the claim next. Fix
t\geq 0
. First note that since
\mu
is continuous in the topology of weak convergence, we can find
\epsilon>0
such that
\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})\geq\epsilon
for all
s\in[0,T]
, where
D_{\delta}=\{x\in D\mathpunct{:}d(x,\partial D)>\delta\}
for
\delta>0
. Now, we set
A_{t,s}=\{\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\}
and
X^{s}=X^{s,\mu_{s}}
, and decompose

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon}\bigr)\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})+\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\notin D_{\epsilon}\bigr)\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon}\bigr)\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})+\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon}^{c})

\displaystyle=1-\bigl(1-\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon}\bigr)\bigr)\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq 1-\epsilon\bigl(1-\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon}\bigr)\bigr).

Hence, if we can show that there is
q_{t}\in[0,1)
with
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon})\leq q_{t}
, then setting
p_{t}=1-\epsilon(1-q_{t})
, the claim follows. However, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s\big|X^{s}_{0}\in D_{\epsilon}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})}\int_{D_{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s|X^{s}_{0}=x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{s}(D_{\epsilon})}\int_{D_{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\delta_{x}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}(x)

For any
x\in D_{\epsilon}
, we can bound
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\delta_{x}}\leq t-s)
by the probability that
X^{s,\delta_{x}}
exits
B_{\epsilon}(x)\subset D
before time
t-s
. The latter can be shown to be bounded by
q_{t}\in[0,1)
independently of
s\in[0,t]
. This concludes the proof of the claim. Note that the function
t\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\tau_{s},\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)
is nondecreasing, so we may choose
p_{t}
such that
t\mapsto p_{t}
is nondecreasing and right-continuous.

Next, we will show that (
3.4
) also holds for
-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)
in place of
F_{t}
. Note that
X
and
X^{\prime}
solve the same SDE up to their respective first hitting times
\tau_{1}
and
\tau^{\prime}
of
D
. Hence, for
0\leq s\leq t
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>s)\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t|\tau_{1}>s)=\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}>t-s).

(3.5)

Rearranging gives
-1=-\frac{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)}{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)}-\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)
. We divide by
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)
on both sides and integrate over
s\in[0,t]
with respect to
s\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\tau>s)
, whence

\displaystyle-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)}{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}\leq t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)

(3.6)

as required. Now, if we can show that the convolution equation
f_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}\leq t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}f_{s}
has a unique solution, then it follows from (
3.4
) and (
3
) that
F_{t}=-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)
. We shall establish this next. Let
L
be the space of nondecreasing càdlàg functions
f\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such
f_{t}\leq\frac{1}{1-p_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)
for
t\geq 0
. Since
t\mapsto p_{t}
is nondecreasing and right-continuous, the space
L
is a complete lattice with the partial order
\leq
defined by
f\leq g
if
f_{t}\leq g_{t}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
and the meet and join of a subset
A\subset L
given by the right-continuous modifications of the nondecreasing functions
t\mapsto\inf_{f\in A}f_{t}
and
t\mapsto\sup_{f\in A}f_{t}
.
Moreover, the function from
L
to
L
that maps
f\in L
to
t\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t\lor 0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}f_{s}
is monotonic. Thus, the Tarski fixed-point theorem guarantees the existence of a minimal fixed point
f^{\ast}\in L
. Clearly, this fixed point solves the equation
f^{\ast}_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\leq t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}f^{\ast}_{s}
. Next, assume that
f
is another solution, so that by minimality of
f^{\ast}
, we have
\Delta_{t}=f_{t}-f^{\ast}_{t}\geq 0
for any
t\geq 0
. Then we find

\Delta_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}\leq t-s)\,\mathrm{d}\Delta_{s}\leq p_{t}\Delta_{t}.

Since
p_{t}\in[0,1)
, this can only be the case if
\Delta_{t}=0
, so
f^{\ast}
is indeed the unique solution to the convolution equation.

Next, we use an idea similar to (
3
) to derive a convolution identity for
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
. We have for any
k\geq 1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,t\in[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,t\in[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1})\big|\tau_{k}=s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k}>t-s\big|\tau_{k}=s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\mu_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}>t-s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq s).

Consequently, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{1}>t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,t\in[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{1}>t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\mu_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}>t-s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}F_{s}.

(3.7)

An analogous identity can be deduced for
\mu_{t}
. Indeed, similarly to (
3.5
), we have

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)}\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau^{\prime}>t)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>s)}{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\mu_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}>t-s\bigr).

Then we can proceed in the same fashion as below (
3.5
) to derive

\mu_{t}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{1}>t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\mu_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\mu_{s}}>t-s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t).

Since
\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)=F_{t}
, the right-hand side above coincides with the right-hand side of Equation (
3
), so we can conclude that
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
. Hence, we have constructed a weak solution
X
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) for which
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
.

It remains to establish uniqueness in law for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
). To achieve this, we can simply invert the sequence of arguments from above. For any weak solution
\tilde{X}
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) with corresponding hitting times
\tilde{\tau_{k}}
,
k\geq 1
, and law
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})
for
t\geq 0
, it follows that
\tilde{F}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\tau}_{k}\leq t)=-\log\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\tau}_{1}>t)
. Then we proceed as below Equation (
3
) to show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tau_{1}>t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tilde{\tau}_{1}>t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\tilde{\mu}_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\tilde{\mu}_{s}}>t-s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\log\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\tau}_{1}>t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\in\cdot,\,\tilde{\tau}_{1}>t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\bigl(X^{s,\tilde{\mu}_{s}}_{t-s}\in\cdot,\,\tau_{s,\tilde{\mu}_{s}}>t-s\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}F_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}).

Now, let
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
be the unique strong solution to

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}=b\bigl(t,\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t},\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}),a(t,\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

with initial condition
\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{0}=\xi
. By uniqueness in law, the distributions of
(\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t\land\tilde{\tau}^{\prime}})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{X}_{t\land\tilde{\tau}_{1}})_{t\geq 0}
coincide, which means that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t}|\tilde{\tau}_{1}>t)=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}|\tilde{\tau}^{\prime}>t)
. Consequently,
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
is a weak solution to the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
2.1
) which exhibits uniqueness in law by Proposition
2.4
. Hence,
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}_{t})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
must coincide with the distribution
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
from the solution constructed above. But the SDE (
2.1
), where the flow of measures
\mu
is viewed as a fixed input, has a unique in law solution, so we can conclude that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})=\mathcal{L}(X)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Extending the work of Burdzy, Hołyst, Ingerman & March
[
3
]
, Tough & Nolen
[
18
]
introduced a particle system associated with McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) in the uncontrolled setting, where, upon hitting
\partial D
, a particle is reinserted at a position sampled uniformly at random amongst the current locations of the remaining particles. They show (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 2.9]
) that, under suitable continuity assumptions on the (uncontrolled) drift coefficient
b
, the empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}
of the particle system of size
N\geq 1
converges in probability to the conditional law
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}_{t}|\tau^{\prime}>t)
, where
X^{\prime}
solves the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
). The converge is with respect to the weak convergence of measures on the space
\mathcal{P}(D)
and locally uniform in time. In view of Theorem
3.1
,
\mu^{N}_{t}
also converges to the time marginal of the Fleming–Viot dynamics of McKean–Vlasov type (
3.1
) and it stands to reason that this convergence can be extended to whole trajectories of the particles.

Let us further note that
[
18
, Theorem 2.9]
shows that the average number of reinsertions
F^{N}_{t}
in the particle system up to time
t
tends to
-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)
. This is in accordance with our finding that
F_{t}=-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)
and implies that
F^{N}_{t}\to F_{t}
in probability locally uniformly in time.

We can now introduce a control problem for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) in closed-loop form, which generalises the control problem for the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
). The controller’s objective is to maximise the reward functional

J_{\textup{FV}}(a)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}f\bigl(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},a(t,X_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t-cF_{T}+g(\mu_{T})\biggr]

(3.8)

subject to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) over all measurable functions
a\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to A
. Here
F_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k}\leq t)
and
c\geq 0
captures the cost of reinsertion. If
c=0
, it follows from Theorem
3.1
that
J_{\textup{FV}}(a)=J_{\textup{CP}}(a)
, where
J_{\textup{CP}}
is the closed-loop reward functional for the control of conditional processes defined above Proposition
2.4
. Hence, the control problem for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
3.1
) and for the conditional McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.1
) are equivalent. Even if
c>0
,
J_{\textup{FV}}(a)
can be written in terms of the conditional dynamics (
1.1
) by using the correspondence
F_{t}=-\log\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}>t)
established in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. We leave the analysis of this McKean–Vlasov control problem for future work.

Appendix A
Auxiliary Results

Proposition A.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy the Poincaré–Zaremba cone condition and assume
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is invertible. Let
W
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, let
\beta=(\beta_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process, and define
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by
X_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sigma W_{t}
for an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\xi
with values in
\partial D
. Then a.s. for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
t\in(0,\epsilon]
such that
X_{t}\notin\bar{D}
.

Proof.

First, we reduce to the case that
\beta
vanishes and
\sigma=I_{d}
. Using that
\beta
is uniformly bounded and
\sigma
is invertible, we can apply the change of measure

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}=\mathcal{E}\biggl(\int_{0}^{\cdot}-\sigma^{-1}\beta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\biggr)_{1},

which is well-defined because the stochastic exponential is a martingale by Novikov’s condition. Under
\mathbb{Q}
, the process
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by
B_{t}=\int_{0}^{t\land 1}\beta_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+W_{t}
is a Brownian motion. Now, since we are interested in proving that a statement has probability one and
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
, we may show the result under
\mathbb{Q}
, where
X
is driftless. Thus, we may as well assume that
\beta
vanishes. Next, since
\sigma
is invertible, we may consider the process
X^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by
X^{\prime}_{t}=\sigma^{-1}X_{t}=\sigma^{-1}\xi+W_{t}
. Let us set
D^{\prime}=\sigma^{-1}D=\{\sigma^{-1}x\mathpunct{:}x\in D\}
, so that
D^{\prime}
satisfies the Poincaré–Zaremba cone condition,
\partial D^{\prime}=\{\sigma^{-1}x\mathpunct{:}x\in\partial D\}
, and
\bar{D}^{\prime}=\{\sigma^{-1}x\mathpunct{:}x\in\partial\bar{D}\}
. The process
X^{\prime}
is started from the
\partial D^{\prime}
-valued random variable
\sigma^{-1}\xi
and a.s. for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
there exists
t\in(0,\epsilon]
such that
X_{t}\notin\bar{D}
if and only if a.s. for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
there exists
t\in(0,\epsilon]
such that
X^{\prime}_{t}\notin\bar{D}^{\prime}
. Consequently, it suffices to establish the proposition for
\sigma=I_{d}
.

Next, we want to further reduce to the case that
\xi=x_{0}
for some
x_{0}\in\partial D
. For any
A\in\mathcal{F}
, we can write
\mathbb{P}(A)=\int_{\partial D}\mathbb{P}(A|\xi=x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\xi)(x)
. Hence, if we can prove the proposition under the law
\mathbb{P}_{x}=\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\xi=x)
for
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
-a.e.
x\in\partial D
, then the general case follows as well. However, for
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
-a.e.
x\in\partial D
,
W
is a Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\xi=x)
and
X
satisfies
X_{t}=x+W_{t}
, meaning that the assumptions of the proposition hold with
\xi=x
. Consequently, we are done if we can prove the proposition for the case that
\xi=x
for some
x\in\partial D
. We shall do this next.

Owing to the Poincaré–Zaremba cone condition, we can find an open cone
C
with apex
x_{0}
and a
\delta>0
such that
C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\subset\bar{D}^{c}
. We must show that a.s. for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
there exists
t\in(0,\epsilon]
such that
X_{t}\notin\bar{D}
. Note that this event, call it
E
, belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{W}_{0+}
, where
(\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the filtration generated by
W
and
(\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t+})_{t\geq 0}
is its right-continuous extension. Hence, Blumenthal’s zero-one law for Brownian motion implies that
E
occurs either with probability zero or one. Consequently, it is enough to show that
\mathbb{P}(E)>0
. However, by continuity from above of the measure
\mathbb{P}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(E)=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,\epsilon]\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\in\bar{D}^{c}\bigr)\geq\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,\epsilon]\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr).

(A.1)

Now, the scaling properties of Brownian motion imply that the process
W^{\epsilon}=(W^{\epsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by
W^{\epsilon}_{t}=\epsilon^{-1/2}W_{\epsilon t}
is a Brownian motion. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,\epsilon]\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,1]\mathpunct{:}X_{\epsilon t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,1]\mathpunct{:}x+\epsilon^{1/2}W^{\epsilon}_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,1]\mathpunct{:}x+W^{\epsilon}_{t}\in C\cap B_{\epsilon^{-1/2}\delta}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,1]\mathpunct{:}x+W_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr),

where we used in the third equality that
C
is a cone with apex
x
. The probability on the right-hand side is clearly positive, so in view of (
A.1
), we have

\mathbb{P}(E)\geq\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,\epsilon]\mathpunct{:}X_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists t\in(0,1]\mathpunct{:}x+W_{t}\in C\cap B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)>0,

as required. This concludes the proof.
∎
