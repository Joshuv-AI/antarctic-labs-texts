Lower bounds on the essential dimension of reductive groups Thanks: Danny Ofek was partially supported by a graduate fellowship from the University of British Columbia.
Danny Ofek

Abstract
. We introduce a technique for proving lower bounds on the essential dimension of split reductive groups. As an application, we strengthen the best previously known lower bounds for various split simple algebraic groups, most notably for the exceptional group E 8 E_{8} . In the case of the projective linear group PGL n \operatorname{PGL}_{n} , we recover A. Merkurjev’s celebrated lower bound with a simplified proof. Our technique relies on decompositions of loop torsors over valued fields due to P. Gille and A. Pianzola. Résumé (Bornes inférieures sur la dimension essentielle des groupes réductifs). Nous introduisons une méthode pour établir des bornes inférieures sur la dimension essentielle d’un groupe réductif déployé. À titre d’application, nous renforçons les meilleures bornes inférieures connues jusqu’à présent pour divers groupes algébriques simples déployés, notamment pour le groupe exceptionnel E 8 E_{8} . Dans le cas du groupe projectif linéaire PGL n \operatorname{PGL}_{n} , nous redémontrons, par une preuve simplifiée, la célèbre borne inférieure due à A. Merkurjev. Notre approche repose sur les décompositions de torseurs de lacets sur des corps valués, dues à P. Gille et A. Pianzola.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a smooth linear algebraic group over a field
k_{0}
and
\gamma\in H^{1}(L,G)
a
G
-torsor over a field
k_{0}\subset L
. A
field of definition
for
\gamma
is a subfield
k_{0}\subset F\subset L
such that
\gamma
lies in the image of the natural map:

H^{1}(F,G)\to H^{1}(L,G).

The
essential dimension
of
\gamma
is the minimal number of parameters needed to define
\gamma
. It is given by the formula:

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)=\min\Big\{\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F)\mid F\ \text{is a field of definition of }\gamma\Big\}.

The
essential dimension
of
G
is defined as the supremum
\operatorname{ed}(G)=\sup\{\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)\}
taken over all overfields
k_{0}\subset L
and torsors
\gamma\in H^{1}(L,G)
. It should be thought of as the minimal number of algebraically independent parameters needed to define an arbitrary
G
-torsor. Often
G
-torsors correspond bijectively to a class of algebraic objects, in which case
\operatorname{ed}(G)
is the minimal number of independent parameters needed to define a member of that class. For example:

•

The essential dimension of the symmetric group
S_{d}
is the number of independent variables required to define an arbitrary separable field extension of degree
d
. Equivalently, it is the number of parameters needed to define a generic polynomial of degree
d
up to Tschirnhaus transformations. In this form, mathematicians have tried to compute it as early as the 17th century. For an overview, see
[
23
,
10
,
9
]
.

•

The essential dimension of
\operatorname{PGL}_{d}
is the number of independent variables required to define an arbitrary central division algebra of degree
d
. This quantity has been studied since generic division algebras were first defined by C. Procesi
[
52
, Section 2]
.

The problem of computing
\operatorname{ed}(G)
for a general algebraic group
G
has been studied by many authors since it was first posed by J. Buhler-Z. Reichstein
[
9
]
and Reichstein
[
53
]
. For a comprehensive survey of the developments in the field, we refer the reader to
[
45
]
.

Let
p
be a prime integer. The
essential dimension at
p
of
G
, denoted
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
, measures how many parameters are required to construct an arbitrary
G
-torsor up to prime-to-
p
extensions. See Section
2
for a precise definition. The inequality
\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
always holds, and almost all existing techniques to prove lower bounds on
\operatorname{ed}(G)
apply to
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
as well; see
[
54
, Section 5]
. The same is true of the technique introduced in this paper.

1.1.
Overview of previous techniques

Z. Reichstein-B. Youssin gave the first systematic way to prove lower bounds on the essential dimension of algebraic groups. We recall their main theorem, commonly referred to as “the fixed-point method” because it was proven by an analysis of fixed points on generically free
G
-varieties. See
[
28
,
14
]
for generalizations to positive characteristic.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
56
, Theorem 7.7]

Assume
G
is defined over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero
k_{0}
and
G^{\circ}
is semisimple. Let
A\subset G(k_{0})
be a finite abelian group and
p
a prime number.

(1)

If
C_{G}(A\cap G^{\circ})
is finite, then
\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{rank}(A)
.

(2)

If
A
is a
p
-group and
C_{G}(A\cap G^{\circ})
is finite, then
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{rank}(A)
.

While Theorem
1.1
applies to many groups, it usually gives bounds which are far from tight. This is partially explained by the fact that the
G
-torsors witnessing the lower bound can be constructed over an iterated Laurent series field
k_{0}(\!(t_{1})\!)\dots(\!(t_{r})\!)
which is a relatively simple field when
k_{0}
is algebraically closed (here
r=\operatorname{rank}(A)
).

P. Brosnan-Reichstein-A. Vistoli
[
8
,
6
]
and later N. Karpenko-A. Merkurjev
[
33
]
introduced stack-theoretic techniques to construct
G
-torsors of high essential dimension over function fields of Severi-Brauer varieties. Stack-theoretic techniques give much stronger lower bounds than is possible using Theorem
1.1
for some groups, like finite
p
-groups and algebraic tori
[
33
,
37
]
. However, they give trivial lower bounds for most semisimple groups, including all adjoint simple groups. In
[
44
]
, Merkurjev overcame this limitation of the stack-theoretic methods for adjoint groups of type
A_{n}
by proving
\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{n};p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(T;p)
for a certain torus
T
. He then computed
\operatorname{ed}(T;p)
using
[
37
]
to obtain the lower bounds

(1.1)

\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{r}};p)\geq(r-1)p^{r}+1

which are orders of magnitude stronger than the lower bounds previously obtained by Theorem
1.1
. Merkurjev’s arguments are specific to groups of type
A_{n}
because they rely on explicit computations in the Brauer group (see
[
13
]
for generalizations to
\operatorname{SL}_{n}/\mu_{d}
). We note that a proof of (
1.1
) for
p=r=2
was first announced by M. Rost
[
57
]
. Merkurjev proved (
1.1
) for
r=2
and
p
arbitrary by different means in
[
43
]
.

1.2.
Main results

In this paper, we introduce a technique to prove lower bounds on
\operatorname{ed}(G),\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
, which applies whenever
G^{\circ}
is split reductive. We proceed in two steps:

(1)

We first prove
\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))
for any finite diagonalizable subgroup
A\subset G
satisfying certain conditions.

(2)

We choose
A
in a systematic way, so that
C_{G}(A)
is an extension of a torus by a finite group. This allows us to apply the results of
[
38
]
to give a strong lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))
.

In this way we obtain new lower bounds on the essential dimension of some simple groups as well as recover (
1.1
), see Theorem
1.5
below and Section
14
. The next theorem gives sufficient conditions for the inequality
\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))
to hold. Note that we do not assume
G^{\circ}
is split. Thus the first step in our technique works more generally and the assumption that
G^{\circ}
is split is only needed for the second step. Recall that a
G
-torsor
[c_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}(F,G)
is called
anisotropic
if the twisted group
{}_{c}G
contains no copy of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. A finite algebraic group
A
over
k_{0}
is called
diagonalizable
, if it is isomorphic to
\mu_{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mu_{n_{r}}
for some
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}
coprime to
\operatorname{char}k_{0}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a smooth linear algebraic group over a field
k_{0}
. Assume either
k_{0}
is perfect or
G^{\circ}
is reductive. Let
A\subset G
be a finite diagonalizable subgroup.

(1)

Let
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
be a prime. If
A
is a
p
-group and
C_{G}(A)
admits an anisotropic torsor over some
p
-closed field
k_{0}\subset k
, then we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p).

(2)

Assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}
is good for
G
(see Definition
2.1
). If
C_{G}(A)
admits an anisotropic torsor over some field
k_{0}\subset k
, then we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A)).

Let
F
be a Henselian valued field with value group of finite rank. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on the decompositions of
loop torsors
over
F
. Loop torsors and their decompositions were introduced by P. Gille-A. Pianzola for iterated Laurent series over a characteristic zero field in the context of the classification of loop algebras
[
26
]
. We will use both
[
26
]
and the recent generalizations to valuation rings of positive characteristic obtained by Gille
[
25
]
.

Remark 1.3
.

Theorem
1.1
is a special case of Theorem
1.2
. Indeed,
if
C_{G}(A\cap G^{\circ})
is finite, then so is
C_{G}(A)
.
Combining
[
9
, Lemma 4.1]
and
[
9
, Theorem 6]
gives:

\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))\geq\operatorname{ed}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A).

Since
C_{G}(A)
is finite, all
C_{G}(A)
-torsors are anisotropic. Therefore Theorem
1.2
implies:

\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))\geq\operatorname{rank}(A).

Note that the above argument works under the assumption
|C_{G}(A)|<\infty
, which is strictly weaker than
|C_{G}(A\cap G^{\circ})|<\infty
.

As noted in the previous remark, any
C_{G}(A)
-torsor is anisotropic when
C_{G}(A)
is finite. However, in general, there is no simple criterion to determine whether
C_{G}(A)
admits anisotropic torsors. J. Tits classified the simple simply connected split groups that do not admit anisotropic torsors
[
65
]
. For a generalization of his work to arbitrary simple groups and for semisimple groups of certain types, see
[
49
]
.
We have no examples where the inequalities of Theorem
1.2
fail, so it is natural to ask if one may improve the theorem as follows:

Question 1.4
.

Is the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
true without the assumption that
C_{G}(A)
admits anisotropic torsors?

After the proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section
10
, we give an example where all
C_{G}(A)
-torsors are isotropic and our proof breaks down. However, we have no reason to expect a negative answer to Question
1.4
.

In Section
12
, we give a streamlined root-theoretic approach to choosing diagonalizable subgroups of split groups that satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.2
. This leads to the following new lower bounds:

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}\neq 2,3
.

(1)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};2)=\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{HSpin}_{16};2)\geq 56

(2)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};3)\geq 13

(3)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{6}^{\mathrm{ad}};3)\geq 6

(4)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{7}^{\mathrm{ad}};2)\geq 19

(5)

If
n=2^{r}m\geq 4
for
r\geq 1
and
m
is odd, then

\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n};2)\geq(r-1)2^{r+1}+n.

(6)

If
n\geq 3
is odd, then

\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n};2)\geq 3n-4.

The inequalities in Theorem
1.5
improve on long-standing lower bounds. To put them into context, we include a list indicating the best lower and upper bounds for these groups that were known prior to this paper.

Previously known bounds
.

Assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}\neq 2,3
.

(1)

120\geq\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};2)\geq 9

(2)

73\geq\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};3)\geq 5

(3)

21\geq\operatorname{ed}(E_{6}^{\mathrm{ad}};3)\geq 4

(4)

57\geq\operatorname{ed}(E_{7}^{\mathrm{ad}};2)\geq 8

(5)

If
n=2^{r}m
for some
r\geq 0
and
m\geq 3
is odd, then

(m-1)2^{2(r+1)}-n\geq\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n};2)\geq 2n-2.

(6)

If
n=2^{r}
for some
r\geq 2
, then

n^{2}-n\geq\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n};2)\geq r2^{r}.

1.1
; see
[

,
28
]
. For references for the upper bounds in (1)-(4), see
[

, 3h]
.
The upper bounds in (5) and (6) are due to M. Macdonald
[

, Section 0.2]
. For the lower bounds in (5),(6), see
[

and

Recall that the
reductive rank
of
G
is the dimension of a maximal torus
T\subset G
. Clearly if
H\subset G
is a subgroup, then
\operatorname{rank}(H)\leq\operatorname{rank}(G)
. A subgroup

is said to be of

if
\operatorname{rank}(H)=\operatorname{rank}(G)
. In Section

to prove

\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(H),\ \ \operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(H;p)

]

1.3.
Outline of the paper

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
Sections
2
-

In Section

if one ignores prime-to-
p
extensions:

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma;p)=\min\Big\{\operatorname{ed}(\gamma_{L})\mid F\subset L\text{ is a prime-to-}p\ \text{field extension}\Big\}.

We define the
essential dimension at
p
of
G
by
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)=\sup\{\operatorname{ed}(\gamma;p)\}
, the supremum taken over all
G
-torsors as before.

3.
Preliminaries about Henselian valued fields

3.1.
A fundamental exact sequence

In this section and in all remaining sections we keep the notation from Section
2
.
The Galois extensions
F\subset F_{\operatorname{in}}\subset F_{\operatorname{tr}}
induce an exact sequence

(3.1)

1\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)\to\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)\to 1.

The groups
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})
and
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
can be understood in terms of the value group
\Gamma_{F}
and the residue field
k
respectively.
There is a natural isomorphism
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)\cong\operatorname{Gal}(k)
given by taking an automorphism
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
to the unique automorphism
\overline{\sigma}\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
satisfying for all
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
:

(3.2)

\overline{\sigma}(\overline{x})=\overline{\sigma(x)},

where
\overline{x}\in k_{\operatorname{sep}}
is the residue class of
x

[
64
, Theorems A.23]
.

Convention 3.1
.

We will often identify
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
and
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
using this isomorphism. For example, we might act on
G(F_{\operatorname{in}})
using
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
or act on
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
using
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
.

The group
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})
is the
tame inertia group
of
F
. Let
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime}=\prod_{p\neq\operatorname{char}k}\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
be the prime-to-
\operatorname{char}k
part of
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
and set
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}:=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma_{F},\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})
. There is an isomorphism

(3.3)

\Phi:\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})\tilde{\to}\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}.

The isomorphism
\Phi
is determined by a choice of roots of unity (or equivalently by an isomorphism between
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime}
and the Tate module
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime}(1)=\underset{(n,\operatorname{char}k)=1}{\lim}\mu_{n}
) and by the following equation, which holds for all
n
coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
,
x\in F^{*}
and
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})
:

(3.4)

{\frac{\sigma(x^{1/n})}{x^{1/n}}}={\zeta_{n}}^{\Phi(\sigma)(\nu(x))}.

See
[
64
, Theorem A.24]
for example. Note that the above expression does not depend on a choice of an
n
-th root of
x
because
F_{\operatorname{in}}
contains all
n
-th roots of unity.

Remark 3.2
.

Since we are assuming
\Gamma_{F}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some
r
, we have
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\cong\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{r},\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})\cong\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime r}
. In particular, any finite quotient of
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
is an abelian group of order coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
.

Next we introduce uniformizers. They will help us describe splittings of (
3.1
) in a systematic way.

Definition 3.3
.

A left inverse
\pi:\Gamma_{F}\to F^{*}
to
\nu:F^{*}\to\Gamma_{F}
will be called a
uniformizing parameter
.
Since the group operation in
\Gamma_{F}
is written additively while
F^{*}
is written multiplicatively, it will be convenient for us to use the exponential notation
\pi^{\gamma}
in place of
\pi(\gamma)
, for any

\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
.

The next proposition gives a convenient splitting of (
3.1
). It is originally due to J. Neukirch
[
48
, Satz 2]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\pi:\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}\to F_{\operatorname{tr}}^{*}
be a uniformizer such that
\pi^{\gamma}\in F
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
.

(1)

The field
F_{\pi}:=F(\pi^{\gamma};\gamma\in\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}})
is a complement of
F_{\operatorname{in}}
in
F_{\operatorname{tr}}
. That is, it satisfies
F_{\pi}F_{\operatorname{in}}=F_{\operatorname{tr}}
and
F_{\pi}\cap F_{\operatorname{in}}=F
.

(2)

There exists a unique section
s_{\pi}:\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
of the homomorphism
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)\to\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
in (
3.1
) whose image fixes
F_{\pi}
.

(3)

Let
\Phi:\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}})\to\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
be the isomorphism (
3.3
) and denote by

\overline{s_{\pi}}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)

the composition of
s_{\pi}
with the isomorphism
\operatorname{Gal}(k)\tilde{\to}\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
. There exists an isomorphism:

\Psi_{\pi}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\tilde{\to}\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F),\ (\sigma,f)\mapsto\overline{s}_{\pi}(\sigma)\Phi^{-1}(f)

Here the
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-action on
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
is given by pointwise multiplication by the cyclotomic character
\theta:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})^{*}
.

(4)

For any
(\sigma,f)\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
,
u\in F_{\operatorname{in}}
,
m
coprime to
\operatorname{char}(k)
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
, we have:

(3.5)

\Psi_{\pi}(\sigma,f)(u\pi^{\gamma/m})=\sigma(u)\zeta_{m}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)}\pi^{\gamma/m}.

Here we let
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
act on
F_{\operatorname{in}}
as in Remark
3.1
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
\Gamma_{F}
. The field
F_{n}=F(\pi_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/n},\dots,\pi_{r}^{\gamma_{r}/n})

is totally tamely ramified with value group
\frac{1}{n}\Gamma_{F}
for all
n
coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
. Since
\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}=\bigcup_{n\text{ coprime to }p}\frac{1}{n}\Gamma_{F}

[
64
, Theorem A.24]
, we have:

F_{\pi}=\bigcup_{n\text{ coprime to }p}F_{n}.

Therefore the residue field of
F_{\pi}
is
k
and
\Gamma_{F_{\pi}}=\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}
.
This implies Parts (1) and (2) by
[
48
, Satz 2]
and the discussion following it; see also the proof of
[
19
, Theorem 22.1.1]
. We note that the restriction map
\operatorname{res}^{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}_{F_{\operatorname{in}}}:\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{tr}}/F_{\pi})\to\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F)
is an isomorphism and
s_{\pi}
is the composition of
(\operatorname{res}^{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}_{F_{\operatorname{in}}})^{-1}
with the canonical inclusion
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{tr}}/F_{\pi})\subset\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
. Part (3) follows from the fact that
s_{\pi}
splits the exact sequence (
3.1
); see
[
19
, Corollary 22.1.2]
. To prove Part (4), we compute using Part (3):

\displaystyle\Psi_{\pi}(\sigma,f)(u\pi^{\gamma/m})

\displaystyle=\overline{s}_{\pi}(\sigma)\Phi^{-1}(u\pi^{\gamma/m})

\displaystyle=\overline{s}_{\pi}(\sigma)(u\zeta_{m}^{f(\gamma)}\pi^{\gamma/m})

\displaystyle=\sigma(u\zeta_{m}^{f(\gamma)})\overline{s}_{\pi}(\sigma)(\pi^{\gamma/m})

\displaystyle=\sigma(u)\zeta_{m}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)}\pi^{\gamma/m}.

Here the last three inequalities follow from (
3.4
), (
3.2
) and the fact that
\overline{s}_{\pi}(\sigma)
fixes
F_{\pi}
.
∎

The decomposition
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)\cong\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
of Proposition

(3.6)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{\operatorname{tr}}=F_{\pi}\otimes_{F}F_{\operatorname{in}}}

For example, if

is equipped with the

Remark 3.5
.

From now on, whenever we use a uniformizer
\pi
of

\pi^{\gamma}\in F
for all

3.2.
Extensions of Henselian fields

Let
L/F
be an extension of Henselian fields and denote the corresponding extension of residue fields by
l/k
. Pick separable closures such that
F_{\operatorname{sep}}\subset L_{\operatorname{sep}}
. Then we have
F_{\operatorname{tr}}\subset L_{\operatorname{tr}}
and
F_{\operatorname{in}}\subset L_{\operatorname{in}}
. Therefore there are well-defined restriction maps:

\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(L)\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F),\ \ \operatorname{Gal}(L_{\operatorname{in}}/L)\to\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\operatorname{in}}/F),\ \operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(L_{\operatorname{in}})\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F_{\operatorname{in}}).

There is also a restriction map
\Gamma_{L}^{\vee}\to\Gamma_{F}^{\vee},f\mapsto f_{|F}
, given by restricting a homomorphism
f\in\Gamma_{L}^{\vee}
to
\Gamma_{F}\subset\Gamma_{L}
.
Next we state a functoriality lemma for the decomposition of
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
F\subset L
be an extension of Henselian valued fields and denote by
\nu
the valuation on
L
. Denote the residue field of
F,L
by
k
and
l
respectively. Let
\pi,\tau
be uniformizers for
F_{\operatorname{tr}}
and
L_{\operatorname{tr}}
respectively. Denote the induced section
\overline{s}_{\tau}:\operatorname{Gal}(l)\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(L)
and isomorphisms as in Proposition
3.4
by

\Psi^{F}_{\pi}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F),\ \Psi^{L}_{\tau}:\operatorname{Gal}(l)\ltimes\Gamma_{L}^{\vee}\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(L).

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}
set
u^{\gamma}=\pi^{\gamma}\tau^{-\gamma}
. For any
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(l)
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\chi_{\sigma}\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
satisfying the equation:

(3.7)

\frac{\overline{s}_{\tau}(\sigma)(u^{\gamma/n})}{u^{\gamma/n}}=\zeta^{\chi_{\sigma}(\gamma)}_{n}

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
and
n
coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
.
We have for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(l),f\in\Gamma_{L}^{\vee}
:

\Psi^{L}_{\tau}(\sigma,f)_{|F_{\operatorname{tr}}}=\Psi^{F}_{\pi}(\sigma_{|k_{\operatorname{sep}}},f_{|F}+\theta(\sigma)^{-1}\chi_{\sigma}).

Proof.

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
and
n
coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
,
u^{\gamma/n}
is an
n
-th root of
u^{\gamma}\in L_{\operatorname{in}}
. Since
u^{\gamma}
is a unit and the residue field of
L_{\operatorname{in}}
is separably closed, Hensel’s lemma implies
u^{\gamma/n}\in L_{\operatorname{in}}
. In particular, we have:

\overline{s}_{\tau}(\sigma)(u^{\gamma/n})=\sigma(u^{\gamma/n}).

Therefore for any
v\in F_{\operatorname{in}},\gamma\in\Gamma_{F}
applying (
3.5
) twice gives:

\displaystyle\Psi^{L}_{\tau}(\sigma,f)(v\pi^{\gamma/n})

\displaystyle=\Psi^{L}_{\tau}(\sigma,f)(vu^{\gamma/n}\tau^{\gamma/n})

\displaystyle=^{\eqref{e.phi(1,1)}}\sigma(v)\sigma(u^{\gamma/n})\zeta_{n}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)}\tau^{\gamma/n}

\displaystyle=\sigma(v)\frac{\sigma(u^{\gamma/n})}{u^{\gamma/n}}\zeta_{n}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)}u^{\gamma/n}\tau^{\gamma/n}

\displaystyle=\sigma(v)\zeta_{n}^{\chi_{\sigma}(\gamma)}\zeta_{n}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)}\pi^{\gamma/n}

\displaystyle=\sigma(v)\zeta_{n}^{\theta(\sigma)f(\gamma)+\chi_{\sigma}(\gamma)}\pi^{\gamma/n}

\displaystyle=^{\eqref{e.phi(1,1)}}\Psi_{\pi}^{F}(\sigma_{k_{\operatorname{sep}}},f_{|F}+\theta(\sigma)^{-1}\chi_{\sigma})(v\pi^{\gamma/n})

The claim follows because elements of the form
v\pi^{\gamma/n}
generate
F_{\operatorname{tr}}
over
F
by Proposition
3.4
(1).
∎

Next, we recall A. Ostrowski’s foundational theorem in valuation theory. It will be used often and sometimes implicitly; see
[
19
, Theorem 17.2.1]
for a modern proof.

Theorem 3.7
.

[
50
]

Let
F\subset L
be a finite extension of Henselian valued fields with value groups
\Gamma_{F}\subset\Gamma_{L}
and residue fields
k\subset l
. There exists an integer
\delta_{L/F}
such that:

(3.8)

[L:F]=\delta_{L/F}[\Gamma_{L}:\Gamma_{F}][l:k].

If
\operatorname{char}k>0
, then
\delta_{L/F}
is a power of
\operatorname{char}k
and
\delta_{L/F}=1
otherwise. In particular, both
[\Gamma_{L}:\Gamma_{F}]
and
[l:k]
divide
[L:F]
.

We finish this section with a technical lemma that will be used to avoid working with infinite algebraic extensions. Recall that a finite extension
L/F
is tamely ramified if and only if
[\Gamma_{L}:\Gamma_{F}]
is coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
,
l/k
is separable and
\delta_{L/F}=1
in (
3.8
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be an ascending union of a countable chain of valued field
F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset\dots
and let
L/F
be a finite tamely ramified Galois extension. For all large enough
i
there exists a tamely ramified Galois extension
L_{i}/F_{i}
such that
L=L_{i}\otimes_{F_{i}}F
and the restriction map
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)\to\operatorname{Gal}(L_{i}/F_{i})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
x\in L
be an element such that
L=F(x)
and let
P(t)\in F[t]
be the minimal polynomial of
x
. For large enough
i
,
P(t)\in F_{i}[t]
and so
P(t)
is also the minimal polynomial of
x
over
F_{i}
. Set
L_{i}:=F_{i}(x)
and note

[L_{i}:F_{i}]=\deg P=[L:F].

By enlarging
i
further we can make
P(t)
factor fully over
L_{i}
so that
L_{i}/F_{i}
is Galois. Let
l_{i}/k_{i}
denote the residue field extension of
L_{i}/F_{i}
. A similar argument shows that
l_{i}/k_{i}
is Galois of degree
[l:k]
for large enough
i
. One also easily checks that for large enough
i
we have
[\Gamma_{L_{i}}:\Gamma_{F_{i}}]=[\Gamma_{L}:\Gamma_{F}]
. Combining all of this together, we find that for large
i
:

[L_{i}:F_{i}]=[L:F]=[\Gamma_{L}:\Gamma_{F}][l:k]=[\Gamma_{L_{i}}:\Gamma_{F_{i}}][l_{i}:k_{i}].

Therefore
L_{i}/F_{i}
is defectless (i.e.
\delta_{L_{i}/F_{i}}=1
) and tamely ramified. The restriction map
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)\to\operatorname{Gal}(L_{i}/F_{i})
is an isomorphism because it is injective and

|\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)|=[L:F]=[L_{i}:F_{i}]=|\operatorname{Gal}(L_{i}/F_{i})|.

Indeed, any two different elements
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
differ on
L_{i}
for large enough
i
.
∎

4.
Preliminaries about anisotropic torsors

In this final section of preliminaries, we record a couple of facts about versal torsors and anisotropic torsors. We first recall the definition of a versal torsor.

Definition 4.1
.

A versal torsor
\gamma\in H^{1}(l,G)
is the generic fiber of a
G
-torsor for the étale topology
T\to B
such that:

(1)

B
is a geometrically irreducible smooth variety over
k_{0}
.

(2)

For any field extension
E/k_{0}
with
|E|=\infty
and any open dense subset
U\subset B
, there exists

such that:

\gamma\cong T_{u}:=T\times_{u}\operatorname{Spec}E.

Note that there exists a versal
G
-torsor
[
61
, Section 5.3]
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
G
-torsor
T
over a
k_{0}
-scheme
B
is called isotropic if there exists an embedding
\mathbb{G}_{m}\to{}_{T}G
over
B
, where
{}_{T}G=\operatorname{Aut}_{G}(T)
is the group of
G
-equivariant automorphisms of

; see
[
24
, Page 6]
.

The following spreading out lemma shows that the notion of isotropy plays well with inductive limits of base rings.

.

Let

be a
G
-torsor over an irreducible variety
B
over

.

(1)

If

is isotropic, then so is

for any
k_{0}
-algebra

and

.

(2)

for all

. Let

Proof.

Let

be the twisted group over

(1)

If

, then

is anisotropic.

(2)

Let

be a prime. If

\eta
over some
p
-closed field

, then
\gamma_{k^{(p)}}
is anisotropic for any

.

Proof.

Assume
\gamma
is the generic fiber of a
G

as in Definition

. Let

be an arbitrary
G
-torsor over a field extension

.

(1) Let

be a generic point of

is isotropic. By Lemma

(3), there exists a dense open

is isotropic. We may assume

. By versality, there exists a point

. Therefore

(1). This proves Part (1).

(2) Assume

is
p

is isotropic for some prime-to-

(4). The extension

. By Lemma
4.3

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

be a
p
-closed field for some prime

. Let

. If the degree of

Proof.

Let
u:\operatorname{Spec}l\to U
be a point. The scheme
\operatorname{Spec}l\times_{U}V
is finite of degree coprime to

over
\operatorname{Spec}l
by our assumption on

. Therefore
\operatorname{Spec}l\times_{U}V=\operatorname{Spec}R
for some finite

. By the structure theorem for Artin rings there exist local Artin rings

such that:

R\cong R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{n}

as

\dim_{l}(R_{i})=d\operatorname{len}(R_{i}),

where

gives a section of the inclusion

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Spec}R=\operatorname{Spec}l\times_{U}V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Spec}l}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}(u)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}

∎

The next lemma will be used in Section

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f:G\to H
be a homomorphism of smooth linear algebraic groups over

. Let
k_{0}\subset k
be a field and denote the induced pushforward map by
f_{*}:H^{1}(k,G)\to H^{1}(k,H)
. If

is anisotropic and one of the following conditions hold, then
f_{*}(\gamma)
is anisotropic.

(1)

The restriction of
f
to

is an isomorphism onto

(2)

The base field

is perfect and

is a quotient map with a unipotent kernel.

Proof.

Assume
\gamma=[c_{\sigma}]
for some cocycle

and denote its pushforward to

. Consider the homomorphism of twisted groups defined by

{}_{c}f:{}_{c}G\to{}_{f_{*}(c)}H.

(1) By assumption
{}_{c}f
restricts to an isomorphism
{}_{c}G^{\circ}\cong{}_{f_{*}(c)}H^{\circ}
. A split torus

is contained in

because it is connected. Since
{}_{c}G^{\circ}\cong{}_{f_{*}(c)}H^{\circ}

. Therefore

(2) Assume

(4.1)

. We obtain an embedding

∎

5.
Loop torsors

Loop torsors were introduced by Gille and Pianzola in the monograph
[
26
]
. They can be defined in a few equivalent ways. The useful point of view for us will be:

Definition 5.1
.

A torsor
\gamma\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G)
is called a
loop torsor
if it can be represented by a
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
-cocycle taking values in
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
. We denote the subset of all loop torsors by
H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)\subset H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G)
.

We use loop torsors for two reasons. They can be decomposed in tandem with the decomposition of
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
, and they are integral, so one can apply the homomorphism
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
to obtain
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
-valued cocycles from them. We start by describing the decomposition of loop torsors introduced in
[
26
, Section 3.3]
.

Let
\Psi^{F}_{\pi}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
be the isomorphism induced by a uniformizer
\pi
as in Proposition
3.4
.
Any loop cocycle
c_{\tau}\in Z^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F),G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
defines a
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-cocycle
a_{\sigma}\in Z^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
and a homomorphism
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
by the formulas:

a_{\sigma}=c_{\Psi_{\pi}(\sigma,0)},\varphi(x)=c_{\Psi_{\pi}(1,x)}.

Note that
\varphi
is a homomorphism because the tame inertia group of
F
acts fixes
G(F_{\operatorname{in}})
. The cocycle
a_{\sigma}
is called
the arithmetic part
of
c_{\tau}
and
\varphi
is called its
geometric part
. Clearly
c_{\tau}
is uniquely determined by
a_{\sigma}
and
\varphi
. We will use the notation:

c_{\tau}=\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi},

to denote a loop cocycle
c_{\tau}
with arithmetic part
a_{\sigma}
and geometric part
\varphi
. The corresponding loop torsor will be denoted:

[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}:=[\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}]\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G).

Any torsor
\gamma\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)
is by definition of the form
\gamma=[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}
for some
a_{\sigma},\varphi
, but the symbol
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
is not defined for an arbitrary pair
a_{\sigma},\varphi
.

Definition 5.2
.

We will call a cocycle
a_{\sigma}\in Z^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
and homomorphism
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})

compatible
if there exists a cocycle
c_{\tau}\in Z^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F),G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
such that
c_{\tau}=\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
.

Let
\theta:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})^{*}
be the cyclotomic character. One can check that a cocycle
a_{\sigma}
and a homomorphism
\varphi
as above are compatible if and only if they satisfy:

(5.1)

a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\varphi(f)a_{\sigma}^{-1}=\varphi(f)^{\theta(\sigma)},

for all
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee},\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
; see
[
26
, Lemma 3.7]
for a similar computation. Using this, we see that centralizers of finite abelian subgroups are a natural source for loop torsors.

Example 5.3
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
be a continuous homomorphism onto
A(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
for some finite diagonalizable
\mathcal{O}
-subgroup
A\subset G_{\mathcal{O}}
and let
a_{\sigma}\in Z^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C_{G}(A)(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
a cocycle. The formula:

c_{\Psi_{\pi}(\sigma,f)}=a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\varphi(f)

defines a loop cocycle
c_{\tau}=\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
because

{}^{\sigma}\varphi(f)=\varphi(f)^{\theta(\sigma)}

for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k),f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
by (
2.1
). That is,
a_{\sigma}
and
\varphi
are compatible in the sense of Definition
5.2
.

One can often assume a loop torsor is of the form given in Example
5.3
using the next lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
c_{\tau}=\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
be a loop cocycle. If the group

\overline{A}(k_{\operatorname{sep}}):=\{\overline{\varphi(f)}\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\mid f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\}

is the
k_{\operatorname{sep}}
-points of a diagonalizable
k
-subgroup
\overline{A}\subset G_{k}
, then
\overline{a_{\sigma}}\in C_{G}(\overline{A})(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
.

Proof.

Let
\theta:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})^{*}
denote the cyclotomic character. Since
c_{\tau}
is a cocycle, (
5.1
) gives:

a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\varphi(f)a_{\sigma}^{-1}=\varphi(f)^{\theta(\sigma)}.

Reducing modulo the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
we get:

\overline{a_{\sigma}}{}^{\sigma}\overline{\varphi(f)}\overline{a_{\sigma}}^{-1}=\overline{\varphi(f)}^{\theta(\sigma)}.

We have

{}^{\sigma}\overline{\varphi(f)}=\overline{\varphi(f)}^{\theta(\sigma)}

because
\overline{A}
is diagonalizable by Fact
2.2
. Therefore
\overline{a_{\sigma}}
centralizes
{}^{\sigma}\overline{\varphi(f)}
. Since
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\overline{a_{\sigma}}\in C_{G}(\overline{A})(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
.
∎

We finish this section by examining when two loop cocycles
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi},\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
give rise to the same class in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Two loop cocycles
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi},\langle b_{\sigma},\psi\rangle_{\pi}
are cohomologous in
Z^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
if and only if there exists
s\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
such that:

(5.2)

s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s=b_{\sigma},\ \ s^{-1}\varphi s=\psi.

Proof.

For any
s\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
,
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k),f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee},\tau:=\Psi_{\pi}(\sigma,f)
we have:

\displaystyle s^{-1}\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}(\tau){}^{\tau}s

\displaystyle=s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\varphi(f){}^{\sigma}s

\displaystyle=s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s{}^{\sigma}(s^{-1}\varphi(f)s)=\langle s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s,s^{-1}\varphi s\rangle_{\pi}(\tau).

This proves the claim.
∎

6.
A theorem of Gille and Pianzola

Recall that a valued field
(F,\nu)
is an iterated Laurent series field, if it is isomorphic to
k(\!(t_{1})\!)\dots(\!(t_{r})\!)
equipped with the usual
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
-adic valuation as in (
2.2
).
In this section we prove the following adaptation of a theorem of Gille and Pianzola.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be an iterated Laurent series field and assume
G^{\circ}
is reductive. Two anisotropic loop torsors
[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi},[b_{\sigma},\psi]_{\pi}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)_{\operatorname{an}}
are equal if and only if there exists
s\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
such that:

(6.1)

s^{-1}\overline{a_{\sigma}}{}^{\sigma}s=\overline{b_{\sigma}},\ \ s^{-1}\overline{\varphi(f)}s=\overline{\psi(f)}

for all
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee},\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
.

Since
F
is an iterated Laurent series field, it admits a coefficient field. That is, there exists an inclusion
k\subset\mathcal{O}
splitting the map onto the residue field
\mathcal{O}\to k
. This inclusion extends to an embedding
k_{\operatorname{sep}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
splitting the homomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}\to k_{\operatorname{sep}}
, which in turn gives a section
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\subset G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
of the homomorphism
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
. Gille proved Theorem
6.1
in case
a_{\sigma},\varphi,b_{\sigma},\psi
all take values in
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\subset G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})

[
25
, Corollary 4.11]
(before that Gille-Pianzola proved it under the additional assumption
\operatorname{char}k=0
in
[
26
]
).
Thus in order to prove Theorem
6.1
it suffices to establish the following proposition:

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\subset G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
denote the inclusion induced from the inclusion of a coefficient field
k_{\operatorname{sep}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
. The corresponding map of cohomology sets

(6.2)

H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(k_{\operatorname{sep}}))\to H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))

is a bijection.

For the proof of this proposition we will need the following consequences of Hensel’s lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\varphi,\psi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
be two continuous homomorphisms and let
\overline{\varphi},\overline{\psi}:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
be the composition of
\varphi,\psi
with the reduction homomorphism
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}}),g\mapsto\overline{g}
. If
s\overline{\varphi}s^{-1}=\overline{\psi}
for some
s\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
, then
\tilde{s}\varphi\tilde{s}^{-1}=\psi
for some
\tilde{s}\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to A
be a large enough finite quotient of
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
so that both
\varphi
and
\psi
factor through
A
. Let
\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}:A\to G_{\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}}
denote the induced homomorphisms and denote by
\operatorname{Tran}_{G}(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
the transporter of
\varphi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime}
. This is a closed subscheme of
G_{\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}}
such that for any ring homomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}\to R
:

\operatorname{Tran}_{G}(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})(R)=\{g\in G(R)\mid g\varphi^{\prime}_{R}g^{-1}=\psi^{\prime}_{R}\}.

Note that
A
is a finite abelian group of order coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
by Remark
3.2
. Therefore
\operatorname{Tran}_{G}(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
is smooth because
A
is of multiplicative type; see
[
27
, Expose XI, Corollaire 5.2]
. By assumption
\operatorname{Tran}_{G}(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\neq\emptyset
. It follows from Hensel’s lemma that
\operatorname{Tran}_{G}(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\neq\emptyset
and the result follows.
∎

Next we restate
[
27
, Expose XXIV,Proposition 8.1]
in the language of Galois cohomology.

Lemma 6.4
.

For any smooth algebraic group
C
over
\mathcal{O}
, the reduction homomorphism
C(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to C(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
induces a bijection on Galois cohomology sets:

H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\to H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(k_{\operatorname{sep}})).

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
is the strict Henselization of
\mathcal{O}
. Any
C
-torsor for the étale topology over
\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}
is split by the universal cover
\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}
because
\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}
is local. The fundamental group of
\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}
is
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
by
[
46
, Section 5, Example 5.2.d]
. Therefore
[
24
, Section 2.2]
gives isomorphisms

H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\tilde{\to}H^{1}_{\text{\'{e}tale}}(\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O},C),\ \ H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(k_{\operatorname{sep}}))\tilde{\to}H^{1}_{\text{\'{e}tale}}(\operatorname{Spec}k,C).

Now the lemma follows from
[
27
, Expose XXIV,Proposition 8.1]
, which states that the bottom row in the following commutative square is a bijection:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C(k_{\operatorname{sep}}))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{1}_{\text{\'{e}tale}}(\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O},C)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{1}_{\text{\'{e}tale}}(\operatorname{Spec}k,C)}}

∎

Next we prove that any loop torsor is represented by a cocycle taking values in
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
. Note that
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
acts on the coefficient field
k_{\operatorname{sep}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
and on
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\subset G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
because
k\subset F
is fixed by
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
and
k_{\operatorname{sep}}/k
is Galois. Let
[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)
be a loop torsor. Denote the images of
a_{\sigma}
under the reduction homomorphism
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
by
\overline{a}_{\sigma}
and define
\overline{\varphi}:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
by the formula

\overline{\varphi}(f)=\overline{\varphi(f)}

for all
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
. By Remark
2.4
, the map :

\langle\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}),\ (\sigma,f)\mapsto\overline{a}_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\overline{\varphi}(f)

is a
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
-cocycle in
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
. Therefore
\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}
are compatible in the sense of Definition
5.2
and the loop torsor
[\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}]_{\pi}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)
is defined.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\subset G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
denote the inclusion induced from the inclusion of a coefficient field
k_{\operatorname{sep}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}
. Any loop cocycle
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
is cohomologous in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
to
\langle\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}
. In particular, we have:

[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}=[\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}]_{\pi}.

Proof.

Let
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
be a loop cocycle. We need to prove
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
and
\langle\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}
are cohomologous in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
. By Lemma
5.5
, it suffices to find
s\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
such that:

(6.3)

s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s=\overline{a}_{\sigma},\ \ s^{-1}\varphi(f)s=\overline{\varphi}(f)

for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k),f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
.
We start by tackling the special case where
\varphi(f)\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
for all
f
and so
\overline{\varphi}=\varphi
. Define for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
:

c_{\sigma}=\overline{a_{\sigma}}a_{\sigma}^{-1}.

It is simple to check that
c_{\sigma}
is a cocycle in
{}_{a}G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
(here
{}_{a}G
is the twisted group defined by
a
, see Section
2
). We check
c_{\sigma}
centralizes
\operatorname{im}\varphi
. Since both
a_{\sigma},\varphi
and
\overline{a_{\sigma}},\varphi
are compatible (
5.1
) gives for any
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
:

\displaystyle a_{\sigma^{-1}}{}^{\sigma^{-1}}\varphi(f)a_{\sigma^{-1}}^{-1}=\varphi(f)^{\theta(\sigma^{-1})}=\overline{a_{\sigma^{-1}}}{}^{\sigma^{-1}}\varphi(f)\overline{a_{\sigma^{-1}}}^{-1}.

Therefore
\overline{a_{\sigma^{-1}}}^{-1}a_{\sigma^{-1}}
centralizes
{}^{\sigma^{-1}}\varphi(f)
. Applying
{}^{\sigma}(\cdot)
to both sides and using the cocycle identity we get:

C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi(f))(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\ni{}^{\sigma}(\overline{a_{\sigma^{-1}}}^{-1}a_{\sigma^{-1}})={}^{\sigma}\overline{a_{\sigma^{-1}}}^{-1}{}^{\sigma}a_{\sigma^{-1}}=\overline{a_{\sigma}}a_{\sigma}^{-1}=c_{\sigma}.

Since
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
was arbitrary this shows
c_{\sigma}\in C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi)
.
The class
[c_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi)(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
clearly goes to zero under the map

H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi)(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\to H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi)(k_{\operatorname{sep}})).

This implies
[c_{\sigma}]
is split in
H^{1}(\operatorname{Gal}(k),C_{{}_{a}G}(\varphi)(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
by Lemma
6.4
(note that
C_{G}(\varphi)
is smooth by
[
27
, Expose XI, Corollaire 5.3]
). Therefore there exists

such that for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
:

\overline{a_{\sigma}}a_{\sigma}^{-1}=c_{\sigma}=s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}sa_{\sigma}^{-1}.

Multiply by
a_{\sigma}
on the right to obtain

s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s=\overline{a_{\sigma}}.

Since
s
centralizes
\varphi=\overline{\varphi}
, we conclude that it satisfies (
6.3
).

We now tackle the case where
\varphi\neq\overline{\varphi}
. By Lemma
6.3
,
\varphi
and
\overline{\varphi}
are conjugate by an element
s\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
. That is, for any
f\in\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}
we have:

s^{-1}\varphi(f)s=\overline{\varphi}(f).

Applying the reduction map
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}}),g\mapsto\overline{g}
to the above equation we get:

\overline{s}^{-1}\overline{\varphi}(f)\overline{s}=\overline{\varphi}(f).

Therefore the element
t=s\overline{s}^{-1}\in G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
satisfies
\overline{t}=1
and

(6.4)

t^{-1}\varphi(f)t=\overline{s}s^{-1}(f)s\overline{s}^{-1}=\overline{s}\overline{\varphi}(f)\overline{s}^{-1}=\overline{\varphi}(f).

By Lemma
5.5
, (
6.4
) implies
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
and
\langle t^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}t,\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}
represent the same class in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
. By the first case we tackled and because
\overline{t}=1
, we conclude:

[\langle t^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}t,\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}]=[\langle\overline{t^{-1}}\overline{a}_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}\overline{t},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}]=[\langle\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}]\ \text{in }H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})).

Therefore
\langle\overline{a}_{\sigma},\overline{\varphi}\rangle_{\pi}
and
\langle a_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle_{\pi}
represent the same class in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We now prove Proposition
6.2
, which completes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Proof.

The map (
6.2
) is injective because it has a left inverse induced by the reduction homomorphism
G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to G(k_{\operatorname{sep}}),g\mapsto\overline{g}
. To prove surjectivity, one needs to show any loop cocycle is cohomologous in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))
to a cocycle taking values in
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
. This is immediate from Lemma
6.5
.
∎

In the proof of Theorem

is reductive and the characteristic of

is good for

, all

-torsors are loop torsors.

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume

is reductive. If the characteristic of

is good for

(see Definition

), then

H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)=H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G)=H^{1}(F,G).

A torsor induced from a finite subgroup

of order coprime to
\operatorname{char}k_{0}
is a loop torsor; see
[
26
, Lemma 3.12]
. If the characteristic of

is good for

, then all

-torsors are induced from some such subgroup

defined over

[

, Corollary 18]
. The result follows.
∎

The next lemma is the only occasion on which we relax Assumption

. The proof is inspired by
[

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a Henselian valued field with residue field
k
and value group
\Gamma_{F}
not necessarily finitely generated. Assume

. If

is a torus, then

H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G)=H^{1}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F,G)=H^{1}(F,G).

Set

and

. We have an exact sequence:

Since
G
is smooth,

is an étale group over

[

. This implies that

is a constant finite group and in particular

Therefore

is a

(8.1)

Let

and denote by

the image of

under the natural map:

The torsor
\gamma
lies in the image of

if and only if
\gamma^{\prime}
lies in the image of the map

H^{1}(F,G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})/T(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\to H^{1}(F,G(F_{\operatorname{sep}})/T(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})).

See for example
[

, Lemma 3.1]
.

The exact sequence (

) splits.
Indeed, the subgroup

trivially and it surjects onto

because

. Therefore we may rewrite (

1\to T(F_{\operatorname{sep}})/T(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})\to(T(F_{\operatorname{sep}})/T(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\rtimes(G(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})/T(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}))\to W(k_{0,\operatorname{sep}})\to 1.

Since (

) is split, to show that

is induced from

it suffices to prove that

is a uniquely divisible abelian group by Lemma
8.3
below. Let

be the
\operatorname{Gal}(F)
-module of cocharacters of

. We have isomorphisms of
\operatorname{Gal}(F)
-modules:

Therefore to show

is uniquely divisible. The group

is divisible because

F\subset L
with value groups
\Gamma_{F}\subset\Gamma_{L}
and residue fields
k\subset l
is called
purely wild
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

The group
\Gamma_{L}/\Gamma_{F}
is a
q
-group.

(2)

The field extension
l/k
is algebraic and purely inseparable.

We will use the following proposition in the proof of part (1) of Theorem
1.2
to avoid assuming
\operatorname{char}k
is good for
G
.

Proposition 8.5
.

Assume
G^{\circ}
is reductive. For any
G
-torsor
\gamma\in H^{1}(F,G)
there exists a purely wild finite extension
F\subset L
of degree a power of
\operatorname{char}k=q
such that
\gamma_{L}
is a loop torsor.

Proof.

Let
F\subset L
be a maximal algebraic purely wild extension of
F
; see
[
34
, Section 4]
. The field
L
satisfies

F_{\operatorname{tr}}\cdot L=F_{\operatorname{alg}},\ F_{\operatorname{tr}}\cap L=F,\ \ L_{\operatorname{tr}}=L_{\operatorname{sep}}=L_{\operatorname{alg}}.

See
[
34
, Theorem 4.3]
and
[
34
, Lemma 6.3]
respectively. Moreover,
L/F
is a pro-
q
extension. Indeed, for any finite subextension
F\subset L^{\prime}\subset L
with residue field extension
l^{\prime}/k
, both
[\Gamma_{L^{\prime}}:\Gamma_{F}]
and
[l^{\prime}:k]
are powers of
q
because
L^{\prime}/F
is purely wildly ramified
[
34
, Section 4]
. Therefore
[L^{\prime}:F]
is a power of
q
by Ostrowski’s theorem.

Let
T\subset G
be a maximal torus defined over
k_{0}
and
N=N_{G}(T)
its normalizer. Note that
N
is smooth because
T
is of multiplicative type and
G
is smooth
[
27
, Expose XI, Corollaire 2.4]
. By Lemma
8.2
, we have

H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(L,N)=H^{1}(L,N).

Since
H^{1}(L,N)\to H^{1}(L,G)
is surjective
[
30
, Corollary 5.3]
, this gives

H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(L,G)=H^{1}(L,G).

Write
L
as a union
L=\bigcup L_{i}
of a chain
L_{1}\subset L_{2}\subset\dots
of finite purely wild extensions of
F\subset L_{i}
of degree a power of
q
. We need to show
\gamma_{L_{i}}
is a loop torsor for some
i
. Since
\gamma_{L}
is a loop torsor, it is represented by a cocycle:

c_{\sigma}:\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(L)\to G(\mathcal{O}_{L,\operatorname{in}}).

Since
c_{(\cdot)}
is continuous it factors through
\operatorname{Gal}(E/L)
for some finite Galois extension
E/L
. By Lemma
3.8
, for all large enough
i
,
E
is a compositum
E=E_{i}L
for some tamely ramified Galois extension
E_{i}/L_{i}
such that the restriction map

\operatorname{res}_{i}:\operatorname{Gal}(E/L)\to\operatorname{Gal}(E_{i}/L_{i})

is an isomorphism. We have
\mathcal{O}_{L,\operatorname{in}}=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{O}_{L_{i},\operatorname{in}}
, which implies
G(\mathcal{O}_{L,\operatorname{in}})=\bigcup_{i}G(\mathcal{O}_{L_{i},\operatorname{in}})
by
[
31
, Lemma 10.62]
. Choose an integer
i
large enough such that:

\forall\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(E/L),\ c_{\sigma}\in G(\mathcal{O}_{L_{i},\operatorname{in}}).

Therefore
c_{\sigma}
is the restriction of the cocycle
c^{(i)}_{\sigma}:\operatorname{Gal}(E_{i}/L_{i})\to G(\mathcal{O}_{L_{i},\operatorname{in}})
given by:

c^{(i)}_{\sigma}=c_{\operatorname{res}_{i}^{-1}(\sigma)}.

Set
\eta=[c^{(i)}_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(L_{i},G)
. Since
c_{\sigma}
is the restriction of
c^{(i)}_{\sigma}
to
L
, we have

\gamma_{L}=\eta_{L}.

Since
H^{1}(L,G)=\operatorname{colim}H^{1}(L_{i},G)
by
[
41
, Theorem 2.1]
, this implies

\gamma_{L_{j}}=\eta_{L_{j}}

is a loop torsor for large enough
i\leq j
.
∎

We have seen that in the absence of wild ramification all torsors are loop torsors. This might lead one to guess that all tamely ramified torsors are loop torsors. We finish with an example showing this is not the case.

Example 8.6
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
2
equipped with a surjective homomorphism

\varepsilon:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to\operatorname{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{Z})=\{\pm 1\}.

The homomorphism
\varepsilon
defines a
1
-dimensional torus
T_{\varepsilon}:={}_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{G}_{m}
. The
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-action on an element
\alpha\in T_{\varepsilon}(k_{\operatorname{sep}})=k_{\operatorname{sep}}^{*}
is given by:

{}^{\sigma}\alpha=\sigma(\alpha)^{\varepsilon(\sigma)}.

Let
F=k(\!(t)\!)
and consider the decomposition
\Psi_{t}:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\ltimes\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime}\to\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
of Proposition
3.4
. We claim the following
\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
-cocycle represents a tamely ramified
T_{\varepsilon}
-torsor which is not a loop torsor:

c_{\Psi_{t}(\sigma,n)}=\begin{cases}t&\text{ if }\varepsilon(\sigma)=-1\\
1&\text{ if }\varepsilon(\sigma)=1\end{cases}

To see this, we assume that
c_{\tau}
is cohomologous to a cocycle taking values in
T_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})
and reach a contradiction. Let
s\in T_{\varepsilon}(F_{\operatorname{tr}})=F_{\operatorname{tr}}^{*}
be such that for all
\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}_{\operatorname{tr}}(F)
:

(8.5)

s^{-1}c_{\tau}{}^{\tau}s\in T_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}})=\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{in}}^{*}.

We choose
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k)
such that
\varepsilon(\sigma)=-1
and compute:

s^{-1}c_{\Psi_{t}(\sigma,0)}{}^{\Psi_{t}(\sigma,0)}s=ts^{-1}{}^{\sigma}s=ts^{-1}\sigma(s)^{\varepsilon(\sigma)}=t(s\sigma(s))^{-1}.

Let
\nu
be the Henselian valuation on
F_{\operatorname{tr}}
extending the
t
-adic valuation on
F
. Equation (
8.5
) gives
\nu(t(s\sigma(s))^{-1})=0
and therefore:

2\nu(s)=\nu(s)+\nu(\sigma(s))=\nu(t).

This contradicts the fact that
F_{\operatorname{tr}}/F
is a tamely ramified extension and so
\Gamma_{F_{\operatorname{tr}}}/\Gamma_{F}
contains no
2
-torsion by definition
[
64
, Appendix A.1]
. Let
l/k
be the separable quadratic extension defined by
\varepsilon
. We note that a standard computation shows
T_{\varepsilon}
is isomorphic to the norm one torus
R^{(1)}_{l/k}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
.

9.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
: First reduction

In this section we reduce Theorem
1.2
to the case where
G^{\circ}
is reductive. We will assume
k_{0}
is perfect throughout the section, because this is the only case in which
G^{\circ}
being reductive is an extra assumption. The following lemma is false over imperfect base fields; see
[
63
]
.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
U\subset G
be a normal smooth and connected unipotent subgroup. For any
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
, we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G)=\operatorname{ed}(G/U),\ \ \operatorname{ed}(G;p)=\operatorname{ed}(G/U;p).

Proof.

This follows from the fact that the induced map

(9.1)

H^{1}(k,G)\to H^{1}(k,G/U)

is a bijection for all fields
k_{0}\subset k

[
59
, Lemma 1.13]
.
∎

Let
A\subset G
be a finite diagonalizable subgroup. Denote the unipotent radical of
G
by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)
and set
\overline{G}:=G/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)
. Note that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)
is normal in
G
because it is a characteristic subgroup of
G^{\circ}
. Let
\pi:G\to\overline{G}
be the natural quotient map. By
[
3
, Proposition 9.6]
and the remark following it, the restriction of
\pi
to
C_{G}(A)
gives a quotient map:

\pi_{|C_{G}(A)}:C_{G}(A)\to C

onto a smooth subgroup
C\subset C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A))
with

(9.2)

\dim(C)=\dim(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A))).

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)^{A}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)\cap C_{G}(A)
be the subgroup fixed by the conjugation action of
A
on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)
. There is a short exact sequence:

1\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)^{A}\to C_{G}(A)\overset{\pi}{\to}C\to 1.

The unipotent group
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)^{A}
is smooth and connected by
[
47
, Theorem 13.7]
and
[
3
, Proposition 9.4]
respectively. Therefore Lemma
9.1
gives:

(9.3)

\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))=\operatorname{ed}(C),\ \ \operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p)=\operatorname{ed}(C;p).

Since
\dim(C)=\dim(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A)))
,
[
45
, Proposition 3.15]
and (
9.3
) give:

(9.4)

\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A)))\geq\operatorname{ed}(C)=\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))

Similarly, we obtain for any prime
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
:

(9.5)

\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A));p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p)

To finish the reduction to the reductive case we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 9.2
.

If
C_{G}(A)
admits anisotropic torsors over a field
k_{0}\subset k
, then so does
C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A))
.

Proof.

Assume
\gamma\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A))
is anisotropic. The push-forward
\pi_{*}(\gamma)\in H^{1}(k,C)
is anisotropic by Lemma
4.6
(2). The push-forward of
\pi_{*}(\gamma)
to
H^{1}(k,C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A)))
is anisotropic by Lemma
4.6
(1) because
\dim(C)=\dim(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A)))
.
∎

Proposition 9.3
.

To establish Theorem
1.2
, it suffices to prove it under the additional assumption that
G^{\circ}
is reductive.

Proof.

We assume that Theorem
1.2
holds for
G/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)=\overline{G}
and show that it holds for
G
(note that
\overline{G}^{\circ}=G^{\circ}/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{u}}(G)
is reductive because
k_{0}
is perfect
[
47
, Proposition 19.11]
).

Let
A\subset G
be a finite diagonalizable subgroup such that
C_{G}(A)
admits anisotropic torsors over a field
k_{0}\subset k
. Then
C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A))
admits anisotropic torsors over
k
as well by Lemma
9.2
. We prove the two parts of Theorem
1.2
separately.

(1) Let
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
be a prime. If
k
is
p
-closed, and
A
is a
p
-group, then Theorem
1.2
(1) gives:

\operatorname{ed}({\overline{G}};p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A));p).

Combining this with Lemma
9.1
and (
9.5
), we get:

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)=\operatorname{ed}({\overline{G}};p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A));p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p).

Therefore the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
(1) holds for
G
.

(2) If the characteristic of
k_{0}
is good for
G
, then it is good for
\overline{G}
as well by Definition
2.1
. Therefore Theorem
1.2
(2) gives:

\operatorname{ed}({\overline{G}})\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A))).

By Lemma
9.1
and (
9.4
), we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G)=\operatorname{ed}({\overline{G}})\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{\overline{G}}(\pi(A)))\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A)).

This shows Theorem
1.2
(2) holds for
G
.
∎

10.
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section,
F
will be an iterated Laurent series field
k(\!(t_{1})\!)\dots(\!(t_{r})\!)
over
k
for some field
k_{0}\subset k
. The valuation
\nu
on
F
is the
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
-adic valuation. We can and shall assume that
G^{\circ}
is reductive by Proposition
9.3
. We start the proof of Theorem
1.2
with an elementary lemma.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})^{d}
be
d
-tuples of elements. Assume that
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
generate a finite abelian group
A\subset G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
such that
|A|
is coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
. Let
G
act on
G^{d}
by conjugation. If
x,y
are conjugate in
G(l_{\operatorname{sep}})^{d}
for some field extension
k\subset l
, then
x,y
are conjugate in
G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
X\subset G^{d}
denote the
G
-orbit of
x
under the conjugation action on
G^{d}
. By assumption
y\in X(l_{\operatorname{sep}})
. Since
y\in G^{d}(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
and
X\subset G^{d}
is locally closed, we get

y\in X(l_{\operatorname{sep}})\cap G^{d}(k_{\operatorname{sep}})=X(k_{\operatorname{sep}}).

The stabilizer
\operatorname{stab}_{G}(x)
is
C_{G}(A)
and so the orbit map induces an isomorphism
X\cong G/C_{G}(A)

[
47
, Corollary 7.13]
over
k_{\operatorname{sep}}
. The homomorphism

G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})\to G/C_{G}(A)(k_{\operatorname{sep}}),\ g\mapsto gxg^{-1}

is surjective because
C_{G}(A)
is smooth
[
47
, Theorem 13.9]
. Therefore
y\in X(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
implies
y=gxg^{-1}
for some
g\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
.
∎

The following descent lemma is the main ingredient of the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
be a continuous surjection onto a diagonalizable
k
-subgroup
A\subset G_{k}
and
[a_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{an}}(k,C_{G}(A)(k_{\operatorname{sep}}))
an anisotropic torsor. Let
F_{1}\subset F
be a Henselian subfield with
\Gamma_{F_{1}}=\Gamma_{F}
and choose a uniformizer
\pi
for
F_{1,\operatorname{tr}}
. If
[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}\in H^{1}(F,G)
descends to a loop torsor
\gamma_{1}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F_{1},G)
, then
[a_{\sigma}]
descends to the residue field of
F_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
k_{1}
denote the residue field of
F_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{F_{1},\operatorname{in}}
denote the inertial closure of the valuation ring of
F_{1}
. By assumption
\gamma_{1}
is a loop torsor and so we can write
\gamma_{1}=[a^{\prime}_{\sigma},\varphi^{\prime}]_{\tau}
for some
\varphi^{\prime}:\Gamma_{F_{1}}^{\vee}\to G(\mathcal{O}_{F_{1},\operatorname{in}}),\ a^{\prime}_{\sigma}\in G(\mathcal{O}_{F_{1},\operatorname{in}})
. Since
\Gamma_{F_{1}}=\Gamma_{F}
, we have by Corollary
7.2
:

[\operatorname{Res}_{k/k_{1}}(a^{\prime})_{\sigma},\varphi^{\prime}]_{\pi}=(\gamma_{1})_{F}=\gamma=[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}.

Since
[a_{\sigma}]
is anisotropic, so is
\gamma
by
[
25
, Proposition 4.9]
. Therefore Theorem
6.1
implies that there exists
s\in G(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
such that:

(10.1)

s^{-1}a_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}s=\overline{\operatorname{Res}_{k/k_{1}}(a^{\prime})_{\sigma}},\ \ s^{-1}{\varphi(x)}s=\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}.

By Lemma
10.1
, there exists
s_{1}\in G(k_{1,\operatorname{sep}})
such that

s_{1}^{-1}{\varphi(x)}s_{1}=\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}

Lift
s_{1}
to an element
h\in G(\mathcal{O}_{F_{1},\operatorname{in}})
using Hensel’s lemma to get

\overline{h}^{-1}\varphi(x)\overline{h}=\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}.

We replace
\langle a^{\prime}_{\sigma},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle_{\pi}
by the cohomologous cocycle
\langle h^{-1}a^{\prime}_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}h,h^{-1}\varphi^{\prime}h\rangle_{\pi}
using Lemma
5.5
to assume

\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}={\varphi(x)}

without loss of generality.
Then (
10.1
) shows
s\in C_{G}(A)(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
because
A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})=\operatorname{im}\varphi
.
We have
\overline{a^{\prime}_{\sigma}}\in C_{G}(A)(k_{0,\operatorname{sep}})
for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(k_{1})
by Lemma
5.4
. Since
s\in C_{G}(A)(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
, we get

(10.2)

[\operatorname{Res}_{k/k_{1}}\overline{a^{\prime}_{\sigma}}]=[a_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A)).

Therefore (
10.2
) proves
[a_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A))
descends to
k_{1}
as we wanted to show.
∎

Without the assumption
\Gamma_{F_{1}}=\Gamma_{F}
in Lemma
10.2
, we cannot say with certainty that
[a_{\sigma}]
descends to the residue field of
F_{1}
. However, we can still get a lower bound on the transcendence degree of

in terms of the essential dimension of
[a_{\sigma}]
.

Corollary 10.3
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
be a continuous surjection onto a diagonalizable
k
-subgroup
A\subset G_{k}
and
\eta=[a_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{an}}(k,C_{G}(A)(k_{\operatorname{sep}}))
be an anisotropic torsor. If
[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}\in H^{1}(F,G)
descends to a loop torsor
\gamma_{1}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F_{1},G)
for some Henselian subfield
F_{1}\subset F
(with respect to
\nu_{|F_{1}}
), then
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1})\geq\operatorname{ed}(\eta)
.

Proof.

Set
F_{2}=F_{1}(\pi^{\gamma};\gamma\in\Gamma_{F})
and let
k_{2}
denote the residue field of
F_{2}
. Since
\Gamma_{F_{2}}=\Gamma_{F}
, Lemma
10.2
implies
\eta
descends to
k_{2}
and so
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(k_{2})\geq\operatorname{ed}(\eta)
. Therefore:

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1})\geq\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{2})-r\geq\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(k_{2})\geq\operatorname{ed}(\eta).

Here the first inequality follows because
F_{2}
is generated by
r
elements over
F_{1}
. The second inequality from the fact that
k_{2}
is the residue field of a valuation of rank
r
on
F_{2}
; see
[
66
, Chapter VI, Theorem 3, Corollary 1]
.
∎

We proceed to prove Theorem
1.2
. Assume
C_{G}(A)
admits an anisotropic torsor
[a_{\sigma}]=\eta\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{an}}(k,C_{G}(A))
over
k
. We may assume that
\eta
is versal by Proposition
4.4
and so:

(10.3)

\operatorname{ed}(\eta)=\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{ed}(\eta;p)=\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p).

Set
r=\operatorname{rank}(A)
, let
F=k(\!(t_{1})\!)\dots(\!(t_{r})\!)
be a field of iterated Laurent series equipped with the
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
-adic valuation. Since
\Gamma_{F}=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, we have
\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma_{F},\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime})=\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\prime r}
. Choose a surjection
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
and set

\gamma=[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loop}}(F,G).

Note that
[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}
is well-defined by Example
5.3
and anisotropic by
[
25
, Proposition 4]
. We prove the two parts of Theorem
1.2
separately. We start from Part (2) because it is simpler.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(2).

Assume
\gamma
descends to a field

with:

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1})=\operatorname{ed}(\gamma).

We may replace

by its Henselization inside of

to assume

is Henselian; see
[

, Appendix A.3]
. Indeed, the Henselization

and therefore
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1})=\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1}^{h})
.
Under the characteristic assumptions in Part (2),

by Proposition

. Therefore Corollary

) give

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)=\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(F_{1})\geq\operatorname{ed}(\eta)=\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A)).

This finishes the proof because
\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)
by definition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(1).

Assume
A
is a

-group and

is
p
-closed. Let

be a prime-to-

a field such that
\gamma_{L}
descends to
\gamma_{1}\in H^{1}(L_{1},G)
and:

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(L_{1})=\operatorname{ed}(\gamma;p).

Note that
L
is Henselian
[

, Proposition A.30]
. As in the proof of Part (1), we replace
L_{1}
by its Henselization inside of

to assume it is Henselian without changing
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k_{0}}(L_{1})
.
By Proposition

, there exists a prime-to-

such that

is a loop torsor. Note that

is contained in a prime-to-

[

, Lemma 6.1]
. The residue field of

is

because

is

By Corollary

, there exists a uniformizer

for

such that:

\gamma_{L^{\prime}}=[a_{\sigma},\varphi_{|L^{\prime}}]_{\tau}

Note that

is anisotropic and
\operatorname{im}\varphi_{|L^{\prime}}=A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
by Lemma

. The field

; see the first paragraph of

.
Since

We end this section with an example showing why the assumption that

Example 10.4
.

Let

be the projective linear group associated to the vector space

. Let

be the subgroup generated by the equivalence class of the matrix

where
d\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is the diagonal matrix:

d=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\zeta_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\zeta_{3}
is a primitive
3
-rd root of unity. We will prove that for any torsor
[a_{\sigma}]\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A))
and any surjection
\varphi:\Gamma_{F}^{\vee}\to A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
, (
10.4
) fails for the loop torsor
\gamma=[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}
. That is, we will prove:

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)\lneq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A)).

The failure of (
10.4
) for all
C_{G}(A)
-torsors is explained by the fact that, as we shall see,
C_{G}(A)
does not admits anisotropic torsors.

A choice of a basis
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
allows us to view matrices
g\in\operatorname{GL}(V)
as block matrices:

g=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}\end{pmatrix},

where
g_{ij}\in\operatorname{M}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
are four by four matrices. The action of
g
on
u\otimes b_{j}
is given by

gu\otimes b_{j}=g_{1j}u\otimes b_{1}+g_{2j}u\otimes b_{2}.

The corresponding block decomposition of
a=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}\otimes d
is

a=\begin{pmatrix}I_{4}&0\\
0&\zeta_{3}I_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Here
I_{4}\in\operatorname{M}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
is the identity matrix. Using this, one see computes the centralizer of
A=\langle a\rangle
:

C_{G}(A)=\Big\{\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&0\\
0&g_{22}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]\mid g_{11},g_{22}\in\operatorname{GL}_{4}(\mathbb{C})\Big\}.

We identify the right hand side with
\operatorname{GL}_{4}\times\operatorname{GL}_{4}/\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{m})
, where
\lambda:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\operatorname{GL}_{4}\times\operatorname{GL}_{4}
is the embedding
\lambda(s)=(sI_{4},sI_{4})
. Consider the diagonal embedding:

\Delta:\operatorname{PGL}_{4}\to C_{G}(A),\ \ [g]\mapsto[g\otimes I_{2}]=\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&g\end{pmatrix}\bigg].

Let
\mathbb{C}\subset F
be a field. By
[
11
, Theorem A.1]
, the quotient map
\pi:C_{G}(A)\to\operatorname{PGL}_{4}\times\operatorname{PGL}_{4}
induces an embedding

\pi_{*}:H^{1}(F,C_{G}(A))\hookrightarrow H^{1}(F,\operatorname{PGL}_{4})^{\times 2}

onto the diagonal subset consisting of all pairs
(x,x)
with
x\in H^{1}(F,\operatorname{PGL}_{4})
. This subset is precisely the image of
\pi_{*}\circ\Delta_{*}
, where
\Delta_{*}:H^{1}(F,\operatorname{PGL}_{4})\to H^{1}(F,C_{G}(A))
is the map induced by
\Delta
. We conclude that
\Delta_{*}
must be an isomorphism. In particular, we get:

\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))=\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{4})=5

by
[
43
]
. Let
\gamma\in H^{1}(F,G)
be induced from a
C_{G}(A)
-torsor. Since
\Delta_{*}
is an isomorphism,
\gamma
is represented by a cocycle of the form
\Delta(c_{\sigma})
for some cocycle
c_{\sigma}\in\operatorname{PGL}_{4}
. Recall that
\operatorname{PGL}_{n}
-torsors classify central simple algebras of degree
n
up to isomorphism; see for example
[
29
, Chapter 2]
. If the cocycle
c_{\sigma}
corresponds to some central simple algebra
B
of degree
4
over
F
, then the cocycle
\Delta(c_{\sigma})
corresponds to the matrix algebra
M_{2}(B)=B\otimes M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
over
B
because
\Delta(c_{\sigma})=c_{\sigma}\otimes I_{2}
. Both the algebra
M_{2}(B)
and
\gamma
descend to a field
F_{0}\subset F
with
\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}(F_{0})\leq 4
by
[
36
, Corollary 1.4]
. This gives the upper bound

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)\leq 4.

In particular, for any loop torsor
\gamma=[a_{\sigma},\varphi]_{\pi}
with
\operatorname{im}\varphi=A(k_{\operatorname{sep}})
over any Henselian field
\mathbb{C}\subset F
the essential dimension of
\gamma
is strictly smaller than
\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A))
:

\operatorname{ed}(\gamma)\leq 4<5=\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A)).

This shows the assumption that
\gamma
is anisotropic is necessary for our proof of Theorem
1.2
to work. Note that in this example any
C_{G}(A)
-torsor
\eta=[a_{\sigma}]
is isotropic because
Z(C_{G}(A))\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
embeds into the twisted group
{}_{a_{\sigma}}C_{G}(A)
.

11.
Reductive subgroups of maximal rank

Some of the inequalities in Theorem
1.5
follow from the next useful corollary of Theorem
1.2
whose proof relies on Borel–de Siebenthal theory. Recall that an algebraic subgroup
H\subset G
is called a subgroup of maximal rank if a maximal torus in
H
is remains maximal as a torus in
G
.

Corollary 11.1
.

Let
G
be a group over a field
k_{0}
with
G^{\circ}
reductive and assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}\neq 2,3
. Let
H\subset G
be a (connected) reductive subgroup of maximal rank. Assume that
Z(H)
is finite and diagonalizable.

(1)

Let
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
be a prime. If
Z(H)
is a
p
-group and
H
admits an anisotropic torsor over some
p
-closed field
k_{0}\subset k
, then we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(H;p).

(2)

Assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}
is good for
G
(see Definition
2.1
). If
H
admits an anisotropic torsor over some field
k_{0}\subset k
, then we have:

\operatorname{ed}(G)\geq\operatorname{ed}(H).

Proof.

By Borel-de Siebenthal’s theorem, we have

C_{G}(Z(H))^{\circ}=C_{G^{\circ}}(Z(H))^{\circ}=H.

See
[
4
]
for the characteristic zero case and
[
51
]
for the general case. Therefore

\dim(C_{G}(Z(H)))=\dim H.

By
[
7
, Lemma 2.2]
, we get:

\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(Z(H)))\geq\operatorname{ed}(H),\ \ \operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(Z(H));p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(H;p).

Now the corollary follows from Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Corollary
11.1
allows us to prove the following parts of Theorem

.

Proposition 11.2
.

Assume

.

(1)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};2)=\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{HSpin}_{16};2)

(2)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};3)\geq 13

(3)

\operatorname{ed}(E_{7}^{\mathrm{ad}};2)\geq 19

Proof.

(1) There exists an embedding
\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}\subset E_{8}

[

, Example 4.3]
and an anisotropic
\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}

. Therefore by Corollary

\operatorname{ed}(E_{8};2)\geq\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{HSpin}_{16};2).

(2) By

(3) There exists an embedding

Remark 11.3
.

In
[
49
]
, a torsor

is defined to be isotropic if there exists a proper parabolic subgroup

such that

lies in the image of the induced map

. In the proof of Proposition

above, we used the fact that this alternative definition of isotropy is equivalent to our notion of isotropy when

is a quasi-split semisimple group. To prove this fact, assume that

is quasi-split and semisimple. Then

is isotropic in the sense of
[
49
]
if and only if
{}_{\gamma}G
admits proper parabolic subgroups
[

admits proper parabolic subgroups if and only if it contains non-trivial split tori

12.
Abelian subgroups arising from gradings on the character lattice

In this section we assume
G^{\circ}
is a split reductive group. Let
T\subset G
be a split maximal torus defined over
k_{0}
. We will give a systematic root-theoretic approach to choosing diagonalizable
p
-subgroups
A\subset T
such that:

(1)

The connected centralizer
C_{G}(A)^{\circ}
is
T
.

(2)

The group
A
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.2
(1).

These conditions allow us to bound
\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p)
from below using
[
38
]
and to upgrade our lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A);p)
to a lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
using Theorem
1.2
. We will obtain a combinatorial formula for lower bounds on
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
in terms of gradings on the character lattice
X(T)
.

Definition 12.1
.

Fix a prime
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
and an abstract abelian
p
-group
\mathcal{V}
. A
\mathcal{V}
-grading on
X(T)
is a surjective homomorphism

\varepsilon:X(T)\to\mathcal{V}.

Let
X(\mathcal{V})=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{G}_{m})
be the group of characters of
\mathcal{V}
. The group
X(\mathcal{V})
is diagonalizable of order
|\mathcal{V}|
. Any
\mathcal{V}
-grading induces an embedding
\varepsilon^{*}:X(\mathcal{V})\to T
by the anti-equivalence between finitely generated abelian groups and groups of multiplicative type and finite type over
k
; see e.g.
[
5
, Corollary 8.3]
. We will denote the image of this embedding by

(12.1)

A_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{*}(X(\mathcal{V})).

The following lemma gives us a way to verify the condition
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}=T
holds.

Lemma 12.2
.

Let
\Phi\subset X(T)
be the root system associated to
T\subset G
.
We have
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}=T
if and only if

(12.2)

\forall\alpha\in\Phi,\ \varepsilon(\alpha)\neq 0.

Proof.

The inclusion
T\subset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}
holds because
A_{\varepsilon}\subset T
. Therefore the equality
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}=T
is equivalent to:

(12.3)

\dim T=\dim C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon}).

To check this equality of dimensions we can base change to
k_{0,\operatorname{alg}}
to assume that
k_{0}
is algebraically closed. Moreover, we may assume
G
is connected by replacing
G
with
G^{\circ}
because this does not affect (
12.3
).
The proof is is a variation of
[
32
, Theorem 2.2]
. We include the details for completeness. Let
H\subset G
be the subgroup generated by
N_{G}(T)\cap C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
and all root subgroups
U_{\alpha}
with
\varepsilon(\alpha)=0
. It will suffice to prove

H=C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon}).

To prove the inclusion
H\subset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
it suffices to prove
U_{\alpha}\subset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
for any
\alpha\in\Phi
such that

\varepsilon(\alpha)=0.

The group
A_{\varepsilon}
commutes with
U_{\alpha}
if and only if

\alpha(\varepsilon^{*}(X(\mathcal{V})))=1.

Now note that
\alpha\circ\varepsilon^{*}
is dual to the homomorphism
\varepsilon\circ\alpha^{*}:X(\mathbb{G}_{m})\to\mathcal{V}
. Therefore:

(12.4)

\alpha(\varepsilon^{*}(A_{\varepsilon}))=1\iff\varepsilon(\alpha)=0\iff U_{\alpha}\subset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon}).

To prove
H\supset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
, choose representatives
n_{w}\in N_{G}(T)
for Weyl group elements
w\in W=N_{G}(T)/T
. Choose an ordering of the roots
\Phi
and let
U_{+},U_{-}\subset G
be the corresponding unipotent groups generated by the positive and negative root groups respectively. An element
x\in C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})(k_{0})
has a unique Bruhat decomposition:

x=un_{w}tv,

where
v\in U_{+},t\in T,u\in U_{+}\cap n_{w}U_{-}n_{w}^{-1}
. The uniqueness of the decomposition forces
u,n_{w},v\in C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
because
A_{\varepsilon}
normalizes
U_{+},N_{G}(T),U_{-}
. Therefore we can assume
x=u
or
x=v
. The two cases are similar, we will focus on the case
x=u\in U_{+}
. Up to an ordering
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
of the positive roots,
u
can be written uniquely as a product:

u=p_{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})\dots p_{\alpha_{d}}(t_{d}),

where
p_{\alpha}
is a parametrization of
U_{\alpha}
and
t_{i}\in k_{0}
. For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
u\in C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
implies:

p_{\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})\dots p_{\alpha_{d}}(t_{d})=\varepsilon^{*}(a)u\varepsilon^{*}(a)^{-1}=p_{\alpha_{1}}(\alpha_{1}(\varepsilon^{*}(a))t_{1})\dots p_{\alpha_{d}}(\alpha_{d}(\varepsilon^{*}(a))t_{d}).

By uniqueness of the decomposition, we get for all
1\leq i\leq d
:

\alpha_{i}(\varepsilon^{*}(a))t_{i}=t_{i}.

If
t_{i}\neq 0
, then
\alpha_{i}(\varepsilon^{*}(A_{\varepsilon}))=1
and (
12.4
) implies
\varepsilon(\alpha)=0
. Therefore
p_{\alpha_{i}}(t_{i})\in H
for all
1\leq i\leq d
and
u\in H
, as we wanted to show.
∎

Let
\varepsilon
be a
\mathcal{V}
-grading such that
\varepsilon(\alpha)\neq 0
for any
\alpha\in\Phi
. By Lemma
12.2
we have

C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}=T.

We denote the component group by

W(\varepsilon):=C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})/C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}.

Since
T
is commutative
W(\varepsilon)
acts naturally on
T=C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}
by conjugation. We consider the inclusion into the Weyl group:

(12.5)

W(\varepsilon)\subset W=N_{G}(T)/T.

Denote the natural action of
W
on
\chi\in X(T)
by
w.\chi
. The subgroup
W(\varepsilon)\subset W
can be explicitly described as the subgroup of
W
preserving
\varepsilon
:

(12.6)

W(\varepsilon)=\{w\in W\mid\varepsilon(w.\chi)=\varepsilon(\chi),\ \text{for any }\chi\in X(T)\}.

Indeed, we have
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})\subset N_{G}(T)
and therefore by definition:

C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})=\{n\in N_{G}(T)\mid\operatorname{Ad}(n)\circ\varepsilon^{*}=\varepsilon^{*}\}.

Here
\operatorname{Ad}(n)\in\operatorname{Aut}(N_{G}(T))
denotes conjugation by
n
.
Since the functor
A\mapsto X(A)
is an anti-equivalence between the category finitely generated abelian groups and the category of diagonalizable groups
[
5
, Corollary 8.3]
,
\operatorname{inn}(n)\circ\varepsilon^{*}=\varepsilon^{*}
if and only if
nT\in W
preserves
\varepsilon
.

To give a lower bound on the essential dimension of
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
, it is useful to introduce the following definition:

Definition 12.3
.

[
40
]

Let
S
be a finite group acting on a finitely generated abelian group
\mathcal{U}
. A subset
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{U}
is called
p
-generating
if the subgroup generated by
\Gamma
is of finite and coprime to
p
index in
\mathcal{U}
.
Choose a Sylow
p
-subgroup
S_{p}\subset S
. The
symmetric
p
-rank
of the
S
-action on
\mathcal{U}
is the following integer:

\operatorname{Rank}(S,\mathcal{U};p)=\min\{|\Gamma|\mid\Gamma\subset\mathcal{U}\text{ is }S_{p}\text{-invariant and }p\text{-generating}\}.

The notion of
p
-symmetric rank is related to essential dimension at
p
by work of M. Macdonald-R. Lötscher-A. Meyer-Reichstein.

Proposition 12.4
.

[
40
, Theorem 1.10]

Let
N
be a smooth linear algebraic group over
k_{0}
such that
N^{\circ}=T
is a split torus. We have:

\operatorname{ed}(N;p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(N/T,X(T);p)-\dim T.

Here the
N/T
action on
X(T)
is induced from the conjugation action of
N/T
on
T
.

Note that while
N
is assumed to be the normalizer of a split maximal torus in a simple algebraic group in
[
40
, Theorem 1.10]
, this assumption is not used in the proof of the lower bound
\operatorname{ed}(N;p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(N/T,X(T);p)
. Setting
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})=N
in Proposition
12.4
, we obtain:

Corollary 12.5
.

If
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
), then

\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon});p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)-\dim T.

Next we formulate a condition on
\varepsilon
that ensures the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
(1) hold for
A_{\varepsilon}
and
G
.

Lemma 12.6
.

Assume
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
) and choose a Sylow
p
-subgroup
W(\varepsilon)_{p}\subset W(\varepsilon)
. If we have

(12.7)

X(T)^{W(\varepsilon)_{p}}=\{0\},

then
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
admits anisotropic torsors over some
p
-closed overfield
k_{0}\subset k
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})\to W(\varepsilon)=C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})/C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})^{\circ}
be the natural projection. There exists a finite
p
-group
P\subset C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
such that
\pi(P)=W(\varepsilon)_{p}
by
[
38
, Lemma 5.3]
. By assumption we have:

(12.8)

X(T)^{P}=\{0\}.

Replace
k_{0}
by its algebraic closure
k_{0,\operatorname{alg}}
to assume
P
is a constant group without loss of generality.
There exists a
p
-closed field
k
containing
k_{0}
and a surjection
\phi:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to P
. For example, if
k_{1}=k_{0,\operatorname{alg}}(t)
and
k_{1}\subset k
is a
p
-closure of
k_{1}
, then
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
\operatorname{Gal}(k_{1})

[
20
, Proposition
101.16]
. By
[
60
, Page 80, Example (b)]
the cohomological dimension of
k
is one. Note that
k_{0,\operatorname{alg}}(t)^{*}/k_{0,\operatorname{alg}}(t)^{*p}=H^{1}(k_{1},\mathbb{F}_{p})
embeds into
H^{1}(k,\mathbb{F}_{p})
via the restriction map
H^{1}(k_{1},\mathbb{F}_{p})\to H^{1}(k,\mathbb{F}_{p})
because the degree of any finite subextension
k_{1}\subset k_{2}\subset k
is coprime to
p
. Therefore
H^{1}(k,\mathbb{F}_{p})
is infinite and
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
is a free pro-
p
-group of infinite rank; see
[
60
, Page 30, Proposition 24]
. In particular, there exists a surjection
\phi:\operatorname{Gal}(k)\to P
. This surjection defines a
C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
-torsor
[\phi]\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon}))
because
P
is constant. The connected component of the twisted group
{}_{\phi}C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon})
is the torus
{}_{\phi}T
whose character
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-module is
X(T)
equipped with the
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-action given by:

\sigma.\chi=\phi(\sigma)(\chi).

To show
[\phi]\in H^{1}(k,C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon}))
is anisotropic we assume there exists an embedding
f:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to{}_{\phi}T
and reach a contradiction. The embedding
f
corresponds by duality to a
P
-invariant surjection:

f^{*}:X(T)\to X(\mathbb{G}_{m}).

See
[
5
, Corollary 8.3]
.
Let
\chi\in X(T)
be such that
f^{*}(\chi)\neq 0
. By the
P
-invariance of
f^{*}
:

f^{*}(\sum_{p\in P}p.\chi)=|P|f^{*}(\chi)\neq 0.

In particular,
\sum_{p\in P}p.\chi\in X(T)^{P}
is non-zero, contradicting (
12.8
).
∎

Lemma
12.6
allows us to apply Corollary
12.5
and Theorem
1.2
to get a lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)
.

Proposition 12.7
.

Let
G,T
and
\varepsilon:X(T)\to\mathcal{V}
be as above. If
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
) and (
12.7
), then

(12.9)

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)-\dim T.

Proof.

Lemma
12.6
and Theorem
1.2
(1) imply
\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{ed}(C_{G}(A_{\varepsilon});p)
. Therefore (
12.9
) follows from Corollary
12.5
.
∎

13.
Proof of Theorem 1.5: The overall strategy

13.1.
Setup for the next sections

In the next four sections we prove all parts of Theorem
1.5
except for Parts (2) and (4) which are included in Proposition
11.2
. We also reprove Merkurjev’s lower bound (
1.1
). In each section we focus on the essential dimension of a group
G
at a prime
p
. We choose a grading
\varepsilon
on the character lattice
X(T)
of a maximal split torus
T\subset G
. We check that
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
) and
W(\varepsilon)
satisfies (
12.7
). Applying Proposition
12.7
then gives:

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)-\dim T.

Finally, we prove a lower bound on
\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)
using Lemma
13.1
. This last step involves proving lower bounds on the size of
W(\varepsilon)
-orbits. We will use the following notation:

•

For any set
I
and ring
R
,
R^{I}
is the free
R
-module with standard basis
\{e_{i}|i\in I\}
. The coordinates of an element
x\in R^{I}
in the standard basis are denoted
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
.

•

The group of permutations of a set
X
is denoted
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. If a finite group
S
acts on
X
, we denote the
S
-orbit of
x\in X
by
Sx
and its stabilizer by
\operatorname{Stab}_{S}(x)
.

•

If
G
is an adjoint simple group of type
\Delta
, we identify the character lattice
X(T)
with the root lattice
Q(\Delta)
. We use the description of the roots
\Phi\subset Q(\Delta)
and the Weyl group
W
action given in
[
17
]
.

•

We always assume
\operatorname{char}k_{0}\neq p
.

We also note that for any subgroup
S\subset W(\varepsilon)
we have:

(13.1)

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)\geq\operatorname{Rank}(S,X(T);p).

To see this, choose Sylow
p
-subgroup
S_{p},W(\varepsilon)_{p}
of
S
and
W(\varepsilon)
such that
S_{p}\subset W(\varepsilon)_{p}
. Then (
13.1
) follows from the fact that a
p
-generating
W(\varepsilon)_{p}
-invariant subset
\Gamma\subset X(T)
is
S_{p}
-invariant.

13.2.
A lower bound on the symmetric rank

Let
p
be a prime and
S
a finite
p
-group. Assume
S
acts on a finitely generated abelian group
\mathcal{U}
. The next elementary lemma gives a useful lower bound on
\operatorname{Rank}(S,\mathcal{U};p)
.

Lemma 13.1
.

Let
\varepsilon:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
be an
S
-invariant surjective homomorphism and assume we are given a direct sum decomposition
L_{1}\oplus L_{2}=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
. Let
\varepsilon_{i}:U\to L_{i}
be the composition of
\varepsilon
with the projection
\pi_{i}:U\to L_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
If
|Su|\geq C_{i}
whenever
\varepsilon_{i}(u)\neq 0
, then:

\operatorname{Rank}(S,\mathcal{U};p)\geq C_{1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}L_{1}+C_{2}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}L_{2}.

Proof.

Denote
d_{1}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}L_{1}
and
d_{2}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}L_{2}
.
Let
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{U}
be an
S
-invariant,
p
-generating subset such that

|\Gamma|=\operatorname{Rank}(S,\mathcal{U};p).

The image
\varepsilon(\Gamma)
is
p
-generating in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
because
\varepsilon
is surjective. Because
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
contains no proper subgroups of index coprime to
p
,
\varepsilon(\Gamma)
has to generate
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
. Therefore
\Gamma
contains elements
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}
such that
\varepsilon(\gamma_{1}),\dots,\varepsilon(\gamma_{d})
is a basis for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
. We denote
b_{i}=\varepsilon(\gamma_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

An elementary linear algebra argument shows that, up to relabeling, we can assume that the projections
\pi_{1}(b_{1}),\dots,\pi_{1}(b_{d_{1}})
to
L_{1}
are a basis for
L_{1}
, and the projections
\pi_{2}(b_{d_{1}+1}),\dots,\pi_{2}(b_{d_{2}})
are a basis for
L_{2}
. We have for all
1\leq i\leq d_{1}
:

\varepsilon_{1}(\gamma_{i})=\pi_{1}\varepsilon(\gamma_{i})=\pi_{1}(b_{i})\neq 0.

By the last assumption of the Lemma, this implies
|S\gamma_{i}|\geq C_{1}
.
Similarly, we have

|S\gamma_{i}|\geq C_{2}\text{ for all }d_{1}<i\leq d_{2}.

Since
\varepsilon
is
S
-invariant, the orbits
S\gamma_{1},\dots,S\gamma_{d}
are disjoint. We conclude:

\operatorname{Rank}(S,\mathcal{U};p)=|\Gamma|\geq|S\gamma_{1}|+\dots+|S\gamma_{d}|\geq C_{1}d_{1}+C_{2}d_{2}.\qed

14.
Essential dimension of
\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{n}}
at
p

Our first application of Proposition
12.7
is to give a new proof of Merkurjev’s lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{n}};p)
(
1.1
). By Proposition
12.7
, it suffices to prove:

Proposition 14.1
.

Let
p\neq\operatorname{char}k_{0}
be a prime and
n\geq 1
.
Let
T\subset\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{n}}
be a maximal split torus with corresponding Weyl group
W
. There exists an
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
-grading
\varepsilon:X(T)\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
satisfying (
12.2
) and (
12.7
) such that

(14.1)

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)\geq np^{n}.

Definition of
\varepsilon

We start by introducing the notation needed to define
\varepsilon
. We set
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and identify the character lattice
X(T)
of the diagonal torus
T\subset\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{n}}
with the following sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}
as in
[
17
, Section 6]
:

\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}\mid\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}=0\}

Define
\varepsilon:\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\to\mathcal{V}
to be the surjective homomorphism given by:

\varepsilon(x)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v.

Verification of Condition (
12.2
)

The roots of
\Phi\subset\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
are the vectors
e_{u}-e_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
with
u\neq v
. For any such root we have:

\varepsilon(e_{u}-e_{v})=u-v\neq 0.

Therefore
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
).

Description of the Weyl group

The Weyl group
W
is the symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})
acting on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
by permuting the standard basis vectors. For any
u\in\mathcal{V}
, let
\lambda_{u}\in\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})
be the translation by
u
. The subgroup
W(\varepsilon)\subset\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})
of permutations preserving
\varepsilon
is the subgroup consisting of all such translations. Indeed, if
u\in\mathcal{V}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
then
\lambda_{u}\in W(\varepsilon)
because:

\varepsilon(\lambda_{u}(x))=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}(u+v)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v+(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v})u=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v=\varepsilon(x).

Here the second to last equality follows from the fact that
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}=0
.
Conversely, if
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})
preserves
\varepsilon
, then for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
:

v=\varepsilon(e_{0}-e_{v})=\varepsilon(e_{\sigma(0)}-e_{\sigma(v)})=\sigma(0)-\sigma(v).

Therefore
\sigma(v)=v+\sigma(0)
. That is,
\sigma=\lambda_{\sigma(0)}
. This proves:

W(\varepsilon)=\{\lambda_{u}\mid u\in\mathcal{V}\}.

We will identify
W(\varepsilon)
with
\mathcal{V}
using the isomorphism
\mathcal{V}\tilde{\to}W(\varepsilon),u\mapsto\lambda_{u}
.

Verification of Condition (
12.7
)

Note that
\mathcal{V}
is its own Sylow
p
-subgroup. If an element
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
is fixed by
\mathcal{V}
, then for any
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
we have:

(14.2)

x_{v}=x_{u+v}.

Since
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
this implies

0=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}=|\mathcal{V}|x_{0}.

Therefore
x_{v}=x_{0}=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
by (
14.2
). We conclude that
W(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{V}
satisfies (
12.7
).

Proof of the lower bound on
\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)

To finish the proof of (
14.1
) we will show that for any element
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
with
\varepsilon(v)\neq 0
we have

(14.3)

|\mathcal{V}x|=p^{n}.

Once we do so, Lemma
13.1
will give:

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);p)\geq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathcal{V}\cdot p^{n}=np^{n}.

By the stabilizer-orbit formula, (
14.3
) is equivalent to

\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(x)=\{0\}.

Therefore we need to prove that
\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(x)=\{0\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
with
\varepsilon(x)\neq 0
.
We prove a slightly more general lemma:

Lemma 14.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}
. Assume either
p
is odd, or
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}
is divisible by four. If
\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(x)\neq\{0\}
, then

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v=0.

In particular, if
\varepsilon(x)\neq 0
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
, then
\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(x)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Assume that
x
is fixed by
0\neq u\in\mathcal{V}
and let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a set of coset representatives for the quotient
\mathcal{V}/\langle u\rangle
. Since
x
is fixed by
u
, we have for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
:

x_{v}=x_{u+v}.

Using this, we compute:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=0,1,\dots,p-1}x_{c+iu}(c+iu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}\sum_{i=0,1,\dots,p-1}(c+iu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}(pc+\binom{p}{2}u)=(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c})\binom{p}{2}u.

Here in the last equality we used the fact that
pc=0
in
\mathcal{V}
. If
p
is odd, then it divides
\binom{p}{2}
and we get

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v=(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c})\binom{p}{2}u=0.

Otherwise
p=2
and
4\mid\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}
. We have:

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}+x_{c+u}=2\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}.

Since
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}
is divisible by four, this implies
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}
is even.
Therefore in
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
:

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}v=(\sum_{c\in C}x_{c})\binom{p}{2}u=0.\qed

15.
Essential dimension of
\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n}
at
2

In this section we prove Parts (5) and (6) of Theorem
1.5
. By Proposition
12.7
, it suffices to prove:

Proposition 15.1
.

Let
n=2^{r}m\geq 4
be an integer with
r\geq 0
and
m
odd. Let
T\subset\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n}
be a split maximal torus with corresponding Weyl group
W
. There exists a grading
\varepsilon:X(T)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r+m-1}
satisfying (
12.2
) and (
12.7
) such that

(15.1)

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);2)\geq\begin{cases}(r+m-1)2^{r+1}&\text{ if }r\geq 1\\
4(m-1)&\text{ if }r=0.\end{cases}

Definition of
\varepsilon

We set notation in order to describe
X(T)
and define
\varepsilon
. Define
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
,
K=\mathcal{V}\times\{1,\dots,m\}
and

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m})_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\mid\sum_{i=1,\dots,m}x_{i}=0\}.

Since
\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n}
is the split adjoint group of type
D_{n}
, we can choose a split maximal torus
T\subset\operatorname{PGO}^{+}_{2n}
such that the character lattice
X(T)=Q(D_{n})
is identified with the following sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{K}

[
17
, Section 7.1]
:

Q(D_{n})=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{K}\mid\sum_{k\in K}x_{k}\text{ is even}\}.

Pick a basis
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}
for
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
and let
\varepsilon:Q(D_{n})\to\mathcal{V}\oplus(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m})_{0}
be the grading given by

\varepsilon(x)=\sum_{(v,i)\in K}x_{v,i}(v+b_{i})\in\mathcal{V}\oplus(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m})_{0}.

Note that
\varepsilon
is surjective because for any
i\neq j
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
we have:

\varepsilon(e_{0,i}+e_{0,j})=b_{i}+b_{j},\ \ \varepsilon(e_{0,1}+e_{v,1})=(v,0).

Verification of Condition (
12.2
)

The roots
\Phi\subset Q(D_{n})
are given by:

\Phi=\{\pm e_{k}\pm e_{s}\mid k,s\in K,\ k\neq s\}.

For any root
\alpha=\pm e_{(v,i)}\pm e_{(v^{\prime},j)}
we have:

\varepsilon(\alpha)=v+v^{\prime}+b_{i}+b_{j}\neq 0.

Therefore
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
).

Description of the Weyl group

The Weyl group of
G
is
\operatorname{Sym}(K)\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
, where
\operatorname{Sym}(K)
is the group of permutations of
K
and
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
is the group:

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}=\{\delta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K}\mid\sum_{k}\delta_{k}=0\}.

The action of
(\sigma,\delta)\in\operatorname{Sym}(K)\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
on
Q(D_{n})
is the restriction of the action on
\mathbb{Z}^{K}
defined by:

(15.2)

(\sigma,\delta)e_{k}=(-1)^{\delta_{k}}e_{\sigma(k)}

for all
k\in K
.

Lemma 15.2
.

Identify
\mathcal{V}
with the subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(K)
consisting of all translations
\lambda_{u}:K\to K
by an element
u\in\mathcal{V}
:

\lambda_{u}(v,i)=(u+v,i).

We have
W(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{V}\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
.

Proof.

The inclusion
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}\subset W(\varepsilon)
is easy to see. The inclusion
\mathcal{V}\subset W(\varepsilon)
follows from the computation:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\lambda_{u}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum x_{v,i}(u+v+b_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum x_{v,i}(v+b_{i})+\sum x_{v,i}u

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(x)+\sum x_{v,i}u=\varepsilon(v).

Here in the last equality we used the fact that
\sum x_{v,i}
is even.
To verify the inclusion
W(\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{V}\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
, we assume
w=(\sigma,\delta)\in W(\varepsilon)
and show
\sigma=\lambda_{u}
for some
u\in\mathcal{V}
. Since
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}\subset W(\varepsilon)
, we can assume
w=\sigma
without loss of generality. We start by denoting for any
(v,i)\in K
:

\sigma(v,i)=(f(v,i),g(v,i))\in K.

For any
(v,i),(v^{\prime},j)\in K
we have:

(15.3)

f(v,i)+f(v^{\prime},j)+b_{g(v,i)}+b_{g(v^{\prime},j)}=\varepsilon(\sigma(e_{v,i}+e_{v^{\prime},j}))=\varepsilon(e_{v,i}+e_{v^{\prime},j})=v+v^{\prime}+b_{i}+b_{j}.

Assume
i\neq j
. Then if
g(v,i)\neq i
, (
15.3
) implies

g(v,i)=j.

Since
m
is odd if
i\neq j
then
m\geq 3
. Therefore repeating the same argument with
1\leq j^{\prime}\leq m
different from
i
and
j
gives the contradiction
g(v,i)=j^{\prime}
. Therefore we see that

g(v,i)=i

for all
(v,i)\in K
. Setting
v^{\prime}=0,j=1
in (
15.3
), we get for all
(v,i)\in K
:

f(v,i)-v=f(0,1).

Therefore
\sigma=\lambda_{f(0,1)}
is the translation by
f(0,1)
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Verification of Condition (
12.7
)

Let
x\in Q(D_{n})^{W(\varepsilon)}
be fixed by
W(\varepsilon)
and let
k\in K
be some element. Since
|K|=n\geq 3
there exists
\delta\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
such that
\delta_{k}=1
. The equation

\delta x=x

implies
x_{k}=0
because
\delta
flips the sign of the
k
-th coordinate. Since
k\in K
was arbitrary, we conclude that
x=0
.
Therefore

Q(D_{n})^{W(\varepsilon)}=\{0\}.

Since
W(\varepsilon)
is its own Sylow
2
-subgroup, (

) follows.

Proof of the lower bound on
\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);2)

For the proof we will need the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 15.3
.

Let
Y\subset K
be a non-empty finite subset of
K=\mathcal{V}\times\{1,\dots,m\}
. For any
u\in\mathcal{V}
denote:

u+Y=\{(u+v,i)\mid(v,i)\in Y\}.

There are at most
{|Y|}
elements
u\in\mathcal{V}
such that:

(15.4)

u+Y=Y.

Proof.

Replace
Y
with
u+Y
for some
u\in\mathcal{V}
such that
(-u,i)\in Y
to assume that
(0,i)\in Y
without loss of generality. Then

implies
(u,i)\in Y
and so there are at most

elements satisfying (
15.4
).
∎

Proof of (
15.1
).

Note that
W(\varepsilon)
is its own Sylow
2
-subgroup. By Lemma
13.1
, it suffices to prove that for any
x\in Q(D_{n})
with
\varepsilon(x)\neq 0
, we have

|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq\begin{cases}2^{r+1}&\text{ if }r\geq 1\\
4&\text{ if }r=0.\end{cases}

Let
x\in Q(D_{n})
be such that
\varepsilon(x)\neq 0
. We define

Y_{x}=\{(v,i)\in K\mid x_{a,i}\neq 0\},

and split into cases.

Assume
r=0
.
We have
|Y_{x}|\geq 2
. Otherwise,
x=x_{k}e_{k}
for some
k\in K,x_{k}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
and so
\varepsilon(v)=0
. Write:

x=x_{k_{1}}e_{k_{1}}+x_{k_{2}}e_{k_{2}}+\sum_{k\neq k_{1},k_{2}}x_{k}e_{k},

for some
x_{k_{1}},x_{k_{2}}\neq 0
.
Since
|K|=n\geq 3
, for any
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
there exists
\delta\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
such that
\delta_{k_{1}}=\delta_{k_{2}}=1
. Therefore the orbit
W(\varepsilon)x
contains elements of the form:

(-1)^{\delta_{1}}x_{k_{1}}e_{k_{1}}+(-1)^{\delta_{2}}x_{k_{2}}e_{k_{2}}+\sum_{k\neq k_{1},k_{2}}x^{\prime}_{k}e_{k},

for some
x^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We conclude that
|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq 4
.

Assume
r\geq 1
.
The stabilizer-orbit formula gives:

|W(\varepsilon)x|=|W(\varepsilon)|/|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|=2^{r+n-1}/|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|.

Therefore our goal is to show:

(15.5)

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq 2^{n-2}

If
w=(u,\delta)\in W(\varepsilon)
fixes

, then:

u+Y_{x}=Y_{x}.

Therefore the projection onto the first component gives a short exact sequence:

0\to\operatorname{Stab}_{(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}}(x)\to\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)\to\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x}).

Here
\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})
is the stabilizer of
Y_{x}
with respect to the action of
\mathcal{V}
on subsets of
K
by translation. Thus we get an inequality:

(15.6)

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|\operatorname{Stab}_{(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}}(x)||\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|.

By (
15.2
), the stabilizer
\operatorname{Stab}_{(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}}(x)
is the following subspace of
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}
:

(15.7)

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}:=\{\delta\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K})_{0}\mid\forall k\in Y_{x}:\delta_{k}=0\}.

If
Y_{x}=K
then
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}=\{0\}
and (
15.6
) gives:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|=|\mathcal{V}|=2^{r}.

One can check that
2^{r}\leq 2^{n-2}
using
n\geq 3
(recall that
n=2^{r}m)
.
Therefore (
15.5
) holds if
Y_{x}=K
.
Assume
Y_{x}\neq K
. We have:

|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}|=|\mathbb{F}_{2}^{|K\setminus Y_{x}|-1}|=2^{n-|Y_{x}|-1}.

Since
x\neq 0
,
Y_{x}
is non-empty. Applying Lemma
15.3
gives:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|\leq|Y_{x}|.

Plugging this inequality into (

) we get:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq 2^{n-|Y_{x}|-1}|Y_{x}|\leq 2^{n-|Y_{x}|-1}2^{|Y_{x}|-1}=2^{n-2}.

Here the last inequality follows from the inequality
n\leq 2^{n-1}
which holds for all natural numbers

. This shows (
15.5
) holds and finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 15.4
.

Assume

|W(\varepsilon)x|=2^{r+1}

Essential dimension of
\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}
at

In this section we prove Part (1) of Theorem

. We have already proven

in Proposition

(1). It remains to show

12.7
, it suffices to prove:

Proposition 16.1
.

Let

be a split maximal torus with corresponding Weyl group

. There exists a grading

) and (

(16.1)

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);2)\geq 2^{6}.

Definition of
\varepsilon

We start by introducing the notation needed to define
\varepsilon

\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}\text{ and }\nu=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}e_{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{V}}.

There exists a split maximal torus
T\subset\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}
such that

is the following sublattice of
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{V}}

[
40

(16.2)

\displaystyle X(T)

\displaystyle=Q(D_{8})\cup(\nu+Q(D_{8}))

(16.3)

\displaystyle=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{V}}\ {\Big|}\ \begin{matrix}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}\text{ is even.}\\
\text{For all }v\in\mathcal{V},\ x_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{or}\\

Let
\varepsilon_{0}:Q(D_{8})\to\mathcal{V}

\varepsilon_{0}(y)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}y_{v}v.

Any vector
x\in X(T)
can be written uniquely as
x=d\nu+y
, where

and

.
We define

One can check that

Verification of Condition (

)

Any root

Description of the Weyl group

As in the previous section, the Weyl group of
\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}

W=\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{0}.

The action of
(\sigma,\delta)\in\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{V})\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{0}
on

is the restriction of the action on
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{V}}

(16.5)

(\sigma,\delta)e_{v}=(-1)^{\delta_{v}}e_{\sigma(v)}

for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Since
\varepsilon_{0}:Q(D_{8})\to\mathcal{V}

15.2
implies that the subgroup of

2

Note that

by the definition of
\varepsilon
. Since

Let

Therefore

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}=\{\delta\in(\mathbb{F}^{\mathcal{V}}_{2})_{0}\mid\\

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{0}\to\mathcal{V},\ \ \delta\mapsto\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\delta_{v}v.

Therefore we have

and:

|W(\varepsilon)|=|\mathcal{V}\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}|=2^{3+4}=2^{7}.

Verification of Condition (

)

The element
(1,\dots,1)\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}\subset W(\varepsilon)
acts on

by multiplication by

. Therefore

) is satisfied. We note that this implies a fact we used earlier in the proof of Proposition

(1).

.

There exists an anisotropic
\operatorname{HSpin}_{16}

2

k

.

Proof.

be the finite diagonalizable

).
By Lemma

,

admits anisotropic torsors over some

.
∎

Proof of the lower bound on
\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);2)

Proof of (
16.1
).

By Lemma
13.1
, in order to prove (
16.1
) it suffices to prove that for any
x\in X(T)
with
\varepsilon(x)=(v,d)\neq 0
, we have

|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq 2^{4}.

We handle the cases
d\neq 0
and
v\neq 0
separately.

Assume
d\neq 0
.
Then (
16.4
) implies

x=\nu+y

for some
y\in Q(D_{8})
. In particular, the coordinates
x_{v}=\frac{1}{2}+y_{v}
are all half-integers and therefore non-zero. We conclude that
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}
acts freely on
x
by sign changes and we have:

|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}x|=2^{4}.

Assume
v\neq 0
.
If
d\neq 0
, then
|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq 2^{4}
by the previous case. Therefore we can assume
d=0
, which implies
x\in Q(D_{8})
by (
16.4
). We set:

Y_{x}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\mid x_{v}\neq 0\}.

The stabilizer-orbit formula gives:

|W(\varepsilon)x|=|W(\varepsilon)|/|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|=2^{7}/|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|.

Therefore to show
|W(\varepsilon)x|\geq 2^{4}
it suffices to prove:

(16.6)

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq 2^{3}.

A similar argument to the proof of (
15.6
) shows:

(16.7)

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|\operatorname{Stab}_{(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}}(x)||\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|.

By (
16.5
), the stabilizer
\operatorname{Stab}_{(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}}(x)
is the following subspace of
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}
:

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}=\bigg\{\delta\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}})_{1}\ {\Big|}\ \forall v\in Y_{x}:\delta_{v}=0\bigg\}.

The set
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}
can also be described as the set of all relations satisfied by an even number of elements of
{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}}
in
\mathcal{V}
. This gives the explicit formula:

(16.8)

|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}|=\bigg|\bigg\{\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}\ {\Big|}\ \begin{matrix}|\mathcal{C}|\text{ is even}\\
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}c=0\end{matrix}\bigg\}\bigg|

We divide further into three cases based on
|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|
. If
|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|\leq 3
, then
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}=\{0\}
. Indeed, if
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}\neq\{0\}
, then (
16.8
) implies that there exists a pair

such that

u+v=0.

This is impossible because in characteristic two, the above equation implies
u=v
. Plugging
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}=\{0\}
into (
16.7
) gives:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|\leq|\mathcal{V}|=2^{3}.

Therefore (
16.6
) holds if
|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|\leq 3
.

If
|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|=4
, then by the previous case, the only non-empty subset of
\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}
that might show up in the set (
16.8
) is
\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}
itself. Therefore (
16.8
) gives:

|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}|\leq 2.

Together with (
16.7
) this implies:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}||\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|\leq 2|\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|.

Apply Lemma
15.3
to get:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq 2|\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|\leq 2|Y_{x}|.

This implies (
16.6
) because
|Y_{x}|=|\mathcal{V}|-|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|=4
.

If
|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|\geq 5
, then
\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}
contains an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-basis

for
\mathcal{V}
and two other vectors. Therefore
\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}
contains either
0
or
v_{i}+v_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. Either way, the homomorphism

f:(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}\to\mathcal{V},\ \delta\mapsto\sum_{v\in V}\delta_{v}v

is easily seen to be surjective in this case (here
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}
is defined as in (
15.7
)). Since
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}=\ker f
, this gives:

\dim(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}=\dim(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{0}-\dim\mathcal{V}=|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|-4.

In particular, we have

|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}|=2^{|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|-4}.

Plugging this into (
16.7
) and applying Lemma
15.3
we get:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}})_{1}||\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{x})|\leq 2^{|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|-4}|Y_{x}|.

Note that we can apply Lemma

because
x\neq 0
implies

. Using the inequality

we find:

|\operatorname{Stab}_{W(\varepsilon)}(x)|\leq 2^{|\mathcal{V}\setminus Y_{x}|-4}2^{|Y_{x}|-1}=2^{|\mathcal{V}|-5}=2^{3}.

Therefore (
16.6
) holds in all cases and (
16.1
) follows.
∎

In this section we prove Part (3) of Theorem
1.5
. By Proposition
12.7
, it suffices to prove:

Proposition 17.1
.

Let
T\subset E_{6}^{\mathrm{ad}}
be a split maximal torus with corresponding Weyl group

. There exists a grading
\varepsilon:X(T)\to\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
satisfying (

) and (

) such that

\operatorname{Rank}(W(\varepsilon),X(T);3)\geq 12.

Definition of
\varepsilon

We start by introducing the notation needed in order to define
\varepsilon
.
We identify the character lattice
X(T)
of a split maximal torus
T\subset E_{6}^{\mathrm{ad}}
with the root lattice
Q(E_{6})
. We will use the following labeling of the extended Dynkin diagram of
E_{6}
:

\alpha
\beta_{11}
\beta_{12}
\beta_{21}
\beta_{22}
\beta_{31}
\beta_{32}

Here
-\beta_{11}
is the highest root given by

-\beta_{11}=3\alpha+\beta_{12}+2\beta_{21}+\beta_{22}+2\beta_{31}+\beta_{32}.

Since the simple roots form a basis for
Q(E_{6})
, any element in
x\in Q(E_{6})
can be written uniquely as

(17.2)

x=d\alpha+\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\beta_{ij},

for some
d\in\{0,1,2\}
and integers

. We define a grading
\varepsilon:Q(E_{6})\to\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
by:

\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon(d\alpha+\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\beta_{ij})=(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij},d).

Note that
\varepsilon
is surjective.

Verification of Condition (
12.2
)

Let
\beta\in\Phi
be a root and express it as above:

\beta=d\alpha+\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\beta_{ij},

for some
d\in\{0,1,2\}
and integers
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
d\neq 0
, then clearly

\varepsilon(\beta)=(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij},d)\neq 0.

If
d=0
, then
\beta
lies in the closed subsystem
A_{2}^{\times 3}\subset E_{6}
generated by the
\beta_{ij}
’s; see
[
12

. Therefore there exists
1\leq i\leq 3
such that:

\pm\beta\in\{\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\beta_{i1}+\beta_{i2}\}_{\tiny i=1,2,3}.

This implies
\varepsilon(\beta)\neq 0
and so
\varepsilon
satisfies (
12.2
).

Description of the Weyl group

For any root
\beta\in\Phi\subset Q(E_{6})
, let
r_{\beta}\in W
be the corresponding reflection. Set
\sigma_{i}=r_{\beta_{i1}}r_{\beta_{i2}}
for

and define:

\sigma:=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}.

By
[
12
, Section 4]
, there exists an element
\tau\in W
whose action on the set of roots
\Phi
is determined by a
2\pi/3
-rotation of the extended Dynkin diagram above. Explicitly, we have
\tau(\alpha)=\alpha
and for any
1\leq i\leq 3,1\leq j\leq 2
we have:

(17.3)

\tau(\beta_{ij})=\beta_{i+1,j},

where we set
\beta_{4,j}:=\beta_{1,j}
.

Lemma 17.2
.

The elements
\sigma,\tau
generate a subgroup
\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\subset W(\varepsilon)
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}
is invariant under permuting
1,2,3
, we conclude from (
17.3
) that
\tau
commutes with
\sigma
. Since
\tau
fixes
\alpha
and commutes with
\sigma
,
\tau
preserves
\varepsilon
. To check that
\sigma
preserves
\varepsilon
, one first computes:

\sigma_{i}(\alpha)=r_{\beta_{i1}}r_{\beta_{i2}}(\alpha)=r_{\beta_{i1}}(\alpha+\beta_{i2})=\alpha+\beta_{i1}+\beta_{i2}.

Since
\sigma_{i}(\beta_{jk})=\beta_{jk}
for any

, this gives:

(17.4)

\sigma(\alpha)=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}(\alpha)=\alpha+\sum_{\tiny i,j}\beta_{ij}.

Here we are summing over all
1\leq i\leq 3,1\leq j\leq 2
. Therefore in
\mathbb{F}^{2}_{3}
:

(17.5)

\varepsilon(\sigma(\alpha))=(6,1)=(0,1)=\varepsilon(\alpha).

Any vector
x\in Q(E_{6})
is of the form:

x=d\alpha+\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\beta_{ij},

as in (
17.2
). By (

), to prove

it suffices to check:

(17.6)

\varepsilon(\sigma(\beta_{ij}))=\varepsilon(\beta_{ij})=(1,0).

for all

and
1\leq j\leq 2
. A computation shows that for all

:

(17.7)

\sigma(\beta_{i1})=\beta_{i2},\ \sigma(\beta_{i2})=-\beta_{i1}-\beta_{i2}.

Therefore (

) follows from the fact that in

we have

.
∎

Remark 17.3

We start with two lemmas which help us understand the

.

then

.

(2)

If
\sigma(x)\in\{x,\tau(x),\tau^{2}(x)\}
then

.

(3)

If
\tau(x)=x

u=0
.
