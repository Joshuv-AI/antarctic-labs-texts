Excess Coverage Arrays and Levenshtein’s Conjecture
Amber E. Gentle, Daniel Horsley, Ian M. Wanless

Abstract
A sequence covering array, denoted SCA ( N , t , v ) (N;t,v) , is a set of N N permutations of { 0 , … , v − 1 } \{0,\dots,v-1\} such that each sequence of t t distinct elements of { 0 , … , v − 1 } \{0,\dots,v-1\} reads left to right in at least one permutation. The minimum number of permutations such a sequence covering array can have is t ! t! and Levenshtein conjectured that if a sequence covering array with t ! t! permutations exists, then v ∈ { t , t + 1 } v\in\{t,t+1\} . In this paper, we prove that if an SCA ( 7 ! , 7 , v ) (7!;7,v) exists, then v ⩽ 9 v\leqslant 9 . We do this by analysing connections between sequence covering arrays and a special kind of covering array called an excess coverage array. A strength 2 excess coverage array, denoted CA ( N ; 2 , k , v ) X {}_{X}(N;2,k,v) , is an N × k N\times k array with entries from { 0 , … , v − 1 } \{0,\dots,v-1\} such that every ordered pair of distinct symbols appear at least once in each pair of columns and all other pairs appear at least twice. We demonstrate computationally that there is a unique CA ( 42 ; 2 , 5 , 6 ) X {}_{X}(42;2,5,6) , and we prove that this array alone does not satisfy necessary conditions we establish for the existence of an SCA ( 7 ! , 7 , 10 ) (7!;7,10) . Furthermore, we find the maximum possible number of columns for strength 2 excess coverage arrays with symbol sets of sizes between 2 and 6, and more broadly investigate binary excess coverage arrays.



Keywords

Sequence covering array, covering array, orthogonal array, exact covering

A

(N;t,v)

Given a

(t,v)=t!
has received particular attention in part because of its connections to coding theory
[
11
]
. Any permutation in

(1;1,v)
. Any permutation in
\mathcal{S}_{v}
and its reverse form an

(2;2,v)
, and the set

[

]
. Moreover, it is known that an

does not exist
[

]
. In this paper, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1

If an

(7!;7,v)
exists, then

.

This reduces the best known upper bound on order of an

We prove Theorem

[

, and each

is an element of

. The row

covers

For an interaction

Excess coverage arrays were introduced by Chee et al.

We perform computations to find the maximum number of columns

Up to isomorphism, there is a unique

This unique

In an

In Section

Covering arrays with excess coverage

In this section, we discuss the connections between sequence covering arrays and excess coverage arrays established by Chee et al.
[
3
]
. We extend these results in Lemma
4
. We then use this lemma to prove Lemma
6
, which in turn allows us to deduce Theorem
1
from Theorem
2
. Let
CAN
(t,k,v)
be the smallest
N
for which a
CA
(N;t,k,v)
exists and let
CAN
{}_{X}(t,k,v)
be the smallest

{}_{X}(N;t,k,v)

Construction 3
.

Let
X
be an
SCA
(N;t,v)
and let
A
be a set of
a
symbols of
[v]
with
0<a<t
. Let
u
be some permutation of the symbols of
A
, let
X_{u}
be the set of permutations in
X
that cover
u
, and let
n=|X_{u}|
. Now, let
C
be an
n\times(v-a)
array with rows indexed by the permutations in
X_{u}
and columns indexed by the symbols in
[v]\setminus A
. The entry of row
\rho
and column
\nu
of
C
is the number of symbols of
A
that precede
\nu
in the permutation
\rho
.

Note that Construction
3
can be performed for any choice of
A
and then for any choice of

\binom{v}{a}a!=v!/(v-a)!
arrays in this way. We let
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
be the multiset of all the arrays that can be built through the removal of
a
symbols from

via Construction
3
. Chee et al.
[
3
]
prove that each array
\textsf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
is a
CA
{}_{X}(|\textsf{C}|;2,v-t+2,t-1)
. The proof is short, and we provide it here for completeness with a small extension in the case where

is an

(t!;t,v)

Lemma 4
.

Let
X
be an
SCA
(N;t,v)
and let
0<a<t
. Then every array
\textup{{C}}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
is a
CA
{}_{X}(|\textup{{C}}|;2,v-t+2,t-1)
. Moreover, if
X
is an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
, then each
(t-a)
-way interaction

must be covered by exactly

rows of
C
.

Proof.

Let
X
be an
SCA
(N;t,v)
and let
\textsf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
. Then the rows of
C
correspond to the permutations in
X
that cover some permutation
(x_{0},\dots,x_{a-1})
of the elements of some symbol set
A
. Let
D=\{y_{0},\dots,y_{t-a-1}\}
be a subset of
[v]
that is disjoint from
A
and let
T=\{(y_{i},\nu_{i}):0\leqslant i\leqslant t-a-1\}
be a
(t-a)
-way interaction where each
\nu_{i}
is an element of
[a+1]
. Consider the permutations of
A\cup D
that cover
(x_{0},\dots,x_{a-1})
and have the elements of
\tau_{0}(T)
preceding
x_{0}
, the elements of
\tau_{a}(T)
succeeding
x_{a-1}
and the elements of
\tau_{i}(T)
lying between
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant a-1
. There are
\mu(T)
such permutations. Each of these corresponds to a sequence in
\mathcal{S}_{v,t}
which must be covered by at least one permutation in
X
. Moreover, a row of
C
covers

if and only if the corresponding permutation in

covers one of these sequences. Therefore, there are at least
\mu(T)
rows of
C
that cover

If
X
is an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
, then each sequence of length

is covered by exactly one permutation in

. Using the above argument, it can be seen that there are exactly
\mu(T)
rows of
C
that cover
T
.
∎

Suppose
X
is an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
. Then every sequence of length

is covered by exactly one permutation in

. Therefore, every sequence of length

Theorem 5

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let

be integers such that

(t,v)\geqslant a!\,\textup{{CAN}}_{X}(t-a,v-a,a+1)
.

For a row
\rho
of an array
\textsf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
, let

be the number of times the symbol

appears in
\rho
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant a
. We call
(m_{0},\dots,m_{a})
the

of

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
be an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
, let
0<a<t
, and let
m_{0},\dots,m_{a}
be non-negative integers that sum to

. Then, across all the arrays in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)

t!
rows with multiplicity vector
(m_{0},\dots,m_{a})
.

Proof.

Let
\textsf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
and let
A
be the set of symbols that were deleted from
X
to generate
C
. In order to generate a row in
C
with multiplicity vector
(m_{0},\dots,m_{a})
, the symbols in
A
must appear in positions
j+\sum_{i=0}^{j}m_{i}
for
0\leqslant j\leqslant a-1
in the corresponding permutation in
X
where positions are indexed from 0. Now, there are
t!
permutations in
X
and for each permutation, there is one set of symbols in these positions. Therefore, across all the arrays in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
, there must be exactly
t!
rows with multiplicity vector
(m_{0},\dots,m_{a})
.
∎

Recall that a row of a
\textsf{CA}_{X}(N;t,k,v)
is
constant
if it contains only one symbol (i.e.
m_{i}=k
for some
i
). Since the alphabet for each array in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
has

symbols, then by Lemma

(a+1)t!
constant rows across all the arrays in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
. Recall that there are
v!/(v-a)!
arrays in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
. Therefore, the average number of constant rows per array in
\mathcal{C}_{a}(X)
is
(a+1)t!(v-a)!/v!
.

Excess coverage arrays with strength 2

In this section, we describe computations relating to the existence of excess coverage arrays with strength 2. The results of these computations allow us to prove Theorem
2
. Consider a strength 2 covering array with excess coverage. Let
T=\{(c_{1},\nu_{1}),

(c_{2},\nu_{2})\}
be a 2-way interaction. If
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}
, then
\mu(T)=2
but if
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{2}
, then
\mu(T)=1
. Following the convention of Chee et al.
[
3
]
, we refer to the former kind of pair as
constant pairs
and the latter as
non-constant pairs
. Let
C
be a
CA
{}_{X}(N;2,k,v)
where every 2-way interaction
T
is covered exactly
\mu(T)
times. For distinct columns
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, there are
v^{2}
interactions of the form
\{(c_{1},\nu_{1}),(c_{2},\nu_{2})\}
. Of these,
v
are constant pairs which are covered exactly twice by
C
while the remaining
v^{2}-v
interactions are non-constant pairs and are covered exactly once by
C
. Therefore,
C
has
N=v^{2}-v+2v=v(v+1)
rows. Therefore,
CAN
{}_{X}(2,k,v)\geqslant v(v+1)
. By Lemma
4
, a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
is necessary for the existence of an
SCA
((v+1)!;v+1,v+k-1)
. Chee et al. proved the following upper bound on the number of columns of a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
.

Theorem 7

(
[
3
]
)
.

For
v\geqslant 4
, if a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
exists, then
k\leqslant v+2
.

This theorem, with Theorem
5
, implies that for
t\geqslant 5
, if an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
exists, then
v\leqslant 2t-1
.

We performed a series of computations to find the maximum number of columns
k
for which a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
exists for
v\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}
. The computations for
v=6
are of particular interest since they relate to the
t=7
case of Levenshtein’s conjecture. This is the smallest value of
t
for which the existence of
SCA
s with
t!
permutations has not been fully resolved. In particular, the results for
v=6
allow us to prove Theorem
1
. Another consequence of these computations is that the upper bound of
k\leqslant v+2
on the number of columns in a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
proved for
v\geqslant 4
in Theorem
7
also holds for
v\in\{2,3\}
.

For fixed
v\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}
we recursively found all ways of extending a
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
to a
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k+1,v)
. The base of this recursion was the unique
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,2,v)
(this array has two columns and simply contains every non-constant pair once and every constant pair twice). At each step of the recursion, we took a
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
and for each symbol found all possible
placements
. A placement for symbol
\nu
is a set of
v+1
rows in the
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
such that in each column, the symbol
\nu
appears twice among these rows and all other symbols appear once each. So, if we were to add
\nu
to a
(k+1)
th column
c
of these rows, then all 2-way interactions containing the pair
(c,\nu)
would be covered the suitable number of times. We note that because constant pairs must be covered twice, a placement for symbol
i
will not be a placement for symbol
j
for distinct
i
and
j
. Once all placements were computed, we then found all ways of choosing a placement for each symbol such that these placements partition the rows of the
\textsf{CA}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
. Then, to build a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k+1,v)
, we take our original array and add a new column
c
where the symbol in row
r
is the symbol whose placement
r
belongs to.

As part of these computations, it was important to screen for isomorphisms. Two arrays are isomorphic if one can be obtained from the other by permuting the symbols and permuting the columns. It is important to note that any permutation of the symbols must be applied throughout the whole array. Say we were to swap the symbols 0 and 1 in only column 0 of a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
. Then, the constant pair
\{(0,0),(1,0)\}
would now only be covered once while the non-constant pair
\{(0,0),(1,1)\}
would be covered twice and so the resulting array would not be an excess coverage array. However, if we were to apply the same symbol permutation to every column, we would obtain a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
. This behaviour contrasts with that of traditional covering arrays in which one can apply different symbol permutations to different columns and still obtain a covering array.

Strength 2 orthogonal arrays

In this section, we discuss connections between strength 2 excess coverage arrays and strength 2 orthogonal arrays and analyse the computational results of Section
3
in this context. An
orthogonal array
, denoted
OA
(t,k,v)
is a
CA
(N;t,k,v)
with the property that every
t
-way interaction is covered by exactly 1 row. Unless otherwise specified, when we refer to orthogonal arrays, we are referring to strength 2 orthogonal arrays. We begin by observing upper and lower bounds for
CAN
{}_{X}(2,k,v)
.

Lemma 8
.

For integers
v,k\geqslant 2
,

\textup{{CAN}}(2,k,v)\leqslant\textup{{CAN}}_{X}(2,k,v)\leqslant\textup{{CAN}}(2,k,v)+v.

Proof.

A
CA
{}_{X}(N;2,k,v)
is also a
CA
(N;2,k,v)
so the first inequality holds. Let
N=\textsf{CAN}(2,k,v)
and let
N^{\prime}=\textsf{CAN}(2,k,v)+v
. Let
C
be a
CA
(N;2,k,v)
and let
D
be a
v\times k
array such that
\textsf{D}[i,j]=i
for
i\in[v]
and
j\in[k]
. Then, every 2-way interaction is covered at least once in
C
and every constant pair is covered exactly once in
D
. Therefore the rows of
C
and
D
form a
CA
{}_{X}(N^{\prime};2,k,v)
. Thus, the second inequality holds.
∎

Recall also that
CAN
{}_{X}(2,k,v)\geqslant v(v+1)
. By considering strength 2 orthogonal arrays, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 9
.

If an
OA
(2,k,v)
exists, then
CAN
{}_{X}(2,k,v)=v(v+1)
.

If

is a prime power, then an

(2,v+1,v)
exists
[
6
]
and so a

{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,v+1,v)
also exists. Hence, for infinitely many values of

, we can construct a

{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
whose number of columns is one less than the upper bound given by Theorem
7
. We continue our discussion of orthogonal arrays by characterising exactly when a

{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
contains an

(2,k,v)
.

Lemma 10
.

A
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
contains a subarray that forms an

(2,k,v)
if and only if it contains a constant row for each symbol in
[v]
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
that contains an
OA
(2,k,v)
. In the rows of the
OA
, every 2-way interaction is covered exactly once so the remaining
v
rows of
C
must cover each constant pair once while avoiding all non-constant pairs. The only way to achieve this is if these
v
rows are all constant and contain distinct symbols of
[v]
. Conversely, if
C
contains constant rows for each symbol, then the remaining rows must cover every 2-way interaction exactly once and so must form an
OA
(2,k,v)
.
∎

After
v=6
, the next three values of
v
are all prime powers. Hence, for these values of
v
, we will find
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
containing orthogonal arrays for all
3\leqslant k\leqslant v+1
. Recall our definition of isomorphic excess coverage arrays. If we were to permute the symbols of some column in an excess coverage array, in order to obtain another excess coverage array we need to apply the same symbol permutation to all columns. However, if we apply different symbol permutations to different columns of an orthogonal array, we will still obtain an orthogonal array. Hence, we have fewer symmetries to exploit when it comes to excess coverage arrays. Ideally, given that certain sequence covering arrays require the existence of excess coverage arrays that do not contain an orthogonal array, we would like a computational search method that avoids having to generate excess coverage arrays containing orthogonal arrays. If we were to employ the same search methods used for
v\in\{2,3,4,5\}
in Section
3
for
v>6
, our catalogues of excess coverage arrays would necessarily include those that contain orthogonal arrays. The relative lack of symmetries of excess coverage arrays would further inflate our catalogue. The following lemma informs one possible strategy for cataloguing excess coverage arrays that do not contain an orthogonal array.

Lemma 11
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
and let
C
be a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k,v)
that does not contain an orthogonal array. Then there is some column of
C
such that if that column is removed from
C
, then the resulting
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k-1,v)
does not contain an orthogonal array either.

Proof.

As
C
does not contain an orthogonal array, then by Lemma
10
and without loss of generality, there is no constant row in
C
containing only the symbol 0. Suppose that by deleting any column from
C
, we obtain a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k-1,v)
that does contain an orthogonal array. Then, by deleting any column of
C
, we create a constant row containing the symbol 0. This means that for
0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
, there is a row
r
of
C
such that
\textsf{C}[r,i]\neq 0
and
\textsf{C}[r,j]=0
for
j\neq i
. However, since
k\geqslant 5
, this means there are at least three rows of
C
in which the symbols in columns 0 and 1 are both 0. Thus, the 2-way interaction
\{(0,0),(1,0)\}
is covered at least three times which contradicts the fact that
C
covers every constant pair twice. Therefore, we must be able to delete some column of
C
to obtain a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,k-1,v)
that does not contain an orthogonal array.
∎

The consequence of Lemma
11
is that a complete catalogue of
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,4,v)
that do not contain an orthogonal array can be used to generate a complete catalogue of
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,5,v)
that do not contain an orthogonal array which in turn can be used to generate a complete catalogue of
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,6,v)
that do not contain an orthogonal array and so on. The key is to generate this initial catalogue. This may be achieved by using a method similar to the computations in Section
3
but instead adding two (or more) placements for each symbol to the unique
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,2,v)
. For each symbol, each of these placements would correspond to the rows in which that symbol would appear in one of the new columns being added. The placements chosen for each symbol have to intersect in exactly two rows to ensure that each constant pair in the new columns are covered exactly twice. For two distinct symbols, placements corresponding to the same column must be disjoint so that exactly one symbol appears in every entry of the resulting
CA
X
. The placement corresponding to one symbol in one column and the placement corresponding to the other symbol in a different column must intersect in one row to ensure every non-constant pair in the new columns is covered exactly once.

Furthermore, to ensure that the arrays we build do not contain orthogonal arrays, we can without loss of generality ensure that these arrays do not contain a constant row of 0s. This can be done by choosing the placements for the symbol 0 such that for each of the two constant rows containing 0s in the
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,2,v)
, there is a placement avoiding that row.

Binary covering arrays

In the previous sections, we have been concerned with excess coverage arrays with strength 2. We can build such arrays using Construction
3
with
a=t-2
. However, Construction
3
can be applied for any
1\leqslant a\leqslant t-1
so we are not bound to looking solely at strength 2 excess coverage arrays. In this section, we consider excess coverage arrays with
v=2
. That is,
CA
{}_{X}(N;t,k,2)
. These arrays may be built by letting
a=1
in Construction
3
. In particular, we study these arrays to see whether they may provide additional insight into Levenshtein’s conjecture and the existence of an
SCA
(t!;t,v)
. We consider rows of these arrays as being elements of
\{0,1\}^{k}
. For
u\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, the
weight
of
u
is the number of 1s in
u
.

Lemma 12
.

If
C
is a
CA
{}_{X}(N;t,k,2)
such that
C
covers each
t
-way interaction
T
exactly
\mu(T)
times, then
N=(t+1)!
.

Proof.

Let
T=\{(c_{i},\nu_{i}):0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1\}
be a
t
-way interaction with
|\tau_{0}(T)|=j
. Then
\mu(T)=j!(t-j)!
. For
t
distinct columns, there are
\binom{t}{j}
interactions on those columns with
|\tau_{0}(T)|=j
. Therefore,

N=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{t}{j}j!(t-j)!=\sum_{j=0}^{t}t!=(t+1)!.\qed

We next analyse and count the number of
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
C
be a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
. For
0\leqslant i\leqslant t+1
, there is a constant
x_{i}
such that each element in
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight
i
appears as a row of
C
exactly
x_{i}
times. Furthermore,

x_{i}+x_{i+1}=i!(t-i)!,\text{ for }0\leqslant i\leqslant t.

(1)

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i
. As there is a single element of
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight 0, we can take
x_{0}
to be the number of weight 0 rows in
C
. Let
x_{i}
be the number of times each element of
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight
i
appears as a row of
C
. Let
u\in\{0,1\}^{t+1}
have weight
i+1
and let
T
be a
t
-way interaction with
|\tau_{1}(T)|=i
such that
u
covers
T
. Let
w
be the unique vector in
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight
i
covering
T
. Then
u
and
w
are the only vectors in
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
that cover
T
. As
|\tau_{1}(T)|=i
, we must have
\mu(T)=i!(t-i)!
. As there are
x_{i}
rows in
C
equal to
w
, there must be
i!(t-i)!-x_{i}
rows equal to
u
. Therefore, each element of
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight
i+1
appears as a row of
C

x_{i+1}
times where
x_{i+1}=i!(t-i)!-x_{i}
.
∎

Theorem 14
.

The number of distinct
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
is exactly
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!+1
for
t\geqslant 1
. When
t
is odd, no two of these arrays are isomorphic but when
t
is even, there are
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!/2
isomorphism classes each containing two arrays and one isomorphism class containing a single array.

Proof.

From each non-negative integer solution
x_{0},\dots,x_{t+1}
to the system in (
1
), we can build a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
by adding
x_{i}
rows corresponding to each vector in
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with weight
i
. Therefore, the number of distinct
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
is equal to the number of non-negative integer solutions to (
1
).

Let
s=\lfloor t/2\rfloor
. Now consider
x_{s}
. By (
1
),
x_{s}+x_{s+1}=\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!
. Let
x_{s}
be some integer between 0 and
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!
. Then, in order to satisfy each equation in (
1
), fixing
x_{s}
fixes the values for all other
x_{i}
. Suppose
0\leqslant i\leqslant s-1
. Then
i!(t-i)!>(i+1)!(t-(i+1))!
. Therefore, if
x_{i+1}
is a non-negative integer that is at most
(i+1)!(t-(i+1))!
, then
x_{i}
is a non-negative integer that is at most
i!(t-i)!
. As
x_{s}
is a non-negative integer that is at most
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!
, then
x_{i}
is a non-negative integer for all
i\in\{0,\dots,s\}
. Similarly,
x_{s+1}
is a non-negative integer that is at most
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!
and
i!(t-i)!<(i+1)!(t-(i+1))!
for
s+1\leqslant i\leqslant t-1
, so
x_{i}
is a non-negative integer for all
i\in\{s+1,\dots,t+1\}
. Thus, each non-negative integer choice for
x_{s}
gives rise to a non-negative integer solution to (
1
) and thus, a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
. Therefore, the number of
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
is exactly
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!+1
.

Now we can consider isomorphisms of these arrays. Let
C
be a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
. Since each vector in
\{0,1\}^{t+1}
with the same weight appears the same number of times in
C
, permuting the columns of
C
will not generate a new array. The only other possible isomorphism to consider then is swapping all 1s and 0s in
C
. By (
1
),

x_{i}+x_{i+1}=x_{t+1-i}+x_{t-i}.

(2)

Suppose
t
is odd. Then,
x_{(t+1)/2-1}+x_{(t+1)/2}=x_{(t+1)/2}+x_{(t+1)/2+1}
. Thus,
x_{(t+1)/2-1}=x_{(t+1)/2+1}
and so
x_{i}=x_{t+1-i}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant(t+1)/2
. Hence, swapping 1s and 0s in a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
when
t
is odd will not generate a new array. Therefore, the
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!+1
possible arrays with those parameters must be in distinct isomorphism classes.

Now suppose
t
is even. Then it can be shown by induction on
i
and using (
2
) that
x_{t/2-i}=x_{t/2+1+i}+(-1)^{i}(x_{t/2}-x_{t/2+1})
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant t/2
. In particular,
x_{i}=x_{t+1-i}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant t+1
if and only if
x_{t/2}=x_{t/2+1}
. Thus, if
x_{t/2}\neq x_{t/2+1}
swapping 1s and 0s will generate a new array. Therefore, when
t
is even, there are
\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor!\lceil\frac{t}{2}\rceil!/2
isomorphism classes of
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,t+1,2)
each containing 2 arrays and 1 isomorphism class containing a single array.
∎

To conclude this section, we consider whether analysing the existence of

{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,k,2)
may lead to improvements to the upper bound of Chee et al.
[
3
]
that the number of symbols in an

is at most
2t-1
. Suppose an

{}_{X}(t!;t-1,2t-2,2)
. That is, we obtain an excess coverage array with 2 symbols and where the number of columns is twice the strength. Moreover, this array will cover every interaction

times. Therefore, if we can establish the non-existence of a

{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,2t,2)
, for some value of
t
, then we can improve upon the bound of Chee et al.

In building a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,2t,2)
we essentially need to decide how many times each vector in
\{0,1\}^{2t}
will appear in the array. For a
t
-way interaction
T
, there are
2^{t}
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{2t}
that cover
T
. The number of times these vectors collectively appear in a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,2t,2)
must be
\mu(T)
. If we apply this logic to all possible
t
-way interactions, we can obtain a system of
\binom{2t}{t}2^{t}
equations with
2^{2t}
variables. A non-negative integer solution to this system can be used to generate a
CA
{}_{X}((t+1)!;t,2t,2)
by adding the appropriate number of each vector as specified by the solution.

We can simplify this system by only considering arrays in which vectors with the same weight appear the same number of times. Note that this restriction is not necessary as it was in the case where
k=t+1
. By making this restriction, we reduce the number of variables to
2t+1
, one for each possible weight, and the number of equations to
t+1
since all interactions with the same value of
|\tau_{1}|
will generate the same equation. Specifically, if
|\tau_{1}(T)|=i
for some interaction
T
, then there are
\binom{t}{j}
vectors with weight
i+j
that cover
T
. Let
x_{i}
be the number of times each vector with weight
i
appears in the array. Then, the relevant system of equations is

\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{t}{j}x_{i+j}=i!(t-i)!,\text{ for }0\leqslant i\leqslant t.

(3)

We have found non-negative integer solutions to (
3
) for all
t\leqslant 17
. Therefore, a
\textsf{CA}_{X}((t+1)!;t,2t,2)
exists for all
t\leqslant 17
. These arrays mean that at present, we cannot disprove the existence of an
SCA
(t!;t,2t-1)
for
t\leqslant 18
. We found that no non-negative integer solution to (
3
) exists for
t=18
. However, this does not necessarily rule out the existence of a
CA
{}_{X}(19!;18,36,2)
. Hence, we are also at present unable to disprove the existence of an
SCA
(19!;19,37)
.

6
Conclusion

In closing, we highlight some open problems in the area. Perhaps the most obvious is the resolution of Levenshtein’s conjecture for
t=7
. By Theorem
1
,
v=9
is the only value of
v
for which the existence of an
SCA
(7!;7,v)
is unresolved.

Less is known about the conjecture for
t\geqslant 8
. In an attempt to address the
t=8
case, we briefly tried computing
CA
{}_{X}(56;2,k,7)
however we found the large number of possible placements in the
CA
{}_{X}(56;2,2,7)
a significant barrier to these efforts. Moreover, given the sharp increase in the number of
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,3,v)
and
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,4,v)
as
v
increases shown in Table
2
, we suspect the number of
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,3,7)
and
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,4,7)
may be so large as to render our method of generating exhaustive catalogues difficult.

We believe excess coverage arrays are interesting objects in their own right. One potential problem may be to consider whether a
CA
{}_{X}(v(v+1);2,v+2,v)
exists for any value of
v
other than
v=2
.
