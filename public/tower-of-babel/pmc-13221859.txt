Development and Validation of a Prediction Model for Cardiovascular Risk in Reproductive-Aged Women
Authors: Sonia M. Grandi, Kristian B. Filion, Jennifer A. Hutcheon, Graeme N. Smith, Robert W. Platt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrently available risk scores for cardiovascular disease (CVD) were developed in older populations and do not incorporate sex-specific factors, which limits their ability to provide accurate estimates in younger women.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to develop and validate a prognostic model, including female-specific risk factors, that can identify women at high risk.MethodsWe created a cohort of 262,891 women aged 15 to 45 years with 1 randomly selected delivery from 1999 to 2017 in the Clinical Practice Research Datalink database to develop models for screening women within the first-year postpartum. The primary outcome was incident CVD. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator method and an accelerated failure time Weibull model were used to determine the inclusion of predictors and to estimate the final model. Internal validation via bootstrapping was used to estimate the optimism-corrected measures of model performance.ResultsA total of 943 women (0.81 per 1,000 person-years) experienced a cardiovascular event over a median follow-up of 3.8 years (Q1-Q3 1.5-7.9). Predictors in the final model included traditional CVD risk factors, along with social deprivation, polycystic ovary syndrome, prior use of oral contraceptives, depression, thyroid disorders, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age birthweight, parity, and history of pregnancy complications. Optimism-corrected performance median (Q1-Q3) measures showed modest discrimination (C-statistic: 0.637 [0.623-0.650]) and good calibration (slope: 0.919 [0.905-0.930]).ConclusionsAlthough the model showed modest predictive accuracy and performance, the findings highlight the importance of sex-specific risk factors for identifying women at high risk of CVD in the postpartum period.

Over the last decade, the incidence of serious pregnancy complications has steadily increased.1, 2, 3 These complications, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), gestational diabetes, preterm birth, stillbirth, and placental abruption, are associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes,1,2,4,5 and long-term cardiovascular disease (CVD),6 particularly after pre-eclampsia.5,7,8

Several clinical risk prediction models exist to predict a woman’s longer-term risk of CVD, including some specific to women. However, widely used tools, such as the Framingham and Atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) risk scores, were developed and validated predominantly in older populations (>50 years),9,10 and may not perform well in postpartum women. Although the QRISK3 score—the most commonly used tool in the United Kingdom11— was developed using a cohort that included younger women (mean age 43.3 years) and incorporated several sex-specific conditions (eg, autoimmune conditions),11 none of the commonly used scores account for pregnancy complications or the earlier onset of risk factors for CVD.12, 13, 14 Furthermore, the accuracy of these models for reproductive-aged women is unclear.15, 16, 17 Prior studies evaluating the incremental value of pregnancy complications to established risk scores have used cohorts of women beyond their reproductive years,18, 19, 20 limiting their relevance for early prevention. Given that reproductive-aged women have a low, but clinically meaningful risk of CVD, the routine 6-week postpartum visit represents an opportune time for early risk stratification.21 Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a CVD risk prediction model for reproductive-aged women that integrates pregnancy complications and other sex-specific factors.

Methods

The study followed the Prognosis Research Strategy guidelines for prognostic research,22 and is reported according to the Transparent Reporting of Multivariable Prediction Model statement.23 The data are not available due to the data privacy policies of the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD).

Data source

We constructed population-based cohorts using data from the UK’s CPRD, a primary care database covering over 15 million patients at over 650 general practitioners, and considered representative of the UK population.24 Although CPRD does not capture specialist visits, general practitioners act as gatekeepers, with other providers required to report back.24 CPRD contains comprehensive demographic information, prescriptions, clinical diagnoses, and specialist referrals, with diagnoses recorded using Read codes,24 and prescriptions classified via the British National Formulary. CPRD also captures clinical measures such as blood pressure, laboratory results, and lifestyle variables (eg, smoking, body mass index [BMI], alcohol use), which are often unavailable in administrative databases. Women with one or more deliveries were identified in the CPRD Pregnancy Register, created using over 4000 Read codes and including more than 5,000,000 pregnancies in approximately 1,500,000 women since 2000.25

CPRD data were linked to the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) data, which provide complete hospitalization records (including International Classification of Disease [ICD]-10 diagnostic and Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures, 4th Revision procedure codes], and to the Office for National Statistics mortality data, which includes dates and causes of death (ICD-9 before 2001; ICD-10 thereafter). This linkage allowed identification of diagnostic and procedural codes from the delivery admission and any subsequent CVD events requiring hospitalization or resulting in death. Linkage is available for approximately 75% of English practices and is representative of the broader CPRD population,26 and the linkage process has been well validated.27

Study populations

Women aged 15 to 45 years with a recorded delivery (live birth or stillbirth) in the CPRD Pregnancy Register between April 1, 2000, and March 31, 2017, were eligible. Cohort entry was defined as 42 days postdelivery to align with routine postpartum follow-up—an optimal time for CVD screening. Eligible women were required to have at least 1 year of CPRD registration before cohort entry and at least 1 year of follow-up after a delivery. We excluded women with a history of CVD before cohort entry. For the first deliveries cohort, women with a prior delivery at ≥20 weeks’ gestation were also excluded. Follow-up continued until a CVD event or censoring due to end of registration in the database, last data collection, or March 31, 2018, whichever occurred first. Two cohorts were constructed based on parity: a first delivery cohort and a random delivery cohort, the latter selecting 1 delivery per woman using a random stratified sampling method based on parity (Figure 1). Candidate predictors

Candidate predictors were identified a priori based on clinical knowledge (Supplemental Table 1). Pregnancy-related predictors were measured between the start of pregnancy and 6 weeks postdelivery, and other predictors within 5 years preconception, except postpartum systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure, which were measured anytime from cohort entry up to 6 months postpartum to reflect a blood pressure reading at the time of screening.

Pregnancy-related predictors

Pregnancy-related predictors were defined based on diagnostic codes in the CPRD, HES, or Office for National Statistics (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Preterm birth, stillbirth, multifetal gestation, and miscarriage were recorded in the pregnancy register. Small-for-gestational-age (SGA) birth weight <3rd percentile was defined based on the UK-World Health Organization’s (WHO) reference charts,29,30 and neonatal death based on ICD-10 codes from the WHO’s consensus document (Supplemental Table 2).31 Gestational diabetes, placental abruption, infertility, and HDP were defined based on the criteria outlined in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2.

Other candidate predictors

Clinical and lifestyle predictors were defined using diagnostic codes and/or condition-specific prescriptions before the start of the pregnancy (Supplemental Table 1). Maternal age was defined as the age at the time of delivery. Additional candidate predictors considered in our models included ethnicity, social deprivation, and family history of hypertension and CVD. Interactions between age and each pregnancy complication and between multiple pregnancy complications were also considered. The functional form, lookback periods, and databases used are outlined in Supplemental Table 3. Continuous variables were modeled using restricted cubic splines to flexibly capture their distribution over time. Ordinal variables were specified as linear.

Missing data

Candidate predictors with missing values were imputed using the multivariable imputation by the chained equations method with 20 data sets, based on an imputation model including the predictors, variables that predict missingness, time-to-event, and the outcome. The imputed data sets were split into the training and validation data sets (10 each) for predictor selection and for assessment of model accuracy and performance, respectively. Sensitivity analyses in which women were assumed to be nonsmokers, to have a normal BMI and SBP (defined by the mean value in the cohort), and no other comorbidities were performed to assess the robustness of using the multivariable imputation by the chained equations method on predictors with <50% missing.

Outcomes

The outcome of interest was a composite outcome of coronary artery disease, coronary revascularization, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, unstable angina, and cardiovascular-related death, ascertained using Read codes and ICD-10 codes from cohort entry to the end of the study period.

Data reduction and predictor selection

Predictors with a prevalence <1% were removed. Collinearity was assessed by principal components analysis, and further reduction was performed based on the prevalence of the respective predictor. We developed 2 models using each cohort: a base model with established CVD risk factors (age, BMI, ethnicity, SBP, ethnicity, smoking, hypertension, and family history of CVD) (Table 1), and a full model with predictors selected by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator method using the training data set (predictors selected in >70% of the imputed data sets were retained in the full model) (Supplemental Tables 4 to 6). The 2 models were chosen to assess and contrast the relative contribution of pregnancy and female-predominant factors for CVD prediction, above established risk factors included in existing models. Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of women in the random sample of deliveries and the first deliveries cohorts were presented as means and SDs, medians and 25th-75th percentiles (Q1-Q3), or counts (percentage).

Model development

We fit an accelerated failure time model with a Weibull distribution for both the base and full models, within each of the 10 imputed validation data sets, and pooled the estimates using Rubin's rule.31,32 We chose an accelerated failure time model based on its flexibility in modeling time-to-event data, efficiency in handling censored data, its interpretable measure of survival-free time from CVD, and because it provides an estimate of the baseline survival rate, which facilitates external validation. Importantly, this model allowed us to flexibly model the HR to reflect an increasing risk with time, which aligns with the pattern of risk seen in prior studies.6,33 The goodness-of-fit of the Weibull model was verified using cumulative incidence curves and residual plots (Supplemental Tables 7 and 8). Based on the diagnostic assessments of model fit, the data were determined to be consistent with a Weibull model. The estimates from the Weibull model are presented as survival ratios and corresponding 95% CIs.

Internal validation and assessment of model performance

Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 250 iterations per imputed data set to obtain optimism-corrected performance measures.34,35 This approach improves accuracy by sampling from the distribution of the full population,36 and is recommended when outcomes are rare to reduce bias and overall optimism.37 To reduce computational burden, a random sample of 100,000 individuals was drawn from each imputed data set (∼20% of original sample). Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-statistic, calibration slope and intercept, Somer D-statistic, and Nagelkerke R2. Results across the 20 imputed data sets were summarized using medians and 25th - 75th percentiles (Q1-Q3) to appropriately reflect skewed distributions.38, 39, 40 Risk classification was assessed across a range of cutoff values for the predicted probability of disease using sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios (LRs).

Deviations from preanalysis plan

Our original statistical plan was to evaluate the incremental value of novel predictors beyond established risk scores (QRISK3 and ASCVD). However, because cholesterol data were available for <10% of women, this was not possible. Instead, we compared the full model with a base model including traditional risk factors—age, BMI, smoking, ethnicity, SBP, hypertension, and family history of CVD—closely aligning with QRISK3 and the ASCVD, except for cholesterol.

Software

Data management, cohort development, and multiple imputation were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute). Model development, validation, and predictive performance were conducted using R Statistical Software (versions 3.5.2 and 4.4.1, R Core Team, 2018 and 2024).

Ethics approval

The protocol was reviewed by the International Scientific Advisory Committee of the CPRD and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Jewish General Hospital (site of data storage and analysis). As the data are anonymized, participant consent was not required.

Results

Cohort description

Approximately 850,000 women with HES-linkable data were identified (Figure 1). After excluding those with insufficient medical history or prior CVD or death, about 650,000 women linked to 1.76 million pregnancies remained. Following additional exclusions, the final cohort had 262,891 women with 363,751 eligible pregnancies, comprising 262,891 randomly sampled deliveries and 109,858 first deliveries.

Predictor selection and relative contribution

Of 34 candidate predictors considered, 16 were removed due to collinearity, leaving 20 predictors in the random deliveries model and 14 in the first deliveries model (Table 1, Supplemental Table 6). Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a risk prediction model for CVD in reproductive-aged women that accounts for a full range of pregnancy complications and sex-specific factors. Pregnancy- and sex-specific factors were found to provide important contributions to the overall prediction of survival from CVD in this younger population. The full models showed modest discrimination and moderate-to-good calibration. Despite internal validity, the models need further refinement in diverse cohorts outside of the United Kingdom with a longer follow-up.

The etiology of CVD is multifactorial and varies by sex; yet, most existing risk scores do not adequately incorporate sex-specific factors such as pregnancy complications, polycystic ovary syndrome, depression, and infertility.36,37 Their applicability is further limited because these risk scores were largely developed in cohorts that excluded women under 40 and rely on predictors, like high-density lipoprotein and total cholesterol, not routinely measured in younger women. Given this, we developed models that incorporated pregnancy-related and sex-specific predictors rather than benchmarking the incremental value against existing scores. The identification of social deprivation, mental health indicators, and pregnancy complications, as key predictors, underscores the need for integrated screening strategies to identify, young, high-risk women who may benefit from closer follow-up.

Although the absolute risk of CVD in this population is quite low and many events occurred outside the observation period, the models have the potential to identify women with pregnancy complications who often have underlying, unrecognized cardiovascular risk factors that puts them at higher risk for future CVD.41 The full models highlight that pregnancy-specific risk factors, particularly HDP, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth, are stronger predictors of CVD risk in young women than established risk factors. Moreover, the contribution of blood pressure-related predictors (hypertension, SBP, and HDP) further underscores the central role of hypertension to the etiology of CVD in younger women. The inclusion of HDP supports the notion that pregnancy acts as a cardiovascular stress test, with gestational hypertension potentially signaling an underlying predisposition to later hypertension and long-term CVD.

The risk prediction models in this study were developed for screening women at the 6-week postpartum visit, a time when follow-up rates are high and women affected by a pregnancy complication may be particularly motivated to engage in preventative care. Although the LRs indicate modest stratification, the high specificity and negative predictive values suggest that the models accurately identify women not at risk of CVD, and their low false positive rates minimize unnecessary harm. Although only a small proportion of women are classified as high risk, targeted follow-up and early management of risk factors for CVD could reduce long-term cardiovascular events and lessen the burden on patients and the health care system. The use of population-based cohorts and primary care clinical data enhances the models’ generalizability, making them applicable across diverse and resource-limited clinical settings.

Comparisons with previous studies

To our knowledge, no prior studies have developed a CVD risk prediction, specifically for reproductive-aged women, that incorporates the full range of pregnancy complications and postpartum conditions. Existing research has only evaluated a limited set of pregnancy complications added to traditional risk scores.11,18,20,33,42 Studies from the United States, Norway, and Sweden found little to modest incremental value of HDP, low birth weight, parity, SGA birthweight, and preterm birth to established risk scores (Framingham risk score, ASCVD risk score, and the NORRISK 2).18,20,42 A more recent U.S. study demonstrated that gestational diabetes, HDP, preterm birth, and abnormal birthweight, added to the ASCVD score, doubled the likelihood of women having a predicted CVD risk >10% in the 2 to 7 years following a delivery, with the risk increasing in a dose-dependent manner for each additional complication.33 A 2024 study from England also showed that adding preeclampsia and postnatal depression to the QRISK3 score substantially improved discrimination and calibration, which was consistent in an external validation cohort.43

The discrepancy between prior studies and our findings likely reflect 2 factors. First, most previous cohorts predominantly included older women (≥50 years),18,20,42 in whom traditional CVD risk factors are more prevalent, reducing the relative contribution of pregnancy complications to the overall prediction of CVD risk in younger women.44,45 Second, earlier studies did not evaluate the full spectrum of serious pregnancy complications. Evidence from our meta-analysis shows that 6 pregnancy complications, including preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, placental abruption, and stillbirth, each confer a 1.5- to 3-fold increased risk of later CVD.6 In addition, a Swedish study found that 20 to 50% of the early postpartum mortality risk is attributable to complications, such as gestational diabetes, preterm birth, pre-eclampsia, and SGA.46 Together, these findings emphasize the need to refine existing risk scores using younger cohorts of women and to consider a broader set of pregnancy-related and sex-specific conditions (ie, depression, autoimmune conditions).

Study Limitations

The study has several limitations. First, the low prevalence of CVD events may have reduced the discriminative performance of the models in women at the lowest risk. However, this may be less concerning given the study’s focus on identifying those at highest risk, which is supported by the good overall concordance between the observed and predicted risks. Second, the relatively short follow-up period did not allow for the estimation of lifetime risk of CVD, largely due to censoring of women who transfer out of a practice.24,26 Third, the modest performance of our base models precluded any comparisons between the base and full models using reclassification methods. Fourth, several predictors had a high degree of missingness (>30%). However, multiple imputation likely minimized bias, which was supported by the similar predictor distributions in the sample with and without imputation (Supplemental Table 15). Moreover, sensitivity analyses assuming that women with missing values were nonsmokers with normal BMI and SBP values showed similar model performance (data not shown). Fifth, excluding 15% of pregnancies due to poor data quality may introduce selection bias. This is of minimal concern as these were likely low-risk pregnancies seen by midwives, and therefore would not contribute to the overall prevalence of outcomes and predictors. Finally, external validation in more ethnically diverse populations outside of the United Kingdom with longer follow-up is needed to assess potential improvements in the model’s predictive performance and to assess variations in the relative importance of risk factors, predominantly nontraditional risk factors, as women age.

Conclusions

Pregnancy-related and sex-specific risk factors provide additional prognostic information beyond established CVD risk factors for the identification of reproductive-aged women at risk of CVD. Despite modest discrimination, our models are a first step toward risk stratification in this patient population.
