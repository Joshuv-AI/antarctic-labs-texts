RetroScore: graph edit distance-guided retrosynthesis for accessibility scoring with route metrics
Authors: Sinuo Gao, Xiaofei Zhou, Lu Liang, Jianping Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular generation is a critical method in drug design, but its practical application is often limited by the difficulty of synthesizing the generated molecules. To address this challenge, we present RetroScore, a synthetic accessibility evaluation framework guided by multistep retrosynthesis. Our methodology integrates the semi-template model Graph2Edits with the multistep retrosynthesis planning algorithm Retro*, forming the Graph2Edits-Retro*d system. By incorporating the green chemistry metric of graph edit distance into the reaction cost function and a multistage screening protocol, this system identifies optimal routes while balancing reliability, synthetic efficiency, and economic feasibility. Benchmark evaluations demonstrate a 97.37% planning success rate with balanced optimization across route length, confidence score, and graph edit distance. In the molecular generation task, the RetroScore outperforms six of the seven synthetic accessibility metrics, yielding molecules with enhanced synthetic accessibility profiles across heterogeneous evaluation frameworks. To facilitate practical implementation, we developed an open-access web platform for automated retrosynthesis route prediction and RetroScore calculation, providing researchers with rapid synthetic accessibility assessments. Introduction

De novo drug design constitutes a crucial strategy for combating human diseases; however, it is constrained by prohibitively high development costs. Established computer-aided drug design (CADD) techniques—such as virtual screening (VS) [1], scaffold hopping [2], molecular docking, and pharmacophore modelling [3]—have been extensively employed to reduce discovery expenditures. Nevertheless, these methods often fail to sufficiently address the immense, fragmented drug-like chemical space (~ 1060–10100 entities) [4] or enable de novo compound generation. To circumvent these constraints, AI-powered molecular generative models have emerged, harnessing prior knowledge, inductive biases, and efficient validation workflows to supplant resource-intensive molecular space exploration [5], thereby yielding structurally novel compounds with optimized properties. Approximately 200 innovative approaches have been substantiated in recent years [6]. Typically, Merk et al. [7] implemented a deep recurrent neural network (RNN) trained on bioactive natural products to generate experimentally confirmed retinoid X receptor (RXR) modulator leads; Zhavoronkov et al. [8] pioneered the generative tensor reinforcement learning (GENTRL) framework for small-molecule de novo design, achieving preclinical validation of novel DDR1 kinase inhibitors within 21 days. Despite these achievements, the principal impediment remains the synthetic accessibility (SA) of AI-generated molecules, which critically undermines their acceptance by synthetic and medicinal chemists [6]. Fundamentally, the limited translational success of AI-generated lead compounds stems primarily from inherent SA limitations [6, 9, 10].

The widely adopted metric for SA assessment is the SAScore [11], which is calculated as the sum of fragment contributions and complexity penalties, yielding a quantitative scale from 1 (easy to synthesize) to 10 (challenging to synthesize). While molecules with multiple functional groups, intricate ring systems, or numerous stereocentres are commonly perceived as structurally complex—implying some correlation between SA and molecular complexity—these concepts are substantially distinct. Molecular complexity remains a context-dependent and often ambiguous construct lacking objective quantification methods [4]. Crucially, when evaluating precursor availability, synthetic route length, and operational efficiency, structural complexity does not inherently equate to SA.

Recent advancements in computer-aided synthesis planning (CASP) algorithms have led researchers to systematically generate virtual synthetic routes for target molecules, which can take into account the availability of source reactants, the length of the synthetic route and other factors[12, 13]. This paradigm has catalysed the development of pathway-informed SA assessment frameworks: RAscore [14] leverages AiZynthFinder [15] to annotate molecules with validated routes as “easy-to-synthesize” (ES) and route-unattainable molecules as “hard-to-synthesize” (HS), whereas DeepSA [10], GASA [16], and BR-SAScore [9] employ Retro* [17] to designate molecules with ≤ 10-step pathways as ES and those requiring > 10 steps as HS [14, 16, 18, 19]. However, Retro* provides only a single synthetic route, which may lead to label deviation and inaccurate final evaluation results.

Furthermore, contemporary synthetic chemists exhibit multifaceted considerations when evaluating synthesis complexity, particularly when prioritizing green chemistry principles [20, 21], such as the atom economy and E-factor [22], which emphasize economical, efficient, and environmentally benign synthetic strategies. To satisfy these requirements, we propose RetroScore—a synthetic accessibility metric that integrates green chemistry indicators with a multistep retrosynthesis and multistage screening protocol. Our methodology integrates the one-step retrosynthesis model Graph2Edits [23] with the multistep retrosynthesis planning algorithm Retro* [17], introducing the novel incorporation of graph edit distance [24] as a green economic parameter during retrosynthesis analysis. The graph edit distance quantifies the bond and chirality contributions of intermediates to the final product, simultaneously reflecting the economic feasibility of synthesis. Maximizing the graph edit distance of synthesis routes represents advancing towards the final product with the greatest contribution and speed, effectively reducing unnecessary synthesis steps and waste generation, in line with the principles of green chemistry. This hybrid system (designated Graph2Edits-Retro*d) enables systematic screening of synthetically favourable routes that balance reliability, synthetic efficiency, and economic feasibility. Compared with conventional scoring systems, the RetroScore quantitatively evaluates molecular SA by considering both route abundance and optimized length from the selected “gold-standard” route, thereby demonstrating superior comprehensiveness in assessing synthesis challenges. Notably, when applied as a constraint or scoring component in molecular generation, the RetroScore significantly enhances the SA of AI-designed molecules.

This study utilized three datasets (Table 1): (1) the USPTO Full dataset derived from the work of Lowe [25], which was rigorously reprocessed through the removal of erroneous mappings, duplicate entries, and the exclusive retention of reactions generating a single product (excluding solvents), yielding 1,272,731 reactions partitioned into 8:1:1 train/validation/test splits; (2) the synthetic challenge dataset curated by Chen et al. [17], which contained 190 molecules filtered via heuristic search-based BFS algorithms to evaluate multistep retrosynthesis planning capabilities; and (3) the drug and natural product dataset collected by Ochiai et al. [26], which originally aggregated ~ 30,000 compounds from DrugBank and the “Frontier Research on Chemical Communications” initiative, which was later refined by excluding eMolecules-available entries to yield 11,549 unique compounds.

Graph2Edits-Retro*d

For multistep retrosynthesis planning, our framework extends Retro* [17]—a hybrid methodology synergizing A* search with neural heuristics through a template-based one-step retrosynthesis model. The algorithm employs AND-OR tree representations to codify reaction pathways, augmented by an auxiliary neural network trained on historical planning data to direct exploration towards promising synthetic routes. To this end, we introduce two critical innovations: (1) a reaction cost function driven by the graph edit distance, which governs node expansion prioritization, and (2) a multistage screening protocol (Fig. 1c) for route optimization. Here, we implement Graph2Edits [23]—a semi-template retrosynthesis predictor trained on the USPTO Full dataset (Table S1‒S2)—that outperforms template-based methods through reduced computational overhead and enhanced generalizability to novel molecular entities. The resultant Graph2Edits-Retro*d system enables diversity-driven enumeration of synthetic routes, systematically identifying “optimal synthetic routes” that balance reliability, synthetic efficiency, and economic feasibility, thereby transcending the limitations inherent to single-route proposals. The calculation principle of Graph2Edits-Retro*d is introduced in detail in the following section.

Reliability: In one synthetic route, given the sum of probabilities for each set of precursors, the higher the probability, the more likely the route is to be correct. Synthetic efficiency: The fewer the reaction steps in one synthetic route, the higher the efficiency of synthesizing the target compound. Economic feasibility: Add only the structural elements required for target product at each reaction step within one synthetic route, progressing toward the final product as efficiently as possible

Graph edit distance

The Green Chemistry Principles (GCPs) establish systematic frameworks to minimize or eliminate hazardous substances across chemical product lifecycles [20, 21]. Among the predominant metrics developed over three decades of GCP advancement, atom economy (%, percentage of reactant atoms retained in final products) serves as a critical indicator, where higher values correlate with enhanced environmental sustainability through reduced waste [22].

Building upon Lin et al.’s foundational work [24], we operationalize the graph edit distance (edit_dist) through a GCP-aligned lens: the cumulative structural discrepancies in chirality and bonds across reaction steps. The reaction step (including the final product and intermediates or starting reactants) is transformed into an edit graph (Fig. 2), where matrix axes systematically map atomic indices. Changes in bond connectivity are represented through off-diagonal elements populated by decimal-encoded bond types (e.g., single = 1.0, double = 2.0) at positions (i, j) for each atom pair (i, j) with chemical connectivity. Chirality changes are encoded along the matrix diagonal, where position (i, i) is assumed to be 1 if atom i exists in both and exhibits a change in chirality from the product to intermediates or reactants; otherwise, it is 0. Thus, the graph edit distance of a reaction step R can be easily calculated as the sum of the upper triangular edit graph A∈n×n, as shown in Eq. (1):

A lower end_dist value is strongly correlated with heightened structural similarity between the starting reactants and the final product, which is typically indicative of shorter synthetic routes. Conversely, elevated step_dist magnitudes identify reaction steps requiring substantial bond reorganization—a key step—where maximal molecular complexity is resolved. Synthetic routes exhibiting multiple key steps demonstrate enhanced economic feasibility through an optimized step economy. Thus, edit_dist emerges as a dual-parameter metric for holistically evaluating both synthetic efficiency and economic feasibility.

Reaction cost function

Referring to Retro* [17], in this paper, we designed a reaction cost function (Eqs. 2–3), which is used to guide the retrosynthesis planning direction.

Here, rcR|T and rcm|T calculate the cost of the reaction node R and the molecular node m, respectively, occurring in the current tree T. rcR|T is calculated as the sum of the true cost of reaction cR and the costs of all its child molecular nodes m∈chR; for rcm|T, if the molecular node m belongs to the frontier of the tree FT, indicating that the node has not yet been expanded, it is Vm; otherwise, it is calculated as the minimum cost of its child reaction nodes R∈chm. For Vm, it is calculated using a single-layer fully connected neural network model derived from Retro* [17], trained on a large number of molecules, used for predicting the success probability of retrosynthesis planning.

The definition of cR is as shown in Eqs. (4–6), where confidence_scoreR is the probability corresponding to the top-K groups of candidate precursors output by Graph2Edits [23]. Given that the range of confidence_scoreR is [0,1], to adapt it, the edit_distR of the top-K groups of candidate precursors needs to undergo normalization processing as shown in Eq. (4). The optimal weights w are determined through further experiments, as introduced in the following section. Referring to Retro* [17], the true cost cR remains to be calculated as a negative log-likelihood, ensuring continuity with Vm.

Multistage screening

The multistage screening protocol systematically identifies optimal synthetic routes from the top-N candidate routes by evaluating reliability, synthetic efficiency, and economic feasibility, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 4. Initial filtering retains the top filter_ratio routes based on the confidence_score sum of each reaction step within one route. Subsequently, the relative range [27] (Eqs. 7–8)—a dimensionless dispersion metric for end_dist values—is computed across the remaining candidates. Iterative pruning eliminates the route with maximal end_dist when the relative range exceeds a predefined threshold (coef), continuing until either the relative range ≤ coef or the candidate count ≤ 2.

Flow of the multistage screening protocol. Initially, the top-N routes are sorted according to the sum of their confidence_scores, and the top filter_ratio (90%) of these routes is selected for the subsequent procedure. The relative range of end_dist for the current candidate routes is subsequently calculated. Through iterative comparison of the relative range value with a predetermined threshold value coef (30%), the optimal route is ultimately identified

The ultimate selection prioritizes the route with the maximal average step_dist, designating it as the “optimal route” because of its high density of key synthesis steps. Here, the parameter optimization via combinatorial testing established a filter_ratio = 90% and coef = 30% (Table S3, Supporting Information).

RetroScore

After the above steps, Graph2Edits-Retro*d generates both the enumerated route count (route_num), detailed in Fig. S1, and an “optimal route”. Therefore, the RetroScore is formulated as a composite metric as shown in Eqs. (9–11), comprising two normalized components:

RNScore, calculated by the clipped route_num against a predefined enumeration threshold N, which confines the metric to the [0,1] interval, where elevated values reflect greater synthetic optionality.

RNScore, calculated by the clipped route_num against a predefined enumeration threshold N, which confines the metric to the [0,1] interval, where elevated values reflect greater synthetic optionality.

RLScore, derived through a negative base-10 logarithmic transformation of the clipped “optimal route” step count (route_len), with the addition of 1 to adjust the metric to [− 1,1], exhibiting nonlinear decay dynamics—rapid initial decline for 1–10 step routes (emphasizing step economy) followed by asymptotic attenuation beyond 10 steps (preserving step-length sensitivity while mitigating drastic scoring fluctuations).

Both components are combined via experimentally optimized weights (0.50: RNScore; 0.50: RLScore) through combinatorial parameter optimization (Tables S5–S7, Fig. S2), clipping and yielding a unified [0,1] synthetic feasibility index, which is then linearly scaled to [0,9]. Predefined exceptions include maximal scoring (9/9) for commercially available compounds and minimal scoring (0/9) for unplannable molecules.

Conditional molecule generative model

The natural product-variational autoencoder (NP-VAE), a specialized framework for managing complex molecular architectures in DrugBank [26], serves as the foundation for our conditional generation framework. A total of 7 metrics of molecular SA were collected (Table S4), including the SAScore [11], SCScore [18], SYBA [28], BR-SAScore [9], DeepSA [10], RAScore [14] and GASA [16]. The SAScore and SCScore generated specific score values, whereas the remaining metrics categorized molecules into only two distinct classes: hard to synthesize (HS) and easy to synthesize (ES). To achieve more precise control over the generation of molecules, the SAScore and SCScore were selected as the baselines.

We extend the NP-VAE by integrating synthetic accessibility metrics as conditional vectors to steer molecular generation. These metrics—the SAScore, SCScore, and RetroScore—are encoded as one-hot vectors and injected into both the encoder and decoder modules (Fig. 5), enabling controllable molecular generation through metric-guided latent space navigation. Specifically, we trained four variants: (1) unconditional baseline, (2) SASscore-conditioned, (3) SCScore-conditioned, and (4) RetroScore-conditioned models. Each conditional vector’s dimensionality matches the maximum value of its corresponding metric.

Conditional generation framework based on the NP-VAE. The molecular structure information and the synthetic accessibility metrics are jointly input into the NP-VAE encoder, which subsequently compresses them into the chemical latent space. For the generation process, samples combined with the score are input into the NP-VAE decoder, which then converts them into molecular structures

Reinforcement learning demo

To further investigate the application of the RetroScore in molecular generation, a reinforcement learning demo is explored. REINVENT [29] is an open-source generative AI framework that uses recurrent neural networks and transformer architectures to drive molecule generation. Using REINVENT as an agent, SAScore, SCScore, and RetroScore are employed as scoring components. The score values are linearly rescaled to range from 0 (worst) to 1 (best) according to Eq. (12), followed by a 2000-step reinforcement learning pipeline. Specifically, we obtained four variants: (1) baseline (REINVENT), (2) SAScore-reinforced, (3) SCScore-reinforced, and (4) RetroScore-reinforced models.

Results and discussion

True reaction cost analysis

In multistep retrosynthesis planning, the “selection” phase at each iteration is governed by a reaction cost function (Eqs. 2–3), including the true cost cR (Eqs. 4–6). The true cost integrates confidence_score and edit_dist through weighted summation. To evaluate their relative impacts, we benchmarked varying edit_dist-to-confidence_score weight ratios using Chen et al.’s synthetic challenge dataset [17]. As shown in Table 2, the results demonstrated a monotonic increase in planning success rates with elevated edit_dist weighting, empirically validating its critical role in resolving synthetic complexity. Consequently, an experimentally optimized weighting ratio of 0.9 (edit_dist) to 0.1 (confidence_score) was adopted for subsequent implementations.

Reinforcement learning with RetroScore

Beyond conditional generation, reinforcement learning (RL) is routinely employed to steer molecular generators towards preferred chemical spaces. To benchmark RetroScore in this context, we adopted the default protocol of REINVENT [29] and trained three independent agents whose reward component was a single synthetic accessibility metric—the SAScore, SCScore or RetroScore. After an identical number of RL steps, each policy and the baseline (REINVENT) were prompted to sample 1024 de novo structures. The sampled molecules are provided in Figs. 10a and S4–S7. The reward trajectory is displayed in Fig. 10b, corroborating successful policy optimization. Inspection of the sampled structures reveals that SAScore steers the model towards a minimized number of rings and linearly fused scaffolds between aromatic rings, whereas SCScore favours simple long-chain structures—both strategies artificially inflate the respective reward but sacrifice the accessible chemical space. In contrast, RetroScore achieves a better SA profile while preserving the diversity of the baseline distribution (Figs. S4 and S7), underscoring its utility for SA-oriented RL.

Constructing a web server for public use

A web server has been deployed to facilitate the use of Graph2Edits-Retro*d and RetroScore by researchers (Fig. 11a). The server is publicly accessible at http://aidd.bioai-global.com/RetroScore/. The web interface integrates functionalities for precursor prediction, multistep retrosynthesis planning, and SA evaluation (Fig. 11b). For quick predictions, users can directly input or draw a molecule. Alternatively, CSV files containing the SMILES of the molecules to be evaluated can be uploaded for batch prediction. After clicking the “Start” button, the user is redirected to a results page where the output file can be downloaded (Fig. 11c).

Conclusion

This study introduces RetroScore, a novel synthetic accessibility (SA) evaluation framework that integrates a semi-template one-step retrosynthesis model (Graph2Edits) with a multistep planning algorithm (Retro*), into the Graph2Edits-Retro*d system. Key innovations include (1) the incorporation of green chemistry metrics (e.g., graph edit distance) into the reaction cost function to guide the planning process and (2) a multistage screening protocol for identifying balanced synthetic routes that considers reliability, synthetic efficiency, and economic feasibility.

Benchmark evaluations demonstrate the strong performance of the Graph2Edits-Retro*d system, which achieves a 97.37% planning success rate with optimized trade-offs between route length, confidence score, and graph edit distance. RetroScore, derived from route enumeration and optimal route analysis based on this system, was subsequently applied to guide the generation of synthetically challenging natural products. A comparative evaluation against seven existing SA metrics revealed that the RetroScore outperformed six of them, demonstrating an enhanced sensitivity to synthetic complexity and a significant reduction in the synthesis difficulty of the generated molecules. These results validate the utility of the RetroScore in prioritizing synthesizable compounds within drug discovery pipelines. To facilitate practical implementation, we developed a web platform enabling rapid retrosynthetic route planning and RetroScore calculation (http://aidd.bioai-global.com/RetroScore/). Additionally, RetroScore can be used through a Python API (pip install retroscore).

While RetroScore exhibits robust performance, future work will address computational scalability through machine learning surrogate models, particularly for large-scale virtual screening applications. This framework advances computable synthetic feasibility assessment, offering significant value for generative chemistry and natural product retrosynthesis.
