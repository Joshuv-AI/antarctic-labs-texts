Decoding brain arteriovenous malformations: from genetic insights to modeling the vascular maze
Authors: Kang Xie, Ying Wang, Shifu Li, Hua Chen, Longbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) are complex cerebrovascular anomalies characterized by a tangle of dysplastic vessels, including feeding arteries, a nidus, and draining veins, with the absence of an intervening capillary bed. bAVMs pose a significant risk of severe complications, such as intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, and progressive neurological deficits, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. While advancements in treatments, including microsurgical resection, endovascular embolization, and stereotactic radiosurgery, have improved patient outcomes, the underlying pathogenesis of bAVMs remains poorly understood. The emergence of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has identified critical genetic mutations implicated in bAVMs, such as KRAS and BRAF, which dysregulate the MAPK signaling pathway. Additionally, hereditary forms of bAVMs, including those associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), are linked to mutations in TGF-β signaling pathway genes, such as ENG and ALK1. These discoveries have spurred the development of genetically engineered animal models (GEAMs) that faithfully recapitulate key pathological features of bAVMs, including vascular dysplasia, arteriovenous shunting, and intracranial hemorrhage. However, traditional surgical hemodynamic models and current GEAMs have limitations, particularly in replicating the spontaneous onset and progression of bAVMs or capturing the role of the perivascular microenvironment in disease development. This review evaluates the strengths and limitations of existing bAVM models, emphasizing their contributions to understanding the hemodynamic, molecular, and cellular mechanisms underlying bAVM pathogenesis. Emerging trends, such as advanced imaging, transcriptomic analyses, and patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells, offer new avenues for studying bAVM development and progression. By integrating diverse modeling strategies and advancing translational efforts, innovative diagnostic and therapeutic approaches can be developed. Introduction

Brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) are serious cerebrovascular anomalies that affect approximately 18 out of every 100,000 individuals, predominantly impacting young adults between the ages of 20 and 40 [38, 41]. These malformations are high-flow vascular lesions characterized by a tangle of dysplastic vessels composed of feeding arteries, a nidus, and draining veins, with the absence of a capillary bed that typically mediates pressure and flow (Fig. 1). This abnormal vascular architecture predisposes patients to complications such as intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, and progressive neurological deficits, all of which significantly contribute to morbidity and mortality [67]. Treatment of bAVMs involves microsurgery, endovascular embolization, and stereotactic radiosurgery, which have progressively improved through advances in functional mapping, embolic technology, and treatment precision [15, 58, 85]. Concurrently, multidisciplinary, multimodal strategies have enabled more effective management of complex lesions that were previously difficult to treat [60, 64]. However, despite these successes, the underlying mechanisms driving bAVMs formation and progression remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap presents a significant obstacle to developing preventive and targeted therapeutic strategies.

The pathogenesis of bAVMs is highly complex and thought to involve a combination of genetic, cellular, and environmental factors [82]. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have facilitated the identification of numerous somatic mutations associated with sporadic bAVMs, particularly in the MAPK signaling pathway [13, 30, 52]. Mutations in key regulatory genes such as KRAS and BRAF have been shown to drive abnormal endothelial cells (ECs) behavior, leading to vascular dysplasia and arteriovenous shunting [52, 75]. These discoveries have highlighted the central role of ECs in bAVM pathogenesis, shifting the focus from hemodynamic abnormalities to molecular and genetic contributors. Hereditary forms of bAVMs, such as those associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), add another dimension to disease understanding [1]. HHT arises from germline mutations in the TGF-β signaling pathway, most commonly involving the ENG and ALK1 genes [1]. These mutations disrupt the recruitment and stabilization of perivascular cells, thereby contributing to the formation of abnormal vascular structures [7, 97]. The “double-hit” hypothesis, supported by studies using VEGF stimulation in genetically predisposed mice, suggests that a combination of genetic susceptibility and angiogenic triggers is required for bAVMs formation [28, 82, 91].

Studying bAVMs poses significant challenges due to the inaccessibility of human brain tissue and the limitations of existing disease models. Traditional surgical hemodynamic models, which involve the creation of direct arteriovenous connections, have been instrumental in replicating certain pathological features of bAVMs, such as abnormal hemodynamics, EC proliferation, and smooth muscle cell (SMC) dysfunction [68, 74]. These models have also been used to assess treatment efficacy, particularly for radiosurgery and embolization [21, 42, 70]. However, they fall short in replicating the spontaneous development and progression of bAVMs. Key features of human bAVMs, such as the nidus and the dynamic remodeling of vascular lesions, are often absent in these models. Genetically engineered animal models (GEAMs) have emerged as powerful tools for studying the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying bAVMs. By introducing mutations identified in human bAVMs, these models provide a more physiologically relevant framework for understanding the disease. For example, mouse models with EC-specific Kras mutations have successfully recapitulated key pathological features, including vascular dilation, intracranial hemorrhage, and neurological deficits [18, 63]. Similarly, models targeting the Hedgehog and Notch signaling pathways have elucidated their roles in angiogenesis and vascular remodeling [22, 47].

This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of current approaches to bAVM modeling, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and implications for future research. By examining the anatomical, genetic, and signaling features of various models, we seek to guide researchers in selecting the most appropriate tools for their studies. Building on previous reviews, we integrate recent advances in genetic and experimental models and organize them according to major signaling pathways involved in bAVM pathogenesis [3, 20, 51, 62]. We also emphasize the importance of developing innovative models that address the current gaps in understanding, with the ultimate goal of improving diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for bAVMs.

Traditional hemodynamic and rete mirabile models for bAVMs

Anatomically, bAVMs are characterized by a direct shunt between feeding arteries and draining veins, without an intervening capillary network. To replicate this defining pathophysiological feature, researchers have commonly developed hemodynamic models by creating an anastomosis between the common carotid artery and the jugular vein, bypassing the capillary network entirely [68] (Fig. 2). This approach effectively establishes an arteriovenous shunt, allowing blood from the basilar arteries and contralateral carotid artery to flow retrogradely through the circle of Willis and into the carotid-jugular anastomosis. Consequently, an extracranial steal phenomenon is induced, diverting blood flow from the cerebral circulation. These models were initially introduced in cats to investigate normal perfusion pressure breakthrough (NPPB), a phenomenon observed in bAVMs when autoregulation of blood flow is disrupted [68]. Subsequently, the model was adapted for rats using various anastomosis techniques to better replicate NPPB and explore the hemodynamics associated with bAVMs [10, 39, 44, 70]. Notably, these rodent models have shown pathological similarities to human bAVMs, including heterogeneously thickened vessel walls, splitting of the elastic lamina, thickened endothelial layers, and loss of endothelial continuity [74]. Moreover, ECs in these models exhibit increased proliferation rates, enhanced migratory capabilities, and more robust tube formation compared to control ECs [34]. These pathological and cellular changes mirror key features of human bAVMs.

Hemodynamic models have proven invaluable not only for investigating NPPB but also for developing therapeutic approaches, such as radiosurgery. For example, radiosurgery’s effects on bAVMs, including vessel wall hyperplasia, thrombosis, and inflammatory responses, have been extensively studied using these models [37, 70]. However, it is important to highlight that these models do not include an bAVM nidus, a hallmark structure of human bAVMs, which limits their applicability for studying bAVM formation and its triggering factors. In addition to rodent models, angiographic models using the rete mirabile in cloven-hoofed animals, such as pigs and sheep, have gained prominence [16, 46, 61] (Fig. 2). The rete mirabile is a dense network of arterioles branching from the maxillary artery, which subsequently rejoin the internal carotid artery. Under physiological conditions, the rete mirabile exhibits low blood flow and lacks key features of a bAVM, such as high flow and associated vasculopathy. However, researchers have addressed this limitation by creating a carotid-jugular fistula via neck surgery, exposing the rete mirabile to high flow rates [46]. This approach has proven particularly valuable for studying bAVM responses to radiosurgery. The rete mirabile model closely mimics many of the vascular and cellular changes observed in human bAVMs following radiosurgery, including hyperplasia, thrombosis, and inflammatory processes [32, 81]. Although these traditional animal models have significantly advanced our understanding of bAVM pathophysiology and the therapeutic effects of interventions such as radiosurgery, they have notable limitations. These models do not spontaneously replicate the formation of bAVMs, nor do they mimic the dynamic growth and remodeling of human bAVM lesions. Therefore, they are not suitable for studying the mechanisms underlying bAVM initiation or for identifying the factors that trigger their development.

GEAMs of bAVMs

NGS insights into bAVMs

NGS has emerged as a revolutionary tool in the field of genomics, providing unparalleled capabilities for identifying potential pathogenic mutations associated with various diseases [23, 95]. In the context of bAVMs, NGS enables a comprehensive analysis of the genomic landscape, facilitating the discovery of mutations that may contribute to their pathogenesis [13]. Through high-throughput sequencing, researchers can identify somatic mutations, copy number variations, and epigenetic alterations within the genomes of individuals with bAVMs [4, 52, 73]. By comparing the genomic profiles of bAVM patients to those of healthy individuals, potential pathogenic variants can be pinpointed. This genomic data offers valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the formation and progression of bAVMs, which is critical for understanding how these vascular anomalies develop over time. Once potential pathogenic mutations and altered signaling pathways have been identified, researchers can replicate these genetic changes in animal models [18, 30, 52, 76]. The establishment of spontaneous animal models that reflect the genetic and phenotypic characteristics of bAVMs allows for in-depth investigation into the disease’s etiology. These models enable scientists to study not only the direct effects of the identified mutations but also the interactions between these genetic factors and the local microenvironment of bAVMs.

In contrast to traditional hemodynamic models, which primarily focus on the mechanical aspects of blood flow and pressure in bAVMs, GEAMs provide a more holistic view of the disease. By incorporating genetic modifications, researchers can explore how the local microenvironment, influenced by the identified mutations, impacts bAVM development and progression. This includes examining the roles of inflammatory responses, cellular interactions, and extracellular matrix components in the formation and maintenance of bAVMs [7, 63]. Moreover, these models can facilitate the testing of novel therapeutic interventions aimed at targeting the underlying genetic and molecular alterations [19, 97]. By leveraging the power of NGS in conjunction with innovative animal modeling, researchers are well-positioned to uncover new insights into bAVMs, ultimately leading to improved diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic strategies for affected individuals.

MAPK signaling-related animal models

Recent genomic studies have revealed that aberrant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway represents a major molecular driver of sporadic bAVMs. In particular, somatic activating mutations in genes such as KRAS and BRAF have been identified in ECs within human bAVM lesions, resulting in constitutive activation of the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK cascade [24, 30, 52, 53, 59] (Fig. 3). The MAPK signaling plays a central role in regulating endothelial proliferation, vascular remodeling, and angiogenic responses, processes that are essential for maintaining normal cerebrovascular architecture [88]. Dysregulation of this pathway can therefore promote abnormal arteriovenous connections, vascular dilation, and hemorrhagic instability characteristic of bAVMs [62]. In addition to these driver mutations, emerging studies have suggested that disruption of regulatory genes influencing MAPK signaling, such as GPRASP1, may also contribute to vascular malformation development [40]. To investigate these mechanisms, a variety of animal models have been established, which have provided important insights into the mechanisms underlying bAVM initiation, progression, and hemorrhage.

MAPK pathway activation, triggered by somatic gain-of-function mutations in KRAS or BRAF, or loss-of-function in GPRASP1, promotes abnormal endothelial proliferation and survival, facilitating bAVM formation. Elevated SHH expression enhances Hedgehog signaling, activating Gli by relieving SMO inhibition, which induces localized endothelial activation and abnormal vessel growth. Dysregulated Notch signaling, due to constitutively active Notch1/Notch4 or RBPJ deletion, disrupts downstream gene regulation, causing vascular structural abnormalities. Altered TGF-β signaling, resulting from loss of BMP10, ENG, ALK1, or MGP, impairs extracellular matrix homeostasis, compromises vessel integrity, and hinders vascular maturation, collectively promoting bAVM progression

KRAS mutation-driven bAVMs

Among the MAPK pathway components implicated in bAVM pathogenesis, activating mutations in KRAS (~ 60%) represent the most frequently identified genetic alteration in sporadic bAVM lesions [24, 30, 52, 53, 59]. These discoveries have prompted the development of multiple animal models aimed at recapitulating Kras-driven cerebrovascular malformations and investigating how aberrant MAPK signaling disrupts vascular architecture. Studies using these systems further suggest that the timing, spatial specificity, and mode of Kras activation critically influence the resulting vascular phenotypes.

Early investigations of Kras activation in bAVM pathogenesis primarily relied on pan-endothelial genetic models using the Cdh5-CreERT2 driver to induce endothelial-specific expression of mutant Kras [18, 49]. Conditional activation of KrasG12D during early postnatal development produced widespread vascular abnormalities, including dilated and irregular vessels, excessive vascular branching, and focal intracranial hemorrhage [18, 49]. However, these neonatal induction models did not consistently generate the complex angioarchitecture characteristic of human bAVMs, such as well-defined nidus structures or stable arteriovenous shunting. In addition, early postnatal activation frequently produced systemic vascular defects affecting multiple organs and was often associated with substantial early mortality [18, 49]. Together, these observations indicate that widespread endothelial activation of mutant KRAS during early development disrupts vascular homeostasis but does not reliably reproduce the structural features of human bAVMs. These limitations prompted the development of refined modeling strategies aimed at improving lesion specificity, survival, and phenotypic fidelity.

To address these limitations, later studies introduced strategies providing improved temporal control or brain-specific targeting of Kras activation. Induction of KrasG12D in adult ECs using Cdh5-CreERT2 produced bAVM-like vascular lesions with a penetrance of approximately ~ 55% within about eight weeks after induction [18] (Table S1). These lesions exhibited abnormal arteriovenous connections and dilated, irregular vessels but were generally not associated with spontaneous hemorrhage, and animals maintained normal long-term survival. Subsequent studies further improved anatomical specificity by using brain endothelial-restricted drivers such as Slco1c1-CreER, enabling Kras activation primarily within the cerebrovascular endothelium [18] (Table S1). This approach generated vascular tangles composed of tortuous and dilated vessels resembling human bAVM niduses in approximately half of the animals, most commonly within the cortex, cerebellum, and olfactory bulb. The relatively stable phenotype and preserved survival in these models enabled longitudinal investigation of unruptured bAVM development.

In addition to models based on KrasG12D, more recent studies have explored alternative activating variants such as KrasG12C to further investigate the mechanisms underlying vascular malformation development. Endothelial-specific induction of KrasG12C using the Cdh5-CreERT2 driver in young adult mice produced slowly progressive vascular malformations characterized by visible nidus-like structures, tortuous arteries, and dilated veins with loss of normal capillary architecture [19] (Table S1). Lesions became detectable several weeks after induction and involved both intracerebral and peripheral vasculature, including the spleen, with relatively low mortality during the observation period. In contrast, a second model employing the broadly expressed CAGG-CreERT2 driver resulted in a markedly more severe phenotype [19] (Table S1). Following tamoxifen induction, animals initially appeared normal but rapidly developed disseminated vascular malformations and experienced sudden lethality approximately three to four weeks after induction, accompanied by widespread intracranial hemorrhage and multi-organ vascular involvement. These models highlight how the spatial extent of Kras activation can strongly influence disease severity.

Recent studies have also developed viral-based strategies to model KRAS-driven cerebrovascular malformations, enabling rapid induction of mutant KRAS expression without the need for complex genetic breeding. Among these approaches, systemic delivery of AAV-BR1 encoding KRASG12V produced one of the most robust models reported to date [55] (Table S1). Intravenous injection of AAV-BR1-CAG-hKRASG12V in adult mice resulted in bAVM formation with nearly complete penetrance by approximately 8 weeks post-injection. The resulting lesions exhibited human-like tangled niduses with direct arteriovenous shunting, frequently accompanied by aneurysm-like vascular dilations and spontaneous multifocal intracranial hemorrhages, while animals maintained full survival during the observation period. A complementary strategy combined tamoxifen-inducible endothelial Cre activation with viral delivery of mutant Kras [63] (Table S1). In this model, AAV-PHP.V1-mediated delivery of Cre-dependent KrasG12D in Cdh5-CreERT2 mice generated complex nidus structures composed of multiple feeding arteries and draining veins, with tortuous and dilated vessels and frequent hemorrhagic foci throughout the brain. Lesion burden and survival could be modulated in a dose-dependent manner, providing experimental flexibility for mechanistic studies. However, not all viral approaches achieved endothelial-specific Kras activation. Models based on AAV-PHP.V1-Ple261-iCre exhibited substantial off-target expression in neurons and astrocytes due to Cre leakiness, producing vascular tangles with dilated and plexiform vessels but compromising endothelial specificity [63]. In more aggressive dual-virus configurations, widespread hemorrhagic lesions developed rapidly and animals exhibited complete mortality within the early postnatal period [63]. Collectively, these viral strategies recapitulate key structural features of bAVMs while highlighting the importance of viral design and cell-type specificity when interpreting experimental outcomes.

Although numerous Kras-driven animal models have been developed, these systems differ substantially in their penetrance, anatomical specificity, disease progression, and hemorrhagic phenotype, which should be carefully considered when selecting an appropriate experimental platform. Genetic models based on endothelial-specific activation of Kras using drivers such as Cdh5-CreERT2 or Slco1c1-CreER generally produce relatively stable vascular malformations with moderate penetrance and limited hemorrhage, making them particularly suitable for investigating chronic disease progression and mechanisms of bAVM initiation. In contrast, viral-based approaches, such as AAV-BR1-mediated KRAS delivery, typically generate lesions with higher penetrance and more pronounced hemorrhagic features within shorter timeframes, providing useful platforms for studying rupture mechanisms or therapeutic intervention. Other models, such as broadly expressed Cre drivers or systemic Kras activation strategies, can produce severe multi-organ vascular phenotypes and early lethality, which may be advantageous for rapid therapeutic screening but may complicate interpretation of brain-specific vascular mechanisms. In addition, several technical considerations should be taken into account, including potential Cre leakiness, viral tropism, and variability in recombination efficiency, all of which can influence the reproducibility and interpretation of experimental outcomes. Consequently, the choice of model should be guided by the specific research objective and experimental design.

BRAF mutations in bAVM models

Mutations in BRAF have also been identified in sporadic bAVMs, further implicating MAPK signaling in disease development [24, 30]. The activating BRAFV600E mutation has been detected in ECs within human lesions and results in sustained activation of the RAF-MEK-ERK pathway [75] (Fig. 3). These findings have prompted the development of experimental models to investigate Braf-driven bAVM formation.

To investigate the effects of endothelial Braf activation in the brain, genetic models have been developed that enable cerebrovascular-specific expression of BrafV600E. One such model uses the brain endothelial-restricted driver Slco1c1-CreER to induce conditional activation of BrafV600E during early postnatal development [76] (Table S1). Tamoxifen administration between P1 and 5 produces vascular abnormalities including vessel dilation, increased permeability, and focal hemorrhagic lesions. Longer induction protocols lead to more pronounced vascular dilation, nidus-like structures, and cerebral edema. Despite these abnormalities, animals generally survive during the observation period, allowing longitudinal evaluation of disease progression. These models reproduce several pathological features of human bAVMs and provide a platform for studying the vascular consequences of sustained MAPK activation in brain ECs.

Viral approaches have also been developed to activate Braf in the cerebrovascular endothelium. Systemic delivery of AAV-BR1-Cre in mice carrying the conditional BrafLSL−V600E allele induces vascular abnormalities but does not produce typical bAVM lesions [75]. Intravenous injection in adult Braf fl/+ mice leads to scattered intracranial hemorrhages and approximately 80% mortality within six weeks, whereas Braf fl/+ mice exhibit more severe disease with complete mortality within three weeks. To generate localized lesions in the brain, stereotaxic injection of AAV-BR1-Cre has been used to activate Braf V600E in defined regions [75] (Table S1). In Braf fl/+ mice, this approach produces bAVM-like lesions with feeding arteries, a nidus, and draining veins, with approximately 50% penetrance at two weeks and complete penetrance by four weeks after induction. Lesions progress gradually and are frequently accompanied by spontaneous hemorrhage, while animals can be followed for up to 26 weeks. In Braf fl/fl mice, the same approach results in more aggressive disease, with earlier lesion formation, larger vascular malformations, and reduced survival, as many animals die within the first two weeks after induction. In addition to Cre-based systems, direct viral expression of BrafV600E has also been used to model Braf-driven cerebrovascular malformations. Retro-orbital delivery of AAV-BR1-Braf V600E induces vascular abnormalities with dilated vessels and multifocal hemorrhages in multiple brain regions, including the olfactory bulb, cerebrum, cerebellum, and medulla, while animals remain viable for long-term observation [77] (Table S1).

Overall, Braf-driven models show notable differences in disease severity, lesion distribution, and survival depending on how Braf activation is induced. Genetic models provide controlled systems for examining the effects of endothelial MAPK activation in the brain and allow longitudinal observation of disease progression. Viral approaches, particularly those using localized delivery, can generate more defined bAVM-like lesions with features such as feeding arteries, a nidus, and draining veins within relatively short timeframes. However, widespread viral activation may lead to severe vascular instability and early mortality, which can limit long-term studies. These differences should be considered when selecting an experimental system to study Braf-driven cerebrovascular malformations. Genetic Braf models may therefore be more suitable for mechanistic studies of endothelial MAPK signaling activation, whereas viral or stereotaxic delivery systems are particularly useful for generating localized lesions that facilitate studies of lesion progression and hemorrhagic transformation.

Modeling GPRASP1 mutations in bAVMs

Beyond canonical MAPK signaling driver mutations, disruption of genes that regulate MAPK signaling may also contribute to vascular malformation development (Fig. 3). GPRASP1 encodes a member of the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-associated sorting protein family that regulates the endocytic trafficking and signaling homeostasis of GPCRs, key modulators involved in vascular development and EC function [31, 84]. Recent exome sequencing in AVM patients has identified rare, likely GPRASP1 loss-of-function variants, implicating its dysfunction in cerebrovascular malformation [40]. One such model involves endothelial-specific deletion of Gprasp1 using the inducible Cdh5-(PAC)-CreERT2 driver, with tamoxifen administration between P3 and 5 [40] (Table S1). Loss of Gprasp1 in ECs results in vascular abnormalities in multiple organs. In the brain, more than half of the animals develop bAVM-like lesions characterized by vessel enlargement, vascular tangles, and venous filling adjacent to the middle cerebral artery. Cerebral hemorrhage occurs in approximately 44% of animals and is frequently accompanied by hemosiderin deposition and brain edema. In addition to cerebrovascular lesions, intestinal AVMs are observed in a substantial proportion of animals, indicating systemic vascular involvement. Survival decreases after P17, suggesting that widespread vascular abnormalities may limit long-term observation.

Although Kras, Braf, and Gprasp1 models all implicate MAPK signaling in bAVM pathogenesis, they differ in their genetic basis and experimental utility. Kras mutations, which represent the most frequently detected somatic alterations in sporadic bAVMs, directly activate the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK cascade and are widely considered the most representative genetic drivers of the disease. Accordingly, Kras-driven models are particularly suitable for studying the mechanisms underlying sporadic bAVM initiation and progression. Braf-based models, which activate MAPK signaling downstream of RAS, are less commonly associated with human sporadic bAVMs but can produce pronounced vascular phenotypes in experimental systems, including larger lesions and more extensive parenchymal hemorrhage in some models. These characteristics make them useful for investigating vascular remodeling and hemorrhagic progression. In contrast, Gprasp1 deficiency influences MAPK signaling through a distinct regulatory mechanism. Loss of Gprasp1 enhances GPR4-dependent cAMP signaling and subsequently activates MAPK pathways, representing a mode of pathway dysregulation that differs from the direct oncogenic activation seen in Kras or Braf mutations. Together, these models provide complementary approaches for studying different aspects of MAPK-related vascular malformation biology. Consequently, Kras-driven models are generally preferred for studying sporadic bAVM initiation, whereas Braf- and Gprasp1-related systems may offer additional insights into vascular remodeling, hemorrhagic instability, or alternative modes of MAPK pathway dysregulation.

Notch pathway modeling in bAVMs

The Notch signaling pathway plays a key role in vascular development and ECs fate determination [25] (Fig. 3). When Notch signaling is disrupted, abnormal arteriovenous connections and vascular remodeling defects can occur [47, 48, 50]. These observations have led to the development of experimental models that examine how changes in Notch signaling contribute to bAVM formation and progression.

Notch1 and Notch4 in bAVMs

Among the four mammalian Notch receptors, Notch1 and Notch4 are the primary receptors expressed in vascular endothelial cells and play essential roles in regulating arterial specification and vascular remodeling [17] (Fig. 3). Abnormal activation of Notch signaling, including elevated expression of Notch1 and Notch4, has been reported in bAVM tissues, suggesting that dysregulated Notch activity may contribute to disease pathogenesis [29, 98, 99]. These observations have motivated the development of experimental models in which endothelial Notch signaling is artificially activated.

One of the most widely used systems is the Tie2-tTA; TRE-Notch4int3 model, in which the intracellular domain of Notch4 is constitutively expressed in ECs [47] (Table S1). When activation begins at birth, mice develop fully penetrant bAVMs characterized by enlarged and disorganized vessels with direct artery-vein connections. Vascular enlargement typically precedes the onset of hemorrhage, which occurs primarily in the cerebellum and neocortex, while the brainstem is usually spared. Affected animals often develop neurological symptoms such as ataxia or seizures and generally die between two and five weeks of age. A comparable phenotype is observed in mice with endothelial activation of Notch1 using the Tie2-tTA; TRE-Notch1int3 system [48] (Table S1). In this model, large vascular malformations with prominent arteriovenous shunts develop by P14 and are frequently accompanied by neurological abnormalities including ataxia and lethargy. The vascular lesions display several features that resemble human bAVMs, including abnormal vessel enlargement and direct arterial-venous connections. Because vascular malformations develop rapidly and with high penetrance, Notch1 and Notch4 activation models have been widely used to investigate mechanisms of arteriovenous shunt formation and endothelial cell fate regulation. However, these systems also have important limitations. Disease progression is rapid and animals often die at an early age, which restricts their use for studying long-term lesion evolution or evaluating therapeutic interventions. Consequently, these models are most useful for examining early pathogenic mechanisms of arteriovenous malformation development rather than chronic disease progression.

RBPJ-based modeling

Additional insight into the role of Notch signaling in vascular malformations has been obtained from loss-of-function models targeting the transcription factor RBPJ, the central mediator of canonical Notch signaling [25] (Fig. 3). Endothelial-specific deletion of Rbpj has been achieved using the inducible Cdh5-(PAC)-CreERT2 system [50] (Table S1). When Rbpj deletion is induced in adult mice, no obvious brain arteriovenous shunts are observed during the study period, although mild vascular abnormalities may occur. In contrast, deletion of Rbpj during early postnatal development produces a markedly more severe phenotype [50]. Induction at P1-2 results in fully penetrant arteriovenous shunts by P14, characterized by direct artery-vein connections, nidus-like vascular tangles, and intracranial hemorrhage. These animals also exhibit multi-organ vascular abnormalities and rapid disease progression, with substantial mortality during the early postnatal period.

Collectively, these studies highlight the importance of tightly regulated Notch signaling in maintaining normal cerebrovascular architecture. Gain-of-function models involving endothelial activation of Notch1 or Notch4 demonstrate that excessive Notch signaling can disrupt arterial-venous identity and promote the formation of abnormal arteriovenous shunts. Conversely, loss-of-function models targeting Rbpj show that insufficient canonical Notch signaling can also lead to severe vascular malformations, particularly during periods of active vascular remodeling. These findings suggest that proper levels of Notch activity are critical for preserving vascular patterning and stability, and that both increased and reduced signaling can contribute to bAVM formation. From an experimental perspective, Notch1 and Notch4 activation models are particularly useful for studying mechanisms of arteriovenous shunt formation and endothelial fate specification, whereas Rbpj deletion models provide insight into how loss of canonical Notch signaling affects vascular organization during developmental remodeling. Together, these complementary systems provide valuable platforms for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying bAVM pathogenesis.

Hedgehog signaling-related animal models

The Hedgehog signaling pathway plays an important role in vascular development and angiogenesis [92] (Fig. 3). Sonic hedgehog (SHH), one of its key ligands, regulates EC proliferation, migration, and vascular patterning [6, 36]. Notably, increased SHH expression has been reported in the endothelial layers of human bAVMs, suggesting a potential involvement of Hedgehog signaling in bAVM pathology [22]. These observations have led to the development of experimental models to examine how elevated SHH activity may affect cerebrovascular organization. One such model uses intracerebral delivery of a human SHH plasmid in rats [22] (Table S1). Local SHH overexpression induces abnormal vascular structures at the injection site. The resulting lesions contain enlarged and tortuous vessels that form vascular tangles, with dilated lumens and direct arteriovenous connections. Surrounding regions often show vascular rarefaction, indicating local hemodynamic imbalance consistent with a vascular steal phenomenon. Overall, these abnormalities resemble several structural features observed in human bAVMs, including vessel enlargement and arteriovenous shunting. Despite these similarities, this model relies on plasmid-mediated overexpression rather than genetic alteration and therefore represents an induced angiogenic response rather than a stable hereditary model of bAVM formation. Nevertheless, it provides experimental evidence that increased SHH signaling can disturb cerebrovascular organization and promote arteriovenous connections in the brain. Further studies using genetic or inducible systems may help clarify the role of Hedgehog signaling in bAVM pathogenesis.

Animal models of TGF-β signaling in brain AVMs

The TGF-β signaling pathway plays a central role in vascular development and remodeling by regulating ECs proliferation, migration, and differentiation, as well as the recruitment of pericytes and vascular smooth muscle cells to newly formed vessels [72, 82] (Fig. 3). Dysregulation of this pathway has been strongly linked to HHT, an autosomal dominant vascular disorder characterized by fragile blood vessels, recurrent mucocutaneous bleeding, and AVMs in multiple organs, including the brain [1]. Pathogenic variants in several components of the TGF-β signaling, including BMP9, ENG, ALK1, and SMAD4, are frequently identified in patients with HHT [1]. To investigate how disruption of this pathway contributes to AVM formation, a variety of genetically engineered animal models have been developed targeting Eng, Alk1, Smad4, and related regulators such as Bmp10 and Mgp.

ENG deficiency in bAVM models

ENG is an endothelial co-receptor in the TGF-β signaling pathway that modulates cellular responses to the circulating ligands BMP9 and BMP10 [72] (Fig. 3). Complete loss of Eng disrupts early vascular development: germline homozygous Eng−/− mice die during mid-gestation due to severe vascular defects, demonstrating the essential role of ENG in embryonic vessel formation [5]. As a result, Eng+/− mice have been widely used as a genetic model of HHT1.

In several genetic backgrounds, Eng+/− mice (C57BL/6 and 129/Ola) develop cerebrovascular abnormalities in adulthood, such as micro-AVMs containing nidus-like vascular structures, direct arteriovenous connections, and occasional perinidal aneurysms [65] (Table S1). However, lesion penetrance is relatively low (approximately 30%) and varies among strains, which limits reproducibility. Despite these limitations, this model provided early genetic evidence linking ENG haploinsufficiency to susceptibility to AVM formation.

More consistent vascular phenotypes have been observed in conditional models. Endothelial-specific deletion of Eng using Cdh5-(PAC)-CreERT2; Engflox/flox during early postnatal development results in rapid formation of focal bAVMs and retinal AVMs within the first postnatal week [35, 96] (Table S1). These models directly demonstrate the requirement for endothelial ENG in maintaining vascular integrity during vascular maturation, although vascular abnormalities are not restricted to the brain. In adult animals, loss of Eng alone rarely induces AVM formation [51]. Several models therefore combine Eng deletion with angiogenic stimulation to promote lesion development. For example, global Eng deletion induced by Rosa26-CAG-CreER; Eng flox/flox, followed by focal AAV-VEGF delivery, produces localized bAVMs at sites of VEGF-driven angiogenesis several weeks after gene deletion [11] (Table S1). The resulting lesions display enlarged, dysplastic vessels resembling human bAVM nidus structures and provide experimental support for a “double-hit” mechanism in bAVM pathogenesis. However, lesion formation in this system depends on VEGF stimulation and may be influenced by variability in viral delivery.

The contribution of mural cells to AVM development has also been explored using smooth muscle-specific models. Deletion of Eng in vascular SMCs with SM22-Cre; Eng flox/flox leads to highly penetrant AVMs characterized by enlarged and tortuous vessels, arteriovenous shunting, hemorrhage, and multi-organ vascular involvement affecting the brain, spinal cord, and intestine [11] (Table S1). While this model highlights the importance of mural cell function in maintaining vascular stability, the widespread vascular pathology and early mortality limit its utility for studying brain-specific AVM progression.

ALK1 deficiency in bAVM models

ALK1 is a type I receptor in the TGF-β signaling pathway that regulates ECs behavior and vascular remodeling in response to BMP9 and BMP10 [72] (Fig. 3). Similar to ENG, complete loss of Alk1 disrupts vascular development: germline Alk1−/− mice die during embryogenesis due to severe vascular defects [78]. Heterozygous Alk1+/− mice therefore serve as a genetic model of HHT2. However, spontaneous cerebrovascular lesions occur only rarely in these animals, with occasional vascular abnormalities reported in aged mice, suggesting that Alk1 haploinsufficiency alone is generally insufficient to initiate AVM formation [69].

More pronounced vascular phenotypes arise when Alk1 is deleted in ECs. Embryonic endothelial-specific deletion using L1-Cre; Alk1flox/flox results in fully penetrant arteriovenous shunts affecting multiple organs, including the brain, lung, and intestine [54] (Table S1). These lesions are associated with dilated and disorganized vessels, widespread hemorrhage, and early postnatal lethality. Although this model clearly demonstrates the essential role of ALK1 in vascular development, the systemic vascular defects limit its use for studying brain-specific disease progression. Postnatal endothelial deletion models have provided additional insight into AVM initiation during vascular remodeling. Deletion of Alk1 using Cdh5-(PAC)-CreERT2; Alk1flox/flox during early postnatal stages induces rapid formation of AVM-like vascular lesions near major cerebral arteries [96] (Table S1). Similarly, deletion of Alk1 in venous and capillary ECs using Mfsd2a-CreER; Alk1flox/flox leads to brain and retinal AVMs characterized by direct arteriovenous shunting shortly after gene deletion [56] (Table S1). These models indicate that disruption of Alk1 during postnatal vascular maturation can trigger abnormal arteriovenous connections, although vascular abnormalities frequently extend beyond the brain and disease progression can be rapid.

Adult models combining Alk1 loss with angiogenic stimulation have also been developed. In systems such as Ad-Cre; Alk1flox/flox + AAV-VEGF or Pdgfb-CreERT2; Alk1flox/flox + AAV-VEGF, focal endothelial deletion together with VEGF-induced angiogenesis leads to localized brain AVMs characterized by enlarged, dysmorphic vessels and direct arteriovenous shunting [8, 83] (Table S1). These models support the concept that ALK1 deficiency cooperates with angiogenic signals to promote AVM formation and provide a useful platform for studying lesion initiation in the adult brain. However, the requirement for viral delivery and angiogenic stimulation introduces technical variability.

Additional insight into vascular cell interactions has been obtained from smooth muscle-specific models. Deletion of Alk1 in mural cells using SM22-Cre; Alk1flox/flox results in spontaneous AVM formation accompanied by central nervous system hemorrhage [43] (Table S1). As in the corresponding Eng models, widespread vascular involvement and reduced survival limit the applicability of this model for studying brain-restricted AVM development.

SMAD4 knockout models

SMAD4 is a central intracellular mediator of TGF-β signaling and functions downstream of receptors such as ENG and ALK1 to regulate transcriptional responses in ECs [72] (Fig. 3). Mutations in SMAD4 are associated with a combined syndrome of juvenile polyposis and HHT, indicating a key role for SMAD4 in vascular homeostasis [1]. Experimental models have shown that endothelial loss of Smad4 can promote AVM formation. Endothelial-specific deletion using Cdh5(PAC)-CreERT2; Smad4 flox/flox during early postnatal development results in the rapid appearance of bAVMs within the first postnatal week [96] (Table S1). These lesions exhibit abnormal vascular connections consistent with arteriovenous shunting. As observed in other models targeting the TGF-β pathway, vascular abnormalities are not restricted to the brain and may occur in multiple tissues. Despite this limitation, Smad4 conditional models provide important evidence that impaired downstream transcriptional signaling within the TGF-β pathway can directly contribute to AVM pathogenesis.

BMP10 knockout models

BMP10, together with BMP9, functions as a circulating ligand for the endothelial receptors ENG and ALK1 and contributes to maintaining vascular quiescence and stability [72] (Fig. 3). Disruption of BMP9/10 signaling has therefore been proposed as an upstream mechanism contributing to AVM formation. Postnatal global deletion of Bmp10 using Rosa26-CreER; Bmp10 flox/flox produces highly penetrant brain AVMs characterized by enlarged arteries and veins with direct arteriovenous shunting during the first postnatal week [12] (Table S1). Hemorrhage occurs in a subset of animals, and most mice die shortly after lesion formation. An even more severe phenotype is observed when Bmp9 deficiency is combined with conditional Bmp10 deletion, resulting in fully penetrant AVMs and earlier lethality [12] (Table S1). While these models highlight the importance of BMP9/10 signaling for maintaining normal cerebrovascular architecture, the global nature of gene deletion and the rapid disease progression limit their utility for studying long-term AVM development or tissue-specific mechanisms.

MGP-deficient models of bAVMs

MGP is a non-collagenous protein that regulates vascular mineralization by inhibiting excessive calcium deposition in the vascular wall [66] (Fig. 3). This function is vital for maintaining vascular elasticity and preventing arteriosclerosis [93]. Loss-of-function studies have demonstrated that disruption of Mgp can lead to severe vascular abnormalities. Germline deletion of Mgp (Mgp−/−) results in highly penetrant bAVMs characterized by enlarged vessels, direct arteriovenous connections, and nidus-like vascular structures, frequently accompanied by hemorrhage [94] (Table S1). Although the Mgp−/− model provides a robust system for studying AVM pathogenesis, the global nature of gene deletion limits its usefulness for dissecting cell-specific mechanisms or tissue-specific mechanisms.

Although models targeting Eng, Alk1, Smad4, Bmp10, and Mgp all demonstrate the importance of TGF-β signaling in the development of bAVMs, they differ in their genetic basis and experimental applications. Eng and Alk1, which encode endothelial receptors in this pathway and are frequently mutated in HHT, represent the most clinically relevant genetic drivers. Accordingly, Eng and Alk1 models are widely used to investigate how endothelial dysfunction contributes to AVM formation in HHT. Smad4 models disrupt intracellular signaling downstream of these receptors and show that impaired transcriptional responses within the pathway can also lead to abnormal arteriovenous connections during vascular remodeling. In contrast, Bmp10 models target a circulating ligand of the pathway and produce highly penetrant vascular malformations, highlighting the importance of ligand mediated signaling in maintaining cerebrovascular stability. Finally, Mgp deficiency affects TGF-β signaling through extracellular regulation and represents a different mechanism by which dysregulated pathway activity can promote AVM formation. Together, these models demonstrate that disturbances in TGF-β signaling at multiple levels, including ligand signaling, receptor function, intracellular signaling, and extracellular regulation, can contribute to the development of bAVMs. While Eng and Alk1 models remain the most widely used systems for studying HHT related AVM mechanisms, models based on Smad4, Bmp10, and Mgp provide complementary tools for investigating additional aspects of pathway regulation and vascular malformation biology.

Comparative perspectives on bAVM animal models

Animal models have played a central role in advancing the understanding of bAVMs. Over time, these models have evolved from surgically induced hemodynamic systems to genetically engineered platforms that reproduce key molecular drivers of disease. Each approach provides distinct advantages but also presents specific limitations, and their applicability depends largely on the research question being addressed.

Traditional hemodynamic and rete mirabile models primarily reproduce the abnormal blood flow patterns that characterize bAVMs. By creating direct arteriovenous shunts, these systems allow investigators to study hemodynamic consequences such as altered perfusion pressure, vascular remodeling, and responses to therapeutic interventions including radiosurgery [32, 37]. Because these models do not spontaneously generate a true nidus or arise from defined genetic alterations, they are less suitable for studying the molecular mechanisms that initiate bAVM formation. Nevertheless, they remain valuable for investigating the physiological consequences of arteriovenous shunting and for evaluating treatment responses.

GEAMs have provided a complementary framework for studying the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying bAVM development. Advances in next generation sequencing have identified several signaling pathways that contribute to vascular malformation biology, enabling the creation of models that replicate disease associated mutations [1, 30, 40]. Among these, MAPK pathway models driven by Kras or Braf mutations have been particularly informative for understanding sporadic bAVMs. These systems reproduce abnormal endothelial signaling and can generate vascular malformations with features resembling human bAVM lesions. Depending on the experimental design, they can be used to study lesion initiation, vascular remodeling, or hemorrhagic progression (Table S1). Models targeting developmental signaling pathways such as Notch, Hedgehog, and TGF-β further highlight the importance of endothelial fate specification and vascular homeostasis in maintaining normal cerebrovascular architecture. Notch based models demonstrate that both excessive and insufficient signaling can disrupt arterial venous identity and lead to abnormal arteriovenous connections [48, 50]. Hedgehog related systems suggest that elevated angiogenic signaling can disturb vascular organization and promote arteriovenous shunting [22]. In contrast, models involving components of the TGF-β pathway including Eng, Alk1, Smad4, Bmp10, and Mgp illustrate how impaired vascular stabilization and endothelial mural cell interactions can drive AVM formation, particularly in the context of HHT [2].

Taken together, these experimental systems reflect the multifactorial nature of bAVM pathogenesis. Traditional hemodynamic and rete mirabile models reproduce the physiological environment created by arteriovenous shunting, whereas GEAMs capture the molecular drivers that initiate and regulate lesion formation. Because no single model fully recapitulates all aspects of human disease, combining insights from multiple systems remains essential for understanding bAVM biology. Continued refinement of these models, particularly those that integrate precise genetic alterations with controlled spatial and temporal induction, will further improve their value for mechanistic studies and for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion, challenges and prospective

bAVMs remain a significant clinical and research challenge due to their complex pathogenesis and associated morbidity and mortality. Advances in treatment modalities, including microsurgical resection, embolization, and radiosurgery, have improved patient outcomes [9, 27, 79]. However, the underlying mechanisms driving bAVM formation and progression are not fully understood. Research into bAVM pathogenesis has been greatly facilitated by the development of disease models, particularly GEAMs, which have elucidated the roles of genetic mutations, such as those in the MAPK and TGF-β signaling pathways [80]. Despite these advances, current models have limitations, including their inability to replicate the full range of pathological phenotypes, particularly the interactions between ECs and the perivascular microenvironment [90]. Moreover, while evidence suggests that multiple genes and signaling pathways act in concert to drive bAVM development, most available models focus on a single genetic alteration or pathway [57]. This reductionist approach provides valuable insights but fails to capture the synergistic and multifactorial nature of bAVM pathogenesis. The neurovascular unit, comprising astrocytes, pericytes, microglia, and neurons, plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular integrity and responding to injury [45]. Nevertheless, the contributions of these cell types to bAVM initiation and progression remain poorly understood. Furthermore, existing models often lack the capacity to fully mimic the dynamic remodeling and spontaneous development of bAVMs, limiting their utility for studying disease triggers and progression.

Clinical observations also highlight gaps in our current understanding of bAVM biology. Recurrence of bAVMs has occasionally been reported in pediatric patients despite angiographic confirmation of complete surgical resection [26, 33]. Although angiographic obliteration is generally considered curative, these cases suggest that the biological drivers of bAVM formation may persist even after the visible lesion has been removed [33]. Several mechanisms may contribute to this phenomenon. Microscopic arteriovenous connections that remain below the resolution of current imaging techniques may gradually enlarge under altered hemodynamic conditions following surgery. In addition, pathogenic mutations within ECs may persist in the surrounding vasculature and continue to promote abnormal vascular growth. The developing pediatric cerebrovascular system may further increase susceptibility to recurrence because of its greater capacity for vascular remodeling and angiogenesis. Future experimental models that incorporate genetic alterations and developmental context may help clarify the mechanisms underlying recurrence and provide a framework for studying bAVM progression.

The future of bAVM research lies in developing innovative models that integrate genetic, cellular, and environmental factors to more accurately mimic human disease [87]. Incorporating advanced imaging techniques and transcriptomic analyses into research frameworks offers the potential to study the dynamic processes of bAVM formation and progression in real time [82, 89]. Patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells present an exciting avenue for creating personalized models, enabling researchers to investigate patient-specific genetic and molecular abnormalities [86]. Exploring the perivascular microenvironment is another promising direction [77]. Perivascular cells, including astrocytes and pericytes, likely play key roles in bAVM initiation and progression through their involvement in vascular stability, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling [14, 71]. Understanding these interactions could reveal novel therapeutic targets [77]. Together, developing animal models that reflect the genetic and phenotypic diversity of bAVMs will be essential for testing new therapies. Additionally, integrating biomarkers and molecular imaging techniques into these disease model studies could enhance early detection, disease monitoring, and therapeutic evaluation, ultimately improving patient care.
