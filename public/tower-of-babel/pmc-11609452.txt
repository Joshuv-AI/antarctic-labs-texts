Outcomes of ophthalmology telemedicine in a large health care system beyond initial pandemic crisis☆
Authors: Elaine I. Wu, Mary Reed, Bradley Frueh, Weilu Wu, H. Jane Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the outcome of telemedicine versus in-person visit in a large health care system.DesignRetrospective cohort study.Methods174,245 patients with a telemedicine or in-person visit from September 2020 to June 2021 in the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Health Care System were included. Main outcome measures were whether an initial telemedicine or in-person visit required a subsequent in-person visit and patient characteristics that differed between telemedicine and in-person groups. Charts of selected televisits with return in-person visits were reviewed for diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine.Results27 % of patients who had telemedicine visit had a subsequent in-person visit compared to 13 % of patients who had initial in-person visit. Telemedicine visit was associated with higher risk of needing a subsequent in-person visit compared to an initial in-person visit (RR 2.35, CI 2.30–2.40). Patient characteristics associated with higher risk of telemedicine visit compared to in-person visit included female gender (RR 1.07, CI 1.05–1.08), Asian/Black/Hispanic/other race (RRAsian 1.06, CI 1.04–1.08; RRBlack 1.07, CI 1.04–1.11; RRHispanic 1.03, CI 1.02–1.05; RROther 1.04, CI 1.03–1.06), and English language preference (RR 1.08, CI 1.04–1.11). Age had bimodal distribution with patients younger than 18 and 65 and older associated with less usage of telemedicine. Diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine was 93 %.ConclusionsAlthough telemedicine visit was associated with higher risk of needing return in-person visits, telemedicine was able to address ophthalmic needs in majority of patients with high diagnostic accuracy. This large health care system study supports the continued used of telemedicine in ophthalmology in post pandemic setting.

Introduction

Ophthalmology, traditionally a specialty with the lowest telemedicine usage, was especially impacted amongst specialties during the Covid-19 shutdown with largest patient volume loss of 50–75 % [1,2]. While the pandemic expedited the expansion of telemedicine, ophthalmology had the lowest telemedicine adoption as a specialty, peaking around 30 % which declined with reopening of clinics [3].

Studies on telemedicine in ophthalmology during the early months of pandemic crisis described the adoption of telemedicine and highlighted the socio-economic disparity in patient populations accessing telemedicine [[4], [5], [6]]. While outcomes of telemedicine compared to in-person care has been described in another specialty [7], to our knowledge, there are no studies comparing the effectiveness of telemedicine with in-person visits in addressing patients’ ophthalmology care needs. Evidence is needed to inform continued use of ophthalmology in telemedicine as a permanent option of care delivery in the post pandemic period, particularly to explore outcomes by specific diagnosis.

To this end, we compared telemedicine to in-person visits in terms of requiring subsequent in-person visits within a large integrated health system, how telemedicine performance differed between diagnosis categories, diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine, as well as disparities in telemedicine utilization based on patient characteristics.

Methods

Setting

A retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Health Care System, a large integrated health system with more than 4 million members that uses a comprehensive Electronic Health Record (EHR, Epic system) and patient portal (website and mobile applications). The ophthalmology practice comprises of over 200 ophthalmologists across all 15 medical centers in Northern California. Scheduled telephone visit technology had been widely available for clinical use since 2008 and video visit technology available since 2014 [8]. The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the Kaiser Foundation Research Institute, which waived the requirement for informed consent in this data-only study. This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Study population and outcome measures

We studied visits between September 1, 2020 and June 30, 2021. This study period was chosen for greater generalizability, as pandemic policies had relaxed to freely allow in-person visits and may reflect more accurately potential telemedicine utilization in post pandemic time, while remaining at a significant volume to allow for robust analysis. To capture relatively distinct patient-initiated care episodes, patients who had an in-person, telephone, or video visit with an ophthalmologist (excluding retina subspecialists who do not engage in telemedicine) with no visits within the prior 28 days were included (Fig. 1). A triage protocol guided booking for telemedicine. Patients deemed inappropriate for telemedicine included those who had recent eye surgery or trauma, were referred from outside provider for glaucoma or retina evaluation, presented for cataract evaluation, or complained of acute severe eye pain, acute vision loss, or flashes and floaters. Patients were encouraged to electronically submit photos of their eyes prior to the telemedicine visit via Epic secure messaging.Fig. 1Flow chart diagram of inclusion and exclusion criteria for study.Fig. 1

The EHR and other administrative databases were queried for patient socio-demographics (including language preference and socioeconomic status using the 2010 US census measures at the census block group level and neighborhood residential high-speed internet access level using FCC census tract level data), ocular health (most recent recorded visual acuity, presence of ocular diagnosis within preceding year), non-ocular comorbidities (presence of diabetes/A1c level, presence of hypertension, presence of smoking history, Charlson score), and visit diagnosis. Diagnoses were grouped into categories where possible. Non-specific final diagnoses that were not coded to a specific etiology such as blurred vision, eye pain, red eye, diplopia, and epiphora remained as stand-alone diagnoses. We used multivariate modified-Poisson to examine associations between patient characteristics and receiving initial telemedicine vs in-person visit [9]. [10].

The primary study outcome was whether the patient had a subsequent in-person visit within 28 days of their initial telemedicine or in-person visit. We used multivariate modified-Poisson to examine determine risk of having a subsequent in-person visit in patients who had initial telemedicine versus in-person visit, with adjustment for patient characteristics (sex, age, race, English language preference, socioeconomic status, ocular health, non-ocular comorbidities). We used this modeling approach to examine telemedicine and in-person visits overall, and within the telemedicine group, also repeated stratified models for the most common diagnosis categories to examine potential subgroup associations. We also analyzed whether the subsequent in-person visit was physician-initiated follow-up (defined as time of booking made within 2 business days after initial visit).

To evaluate diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine, chart review of 299 randomly selected patients who had telemedicine visit followed by in-person visit was performed by two ophthalmologists. Charts were reviewed for agreement in diagnosis between the telemedicine visit and in-person follow-up in two analysis – first, allowing patients with non-specific diagnosis from the telemedicine visit (such as blurred vision, eye pain, red eye, diplopia, epiphora without an etiology specific ICD10 code) as having an accurate diagnosis regardless of eventual diagnosis in the in-person follow-up, and then second, excluding these records from analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed in SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute). All hypothesis tests were 2-tailed, and P < .05 was chosen as the significance level.

Telemedicine use expanded rapidly during the initial pandemic shutdown, then declined over time with reopening (Fig. 2), accounting for 28 % of visits in September 2020 and 17 % by June 2021. Physician-initiated returns were similar between patients who had telemedicine visit (68 %, n = 6977) versus initial in-person visit (68 %, n = 11,854).

Chart review of 299 patients who had telemedicine followed by in-person visit showed diagnostic accuracy in 298 patients (99.7 %) when records of 21 patients with non-specific telemedicine visit diagnoses (such as blurred vision, eye pain, red eye, diplopia, epiphora) were included as accurate diagnosis. The one misdiagnosed patient was diagnosed with allergic versus viral conjunctivitis during the telemedicine visit and was subsequently diagnosed with dacryocystitis in the in-person visit two days later which resolved with treatment with no loss of vision. When records of the 21 patients with non-specific diagnoses were classified as misdiagnosis instead, diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine was 93 % (277/299).

Discussion

While several studies described adoption of telemedicine in ophthalmology, its usage trends, and socio-demographic disparity in the early months of pandemic [[3], [4], [5], [6],11,12], our study evaluated telemedicine at a later time when policies relaxed to freely allow in-person visits and likely more closely represent the potential for telemedicine as a permanent delivery adjunct. In contrast to earlier studies which evaluated 2020 trend and found that ethnic minorities and older patients were less likely to receive telemedicine [5,13,14], our study which extended to summer 2021 showed that ethnic minorities were more likely to receive telemedicine and that age had a bimodal rather than linear association, with pediatric patients and patients older than 65 less likely to receive telemedicine. The difference in results may be due to lessening of initial barriers to telemedicine with increased familiarity and availability of technology as well as our study in uniquely adjusting for factors beyond socio-demographics including ocular health and non-ocular co-morbidities which likely confound patient suitability for telemedicine.

To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating outcomes of telemedicine in patients with no visits in the preceding month to evaluate the effectiveness of telemedicine for new complaints (rather than including all telemedicine visit types including for pre-existing complaints and post operative follow-up). In ophthalmology, a field where telemedicine may be thought to be of limited efficacy, our study found that patients seen by telemedicine returned for in-person visits at a rate comparable to that in medicine specialty within KPNC (27 % our study vs 25 % in Reed et al. [7]). As expected, telemedicine required in-person follow up visits more often than initial in-person visit, however, that telemedicine prevented in-person visit in 68 % of visits and generated only 14 % more returns than office visit suggest that telemedicine can be effective in addressing ophthalmic needs. When complemented by imaging studies, telemedicine effectiveness is likely to be augmented as shown by Sanayei et al. who reported in a hybrid model where imaging visit preceded virtual visit with a physician, less than 10 % of patients required in-person follow-up, despite 94 % of the hybrid visits being done for glaucoma and retina indications [15]. In our study, the rate of physician-initiated return (and hence patient initiated return) was similar amongst patients who received telemedicine versus in-person visit, suggesting patient confidence with telemedicine visits may not be dissimilar to office visit. Several prior survey-based studies also show that telemedicine visits were associated with high patient satisfaction of 93–99 % [4,16,17], and similar satisfaction between telemedicine and office visit [3,18], though certain populations such as retina patients may be hesitant towards telemedicine [19]. Surprisingly, in our study, video visits did not perform better than telephone visits in reducing return visits, likely a result of telephone visit patients being asked to submit photos ahead of their visit where applicable, enhancing the effectiveness of telephone visit.

Our result of telemedicine diagnostic accuracy of 93 % (classifying non-specific diagnoses such as blurred vision, eye pain, red eye, diplopia, epiphora as misdiagnosis) in 299 telemedicine patients with return office visit is comparable to previous studies (accuracy of 91–100 % in studies involving 40–500 patients [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]] though specific patient population like glaucoma had lower agreement [25]).

Although telemedicine addressed ophthalmic needs in the majority of patients receiving it, telemedicine clearly has its limitations. Despite employing a triage protocol to determine patients appropriate for telemedicine, the 10 % frequency of final nonspecific diagnoses that could not be coded to an etiology specific ICD10 code shows the diagnostic limitations of telemedicine. Triage protocols may be refined for complaints such as blurred vision and eye pain using expanded questionnaires such as one proposed in Murthy et al. for eye pain [26]. Our study shows that even though telemedicine generated more return in-person visits across all diagnoses, certain diagnoses such as retina/choroid and neuro-ophthalmology generated disproportionally more return visits. This highlights the need for triage protocol formulated during pandemic to evolve in a contemporary fashion.

A significant strength of our study is the rigorous comparison of telemedicine to in-person visit in a geographically expansive health care system with well-developed telemedicine infrastructure at a time beyond the initial pandemic crisis. We quantified the risk of needing a return visit as a proxy of telemedicine effectiveness, identified diagnoses less suited for telemedicine, as well as diagnostic accuracy of telemedicine.

However, our study has several limitations. In addition to being a retrospective study, our telemedicine result is likely influenced by our triage protocol and specific selection of patients appropriate for telemedicine. Our results from a large HMO practice may not be generalizable to other practice types with different patterns of clinical practice and reimbursement. The exclusion of patients who were seen in the retina practice as retina specialists did not engage in telemedicine likely led to an underestimation of patients with retina complaints who require in-person visit. Our study did not evaluate telemedicine in conjunction with asynchronous imaging visits, thereby limiting the generalizability where asynchronous imaging has become more commonplace. Our study evaluated in-person return visits within one month of index visit, as the concern was the longer the return window is extended, the more likely the return visit might be unrelated to the index visit. However, patients may have returned after this period for related complaints and further research may be needed to explore longer follow-up. In addition, as we hoped to include a large study sample, this precluded chart review of all included records which would have allowed an even more accurate assessment of telemedicine diagnostic accuracy. Lastly, this study was not designed to study the outcomes of the patients who had televisit but did not require or return for an office visit.

With Medicare payment for telemedicine services extended until at least until the end of 2024, the establishment of telemedicine infrastructure, patient demand and satisfaction with telemedicine, telemedicine can have an established role in ophthalmic care beyond the pandemic period. While telemedicine expectedly is not equivalent to in-person visit, it appears to reasonably address ophthalmic concerns with good accuracy. Triage protocol may need to evolve to best determine which patients may not be appropriate for telemedicine. Telemedicine effectiveness is likely to be augmented by other developing digital technology including home monitoring devices and artificial intelligence [27,28].
