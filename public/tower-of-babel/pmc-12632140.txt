Associations of adverse childhood experiences with mental health and behaviors among adolescents
Authors: Firoj Al-Mamun, Abdullah Al Habib, Md Joynal Abedin, Moneerah Mohammad Almerab, Paweł A. Atroszko, Mohammed A. Mamun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health and behavioral problems among adolescents are growing public health concerns, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Bangladesh. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are key contributors to adolescent mental health and behavioral problems, yet evidence from LMIC contexts remains limited. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of mental health and behavioral problems and their associations with ACEs among Bangladeshi adolescents. MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,159 adolescents using multistage sampling. Data were collected on sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics, ACEs, health-behaviors, and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire. Logistic regression analyses were performed to assess associations between ACEs and emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs), smoking, physical activity, and suicidal ideation.ResultsOverall, 40.6% of adolescents experienced at least one ACE, with psychological abuse (19.1%) being the most common. EBPs were prevalent: 9.6% had emotional problems, 22.3% had conduct problems, 6.8% had hyperactivity, and 14.2% had peer problems. Lifetime smoking and suicidal ideation were reported by 6.8% and 12.4%, respectively. Daily physical activity was reported among 71.9% of adolescents. ACEs were significantly associated with male gender, high maternal education, poor parental supervision, lack of peer support, parental tobacco or drug use, school truancy, being bullied, exposure to school violence, and loneliness. Exposure to psychological abuse [OR = 1.81 (95% CI: 1.11 – 2.94)], household mental illness [OR = 1.65 (1.00 – 2.70)], and maternal exposure to violence [OR = 2.21 (1.10 – 4.41)] were associated with a higher likelihood of suicidal ideation. Sexual abuse was associated with higher odds of peer problems [OR = 3.13 (1.52 – 6.43), while maternal exposure to violence [OR = 3.86 (1.53 – 9.70)] was associated with higher odds of hyperactivity. Household mental illness was associated with higher odds of emotional problems [OR = 2.19 (1.30 – 3.67)]. A dose–response association was observed, with multiple ACEs being associated with increased odds of EBPs, smoking, and suicidal ideation.ConclusionThe high burden and cumulative association of ACEs with adolescent mental health and behavioral outcomes underscore the need for trauma-informed, culturally appropriate mental health interventions to support adolescent well-being in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25246-y.

Introduction

Adolescence represents a pivotal stage of human development, characterized by rapid psychological, emotional, and social transitions. During this sensitive period, adolescents are particularly vulnerable to mental health and behavioral problems [1, 2]. Globally, mental health disorders account for over 13% of the disease burden among adolescents aged 10–19 years, contributing significantly to disability and premature mortality [2]. If left unaddressed, these problems often persist into adulthood and may adversely impact educational attainment, employment, interpersonal relationships, and overall quality of life. In South Asia, mental disorders account for 16.3% of total disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among adolescents aged 10–14 years, representing one of the highest regional burdens worldwide [3]. Despite this, most adolescents in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Bangladesh, lack access to timely mental health care and early interventions [4]. These data highlight the urgent need to identify early-life factors associated with adolescent mental health in LMIC settings, such as Bangladesh. In particular, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to adolescent well-being.

In Bangladesh, emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs) are increasingly prevalent among adolescents and represent a major public health concern. Studies consistently show that many adolescents experience emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer-relationship problems, with prevalence rates notably higher during adolescence than in early childhood [1, 5].The higher prevalence of EBPs during adolescence highlights the importance of identifying associated factors early in this developmental stage.

Beyond EBPs, health-related behaviors such as smoking and physical inactivity also contribute significantly to the public health burden among adolescents [6, 7]. Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that a significant proportion of adolescents engage in smoking [8, 9], with gender, urban residency, and mental health problems identified as associated factors [9]. Likewise, many adolescents fail to achieve recommended levels of physical activity, with 81% of adolescents aged 11–17 years reporting inadequate activity levels [10]. Conversely, another survey found that 66% of adolescents met recommended physical activity levels [11], with association of physical inactivity including gender, younger age, urban residency, higher paternal education, and more screen time [12]. These behaviors not only affect current well-being but also predict chronic health conditions in adulthood, underscoring the need for early prevention.

Suicidal ideation and behavior remain another critical concern among Bangladeshi adolescents. National and regional surveys report concerning rates of suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts among Bangladeshi adolescents [13, 14]. Such outcomes are closely associated with psychosocial factors, including loneliness, bullying, lack of peer support, and infrequent parental involvement [13], highlighting the complex interplay between mental health and social environments.

Among the factors shaping adolescent health, ACEs have gained significant attention. These include physical, emotional, or sexual abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, all of which are strongly associated with mental health problems in adolescence and beyond [15, 16]. ACEs are associated with poor emotion regulation, heightened stress reactivity, and greater engagement in risk-taking behaviors, including substance use, suicidality, and physical inactivity [15]. While most ACE-related research originates from high-income countries, adolescents in LMICs like Bangladesh may face even greater vulnerability due to poverty, inadequate child protection systems, overcrowding, and limited access to mental health care [15]. Understanding how ACEs are associated with EBPs, smoking, physical inactivity, and suicidal ideation is therefore essential to inform trauma-informed interventions in resource-constrained settings.

Despite the high burden of ACEs and psychosocial adversities in Bangladeshi adolescents, few studies have examined how these factors jointly affect adolescent health outcomes [17–19]. Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that a large proportion of adolescents report exposure to at least one form of adversity [18], with high levels of physical and psychological abuse commonly documented [17, 19]. Such findings highlight that ACEs are not isolated experiences but widespread realities that can profoundly influence adolescent development. However, most existing research has largely focused on prevalence estimates or specific associations, with limited attention to the cumulative burden or dose–response associations of multiple adversities [1, 5, 8, 9, 11–13]. Addressing this gap is essential to provide a comprehensive picture of adolescent vulnerability and resilience in this context.

Socio-demographic and psychosocial factors are also examined in relation to ACEs and their consequences [15, 20–22]. Females typically report more internalizing problems (e.g., emotional symptoms), while males are more prone to externalizing behaviors (e.g., peer-relationship problems) [1]. Factors such as parental education, household income, and location interact with school environment, peer relationships, and parental support to shape adolescent mental health outcomes [1, 23]. Additionally, poor academic performance and weak parent–child bonding have been associated with both ACEs and EBPs [24, 25]. Examining these factors alongside ACEs may provide a more nuanced understanding of adolescent health risks in Bangladesh.

Guided by the ecological model of child development [26, 27], this study conceptualizes adolescent health as shaped by multiple interacting layers of influence, including early adversity, sociodemographic context, and psychosocial environment [28, 29]. This framework provides a basis for examining how individual, family, and contextual factors jointly relate to adolescent health outcomes.

Objectives and contribution of the present study

The present study aimed to (i) estimate the prevalence of emotional and behavioral problems and health-related behaviors among Bangladeshi adolescents; (ii) examine how sociodemographic and psychosocial factors relate to ACEs; (iii) assess the combined role of these factors in relation to adolescent health outcomes; and (iv) explore the cumulative associations of ACEs (the graded relationship where greater exposure to multiple adversities is associated with increased odds of adverse outcomes) with these outcomes. By addressing these objectives, the study offers generalizable insights into how early adversities and contextual factors jointly relate to adolescent mental health and behaviors in a resource-constrained LMIC setting, such as Bangladesh.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2024 and October 2024 among high school students (grades 7–10, generally equivalent to 12–17 years of age) in Phulbari, Kurigram, Bangladesh. This upazila (sub-district) was selected for logistical feasibility. It covers an approximate area of 156.40 km2 and has an estimated population of around 160,000. The sub-district includes approximately 30 secondary schools, serving both urban and rural communities. Located in the northern Kurigram district, Phulbari is among the most flood-prone and socioeconomically disadvantaged areas of Bangladesh. Like many rural sub-districts, it faces high poverty, limited healthcare access, and resource-constrained schools. However, its mix of urban and rural schools makes it broadly comparable to other semi-rural regions in the country.

Participants

A two-stage stratified cluster sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. In the first stage, schools were stratified based on their geographical location (urban vs. rural), followed by a random selection of eight schools, three from urban areas and five from rural regions. In the second stage, four specific grades (7, 8, 9, and 10) were identified within each selected school, and all students enrolled in these grades were invited to participate in the study. A total of 1183 students were initially approached. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 24 students were excluded because they were absent on the survey day, lacked parental consent, or provided incomplete outcome data. Given that this represents only about 2% of the sample, these exclusions are unlikely to have biased the study findings. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (i) being present in class during the survey and (ii) enrollment in grades 7–10. Students were excluded if they (i) did not provide consent, (ii) had disabilities (such as severe physical, visual, hearing, or cognitive impairments) that prevented participation, (iii) were absent during data collection, or (iv) were excluded during data analysis if they submitted incomplete responses for outcome variables.

Data collection process

Prior to data collection, approval was obtained from school authorities, including school principals and class teachers. Students received a written informed consent form, which required review and approval by their parents or guardians before participation. Upon receiving written consent from both students and their guardians, the research team conducted the survey. To ensure clarity, any questions or concerns regarding the questionnaire were addressed by class teachers and research team members present during the survey. A pilot study was carried out with 30 randomly selected students to assess the clarity and readability of the questionnaire. Responses from these students were excluded from the final analysis. The structured questionnaire incorporated validated instruments and context-specific items. The full English version is available in Supplementary File 1.

Measures

Sociodemographic factors

The survey gathered a range of sociodemographic variables, including participants' location, gender, age, grade, family type, parental education, and occupational status. Age was categorized as 12–14 years and 15–17 years, consistent with distinctions between early and middle-to-late adolescence. Level of education was divided into four groups: grade 7, grade 8, grade 9, and grade 10. Parental education levels were categorized into three groups: up to primary (combining no formal education and primary level), secondary (high school and college), and tertiary (undergraduate and postgraduate degrees). Furthermore, fathers' occupations were divided into four categories: job (salaried employment in government or private organizations), business, farming, and other (including unemployed and daily wage earners, rickshaw pullers, and those involved in informal labor). Mother’s occupations were classified into two categories: homemaker and non-homemaker (including business, job holder, and maid).

Psychosocial characteristics

Psychosocial indicators were recorded as binary response (yes or no) across defined timeframes (past month, past year, and lifetime) aligned with Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) practice [13]. Using these intervals helps capture both recent and chronic exposures while improving the accuracy and relevance of participant recall. Lifetime factors included whether their parents had a history of tobacco or drug usage and whether they had any close friends. Past year-related factors were used to assess their involvement in physical fights, experiences of serious injury leading them to miss at least one school day, and feelings of loneliness. Lastly, past month-related factors included parental homework checking, poor parental understanding, lack of peer support, truancy, and experiences of bullying. These psychosocial indicators were adapted from the World Health Organization’s GSHS and have been used in prior studies in South Asia, including Bangladesh [13]. This supports the contextual relevance and content validity of the items used.

Adverse childhood experiences

ACEs were assessed using a standardized questionnaire that captured self-reported responses across various domains of childhood abuse and household dysfunction [15, 16]. The abuse domains included psychological, physical, and sexual abuse. Psychological abuse was evaluated through items addressing verbal insults or actions instilling fear of physical harm by a parent or household adult. Physical abuse involved instances of being pushed, grabbed, slapped, or hit hard enough to cause marks or injuries. Sexual abuse focused on incidents of being touched or fondled inappropriately, or being coerced to touch someone else's body sexually.

Household dysfunction was assessed through indicators of mental illness, maternal exposure to violence, and criminal behavior within the household. Mental illness included the presence of a depressed or mentally ill household member or instances of attempted suicide by a household member. Maternal exposure to violence was measured through items detailing physical abuse toward the mother, such as pushing, slapping, or being threatened with weapons. Criminal behavior was captured by identifying cases where a household member was incarcerated.

Respondents were defined as exposed to a category if they responded “yes” to 1 or more of the questions within that category. Prevalence of individual items and category-level exposures is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Although ACE questionnaires were originally developed in Western contexts, the items were carefully adapted and reviewed by local public health and psychology experts. The questionnaire was translated into Bengali and back-translated into English following standard WHO procedures, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. To further ensure clarity, the Bengali version was piloted among 30 randomly selected adolescents (aged 11–17 years), who reported no major difficulties in comprehension. Internal consistency of the adapted ACE scale was modest (Cronbach’s α = 0.69), consistent with prior ACE research reporting similar values (e.g., Cronbach’s α = 0.64) [30].

Health-related behaviors

Health-related behaviors were assessed using three binary (yes/no) items on lifetime smoking history, physical activity, and lifetime suicidal ideation. Physical activity was examined by asking participants if they engaged in leisure-time activities such as brisk walking, running, playing sports, or exercising, in accordance with the World Health Organization's recommendation of at least 60 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity daily [11]. Smoking history was assessed with the question: "Have you ever smoked a bidi, cigarette, or used any tobacco products?" [9]. To assess lifetime suicidal ideation, participants were asked, “In your lifetime, have you ever wanted to die or thought about ending your own life?" [14].

Emotional and behavioral problems

Emotional and behavioral problems were assessed using the Bengali version of the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), which is freely available for non-commercial use from the official SDQ website [31, 32]. It includes five subscales: Emotional Symptoms (e.g., often unhappy, downhearted), Conduct Problems (e.g., fights with other children), Hyperactivity/Inattention (e.g., constantly fidgeting or squirming), Peer Relationship Problems (e.g., tends to play alone) and Prosocial Behavior (e.g., considerate of other people’s feelings). Each subscale consists of five items scored as Never = 0, Somewhat True = 1, or Certainly True = 2, yielding a maximum of 10 points per subscale. Following the established scoring guidelines, a total difficulties score was calculated by summing the four problem subscales (Emotional, Conduct, Hyperactivity/Inattention, and Peer problems), excluding prosocial behavior. A total score of ≥ 20 was considered abnormal. The cut-off score for the subscales is as follows (out of a possible 10): Emotion ≥ 7, Conduct ≥ 5, Hyperactivity ≥ 7, Peer Problems ≥ 6, and Prosocial Behavior ≤ 4 [32]. The scale has been previously used in the Bangladeshi context to measure adolescent emotional and behavioral problems [1, 5].

Ethical consideration

The authors confirm that all procedures involved in this study adhere to the ethical standards guideline outlined in the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the CHINTA Research Bangladesh Ethics committee [Ref: chinta/2024/08(1)]. Prior to data collection, approval was secured from relevant school authorities, including principals and class teachers, to ensure institutional support and ethical alignment. Informed consent statement was obtained from the students and their parents/legal guardians. This ensured that participation was voluntary and based on informed decision-making. Both student assent and parental consent were mandatory for participation, and students were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Additionally, no financial compensation or incentives were provided to participants in accordance with ethical standards for recruitment practices. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Bangladesh, offering monetary incentives may introduce coercion or undue influence due to prevalent economic vulnerabilities, thereby compromising voluntary participation. These measures were implemented to uphold the integrity and ethical rigor of the research process.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY., USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics and the prevalence of ACEs, emotional and behavioral problems, and health-related behaviors (i.e., smoking, physical activity), and suicidal ideation. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were first conducted to examine bivariate associations between demographic and psychosocial characteristics and ACEs, categorized into three groups (no exposure, one exposure, and two or more exposures) to ensure adequate cell sizes for reliable statistical testing. In addition, chi-square tests were used to assess associations between childhood exposure to abuse and the outcome variables: ever smoking, physical inactivity, suicidal ideation, and emotional and behavioral problems (emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer problems).

To address the first set of research questions (associations between ACEs and outcomes), binary logistic regression models were constructed for each outcome variable, with individual ACE categories as predictors. All regression models were adjusted for potential sociodemographic and psychosocial confounders, including location, gender, age, grade, family type, father’s education, mother’s education, father’s occupation, mother’s occupation, parental supervision (e.g., rarely checking homework), poor parental understanding, lack of peer support, parental tobacco or drug use, school truancy, bullying, absence of close friends, physical fighting, serious injury, and loneliness during the past year. These covariates were selected a priori based on the ecological model of child development [26, 27] and prior evidence linking them to either exposure to ACEs or adolescent health outcomes. Although some of these psychosocial confounders (e.g., bullying, fighting, loneliness) appear similar to SDQ outcomes such as conduct or peer problems, they were measured as discrete social-environmental exposures with specific timeframes (e.g., past month, past year). By contrast, the SDQ subscales capture internalized or externalized psychological symptoms via a validated psychometric tool. This distinction justifies their inclusion as confounders rather than overlapping outcome variables.

To assess the cumulative association of ACEs on health outcomes, we performed binary logistic regression analyses with ACE exposure as the main independent variable [categorized into no ACE (no exposure), one ACE (one exposure), two ACEs (two exposures), and three or more ACEs (three or more exposures)] to capture a graded dose–response relationship. Separate regression models were constructed for each health-related behavior and emotional/behavioral outcomes. All cumulative models were adjusted for age and gender. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factor (< 5) and tolerance (> 0.2). Missing data were handled using listwise deletion in the analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke R2 values to indicate the proportion of variance explained by each model.

Results

Prevalence of childhood exposure to abuse and household dysfunction

Psychological abuse emerged as the most common form of ACEs, reported by 19.1% of adolescents. Specifically, 14.9% indicated that they were frequently sworn at, insulted, or put down by a parent or adult in the household, while 8.8% reported being made to feel afraid of physical harm.

Physical abuse was reported by 7.7% of participants. Among them, 5.7% experienced frequent pushing, grabbing, or slapping, and 3.4% were hit so hard that they sustained marks or injuries. Sexual abuse was reported by 5.5% of the adolescents; 3.7% were inappropriately touched, and 2.7% were coerced into touching someone else's body inappropriately.

Regarding household dysfunction, 14.2% reported that a household member was depressed or mentally ill, while 5.3% had a household member who attempted suicide. In terms of domestic violence, 7.2% of participants witnessed their mother being physically assaulted. Specifically, 2.8% saw their mother being pushed, slapped, or having objects thrown at her; 3.0% witnessed her being kicked, bitten, or hit with a hard object; and 3.1% reported she had been threatened with or harmed by a knife or gun. Additionally, 8.1% of adolescents reported that a household member had been imprisoned, indicating exposure to criminal behavior (Supplementary Table 1).

Description of the participants

The study included 1,159 Bangladeshi adolescents with a nearly even urban–rural distribution (51.1% urban, 48.9% rural). The sample consisted of 52.4% boys and 47.6% girls, with most participants aged 12–14 years (59.1%) and enrolled in grade 7 (33%). A majority lived in nuclear families (70.3%). Regarding parental education, 47.6% of fathers and 54.2% of mothers had attained secondary-level education. Fathers were most engaged in farming (44.3%), whereas most mothers were homemakers (90.1%). Psychosocial indicators revealed that 52.6% of adolescents reported poor parental supervision, 33.0% reported truancy, and 33.3% experienced bullying. Additionally, 58.2% reported having no close friends, and 13.8% experienced loneliness in the past year (Supplementary Table 2).

Distribution of socio-demographic and psychosocial factors by ACEs

Analysis of socio-demographic characteristics revealed significant associations with exposure to multiple ACEs. Boys had a significantly higher prevalence of two or more ACEs than girls (19.3% vs. 11.2%; p < 0.001). Maternal education was also associated with ACE exposure: adolescents whose mothers had tertiary education reported the highest prevalence of multiple ACEs (24.0%), while those with mothers who had secondary-level education had the lowest prevalence (12.9%; p = 0.002). Paternal occupation was another significant factor; with adolescents whose fathers worked in jobs other than farming reporting higher ACE exposure (p = 0.019).

Parental supervision was associated with ACEs; those whose parents rarely checked their homework had a higher prevalence of multiple ACEs compared to those receiving regular supervision (16.6% vs. 14.2%; p = 0.020). A lack of peer support showed a strong association with ACEs: 21.0% of adolescents without peer support reported multiple ACEs compared to 12.2% among those with strong peer networks (p < 0.001).

Further, parental tobacco or drug use was associated with a higher prevalence of multiple ACEs (22.3% vs. 13.7%; p < 0.001). Truancy (23.0% vs. 11.7%) and being bullied (23.1% vs. 11.6%) were also significantly associated with multiple ACEs (p < 0.001 for both). Similarly, adolescents involved in physical fights (22.3% vs. 11.5%) or those who reported serious injuries (16.4% vs. 13.7%) had higher ACE prevalence than their peers (p < 0.001 and p = 0.008, respectively). Finally, loneliness was associated with the highest prevalence of multiple ACEs (32.5% vs. 12.7%; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2).

Associations between adverse childhood experiences and smoking, physical activity, suicidal ideation

Chi-square analyses revealed several significant associations between childhood abuse exposures and health-related outcomes. These results indicate statistical associations and do not imply causation; effect sizes were explored in regression models.

For smoking, 10.4% of adolescents who were psychologically abused, particularly being frequently sworn at, insulted, or put down reported smoking (p = 0.042). Higher smoking prevalence was observed among those exposed to sexual abuse, including 23.3% of adolescents who were touched or fondled sexually (p < 0.001) and 16.7% of those coerced to touch another person inappropriately (p = 0.030). Severe physical abuse, such as being hit hard enough to leave marks or cause injury, was also significantly associated with smoking, with 23.1% reporting this behavior (p < 0.001). In addition, 16.7% of adolescents who witnessed maternal exposure to violence involving a weapon (p = 0.017) and 14.8% of those with a household member who attempted suicide (p = 0.011) reported smoking. Among adolescents who experienced psychological abuse, 28.3% of those frequently insulted or put down (p < 0.001) and 32.4% of those made to feel afraid of being physically hurt (p < 0.001) reported suicidal ideation. Severe physical abuse was also significant, 46.2% of adolescents who were hit hard enough to leave marks reporting suicidal ideation (p < 0.001). Similarly, 46.5% of adolescents who experienced being touched or fondled in a sexually reported suicidal ideation (p < 0.001). Witnessing maternal exposure to violence was also significant; 55.6% of adolescents whose mothers were threatened with or hurt by a knife or gun reported suicidal ideation (p < 0.001). Adolescents who witnessed a household member attempting suicide had a suicidal ideation rate of 32.8% (p < 0.001), while those exposed to criminal behavior in the household reported a suicidal ideation rate of 20.2% (p = 0.017) (Table 1).

Associations between adverse childhood experiences and emotional and behavioral problems

Psychological abuse showed strong and consistent associations with emotional and behavioral problems. Adolescents frequently insulted or put down (Item 1) exhibited significantly higher rates of emotional problems (17.3%, p < 0.001), conduct problems (28.9%, p = 0.025), and hyperactivity (11.0%, p = 0.018). Those made to feel afraid they would be physically hurt (Item 2) also had elevated emotional (17.6%, p = 0.004) and conduct problems (32.4%, p = 0.011), though associations with hyperactivity and peer problems were not statistically significant.

Severe physical abuse, such as being hit hard enough to leave marks (Item 2), was significantly associated with all behavioral domains except peer problems. Among these adolescents, 23.1% reported emotional problems (p = 0.004), 46.2% had conduct problems (p < 0.001), and 15.4% experienced hyperactivity (p = 0.031). A similar pattern was seen for less severe physical abuse (Item 1), where hyperactivity was notably elevated (16.7%, p < 0.001).

Sexual abuse also demonstrated significant links with adverse outcomes. Adolescents who reported being touched or fondled in a sexual way (Item 1) had high rates of emotional (23.3%, p = 0.002), conduct (41.9%, p = 0.002), and peer problems (25.6%, p = 0.028). Being coerced to touch another person inappropriately (Item 2) was associated with higher hyperactivity (20.0%, p = 0.004) and peer problems (26.7%, p = 0.046), although emotional and conduct problems did not reach statistical significance.

Witnessing maternal exposure to violence was particularly associated with emotional problems. Adolescents who witnessed their mother being threatened with or hurt by a knife or gun (Item 4) had significantly elevated rates of emotional (30.6%, p < 0.001), conduct (47.2%, p < 0.001), and hyperactivity problems (27.8%, p < 0.001). Similar associations were observed across other items related to maternal exposure to violence, particularly for emotional and hyperactivity issues.

Having a household member with mental illness who attempted suicide (Item 2) was strongly linked to emotional (34.4%, p < 0.001) and conduct problems (39.3%, p = 0.001), but not to hyperactivity or peer problems.

All models were adjusted for age and gender, and "No ACE" served as the reference group. Adolescents with three or more ACEs were 3.83 times more likely to have ever smoked compared to those with no ACEs (AOR = 3.83; 95% CI: 1.92–7.66, p < 0.001). The odds of smoking were not significantly elevated among those with one or two ACEs. This suggests a dose–response relationship, where higher cumulative ACE exposure significantly associated with an increased likelihood of smoking in adolescence. No statistically significant associations were observed between ACE exposure and physical activity across any ACE category. Interestingly, the odds ratios for two ACEs (AOR = 0.79) and three or more ACEs (AOR = 0.64) were below 1, suggesting a potential trend toward reduced physical activity with increased ACE exposure, although these findings were not statistically significant. There was a strong, positive and significant association between the number of ACEs and suicidal ideation. Adolescents with one ACE had 2.53 times higher odds (AOR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.63–3.93, p < 0.001), two ACEs had 3.77 times higher odds (AOR = 3.77; 95% CI: 2.16–6.58, p < 0.001), and three or more ACEs had a 9.42-fold increase in odds (AOR = 9.42; 95% CI: 5.38–16.50, p < 0.001) compared to no ACE. This clearly demonstrates a strong dose–response association, with greater ACE exposure linked to higher odds of suicidal ideation.

Adolescents exposed to increasing levels of ACEs demonstrated significantly higher odds of developing emotional problems, highlighting a clear cumulative association. Those with one ACE had adjusted odds ratios of 2.07 (95% CI: 1.29–3.32, p < 0.01), indicating more than double the likelihood of emotional symptoms compared to those with no ACEs. This risk further escalated with two ACEs, showing an AOR of 2.48 (95% CI: 1.29–4.75, p < 0.01). Adolescents exposed to three or more ACEs exhibited the most pronounced association, with an AOR of 5.27 (95% CI: 2.77–10.02, p < 0.001), signifying a fivefold increase in the likelihood of emotional issues. This supports a strong cumulative association of ACEs on emotional symptoms.

Significant associations were found only among those with three or more ACEs, who had 2.51 times higher odds of conduct problems (AOR = 2.51; 95% CI: 1.51–4.19, p < 0.001). One ACE was also significant (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.02–1.96, p < 0.05), indicating that even minimal exposure can elevate risk, but cumulative ACEs amplify it further.

Adolescents with increased exposure to ACEs exhibited significantly elevated odds of hyperactivity and attention-related symptoms. Specifically, individuals with two ACEs showed AOR of 2.17 (95% CI: 1.06–4.44, p < 0.05), while those with three or more ACEs demonstrated an even greater association, with an AOR of 3.22 (95% CI: 1.54–6.71, p < 0.01). Notably, the presence of only one ACE did not yield a statistically significant association. No statistically significant associations were observed between ACE categories and peer relationship problems.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs) among school-going adolescents in Bangladesh and explores how these outcomes are associated with ACEs, sociodemographic, and psychosocial factors. The findings indicate that 59.4% of adolescents reported no ACEs, 25.2% experienced one, and 15.4% reported two or more. ACEs were significantly associated with key sociodemographic and psychosocial indicators, including gender, maternal education, paternal occupation, parental supervision, lack of peer support, parental tobacco or drug use, school truancy, bullying, physical fights, serious injury, and loneliness. Regarding health-related behaviors, 6.8% of adolescents reported smoking, 71.9% were physically active, and 12.4% reported suicidal ideation. Physical abuse (e.g., being hit hard enough to sustain injuries) was significantly associated with smoking, while fear of physical harm and witnessing threats against mother involving weapons were associated with suicidal ideation. The prevalence of emotional problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer problems were 9.6%, 22.3%, 6.8%, and 14.2%, respectively. Emotional difficulties were more common among adolescents exposed to frequent verbal abuse by a parent or adult, while hyperactivity was significantly higher among those who experienced sexual coercion. Witnessing maternal exposure to violence, such as being pushed, grabbed, slapped, or having objects thrown, was associated with emotional problems, whereas witnessing threats against mother involving weapons was associated with elevated odds of hyperactivity symptoms. Overall, ACEs demonstrated a strong cumulative association with EBPs and smoking and suicidal ideation.

The present study found that 40.6% of Bangladeshi adolescents had experienced at least one ACE, and 15.4% reported two or more exposure. These findings are broadly consistent with global estimates, although prevalence varies across contexts. For instance, a study in China reported that 27.1% had experienced at least one ACE, and among them 37.6% reported multiple ACEs [33]. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis of 22 countries reported pooled estimates of 22.4% for one ACE, 13% for two, 8.7% for three, and 16.1% for four or more ACEs [34]. These differences may stem from variations in age groups studied, cultural and socioeconomic contexts, ACE measurement tools, and definitions or thresholds applied. Nonetheless, the prevalence observed in this Bangladeshi sample highlights a substantial burden of childhood adversity that is comparable to or higher than many other LMIC contexts, reinforcing the urgent need for targeted intervention and prevention efforts.

In line with prior literature, our study identified significant associations between ACEs and several sociodemographic and psychosocial factors. Notably, boys in our sample reported more ACEs than girls, which diverges from findings from a Brazilian birth cohort, where girls were more likely to report a higher number of adversities [35]. This inconsistency may be due to gender-based differences in disclosure patterns, societal norms around emotional expression, or gendered exposure to certain types of adversity across contexts. Surprisingly, adolescents whose mothers had tertiary education reported the highest prevalence of multiple ACEs. This contradicts the common assumption that higher maternal education is protective against childhood adversity [35]. However, this observation may reflect greater awareness, recognition, and communication of ACEs in more educated households, rather than a genuinely higher prevalence. Children of mothers with tertiary education may be more sensitized to the emotional and psychological boundaries that define abusive or neglectful behavior and therefore more likely to recognize and report such experiences [36]. Conversely, in households where maternal education is lower, ACEs may be underreported due to cultural normalization of harsh parenting practices, limited awareness of non-physical abuse forms (e.g., emotional neglect, psychological abuse), or fear of social consequences [37, 38]. Stigma around reporting parental or household dysfunction may be more prominent in less educated or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, further contributing to reporting bias. It is also plausible that in more educated households, there is greater openness in parent–child communication, which may increase children's comfort in disclosing sensitive information. These findings emphasize the need for culturally sensitive ACE screening tools that consider awareness, perception, and reporting barriers, especially in low-resource settings. Furthermore, our findings highlight the importance of social support systems. Adolescents lacking parental supervision and peer support were significantly more likely to report ACEs, supporting the ecological model of child development, which emphasizes the protective role of positive family environments and supportive peer networks [27, 39]. In addition, it is important to acknowledge that adolescents with the most severe trauma or cognitive-emotional distress may have been unable to complete the survey, and were thus excluded from the final analytic sample. This exclusion could result in an underestimation of the true burden of ACEs in the population.

The prevalence rates of smoking (6.8%), physical activity (71.9%), and suicidal ideation (12.4%) observed in this study are in line with previous findings. Earlier studies reported smoking rates of 10.4% among Bangladeshi adolescents [8] and 10% among pre-university students [9]. While the current physical activity level (71.9%) is higher than the 66% reported in a prior Bangladeshi study [11], it still exceeds global estimates indicating 81% of adolescents aged 11–17 years are insufficiently active [10]. This comparatively higher level of activity in our sample might be influenced by the inclusion of rural adolescents who may engage in more physically demanding daily activities, such as walking long distances to school or participating in household chores and farming work. The 12.4% prevalence of suicidal ideation in the present study closely aligns with the 11.7% reported in the Global School-Based Student Health Survey 2014 conducted in Bangladesh [13], although it is higher than a more recent Bangladeshi study, which reported a 6.8% lifetime suicidal ideation, 2.3% with a plan and 0.8% with attempts, declining to 3.2%, 1.5%, and 0.6%, respectively, over the past year [14]. The current findings may reflect the increased psychological distress, possibly due to the associations with psychosocial and childhood adversities, which were not considered in previous studies [14].

Psychological abuse, such as being repeatedly insulted, humiliated, or threatened by caregivers, was strongly associated with suicidal ideation in our sample. This aligns with previous research showing that emotional maltreatment is associated with internalizing symptoms, including depression and suicidality [40, 41]. Unlike physical abuse, which may draw more attention due to visible signs, psychological abuse often remains unrecognized and unaddressed; yet can be linked to chronic emotional dysregulation, diminished self-worth, and feelings of hopelessness, which are key factors of suicidal ideation [42]. A study in China similarly found emotional abuse to be a significant correlate of suicidal ideation among adolescents, independent of other ACEs [43], suggesting the cross-cultural relevance of this association.

Household mental illness was also significantly associated with suicidal ideation, consistent with prior evidence showing that growing up with a mentally ill family member can profoundly affect adolescents’ mental health [44]. The presence of household mental illness, such as family history of mental disorder, family history of suicide and psychological trauma has been associated with suicidal ideation [45]. In Bangladesh, where mental illness remains highly stigmatized and underdiagnosed, the psychosocial burden on children may be even greater due to limited access to support systems. Witnessing threats against mothers involving weapons, such as knives or guns, was another strong factor associated with suicidal ideation in our study. These findings are supported by previous studies indicating that exposure to severe domestic violence, especially maternal victimization, can be linked to chronic stress, helplessness, and internalizing symptoms such as depression and suicidal thoughts in adolescents [46]. Adolescents may internalize these traumatic experiences, increasing their vulnerability to suicidal ideation due to a perceived lack of safety, support, or control over their environment. Collectively, these results highlight how both direct abuse (psychological maltreatment) and indirect exposure to trauma (household dysfunction and witnessed maternal exposure to violence) can be associated with suicidal ideation in adolescents. The consistency of our findings with previous studies reinforces the universality of these risk pathways while also pointing to urgent intervention needs in the Bangladeshi context, where child protection systems and adolescent mental health services are insufficiently developed. School-based screening for ACEs, psychoeducation for caregivers, and trauma-informed counseling could be critical in addressing this growing public health issue.

This study revealed that 9.6% of adolescents experienced emotional problems, 22.3% had conduct problems, 6.8% showed hyperactivity, and 14.2% reported peer problems. These findings are broadly consistent with earlier research conducted in Bangladesh. For instance, a recent study of 1496 adolescents reported prevalence rates of 9.09% for emotional disorder, 21.72% for conduct disorder, 6.21% for hyperactivity, and 15.10% for peer problems [1]. The close alignment between these studies suggests a stable pattern of emotional and behavioral problems among Bangladeshi adolescents. In contrast, a study among children aged 3–4 years reported lower prevalence rates, with 4.4% experiencing emotional disorder, 5.6% exhibiting conduct disorder, and 1.9% showing hyperactivity [5]. These discrepancies are likely due to developmental differences in emotional regulation, behavioral expression, and social interaction. Younger children may not yet display the full spectrum of internalizing or externalizing behaviors that typically emerge during later childhood or adolescence, when cognitive, emotional, and social demands become more complex. Additionally, differences in informants such as reliance on parental reports for younger children versus self-report measures for adolescents may contribute to variation in prevalence estimates.

This study found that specific forms of ACEs, such as sexual abuse, witnessing maternal exposure to violence, and household mental illness were significantly associated with distinct emotional and behavioral problems among school-going adolescents in Bangladesh. These findings are in line with a growing body of global evidence demonstrating that particular types of childhood adversity are linked to unique psychological outcomes. Sexual abuse was found to be a strong correlate of peer relationship problems, with adolescents who experienced sexual abuse having over three times the odds of reporting peer difficulties compared to their non-abused counterparts. This is consistent with previous studies showing that sexual abuse is often associated with difficulties such as peer-victimization [47]. The long-term psychological distress associated with sexual trauma may hinder adolescents’ ability to form and maintain healthy peer relationships, particularly in socio-cultural contexts such as Bangladesh, where victims may face stigma, shame, and isolation.

Witnessing maternal exposure to violence specifically instances where mothers were threatened with weapons, was significantly associated with greater likelihood of hyperactivity symptoms in adolescents. This association may reflect the psychological dysregulation observed in children exposed to chronic domestic violence [48]. Children in such environments often experience heightened arousal, hypervigilance, and difficulty with attention and impulse control, which are core features of hyperactivity [48, 49]. Our study supports the theory that witnessing maternal victimization may be particularly distressing, potentially because of the child’s emotional attachment to the mother figure and the perceived threat to her safety. Household mental illness emerged as a significant factor associated with emotional problems, echoing results from studies conducted previously [50, 51]. Parental mental illness can be linked with a chaotic home environment, inconsistent caregiving, and emotional unavailability, all of which are associated with the development of mental disorders in children [44, 51]. The observed association in our sample may be exacerbated by the limited access to mental health support in Bangladesh, which can leave both caregivers and children without necessary interventions. Moreover, cultural stigma around mental illness may prevent families from seeking help, further increasing psychological strain on adolescents. Together, these findings emphasize the associations of various ACEs on adolescents' psychosocial development and underscore the importance of context-specific, trauma-informed prevention and intervention strategies. Recognizing and addressing the mental health needs of adolescents reporting exposure to these adversities is vital, especially in LMICs like Bangladesh, where resources are scarce and stigma remains high.

Notably, this study demonstrated a strong cumulative, dose–response association between ACEs and both emotional and behavioral problems, as well as health-related behaviors like smoking and suicidal ideation. It is worth noting that only 6.4% of the sample (n = 74) reported exposure to three or more ACEs. While this subgroup was relatively small, it was sufficient for statistical analysis and revealed meaningful dose–response associations. Nevertheless, results involving this subgroup should be interpreted with caution due to the potential for reduced statistical power. Adolescents with two or more ACEs had significantly higher odds of experiencing emotional distress, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer relationship difficulties, as well as engaging in smoking and reporting suicidal thoughts. This aligns with the extensive literature suggesting that the risk of poor mental health and health-compromising behaviors increases in a dose–response manner with the number of ACEs encountered [15, 16, 40]. The cumulative burden of multiple adversities may disrupt key biological and psychosocial processes during critical development periods, particularly affecting stress-response systems, emotional regulation, cognitive functioning, and interpersonal relationships [52]. Recent large-scale evidence demonstrates a clear dose–response relationship, with increasing number of ACEs linked to higher odds of depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation [53]. Repeated exposure to trauma and neglect has also been associated with increased odds of suicidal behavior in LMICs, where structural inequities and limited resources exacerbate these effects [54]. These findings are particularly concerning in the Bangladeshi context, where stigma and limited access to mental health services may intensify the long-term consequences of childhood adversities. Our study adds to the growing global evidence base by reinforcing the need for early identification of ACEs and the implementation of culturally tailored, trauma-informed school and community-based mental health interventions in LMICs.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several notable strengths. It is among the first to provide nationally relevant data on the prevalence and psychosocial correlates of emotional and behavioral problems among Bangladeshi adolescents, incorporating a comprehensive assessment of ACEs and their cumulative associations. The use of a large, school-based sample enhances the generalizability of the findings to in-school adolescents across diverse settings. Additionally, the inclusion of multiple domains ranging from sociodemographic and behavioral to psychosocial factors allowed for a robust examination of complex associations between childhood adversity and adolescent mental health and behavior. Furthermore, the analysis evaluates dose–response relationships, demonstrating how greater exposure to ACEs is associated with elevated odds of adverse outcomes. Finally, the findings carry practical implications for designing culturally tailored, trauma-informed interventions in schools and communities, particularly in resource-limited settings.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study restricts causal interpretations between ACEs and mental health outcomes. Longitudinal research is needed to establish temporal relationships. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall and reporting biases, particularly for sensitive topics such as abuse and mental health symptoms. It is possible that some of the excluded participants had experienced more severe trauma or associated psychosocial challenges, leading to non-response and potentially underestimating the prevalence and severity of ACEs in the final analytic sample. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a single sub-district (Phulbari, Kurigram), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to adolescents in other regions of Bangladesh. Although the use of stratified cluster sampling enhances internal validity, regional variations in socio-cultural, economic, and educational factors may influence the prevalence and impact of ACEs. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings nationally, and future studies should aim to replicate this research in diverse geographic locations. Third, as the sample included only school-going adolescents, findings may not be representative of out-of-school youth, who might be at greater risk of ACEs and emotional or behavioral issues. Fourth, the study did not include protective factors such as resilience, social capital, or access to mental health services, which could moderate the effects of adversity. Also lower statistical power for low frequency dichotomous predictors in binary logistic regression analyses increases Type II Error probability. The results need to be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, the subgroup of adolescents exposed to three or more ACEs comprised only 6.4% of the sample (n = 74). While this size was sufficient for analysis, it may limit the statistical power and generalizability of findings involving this group. Finally, the internal consistency of the ACE scale was modest (Cronbach’s α = 0.69). This is not uncommon for ACE instruments, which capture diverse and multidimensional forms of adversity, and similar reliability values have been reported in prior research (e.g., α = 0.64) [30].

Policy and practice implications

The findings carry important implications for both policy and practice in low- and middle-income countries. The observed high burden of emotional and behavioral problems, alongside their strong associations with ACEs, highlights the urgent need for integrated, trauma-informed mental health services within the school system in Bangladesh. Routine screening for ACEs and psychosocial difficulties should be incorporated into existing school health programs. Additionally, teachers, school counselors, and healthcare providers should receive training to recognize signs of trauma and to provide appropriate referrals or support.

Community-level and national child protection strategies must also prioritize prevention and early intervention, focusing on reducing family violence, promoting positive parenting practices, and improving mental health literacy. Evidence from other studies shows that school-based life skills programs and community parenting interventions can reduce psychological difficulties, strengthen parent–child relationships, and improve adolescent psychosocial outcomes [55–58]. Adapting such strategies in Bangladesh, with cultural tailoring and resource-sensitive delivery, could provide feasible and impactful approaches to adolescent mental health.

Conclusions

This study highlights a substantial burden of emotional and behavioral problems among school-going adolescents in Bangladesh and demonstrates associations between ACEs and multiple health outcomes. Specific exposures such as sexual abuse, household mental illness, and maternal exposure to violence were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of peer relationship problems, emotional problems, and hyperactivity, respectively.

These findings emphasize the critical need for early identification and context-appropriate interventions. Strengthening school-based mental health services, improving parental education and engagement, and enhancing national child protection systems may help address the widespread burden of ACEs. Investments in trauma-informed care and resilience-building initiatives could play an important role in promoting healthier developmental trajectories for adolescents in Bangladesh and similar LMIC contexts.
