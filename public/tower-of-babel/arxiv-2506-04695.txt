On the Mechanism of Reasoning Pattern Selection in Reinforcement Learning for Language Models
Xingwu Chen, Tianle Li, Difan Zou

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated remarkable success in enhancing model capabilities, including instruction-following, preference learning, and reasoning. Yet despite its empirical successes, the mechanisms by which RL improves reasoning abilities remain poorly understood. We present a systematic study of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), showing that its primary benefit comes from optimizing the selection of existing reasoning patterns. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that RLVR-trained models preferentially adopt high-success-rate reasoning patterns while mostly maintaining stable performance on individual patterns. We further develop theoretical analyses on the convergence and training dynamics of RLVR based on a simplified question-reason-answer model. We study the gradient flow and show that RLVR can indeed find the solution that selects the reason pattern with the highest success rate. Besides, our theoretical results reveal two distinct regimes regarding the convergence of RLVR training: (1) rapid convergence for models with relatively strong initial reasoning capabilities versus (2) slower optimization dynamics for weaker models. Furthermore, we show that the slower optimization for weaker models can be mitigated by applying the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) before RLVR, when using a feasibly high-quality SFT dataset. We validate the theoretical findings through extensive experiments. This work advances our theoretical understanding of RL’s role in LLM fine-tuning and offers insights for further enhancing reasoning capabilities.

Preliminaries

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).
Given a fine-tuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
consisting of token sequence samples
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, supervised fine-tuning the model on this dataset means that the model is trained to autoregressively generate the next token
x_{i}
based on the previous token
x_{<i}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}}\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i})\right],

(3.1)

where
x_{<i}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1})
denotes the token sequence before
x_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
represents the parameters of the model.

Supervised fine-tuning is a general and efficient method for aligning large language models. It is believed that SFT can incentivize the model to learn a specific response format using only a small number of carefully designed samples
[
78
]
. In this paper, we construct SFT datasets of different sizes for different tasks, ranging from 256 to 1560 samples. We first perform supervised fine-tuning of the model with small samples and few epochs as a cold start
[
17
]
, the purpose is just to force the model’s responses to conform to a specific format, which helps us to perform RLVR later.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
RLVR refers to reinforcement learning optimization of models using rewards that can be automatically calculated and verified
[
30
,
17
,
52
,
70
]
, which is suitable for problems with definite answers that can be easily calculated. Therefore, it is often used in mathematics and coding tasks, and is believed to improve the reasoning ability of language models.

Let
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be an language model with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, also the policy to be optimized. Suppose that for input question
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, the policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
can output answer
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
. Then the optimization objective of RLVR can be written as:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{RLVR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}\left[r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\parallel\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right],

(3.2)

where
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the base reference policy, usually the SFT model
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
. And
\beta
is a factor used to control the KL divergence to prevent the deviation between
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. In a general reinforcement learning setting, the rewards for
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
are usually given by a trained reward model. However, in RLVR, the reward model
r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is a direct calculation of whether answer
\boldsymbol{y}
is equal to the correct answer to question
\boldsymbol{x}
. A typical reward function is:

\displaystyle r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\boldsymbol{y}=$ the ground truth of $\boldsymbol{x}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3.3)

In this paper, we use the PPO
[
49
]
algorithm to optimize the objective of RLVR.

Experiment Insights

To investigate how reinforcement learning improves model reasoning abilities, we compare responses before and after reinforcement learning enhancement. We evaluate these models across various reasoning tasks, including countdown, long arithmetic and difficult math problems sampled from MATH
[
20
]
. We apply reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to models that have been finetuned to output in our desired format. During the SFT and RLVR training process, we observe an object that is often overlooked by previous work but has a huge impact on model accuracy:

Our investigation focuses on the reasoning pattern differences introduced by RL, with our experimental pipeline consisting of three main components, as illustrated in Figure

4.1
Experimental Settings

We focus on three reasoning tasks that require mathematical reasoning and planning capabilities, and can utilize different reasoning strategies. These tasks present significant challenges for base reasoning models. The task details are as follows:

•

Easy Countdown
: Given a list of numbers, the model must use addition or subtraction operations to reach a target number. For example, using
[1,2,3,7]
to obtain
9
, where each number from the list can be used at most once. We sampled a task-specific Q&A dataset from the Qwen2.5-7b-Instruct
[
66
]
by filtering out responses that failed to provide correct solutions or used incorrect response formats.

•

Long Arithmetic
: Given a very long arithmetic expression consisting of numbers, plus signs, minus signs, and parentheses, the model needs to calculate the result from the inside to the outside according to the calculation order specified by the parentheses. The model is required to solve the problem with different number of lines. Based on previous work
[
61
]
, using different numbers of lines to solve this calculation problem is considered to represent different chain-of-thought lengths. We programmatically generate samples with different expression lengths and solution rows.

•

Difficult Math
: We sample multiple difficult problems (level 4 and level 5) from MATH
[
20
]
as basic templates to synthesize a set of math problems. We ask the model to solve these problems and give answers. These problems are cross-domain, such as number theory and geometry, which challenges the general reasoning ability of the model.

Each task supports various solving strategies. We first collected reasoning processes from the base model and grouped them into distinct reasoning patterns. For the Easy Countdown and Difficult Math tasks, we employed Deepseek V3
[
34
]
to analyze example responses, identify their characteristics (including keywords and logical structure), group similar reasoning processes into patterns, and provide pattern descriptions with common elements. For the Long Arithmetic task, we use hard rules to extract the patterns of results. We then use these patterns to analyze the reasoning pattern distribution and their success rates for both models before and after RLVR.

4.2
Experimental Insights: RLVR Incentivizes Models to Adopt Suitable Reasoning Patterns

Figure 2
:
Evaluation results for reasoning pattern and corresponding success rate of model with/without RL enhancement for varying tasks. After RLVR, the model tends to select patterns with higher accuracy and reduce the use of patterns with lower accuracy. For ease of observation, we sort and name the different patterns from high to low according to their corresponding accuracy.

Here we evaluate reasoning pattern and corresponding success rate of models with and without RL enhancement for varying tasks, the results are shown in
Figure 2
, based on our experimental results, we identify three key findings:

•

Models trained with and without RL exhibit distinct reasoning pattern distributions.
For example, in the countdown task, while the base model primarily adopted three reasoning patterns, the RL-trained model showed a significant decrease in the use of pattern 4, shifting predominantly toward pattern 1. Similarly, for the Long Arithmetic task, the patterns converge significantly to pattern 1. For the Number Theory task, the proportion of patterns 4, 5, and 6 will decrease. For the Geometry task, only the proportion of pattern 1 increases significantly, while the other patterns decrease.

•

RL-trained models tend to adopt reasoning patterns with higher success rates.
By examining the success rates of different reasoning patterns, as explained previously, we found that RL-enhanced models preferentially use more successful patterns. For instance, in the countdown task, the RL-enhanced model favored pattern 1, which demonstrated higher success rates across both enhanced and non-enhanced models. The same phenomenon can also be observed in other tasks. After RLVR, the patterns preferred by the model are those with higher accuracy, while the proportion of those with lower accuracy is greatly reduced.

•

Success rates for individual reasoning patterns sometimes remain consistent.
Surprisingly, we found that once a reasoning pattern is established, its success rate often remains stable even after RL enhancement. This phenomenon is particularly evident in the countdown task, suggesting that the selection of reasoning patterns, rather than their individual optimization, is key to achieving higher success rates.

Our experiments provide clearer insights into how RL enhances model performance: RL-enhanced models tend to adopt reasoning patterns with higher success rates, while the success rates of individual patterns sometimes remain stable. This suggests that incentivizing models to adopt suitable reasoning patterns is the primary mechanism through which RL improves reasoning abilities.

5
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we present a theoretical framework to explain how RLVR improves model performance. We first formulate a model’s reasoning procedure as a two-step process: selecting a reasoning pattern and then generating the final answer. Based on this framework, we explain our empirical findings. Additionally, we analyze RLVR’s training dynamics by considering language models as tabular policies, examining two special cases with distinct optimization behaviors.

5.1
A Theoretical Framework for Reasoning Model

Abstract Reasoning Process.

We formalize the reasoning process as follows: given a question
\boldsymbol{q}
, the model will (1) select a reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}
from candidate paths
\mathcal{R}=\{\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2},\dots\}
and (2) generate a final answer
a\in\mathcal{A}=\{\boldsymbol{a}_{T},\boldsymbol{a}_{F}\}
accordingly. The model selects reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
with probability
p(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|q)
, and each reasoning pattern will have a distinct success rate. The diagram of the theoretical reasoning model is illustrated in Figure

(

Policy Parameterization.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{\boldsymbol{q}\}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{A}
denote the vocabulary set and
\mathcal{Y}={(r,a)}_{r\in\mathcal{R},a\in\mathcal{A}}
represent the set of output sequences. Given a question
\boldsymbol{q}
, the language model produces a distribution over output sequences
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
autoregressively:

\displaystyle\text{(general policy)}\hskip 30.0pt\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{l=1}^{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{l}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{<l})=\prod_{l=1}^{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\text{softmax}(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{<l}))_{\boldsymbol{y}_{l}},

(5.1)

where
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
is a function parametrized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and the model predict
l
-th token base on the previous context
\boldsymbol{y}_{<l}
, the next token follows the distribution
\text{softmax}(\boldsymbol{z})_{v}:=\exp(\boldsymbol{z}_{v})/\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(\boldsymbol{z}_{v^{\prime}})
for
\boldsymbol{z}=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{<l})
.

that each output is assigned by the own trainable logit for corresponding last token, i.e. for

\displaystyle\text{(tabular policy)}\hskip 30.0pt\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{l}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{<l})=\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{l}|\boldsymbol{y}_{l-1})=\text{softmax}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{:,\boldsymbol{y}_{l-1}})_{\boldsymbol{y}_{l}},

For our analysis of training dynamics, we consider the tabular policy from Eq

\phi_{\mathrm{RLVR}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)=\nabla\phi_{\mathrm{RLVR}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)),\quad t\geq 0

The success rate for each reasoning pattern

5.2
Theoretical Explanation for Empirical Findings

Our empirical results demonstrate that the RLVR-enhanced model utilizes reasoning patterns with higher success rates. We provide a theoretical explanation through the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose we maximize the RLVR objective (Eq
3.2
) using a general autoregressive policy (Eq
5.1
) and Assumption
5.1
holds. Then, the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization objective satisfies:

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R},

(5.4)

where
Z=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})
is the normalizing coefficient.

Theorem
5.2
provides an optimal solution for RLVR. If we further assume
\pi_{\mathrm{opt}}
belongs to the general autoregressive policy space
\{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}
(which always holds for LLMs and tabular policies discussed later), we find that the RL-enhanced model increases the probability of choosing pattern
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
when the corresponding success rate
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
satisfies
\exp\big(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})\big)/Z>1
.
This aligns with our empirical finding that RL enhances the model’s tendency to choose reasoning patterns with higher success rates.

Moreover, since
\beta
is typically small (e.g.,
0.001
) in practice
[
43
,
6
,
73
]
, as
\beta\rightarrow 0
, we have:

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=\begin{cases}1&\boldsymbol{r}=\mathop{\text{\rm arg\,max}}_{\boldsymbol{r}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(5.5)

In this case, the model will adopt the optimal pattern for reasoning, regardless of how the initialized reference model chooses reasoning patterns. However, due to the non-convexity of the policy, a more detailed analysis of how RLVR helps the model find optimal reasoning patterns remains unclear. Next, we analyze the training dynamics of RLVR for tabular policy (Eq
5.2
).

5.3
Training Dynamic Analysis for RLVR

Recent studies demonstrate that RLVR improvements heavily depend on the capacity of the base model
[
73
,
15
]
. In our analysis, we focus on the probability of the optimal reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}=\mathop{\text{\rm arg\,max}}_{\boldsymbol{r}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, and reveal two distinct regimes in terms of the training dynamics of RLVR via gradient flow. In the first regime, the probability of the optimal reasoning pattern
\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}|\boldsymbol{q})
steadily increases until convergence to
1
. In the second regime, the model will experience an entanglement stage initially, where a suboptimal reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
hinder the optimizing process for the optimal reasoning pattern. After this entanglement stage, the model eventually switch to the stage in the first case and converges to the optimal reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
.

Theorem 5.3

(Regime 1: Sufficient Condition for Efficient Convergence)
.

Consider the RLVR objective (Eq
3.2
) with
\beta=0
, using a tabular policy (Eq
5.2
) with Assumption
5.1
holds. Let
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
be the optimal reasoning pattern, if the overall accuracy of the initialized model
\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}
, defined as
\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}>p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{r}\neq\boldsymbol{r}^{*}

(5.6)

Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
T_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
such that for
t>T_{1}
, we have
1-\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}|\boldsymbol{q})<\epsilon

In Theorem
5.3
, we consider a case where the base reference model is sufficiently strong such that its overall accuracy exceeds the success rate of all non-optimal reasoning patterns (Eq
5.6
). That being said, the optimal reasoning pattern
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
has already achieved a higher selection probability
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}|\boldsymbol{q})
than other patterns with a certain margin.
In this case, RLVR can efficiently guide the model to select the optimal reasoning pattern, achieving higher overall accuracy.

However, practical experience shows that in some scenarios, RLVR optimization can be challenging
[
73
,
62
]
, which typically occurs when the base reference model is less powerful. We then focus on the second case, where the model experiences an entanglement stage initially, and the suboptimal reasoning pattern with the second highest success rate, defined as
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=\mathop{\text{\rm arg\,max}}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}\neq\boldsymbol{r}^{*}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, would slow down the optimization process for the optimal reasoning pattern:

Theorem 5.4

(Regime 2: Slow Convergence of RLVR Optimization)
.

Consider the RLVR objective (Eq
3.2
) with
\beta=0
, using a tabular policy (Eq
5.2
) with Assumption
5.1
holds. Let
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
be the optimal and second optimal reasoning patterns, if the overall accuracy of the initialized model satisfies:

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})>\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}>p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}/\{\boldsymbol{r}^{*},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\},

(5.7)

where
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
is the reasoning pattern with the second highest success rate, then there exists:

\displaystyle T_{0}=\frac{1}{2-2\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{q})}\left(\left(C_{1}\cdot\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{2C_{2}\cdot\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}-1\right),\text{ where }\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}/\{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\}}\frac{\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})}{\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}|\boldsymbol{q})}

(5.8)

with constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending on the success rates of reasoning patterns, such that for we can guarantee
\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}>p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}),\forall\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{r}\neq\boldsymbol{r}^{*}
for
t\geq T_{0}
.

In Theorem
5.4
, we consider a kind of special regime that only the success rate of the optimal and the suboptimal reasoning patterns are above the average success rate. We consider this case for the ease of theoretical analysis and believe it can be extended to more general setting, where at least two reasoning patterns are allowed to acheive higher success rate than the average, with more complicated theoretical analysis.

Besides, the critical part of Theorem
5.4
is that we show RLVR may take
T_{0}
time to ensure that the overall success rate exceeds the success rate of the suboptimal reasoning pattern, i.e., the selection probability of the optimal reasoning pattern is sufficiently large. When comparing to Theorem
5.3
that the convergence time
T_{1}
is polynomial in
1/\epsilon
, the time step
T_{0}
in Theorem
5.4
may grow
super-exponentially with respect to
\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
—-the ratio between the total success rate through
\mathcal{R}/\{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\}
and the success rate through
r^{*}
. Clearly, when the base model admits a quite small selection probability of the optimal reasoning pattern, we may have a very large
\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
, which further leads to a prohibitively large
T_{0}
. As a consequence, it can take a very long RLVR training period for the transformer model to select the optimal reasoning pattern with a reasonably large probability, which we refer to as entanglement stage.

Moreover, as the overall accuracy increases with
t
(see details in Appendix C.3), when the initialized accuracy is lower, there may be multiple suboptimal reasoning patterns
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}/\{\boldsymbol{r}^{*}\}
where the corresponding success rate
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
is larger than
\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}
. In such cases, it would take substantially longer for
\mathrm{Acc}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}
to first increase until it satisfies Eq
5.7
to reach Regime 2, and then tackle the entanglement stage discussed in Theorem
5.4
to reach Regime 1. This results in a more challenging optimization process, consistent with the real-world training observations: RLVR cannot effectively enhance reasoning performance within reasonable training time when the base model is not sufficiently powerful
[

,

,

]
.

5.4
Implication to the SFT-then-RLVR Pipeline

Our theoretical and experimental results reveal that the convergence rate of RLVR is highly dependent on the base model’s performance, particularly two factors: (1) the ratio of the reasoning pattern selection probability to the optimal reasoning pattern
\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})/\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}|\boldsymbol{q})
, and (2) the success rates for each reasoning pattern (
C_{1},C_{2}
). While optimizing the success rate can be challenging due to the possible model’s inherent limited expressive power,
\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})
can be readily adjusted through supervised fine-tuning. Specifically, let
p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})
denote the distribution of reasoning patterns in the fine-tuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
. We have the following theorem:

Theorem 5.5

(Supervised Fine-tuning Converges to Training Dataset Distribution)
.

Consider the SFT objective (Eq
3.1
) using a tabular policy (Eq
5.2
) with Assumption
5.1
holding. Let
p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})
denote the distribution of reasoning patterns in the fine-tuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
T_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
such that for
t>T_{1}^{\prime}
, we have
\sup_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}|p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})|<\epsilon
.

Similar results can be found in
[
29
]
. Theorem
5.5
demonstrates that implementing SFT achieves
\sup_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}|p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})|<\epsilon
within
T=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
steps. Given that SFT datasets typically maintain high quality, the SFT model can be more suitable for RLVR: SFT provides a feasible initialization on the distribution of reasoning patterns, and then RL can quickly converge from such a good initialization, as stated in Theorem
5.3
.

Furthermore, if we assume that reasoning patterns are difficult to optimize and remain static during training (Assumption
5.1
), the final accuracy after SFT can be expressed as
\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, which would be substantially lower than the success rate of the optimal reasoning pattern
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*})
. In this scenario, assuming SFT positions the model in a suitable landscape for RLVR, the final accuracy limit
\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{SFT-RLVR}}
becomes
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*})
which demonstrates that RLVR can further enhance the model’s capacity through reasoning pattern selection and drive it toward its performance limit. Notably, the total running time for SFT and RLVR would be
T_{1}^{\prime}+T_{1}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
, which is substantially smaller than
T_{0}
(see Theorem
5.4
) when
\gamma_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
is large.

6
Conclusions and Limitations

In this work, we investigate how RLVR enhances language models’ reasoning abilities through the lens of reasoning patterns. Our empirical investigations reveal that RLVR primarily improves reasoning performance by incentivizing models to adopt high-success-rate reasoning patterns. We develop a theoretical framework to explain these empirical findings, provide a detailed analysis of RLVR training dynamics, and present additional experimental evidence to support our conclusions. Furthermore, we offer insights into how supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning cooperate to achieve superior performance.

While our work offers valuable insights into RLVR mechanisms for LLMs, several limitations warrant discussion. First, while we leverage LLMs to identify and categorize reasoning patterns, the precision and fundamental interpretability of these patterns require further validation. Second, our theoretical analysis adopts a simplified framework, whereas real-world reasoning patterns often exhibit greater complexity. Extending our models to more practical settings remains an important future direction.

C.1
Proof of Theorem 5.2

Proof of Theorem 5.2.

In this proof, we utilize the proof techniques in
[
43
]

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{RLVR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}\left[r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right],

(C.1)

Under our framework, the reward
r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
can be written as

\displaystyle r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{a})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{a}_{T}$,}\\
0&\text{if $\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{a}_{F}$.}\end{cases}

(C.2)

Moreover, as state in section 5.1, we consider a policy conduct reasoning by first sample
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
based on
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})
and then provide the final answer
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
by
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
, we can rewrite Eq
C.1
with

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{RLVR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}\left[r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{a}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{r})}\left[r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{a})-\beta\ln\left(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{r})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{r})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{a}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{r})}\left[p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\beta\ln\left(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{r})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{r})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{q})}\left[p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\beta\ln\left(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\beta\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{q})}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})}{\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})}\right)-\ln Z\right]

\displaystyle=-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\beta\ln Z.

Where the third equation is by Assumption 5.1 and
Z=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})
is the partition constant that ensures

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q}),

is a valid probability distribution such that
\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\pi^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=1
. Since
Z
is not a function of
\boldsymbol{r}
.

Therefore, maximizing the objective in Equation
C.1
is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\pi(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}\left[r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right]-\beta\ln Z.

By the properties of KL-divergence, we know that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization objective satisfies:

\displaystyle\pi_{opt}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{q})\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}.

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\in\mathcal{R}}\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j},\boldsymbol{q}})\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\in\mathcal{R}}\Delta\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})-\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})=0

In this case,
\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})\geq 0
, thus
\frac{d}{dt}\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})=-\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})\leq 0
.

•

If
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})<0
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j},\boldsymbol{q}})\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\in\mathcal{R}}\Delta\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})-\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{q})=0

In this case,
\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})\geq 0
, thus
\frac{d}{dt}\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})=-\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{\theta(t)}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}|\boldsymbol{q})\geq 0
.

As a result, in both cases,
|\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})|
will not increase with
t
. Next, for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}}(t))
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}}(t))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r}\mid\boldsymbol{q})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\sup_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q}})\right)^{2}

Then as
\inf\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}})=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})\log p_{\mathrm{sft}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}}(0))-\inf\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}})}{\epsilon^{2}}

such that when
t=T_{1}^{\prime}
,
\sup_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q}})\right)\leq\epsilon
. Otherwise, if
\sup_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q}})\right)>\epsilon
, as
|\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}})|
will no increase with
t
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}}(T_{1}^{\prime}))<\inf\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{:,\boldsymbol{q}})
.

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
