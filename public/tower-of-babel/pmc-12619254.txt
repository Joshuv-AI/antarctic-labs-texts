Development of a novel prognostic model based on laminin α5, associated with immune infiltration and therapeutic response in ovarian cancer utilizing online resources
Authors: Yan Wang, Zouyu Zhao, Chongfeng Sun, Jishuai Hou, Panpan Yu, Weirui Gao, Ping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) is one of the most pervasive malignancies in women and is recognized for its high recurrence and metastasis rates, resulting in a poor prognosis. Regrettably, reliable indicators for the timely detection and prognosis of OC are currently insufficient. Our research aimed to evaluate the immune therapeutic and prognostic possibilities of laminin α5 (LAMA5) in OC using bioinformatics analysis.MethodsLAMA5 expression and clinical features were analyzed in OC tissues using TCGA and GEO databases. Cox and LASSO analyzes were utilized for the identification of genes with differential expression, leading to the development of a prognostic model centered around LAMA5. Time-based receiver operating characteristic curves were examined to assess the nomogram’s accuracy. Furthermore, we explored the correlation between risk score and tumor mutational burden (TMB), infiltration of immune cells, immune response, and chemotherapy. A signaling pathway analysis was performed to determine the associations between our findings. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was conducted to validate the prognostic significance of LAMA5.ResultsOur study demonstrated that LAMA5 was overexpressed in OC patients. According to the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, the patients with low LAMA5 expression exhibited a significantly higher survival rate compared to those with high LAMA5 expression, indicating a negative impact on the overall prognosis. Additionally, LAMA5 can serve as a distinct prognostic determinant. We developed a sophisticated predictive model that integrated six genes with distinct expression patterns. A clear correlation was found between the predictive model and TMB, immune cell infiltration, as well as the efficacy of immunotherapy and chemotherapy. IHC revealed that LAMA5 protein expression was significantly increased in OC tissues. An increased expression of LAMA5 was associated with an unfavourable outcome in relation to OS and PFS in patients.ConclusionThis study offers novel perspectives on immunotherapy for OC by demonstrating the reliability of a prognostic model based on LAMA5 in predicting patient outcomes. Our risk model holds promise as an efficient biomarker for enhancing prognostic prediction accuracy and facilitating personalized treatment strategies for OC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03380-y.Keywords: LAMA5, Ovarian cancer, Immune infiltration, Prognosis model

Introduction

Among all types of gynecological malignancies, ovarian cancer (OC) exhibits the most elevated fatality rate. According to previous studies, OC affected approximately 19,880 individuals and caused 12,810 deaths, as recorded in the United States in 2022 [1]. These figures corresponded to approximately 2.1% of all newly reported cancer cases and 4% of all cancer-related fatalities [2].

The three primary types of OC are epithelioid, germinal, and stromal cancers. However, epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the most prevalent form of OC. There are two primary histological classifications of EOC, types I and II [3]. Type I tumors have a stable genetic makeup and include low-grade plasma cells, endometrioids, clear cells, and mucinous subtypes. Type II tumors are characterized by a high disease grade and rapid progression. The most common type II tumor is high-grade serous ovarian cancer, which makes up around 75% of all cases of EOCs [4]. Most OC patients are detected at an advanced clinical level due to the slow progression of the disease, its tendency to invade and spread, and the challenges of early detection. Although several tumour biomarkers such as carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) [5], HE4 [6], CA19-9 [7], AFP [8] and CEA [9] are currently used as diagnostic tools in clinical settings, these markers have not been proven to be the most accurate in assessing prognosis and response in individual patients. Hence, it is crucial to promptly detect distinctive biomarkers in individuals with OC to enhance our understanding of disease advancement and explore novel therapeutic targets.

Laminin is an integral glycoprotein that has a major role in the formation of basement membranes in blood vessels and tissues. It serves as the fundamental structure of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [10]. One notable feature of different cancer types is the disruption of crucial interactions between proteins responsible for cell–cell adhesion [11]. Laminin α5 (LAMA5) belongs to the laminin family and encodes the alpha-chain ECM protein laminin 5 [12]. Several studies have revealed a significant correlation between OC progression and LAMA5. Our previous study demonstrated that the downregulation of LAMA5 expression could potentially modulate the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and impede OC cell invasion and metastasis by suppressing the Notch signaling pathway [13, 14]. Further studies have provided additional insights into the diverse roles of LAMA5. It not only binds to receptors involved in cell migration and proliferation but also influences other crucial processes within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [15]. A recent study revealed that LAMA5 exhibited a substantial and inverse correlation with B cells, CD8 + T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells [13]. Multiple research studies have confirmed the involvement of LAMA5 in the progression and development of various types of tumors, such as non-small cell lung cancer [16], breast cancer [17], fibrosarcoma [18], and colorectal cancer [19, 20]. Li et al. discovered that the modulation of laminin immune response was facilitated by LAMA5 in stromal cells of murine lymph nodes, which resulted in alterations to both the structure of the lymph nodes and the behavior of T cells [21]. Moreover, Bartolini et al. identified that LAMA5 exhibits increased expression in TME of colorectal cancer cells with KRAS mutations, making it a unique therapeutic target [15]. However, a comprehensive and validated prognostic risk model based on this gene is lacking. Hence, it is crucial to focus on developing a proficient predictive risk model utilizing LAMA5 and investigating its potential in immunotherapy, while concurrently unraveling its complex biological mechanisms to facilitate enhanced results for patients with OC.

The main aim of this investigation was to examine the correlation between LAMA5 expression and the clinical and pathological characteristics exhibited by individuals with ovarian cancer. We aimed to assess LAMA5 expression in both the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases and construct a prognostic model for OC patients by integrating clinical characteristics. The validity of the nomogram was confirmed by assessing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Furthermore, we systematically investigated the association between the risk prognosis model and tumor mutation burden (TMB), immune cell infiltration, and response to immunotherapy and chemotherapy. An investigation was conducted to examine diverse biological processes through enrichment analysis. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was employed to validate the presence of LAMA5 and its correlation with clinicopathological characteristics and patient prognosis. Overall, our risk model holds promise as an efficient biomarker for enhancing prognostic prediction accuracy and facilitating personalized immune infiltration and treatment strategies for OC patients.

Material and method

Data download

We obtained mRNA profiles and corresponding historical clinical data for 375 OC patients from TCGA website (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). As there were no matched normal samples available in TCGA database, we downloaded gene expression data for 88 normal ovarian tissues from the GTEx portal (http://www.gtexportal.org/home/). Additionally, we retrieved the LAMA5 array expression data from the GENT2 website, which encompassed 605 samples from OC patients and 22 normal samples. As part of our research, we collected microarray gene expression data and corresponding clinical information from seven datasets (GSE14407, GSE27651, GSE36668, GSE38666, GSE40595, GSE52460, and GSE69428) available on the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

The KM plotter tool (http://kmplot.com/analysis) was utilized to assess the survival outcomes of more than 5400 genes across 21 distinct cancer types, such as breast, ovarian, lung, and gastric cancers [22]. Based on OC patients, the prognostic value of LAMA5 was investigated in relation to the progression-free survival (PFS), post-progression survival (PPS), and overall survival (OS). To determine the PFS, PPS, and OS of OC patients, hazard ratios were calculated along with log-rank P-values within a 95% confidence interval. Statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Construction of the LAMA5-based risk model

TCGA database was used to categorize 375 OC patients into high- and low-risk groups using XTILE software to identify the most suitable threshold values [23]. The evaluation of survival differences between subgroups was performed using KM survival analysis implemented using the “survival” package. The predictive performance of the signature was assessed by analyzing ROC curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The timeROC package was utilized for conducting the analysis. To evaluate the independence of the risk score, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, taking into account clinical factors including age, stage, grade, and LAMA5 mRNA expression. The “limma” R package was used to identify genes that displayed differential expression between subgroups, employing a filter threshold of |log2 Fold Change (FC)|> 0.6 and a false discovery rate (FDR) set at < 0.05. The dissimilarities in gene expression were illustrated by creating a volcano plot utilizing the “ggpubr” R package [24]. Using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, we employed coefficient identification and implemented minimum thresholds to further screen the 21 genes. Finally, by employing stepwise regression analysis, we derived a more practical and optimal model consisting of six genes. Additionally, the risk score formula was computed as follows: Risk score=∑n-1nexp Genei∗coefficient Genei.

To stratify TCGA-OC cohort into different subgroups, we employed a specific formula to compute the average risk score. Subsequently, we evaluated the performance of our models by conducting KM and ROC curve analyses to determine their predictive precision.

Nomogram construction

To assess the independent predictive value of the risk score in OC patients, we conducted Cox regression analyses. A clinical nomogram was developed using the “rms” R package for survival prediction, considering age, stage, grade, and risk score as independent factors. The effectiveness of the nomogram was confirmed by conducting an analysis on the ROC curve.

Tumor mutation burden

Based on the somatic mutation data acquired from TCGA, we employed the “maftools” software package to determine the TMB of OC patients [25].

Next, we examined the differences between the groups with low and high TMB levels and evaluated the correlation between risk and TMB scores. Furthermore, by integrating the TMB and model scores, we categorized the patients into four groups: H-TMB + H-Risk, H-TMB + L-Risk, L-TMB + H-Risk, and L-TMB + L-Risk. These groupings were then employed to evaluate their prognostic significance using the “survminer” R package.

Enrichment analysis

We used the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software (version 4.2.3) to perform pathway analysis on the two groups to investigate relevant biological processes and potential mechanisms. The reference gene sets used were c5go and c2kegg from the human collection section. Significantly enriched biological processes and pathways were determined based on P-values < 0.05, FDR q-values < 0.25, and |normalized enrichment score (NES)|> 1.

Analysis of immune infiltrating cells

A previous study provided a comprehensive list of genes for labeling 29 distinct immune cell types, which is found in Supplementary Table S1 [26]. To measure the variances in immune cell infiltration, we employed the ssGSEA algorithm. In addition, we utilized a range of algorithms such as CIBEROST, MCPcounter, TIMER [25], and xCell to assess the variances in immune cell abundance across distinct risk score subgroups. The findings are visually represented using plots.

Analysis of the effectiveness of immunotherapy and chemotherapy

By combining the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm [27], we analyzed the predictive efficiency of immunotherapy responses in specific subgroups. Additionally, a comprehensive investigation was conducted on the gene expression profiles of publicly accessible samples from patients treated with immunotherapy, specifically focusing on the metastatic urothelial tumor cohort (IMvigor210: http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies) [28]. The cohort provided extensive mRNA data, OS data, and details regarding the immunotherapy response.

Meanwhile, to investigate the chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity commonly observed in OC patients, we utilized the R package “pRRophetic” to ascertain the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value, which signifies the concentration at which half of the inhibitory effect is attained, for each individual sample [29]. Subsequently, we assessed the therapeutic effects of the IC50 value in the different subgroups.

Human tissue sample collection

Clinicopathological data were obtained from 110 patients diagnosed with OC at the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College of Shihezi University. All patients comprised the trial group. Normal fallopian tube epithelial tissues from 30 patients with benign diseases were collected during the same period and served as the control group. The clinicopathological data of OC patients, including age, histological type, grade, FIGO stage (2014), lymph node metastasis (LNM), lymphovascular space infiltration (LVSI), and CA125 expression, were collected. Patients with OC were included according to the following criteria: (a) Absence of prior chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy before surgery; (b) Sufficient clinical and pathological data; and (c) No history of concurrent malignant tumors. Patients who did not meet these criteria were excluded. The follow-up methods employed in such cases may include telephone interviews, outpatient follow-up visits, or inpatient data retrieval. The designated follow-up period is scheduled to reach its conclusion on 15 October 2024. Follow-up content includes patient survival status (whether recurrence has occurred and whether the patient is alive or deceased), the date of the most recent follow-up examination and clinical data. Key collection points include gynaecological examination results, laboratory tests (tumour markers) and imaging findings (vaginal ultrasound, abdominal ultrasound, CT scan, MRI scan, etc.). Overall survival (OS): The period from the patient’s surgery date to their death or the end of follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS): The period from the patient’s surgery date to disease recurrence or the end of follow-up. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Shihezi University, approved the study (KJX2022-038-01), and all participants provided informed consent by signing a consent form.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed human OC tissue specimens of 4 µm thickness were cut off. Tissue sections were post-embedded at 60 °C for 12 h, deparaffinized in xylene, dehydrated using an ethanol gradient, and heat-repaired in citrate solution. LAMA5 antibody (dilution of 1:200, ab210957, Abcam) was used to probe the slices at 4 °C overnight. The sections were incubated the following day. The samples were later treated with diaminobenzidine for staining, followed by counterstaining using hematoxylin and dehydration through a series of ethanol solutions with varying concentrations. The extent of staining was evaluated based on the proportion of cells that showed positive results. A score of 1, 2, 3, and 4 represented 0–10%, 10–25%, 50–75%, and 75–100% of staining, respectively. Staining intensity was assessed using the following grading system: no stain (score = 0), low stain (score = 1), moderate stain (score = 2), and high stain (score = 3). The final IHC scores were calculated by summing the aforementioned scores. The group with low LAMA5 expression had a total IHC score ≤ 6, whereas the group with high LAMA5 expression had an IHC score > 6.

Statistical analysis

The majority of statistical analyses were performed utilizing R Studio software (version 4.1.2). The comparison of expression levels between two groups was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The KM method and log-rank test were employed to assess disparities in survival curves. The association between the target gene and the clinical features was assessed using chi-square analysis. Relevant statistical analyses, including univariate and multivariate Cox regression models, were performed to assess the use of LAMA5 expression levels as an independent prognostic factor for both OS and PFS predictions. P < 0.05 was set as the level of statistical significance.

Result

Elevated LAMA5 expression was associated with poor prognosis in OC patients

We explored LAMA5 mRNA expression in different databases, such as TCGA and GENT2 databases, and several GEO datasets and found that LAMA5 was highly expressed in OC (Fig. 1a). Second, the association between LAMA5 expression and prognosis was assessed using various databases available in the KM database. The results suggested that patients with high levels of LAMA5 expression had a significantly poorer prognosis in terms of PFS, OS (Fig. 1b, c) and PPS (Fig. S1).

Construction of the LAMA5-based prognostic model

According to the clinical information of 375 patients obtained from the TCGA database, we employed XILE software to classify them into two distinct groups using a specific cutoff value: high LAMA5 expression group and low LAMA5 expression group (Fig. 2a). Further examination of the disparities among the groups regarding prognosis revealed noteworthy variations in prognostic outcomes. Specifically, the group with higher scores exhibited notably inferior outcomes than the group with lower scores (Fig. 2b).

Construction of a LAMA5-baesd Prognostic Model. A Divide OC patients into two groups based on the cut-off value. B The KM curves and C ROC analysis of LAMA5 subgroups for TCGA cohort. D Univariate and E multivariate cox regression analysis. F The distribution of different expression genes with volcano plot. G Lasso analysis. H Multivariate cox regression analysis for prognosis model. I Survival analyses for the high and low risk groups. J The ROC curves for 3-, 5-, 10-years predict the ability of risk score

The effectiveness of LAMA5 expression in predicting prognostic status was evaluated using ROC analysis. The AUCs at 3, 5, and 10 years were exhibited respectively (Fig. 2c). Next, Cox regression analyses were performed adjusting for LAMA5 expression and clinical factors including age, stage and grade. The findings suggested that the levels of LAMA5 and patient age were identified as separate prognostic factors in OC patients (Fig. 2d, e).

We analyzed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in LAMA5 subgroups and identified 326 and 204 genes whose expressions were upregulated and downregulated, respectively (Fig. 2f). A set of 530 DEGs was subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis, resulting in the identification of 101 genes (P < 0.05) for inclusion in the LASSO regression (Fig. 2g). Multivariate Cox analysis using stepwise regression for the remaining six genes. The model consisted of NTN1, SPOCK2, CXCL9, IGF2, NSG1, and FCGBP (Fig. 2h). Next, the LAMA5-related gene risk model was constructed using the following formula: Risk score = (0.2981) * NTN1 + (0.5840) * SPOCK2 + (− 0.3593) * CXCL9 + (0.1047) * IGF2 + (0.4591) * NSG1 + (0.3800) * FCGBP.

According to their risk score’ median, OC patients were reclassified by dividing two groups: a high-risk group and a low-risk group. The KM curves demonstrated that high-risk patients had a worse prognosis than low-risk patients (Fig. 2i).

The ROC analysis, which considered the progression of time, revealed that the gene signature associated with LAMA5 had prognostic accuracies of 0.699, 0.703, and 0.739 after 3, 5, and 10 years, respectively (Fig. 2j). Consequently, the accuracy of the risk score in forecasting the future prognosis of individuals with OC was enhanced.

Combining clinicopathological features to construct the prognostic model

Based on the risk score distribution and survival analysis, there was a positive association between mortality and the risk score of individuals in the high-risk group, whereas an inverse relationship was observed for those in the low-risk group. We also generated heat maps for the two groups (Fig. 3a). Combined with clinical features (age, stage, and grade), we performed Cox analysis and found that risk score and age were independent factors of prognosis in OC patients (Fig. 3b). We then generated a predictive nomogram by combining the risk score and clinical characteristics (Fig. 3c). A nomogram can be used as a reference for clinical decision-making by quantitatively predicting a patient’s prognosis. In addition, the ROC curves showed better accuracy in predicting the OS at 3, 5, and 10 years. The AUCs were 0.722, 0.689, and 0.713 after 3, 5, and 10 years, respectively (Fig. 3d). Meanwhile, the AUCs demonstrated that the nomogram exhibited superior sensitivity and specificity compared with other individual prognostic factors such as age, stage, grade, and risk score (Fig. 3e). Therefore, this study validated the remarkable predictive capacity of the tumor prognostic signature and demonstrated its significant influence on clinical practice from various perspectives.

Association between the risk score and the TMB

Growing evidence suggests that tumorigenesis is associated with the accumulation of gene mutations. To analyze the patterns of somatic mutations accurately, we generated waterfall plots for both cohorts. Higher bars indicated the high-risk group had comparatively lower TMB than low-risk. We observed that TP53, TTN, CSMD3, RYR2, and FAT3 exhibited the most frequent mutations in the low-risk cluster. In contrast, TP53, TTN, CSMD3, USH2A and MUC16 were common mutations in the high-risk cluster (Fig. 4a, b). Violin plots show that TMB is significantly more widely distributed in this group than in others (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, we categorized OC patients into groups with high or low TMB based on the median value. Patients with higher TMB exhibited better OS outcomes compared to those with lower TMB in OC (Fig. 4d). Simultaneously, patients with high risk but low TMB experienced a notably poor OS, whereas those with low risk but high TMB demonstrated high OS. (Fig. 4e).

Enrichment analysis

To investigate the natural differences in biological functions and communication pathways among different groups at risk, we conducted GO and KEGG analyses utilizing the GSEA software. GO analysis was used to establish the categories of biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The BP categories were enriched in the control of signal transduction mediated by small GTPases, cellular adhesion by plasma membrane adhesion molecules, and the assembly of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes. The CC categories were primarily enriched in the basement membrane, mitochondrial protein-containing complexes, and respirasomes. The MF categories were primarily enriched in ECM structural constituents, nucleoside triphosphatase regulator activity, and structural constituents of ribosomes (Fig. 5a).

Enrichment Analysis. A GO terms and B KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of two groups

The KEGG pathway analysis results demonstrated that many cancer proliferation pathways were mostly associated with the Notch and WNT signaling pathways. Moreover, the low-risk group exhibited an elevated enrichment of various immune pathways, including those associated with the production of IGA in the intestinal immune network and primary immunodeficiency (Fig. 5b). Altogether, these results suggest that variable LAMA5 expressions may play intrinsic roles in OC progression.

Correlation between the risk score and the immune microenvironment

We evaluated the association between the risk score and the tumour microenvironment (TME). To enhance the comprehensiveness of our findings, we investigated various approaches for evaluating the immune cells infiltration in both the low- and high-risk cohorts. By employing the ssGSEA algorithm, we identified a significant association between 29 immune cell types and both the risk score and clinical characteristics. We noticed a notable rise in the quantities of aDCs, NK cells, B cells, CD8 + T cells, and Tregs among individuals classified as low-risk when compared to those categorized as high-risk. This discovery implies that individuals categorized as high-risk may have a worse outcome due to a decrease in the presence of immune cells (Fig. 6a).

Correlation between risk score with immune micro-environment. A Heatmap for immune responses based on ssGSEA algorithm. B CIBERSORT algorithm, C MCPcounter and D TIMER were used to analyze the expression of immune infiltration cells among high and low risk groups. E The correlation between risk score and 28 types of immune cells through xCELL algorithm. F The correlation between risk score and immune checkpoints

According to the CIBERSORT algorithm, individuals in the low-risk category had elevated levels of plasma cells, CD8 + T cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, follicular helper T cells, and M1 macrophages compared to those in the high-risk group. Conversely, relevant populations with increased risk demonstrated a notable rise in the infiltration of quiescent memory CD4 + T cells, monocytes, and M2 macrophages (Fig. 6b). Analysis of MCPcounter and TIMER, revealed significant variations in the subgroups of 10 and 6 distinct immune cell types, respectively (Fig. 6c, d). Simultaneously, we employed the xCELL algorithm to examine the correlation between the risk score and 28 different immune cell types, along with six unique prognostic biomarkers (Fig. 6e). The relationship between the risk score and immune checkpoint (ICP) is visualized in a heat map, revealing that several genes including PDCD1, CD274, and CTLA4 exhibit negative associations with most ICPs based on their respective risk score (Fig. 6f). A possible connection was also observed between expression levels and unfavorable outcomes in high-risk patients.

Relevance of the risk score in predicting immunotherapy and chemotherapy effectiveness

We explored the possibility of utilizing risk score to predict the efficacy of immunotherapy and chemotherapy. Our analysis centered on TIDE disruption, which indicated that individuals in the high-risk group had greater dysfunction scores compared to those in the low-risk group. However, relevant statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in the TIDE and exclusion scores between the two groups (Fig. 7a). We also investigated the immune response in patients with risk score and found that the proportion of non-responsive individuals was higher compared to the responsive group (Fig. 7b). However, the disparity did not yield noteworthy results.

In addition, we evaluated the potential of the risk score signature to serve as a predictive indicator for the efficacy of anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies in both cohorts. The risk score distributions for complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD) were investigated (Fig. 7c). As depicted in Fig. 7d, patients classified as low-risk exhibited a greater proportion of CR/PR, whereas high-risk individuals were more likely to experience SD/PD. Furthermore, the analysis of KM demonstrated that individuals classified as high-risk had a reduced duration of survival, aligning with our own research findings (Fig. 7e).

The effective treatment of individuals diagnosed with OC frequently necessitates the use of chemotherapy and targeted medication as pivotal approaches. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate how different risk score-expressing groups differ in drug sensitivity. In our research, the group classified as low-risk exhibited increased sensitivity to commonly employed chemotherapy medications such as cisplatin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel, gemcitabine, olaparib, and niraparib (Fig. 7f). This implies that individuals with minimal risk profiles may experience enhanced advantages from the administration of these chemotherapy medications. Simultaneously, these results suggest that the evaluation of risk score has the potential to be a valuable method for predicting the efficacy of drugs in patients with ovarian cancer.

Correlation between LAMA5 protein expression level and clinicopathological features

To evaluate the protein expression levels of LAMA5, we obtained cancerous tissues from 110 ovarian cancer patients and 30 normal fallopian tube epithelial samples for immunohistochemical analysis. Representative images showing different levels of LAMA5 protein expression based on IHC staining are illustrated in Fig. 8a. The IHC results indicated remarkably higher levels of LAMA5 expression in OC tissue samples than in non-OC tissues (Fig. 8b). Simultaneously, a Kaplan–Meier analysis of the collected data indicated that patients with elevated LAMA5 expression levels experienced lower OS and PFS (Fig. 8c).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the mRNA levels of LAMA5 in OC using TCGA and GEO databases. Our results indicate a correlation between elevated LAMA5 expression and unfavorable survival outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated a notable occurrence of laminin mutations in specific OC patients, particularly in those with a higher prevalence of LAMA5 mutations. Conversely, the occurrence of laminin mutations has been linked to an adverse prognosis in OC. We performed a comprehensive analysis by integrating the DEGs into univariate Cox analysis, LASSO regression, and multivariate Cox analysis. Subsequently, we created a prognostic risk model using LAMA5 as the basis, which incorporated NTN1, SPOCK2, CXCL9, IGF2, NSG1, and FCGBP. Furthermore, a nomogram was used to optimize the accuracy and efficacy of the prognosticating clinical outcomes for patients with OC. We also investigated TMB, immune cell infiltration, and chemotherapy in relation to the risk score. Our results highlight the importance of early detection and targeted therapies for individuals with laminin mutations, as they may benefit from personalized treatment approaches aimed at mitigating disease progression.

Previous studies have explored numerous genes involved in this risk model. NTN1 expression is closely associated with nerve regeneration [30]. Several studies have suggested that human macrophage long intergenic non-coding RNA may enhance the survival of inflammatory macrophages by preventing apoptosis via NTN1 activation [31]. SPOCK2, a 424-amino acid protein, interacts with glycosaminoglycans to generate proteoglycans [32]. Studies have demonstrated elevated levels of SPOCK2 in OC patients, indicating its correlation with an unfavorable prognosis and reduced survival rates among these patients [33]. CXCL9 can be generated by myeloid cells within the TME in response to inflammatory conditions [34]. In prostate cancer, CXCL9 can facilitate disease advancement via the T cell mediated inhibition of IL-6 and TGF-β signaling pathways [35]. IGF2 is a peptide hormone weighing approximately 7.5 kDa [36]. Relevant studies have found a positive association between higher circulating IGF2 levels and an elevated risk of developing colorectal, breast, prostate, and lung cancers [37]. NSG1 is a member of a neuron-specific gene family [38]. Some studies have reported that NSG1 expression is dysregulated in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and could potentially promote the EMT of ESCC cells by activating the ERK signaling pathway [39]. FCGBP, a protein that binds to IgGFc, has a significant impact on tumor metastasis and immunity [40]. Recent studies have revealed a notable increase in FCGBP expression in aggressive forms of breast cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, pancreatic tumors, rectal tumors, and OC. Notably, an increased expression of this protein frequently acts as a predictor of unfavorable prognosis in OC [41].

The TMB acts as a reliable biomarker across different types of cancer and serves as an indicative measure for assessing the efficacy of immunotherapy [42, 43]. Cancer-associated genetic alterations include non-synonymous mutations, synonymous mutations, insertions, deletions, and copy number variations [44]. Notably, the abundance of each genetic alteration type exhibits substantial variability among individual tumors and different tumor subtypes. In this study, we examined how mutated genes were expressed in different subcategories and assessed their potential prognostic significance in individuals diagnosed with OC. The association between the risk score and TMB was found to be inverse, suggesting that a higher risk score corresponded to a lower TMB. Additionally, combining TMB and differential risk score could improve prognostic accuracy in predicting patient survival outcomes.

Tumor development is markedly influenced by the TME, which facilitates interactions between neighboring and tumor cells via the circulatory and lymphatic systems, thereby affecting cancer initiation and progression [45, 46]. Hence, it is crucial to recognize TME to assess the efficacy of immunotherapy. Examination of immune associations indicated that the group with a low risk score exhibited notable levels of CD8 + T cells, plasma cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, follicular helper T cells, gamma-delta T cells, and M1 macrophages. Conversely, the group characterized by a high risk score showed notable levels of quiescent memory CD4 + T cells and M2 macrophages. Macrophages are key phagocytes within the monocyte-macrophage system and play a crucial role in non-specific and specific immune regulation by engulfing bacteria, deceased cells, and cellular debris [47]. Studies have indicated that OC patients may face an increased risk of a poor prognosis if they have low levels of M1 macrophages or high levels of M2 macrophages [48]. Macrophages promote the production of CXCL9 by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to increased infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and hindering the progression of EOC [49]. The TME is significantly infiltrated by M2 macrophages, which play a crucial role in promoting OC progression and suppressing immune responses. Consequently, targeting these M2 macrophages holds great promise as a potential therapeutic strategy for the management of OC [50]. In terms of immunotherapy guidance, despite the non-significant findings from the TIDE analysis, we successfully applied the risk score to IMvigor210 and obtained consistent results. Our findings reaffirm that patients with low risk score exhibited enhanced clinical benefits and prolonged survival following anti-PD-L1 and anti-PD-1 treatments, thereby validating the robustness of our prediction model. Our findings can serve as a catalyst for further investigation of enhanced immunotherapeutic strategies for OC.

In addition, IHC analysis was performed to assess the levels of LAMA5 protein expression and investigate their association with the clinical features observed in individuals diagnosed with OC. Our results indicated a strong association between elevated LAMA5 protein levels and various clinicopathological factors, including histological subtype, tumor grade, FIGO stage, presence of LNM, invasion of lymphovascular spaces, and CA125 expression. KM analysis demonstrated a notable association between increased LAMA5 expression and adverse outcomes in terms of OS and PFS. Furthermore, the multivariate Cox model findings indicated that increased LAMA5 expression was a self-governing prognostic determinant of the OS in OC patients. However, it did not exhibit autonomous significance as a risk factor for PFS, which could be attributed to the limited size of our clinical study cohort. This evidence suggests that LAMA5 has a substantial impact on OC progression, indicating its potential as an innovative prognostic biomarker for OC.

This study had some limitations that must be addressed. First, instead of relying on in vitro experiments to validate the bioinformatics results, data and clinical information were sourced from public databases. Second, the samples used in our study were retrospectively obtained from our medical facility. Third, it is important to note that the association between LAMA5 and immunotherapy response was partially derived from a urothelial carcinoma cohort due to the lack of a large, publicly available OC immunotherapy dataset. Future studies with access to clinical OC immunotherapy cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings. Therefore, future research should focus on prospective clinical and experimental studies for further validation. Finally, there is an urgent need for comprehensive experimental studies to investigate the functional impact of risk score on tumour immunity.

Conclusion

In summary, a predictive model for OC was created using LAMA5 as the basis. A risk model was developed by identifying and utilizing a set of six genes with varying expression levels. This particular model has the capability to make accurate predictions regarding the survival rates of patients with OC, as well as their immune response. We believe this study will offer a novel tool for prognostic and immune response prediction regarding OC patients.
