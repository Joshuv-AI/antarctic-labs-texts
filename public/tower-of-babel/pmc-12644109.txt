Parent–adolescent discrepancies in reports of health-related quality of life in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis
Authors: Yafei Zhang, Tingting Chai, Mingzheng Dai, Hao Weng, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) can adversely affect health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and psychosocial functioning. Parents are integral to AIS management, but discrepancies between parent and adolescent perceptions of the adolescent's HRQoL may influence treatment outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate differences in HRQoL ratings between adolescents with AIS and their parents and to examine whether these discrepancies correlate with adolescents' psychological symptoms and adherence to exercise therapy. Methods: In this cross-sectional study, 71 adolescent–parent dyads completed the Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) questionnaire. Adolescents and parents also completed the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales. Exercise adherence was assessed two weeks after initiating home-based exercise. Results: Parents rated adolescents' self-image and mental health significantly lower than the adolescents themselves (median self-image: 3.4 vs. 3.6, p < 0.001; median mental health: 3.8 vs. 4.2, p = 0.020). Larger parent–adolescent discrepancies in pain and mental health domain scores correlated with higher levels of adolescent psychological symptoms (p < 0.001). Greater discrepancy in functional appraisal predicted poorer exercise adherence (OR = 0.109, p = 0.015), whereas higher adolescent self-rated mental health predicted better adherence (OR = 3.523, p = 0.011). Conclusions: Significant discrepancies exist between parent and adolescent HRQoL assessments in AIS, particularly regarding self-image and mental health. These perceptual differences are associated with adolescent psychological distress and exercise adherence. Clinical management should incorporate dual-perspective evaluations to align perceptions, improve communication, and enhance treatment outcomes. What is Known:• Parent–adolescent discrepancies in HRQoL have been documented in other pediatric chronic conditions, but they remain less explored in AIS.• Treatment adherence remains a major challenge in exercise therapy for AIS.What is New:• Focusing on adolescents with AIS receiving exercise therapy, this study identified a parental underestimation of adolescent self-image and mental health.• Greater discrepancies were associated with higher levels of psychological distress.• Discordance in functional appraisal predicted poorer exercise adherence, whereas better self-rated mental health predicted improved adherence, suggesting new targets for clinical intervention in AIS.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), the most common form of scoliosis, affects 1–3% of adolescents worldwide between the ages of 10 and 18 years. It is defined as a lateral spinal curvature with a Cobb angle exceeding 10 degrees and has no known etiology. The condition typically progresses during adolescence until skeletal maturity is reached [1, 2]. Current treatment strategies for AIS include physiotherapy, bracing, and surgery, with the choice of intervention guided by the adolescent’s skeletal maturity and risk of curve progression [3, 4]. Scoliosis-specific exercises, such as the Schroth method, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing curve progression and are established as a primary non-operative treatment for mild to moderate AIS [5, 6].

Beyond cosmetic concerns, adolescents with scoliosis often exhibit impairments in psychosocial functioning and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) compared to their healthy peers [4, 7–9]. This chronic condition can also adversely affect mental well-being, leading to negative psychological responses such as anxiety and depression [8–12]. Consequently, integrated care models that address both physical and psychosocial aspects are increasingly emphasized, as psychosocial functioning may influence adolescents’ treatment adherence and their quality of life [1, 7, 11, 13–15]. A comprehensive assessment of adolescent-reported HRQoL and psychological status is therefore essential in managing AIS.

Parents play a crucial role in the long-term management of AIS, assisting with care coordination, medical decision-making, and emotional support. Their perceptions of the adolescent’s HRQoL and emotional state may substantially influence the adolescent’s treatment experience and rehabilitation outcomes [16, 17]. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of a adolescent’s HRQoL should incorporate reports from both the adolescent and their caregiver. However, discrepancies often exist between parent and adolescent perceptions of physical function, HRQoL, and psychological symptoms, differences that may negatively affect recovery and quality of care [16, 18–20]. Previous studies have examined parent–adolescent agreement in evaluations of postoperative appearance or deformity in AIS, consistently demonstrating poor concordance regarding cosmetic outcomes after surgery [21–23].

Identifying and understanding these discrepancies between adolescent and parent reports of HRQoL can inform clinical decision-making and improve treatment planning [20]. Nonetheless, there is a lack of research examining parent–adolescent disparities in HRQoL and psychosocial functioning within the context of exercise-based therapy for AIS.

For adolescents with mild to moderate AIS, regular performance of physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises is recommended throughout treatment to prevent curve progression [4]. The efficacy of such exercise therapies is highly dependent on sustained patient engagement, particularly with home-based exercise regimens. Poor adherence can significantly compromise treatment outcomes [24, 25]. As previously noted, discrepancies between self- and proxy-reported HRQoL may adversely affect treatment adherence [20], highlighting the need to investigate how such differences influence compliance with home exercise programs.

To our knowledge, no study has examined parent–adolescent discrepancies in HRQoL perceptions among adolescents undergoing exercise-based therapy for AIS. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate differences in HRQoL ratings between adolescents with mild to moderate AIS and their parents. The secondary aim was to examine whether these perceptual discrepancies correlate with psychological symptoms in adolescents and their adherence to exercise-based therapy.

Methods

Procedure and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [26]. A convenience sampling method was used. This study was approved by the Ethics Research Committee of Aerospace Center Hospital, Beijing, China (Approval No. JAH-ER-2024–047). Data were collected at the Aerospace Center Hospital from early August 2024 to late April 2025. Participants were recruited from the outpatient Department of Rehabilitation for adolescent scoliosis at Aerospace Center Hospital in Beijing, China, during routine visits. This tertiary referral center specializes in non-operative AIS management, providing exclusively physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises (e.g., Schroth method) for an estimated 140–180 adolescents with AIS annually. Surgical correction is not available at this facility.

Adolescents diagnosed with mild to moderate idiopathic scoliosis who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled until the predetermined sample size was achieved. Following a comprehensive explanation of the study protocol, written informed consent was obtained from all eligible patients and one accompanying parent.

At the initial visit, each adolescent completed the Scoliosis Research Society-22 Questionnaire (SRS-22). One parent also provided proxy ratings using the same instrument based on their perception of the adolescent’s health status. While adolescent self-report is considered the standard for perceived HRQoL, parent proxy-reports were obtained in this study specifically to enable the investigation of parent–adolescent perceptual discrepancies, which was a central research aim. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were evaluated in both informants using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, respectively.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 10–18 years; (2) diagnosis of AIS with a Cobb angle between 10° and 45°; (3) prescription of exercise therapy following consultation; (4) patient ability to provide written informed consent; and (5) availability of an accompanying parent or guardian who could also provide consent (if multiple guardians were present, one was chosen to participate).

The exclusion criteria were: (1) evidence of other causes of scoliosis (e.g., congenital vertebral malformation, neuromuscular disorder, syndromic disorders); (2) family history of spinal deformity; (3) history of severe psychological or mental illnesses (e.g., anxiety, depression) in either the patient or parent; and (4) history of spinal/pelvic physiotherapy, bracing, or spinal surgery.

Demographics and clinical measurements

Demographic data collected included age, sex, education level, menarche or voice-breaking status, and family composition (single-child or not). Clinical measures comprised body mass index (BMI), the Cobb angle of the major curve, and the angle of trunk rotation (ATR).

The Cobb angle was assessed from standard standing full-spine posteroanterior radiographs by an experienced rehabilitation physician using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0, Media Cybernetics, Inc., USA). The angle was defined by drawing lines along the endplates of the superior and inferior end vertebrae of the major curve and measuring the angle between their perpendiculars [27], with a larger value indicating greater severity. The ATR was measured with a scoliometer by a physiotherapist while the adolescent performed a standing forward bend [28], and the maximum rotation value (range: 0°–30°) was recorded. All angular measurements were performed three times, and the average value was used for analysis.

HRQoL assessment

The HRQoL of adolescents was evaluated using the SRS-22 questionnaire, a widely used instrument for assessing HRQoL in AIS patients [4]. The Chinese version, which has proven reliability and validity [29], was employed. The SRS-22 comprises five domains: function/activity, pain, self-image, mental health, and satisfaction with management. As the assessment was conducted before treatment, the two questions related to treatment satisfaction were omitted. All items are scored on a 1–5 Likert scale, with 1 indicating the "worst condition" and 5 the "best condition". The score for each domain is calculated as the mean of its items, with a higher score indicating better HRQoL. Adolescents completed the questionnaire independently, while parents completed it based on their perception of the adolescent's condition.

Psychological symptoms

Depressive and anxiety symptoms over the preceding two weeks were assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, respectively. Both instruments have validated Chinese versions with confirmed reliability and validity in adolescent populations [30–33].

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9 [34]. Its nine items correspond to the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder. Each item is scored from 0 ("not at all") to 3 ("almost every day"). Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Severity categories were defined as: none (0–4), mild (5–9), or moderate-severe (≥ 10) [35].

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the GAD-7 [36], a seven-item scale. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. Severity categories were: none (0–4), mild (5–9), or moderate-severe (≥ 10).

Exercise adherence

After the initial visit and questionnaire assessments, adolescents began a structured outpatient exercise therapy program consisting of six supervised sessions; each session lasted 40 min and was delivered at a frequency of 2 to 3 times per week. The intervention focused on Schroth-based scoliosis self-correction techniques administered one-on-one by a certified therapist. Following the supervised sessions, adolescents were instructed to continue the exercises independently at home.

Adherence was assessed primarily through adolescent self-report, with supplementary information from family interviews. Two weeks after initiating the home-based exercise program, adolescents and their parents were surveyed on exercise continuation, frequency, and duration. Total exercise time (in minutes) was calculated as the number of weeks exercised multiplied by the weekly frequency and session duration. Adherence was defined as the ratio of accumulated to prescribed exercise time, with a weekly target of 200 min. Participants were categorized as adherent if they achieved ≥ 200 min over two weeks (≥ 50% of the prescribed 400 min) and non-adherent otherwise.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test assessed normality. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compared SRS-22 scores between adolescents and parents. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test; continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman's correlation coefficient analyzed correlations between adolescent and parent PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores, and between SRS-22 and PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores. A Bonferroni-corrected alpha of 0.003 (0.05/16 tests) was used for correlations involving SRS-22 domains. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of exercise adherence, including variables with significant univariate associations, using the backward likelihood ratio method. Significance was set at α = 0.05.

A priori sample size calculation using PASS 15 software was based on a mean difference of 0.3 ± 0.7 points in the SRS-22 self-image domain. With 90% power and a 5% significance level, the minimum sample size was 60 dyads. Allowing for a 15% attrition rate, the target sample size was set at 71 dyads.

Results

Factors associated with exercise adherence

Univariate analyses (Table 4) showed that adherent adolescents (≥ 50% exercise dose) had a significantly larger major curve Cobb angle (median [IQR]: 20.0° [16.5–24.0] vs. non-adherent: 16.0° [10.0–21.5]; p = 0.047), higher parent-rated SRS-22 pain scores (4.60 [4.40–4.80] vs. 4.40 [4.15–4.80]; p = 0.037), and higher adolescent-rated SRS-22 mental health scores (4.40 [4.00–4.90] vs. 4.00 [3.55–4.45]; p = 0.016). A significant between-group difference was also observed in the parent–adolescent discrepancy score for the SRS-22 function domain (0.00 [−0.40–0.10] vs. 0.00 [0.00–0.40]; p = 0.036). Discussion

This study revealed significant discrepancies between parents and adolescents with AIS in their assessments of adolescent’s HRQoL, particularly in the domains of self-image and mental health, with parents holding more negative views. These perceptual gaps were associated with higher levels of psychological distress in adolescents. Furthermore, disagreement in functional appraisal was associated with poorer exercise adherence, while the adolescent's positive perception of their own mental health was linked to better adherence.

Although evidence on parent–adolescent HRQoL discrepancies in AIS remains limited, studies of other chronic pediatric conditions suggest that parents and adolescents often differ in their appraisal of the adolescent’s HRQoL [16, 37, 38]. Specifically, concordance is typically higher for observable domains like physical functioning and symptoms, while greater discordance occurs in assessments of internal states like emotional functioning [18–20]. Our findings align with this pattern: parents rated adolescents’ mental health more negatively, whereas concordance was observed for physical function and pain. Similarly, Misterska et al. [39] reported that parents of AIS patients treated with a Cheneau brace perceived higher levels of emotional distress related to body deformity than the adolescents themselves, suggesting potential parental overestimation of psychological burden. This may reflect adolescents' reluctance to disclose private mental experiences, which can impair parent–adolescent communication and treatment engagement [18].

Perceptions of body image in AIS vary considerably across studies. In our study, parents rated their adolescents' self-image significantly lower than the adolescents' self-ratings. This observation aligns with Xiao et al. [40] and Sanders et al. [23], who suggested that parents' direct observation of dorsal deformities might lead them to overestimate the severity of the condition. Conversely, Rinella et al. [22] reported that parents provided higher self-image ratings in a postoperative setting. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in clinical context: postoperative assessments likely reflect satisfaction with surgical outcomes, whereas evaluations during conservative management may be influenced by persistent concerns about trunk deformity.

Psychological symptoms were also assessed in adolescents with AIS and their parents. Using a cutoff score of 10 on the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 [41, 42], we found a prevalence of 4.2% for both anxiety and depressive symptoms among adolescents—lower than rates reported in studies including surgical candidates [11]. This is expected, as our sample comprised adolescents with milder conditions undergoing exercise therapy.

Notably, larger parent–adolescent discrepancies in SRS-22 pain and mental health scores were significantly associated with higher adolescent psychological distress. This suggests that discordance in subjective health assessments may exacerbate the adolescent's psychological burden. Lin et al. [17], similarly found that negative parental attitudes were associated with more severe depressive symptoms in adolescents with AIS during brace treatment. Furthermore, parental cognitions characterized by exaggerated fears may promote overprotective behaviors, potentially increasing the adolescent's risk for anxiety [43, 44].

Poor adherence to home-based exercise remains a major barrier in AIS management [24, 25]. We identified two key predictors: functional appraisal discordance, which reduced adherence, and positive self-perceived mental health, which enhanced it. This underscores the recognized role of psychological well-being in promoting adherence to chronic disease management [16, 38, 45]. Mirroring this finding, mental health is also linked to adherence in AIS brace treatment, with adherent adolescents reporting better quality of life [46]. Our work further reveals that parent–adolescent perceptual misalignment is a specific factor undermining exercise adherence, potentially by leading parents to withdraw active monitoring and support when adolescents faced challenges [37].

Although parents and adolescents differed significantly in self-image perceptions, this discrepancy was not associated with psychological symptoms or adherence. This suggests that appearance-related perceptual differences may be a common yet relatively independent aspect of the AIS experience within families, assimilated without substantially affecting emotional well-being or health behaviors. This interpretation is consistent with Schwieger et al. [47], who found that body image did not significantly affect brace adherence in female adolescents with AIS, possibly due to the development of adaptive coping strategies over time.

This study has several limitations. The first limitation concerns the potential ceiling effects of the SRS-22 questionnaire [48], particularly evident in our data from the pain and function/activity domains, where scores clustered at the upper end of the scale, potentially limiting their discriminative ability. Notably, even the Scoliosis Quality of Life Index (SQLI), a modified instrument designed specifically to mitigate this issue, has been shown not to sufficiently reduce ceiling effects [49], underscoring a well-recognized limitation in HRQoL assessment for this population. Despite this constraint, statistically significant parent–adolescent discrepancies persisted in the self-image and mental health domains, suggesting that while ceiling effects should be approached with caution when interpreting absolute scores, they do not invalidate the core findings regarding inter-rater perceptual gaps. Second, although the observed discrepancies exceeded the reported minimal important difference of 0.11 points for the SRS-22 in non-surgical patients [50], their full clinical significance requires further validation. Third, we did not differentiate between maternal and paternal raters. Given that mothers are often more involved in daily care, they may be more attuned to the adolescent's condition than fathers, which could have influenced the proxy ratings. Fourth, the cross-sectional design prevents any causal inferences regarding the relationships among the variables. Furthermore, this single-center study was conducted in Beijing, China, with a sample primarily comprising local outpatients. This may limit the generalizability of our findings, as specific socio-cultural contexts could influence the observed parent–adolescent interactions.

Building on these findings and limitations, we propose the following directions for further research. First, a novel HRQoL instrument is needed for adolescents with conservatively managed AIS. By overcoming the ceiling effects of current measures and providing greater discriminative ability in domains like physical function, this tool would more accurately represent their quality of life. Second, to advance beyond the correlational evidence from this cross-sectional study, further research employing both longitudinal and interventional designs is warranted. Longitudinal studies are essential to establish the temporal relationships and potential causal pathways linking perceptual discrepancies, psychological symptoms, and treatment adherence. In parallel, interventional studies are needed to translate evidence into clinical practice by evaluating strategies, such as targeted parent–adolescent communication training or shared decision-making protocols, that are designed to align perceptions, improve psychological well-being, and enhance exercise adherence. Additionally, future studies should seek to differentiate between maternal and paternal influences on proxy-reported outcomes. Finally, multi-center studies involving diverse cultural and socioeconomic populations are needed to validate the generalizability of the observed parent–adolescent discrepancies and their impact on treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

This study revealed substantial parent–adolescent discrepancies in HRQoL assessments, particularly in self-image and mental health, which were associated with greater adolescent psychological distress. Furthermore, functional appraisal discordance was associated with poorer exercise adherence, while the adolescent's positive mental health perception was linked to better adherence. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of dual-perspective evaluations for identifying parent–adolescent perceptual gaps. Such gaps represent a potential target for future interventions aimed at improving psychological well-being and exercise adherence.
