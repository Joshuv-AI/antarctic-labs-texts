“Integrating Patients’ Perspectives into Continuing Medical Education (CME) to Address Disparities in Gynecologic Cancer Care”
Authors: Leen Alyaseen, Sarah A Nisly, Kathryn Burn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOngoing disparities exist in gynecologic cancer outcomes, and gaps remain in healthcare professional (HCP) education related to communication, equity, and survivorship. This retrospective mixed-methods analysis examined whether embedding patient narratives in continuing medical education (CME) for gynecologic oncology was associated with shifts in HCPs’ reported intentions to change practice. Accredited CME activities delivered in the United States from 2021 to 2023 were reviewed. Provider post-activity evaluation data and de-identified patient narratives from Smart Patients®, an online community for patients and families, were included. Categorical evaluation outcomes were summarized using frequencies and percentages; differences in behavioral-change response categories across years were assessed using chi-square tests of independence; and patient narrative themes were compared descriptively with HCP-reported intent-to-change categories. The analysis included 4,057 unique learners in 2021, 16,970 in 2022, and 10,278 in 2023, alongside 171 patient narratives. The proportion of HCPs planning to implement changes remained stable across years (44–49%; χ² = 7.81, df = 4, p = 0.099). However, the focus of intended changes shifted from primarily applying guidelines in 2021 (77%) towards greater emphasis on educating other healthcare professionals (45% in 2022) and contributing to team-based change (35% in 2023). Thematic shifts paralleled year-to-year changes in the narratives featured. Introduction

Significant disparities in gynaecologic cancer outcomes persist despite advances in treatment and screening. Black patients experience disproportionately higher mortality from cervical, ovarian, and uterine cancers [1–3]. Mortality from cervical cancer is approximately 60% higher among Black women than among non-Hispanic White women, and mortality from uterine cancer is more than twice as high as in other groups [3,4]. These inequities reflect systemic and structural barriers that affect access, communication, and continuity of care.

Communication quality between healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patients strongly influences outcomes in gynaecologic oncology, where decisions often involve complex prognostic uncertainty and significant symptom burden [5]. Incorporating patient perspectives can be particularly important for patients with diverse cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic backgrounds, whose needs and lived experiences shape decision-making [6]. Targeted educational strategies that highlight patient-reported priorities may help clinicians better recognise communication gaps, address psychosocial needs, and contextualise disparities influencing care delivery.

Despite progress in therapeutics and management guidelines, gaps remain in HCP education related to patient-centred communication, survivorship, and equity-focused care [7]. CME activities must increasingly incorporate patient narratives to elevate real-world patient concerns, including structural inequities, treatment barriers, communication challenges, and unmet survivorship needs. This integration may offer a scalable strategy for aligning continuing education with patient-identified priorities. Because equity, communication gaps, and survivorship needs are major concerns in gynaecologic oncology, this study examined whether CME activities could support HCP alignment with these priorities.

Methods

Study Design & Research Context

This retrospective mixed-methods study examined accredited CME activities in gynaecologic oncology delivered in the United States from 2021 through 2023. Activities were accessible to healthcare professionals globally through an online learning platform. The primary objective was to examine whether integrating patient narratives into CME activities was associated with shifts over time in the type and focus of HCPs’ reported intentions to change practice. Secondary objectives included describing participant demographics and summarising year-to-year variation in behavioural-change responses.

CME Activity Format

Data were retrospectively captured from an online learning management system and individual activity files. The overall educational structure remained consistent across the three years, with content updated annually to incorporate evolving patient narratives into the activities.

Patient Narratives

Patient perspectives informing the educational design were derived from a Smart Patients® asynchronous online focus group conducted over a one-week period. Smart Patients® is an online community for patients and families affected by a variety of illnesses, where members learn about scientific developments and share questions and concerns. The platform includes more than 100 condition-specific communities and provides a space for patients to exchange experience-based insights. As a community-facing platform, Smart Patients® is not funded, sponsored, or supported by pharmaceutical companies, medical device manufacturers, or other entities classified as ineligible companies by ACCME standards. Qualitative thematic analysis was applied to participant narratives to identify patient-prioritised needs. These patient-identified priorities informed the structure of the CME activities. All patient narrative contributors were US-based. Themes were identified from the patient narratives and supported with verbatim patient quotes (Table 1).

Participants

Participants were HCPs who completed one or more CME activities and voluntarily submitted post-activity evaluation data. Participants represented multiple professions, including physicians, nurse practitioners (NPs), nurses, and pharmacists. Participants were excluded if they did not complete a post-activity evaluation or if evaluation records were incomplete or missing variables required for analysis.

Data Preparation

Open-ended behavioural-change responses and patient narratives were reviewed and grouped into descriptive thematic categories by the primary investigator, LA. Initial thematic analysis of the patient narratives was conducted by KB and validated by LA, with SN adjudicating discrepancies. Themes were identified through iterative review and refined through discussion to ensure consistency across years. The manuscript was reviewed for accuracy and clarity by SN. All data were de-identified before analysis.

Quantitative Analysis

Frequencies and percentages were used to summarise categorical evaluation outcomes. Behavioural-change response percentages were calculated among participants who submitted a behavioural-change evaluation response for that year; these respondent counts (Table 2) are substantially smaller than total activity completer counts (Table 3) because completion of this evaluation item was voluntary. Differences in the distribution of behavioural-change response categories across independent activity years were assessed using chi-square tests of independence. Analyses were conducted in Microsoft Excel using the Real Statistics add-in.

Qualitative Analysis

Descriptive labels were applied to identify prominent themes within patient narratives and HCP open-ended comments for each year. Themes were identified through iterative review by the primary investigator LA and refined through discussion to ensure consistency across years. Themes identified in patient narratives were compared descriptively with HCP-reported intent-to-change categories to assess alignment over time.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Across the three-year period, the analysis included 4,057 unique learners in 2021, 16,970 in 2022, and 10,278 in 2023 (Table 3). Physicians comprised the majority of participants in all years, with a marked increase in physician representation in 2023. Correspondingly, participation by nurses, pharmacists, and NPs declined in 2023. Oncology was the most frequently reported speciality in each year (57%,59% and 66% in 2021, 2022, and 2023 respectively; Table 4).Country of practice was not captured in the CME evaluation data.

Behavioural-change responses were provided by 78 participants in 2021, 148 in 2022, and 139 in 2023 (Table 2). No statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of behavioural-change response categories across years (χ2 = 7.81, df = 4, p = 0.099).

A total of 171 de-identified patient narratives were included across CME activities from 2021 to 2023. Themes in 2021 centred on diagnosis and risk factors, treatment options and prognosis, treatment trade-offs, and alignment of expectations (Figure 1). In 2022, narratives increasingly highlighted racial and ethnic disparities, communication gaps, barriers to therapy access, and individualised treatment needs. In 2023, themes shifted towards long-term survivorship, including decision-making gaps, symptom burden, psychosocial and sexual health needs, financial toxicity, and communication during remission.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of incorporating patient stories into gynaecologic oncology CME on healthcare professionals stated intentions to change practice over a three-year period. In this analysis, the proportion of participants who reported an intention to make practice changes remained relatively stable over time (44–49%; χ2 = 7.81, p = 0.099). However, the thematic focus of intended changes shifted across years, from primarily guideline-related changes in 2021 (77%) to greater emphasis on patient education in 2022 (45%) and 2023 (26%), as well as team-based process improvements in 2022 (12%) and 2023 (35%) (Table 5).

These shifts occurred alongside changes in patient narrative content included in the CME activities, which progressed from diagnosis-centred themes in 2021 to disparity-focused narratives in 2022 and survivorship-centred narratives in 2023.

Jackson et al. evaluated a cancer survivorship CME course attended by 1,202 healthcare professionals and reported significant improvements in participants’ attitudes and intent to change practice related to survivorship care immediately following the education [8]. However, the single-timepoint design did not allow assessment of long-term effects or whether increased confidence translated into practice change. McKay et al. reported that incorporating patients’ voices into interprofessional CME improved clinicians’ understanding of patient concerns and shared decision-making perspectives, but the analysis did not examine longitudinal changes in narrative content over time [9]. Paul and Kaye proposed a theoretical framework for narrative expertise in oncology education and presented empirical data for a targeted fellowship training model, but did not evaluate outcomes across a broad interprofessional CME cohort [10]. The present study builds on this prior work through three distinct contributions: (1) longitudinal tracking of participants across three years, (2) inclusion of an interprofessional cohort rather than a single-profession sample, and (3) examination of year-specific correspondence between patient narrative themes and HCP-reported intentions to change practice.

Salyer et al. identified persistent deficiencies in HCP education related to patient-centred communication, primary palliative care, symptom management, and end-of-life communication [7]. These gaps are particularly relevant given documented disparities in gynaecologic cancer outcomes among Black women, who face a 1.3-fold higher risk of death from ovarian cancer and more than double the mortality from uterine cancer compared with other groups, reflecting systemic, socioeconomic, and access-related barriers [1–3]. Bylund et al. demonstrated that communication training increased trainee skill use in simulation, although the study did not assess transfer to clinical practice or examine patient narratives and interprofessional intentions [5]. In the present study, changes in narrative content were accompanied by shifts in HCP-reported intentions towards communication, equity, and survivorship support.

The ACCME Patient Partner Roadmap defines patient voice as the meaningful inclusion of patients lived experiences, values, and preferences within medical education and emphasises evidence that narrative-based learning enhances retention and contextual understanding [11]. The roadmap identifies communication, equity, and shared decision-making as key areas where patient engagement can strengthen education. In this study, when patient narratives highlighted racial inequities and communication barriers in 2022, a greater proportion of HCPs reported intentions focused on educating other professionals (45%), and when narratives emphasised survivorship-related decision-making gaps in 2023, more participants reported intentions related to team-based change (35%). These findings align with ACCME priorities related to patient engagement in accredited education.

McMahon described outcomes-centred CME as education that extends beyond knowledge acquisition to address competence, performance, and patient outcomes [12]. Price et al. similarly emphasised systems-integrated CME as a means of linking education to real-world care delivery gaps [13]. The present study aligns with these frameworks by focusing on intention-to-change outcomes rather than knowledge measures and by incorporating patient narratives that reflect lived experiences relevant to gynaecologic oncology care. While intention-to-change is a widely used outcome measure in CME research, it is a self-reported proxy and may not reliably reflect what occurs in practice. Future work linking CME participation to objective practice or patient-level outcomes would strengthen the evidence base.

Limitations

This study has important limitations. First, response rates varied across demographic, behaviour-change, and intent-to-change items, resulting in uneven sample sizes that may affect the stability of year-to-year comparisons. Second, behaviour-change and intent-to-change data were collected through separate evaluation items, preventing linkage at the individual level and limiting the ability to examine direct relationships between specific CME elements and reported intentions. Finally, the retrospective use of existing CME evaluation data restricted the available variables and limited control over potential confounders. This study was conducted within a US-based accredited CME programme; findings may not be transferable to continuing education systems in other countries. These factors contextualise the findings and highlight opportunities for future work to incorporate linked datasets and objective indicators of practice change.

Conclusion

Across three years of CME activities in gynaecologic oncology, changes in the content of patient narratives corresponded with shifts in HCP-reported intentions to modify aspects of practice, particularly communication, equity considerations, and survivorship support. While the proportion of HCPs planning to implement changes remained stable, the thematic focus of these intended changes evolved in parallel with patient-reported concerns. These findings support the value of incorporating patient experiences into CME design to reinforce patient-centred priorities and highlight real-world barriers affecting gynaecologic cancer care.
