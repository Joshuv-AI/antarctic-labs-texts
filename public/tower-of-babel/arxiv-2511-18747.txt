Abstract

Introduction

1.1
Background

All graphs considered are simple, and we use standard graph theory terminology and notation throughout; refer
[
17
]
for any undefined terms. Given graphs
G
and
H
,
the
Cartesian product
of
G
and
H
, written
G\Box H
,
is the graph with
V(G\Box H)=V(G)\times V(H)

and
(u,x)(v,y)\in E(G\Box H)
if and only if

u=v
and
xy\in E(H)
or
x=y
and
uv\in E(G)
.
For an integer
k\geq 2
, we let

G^{\Box k}=G^{\Box k-1}\Box G
, where we assume
G^{\Box 1}=G
. We denote the
independence number, clique number, chromatic number
and
fractional chromatic number
of a graph
G
by
\alpha(G)
,
\omega(G)
,
\chi(G)
, and
\chi_{f}(G)
, respectively. See
[
13
]
for more on fractional chromatic number. The
independence ratio
of
G
, written
i(G)
, is the relative size of a maximum independent set in
G
compared to its order, i.e.,
i(G)=\frac{\alpha(G)}{|V(G)|}
.
The
ultimate independence ratio
of
G
is then defined to be

\mathscr{I}(G)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}i(G^{\Box k}).

Investigation of this type of limiting behaviour, for different parameters and various notions of graph products, is long established in the literature, see for instance
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
14
,
15
]
. A well-known example is the notion of Shannon capacity, originally introduced in an influential paper by Shannon
[
14
]
,
which is defined by
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[k]{\alpha(G^{\boxtimes k})}
for a given graph
G
.
Here
\boxtimes
denotes the strong product of graphs, refer
[
9
]
for the definition.

The study of ultimate independence ratio began in
[
3
]
as a means of proving that there is no homomorphism between two graphs. Recall, a

graph homomorphism
from a graph
G
to a graph
H

is a function
h:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
such that if
uv\in E(G)
, then
h(u)h(v)\in E(H)
. For a gentle introduction to graph homomorphisms, we recommend
[
8
]
; for a broader survey, we recommend
[
10
]
. The connection between the ultimate independence ratio and graph homomorphisms
was later established in a more general context by Zhou
[
19
,
18
]
. Motivated by these early results, the ultimate independence ratio was formally introduced by Hell, Yu, and Zhou
[
11
]
. They proved that for any
k
,
i(G^{\Box k+1})\leq i(G^{\Box k})
,
implying that
\{i(G^{\Box k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a decreasing sequence of positive real numbers and so the limit
\mathscr{I}(G)
exits.

The first major progress in understanding the ultimate independence ratio came from Hahn, Hell, and Poljak
[
7
]
,
who proved the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
7
]
)
.

If there is a homomorphism from
G
to
H
,
then
\mathscr{I}(H)\leq\mathscr{I}(G)
.

A valuable corollary is that for all graphs
G
,

\frac{1}{\chi(G)}\leq\mathscr{I}(G)\leq\frac{1}{\chi_{f}(G)}.

(1)

Note that
\omega(G)\leq\chi_{f}(G)\leq\chi(G)
for any graph
G
.
It follows that for weakly perfect graphs, i.e., graphs with
\chi=\omega
, the ultimate independence ratio is determined if either
\chi
or
\omega
is known.

It was a question for some time if
\mathscr{I}(G)
is always equal to
\frac{1}{\chi(G)}
or
\frac{1}{\chi_{f}(G)}
. It was shown in
[
7
]

that there exist graphs
G
with
\frac{1}{\chi(G)}<\mathscr{I}(G)<i(G)
. This was later extended by Zhu
[
20
]

who constructed a graph
G
with
\frac{1}{\chi(G)}<\mathscr{I}(G)<\frac{1}{\chi_{f}(G)}
. To prove this, Zhu
[
20
]

provided a new bound for the ultimate independence ratio
in terms of another colouring parameter called the star chromatic number, initially introduced by Vince
[
16
]
.
Somewhat confusingly, this parameter is not the least number of vertex colours required, so that any pair of colour classes induces a forest of stars (which is also called the star chromatic number, see for instance
[
2
]
).

Determining the exact value of the ultimate independence ratio is, in general, a challenging problem. The smallest graph whose ultimate independence ratio is unknown is the
5
-wheel. Recall that for any integer
t\geq 1
, a
t
-
wheel
, denoted
W_{t}
, is the graph obtained from the join of cycle
C_{t}
with
K_{1}
.
When
t
is even,
\chi(W_{t})=\omega(W_{t})=3
, and so for all
t
,
\mathscr{I}(W_{2t})=\frac{1}{3}
.
Odd wheels are more interesting, which led Hahn, Hell, and Poljak
[
7
]
to conjecture the following. Since
\chi(W_{2t+1})=4
, note here that
\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})\geq\frac{1}{4}
by (
1
).

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

For all integers
t\geq 1
,

\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})=\frac{1}{4}.

This 30-year-old conjecture, in particular, the special case of determining
\mathscr{I}(W_{5})
, was recently resurrected by Hahn in a talk
1
1

1

Hahn’s talk was titled
Resurrection – revisiting old problems
.
at the Canadian Math Society winter meeting in 2024. The best known progress on Conjecture
1.2

comes in
[
7
]
where it was shown that

\mathscr{I}(W_{5})\leq\frac{11}{41}.

(2)

This bound was obtained by proving that
\chi_{f}(W_{5}^{\Box 2})=\frac{41}{11}
.

For
t\geq 3
, the best known upper bound for the ultimate independence ratio of
W_{2t+1}
is

\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})\leq\frac{t}{3t+1}.

(3)

The bound in (

) has never been observed before, but it follows from known results in the literature. Precisely, the bound in (
3
) follows using (
1
) and the fact that
\chi_{f}(W_{2t+1})=3+\frac{1}{t}
. This fact is true because the fractional chromatic number of a join

satisfies

\chi_{f}(G\lor H)=\chi_{f}(G)+\chi_{f}(H)
,
see
[

,
while it is well known that
\chi_{f}(C_{2k+1})=2+\frac{1}{k}
, see
[

1.2
Our Results

We improve the upper bounds on

\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})
for all
t\geq 2
, thus making the first progress towards
Conjecture
1.2
in three decades. We note that any progress here is of interest,
since estimating
\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})
up to an arbitrary error is algorithmically impractical using known methods.

Let
\ell
be a fixed positive integer, preferably small. Clearly, for a graph
G
,
\mathscr{I}(G)\leq i(G^{\Box\ell})
, and so if one can compute
\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})
, then it would lead to an upper bound on
\mathscr{I}(G)
. When the graph is
W_{2t+1}
, we know the value of
\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box 2})
(see Proposition
4.3
), but it is difficult to compute
\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box 3})
for large

. For some small values of

,
\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box 3})
is known (see Table
1
), but computing even
\alpha(W_{5}^{\Box 4})
is difficult using our machines.
To overcome this,
we prove a new upper bound for
\mathscr{I}(G)
.
Notice that this generalizes a result by Hahn, Hell, and Poljak
regarding vertex transitive graphs, see Theorem 4.1 in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k,\ell\geq 1
be fixed integers.
If
G
is a graph containing a clique of order
k
,
then

\mathscr{I}(G)\leq\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{k|V(G)|^{\ell}}.

Moreover,

\mathscr{I}(G)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{k|V(G)|^{\ell}}.

Applying Theorem
1.3
to

W_{2t+1}

with
k=3
gives the following corollary.
Since we are focused on odd wheels in this paper, this corollary is the principle application of Theorem
1.3
we will use.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
t\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 1
be fixed integers. Then

\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})\leq\frac{\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{3})}{3(2t+2)^{\ell}}.

We first use Theorem
1.3
to obtain a non-trivial upper bound on

for any

\alpha(G\Box K_{k})\leq|V(G)|

(4)

It is easily seen that the upper bound in (

.
We improve this even further for all

Assuming

Theorem 1.5
.

For all

\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})\leq\frac{4t^{2}+6t}{3(2t+2)^{2}}.

, let

V(W_{2t+1})=\{w_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2t+1}\}\cup\{w_{*}\}

where
w_{*}
denotes the dominating vertex in
W_{2t+1}
. Here
\mathbb{Z}_{2t+1}
denotes the cyclic group of order
2t+1
. This is helpful in the indexing of the vertices. Then

V(W_{2t+1}^{\Box k})=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}):a_{i}\in V(W_{2t+1})\}.

We define
H_{p}^{(k)}
to be the subgraph of
W_{2t+1}^{\Box k}
induced by the vertices

\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},w_{p}):a_{i}\in V(W_{2t+1}),1\leq i\leq k-1\}.

Clearly,
H_{p}^{(k)}\cong W_{2t+1}^{\Box k-1}
.
We define
H_{p,q}^{(k)}
to be the subgraph of
W_{2t+1}^{\Box k}
induced by the vertices

\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-2},w_{q},w_{p}):a_{i}\in V(W_{2t+1}),1\leq i\leq k-2\}.

Note that
H_{p,q}^{(k)}\cong W_{2t+1}^{\Box k-2}
. Also,
V(H_{p,q}^{(k)})\subset V(H_{p}^{(k)})
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G
and

be graphs. Then

\alpha(G\Box H)\leq\min\{|V(G)|\alpha(H),|V(H)|\alpha(G)\}.

3
A General Bound for Ultimate Independence

In this short section, we provide a proof of
Theorem
1.3
.
Our argument proceeds by using counting techniques to obtain a recurrence relation that upper bounds

i(G^{\Box\ell+1})
.
From here we use the limit in the definition of
\mathscr{I}(G)
,
together with this recurrence relation,
to obtain the desired bound.
We note that our argument does not imply that
the bound in
Theorem
1.3

is true when
i(G^{\Box q})
replaces
\mathscr{I}(G)
,
for any choice of
q
.
We simply guarantee the inequality in the limit.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose that
G
has
n
vertices.
Let
I
be a maximum independent set in
G^{\Box p+1}
.
By assumption, there exists a set clique
K
of order
k
in
G
.
For each
v\in V(G)
, define
I_{v}=I\cap\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{p},v):(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in V(G^{\Box p})\}
.
Then

\displaystyle|I|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G)}I_{v}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{v\in V(K)}I_{v}\right)+\left(\sum_{u\notin V(K)}I_{u}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(G^{\Box p}\Box K_{k})+\left(n-k\right)\alpha(G^{\Box p}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha(G^{\Box p+1})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(G^{\Box p}\Box K_{k})+\left(n-k\right)\alpha(G^{\Box p})

\displaystyle\leq n^{p-\ell}\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})+\left(n-k\right)\alpha(G^{\Box p})

Using the above recurrence, we see that

\displaystyle i(G^{\Box p+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box p+1})}{n^{p+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{n^{\ell+1}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{p-\ell}\left(\frac{n-k}{n}\right)^{i}\right)+\frac{(n-k)^{p-\ell+1}\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})}{n^{p+1}}

Recall that
\ell
is a constant.
By applying the definition of
\mathscr{I}(G)
and taking limits we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}(G)

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}i(G^{\Box p})

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}i(G^{\Box p+1})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{n^{\ell+1}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{p-\ell}\left(\frac{n-k}{n}\right)^{i}\right)+\frac{(n-k)^{p-\ell+1}\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})}{n^{p+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{n^{\ell+1}}\left(\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{p-\ell}\left(\frac{n-k}{n}\right)^{i}\right)\right)+\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\Bigg(\frac{(n-k)^{p-\ell+1}\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})}{n^{p+1}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{n^{\ell+1}}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)+0

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}.

We conclude that
\mathscr{I}(G)\leq\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}
as desired.

Now we turn our attention to proving

\mathscr{I}(G)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}.

Let
L
denote the value of the limit on the right-hand side.
For all
\ell\geq 1
,
the first part of the proof implies

\mathscr{I}(G)\leq\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}
.
Hence,
L\geq\mathscr{I}(G)
.
It remains to be shown that
L\leq\mathscr{I}(G)
.
To start,
observe that for all
\ell\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}\leq\frac{k\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})}{kn^{\ell}}=\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell})}{n^{\ell}}=i(G^{\Box\ell}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\alpha(G^{\Box\ell}\Box K_{k})}{kn^{\ell}}\leq\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}i(G^{\Box\ell})=\mathscr{I}(G).

This completes the proof.
∎

4
On Large Odd Wheels

This section is spent leveraging Theorem
1.3
to obtain a non-trivial upper bound on
\mathscr{I}(W_{2t+1})
for all
t
.
Specifically, we prove
\alpha(W^{\Box 2}_{2t+1}\Box K_{3})\leq 4t^{2}+6t+1
.
Before bounding the size independent sets in
W^{\Box 2}_{2t+1}\Box K_{3}

we examine independent sets in
W^{\Box 2}_{2t+1}
.
We first note some easy facts.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
t\geq 2
, we have

(i)

\alpha(W_{2t+1})=t.

(ii)

\alpha(W_{2t+1}\Box K_{3})=2t+1
.

Proof.

Assertion
(i)
is trivial. We give a proof for
(ii)
. Clearly,
given
\chi(W_{2t+1})=4
,

\alpha(W_{2t+1}\Box K_{3})\leq 2t+1.

Now, assume that
V(K_{3})=\{w_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}\}
. Then

\{(w_{2i+j},w_{j}):0\leq i\leq t-1,0\leq j\leq 1\}\cup\{(*,2)\}

is an independent set of size
2t+1
in
W_{2t+1}\Box K_{3}
.
Hence,
\alpha(W^{\Box 2}_{2t+1})\geq 2t+1
completing the proof.
∎

Next, we will determine
\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box 2})
. We first prove a more general lemma about independent sets in
W_{2t+1}^{\Box 2}
. Let
I
denote an independent set in
W_{2t+1}^{2}
, and for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2t+1}\cup\{*\}
define

I_{i}=I\cap V(H_{i}^{(2)})=\{(a,w_{i})\in I:a\in V(W_{2t+1})\}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
|I_{*}\backslash\{(w_{*},w_{*})\}|=k
. Then

|I|\leq t(2t+1)+1+(1-t)k.

Proof.

If
k=0
, then

|I|\leq\alpha(W_{2t+1}\Box C_{2t+1})+1\leq\alpha(W_{2t+1})\cdot(2t+1)+1=t(2t+1)+1,

and the assertion holds.

Now, assume that
k\geq 1
. Observe that if
(a,w_{*})\in I_{*}
, then
(a,w_{i})\notin I_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2t+1}
. For any
i
, the set
I_{i}-\{(b,w_{i}):(b,w_{*})\in I_{*}\}
induces a subgraph in

\displaystyle|I|

\displaystyle\leq|I_{*}|+\alpha(\cup_{j=1}^{k}P_{s_{j}}\Box C_{2t+1})

\displaystyle=|I_{*}|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha(P_{s_{j}}\Box C_{2t+1})

\displaystyle=k+1+t(2t+1-k),

Proof.

5.1
Understanding Independent Sets

Here, we outline the integer linear programming (IP) formulations that we use for calculations related to the existence of independent sets.
This goes beyond computing the independence number, as we are interested not only in the size of independent sets,
but their structure.
Our calculations are run using Gurobi’s linear programming solvers.

For a graph
G
, define the matrix
B(G)=[b_{ue}]\in\{0,1\}^{V(G)\times E(G)}
, where
b_{ue}=1
if
u
is an endpoint of the edge
e
, and
b_{ue}=0
otherwise. The matrix
B(G)
is called the
incidence matrix
of
G
.
Let
\mathbf{1}
denote the all-ones vector of appropriate order. The standard IP formulation for the independence number
\alpha(G)
is given by:

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}

(6)

subject to

\displaystyle B(G)^{T}x\leq\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle x\in\{0,1\}^{V(G)}.

Notice here that for any independent set
S
in the graph
G
, the indicator vector for
S
is an element of the feasible region of this IP. Thus, if we wish to study independent sets of
G
satisfying additional properties
\mathcal{P}
, it may be sufficient to add constraints to this program, depending on the choice of
\mathcal{P}
.
In particular, if one wishes to show that there is no independent set of
G
with a certain property
\mathcal{P}
, then it is sufficient to show that a version of this program with added constraints is infeasible.

Using the simple formulation above, the number of variables and constraints grows quickly in higher powers of Cartesian products.
To mitigate this, we use the structure of the Cartesian product to obtain finer control over the structure of independent sets.
For simple graphs
G
and
H
, the following IP gives
\alpha(G\Box H)
:

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(H)}\mathbf{1}^{T}x_{v}

(7)

subject to

\displaystyle B(G)^{T}x_{v}\leq\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\forall\ v\in V(H)

\displaystyle x_{u}+x_{v}\leq\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\forall\ uv\in E(H)

\displaystyle x_{v}\in\{0,1\}^{|V(G)|}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\forall\ v\in V(H).

This is a straightforward generalization of the standard program for independence number. The finer control this gives us to examine the structure of independent sets satisfying additional properties is worth stating explicitly.

Note here that
V(G\Box H)
has a natural partition of the form
\{\{(u,v):u\in V(G)\}:v\in V(H)\}
,
where the subgraph induced by each of these sets is isomorphic to
G
.
Let
I
be an independent set in
G\Box H
.
In Program
7
each variable
x_{v}
is the indicator vector for the subset

I_{v}=I\cap\{(u,v):u\in V(G)\}

of
I
.
Observing this, Program
7
provides the following advantage.
Recalling how Lemma
4.2
is used to prove Theorem
1.5
, it is sometimes useful to understand how forcing a subgraph to contain many vertices in an independent set
affects the structure of the independent set in the rest of the graph.
By introducing the variable vectors
x_{v}
, rather than giving each vertex of the graph
G\Box H
its own variable, there is an easy-to-implement and intuitive way to study independent sets
I
where

\{(u,v):u\in V(G)\}
contains at least
k
vertices of
I
.
To do so, one can simply add the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{T}x_{v}\geq k
to Program
7
.
Of course, one can add several such constraints simultaneously as well.

In Lemma
6.2
,
Lemma
6.3
, and Lemma
6.4
we do exactly this to prove certain kinds of independent sets do not exist in
W^{\Box 3}_{5}
.
That is, we add additional constraints of the form
\mathbf{1}^{T}x_{v}\geq k
or
\mathbf{1}^{T}x_{v}=k
to Program
7
,
then run the resulting program in Gurobi, which certifies the modified program is infeasible,
implying that no independent sets with the prescribed structure exist.
Leveraging the non-existence of independent sets with these structures, we then prove theoretically
that
\alpha(W^{\Box 3}_{5})=170
,
\alpha(W_{5}^{\Box 4})\leq 343
, and
\alpha(W^{\Box 4}_{5}\Box K_{3})\leq 1019
, respectively.

Each time that we conduct such a procedure, we must run a different integer program in Gurobi.
For each instance of such a calculation, we provide a distinct file, linked in the text, so that the reader
can reproduce and verify our computational work.
All files are commented to assist the reader’s understanding
and files may be read in any order.

5.2
Calculating Fractional Chromatic Number

Hahn, Hell, and Poljak
[
7
]
proved the following linear programming characterization of the fractional chromatic number
\chi_{f}(G)
of a graph
G
. Let
\mathcal{I}(G)
denote the family of all independent sets of
G
. Then the following is a program for
\frac{1}{\chi_{f}(G)}
:

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle z

(8)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}=1

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in I}x_{v}\leq z

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt\forall\,I\in\mathcal{I}(G)

\displaystyle x_{v}\geq 0

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt\forall\,v\in V(G).

It is straightforward to observe that it suffices to impose the constraints

\sum_{v\in I}x_{v}\leq z

only for the
maximal
independent sets
I
of
G
, any non-maximal independent set is contained in a maximal one, and therefore its corresponding constraint is dominated.

To simplify the program further, we exploit the symmetries of the graph via its automorphism group.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph, and let
\mathcal{I}(G)
denote the family of independent sets of
G
. Let
\Gamma=\mathrm{Aut}(G)
act on
V
and on
\mathcal{I}
in the natural way. Then there exists an optimal solution
x
to Program
8
that is constant on the orbits of maximal independent sets under
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
y
be any feasible solution of the Program
8
. For each
g\in\Gamma
define another feasible solution
g\cdot y
by
(g\cdot y)_{v}:=y_{g^{-1}(v)}
. Since
g
permutes vertices and maximal independent sets,
g\cdot y
satisfies all constraints of the Program
8
and has the same objective value as
y
.

Now consider the following feasible solution:

x:=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}g\cdot y.

Then
x
is feasible, has the same objective value as
y
, and is invariant under
\Gamma
, that is to say, for all
h\in\Gamma
we have
h\cdot x=x
. Thus
x
is constant on the orbits of
V
under
\Gamma
and we are done.
∎

The automorphism group partitions the vertex set into the following orbits:

T_{1}=\{(w_{*},w_{*})\},\quad T_{2}=\{(w_{i},w_{*}),(w_{*},w_{i}):0\leq i\leq 2t\},\quad T_{3}=\{(w_{i},w_{j}):0\leq i,j\leq 2t\}.

By Theorem
5.1
, the LP variables may be taken to be constant on these orbits, and each constraint depends only on the number of vertices from each orbit appearing in an independent set.

Following the notation of

For all

(2t+2)\alpha(W_{2t+1})-\alpha(W_{2t+1}^{\Box 2}\Box K_{3})=t-1.
