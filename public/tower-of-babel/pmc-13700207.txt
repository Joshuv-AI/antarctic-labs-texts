Simultaneous X-chromosome inactivation and tissue-/cell-type-specific DNA methylation in the transcriptional regulatory region of the X-linked recessive disease gene OTC
Authors: Fuko Yamada, Shiori Hinata, Ryohei Shigeta, Hiroshi Nagashima, Kenji Yamatoya, Jun Ohgane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVariability in disease onset and severity among heterozygous females with X-linked recessive disorders, such as ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency, may result from differences in the expression level of the normal allele caused by skewed X-chromosome inactivation (XCI). However, previous studies on the mechanisms of XCI lack relevance to humans. By applying the concept of hypomethylated allele frequency, gene expression can be estimated through DNA methylation analysis of transcriptional regulatory regions, including tissue-/cell-type-specific differentially methylated regions (T-DMRs) and XCI-affected regions.ResultsIn this study, we identified a porcine OTC transcriptional regulatory region that functions both as a T-DMR and XCI-affected differentially methylated region. Furthermore, by distinguishing alleles using a single nucleotide polymorphism, we detected evidence of skewed XCI in female pigs.ConclusionsAccordingly, by demonstrating that hypomethylated allele frequency reflects the active X allele, this study provides possible molecular evidence of skewed X inactivation through DNA methylation within the OTC T-DMR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02217-7.Keywords: DNA methylation, XCI, XCI skewing, T-DMR, X-linked recessive disorders, OTC

Background

In mammalian cells, sex chromosomes exist as XX in females and XY in males. In females, the mechanism of X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) equalizes the dosage of X-encoded genes. Generally, promoters of genes silenced by XCI exhibit high levels of DNA methylation, whereas promoters of genes that escape XCI remain hypomethylated [1]. Furthermore, genomic regions known as tissue-/cell-type-specific differentially methylated regions (T-DMRs) exhibit variable DNA methylation states depending on tissue or cell type [2, 3]. In mammals, numerous T-DMRs are located within transcriptional regulatory regions, and their DNA methylation patterns determine transcriptional activity [4]. Bisulfite sequencing can be applied for DNA methylation analysis, whereby each sequenced clone represents one allele within the analyzed cell population or tissue, reflecting either a hypomethylated or hypermethylated allele in a single cell. Examining the CpG methylation patterns of individual clones can reveal cell-type-specific and transcription-permissive DNA hypomethylated alleles (hypo-alleles) within heterogeneous cell populations or tissues [5].

The OTC gene encodes ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC), an enzyme of the urea cycle that catalyzes the condensation of carbamoyl phosphate with ornithine. OTC deficiency, classified as a urea cycle disorder, results in impaired ammonia detoxification, leading to hyperammonemia [6, 7]. The OTC gene in mammals is located on the X chromosome, and OTC deficiency is recognized as an X-linked recessive genetic disease. Although most patients with OTC deficiency and other X-linked recessive disorders are males with a single X chromosome, heterozygous females may also develop symptoms despite the recessive inheritance pattern [6]. This phenomenon is considered to arise from XCI, whereby transcriptional output from the normal allele is reduced, and XCI skewing accounts for differences in clinical severity among affected individuals [8, 9]. Estimating expression from functional normal alleles in heterozygous females can reveal disease manifestations and severity. However, when estimating X‑linked gene expression using hypomethylated allele ratios, both XCI and T‑DMR effects must be considered.

The mechanisms of XCI differ between mice and humans. For example, in eutherians, the choice of which X chromosome is inactivated is generally random, the inactive state is stably inherited, and adults represent mosaics of two cell types that express either the maternal or paternal X chromosome. In contrast, during early embryonic development in marsupials and some eutherians such as rodents, the paternal X chromosome is inactivated [10]. However, studies using model organisms that are more closely related to humans than mice are currently lacking. Genetically engineered OTC deficiency pigs closely mimic the human disease phenotype and have been established as a clinically relevant large-animal model for translational research [11, 12]. Therefore, we use pigs [13] to examine XCI and tissue-/cell-type-specific DNA methylation at the X-linked OTC locus by comparing the liver, where the X-linked recessive disease gene OTC is expressed, with other non-expressing tissues.

Result

Tissue-/cell-type-specific DNA methylation and XCI-induced DNA methylation overlap in the region surrounding the porcine OTC transcription start site

We analyzed DNA methylation states of the region surrounding the transcription start site (TSS) of the porcine OTC gene. Approximately 2,000 bp surrounding the OTC TSS was divided into four subregions and analyzed by bisulfite sequencing (Fig. 1A).

First, we analyzed DNA methylation patterns in male porcine tissues, which are not affected by XCI. In the liver, where OTC is expressed, the proportion of hypomethylated alleles, which our previous report [5] defined as hypo-alleles, was high compared with that in the OTC-non-expressing heart and kidney. Sequenced reads with ≥ 75% unmethylated CpGs were defined as hypo-alleles [5]. This trend was particularly evident in regions 2–4, which had 62–67% hypo-alleles in the liver but only 0–7% hypo-alleles in the heart and kidney (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the CpG island of the adjacent housekeeping gene, RPGR, was predominantly hypomethylated in all tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our RT-PCR analysis indicated specific expression of OTC only in livers from both male and female individuals, where obvious hypomethylated alleles were detected, but not in the other tissues analyzed (Fig. 1B). These findings indicate that regions 2–4 surrounding the TSS are recognized as the T-DMR within the porcine OTC locus.

Next, we analyzed female porcine tissues, which are subject to XCI. Similar to the male data, we found that regions 2–4 exhibited a higher proportion of hypo-alleles in the liver (40–45%) than in the heart and kidney (0–21%). In all regions identified as the T-DMR in males, the proportion of hypo-alleles in females was less than 50% (Fig. 1D). In the liver, the proportion of hypo-alleles in regions 2–4 of the T-DMR was consistently higher in males, with male–female differences of 27%, 45%, and 41%, respectively. In the RPGR gene, hypo-alleles were detected at approximately equal frequencies in the female liver and heart (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Porcine OTC promoter activity is regulated by DNA methylation

Next, we performed promoter assays on the region surrounding the OTC TSS, including the T-DMRs identified in this study. We analyzed promoter activities of the sequences approximately 1,000 bp upstream (Pro1) and downstream (Pro2) of the TSS, excluding the translation start site, using a hepatocyte cell line, HepG2, by luciferase assay (Fig. 2A). Both Pro1 and Pro2 exhibited promoter activity, with Pro1 showing particularly strong activity in the forward orientation, equivalent to the endogenous OTC locus (Fig. 2B).

When the Pro2 region was located either in the forward or reverse orientation downstream of the Pro1-forward vector, both Pro1 and Pro2 showed significantly weaker promoter activities than the vector containing only the Pro1 region (Fig. 2C). To exclude any effects of the artificial location of Pro2 before TSS, we constructed reporter vectors in which the Pro2 region was located upstream of Pro1. These constructs with Pro2 upstream of Pro1 also exhibited significantly lower promoter activities—regardless of the Pro2 direction—than the Pro1-only vector (Fig. 2D). These results strongly suggest that the Pro1 region functions as the main promoter, whereas the Pro2 region acts as a transcriptional suppressor in hepatocytes.

Next, we introduced DNA methylation in vitro into the OTC promoter sequence (Supplementary Fig. 2). The methylated constructs showed significantly reduced promoter activity (Fig. 2E). The luciferase vector was completely CpG-free; thus, the vector backbone did not influence reporter activity after in vitro treatment with the CpG methylase M.SssI. This finding indicates that the region identified as a promoter is subject to transcriptional repression by DNA methylation.

Skewed XCI has possibly occurred in the liver

After bisulfite conversion, we identified a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) located in region 2, which is also part of region 3 (Fig. 3A). Among the three individual female pigs examined, the two alleles with the SNP were distinguishable in two individuals. We then performed simultaneous DNA methylation and SNP distinction analyses on the liver samples of the two SNP-distinguishable female pigs. In individual 1 (ID: Y279), among all 49 alleles analyzed, 24 were guanine (G) and 25 were adenine (A), showing no significant deviation from a 1:1 distribution (χ2(1) = 0.0204, p = 0.886). In contrast, among the 17 hypo-alleles identified, only 3 were G and the remaining 14 were A, indicating a significant deviation from the expected 1:1 ratio (χ2(1) = 7.12, p = 0.00763). In individual 2 (ID: K63), the overall distribution of 12 G and 22 A alleles among 34 analyzed alleles did not significantly deviate from 1:1 (χ2(1) = 2.94, p = 0.0863). Among the 14 hypo-alleles, all were A (14 A, 0 G), showing a marked deviation from the expected ratio (χ2(1) = 14, p = 0.000183) (Fig. 3B). These results imply that XCI was skewed in the liver of both individuals. Among the two SNP-distinguishable pigs, RT-PCR analysis of SNP-containing 5’UTR indicated individual 1 exhibited skewed mRNA expression of the T allele (sense strand) in positive correlation with the SNP detected in hypo-alleles (antisense A allele) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although individual 2 did not show an obvious correlation of allelic hypomethylation and mRNA expression, skewed expression from one allele was again observed (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Identification of a liver-hypomethylated T-DMR in the upstream region of the human OTC gene, including the TSS

Next, we investigated whether the T-DMR and XCI-affected differentially methylated regions identified in pigs are conserved in humans. Sequence alignment analysis between pig and human OTC promoter regions showed that the OTC promoter sequence of humans was highly conserved with that of pigs, including the porcine OTC T-DMR (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the orthologous genomic region of mouse showed relatively lower sequence homology to human and CpG frequency than that of pig (Supplementary Fig. 4). We then analyzed DNA methylation states in the human OTC promoter exhibiting high sequence homology to the porcine OTC gene (Fig. 4B). In male tissues, hypo-allele rates were 55% and 70% in the liver, where OTC is expressed, and 0% in the skeletal muscle, where OTC is not expressed. This DNA methylation pattern closely resembled that observed in the porcine OTC T-DMR (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

The proportions of hypo-alleles at T-DMRs identified in male pig livers corresponded to previously reported proportions of hepatocytes [14]. In female pig livers, when considering the inactive allele due to XCI, the proportions were approximately consistent with the expected proportion of active alleles in hepatocytes (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that hypo-alleles within the OTC T-DMR can be used to estimate both cell-type-specific DNA methylation and XCI-affected methylation.

Schematic summary of the effects of cell type composition and X chromosome inactivation (XCI) on hypomethylated allele frequency in the OTC promoter

Moreover, by focusing on the proportions of hypo-alleles, we confirmed the presence of tissue-/cell-type-specific DNA methylation together with possible XCI-affected methylation within the same region. This finding strongly suggests that the region identified as a T-DMR is also subject to XCI, at least in the porcine OTC gene. The approach used in this study enabled the simultaneous detection of T-DMR and XCI regulation within the same genomic region and could likewise be used to concurrently analyze similar overlapping regulation in the transcriptional regulatory regions of other X-linked genes.

In the present study, several tens of sequenced clones each were analyzed for cell-type-specific and XCI-affected DNA methylation within the porcine OTC T-DMRs using liver samples of both male and female individuals. Our previous data indicated that a middle range of hypo-allele ratio around 50% can be reproducibly detected by analysis of about 20 sequenced clones [5]. Thus, the number of sequenced clones of pig and human OTC T-DMRs must be sufficient to distinguish hepatocyte-specific DNA hypomethylation together with an influence of XCI including skewed inactivation of one allele. Although human female liver could not be analyzed in the present study due to limited availability of human samples, it must be confirmed whether human female liver also exhibit a reduction in hypo-allele ratio compared with the male liver sample we analyzed in the present study.

Based on the promoter assay results using the HepG2 cell line, the Pro2 region likely contains both promoter and suppressor elements. Furthermore, the construct containing both DNA-methylated Pro1 and Pro2 regions exhibited higher activity than the construct containing only the DNA-methylated Pro1 region. Although HepG2 is a cell line derived from human liver cancer and may not be completely identical to normal hepatocytes, hepatocyte-specific gene expression has been reported to be maintained in HepG2 cells [15]. Thus, our promoter assay data suggest that the suppressive activity of the Pro2 region was also inhibited by DNA methylation.

In the present study, evidence of skewed XCI was obtained from only two female pigs with sufficient SNP information within the porcine OTC T-DMR. Although allele imbalance detected in hypo-alleles and RT-PCR products suggesting skewed XCI was observed in the liver samples of two female individuals, the limited sample size prevents generalization regarding the frequency and biological significance of skewed XCI in the whole pig population. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as an evidence that skewed XCI can occur in porcine tissues, and its generality warrants further validation using a larger sample size of highly informative individuals.

The manifestation of X-linked recessive disorders in heterozygous females may be caused by XCI skewing [16, 17]. In this study, we distinguished between active and inactive X chromosomes using the DNA methylation status of the OTC promoter, and simultaneously identified alleles using an SNP within the same region. This allowed us to confirm skewing of the inactivated allele at the molecular level to a certain extent, providing insights into the molecular mechanism underlying the onset of OTC deficiency in heterozygous females. In female pigs, one of the two alleles more frequently observed among active X alleles, suggesting that cell fate may be partially determined at an early developmental stage of liver cells possibly resulting in XCI skewing.

In addition, comparative sequence alignment between humans and pigs, along with human DNA methylation analysis, suggested that transcriptional regulation mediated by DNA methylation in pigs may also occur in humans. However, the number of CpG sites within the corresponding region is lower in humans than in pigs, highlighting the need for further research to confirm the similarity of this mechanism. Although the positions of CpG dinucleotides in human and pig sequences near the OTC TSS do not align precisely between the two species, the overall regulatory region is highly conserved. Thus, the presence of CpG sequences within the region, rather than specific CpG sites, is likely essential for regulatory function via DNA methylation.

It should be noted that this study did not analyze patients with OTC deficiency, disease-associated OTC mutations, nor clinical phenotypes; thus, it does not directly elucidate the pathogenic mechanism of OTC deficiency in heterozygous females. This study, rather, demonstrated the occurrence of skewed XCI at the porcine OTC locus and identifies regulatory regions potentially involved in epigenetic silencing by DNA methylation. Previous reports have indicated usefulness of porcine model of human genetic diseases. For instance, IL2RG-deficient pigs have been shown to recapitulate the core pathology of human X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency [18], suggesting that the regulatory mechanisms of disease-associated genes may be conserved between pigs and humans. Furthermore, blastocyst complementation studies have established pigs as a valuable experimental platform for organogenesis and regenerative medicine research. In addition, clinical reports of hepatocyte transplantation in OTC deficiency suggest importance of the OTC gene regulation in pigs [19, 20]. Taken together, whereas our findings do not provide direct proof of the pathogenic mechanism of human OTC deficiency, they can provide foundational molecular insights toward understanding OTC regulation and X-linked diseases in humans.

In the present study, molecular evidence of skewed X inactivation through DNA methylation within the OTC T-DMR has been obtained by demonstrating that hypomethylated allele frequency reflects the active X allele in female.

Material and method

Genomic DNA extraction and sodium bisulfite conversion

Tissue samples were frozen and stored at − 80 °C until use. Porcine tissues used in this study were collected from Landrace × Large White × Duroc (LWD) pigs or purchased from Funakoshi Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). All of the animal experiments using pig performed in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Meiji University. Human genomic DNA from male liver (D1234171, lot C404102) and male skeletal muscle (D1234149, lot C510067) was obtained from Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) (Table 1). The pig tissue samples were homogenized and incubated with 300–500 μL of lysis solution [10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 0.2% SDS, and 200 μg/mL proteinase K (TaKaRa) at 55 °C for 30 min. Genomic DNA was purified by successive phenol–chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation, then dissolved in 20 μL of 10 mM Tris-1 mM EDTA (TE) buffer (pH 8.0). Genomic DNA was digested with HindIII (TaKaRa) or BamHI (TaKaRa) and purified by ethanol precipitation. Bisulfite reactions were performed according to the protocols of Frommer et al. [21] or Shiraishi et al. [22], and purified using a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen).

DNA methylation analysis

Bisulfite-treated DNA was amplified with BIOTAQ HS DNA polymerase (NIPPON Genetics) or KOD One PCR master mix (TOYOBO) using specific primers for regions around the OTC TSS. PCR was performed under the following conditions: 43 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, or 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 5 s, and 68 °C for 5 s (Table 2). The PCR products were subcloned into pGEM-T Easy vector (#A1360, Promega) or pCR-BLUNT vector (Invitrogen) for Sanger sequencing.

A minimum of 10 clones were selected, and the plasmids were either isothermally amplified using phi29 DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) or directly amplified using KOD One PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO). Cell culture

HepG2 cells (JCRB1054) were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank. Cells were seeded on type I collagen-coated dishes containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (high glucose, with L-glutamine, phenol red, and sodium pyruvate; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin (both from Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2at 37 °C. Cells were passaged when cultures reached 80–90% confluence. Cells cultured for more than 24 h after passage reaching 60–70% confluence were used for reporter assays.

Luciferase reporter assay

The OTC_pro1 and OTC_pro2 regions (Table 2) were cloned into the CpG-free reporter vector pCpGL [23]. Vectors were constructed using restriction enzymes or Gibson assembly (New England Biolabs). HepG2 cells were transfected with each construct for reporter assays using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen). Reporter assays were performed 48 h after transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega).

In vitro DNA methylation of OTC promoter sequences in reporter vectors

The enzyme reaction mixture (total volume of 50 µL) for in vitro CpG methylation [4 U/µL M.SssI (New England Biolabs) 5 µL, 10 × NEBuffer 2 (New England Biolabs) 5 µL, 32 mM SAM (New England Biolabs) 1 µL, plasmid DNA 10 µL (6–15 μg), H2O 29 µL] was incubated overnight at 37 °C. Plasmid DNA was purified from the reaction mixture by PCI extraction followed by ethanol precipitation and dissolved in 20 µL of H2O, and the complete in vitro CpG methylation was confirmed by digestion with a CpG-methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme, Acc II (Supplementary Fig. 2).

RT-PCR analysis

Pig tissue samples were homogenized, and total RNAs were purified using TRIzol RNA Isolation Reagents (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by isopropanol precipitation. Purified total RNAs were, then, dissolved in DEPC-water. SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen) was used to synthesize cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR was performed using either LA Taq DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa) with 2 × GC Buffer II or KOD One PCR master mix (TOYOBO) with OTC_RT (OTC_RT_F: 5'-ACTAAGCAGGGGCGTTAGATAAG-3', OTC_RT_R: 5'-CCTTCAGCTGCACTTTATTTTGTA-3') and GAPDH [5] primers (the primer sequences were designed based on the porcine reference genome sequence (Sscrofa11.1) available the UCSC Genome Browser). Cycle conditions were either 25–40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min for LA Taq DNA Polymerase or 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 5 s, and 68 °C for 5 s for KOD One PCR master mix. The PCR products for allelic distinction were amplified using KOD One PCR master mix and subcloned into pCR-BLUNT vector (Invitrogen) for Sanger sequencing. Sequencing was performed using an Applied Biosystems 3500xL Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Detection of allele frequencies by SNP analysis

Sequencing of the pig OTC region 2 (Figs. 1A, 3A OTC_bis2r) identified a cytosine/thymine SNP. To enable distinction of the G/A SNP even after bisulfite PCR, DNA methylation analysis of region 2 was performed by bisulfite sequencing using a pair of primers (OTC_bis2r) capable of amplifying the reverse strand (Fig. 3A, Table 2). For allelic detection of expressed mRNA, cDNAs cloned were sequenced, and the SNP of sense strand (C/T) in the sequenced cDNA clones was distinguished.

Alignment of genomic sequences around the OTC TSS in pigs, humans and mice

Sequences from pig T-DMR (regions 1–4) and the human OTC TSS-including region (1,000 bp) were aligned using T-Coffee (https://tcoffee.crg.eu/). In addition, the approximately 1,000-bp porcine genomic region showing high sequence conservation with the human OTC TSS-including region was aligned with the corresponding 1,000-bp regions of the human and mouse OTC genes using T-Coffee. Pairwise sequence identity was calculated using EMBOSS Needle (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/psa/emboss_needle).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were presented as mean values and standard deviations. Statistical significance of two independent experiments was determined using Student’s t-test, where p < 0.05 was defined as significant. A chi-squared test was used to test for goodness of fit.
