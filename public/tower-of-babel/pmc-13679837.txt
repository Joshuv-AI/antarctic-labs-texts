ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer has become one of the major diseases threatening the health of the Chinese population. The incidence of cancer in high‐altitude regions of China is equally concerning. However, due to the unique geographical environment, population, and economic factors in high‐altitude regions, there is an urgent need for a consensus on cancer prevention and management tailored to real‐world clinical practice. To promote and standardize the diagnosis and treatment of cancer in high‐altitude regions, under the leadership of the Breast Cancer Expert Committee of the National Cancer Quality Control Center and the Cancer Prevention and Treatment Committee of the Healthy China Research Center, experts in the field of high‐altitude medicine were convened to jointly formulate the “Expert Consensus on the Standardized Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions of China.” This consensus serves as a reference for clinicians in high‐altitude regions.

Introduction

Cancer has become a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, severely threatening the health of the Chinese population. According to recent cancer statistics published by the National Cancer Center, the number of new malignant tumor cases in China reached approximately 5.15 million in 2024. Over the past decade, both the incidence and mortality patterns of cancer in China have shown notable changes [1].

High‐altitude regions, typically defined as areas above 1500 m, and especially those above 2000 m, can induce significant biological responses in the human body [2]. China, with its vast and varied topography, has several high‐altitude regions, mainly concentrated in the provinces of Xizang, Qinghai, Gansu, Yunnan, and Sichuan.

Cancer incidence in high‐altitude regions is similarly concerning. According to 2022 cancer statistics in China [1], the top five cancers in Xizang Autonomous Region are liver, lung, stomach, esophageal, and breast cancers; in Qinghai Province, stomach, lung, liver, colorectal, and breast cancers; and in Gansu Province, gastric, lung, liver, colorectal, and esophageal cancers. A large‐scale study by Xizang Autonomous Region People's Hospital found the top five in Xizang to be stomach, liver, breast, esophageal, and colorectal cancers. [3] Compared with plain regions, digestive tract tumors are notably more common at high altitude. The most common cancer deaths are gastric, liver, brain, ovarian, and cervical cancers in Xizang, and gastric, lung, liver, esophageal, and colorectal cancers in Qinghai.

Whether high altitude is a risk or protective factor for cancer remains debated. Since the 1970s, studies have linked high altitude to lower leukemia mortality. [4, 5, 6] A 1982 study associated high altitude with lower mortality from tongue, oral, laryngeal, esophageal, and lung cancers [7], and a 2015 epidemiological study linked it to lower lung cancer incidence. [8] However, other research suggests a higher incidence of gastric, liver, and colorectal cancers. [9] One study found that high altitude may protect against some cancers (e.g., lung cancer), increase risk for others (e.g., gastric cancer), and show no association with certain cancers (e.g., cervical cancer). [10]

Cancer diagnosis and treatment in high‐altitude regions face specific challenges: a distinct cancer spectrum with later‐stage diagnosis and low early diagnosis/treatment rates; vast, sparsely populated areas far from central cities, causing transport difficulties, uneven medical resources, and barriers to care; economic and geographical limits causing shortages of equipment, personnel, supplies, and drugs; poor treatment adherence due to cognitive, educational, isolation, and climate factors; altered anticancer drug metabolism and adverse events under hypoxia and low pressure; and scarce high‐altitude‐specific evidence, complicating clinical decisions and forcing the panel to prioritize treatment safety and healthcare accessibility.

High‐altitude regions have unique geography, population, economy, and medical conditions, so current Chinese cancer guidelines do not fully meet local needs. A tailored consensus is urgently needed. To improve and standardize cancer diagnosis and treatment in these areas, high‐altitude medicine experts, led by the Breast Cancer Expert Committee of the National Cancer Quality Control Center and the Cancer Prevention and Treatment Committee of the Healthy China Research Center, developed this consensus. It adapts to high altitude by adjusting treatment intensity, screening strategies, and adverse event management. It also adds ethnic‐specific recommendations, hypoxia‐adaptive treatment regimens, and hierarchical management models for remote areas not covered by existing guidelines.

This consensus was developed through a systematic literature search, with literature screening and evidence synthesis performed in strict accordance with evidence‐based medicine standards. A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and WanFang databases (January 2000 to December 2024) using keywords: “high altitude/plateau,” “cancer/malignant tumor,” “diagnosis,” “treatment,” “screening,” and “adverse event.” A total of 1286 articles were initially identified, and 156 articles were included after screening (inclusion criteria: high‐altitude‐specific data, sample size ≥ 50, peer‐reviewed; exclusion criteria: non‐Chinese high‐altitude regions, duplicate studies).

Hierarchical Diagnosis and Treatment of Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions

High‐altitude regions in China are characterized by complex terrain, inconvenient transportation, and a relatively dispersed population. Therefore, hierarchical diagnosis and treatment are particularly emphasized in the management of cancer. It is crucial to streamline the referral process for cancer and establish standardized screening, comprehensive management, and follow‐up systems. For key county‐level hospitals, especially those already capable of diagnosing and treating cancer, specialized training should be provided to enhance their medical service capabilities. Furthermore, practical, standardized protocols and pathways for cancer prevention and management should be established.

Roles of Hospitals at Different Levels

The roles of hospitals at different levels in the prevention and treatment of cancer in high‐altitude regions are as follows:

County‐Level Hospitals

County‐level hospitals are primarily responsible for conducting health education on cancer, screening, primary diagnosis, adverse event management, patient follow‐up, rehabilitation guidance, and two‐way referrals. For patients with suspected malignant tumors but lacking diagnostic confirmation, county‐level hospitals should initiate referral to a higher‐level facility for further evaluation. Hospitals with sufficient capacity may also undertake surgical and medical treatments, and even participate in clinical trials.

Tertiary Hospitals and Oncology Specialty Institutions

Tertiary hospitals and oncology specialty institutions should actively play a leading role in setting an example, proactively extending high‐quality tumor prevention and treatment resources to local and surrounding areas, and assisting in improving standardized tumor treatment levels. They should also actively construct a mechanism for primary diagnosis at the grassroots level, two‐way referrals, and collaboration between higher‐ and lower‐tier institutions. Specifically, provincial cancer centers should establish clear referral pathways and maintain direct communication channels with county‐level hospitals to facilitate timely patient transfer for complex cases or multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions. Especially, they should fully utilize information platforms to realize hierarchical diagnosis and treatment, thereby providing patients in high‐altitude regions with higher‐quality, more convenient, and advanced healthcare services.

Enhancing Health Literacy of Cancer Among Residents in High‐Altitude Regions

Health literacy refers to an individual's ability to obtain and understand basic health information and services, and to apply this information and these services in making appropriate decisions that maintain and promote personal health. In high‐altitude regions, where ethnic minority populations are concentrated and educational resources vary considerably, residents’ health literacy remains an area requiring improvement. Therefore, this consensus recommends that enhancing cancer health literacy among residents merits particular emphasis compared with plain regions.

The “Chinese Citizens’ Health Literacy—Basic Knowledge and Skills” states that the occurrence of cancer is a process of accumulating risk factors throughout the life cycle, and adopting a healthy lifestyle can effectively prevent the occurrence of cancer. The prevention of cancer is divided into three levels.

Primary Prevention

Also known as etiological prevention, it involves preventing the occurrence of cancer by controlling causes or risk factors, representing the most proactive and fundamental preventive measure. The risk factors for different types of cancer may vary. General recommendations include: (1) The diet of high‐altitude residents is relatively simple, with a high intake of meat. It is recommended to increase the consumption of vegetables and fruits, supplement dietary fiber and vitamins, and limit foods high in sugar and fat. (2) Alcohol consumption is prevalent in high‐altitude regions, and alcoholic liver cirrhosis is a significant risk factor for liver cancer. Therefore, there should be a stronger promotion of healthy lifestyles, including abstaining from alcohol and quitting smoking. (3) Due to the low oxygen levels, residents may engage in limited physical activity; thus, moderate exercise should be encouraged to enhance immunity. However, some high‐risk factors, such as age, family history of tumors, and carcinogenic genetic mutations, cannot be controlled. These factors can be actively addressed through secondary prevention.

Secondary Prevention

Refers to early detection, diagnosis, and treatment, with the goal of identifying cancer and precancerous lesions as early as possible to improve survival. The primary measure for this is early screening.

Tertiary Prevention

Also known as rehabilitation prevention, it involves providing patients diagnosed with cancer with appropriate and effective treatment to reduce the likelihood of tumor recurrence, metastasis, and other complications, thereby extending survival and improving quality of life. The measures for secondary and tertiary prevention are detailed in the screening and treatment sections of this consensus.

Screening for Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions

High‐altitude regions are characterized by cold temperatures, low oxygen levels, and intense ultraviolet radiation, combined with diverse religious and cultural backgrounds, making tumor screening region‐specific. Additionally, the vast and remote nature of these areas, along with poor transportation and healthcare conditions, results in weak awareness of early diagnosis among residents in rural and pastoral areas. Thus, enhancing early screening for cancer in high‐altitude regions is of great significance [11]. In these multi‐ethnic areas, ethnic differences are also associated with distinct disease patterns [11, 12]. Gastric cancer and liver cancer are among the top three cancers in Xizang, Qinghai, and Gansu, severely threatening the health of high‐altitude populations [1]. Below, we will take gastric and liver cancer screening as examples. Early screening for cancer in high‐altitude areas must balance effectiveness and cost, and emphasize the coordination between primary and higher‐level hospitals to improve early cancer diagnosis.

Gastric Cancer Screening

Residents in high‐altitude areas tend to consume dry, high‐salt foods, and those in pastoral regions predominantly consume meat, especially dried beef and lamb, with a low intake of vegetables and fruits, which may contribute to the high incidence of gastrointestinal tumors [13, 14, 15]. The likelihood of Helicobacter pylori infection is higher among high‐altitude residents [16], increasing the risk of gastric cancer. Some studies suggest that the CYP2E1 gene 5’‐end RsaI site allele is a susceptibility gene for gastric cancer in the Hui ethnic group of Qinghai [17].

Gastric cancer screening can use imaging techniques such as endoscopy, computed tomography (CT), and X‐ray, with endoscopy being the gold standard. Screening guidelines recommend prioritizing high‐risk populations for endoscopic examination [18] (Grade A). However, due to the vast and sparsely populated nature of high‐altitude areas, many counties and primary healthcare facilities lack resources. Consideration could be given to initial screening using Helicobacter pylori testing, urea breath tests, and some biomarkers (such as PG, G‐17, MG7‐Ag, and Hp‐Ag combined), with referral to facilities equipped for endoscopic examination if necessary [19] (Grade B).

Liver Cancer Screening

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is the primary cause of the high incidence of liver cancer in Qinghai and Xizang, which may be related to factors such as high family clustering, low mother‐to‐child interruption rates, low vaccination rates in rural areas, predominance of HBV genotype C, low rates of symptomatic medical consultation, and HBV combined with fatty liver [20].

Individuals meeting any of the following criteria are defined as high‐risk for liver cancer [21]: (1) Patients with liver cirrhosis caused by various reasons, including alcoholic liver disease, metabolic dysfunction‐ associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD); (2) Chronic infection with HBV or hepatitis C virus (HCV), and age ≥ 40 years. For high‐risk individuals, it is recommended to use ultrasound combined with serum alpha‐fetoprotein for screening, at least every 6 months [22] (Grade A). Emphasis should also be placed on using liver cancer risk prediction scores as effective tools and developing integrated community and hospital‐based precision screening models [23] (Grade B).

General Principles of Cancer Screening in High‐Altitude Regions

Overall, screening for cancer in high‐altitude areas should focus on promoting screening methods that are easy to train, implement, and operate, in addition to conventional techniques. For example, clinical breast exams can be used for breast cancer screening in women, with positive cases undergoing further breast ultrasound or mammography [24] (Grade B). Additionally, incorporating remote imaging, mobile screening units, and artificial intelligence to optimize human and material resources can support screening efforts in primary and remote counties and help establish a four‐level cancer prevention and control network (national‐province‐city‐county) to better safeguard the health and well‐being of high‐altitude residents.

Diagnosis of Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions

For the diagnosis of malignant tumors in high‐altitude areas, priority should be given to ensuring accuracy, standardization, and quality control. For key county‐level hospitals that already have the capacity to diagnose and treat malignant tumors, targeted training programs related to tumor diagnosis should be actively implemented, along with regular quality supervision and control, to ensure that the relevant medical personnel are proficient in standardized diagnostic procedures and norms, such as tumor risk factors, interpretation of tumor markers, endoscopic techniques, and integration and interpretation of imaging data. If relevant examination equipment is lacking or the diagnosis is difficult, remote consultation can be sought, or patients can be referred to higher‐level hospitals. The pathology department is crucial for the diagnosis of malignant tumors. In cases where slides are difficult to interpret or relevant diagnostic marker technologies are unavailable, tumor samples can be referred to the pathology department of a hospital with diagnostic capabilities or to a certified third‐party testing institution. Remote consultation can also be conducted to obtain accurate histopathological results and molecular characteristics to guide treatment. At the same time, attention should be paid to the importance of multidisciplinary diagnosis, including radiology, pathology, medical oncology, surgery, and radiation oncology. It is recommended that MDT discussions be completed at the local hospital, a higher‐level hospital, or via remote consultation before initial treatment to ensure the standardization of tumor diagnosis and individualization of treatment.

Treatment of Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions

Surgical Treatment

Overall Late‐Stage Presentation at Diagnosis

Many patients have undergone preoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which may affect their general condition and reduce surgical tolerance. Emphasis should be placed on preventing and promptly addressing the side effects of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy, aiming to avoid grade 3–4 adverse events. Nutritional support should be strengthened to improve patient tolerance.

Increased Difficulty in R0 Resection Due to Post‐Neoadjuvant Therapy Edema and Fibrosis

For patients after neoadjuvant radiotherapy, surgery should be considered only after the edema period has passed. For instance, patients with low rectal cancer undergoing long‐course neoadjuvant radiotherapy should wait 8–12 weeks [25] before proceeding with rectal cancer surgery (Grade A). Preoperative assessments, including ultrasound, CT, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), should be arranged to more accurately evaluate the extent and degree of tissue edema and fibrosis. Preoperative correction of hypoalbuminemia should be performed, and principles of complete tumor resection and lymph node dissection should be followed during surgery.

Potential Intraoperative Change in Surgical Approach

Advanced disease stages may necessitate changes in surgical plans, such as debulking surgery, palliative surgery, combined organ resections, or inoperability. Preoperative multidisciplinary consultations should be conducted to evaluate and develop surgical plans and determine the extent of resection. During surgery, careful exploration of the tumor and surrounding tissues should define the final resection range. If extensive resection is required, particularly with combined organ resections, consultation with relevant experts should be sought to weigh the pros and cons and develop individualized treatment plans, aiming to maximize tumor removal while preserving normal tissue functions.

High Incidence of Comorbidities

High Incidence of Thrombosis

The special environment of high‐altitude areas, including low oxygen, low pressure, and cold temperatures, increases the risk of thrombosis. The tendency toward thrombosis in populations living at an altitude of 2000 m is more than twice that in low‐altitude populations, and the risk for those above 3000 m is 30 times higher [26]. Cancer itself is also a risk factor for thrombosis.

It is recommended to routinely complete a thrombosis risk assessment form upon admission and implement corresponding measures based on the results, with anticoagulant intervention if necessary (Grade A). Preoperative oxygen therapy, wearing anti‐thrombosis compression stockings during surgery, completing the thrombosis risk assessment form again after surgery, encouraging early ambulation, and using lower limb compression massage devices for prevention are advised. Closely monitor patient symptoms and D‑dimer levels, and perform venous and arterial duplex ultrasound of the lower limbs if needed to detect thrombosis early and manage it promptly.

High Incidence of Hypertension and Cor Pulmonale

The incidence of hypertension and cor pulmonale is high in high‐altitude areas, which increases surgical risks and reduces patient tolerance, thereby raising surgery‐related mortality [27]. Adjustments to the patient's blood pressure and cardiopulmonary function around the time of surgery are recommended to ensure smooth surgery and rapid postoperative recovery (Grade B). Patients from remote areas may have long‐standing undiagnosed hypertension, so preoperative history taking and blood pressure monitoring, including 24–hour monitoring if necessary, are important. Continue antihypertensive medications preoperatively to avoid withdrawal syndrome, monitor blood pressure continuously during surgery, maintain stable blood pressure, and select appropriate antihypertensive medications and pain management postoperatively. For patients with poor cardiopulmonary function, a comprehensive evaluation including ECG, cardiac ultrasound, and pulmonary function tests should be performed preoperatively. Intermittent low‐flow oxygen therapy should be administered preoperatively, and patients should be instructed on coughing, expectoration, and breathing exercises to improve lung function. Smokers should be advised to quit smoking 2–4 weeks before surgery, and those with impaired cardiopulmonary function should receive appropriate supportive care, with careful control of fluid volume and infusion rates perioperatively.

High Prevalence of Poor Nutritional Status

Dietary habits in high‐altitude areas are relatively monotonous, with high meat consumption and low intake of fish, legumes, and vegetables, which can lead to inadequate nutritional intake, particularly in patients undergoing preoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Cancer itself can cause significant metabolic demand, and digestive system cancers are particularly prone to malnutrition. Postoperative malnutrition severely impacts recovery and subsequent adjuvant treatments. A balanced diet rich in easily digestible, high‐quality protein and vitamins should be recommended. Small, frequent meals can reduce gastrointestinal burden and improve absorption. For patients with gastrointestinal obstruction, enteral nutrition tubes may be necessary. For those with inadequate dietary intake or high nutritional needs, oral nutritional supplements (e.g., whey protein, multivitamins) may be used under medical guidance to meet nutrient and calorie requirements.

Establishing a Rational Referral System

County hospitals in high‐altitude areas should perform surgeries within their capability. If a procedure is beyond their capacity and the patient's condition allows, patients can be referred to higher‐level hospitals. Due to the vast and sparsely populated nature of high‐altitude areas, some emergency surgeries can be performed at county hospitals, such as simple gastric perforation repair or bowel stoma creation. These approaches help alleviate critical conditions and gain time for patient referral to higher‐level hospitals.

Establishing a robust support system is recommended, with senior doctors from tertiary hospitals providing training for county hospital staff in less complex tumor surgeries and related emergency procedures, such as biopsy techniques and complete resection of benign tumors. County hospital staff should regularly attend training at higher‐level hospitals to learn surgical techniques and perioperative patient management, progressively standardizing tumor resection surgeries under expert guidance.

A comprehensive remote consultation system should be established, covering surgical indications, methods, complication management, and postoperative care. Developing an emergency remote consultation system during surgery is also necessary to leverage the internet to address the uneven development of medical technology and transportation challenges in high‐altitude areas.

Perioperative Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Perioperative monitoring should include routine assessment of oxygen saturation with appropriate oxygen supplementation to maintain SpO2 ≥ 90% [2]. Thromboprophylaxis measures should be implemented according to risk stratification [26]. For patients with pre‐existing cardiopulmonary conditions, comprehensive preoperative evaluation, including electrocardiography, echocardiography, and pulmonary function testing, is recommended.

Principles for Chemotherapy in High‐Altitude Regions

The basic principles of chemotherapy in high‐altitude regions generally follow major national and international cancer treatment guidelines. However, when selecting chemotherapy regimens in these areas, it is essential to consider the unique environmental conditions and the patient's physiological state, including altitude‐related reactions, circulatory function, immune system status, and oxygenation levels. The following are general principles for chemotherapy in high‐altitude regions:

1.Develop individualized chemotherapy regimens based on the specific conditions of the patient, including the type of drugs, dosage, administration frequency, and route. Dosage adjustments and fractionated administration may be necessary.2.The low‐oxygen environment may potentiate the toxicity of chemotherapy drugs. For example, reduced oxygen supply to myocardial, pulmonary, and renal tissues may increase the toxic effects on these organs [39]. The metabolic process of drugs in the liver may be impaired, further damaging liver function [28]. Therefore, close monitoring of physiological parameters during chemotherapy is required (Grade A).3.The low‐oxygen environment may increase the cardiovascular burden, necessitating regular assessment of cardiopulmonary function to detect and manage potential complications (Grade A).4.Since immune function may be compromised in high‐altitude environments, patients are more prone to infections, requiring effective preventive and control measures (e.g., prophylactic antibiotics for neutropenic fever) (Grade B).5.Emphasize rehabilitation training, including moderate exercise and breathing exercises, to improve pulmonary ventilation and alleviate hypoxia symptoms (Grade B).6.Given the altered drug metabolism and increased toxicity risks associated with high‐altitude hypoxia [28, 39], chemotherapy may be administered with individualized dose adjustments based on patient tolerance and laboratory parameters. Close monitoring of hematological, cardiac, and hepatic function is recommended throughout treatment (Grade A).

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is a crucial treatment modality for cancer. However, only 20%–40% of patients with cancer in China receive radiotherapy, and the distribution of radiotherapy resources in high‐altitude areas is uneven, with an overall lower radiotherapy rate [40, 41]. Provincial radiotherapy centers possess advanced radiotherapy equipment comparable to top national facilities and routinely perform advanced techniques, such as three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy, intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), image‐guided radiotherapy (IGRT), volumetric‐modulated arc therapy (VMAT), stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), and total body irradiation (TBI). These centers also employ surface tracking, 4D‐CT, respiratory gating, and abdominal compression techniques to improve radiotherapy quality, achieving a level of technical proficiency consistent with national averages. However, lower‐level facilities, including district, county, township, and pastoral areas, often lack adequate resources and knowledge regarding cancer diagnosis and radiotherapy. It is recommended to establish robust referral mechanisms and disseminate radiotherapy knowledge within the region, ensuring that patients with cancer receive timely and standardized radiotherapy as part of comprehensive treatment. Given the differences between high‐altitude and low‐altitude areas in terms of cancer etiology, epidemiology, comorbidities, radiotherapy toxicity, and prognosis, this consensus summarizes the following radiotherapy‐related considerations.

Combined Radiotherapy With Chemotherapy, Targeted Therapy, and Immunotherapy

Combining radiotherapy with other cancer treatments may have synergistic effects, but it is unclear whether it also increases toxicity [44, 45]. Whether combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy or immunotherapy results in more severe cardiopulmonary injury remains to be determined. Combining radiotherapy with targeted therapy, particularly when radiotherapy is administered at the peak efficacy of targeted therapy, may reduce radiation‐induced damage by shrinking the target area, thereby enhancing the synergistic effect [46]. Therefore, in clinical practice involving combination therapy, careful monitoring of adverse reactions is necessary to prevent severe side effects, and further conclusions require additional clinical data (Grade B).

Management strategies for immune‐related adverse event (irAE): (1) Weekly monitoring of respiratory symptoms and thyroid function during treatment; (2) Early use of corticosteroids (1–2 mg/kg/day) for grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis; (3) Withhold immunotherapy for grade ≥ 3 irAEs until symptoms improve, with permanent discontinuation when indicated (Grade B).

However, individualized treatment plans should be formulated based on the radiotherapy site, whether combination therapy is used, and whether the patient has high‐altitude‐related comorbidities, to minimize radiotherapy‐related side effects such as cardiopulmonary injury and to achieve better therapeutic outcomes.

Palliative Care for Cancer in High‐Altitude Regions

Palliative care for cancer in high‐altitude regions requires integrating environmental characteristics (hypoxia, cold, dryness) and ethnic cultural backgrounds to prioritize symptom control and quality of life improvement.

Principles of Palliative Care

(1) Multidisciplinary Team Collaboration: Palliative care requires the involvement of specialists in oncology, respiratory medicine, cardiology, anesthesiology, psychology, and ethnic medicine. For ethnic minority patients, consideration should be given to involving healthcare staff proficient in the relevant ethnic minority languages to ensure accurate and effective communication. (2) Individualized Treatment Plans: Tailor measures based on hypoxia tolerance, comorbidities (e.g., cor pulmonale), and ethnic customs (e.g., Hui patients avoiding alcohol‐containing medications). (3) Symptom Control: Prioritize hypoxia relief, pain management, and infection prevention, which are more prominent in high‐altitude regions.

High‐Altitude‐Specific Palliative Care Measures

(1) Hypoxia and respiratory support: In high‑altitude areas, the low oxygen content makes patients prone to hypoxic symptoms. For patients experiencing dyspnea, additional oxygen support should be provided. Regularly monitor blood oxygen saturation to ensure adequate oxygenation. (2) Cardiovascular support: The high‑altitude environment can affect the cardiovascular system [39]. For patients with cardiovascular issues, closely monitor blood pressure, heart rate, and electrocardiogram, and adjust the treatment plan promptly based on the situation. Pharmacological support may be given if necessary. (3) Analgesic drug selection: Pain management is an important part of palliative care. The high‑altitude environment may affect drug metabolism, so this factor should be taken into account when selecting and adjusting analgesic drugs [35, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52]. (4) Non‑pharmacological therapies: In addition to pharmacological treatment, non‑pharmacological therapies should also be considered in high‑altitude areas to improve patients’ symptoms and quality of life, including psychological support, physical therapy, nutritional support, and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) treatment. (5) Diet and water‑electrolyte balance: In high‑altitude environments, decreased appetite and increased fluid loss are common issues, so patients should be encouraged to drink plenty of water, consume electrolyte‑containing beverages, and receive nutritional support as needed. (6) Monitoring of hemoglobin and hematocrit: In high‑altitude environments, hematocrit and hemoglobin levels may change [29]; therefore, regular monitoring is required, especially for patients with anemia or a bleeding tendency. (7) Infection prevention: Due to low temperatures and thin air in high‑altitude areas, respiratory infections are relatively common. For tumor patients, especially those with compromised immune function, infection prevention measures should be strengthened, and prophylactic antibiotics may be used when necessary.

Nursing Care for Cancer Patients in High‐Altitude Areas

Hypoxia Management in Nursing

In high‐altitude environments, patients commonly experience symptoms, such as shortness of breath, rapid breathing, dyspnea, and headache. Nurses should comprehensively assess patients’ blood oxygen saturation and symptoms and promptly provide oxygen supplementation and respiratory support to alleviate hypoxic symptoms. Patients are encouraged to take a comfortable position (such as semi‐Fowler's position or lateral position) to improve respiratory function. Vital signs should be monitored regularly, nursing measures adjusted in a timely manner, and emotional support provided to help patients cope with anxiety. Patients and their families should be educated to identify symptoms and respond appropriately to promote adaptation to the high‐altitude environment.

Deep Vein Thrombosis Prevention and Management in Nursing

The high‐altitude environment increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis. Nurses need to assess the thrombosis risk of each patient and develop personalized management strategies using assessment scales. Patients and their families should be educated on the symptoms of thrombosis and the importance of early intervention. Patients are encouraged to engage in appropriate activities, avoid prolonged bed rest, and maintain adequate dietary intake. For high‐risk patients, individualized anticoagulant therapy and physical prevention measures (such as elastic stockings, air pressure therapy devices) should be considered. Close observation during nursing is required to promptly address thrombosis risks.

Nutritional Management in Nursing

The key to nutritional care is to conduct a comprehensive nutritional assessment of patients, develop a nutritional management plan and targeted nutritional supplement programs, strengthen fluid management to prevent dehydration, and provide nutritional education and regular nutritional monitoring and intervention to ensure that patients receive adequate nutritional support.

Nuring Care for Other Malignant Tumor Symptoms

Pain Nursing

Malignant tumor patients often experience various types of pain. Individualized assessment is crucial and should combine effective assessment tools and take into account the patient's physical, psychological, and social factors. Medication management should be adjusted according to the doctor's advice and the patient's condition, with correct administration of analgesics in appropriate doses and combinations. Nursing techniques, such as patient‐controlled analgesia, nerve block, and psychological support, also play a key role. Multidisciplinary team collaboration and patient education can improve the effectiveness of pain treatment and the patient's quality of life.

Nausea and Vomiting Nursing

For nausea and vomiting caused by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgical anesthesia, individualized assessment and comprehensive nursing plans are essential. Nurses should assess the patient's symptoms and psychological status to develop targeted nursing strategies. Medication should be individually adjusted according to current evidence and the physician's advice. Nutritional support, acupuncture, massage, music therapy, etc., can effectively relieve symptoms; psychological support and education help patients cope with psychological pressure and improve their self‐management ability. A multidisciplinary team, including doctors, nurses, dietitians, and psychologists, jointly develops and implements comprehensive treatment plans to ensure patients receive comprehensive support.

Fatigue Nursing

Cancer‐related fatigue is a common symptom involving physical, emotional, and cognitive fatigue in patients with cancer, which usually cannot be relieved by rest alone. Nurses should conduct a comprehensive assessment of patients using accurate assessment tools and provide health education and consultation. Patients are advised to engage in appropriate physical activities (such as walking, jogging, cycling, Baduanjin, Tai Chi, etc.), and psychological and social support should be provided to improve sleep quality and alleviate fatigue.

Radiation Dermatitis Nursing

Radiotherapy can cause skin reactions. Nurses should educate patients on skin care: keep the skin clean and dry, avoid friction and irritation, clean the skin with warm water, neutral soap [53], and use specific ointments or gels containing moisturizing, anti‐inflammatory, or analgesic ingredients (Grade B). Alcohol‐ and fragrance‐containing skin care products should be avoided, and sun protection should be paid attention to. The patient's skin reactions should be monitored in a timely manner, and nursing measures adjusted accordingly.

Radiation‐ and Chemotherapy‐Induced Oral Mucositis Nursing

Radiation‐ and chemotherapy‐induced oral mucositis seriously affects the patient's quality of life. Nursing should include individualized assessment to identify high‐risk groups (such as patients receiving head and neck radiotherapy). Corresponding measures should be formulated: encourage gentle gargling, cold therapy (cold therapy is not recommended for patients receiving oxaliplatin treatment) [54], choose soft foods, and avoid irritating and acidic foods (Grade B). Medication treatment includes local anesthetics, oral antifungals, and local steroids. Attention should be paid to nutritional and fluid supplementation, standardized treatment, and anti‐infection nursing.

Cancerous Wound Nursing

Cancerous wounds are often accompanied by pain, increased infection risk, and delayed healing. The focus of nursing is to comprehensively assess the wound site, appearance, exudate, odor, pain, and the condition of the surrounding skin. Management strategies include effective control of exudate, selection of appropriate dressings to reduce odor, maintenance of ward ventilation, pain management to ensure patient comfort, and special attention to skin protection and promotion of wound healing to improve the patient's quality of life.

Management of Specific Adverse Reactions in Cancer Treatment in High‐Altitude Regions

The most prominent environmental characteristic of high‐altitude regions is hypoxia, with atmospheric oxygen partial pressure at certain high altitudes being only 60% of that at sea level. Hypoxia can alter the pharmacokinetic properties of drugs, thereby affecting drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. In addition, the characteristics of high‐altitude environments include, but are not limited to, hypobaric hypoxia, low temperature, high solar radiation, and arid climate; all these factors can adversely affect normal physiological functions. Studies have shown that in a high‐altitude hypoxic environment, the metabolism of some drugs is reduced, while the mean residence time, half‐life, and area under the curve are increased, with a decrease in clearance rate. It is generally believed that high‐altitude hypoxia reduces the activity and expression of CYP1A1, CYP1A2, CYP2E1, and CYP3A1, while increasing the activity and expression of CYP3A6 and CYP2D1 in animal models. Research indicates that hypoxia affects the metabolism of various drugs and significantly prolongs the half‐life of most agents such as prednisolone. Prolonged half‐life of anti‐tumor drugs will markedly increase adverse reactions in patients. Therefore, patients receiving pharmacotherapy in high‐altitude regions should be closely monitored, and dosage adjustments should be made for certain metabolized drugs [50] (Grade A).

Currently, there are studies exploring the incidence of adverse reactions related to anti‐tumor treatment in high‐altitude regions. For example, one study conducted mainly in Qinghai Province showed that among patients with breast cancer receiving highly emetogenic regimens, the incidence of post‐chemotherapy vomiting was approximately 25%, while the incidence of post‐chemotherapy nausea was over 85% [51]. Another small‐sample retrospective study analyzed post‐chemotherapy myelosuppression in patients with breast cancer in Qinghai and plain areas, and the results showed that the incidence of grade 3 or higher neutropenia in high‐altitude regions was higher than that in plain areas [52]. Additionally, one prospective study indicated that the incidence of cardiotoxicity in patients receiving anti‐HER2 targeted therapy in high‐altitude regions was consistent with that reported in previous literature (data unpublished). However, there is still a lack of more studies on adverse reactions to anti‐tumor treatment in high‐altitude regions.

Based on the above studies, antitumor drug treatment in high‐altitude regions should fully consider the potential for more severe adverse reactions. When formulating treatment plans, this consensus does not recommend routine drug dose reduction, but full consideration should be given to individual patient characteristics to provide individualized treatment (Grade A). In particular, close monitoring of relevant clinical indicators of patients during treatment is required, such as patient symptoms, laboratory indicators, and relevant auxiliary examination indicators (Grade A). Patients in high‐altitude regions may require more frequent dose reductions and corresponding supportive therapy (Grade B). It is important to note that the use of other drugs to treat adverse reactions may also increase corresponding adverse reactions. Appropriate non‐pharmacological treatment measures can be used. For example, recent studies have shown that electroacupuncture has a prominent effect in the treatment of post‐chemotherapy nausea in high‐altitude regions, with mild adverse reactions, and can be recommended for the treatment of post‐chemotherapy vomiting [51] (Grade B). A small‐sample study showed that acupuncture may be effective in relieving post‐chemotherapy fatigue in high‐altitude regions [55] (Grade B). In addition, acupuncture has good effects in the treatment of peripheral neurotoxicity, bone and joint pain caused by aromatase inhibitors, etc. In clinical practice, non‐pharmacological treatment methods can be adopted according to actual conditions to alleviate adverse reactions (Grade B).

Follow‐Up of Cancer Patients in High‐Altitude Regions

The purpose of follow‐up for patients with cancer is to understand their survival status, evaluate whether the disease has recurred or metastasized, as well as to assess patients’ adherence to adjuvant therapy and adverse reactions, to implement corresponding clinical interventions, promote better rehabilitation, and improve prognosis. Cancer rehabilitation encompasses the recovery of physiological functions, psychological adjustment, and restoration of social functioning.

Follow‐Up Frequency and Content

Follow‐up frequency and items are recommended to be conducted in accordance with the guidelines and consensus for each cancer type. For instance, digestive function recovery should be evaluated for patients with gastrointestinal tumors, pulmonary function recovery for those with lung cancer, and upper limb function recovery and edema on the affected side for patients with breast cancer. Patients should seek medical attention promptly if any discomfort occurs, without being restricted by fixed schedules or fixed examination items.

Risk Assessment of Comorbidities

With prolonged survival of patients with cancer, a variety of comorbidities and treatment‐related adverse reactions may emerge during therapy. Therefore, beyond focusing on the epidemiological characteristics and standardized diagnosis and treatment of cancer itself, attention should also be paid to patients’ respiratory health, cardiovascular health, bone health, mental health, and fertility needs. In particular, patients with cancer living in harsh high‐altitude environments, constrained by limited financial resources and inadequate medical conditions, should prioritize a comprehensive, full‐cycle health management model.

Follow‐Up Methods

High‐altitude regions are mostly located in central and western China, where primary medical services are relatively underdeveloped, supporting systems and policies are imperfect, and the hierarchical diagnosis and treatment policy cannot be fully implemented. Internet‐based clinics eliminate temporal and geographical barriers, enabling patients with cancer to access timely medical care more easily. It is recommended that cancer treatment centers in high‐altitude areas establish well‐developed internet‐based clinics, enhance follow‐up management for patients with cancer through internet clinics, WeChat platforms, and other channels, and promote other smart medical approaches for intelligent patient management, as detailed in the following section. For patients residing in remote pastoral areas without reliable internet access, follow‐up can be conducted via regular telephone calls coordinated through township health centers, with visit frequency adjusted based on individual risk stratification.

Smart Healthcare for Cancer Patients in High‐Altitude Regions

High‐altitude regions are characterized by extensive territories, inconvenient transportation, and uneven distribution of medical resources, making it difficult for residents in remote areas to access timely and high‐quality medical services. Moreover, post‐treatment follow‐up and rehabilitation are also challenging to manage. Therefore, full use should be made of advances in information technology to promote smart healthcare, facilitate the downward deployment of high‐quality medical resources, and achieve effective patient management. Specifically, this can include the following aspects.

Remote Consultation

Allowing specialists to conduct consultations anytime and anywhere, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and guidance efficiency. High‐definition, low‐latency remote ultrasound systems enable distant specialists to manipulate robotic arms to perform ultrasound examinations on patients in remote areas. This helps realize cross‐regional and cross‐hospital professional guidance and quality control, enhancing the capacity of primary medical services.

Remote Education and Training

Improvement of medical proficiency is inseparable from medical education. The advantages of smart data platforms can be fully utilized to provide remote medical education and training to healthcare workers in high‐altitude regions. Traditional audio and video conferencing systems can be used for case discussions, teaching ward rounds, and other training activities. Scenario‐based teaching platforms can be connected to corresponding medical equipment for demonstrations, such as procedures in cardiac catheterization labs and neurosurgical operations. AR/VR‐based virtual teaching platforms can integrate 3D digital models for educational training. 5G‐enabled surgical demonstration systems can enhance the case experience and practical skills of medical staff through surgical image capture, live broadcast, real‐time guidance, and applications on mobile devices such as smartphones.

AI‐Assisted Healthcare

Based on imaging data and combined with big data and artificial intelligence modeling and analysis, AI‐assisted diagnostic applications can be developed. Intelligent clinical decision support systems can provide evidence‐based support for physicians, thereby improving the efficiency and quality of medical care in high‐altitude areas.

Smart Patient Management

In high‐altitude regions, factors such as geographical constraints, poor transportation, uneven medical resource distribution, and suboptimal health literacy pose challenges to patients’ ability to take medications regularly, attend follow‐up visits, and manage adverse reactions. A study published in The Lancet explored the effectiveness of electronic pillboxes in improving tuberculosis treatment outcomes in Xizang. It found that an electronic pillbox system that reminds patients to take medication via SMS and mobile applications while recording adherence increased medication compliance by 29.2% (p < 0.0001) and treatment success rate by 21% (p < 0.0001) [56]. Meta‐analyses have also shown that proactive reminder interventions for patients with breast cancer, especially those receiving adjuvant endocrine therapy in early stages, help improve adherence [57]. Wearable smart devices or mobile smart devices such as smartphones can remind patients to attend follow‐up visits on time, take medications as scheduled, and report adverse reactions.

In addition, high‐altitude regions are often home to concentrated ethnic minority populations where multiple languages are used. When implementing smart patient management, it is recommended to develop multilingual follow‐up systems supporting Chinese, Tibetan, and other languages. It is advisable to involve ethnic minority medical professionals in follow‐up care and flexibly use voice or graphical interfaces to improve the accuracy of follow‐up and develop rehabilitation pathways consistent with ethnic customs.

Conclusion

High‐altitude regions have unique characteristics in terms of cancer incidence patterns, early detection and screening, diagnosis, drug accessibility, and follow‐up management. In cancer prevention and treatment, a tiered diagnosis and treatment system should be improved by establishing standardized protocols and pathways through targeted support, two‐way referrals, and coordinated linkages between different levels of healthcare facilities. Additionally, given the specific features of high‐altitude regions, efforts should be made to actively promote health literacy among residents, implement tailored prevention and management of adverse reactions, and fully utilize information technology and platforms to enhance healthcare quality and enable smarter, more convenient patient management—ultimately aiming to achieve high‐quality, long‐term survival for patients with cancer in high‐altitude regions.

Limitations of This Consensus

Several limitations should be acknowledged when applying this consensus in clinical practice: (1) Quality of evidence: High‐quality clinical trials (particularly randomized controlled trials) conducted specifically in high‐altitude populations are scarce. Many recommendations are therefore based on moderate‐ or low‐level evidence, supplemented by expert clinical experience. (2) Geographic coverage: Although the consensus incorporates data and experiences from Xizang, Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan, its applicability to other high‐altitude regions in China (e.g., parts of Xinjiang) requires further validation. (3) Ethnic diversity: While ethnic medicine advisors for Tibetan and Hui populations were consulted, the consensus does not fully capture the cultural and linguistic diversity of all ethnic minority groups in high‐altitude regions. (4) Need for regular updates: As new evidence emerges and healthcare conditions in high‐altitude regions improve, this consensus should be updated regularly (recommended interval: 3–5 years).

Clinicians are advised to apply these recommendations in conjunction with individual patient circumstances, local resource availability, and the most current research evidence.

Future Research Directions

The consensus development process identified the following critical knowledge gaps warranting future investigation: (1) Pharmacokinetics and toxicity registries: Systematic pharmacokinetic studies of commonly used anticancer agents in high‑altitude populations are needed to establish altitude‑specific dosing guidelines, alongside prospective registries to characterize treatment‑related adverse events across all treatment modalities. (2) Altitude‑specific treatment optimization: Clinical research is required to determine whether surgical techniques, radiotherapy protocols, and rehabilitation programs should be modified for high‑altitude patients to improve safety and efficacy. (3) Cost‑effectiveness of screening: Economic evaluations of screening strategies in remote high‑altitude settings are needed to guide resource allocation and improve early detection rates. (4) Smart healthcare interventions: High‑quality studies are required to evaluate whether remote consultation, AI‑assisted diagnosis, and digital adherence tools can improve treatment outcomes and quality of life in high‑altitude patients. (5) Integrative medicine: Clinical studies exploring the integration of traditional Tibetan or Hui medicine with conventional cancer therapies are encouraged, particularly for symptom management and supportive care.
