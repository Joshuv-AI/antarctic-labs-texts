First Latin American Experience With a Hybrid Dissection Stent in Type A Aortic Dissection
Authors: Helen S. Choquehuanca-Bedoya, Johan M. Fernández-Quiroz, Ciro Barrantes-Alarcón, Luis A. Tsuha-Alvarado, Rossi E. Ramos-Isidro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency requiring immediate surgical repair. Hybrid strategies using the Ascyrus Medical Dissection Stent (AMDS) have emerged as adjunctive techniques to improve distal aortic remodeling.Case SummaryA 63-year-old woman with uncontrolled hypertension presented with sudden chest pain and dyspnea. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated ATAAD extending from the aortic root to the aortic bifurcation, associated with severe aortic regurgitation. The patient underwent hybrid repair with a Bentall procedure combined with AMDS implantation for distal aortic stabilization. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, without neurological complications or reintervention. Follow-up imaging demonstrated false lumen obliteration, preserved visceral perfusion, and favorable aortic remodeling.DiscussionThis case highlights the feasibility and safety of AMDS as an adjunctive strategy during hybrid ATAAD repair, potentially enhancing distal aortic remodeling without increasing surgical complexity.Take-Home MessageAMDS may improve distal aortic remodeling during hybrid repair of ATAAD while maintaining procedural safety and reducing the need for additional interventions.

History of Presentation

A 63-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset severe chest pain associated with resting dyspnea. On admission, she was hypertensive, with blood pressure values reaching 198/80 mm Hg. Physical examination demonstrated symmetric peripheral pulses without focal neurological deficits. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm without ischemic changes.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of poorly controlled arterial hypertension. No previous cardiovascular surgery or connective tissue disorders were reported.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, hypertensive emergency, acute aortic syndrome, and severe valvular heart disease.

Investigations

Computed tomography angiography revealed an acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) extending from the aortic root to the aortic bifurcation, with the primary entry tear located at the noncoronary sinus (Figure 1). Management

Intravenous antihypertensive therapy was initiated, followed by emergency surgical repair through median sternotomy. Arterial cannulation was established via the right axillary artery using a 10-mm Dacron graft, with venous cannulation in the right atrium. Myocardial protection was provided with cold crystalloid cardioplegia administered through both antegrade and retrograde routes. Following longitudinal aortotomy, the native aortic valve leaflets were excised and the coronary buttons were prepared. Intraoperatively, the primary entry tear was identified at the level of the noncoronary sinus without involvement of the coronary ostia. The aortic root demonstrated aneurysmal dilatation associated with severe valvular compromise; therefore, a Bentall-De Bono procedure was performed.

The brachiocephalic trunk, renal arteries, and visceral vessels originated from the true lumen. A 24-mm GELWEAVE Valsalva valved Dacron graft with a size 23 prosthetic valve was used. Valvular sutures were secured and the coronary ostia were reimplanted before initiation of circulatory arrest. Cerebral neuromonitoring using near-infrared spectroscopy was maintained throughout the procedure. After completion of the proximal repair and achievement of the target temperature, moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest (28 °C) was initiated, maintaining selective antegrade cerebral perfusion through the axillary cannulation at flows of 7.5 to 10 mL/kg/min. A transverse aortotomy was performed at the level of the aortic arch (zone 0, proximal to the brachiocephalic trunk), without evidence of additional entry tears. Subsequently, an Ascyrus Medical Dissection Stent (AMDS) (40-LA, Artivion®) with a 40-mm stent diameter was implanted. The guidewire was advanced through the true lumen, and the device was deployed under direct vision. Distal anastomosis between the native aorta and the polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE) segment of the device was performed using continuous 5-0 polypropylene suture. Anastomoses between grafts and at the aortic annulus were completed with double-layer 4-0 polypropylene sutures, followed by completion of the Dacron graft anastomosis. The distal anastomosis was reinforced with Teflon felt strips using a sandwich (“scarf”) technique to optimize anastomotic strength (Figure 2). Meticulous deairing of the aortic arch was performed, followed by gradual rewarming to normothermia. Follow-Up

The patient was extubated within the first 24 hours, remained in the intensive care unit for 6 days, and was discharged on postoperative day 13 without major complications. During the immediate postoperative period, she developed medically related bleeding that required correction of coagulation parameters and transfusion of blood products; however, surgical reintervention was not necessary.

Predischarge transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular function and a normally functioning aortic prosthesis without paravalvular leak (Figure 3). Follow-up computed tomography angiography at 10 and 90 days demonstrated false lumen obliteration at the level of the aortic arch, with supra-aortic and visceral vessels arising from the true lumen, as well as absence of new re-entry tears.Figure 3Transthoracic Echocardiographic Follow-UpTransthoracic echocardiography in the suprasternal view demonstrating the descending thoracic aorta with preserved patency of the left subclavian artery (LSA) and left common carotid artery (LCCA), without evidence of new dissection flaps (A). Discussion

ATAAD represents a potentially lethal cardiovascular emergency, with an estimated mortality ranging from 25% to 50% within the first 48 hours in the absence of timely surgical treatment.1,2 Its annual incidence is estimated at approximately 2.6 to 3.5 cases per 100,000 inhabitants.3, 4, 5 Although contemporary surgical management has improved early survival, persistent perfusion of the distal false lumen remains a major determinant of late aneurysmal degeneration, aortic enlargement, and need for secondary intervention. Consequently, modern surgical strategies increasingly seek not only to repair the proximal aorta but also to promote favorable distal aortic remodeling and improve long-term outcomes.2,6,7

The AMDS is a hybrid device developed to address this unmet need during conventional ATAAD repair. The system combines a proximal PTFE cuff incorporated into the distal hemiarch anastomosis with a distal uncovered self-expanding nitinol stent that extends into the proximal descending thoracic aorta. This design aims to stabilize the intimal flap, expand the true lumen, maintain perfusion of branch vessels, and facilitate false lumen thrombosis while avoiding extensive arch reconstruction.8, 9, 10

This case does not represent the first human use of AMDS, as the device has previously been evaluated in prospective registries and multicenter observational studies5,6,8; however, to our knowledge, it represents one of the first reported clinical experiences in Latin America. Its educational value lies in illustrating patient selection, procedural planning, device sizing, and practical implantation of AMDS in a patient with a proximal entry tear confined to the aortic root and ascending aorta, without additional arch tears or connective tissue disease.

Appropriate patient selection is fundamental for successful AMDS use. The device is particularly suitable for patients undergoing ascending aortic or hemiarch replacement when the primary intimal tear is located proximally and the aortic arch does not require complete replacement. In these patients, AMDS may provide distal stabilization of the dissection while avoiding the additional complexity of total arch reconstruction. Conversely, extensive arch destruction, large arch entry tears, connective tissue disorders, chronic dissections, or situations requiring complete arch replacement may favor more extensive strategies such as the frozen elephant trunk (FET) technique.5,6,8,9 Device sizing is generally based on preoperative computed tomography measurements of the native aortic diameter and true lumen dimensions at the intended landing zone. Excessive oversizing should be avoided because it may increase the risk of intimal injury or stent-induced new entry tears.

The principal alternative strategy for distal aortic management in ATAAD is total arch replacement with FET. Although FET offers simultaneous treatment of the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta and may be preferable in patients with extensive distal disease, it is associated with greater operative complexity, longer cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest times, and increased risk of procedure-related complications. Contemporary series have reported operative mortality rates ranging from 17% to 26%, as well as risks of spinal cord ischemia, stroke, and distal stent-induced new entry tears. In contrast, AMDS can be incorporated into a standard hemiarch repair without routine total arch replacement, potentially reducing operative burden while still promoting distal aortic remodeling.3,4,7

Available clinical evidence has demonstrated encouraging short- and mid-term outcomes with AMDS. Multicenter registries have reported high rates of true lumen expansion, false lumen thrombosis at the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta, and favorable aortic remodeling during follow-up. Importantly, these benefits have been achieved without significant increases in operative mortality, stroke, or other major neurological complications compared with conventional repair strategies. Follow-up studies extending beyond 1 year have shown sustained remodeling effects and low rates of device-related adverse events, although long-term evidence remains limited and randomized comparisons with FET are not yet available.5,7,8,10 Experimental studies have further supported the hemodynamic rationale of the device by demonstrating improved true lumen flow and reduced false lumen pressurization.

In the present patient, serial computed tomography angiography performed at 10 and 90 days demonstrated persistent false lumen obliteration at the aortic arch level, preservation of visceral perfusion through the true lumen, and absence of new re-entry tears or device-related complications. Although this follow-up period remains relatively short and does not allow definitive assessment of long-term durability, the imaging findings are consistent with the favorable remodeling patterns reported in previously published human series.

This case highlights the feasibility of incorporating AMDS into routine ATAAD repair in carefully selected patients and demonstrates how distal aortic stabilization may be achieved without increasing operative complexity. Additional studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are required to better define the long-term durability, optimal indications, and comparative effectiveness of AMDS within the contemporary treatment algorithm for ATAAD.

Conclusions

ATAAD remains a highly complex surgical emergency in which both immediate survival and long-term outcomes depend on timely intervention and effective distal aortic management.

In this case, incorporation of the AMDS during Bentall repair was feasible and safe, allowing distal stabilization of the dissection without substantially increasing operative complexity.

Early postoperative imaging demonstrated false lumen obliteration at the aortic arch level and preservation of visceral perfusion through the true lumen. Although longer follow-up is required, these findings support the potential role of AMDS as an adjunctive strategy for selected patients undergoing repair of ATAAD.
