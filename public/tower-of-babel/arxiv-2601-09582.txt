Introduction

We start with the following definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}

\Phi

Definition 1.2
.

A quadratic polynomial

The main purpose of this paper is to study the smoothed

\int h(t)d\nu(t)=\int h(f(x,y,z))d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z).

Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
f(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
be a fixed non-degenerate quadratic polynomial.
Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. There exist
\epsilon>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
, depending only on
\alpha
and coefficients of
f
, such that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Let
\mu
be an
\alpha
-Frostman probability measure supported on
[0,1]
. We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi_{\delta}\ast\nu)^{2}(t)dt\lesssim\delta^{\alpha+\epsilon-1}.

We note that this theorem fails when
\mu
is not a Frostman measure (Proposition
4.1
) or when
\mu
has unbounded support (Proposition
4.2
). We do not attempt to optimize
\epsilon
throughout the proof.
The argument produces a positive gain
\epsilon
depending on the parameters of the problem.

Main ideas and novelties.

The proof begins by rewriting the
L^{2}
energy in a form that exposes a geometric coincidence problem, first under the additional assumption that
\mu
is AD-regular.
Expanding the square, inserting the definition of
\nu=f_{\#}(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)
, and applying Fubini, the estimate is reduced to bounding a six-fold integral that measures, with respect to
\mu^{\otimes 3}\times\mu^{\otimes 3}
, the number of approximate solutions to

f(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\approx f(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})

at scale
\delta
.
Equivalently, one needs to control a
\delta
-thickened energy integral of the form

\int\mathbf{1}_{\{|f(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})-f(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})|\lesssim\delta\}}\,d(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\,d(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2}).

The argument then partitions this six-fold integral into a
transverse
contribution, where at least one coordinate derivative of
f
is

\gtrsim\delta^{\kappa}
, for some
\kappa>0
, and a
small-gradient
contribution, where all relevant derivatives are
\lesssim\delta^{\kappa}
and the interaction is confined to a
\delta^{\kappa}
-neighborhood of the affine critical set
K=\{\nabla f=0\}
.
In the transverse contribution, the key new input is an incidence theorem for
\delta
-separated lines and point sets of the form
\Phi(M\times M)
, where
M
is a
\delta
-separated and non-concentrated set,
|M|=\delta^{-\alpha}
, and
\Phi
is bi-Lipschitz on
M\times M
with controlled distortion (Theorem
2.1
).
This product structure yields a power saving
\delta^{\epsilon}
beyond what is available from non-concentration alone, and this is the gain that drives the improved energy estimate. The incidence theorem is new and most interesting when
\alpha
is small, namely,
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
. In the small-gradient contribution, the proof uses that
\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}f)\geq 2
, so
K
is either a point or an affine line, and combines Frostman tube bounds with uniform estimates for
\delta
-neighborhoods of level sets of rank-two quadratic forms to control the contribution.
A further novelty is a reduction from AD-regular measures to general Frostman measures via a weighted dyadic discretization and a repartition into uniformly non-concentrated classes, which preserves the incidence mechanism while accommodating variable local mass.
Finally, we also provide obstruction examples showing that neither the Frostman condition nor bounded support can be dropped in general for this type of
L^{2}
control (Section
4
).

Structure of the paper.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 establishes the main geometric input, namely, an incidence theorem controlling the number of
\delta
-incidences between a
\delta
-separated collection of lines and a planar point set of the form
\Phi(M\times M)
, where
M
is
\delta
-separated and non-concentrated and
\Phi
is bi-Lipschitz with controlled distortion.
Section 3 contains the proof of the main theorem. Section 4 presents obstruction examples showing that neither the Frostman hypothesis nor bounded support can be omitted in general for the stated
L^{2}
estimates, and it also records further remarks, including an explicit construction for which
\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{L^{2}}
diverges as
\delta\to 0
.
Finally, Section 5 (Appendix) gathers auxiliary calculations used in the proofs of the incidence theorem and the main theorem.

2
An incidence theorem for bi-Lipschitz images of Cartesian products

In this section, we prove an incidence theorem for
\delta
-separated line sets and point sets with semi-grid structure. This incidence bound provided by Theorem
2.1
will play a crucial role when proving Theorem
1.6
.

To start with, we define a metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
between lines. For
\vartheta\in[0,2\pi)
and

a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, denote the line

\{\,t(\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta)+a(-\sin\vartheta,\cos\vartheta)\mid t\in\mathbb{R}\,\}

by
\ell_{\vartheta,a}
. Every line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be parameterized in this form. Then the
distance between two lines is defined by

\begin{split}d(\ell_{\vartheta,a},\ell_{\vartheta^{\prime},a^{\prime}}):=&\sqrt{(\cos\vartheta-\cos\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}+(\sin\vartheta-\sin\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}}\\
&+\sqrt{(-a\sin\vartheta+a^{\prime}\sin\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}+(a\cos\vartheta-a^{\prime}\cos\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}}.\end{split}

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Then, there exist constants
\theta,\epsilon_{0},\delta_{0},
depending on
\alpha
such that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
:

Let
M\subset[0,1]
be a
\delta
-separated subset,
|M|=\delta^{-\alpha}
, with the non-concentration condition

|M\cap I|\leq{|I|}^{\alpha}\delta^{-\alpha}\text{ for all interval }I\text{ of length at least $\delta$}.

Let
L
be a set of
\delta
-separated lines with
|L|=\delta^{-2\alpha}
and containing at most
O(|M|)
vertical lines.
Let
F:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function such that the map

\Phi:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\Phi(x,y):=(F(x,y),y),

is bi-Lipschitz on
M\times M
with constants
O(\delta^{-\kappa})
, for some small
\kappa
satisfying

0<\kappa\leq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon_{0}\alpha}{2(\alpha+8)},~\frac{\theta}{4}\right\},

and define a point set

.

Then the number of pairs
(p,\ell)\in P\times L
with

is at most
\delta^{-3\alpha+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(\alpha,\kappa)>0
. In particular, one has

0<\epsilon\leq\min\left\{\frac{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}\alpha-\kappa(\alpha+8)}{39},~~\frac{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}\alpha-8\kappa}{\left(27+\frac{24}{\alpha}\right)},~~\frac{\theta-4\kappa}{\,2+\frac{24}{\alpha}+\frac{3\theta}{\alpha}\,},~\frac{\alpha(\alpha-16\kappa)}{48(\alpha+1)}\right\}.

The main contribution of Theorem

lies in the range
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
. When
\alpha>1/2
, a sharp upper bound with an explicit value of

can be found in the work of

[
5
]
. They also provided a construction showing that in the range

, the upper bound of
\delta^{-3\alpha}
might be best possible if we only assume non-concentration conditions on

and
L
. Theorem
2.1
tells us that a gain of a factor
\delta^{\epsilon}
is possible if the point set also has the product-like structure. A direct computation shows that Fu-Ren’s sharpness example in the range
\alpha\leq 1/2
does not apply to our incidence theorem unless
\alpha=0
. For recent progress in higher dimensions, we refer the reader to
[

,

]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 3.2,
[

.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\eta\in(0,1)
with
\alpha\geq\beta
. There exists
\theta,\epsilon_{0},\delta_{0}\in(0,1/2]
, depending only on
\beta,\gamma,\eta
, such that the following holds for
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
:

Let
A,B\subset\delta\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1]
and
C\subset[1/2,1]
be
\delta
-separated sets with
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
.
Assume in addition that
|A|\leq\delta^{-\alpha}
,
|B|\geq\delta^{-\beta}
,

, and

\displaystyle|B\cap B(x,r)|\lesssim r^{\eta}|B|,~|C\cap B(x,r)|\lesssim r^{\gamma}|C|,\quad~\forall x\in\mathbb{R},~\delta\leq r\leq\delta^{\epsilon_{0}}.

Then,

\sum_{c\in C}|\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}:|(a_{1}+cb_{1})-(a_{2}+cb_{2})|\leq\delta\}|_{\delta}\lesssim\delta^{\theta}|A|_{\delta}^{3/2}|B|_{\delta}^{3/2}|C|.

Although the incidence proof follows the by now energy-incidence paradigm (ultimately
reducing to an application of Theorem
2.2
), its implementation in the present
setting requires additional care.
The key source of difficulty is that the map
\Phi
is only known to be bi-Lipschitz on

M\times M
with constants allowed to depend on
\delta
(indeed of size
O(\delta^{-\kappa})
),
so quantitative separation and non-concentration must be tracked through several

\delta
-dependent normalizations and decompositions.

First, the
\delta
-dependent distortion interacts nontrivially with separation, so one must
isolate and discard near-degenerate configurations (notably near-vertical and near-horizontal
directions) and work at a scale compatible with the distortion parameter.
Second, after reducing to incidences across
two fibers
R(y_{0})
and
R(y_{1})
, the resulting
approximate collinearity relations involve coefficients depending on the relative
position
of
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
. To bring these coefficients into the regime where existing energy bounds
apply, one needs additional pigeonholing and normalization (including rescaling to a fixed
coefficient range).
Finally, these reductions and normalizations must be carried out in a multiscale manner that
preserves quantitative non-concentration and the underlying product structure, so that one
can ultimately contradict the discretized sum-product energy estimate.

It is precisely this combination of product structure,
\delta
-dependent distortion control,
and multiscale normalization that makes the energy bound accessible at the required scale.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.6
for regular measures

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We say that a probability measure
\mu
is
\alpha
-AD regular on its support with the constant
C_{\mu}>0
if for every

and every

C_{\mu}^{-1}r^{\alpha}\leq\mu\bigl(B(x,r)\bigr)\leq C_{\mu}r^{\alpha}.

When

is an absolute constant and the support of

is clear from the context, we simply say

is
\alpha
-AD regular.

In this section, we are going to prove Theorem
1.6

is AD-regular.

Let
\nu
be a measure defined by

\int h(t)d\nu(t)=\int h(f(x,y,z))d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z),

where

is a continuous function with compact support. From definition, for a fixed

, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\delta}\ast\nu(t)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi_{\delta}\left(t-f(x,y,z)\right)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{-1}\int\chi_{\{|t-f(x,y,z)|\leq\delta\}}(x,y,z)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z),

where

denotes the indicator function of a set

.

Hence, applying the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi_{\delta}\ast\nu)^{2}(t)dt\\
&\lesssim\delta^{-2}\int\chi_{\{\left|t-f(x,y,z)\right|\leq\delta\}}\chi_{\{\left|t-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\right|\leq\delta\}}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z)d\mu(x^{\prime})d\mu(y^{\prime})d\mu(z^{\prime})dt.\\
&\lesssim\delta^{-2}\int\chi_{\{\left|t-f(x,y,z)\right|\leq\delta\}}\chi_{\{\left|f(x,y,z)-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\right|\leq 2\delta\}}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z)d\mu(x^{\prime})d\mu(y^{\prime})d\mu(z^{\prime})dt\\
&\lesssim\delta^{-1}\int\chi_{\{\left|f(x,y,z)-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\right|\leq 2\delta\}}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z)d\mu(x^{\prime})d\mu(y^{\prime})d\mu(z^{\prime}).\end{split}

(9)

Lemma 3.3

\kappa>0

\alpha

Lemma 3.4

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a constant

, depending only on

Proof.

Fix

Let

Lemma 3.6

(14)

Proof.

We next control the mass of a
\delta
-thick level set of a rank two quadratic form under
\mu\times\mu
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mu
be
\alpha
-Frostman probability measure supported on
[0,1]
.
Let
Q(u,v)
be a nonzero quadratic polynomial in two variables with
\mathrm{rank}(D^{2}Q)=2
.
Then, there exists a constant
C_{\mu,Q}\geq 1
such that for all
0<\delta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

(\mu\times\mu)\bigl(\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon|Q(u,v)-t|\leq\delta\}\bigr)\leq C_{\mu,Q}\,\delta^{\alpha}\,\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

Here,

D^{2}Q(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q}{\partial u^{2}}(u,v)&\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q}{\partial u\,\partial v}(u,v)\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q}{\partial v\,\partial u}(u,v)&\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q}{\partial v^{2}}(u,v)\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We first prove the lemma when
Q
is one of the canonical forms

Q(u,v)=uv,\qquad Q(u,v)=u^{2}+v^{2},\qquad Q(u,v)=u^{2}-v^{2}.

Case 1:
Q(u,v)=uv
.

We have
Q(u,v)\geq 0
for all
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
.
If
t\leq-\delta
, then the sublevel set is either empty or has zero mass. In the following, we assume
t>-\delta
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and let

E_{t}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon|uv-t|\leq\delta\}.

Decompose
u
dyadically.
For each integer
0\leq m\leq\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\delta})\rceil
, define

U_{m}:=\{u\in[0,1]\colon 2^{-(m+1)}<u\leq 2^{-m}\},\qquad U_{\infty}:=\{u\in[0,1]\colon 0\leq u\leq\delta\}.

Then

(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t})\leq\sum_{m\geq 0}\int_{U_{m}}\mu\bigl(\{v\in[0,1]\colon|uv-t|\leq\delta\}\bigr)\,d\mu(u)+\mu(U_{\infty}).

By the definition of
\mu
, one has
\mu(U_{\infty})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
.
Fix
m\geq 0
and
u\in U_{m}
.
Then
\{v\colon|uv-t|\leq\delta\}
is an interval of length at most
2\delta/u\leq 2\delta\cdot 2^{m+1}
.
Thus,

\mu\bigl(\{v\in[0,1]\colon|uv-t|\leq\delta\}\bigr)\lesssim\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{u}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\lesssim(\delta\cdot 2^{m})^{\alpha}.

Thus

\int_{U_{m}}\mu(\{v\colon|uv-t|\leq\delta\})\,d\mu(u)\lesssim(\delta\cdot 2^{m})^{\alpha}\,\mu(U_{m}).

Note that
\mu(U_{m})\lesssim|U_{m}|^{\alpha}\lesssim 2^{-m\alpha}
.
Therefore, each
m
-term is
\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
.
Summing over
m
yields a factor
\log(2/\delta)
.
This gives

(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

Case 2:
Q(u,v)=u^{2}+v^{2}
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and set

E_{t}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon|u^{2}+v^{2}-t|\leq\delta\}.

For
u\in[0,1]
, define

V(u):=\{v\in[0,1]\colon|u^{2}+v^{2}-t|\leq\delta\}.

We first treat the region
t-u^{2}\leq 0
more carefully.
Assume
t-u^{2}\leq 0
and
V(u)\neq\emptyset
.
Then there exists
v\in[0,1]
such that
|u^{2}+v^{2}-t|\leq\delta
.
Since
v^{2}\geq 0
, this implies
u^{2}\leq t+\delta
.
Combined with
t-u^{2}\leq 0
, we obtain
u^{2}\in[t,t+\delta]
.
In particular, if
t\geq 0
, then

u\in[\sqrt{t},\sqrt{t+\delta}]\quad\text{or}\quad u\in[-\sqrt{t+\delta},-\sqrt{t}],

and each of these intervals has length
\lesssim\sqrt{\delta}
.
If
t<0
, then
t-u^{2}\leq 0
holds for all
u
and
V(u)\neq\emptyset
forces

t\geq-\delta
, in which case the same conclusion
u^{2}\leq t+\delta\lesssim\delta
gives

u\in[-C\sqrt{\delta},C\sqrt{\delta}]
for an absolute constant
C
.

Therefore, in all cases the set

U_{-}:=\{u\in[0,1]:t-u^{2}\leq 0,\ V(u)\neq\emptyset\}

is contained in a union of
O(1)
intervals of total length
\lesssim\sqrt{\delta}
.
Moreover, in this region we also have
v^{2}\leq t+\delta-u^{2}\leq\delta
, hence
V(u)\subset[0,\sqrt{\delta}]
,
so
\mu(V(u))\lesssim\delta^{\alpha/2}
.
Hence,

\int_{t-u^{2}\leq 0}\mu(V(u))\,d\mu(u)\lesssim\delta^{\alpha/2}\,\mu(U_{-})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha/2}\,(\sqrt{\delta})^{\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha}.

If
t-u^{2}>0
, then
v^{2}
is constrained to an interval of length at most
2\delta
around
t-u^{2}
.
Thus,
v
is constrained to an interval of total length

|V(u)|\lesssim\min\Bigl(1,\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr).

By the definition of
\mu
,

\mu(V(u))\lesssim\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr).

Therefore,

(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t})=\int\mu(V(u))\,d\mu(u)\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}+\int_{\{t-u^{2}>0\}}\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\,d\mu(u).

We estimate the integral by a dyadic decomposition.
Let

W_{\infty}:=\{u\in[0,1]\colon 0<t-u^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\},

and for integers
0\leq m\leq\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2}{\delta}\rceil
, let

W_{m}:=\{u\in[0,1]\colon 2^{-(m+1)}<t-u^{2}\leq 2^{-m}\}.

Then
\{u\in[0,1]\colon t-u^{2}>0\}=W_{\infty}\cup\bigcup_{m\geq 0}W_{m}
.

On
W_{\infty}
, the integrand is trivially bounded by
1
.
Moreover,
W_{\infty}
is contained in a union of
O(1)
intervals of length
O(\delta)
, since

0<t-u^{2}\leq\delta^{2}
forces
u
to lie within
O(\delta)
of the (at most two) points

\sqrt{t}\in[0,1]
.
Therefore,
\mu(W_{\infty})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
, and the contribution of
W_{\infty}
is
\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
.

We now bound the contribution of the sets
W_{m}
.
For
u\in W_{m}
, one has
2^{-(m+1)}<t-u^{2}\leq 2^{-m}
, hence

\sqrt{t-u^{2}}\geq 2^{-(m+1)/2}
and therefore

\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\leq\min\Bigl(1,\bigl(\delta\cdot 2^{(m+1)/2}\bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\lesssim\min\Bigl(1,\bigl(\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}\bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr).

Moreover,
W_{m}
is contained in a union of
O(1)
intervals of length
\lesssim 2^{-m/2}
.
Therefore,

\mu(W_{m})\lesssim(2^{-m/2})^{\alpha}=2^{-m\alpha/2}.

It follows that the contribution of
W_{m}
is

\int_{W_{m}}\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\,d\mu(u)\lesssim\mu(W_{m})\,\min\Bigl(1,\bigl(\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}\bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\lesssim 2^{-m\alpha/2}\,\min\Bigl(1,\bigl(\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}\bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr).

If
\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}\leq 1
, then the right-hand side is
\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
.
If
\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}>1
, then
2^{-m/2}<\delta
, hence
2^{-m}<\delta^{2}
, which is excluded by
the definition of
W_{m}
(since
W_{\infty}
already contains the region
0<t-u^{2}\leq\delta^{2}
).
Therefore for all
m
with
W_{m}\neq\emptyset
one has
\delta\cdot 2^{m/2}\leq 1
, and consequently

\int_{W_{m}}\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\,d\mu(u)\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}.

Summing over
m
yields

\sum_{m\geq 0}\int_{W_{m}}\min\Bigl(1,\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{t-u^{2}}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr)\,d\mu(u)\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

Case 3:
Q(u,v)=u^{2}-v^{2}
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and set

E_{t}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon|u^{2}-v^{2}-t|\leq\delta\}.

Write
u^{2}-v^{2}=(u-v)(u+v)
, and set
s=u+v
and
w=u-v
.
Then, the constraint
|u^{2}-v^{2}-t|\leq\delta
becomes
|sw-t|\leq\delta
.

Decompose according to the size of
s
.
Let

S_{\infty}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon s\leq\delta\},\qquad S_{m}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon 2^{-(m+1)}<s\leq 2^{-m}\}\quad(0\leq m\leq\lceil\log_{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\rceil),

and

S_{-1}:=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon 1\leq s\leq 2\}.

On
S_{\infty}
, one has
S_{\infty}\subset[0,\delta]\times[0,\delta]
, hence

(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t}\cap S_{\infty})\leq(\mu\times\mu)(S_{\infty})\leq\mu([0,\delta])^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2\alpha}\leq\delta^{\alpha}.

Fix
m\geq 0
.
On
S_{m}
, one has
s\sim 2^{-m}
, so
|sw-t|\leq\delta
implies

|w-t/s|\leq\delta/s\lesssim\delta\,2^{m}.

Thus,
E_{t}\cap S_{m}
is contained in a set of the form

\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon 2^{-(m+1)}<u+v\leq 2^{-m},\ |u-v-w_{0}|\leq C\delta\,2^{m}\},

for some
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and an absolute constant
C
.
This set is the intersection of two strips of widths
\sim 2^{-m}
and
\sim\delta 2^{m}
,
and hence it can be covered by
O(1)
axis-parallel rectangles with side lengths

\lesssim 2^{-m}
and
\lesssim\delta 2^{m}
.
For each such rectangle
I\times J
, we have

(\mu\times\mu)(I\times J)\leq\mu(I)\mu(J)\lesssim|I|^{\alpha}|J|^{\alpha}\lesssim(2^{-m})^{\alpha}(\delta 2^{m})^{\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha}.

Therefore
(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t}\cap S_{m})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
. Similarly,
\mu\times\mu(E_{t}\cap S_{-1})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}
. Summing over all terms gives

(\mu\times\mu)(E_{t})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

We now show that the case of a general form of
Q
can be reduced to one of the three above cases.
Write

Q(u,v)=Q_{2}(u,v)+L(u,v)+c,

where
Q_{2}
is the homogeneous quadratic part,
L
is affine-linear, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\mathrm{rank}(D^{2}Q)=2
, the symmetric matrix of
Q_{2}
is invertible.

Let
x=(u,v)^{T}
and write

Q(x)=x^{T}Ax+\ell^{T}x+c,

where
A
is a real symmetric
2\times 2
matrix and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Choose

x_{0}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}A^{-1}\ell,\qquad\text{and set}\qquad y:=x-x_{0}.

Then

Q(x)=y^{T}Ay+c^{\prime},

for some constant
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, so the linear term has been removed by translation.

Since
A
is invertible, the quadratic form
y\mapsto y^{T}Ay
is non-degenerate on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Therefore, there exists an invertible linear map
B\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and a canonical quadratic form

Q_{\mathrm{can}}(s,t)\in\{\,st,\ s^{2}+t^{2},\ s^{2}-t^{2}\,\}

such that

y^{T}Ay=Q_{\mathrm{can}}(By)\qquad\text{for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Define the invertible affine map

\Phi(x):=B(x-x_{0}).

Then

Q(x)=Q_{\mathrm{can}}(\Phi(x))+c^{\prime}.

Note that each step above uses only translations, dilations in one variable, the swap
(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)
, and a map of the form

(u,v)\mapsto(u+\lambda v,v)\quad\text{or}\quad(u,v)\mapsto(u,v+\lambda u).

For such a map, and for each fixed value of one coordinate, the other coordinate undergoes a one-dimensional affine change.
Hence, for each fixed
u
the fiber

\{v\in[0,1]:|Q(u,v)-t|\leq\delta\}

is still a union of at most two intervals, and their lengths are multiplied by at most a constant depending only on the map.
Since
\mu
satisfies the Frostman bound uniformly over all intervals in
\mathbb{R}
, the estimates in the three canonical cases imply the same bound for
Q
, with a constant depending only on
Q
and the Frostman constant of
\mu
.
∎

We are now ready to bound
I_{0}
.

Proof of Lemma

.

Set

, and for simplicity, we write
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}_{r}
.
Let
\nu_{3}:=\mu\times\mu\times\mu
.
By (

) and Fubini,

I_{0}=\int\nu_{3}\bigl(\mathcal{G}\cap f^{-1}([f(u^{\prime})-2\delta,f(u^{\prime})+2\delta])\bigr)\,\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathcal{G}}(u^{\prime})\,d\nu_{3}(u^{\prime})\leq\nu_{3}(\mathcal{G})\,\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\nu_{3}\bigl(\mathcal{G}\cap\{|f-t|\leq 2\delta\}\bigr).

(17)

We first bound
\nu_{3}(\mathcal{G})
.
By Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
,
K
is either a point or a line, and
\mathcal{G}\subset N_{Cr}(K)
.
If
K
is a point, then Lemma
3.6
gives

\nu_{3}(\mathcal{G})\ \lesssim\ r^{3\alpha}.

(18)

If
K
is a line, then Lemma
3.6
gives

\nu_{3}(\mathcal{G})\ \lesssim\ r^{2\alpha}.

(19)

We next bound the slice mass uniformly in
t
.
Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Choose
u_{0}\in K
.
Since
\nabla f(u_{0})=0
, one has

f(u)=f(u_{0})+\tfrac{1}{2}(u-u_{0})^{T}H(u-u_{0})

(20)

for all
u
, where
H
is the Hessian from (

).
By Lemma
3.4
, one has
\mathrm{rank}(H)\in\{2,3\}
.

Since
\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 2
and

is symmetric, there exist indices

After permuting coordinates, we may assume

For each

.
Moreover, the remaining coefficients of

is fixed. Therefore, Lemma

applies to

This yields

(\mu\times\mu)\bigl(\{(y,z)\in[0,1]^{2}\colon|f(x,y,z)-t|\leq 2\delta\}\bigr)\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}\,\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr),

\mu\bigl(B(x,r)\bigr)\leq C_{\mu}\,r^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }r>0,

For

w(I)>0
, choose a representative point
x_{I}\in I\cap\text{supp}(\mu)
.
If
w(I)=0
, we choose
x_{I}\in I
arbitrarily.
For a function
F\colon[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
, we observe

\int_{[0,1]}F(x)\,d\mu(x)\leq\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}w(I)\,\sup_{x\in I}F(x).

(23)

For each integer
k\geq 0
, define

\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k}=\Bigl\{I\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}\colon 2^{-(k+1)}\delta^{\alpha}<w(I)\leq C_{\mu}2^{-k}\delta^{\alpha}\Bigr\}.

Set

M_{k}:=\{m\delta\colon I_{m}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k}\}\subset\delta\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1].

Each
M_{k}
is
\delta
-separated and satisfies the following non-concentration condition.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
k\geq 0
and every interval
J\subset[0,1]
with
|J|\geq\delta
, one has

|M_{k}\cap J|\leq C\,2^{k}\,|J|^{\alpha}\,\delta^{-\alpha},

(24)

where
C
depends only on
\alpha
and the Frostman constant.

Proof.

Let
J\subset[0,1]
with
|J|\geq\delta
.
Let
J^{+}
be the
\delta
-fattening of
J
, namely, the union of all
I\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
that intersect
J
.
Then
|J^{+}|\leq|J|+2\delta\leq 3|J|
.

Let
\mathcal{I}(J^{+})
be the family of intervals
I\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k}
contained in
J^{+}
.
By the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k}
,

\mu(J^{+})\geq\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(J^{+})}\mu(I)>|\mathcal{I}(J^{+})|\,2^{-(k+1)}\delta^{\alpha}.

Here,
|\mathcal{I}(J^{+})|
means the number of intervals contained in
J^{+}
. Thus,
|\mathcal{I}(J^{+})|\leq 2^{k+1}\delta^{-\alpha}\mu(J^{+})
.
The Frostman bound (
22
) gives
\mu(J^{+})\leq C_{\mu}|J^{+}|^{\alpha}\leq C_{\mu}3^{\alpha}|J|^{\alpha}
.
Since
|M_{k}\cap J|\leq|\mathcal{I}(J^{+})|
, the estimate (
24
) follows.
∎

Let
M\subset\delta\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1]
be
\delta
-separated.
We say that
M
satisfies a non-concentration bound with constant
K\geq 1
if

|M\cap J|\leq K\,|J|^{\alpha}\,\delta^{-\alpha}\qquad\text{for all intervals }J\subset[0,1]\text{ with }|J|\geq\delta.

(25)

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that
M
is
\delta
-separated and satisfies (
25
).
Then there exists an integer
L=\lceil 2K\rceil+1
and a partition

M=M^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup M^{(L)}

such that each
M^{(\ell)}
satisfies

|M^{(\ell)}\cap J|\leq 3\,|J|^{\alpha}\,\delta^{-\alpha}\qquad\text{for all intervals }J\subset[0,1]\text{ with }|J|\geq\delta.

(26)

Proof.

List
M=\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}\}
.
Let
L:=\lceil 2K\rceil+1
.
For
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, define
M^{(\ell)}:=\{x_{j}\colon j\equiv\ell\ (\mathrm{mod}\ L)\}
. We identify
M^{(\ell)}
and
M^{(0)}
. These sets form a partition of
M
.

Fix an interval
J
with
|J|\geq\delta
.
Set
m:=|M\cap J|
.
The set
M\cap J
corresponds to
m
consecutive indices, so
|M^{(\ell)}\cap J|\leq m/L+1
.
By (
25
), we have
m\leq K|J|^{\alpha}\delta^{-\alpha}
.
Since
L\geq 2K
, this gives

|M^{(\ell)}\cap J|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}|J|^{\alpha}\delta^{-\alpha}+1\leq 3\,|J|^{\alpha}\,\delta^{-\alpha}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Applying Lemma
3.9
to
M_{k}
and using Lemma
3.8
yield the following corollary.

Corollary 3.10
.

For each
k\geq 0
, there exist an integer
L_{k}\lesssim 2^{k}
and a partition

M_{k}=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L_{k}}M_{k,\ell}

such that each
M_{k,\ell}
is
\delta
-separated and satisfies (
26
).
All implicit constants depend only on
\alpha
and the Frostman constant.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/2]
. For each
k\geq 0
, let
L_{k}
be the number of classes in the partition

M_{k}=\bigcup_{\ell\leq L_{k}}M_{k,\ell}

given by Corollary
3.10
. Then, one has

L_{k}\lesssim\min\{2^{k},\delta^{-1}\}.

Consequently,

\sum_{k\geq 0}2^{-k}L_{k}\lesssim\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

Proof.

By Corollary
3.10
, one has
L_{k}\lesssim 2^{k}
.
On the other hand,
L_{k}\leq|M_{k}|\lesssim\delta^{-1}
.
Therefore,
L_{k}\lesssim\min\{2^{k},\delta^{-1}\}
.

Set
\mathcal{K}:=\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\delta})\rceil
.
Then

\sum_{k\geq 0}2^{-k}L_{k}\lesssim\sum_{0\leq k\leq\mathcal{K}}2^{-k}2^{k}+\sum_{k>\mathcal{K}}2^{-k}\delta^{-1}=(\mathcal{K}+1)+\delta^{-1}\sum_{k>\mathcal{K}}2^{-k}.

Note that
\sum_{k>\mathcal{K}}2^{-k}\leq 2^{-\mathcal{K}+1}\lesssim\delta
, hence

\sum_{k\geq 0}2^{-k}L_{k}\lesssim\mathcal{K}+1\lesssim\log\Bigl(\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr).

The proof is complete.
∎

We now present the reduction for
I_{1}
. The remaining
I_{i}
are treated in the same way.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
for Frostman measures.

We follow the proof of Lemma
3.2
and only replace the discretization step by a weighted dyadic decomposition.

Apply (

) in each of the six variables in

.
This bounds

by a weighted sum over

\sum_{I_{1},\dots,I_{6}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}w(I_{1})\cdots w(I_{6})\,\chi_{\{|f(x_{I_{4}},x_{I_{5}},x_{I_{6}})-f(x_{I_{1}},x_{I_{2}},x_{I_{3}})|\leq C\delta\}}\chi_{\{x_{I_{1}}\in J_{1,x_{I_{5}},x_{I_{6}}}\}},

up to an absolute implicit constant.

We decompose the above sum according to the dyadic levels of the six intervals, namely,

\sum_{I_{1},\dots,I_{6}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{6}\geq 0}\sum_{I_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{I_{6}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta,k_{6}}}.

Therefore,

It follows that

(27)

where

Substituting this into (

I_{11}\lesssim\delta^{6\alpha}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{6}\geq 0}2^{-(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{6})}\sum_{\ell_{1}\leq L_{k_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{\ell_{6}\leq L_{k_{6}}}\Sigma(k_{1},\ell_{1};\dots;k_{6},\ell_{6}).

(28)

Fix

and fix

4
Necessity of hypotheses and remarks

4.1
Necessity of the Frostman condition

We now demonstrate that the Frostman condition is essential for gaining an improvement for the
L^{2}
energy.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f(x,y,z)=x+(y+z)^{2}
and let
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.
For all sufficiently small
\delta>0
there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
which is not
\alpha
-Frostman such that, with
\nu=f_{\#}(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)
, one has

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{\alpha-1}.

The implicit constant may depend on
\alpha
,
\varphi
, and the fixed parameters in the construction, but not on
\delta
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
c\in(0,1/4)
.
Define

U:=\{n\delta^{1/2}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,\delta^{-\alpha}]\},\qquad A=U(\delta):=\bigcup_{u\in U}\bigl([u-\delta/2,u+\delta/2]\cap[0,1]\bigr),\qquad S:=[0,c\delta].

Let
\mu_{A}:=|A|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{A}\,dx
and
\mu_{S}:=|S|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S}\,dx
, and fix
p_{A},p_{S}\in(0,1)
with
p_{A}+p_{S}=1
.
Set

\mu:=p_{S}\mu_{S}+p_{A}\mu_{A}.

Since
\mu_{S}(S)=1
, we have
\mu(S)\geq p_{S}
.
If
\mu
were
\alpha
-Frostman, then
\mu(S)\leq C|S|^{\alpha}=C(c\delta)^{\alpha}
for some constant
C>0
.
This is impossible for
\delta
sufficiently small, since
\mu(S)\geq p_{S}
while
(c\delta)^{\alpha}\to 0
, hence
\mu
is not
\alpha
-Frostman.

We now prove the lower bound for
\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{2}
.
Let
\widetilde{\varphi}(t):=\varphi(-t)
and
K:=\varphi*\widetilde{\varphi}
.
Then

K(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)\widetilde{\varphi}(-t)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)^{2}\,dt>0.

By continuity of
K
, there exist
c_{0}>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
(depending only on
\varphi
) such that

K(u)\geq c_{0}\qquad\text{for all }|u|\leq\eta.

Define
K_{\delta}(t):=\delta^{-1}K(t/\delta)
.
Expanding the square and applying Fubini theorem (recall
\varphi\geq 0
) gives the standard kernel identity

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{2}^{2}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{\delta}(x-y)\,d\nu(x)\,d\nu(y).

(30)

Set

I_{m}=[m\delta,(m+1)\delta)\cap[0,1],\qquad L_{m}=[m\delta,m\delta+\tfrac{\eta}{8}\delta)\cap[0,1],\qquad m=0,1,\dots,\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor.

If
x,y\in L_{m}
, then
|x-y|\leq(\eta/8)\delta
, hence
|(x-y)/\delta|\leq\eta/8<\eta
and therefore

K_{\delta}(x-y)\geq c_{0}\delta^{-1}
on
L_{m}\times L_{m}
.
Using (
30
) and restricting to
\bigcup_{m}(L_{m}\times L_{m})
yields

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}\iint_{L_{m}\times L_{m}}K_{\delta}(x-y)\,d\nu(x)\,d\nu(y)\geq c_{0}\,\delta^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}\nu(L_{m})^{2}.

(31)

It remains to show that

\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}\nu(L_{m})^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{\alpha}.

(32)

Since the points of
U
are
\delta^{1/2}
-separated and
\delta\lesssim\delta^{1/2}
, the intervals forming
A=U(\delta)
are disjoint, and hence

|A|\sim|U|\delta\sim\delta^{-\alpha}\delta=\delta^{1-\alpha}.

Fix
c_{1}\in(0,1/32]
and define

\mathcal{S}:=\Bigl\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\ \lfloor c_{1}\delta^{-\alpha}\rfloor\leq s\leq\lfloor 2c_{1}\delta^{-\alpha}\rfloor\Bigr\}.

For integers
n\geq 0
, set

J_{n}:=[n\delta^{1/2},\,n\delta^{1/2}+\delta/2]\cap[0,1].

Then
J_{n}\subset A
and
|J_{n}|=\delta/2
, so

\mu_{A}(J_{n})=\frac{|J_{n}|}{|A|}\sim\frac{\delta}{\delta^{1-\alpha}}=\delta^{\alpha}.

(33)

We now choose
c\in(0,1/4)
small enough (depending only on
\eta
) so that

c\leq\eta/16.

(34)

Since
\alpha<1/2
, we have
3/2-\alpha>1
, and therefore for
\delta
sufficiently small (depending on
\alpha
,
\eta
, and
c_{1}
) one has

8c_{1}\delta^{3/2-\alpha}+4\delta^{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{16}\delta.

(35)

Fix
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
n\in\{0,1,\dots,s\}
.
For
(y,z)\in J_{n}\times J_{s-n}
we may write

y=n\delta^{1/2}+v,\qquad z=(s-n)\delta^{1/2}+w,\qquad v,w\in[0,\delta/2],

so
0\leq v+w\leq\delta
and hence

(y+z)^{2}=\bigl(s\delta^{1/2}+(v+w)\bigr)^{2}=s^{2}\delta+2s\delta^{1/2}(v+w)+(v+w)^{2}.

Using
s\leq 2c_{1}\delta^{-\alpha}
and
0\leq v+w\leq\delta
gives

0\leq 2s\delta^{1/2}(v+w)\leq 4c_{1}\delta^{3/2-\alpha},\qquad 0\leq(v+w)^{2}\leq\delta^{2}.

By (
35
), for
\delta
sufficiently small, we obtain

(y+z)^{2}\in\Bigl[s^{2}\delta,\ s^{2}\delta+\frac{\eta}{16}\delta\Bigr].

If moreover
x\in S=[0,c\delta]
, then by (
34
),

f(x,y,z)=x+(y+z)^{2}\in\Bigl[s^{2}\delta,\ s^{2}\delta+\frac{\eta}{8}\delta\Bigr]\subset L_{s^{2}}.

Therefore, for each fixed
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\nu(L_{s^{2}})\geq\sum_{n=0}^{s}\mu(S)\mu(J_{n})\mu(J_{s-n})\geq p_{S}\,p_{A}^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{s}\mu_{A}(J_{n})\mu_{A}(J_{s-n}).

Using (
33
) and
s\sim\delta^{-\alpha}
on
\mathcal{S}
yields

\nu(L_{s^{2}})\gtrsim p_{S}p_{A}^{2}(s+1)\delta^{2\alpha}\sim\delta^{-\alpha}\delta^{2\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha}.

The indices
s^{2}
are distinct and satisfy
s^{2}\delta\lesssim\delta^{1-2\alpha}\lesssim 1
for
\delta
small, hence the intervals
L_{s^{2}}
are disjoint and contained in
[0,1]
.
Consequently,

\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\delta^{-1}\rfloor}\nu(L_{m})^{2}\geq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\nu(L_{s^{2}})^{2}\gtrsim|\mathcal{S}|\,\delta^{2\alpha}\sim\delta^{-\alpha}\delta^{2\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha},

which is (
32
).
Combining (
31
) with (
32
) gives

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{2}^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{-1}\cdot\delta^{\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha-1},

as claimed.
∎

4.2
Necessity of the bounded support

In this subsection, we demonstrate that the bounded support condition is necessary for gaining an improvement for the
L^{2}
energy.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
f=x+yz
. For sufficiently small
\delta>0
, there exists a
\frac{1}{2}
-Frostman measure
\mu
with unbounded support such that, with
\nu=f_{\#}(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)
, one has

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Given a small parameter
\delta
, let
U=\{0,\delta^{1/2},2\delta^{1/2},\ldots,1\}
,
V=\{\frac{1}{\delta^{1/2}},\ldots,\frac{1}{2\delta},\frac{1}{\delta}\}
, and
V^{-1}=\{\delta,2\delta,\ldots,\delta^{1/2}\}
. Set
W=V\cup(-V)
.

Let

\displaystyle U(\delta)=\bigcup_{u\in U}[u-\delta/2,u+\delta/2],\quad V(\delta)=\bigcup_{v\in V}[v-\delta/2,v+\delta/2]

\displaystyle V^{-1}(\delta)=\bigcup_{v\in V}[v^{-1}-\delta/2,v^{-1}+\delta/2],\quad W(\delta)=\bigcup_{w\in W}[w-\delta/2,w+\delta/2].

Set
A=U(\delta)\cup W(\delta)
. Define a measure
\mu=|A|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{A}dx
on
A
, i.e.

\mu(B(x,r))=\frac{|B(x,r)\cap A|}{|A|}.

Note that the diameter of
\text{supp}(\mu)
is about
\delta^{-1}
, which is unbounded as
\delta\to 0
.

We now show that
\mu
is
\frac{1}{2}
-Frostman.

For an interval of length
r
, say
B(x,r)
, we write as
B(x,r)=B_{1}(x,r)\cup B_{2}(x,r)
, where
B_{1}(x,r)=B(x,r)\cap[0,1]
and
B_{2}(x,r)=B(x,r)\cap[1,\delta^{-1}]
.

The argument about the case of
B_{1}(x,r)
:

For
\delta<r<1
, we have

\mu(B_{1}(x,r))=\frac{r\delta}{\delta^{1/2}|A|}\leq r\leq r^{\alpha},

for any
\alpha<1
.

For
r\leq\delta
, we have

\mu(B_{1}(x,r))\leq\frac{r}{\delta^{1/2}}\leq r^{\alpha},

when
r^{1-\alpha}\leq\delta^{1/2}
, or
\alpha\leq 1/2
.

The argument about the case of
B_{2}(x,r)
:

For
1\leq r\leq\delta^{-1}
, using the fact that
W(\delta)
is
1
-separated, we have

\mu(B_{2}(x,r))\leq\frac{r\delta}{|A|}\leq r\delta^{1/2}\leq r^{1/2}.

For
\delta\leq r\leq 1
, we have

\mu(B_{2}(x,r))\leq\frac{\delta}{\delta^{1/2}}=\delta^{1/2}\leq r^{1/2}.

For
r\leq\delta
, we have

\mu(B_{2}(x,r))\leq\frac{r}{\delta^{1/2}}\leq r^{1/2}.

Therefore,
\mu
is a
\frac{1}{2}
-Frostman measure.

We now estimate a lower bound of the integral below.

\delta^{-2}\int\chi_{\{\left|t-f(x,y,z)\right|\leq\delta\}}\chi_{\{\left|t-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\right|\leq\delta\}}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z)d\mu(x^{\prime})d\mu(y^{\prime})d\mu(z^{\prime})dt,

where
f(x,y,z)=x+yz
.

The choice of the polynomial
f
is motivated by its correspondence to a line equation with appropriate parameters. More precisely, a direct computation shows that each point
(0,x)
with
x\in V^{-1}=\{\delta,2\delta,\ldots,\delta^{1/2}\}
belongs to
\delta^{-1/2}

\delta
-neighborhoods of lines defined by
y=cx
with
c\in V
. Thus, for any
d\in U
, each point
(d,x)
with
x\in V^{-1}=\{\delta,2\delta,\ldots,\delta^{1/2}\}
belongs to
\delta^{-1/2}

\delta
-neighborhoods of lines defined by
y=c(x+d)
with
c\in V
.
Hence, the above integral is at least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int\chi_{\{\left|t-(x+yz)\right|\leq\delta\}}\chi_{\{\left|t-(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}z^{\prime})\right|\leq\delta\}}d\mu_{|U(\delta)}(x)d\mu_{|V(\delta)}(y)d\mu_{|U(\delta)}(z)d\mu_{|U(\delta)}(x^{\prime})d\mu_{|V(\delta)}(y^{\prime})d\mu_{|U(\delta)}(z^{\prime})dt.

By considering
t\in V^{-1}(\delta)
, the above integral is at least
\delta^{-1/2}
.
∎

4.3
Some remarks

We now provide an example showing that the
L^{2}
energy can go to infinity when
\delta\to 0
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be an
\alpha
-AD-regular probability measure on
[0,1]
with
0<\alpha<1/2
. For sufficiently small
\delta>0
, there exists
\sim\delta^{-\alpha}
disjoint intervals
J
of length
\delta/2
with
\mu(J)\sim\delta^{\alpha}
, and consider
f(x,y,z)=x+(y-z)^{2}
and let
\nu=f_{\#}(\mu\times\mu\times\mu)
. Then

\|\varphi_{\delta}*\nu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{2\alpha-1}\to\infty\quad\text{as }\delta\to 0.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon=\sqrt{\delta/2}
and define
S=\{(y,z):|y-z|\leq\epsilon\}
. For
(y,z)\in S
, we have
(y-z)^{2}\leq\delta/2
. By the AD-regularity of
\mu
,

(\mu\times\mu)(S)\sim\epsilon^{\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha/2}.

For each
\delta/2
interval
J
contained in the left half of some
\delta
interval
I
, and for
(y,z)\in S
, we have

f(x,y,z)=x+(y-z)^{2}\in I\quad\text{for all }x\in J,

since
x\in J\subset[k\delta,k\delta+\delta/2]
and
(y-z)^{2}\leq\delta/2
. Therefore,

\nu(I)\geq\mu(J)\cdot(\mu\times\mu)(S)\sim\delta^{\alpha}\cdot\delta^{\alpha/2}=\delta^{3\alpha/2}.

Summing over the
\sim\delta^{-\alpha}
such intervals gives

\sum_{I}\nu(I)^{2}\gtrsim\delta^{-\alpha}\cdot(\delta^{3\alpha/2})^{2}=\delta^{2\alpha}.

Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
yields

f
depends on each variable.

For each choice of a distinguished variable, we define an associated Jacobian determinant.
For instance, if
y
is distinguished, we write

f(x,y,z)=e\,y^{2}+i\,y+m_{y}(x,z)\,y+k_{y}(x,z),\qquad m_{y}(x,z)=ax+cz,\qquad k_{y}(x,z)=bxz+dx^{2}+gz^{2}+hx+jz,

and set

J_{y}(x,z):=\det\frac{\partial(m_{y},k_{y})}{\partial(x,z)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}m_{y}&\partial_{z}m_{y}\\
\partial_{x}k_{y}&\partial_{z}k_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

Define
J_{x}(y,z)
and
J_{z}(x,y)
analogously.

Equivalently, one may define three polynomials by

J_{x}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}f&\partial_{z}f\\
\partial_{xy}f&\partial_{xz}f\end{pmatrix},\qquad J_{y}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f&\partial_{z}f\\
\partial_{xy}f&\partial_{yz}f\end{pmatrix},\qquad J_{z}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f&\partial_{y}f\\
\partial_{xz}f&\partial_{yz}f\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

For the polynomial (
44
), these two viewpoints agree up to harmless sign conventions.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f
be as in (
44
) and assume that
f
depends nontrivially on each variable.
Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

J_{x}\equiv J_{y}\equiv J_{z}\equiv 0
as polynomials.

(ii)

There exist polynomials
G,I,J,K\in\mathbb{R}[t]
such that

f(x,y,z)=G\bigl(I(x)+J(y)+K(z)\bigr).

In particular, if
f
is non-degenerate in the sense of Definition
1.2
, then at least one of

J_{x},J_{y},J_{z}
is not identically zero.

Proof.

We split
f
into its homogeneous quadratic part and its affine-linear part:

f(x,y,z)=Q(x,y,z)+L(x,y,z),

where

Q(x,y,z)=dx^{2}+ey^{2}+gz^{2}+axy+bxz+cyz,\qquad L(x,y,z)=hx+iy+jz.

For (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

Assume
f(x,y,z)=G(I(x)+J(y)+K(z))
.
Since
f
is quadratic,
G
has degree at most
2
and
I,J,K
are affine-linear.
A direct differentiation shows that in (
45
) the second row is always a scalar
multiple of the first row, so each determinant vanishes identically. Thus
J_{x}\equiv J_{y}\equiv J_{z}\equiv 0
.

For (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

Assume
J_{x}\equiv J_{y}\equiv J_{z}\equiv 0
.
A direct computation using (
44
) shows that these identities are equivalent to the
following algebraic relations among the coefficients:

a^{2}=4de,\qquad b^{2}=4dg,\qquad c^{2}=4eg,\qquad ab=2dc,\qquad ac=2eb,\qquad bc=2ga,

(46)

together with the linear relations

ah=2di,\qquad bh=2dj,\qquad ai=2eh,\qquad ci=2ej,\qquad bj=2gh,\qquad cj=2gi.

(47)

The relations (
46
) imply that the symmetric
3\times 3
matrix associated to the
quadratic form
Q
has rank at most
1
. Since
f
depends nontrivially on each variable, we have

Q\not\equiv 0
, so that matrix has rank
1
. Therefore, there exist
\lambda\neq 0
and

(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\neq(0,0,0)
such that

Q(x,y,z)=\lambda(\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z)^{2}.

Write
\ell(x,y,z):=\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z
.

Next, (
47
) forces the linear coefficient vector
(h,i,j)
to be proportional to

(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Concretely, substituting

d=\lambda\alpha^{2},\quad e=\lambda\beta^{2},\quad g=\lambda\gamma^{2},\quad a=2\lambda\alpha\beta,\quad b=2\lambda\alpha\gamma,\quad c=2\lambda\beta\gamma

into (
47
) yields

\beta h=\alpha i,\qquad\gamma h=\alpha j,\qquad\gamma i=\beta j,

which implies that
(h,i,j)
is proportional to
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Thus, there exists
\mu\in\mathbb{R}

such that

L(x,y,z)=\mu\,\ell(x,y,z).

Consequently,

f(x,y,z)=\lambda\,\ell(x,y,z)^{2}+\mu\,\ell(x,y,z)=G(\ell(x,y,z)),

where
G(t)=\lambda t^{2}+\mu t
.

Finally, set
I(x)=\alpha x
,
J(y)=\beta y
, and
K(z)=\gamma z
. Then

f(x,y,z)=G(I(x)+J(y)+K(z))
, which proves (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).
∎
