Emergency donor pericardial conduit for ruptured pulmonary artery in lung transplantation for severe PH: a case report
Authors: Jun Chen, Dong Liu, Ji Zhang, Weili Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Effective salvage strategies in this setting are not well described. Background

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) with severe pulmonary hypertension (PH) carries a poor prognosis, and bilateral lung transplantation is the only definitive treatment [1–4]. Chronic severe PH renders the pulmonary artery (PA) wall exceptionally friable, making intraoperative rupture a rare but often fatal complication [5, 6]. While PA reconstruction using pericardium has been described in elective settings [7], its emergency application as an on-site salvage maneuver following an acute, uncontrollable PA rupture in a patient with severe PH is rarely detailed. This case report describes our decision-making, technical execution, and lessons learned using a donor pericardial conduit in this extreme scenario.

Case presentation

Preoperative clinical course

A 58-year-old woman (height 158 cm, weight 52 kg) with a twenty-year history of dry cough and progressive dyspnea was diagnosed with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and severe pulmonary hypertension (PH). Pre-transplantation assessment revealed a pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) of 105 mmHg, a 42% decrease in diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and computed tomography (CT) imaging showing pulmonary artery enlargement and pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 1A). She was subsequently listed for bilateral lung transplantation.

Her condition was optimized through treatment with pirfenidone, methylprednisolone, sildenafil, and macitentan. This therapy reduced her pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) to 65 mmHg. Despite this, her clinical condition deteriorated. Six months after being listed, she was hospitalized for pulmonary embolism and stabilized with anticoagulant therapy. Preoperative CT angiography measured a main pulmonary artery diameter of 50 mm and confirmed the presence of distal pulmonary embolism (Fig. 1B).

Intraoperative course and surgical technique

Bilateral sequential lung transplantation was performed via anterolateral thoracotomies under peripheral veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) support, using right femoral cannulation (15-Fr arterial, 21-Fr venous). Intraoperatively, the lungs were firm and fibrotic, with notably dilated and atheromatous pulmonary arteries. The right lung was implanted without incident. The cold ischemic time for the right lung was 5 h and 30 min.

For the left pulmonary artery (PA), an anastomosis was completed using a running 5 − 0 polypropylene suture. Upon reperfusion of the left lung, however, an acute longitudinal dissection and rupture of the recipient’s left main PA occurred at the anastomosis, leading to massive hemorrhage. Immediate attempts to re‑clamp the left PA proximally were made, but the vessel wall was so friable that the clamp caused the PA to shatter, making direct repair impossible. In addition, in cases of continuous bleeding, we found that the existing extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) flow rate was insufficient (< 3 L/min), unable to maintain hemodynamic stability, and the patient’s right lower limb turned purple and cold.

Because the recipient’s left PA stump was too short, the left main pulmonary artery trunk had been clamped within the pericardium to perform the anastomosis. After the rupture, any attempt at direct repair would have risked catastrophic bleeding without adequate circulatory support. A multidisciplinary team (lung transplant, cardiac surgery, anesthesia, and perfusion) therefore emergently convened and decided to establish full cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). The cardiac surgery team performed central aortic and right atrial cannulation because the existing groin cannulas were too small to support full CPB flow. This allowed safe control of the left PA clamp and provided a bloodless field for vascular reconstruction. To ensure normal blood flow in the right lower limb, we reconfigured the original ECMO circuit to the left femoral vessel.

After stabilization, the damaged recipient PA segment was resected. A conduit was fashioned intraoperatively from donor pericardium: a pericardial segment was trimmed, wrapped around a 24-mm metal dilator, and the seam was closed with interrupted 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures. This 3-cm tubular conduit was then anastomosed end-to-end to the PA using a running 5 − 0 polypropylene suture, restoring vascular continuity (Fig. 1C). The transplantation was thereafter completed. The cold ischemic time for the left lung was 12 h and 15 min.

Total operative time was 18 h and 40 min, with an estimated blood loss of 5,000 mL. After the procedure, the patient was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU).

Postoperative outcome

The patient was weaned from ECMO on postoperative day 3. Her recovery was complicated by a resistant Burkholderia cepacia respiratory infection, requiring prolonged targeted therapy and a tracheostomy. The CTA performed two months later showed that the pericardial duct was widely patent with no thrombus formation; a mild anastomotic stenosis (approximately 20%) was observed in the main left pulmonary artery, but it was not hemodynamically significant (Fig. 1D). After a long recovery period, the patient was discharged on the 86th postoperative day. A pulmonary CT scan performed three months after surgery showed that pulmonary edema had resolved and the allograft lung function was stable (Fig. 1E).

Discussion and conclusions

We acknowledge that PA reconstruction using pericardium is not a novel technique and is part of the standard armamentarium of experienced lung transplant surgeons [7]. However, its application as an immediate salvage maneuver following an acute, uncontrollable PA rupture during reperfusion—in a patient with a PASP of 105 mmHg and severely atheromatous, friable vessels—is rarely detailed in the literature. In this extreme scenario, direct reclamping was impossible, and the successful use of a donor pericardial conduit demonstrates its life‑saving value.

Why donor pericardium? Our choice was multifactorial: (1) biocompatibility and compliance, which approximate native PA and may reduce anastomotic stenosis [7, 8]; (2) lower infection risk in contaminated or immunosuppressed fields compared to synthetic grafts [9]; and (3) immediate availability and versatility, allowing on‑site custom sizing without delays. Alternative materials include bovine pericardium (commercially available but less compliant), cryopreserved homografts (ideal but rarely emergent), and synthetic grafts (associated with higher infection risk). In our emergent, infected field, donor pericardium offered the best balance.

Perioperative lessons - For patients with severe PH (PASP > 60–80 mmHg) and PA atheroma, teams should anticipate extreme vascular fragility and have pericardium readily available. Moreover, a flexible circulatory support strategy is essential. In retrospect, a “hybrid” circuit allowing rapid conversion from peripheral ECMO to full cardiopulmonary bypass would have minimized blood loss. We recommend this preparedness measure.

Conclusion

Catastrophic PA rupture during lung transplantation for severe PH is a life‑threatening emergency. This case demonstrates that emergency reconstruction using an on‑site fabricated donor pericardial conduit is a feasible and effective salvage technique. We recommend that this biological option remain readily available in every lung transplant surgeon’s armamentarium for managing the worst‑case intraoperative complication.
