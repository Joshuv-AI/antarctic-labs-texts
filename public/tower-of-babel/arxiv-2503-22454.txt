A Causal Framework to Measure and Mitigate Non-binary Treatment Discrimination
Ayan MajumdarMPI-SWS, Saarland University ayanm@mpi-sws.org, Deborah D. KanubalaSaarland University kanubala@cs.uni-saarland.de Kavya GuptaSaarland University gupta@cs.uni-saarland.de Isabel ValeraSaarland University ivalera@cs.uni-saarland.de

Abstract
Fairness studies of algorithmic decision-making systems often simplify complex decision processes, such as bail or loan approvals, into binary classification tasks. However, these approaches overlook that such decisions are not inherently binary (e.g., approve or not approve bail or loan); they also involve non-binary treatment decisions (e.g., bail conditions or loan terms) that can influence the downstream outcomes (e.g., loan repayment or reoffending). In this paper, we argue that non-binary treatment decisions are integral to the decision process and controlled by decision-makers and, therefore, should be central to fairness analyses in algorithmic decision-making. We propose a causal framework that extends fairness analyses and explicitly distinguishes between decision-subjects’ covariates and the treatment decisions. This specification allows decision-makers to use our framework to (i) measure treatment disparity and its downstream effects in historical data and, using counterfactual reasoning, (ii) mitigate the impact of past unfair treatment decisions when automating decision-making. We use our framework to empirically analyze four widely used loan approval datasets to reveal potential disparity in non-binary treatment decisions and their discriminatory impact on outcomes, highlighting the need to incorporate treatment decisions in fairness assessments. Moreover, by intervening in treatment decisions, we show that our framework effectively mitigates treatment discrimination from historical data to ensure fair risk score estimation and (non-binary) decision-making processes that benefit all stakeholders.

Data-driven approaches increasingly leverage historical data to automate decision-making processes across societal domains, such as finance

, healthcare

]
, and criminal justice
[

. These systems typically reduce complex decision-making to a binary classification task, predicting either ground-truth outcomes (e.g., loan repayment, recidivism after bail) or imperfect proxies (e.g., healthcare expenditures as a proxy for health status)
[
44
,
53
]
. However, real-world decisions are rarely as simple as a binary choice; they involve multiple, non-binary treatment decisions. As shown in Table

, decision-makers often determine not only whether to give a positive decision (provide treatment, e.g., approve loan) but also its specific terms (e.g., loan amount, duration, interest rate). Similar oversimplifications occur in other domains, such as bail decisions
[
61
]
.
This simplistic approach overlooks the impact of non-binary treatment decisions on outcomes, leading to fairness analyses that fail to provide a holistic view of existing systems
[
46
]
.

Despite growing research on fairness in algorithmic decision-making, existing analyses simplify complex decision-making mechanisms in three key ways. First, many (if not most) studies
[
69
,
21
,
11
]
include non-binary treatments as covariates of the decision-subject (covariate examples in Table
1
).
This assumption obscures the mechanisms that assign treatment decisions, which are not controlled by decision-subjects but by decision-makers and, thus, may reveal discriminatory patterns.
This assumption thus limits the ability of these works to account for all sources of unfairness and their implications.
Second, alternative works
[
39
,
12
,
57
]
examine the fairness implications of treatment decisions but assume they are binary, ignoring the multivariate, non-binary nature of treatment decisions (such as, e.g., loan or bail amount).
Finally, only a few works
[
2
,
17
,
3
]
have focused on disparities in non-binary treatment decisions across different groups. Still, they
lack a generalizable framework
, providing only empirical insights in financial domains and, more importantly, neglecting the discriminatory downstream effects of these disparities on outcomes.

To bridge gaps in the existing approaches, we propose a fundamental
paradigm shift
in fairness analysis of automated decision-making systems that brings
fairness considerations to non-binary treatment decisions and their downstream outcome effects
.
Our paradigm shift leverages the following key insight: decision-makers do not simply make binary decisions (whether to give treatment) but also control the assignment of adjacent non-binary treatment decisions, which can introduce disparities and negatively impact outcomes.
To incorporate this insight into fairness analysis, we introduce a
broadly applicable causal framework
that allows decision-makers to analyze the fairness of underlying decision-making mechanisms systematically.
This systematic analysis allows our framework to provide novel measures of treatment disparities, assess their discriminatory effects on outcomes, and enable mitigation strategies to prevent biases in non-binary treatment decisions from perpetuating unfairness in automated systems.

Our approach explicitly differentiates between a decision-subject’s covariates and a decision-maker’s treatment decisions in the causal data-generating process, as illustrated in Figure
1
. This causal formulation allows us to measure disparities in non-binary treatment decisions
Z
induced by sensitive attributes
S
, both directly (solid red path) and indirectly through covariates
X
(dotted red path). Furthermore, our framework quantifies how these disparities propagate to outcomes
Y
via the causal path from
Z
to
Y
(dashed orange path), thereby inducing
treatment discrimination
. Our framework supports mitigation in scenarios impacted by treatment discrimination through two
pre-processing
strategies. These techniques generate
treatment-fair counterfactual datasets
, enabling fairer non-binary automated decisions and more equitable risk score estimation to inform future decision-making.

Our framework advances fairness analyses by leveraging
path-specific counterfactuals
to quantify direct and indirect disparities in non-binary treatment decisions and their impact on outcomes. While path-specific counterfactuals provide a powerful tool for causal fairness analyses, existing methods
[
52
,
8
]
suffer from significant limitations, such as being restricted to linear data generation processes or requiring inefficient Monte Carlo estimation with strong regularization.
To overcome these challenges, we build on the state-of-the-art in causal inference, the causal normalizing flows
[
26
]
, and propose a
novel path-specific counterfactual generation method
.
This advancement enables our framework to be
theoretically grounded
while being
practical
to be applied in real-world decision-making systems.

Finally, we validate our framework using four real-world lending datasets across two decision-making scenarios. Our empirical results demonstrate that explicitly accounting for non-binary treatment decisions helps provide more holistic fairness assessments, identifies systemic biases, and supports mitigation efforts. These findings underscore the importance of addressing treatment discrimination in achieving fairer outcomes, bridging a critical gap in fairness research, and laying the foundation for more robust causal fairness analyses.

Background

Our framework for measuring and mitigating treatment discrimination in decision-making relies on causal analyses. This section briefly outlines the core causal concepts used throughout the paper.

Structural causal models (SCMs).

An SCM
[
56
]

\mathcal{M}=(V,U,F)
is defined by the observational features
V
,
independent, unobserved, exogenous
variables
U
(no hidden confounding) and structural equations
F
.
A structural equation for some
V_{i}
gives the functional mapping from the corresponding exogenous
U_{i}
and causal parents
\operatorname{pa}(V_{i})
to the value of
V_{i}
as
V_{i}=F_{i}(\operatorname{pa}(V_{i}),U_{i})
.
These cause-effect relations in
\mathcal{M}
can also be represented by a directed acyclic graph
\mathcal{G}
like in Figure
1
, where the nodes represent
V
, and the directed edges represent the cause-effect relations.

Performing interventions.

The SCM
\mathcal{M}
helps us estimate the effects of
hypothetical interventions

\mathcal{I}
on features.
For instance, to analyze the effect of the intervention
\mathcal{I}
setting
V_{i}=\alpha
, we use the
{\operatorname{do}}
-operator
[
56
]

{\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha)
on
\mathcal{M}
that generates the
intervened
SCM
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Counterfactuals.

In the absence of hidden confounders, this intervened SCM
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{I}}
can also be used to perform
counterfactual analyses
for a particular
factual
decision-subject with features
\vartheta^{F}
to ask “what would be the counterfactual features
\vartheta^{CF}
had we intervened with
{\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha)
, and
all exogenous factors

u^{F}
remaining unchanged.”
Counterfactuals
\vartheta^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha))
are computed following the three steps
[
56
]
: i).
abduction
: estimating
u^{F}
from
\vartheta^{F}
using
\mathcal{M}
, ii).
action
: performing
{\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha)
to get
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{I}}
, iii).
prediction
: compute
\vartheta^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha))
from
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{I}}
using
u^{F}
and setting
V_{i}=\alpha
.
In simple terms, computing counterfactuals estimates the
total effect
of an intervention on the downstream causal features of the intervened variable in the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
for the factual decision-subject
\vartheta^{F}
.

Path-specific counterfactuals.

In certain scenarios, it is crucial to understand the effects of a particular feature
V_{i}
on a downstream feature
V_{j}
along different causal paths in
\mathcal{G}
. This analysis can be done by computing
path-specific counterfactuals

[
8
]
, which use multiple
{\operatorname{do}}
operations to control for
specific causal paths
.
Consider the two paths in an example causal structuring between variables
\langle V_{i},V_{j},V_{k}\rangle
, where we have
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{k}
and
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{j}\rightarrow V_{k}
.
For a factual instance
\vartheta^{F}=\langle v_{i}^{F},v_{j}^{F},v_{k}^{F}\rangle
, we can compute the
path-specific
counterfactual effect of
{\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha)
along these paths.
For the path
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{k}
, we measure the counterfactual effect using
v_{k}^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha),{\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow v_{j}^{F}))
. Similarly, for the path
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{j}\rightarrow V_{k}
, we use
v_{k}^{CF}(v_{i}^{F},{\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow v_{j}^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{i}\rightarrow\alpha))))
.
For the path
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{j}\rightarrow V_{k}
, we use the counterfactual
v_{j}^{CF}
obtained from the intervention on
V_{i}
but keep
V_{i}=v_{i}^{F}
fixed along the other path.

Causal fairness analyses.

The SCM framework has been extensively used to
causally
study
fairness problems
in ML
[
35
,
8
]
.
In these scenarios, target labels
Y
(e.g., bail, loan repayment) are considered causally affected by the sensitive feature
S
(e.g., gender/sex, race).
We can estimate the
total effect
of
S
on
Y
by finding
sensitive counterfactuals

y^{\mathrm{SCF}}=y^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(S\rightarrow\alpha))
, as was shown in
counterfactual fairness

[
35
]
.
Similarly,
Chiappa 2019
used
path-specific counterfactuals

y^{\mathrm{PSCF}}
to analyze the potentially discriminatory effect of
S
on
Y

along different causal paths
in
\mathcal{G}
for linear SCMs. In this work, we extend the concept of counterfactual fairness and path-specific counterfactual fairness for treatment decisions and their downstream effects.

3
Measuring treatment disparities and their downstream effects

In this section, we introduce our causal framework to address the limitations of the existing fair decision-making literature by

explicitly measuring
disparities in treatment decisions and their downstream effects and
mitigating
potential discriminatory consequences.
To this end, we assume that the
data generation process
that produces the observed (factual) historical data
\mathcal{D}
follows the causal graph depicted in Figure
1
, where:

1.

The
treatment decision

Z
is a causal child of both
X
and (potentially)
S
. We assume that the causal mechanism that
generated

Z
is under the control of the decision-maker (e.g., we assume that banks decide not only whether or not to grant a loan but also the loan terms) and may be discriminatory. The direct causal path from
S
to
Z
is shown in solid red, while the indirect path mediated by
X
is shown in dashed red.

2.

The
treatment decision

Z
is a causal parent of the outcome variable
Y
(e.g., we assume loan terms can impact the repayment ability of applicants). Hence,
S
can influence
Y
mediated by
Z
(orange dashed line in Figure
1
), leading to the possible propagation of treatment disparities to the outcome. Moreover, we maintain the direct
S\rightarrow Y
since such effects may occur in specific scenarios, e.g., in healthcare.

Remarks.

The edges of our causal graph
\mathcal{G}
(Figure
1
) indicate the structural
causal ordering

[
26
]
among the different groups of features, where the
cause-effect
relation for some edges may be zero.
Moreover, although the figure shows a single sensitive feature for simplicity, our
\mathcal{G}
can consider multiple sensitive features as independent root nodes. We treat
X
and
Z
in
\mathcal{G}
as multivariate blocks, focusing our causal analyses on relations between these “blocks” without making explicit assumptions about their causal relations.

3.1
Disparities in non-binary treatment decisions

Given our proposed causal graph in Figure
1
, we use the SCM framework to measure disparities in treatment decisions by measuring counterfactual and path-specific counterfactual effects of the sensitive feature (e.g., gender) on the treatment decision variables. For simplicity and without loss of generalization, here we assume the sensitive feature
on which we intervene
to be binary, i.e.,
S\in\{0,1\}
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{DTD}(s^{F})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\mathcal{F}(z^{\mathrm{SPCF}},z^{F})\right),\,\mathrm{where}\,z^{\mathrm{SPCF}}=z^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(S\rightarrow(1-s^{F})),{\operatorname{do}}(X\rightarrow x^{F}))\end{split}

Remarks.

First, the nature of
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F}
in the above definitions depends on the nature of the treatment decision variables.
For instance, when treatment decisions are continuous,
\mathcal{A}
would be the expectation
\mathbb{E}

\mathcal{D}

Second, by drawing parallels with predictive fairness concepts, we can better understand the fairness implications of treatment disparities.
For instance,
\operatorname{DTD}

Similarly, zero

[

However, like predictive fairness, the relevance of perfect treatment parity depends on context.
Similar to

is unavailable (e.g., admissions, hiring), treatment disparities

.
On the other hand, as separation-based frameworks
[

69
]
show, the outcome

can explain

is a ground truth as in lending.
Hence, studying the downstream impact on
Y
is imperative to understand if treatment disparities are discriminatory.

3.2
Downstream effects of treatment disparities

While Definitions
1
and
2
help measure treatment disparities, it is crucial to assess the downstream impacts of these disparities on the outcome

(e.g., when

is a ground truth as in lending) to determine whether they are discriminatory. Hence, we introduce two novel measures to quantify the counterfactual downstream outcome effects of total and direct treatment disparities.

.

s^{F}
with covariates

.

\displaystyle\operatorname{DTD-E}(s^{F},y^{F})

where

Remarks.

As before, we establish connections to predictive fairness concepts to understand when treatment disparities can result in discrimination through unfair decision outcomes.
For specific contexts, we can relate to the perspective of

[
15
]
and consider any

of

in assigning

In other scenarios, e.g., healthcare, such effects may not be discriminatory
if the effect
of DTD on

Similarly, disparities between treatment decisions

For instance, in predictive fairness, separation

Similarly, treatment disparities regarding

3.3
Practical implementation

Prior works that computed path-specific counterfactuals for predictive fairness
[
52
,
8
]
are restricted to linear settings or must rely on imprecise Monte Carlo approximations and complex regularization.
To compute path-specific counterfactuals in more practical settings, we propose a novel framework that helps estimate our proposed disparity measures.
Assuming access only to factual (potentially unfair) data
\mathcal{D}=\{s^{F},x^{F},z^{F},y^{F}\}
and the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
(Figure
1
),
our framework builds on the
causal normalizing flows
(CNF)
[
26
]
.
CNF has demonstrated accurate causal estimates in societal decision-making settings
[
41
]
.
Hence, by building our framework based on CNF, we develop a
practical yet theoretically grounded
approach to compute path-specific counterfactuals.

3.3.1
Background on causal normalizing flows

Causal normalizing flows (CNF) approximate the true and unknown SCM
\mathcal{M}
using a single, invertible normalizing flows model
T_{\psi}
with parameters
\psi
.
Note that CNF can handle
partial causal graphs
, in which each random variable in the graph may be multivariate without explicitly modeling the causal relationships across its dimensions.
Moreover, given a
causal ordering
across the random variables, CNF is
highly flexible
in learning the
presence or absence
of causal effects from observational data.
Hence, even with data collected from a (potentially unfair) decision-making process, the CNF model provides an ideal practical framework to estimate our discrimination measures regarding treatment decisions and their downstream effects. Importantly, a causal normalizing flows model fitted on observational data can be used to estimate the counterfactual datapoint
\vartheta^{CF}
resulting from a causal intervention
({\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow\alpha))
on a given factual datapoint
\vartheta^{F}=\{v_{1}^{F},\ldots,v_{K}^{F}\}
via:

1.

abduction step
computing the exogenous values for
\vartheta^{F}
as
{u}^{F}=T_{\psi}(\vartheta^{F})
;

2.

action step
constructing the exogenous values for
\vartheta^{CF}
as
{u}^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow\alpha))=\{{u}^{F}_{1:j-1},T_{\psi}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow\alpha)),{u}^{F}_{j+1:K}\}
;

3.

prediction step
estimating the resulting counterfactual feature values from the exogenous variables as
\vartheta^{CF}=T_{\psi}^{-1}\left({u}^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(V_{j}\rightarrow\alpha))\right)
.

Refer to Appendix
A
for more details on causal normalizing flows. Next, we show how to adapt the causal normalizing flows for our non-binary decision-making fairness analysis framework.

3.3.2
Approximating counterfactual treatments and outcomes

We can use a causal normalizing flows model trained using factual
\mathcal{D}
to compute counterfactual quantities for some factual
\vartheta^{F}\in\mathcal{D}
.
Importantly, we can readily utilize the
existing
counterfactual estimation method shown above to compute the
sensitive total treatment counterfactual

z^{\mathrm{SCF}}
as:

\displaystyle z^{\mathrm{SCF}}=T^{-1}_{\psi}(\langle T_{\psi}({\operatorname{do}}(s\rightarrow(1-s^{F}))),{u}^{F}_{X},{u}^{F}_{Z},{u}^{F}_{Y}\rangle)

(5)

Similarly, we can directly use the approximation process in Section
3.3.1
to compute the
counterfactual labels
in Definitions

and

resulting from an treatment intervention
{\operatorname{do}}(Z\rightarrow\hat{z})

\displaystyle y^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(Z\rightarrow\hat{z}))=T^{-1}_{\psi}(\langle{u}^{F}_{S},{u}^{F}_{X},T_{\psi}({\operatorname{do}}(z\rightarrow\hat{z})),{u}^{F}_{Y}\rangle),

where

However, note that to compute the effect of the counterfactual treatments on the outcome, we need to perform counterfactual approximations

Extension for path-specific disparity measures.

To analyze our path-specific measures in Definitions

and
4
, we cannot apply the existing causal normalizing flows counterfactual estimation process directly since it does not work for multiple sequential interventions.
Moreover, since we want to compute path-specific measures in non-linear settings, we cannot directly apply the existing linear approach in
[

]
either.
Hence, we provide a
novel procedure
that
extends
the existing non-linear causal normalizing flows counterfactual approach to approximate
path-specific counterfactuals

For computing

in Definitions

and

, we need to perform

in the causal normalizing flows model
following the causal ordering
of the features to obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&z^{\mathrm{SPCF}}=T^{-1}_{\psi}(\langle{u}^{CF}_{S},\hat{{u}}_{X}^{F},{u}^{F}_{Z},{u}^{F}_{Y}\rangle),\,\mathrm{where}\\
&{u}^{CF}_{S}=T_{\psi}({\operatorname{do}}(S\rightarrow(1-s^{F}))),\,\mathrm{and}\,\,\hat{{u}}_{X}^{F}=T_{\psi}(s^{CF},{\operatorname{do}}(X\rightarrow x^{F}))\end{split}

Hence, to compute

First we use
{\operatorname{do}}(S\rightarrow(1-s^{F}))
on

to get
\vartheta^{\mathrm{SCF}}
.
Then, we use
{\operatorname{do}}(X\rightarrow x^{F})

3.3.3
Assumptions and considerations

While our implementation currently utilizes causal normalizing flows, our causal framework is inherently flexible, allowing for the seamless integration of
future advancements
in causal generative modeling to generate counterfactual quantities. However, examining the assumptions and certain considerations specific to the current implementation is crucial for understanding its practical implications.

First, we highlight the causal assumptions central to our causal framework. Consistent with previous works in causal modeling
[
30
,
62
,
26
]
and causal fairness
[
35
,
8
,
31
]
, we assume the causal data generation process has
no hidden confounders
.
We base our analysis on the
decision-maker’s perspective
and assume our framework has access to
all treatment decisions

Z
and all covariates
X
and sensitive
S
used to assign
Z
.
These considerations eliminate confounding between
Z,Y
and
X,Z
.
However, in many real-world scenarios, decision-makers may be unable to account for all
X
that impact
Y
, leading to unseen confounding amongst
X,Y
.
This hidden confounding does not impact
total treatment disparity
(TTD) but could influence
direct treatment disparity
(DTD), which relies on interventions on
X
.

Alternatively, future work could consider an alternative
external auditor perspective
, in which case
a lack of transparency
on the treatment assignment procedure leads to not accounting for all
Z
or all
X
that were used to assign
Z
, thereby introducing confounding across
X,Z,Y
.
While some studies
[
31
,
7
]
have attempted to provide fairness estimation bounds under confounding, they require specific modeling considerations or do not account for multivariate, non-binary treatment decisions.
Other works
[
68
,
67
,
45
]
have explored causal inference using instrumental and proxy variables to address hidden confounding. However, these methods only estimate population-level average effects and
lack the decision-subject specificity
needed to generate counterfactuals in the presence of hidden confounders.
Therefore, further research is necessary to understand the impact of confounding on treatment fairness in real-world scenarios and to develop more robust estimation methods for generating counterfactuals in the presence of confounding.

Beyond confounding, another causal assumption central to our framework and CNF is
overlap

[
54
]
.
We assume that intervened values in the SCM are plausible and have
non-zero support
in the original causal data-generating process.
However, this assumption is reasonable and holds in many
real-world
scenarios.
For instance, the treatment
Z
distributions across different sensitive
S
for real-world datasets (Appendix
C
) show significant overlap.

Second, to ensure accurate treatment fairness measures, it is essential to consider the
accuracy
of the generated counterfactual entities. Assuming underlying causal assumptions hold in a real-world scenario,
[
26
]
have already shown how causal normalizing flows can generate highly accurate counterfactuals.
However, testing for the validity of the assumptions in real-world settings is challenging.
Although we do not explicitly pursue this avenue in this work, our framework and setup offer a unique advantage: it allows decision-makers to assess and improve counterfactual quality through
real-world studies
. Specifically, our counterfactuals
only modify treatments
, which are entirely under the control of decision-makers.
Hence, decision-makers can adjust treatment policies (e.g., adopting fair counterfactual treatments) and conduct real-world interventional studies on small subpopulations. This possibility enables decision-makers to conduct real-world analyses of counterfactual treatment accuracy, ultimately improving our framework’s
applicability
.

Mitigating treatment discrimination in historical data

In this section, we focus on settings where historical data serves a data-driven decision-making pipeline with one of the following purposes: i) automating the (non-binary) decision-making processes or ii) informing decision-making via risk score estimation.
Unfortunately, as discussed in previous sections, historical data may have been collected under
discriminatory
treatment policies, where
disparities
in past treatments
Z
led to

undesired harm
in the outcome
Y
.
In such scenarios, we want to ensure that past discriminatory effects from treatment disparities do not propagate through automated decision-making processes and cause
unfair future decisions
.
To enable future fair decisions, we propose an extension of our framework, providing an
automated pre-processing procedure
to transform biased historical datasets
\mathcal{D}
to
treatment-fair
datasets
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fair}}
.
Our pre-processing approach helps prevent the propagation of past treatment discrimination in future automated systems and further improves them through fair risk score estimates.

In the following sections, we assume an observed and historically collected
unfair
dataset,
\mathcal{D}=\{s^{F},x^{F},z^{F},y^{F}\}
, comprising a representative sample
3
3

3

We assume the dataset consists of independent and identically distributed samples drawn from an underlying data-generation process. Relaxations of this assumption, such as algorithmic decision-making under selective labels, are discussed in
[
36
]
.
of the random variables
S
,
X
,
Z
, and
Y
, representing sensitive attributes, covariates, non-binary treatment decisions, and outcomes, respectively.

4.1
Fairness in non-binary decision-making

To automate decision-making processes, decision-makers often use historical data to train a binary classifier,
h_{y}:={S,\tilde{X}}\mapsto Y
, where
S
,
\tilde{X}
, and
Y
represent, respectively, sensitive attributes, input features, and the target label. To the best of our knowledge, prior works assume that
\tilde{X}=\{X,Z\}
contains the applicant covariates
X
(e.g., income, savings, etc.) along with the non-binary treatment decisions
Z
(e.g., loan terms). In this context, the objective is to achieve both accuracy and fairness by learning a function that produces predictions
\hat{Y}\in\{0,1\}
, which act as proxies for the true target labels
Y\in\{0,1\}
. However, if the decision-maker’s policy to assign treatments is unfair, the resulting automated decision-making process will also be unfair.
We address this problem by extending our framework with an automated procedure to transform historically treatment-biased data. This procedure allows the decision-makers to assign
fair treatments
and ultimately make fair (non-binary) treatment decisions. The idea is that fair treatments should be assigned to lead to better, and thus fairer, outcomes
Y
.
Predictive algorithms trained on these fair treatments and outcomes would lead to fairer subsequent decisions.

More in detail, we first identify a fair treatment policy
\pi_{z}:=X\mapsto Z
. However, assigning non-binary treatments is often inherently unfair, complex, or unknown, making the selection of an appropriate
\pi(Z)
that is
optimal and fair for all stakeholders
a challenging task.
To address this issue, we propose an approach that ensures automated treatment assignments are non-discriminatory while improving decision outcomes for disadvantaged subpopulations.
Specifically, when we observe treatment disparities lead to discriminatory effects on outcomes, we leverage our causal framework and the generative capacity of the estimated SCM to
augment existing potentially unfair data
to construct
a fair dataset

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fair}}
.
In this automated transformation process, for applicants in the disadvantaged group
S^{F}=s^{-}
who receive
less favorable treatments
, we propose
intervening
in their treatment decisions
Z
to construct
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fair}}
as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fair}}=\left\{s^{F},x^{F},z^{F},y^{F}\right\}_{s=s^{+}}\bigcup\left\{s^{F},x^{F},z^{\mathrm{SCF}},y^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(Z\rightarrow z^{\mathrm{SCF}}))\right\}_{s=s^{-}}\end{split}

(8)

where
z^{\mathrm{SCF}}
follows Definition
1
.
For applicants in the original (unfair) dataset
\mathcal{D}
with
S=s^{-}
, we intervene on
Z
by replacing it with
z^{\mathrm{SCF}}
, derived from the advantaged group.
Consequently, the intervened
Z
and the resulting
Y
are replaced with their counterfactual values, as specified in Definitions
1
and
3
.
This intervention on the disadvantaged subpopulation ensures that the revised treatment assignments
z^{\mathrm{SCF}}
remove treatment discrimination and lead to improved decision outcomes, satisfying
P[Y^{CF}({\operatorname{do}}(Z\rightarrow z^{\mathrm{SCF}}))=1\mid S^{F}=s^{-}]\geq P[Y^{F}=1\mid S^{F}=s^{-}]
.
This process ensures that disadvantaged applicants receive treatment decisions that eliminate bias while leading to fair decision outcomes.
In summary, to automate fair non-binary decision-making processes based on an unfair dataset, one can use our causal framework to generate a fair dataset and then use it to learn a fair treatment policy
\pi_{z}
and a fair binary classifier
h_{y}
.

4.2
Fair risk score estimation

Risk scores are a critical tool in algorithmic decision-making that decision-makers use to inform decisions and treatment policies
[
23
,
53
,
6
]
.
For example, in lending, banks rely on risk score estimates from third-party institutions such as FICO and SCHUFA
[
14
,
64
]
to understand an applicant’s creditworthiness, helping them inform the loan decisions and justify interest rate variations
[
65
,
38
]
.
Risk scores in decision-making are usually estimated by leveraging historically collected data
\mathcal{D}
and past decision outcomes
[
40
,
51
]
.
Unfortunately, potential disparities in prior treatment decisions may have a discriminatory influence in past data and outcomes.
Hence, when estimating risk scores as the probability of outcome
y^{F}
using past data covariates
x^{F}
and past treatments
z^{F}
, the unfairness can propagate and lead to biased risk score estimates
R_{\mathrm{unfair}}
, often modeled as:

R_{\mathrm{unfair}}(x,z)={P}(y^{F}=0\mid X=x^{F},Z=z^{F}),

(9)

Importantly, these risk score estimations may remain biased even if we remove treatment decisions from the estimation process due to the causal effect of the treatment
Z
in the outcome
Y
.
Therefore, directly incorporating these risk score estimates as additional applicant features in decision-making will perpetuate or amplify unfairness.

Risk score estimates should be
free from potential past treatment discrimination
to ensure decision-makers can make well-informed, fair decisions. For instance, in insurance underwriting, fair risk scores allow providers to offer premium rates, mitigate systemic inequities, and prevent perpetuating historical biases in insurance coverage decisions.
To ensure fairness, we propose that risk scores designed to estimate an applicant’s inherent risk should exclude historical
treatment decisions
, as these factors go beyond an applicant’s control and may reflect past biases
[
33
,
12
]
.
Hence, for an applicant, we formulate fair risk score estimates
R_{\mathrm{fair}}
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{\text{fair}}(x,s)=\mathbb{E}_{z^{\prime}\sim\pi(Z)}\left[P\left(y^{CF}(s^{F},x^{F},{\operatorname{do}}(Z\rightarrow z^{\prime}))=0\right)\right],\end{split}

(10)

where
\pi(Z)
denotes an interventional distribution over treatments, which we use to
marginalize out
the impacts of treatments on the risk estimation. These fair risk scores,

R_{\text{fair}}
, can then be integrated into decision-making pipelines with covariates (
X
) to make future fair treatment decisions.

The interventional treatment distribution
\pi(Z)
in our risk score estimation ensures that
R_{\mathrm{fair}}
is both
fair
and
treatment-agnostic
.
Thus,
\pi(Z)
cannot be any arbitrary distribution; instead, the treatments in
\pi(Z)

should not systematically disadvantage
any sensitive group through adverse downstream effects.
As a simple approach, when we observe past treatment disparities are discriminatory, we set
\pi(Z)
as the
interventional treatment distribution
of the group
S=s^{+}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fair}}
.
Since the group
S=s^{+}
historically received favorable treatment assignments and outcomes, our choice of
\pi(Z)
helps maximize positive outcomes for both groups and mitigates the effects of treatment discrimination.
Moreover, since real-world scenarios show
overlap
across the factual treatments of the intervened groups (Appendix
C
), our selection of
\pi(Z)
results in a distribution over
realistic treatment values
.
We leave the challenging exploration of alternative fair interventional distributions that are globally fair policies for future work.

Related work

Fairness in lending.

Fairness analyses in automated lending decisions have focused mainly on statistical methods
[
21
,
24
,
37
]
, emphasizing metrics like equality of opportunity
[
21
]
, as noted in surveys
[
34
,
19
]
. However, these approaches often overlook biases embedded in the
ground-truth labels
, which stem from unfair treatment during loan processing. While prior work has separately examined treatment discrimination
[
60
,
1
]
and biased outcomes in lending decisions
[
50
,
3
,
47
]
, the causal link between
biased treatment
and its
impact on outcomes
remains underexplored.
Our novel framework bridges this gap and jointly analyzes treatment discrimination and its causal effects on lending outcomes.

Causal fairness notions in algorithmic predictive systems.

Causal fairness in algorithmic decision-making examines the
causal
influence of sensitive features on decision labels and outcomes. Counterfactual fairness
[
35
]
introduced the study of the
total causal effect
of sensitive attributes, while subsequent work explored effects along specific pathways, distinguishing
fair
from
unfair
pathways
[
52
,
8
]
. Later frameworks expanded to include multi-level attributes
[
43
]
and focused on fairness in decision outcomes
[
57
]
. However, these approaches primarily address
biases in binary labels or outcomes
, neglecting complex mechanisms like
treatment decisions
(e.g., loan terms) controlled by decision-makers. Although
[
39
]
introduced a causal framework for treatment fairness, it simplified multi-faceted treatments into binary variables, limiting its granularity. Our work advances this by incorporating the decision-maker’s perspective to analyze treatment decisions and their causal effects. We develop a novel framework for fine-grained fairness analyses using
path-specific counterfactuals

[
8
]
. Additionally, following
[
41
]
, we enhance counterfactual accuracy in societal contexts through state-of-the-art
causal normalizing flows

[
26
]
, surpassing previous variational methods
[
8
,
39
]
.

Multi-stage selection in decision-making.

Multi-stage selection problems
[
16
,
27
]
explore automating decision-making with binary decisions across stages, revealing additional candidate covariates for subsequent decisions. Concerning this line of work, our causal framework addresses a fundamentally different context governed by different mechanisms.
Our work focuses on a
single-stage
decision process where binary decisions (e.g., loan approval) are made in one step, with treatments directly controlled by the decision-maker. In contrast, multi-stage problems rely on observing additional applicant covariates. We complement existing studies by analyzing fairness in automated decision-making, specifically the fairness of assigned multivariate, non-binary treatments and their causal impact on decision outcomes.

Discussion

This work highlights the importance of incorporating
non-binary treatment decisions
in fairness analyses for automating decision-making systems. We proposed a novel causal framework to measure disparities in treatment decisions across sensitive groups and its impact on downstream outcomes. We also demonstrate how our framework can help mitigate unfairness and promote fairer decisions. However, ensuring its practical and reliable implementation requires addressing and discussing several critical challenges.

Ensuring robust causal frameworks for real-world scenarios.
Our analyses assume access to ground-truth labels for outcomes and the absence of hidden confounders in the causal generative process. However, these assumptions often fail in real-world settings and specific use cases (e.g., decision-maker vs auditor as user). Hidden confounders can bias treatment fairness estimates while using
proxy labels
for unobservable outcomes
[
66
]
, which introduces additional complexities. Addressing these challenges by accounting for
hidden confounders
is a promising direction for future research.

Designing mitigation strategies for diverse stakeholders.

Similar to pre-processing approaches in predictive fairness, our pre-processing mitigation methods for treatment discrimination prove
empirically effective
but they do not guarantee optimality.
Moreover, these approaches may not fully align with the diverse and often conflicting objectives of
all stakeholders
, raising a crucial question:

What defines a fair treatment policy, and how should it be formulated?

For instance, banks prioritize repayment probability in loan approval, while applicants seek lower interest rates
[
55
]
.
Addressing fairness in such settings requires a
holistic approach
that considers all stakeholders’ utilities and relies on carefully crafted
utility functions
to capture these objectives reliably.
Hence, future work should compare our methods to existing simple treatment interventions
[
12
]
while also focusing on novel
in-processing
methods to develop
context-aware policies
that can balance fairness and diverse stakeholder utilities.

Improving data collection to enable treatment discrimination analysis.
As shown in Table
1
, while our framework focuses on loan datasets, it is broadly applicable across decision-making domains. However, its practical application is constrained by significant data limitations. Many datasets fail to distinguish covariates
X
from treatments
Z
, omit critical treatment details (e.g., COMPAS
[
5
]
lacks bail terms, lending datasets exclude loan interest rates), or oversimplify multifaceted treatments (e.g., IHDP
[
39
]
reduces childcare factors to binary). Moreover, most datasets are collected under selective labeling settings
[
36
,
32
]
, where ground-truth outcomes are observed only for cases with a favorable agreement between the decision-maker and the decision-subject. This setting is problematic, as it overlooks
unknown agreement-phase discrimination
that may have occurred during the agreement process. These gaps emphasize the need for more transparent and detailed data collection practices that explicitly capture treatment decisions, including those made during the agreement phase, to enable comprehensive and fair analyses.

Concluding remarks.

Our work introduces a paradigm shift by integrating fairness considerations into non-binary treatments conditional on positive binary decisions (e.g., granting a loan).
By proposing treatment disparity measures, we complement existing literature to advance a more holistic analysis of fairness.
Our novel perspectives and empirical analyses demonstrate that fairness in algorithmic decision-making remains unresolved, necessitating
interdisciplinary socio-technical
approaches to address the needs of diverse stakeholders.
By accounting for the complexity of decisions and their impact on stakeholders’ utility, our work signifies a major step toward aligning algorithmic systems with societal expectations.
Our approach also paves the way for future research to adapt established predictive fairness methods to redefine and mitigate treatment unfairness.

Appendix A
Causal normalizing flows

Causal normalizing flows
[
26
]
enable causal inference from observational data under minor assumptions like invertible and differentiable structural equations, acyclic (known) graphs
\mathcal{G}
, and causal sufficiency. Under these assumptions, causal flows represent a SCM
\mathcal{M}
as Triangular Monotonic Increasing (TMI) maps and model them using single, invertible normalizing flows models
T_{\psi}
with parameters
\psi
.
Note that causal flows can handle
partial causal graphs
, in which each random variable in the graph may be multivariate without explicitly modelling the causal relationships across its dimensions.
Thus, it provides an ideal practical framework to estimate our measures of fairness with respect to treatment decisions and the downstream effects from available (potentially unfair) data collected from a decision-making process.

C.2
Losses

1.

Loss Given Default (LGD):

We estimate the loss incurred by the bank in the event an applicant defaults. Quantifying the loss will require additional analysis of other variables such as the value of collateral,
instalments payments etc
[
25
]
. There are a number of different ways to estimate
LGD
however a common approach considers the exposure at default and the recovery rate that is:

LGD

\displaystyle=(1-\text{recovery rate})\times\text{exposure at default (EAD)}]

(11)

In our estimation, we assume that EAD is equivalent to the credit amount loaned out. EAD is a crucial metric that estimates the total loss a bank is exposed to when a loan defaults. However, it is important to note that the calculation of EAD can be more complex, as it may involve factors beyond the simple loan amount, such as commitment details
[
58
]
.

The recovery rate, on the other hand, is a risk-adjusted measure that reflects the portion of the loan that the bank expects to recover after default. In our analysis, we considered it as the outcome of the loan (i.e., the actual repayment).

Finally, we formalize the expected loss given default for the bank as:

LGD

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[(1-y)\times\text{amount}]

(12)

where
y
represents the applicant’s repayment probability.

2.

Expected Simple Interest (ESI):

We estimate the loss of the individuals using the simple interest formula:

\textbf{SI}=\frac{(\text{amount}\times\text{rate}\times\text{time})}{100}

For the Home Credit dataset, we used slightly modified formulae to estimate the ESI for Home Credit, as the dataset did not include information on the rate and time, making ESI estimation less straightforward.
We estimate ESI using annuity and loan amount as follows: we assume
duration
=15 years. Annuity can be written as :

\text{annuity}=\frac{(\text{amount + interest})}{\text{total durations(months)}}=\frac{(\text{amount + interest})}{12\times\text{duration}}

Using this the simple interest can be calculated as :

\displaystyle\textbf{ESI}\text{(Home Credit)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\text{annuity}\times 12\times\text{duration}-\text{amount}]

(13)

In our formulae for the German Credit dataset, the loan duration is in months and we assume in our implementation that everyone was given the same interest rate of 10%, hence enabling us to rewrite the ESI as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{ESI}\text{(German Credit)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\left[y\times\text{amount}\times\frac{\text{rate}}{100}\times\frac{\text{time}}{12}\right]

(14)

Results

Using the synthetic data generation process emulating the lending scenario as shown above, we generate balanced and unbalanced datasets and repeat our empirical analysis to i) use our novel notions to analyze
the data for treatment discrimination, and ii) explore our mitigation strategies to enable treatment-fair decisions.
Additionally, with access to the ground-truth generative process, we can consider an oracle model with the ground-truth knowledge.
Using the oracle, we can perform interventions to compute the oracle treatment discrimination values. This allows us to compare the results given by our CNF-based causal framework with the oracle.

Takeaway:

While our risk score estimation strategy shows improvement in mitigating treatment discrimination, our fairness in the decision-making strategy falls short of improving conditions for all stakeholders in the decision-making setup by merely adjusting the treatment decisions. To address this shortcoming, we need to develop more balanced and fair treatment policies

The results highlight the necessity of developing treatment policies that are not only fair but also tailored to optimize utility across diverse groups in the dataset. Designing fair and optimal policies
