Random Processes with Stationary Increments and Intrinsic Random Functions on the Real Line
Jongwook Kim, Chunfeng Huang

Abstract
Random processes with stationary increments and intrinsic random processes are two concepts commonly used to deal with non-stationary random processes. They are broader classes than stationary random processes and conceptually closely related to each other. This paper illustrates the relationship between these two concepts of stochastic processes and shows that, under certain conditions, they are equivalent on the real line.

Introduction

Although stationary assumption is widely used to analyze random processes, it is often too strict in real world. To relax the assumption of stationarity, Yaglom (1958)
[
1
]
introduced a random process with stationary increments in which distribution of increments of random variables at different temporal or spatial points only depend on time lags or distances. He also developed the spectral representations of the processes with stationary n-th increments and their structure functions, which are essential for understanding the covariance structures of the processes. When the process is intrinsic stationary, the structure function can be seen as variogram that can replace the covariance function to analyze the spatial or temporal dependencies of the stochastic processes (Cressie, 1993)
[
2
]
. Moreover, notion of structure function and its application is essential in the prediction model such as universal kriging (Zhang & Huang, 2014)
[
3
]
. Given their practicality and usefulness, they are broadly used across various fields, including statistics, physics, engineering, etc.

To address non-stationarity for similar purposes, the concept of an intrinsic random function was suggested by Matheron (1973)
[
4
]
. Such random processes are characterized by their intrinsic properties under more abstract and profound mathematical settings. Therefore, compared to a random process with stationary increments, an intrinsic random function is more flexible and applicable not only to the real line but also to various other mathematical spaces. For example, Matheron (1973)
[
4
]
illustrated how intrinsic random functions (IRFs) can be defined in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces. He demonstrated that the mean of the IRF consists of lower monomials and that the transformed process becomes stationary. Similarly, Huang (2016; 2019)
[
5
]

[
6
]
extended the concept of intrinsic random functions to the circle and sphere, highlighting the critical role of low-frequency truncation to achieve stationarity.

While a random process with stationary increments and an intrinsic function are frequently referenced and used together to address a broad class of non-stationary stochastic processes, there is insufficient literature clearly explaining the relationship between these two concepts on the real line. In this paper, we show these two concepts are in fact equivalent under specific conditions on the real line. This deepens our understanding of non-stationary random processes on the real line and allows us to utilize them in practical applications.

1.1
Random Processes with Stationary Increments

To identify random processes with stationary increments, it is necessary to define the (weakly) stationarity and its associated properties.

Definition 1.1
.

Suppose that
X(\cdot)
is a random process on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. Then, the process is called (weakly) stationary if

E\biggl(X(t)\biggl)=E\biggl(X(\tau t)\biggl)

and

Cov\biggl(X(t),X(s)\biggl)=cov\biggl(X(\tau t),X(\tau s)\biggl)

for any
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\tau\in\mathrm{T}
where
\mathrm{T}
is a group of shift operators.

Remark 1
.

(Hannan 1970)
[
7

A temporal random process

E\biggl(X(t)\biggl)=\mu\text{ where $\mu$ is a constant}

and

Cov\biggl(X(t),X(s)\biggl)=R(t-s)

for any
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1.2
.

(Wei Chen et al. 2024; Yaglom 1958)
[
8
]

[
1
]

\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is called a random process with stationary increments of order
d\in\mathbb{Z}

(d\geq 0)

is stationary where

\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}X(t-kh),\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

Additionally,

is its structure function where

D^{d}(\iota;h_{1},h_{2})=E\biggl([\Delta_{h_{1}}^{d}X(t+\iota)][\Delta_{h_{2}}^{d}X(t)]\biggl).

This is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Note that when

and

is a variogram. If

\Delta_{h}^{2}X(t)=X(t)-2X(t-h)+X(t-2h)=\{X(t)-X(t-h)\}-\{X(t-h)-X(t-2h)\}

is stationary. For a discrete time series, that is,
t\in\mathbb{Z}

d
is also called an integrated process. According to Brockwell and Davis (1991)
[
9
]

[
8
]
.

Remark 2

When

\displaystyle D(t,s)=E\biggl((X(t)-X(0))(X(s)-X(0))\biggl)=Cov(X(t),X(s))

In other words, a valid covariance function of random processes with stationary increments

1.2
Intrinsic Random Functions on the Real line

In this section, the definition and properties of an IRF on the real line
\mathbb{R}
will be examined. For this aim, it is required to identify its allowable measure.

Definition 1.3
.

(Chiles 1999)
[
11
]

A discrete measure
\lambda
is an allowable measure of order
d
on
\mathbb{R}
if it annihilates polynomials of degree less than
d
. That is, a set of
\lambda_{i}
s to
n
points
x_{i}
s on
\mathbb{R}
defines a discrete measure

\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\quad\text{where $\delta_{x_{i}}$ is the Dirac measure at $x_{i}$}

and

f(\lambda)=\int f(x)\lambda(dx)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})

Then, for a polynomial function
p(\cdot)
of degree less than
d
,

\displaystyle p(\lambda)=\int p(x)\lambda(dx)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{\ell}=0

where
\ell=0,1,2,\cdots,d-1
. We denote that
\Lambda_{d}
is a class of such allowable measures, and then it is clear that
\Lambda_{d+1}\subset\Lambda_{d}
.

Remark 6
.

(Chiles 1999)
[
11
]

An allowable measure
\lambda\in\Lambda_{d}
is closed under translations of shift. That is,

p_{d-1}(\tau_{h}\lambda)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}p_{d-1}(x_{i}+h)=0

where
p_{d-1}(\cdot)
is a polynomial function of degree
d-1
and
\tau_{h}
is a shift operator by
h
.

Definition 1.4
.

A temporal random process
X(t)
is an intrinsic random function with its order
d
, denoted IRF(d), if the process
X(\lambda)
is shift invariant for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{d}
. That is, for any allowable measure
\lambda\in\Lambda_{d}
and any
\tau\in\mathrm{T}
where
\mathrm{T}
is a group of shift operators, it satisfies that

\displaystyle E\biggl(X(\lambda)\biggl)=E\biggl(X(\tau\lambda)\biggl)

\displaystyle Cov\biggl(X(\lambda_{1}),X(\lambda_{2})\biggl)=Cov\biggl(X(\tau\lambda_{1}),X(\tau\lambda_{2})\biggl)\quad\text{where}\quad\text{any }\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{d}

Remark 7
.

A stationary random process is denoted as an IRF(0). Note that this notation differs slightly from Matheron’s (1793)
[
4
]
, where a stationary process is represented by IRF(-1). In this paper,
\kappa\geq 1
is used to describe nonstationary random processes, instead of
\kappa\geq 0
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let a random process X(t) be an IRF(d) and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{d}
. Then, a covariance function of
X(\lambda)
is called a intrinsic covariance function of order
d
(Huang 2016)
[
5
]
, which is also referred to as a generalized covariance function (Matheron 1793)
[
4
]
.

2
Connection between Random Processes with Stationary Increments Intrinsic Random Functions in
\mathbb{R}

Finally, we explore the relationship between random processes with stationary increments and intrinsic random functions, and show they are equivalent on the real line. By using the definition
1.3
and
1.4
, we can easily show that the difference operator used for random processes with stationary increments is in fact an allowable measure for an IRF(d).

Lemma 1
.

Define

\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}:=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\delta_{t-kh},\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

Suppose
X(t)
is an IRF(d). Then,
\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}
is allowable for
X(t)
and
X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})=\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)
. That is, the finite differences
\Delta_{h}^{d}
in definition
1.2
is an allowable measure for an IRF(
d
), which annihilates polynomials of degree up to
d-1
.

Proof.

First, suppose
p_{d-1}(x)
is polynomial of degree
d-1
. Then, by Remark
5
,

p_{d-1}(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}})=\Delta_{h}p_{d-1}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}a_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{i}{j}x^{j}(-h)^{i-j}

Therefore,
\Delta_{h}p_{d-1}(x)
becomes a polynomial of order
d-2
. Then, it is obvious that
\Delta_{h}^{d-1}p_{d-1}(t)
is a polynomial with degree 0, which is a constant.

Since

p_{d-1}(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})=\Delta_{h}^{d}p_{d-1}(x)=\Delta_{h}\biggl\{\Delta_{h}^{d-1}p_{d-1}(x)\biggl\},

we can conclude that

\Delta_{h}^{d}p_{d-1}(x)=0

To sum up,
\Delta_{h}^{d}
annihilates polynomials of degree
d-1
; thus, it is an allowable measure of order
d
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
Y(t)
is stationary for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
Y(\lambda)
is stationary for any allowable measure with any order, where
\lambda\in\Lambda
. That is,

E\biggl(Y(\lambda)\biggl)=E\biggl(Y(\tau_{h}\lambda)\biggl)

Cov\biggl(Y(\lambda_{x}),Y(\lambda_{y})\biggl)=Cov\biggl(Y(\tau_{h}\lambda_{x}),Y(\tau_{h}\lambda_{y})\biggl)

for any
\lambda,\lambda_{x},\lambda_{y}\in\Lambda
, and
\tau_{h}
, a shift operator with any
h\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose a random process
Y(t)
is stationary for
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
is any allowable measure with any order.

E\biggl(Y(\lambda)\biggl)=E\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Y(t_{i})\biggl)

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}E\biggl(Y(t_{i})\biggl)

By stationarity of
Y(t)
in terms of t, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}E\biggl(Y(t_{i}+h)\biggl)

Since
\tau_{h}\lambda
is still a finite measure,

=E\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Y(t_{i}+h)\biggl)=E\biggl(Y(\tau_{h}\lambda)\biggl)

Similarly, for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda
,

Cov\biggl(Y(\lambda_{1}),Y(\lambda_{2})\biggl)=Cov\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{1i}Y(t_{i}),\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j}Y(t_{j})\biggl)

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{1i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j}Cov\biggl(Y(t_{i}),Y(t_{j})\biggl)

By stationarity of
Y(t)
in terms of t, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{1i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j}Cov\biggl(Y(t_{i}+h),Y(t_{j}+h)\biggl)

=Cov\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{1i}Y(t_{i}+h),\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j}Y(t_{j}+h)\biggl)

=Cov\biggl(Y(\tau_{h}\lambda_{1}),Y(\tau_{h}\lambda_{2})\biggl)

Therefore, if
Y(t)
is stationary for
t
, then
Y(\lambda)
is stationary for
\lambda
, an allowable measure with any order.
∎

With the definitions, remarks and lemmas provided, we can now establish the connection between intrinsic random functions and random processes with stationary increments on the real line.

Theorem 1
.

For
d\geq 1
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, a stochastic process
X(t)
is an intrinsic random function of order
d
(IRF(d)) on the real line if and only if
X(t)
is a random process with stationary increments order
d
provided that the condition in Remark
4
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Let a random process
X(\cdot)
be an intrinsic random function with order
d
, and define

\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}:=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\delta_{t-kh},\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

Then,
X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})=\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)
and by Lemma
1
,
\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}
is allowable with its degree
d
on
\mathbb{R}
.

By the definition
1.4
of an intrinsic random function, for an allowable measure
\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}},\lambda_{\Delta_{h,s}^{d}}\in\Lambda_{d}
and
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle E\biggl(X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})\biggl)=E\biggl(X(\tau\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})\biggl)

(6)

\displaystyle Cov\biggl(X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}),X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,s}^{d}})\biggl)=Cov\biggl(X(\tau\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}}),X(\tau\lambda_{\Delta_{h,s}^{d}})\biggl)

(7)

We know that
X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}^{d}})=\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)
and
X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,s}^{d}})=\Delta_{h}^{d}X(s)
. Thus, (
6
) and (
7
) can also be expressed as

\displaystyle E\biggl(\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)\biggl)=E\biggl(\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)\biggl)

\displaystyle Cov\biggl(\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t),\Delta_{h}^{d}X(s)\biggl)=Cov\biggl(\Delta_{h}^{d}X(\tau t),\Delta_{h}^{d}X(\tau s)\biggl)

Hence,
\Delta_{h}^{d}X(t)
is stationary, which implies that
X(t)
is a random process with stationary increments of order
d
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose
X_{d}(t)
is a random process with stationary increments of order
d
. Then, by its spectral representation given in Remark
3
and the condition in Remark
4
,

X_{d}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(e^{it\omega}-1-it\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)

Then,

\displaystyle X_{d}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(e^{it\omega}-1-it\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)\lambda(dt)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(e^{it_{j}\omega}-1-it_{j}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{j}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\biggl(e^{it_{j}\omega}-1-it_{j}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{j}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)

By the definition of an allowable measure, any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{d}
annihilates polynomials of its order
d-1
. Hence,

=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{it_{j}\omega}dZ(\omega)

By defining
Y^{\prime}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{it\omega}dZ(\omega)
, which is a stationary process for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}Y^{\prime}(t_{j})=Y^{\prime}(\lambda)

Since
Y^{\prime}(t)
is stationary for
t
,
Y^{\prime}(\lambda)
is stationary for
\lambda
by Lemma
2
. Therefore,
X_{d}(\lambda)
is stationary for
\lambda
.
In conclusion,
X(t)
is an intrinsic random function of order
d
.

∎

As a result of Theorem
1
, we can conclude that for an I(d) process

, the stationary covariance function of

3
Applications and Discussions

So far, we have demonstrated the equivalence between random processes with stationary increments and intrinsic random functions on the real line. This fact allows us to apply the theories of each concept to the other, enhancing our understanding of both. In this section, we explore applications and examples that illustrate how the combined theories of random processes with stationary increments and an intrinsic random function can be used to deepen our understanding of non-stationary random processes on the real line.

3.1
Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is a well-known random process with stationary increments of order 1, where differencing induces stationarity in the process. Therefore, its spectral representation and covariance function can be derived using the form outlined in Remark
2
and
3
, under the conditions that
X(0)=X_{0}=0
(Yaglom 1987)
[
10
]
.

\displaystyle X(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}-1}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

\displaystyle D(t,s)=Cov\biggl(X(t),X(s)\biggl)=\begin{cases}C\text{ min}\biggl\{|t|,|s|\biggl\}\quad&\text{for}\quad ts\geq 0\\
0\quad&\text{for}\quad ts<0\end{cases}

where
dZ^{*}(\omega)=\sqrt{2\pi}i\omega dZ(\omega)
and C is a positive constant.

By Theorem
1
, Brownian Motion can also be considered as an IRF(1). Therefore, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
,
X(\lambda)
should be stationary in terms of
\lambda
.

\displaystyle X(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}-1}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)\lambda(dt)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}-1}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\frac{e^{it\omega}-1}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

Since
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
,

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\frac{e^{it\omega}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)\lambda(dt)

\displaystyle=Y(\lambda)\text{ where }Y(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

This works for any allowable measure
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
. Thus, if we use the differencing measure
\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}}\in\Lambda_{1}
,

\displaystyle X_{\Delta_{h,t}}(t):

\displaystyle=X(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}})=Y(\lambda_{\Delta_{h,t}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{i(t-h)\omega}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{it\omega}\biggl\{1-e^{-ih\omega}\biggl\}}{i\omega}dZ^{*}(\omega)

Then, the corresponding ICF(1) is

\displaystyle Cov\biggl(X_{\Delta_{h,t}}(t),X_{\Delta_{h,s}}(t+\iota)\biggl)

\displaystyle=D(\iota)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\iota\omega}\frac{(1-e^{-ih\omega})(1-e^{ih\omega})}{\omega^{2}}dF^{*}(\omega)

where
dF^{*}(\omega)=<|dZ^{*}(\omega)|^{2}>
. Hence, the intrinsic covariance function
Cov\biggl(X_{\Delta_{h,t}}(t),X_{\Delta_{h,s}}(t+\iota)\biggl)
is stationary with respect to
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.2
Universal Kriging

Universal kriging is a commonly used application of an IRF(d) in spatial statistics, where the goal is to find the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE), which minimizes the mean-squared prediction error, to predict values in unsampled locations. Let
X(t)
be an intrinsic random process with order
d
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, given the allowable measure
\lambda_{t_{0}}\in\Lambda_{d}
such that
\lambda_{t_{0}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\delta_{t_{i}}-\delta_{t_{0}}
, we can express the mean-squared prediction error for any unsampled index
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
as:

\displaystyle E\biggl(X(\lambda_{t_{0}})\biggl)^{2}=E\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}X(t_{i})-X(t_{0})\biggl)^{2}\quad\text{where $t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ are the observed indices from the dataset.}

Then, by Theorem
1
and Remark
3
,

\displaystyle=E\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(e^{it_{i}\omega}-1-it_{i}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{i}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(e^{it_{0}\omega}-1-it_{0}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{0}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)dZ(\omega)\biggl]^{2}

\displaystyle=E\Biggl[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\biggl(e^{it_{i}\omega}-1-it_{i}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{i}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)-\biggl(e^{it_{0}\omega}-1-it_{0}\omega-\cdots-\frac{(it_{0}\omega)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\biggl)\Biggl\}dZ(\omega)\Biggl]^{2}

\displaystyle\text{Since }\lambda_{t_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\delta_{t_{i}}-\delta_{t_{0}}\in\Lambda_{d},

\displaystyle=E\biggl[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}e^{it_{i}\omega}-e^{it_{0}\omega}\biggl)dZ(\omega)\biggl]^{2}=E\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{it_{i}\omega}dZ(\omega)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{it_{0}\omega}dZ(\omega)\biggl]^{2}

\displaystyle\text{Define }Y^{\prime}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{it\omega}dZ(\omega).

\displaystyle=E\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}Y^{\prime}(t_{i})-Y^{\prime}(t_{0})\biggl]^{2}=E\biggl[Y^{\prime}(\lambda_{t_{0}})\biggl]^{2}

Therefore, by considering the measurement of error
\sigma^{2}
and the constraints from the allowable measure
\lambda_{t_{0}}
, the object function to minimize is

M(\underset{\sim}{\eta})=\sigma^{2}\underset{\sim}{\eta}^{T}\underset{\sim}{\eta}+\underset{\sim}{\eta}^{T}\Psi\underset{\sim}{\eta}-2\underset{\sim}{\eta}^{T}\underset{\sim}{\phi}+\phi(0)+2(\underset{\sim}{\eta}^{T}Q-\underset{\sim}{q}^{T})\underset{\sim}{\rho}

where

\underset{\sim}{\eta}=\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\cdots,\eta_{n}\}^{T}
,
\Psi=\{\phi(t_{i}-t_{j})\}_{n\times n}
,
\underset{\sim}{\phi}=\{\phi(t_{1}-t_{0}),\phi(t_{2}-t_{0}),\cdots,\phi(t_{n}-t_{0})\}^{T}
,
\underset{\sim}{q}=\{1,t,t^{2},\cdots,t^{d-1}\}^{T}
,  and
Q=\underset{\sim}{q}\underset{\sim}{q}^{T}
.

The function
\phi(\cdot)
is an intrinsic covariance function of
X(\cdot)
; that is, it is the stationary covariance function of
Y^{\prime}(\cdot)
. The
d\times 1
vector
\underset{\sim}{\rho}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraints on the allowable measure
\lambda_{t_{0}}
. As a result, we can obtain the coefficient for the kriging predictor such that

\underset{\sim}{\eta}=(\Psi+\sigma^{2}I)^{-1}\Biggl[\underset{\sim}{\phi}+Q\biggl\{Q^{T}(\Psi+\sigma^{2}I)^{-1}Q\biggl\}^{-1}\biggl\{\underset{\sim}{q}-Q^{T}(\Psi+\sigma^{2}I)^{-1}\underset{\sim}{\phi}\biggl\}\Biggl].

This demonstrates that the kriging estimate for intrinsic random functions can also be derived using the spectral representations of random processes with stationary increments on the real line.
