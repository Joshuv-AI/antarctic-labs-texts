Renormalization group for spectral collapse in random matrices with power-law variance profiles
Philipp Fleig

Abstract
We propose a renormalization group (RG) approach to compare and collapse eigenvalue densities of random matrix models of complex systems across different system sizes. The approach is to fix a natural spectral scale by letting the model normalization run with size, turning raw spectra into comparable, collapsed density curves. We demonstrate this approach on generalizations of two classic random matrix ensembles—Wigner and Wishart—modified to have power-law variance profiles. We use random matrix theory methods to derive self-consistent fixed-point equations for the resolvent to compute their eigenvalue densities, we define an RG scheme based on matrix decimation, and compute the Beta function controlling the RG flow as a function of the variance profile power-law exponent. The running normalization leads to spectral collapse which we confirm in simulations and solutions of the fixed-point equations. We expect this RG approach to carry over to other ensembles, providing a method for data analysis of a broad range of complex systems.



I
Introduction

Many large data sets of complex systems with
N
interacting units
are naturally summarized by matrices: correlations between neurons
Cunningham and Yu 2014
or genes
Eisen et al. 1998
, couplings in materials
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
, network connectivities
Albert and Barabási 2002
, or interaction kernels in collective behaviour
Bialek et al. 2012
. A compact, system‑level descriptor is the spectral distribution—the distribution of matrix eigenvalues—which encodes properties such as variability, stability, and characteristic scales of the system
Wigner 1958
;
Marčenko and Pastur 1967
;
Sompolinsky et al. 1988
;
Mastrogiuseppe and Ostojic 2018
. In practice, across different studies or different runs of an experiment, data matrices may be recorded or simulated at different system sizes
N
. However, one would like to extract properties of the system in a size-independent way. This raises a basic technical question: how should one compare spectra across matrix sizes, and how can they be
collapsed
onto a common reference curve?

Random matrix theory (RMT) provides a useful language to address this question. It offers a foundation for high‑dimensional statistics with macroscopic laws that are universal, i.e. insensitive to microscopic details
Wigner 1958
;
Bai and Silverstein 2010
;
Anderson et al. 2010
;
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
;
Couillet and Debbah 2011
. Predictions include principled null models, signal–noise separation, detection and stability thresholds, and phase transitions in high‑dimensional inference
Marčenko and Pastur 1967
;
Laloux et al. 1999
;
Fleig and Nemenman 2022
;
Sompolinsky et al. 1988
;
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
;
Lesieur et al. 2017
. Random matrix theory also supplies standard normalizations and finite‑
N
edge fluctuations—e.g. Wigner’s variance scaling, the aspect ratio in Marčenko–Pastur, and soft Tracy–Widom edges
Wigner 1958
;
Marčenko and Pastur 1967
;
Tracy and Widom 1994
;
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
.
However, these asymptotic normalizations and edge scalings standardize particular regimes (e.g. bulk and microscopic edge regimes), but they do not provide a general scale‑setting rule for collapsing entire spectral curves across different matrix sizes.

We address this gap by adopting a
renormalization group
(RG) viewpoint
Kadanoff 1966
;
Kogut 1979
;
Goldenfeld 2018
. The idea is simple and operational: choose a natural spectral scale (e.g. mean level, bandwidth, etc.) to hold fixed as the matrix size changes—analogous to a renormalization condition—and let a single normalization factor run with matrix size under a coarse‑graining scheme. We show that with such a running normalization, spectra at different sizes collapse and can be compared on equal footing, while the residual size‑dependence is funneled into a single running coupling governed by a Beta function
Wilson and Kogut 1974
. We note that an RG‑flavored perspective of RMT in high‑dimensional regression has been explored in
Atanasov et al. 2024
.

We illustrate the recipe on two generalizations of classic random matrix (RM) ensembles—Wigner
Wigner 1958
and Wishart
Marčenko and Pastur 1967
—
with
power‑law variance profiles
. Ensembles with such a power-law structure are motivated by heavy‑tailed participation and coupling strengths in neural circuits and networks
Buzsáki and Mizuseki 2014
;
Rajan and Abbott 2006
;
Masuda et al. 2017
, and by heterogeneous sampling in high‑dimensional data
Alter et al. 2000
;
Cunningham and Yu 2014
;
Luecken and Theis 2019
.
Using RMT methods, we derive self‑consistent fixed-point equations for the finite‑
N
RM resolvent, solve them numerically to obtain the eigenvalue density, and find excellent agreement with simulations. We then derive the running normalization that fixes a chosen spectral scale, demonstrate spectral collapse, define a coarse-graining scheme (via matrix decimation) and compute the Beta function to delineate relevant, marginal, and irrelevant regimes of the RG flow.
At the marginal exponent, the Wigner-type ensemble has a non-trivial RG fixed point, whereas the Wishart-type ensemble exhibits marginal flow with logarithmic corrections and approaches the trivial limit only logarithmically slowly.
Finally, we discuss the large‑
N
limit where the coupling flow is governed by a Callan–Symanzik equation
Callan 1970
;
Symanzik 1970
and derive an integral equation for the large-
N
RM resolvent. We will provide full details for the Wigner-type ensemble and present the key results for the Wishart-type ensemble.

II
Wigner ensemble with power-law variance profile

II.1
Ensemble definition

Given system size
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we construct the symmetric
N\times N
random matrix
\mathbf{X}_{N}=(X_{jk})
with independent centered entries:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{jk}]

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad X_{jk}=X_{kj}\,,\quad 1\leq j\leq k\leq N\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{jk})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\,j^{-\alpha}k^{-\alpha}\,,\quad j<k\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{jj})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\,j^{-2\alpha}\,,

(3)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the power-law exponent,
\sigma^{2}>0
a constant variance scaling factor, and
\gamma>0
the normalization factor whose form we are going to determine through our RG analysis. Thus, the RM ensemble consists of matrices with independent, centered entries and variances as defined in Eqs. (
2
)–(
3
). The variances follow a power-law profile that decays as we move diagonally across the matrix, starting from the upper left corner.
For
\alpha=0
the variance profile becomes constant across matrix entries and by setting the normalization to scale linearly with matrix size,
\gamma\equiv N
, the definition of the standard Wigner ensemble is recovered
Wigner 1958
;
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
.

II.2
RMT definitions

Our first goal will be to determine the eigenvalue density and we start by setting some notation. For a random matrix
\mathbf{X}_{N}
of size
N\times N
, we define the
empirical resolvent
(or Green’s function) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z):=\big(z\,\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{X}_{N}\big)^{-1}\,,\quad z=x+i\eta\,,\ \eta>0\,.

(4)

Taking the normalized trace yields at finite
N
:

\displaystyle G_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z):=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)\,.

(5)

For a self-averaging normalized trace,
G_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)
concentrates around its
deterministic equivalent
,
G_{N}(z)

\displaystyle G_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)\simeq G_{N}(z)\,,

(6)

up to fluctuations of order
(N\eta)^{-1/2}
(see Appendix
A
). The finite-
N
eigenvalue density is obtained as

\displaystyle\rho_{N,\eta}(x)=-\,\frac{1}{\pi}\,\Im\,G_{N}(x+i\,\eta)\,,

(7)

and analogously from
G_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}
, up to errors. If
G_{N}(z)
converges in the large-
N
limit, we denote the limit by
\overline{G}(z)
and obtain the eigenvalue density from
\rho(x)=-\,\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,\Im\,\overline{G}(x+i0^{+})
. In what follows we work with the deterministic fixed-point objects and only write out the label “
\mathrm{emp}
” explicitly, when needed to avoid ambiguity.

In the derivation of deterministic fixed-point equations for the resolvent, we will for simplicity sometimes assume that the matrix entries
X_{ik}
are Gaussian. However, we note that the fixed‑point structure depends only on the variance profile rather than exact Gaussianity. The same set of equations can be derived without assuming Gaussianity (assuming standard moment and tail control
Ajanki et al. 2017
;
Ajanki et al. 2019
;
Knowles and Yin 2017
instead). In simulations we use Gaussian entries. Additionally, we provide numerical evidence hinting at insensitivity of the spectral density to the entry law within a finite‑variance class (including a heavy‑tailed example). Throughout the article we adopt physics-style reasoning, instead of full mathematical rigor.

II.3
Fixed-point equation

Our first goal is to write down a system of
self-consistent
equations for the entries of the resolvent and solve it to obtain the eigenvalue density. Here, we provide a heuristic derivation of the self-consistent equations, giving the full-length derivation in Appendix
B
.
We start from the
matrix Dyson equation
(MDE)
Ajanki et al. 2019
;
Alt et al. 2020
for the deterministic equivalent:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=z\,\mathbf{I}_{N}-\bm{\Sigma}(z)\,,\text{ with }\bm{\Sigma}(z)=\mathcal{S}\!\big[\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\big]\,,

(8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}
is the Dyson self–energy, and is given by a variance operator
\mathcal{S}
(determined by second moments) acting on the resolvent.

To treat the Wigner ensemble with power-law variance profile it is convenient to introduce the following notation

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle:=j^{-\alpha}\,,\quad w:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{N})^{T}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{diag}(w_{1}^{2},\ldots,w_{N}^{2})\,,

(10)

where we refer to the
w_{j}
’s as variance weights. The variance operator
\mathcal{S}
for the Wigner ensemble with power-law profile is given by the variance matrix
\mathbf{S}
which, by Eqs. (
2
)-(
3
), is given by

\displaystyle s_{jk}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\,w_{j}w_{k}\,,&j\neq k\\[6.0pt]
\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\,w_{j}^{2}\,,&j=k\end{cases}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{S}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}[ww^{T}+\,\mathbf{D}]\,.

(11)

This variance operator preserves diagonality.
In other words, at the deterministic fixed point, both
\mathbf{G}_{N}
and
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{G}_{N}
are diagonal

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{G}_{N}\big)_{jj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{jk}\,(\mathbf{G}_{N})_{kk}(z)\,,\quad\big(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{G}_{N}\big)_{ij}=0\,,

(12)

for
i\neq j
and the self-consistent equations close on the diagonal

\displaystyle(\mathbf{G}_{N})_{jj}(z)=\frac{1}{z-\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{jk}\,(\mathbf{G}_{N})_{kk}(z)}\,.

(13)

With
s_{ik}
from Eq. (
11
) this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{j}}=z-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\big[w_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}G_{k}+\,w_{j}^{2}\,G_{j}\big]\,,

(14)

where we have defined,
G_{j}:=(\mathbf{G}_{N})_{jj}
.
From this equation, we define two parameters

\displaystyle c:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\,,\quad\text{and }\quad u(z):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}\,G_{k}(z)\,,

(15)

which we refer to as the
coupling
and the
variance‑weighted mean field
, respectively.
In Eq. (
14
), site
j
experiences the variance‑weighted mean field,
w_{j}\,u(z)
, while the diagonal contribution,
w_{j}^{2}\,G_{j}
, is a local term.
Overall, for fixed
u(z)
, each
G_{j}
solves a scalar quadratic equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{j}}=z-c(w_{j}u+w_{j}^{2}G_{j})\,,

(16)

with the physical branch given by
\Im\,G_{j}(z)<0
.
Equations (
15
)–(
16
) reduce the problem to a one-dimensional, self-consistent fixed-point equation for
u(z)
, from which
G_{j}(z)
and hence also the normalized trace,
G_{N}(z)
, follow. The fixed point serves as a deterministic predictor that approximates
G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{N}(z)
up to local-law errors, cf. Appendix
A
.

As a sanity check, we verify the reduction to the Wigner semicircle law.
For
\alpha=0
,
w_{j}=1
for all
j
. By symmetry,
G_{j}(z)=G_{N}(z)
, and
u=NG_{N}
. Summing the self-consistent equation in Eq. (
16
) over sites, we obtain a scalar Dyson equation of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{N}}=z-c(u+G_{N})=z-c\,(N+1)G_{N}\,,

(17)

such that

\displaystyle G_{N}(z)=\frac{z\,\pm\,\sqrt{z^{2}-R^{2}}}{2\,c\,(N{+}1)}\,,\quad R=2\,\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}(N{+}1)}\,.

(18)

Thus, the limiting eigenvalue density is the semicircle with radius
R
. Keeping the normalization
\gamma
general makes the scaling explicit. The standard Wigner scaling
\gamma=N
yields
R\to 2\sigma\,\sqrt{1+1/N}\to 2\sigma
as
N\to\infty
, i.e. the classical Wigner semicircle law with variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
.

We now turn to an RG analysis of the power-law-structured Wigner ensemble and start by deriving a running normalization
\gamma(N)
that collapses eigenvalue densities onto each other.

III.1
RG condition and spectral collapse

The second moment of
\mathbf{X}_{N}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{2}=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{X}_{N}^{2}]\,,

(19)

which equals the expected average of squared eigenvalues (the first moment vanishes by Eq. (
1
)). We choose the second moment (the typical bandwidth) as the spectral scale that we want to keep constant under renormalization. Fixing the second moment to a constant value
m_{2}^{\star}
:

\displaystyle m_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}m_{2}^{\star}\,,

(20)

is analogous to fixing an energy density per degree of freedom in statistical mechanics or a variance per site in disordered systems
Kogut 1979
;
Mézard et al. 1987
. In the next section we introduce an RG scheme and we are going to interpret Eq. (
20
) as a
renormalization condition
. To satisfy Eq. (
20
) we introduce the normalization
\gamma(N)
which
runs
with system size and equally the running coupling,
c(N)=\sigma^{2}/\gamma(N)
, cf. Eq. (
15
). It is natural to impose
m_{2}^{\star}
to be of order unity and without loss of generalization, we set
m_{2}^{\star}\equiv 1
in all calculations and figures.

Using the ensemble symmetry
X_{jk}=X_{kj}
, from Eq. (
19
) we compute the following expression for the second moment

\displaystyle m_{2}=c\,r_{N}(\alpha)\,,

(21)

where

\displaystyle r_{N}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\Big[\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\big)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{N}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N}(2\alpha)}{N}\,,

(22)

and we have used the definition of the
generalized harmonic sum

\displaystyle H_{N}(\theta):=\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}j^{-\theta}\,.

(23)

With the definition of the coupling in Eq. (
15
), and the renormalization condition Eq. (
20
), Eq. (
21
) implies:

\displaystyle\gamma(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{m_{2}^{\star}}\,r_{N}(\alpha)\,,

(24)

such that the normalization is
N
-dependent.
For the RG analysis we are interested in the dominant scaling behaviour with
N
. We note the following scaling regimes of the generalized harmonic sum:
H_{N}(\theta)\sim\tfrac{N^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta}
for
\theta<1
,
H_{N}(\theta)\sim\log N
for
\theta=1
, and
H_{N}(\theta)\to\zeta(\theta)
for
\theta>1
, cf. Appendix
E
. We find the dominant large-
N
behaviour of
r_{N}(\alpha)
is

\displaystyle r_{N}(\alpha)\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\,N^{1-2\alpha},&0\leq\alpha<1,\ \alpha\neq 1/2\,,\\[6.0pt]
\mathrm{const.},&\alpha=1/2\,,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{\log^{2}N}{N},&\alpha=1\,,\\[6.0pt]
N^{-1},&\alpha>1\,,\end{cases}

(25)

which we confirm numerically in Appendix Fig.
5
.
Thus, we find
\alpha
-dependent power-law scaling of the normalization with the system size
N
for
\alpha\geq 0
, except at
\alpha=1/2
where
\gamma(N)=O(1)
, and a logarithmic correction at
\alpha=1
.
We note that by Eq. (
15
) the coupling scales inverse to the normalization.

With the normalization
\gamma(N)
chosen to keep the second moment constant, the finite-
N
spectral densities obtained from simulations of the ensemble at different values of
N
and fixed exponent
\alpha

collapse
onto a common curve. In Figs.
2(a)
–
2(b)
we show a comparison of raw and collapsed eigenvalue densities. For
N=200
, we also show the one standard deviation band due to simulation sampling noise. While the raw curves (left subplots with
\gamma=1
normalization) generally do not lie within the sampling noise band, the collapsed curves (right subplots with
\gamma(N)
normalization) do and largely collapse onto each other. From the collapsed curves we observe some small effects of non-uniform edge convergence. Overall, this demonstrates approximate spectral invariance along the RG trajectory in finite-
N
simulations: after fixing the second moment via the running normalization
\gamma(N)
, the eigenvalue density is independent of system size up to finite‑
N
corrections. We note that the collapse discussed above concerns the bulk density. For
\alpha>1
the bulk density degenerates to a delta mass at zero, and only a finite number of
O(1)
–outliers associated with small index values remain. These outliers are not self-averaging.

III.2
Decimation (coarse-graining) procedure

Next, we introduce an RG coarse-graining procedure via matrix
decimation
and interpret the running normalization and coupling in this context. We define that one decimation step consists of removing a fraction of matrix indices corresponding to the smallest weights
w_{j}
(largest index values). We fix a scale factor
b>1
and retain indices
j\leq\lfloor N/b\rfloor
. The retained block,
\mathbf{X}^{(\mathrm{dec})}
, inherits the same variance profile with weights
w^{(\mathrm{dec})}_{j}=j^{-\alpha}
with
j=1,\ldots,\lfloor N/b\rfloor
, up to rescaling.
From Eq. (
21
) we obtain the coarse-grained second moment

\displaystyle m_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N/b}\,\mathbb{E}\,[\mathrm{Tr}\,{\mathbf{X}^{(\mathrm{dec})}}^{2}]=c^{\prime}\,\frac{H_{N/b}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N/b}(2\alpha)}{N/b}\,,

(26)

where we implicitly apply the floor function to non-integer values of
N/b
.
To enforce the renormalization condition in Eq. (
20
) under one decimation step
N\mapsto N/b
, we choose the renormalized coupling
c^{\prime}
so that
m_{2}^{\prime}(N/b;c^{\prime})=m_{2}(N;c)
. This yields the discrete RG update for the running coupling and normalization:

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\prime}}{c}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{\prime}}\sim\frac{1}{b}\,\frac{H_{N}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N}(2\alpha)}{H_{N/b}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N/b}(2\alpha)}\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;b^{1-2\alpha}\,.

(27)

If we take a total of
n
decimation steps with fixed scale factor
b>1
, the retained system size
K_{n}
is

\displaystyle K_{n}:=\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{b^{n}}\Big\rfloor\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad n=\frac{\log(N/K_{n})}{\log b}\,,

(28)

with
K_{0}=N
. Iterating the update in Eq. (
27
) yields the
n
-step flow for the coupling

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n}}{c_{0}}\sim\prod_{\bar{n}=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{b}\,\frac{H_{N/b^{\bar{n}}}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N/b^{\bar{n}}}(2\alpha)}{H_{N/b^{\bar{n}+1}}(\alpha)^{2}+H_{N/b^{\bar{n}+1}}(2\alpha)}\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;b^{\,n(1-2\alpha)}\,,

(29)

such that asymptotically,
c_{n}\propto b^{n(1-2\alpha)}
, or, using Eq. (
28
):

\displaystyle c_{n}\propto\Big(\frac{N}{K_{n}}\Big)^{1-2\alpha}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\gamma(K_{n};N)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c_{n}}\propto K_{n}^{\,1-2\alpha}\,,

(30)

where the proportionality is up to a constant that does not depend on
K_{n}
(for fixed initial
K_{0}=N
).

In Figs.
2(c)
–
2(d)
, we show the running of the normalization
\gamma(K)
and coupling
c(K)
with retained system size
K
, respectively.
Dashed lines serve as reference to indicate the theoretical scaling exponent,
1-2\alpha
, for the RG flow under decimation.
Observed deviations from the reference lines for
\alpha>1/2
arise from finite-size effects, competition of scaling, and slow log-corrections.

III.3
Beta function and RG regimes

We define the running log-coupling,
\kappa:=\log c
, with
c(K_{n})=\sigma^{2}/\gamma(K_{n})
. Under one decimation step with per-step scale factor
b
, the discrete update is

\displaystyle\kappa_{n+1}-\kappa_{n}=(1-2\alpha)\,\log b

(31)

which integrates to

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\kappa_{0}+n(1-2\alpha)\,\log b\,.

(32)

Writing the total scale factor as,
B:=b^{n}
, and defining the RG scale,
s:=\log B
, the continuous infinitesimal flow along the RG trajectory with
m_{2}
fixed becomes

\displaystyle\beta^{b}_{\mathrm{RG}}(c):=\frac{d\kappa}{ds}=\frac{d\log c}{d\log B}=1-2\alpha\,.

(33)

This is the
Beta function

Wilson and Kogut 1974
for
c
of the RM ensemble.
It reveals three, physically distinct, regimes under decimation:

Relevant
(
\alpha<1/2
;
\beta^{b}_{\mathrm{RG}}>0
):
Under decimation to a smaller retained size
K
,
\gamma(K)\propto K^{1-2\alpha}
decreases as
K
decreases, while
c(K)
increases under decimation. This is the RG sense in which the coupling is relevant.

Marginal
(
\alpha=1/2
;
\beta^{b}_{\mathrm{RG}}=0
): The coupling sits at an RG fixed point (a fixed line) and the Beta function changes sign at marginality. Since the fixed point remains interacting (non-vanishing self-energy and hence not the free resolvent,
1/z
), the fixed point is non-trivial. The normalization
\gamma(K)
tends to a constant and the coupling
c(K)
is scale-stationary. Any residual drift under decimation is a finite-size effect.

Irrelevant
(
\alpha>1/2
;
\beta^{b}_{\mathrm{RG}}<0
):
Under decimation to smaller
K
,
\gamma(K)
increases and
c(K)
decreases, i.e. the coupling weakens under decimation. As already noted above, for
\alpha>1
, the bulk density degenerates and non-self-averaging outliers can appear.

We now investigate the RG flow in the large-
N
limit.
For this, we set the RG scale,
s:=\log N
, and let the coupling run as
c(s)=\sigma^{2}/\gamma(s)
so that the spectral scale (second moment) remains fixed along the flow. The running of the coupling is characterized by the Beta function

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{RG}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{d\log c}{d\log N}=-(1-2\alpha)

(34)

with a logarithmic correction at
\alpha=1
. In this alternative definition, the Beta function differs by a sign from the Beta function in Eq. (
33
). Both are valid forms of the Beta function for the RM ensemble.
Along this RG trajectory, spectral observables should be invariant to changes in the scale
s
when the induced change in the coupling
c(s)
is taken into account. One such observable is the mean resolvent,
G_{N}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}(z)
. In the large-

\overline{G}
. In Appendix
F
, we derive an integral equation for
\overline{G}
and solve it numerically.

Invariance along the flow is stated as,
d\overline{G}/ds=0
. Separating explicit and implicit scale dependence, through
c=c(s)
, yields the equation

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{s}+\beta_{\mathrm{RG}}\,c\,\partial_{c}\big)\,\overline{G}(z;c)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(35)

where we have used the Beta function in Eq. (
34
). This equation is known as the Callan–Symanzik equation
Callan 1970
;
Symanzik 1970
and holds in the large-
N
limit along the RG trajectory. The equation captures how a shift in the scale
s
is compensated by an induced change of the coupling
c
, leaving the mean resolvent
\overline{G}
invariant.

At finite
N
, the quantity
d\,G_{N}/ds
,
is generally non-zero due to finite-size effects,
and the Callan–Symanzik equation is not satisfied exactly.
However, as we previously showed for simulated data in Figs.

–
2(b)
, finite-size effects are controlled, implying that approximate invariance, and therefore collapse, of the eigenvalue density across different sizes
N
, is still observed for a running coupling.
As an additional step, we demonstrate the approximate invariance on the level of the resolvent.
For fixed
\alpha

)–(
16
), for different

N
to good approximation. In Appendix
D
, we discuss our choice of
\eta
values for the raw and collapsed curves.

The predictive power of the RG comes from
universality
, which means that the RG flow is not an artifact of distributional choices of the model. For our RM ensemble this could mean that its universality class is determined by the power-law exponent and the multiplicative variance weights, but that spectral observables are largely insensitive to the particular choice of matrix entry law (subject to some conditions). In Appendix Fig.

V
Discussion

We used an RG perspective to compare eigenvalue spectra across matrix sizes and implemented it for two structured ensembles—Wigner and Wishart with power‑law variance profiles. These models, or variants thereof, may capture interaction and covariance structure in complex systems, such as heterogeneous synaptic strengths and participation in neural circuits, diffusion and consensus on heterogeneous networks
Olfati-Saber et al. 2007
;
Chung et al. 2003
;
Masuda et al. 2017
, and sample‑covariance spectra in finance, climate, neuroscience, and genomics where heterogeneous sampling is intrinsic
Laloux et al. 1999
;
Plerou et al. 1999
;
von Storch and Zwiers 1999
;
Cunningham and Yu 2014
;
Alter et al. 2000
.

The collapsed spectral distribution yields size‑aware dynamical thresholds across different systems. For linear dynamics built from an interaction matrix, stability changes when the right spectral edge crosses the control baseline, and the slowest relaxation time is set by the distance to that edge
May 1972
.
For consensus and diffusion on networks, the Laplacian spectral gap controls mixing times and convergence rates
Olfati-Saber et al. 2007
;
Chung et al. 2003
. Finally,
in neural network dynamics, the spectral radius marks the onset of unstable or chaotic activity
Sompolinsky et al. 1988
;
Cunningham and Yu 2014
.
Because the RG normalization collapses spectra across sizes, the edge and gap thresholds can be set once at a reference size (in collapsed units) and then applied unchanged across
N
, yielding consistent stability and relaxation criteria. Applying the RG-RMT approach to concrete systems is a natural next step.

To make the recipe operational on data, one should quantify uncertainty—i.e. statistical variability across realizations at fixed
N
(sampling noise)—and separate it from deterministic finite‑size drift (
1/N
bias along the RG trajectory), for example by reporting confidence bands for smoothed densities and edge locations (at fixed resolution). Furthermore, sharper finite‑
N
controls near marginality and at spectral edges would bound these drifts and strengthen guarantees behind collapse and Callan–Symanzik invariance.

We have shown that the RG behaviour is non‑trivial yet analytically tractable. While we do not claim full universality, our evidence provides first hints that after scale fixing, the bulk and edges are largely insensitive to the entry law. A systematic program—varying entry distributions and adding mild structural perturbations—would delineate the empirical domain of attraction of the collapsed spectral distribution and its edge.

Finally, for the two ensembles we have studied, the power‑law structure suggested a natural coarse‑graining procedure via matrix decimation. Extending RG–RMT beyond these cases will likely require model‑specific coarse‑graining rules. Several complementary approaches have been explored on real and synthetic data
Mehta and Schwab 2014
;
Koch-Janusz and Ringel 2018
;
Bradde and Bialek 2017
;
Meshulam et al. 2019
;
Nicoletti et al. 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2025
, and connecting them to the present RG framework is a promising direction.

Appendix A
(Anisotropic) local laws

In RMT we distinguish spectral resolutions by the scaling of the imaginary part
\eta
of
z=x+i\eta
: macroscopic resolution keeps
\eta
fixed as
N\to\infty
; mesoscopic resolution lets
\eta
shrink with
N
but still large enough to cover many levels; microscopic resolution probes the mean spacing scale. Concretely:

\displaystyle\eta=O(1)\ \ \text{(macroscopic)}\,,

(45)

\displaystyle N^{-1+\varepsilon}\leq

\displaystyle\eta\ll 1\ \ \text{(mesoscopic)}\,,

(46)

\displaystyle\eta\lesssim N^{-1}\ \ \text{(microscopic)}\,,

(47)

for some fixed
\varepsilon>0
. On mesoscopic scales,
local laws

Ajanki et al. 2017
;
Ajanki et al. 2019
quantitatively control and imply the self‑averaging of resolvents. Recall that
\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}
and
\mathbf{G}_{N}

denote the empirical resolvent and its deterministic equivalent, respectively.
Then averaged statistics concentrate uniformly off the real axis:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)\simeq\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)\big)-\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\big)\bigg|\ll 1\,,

(49)

with typical error scale
(N\eta)^{-1/2}
. In the equations “
\simeq
” denotes equality up to controlled, vanishing errors. Or, equivalently,
\,\mathbb{E}[G_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)]=G_{N}(z)+O((N\eta)^{-1/2})
on mesoscopic domains. So either route, expectation or deterministic equivalent, yields the same self‑consistent fixed point at the level of accuracy considered here.

The
anisotropic local laws

Knowles and Yin 2017
;
Ajanki et al. 2019
strengthen this to uniform control of quadratic forms:

\displaystyle u^{T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)\,v\simeq u^{T}\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,v\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\sup_{\|u\|=\|v\|=1}\,\big|u^{T}\big(\mathbf{G}_{N}^{\mathrm{emp}}(z)-\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\big)v\big|\ll 1\,,

(51)

where
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for real ensembles are deterministic unit vectors,
\|u\|=\|v\|=1
, independent of the matrix randomness. The anisotropic local law provides uniform control of the quadratic form
u^{T}\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)v
over all such
u
and
v
.
The law holds on mesoscopic domains
with possible model‑dependent modifications near spectral edges or singularities. Physically,
\eta
sets a mesoscopic spectral coarse‑graining scale: a window of width
\eta
averages over
\sim N\,\eta\,\rho(x)
levels (eigenvalues), making resolvent observables effectively deterministic on that scale.
In this sense the imaginary part
\eta
acts as a spectral coarse‑graining parameter: increasing
\eta
broadens the Cauchy kernel and averages finer‑scale fluctuations, while decreasing
\eta
moves one toward microscopic resolution. As
N
grows, self‑averaging random observables converge to their deterministic equivalents, with vanishing fluctuations of order
(N\eta)^{-1/2}
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the self-consistent equation for Wigner-type ensemble

The goal is to derive a site-level Dyson form for the diagonal resolvent. We start from the resolvent identity

\displaystyle(z\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{X}_{N})\,\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{emp}}_{N}(z)=\mathbf{I}_{N}\,,\quad z=x+i\eta\,,\ \eta>0\,.

(52)

The diagonal entries satisfy

\displaystyle z\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}-X_{ii}\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}-\sum_{k\neq i}X_{ik}\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ki}=1\,,

(53)

where for ease of notation we have dropped the label
N
. In the following subsection we also omit the label “emp” from the empirical resolvent to avoid clutter of notation.

B.1
Integration by parts

We derive expressions for the expectation values of the different terms in Eq. (
53
). We assume Gaussian entries and use integration by parts (IBP), which gives the standard relation,

\mathbb{E}[X_{ab}\,F(\mathbf{X})]=\mathrm{Var}(X_{ab})\,\mathbb{E}\big[\partial F/\partial X_{ab}\big]
.
The derivative of the resolvent is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{G}}{\partial X_{ab}}=\mathbf{G}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial X_{ab}}\,\mathbf{G}\,,

(54)

and for symmetric
\mathbf{X}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial X_{ik}}=\mathbf{E}_{ik}+\mathbf{E}_{ki}\,,\quad\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial X_{ii}}=\mathbf{E}_{ii}\,,

(55)

with
i\neq k
and
(\mathbf{E}_{ab})_{uv}=\delta_{au}\delta_{bv}
. In particular

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{ii}}{\partial X_{ii}}=(\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{E}_{ii}\,\mathbf{G})_{ii}=G_{ii}^{2}\,.

(56)

Let
s_{ik}
denote the entrywise variance profile defined in Eq. (
11
).
Taking the expectation value of Eq. (
53
) and applying Gaussian IBP yields for the third term on the left-hand side:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ik}\,G_{ki}\big]=s_{ik}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\partial G_{ki}}{\partial X_{ik}}\Big]=s_{ik}\,\mathbb{E}\big[G_{kk}G_{ii}+G_{ki}^{2}\big]\,,

(57)

with
i\neq k
. Likewise, for the second term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ii}\,G_{ii}\big]=s_{ii}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\partial G_{ii}}{\partial X_{ii}}\Big]=s_{ii}\,\mathbb{E}\big[G_{ii}^{2}\big]\,.

(58)

B.2
Mean-field closure

We now pass from the empirical identity to a deterministic site equation using self‑averaging, cf. Appendix
A
. Taking expectations in Eq. (
53
) gives

\displaystyle z\,\mathbb{E}[G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}(z)]-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ik}\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ki}(z)\big]=1\,.

(59)

On mesoscopic scales (
\eta\geq N^{-1+\varepsilon}
) off the real axis, the anisotropic local law implies self‑averaging of diagonal resolvent entries and suppression of off‑diagonals, uniformly in
i
and
k
:

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{ii}(z)+O\big((N\eta)^{-1/2}\big)\,,

(60)

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ki}(z)

\displaystyle=O\big((N\eta)^{-1/2}\big)\,,

(61)

with
i\neq k
.
Hence in Eqs. (
57
)-(
58
) mixed products factorize up to local‑law errors and off‑diagonal squares are negligible:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{kk}G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}\big]

\displaystyle=G_{kk}G_{ii}+O\big((N\eta)^{-1/2}\big)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ki})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=O\big((N\eta)^{-1}\big)\,.

(63)

Therefore, for Eqs. (
57
)-(
58
) we have the approximations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ik}\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ki}\big]\simeq s_{ik}\,G_{kk}\,G_{ii}\,,\quad\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ii}\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{ii}\big]\simeq s_{ii}\,G_{ii}^{2}\,.

(64)

Substitution into the site equation (
59
), and dividing by
G_{ii}
, yields the closed equation for the deterministic equivalent

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{ii}(z)}=z-\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{ik}\,G_{kk}(z)\,.

(65)

Thus the self‑energy closes as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ii}(z):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{ik}\,G_{kk}(z)\,,\quad\bm{\Sigma}(z)=\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,,

(66)

and, with
s_{ik}
from Eq. (
11
) we obtain the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{i}}=z-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\Big(w_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}G_{k}+w_{i}^{2}G_{i}\Big)\,,

(67)

where we have defined
G_{i}:=G_{ii}
.
Using the definition of the coupling and variance-weighted resolvent in Eq. (
15
),
this becomes the on‑site Dyson form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{i}}=z-c\big(w_{i}\,u(z)+w_{i}^{2}G_{i}\big)\,,

(68)

with the physical branch fixed by
\Im\,G_{i}(z)<0
.

We visualize spectra via Cauchy–kernel smoothing

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{emp}}_{N,\eta}(x)

\displaystyle:=-\,\frac{1}{\pi}\,\Im\,G^{\mathrm{emp}}_{N}(x+i\eta)

\eta

For an
N\times N

\Delta(x)\sim 1/(N\,\rho_{N,\eta}(x))

\eta

\Delta(x)\ll\eta\ll O(1)

\sim N\,\eta\,\rho_{N,\eta}(x)

In collapsed curves, the eigenvalues are rescaled by the running normalization

\gamma(N)
that fixes the second moment, according to
x=\sqrt{\gamma(N)}\,y
(the
spectral bandwidth scales like
\sqrt{\gamma(N)}
in raw units). To compare
raw and collapsed curves with the same spectral resolution, the
smoothing width must transform covariantly under this change of units:
since
dx=\sqrt{\gamma(N)}\,dy
, keeping the kernel width fixed in
y
–units
implies

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{raw}}=\sqrt{\gamma(N)}\,\eta_{\mathrm{coll}}\,.

(71)

Thus, we choose the same
\eta_{\mathrm{coll}}
for all values
N
, and set
\eta_{\mathrm{raw}}
via the above relation. Using a constant value of
\eta
for all raw curves would lead to under- or over‑smoothing at large or small

N
, and break comparability across
sizes.

On mesoscopic scales the smoothed density
\rho_{N,\eta}
is strictly positive outside the true support due to the Cauchy kernel. This “tail” is a visualization artifact of the width set by
\eta
and decays as
\sim\eta/(x-\text{edge})^{2}
. We therefore use a single mesoscopic
\eta
across sizes and, for fair comparison, apply the same kernel to empirical spectra and to the MDE solution.

Appendix E
Large-
N
behaviour of the generalized harmonic sum

We define the generalized harmonic sum

\displaystyle H_{N}(\theta):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}j^{-\theta}\,.

(72)

The large-
N
behaviour of the generalized harmonic sum is classical (see, e.g., Ch. 3 of
Apostol 1976
) and has three regimes:

\displaystyle H_{N}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{1-\theta}}{1-\theta}+O(1)\,,

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta<1\,,

(73)

\displaystyle H_{N}(1)

\displaystyle=\log N+\gamma_{\mathrm{E}}+O(N^{-1})\,,

\displaystyle\theta=1\,,

(74)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\zeta(\theta)+O\!\big(N^{-(\theta-1)}\big)\,,

\displaystyle\theta>1\,,

(75)

where
\gamma_{\mathrm{E}}=0.57721...
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant and
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-\theta}\,,\quad\theta>1\,.

(76)

Thus, the dominant scaling of the generalized harmonic number is

\displaystyle H_{N}(\theta)\sim\begin{cases}N^{1-\theta}\,,&\theta<1\,,\\
\log N\,,&\theta=1\,,\\
1\,,&\theta>1\,.\end{cases}

(77)

Appendix F
Large-
N
limit and the continuum fixed point

We start from the finite-
N
self-consistent Eq. (
16
) and pass to a large-
N
continuum limit
Anderson et al. 2010
. This is useful because it transforms the discrete fixed-point equation into a closed integral equation with a rank‑one self‑energy, providing a universal description and analytic control of the spectrum. As before, we choose the running coupling
c(N)=\sigma^{2}/\gamma(N)
such that the second moment in Eq. (
19
) is scale‑fixed by the RG condition in Eq. (
20
).

We reparameterize indices and weights and assume a smooth resolvent profile:

\displaystyle y_{j}:=\frac{j}{N}\,,\quad w_{j}=j^{-\alpha}=N^{-\alpha}y_{j}^{-\alpha}\,,\quad G_{j}(z)\simeq G(y_{j};z)\,.

(78)

We note that
y_{j}=j/N\in(0,1]
such that the coordinate
y_{j}
becomes continuous in the large-
N
limit.
The profile
G(y;z)
is the leading‑order dependence of the diagonal resolvent on the continuous position
y
induced by the slowly varying weights
w_{j}
in mean‑field self‑energy form.

First, we treat the term capturing the interaction with the variance-weighted mean field in Eq. (
16
) which, using the above relations in Eq. (
78
), we write as

\displaystyle cw_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}G_{k}

\displaystyle=c(N)N^{1-2\alpha}\big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}y_{j}^{-\alpha}\,y_{k}^{-\alpha}\,G_{k}\big]\,.

(79)

We define the continuum amplitude

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\big[c(N)\,N^{1-2\alpha}\big]=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)^{2}\,,&0<\alpha<1\,,\\[4.0pt]
0\,,&\alpha\geq 1\,.\end{cases}

(80)

At
\alpha=1
, the amplitude
h_{\alpha}
vanishes due to log-suppression.
Hence the Riemann sum in Eq. (
79
) becomes an integral and, overall,

\displaystyle c\,w_{j}u\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;h_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}y^{-\alpha}y^{\prime-\alpha}G(y^{\prime};z)\,dy^{\prime}\,,

(81)

and the rank‑one field vanishes in the continuum limit for
\alpha\geq 1
.

Next, we treat the local term in Eq. (
16
) in an analogous manner and find that it vanishes in the large-
N
limit. Specifically:

\displaystyle c\,w_{j}^{2}G_{j}

\displaystyle=c(N)N^{1-2\alpha}\frac{y_{j}^{-2\alpha}}{N}G_{j}

(82)

and in the large-
N
limit

\displaystyle c\,w_{j}^{2}G_{j}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{}\frac{h_{\alpha}}{N}\,y^{-2\alpha}\,G(y;z)\,.

(83)

Thus,
c\,w_{j}^{2}G_{j}=O(N^{-1})
uniformly in
x
for
z=x+i\eta
with fixed
\eta>0
(log‑corrected at
\alpha=1
) .
Since
G(y;z)=O(1)
off the real axis, the local term contribution vanishes in the large‑
N
limit.

Thus, as the continuum limit form of Eq. (
16
), we find the Dyson integral equation

\displaystyle G(y;z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-\Sigma(y;z)}

(84)

with self-energy

\displaystyle\Sigma(y;z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}K(y,y^{\prime})\,G(y^{\prime};z)\,dy^{\prime}

(85)

and the kernel:

\displaystyle K(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=h_{\alpha}\,y^{-\alpha}y^{\prime-\alpha}\,.

(86)

This form of the Dyson equation is consistent with the abstract Dyson equation point of view of
Alt et al. 2020
.
Since the kernel factorizes, we can define

\displaystyle\Sigma(y;z)

\displaystyle=y^{-\alpha}S(z)\,,\quad S(z):=h_{\alpha}\,U(z)\,,

\displaystyle U(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}y^{-\alpha}G(y;z)\,dy\,.

(87)

Then

\displaystyle G(y;z)=\frac{1}{z-\Sigma(y;z)}=\frac{1}{z\big(1-\phi(z)\,y^{-\alpha}\big)}\,,

(88)

where in the last equality we have defined the normalized self‑energy amplitude

\displaystyle\phi(z):=\frac{S(z)}{z}=\frac{h_{\alpha}}{z}\,U(z)\,.

(89)

Using the definition of
U(z)
, we obtain the scalar fixed point

\displaystyle\phi(z)-\frac{h_{\alpha}}{z^{2}}\,Q_{\alpha}\!\big(\phi(z)\big)=0\,,\quad Q_{\alpha}(\phi):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{y^{\alpha}-\phi}\,.

(90)

The mean resolvent follows as

\displaystyle\overline{G}(z)=\int_{0}^{1}G(y;z)\,dy=\frac{1}{z}\,\big(1+\phi\,Q_{\alpha}(\phi)\big)\,.

(91)

At the marginal value
\alpha=1/2
the RG fixed point is non‑trivial: the continuum kernel has
h_{1/2}=1/4
, so the Dyson self‑energy does not vanish and the resolvent is not the free resolvent,
1/z
. On the other hand, for
\alpha>1

\displaystyle\Sigma=0\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{G}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\,,

(92)

i.e. the continuum self-energy vanishes and the bulk of the eigenvalues collapses toward zero, while a finite number of
O(1)
–outliers (associated with small indices) remain. These outliers are not self‑averaging.
In Fig.
7
, we show a comparison of the continuum limit form and the eigenvalue density obtained from simulated ensembles and find a near-perfect match.

Within the class of centered, independent entries with finite variance and standard moment bounds, the large-
N
deterministic limit supplied by the matrix Dyson equation depends on the model only through its variance operator; see, e.g.,
Ajanki et al. 2017
;
Ajanki et al. 2019
;
Knowles and Yin 2017
. Under our RG normalization that fixes
m_{2}
and for the power‑law weights, the variance kernel factorizes, so the continuum self‑energy is rank‑one and its amplitude is
h_{\alpha}
. Consequently, the bulk limiting density depends on
\alpha
only through
h_{\alpha}
, and is insensitive to higher cumulants at leading order on mesoscopic scales.

N
fluctuations can be model‑dependent, and heavy‑tailed (infinite‑variance), sparse, or banded ensembles also lie outside this scope. More generally, (non‑factorizable) variance profiles would lead to higher‑rank kernels and different fixed points.

Derivation of the self-consistent equation for Wishart-type ensemble

We consider
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}
with i.i.d. centered unit-variance entries,
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(v_{1},\dots,v_{T})
with
v_{t}=t^{-2\alpha}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\,,\quad\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}^{T}\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\,\mathbf{Z}^{T}\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{Z}\,.

(93)

G.1
Row decomposition and rank–one update

We write the
t
-th row of
\mathbf{Z}
as
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and set

\displaystyle b_{t}:=\frac{\sqrt{v_{t}}}{\gamma}\,z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\,(v_{t}/\gamma^{2})\,\mathbf{I}_{N}\big)\,,

(94)

so that the covariance matrix is a sum of rank–one outer products:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}b_{t}^{T}\,.

(95)

Starting from the resolvent identity

\displaystyle(z\,\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{C})\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{N}\,,

(96)

and taking the normalized trace, we obtain

\displaystyle z\,G_{N}(z)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}^{\!T}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,b_{t}=1\,,

(97)

with
G_{N}(z):=\tfrac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)
.
We remove the
t
’th row from
\mathbf{Z}
and define the
leave-one-out
quantities

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{(t)}:=\sum_{s\neq t}b_{s}b_{s}^{T}\,,\quad\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z):=(z\,\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1}\,.

(98)

By the Sherman-Morrison formula
Bartlett 1951
;
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}+uv^{T})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{A}^{-1}uv^{T}\mathbf{A}^{-1}}{1+v^{T}\mathbf{A}^{-1}u}

(99)

with
\mathbf{A}^{-1}=\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}
and
uv^{T}=\mathbf{G}_{N}^{-1}-{\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}}^{-1}=-b_{t}b_{t}^{T}
, we obtain the rank‑one update of the resolvent and its trace:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)+\frac{\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}b_{t}^{\!T}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)}{1-b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}}

(100)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,b_{t}

\displaystyle=b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}+\frac{\big[b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}\big]^{2}}{1-b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}}\,,

(101)

where we have used Eq. (
95
). Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,b_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}}{1-b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,b_{t}}\,.

(102)

G.2
Conditional Gaussian averaging

The random objects we average are the quadratic forms in the numerator and denominator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
102
).
We condition on the
leave–one–out
(minor) resolvent
\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}
(i.e., treat
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
as fixed), and note that
b_{t}
is independent of
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
. Since
b_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(v_{t}/\gamma^{2})\mathbf{I})
and
\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}
is fixed under the conditional expectation, the standard Gaussian contraction gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}}\!\big[b_{t}^{T}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}b_{t}\,\big|\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}\big]=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big(\mathrm{Cov}(b_{t})\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}\big)=\frac{v_{t}}{\gamma^{2}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)\,.

(103)

Thus, under the conditional expectation, the quadratic form reduces to a trace against the leave–one–out resolvent, and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}}\!\big[b_{t}^{\!T}\mathbf{G}_{N}(z)\,b_{t}\,\big|\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}\big]=\frac{\frac{v_{t}}{\gamma^{2}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)}{1-\frac{v_{t}}{\gamma^{2}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)}\,.

(104)

G.3
Mean–field replacement and equation closure

The anisotropic local law (cf. Appendix
A
and
Knowles and Yin 2017
;
Ajanki et al. 2019
), asserts that, uniformly for
z=x+i\eta
with
\eta>0
, leave–one–out resolvents
\mathbf{G}^{(t)}
and full resolvents
\mathbf{G}_{N}
differ by subleading corrections in quadratic forms and traces:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(t)}(z)=G_{N}(z)+O\big((N\eta)^{-1/2}\big)\,.

(105)

Substituting into the normalized trace identity yields, up to vanishing errors

\displaystyle z\,G_{N}(z)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\big(\tfrac{v_{t}}{\gamma^{2}}\big)\,N\,G_{N}(z)}{1-\big(\tfrac{v_{t}}{\gamma^{2}}\big)\,N\,G_{N}(z)}=1\,,

(106)

or, equivalently, the closed fixed point

\displaystyle G_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-\Sigma_{N}(z)}\,,

(107)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{v_{t}}{1-\big(N/\gamma^{2}\big)\,v_{t}\,G_{N}(z)}\,.

(108)

These are the finite‑

(N\eta)^{-1/2}
. The same fixed-point equations can also be derived from the Silverstein–Dozier framework for sample covariances
Bai and Silverstein 2010
;
Silverstein and Bai 1995

Dozier and Silverstein 2007

Appendix H
Linearized RG flow in the saturating regime (
\alpha>1/2
)

In the main text we define the RG Beta function in terms of the running coupling
g(T):=\gamma^{-2}(T)
, cf. Eq. (
42
). For
\alpha>1/2
the renormalization condition implies that
\gamma^{2}(T)
saturates and hence
g(T)
approaches a nonzero fixed point
g_{\star}
. In this situation the Beta function
\beta_{\mathrm{RG}}(g)=d\log g/d\log T
necessarily tends to zero, and it no longer exposes the
rate
at which the coupling approaches its fixed point. To quantify this approach we linearize the flow around
g_{\star}
and extract the corresponding decay exponent.

In the Wishart-type model, the running normalization
\gamma^{2}(T)
is fixed by the renormalization condition on the first moment

\displaystyle m_{1}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(T)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}t^{-2\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}m_{1}^{\star}\,,

(109)

so that, up to the fixed constant
m_{1}^{\star}
factor

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}(T)\propto\sum_{t=1}^{T}t^{-2\alpha}\,.

(110)

For
\alpha>1/2
the series converges and
\gamma^{2}(T)
saturates as
T\to\infty
.

H.1
RG fixed point approach

We define the fixed-point normalization and coupling

\displaystyle\gamma_{\star}^{2}:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\gamma^{2}(T)\propto\zeta(2\alpha)\,,\quad g_{\star}:=\gamma_{\star}^{-2}\,,

(111)

and measure the approach to the fixed point via the deviation in
\gamma^{2}

\displaystyle\delta(T):=\gamma_{\star}^{2}-\gamma^{2}(T)\,.

(112)

Using a tail estimate for the convergent series, we obtain the asymptotic scaling of the deviation

\displaystyle\delta(T)\propto\sum_{t=T+1}^{\infty}t^{-2\alpha}\sim\int_{T}^{\infty}t^{\prime-2\alpha}\,dt^{\prime}=\frac{T^{1-2\alpha}}{2\alpha-1}\,.

(113)

Hence, the deviation decays as a power law

\displaystyle\delta(T)\propto T^{-(2\alpha-1)}\,,

(114)

with exponent
2\alpha-1>0
.

H.2
Linearized Beta function

A natural linearized Beta function is the logarithmic derivative of the deviation:

\displaystyle\beta_{\delta}:=\frac{d\log\delta}{ds}=\frac{d\log\delta}{d\log T}\,.

(115)

Using Eq. (
113
) we find

\displaystyle\beta_{\delta}\to 1-2\alpha<0\,,\quad\alpha>1/2\,.

(116)

Thus, in the saturating regime the flow is irrelevant in the RG sense: as the scale
T
increases the deviation from the fixed point decays as a power law, and under decimation (reducing the scale) the deviation grows with exponent
2\alpha-1
.
Equivalently, we may linearize the coupling itself. Since
g(T)=\gamma^{-2}(T)
and
\gamma^{2}(T)=\gamma_{\star}^{2}-\delta(T)
, we have

\displaystyle g(T)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\star}^{2}-\delta(T)}=g_{\star}+\frac{\delta(T)}{\gamma_{\star}^{4}}+O\big(\delta(T)^{2}\big)\,,

(117)

so that
g(T)-g_{\star}\propto\delta(T)
and therefore
g(T)-g_{\star}\propto T^{1-2\alpha}
with the same exponent.

Thus, the linearized Beta function
\beta_{\delta}
characterizes the approach to the fixed point through the deviation
\delta(T)
(or, equivalently,
g(T)-g_{\star}
), and recovers the negative exponent
1-2\alpha
in the saturating regime.
