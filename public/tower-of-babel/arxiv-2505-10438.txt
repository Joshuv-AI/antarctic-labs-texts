Identification and Optimal Nonlinear Control of Turbojet Engine Using Koopman Eigenfunction Model
David Grasev

Abstract
Gas turbine engines represent complex highly nonlinear dynamical systems. Deriving their physics-based models can be challenging as it requires performance characteristics, that are not always available, and one often has to make many simplifying assumptions. In this paper, the limitations of conventional experimental methods used to derive component-level and locally linear parameter-varying models are discussed and addressed by employing identification techniques based on data collected from standard engine operation under closed-loop control. The rotor dynamics were estimated using the sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics. Subsequently, the autonomous part of the dynamics was mapped into an optimally constructed Koopman eigenfunction space. The process included eigenvalue optimization using metaheuristic algorithms and temporal projection, followed by gradient-based eigenfunction identification. The resulting Koopman model was validated against an in-house reference component-level model. A globally optimal nonlinear feedback controller and a Kalman estimator were then designed in the eigenfunction space and compared to the classical and gain-scheduled proportional-integral controllers, as well as a proposed internal model control approach. The eigenmode structure allowed targeting individual modes during the optimization process, resulting in a better performance tuning. The results showed that the Koopman-based controller outperformed the other benchmark controllers in both reference tracking and disturbance rejection, under sea-level and varying flight conditions, due to its global nature.

Introduction

Gas turbine engines (GTEs) represent a class of highly nonlinear and control nonaffine systems posing a challenge for identification and control system design. The nonlinearities in GTE dynamics are dominantly caused by aero-thermodynamic effects in turbomachinery components, ducts, volumes, combustion dynamics and more
Mattingly and Boyer 2016
. A major category of GTE modeling and identification approaches are physics-based models. Among the most popular are the component-level models (CLMs), which are formed by the connection of zero-dimensional models of engine components. These are represented usually by their performance characteristics, also called maps, which are either measured on a test stand or computed using the computational fluid dynamics (CFD). These models can either serve for derivation of linear parameter-varying (LPV) models for the control design, or they can be employed directly as on-board estimators. The on-board real-time adaptive CLM was developed in
Pang et al. 2020
with the work further extended to the limit protection
Pang et al. 2021a
, model predictive control (MPC) with extended Kalman filter (EKF)
Pang et al. 2021b
and direct thrust control
Pang et al. 2022
. On-board CLM with unscented Kalman filter was investigated in
Chen et al. 2023a
presenting a high-level estimation performance with further extension to MPC framework and health parameter estimation
Chen et al. 2023b
. A nonlinear dynamic-inversion controller with a dual EKF was proposed in
Singh et al. 2022
, exploiting a simplified physics-based model with approximated component maps. The model was subsequently converted to a control-affine structure which is broadly considered a valid approximation of the true engine dynamics. A sliding mode controller was utilized to improve the performance of a model-free adaptive controller for a variable-cycle GTE in
Zhou et al. 2025
. A performance seeking controller based on a dual loop structure was proposed in
Yao et al. 2025
. The inner loop stabilizes the system and the outer loop uses a CLM to optimize the variable geometry of the GTE minimizing the fuel consumption, hence addressing the multiobjective nature of the GTE control. In the results, it is reported that the controller decreased the fuel consumption by 5
\%
while retaining an acceptable performance. On-board CLMs often require significant computational resources which may not be currently available on boards of small flying vehicles.

A plethora of GTE control literature is devoted to linear parameter-varying (LPV) models. To construct these models, linear time-invariant (LTI) subsystems are first identified around multiple steady-state points with subsequent interpolation or fitting of the LTI coefficients w.r.t. to some scheduling parameter, e.g. spool speed. For instance, Wei et al.
Wei et al. 2021
combined a piecewise linear model with nonlinear gains to construct an adaptive Wiener model. They results has shown that the Wiener approach can outperform a general describing function from
Lichtsinder and Levy 2004
and the piecewise model without the nonlinear gains. The model is often converted to a polytopic structure suitable for design of robust controllers using quadratic programming and linear matrix inequalities as discussed in, e.g.,
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2021
;
Lv et al. 2022
;
Gu et al. 2022
, where
Lv et al. 2022
is a comprehensive review of GTE control strategies. In
Gu et al. 2022
, the authors also introduce deep learning to engine control. This is a next broad class of nonlinear control approaches - the data-driven control.

The need for component characteristics leads to the first major problem – the construction of some engines does not allow one to measure reliably all characteristics. For instance, the backward-flow combustion chambers makes it almost impossible to install temperature and other sensors, which are necessary for turbine measurements, without invasive operations such as drilling additional holes etc. which might compromise the structural integrity of the engine. Regarding the CFD estimation of compressor and turbine maps, these would require a detailed knowledge of the engine geometry, which is, in many cases, difficult to obtain from measurements or it is classified by the manufacturer. To overcome these problems, the identification of the GTE dynamical models solely from the measurable response data is often employed nowadays for the nonlinear control. Among the data-driven methods are, e.g., artificial neural networks (ANNs)
Gu et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023
and their variation like recurrent networks, long short-term memory networks
Song et al. 2025
;
Kilic et al. 2024
, and auto-regressive nonlinear models with exogenous inputs
Aquize et al. 2023
;
Ibrahem et al. 2021
.

Other methods focus on discovery of the underlying governing equations of the system from data. Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) was developed by Brunton et al. at Washington University and first presented in
Brunton et al. 2016
. The method looks for an interpretable governing equation approximated by a linear combination of nonlinear functions of states and inputs. Firstly, a library of candidate functions is designed, and the least squares (LS) problem is subsequently solved to obtain optimal weight coefficients. SINDy focuses on the trade-off between accuracy and interpretability of the model via the L1 regularization. The LS problem is usually solved using the LASSO algorithm
Tibshirani 1996
. SINDy was successfully used for identification of a small turbojet engine by authors in
Momin et al. 2022
;
L’Erario et al. 2020
, where the accuracy of the model was further enhanced using an EKF leading to a reliable prediction. Although SINDy is a powerful tool, the resulting model is still nonlinear. It can still be converted to control-affine form and utilized for MPC and design of EKF as mentioned above or feedback linearization, sliding mode control and more.

The Koopman operator theory (KOT) represents a way to transform the nonlinear dynamics into a linear framework in an infinite-dimensional lifted state space
Mauroy et al. 2020
. In practice, a finite-rank truncation is demanded with a suitable set of observables. This allows for design of optimal controllers in the lifted state space using quadratic programming approaches known from the regulator optimization as demonstrated, e.g., in
Bevanda et al. 2021
;
Proctor et al. 2018
;
Kaiser et al. 2021
. The lifting is performed using arbitrary nonlinear functions called observables, preserving the nonlinear nature. Although the definition of an optimal set of observables is still a problem-specific and open research question, several data-driven approaches exist to formulate a suitable set of observables without resorting to a trial-and-error approach.

Regarding the KOT, two ways can be distinguished as stated in the following list:

•

Methods based on dynamic mode decomposition (DMD),

•

Koopman eigenfunction (spectral) methods.

The former includes methods such as extended DMD (EDMD)
Williams et al. 2015
, exact DMD
Tu et al. 2014
, generator EDMD
Klus et al. 2020
, residual DMD
Colbrook et al. 2023
, kernel DMD
Williams et al. 2015
, Hankel DMD using Krylov sequences
Mauroy et al. 2020
, and many more. The common goal is to find an optimal approximation of the Koopman operator in form of a finite-rank matrix via solution of a corresponding LS problem with additional constraints. The observables are constructed either by trial and error, building a large library of candidate observables, e.g. radial basis functions (RBFs) or polynomials, with a subsequent model-order reduction, or using some apriori knowledge about the system. Other methods seek to optimize the observables via machine learning
Li et al. 2020
;
Lusch et al. 2018
;
Tabish et al. 2024
;
Grasev 2025
;
Irani et al. 2024
. For instance, the KOT with deep dictionary learning was utilized for identification and fault diagnostics of gas turbines in
Irani et al. 2024
. The observables were represented by ANN with an autoencoder architecture. A deep transfer learning framework was developed to adaptively capture the degraded states of the turbine. The method was key for extraction of a reliable nonlinear model. However, utilization of the autoencoder, despite being a powerful tool, leads to a complex model with a nonlinear inverse transformation back to the original state space making the control design still particularly challenging. Our previous work in
Grasev 2025
presented a way to optimize the observables using metaheuristic global optimization tools for the identification of GTEs. Results pointed to the suitability of the proposed method for discovery of low-order Koopman models for GTEs.

The eigenfunction methods focus more on spectral properties of the Koopman operator. Under the assumption that the aforementioned matrix is diagonalizable, the underlying autonomous dynamics can be decomposed using a linear combination of spectral components represented by eigenvalues, eigenfunctions and eigenmodes. This was thoroughly discussed in literature and various papers and a book by Igor Mezić
Mezić 2015
;
Mezić 2020
;
Mezić 2021
. One of the rather rigorous spectral methods with a strong mathematical foundation is the generalized Laplace analysis
Mauroy et al. 2020
. The method is based on an iterative computation using time averages of a weighted signal obtained by subtraction of the previously computed modes from the measured trajectory. Korda et al.
Korda and Mezić 2020
proposed an optimal construction of Koopman eigenfunctions from data using a dense set of trajectories and mapping only the initial conditions to reveal the spatial structure of eigenfunctions. The method needs to be supplied by guessed eigenvalues which is the main drawback of this approach. The paper also presents ways to optimize the eigenvalue guesses, but it relies on the analytical gradient. These ideas were further extended also to the control synthesis in
Kaiser et al. 2021
, where several methods were proposed including the implicit SINDy algorithm or a conversion to an equivalent eigenvalue problem for discovery of eigenfunctions. The method again assumed apriori knowledge of eigenvalues. The work showed that linear quadratic controllers can be effectively designed in eigenfunction coordinates using simple tools solving the state-dependent algebraic Riccati equation.

The main challenges connected to the utilization of these methods for GTE identification and control are listed as follows:

•

The data required for the identification need to capture the autonomous behavior of the system. This is difficult to address for GTEs. A solution would be to accelerate the engine to the maximum operating point, cut off the fuel delivery, and measure the run-down curves. However, control systems of some engines include a cooling logic that keeps the rotor spinning at low RPM in order to actively cool hot sections. Deactivation of this logic could lead to a reduction of the remaining useful life of the engine.

•

Some necessary performance data cannot be obtained due to the architecture of the engine not allowing for installation of sensors where needed. Thus, the CLM cannot be developed without additional procedures, such as matching of generic maps to meet the measured transient response of the GTE.

•

Experimental derivation of LPV models without CLM is a time-consuming process leading only to a locally linearized dynamical model. Furthermore, the on-board adaptive nonlinear models, and the corresponding controllers, are often computationally expensive. Thus, it complicates their usage for small unmanned aerial vehicles where it might not be possible to install and maintain a processing unit with the required computational power.

To address these challenges in this paper, the SINDy algorithm was first utilized to discover the underlying governing equation of the modeled single-spool GTE from data. The data can be measured during the normal operation of the engine and under the feedback control law with no need for adjustment of the existing control system. The testing signal should be designed such that the trajectory meets the control limits of the engine that are not known a priori, because the engine factory controller settings are not known.

Since we do not currently have access to a measured engine response, the identification and analysis were conducted using an in-house nonlinear CLM of a generic single-spool GTE. This model was developed in the MATLAB® environment and validated against the GasTurb commercial engine simulation software
Kurzke 2018
. A control-affine model was considered as a valid approximation of the GTE dynamics. Subsequently, the autonomous part of the SINDy model was utilized for identification of optimal spectral components yielding a conversion to the Koopman eigenfunction model (KEM). Genetic algorithm (GA) was employed to find optimal eigenvalues via the temporal projection of autonomous trajectories onto a span of exponential trajectories. Gradient-based algorithm was further developed to find the corresponding eigenfunctions. To enhance the prediction accuracy of the KEM, the Kalman observer was designed. Finally, an optimal Koopman Linear Quadratic Gaussian controller with Integral action (K-LQGI) was designed using global optimization methods and compared against a classical and gain scheduled LPV proportional-integral (PI) controllers.

2
Component-level model of a turbojet engine

To validate the proposed approaches, it was necessary to have control over the reference nonlinear model in order to connect various controllers and evaluate their performance. Thus, an in-house CLM of a generic engine was developed in the MATLAB R2022a environment. The CLM was further utilized for generation of the training and test datasets. It is crucial that the model is well-representative of a real engine and thus it was validated against the GasTurb 12 commercial simulation software, which represents the benchmark for engine modeling
Kurzke 2018
.

2.1
Models of GTE components

2.1.1
Atmospheric conditions

Atmospheric conditions were modeled in accordance with the international standard atmosphere. Concerning the pressure losses, these were considered in the inlet, combustion chamber (CC), exhaust duct and exhaust nozzle. The CC losses were modeled as a function of the CC inlet corrected mass flow parameter.

2.1.2
Compressor

Compressor model was represented by the compressor map, where the mass flow, pressure ratio and efficiency were converted to functions of the corrected spool speed and an auxiliary nonlinear coordinate -
\beta_{c}
parameter. The detailed explanation of the modified
\beta_{c}
interpolation approach is provided in
Grasev and Jílek 2024
. Thus, the mass flow
W_{c}
, pressure ratio
\Pi_{c}
and adiabatic efficiency
\eta_{c}
were expressed as

[W_{c,corr},\Pi_{c},\eta_{c}]=f(\beta_{c},N_{corr}),

(1)

where
W_{c,corr}
and
N_{corr}
are the corrected compressor air mass flow and the corrected spool speed, respectively.

The compressor torque
\Gamma_{c}
was given as

\Gamma_{c}=W_{c}\hskip 1.42262ptC_{p,a}\left(\Pi_{c}^{\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}}-1\right)\frac{1}{\eta_{c}\hskip 1.42262ptN},

(2)

where
C_{p,a}
is the heat capacity of air and
\gamma_{a}
is the adiabatic exponent of air.

2.1.3
Combustion chamber

Combustor model consisted of the map of burning efficiency
\eta_{b}
as a function of the combustor loading parameter
\Omega_{cc}
, which was described, e.g., in
Kurzke 2018
;
Walsh and Fletcher 2008
, and which accounts for the changes in the engine state and flight conditions. Regarding the pressure losses, these were modeled as a function of both the mass flow parameter and the mean temperature relative to the design point value. This way, the effect of heat addition on the decrease of the total pressure recovery was captured. The power balance of the combustion chamber is given as

\eta_{b}W_{f}H_{u}=W_{cc}\bar{C}_{p}(T_{cc},T_{t})(T_{t}-T_{cc}),

(3)

where
\eta_{b}
,
W_{f}
and
H_{u}
are the burning efficiency, fuel mass flow and lower heating value of fuel, respectively, and
\bar{C}_{p}
,
W_{cc}
,
T_{cc}
and
T_{t}
are the mean heat capacity of gases in CC, CC inlet air mass flow, CC inlet total temperature and turbine inlet total temperature, respectively.

This equation was utilized to determine the
T_{t}
for a known
W_{f}
. The mean capacity
\bar{C}_{p}
is a known function of both inlet and outlet conditions in CC, making the equation implicit. Thus, the fixed-point iteration was employed to solve for
T_{t}
. Since
\eta_{b}=f(\Omega_{cc})
, and
\Omega_{cc}=f(T_{cc},p_{cc},W_{cc})
, the efficiency can be rewritten as
\eta_{b}=f(T_{cc},p_{cc},W_{cc})
and thus it is a known explicit function of CC inlet conditions.

2.1.4
Turbine

Similarly to the compressor, the turbine was represented by its map. Concerning the turbine map, there was no need for a coordinate transformation and the map could be directly interpolated. The turbine corrected gas mass flow
W_{t,corr}
and the relative temperature difference
\Delta T/T_{t}
were functions of the turbine corrected spool speed
N_{t,corr}
and pressure ratio
\Pi_{t}
generally expressed as

\left[W_{t,corr},\frac{\Delta T}{T_{t}}\right]=f(N_{t,corr},\Pi_{t}).

(4)

The turbine output torque
\Gamma_{t}
was given as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}=W_{t}\hskip 1.42262ptC_{p,g}\hskip 1.42262ptT_{t}\left(\frac{\Delta T}{T_{t}}\right)\frac{1}{N},

(5)

where
C_{p,g}
is the heat capacity of gas (1.33 for the combustion products).

2.1.5
Nozzle

The nozzle model included the pressure losses due to both the friction and contraction. The critical nozzle pressure ratio
\Pi_{n,crit}
was also accounted for during the exhaust velocity and thrust calculation. The nozzle velocity
v_{n}
, mass flow
W_{n}
, and the thrust
F
are given as

\displaystyle v_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2C_{p,g}\hskip 1.42262ptT_{n}\left(1-\Pi_{n}^{\frac{1-\gamma_{g}}{\gamma_{g}}}\right)\eta_{n}}&\quad\text{for }\Pi_{n}<\Pi_{n,crit}\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma_{g}}{\gamma_{g}+1}RT_{n}}&\quad\text{for }\Pi_{n}\geq\Pi_{n,crit}\end{cases},

(6)

\displaystyle W_{n}=\mu_{n}v_{n}\frac{p_{n}}{RT_{n}}\left[1-\eta_{n}\left(1-\Pi_{n}^{\frac{1-\gamma_{g}}{\gamma_{g}}}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{g}-1}}A_{n},

(7)

\displaystyle F=W_{n}v_{n}-W_{0}MN\sqrt{\gamma_{a}RT_{0}}+p_{n,out}A_{n},

(8)

where
\eta_{n}
,
T_{n}
and
p_{n}
are the adiabatic efficiency, total temperature and total pressure in the nozzle, respectively,
p_{0}
and
T_{0}
are the atmospheric pressure and temperature, respectively,
\Pi_{n}=p_{n}/p_{0}
is the nozzle pressure ratio,
\gamma_{g}
is the adiabatic exponent of exhaust gases,
R
is the universal gas constant,
\mu_{n}
denotes the nozzle flow coefficient,
A_{n}
is the nozzle outlet area,
W_{0}
is the engine inlet air mass flow,
MN
is the Mach number of flight, and
p_{n,out}
is the exhaust static pressure measured directly aft the nozzle (
p_{n,out}>p_{0}
for supercritical conditions).

2.1.6
Shaft dynamics and control system

The shaft dynamics were modeled using the following equation of motion:

\frac{dN}{dt}=\frac{30}{\pi I}\left(\Gamma_{t}\eta_{m}-\Gamma_{c}\right),

(9)

where the spool speed
N
is measured in revolution per minute (RPM),
I
is the shaft polar moment of inertia, and
\eta_{m}
is the mechanical efficiency, which is usually very close to 1.

Concerning the fuel system, the response of the true fuel flow
W_{f}
to the change of a fuel flow command
v
from the controller was modeled by the following transfer function:

G_{f}(s)=\frac{W_{f}(s)}{v(s)}=\frac{1}{T_{f}s+1},

(10)

where
T_{f}
is the time constant of the fuel system.

Control system consisted of the main spool-speed controller (governor) and fuel flow limiters connected to the min-max protection logic. The fuel flow limits were calculated as functions of the spool speed accounting for the compressor surge and the turbine over-temperature during acceleration, and the compressor choke and combustor blow-out during deceleration. The clamping method was utilized to suppress the integrator wind up effect.

2.2
Transient solver

For the transient calculation, the constant mass flow (CMF) approach coupled with the Newton-Raphson matching algorithm was utilized. For modeling of single-spool turbojet GTEs, the CMF method is advantageous due to its higher accuracy and suitability for long-term transient simulations, as reported in
Pilidis 1983
;
Yang et al. 2024
. For more complex engines, e.g. two-spool turbofans, the method becomes computationally expensive and less accurate due to the unmodeled volume dynamics.

In each time step of the transient computation, the change of the air/gas mass flow induced by the changes of the engine state is assumed to instantaneously propagate through the entire engine. Therefore, in any instance, the mass flow of air/gas entering a particular component must be equal to the mass flow leaving the component. For a single-spool turbojet engine, this matching condition has to be satisfied between the compressor and the turbine, and between the turbine and the nozzle. The goal is to find the values of two state variables of the Newton-Raphson method. These variables are guessed and updated until the convergence, mass flow equality, is reached. These state variables are the compressor
\beta_{c}
parameter and the turbine pressure ratio
\Pi_{t}
. The matching errors are given by differences between mass flows read off the compressor and the turbine map, and the corresponding mass flows computed based on previous cycle calculations. To update the state variables, the following equation is utilized:

\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{c}\\
\Pi_{t}\end{bmatrix}_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{c}\\
\Pi_{t}\end{bmatrix}_{k}-\begin{bmatrix}\nicefrac{{\partial e_{m,c}}}{{\partial\beta_{c}}}&\nicefrac{{\partial e_{m,c}}}{{\partial\Pi_{t}}}\\
\nicefrac{{\partial e_{m,n}}}{{\partial\beta_{c}}}&\nicefrac{{\partial e_{m,n}}}{{\partial\Pi_{t}}}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}e_{m,c}\\
e_{m,n}\end{bmatrix}_{k},

(11)

where
e_{m,c}
and
e_{m,n}
are the compressor-turbine and turbine-nozzle mass flow errors, respectively, and partial derivatives in the Jacobi matrix were approximated using the small perturbation method and finite differences.

The complete system consists of a controller to be designed, the fuel system which can be accurately represented by a linear model (
10
), and the GTE which is a nonlinear system that needs to be identified from data. The state variables of the GTE and fuel system are the spool speed and fuel flow, respectively. The input to the fuel system is the fuel-flow command from the controller. As only the engine rotor dynamics need to be treated as nonlinear, the following identification procedures and transformations will be applied only to the spool speed. The fuel system dynamics will later be augmented with the identified GTE model in the Koopman observable subspace.

3
Koopman operator theory

The Koopman operator theory provides a powerful framework for representation of nonlinear dynamical systems in a new coordinate system, where the dynamics become linear. The operator itself is theoretically infinite-dimensional. In practice however, the finite-rank approximation is of interest and an entire plethora of tools and methods for its discovery was discussed in literature. One way is represented by DMD-based methods described in the introduction. In this paper, the other approach based on a direct identification of Koopman eigenfunctions was considered.
Consider the following nonlinear continuous-time dynamical system:

\mathbf{\dot{x}}=\textbf{f}(\textbf{x},u),

(12)

where
x
and
u
are the state and input variables, respectively.

Furthermore, consider a set of nonlinear Lipchitz continuous and Lebesgue integrable observable functions
\mathbf{g}(\textbf{x},u)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi(x)}&\mathbf{\tilde{u}}(\textbf{x},u)\end{bmatrix}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{u}}(\textbf{x},0)=\textbf{0}
, representing the Koopman lifting functions. Thus, it is assumed that the unforced dynamics can be described using only the observables
\mathbf{\Psi}(\textbf{x})
. The associated Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}^{t}
is defined by the following composition equation:

\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{S}^{t},

(13)

where
\mathbf{S}^{t}
is the flow map capturing the evolution of system states after time
t
, which parametrizes the flow.

Note that there exists an entire family of Koopman operators parameterized by
t
(sampling period). For the purpose of presenting eigenfunctions, the Koopman generator form is more convenient. When
t\rightarrow 0
, the infinitesimal change of observables is given by a Lie derivative as

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{K}^{t}\mathbf{g-g}}{t}=\mathcal{L}\textbf{g}=\dot{\textbf{g}}=\nabla\textbf{g}\cdot\textbf{f},

(14)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Koopman infinitesimal generator.

Infinitely many Koopman operators
\mathcal{K}^{t}
can be sampled from a single generator given a time step
t
. For the unforced dynamics, special observables called eigenfunctions are particularly of interest. The Koopman eigenfunction
\varphi(\textbf{x})
and the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda
are defined as
Mauroy et al. 2020

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{t}\varphi=\varphi\circ\mathbf{S}^{t}=e^{\lambda t}\varphi,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\varphi=\lambda\varphi,

(15)

which thus directly defines the eigenfunction dynamics as
\dot{\varphi}=\lambda\varphi
with solution
\varphi(t)=\varphi(0)e^{\lambda t}
.

This allows for a spectral decomposition of an observable of interest to a linear combination of
(\lambda,\varphi,c)
tuples given as

g(\textbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\varphi_{i}(0)e^{\lambda_{i}t},

(16)

where
c
are the mode amplitudes, or simply eigenmodes, representing a projection of the observable onto a span of eigenfunctions.

In practice, the theoretical operator
\mathcal{K}
is approximated by a matrix
K
, and associated eigen-tuples obtained from data-driven methods such as EDMD. For a system with inputs, this matrix can be further split as
\mathbf{K=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\textbf{B}\end{bmatrix}}
, where
A
and
B
are the system and input matrices, respectively. The eigenfunctions and modes can be obtained by diagonalization of
A
as it represents the unforced (autonomous) dynamics. EDMD is one of the common methods for the data-driven identification of Koopman systems
Williams et al. 2015
. The continuous-time Koopman linear system is thus given as

\mathbf{\dot{\Psi}=A\Psi+B\tilde{u}}.

(17)

In EDMD, a library of nonlinear observable functions is first constructed for lifting of the state dynamics. Subsequently, the finite-rank matrix
K
is obtained by LS minimization and represents an optimal mapping from one time step to the next across the entire dataset. Finally, diagonalization of the system matrix
A
is carried out to obtain eigenvalues and eigenfunctions as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{\Psi}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A\Psi=V\Lambda V^{-1}\Psi},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V^{-1}\dot{\Psi}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V^{-1}V\Lambda V^{-1}\Psi},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Lambda\Phi}.

(18)

However, as these eigenfunctions are constrained to be linear combinations of the same basis
\mathbf{\Psi}
, the limitations are already given by the selection of
\mathbf{\Psi(x)}
. These observables should thus be selected such that the projection
\mathbf{\Phi=V^{-1}\Psi}
is well-defined for the most prominent eigenfunctions, which is not always guaranteed. Furthermore, EDMD might lead to solutions that are ill-conditioned and possibly contain unstable eigenvalues although the model provides very accurate fit
Kaiser et al. 2021
. The selection of optimal EDMD observables is often a trial-and-error process, which can be supported by approximation and learning methods as discussed in section
1
.

In the spectral approach, these prominent eigenfunctions are identified directly from data with no need for construction of a global set of observables since the eigenfunctions themselves are the optimal intrinsic coordinates directly connected to the rich underlying dynamics. Moreover, they span an invariant subspace due to their algebraic structure
Mezić 2021
;
Korda and Mezić 2020
.
\mathbf{\Phi(x)}
can be expressed as a linear combination of arbitrary basis functions that can adapt to each eigenfunction individually. Thus, there are no constraints given by the projection of eigenfunctions on a common fixed set of observables.

Taking the Lie derivative of eigenfunctions leads to the Koopman partial differential equation (KPDE) given as

\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}=\nabla\Phi\cdot\dot{x}=\nabla\Phi\cdot f(x},0)\mathbf{=\Lambda\Phi}.

(19)

The KPDE is in agreement with the generator formulation (
14
,
3
) and uniquely defines eigenfunctions for a given set of eigenvalues. This is particularly useful for identification of eigenfunctions from data
Kaiser et al. 2021
.

4
Proposed approach - SINDy-KEM

Eigenfunctions can be reliably identified for the autonomous dynamics of the system. However, in the case of GTEs, it is rather challenging to measure autonomous nature of the dynamics as mentioned in Section
1
.

The idea of direct identification of eigenfunctions from data using regression and KPDE originates in the discussed works of Korda et al.
Korda and Mezić 2020
and Kaiser et al.
Kaiser et al. 2021
. In this paper, it is further combined with a SINDy algorithm utilized for estimation of the autonomous dynamics. This enables discovery of the underlying dynamics from data gathered during normal engine operation. Thus, the extensive experimental procedures, e.g. linearization around multiple steady-state points for derivation of an LPV model, are bypassed. Furthermore, there is no need for measurements of the component characteristics and the engine geometry, both of which are not always accessible, and simplifying assumptions that might compromise the accuracy when chosen inappropriately.

The workflow includes the identification of the engine governing equation and extraction of the estimated autonomous dynamics, discovery of the optimal set of eigenvalues, corresponding eigenfunctions and eigenmodes, and transform of the nonlinear governing equation to the KEM. Summary is provided in Fig.
1
.

4.1
Identification of the control-affine governing equation

The SINDy model is the most important part of the entire process since it sets the maximum accuracy of the KEM. Thus, it is crucial to properly capture the nonlinear dynamics by SINDy. The dynamics of single-spool GTEs can be generally represented by

\dot{N}=f(N,W_{f},p_{1t},T_{1t}),

(20)

where
p_{1t}
and
T_{1t}
capture the variations in inlet conditions caused by external effects.

Let us hereafter use the shorter notation
N
and
W_{f}
for corrected quantities
N_{corr}
and
W_{f,corr}
, respectively, given as

\displaystyle N_{corr}=N\sqrt{\frac{288}{T_{1t}}}\rightarrow N,

(21)

\displaystyle W_{f,corr}=W_{f}\sqrt{\frac{288}{T_{1t}}}\frac{101325}{p_{1t}}\rightarrow W_{f},

(22)

where
p_{1t}
and
T_{1t}
are the inlet total pressure and temperature, respectively.

The simplified control-affine model of the GTE is given as

\dot{N}=f(N)+g(N)W_{f},

(23)

where
f
and
g
are the autonomous and input dynamics, respectively, and
N
will hereafter denote the normalized corrected spool speed for simplicity.

The SINDy cost function is given as

J_{S}=\left\|\dot{N}-\mathbf{\Xi_{f}}\mathbf{\Theta_{f}}(N,\mathbf{p_{f}})+\mathbf{\Xi_{g}}\mathbf{\Theta_{g}}(N,\mathbf{p_{g}})W_{f}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{\Xi}\|_{1},

(24)

where
\mathbf{\Xi}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Xi_{f}}&\mathbf{\Xi_{g}}\end{bmatrix}
is the vector of weights,
\mathbf{\Theta_{f}}
and
\mathbf{\Theta_{g}}
are the autonomous and input candidate functions, respectively,
\mathbf{p_{f}}
and
\mathbf{p_{g}}
, are vectors of nonlinear parameters of candidate functions for
f(N)
and
g(N)
, respectively, with
\mathbf{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p_{f}}&\mathbf{p_{g}}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\alpha
is the L1 regularization weight factor.

Since also the nonlinear parameters of basis functions are optimized, this problem is solved using gradient descend (GD) methods with adaptive
momentum (ADAM) optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2017
. Additionally, to promote sparsity of the solution, the L1 regularization is combined with sequential thresholding (ST)
Brunton et al. 2016
. Whenever any weight in
\mathbf{\Xi}
falls below a specified threshold, it is excluded from the equation. After each ST round, the GD algorithm is initialized again to fine-tune the remaining active terms in the model. This procedure repeats until the suitable trade-off between the complexity, given by the number of active terms, and accuracy is reached.

The main equations of GD with ADAM are given below:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Xi}\\
\textbf{p}\end{bmatrix}_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Xi}\\
\textbf{p}\end{bmatrix}_{k}-\frac{\alpha\textbf{v}}{\sqrt{\textbf{s}+\epsilon}},

(25)

where

\textbf{v}=\beta_{1}\textbf{v}+(1-\beta_{1})\nabla J_{S},

(26)

\textbf{s}=\beta_{2}\textbf{s}+(1-\beta_{2}){\nabla J_{S}}^{2},

(27)

\alpha=\eta_{p}\left(\frac{1-{\beta_{2}}^{i}}{1-{\beta_{1}}^{i}}\right),

(28)

\nabla J_{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial J_{S}}{\partial\mathbf{\Xi}}&\frac{\partial J_{S}}{\partial\textbf{p}}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}},

(29)

and where
\epsilon
is an arbitrarily small number,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are tunable constants usually set as 0.9 and 0.999, respectively, and
\eta_{p}
is the baseline learning rate value.

An additional cost term is included to force the autonomous function to satisfy
f(0)=0
. The term and its gradient are given as

J_{0}=K_{0}|f(0)|,

(30)

\nabla J_{0}=K_{0}\frac{|f|}{f}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{\Xi}}&\frac{\partial f}{\partial\textbf{p}}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}\bigg|_{t=0},

(31)

where
f=\mathbf{\Xi_{f}}\mathbf{\Theta_{f}}(N,\mathbf{p_{f}})
and
K_{0}
is the weight factor, which should be high enough to enforce the condition, but not too high to deform
f(N)
in the vicinity of 0, as the learning algorithm might be too focused on
f(0)
.

The ST is performed according to the following condition:

\xi(\xi<\text{threshold})=0,\text{for any }\xi\in\mathbf{\Xi}

(32)

and also according to properties of given basis functions. For instance, if logistic functions (LFs) were utilized, the center shifted too far from the active region would make the function either 0 or 1, which would affectively exclude it or merge it with the other constant functions in the set.

Next step is the extraction of the autonomous dynamics
f(N)
by setting
W_{f}=0\hskip 2.84526ptkg/s
, and generating a set of autonomous trajectories. The function
f(N)
is integrated starting from multiple initial conditions until the zero state is reached. This provides an estimate of the true autonomous dynamics of the GTE. It should be noted that the zero state,
N=0
, corresponds to the minimum sustainable physical spool speed, usually in the ground idle (GI) regime, w.r.t. which the normalization of
N
is performed.

The estimated trajectories can be pictured as a measured response of the GTE to an instantaneous step change of the fuel flow from a value corresponding to the initial spool speed to the GI value.

4.2
Optimization of the eigenvalues via temporal projection

For the optimal construction from
Korda and Mezić 2020
, apriori initial guesses of the eigenvalues need to be supplied. The article mentions a possible optimization of these guesses but it assumes gradient-based approach. However, we want to apply constraints and enforce conditions that must be satisfied by eigenvalues. The penalization terms may not have analytical gradients or their evaluation might be expensive.

The goal is to decompose the estimated autonomous dynamics
f(N)
to a linear combination of Koopman spectral components given as

f(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}(N_{0})e^{\lambda_{i}t},

(33)

or, alternatively, to decompose the trajectories generated by
f(N)
, as

N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\varphi_{i}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\varphi_{i}(N_{0})e^{\lambda_{i}t}.

(34)

Eq. (
33
) represents the spatiotemporal decomposition of the dynamics. Note that due to the properties of the linear combination, the eigenfunctions can be scaled so that the initial conditions are
\varphi_{i}(N_{0})=1
. Thus, the temporal decomposition can be rewritten as

N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}(N_{0})e^{\lambda_{i}t}=\textbf{K}\textbf{E},

(35)

where
k_{i}=c_{i}\varphi_{i},k_{i}\in\textbf{K}
are scaled mode amplitudes and
E
is a matrix of exponential trajectories.

The KPDE offers a powerful tool for discovery of the Koopman intrinsic coordinates - eigenfunctions, and thus no trial-and-error procedure connected to DMD-based methods is needed. The problem to be addressed is finding an optimal set of eigenvalues providing a good basis for projection of the autonomous dynamics. This set is composed of the most prominent eigenvalues, most likely with large negative real parts close to 0, as these are the longest persisting in the dynamics. Heavily damped eigenvalues, that will decay almost immediately, are not a suitable choice. A possible option is to identify a large set of eigenfunctions and subsequently utilize regularized LS to find a sparse vector of eigenmodes. For this, the algebraic structure of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions can be effectively exploited. However, it can be a time-consuming process that can often lead to many spurious eigen-tuples
Kaiser et al. 2021
.

In this paper, a meta-heuristic global optimization method called particle swarm optimization (PSO) was employed to solve the eigenvalue optimization problem since it would be difficult to address the following constraints with gradient-based approaches. This algorithm is a popular choice for global optimization and is supported by the MATLAB Global Optimization Toolbox. For a detailed description, the reader is refer to
Kennedy and Eberhart 1995
;
Eberhart et al. 2001
.

The single-spool GTE is a 1st-order system. Thus, the problem can be decoupled to a temporal projection of autonomous trajectories onto trajectories generated by a given set of eigenvalues, such that the projection error is minimized, and subsequent discovery of spatial shapes of eigenfunctions. The former should be reached by optimization of the given set of eigenvalues. Trajectories starting from
k
different initial conditions are augmented to a single matrix
\mathbf{N_{traj}}=\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}&N_{2}&\cdots&N_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}
. The set of Koopman modes should be optimal across the entire operating region. The complete constrained problem is formulated as

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\|\mathbf{N_{traj}}-\textbf{K}\textbf{E}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\|_{F}^{2},

(36)

s.t.

\displaystyle\textbf{E}(\mathbf{\Lambda})=\begin{bmatrix}e^{\mathbf{\lambda}_{1}t}&e^{\mathbf{\lambda}_{2}t}&\cdots&e^{\mathbf{\lambda}_{n}t}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}},

\displaystyle\textbf{K}=\left(\textbf{E}\textbf{E}^{\textbf{T}}-\alpha_{K}\textbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{EN_{traj}^{T}}\hskip 5.69054pt\bigg|_{\mathbf{\Lambda}=\text{const.}},

\displaystyle\text{Re}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\text{min}}\leq\text{Re}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\leq\text{Re}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\text{max}}<\textbf{0},

\displaystyle\text{Im}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\text{min}}\leq\text{Im}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\leq\text{Im}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\text{max}},

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Im}(\mathbf{\Lambda})}{\text{Re}(\mathbf{\Lambda})}\leq\left(\frac{\text{Im}(\mathbf{\Lambda})}{\text{Re}(\mathbf{\Lambda})}\right)_{\text{max}},

(37)

where
\alpha_{K}
is a regularization weight factor.

The objective function addresses the above-mentioned constraints through penalization. It also includes an innovative additional feature that allows to switch between distinct and repeated real eigenvalues, for which the corresponding generalized eigenfunctions obey the following equations:

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{R}\\
\varphi_{D}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&1\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{R}\\
\varphi_{D}\end{bmatrix},

(38)

where
\varphi_{R}
and
\varphi_{D}
denote eigenfunctions corresponding to the distinct and repeated eigenvalue, respectively.

The solution is given as

\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{R}\\
\varphi_{D}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{\lambda_{1}t}&te^{\lambda_{1}t}\\
0&e^{\lambda_{1}t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{R,0}\\
\varphi_{D,0}\end{bmatrix}.

(39)

The
te^{\lambda_{1}t}
term is called secular term and corresponds to a transient energy growth. In the context of fitting the autonomous dynamics, secular terms can help adapt the model better to the shape of trajectories.

Generation of purely exponential trajectories (distinct real) or secular terms (repeated eigenvalues) is based on a condition of a minimum distance between two consecutive eigenvalues. Eigenvalues are first sorted in a descending order and distances between each subsequent pair are computed as
\mathbf{\Lambda}(2:\text{end})-\mathbf{\Lambda}(1:\text{end}-1)
. If the distance between a given pair of eigenvalues falls below a specified threshold, the old pair is replaced by a new pair of repeated eigenvalues corresponding to the mean of the old pair.

The exponential trajectories represent a basis, onto which the autonomous trajectory is projected in the time domain. Whenever there are repeated eigenvalues, then not only the purely exponential trajectory
e^{\lambda t}
, but also the secular trajectory
e^{\lambda t}+te^{\lambda t}
is added to the basis. This way, the optimization algorithm summarized in Algorithm 1 can switch between the cases of distinct and repeated eigenvalues.

Regarding the complex eigenvalues, even though the single-spool engine is a strictly dissipative system (RPM monotonously decreases to 0), the slightly oscillatory modes can help to better explain the underlying dynamics as the model can mathematically better conform to the shape of autonomous trajectories. Note that the complex eigenvalue is written as
\lambda=\alpha\pm i\beta
. Thus, we use the notation
\text{Re}(\lambda)=\alpha
and
\text{Im}(\lambda)=\beta
.

To avoid excessively oscillatory modes, the imaginary-to-real-part ratio
\beta/\alpha
is checked, and the unsatisfactory modes are penalized according to some function
f(\beta/\alpha)
. The higher the imaginary part, the higher the natural frequency of the mode. Thus, if the ratio is too high, the real part, corresponding to damping, is not sufficiently decaying the amplitude of oscillations. This is summarized in Algorithm 2. In this paper, the function
f(\beta/\alpha)
is a simple comparison to a specified maximum ratio
(\beta/\alpha)_{max}
, exceeding of which leads to exclusion of the mode.

Furthermore, the principle of switching to repeated complex eigenvalues can also be adopted with the distance now being computed in the complex plane. For this purpose, an upper-triangular distance matrix can be computed, and whenever there is a pair of too close eigenvalues, the secular terms are generated as

\begin{bmatrix}\text{Re}(\varphi_{R})\\
\text{Im}(\varphi_{R})\\
\text{Re}(\varphi_{D})\\
\text{Im}(\varphi_{D})\end{bmatrix}=e^{\bar{\alpha}t}\begin{bmatrix}\cos{\bar{\beta}}&-\sin{\bar{\beta}}&t\cos{\bar{\beta}}&-t\sin{\bar{\beta}}\\
\sin{\bar{\beta}}&\cos{\bar{\beta}}&t\sin{\bar{\beta}}&t\cos{\bar{\beta}}\\
0&0&\cos{\bar{\beta}}&-\sin{\bar{\beta}}\\
0&0&\sin{\bar{\beta}}&\cos{\bar{\beta}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\text{Re}(\varphi_{R0})\\
\text{Im}(\varphi_{R0})\\
\text{Re}(\varphi_{D0})\\
\text{Im}(\varphi_{D0})\end{bmatrix},

(40)

where
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})/2
and
\bar{\beta}=(\beta_{i}+\beta_{j})/2
, the initial conditions are all equal to 1, and the distance matrix is given as

\textbf{D}=\begin{bmatrix}0&d_{1,2}&\cdots&d_{1,n}\\
0&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&d_{n-1,n}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},

(41)

where

d_{i,j}=\sqrt{\left(\text{Re}(\lambda_{i})-\text{Re}(\lambda_{j})\right)^{2}+\left(\text{Im}(\lambda_{i})-\text{Im}(\lambda_{j})\right)^{2}}.

(42)

However, the existence of repeated complex modes is rather rare. Furthermore, the real solutions are also often present even when the complex eigenvalues are considered. To further increase the adaptivity of the algorithm, whenever the imaginary part is smaller than a specified value, the pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues is merged to a single real eigenvalue by setting
\alpha=-\infty
for one of these eigenvalues. This effectively reduces the order of the system. Alternatively, the pair could be replaced by a pair of repeated real eigenvalues, but that was not considered in this paper.

4.3
Identification of analytical eigenfunctions from KPDE

The temporal projection optimization produces optimal eigenvalues and mode amplitudes. Furthermore, by plotting the exponential time trajectories as functions of spool speed, it also reveals the shape of eigenfunctions. However, direct fitting of these shapes was met with problems regarding the accuracy of the final model, especially due to the gradient of eigenfunctions being sensitive to shape discrepancies of the fitted eigenfunctions. Therefore, the KPDE cost function can be minimized instead, and the gradient-based algorithm is utilized for this purpose. The revealed shapes can help with selection of basis functions for the subsequent regression.

For a single-spool turbojet GTE, being a 1st-order system, the KPDE (
19
) for a single eigenfunction reduces to the following ODE:

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial N}f(N)=\lambda\varphi.

(43)

This equation can be solved analytically as

\ln{\varphi}=\lambda\int\frac{1}{f(N)}\text{dN}.

(44)

In practice, however,
f(N)
can be arbitrarily complex, especially for highly nonlinear systems such as the GTE. Thus, since the analytical computation can get extremely challenging, it becomes more advantageous to solve Eq. (
44
) numerically via the method of candidate solutions closely related to the SINDy algorithm.

Instead of utilization of generated autonomous trajectories, the function of autonomous dynamics
f(N)
is sampled using a nonlinear sampling pattern which secures increasing density of sample points towards 0. Examples of nonlinear sampling approaches are provided in Fig.
2
, where the
x
and
y
axes represent the relative position of the sample and the corresponding
N
value, respectively.

Figure 2
:
Examples of nonlinear sampling approaches

Similarly to the
f(N)
function, the eigenfunctions of a dissipative system should also satisfy
\varphi(0)=0
for all modes. This is due to the fact, that even the longest persisting mode (largest eigenvalue) should vanish after a finite period of time and thus the
\dot{N}\rightarrow 0
. The nonlinear sampling also indirectly makes the algorithm put more effort to satisfy this condition.

Additionally, as the optimization is two-sided in sense that both the gradient and the eigenfunction (both terms in the error) are optimized simultaneously, it is crucial to prevent the learning to head directly towards the trivial solution
\mathbf{\Xi=0}
. This is avoided by implementation of a penalty that is given by the difference of initial values of eigenfunctions. This way, the cost term acts as an artificial spring, which forces the solution to stay away form the origin of the
\mathbf{\Xi}
parameter space. The larger the distance, the larger the counterforce. Thus, the complete cost function for a single
\varphi=\mathbf{\Xi\Theta}(N,\textbf{p})
and distinct real eigenvalue
\lambda
is given as

J_{e,r,d}=\left\|\mathbf{\Xi}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\Theta}}{\partial N}\dot{N}-\lambda\mathbf{\Xi\Theta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{1}\|\mathbf{\Xi}|_{1}+\alpha_{2}\|\varphi(N_{0})_{1}-\varphi(N_{0})\|_{2}^{2},

(45)

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are the weights of the L1 sparsity promoting regularization and the trivial solution penalty, respectively, and
\varphi(N_{0})_{1}
and
\varphi(N_{0})
represent the initial value of the eigenfunction at the first iteration and at the current iteration, respectively.

Concerning the repeated eigenvalues, the first cost term corresponding to the generalized eigenfunction is given by

J_{e,r,r}=\left\|\mathbf{\Xi}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\Theta}}{\partial N}\dot{N}-\lambda\mathbf{\Xi\Theta}-\varphi_{\lambda,d}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(46)

where
\varphi_{\lambda,d}
is the eigenfunction corresponding to the distinct eigenvalue.

For the complex eigenvalues, the KPDE is given as

\frac{\partial}{\partial N}(\varphi_{R}\pm i\varphi_{I})\dot{N}=(\alpha\pm i\beta)(\varphi_{R}\pm i\varphi_{I}).

(47)

Splitting it into the real and the imaginary part, we obtain

\displaystyle Re:\frac{\partial\varphi_{R}}{\partial N}\dot{N}=\alpha\varphi_{R}-\beta\varphi_{I},

(48)

\displaystyle Im:\frac{\partial\varphi_{I}}{\partial N}\dot{N}=\beta\varphi_{R}+\alpha\varphi_{I}.

(49)

The first term of the cost function is thus given as

J_{e,c}=\left\|\frac{\partial\varphi_{R}}{\partial N}\dot{N}-\alpha\varphi_{R}+\beta\varphi_{I}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\partial\varphi_{I}}{\partial N}\dot{N}-\beta\varphi_{R}-\alpha\varphi_{I}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(50)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the real and the imaginary part of the eigenvalue, respectively, and
\varphi_{R}=\mathbf{\Xi_{R}\Theta_{R}}(N,\mathbf{p_{R}})
and
\varphi_{I}=\mathbf{\Xi_{I}\Theta_{I}}(N,\mathbf{p_{I}})
.

4.4
Autonomous trajectory projection and the complete KEM

Once the eigenfunctions are identified, the mode amplitudes are obtained by solving the following regularized LS problem in the spatial domain:

\textbf{C}=\arg\min_{\textbf{C}}\left\|N-\textbf{C}\mathbf{\Phi}(N)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{C}\|\textbf{C}\|_{1},

(51)

or, equivalently, in the temporal domain:

\textbf{C}=\arg\min_{\textbf{C}}\left\|N(t)-\textbf{C}\mathbf{\Phi}(N_{0})e^{\mathbf{\Lambda}t}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{C}\|\textbf{C}\|_{1},

(52)

where
\alpha_{C}
is the regularization parameter.

Regularization helps excluding non-active modes and preserve only the prominent ones leading to a further dimensionality reduction. The final model is given as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Phi}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}\dot{N}

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}\left(f(N)+g(N)W_{f}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Phi}+\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}g(N)W_{f},

(53)

where the RHS can be further approximated using a suitable basis, e.g. polynomials or RBF, or converted to a look-up-table format.

An example of an affine LPV decomposition of the input dynamics
\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}(N)g(N)
is presented in Appendix
8
. Finally, the discrete-time matrices can be obtained as
\mathbf{L}=\text{exp}({\mathbf{\Lambda}t})
and
\mathbf{G_{d}=\Lambda^{-1}(L-I)\nabla\Phi}g
.

5
Controller design

5.1
Proportional-integral controller

A control system with a PI controller and fuel-flow limiters was designed using the nonlinear CLM. According to the PART 31 regulation, the control system should be able to safely increase the thrust from 15
\%
to 95
\%
of the nominal value in under 5 seconds
Federal Aviation Administration 2025-04-18
. For this purpose, the PI controller was manually tuned to achieve the desired performance. The resulting gains were given as
K_{p}=1
and
K_{i}=3
.

5.2
LPV-PI controller

The SINDy governing equation was linearized analytically using the following equations:

a(N)=\frac{\partial f(N)}{\partial N}+\frac{\partial g(N)}{\partial N}W_{f},

(54)

b(N)=\frac{\partial f(N)}{\partial W_{f}}+\frac{\partial g(N)W_{f}}{\partial W_{f}}=g(N),

(55)

and the transfer function in the frequency domain was

G_{e}(s)=\frac{K_{e}(N)}{T_{e}(N)s+1},

(56)

where
K_{e}=-b(N)/a(N)
and
T_{e}=-1/a(N)
are the engine gain and time constant, respectively.

Subsequently, a finite set of sample steady-state points was selected. At each of these points, the system was assumed to be LTI, and the PI controller was tuned to reach optimal performance. There are several methods for LTI PID tuning, e.g. the Skogestad’s internal model method
Skogestad 2003
accounting for time delays, or the popular, yet not always sufficient, Ziegler-Nichols methods
Ziegler and Nichols 1942
. In this paper, the MATLAB native command
pidtune
was utilized. The primary objective was set to reference tracking. Further fine tuning was performed manually via the Live Editor application ”Tune PID Controller” with focus on overshoot reduction. For each LTI subsystem, the trade-off between overshoots and speed was of interest making the process rather time-consuming. Finally, the controller gains were fitted by 5th-order polynomials. It is important to note that these gains are actually the corrected parameters of
K_{p}
and
K_{i}
. Their relationship is given as

K_{p}=K_{p,corr}\frac{101325}{p_{1t}}

(57)

K_{i}=K_{i,corr}\sqrt{\frac{288}{T_{1t}}}\frac{p_{1t}}{101325}

(58)

5.3
SINDy - IMC

Since the dead-time effects are neglected in this study, the standard internal model controller (IMC) approach was considered. Details can be found, e.g., in
Rivera et al. 1986
;
Ningsih et al. 2024
. The derived SINDy model can be utilized for design of a gain scheduled IMC controller, which is another contribution of this paper. Assuming the fuel system delay as a first-order lag, the second-order internal model in each steady-state point is given as

G_{i}(s)=\frac{K_{e}(N_{i})}{T_{p}T_{e}(N_{i})s^{2}+(T_{p}+T_{e}(N_{i}))s+1}.

(59)

The internal model controller is given as

G_{c}(s)=(G_{i}(s)^{-})^{-1}(\tau_{f}s+1)^{-2},

(60)

where
G_{i}^{-}
denotes the stable part of the system’s transfer function,
G_{i}
, and
\tau_{f}
is a time constant of a filter that renders the controller realizable.

The resulting closed-loop controller is

G_{IMC}(s)=\frac{G_{c}(s)}{1-G_{c}(s)G_{i}(s)}.

(61)

Since the system (
59
) is inherently stable, and thus
G_{i}^{-}=G_{i}
, the resulting controller is

\displaystyle G_{IMC}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{i}^{-1}(\tau_{f}s+1)^{-2}}{1-G_{i}^{-1}G_{i}(\tau_{f}s+1)^{-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{i}^{-1}}{\tau_{f}^{2}s^{2}+2\tau_{f}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{p}T_{e}(N_{i})s^{2}+(T_{p}+T_{e}(N_{i}))s+1}{K_{e}(\tau_{f}^{2}s^{2}+2\tau_{f}s)}.

(62)

This expression can be rewritten to an alternative form for
\dot{v}
control. Multiplying both sides by
K_{e}(\tau_{f}^{2}s^{2}+2\tau_{f}s)E(s)
, performing inverse Laplace transform and integrating both sides yields the following control law:

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K_{e}\tau_{f}^{2}}\left(T_{e}T_{f}\dot{e}+(T_{e}+T_{f})e+\int_{0}^{t}e(\tau)d\tau\right)-\frac{2}{\tau_{f}}v,

(63)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\int\dot{v}d\tau,

(64)

where the first term on the right-hand side represents a PID controller with the gains given as
K_{p}=(T_{e}+T_{f})/(K_{e}\tau_{f}^{2})
,
K_{i}=1/(K_{e}\tau_{f}^{2})
and
K_{d}=T_{e}T_{f}/(K_{e}\tau_{f}^{2})
.

The filter time constant
\tau_{f}
is the only tuning parameter and can be scheduled as a function of
N
. In this paper, it was assumed constant. The higher the
\tau_{f}
, the lower the sensitivity to measurement noise due to the derivative action as the cut-off frequency decreases, but the slower the response. Thus, the optimal tuning is a trade off between the noise sensitivity and performance. The corrected parameters are assumed as in the previous case. 5.4
LQI and Kalman observer design

The LQG controller with integral action (servo LQG or LQGI) can be designed in the eigenfunction space to reach a globally optimal control performance even outside the region of validity of the linearized LPV model. The LQGI design concise of two step - the LQI controller design and the Kalman observer design. Both steps can be performed individually according to the separation theorem.

The advantage of designing controllers in the lifted state space, eigenfunction coordinates, is that we can employ powerful tools known from the control theory of linear systems and conveniently analyze the stability and robustness. Opposed to the locally linearized systems, the stability and robustness hold not only in local regions around steady-state operating points, but also in the wider operating region of the engine, supposed that this region is sufficiently covered by training data.

5.4.1
LQI controller design preliminaries

To design an LQI controller, the eigenfunctions are first augmented with the fuel system dynamics characterized by an FOPDT with time constant
T_{f}
. The first augmented state vector is denoted as
\beta=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}&W_{f}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}
. Since the functions of input dynamics are dependent on the spool speed estimate, the system is evaluated at a finite set of evenly distributed sample points from the
N
interval. At each point, the system is treated as an LTI system, where the matrices are defined as

\textbf{A}_{\textbf{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Lambda}&\textbf{G}(N_{i})\\
\textbf{0}&-\frac{1}{T_{f}}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{B}_{\textbf{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
\frac{1}{T_{f}}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{C}&0\end{bmatrix},

(65)

where
\textbf{G}(N_{i})=(\partial\mathbf{\Phi}(N_{i})/\partial N)g(N_{i})
.

A new input variable
v
is introduced. It represents the fuel flow
command
given by the controller. Furthermore,
\beta
is augmented with an integral state
\eta
, dynamics of which are given as

\eta=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}(N_{d}-N)d\tau,\hskip 14.22636pt\dot{\eta}=N_{d}-\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}\mathbf{\Phi}.

(66)

The final augmented state vector is denoted as
\textbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta&\eta\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}
, and the resulting state-space model is

\dot{\textbf{z}}=\mathbf{A_{aug,i}\hskip 1.42262ptz}+\mathbf{B_{aug,i}}\hskip 1.42262ptv+\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}N_{d},

(67)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A_{aug,i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{A}_{\textbf{i}}&\textbf{0}\\
-\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{B_{aug,i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{B}_{\textbf{i}}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(68)

The optimal controller feedback gain matrix minimizes the following cost function:

J_{LQI}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\left(\textbf{z}^{\textbf{T}}\textbf{Q}\textbf{z}+Rv^{2}\right)d\tau.

(69)

This problem is usually solved using quadratic programming. The final K-LQGI control law is defined for corrected quantities. The corrected integral state is given as

\eta_{corr}=\sqrt{\frac{288}{T_{1t}}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}(N_{d}-N)d\tau=\eta\sqrt{\frac{288}{T_{1t}}}.

(70)

This way, the effects of changing flight conditions are captured to some extent. However, for higher Mach number of flight, the corrections start losing validity due to compressibility effects.

5.4.2
Optimization algorithm

The metaheuristic approach, e.g. genetic algorithm (GA) or PSO, was utilized to optimize the LQI controller. The MATLAB Riccati equation solver provides a simple way to solve the LQI problem described above. Additionally, alongside with the standard integral cost function (
69
) minimized by the solver, the GA minimizes a custom cost function based on the integral of absolute error (IAE). Furthermore, since specified stability margins must be respected, the problem is constrained. Thus, GA and PSO are suitable tools
Eberhart et al. 2001
;
Haupt and Haupt 2004
.

The controller cost function is a sum of a weighted IAE and additional terms accounting for the maximum overshoot and undershoot. These terms also penalize not reaching the maximum and minimum commands fast enough. The weighting by
w_{e}
is performed so that the error during smaller transients has the same magnitude as during the large transients to even out their importance.

The controller optimization problem is given as

\min_{\mathbf{Q_{c}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{s}-1}w_{e}\left(\frac{|e_{k}|+|e_{k+1}|}{2}\right)\Delta t+\alpha_{os}|\max(N)-1|+\alpha_{us}|\min(N)-0.5|,

(71)

s.t.

\displaystyle e_{k}=N_{d}(t_{k})-N(t_{k}),

(72)

\displaystyle v_{min}\leq v\leq v_{max},

(73)

\displaystyle\dot{\eta}=0\quad\text{when}\quad v=v_{min}\lor v=v_{max},\quad(\text{Clamping})

(74)

\displaystyle W_{f}=\left(1-\Delta t/T_{f}\right)W_{f}+\left(\Delta t/T_{f}\right)v,

(75)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}=\Lambda\Phi+G}(N)W_{f},

(76)

\displaystyle N(t_{k})=\mathbf{C\Phi}(t_{k}),

(77)

\displaystyle w_{e}=\begin{cases}5&\quad\text{for }5\leq t<50\\
0&\quad\text{for }t<5\text{, and }50\leq t<55,\\
1&\quad\text{for }55\leq t\leq 70\end{cases}

(78)

\displaystyle GM_{min}>6\text{ dB},

(79)

\displaystyle PM_{min}>60\text{ deg},

(80)

where
\alpha_{os}
and
\alpha_{us}
are the overshooting and undershooting weights, respectively,
n_{s}
is the number of simulation time steps, and
GM_{min}
and
PM_{min}
are the minimum open-loop gain and phase margins based on the entire range of
N_{i}
, respectively.

Among the optimized parameters are the weight matrix of the transient spool speed
\mathbf{Q_{N}}
, integral action
\mathbf{Q_{i}}
, delayed fuel flow
\mathbf{Q_{f}}
, and input
\mathbf{R_{c}}
. These are further complemented by a weight perturbation diagonal matrix
\textbf{dQ}_{\varphi}
targeting individual spectral components. This allows the algorithm to balance a possibly more aggressive integral action. The complete state weighting matrix is given as

\mathbf{Q_{c}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q_{\varphi}+dQ_{\varphi}}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{i}}\end{bmatrix},

(81)

where the
\mathbf{Q_{\varphi}}
matrix is generally obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x^{T}Q_{x}x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{(C\Phi)^{T}Q_{x}C\Phi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi^{T}C^{T}Q_{x}C\Phi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi^{T}Q_{\varphi}\Phi}.

(82)

Thus, for the GTE, it yields
\mathbf{Q_{\varphi}=C^{T}Q_{N}C}
.

The objective function includes a solution of the continuous algebraic Riccati equation (CARE) for a finite set of sample points
N_{i}
evenly distributed across the normalization interval
[N_{min},N_{max}]
. At each point, the system is considered LTI with a controllable pair
(\mathbf{A_{aug,i},B_{aug,i}})
, and the following equations are solved:

\mathbf{K_{LQI,i}=R_{c}^{-1}B_{aug,i}^{T}P_{c}},

(83)

where
\mathbf{P_{c}}
is a solution of the CARE given by

\mathbf{A_{aug,i}^{T}P_{c}+P_{c}A_{aug,i}+Q_{c}-P_{c}B_{aug,i}R_{c}^{-1}B_{aug,i}^{T}P_{c}=0}.

(84)

The LQI control law obtained from the CARE is given as

v=-\mathbf{K_{LQI}}(\hat{N})\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\beta&\eta\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}.

(85)

Subsequently, the feedback gain matrix entries are interpolated or approximated by a suitable function of
N
, and the numerical integration is utilized to simulate the system’s response to a predefined testing spool-speed command profile depicted in Fig.
4
. This profile captures both the small and large transients.

5.4.3
Kalman observer design

To improve the prediction performance of the baseline KEM, a Kalman observer can be designed. The KEM is considered noisy as follow:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}=\Lambda\Phi+\nabla\Phi}gu+\mathbf{w_{p}},\quad\mathbf{w_{p}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0,\Sigma_{p}}),

(86)

\displaystyle\hat{N}=\mathbf{C\Phi}+w_{n},\quad w_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}),

where
\mathbf{w_{p}}
and
w_{n}
are the process and measurement noise, respectively, both sampled from zero-mean Normal distribution.

Since both
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and
C
matrices are time-invariant, the steady Kalman filter can be obtained as a solution to the estimator CARE. This was discussed in, e.g.,
Surana 2016
. The observer state-space model is shown in Fig.
5
.

Figure 5
:
The Kalman observer block diagram

The resulting state equation is given as

\dot{\hat{\beta}}=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{i}}\hat{\beta}+\textbf{B}_{\textbf{i}}v+\textbf{L}_{\beta}(N-\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}\hat{\beta})=(\textbf{A}_{\textbf{i}}-\textbf{L}_{\beta}\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}})\hat{\beta}+\textbf{B}_{\textbf{i}}v+\textbf{L}_{\beta}N,

(87)

where the Kalman gain matrix
\textbf{L}_{\beta}
is considered constant.

As can be seen, the fuel flow state
W_{f}
is also included, since the equation is formulated for
\beta
. The gain matrix can be further written as
\textbf{L}_{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{L}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}
, where, during the design phase, the matrix
L
is optimized only for
\mathbf{\Phi}
, yielding a static Kalman observer, and the fuel flow estimation depends only on the first-order model. Thus,
\hat{W}_{f}=W_{f}
as demonstrated in Eq. (
88
).

\hat{W}_{f}=W_{f}=\left(1-\frac{\Delta t}{T_{f}}\right)\hat{W}_{f}+\frac{\Delta t}{T_{f}}v.

(88)

Thus, the
W_{f}
dynamics are accounted for only during the LQI design. This assumption was deemed valid since the first-order model of the fuel system was considered accurate enough to give reliable information about the fuel flow with no need for correction step.

If the estimation of
W_{f}
would still be of interest, it would be more advantageous to switch to the discrete-time formulation of the KEM and utilize the time-varying Kalman filter, as the
\mathbf{A_{i}}
matrix would become time-varying.

Several approaches can be used for the observer design, see, e.g.,
Surana 2016
;
Raghavan and Hedrick 1994
;
Tewari 2002
. In this paper, similarly to the LQI design step, assuming that the pair
(\mathbf{\Lambda,C})
is observable, the CARE approach is considered, and the Kalman gain is given by the following equations:s

\mathbf{L=P_{o}C^{T}R_{o}^{-1}},

(89)

where
\textbf{P}_{\textbf{o}}
is a solution of the observer CARE:

\mathbf{\Lambda P_{o}+P_{o}\Lambda^{T}+G_{o}Q_{o}G_{o}^{T}-P_{o}C^{T}R_{o}^{-1}CP_{o}=0},

(90)

where
\mathbf{G_{o}}
is the process noise gain matrix usually set to
I
,
\mathbf{R_{o}}
is the measurement noise covariance matrix estimated from data, and
\mathbf{Q_{o}}
is the process noise covariance matrix. The latter can be estimated using the test dataset eigenfunction error covariance
\text{Cov}(\mathbf{\Phi}(t)-\mathbf{\Phi}(N))
, where
\mathbf{\Phi}(N)
are eigenfunctions evaluated using the ground-truth data. Alternatively,
\mathbf{Q_{o}}
can be estimated also from the eigenfunction time derivative error covariance since the process noise
\mathbf{w_{p}}
is acting on the dynamics in Eq. (
86
). Thus, the process noise covariance estimate is given as

\mathbf{\Sigma_{p}}\approx\text{Cov}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{\Phi}(N)}{\partial N}\dot{N}-\mathbf{\Lambda\Phi}(t)-\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}\big(\hat{N}(t)\big)g\big(\hat{N}(t)\big)u(t)\right).

(91)

The resulting Koopman LQGI control law is given using the estimated state vector as

v=-\mathbf{K_{\beta}}(\hat{N})\hat{\beta}-K_{i}(\hat{N})\eta,

(92)

and
\mathbf{K_{LQI}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{K}_{\beta}&K_{i}\end{bmatrix}
.

Inserting the control law in Eq. (

67

\dot{\varepsilon}\\

-\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}&\textbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\\

\eta\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}N_{d},

(93)

\hat{N}=\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}\hat{\beta}=\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}(\beta-\varepsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i }}&-\textbf{C}_{\textbf{i}}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\\

(94)

where

6.1.2
SINDy

For identification of the GTE’s governing equation, the SINDy algorithm with logistic functions (LFs) was utilized. LFs are a popular choice for activation functions in neural networks and they have favorable properties
Ito 1992
. As explained in Section
4.1
, SINDy was combined with GD and ADAM optimizer and subjected to the L1 regularization of linear coefficients. Additionally, specifically for the LFs, and basically for any RBF, the sparsity could be further achieved also through the nonlinear parameters. Thus, these were optimized as well instead of just being guessed, or assumed to be evenly distributed, as is a common practice. The equation of LF is given as

LF(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\varepsilon(x-\mu)}},

(95)

where
\varepsilon
and
\mu
are the steepness parameter and center point, respectively.

The following properties of LFs can help excluding some inactive or constant terms from the SINDy model:

•

\xi\rightarrow 0
for inactive terms via regularization.

•

\mu\rightarrow\infty\ \cup\ \varepsilon>0
or
\mu\rightarrow-\infty\ \cup\ \varepsilon<0
causes LF to be 0.

•

\mu\rightarrow-\infty\cup\varepsilon>0
or
\mu\rightarrow\infty\ \cup\varepsilon<0
causes LF to be 1 (constant).

•

\varepsilon=0
causes LB to be 0.5 (constant).

In the latter two cases, constants can be summed up to form a single constant function. The L1 regularization parameter was set to
10^{-4}
, and the zero derivative condition parameter was set to 0.005. The initial learning rates were set as 0.01, 50 and 0.01 for
\Xi
,
\varepsilon
and
\mu
, respectively. The algorithm minimized a cost function given by the static
\dot{N}
data. Additionally, every 10 iterations, the differential equation (
23

This points at the nonlinear nature of the engine dynamics, further supported by the fuel flow limiting, which could not be captured by the scheduling approach. Since limiters were inactive during small transients, the fact that K-LQGI did not cause overshoots points at the ability of the controller to decrease the acceleration rate close to the desired state accordingly. The Kalman estimates showed the ability of KEM to predict the engine state reliably.

7
Conclusions

To the author’s best knowledge, based on a rather extensive literature review, this is the first paper utilizing the KOT for the design of an optimal GTE controller. Furthermore, it is also first to combine the SINDy algorithm with the KOT for estimation of GTE governing equation with a subsequent Koopman canonical transform to the KEM. Another contribution is the constrained optimization of a low-order set of eigenvalues using metaheuristic global optimization tools, e.g. GA and PSO, and the projection of the autonomous trajectories in the time domain, providing an optimal set of eigenvalues for the eigenfunction construction from data. The objective function rejects the unstable, too oscillatory, and too lightly damped eigenvalues. Moreover, the algorithm can switch from distinct real and complex eigenvalues to repeated ones when the distance of any two eigenvalues is smaller then a specified margin. The discovered set of eigenfunctions and eigenvalues can also be extended by exploitation of the algebraic structure. However, by a product of two eigenfunctions we obtain a new eigenfunction that always decays faster than the original two functions and thus it less contributes to the dynamics.

The KEM with the Kalman observer was capable of an accurate state prediction even outside the region of validity of the LPV system. Furthermore, it allows for more aggressive integral control balanced by targeting of individual modes, where the modes with the lowest damping are the most important ones. This is captured by the reduced overshoots compared to PI and LPV-PI controllers. Furthermore, the LPV-PI and IMC controllers both struggle with the large deceleration, which points at too small PI gains in the low-RPM region for the deceleration. However, further increase of these gains would cause larger overshoots during small accelerations for the LPV-PI controller and increased sensitivity to noise for the IMC controller. Thus, the locally linearized controllers are overall effectively outperformed by the optimized KEM LQGI.

Both the identification method and the subsequently designed controller proved to be efficient for control of a single-spool turbojet GTE. For multi-spool turbojet and turbofan GTEs, the principle of temporal projection onto exponential trajectories is challenging and poses limitations. The problem is primarily caused by the ambiguity in coefficients
K
encoding both the unknown initial conditions of eigenfunctions and the mode amplitudes. These cannot be easily decoupled like in the case of 1st-order systems, where the variation of
K
for different trajectories is caused only by the changing initial conditions of
\Phi
. This will be a subject of future research. Fortunately, the optimization of eigenvalues is the only problem as the presented eigenfunction identification procedure works in higher dimensions as well.

In the future research, we will extend this framework to multi-rotor and turbofan engines.

Parameter-affine LPV approximation of the input dynamics

The input dynamics given by
\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}(N)g(N)
are relatively complex as each of the entries is given by a product of two linear combinations of nonlinear basis functions which, in the case of
\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}
, are different for each entry. Thus, it would be suitable to approximate this complicated expression by an affine LPV representation and, possibly, even polytopic form.

The goal is to decompose the input dynamics as

\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}(N)g(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}(N)\mathbf{\tilde{G}_{i}}=\mathbf{\tilde{G}}\boldsymbol{\rho},

(99)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\tilde{G}_{1}}&\mathbf{\tilde{G}_{2}}&\cdots&\mathbf{\tilde{G}_{m}}\end{bmatrix}
are basis matrices of the decomposition and
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}&\rho_{2}&\cdots&\rho_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{\textbf{T}}
are nonlinear scalar scheduling functions.

The primary goal is to optimize
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, parameterized by
p
, solving the following minimization problem:

p

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\textbf{p}}\|\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}(N)g(N)-\mathbf{\tilde{G}}\boldsymbol{\rho}(N,\textbf{p})\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{G}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\rho\rho}^{\textbf{T}}-\alpha_{g}\textbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\rho}G^{T}}\hskip 5.69054pt\bigg|_{\mathbf{p}=\text{const.}},

(100)

where
p
is the vector of nonlinear parameters of the scheduling functions,
\alpha_{g}
is the regularization parameter and
\textbf{G}=\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}(N)g(N)
.

For the discrete-time system, the matrices can be rewritten as

\mathbf{L}=e^{\mathbf{\Lambda}\Delta t},\quad\mathbf{G_{d}=\Lambda^{-1}(L-I)\tilde{G}\boldsymbol{\rho}=\tilde{G}_{d}\boldsymbol{\rho}},

(101)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{G}_{d}=\Lambda^{-1}(L-I)\tilde{G}}
is a new basis matrix and vector
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is evaluated in discrete time steps
t_{k}=k\Delta t
.

This problem can be handled with known optimization solvers, e.g. GD, GA or PSO. The latter two are capable of a quick search in the parametric space, whereas the GD can be utilized to analytically fine-tune the solution. However, the linear coefficients would need to be optimized as well leading to high-dimensional gradients and slower overall performance. Thus the meta-heuristic global optimization tools might be preferred.

The results of LPV decomposition with KEM prediction MAE, using different number of LFs, are depicted in Fig.
28
. It can be concluded that the approximation is sufficient for
m=8
.

The computation of the input dynamics in each time step reduces only to an evaluation of 8 nonlinear functions and further matrix operations, which are rather fast. This is an improvement compared to the evaluation of
6\times 10
functions for the gradient and 5 functions for
g(N)
. The model can be also converted to the polytopic form.
