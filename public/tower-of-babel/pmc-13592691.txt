Response to “Natural Coumarins as ABC Transporters Modulators: A Key Mechanistic Question for Osteosarcoma Chemotherapy”
Authors: Parastoo Azadbeigi, Sara Seyedshazileh, Ahmadreza Gharaiean‐Morshed, Mehrdad Iranshahi, Fatemeh B Rassouli
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Dr. Shi‐Hong Ren for his interest in our recent article published in Chemistry & Biodiversity [1] and for his thoughtful commentary raising an important methodological consideration regarding the interpretation of our mitoxantrone‐based efflux assay. We appreciate the opportunity to engage in this scientific dialogue, which ultimately strengthens the rigor and impact of our work.

Dr. Ren correctly notes that mitoxantrone serves as a universal substrate recognized and extruded by both ABCB1 and ABCG2, and that the cumulative fluorescence signal measured in our efflux assay reflects the combined activity of both transporters simultaneously. We fully acknowledge this methodological consideration, which we had already addressed in the discussion section of our original manuscript. However, we wish to clarify a critical point that may have been overlooked in the commentary: our study did not claim that GBA or AUR selectively target ABCB1 over ABCG2, or vice versa.

Our conclusion was intentionally framed around “ABC transporters” in the plural, reflecting our functional data showing modulation of transporter activity and molecular docking interactions with both proteins. Specifically, we stated in the conclusion that “GBA and AUR enhanced the efficacy of Alkeran chemotherapy in osteosarcoma cells, partially through targeting the ABC transporters ABCB1 and ABCG2” [1]. The mitoxantrone efflux assay was selected as an initial screening tool to detect functional interaction with ABC transporters collectively—an approach entirely consistent with established methodologies in the field [2, 3]. This assay was never intended to, nor claimed to, distinguish between ABCB1 and ABCG2 activity.

We agree with Dr. Ren that determining the relative contribution of each transporter is a scientifically valuable question that deserves further investigation. Indeed, we explicitly acknowledged this limitation in our original manuscript:

“Although efflux assay and molecular simulations support transporter involvement, mostly consistent with ABCG2, they do not yet conclusively demonstrate ABCB1 and ABCG2 targeting. Conducting further assessments using transporter‐specific controls (e.g., Ko143 for ABCG2 and/or verapamil for ABCB1) would provide deeper insights into the observed effects [1].”

Thus, we view Dr. Ren's commentary not as a critique of our conclusions but as a constructive proposal for extending our findings—which aligns with our own stated future directions.

Supporting Evidence for ABC Transporter Targeting

While we acknowledge the limitation of the mitoxantrone assay regarding isoform specificity, multiple lines of evidence in our study support the conclusion that GBA and AUR functionally interact with ABC transporters:

Functional efflux data: Both GBA and AUR significantly increased intracellular mitoxantrone accumulation compared to untreated and vehicle controls, demonstrating functional modulation of ABC transporter activity [1].

Concluding Remarks

In summary, our study provides a solid foundation demonstrating that GBA and AUR enhance the efficacy of Alkeran in osteosarcoma cells through modulation of ABC transporter activity. Our conclusions were appropriately framed around “ABC transporters” collectively, and we explicitly acknowledged the limitation regarding isoform specificity in our original manuscript. We appreciate Dr. Ren's commentary as it highlights an important direction for future mechanistic studies.

We thank the editorial team at Chemistry & Biodiversity for facilitating this scientific exchange, which ultimately strengthens the rigor and impact of our published work. We look forward to contributing further to this important area of research and to translating these promising findings into clinical benefits for osteosarcoma patients.
