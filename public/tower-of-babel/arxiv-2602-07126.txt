Finding Connections: Membership Inference Attacks for the Multi-Table Synthetic Data Setting
Joshua Ward, Chi-Hua Wang, Guang Cheng

Abstract
Synthetic tabular data has gained attention for enabling privacy-preserving data sharing. While substantial progress has been made in single-table synthetic generation where data are modeled at the row or item level, most real-world data exists in relational databases where a user’s information spans items across multiple interconnected tables. Recent advances in synthetic relational data generation have emerged to address this complexity, yet release of these data introduce unique privacy challenges as information can be leaked not only from individual items but also through the relationships that comprise a complete user entity. To address this, we propose a novel Membership Inference Attack (MIA) setting to audit the empirical user-level privacy of synthetic relational data and show that single-table MIAs that audit at an item level underestimate user-level privacy leakage. We then propose Multi-Table Membership Inference Attack (MT-MIA), a novel adversarial attack under a No-Box threat model that targets learned representations of user entities via Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks. By incorporating all connected items for a user, MT-MIA better targets user-level vulnerabilities induced by inter-tabular relationships than existing attacks. We evaluate MT-MIA on a range of real-world multi-table datasets and demonstrate that this vulnerability exists in state-of-the-art relational synthetic data generators, employing MT-MIA to additionally study where this leakage occurs. 1 1 1 We make a repository of our code available at MT-MIA .

Non-Relational Tabular Data

In the non-relational tabular data case, each row typically represents a complete entity (e.g., a single user with all their associated attributes), with each entity modeled as an independent observation or item associated with a fixed set of features. In contrast to the single-table case, relational tabular data distributes information about a single user or entity across items in multiple tables connected through conditional joint relationships. The release of these data raises unique privacy challenges compared to the non-relational setting as the

2.2
Membership Inference Attacks for Synthetic Data Generation

MIAs are a class of adversarial techniques that aim to distinguish between member records—those used in the training set of a target model—and nonmember records—those drawn from an independent dataset
[
31
]
. Originally proposed in the context of classification models
[
29
,
21
,
4
,
40
,
44
,
47
]
, MIAs exploit the tendency of learning algorithms to behave differently on training data than on unseen data, often due to overfitting or memorization. As a result, MIAs have become a central tool for empirically auditing privacy leakage in machine learning systems, complementing formal guarantees such as differential privacy. More recently, MIAs have been adapted to the setting of tabular synthetic data generation, where the adversary’s goal is to infer whether a record contributed to the training of a generative model based on access to synthetic samples. In this context, successful membership inference indicates that the synthetic data preserves information too faithfully, potentially enabling privacy violations even when direct record linkage is not possible.

2.2.1
Single-Table MIAs

Membership inference attacks against single-table synthetic data aim to determine whether a given real record influenced the training of a generative model, based solely on properties of the released synthetic dataset and, in some cases, limited auxiliary information. Unlike MIAs for discriminative models, where the attacker probes a target model’s outputs directly, attacks in the synthetic data setting must infer membership indirectly through distributional artifacts left behind by the generation process.

A broad class of attacks operates using only the released synthetic table and, in some cases, auxiliary reference data, without access to the generative model. These attacks exploit the observation that records drawn from the training set often induce locally atypical behavior in the synthetic distribution, such as elevated neighborhood density, reduced variability, or near-duplicate synthetic samples. Operationally, they measure the extent a candidate record is memorized or overfit too using using distances, density estimates, or reconstruction scores to classify record membership
[
13
,
14
,
6
,
38
,
35
,
39
]
. By relying only on released samples and optional auxiliary data, these methods avoid assumptions about access to the generative model. Consequently, they are typically classified as operating in a no-box threat model. This setting is particularly relevant for private data release, where a data curator is unlikely to disclose generative model information to a potential adversary.

Stronger attacks assume access to additional information, most commonly partial or query access to the generative model. These attacks explicitly compare how likely a target record is under competing member and non-member hypotheses, often by training multiple shadow generative models to approximate each hypothesis and learning a decision rule to distinguish between them
[
33
,
15
,
23
]
. While such attacks can substantially improve inference accuracy, they are typically extremely computationally expensive, requiring repeated model training or large numbers of model queries. As a result, their practical relevance to synthetic data release is less clear, since data curators generally do not expose generative models or provide the level of access required to support such attacks.

Across this literature, attacks are formulated to audit item-level privacy for non-relational, single-table generative models, implicitly assuming that each record corresponds to an independent, fixed-dimensional feature vector. This assumption enables distance and density-based attacks and ties attack effectiveness directly to the degree of over-representation or memorization of individual training records in the synthetic data. In contrast, multi-table synthetic data induces a hierarchical or relational representation in which membership corresponds to the presence of a training entity across multiple linked tables. Attacks in this setting must implicitly learn a representation over sets of rows or relational subgraphs, rather than operating on a single row input.

2.2.2
Multi-Table MIAs

Compared to the single-table setting, membership inference attacks for multi-table synthetic data remain relatively underexplored. To date, there has been limited effort to formalize a threat model or define a standard MIA setting of entity membership across multiple relational tables.

The primary empirical study in this setting is the MIDST Competition at SATML 2025
[
17
]
, which evaluated membership inference attacks against multi-table synthetic data generated by ClavaDDPM under a range of white-box and black-box threat models. Despite access to multiple linked synthetic tables, the competition hosts noted that the strongest attacks relied exclusively on a designated main table, effectively reducing the problem to a single-table setting. Indeed, the winner of the competition ignored axillary tables entirely
[
41
]
. Attacks that attempted to incorporate information from auxiliary tables or relational structure were noted to not yield improved performance and that they often performed worse than single-table strategies.

Overall, existing empirical results suggest that current membership inference techniques do not successfully exploit relational dependencies in multi-table synthetic data, and that effective attacks in this setting have yet to be demonstrated.

2.3
Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks

Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks (HGNNs) provide the necessary inductive bias to preserve the multi-modal semantics of relational databases by explicitly modeling the distinct node and edge types inherent in database schemas. Unlike homogeneous GNNs, which treat all connections as semantically equivalent, HGNNs utilize type-specific transformation matrices and message-passing protocols to navigate the "web" of relational tables
[
30
]
. This capability is essential for membership inference in multi-table contexts, as privacy leakage often resides not in a single row, but in the specific structural alignment between a parent entity and its conditionally generated child records.

Early architectures in this domain, such as the Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network
[
36
]
, relied on hierarchical attention mechanisms—operating at both the node and semantic levels—to aggregate information across predefined meta-paths. However, the requirement for manual path engineering often limits their flexibility in complex database schemas. To address this, more recent frameworks like the Heterogeneous Graph Transformer
[
16
]
and the Graph Attention Transformer operator
[
3
]
introduce dynamic, typed-attention mechanisms that automatically learn the importance of different relational dependencies.

The recent formalization of Relational Deep Learning
[
9
]
further validates the use of HGNNs for database-centric tasks, demonstrating that structural representations can effectively capture the joint distributions of tabular data without the loss of information inherent in table flattening. MT-MIA leverages these advancements to perform privacy auditing, utilizing HGNNs to detect "memorized motifs"—instances where a synthetic generator reproduces training structures.

3
A User-Level Membership Inference Attack Setting

Releasing relational synthetic data implies a notion of privacy at the user-level which differs from the item-level privacy implied in the single-table setting. In this section, we develop a novel MIA setting to audit the empirical privacy of user entities and show that item-level privacy does not necessarily provide privacy protection at the user level, necessitating MT-MIA, a new membership inference technique proposed in Section
4
that follows this setting.

3.1
Relational Databases as Heterogeneous Graphs

Relational databases naturally induce heterogeneous graphs, providing a representation that explicitly models cross-table dependencies crucial for identifying privacy vulnerabilities.

3.2
Formalism

To begin, in the multi-table synthetic data generation setting, a generative model is trained with a training database or heterogeneous graph that is a random sample of the population
G_{train}\sim\mathbb{P}
.
\mathcal{M}
is then queried to generate
G_{synth}
. An adversary
\mathcal{A}:H\to\{0,1\}
aims to determine if a test subgraph
h^{*}
is an element of
G_{train}
or a holdout sample
G_{holdout}
, from the same distribution:
G_{holdout}\sim\mathbb{P}
. Formally, this classification or Membership Inference Attack can be expressed as:

\mathcal{A}(h^{*})=\mathbb{I}\left[f(h^{\star})>\gamma\right]

(1)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function,
f(h^{*})
is a scoring function of the test subgraph
h^{*}
, and
\gamma
is an adjustable decision threshold. The success of the attack can be measured using traditional binary classification metrics and can be interpreted as a measure of the privacy leakage introduced by the release of
G_{synth}
sampled from the model trained on the original data.

We define
h^{*}
as an entity subgraph of interest, where an adversary aims to determine whether
h^{*}
was included in the training graph, i.e., whether
h^{*}\subseteq G_{\text{train}}
. This differs from traditional MIAs that test if an independent item or row was included in the training dataset where instead we are interested in testing if the generative model preserves the privacy of all
v\in h^{*}
when released together.

Example
: Consider a relational database with two tables:
Customers
and
Transactions
, where each customer may place multiple distinct transactions (one-to-many relationship). This schema induces a heterogeneous graph in which each customer node connects through edges to a set of transaction nodes, forming a connected subgraph for each customer. In this setting, our task is to infer whether such a subgraph, representing a distinct customer and their full transaction history, was used for training.

3.3
Privacy Auditing with Subgraphs

In theory,
h^{*}
could be any subgraph of interest to an adversary or auditor as if
h^{*}\subseteq G_{train},\text{ then }\forall g\subseteq h^{*},g\subseteq G_{train}
. However, it is useful to add several conditions to simplify auditing procedures as conducting membership inference on all possible constructions of
h^{*}
is often computationally infeasible. For one, we restrict
h^{*}
to be a connected subgraph as by the construction of relational databases, two unconnected nodes imply independence. In addition, many real-world databases naturally decompose into disjoint connected subgraphs, where each subgraph represents a unit or entity, such as a customer and their associated transactions. We thus propose auditing a finite, well-structured set of subgraphs by leveraging the relational decomposition of databases.

Let
H_{\text{test}}=\{h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{n}\}
denote the set of all disjoint connected user subgraphs in
G_{\text{test}}=G_{train}\cup G_{holdout}
, where
h_{i}\subseteq G_{\text{test}}
and
h_{i}\cap h_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. We propose to audit membership specifically over
\mathcal{H}_{\text{test}}
. This formulation and these assumptions lead to the result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
G_{\text{test}}=(V,E)
be a graph that is the disjoint union of two subgraphs
G_{\text{train}}
and
G_{\text{holdout}}
, where
V(G)=V(G_{\text{train}})\cup V(G_{\text{holdout}})
and
V(G_{\text{train}})\cap V(G_{\text{holdout}})=\emptyset
. Furthermore, there are no edges in
G
connecting vertices between
G_{\text{train}}
and
G_{\text{holdout}}
(i.e., all edges in
G
have both endpoints in either
G_{\text{train}}
or in
G_{\text{holdout}}
).
Let
h^{*}\subseteq G
be a connected subgraph, and let
g\subseteq h^{*}
.

Then, if
g\subseteq G_{\text{train}}
, it follows that
h^{*}\subseteq G_{\text{train}}
. Likewise, if
g\subseteq G_{\text{holdout}}
, then
h^{*}\subseteq G_{\text{holdout}}
.

A proof is included in Appendix 1. As a sketch, by construction

cannot have nodes nor edges that connect to nodes in both

and

. This exclusivity establishes that the membership of all nodes in
g
and

cannot differ.

Consequence of Multi-Table Synthetic Data Release.
While the assumption of this theorem is not necessarily needed for relational data MIAs, it emphasizes the privacy risk of relational synthetic data release: if privacy leakage occurs over any component of a connected subgraph, it implies that the entire subgraph (and all included nodes) must be a member of the same source graph. In other words, it is not enough to protect the privacy of the observations of one individual table as
all connected information can risk membership inference
.

3.4
Threat Model

In this work, we explore membership inference attacks under a No-Box threat model. In the No-Box setting, the attacker has access only to a single synthetic dataset
{G}_{synth}
as well as a database schema and must reason about membership without any knowledge of the generator architecture, training procedure, or internal parameters. This threat model reflects scenarios where a party has published a synthetic dataset in isolation with no additional model implementation details, and an adversary must assess privacy risks based solely on patterns and statistical properties present in
{G}_{synth}
.

While a variety of other threat models have been studied for synthetic tabular data including Calibrated No-Box where the adversary has an additional reference dataset
[
35
,
39
]
and Shadow-Box
[
33
,
15
,
23
]
where an adversary additionally has implementation knowledge of the generator in which to generate shadow models, we employ a No-Box threat model in the multi-table setting for two reasons. First, it is the most realistic setting: while reference data access may be plausible in the single-table case, it is unlikely that an adversary would have access to a reference database from the same population distribution as the training dataset that well models all node attributes and edge relationships. Furthermore, shadow attacks can be easily defeated by simply not publicly releasing model implementation details.

On the other hand, a No-Box threat model allows MT-MIA to be both model-agnostic and dataset-agnostic, enabling straightforward auditing of newly proposed relational data generators as they are developed. Because the attack operates solely on the released synthetic data and schema, it does not require adaptation to generator-specific interfaces or assumptions, making it applicable in post-hoc privacy evaluations where only the synthetic dataset is available.

3.5
Motivating Example: The Need for Multi-Table Attacks

To illustrate how inter-table dependencies can leak privacy, we construct a toy example (full experimental details are included in the Appendix). Consider a database with two tables:
Customers
and
Transactions
, connected by a one-to-many relationship where multiple transactions belong to a single customer. Both tables have identical feature distributions following
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.

In our constructed scenario, non-member samples contain exactly one transaction per customer, while member samples (training data) contain 100 transactions per customer—a pattern that might arise from data drift or sampling bias. A synthetic data generator produces
G_{\text{synth}}
with the same distributional properties as the training set. Our goal is to infer membership of customer entities based solely on the release of
G_{\text{synth}}
under a No-box threat model.

From an adversarial perspective, this leakage is trivial to exploit: a decision rule that predicts membership based on whether a customer has 100 transactions achieves perfect accuracy. However, existing single-table MIAs applied to this scenario such as Distance to Closest Record
[
6
]
and MC
[
14
]
fail to detect this leakage. As shown in Figure
1
, single-table approaches must make arbitrary choices about how to incorporate multi-table information. A practitioner using existing single-table MIAs would typically either (a) ignore transaction data entirely (Figure
1(a)
), (b) join each customer to a single arbitrarily-chosen transaction (Figure
1(b)
), or (c) aggregate transaction features (e.g., computing means or sums) (Figure
1(c)
). While aggregation approaches
could
capture this signal (such as counting the transactions), this requires apriori adversarial knowledge of the leakage as to which relational aspects matter—an assumption that does not scale to complex schemas or subtle leakage patterns.

In contrast (See Figure
2
), MT-MIA automatically learns from the full relational structure (Figure
1(d)
) and achieves an AUC of 0.999 without manual feature engineering. While this extreme example is unlikely in practice, it exemplifies a fundamental principle:
inter-table relationships can leak membership signal
, and user-level adversarial auditing must account for the full multi-table structure.

4
Methodology: MT-MIA

The motivating example in Section
3.5
demonstrates a fundamental vulnerability in tabulary synthetic data auditing: single-table MIAs are topologically limited. Even when a generator overfits to or memorizes inter-table correlations or cardinality, single-table attacks fail to capture this signal because they lack a mechanism to process non-Euclidean relational dependencies.

To address this, we propose Multi-Table Membership Inference Attack (MT-MIA). MT-MIA is designed to be schema-agnostic, utilizing a heterogeneous graph encoder to map complex relational structures into a low-dimensional embedding space. Instead of relying on manual feature engineering or arbitrary aggregation, MT-MIA instead leverages graph representation learning to identify discriminative structural patterns directly from
G_{\text{synth}}
.

The intuition for MT-MIA is that by incorporating all of a user graph’s information into learning an embedding space, these resulting embeddings will be more discriminative than any one item-table on its own. We first describe the HGNN backbone to map relational structures into a latent space before explaining how we train the model and score the membership of target subgraphs.

4.1
Graph Encoder Architecture

The core of MT-MIA is a heterogeneous graph encoder
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
, parameterized by
\theta
(see Figure
3
). Our choice of an HGNN-based architecture provides the topological inductive bias required to model relational dependencies. Unlike traditional autoencoders,
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
is invariant to specific join relationships as well as table and row cardinality, enabling a unified attack interface across any heterogeneous relational schema.

Heterogeneous Message Passing

To capture the semantics of the relational schema, we utilize a stack of
L
heterogeneous message passing layers. For a node type
t\in\mathcal{T}_{V}
at layer
l
, the node feature update is defined as:

H^{(t)}_{l+1}=\Phi_{l}^{(t)}\left(H^{(t)}_{l},\{A^{(r)}\}_{r\in\mathcal{T}_{E}}\right)

(2)

where
A^{(r)}
represents the adjacency matrix for relation type
r
. Throughout the paper, we use GATv2
[
3
]
as the default instantiation of
\Phi_{l}^{(t)}
due to its expressive attention mechanism. This mechanism enables information propagation both within and across node types by leveraging the typed edges in the heterogeneous subgraph.

Signal Bifurcation and Pooling

Multi-table synthetic data generators typically adhere to the functional dependencies of the database schema, synthesizing records through a
conditional generative hierarchy
. To mirror this inductive bias,
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
bifurcates the resulting node embeddings into two distinct semantic vectors:

•

Parent Signal
(
z_{parent}
): Represents the primary entity or "root" record. In a generative context, this corresponds to the table that is synthesized unconditionally or serves as the parent in a one-to-many relationship.

•

Relational Context
(
z_{context}
): Represents the "neighborhood" of child records. These nodes are synthesized conditionally based on the existence of the parent.

To resolve the variable cardinality of child records across different tables, we apply
type-specific global attention pooling

[
20
]
. This mechanism learns a weighted importance for each node, capturing the
relational footprint
left by the generator’s conditional sampling process. These weighted representations are then aggregated into their respective parent and context vectors, ensuring a fixed-dimensional input for the downstream attack heads regardless of the underlying subgraph topology.

Dynamic Gated Fusion

While the bifurcated signals provide a comprehensive view of the entity, the discriminative properties of these embeddings are unknown to the adversary. Specifically, privacy leakage may manifest in the unconditionally generated attributes (
z_{parent}
), the conditionally generated relational dependencies (
z_{context}
), or a latent intersection of both. To address this uncertainty, we employ a Dynamic Gating Unit that adaptively modulates the integration of these signals.

We compute a learned gating coefficient
g\in[0,1]^{d}
, which serves as an entry-wise modulator for relational influence. Given the parent and context embeddings, the gate is formulated as:

g=\sigma(\text{MLP}_{\text{gate}}([z_{\text{parent}}\parallel z_{\text{context}}]))

(3)

The final composite representation
z_{\text{final}}
is then constructed via a gated residual connection:

z_{\text{final}}=z_{\text{parent}}+(g\odot\phi(z_{\text{context}}))

(4)

where
\phi(\cdot)
is a non-linear transformation and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. This mechanism allows
M_{\theta}

4.2
Training in the No-Box Setting

In the No-Box setting, the adversary has access only to the synthetic output
G_{\text{synth}}
without any knowledge of the generator’s architecture, parameters, or training data
G_{\text{train}}
. Following the assumptions of prior work
[
6
,
14
]
, we hypothesize that the synthetic data contains memorized patterns from
G_{\text{train}}
. The key insight of MT-MIA-NB is that these memorized motifs will manifest as proximity in the learned embedding space: records from
G_{\text{train}}
will have embeddings closer to those of
G_{\text{synth}}
than non-member records.

Multi-Anchor Reconstruction Objective

Without access to ground-truth membership labels, we train
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
using a self-supervised reconstruction objective. The intuition is to force the encoder to learn a compressed representation that preserves both the primary entity’s attributes and its relational neighborhood, the components that generators may inadvertently memorize during training.

We introduce two complementary reconstruction heads: a
parent decoder

\mathcal{D}_{\phi}
that reconstructs the parent node’s features, and a
context decoder

\mathcal{D}_{\psi}
that reconstructs the aggregate neighborhood. For a target node
i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
with neighbor set
\mathcal{N}(i)
spanning all adjacent node types, the composite reconstruction loss is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(\theta,\phi,\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathcal{V}_{t}}\bigg[

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}\underbrace{\|\mathcal{D}_{\phi}(\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(x_{i}))-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{2}}_{\text{Parent Recon.}}

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}\underbrace{\|\mathcal{D}_{\psi}(\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(x_{i}))-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}}_{\text{Context Recon.}}\bigg]

where
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(x_{i})
produces the fused embedding from Equation
4
and
\lambda_{p},\lambda_{c}
are hyperparametesr that balances the reconstruction of parent features versus relational context. By learning to reconstruct both signals from a single bottleneck embedding, the encoder is forced to capture the specific relational motifs and conditional dependencies favored by the generator. Records exhibiting similar motifs will produce embeddings that cluster near
G_{\text{synth}}
in this learned space.

Membership Scoring

While the learned embedding space can support various no-box attacks, we derive our membership scoring from the Distance to Closest Record attack (DCR)
[
6
]
. For a target entity subgraph
h^{*}
, we define
f(h^{*})
from Equation
1
as :

f(h^{*})=-\min_{h\subseteq G_{\text{synth}}}\|\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(h^{*})-\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(h)\|_{2}

(6)

Higher scores indicate that the structural motifs of
h^{*}
were likely memorized and reproduced in
G_{\text{synth}}

5
Experiments

To evaluate the effectiveness of MT-MIA, we conduct a series of experiments on three benchmark multi-table datasets: California Census
[
5
]
, Airline Customers
[
25
]
, and Airbnb
[
18
]
. For each dataset, we begin by constructing training and holdout sets through the sampling of disjoint subgraphs, ensuring no overlap in entities or relationships. The synthetic data generator is then trained with the training subgraphs, after which we sample an equal number of synthetic subgraphs to match the original training size. All numeric features are scaled and categorical features are ordinally encoded in relation to the synthetic data, which are then applied consistently to both real and synthetic samples to prevent data leakage.

We experiment with a training size of 1000 user subgraphs. These subgraphs correspond to thousands of items across all datasets’ tables. To account for randomness in model training and sampling, each experimental configuration is repeated across three independent runs. Following the recommendations of prior work
[
11
]
, we fix the data split (training vs. holdout) across all runs and vary only the generative model initialization seeds. This design helps isolate the variability due to model behavior from that due to evaluation set construction, which is especially important in privacy attack scenarios.

Following
[
35
]
,
[
37
]
and
[
38
]
, all training data are included as the positive membership class with an equal sized holdout dataset as a negative class. All MIAs are then evaluated with the corresponding synthetic data on this evaluation set to then calculate the success of the attack.

We run all experiments on a High Performance Computing Cluster using a Nvidia G200 GPU with a 32 core CPU. The full synthetic data generation procedure was approximately 10 hours of compute on this system while the MT-MIA training and inference procedure was approximately 1 hour of compute time over all runs. Additional compute was used for preliminary experiments.

We evaluate our proposed approach against two representative multi-relational generative models that span autoregressive and diffusion-based architectures.

It utilizes a GPT-based architecture where parent records are encoded to form a context for a sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) model. This approach models conditional distributions without requiring manual schema flattening.

•

This structure captures dependencies across the database schema without the computational overhead of autoregressive or graph-based methods.

5.1.2
Attacks

Since prior work has not investigated MIAs in the context of synthetic database release, we adapt existing single-table attack methods that align with our threat model for benchmarking purposes. In our setting, membership inference on a user subgraph
h^{*}
is equivalent to determining whether all nodes
v\in h^{*}
were present in the training data. Per Theorem
1
, this can be reframed as selecting 1 node for each subgraph in which to use a single-table attack on. We therefore select a parent node in each
h^{*}
(the strategy of Figure
1(a)
) and use its corresponding score as the attack score for
h^{*}
.

We use three common No-box attacks for tabular data as these scoring functions: Distance to Closest Record (DCR)
[
6
]
, a Monte Carlo Density Estimator (MC) proposed by
[
14
]
, and a Kernel Density Estimator (KDE) approach used in
[
15
]
.

5.2
Metrics

5.2.1
Attack Success

To evaluate the effectiveness of MT-MIA, following
[
4
]
, we report the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and the True Positive Rate (TPR) at low False Positive Rates (FPR), such as
0,10^{-3}
and
10^{-2}
. These metrics are preferred over fixed-threshold measures (e.g., Accuracy), which can mischaracterize membership inference attacks by ignoring the full distribution of attack scores.

While the AUC provides a threshold-independent measure of overall discriminative performance, we additionally report the TPR at low FPR to capture privacy-relevant operating regimes. A high TPR at a low FPR is particularly concerning from a privacy perspective, as it indicates that an attacker can identify training records with high confidence while incurring few false alarms. This makes TPR at low FPR more informative for assessing practical privacy risk than global classification metrics. Moreover, fixing the FPR enables meaningful comparisons across attacks that may exhibit similar global classification performance but differ substantially in their ROC curves. We report results at FPR levels of
0
,
10^{-3}
, and
10^{-2}
.

5.2.2
Synthetic Data Fidelity

We evaluate the quality of the multi-table synthetic data using four primary metrics: (1)
cardinality
to measure the foreign key group size distribution for intra-group correlations; (2)
column-wise density estimation (1-way)
to estimate the marginal density of every column across all tables; (3)
pair-wise column correlation (
k
-hop)
to assess dependencies between columns at distance
k
(e.g., 0-hop for intra-table and 1-hop for parent-child relations); and (4)
average k-hop
, which computes the mean of all
k
-hop correlations to capture both short- and long-range dependencies. For each measure, we report the complement of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic and Total Variation (TV) distance, normalized to
[0,1]
where 1 indicates perfect fidelity.

6
Discussion

6.1
Privacy Auditing Multi-Table Synthetic Data

6.1.1
Performance of MIAs

The advantage of MT-MIA is that it incorporates all related data to the user in the attack whereas the baselines can only incorporate an individual row. MT-MIA demonstrates a particular efficacy in the high-precision regime (see Table
1
), consistently achieving the highest True Positive Rates (TPR) at low False Positive Rate (FPR) thresholds. This is most pronounced on the Airlines dataset for ClavaDDPM, where MT-MIA exhibits a mean 10 percentage point (pp) increase in TPR at FPR=0 compared to the next best performing attack. This result is especially notable considering the threat model: while baselines like DCR-Diff and DOMIAS rely on external reference datasets for calibration, MT-MIA uncovers novel privacy signals without such advantage.

The practical severity of this leakage is stark. A TPR@FPR=0 of 0.17 translates to the unambiguous identification of 170 training examples with zero False Positives in our experimental setting. In a production environment, this represents an egregious leak, as an adversary is able to confirm these individual’s sensitive record with absolute certainty. We observe this trend consistently across the California and Airbnb datasets for both ClavaDDPM and RealTabFormer; in instances where conventional attacks fail to detect any signal, MT-MIA reveals that meaningful privacy vulnerabilities do indeed exist in this regime.

6.1.2
Privacy versus Fidelity Tradeoff

We evaluate the privacy-fidelity tradeoff in Table
2
. Across all datasets, ClavaDDPM demonstrates significantly higher fidelity in both parent and child tables compared to RealTabFormer. Specifically, ClavaDDPM achieves superior marginal distribution fidelity, as evidenced by the 1-Way metric. It also better preserves complex intra-tabular relationships; the average pairwise correlations in the k-hop neighborhoods are up to 20 percentage points (pp) higher than those of RealTabFormer. However, this gain in utility comes at a privacy cost. MT-MIA detects greater privacy leakage in ClavaDDPM’s synthetic output across both AUC and TPR at Low FPR metrics.

These results align with an emerging consensus in the single-table synthetic data literature: high data utility is often strongly correlated with increased privacy vulnerability. Recent studies, such as
[
1
]
and
[
37
]
, suggest that diffusion models like ClavaDDPM may be more susceptible to privacy leakage than alternative architectures. Additionally, the lower intra-tabular fidelity of RealTabFormer may explain why single-table attacks sometimes perform as well as or better than MT-MIA. When there is little signal in the conditional relationships of
G_{synth}
, MT-MIA is exposed to essentially more noise and spurious correlations than single-table strategies.

6.2
Sources of Leakage: Node Versus Neighborhood

MT-MIA’s performance improvements stem from its ability to expose and isolate distinct sources of privacy leakage that are inaccessible to single-table attacks. Unlike prior approaches that operate on a single table’s feature space, MT-MIA leverages an HGNN-based architecture to construct multiple intermediate embedding spaces: one capturing information intrinsic to the parent record (
z_{\text{parent}}
), one encoding the relational neighborhood surrounding that record (
z_{\text{context}}
), and a final embedding that aggregates both signals (
z_{\text{final}}
). This architectural decomposition allows us to directly probe which components of the relational structure contribute to membership distinguishability, and to attribute MT-MIA’s gains to specific leakage pathways rather than to increased model capacity alone.

California.

For the California dataset, attacking the parent embedding
z_{\text{parent}}
yields AUC and TPR values that are nearly identical to the Vanilla attack, with relatively low TPR@FPR values across operating points. This indicates that parent-level attributes alone do not provide a substantially stronger membership signal once embedded. In contrast, the attack on the neighborhood embedding
z_{\text{context}}
reveals a markedly stronger and better-calibrated signal, with improvements of 18 percentage points in TPR@FPR=0 and 28 percentage points in TPR@FPR=
10^{-3}
. This behavior suggests that ClavaDDPM memorizes recurring relational motifs in the training data, such as characteristic configurations of child records, which remain invisible to single-table attacks but become exploitable once relational neighborhoods are explicitly encoded. The attack on the combined embedding
z_{\text{final}}
aggregates the complementary signals from
z_{\text{parent}}
and
z_{\text{context}}
, leading to a substantial improvement over the Vanilla baseline.

Airlines.

In contrast, for the Airlines dataset, MT-MIA uncovers little to no membership signal in the neighborhood embedding
z_{\text{context}}
, indicating that relational context contributes minimally to distinguishability in this setting. Instead, the primary gains arise from the parent embedding
z_{\text{parent}}
, which achieves a 30 percentage point increase in TPR@FPR=0 compared to the Vanilla attack. We attribute this improvement to the message-passing mechanism of the HGNN, which repeatedly aggregates information across relational edges during embedding construction. Even when neighborhood nodes do not themselves encode a strong membership signal, message passing can amplify subtle parent-level differences and reshape the geometry of the representation space, yielding embeddings in which members and non-members are more easily separable.

In both datasets, we observe that attacking either the parent embedding
z_{\text{parent}}
or the neighborhood embedding
z_{\text{context}}
in isolation can yield stronger attack performance than attacking the combined embedding
z_{\text{final}}
. However, under our threat model, an adversary does not have a priori knowledge of which component contains the dominant membership signal for a given dataset or generative model. As a result, MT-MIA relies on the combined embedding
z_{\text{final}}
as a robust, model-agnostic attack strategy that does not require such prior assumptions.

At the same time, the availability of separate embedding channels provides valuable diagnostic signal for internal auditing and model development. By independently probing
z_{\text{parent}}
and
z_{\text{context}}
, practitioners can identify whether privacy leakage primarily arises from memorization of parent attributes or from recurring relational motifs in child tables. This decomposition enables targeted mitigation strategies—for example, regularizing parent representations when leakage concentrates in
z_{\text{parent}}
, or modifying relational modeling or sampling procedures when leakage is driven by neighborhood structure—without requiring changes to the external attack interface.

6.3
Implied Privacy of User Items

A core consequence of Theorem
1
is that an item’s privacy is bounded by its least private representation across all connected tables in the relational schema. To empirically validate this "weakest link" idea, we compare MT-MIA against conventional single-table Distance to Closest Record (DCR) attacks applied in isolation to the child tables of each dataset for ClavaDDPM. The results, summarized in Table
4
, highlight a significant gap between localized and relational privacy auditing.

With the exception of the California dataset, conventional DCR attacks detect little privacy leakage when applied to child tables. This failure stems from the underlying i.i.d. assumption of single-table attacks, which treat each child observation as an independent sample and ignore the structural constraints imposed by the parent-child join relationships. In contrast, MT-MIA leverages the HGNN backbone to integrate the entire subgraph, allowing it to treat membership inference on these items as inference on the overall entity. As demonstrated in the Airlines and California benchmarks, MT-MIA substantially outperforms single-table baselines, particularly in the high-confidence regime (TPR at low FPR).

6.4
Limitations

Despite the efficacy of MT-MIA, several limitations warrant further discussion.

Threat Model Constraints

MT-MIA assumes a conservative No-Box threat model where the adversary possesses only the database schema and the synthetic output
G_{synth}
. While the single-table auditing literature often demonstrates that less conservative models, such as those utilizing shadow models
[
33
,
15
,
23
,
1
]
, can achieve superior attack performance, these methods face significant computational bottlenecks in multi-table contexts. Modern multi-table generators like ClavaDDPM or RealTabFormer can require upwards of an hour for a single training run even on high-performance GPUs, making the training of thousands of shadow models computationally prohibitive. In contrast, MT-MIA is model-agnostic and trains in minutes, serving as an efficient tool for establishing a "leakage floor."

Sensitivity to Embedding Quality

As a representation-learning-based attack, the success of MT-MIA is intrinsically tied to the discriminative power of the latent space learned by the HGNN. Graph Neural Networks are known to be sensitive to structural noise and hyperparameter configurations (e.g., learning rate, message-passing depth, and pooling strategies)
[
10
,
8
,
43
]
. In cases where the synthetic generator introduces significant topological distortion or where the schema is extremely sparse, the HGNN may fail to capture the subtle relational motifs required for membership inference. Despite this sensitivity, our experiments demonstrate that MT-MIA remains robust across diverse schemas, consistently outperforming baseline attacks that rely on simpler heuristic-based features.

Signal Integration and Gated Fusion

A final limitation lies in the integration of the bifurcated signals. While the Dynamic Gating Unit adaptively weights
z_{parent}
and
z_{context}
, it does not always yield a composite embedding that is more discriminative than the individual signals themselves. In some experimental settings, we observe that the raw parent or context vectors independently achieve higher AUC than the fused
z_{final}
. However, we emphasize that even in its current form, MT-MIA provides significant auditing utility, as these intermediate embeddings offer granular insights into which specific relational tables are most susceptible to leakage.

7
Conclusion

We present the first systematic study of user-level privacy auditing for synthetic relational data generation, demonstrating both theoretically and empirically that multi-table settings introduce privacy leakage at a user-level. Our proposed Multi-Table Membership Inference Attack (MT-MIA) leverages heterogeneous graph neural networks in a self-supervised manner to detect membership information leakage across connected entities without requiring generator access. Evaluation across multiple real-world datasets shows MT-MIA consistently outperforms existing single-table approaches, particularly in the critical low false-positive regime, revealing that even state-of-the-art relational generators with privacy-preserving mechanisms leak membership information under conservative threat models.

There are many directions for future work in this area. Efforts could focus on refining HGNN architectures to improve the fidelity of learned subgraph representations, which would likely enhance attack performance. Investigating attacks under less conservative threat models would also be valuable, particularly if specific architectures become popular for relational data generation. Additionally, developing user-level differentially private relational data generators would likely be valuable in protecting user privacy. Lastly, this work opens up additional lines of inquiry in extending other privacy auditing paradigms such as link prediction and attribute inference to the relational data setting.
