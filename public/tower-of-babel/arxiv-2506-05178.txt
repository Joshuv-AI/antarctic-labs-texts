Associative Memory and Generative Diffusion in the Zero-noise Limit
Joshua Hess jmh4003@med.cornell.edu, Quaid Morris morrisq@mskcc.org

Abstract
Connections between generative diffusion and continuous-state associative memory models are studied. Morse-Smale dynamical systems are emphasized as universal approximators of gradient-based associative memory models and diffusion models as white-noise perturbed systems thereof. Universal properties of associative memory that follow from this description are described and used to characterize a generic transition from generation to memory as noise levels diminish. Structural stability inherited by Morse-Smale flows is shown to imply a notion of stability for diffusions at vanishing noise levels. Applied to one- and two-parameter families of gradients, this indicates stability at all but isolated points of associative memory learning landscapes and the learning and generation landscapes of diffusion models with gradient drift in the zero-noise limit, at which small sets of generic bifurcations characterize qualitative transitions between stable systems. Examples illustrating the characterization of these landscapes by sequences of these bifurcations are given, along with structural stability criterion for classic and modern Hopfield networks (equivalently, the attention mechanism).

Introduction

Associative memory refers to the ability to recall stored information based on partial input (
Dayan and Abbott 2005
). These models, originally inspired by neuroscience and Hebbian learning theory, were developed in a binary state setting with relatively limited memory capacity (
Hopfield 1982
), but have since become integral components of modern deep learning systems (
Krotov 2023
). For example, the Hopfield model’s memory capacity was increased in
Krotov and Hopfield 2016
and
Demircigil et al. 2017
, and an extension was subsequently developed that is actually equivalent to the attention mechanism of the transformer, an architecture behind
state-of-the-art
natural language processing models (
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Ramsauer et al. 2020
; see
section

5.4
). These results were also studied in the context of the entire transformer block in
Hoover et al. 2023a
. Conversely, diffusion models generate data from a learned probability distribution, but their history dates back to the Boltzmann machine (
Ackley et al. 1985
), a probabilistic version of the Hopfield model, and have become
best-in-class
for tasks such as image generation (
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Ramesh et al. 2022
;
Cao et al. 2024
). Similarities between the random and deterministic models have long been known, and interest in these connections has resurfaced in the context of modern models (e.g.,
Hoover et al. 2023b
).

Spurious memories arise in associative memory models as unintended fixed points. Their behavior is well studied using probabilistic approaches (e.g.,
Gayrard 2025
), and they have also recently been observed in diffusion models (
Pham et al. 2025
). Continuous-state associative memory and diffusion models define deterministic and random dynamical systems, where high-gain activation functions in the former act similarly to a low-noise limit in the latter. It is known that metastable states can emerge and disappear at varying noise magnitudes in random systems, and relationships between the deterministic and random models are similarly well studied at low noise levels, owing largely to large-deviation estimates from the classic Freidlin-Wentsell (FW) theory of random dynamical systems (
Ventsel and Freidlin 1970
), among others (e.g.,
Bovier et al. 2001
;
Bovier et al. 2004
).

These probabilistic approaches are rigorous; however, most computations are local, in the neighborhood of a fixed point, or consider transitions between pairs of them. Consequently, global descriptions of the robustness, stability, learning dynamics (e.g., memory formation), and universal properties of the orbit structure of these models are less developed. These core aspects of machine learning theory are historically related to the notion of
genericity
. A
generic
or
universal property
is one that holds for ”almost all” systems of interest – here, one that holds on an open and dense subset of a suitable function space (see
section

2.2
). A property holding on a dense subset reflects its approximation power
1
1

1

See, e.g., classic work in
Cybenko 1989
, Theorem 1 for a proof of universal approximation using density explicitly; see also
Funahashi 1989
, Theorems 1 and 2 for proofs implicitly using density.
; a property holding on an open subset reflects its robustness and stability (see
section

1.3
).

Search for generic properties also progressed both geometric and probabilistic approaches to dynamical systems. On the geometric side, determining whether certain global notions of stability are generic stimulated work, largely driven by Smale’s school, which laid the basic theory of the subject. Major results pertain to hyperbolic (e.g., Axiom A) and
structurally stable
systems (
Smale 1967
). A special case of these systems, called
Morse-Smale
, are closely related to the topology of manifolds (
Smale 1960
), making the generic properties of these models well-suited for geometric and topological arguments. A probabilistic approach to studying a system’s asymptotic behavior, largely rooted in ergodic theory, developed in parallel to counterexamples showing that these systems are not generic (e.g.,
Newhouse 1970
; see also
Palis 2000
;
Palis 2005
for further context).

Here, these topics are used together with the FW theory to describe the generic properties of these models and a generation-to-memory transition at diminishing noise levels from a global viewpoint. In particular, Morse-Smale gradient systems (
Smale 1960
;
section

2.1
) are emphasized as a generic class of associative memory models and diffusions as their white-noise perturbed counterparts. Zero-noise-limiting descriptions of stationary measures and trajectories of diffusion models are connected to those of associative memory models within this class. Generic properties of associative memory that follow from the Morse-Smale assumptions are used with other results from Morse theory to describe the stability and robustness of stationary measures and trajectories of diffusions in the zero-noise limit. The Morse-Smale conditions are equivalent to the existence of a neighborhood of a gradient field in which all nearby models are topologically equivalent to it (structural stability). These isomorphisms are determined by homeomorphisms mapping the trajectories of two systems to each other, and particular focus is given to zero-noise-limiting behavior under these maps.

Breakdowns of the Morse-Smale conditions for gradient systems are bifurcations that change their topological or qualitative type. The learning and generation processes of these models appear as one- and two-parameter families of gradients, which have well-studied sets of generic bifurcations that we use to globally describe memory formation and the learning and generation dynamics of diffusion models. Examples are given, including Hopfield networks, energy-based models, and denoising diffusion models. Requirements to satisfy the Morse-Smale assumptions are obtained for Hopfield-type networks.

This work provides a step towards understanding the global critical point structure and stability of continuous-state associative memory models using the bifurcation theory of gradients and the geometric theory of dynamical systems. In turn, a generic characterization of the topological properties of associative memory and diffusion-based generative models at vanishing noise levels is obtained, along with a characterization of their dynamics as they learn to store and generate data. The primary results of this paper are summarized in
section

1.3
.

1.1
Dynamical systems and associative memory

Associative memories are often modeled as attractors of a dynamical system with basins of attraction containing points that evolve asymptotically to them. Memory recall consists of evolving the network to these fixed points given initial conditions. Therefore, small perturbations within a neighborhood of a memory decay over time, making these models capable of pattern completion and error correction.

Widely known models define gradient dynamical systems on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or compact subsets thereof, including all models of Hopfield type. More generally, let
M
be a manifold and
\phi^{X}_{t}:\mathbb{R}\times M\rightarrow M
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms generated by a vector field

(
section

2.1
). In the continuous state formulation, memories are
asymptotically stable
points

of

such that for all

as

Hopfield 1982
;
Hopfield 1984
.

In general, if

is a smooth function (
r\geq 2
) and

is given a Riemannian metric
g=\sum g_{ij}\,dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j}
, the derivative

defines a linear function on the tangent space

is an isolated minima of

is asymptotically stable. Consequently, gradient systems are natural models of associative memory, and memory storage consists of finding a ”good” function

whose critical elements coincide with the set of memories to store.

1.2
Generative diffusion models

Broadly, generative models aim to learn a probability distribution

1.3
Overview of main results

Exact samples can be drawn from a stationary distribution of a diffusion model by solving a corresponding equation like (
1.2.5
) as

. Likewise, associative memory evolves network states to fixed points in this limit. Like in the stochastic case, this asymptotic behavior encodes information about invariant probability measures. Moreover, when

\pm\infty

Trajectories and stationary measures.

Given an initial condition

Stability and universal properties.

The elements of a generic set of so-called
Morse-Smale
gradient systems have a finite number of critical points and satisfy two conditions describing their behavior at critical points (the Morse condition) and off critical points (the Smale condition). That these systems are generic implies that they can universally approximate any gradient–based associative memory model (

The Morse condition implies that the critical points of these systems only display three possible types of behavior, each characterized by the

2.1
Stable associative memory

Let
M
be a smooth
n

1.

The flow

2.

5.

Memories are singular points.

Compactness (Condition 1).

The time integration of a differential equation on a compact manifold produces a one-parameter set of diffeomorphisms

(

Since

is

Non-wandering sets (Condition 2).

2.3.1
Asymptotic stability

In

x\in U
for
U
a neighborhood of a stored memory
p
, the network evolves to restore
p
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Given a Morse function
V:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a nondegenerate critical point
p\in M
, the
Morse lemma
implies that, at a non-degenerate critical point
p\in M
, there is a coordinate chart
(U,h)
, called a
Morse chart
, with
U\ni p
and

, so that

(V\,\circ h^{-1})(x_{1},...,x_{n})=V(p)-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}...-x_{\lambda}^{2}+x_{\lambda+1}^{2}+...+x_{n}^{2}

for

and

is the index of

at

(see, e.g.,

, Lemma 2.2).
In these coordinates, the gradient of

has a unique zero at

. Consequently, the critical points of Morse functions are isolated, and the attractors of these gradient systems are asymptotically stable.

2.3.2
Structure of invariant manifolds

The stable and unstable manifolds of gradients of Morse functions have two key properties that are used to describe generic zero-noise limits in
section

3
. First, they provide a finite decomposition of a compact manifold into their disjoint union. In addition, they have simple characterizations as embedded discs that vary continuously in a sense made precise below.

Decomposition.

Let
X=-\nabla_{g}V
be a gradient field of a Morse function
V:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Since
M
is compact and the critical elements of
V
are isolated, there are finitely many of them:
\text{Crit}(V)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}
for
n>0
finite. Moreover,
V(\phi^{X}_{t}(x))
is strictly decreasing off of critical points, and by compactness, it is bounded below. Therefore, the
\omega-
limit set of each
x\in M

\omega(x)=\{y\in M\,|\,\phi_{t}^{X}(x)\rightarrow y\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty\}
, is a single critical point. Consequently, every point
x\in M
lies on the stable manifold of a unique critical point.

The union of the stable manifolds covers
M
, and by uniqueness, any two stable manifolds are disjoint:
W^{s}(\beta_{i})\cap W^{s}(\beta_{j})=\emptyset
for distinct
\beta_{i},\beta_{j}\in\text{Crit}(V)

M
decomposes into a disjoint union
M=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}W^{s}(\beta_{i})
(
Figure 2
). The analogous result holds for unstable manifolds by considering asymptotic orbits as
t\rightarrow-\infty

Stable Manifold Theorem.

By the Hartman-Grobman theorem, if
p\in M
is a hyperbolic fixed point of a vector field
X

is locally (topologically) equivalent to its linearization

(see

defines a hyperbolic linear vector field

where the stable directions have eigenvalues with negative real part and the unstable directions have eigenvalues with positive real part (
Palis and De Melo 2012

Let

and denote the splitting of the tangent space at

under

by
T_{p}M=E^{s}\oplus E^{u}

q\in E^{s}

as

and for

\max{\{||L|_{E^{s}}||,||L^{-1}|_{E^{u}}||\}}<1

and

W_{B_{\delta}}^{u}(p)&=\{x\in B(p,\delta)\,:\,\phi^{X}_{-t}(x)\in B(p,\delta)\textnormal{ for }t\geq 0\}\,,\end{split}

2.3.3
Structural and
\Omega-
stability

The introduction of Morse-Smale systems in

was accompanied by a conjecture that its elements were structurally stable. This is proven in the main theorem of

a gradient vector field is structurally stable if and only if it is Morse-Smale
.

From the definition of non-wandering set, a homeomorphism

establishing a topological equivalence between orbits of

restricts to a homeomorphism between non-wandering sets:

equal to the union of the set of critical elements, denoted

2.3.4
Hierarchical organization of associative memory

Finally, associated with a Morse-Smale system is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose vertices are critical elements
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}
connected by a directed edge, denoted
\beta_{i}\succ\beta_{j}
, if there is a trajectory from
\beta_{i}
to
\beta_{j}
(
Figure 2
). Equivalently,
\beta_{i}\succ\beta_{j}
if
W^{u}(\beta_{i})\cap W^{s}(\beta_{j})\neq\emptyset
.

The DAG for a Morse-Smale system defines a partial order on its set of critical elements. It has the following properties (
Meyer 1968
;
Smale 1960
):

•

(No self-connections)
\beta_{i}\nsucc\beta_{i}
;

•

(Transitive edges) if
\beta_{i}\succ\beta_{j}
and
\beta_{j}\succ\beta_{k}
, then
\beta_{i}\succ\beta_{k}
;

•

(Ordered by index) if
\beta_{i}\succ\beta_{j}
, then
\text{dim}(W^{u}(\beta_{i}))\geq\text{dim}(W^{u}(\beta_{j}))

Equality in the relation
\text{dim}(W^{u}(\beta_{i}))\geq\text{dim}(W^{u}(\beta_{j}))
occurs only if
\beta_{j}
is a closed orbit.

These DAGs are topological invariants in the following sense. Denote the partial order of
X\in S(M)
by
P(X)
and define a
diagram isomorphism
as a bijective, order- and index-preserving map
\rho:P(X)\rightarrow P(Y)
. If two Morse-Smale flows are topologically equivalent, then they are diagram isomorphic – their stable and unstable manifolds agree (modulo homeomorphism), and their critical elements are in one-to-one correspondence with the same indices. In addition,
P(X)
is stable up to diagram isomorphism under small
C^{r}
perturbations to
X
(
Palis 1969
).

Physical measures and resonance

Let

Evidently, if

4
Generation, learning, and stable families of gradients

This section analyzes how qualitative changes, or bifurcations, of associative memory models and their small random perturbations capture their learning and generation dynamics. Let

These examples indicate that learning processes of associative memory models are one-parameter families of gradients, and the generation process of diffusions with time-varying drift are one-parameter families. The learning processes of associative memory models and diffusions with time-varying drift are two-parameter families. One arrives at the following question:
Are one- and two-parameter families of gradients stable?

This section describes that stable families are actually abundant – one- and two-parameter families of gradients are generically stable, and their points of instability are generically described by small sets of bifurcations. A statement is made for diffusion models, but first requires a comparison of their trajectories to deterministic ones at vanishing noise levels.

4.1
Large deviations and stochastic flows

It is convenient to view solutions to equations like (
1.2.5

stochastic flows
, denoted by
\Phi^{X,\epsilon}
for an
\epsilon>0

Kunita and Ghosh 1986

(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold and

\Phi^{X,\epsilon}_{s,t}(\cdot,\omega)
is a measurable map from
M
to itself. Consequently,
\Phi^{X,\epsilon}
is a stochastic flow of measurable maps, here, more strictly, of homeomorphisms or diffeomorphisms.

A
path

\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
is a realization of the stochastic flow
\Phi^{X,\epsilon}(x_{0},\omega)
generated by
\mathcal{X}^{\epsilon}
for an initial condition

and

is a small random perturbation implies that the probability of observing a particular path should concentrate as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Original work on this topic by

is formulated through an action principle, where an
action functional

is defined by

J[x]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}||\dot{x}_{t}-b({x}_{t})||^{2}\,dt\,,

where
||\cdot||
is the Riemannian norm, and the bracket notation indicates that
J[x]
is a functional over all of
x_{t}
; further details are available in, e.g.,
Touchette 2009
, Chapter 6.

The large deviation principle states that the probability
P_{\epsilon}[x]
of observing a path
\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
that deviates from an enclosing
\delta
-tube is given by
P_{\epsilon}[x]\asymp e^{-a_{\epsilon}J[x]}
, where the notation
\asymp
indicates that the dominant part of

is decaying exponentially;

determines the rate at which

decays and is such that

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. For any number

P\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|x^{\epsilon}_{t}-x_{t}|<\delta\right)\asymp e^{-a_{\epsilon}J[x]}\,.

Importantly,

has a unique zero when the path is equal to the trajectory solving the deterministic equations, denoted
x^{*}_{t}
for clarity. Since
P_{\epsilon}[x]
is dominated by
e^{-a_{\epsilon}J[x]}
, which decays exponentially as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the path converges in probability to the deterministic one in this limit. That is,
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}P\left(||x^{\epsilon}_{t}-x^{*}_{t}||_{\infty}\geq\delta\right)=0
for any number
\delta>0
in the supremum norm
||\cdot||_{\infty}
on the space of continuous paths

.

This pathwise convergence is preserved by homeomorphisms that conjugate two limiting deterministic flows; see

A
for a proof:

4.2
Stable families and bifurcations

Recall that a

\{X_{\eta_{1},...,\eta_{k}}\}

\eta\in N^{k}

are given the compact-open

topology.

Let
\chi^{g}_{k}(M)
be the set of
k
-parameter families on
M

the canonical projection map. Two
k
-parameter families,
\{X_{\eta}\},\{Y_{\eta}\}\in\chi^{g}_{k}(M)

if there exist homeomorphisms
H:M\times D\rightarrow M\times D
and
\psi:N\rightarrow N
so that
\pi H=\psi\pi
and for each
\eta\in N
,
h_{\eta}
is an equivalence between
\{X_{\eta}\}
and
\{Y_{\psi(\eta)}\}
, where
H(x,\eta)=\left(h_{\eta}(x),\psi(\eta)\right)
.
\{X_{\eta}\}
is called

if there exists a neighborhood
\{X_{\eta}\}\ni\mathcal{U}
in which all elements are equivalent to
\{X_{\eta}\}
. A value
\eta=(d_{1},...,d_{k})
is called a

for a

\{X_{\eta}\}
if

is (structurally) stable. Otherwise, it is called a

, and will be denoted by

.

4.2.1
Comparison to universal unfoldings

It is worth comparing bifurcations of families of gradients to Thom’s work on universal unfoldings. Let
X=-\nabla V
be a a gradient system with a degenerate singularity
p\in M
, where the Hessian determinant
|\,\partial^{2}V/\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\,|
vanishes. Under an arbitrary perturbation
\delta V
, the local behavior (formally, the
germ
) of
V+\delta V
has either infinite or finite topological types. In the finite case, there exists a
k-
parameter
universal unfolding
of the potential,

V=V(p)+u_{1}g_{1}(p)+u_{2}g_{2}(p)+...+u_{k}g_{k}(p)\,,

from which any perturbation can be recovered up to topological equivalence (
Thom 1969
, §1.D). These unfoldings, describing local behavior of parameterized potentials, form the basis of Thom’s approach and underpin catastrophe theory (e.g.,
Zeeman 2006
).

Contrastingly, loss of stability for families of gradients can occur due to local or global criteria. A degenerate singularity indicates a loss of stability of a gradient system, but it is local. Additional global bifurcations can occur, even for one-parameter families. Generically, these are due to non-transverse intersections of certain invariant manifolds, which are discussed in the following sections.

4.2.2
One-parameter families of gradients

Only three bifurcations are needed to build a Morse-Smale system from the trivial one (
Smale 1960
;
Rand et al. 2021
). Generically, only two arise at bifurcation values for one-parameter families of gradients, described by the following theorem: the saddle-node bifurcation, a local bifurcation, and the heteroclinic flip, a global bifurcation.

Theorem 27

(
Palis and Takens 1983
)
.

The following properties are generic, and hold on an open and dense subset, for one-parameter families of gradients:

1.

The set of regular parameter values is open and dense in
\mathbb{R}
;

2.

At a bifurcation value, the gradient
X_{\bar{\eta}}
has either exactly one (nonhyperbolic) saddle-node singularity, while all other singularities are hyperbolic and all stable and unstable manifolds intersect transversally, or it has exactly one orbit along which a stable and an unstable manifold intersect nontransversally, while all singularities are hyperbolic.

A metric rescales and reorients space to align a potential gradient to a flow, but the non-degeneracy of its critical elements is determined by the potential. Transversal intersections and orbits of tangency, on the other hand, are determined by the potential and the metric.

5.1
Assumptions and generic conditions

One assumption and two generic conditions are imposed. The assumption is that flows remain in a closed manifold or globally attracting region diffeomorphic to a closed disc in

The second condition is used to obtain structural stability criteria for classic and modern Hopfield networks via small modifications to their energy functions in Morse charts, making the dynamics

Energy-based models are Boltzmann-Gibbs distributions

A traditional approach to fitting

5.3.2
Boltzmann machines

The Boltzmann machine is an energy-based model. Its energy function is exactly the Hopfield network energy, and the probability of a network being in a configuration is given by,
P(v)={Z}^{-1}\cdot e^{{-{V(v)}/{T}}}
with
Z=\int e^{{-{V(v)}/{T}}}dv
,
where
T>0
is a noise scaling, or ”temperature”, parameter. The classic formulation considers binary states
v_{i}=\{0,1\}
with an update rule that describes the probability of a neuron firing (

). Following
section

5.2
, a continuous-state formulation can be defined using an overdamped Langevin equation:
d{u}^{\epsilon}_{t}=-\nabla V(u^{\epsilon}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{T}dw_{t}\,.

It is known that as
T\rightarrow 0
, the update rule of the Boltzmann machine is equivalent to the update rule of the Hopfield network. When the Hopfield network is structurally stable (see
section

5.3.1
above), the results of this paper apply in the vanishing noise limit.

6
Conclusion and discussion

This paper describes a generic generation-to-memory transition from generative diffusion to associative memory as noise levels diminish. In particular, universal approximation properties, robustness or stability to perturbations of parameters or inputs, and learning dynamics of associative memory are generically described at a global level using the geometric approach to dynamical systems theory. In parallel, these properties are applied to diffusions in the zero-noise limit by leveraging probabilistic approaches to dynamical systems.

Reliable and stable associative memory satisfies the conditions for gradient fields to be Morse-Smale. Conversely, current models can be universally approximated by this class of systems. Memory retrieval is therefore generically downhill, and associated to each ”memory landscape” is an invariant DAG defining connections between rest points (
Figure 2
). The Morse lemma implies that these models generically satisfy the asymptotic stability property for reliable memory storage. In addition, they are structurally stable – their fixed-point and orbit structure is unchanged in a neighborhood of a model in use. This takes the local form of stability global, and it qualitatively describes the robustness of these systems to model parameter perturbations. Pragmatically, Morse-Smale gradient models of associative memory can be written as an inverse Riemannian metric times the gradient of a potential, which broadens their applicability to more geometrically structured domains.

Generative diffusion appears as small random perturbations of associative memory, defined by stochastic differential equations with gradient drift (
Figure 1
). Their invariant measures are Boltzmann-Gibbs distributions that generically correspond to the stable manifolds of the associative memory model defined by the drift field. The Morse condition implies that these invariant measures are stable to stochastic and deterministic perturbations on stable manifolds. The Morse-Smale assumptions enable a global extension of these results; though, the image of a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution under a homeomorphism between topologically equivalent systems need not preserve its absolute continuity.

Memory formation is characterized by breakdowns of the Morse-Smale assumptions. The learning processes of associative memory appear as one-parameter families of gradients that trace an arc in the space of gradient fields, and memory formation is generically described by the sequential occurrence of only two bifurcations (
Figure 3
and
Figure 4
). The learning processes of time-dependent associative memory appear as two-parameter families that are characterized by eleven possible bifurcations (
section

4.2.3
). These results also apply directly to diffusion models using probability flow ODEs for sample generation, viewed through this deterministic lens, while large deviation type results from classic FW theory imply that stochastic flows of generative diffusion converge in probability to the flows of their gradient drift fields at vanishing noise levels. We introduced a definition for stochastic flows in the zero-noise limit analogous to the topological equivalence of flows (
section

4.1
). Data generation for models with time-varying drift can be understood through this construction by the bifurcations for one-parameter families. Their learning dynamics can be viewed through the bifurcations for two-parameter families.

Our results apply to classic models, including the Hopfield network and Boltzmann machine, along with energy-based models and modern diffusion models (
section

5
). In particular, we gave explicit computations that determine when classic and modern Hopfield networks are not structurally stable (
section

5.3
and
section

5.4
). Even some simple scenarios violate the Morse-Smale conditions for these models, and the results of the modern Hopfield network are directly relevant to the attention mechanism. Although associative memory is generically structurally stable, it is not clear if the Morse-Smale constraints are computationally tractable to satisfy, in general. It is also not clear if the connections made between diffusion models and associative memory models can be used to enhance their performance.
