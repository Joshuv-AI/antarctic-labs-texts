Giant counter-rotating oscillations on the attosecond timescale
Jakob Nicolai Bruhnke, Edvin Olofsson, Axel Stenquist, Jan Marcus Dahlström † {}^{\text{\dagger}}

Abstract
We predict an unexplored type of ultrastrong coupling between atoms and intense ultraviolet light that leads to giant population oscillations on the attosecond timescale. These counter-rotating oscillations can be of similar amplitude as the elementary femtosecond Rabi oscillations between the two strongly coupled states. The effect, which is beyond the two-level atom, is non-reciprocal: It only affects the excited state, while the ground state is unaffected. We propose that two-photon Rabi oscillations (1s 2 –1s3d) in helium is suitable for the generation of this type of ultrastrong coupling with realistic pulses. We use a combination of Floquet theory and effective Hamiltonian theory to test our predictions against ab initio simulations.

Theory
—In the following, we provide the outline for the derivation of the CR population dynamics for the two-photon Rabi cycling case. The theoretical modeling of 2-PRO in atoms is enabled through an essential states formalism. The essential states span a sub-Hilbert space
\mathcal{P}
, and they are coupled effectively via the orthogonal Hilbert space of non-essential states, called
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}^{\perp}
. Let us refer to the essential states as
\ket{a,0}
(GS) and
\ket{b,-2}
(ES), where
a
and
b
refer to the ground and excited atomic states, respectively. The second index labels “photon numbers” of the mode. Unlike quantum optics, the absolute photon number is simply an inconsequential energy offset in Floquet theory. Therefore, the photon numbers may take on negative values. Parametrizing the effective coupling of the essential states to and via the
\mathcal{Q}
-space leads to an effective two-level Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
. If the
\mathcal{Q}
-space contains CR states,
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
incorporates CR effects, such as the Bloch-Siegert shift and CR contributions to effective couplings. Since ionization in strong high-frequency fields can not be neglected, the effective Hamiltonian must be non-Hermitian, and photoionization can occur in a range of different non-linear processes
[
29
,
30
,
36
]
. However, the two-level
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
generates only a
cycle-averaged
time-evolution with populations
|a(t)|^{2}
(GS) and
|b(t)|^{2}
(ES). It can thus not be used to model rapid CR oscillations. In Floquet theory, CR oscillations of an atomic state
\ket{b}
stem from interference of the essential uncoupled atom-photon state
\ket{b,-2}\in\mathcal{P}
with Floquet states comprised of the same atomic state, but a different number of photons,
\ket{b,k}\in\mathcal{Q}
, where
k\neq-2
.

Method
—The non-Hermitian effective Hamiltonians employed in this work are obtained through non-perturbative time-independent calculations, enabled through Floquet theory
[
38
]
. We construct a complex-scaled Floquet Hamiltonian
H_{F}^{\theta}
, whose continuous spectrum has been rotated into the complex plane
[
39
,
40
]
. From
H_{F}^{\theta}
, we parametrize the influence of the
\mathcal{Q}
-space states in an all-order approach
[
41
]
. Specifics on effective Hamiltonian theory can be found in the End Matter.
H_{F}^{\theta}
is constructed in the length gauge from complex-scaled energies and transition dipole moments, both obtained using the configuration-interaction singles (CIS) method
[
42
,
43
]
, with exterior complex scaling (ECS)
[
44
]
. To validate the results, we perform
ab initio
simulations using the time-dependent configuration-interaction singles (TDCIS) method in the length gauge
[
45
]
. The simulations are done with a CIS basis that is constructed using a B-spline basis with a complex-absorbing potential (CAP)
[
46
,
47
]
. The use of length gauge is required: As shown by Kobe (1978), the complex amplitudes of the states are probability amplitudes only if the length gauge is employed
[
48
]
. This means that their squares correspond to the probabilities to find electrons in the respective field-free states.

Analytical formulation
—In Floquet theory, the transition amplitude between the atomic states
\ket{a}
and
\ket{b}
from
t=0
,

U_{ba}(t)=\sum_{k}\braket{b,k|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}H_{F}^{\theta}t}|a,0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\omega t},

(1)

is given as the sum over the transition amplitudes between the atom-photon states
\ket{a,0}
and
\ket{b,k}
, determined by
H_{F}^{\theta}
, and weighted with the Fourier components
\exp(\i k \omega t)

[
6
,
38
]
. In our essential-states approach, only one photon block (
k=-2
) is included. Expanding in the dressed state basis of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}\ket{\pm}=\lambda_{\pm}\ket{\pm}
, we obtain

U_{ba}(t)\approx\sum_{j\in\pm}\braket{b,-2|j}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{j}t}\Braket{\tilde{j}|a,0}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\omega t}.

(2)

The transition probability is the optical cycle-averaged ES population,
|U_{ba}(t)|^{2}=|b(t)|^{2}
. Since
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
is non-Hermitian, we require the bi-orthonormal
\bra{\tilde{\pm}}
, which fulfill the left eigenvalue equation for
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
, so that
1=\outerproduct{+}{\tilde+}+\outerproduct{-}{\tilde-}

[
49
]
. For a brief introduction of Floquet theory, its application to the semiclassical two-level system, and expressions for the damped, effective two-level dynamics, we refer to the Supplemental Material
[
50
]
and references
[
51
,
52
]
therein.

According to Eq. (
1
), CR oscillations are induced when several
\ket{b,k}
– in our case
\ket{b,0}

\ket{b,-2}

\ket{b,-4}

H_{F}^{\theta}\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=\lambda_{\pm}\ket{\phi_{\pm}}
. However, diagonalization of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
only yields the eigenstates
\ket{\pm}\in\mathcal{P}
, while
\ket{b,k}\in\mathcal{Q}
,

, thus requiring the knowledge of
\ket{\phi_{\pm}}
. The operator that produces the

-space components of a given
\mathcal{P}
-space state is known as the
reduced wave operator

\chi

[
53
,
54
]
. With
\chi
, we can obtain
\ket{\phi_{\pm}}\in\mathcal{P}\oplus\mathcal{Q}
from
\ket{\pm}\in\mathcal{P}
simply via

\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=(P+\chi)\ket{\pm},

(3)

where
P\coloneqq\outerproduct{a,0}{a,0}+\outerproduct{b,-2}{b,-2}

\chi
naturally appears in our calculation of

Through Eq. (
3
), the transition amplitude in Eq. (
1
) can be expressed in a form that only involves quantities accessible through effective Hamiltonian theory,

U_{ba}(t)=\sum_{k\in\{0,-2,-4\}}\sum_{j\in\pm}\braket{b,k|P+\chi|j}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{j}t}\Braket{\tilde{j}|a,0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\omega t},

(4)

but retains sub-cycle dynamics. Interference between the CR states, and coupling between the GS and the
\mathcal{Q}
-space can be neglected (

) to obtain a compact form for our transition probability:

|U_{ba}(t)|^{2}=|b(t)|^{2}\bigl[1+|\Lambda^{(0)}|^{2}+|\Lambda^{(-4)}|^{2}\\
+2\real(\Lambda)\cos(2\omega t)\bigr].

(5)

Here,
\Lambda^{(k)}\coloneqq\braket{b,k|\chi|b,-2}
, and thus
|\Lambda^{(k)}|^{2}|b(t)|^{2}
represents the optical cycle-average population of the CR state

\Lambda\coloneqq\Lambda^{(0)}+\Lambda^{(-4)}
is the characteristic quantity determining the magnitude of the CR oscillations. The maximum span of the CR oscillations is given by

M_{\max}=4|\real(\Lambda)|\max(|b(t)|^{2}).

(6)

The 1s
2
-1s3d system displays complete non-reciprocality in the CR oscillations: The GS evolves only with the optical cycle-averaged population
|a(t)|^{2}
due to
\ket{a,0}
, whereas the ES is modulated with CR oscillations that are as large as the 2-PRO themselves. The asymmetry stems from the vastly different strengths in which the GS and ES couple to the
\mathcal{Q}
-space, which we exploited in the derivation of Eq. (
5
). This especially applies to the coupling of the ES with the nearby Rydberg states: The 1s3p, 1s4p, 1s5p, 1s4f, and 1s5f states have the largest population in
\mathcal{Q}
. Remarkably, despite violent exchange of photons to the
\mathcal{Q}
-space via
\ket{b,-2}
, the state coherently returns to the initial
\ket{a,0}
in
\mathcal{P}
after the completed Rabi cycle.
The electron remains bound, yet is highly polarized, switching between d-character and p-/f-character on an attosecond time scale, following the instantaneous laser field. Unlike how free electrons wiggle in laser fields, the underlying assumption in the strong-field approximation
[
14
]
, our reported dynamics arise due to simultaneous confinement by the atomic potential and the action of the instantaneous laser field.

Discussion
—Following the several dismissals of CR phenomena in the literature
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, it is imperative to motivate that the proposed giant CR effects can be generated with realistic pulse forms and observed experimentally through possible detection schemes. We find that the breaking of CR reciprocality is robust against changes in the envelope of the field, as shown in Fig.
3
(c) using TDCIS. The simulation was performed with a Gaussian intensity envelope,
I(t)=I_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\ln{2}[2t/\tau]^{2}}
, where
I_{0}=$2\text{\times}{10}^{14}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\cdot}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}$
and
\tau=\mathrm{FWHM}=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}$
at a frequency
\omega=$11.86\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
, corresponding to
\sim$105\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
. These parameters are reasonable for seeded FEL sources, such as the externally seeded FEL at FERMI
[
32
,
55
,
56
]
.

It is also important to understand the generality of the CR effects. Can they be observed in other UV/XUV-dressed systems? In Fig.
3
(d), we compare the maximum span of CR oscillations
M_{\max}
between different experimentally relevant systems. In 1-PRO, 1s
2
-1s2p, the CR oscillations are weaker for a given intensity, so that intensities as large as
9\text{\times}{10}^{14}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\cdot}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
are required to generate giant CR oscillations. Meanwhile, the two-photon transition 1s
2
-1s2s is too damped, making it unsuitable for our purposes.
A possible way to enhance CR oscillations is to target higher Rydberg states that couple to
\mathcal{Q}
-space states with stronger dipole moments. We demonstrate this in panel (d) by including results for the 1s4d state. Generating giant CR oscillations in higher Rydberg states requires less intense, but longer pulses.

Experimental observation
—We propose that the giant CR oscillations can be probed experimentally in two complementary ways: in the frequency or time domain. In order to detect the CR oscillations in the frequency domain, we require an auxiliary probe field that can ionize the atom from its excited state.
The probe frequency should not be a harmonic of the driving resonant dressing field,
\omega_{\mathrm{probe}}\neq m\omega
. The signature of giant CR oscillations will then be observed at photoelectron kinetic energies close to
E_{\mathrm{kin}}\approx 4\omega\pm\omega_{\mathrm{probe}}-E_{\mathrm{bin}}>0
, where
E_{\mathrm{bin}}
is the binding energy of the atom.

The time-dependence of the CR oscillations can be probed by a synchronized auxiliary attosecond pulse. Prior work with seeded FEL have shown that it is possible to generate
attosecond waveforms

[
57
]
that open the possibility to temporally resolve the CR oscillations by photoelectron spectroscopy.

Conclusion
—We have demonstrated via
ab initio
simulations that giant CR effects can be induced with intense UV/XUV fields in helium on attosecond timescales. This opens the gates to an unexplored perturbative ultrastrong coupling regime. For semi-classical systems, this implies a dependence on the instantaneous electric field. Contrary to the simple two-level system, where CR oscillations are reciprocal, we find that only the ES is modulated on a sub-cycle scale, while the GS is effectively shielded from the
\mathcal{Q}
-space. This effect is remarkably strong in 2-PRO from 1s
2
to 1s3d. We have explored these effects quantitatively through a theoretical approach for describing CR oscillations, enabled by effective Hamiltonian theory and Floquet theory. Given the power of Floquet approaches in different areas of physics
[
38
,
58
,
59
]
, we expect that our approach will be fruitful in a range of fields.

I
Non-Hermitian quantum theory

Quantum theory deals with Hermitian operators that have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenfunctions. If complex scaling is introduced, the Hamiltonian
H
of the system becomes non-Hermitian, with complex eigenvalues
E_{n}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{n}
and complex-scaled eigenfunctions
\ket{n}
that are not orthonormal under the usual scalar product
[
49
]
. However, the eigenfunctions still fulfill bi-orthonormality
\braket{\tilde{m}|n}=\delta_{nm}
, where
\bra{\tilde{m}}
solves the left eigenvalue problem
\bra{\tilde{m}}H=(E_{n}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{n})\bra{\tilde{m}}
. The closure relation then reads
1=\sum_{n}\ket{n}\bra{\tilde{n}}
. Note that with this notation, we do not differentiate if the non-Hermitian
H
is symmetric (
H_{F}^{\theta}
) or non-symmetric (
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
).

II
Effective Hamiltonian theory

II.1
Energy-dependent effective Hamiltonian

The purpose of an effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
is to reproduce a subset of the true eigenvalues and eigenstate projections of the full Hamiltonian. We can obtain such an object easily by partitioning the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE)
H\ket{\Psi}=E\ket{\Psi}

[
54
]
. Let the time-independent full Hamiltonian
H
of our system be non-Hermitian. Suppose we can make a perturbative ansatz,
H=H_{0}+V
, where
H_{0}\ket{n}=E_{n}\ket{n}
. Let us now select a small subset of
N
unperturbed essential states
\ket{n}
that span an
N
-dimensional model space
\mathcal{P}
. We project onto
\mathcal{P}
via the projection operator
P=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{P}}\outerproduct{n}{\tilde n}
. The
\mathcal{Q}
-space is the space spanned by the non-essential states, i.e.
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}^{\perp}
, where
Q=1-P
projects onto
\mathcal{Q}
. From this construction, it follows that
[H_{0},P]=[H_{0},Q]=0
, as well as
PQ=QP=0
,
P^{2}=P
, and
Q^{2}=Q
.

Starting with the TISE, we insert
P+Q=1
before
\ket{\Psi}
, and then project from the left with either
P
or
Q
:

\displaystyle PHP\ket{\Psi}+PHQ\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=EP\ket{\Psi}

(7)

\displaystyle QHQ\ket{\Psi}+QHP\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=EQ\ket{\Psi}

(8)

Since
[H_{0},P]=[H_{0},Q]=0
, we have
PH_{0}Q=QH_{0}P=0
, leading to
PHQ=PVQ
and
QHP=QVP
. Rearranging Eq. (
8
) to
Q\ket{\Psi}
and inserting into Eq. (
7
), we obtain a TISE for the
\mathcal{P}
-space dynamics,

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(PHP+PVQ\frac{Q}{E-QHQ}QVP\right)}_{\eqqcolon H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)}P\ket{\Psi}=EP\ket{\Psi},

(9)

with the caveat that the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)
in
\mathcal{P}
is energy-dependent. Hence, Eq. (
9
) constitutes a non-linear eigenvalue problem and must be solved self-consistently one eigenenergy at a time
[
54
]
.

II.2
Energy-independent effective Hamiltonian

An energy-independent effective Hamiltonian reproduces
all
desired eigenvalues and eigenstate projections simultaneously. Obtaining such an object is the subject of effective Hamiltonian theory
[
53
]
. In this work, we use a first-order approximation for the energy-independent effective Hamiltonian. This keeps the formalism conceptually simple, yet as our results demonstrate, sufficiently sophisticated.

The energy-dependence of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)
is weak if the
\mathcal{P}
-space eigenvalues are close to another and well-separated from the
\mathcal{Q}
-space spectrum. In this case, a suitable approximation is to evaluate
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)
at a fixed energy
E_{0}
that lies approximately in the center of the
\mathcal{P}
-space spectrum. This is the pole approximation
[
1
]
, yielding the zeroth-order (energy-independent) effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}=H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E_{0})=PHP+PVQ\frac{Q}{E_{0}-QHQ}QVP.

(10)

H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
is equivalent to the effective Hamiltonian from adiabatic elimination
[
61
]
. In studies of resonant ionisation using effective Hamiltonians,
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
is the standard choice
[
29
,
36
]
. Often,
[E_{0}-QHQ]^{-1}\equiv[E_{0}-QH_{0}Q-QVQ]^{-1}
is expanded perturbatively for small
V

[
1
]
, enabling convenient implementation of the slowly-varying envelope approximation for smooth pulse envelopes
[
62
]
.

For strong perturbations, the eigenstates of the full Hamiltonian will be perturbed significantly, so that the gap between the
\mathcal{P}
-space and
\mathcal{Q}
-space spectrum decreases. Then, the energy-dependence of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)
can become non-negligible near the
\mathcal{P}
-space eigenvalues and the pole approximation becomes invalid. A first-order correction to the pole approximation, Eq. (
10
), can be obtained by linearly expanding
[E-QHQ]^{-1}
around
E_{0}
:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(E)

\displaystyle\approx H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}-(E-E_{0})\cdot\mathcal{C},

(11)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\coloneqq PVQ\frac{Q}{(E_{0}-QHQ)^{2}}QVP.

(12)

Inserting into the
\mathcal{P}
-space TISE, Eq. (
9
), and rearranging yields the energy-independent

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(P+\mathcal{C})^{-1}(H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}+E_{0}\mathcal{C}).

(13)

When
E_{0}=0
,
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
is equivalent to the first-order effective Hamiltonian obtained by Paulisch
et al.
using higher-order Markov approximations
[
63
]
, revealing the analogy between the energy and time domains in effective Hamiltonian theory. Another more formal angle is to interpret
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
as the first term in the Lee-Suzuki iterative sequence, and
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
as the second term
[
64
]
. The eigenvalues of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
approximately reproduce a subset of the true eigenvalues of the full system,
H\ket{\Psi_{n}}=\lambda_{n}\ket{\Psi_{n}}
, and the eigenstates of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
approximately reproduce the corresponding eigenstate projections
P\ket{\Psi_{n}}
. We note here that a range of iterative and perturbative procedures exist for the calculation of converged effective Hamiltonians, see e.g. Ref.
[
65
,
64
,
54
]
.

In this work, we use Eq. (
13
) to calculate the effective Hamiltonian, which we simply abbreviate as
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
. We obtain the inverses
[E-QHQ]^{-k}
,
k=1,2
, numerically exact via LU decomposition. This is computationally cheap because the Floquet basis in our calculations typically contains between 1000 and 5000 atom-photon states. For Fig.
1
and
2
, we interpolate between effective Hamiltonians
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
in the parameter space spanned by
\omega
and
I_{0}
. The cubic interpolation is performed using the
RegularGridInterpolator
class from SciPy
[
66
,
67
]
. For hydrogen (1s-2s transition), we sample from five frequencies and intensities each. In helium, it depends on the transition. For 1s
2
-1s2p, we sample from nine frequencies and ten intensities. For 1s
2
-1s2s, we sample from eight frequencies and ten intensities. For 1s
2
-1s3d and 1s
2
-1s4d, we sample from ten frequencies and intensities each. This yields
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}(\omega,I_{0})
. Fig.
1
,
2
, and
3
are then calculated through upcoming analytical formulas.

If
H
is complex-symmetric, then
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
will also be complex-symmetric, while
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
will generally be non-symmetric. Analogously, if
H
is Hermitian, then
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
will be Hermitian and
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
will generally be non-Hermitian. This property of higher-order effective Hamiltonians is well-known
[
41
]
: The projected eigenstates
P\ket{\Psi_{n}}
are generally not orthogonal to each other,
\Braket{\Psi_{n}|P|\Psi_{m}}\neq\delta_{nm}
, despite the full eigenstates being orthogonal:
\Braket{\Psi_{n}|\Psi_{m}}=\delta_{nm}
(in the complex-scaled case, this argument applies for bi-orthogonality).

We note here that there also exists a complex-symmetric effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff,cs}}^{(1)}
with the same eigenvalues as
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
, but orthonormal eigenvectors in the c-product.
H_{\mathrm{eff,cs}}^{(1)}
can be obtained from
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
via similarity transform with
(P+\mathcal{C})^{1/2}
. While
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
and
H_{\mathrm{eff,cs}}^{(1)}
give rise to the same physical observables
[
68
]
, we are forced to work with
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
when comparing populations between TDCIS and effective Hamiltonian, since we need to work in the same basis in both approaches.

II.3
Reduced wave operator

Applying the pole approximation,

\displaystyle\chi\coloneqq\frac{Q}{E_{0}-QHQ}QVP,

(14)

gives an estimate for the
\mathcal{Q}
-space wavefunction based on the
\mathcal{P}
-space wavefunction,

Q\ket{\Psi}\simeq\chi P\ket{\Psi}.

(15)

It thus approximates the reduced wave operator from time-independent perturbation theory (also called the correlation operator)
[
54
]
. Note how
\chi
from Eq. (
14
) emerges as a by-product when calculating
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}
or
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
. It involves no further computational effort.

We use
\chi
in this work in order to model the
\mathcal{Q}
-space time-evolution that is due to the essential dressed energies. This enables us to rigorously model counter-rotating effects, since these require knowledge of the
\mathcal{Q}
-space components of the dressed states. In particular, for an essential state
\ket{b,-2}
and the CR states
\ket{b,0}
and
\ket{b,-4}
, the characteristic quantity for the prediction of CR oscillations is given by

\Lambda\coloneqq\braket{b,0|\chi|b,-2}+\braket{b,-4|\chi|b,-2}.

(16)
