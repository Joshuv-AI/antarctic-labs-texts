Kaempferol alleviates rheumatoid arthritis through pyroptosis based on bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation
Authors: Yi Ling, Nina Ren, Yuzheng Yang, Hui Xu, Hong Xiong, Qiuyi Wang, Daomin Lu, Xueming Yao, Wukai Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Kaempferol has an anti-inflammatory effect on rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but the mechanism by which inflammatory reactions are relieved via pyroptosis is still unclear. In the present study, The bioinformatics technology was used to analyze the relationship between pyroptosis the hub genes and the downstream cytokines in RA from three the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE12021, GSE1919 and GSE29746). Molecular docking analysis of kaempferol and caspase1 (CASP1) was executed with Autodock tools. RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS) was used to explore the anti-inflammatory effects of kaempferol on RA via pyroptosis. Thirty-seven differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from the three datasets, and the enrichment analysis was focused mainly on the chemokine signaling pathway. The difference in immune cell infiltration was focused on CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages. The expression of CASP1 and caspase3 (CASP3) was higher in the RA group. CASP1 and CASP3 were positively correlated with cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Moreover, molecular docking revealed that kaempferol strongly interacts with CASP1. Kaempferol inhibited the proliferation and migration of RA-FLS in vitro. The expression of CASP1, CASP3, caspase5 (CASP4), caspase5 (CASP5), gasdermin D (GSDMD), N-terminal domain of gasdermin D (GSDMDNT), IL-1β, and cytokines interleukin-18 (IL-18) were significantly affected by kaempferol. Kaempferol alleviated RA-FLS membrane disruption, and it blocked CASP1 to alleviate RA via pyroptosis. The present study demonstrated that kaempferol binds to CASP1 and affects pyroptosis in RA, thereby serving as an anti-immunotherapy strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81833-w.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disorder associated with chronic inflammation and progressive joint destruction1. The prevalence of RA ranges from 0.4% to 1.3% of the population, depending on the region, resulting in a great burden on society for RA treatment and loss of labor force due to joint dysfunction2,3.

The mechanisms underlying RA remain unclear. Extensive research has shown that RA is associated with pyroptosis4–6. As a core protein, caspase 1 (CASP1) in pyroptosis plays an important role in both the CASP1-mediated canonical pathway and the caspase-3/4/5/11(CASP3/4/5/11)-mediated non-canonical pathway7,8. Once the Nod-like receptor (NLR) family pyrin domain (PYD)-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which is connected by apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC), activates pro-caspase1 into CASP1, the linker region of gasdermin D (GSDMD) relieves the N-terminal domain (GSDMDNT) from its autoinhibition by the C-terminal domain9,10. CASP1 promotes the maturation of pro-interleukin-1β (pro-IL-1β) and pro-interleukin-18 (pro-IL-18) into interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18), respectively. Moreover, the release of GSDMDNT forms pores on liposomes, releasing small intracellular molecules, including IL-1β11–13. Eventually, pyroptosis results in cell swelling and rapid lysis, followed by a forced inflammatory reaction in RA. Therefore, studying the roles of the CASP1/3/4/5/11, NLRP3, GSDMDNT, IL-1β, IL-18 hub genes in pyroptosis in RA is important.

Kaempferol, which is present in many kinds of vegetable foods and herbal medicines, can improve the chronic inflammation symptoms of arthritis14,15. Previous research has shown that kaempferol is the most important active compound of Radix Tripterygium wilfordii that is effective against RA16. Although the therapeutic effect of kaempferol is related to the expression of CASP3, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), the effect of kaempferol on CASP1-mediated pyroptosis is unknown. It is worth exploring whether kaempferol represses the activation of CASP1 in pyroptosis to alleviate the inflammation in RA.

To further validate the relationship between kaempferol and CASP1 in pyroptosis, the expression of genes involved in pyroptosis was investigated using Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO database, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) datasets17. The function of kaempferol in CASP1 was assessed by molecular docking and verified in vitro. The study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

DEG analysis and functional enrichment

There were 7,958 common genes in the three datasets. A total of 33 upregulated DEGs and 4 downregulated DEGs were screened according to criteria of |Log2FC| ≥1 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Fig. 2E). Figure 2 F shows the enrichment analysis results, as well as the KEGG and GO functional enrichment analyses results. The enrichGO function in the ClusterProfiler package was used to enrich DEGs from biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The DEGs were enriched mainly in the cytokine-mediated signaling pathway of BP, the external side of the plasma membrane of CC, and chemokine activity of MF. The KEGG pathways of both the upregulated and downregulated genes were enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, and chemokine signaling pathways.

Identification of immune infiltration

The relative proportion of immune subsets between healthy controls and patients with RA was calculated via the CIBERSORTx algorithm (Fig. 2G). CD8 + T cells, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages had increased infiltration in patients with RA. To some extent, the fraction of CD4 + T cells was lower in RA patients in the processed datasets. CASP1, CASP3, and IL-1β were positively correlated with M1 macrophages, while there was a negative correlation between IL-1β and mast cells (Fig. 2H).

Expression and association of hubs gene in pyroptosis

The expression of CASP1, CASP3, CASP4, CASP5, IL-1β, and NLRP3 was analyzed in both RA patients and healthy controls from the three datasets (Fig. 3A-F). The expression of CASP1, CASP3, IL-1β, and NLRP3 were upregulated, whereas there were no significant differences among CASP4, CASP5. Moreover, the CASP1 and CASP3 hub genes were positively correlated with the cytokines IL-1β and TNF cytokines (Fig. 3G-J).

Kaempferol suppresses RA-FLS proliferation

A multivariate repeated measures analysis of variance was conducted to examine the effects of treatment (blank control group, DMSO group, kaempferol group, and Ac-YVAD-cmk positive control group) and time on the rate of inhibition of cell proliferation. The results demonstrated a statistically significant treatment-by-time interaction, indicating that the effects of treatments on these variables varied across time points. Specifically, no significant changes in suppression ratio were observed over time in the blank control and DMSO groups (P > 0.05). However, both kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups presented time-dependent increases in suppression ratio (P < 0.05). Additionally, the kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups showed significantly greater rates of inhibition of cell proliferation compared to the DMSO group at 36–48 h (P < 0.05). These findings suggested that kaempferol exert time-dependent effects on inhibition of cell proliferation. (Fig. 4A).

Kaempferol inhibits RA-FLS migration

Compared with the blank control, DMSO had no effect on RA-FLS migration. However, treatment with kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk significantly decreased RA-FLS migration compared with that in the DMSO group (Fig. 4(B-F)).

Kaempferol downregulates CASP1-mediated pyroptosis

Among the proteins upstream of the classic pyroptosis pathway, the expression of NLRP3 and ASC did not differ among the four groups, indicating that kaempferol did not prevent pyroptosis from being activated by these two proteins (Fig. 5A). The CASP1 protein levels were lower in both the kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups than in the DMSO group (Fig. 5B). In addition, the levels of pyroptosis-related proteins CASP3, CASP4, and CASP5, were lower in the kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups than in the DMSO group (Fig. 5C). The expression of GSDMD and GSDMNT, downstream protein that associated with CASP1, was decreased after treatment with kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk (Fig. 5D). Therefore, these findings suggested that Kaempferol influences pyroptosis via CASP1.

Kaempferol lowers LDH levels

Pyroptosis causes damage to the cell membrane, resulting in the release of LDH from inside the cell. The level of pyroptosis can be reflected in the concentration of LDH. The LDH concentrations were not significantly different between the blank and DMSO groups (Fig. 6A). However, lower concentrations of LDH were detected in the kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups than in the DMSO group (Fig. 6A).

Kaempferol decreases IL-1β and IL-18 secretion

Compared with the blank control, DMSO had no effect on the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. Compared with DMSO, Kaempferol decreased the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 6B).

Kaempferol affects pyroptosis in RA-FLS

The process of RA-FLS in pyroptosis was targeted by double positivity for FITC anti-caspase-1 and PI in Q2 of every image. There was no significant difference between the blank control and DMSO groups. However, the ratios of pyroptosis cells were significantly lower in the kaempferol and Ac-YVAD-cmk groups than in the DMSO group (Fig. 6(C-G)).

Discussion

With the increasing number of patients diagnosed, there is an urgent need to seek effective drugs to treat RA. Kaempferol stemming from Radix Tripterygium wilfordii is commonly used in Chinese traditional medicine due to its antioxidant activity, anti-Inflammatory activity, anticancer activity, antimicrobial activity19. As oxidative stress and inflammation are known risk factors for RA, it is possible to determine the anti-inflammatory effect of kaempferol against RA20,21. This study demonstrated the effects of kaempferol on CASP1-mediated pyroptosis in RA.

The datasets of GSE12021, GSE1919, and GSE29746 indicated that RA pathogenesis in different individuals may depend on common alterations of the expression of specific key genes, but the specific mechanism is unclear and needs further verification. Analysis of the GSE12021, GSE1919, and GSE29746 datasets identified 37 DEGs. Enrichment analyses of these DEGs identified the involvement of cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, viral protein interactions with cytokines and cytokine receptors, and chemokine signaling pathways, which are associated with inflammation. The pathogenesis of RA involves various cytokines that are implicated in the imbalance between the growth and death of synovial cells, resulting in pannus formation22–24. Immune infiltration indicated that the expression of CD8 + T cells ran counter to CD4 + T cells, suggesting a poor immune state in patients with RA.

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death accompanied by the release of IL-18 and IL-1β, causing injury to adjacent cells and exacerbation of inflammation25. Briefly, unlike NLRP3, GSDMD is cleaved by CASP1/3/4/5, leading to the dissociation of GSDMD-NT from the C-terminal repressor domain. GSDMD-NT then oligomerizes and forms pores in the cell membrane, causing the release of inflammatory molecules and pyroptotic cell death. Inflammatory reactions occur in many immune diseases via NLRP3 activation of CASP126,27. Nevertheless, more evidence is needed to elucidate the mechanism of pyroptosis in the pathogenesis and progression of RA. The expression of NLRP3, CASP1/3/4/5, and IL-1β in the GEO databases confirmed that CASP1 may refer to RA pathology and bring about changes downstream of CASP3/4/5 and IL-1β in pyroptosis. Furthermore, the expression of the CASP1 or CASP3 genes was positively correlated with the expression of the IL-1β or IL-18 cytokines. These results suggested that CASP1 mediates the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 through pyroptosis in RA.

Molecular docking analysis is one of the most frequently used methods in structure-based drug design because it accurately calculates interaction energies and clearly reveals the binding site between small-molecule ligands and the protein receptor28. Molecular docking analysis revealed a strong interaction between kaempferol and CASP1. Kaempferol inhibited the proliferation and migration of RA-FLS in vitro, which is beneficial to the joint cartilage29. Western blot analysis indicated that kaempferol did not affect NLRP3 to activate CASP1, whereas the downstream proteins, including CASP3/4/5 and the GSDMD and GSDMDNT executors, were downregulated. Thus, Kaempferol may bind to CASP1 to repress pyroptosis. Kaempferol intervention decreased the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. Therefore, by inhibiting pyroptosis, Kaempferol alleviates the continuous stimulation of RA-FLS by IL-1β, consequently suppressing the proliferation of RA-FLS. In addition, the LDH results and the flow cytometry results both showed that kaempferol could affect pyroptosis in RA. Briefly, kaempferol bound to CASP1, inhibiting pyroptosis in RA-FLS and potentially leading to beneficial effects in the context of rheumatoid arthritis. However, the role of kaempferol as an inhibitor requires further investigation using a mouse model. Moreover, additional clinical trials are required to verify this finding.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that kaempferol binds to CASP1 to exert obvious effects on pyroptosis in RA. Kaempferol can serve as an inhibitor of CASP1, potentially serving as an anti-immunotherapy strategy.

Methods

Data access and processing

The GSE12021 (GPL96 platform), GSE1919 (GPL91 platform), and GSE29746 (GPL4133 platform) datasets were obtained from the GEO database30–32. The criteria for the datasets were as follows: First, the experiment type was profiling via an array. Second, the samples were organized as synovial membrane samples originating from patients with RA or normal volunteers. Third, these datasets contained processed data or raw data for further analysis.

The probes were matched to their gene symbols in the gene expression matrix according to the annotation information of the corresponding platforms. The average expression value of multiple genes was calculated as the final expression value. A new matrix for the expression of common gene symbols from the GSE12021, GSE1919, and GSE29746 datasets was constructed. The data were normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function of the limma package in R software (version 4.1.3) after log2 transformation33. Finally, the last expression matrix was produced after removing batch effects with the removeBatchEffect function of the limma package in the R software. The results of data preprocessing were assessed via boxplots and principal component analysis plots.

Differential analysis and functional enrichment analysis

The limma package in R software was utilized to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in patients with RA or normal controls with |Log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analyses of the 33 upregulated DEGs and 4 downregulated DEGs were executed under the condition of adjusted P< 0.05 with the clusterProfiler package in R software34,35. The enrichment results were visualized via the ggplot2 package in R software36.

Immune infiltration assessment

CIBERSORTx was used to analyze the relationship between the genes and immune cell infiltration37. The marker genes of 22 immune cells were downloaded from CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). The immune infiltration of the processed dataset was calculated using the R language code (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). Based on relevant literature, CASP1, CASP3, CASP4, CASP5, IL-1β, and NRLP3 were considered hub genes in human pyroptosis38,39. The correlation between the hub genes and the 22 immune cells types were determined via Spearman’s correlation analysis. The Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Hub genes related to pyroptosis and correlation analysis

To explore whether pyroptosis occurs in RA, the pyroptosis hub genes were analysed using the GEO datasets. The relationships between the CASP1 and CASP3 hub genes with the downstream IL-1β and IL-18 cytokines were assessed. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to determine the correlation.

Molecular docking analysis of kaempferol and CASP1

Molecular docking was used to determine the binding affinities between proteins and ligands. Kaempferol and the key protein of CASP1 were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank database (RCSB PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/) in PDB format40. The structure of kaempferol was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF format and converted into PDB format using Open Babel GUI (version 3.1.1). Autodock tools (version 1.5.7) with Autodock vina (version 1.1.2) were used to conduct molecular docking between kaempferol and CASP141. The results were visualized via PYMOL (version 2.4.0a0)42.

RA-FLS culture and treatment

The methods used for cell culture and subsequent analyses were previously described by Yi Ling et al.43. RA-FLS were isolated from three patients according to the 2010ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria for RA and Disease Activity Score in 28 joints and four variables, including C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) ≥ 3.2. The cells were authenticated as RA-FLS by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry with anti-vimentin antibody. Additionally, the purity of RA-FLS was 96.6 ± 1.44% marked with FITC anti-CD90 or 97.1 ± 0.39% marked with APC-conjugated anti-VCAM-1 antibody, which was previously reported in the same batch RA-FLS16. The RA-FLS recovered and was cultured at 37℃ and 5% CO2-enriched atmosphere in complete medium containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, United States) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (BI, Israel) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, United States).

The third passage of RA-FLS was pre-treated with complete medium, 0.2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Solarbio, China), 25 µM kaempferol (CAS: 520-18-3, Shanghai Winherb, China), or 40 µM Ac-YVAD-cmk (Caspase-1 Inhibitor II; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. All reagents were dissolved in DMSO and diluted to working concentrations with complete medium.

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

The proliferative inhibition rate was determined using the CCK-8 assay (CCK-8; NW595, Dojindo, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the third-passage cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 103 cells/well and incubated at 37℃ and 5% CO2 for 24 h. After being cultured with 180 µL of different test agents for 24, 48, or 72 h, 20 µL CCK-8 solution was added to the culture medium of each well. The 96-well plates were then incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (1510, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Three independent experiments were performed and the cell proliferation inhibition rate was calculated using the equation: (OD value of contrast well − OD value of experimental well)/ (OD value of contrast well − OD value of blank well) ×100%.

Migration assays

RA-FLS (1 × 105/ml) were pre-treated with different working concentrations of test agents for 24 h. The RA-FLS were serum-starved overnight one day prior to the transwell migration assay. A total of 3 × 104 cells were resuspended in 100 µL serum-free medium and seeded onto the top chamber of a transwell migration assay plate (8 μm pores, Corning, United States), and 800 µL of complete medium was placed into the bottom chamber. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were fixed with absolute ethanol, and cells that had not migrated to the bottom were carefully removed with a cotton swab. The cells that migrated to the bottom surface were stained with crystal violet (0.1%) for 10 min at room temperature. Three independent experiments were performed. The cells were counted via an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from cells that underwent different treatments with RIPA lysis buffer (20170510, Solarbio, China), and a BCA protein concentration determination kit (20190921, Solarbio, China) was used to measure protein concentrations according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell lysates containing 40 µg total protein were loaded onto 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; 1610185, Bio-Rad, USA). The separated protein bands were transferred onto PVDF membranes. Then the PVDF membranes were cropped according to the position of the target proteins. After blocking with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline-Tween (TBST) for 1.5 h, membranes were conjugated overnight at 4 °C with different primary antibodies, including those against NLRP3 (ab210491, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), caspase-1 (ab62698, Abcam, UK; 1:500), caspase-3 (ab13847, Abcam, UK; 1:500), caspase-4 (ab238124, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), caspase-5 (ab40887, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), GSDMD (ab210070, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), GSDMDNT (ab215203, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC) (ab151700, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), and β-actin (4970 S, Cell Signaling, USA; 1:1,000). Next, peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (019189, Pumei, China; 1:10000) was used as a secondary antibody and incubated for 1 h. An enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (170–5060, Bio-Rad, USA) was used to detect the amount of the target proteins. ImageJ software was used to quantify the relative intensity following normalization.

Lactate dehydrogenase release assay

LDH was rapidly released into the cell culture supernatant when the plasma membrane is damaged. Thus, the release of LDH into the extracellular medium validates the extent of rupture of the plasma membrane. Third-passage cells (1.2 × 106cells) were treated with different agents in a 75 cm2 culture flask for 24 h, the LDH activity in the supernatant was tested using an LDH assay kit (20200630, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (1510, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

After treatment with different agents, the concentrations of IL-1β (ab214025, Abcam, UK) and IL-18 (ab215539, Abcam, UK) were determined using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Flow cytometry assays

After treating third-generation cells (1.2 × 106 cells) with different reagents for 24 h, the cells were incubated with 5 µL FITC anti-caspase-1 (SC-392736, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) in a 37℃ dark room for 1 h. The cells were washed with 2 mL apoptosis buffer and centrifuged at 1500 rpm. The cells were then stained with 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min and thereafter, resuspended in 500 µL of apoptosis buffer and analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACSCanto II, BD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 22 software package (SPSS Inc., United States). To compare the overall impact of the intervention over the treatment period for the two groups, a set of multivariate repeated measures analyses of variance was employed with treatment/intervention as the between-group factor and time as the within-group factor. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess potential correlations. Statistical analysis of CCK-8, migration, western blot, LDH, ELISA, and FCM was conducted using one-way analysis of variance. Statistical analysis of significance was P < 0.05. NS denotes non-signifcant values.

Ethics statement
