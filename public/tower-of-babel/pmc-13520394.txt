Intramedullary cement augmentation in locking plate fixation for Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures: a technique for preventing early mechanical failure
Authors: Zhixiang Chen, Chengjun Hao, Bingjun Cui, Lang Wu, Chuangong Wang, Zhenqing Zhang, Shaoyu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocking plate fixation for Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures is frequently complicated by varus collapse and screw cut-out, leading to mechanical failure, particularly in osteoporotic bone. This study evaluates the clinical and radiographic outcomes of a modified technique involving intramedullary polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement augmentation applied during its thread-forming phase to create an internal support structure.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 66 consecutive patients with Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures treated between January 2023 and June 2024 were enrolled and divided into two groups: 31 patients underwent locking plate ORIF with intramedullary PMMA cement augmentation (augmentation group), and 35 patients received conventional locking plate fixation without cement (control group). Patient demographics, surgical details, and perioperative data were recorded. Primary outcomes were radiographic: fracture union time, and changes in the neck-shaft angle (NSA) and humeral head height (HHH). Secondary outcomes included the Constant-Murley Score (CMS), Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain, and all complications.Results The two groups were well matched demographically (mean age 77.9 ± 3.6 years; P > 0.05). All fractures achieved union, with significantly faster healing in the augmentation group (14.42 ± 1.45 vs. 15.10 ± 1.12 weeks; P = 0.0245). Radiographic stability was markedly superior in the augmentation group: mean loss of NSA was 1.4° ± 1.2° versus 2.8° ± 1.9° (P = 0.001), and mean loss of HHH was 0.6 ± 0.5 mm versus 1.8 ± 1.1 mm (P < 0.001). Critically, no patient (0%) in the augmentation group experienced significant loss of reduction (defined as > 5° change in NSA or > 3 mm loss of HHH), compared to three patients (8.57%) in the control group (P = 0.095). Early postoperative pain was significantly reduced in the augmentation group on day 1 (VAS: 4.35 ± 1.27 vs. 5.20 ± 1.39; P = 0.0065) and day 3 (3.29 ± 0.45 vs. 3.63 ± 0.64; P = 0.0044). Functional recovery was significantly better at 1 month (CMS: 51.61 ± 6.16 vs. 48.54 ± 7.06; P = 0.0447) and 3 months (66.10 ± 10.32 vs. 62.09 ± 8.56; P = 0.0467). The complication rate was low; notably, radiographic avascular necrosis occurred in only one patient (3.23%) in the augmentation group versus five patients (14.29%) in the control group (P = 0.122). No cases of screw cut-out, implant failure, or reoperation were recorded.ConclusionIntramedullary augmentation with thread-forming phase PMMA cement is a highly effective adjunct to locking plate fixation for Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures. In this cohort, no patient in the augmentation group experienced significant loss of reduction, suggesting that the technique may reduce the risk of early mechanical failure, provide superior radiographic stability, reduce early postoperative pain, accelerate early functional recovery, and demonstrate a clinically meaningful trend toward lower AVN incidence. It represents a reliable joint-preserving option for managing these complex fractures, particularly in patients where maintaining the native glenohumeral joint is a priority.

Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures (PHFs) are common injuries, particularly among the elderly osteoporotic population, with their incidence projected to rise alongside global aging trends [1]. Among these, four-part fractures according to the Neer classification represent a particularly challenging subset due to the severe displacement of articular segments and the high risk of compromised blood supply to the humeral head [2, 3]. These fractures are notoriously difficult to manage and are associated with significant morbidity, including chronic pain, stiffness, and functional impairment of the shoulder [4, 5].

Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using locking plates has emerged as a standard surgical treatment for displaced four-part fractures in patients with adequate bone quality, aiming to restore anatomy and enable early rehabilitation [6, 7]. However, despite advancements in implant design, postoperative complications remain distressingly common [8]. These primarily include loss of reduction, varus collapse of the humeral head, screw cut-out, and perforation into the glenohumeral joint [9, 10]. The fundamental challenge lies in the osteoporotic, comminuted metaphyseal bone of the proximal humerus, which often fails to provide sufficient purchase for the locking screws, leading to mechanical failure under axial and rotational loads [11]. This compromised bone-implant interface is the principal Achilles’ heel of conventional ORIF in this context [12, 13].

To augment stability, various augmentation techniques have been explored. These include the use of autologous or allogeneic bone grafts, calcium phosphate injectable cements, and intramedullary fibular strut allografts [14, 15]. While these methods offer some benefits, they are not without limitations. Bone grafts face issues of resorption, donor site morbidity, or limited availability [16, 17]. Injectable calcium phosphate cements, while osteoconductive, are brittle and offer primarily compressive strength with limited capacity to resist varus forces [18, 19]. The fibular strut technique is effective but requires additional grafting material and precise surgical technique [15, 20]. Consequently, there remains a compelling need for a simple, readily available, and biomechanically effective method to enhance the primary stability of the fracture-internal fixation construct, particularly by reinforcing the critical medial calcar region and providing intramedullary support [21].

Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement, a material with a long history of successful use in orthopedic surgery for its excellent immediate mechanical properties, presents a promising candidate for such augmentation [22, 23]. Its unique working properties, especially during the “dough” or “thread-forming” phase, allow it to be molded and injected to conform to the irregular intramedullary cavity [24, 25]. When applied in this manner, PMMA can create a customized, load-bearing intramedullary scaffold that directly transfers stress from the humeral head to the distal diaphysis, thereby offloading the locking screws and mitigating varus collapse [13]. This concept of intramedullary cement augmentation aims to address the core issue of poor bone quality by fundamentally enhancing the stability of the entire construct [26].

While the theoretical biomechanical advantages of intramedullary PMMA augmentation are clear, comprehensive clinical studies reporting its specific application and outcomes in the surgical treatment of Neer four-part fractures are relatively scarce in the literature [27]. Detailed reports on the surgical technique timing (cement application in the thread-forming phase), its impact on radiographic parameters of reduction maintenance, and mid-term functional outcomes are needed to validate its clinical efficacy and safety [20].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of a consecutive series of patients with Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures who were treated with ORIF augmented by intramedullary injection of PMMA bone cement during its thread-forming phase. We hypothesized that this technique would provide a significant enhancement in construct stability, leading to a low rate of postoperative loss of reduction, minimal implant-related complications, and satisfactory functional recovery.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study included consecutively treated patients between January 2023 and June 2024. The decision to perform cement augmentation was made by the attending surgeon based on intraoperative assessment of bone quality and fracture comminution, without randomization. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team, consisting of two senior orthopedic trauma surgeons, to minimize technical variability.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Huai’an Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University (Approval No.: KY-P-2023-002-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Consecutive patients diagnosed with acute, displaced Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures who underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) between January 2023 and June 2024 were retrospectively reviewed.

Surgical procedure

All operations were performed by a senior orthopedic trauma team using a standardized protocol under general anesthesia in the beach-chair position.

Surgical approach and reduction

A deltopectoral approach was utilized in all cases. The cephalic vein was identified and protected. Fracture hematoma was evacuated, and anatomical reduction of the humeral head and tuberosities was achieved under direct vision, followed by temporary fixation with Kirschner wires. Intraoperative blood loss was estimated by the anesthesia team based on suction volume and gauze weight.

Augmentation technique (augmentation group)

After provisional reduction, low-viscosity PMMA bone cement (Palacos R + G, Heraeus) was prepared. During its dough phase (approximately 4–5 min after mixing), the cement was molded into a cylindrical strut matching the diameter of the medullary canal (length 3–5 cm). The cement strut was inserted into the proximal medullary cavity through the fracture site. Local irrigation with chilled saline was performed to mitigate thermal effects. Following initial cement setting, a proximal humeral locking plate (PHILOS, Zhengtian Technology) was applied laterally. Locking screws were inserted, with several deliberately penetrating the cement strut to form an integrated cement–screw composite construct. Calcar screws were used in all cases when anatomically feasible. In the augmentation group, at least one screw was intentionally placed through the cement strut to achieve integrated screw–cement fixation. Closure

After confirming satisfactory reduction and implant position under fluoroscopy, the wound was irrigated, a drain was placed, and closure was performed in layers.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Both groups followed an identical rehabilitation protocol:

Sling immobilization for the first 3 weeks with pendulum exercises only.

Assisted passive and active range-of-motion exercises beginning at 3 weeks.

Strengthening exercises initiated after radiographic evidence of union (typically ≥ 12 weeks).

Gradual return to activities as tolerated.

Outcome assessment

Radiographic parameters, including neck-shaft angle (NSA) and humeral head height (HHH), were measured by two independent observers using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Measurements were repeated by the same observers after a two-week interval. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability were assessed, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior observer. Union was defined as bridging callus across at least three cortices on anteroposterior and lateral radiographs. In equivocal cases, CT was obtained for confirmation. Radiographic assessments were performed at 4-week intervals until union was confirmed.

Primary Radiographic Outcomes

Fracture union time (defined as bridging callus across ≥ 3 cortices on anteroposterior and lateral views).

Changes in neck-shaft angle (NSA) and humeral head height (HHH) between immediate postoperative and final follow-up radiographs were assessed. Significant loss of reduction was defined as a decrease of > 5° in NSA or a loss of > 3 mm in HHH, in accordance with previously published criteria for clinically relevant varus collapse [28].

Implant-related complications (screw cut-out, penetration, or breakage).

Secondary clinical outcomes

Shoulder function evaluated using the Constant-Murley Score (CMS) at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively.

Pain intensity assessed via the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7.

Complications including infection, nerve injury, avascular necrosis (AVN), and reoperation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Demographics and Perioperative Data

A total of 66 patients with Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in this study, including 31 patients in the cement augmentation group and 35 in the conventional locking plate fixation group. The two groups were well matched with respect to age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and mechanism of injury, with no statistically significant differences observed (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). The overall mean age was 77.9 ± 3.6 years, and the majority of fractures (84.8%) resulted from low-energy falls.

Operative time was significantly longer in the control group compared to the augmentation group (75.35 ± 13.32 min vs. 68.57 ± 11.84 min, P = 0.0326). Intraoperative blood loss was also significantly higher in the control group (109.26 ± 10.69 mL vs. 102.86 ± 10.48 mL, P = 0.0162). Superficial surgical site infection occurred in one patient (2.86%) in the control group, while no such infection was observed in the augmentation group (0.00%; P = 0.36). Subacromial impingement was noted in one patient (3.23%) in the augmentation group and two patients (5.71%) in the control group, with no significant intergroup difference (P = 0.631).

Notably, radiographic evidence of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the humeral head was observed in five patients (14.29%) in the control group, compared to only one patient (3.23%) in the augmentation group (P = 0.122). All cases of AVN were asymptomatic at the final follow-up and did not require surgical intervention.

Regarding radiographic parameters of mechanical stability, the mean loss of neck-shaft angle (NSA) was significantly lower in the augmentation group (1.4° ± 1.2°) than in the control group (2.8° ± 1.9°; P = 0.001). Similarly, the mean loss of humeral head height (HHH) was significantly less in the augmentation group (0.6 ± 0.5 mm) compared to controls (1.8 ± 1.1 mm; P < 0.001). Importantly, no patient (0.00%) in the augmentation group experienced significant loss of reduction according to the predefined criteria, whereas three patients (8.57%) in the control group met this criterion (P = 0.095).

No systematic assessment of fracture morphology predictors of AVN (e.g., Hertel criteria) was performed. Therefore, the observed difference in AVN incidence should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence of a protective effect.

No cases of deep infection, nerve injury, implant failure, or reoperation were recorded in either cohort. These findings suggest that intramedullary cement augmentation not only enhances mechanical stability but may also contribute to a reduced risk of AVN and implant-related complications.

Discussion

The management of Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures, particularly in elderly patients with osteoporosis, remains challenging due to high rates of mechanical failure and avascular necrosis (AVN) [29]. In this study, augmentation of locking plate fixation with an intramedullary polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement strut was associated with enhanced construct stability, improved early functional outcomes, and a reduced incidence of key complications. Specifically, the augmentation group demonstrated superior radiographic stability, faster early functional recovery, and a lower AVN rate. These findings align with a growing body of evidence supporting targeted augmentation in osteoporotic fracture care and address a critical gap in achieving reliable fixation for this complex injury pattern [30].

Enhanced construct stability: addressing the core mechanical challenge

The fundamental limitation of conventional locked plating in osteoporotic proximal humeral fractures is poor screw purchase within weak metaphyseal bone, which predisposes to varus collapse, screw cut-out, and loss of reduction. Systematic reviews have reported failure rates of 20% to 35% for complex fractures treated with plate fixation alone [31].

In our series, the augmentation group exhibited significantly superior radiographic stability. Mean loss of neck-shaft angle (NSA) was 1.4° ± 1.2° versus 2.8° ± 1.9° in controls (P = 0.001), and mean loss of humeral head height (HHH) was 0.6 ± 0.5 mm versus 1.8 ± 1.1 mm (P < 0.001). Notably, no patient (0%) in the augmentation group experienced significant loss of reduction according to the predefined criteria (change in NSA > 5° or loss of HHH > 3 mm), compared with three patients (8.57%) in the control group (P = 0.095).

These radiographic advantages can be explained mechanistically. The intramedullary cement strut functions as a load-sharing implant rather than a passive filler. It establishes a continuous rigid column that transfers stress from the humeral head directly to the diaphyseal cortex, effectively bypassing the comminuted and osteoporotic metaphysis [32]. This biomechanical principle has been validated in cadaveric studies, demonstrating that cement augmentation increases load to failure and reduces varus deformation by over 50% compared with standard plating [33]. Moreover, inserting screws through the dough-phase cement creates an integrated cement-screw composite that resists toggle and pull-out forces more effectively than screws anchored in bone alone [34].

Operative time was significantly shorter in the augmentation group (68.57 ± 11.84 min vs. 75.35 ± 13.32 min, P = 0.0326). This difference likely reflects the enhanced intraoperative confidence in construct stability, which may reduce the need for repeated fluoroscopic adjustments and additional provisional fixation maneuvers.

Clinical translation: early pain control and functional recovery

Beyond radiographic outcomes, the clinical benefits of augmented stability were apparent in early pain control. Patients in the augmentation group reported significantly lower pain scores on postoperative days 1 (VAS: 4.35 ± 1.27 vs. 5.20 ± 1.39, P = 0.0065) and 3 (3.29 ± 0.45 vs. 3.63 ± 0.64, P = 0.0044), with comparable scores by day 7 (P = 0.7008). Effective early analgesia is important for facilitating patient participation in rehabilitation, which is critical for regaining shoulder mobility and preventing stiffness—a common source of long-term disability [35]. We attribute this analgesic effect to reduced micromotion at the fracture site, which may decrease mechanical irritation and local inflammatory mediators [36].

Consistent with this, the augmentation group demonstrated significantly better Constant-Murley Scores (CMS) at 1 month (51.61 ± 6.16 vs. 48.54 ± 7.06, P = 0.0447) and 3 months (66.10 ± 10.32 vs. 62.09 ± 8.56, P = 0.0467). By 6 and 12 months, CMS scores were comparable between groups (P = 0.5375 and P = 0.6394, respectively), suggesting that the primary benefit of this technique lies in accelerating early functional recovery rather than conferring long-term functional superiority once healing is complete. Such accelerated recovery may improve patient satisfaction and reduce healthcare utilization [4].

Biological considerations: fracture healing and avascular necrosis

A noteworthy finding was the significantly shorter time to fracture union in the augmentation group (14.42 ± 1.45 weeks vs. 15.10 ± 1.12 weeks, P = 0.0245). Although PMMA is bioinert, the absolute stability it provides creates a low-strain mechanical environment conducive to indirect bone healing [37]. Reduced instability may also attenuate the local inflammatory response that can impede early callus formation [38].

The incidence of radiographic AVN was 3.23% (1/31) in the augmentation group versus 14.29% (5/35) in the control group (P = 0.122). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance—likely due to the limited sample size—the 77% relative reduction is clinically notable. AVN in these fractures results from both the initial vascular insult and subsequent mechanical collapse that disrupts revascularization [39]. By providing stable fixation, the cement-augmented construct may protect the residual blood supply during the critical healing period. This hypothesis, sometimes termed “protective stability,” suggests that preventing collapse may be as important as preserving initial vascularity in avoiding clinically significant AVN [40]. All AVN cases in our cohort were asymptomatic Stage I, suggesting that when collapse is prevented, progression to symptomatic joint compromise may be averted.

The use of chilled saline irrigation during cement application helps mitigate the exothermic reaction of PMMA polymerization [41, 42]. Previous studies have shown that continuous irrigation effectively reduces peak temperatures at the bone–cement interface, minimizing the risk of thermal osteonecrosis in adjacent viable bone [43]. This precaution is particularly important in the proximal humerus, where subchondral bone is thin and the blood supply is tenuous [44].

Technique advantages and evolving context

Compared with fibular strut allografts, this technique avoids graft-related issues such as limited availability, sizing mismatch, and potential disease transmission [45, 46]. Unlike injectable calcium phosphate cements, which are brittle and provide primarily compressive strength, PMMA offers superior immediate tensile and torsional strength—properties critical for resisting varus deformation in the proximal humerus [47]. Recent systematic reviews have confirmed that PMMA augmentation achieves higher load-to-failure rates than calcium phosphate in osteoporotic bone models [48, 49].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective, non-randomized design introduces potential selection bias, although baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between groups. The sample size (n = 66) is adequate for detecting differences in primary radiographic outcomes but may be underpowered for rarer complications such as symptomatic AVN. Follow-up, while exceeding one year for all patients, remains intermediate; longer-term studies (5–10 years) are needed to assess late sequelae such as cement fragmentation, aseptic loosening, or glenohumeral arthritis [21]. Additionally, all procedures were performed by a specialized surgical team, and the reproducibility of these outcomes in broader clinical settings requires validation. Future prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials comparing this technique with other augmentation methods and with primary reverse shoulder arthroplasty are necessary to establish definitive treatment algorithms [7].

Conclusion

In conclusion, intramedullary PMMA cement augmentation represents a safe, reliable, and biomechanically sound adjunct to locked plating for the treatment of Neer four-part proximal humeral fractures. In this study, the technique was associated with a 0% incidence of significant loss of reduction, indicating a potential reduction in early mechanical failure risk. It provides significantly enhanced radiographic stability (superior preservation of NSA and HHH), reduces early postoperative pain, accelerates functional recovery during the first three months, and suggests a clinically meaningful trend toward lower AVN incidence. This joint-preserving strategy is a valuable addition to the orthopedic armamentarium, offering a particularly compelling option for patients who are younger, more active, or for whom preservation of the native glenohumeral joint is a priority.
