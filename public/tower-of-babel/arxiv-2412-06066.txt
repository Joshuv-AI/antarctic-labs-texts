Perturbed Traceless SU(2) Character Varieties of Tangle Sums
Kai Smith

Abstract
. If a link L L can be decomposed into the union of two tangles T ∪ S 2 S T\cup_{S^{2}}S along a 2-sphere intersecting L L in 4 points, then the intersections of perturbed traceless SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU(2)} character varieties of tangles in a space called the pillowcase form a set of generators for Kronheimer and Mrowka’s reduced singular instanton homology, I ♮ I^{\natural} . It is conjectured by Cazassus, Herald, Kirk, and Kotelskiy that with the addition of bounding cochains, the differential of I ♮ I^{\natural} can be recovered from these Lagrangians as well. This article gives a method to compute the perturbed character variety for a large class of tangles using cut-and-paste methods. In particular, given two tangles, T T and S S , Conway defines the tangle sum T + S T+S . Given the character varieties of T T and S S , we show how to construct the perturbed character variety of T + S T+S . This is done by first studying the perturbed character variety of a certain tangle C 3 C_{3} properly embedded in S 3 S^{3} with 3 balls removed. Using these results, we prove a nontriviality result for the bounding cochains in the conjecture of Cazassus, Herald, Kirk, and Kotelskiy.

Background

2.1.
The Lie Group
\mathrm{SU(2)}

\mathrm{SU(2)}
is the Lie group of complex valued 2 by 2 matrices with determinant 1. Any such matrix can be written in the form
A=\begin{bmatrix}a+b\textbf{i}&c+d\textbf{i}\\
-c+d\textbf{i}&a-b\textbf{i}\end{bmatrix}
. The map
\phi:\mathrm{SU(2)}\to\mathbb{H}
given by
A\mapsto a+b\textbf{i}+c\textbf{j}+d\textbf{k}
is a Lie group isomorphism from
\mathrm{SU(2)}
to the unit quaternions. In this context, it is easy to see that
\mathrm{SU(2)}
is homeomorphic to
S^{3}
embedded in the space of all quaternions, which can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

For a matrix
A\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
, the trace is given by
\operatorname{tr}(A)=2a=2\operatorname{Re}(\phi(A))
. Conjugating by an element of
\mathrm{SU(2)}
preserves the trace. Furthermore, this action is transitive on subsets with the same trace, and so the conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{SU(2)}
are completely determined by the trace. For
x\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
, let
C(x)
denote the conjugacy class of
x
. One conjugacy class of particular interest is
C(\textbf{i})
, the
traceless
unit quaternions. Taking
\{\textbf{i},\textbf{j},\textbf{k}\}
as the basis for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
C(\textbf{i})
is precisely the unit 2-sphere. Note that for
x\in C(\textbf{i})
,
x^{-1}=-x
. Going forward,
\overline{x}
will be used as more compact notation for
x^{-1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Call a set of points in
C(\textbf{i})

coequatorial
if all of the points in the set lie on a single great circle in
C(\textbf{i})
.

Since conjugation preserves trace,
C(\textbf{i})
is preserved by conjugation by
x\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
. More explicitly, if
Q\in C(\textbf{i})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, conjugation by
x=e^{tQ}
acts on
C(\textbf{i})
by a rotation of angle
2t
about the axis through
Q
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
S^{1}_{\textbf{k}^{\perp}}
denote the great circle of
C(\textbf{i})
which is perpendicular to
k
,

\{e^{\theta\textbf{k}}\textbf{i}\in\mathrm{SU(2)}\mid\theta\in S^{1}\}.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
x,y\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
let the
angle between
x
and
y
be the angle of the corresponding vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
\arccos(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y})\in[0,\pi]
. Denote the angle between
x
and
y
by
\angle xy
.

Definition 2.4
.

If
x
,
y
, and
z
lie on the same great circle of
C(\textbf{i})
, define the
angle of
x
and
y
relative to
z
to be

\angle_{z}xy:=\begin{cases}\angle xy&\text{if }\angle xz+\angle zy=\angle xy\text{ or }\angle xz-\angle zy=\angle xy\\
2\pi-\angle xy&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Intuitively, for any two points
x
and
y
on a circle such that
y\notin\{\pm x\}
, there are two different angles between
x
and
y
depending on which path one takes around the circle to get from
x
to
y
. By Definition
2.3
,
\angle xy
is always the smaller of these two angles. But sometimes we will want to specify which of these two angles we are talking about, which is what the relative angle allows us to do. Given a third point on the circle
z
which is also not in
\{\pm x\}
, taking the shortest path from
x
to
z
defines an orientation on the circle. Then
\angle_{z}xy
is defined to be the angle spanned by the path from
x
to
y
on the circle following this orientation.
If
y
or
z
is in
\{\pm x\}
, then
\angle_{z}xy
is defined to be
\angle xy
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume
x
,
y
, and
z
lie on the same great circle in
C(\textbf{i})
. Then there exists
w\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
such that
wx\overline{w}=\textbf{i}
,
wz\overline{w}=e^{\gamma\textbf{k}}\textbf{i}
for
\gamma\in[0,\pi]
, and
wy\overline{w}=e^{\theta\textbf{k}}\textbf{i}
for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. If
\sin(\gamma)=0
, then
w
can be chosen to ensure that
\theta\in[0,\pi]
.
Then
\angle xz=\gamma
and
\angle_{z}xy=\theta
.

Proof.

Because conjugation is transitive on
C(\textbf{i})
, there exists some
w^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
such that
w^{\prime}x\overline{w^{\prime}}=\textbf{i}
. Further conjugating by a quaternion of the form
e^{\delta\textbf{i}}
can guarantee the hypothesis. So
w=e^{\delta\textbf{i}}w^{\prime}e^{-\delta\textbf{i}}
.

Because angles are invariant under rotation,
\angle xz=\angle(wx\overline{w})(wz\overline{w})=\gamma
. The invariance of angles under rotation also implies that

\displaystyle\angle_{z}xy

\displaystyle=\angle_{wz\overline{w}}(wx\overline{w})(wy\overline{w})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\arccos(\cos(\theta))&\text{if }\gamma\pm\arccos(\cos(\gamma-\theta))=\arccos(\cos(\theta))\\
2\pi-\arccos(\cos(\theta))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

This splits into four cases depending on the signs of
\cos(\theta)
and
\cos(\gamma-\theta)
. Checking these cases shows that this expression is always equal to
\theta
.
∎

2.5.
Holonomy Perturbations

In order to extract information out of character varieties, it is often necessary to perturb them. The types of perturbations considered in this article are called
holonomy perturbations
and are chosen in this context because they agree with the types of perturbations used for instanton gauge theory.

Definition 2.22
.

Perturbation data
for the tangle
(X,T)
is a set of tuples
\{(D^{\prime}_{i},f_{i},t_{i})\}
where:

•

\{D^{\prime}_{i}\}
is a family of pairwise disjoint embedding

;

•

f_{i}
is a smooth, odd,
2\pi

•

t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The resulting perturbed character variety will only depend on the embeddings
D^{\prime}_{i}
up to isotopy class. Thus, it is often convenient to specify the embedding
D^{\prime}_{i}
with an embedding of a circle
D_{i}:S^{1}\to X\setminus T
along with a framing (a nowhere zero choice of normal section).
Each
D_{i}
is called a
perturbation curve
. Let
\mu_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}
be a choice of meridian and longitude of
D_{i}
with respect to the specified framing.
f_{i}
is called the
perturbation function
for
D_{i}
and
t_{i}
is called the
perturbation parameter
.

Definition 2.23
.

Let
\pi=\{(D_{i},f_{i},t_{i})\}
be the perturbation data for a tangle
(X,T)
.
Following
[
16
]
*Lemma 61, the

is given by the subspace of

satisfying the following

(2.5.1)

\text{If }\rho(\lambda_{i})=e^{u_{i}Q_{i}}\text{ for some }u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }Q_{i}\in C(\textbf{i})\text{, then }\rho(\mu_{i})=e^{t_{i}f_{i}(u_{i})Q_{i}}

Remark
.

For any representation satisfying this condition,
\rho(\mu_{i})
and
\rho(\lambda_{i})
commute.

2.7.
Gluing Characters

Finding the character variety of a complicated space can be achieved using cut and paste methods to decompose the space into simpler pieces and gluing the character varieties of these simpler spaces together. However, there may be many ways to glue two characters together depending on their stabilizers. Applying
[
4
]
*Lemma 6.1 to tangles makes this statement more precise:

Lemma 2.32
.

Let
(X,T)=(X_{1},T_{1})\cup_{(\Sigma,S)}(X_{2},T_{2})
. Let
\pi_{i}
be perturbation data for
(X_{i},T_{i})
. Then there is a surjection

f:R_{\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2}}(X,T)\to R_{\pi_{1}}(X_{1},T_{1})\times_{R(\Sigma,S)}R_{\pi_{2}}(X_{2},T_{2}).

The fiber over
([\rho_{1}],[\rho_{2}])
is homeomorphic to the double coset space

\Stab(\rho_{1})\backslash\Stab(\rho_{1}|_{(\Sigma,S)})/\Stab(\rho_{2}).

These fibers,
\Stab(\rho_{1})\backslash\Stab(\rho_{1}|_{(\Sigma,S)})/\Stab(\rho_{2})
, are called
gluing parameters
(or sometimes
bending parameters
). In the case of
\mathrm{SU(2)}
, representations can have stabilizers isomorphic to one of three subgroups:
\mathrm{SU(2)}
,
\mathrm{U(1)}
, or
\mathbb{Z}/2
. Note that the stabilizer type is conjugation invariant, so any point in an
\mathrm{SU(2)}
character variety has a well defined stabilizer type. For
\rho\in R_{\pi}(X,T)
with
T
non-empty, the stabilizer of
\rho
cannot be
\mathrm{SU(2)}
since there is a traceless element of
\mathrm{SU(2)}
in its image. When
T
is non-empty, an
\mathrm{SU(2)}
representation has stabilizer
\mathrm{U(1)}
if it is abelian, which means that the image is contained in a subgroup of the form
\{e^{\theta Q}\in\mathrm{SU(2)}\mid\theta\in S^{1}\}
for some fixed
Q\in C(\textbf{i})
. In particular, for
\rho\in R_{\pi}(X,T)
with stabilizer
U(1)
and
T
non-empty, each meridian must be sent to
\{\pm Q\}
. If the representation does not satisfy this condition, the stabilizer is
\mathbb{Z}/2
. Following the notation of
[
15
]
, let
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2}
and
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathrm{U(1)}}
be the subspaces of
R_{\pi}(X,T)
with the prescribed stabilizers. So we have a decomposition:
R_{\pi}(X,T)=R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2}\sqcup R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathrm{U(1)}}
.

As an example, recall that
R(S^{2},4)
is the pillowcase
P
. It is not difficult to see that
R(S^{2},4)^{\mathrm{U(1)}}=P^{c}
, the corners of the pillowcase and
R(S^{2},4)^{\mathbb{Z}/2}=P^{*}
, the smooth stratum of the pillowcase.

When restricting a representation to a submanifold, the stabilizer of the restriction is at least as big as the stabilizer of the original representation. Of particular interest is when the representation is restricted to the boundary. Then for a tangle
(X,T)
such that
T\neq\emptyset
and
\partial T\neq\emptyset
, we can decompose its character variety as follows:

R_{\pi}(X,T)=R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/2}\sqcup R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}\sqcup R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathrm{U(1)},\mathrm{U(1)}}

where
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
is the subspace of
R_{\pi}(X,T)
which has stabilizer
\mathbb{Z}/2
, but the restriction to the boundary
(\partial X,\partial T)
has
\mathrm{U(1)}
stabilizer.
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/2}
and
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathrm{U(1)},\mathrm{U(1)}}
are defined analogously.

Definition 2.33
.

For
\rho\in R_{\pi}(X,T)
and
Y\subset X
a submanifold, define

(2.7.1)

\widetilde{\Stab}_{Y}(\rho):=(\Stab(\rho),\Stab(\rho|_{(Y,Y\cap T)}))\text{ and }\widetilde{\Stab}(\rho):=\widetilde{\Stab}_{\partial X}(\rho).

By Lemma
2.32
, the gluing parameters can be non-trivial if
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
are in
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
. For this reason, we are particularly interested in character varieties
R_{\pi}(X,T)
for which
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
is empty. Note that if
(\partial X,\partial T)=(S^{2},4)
, then
R_{\pi}(X,T)^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
is empty if and only if the non-abelian representations map to
P^{*}
.

Lemma 2.34
.

If either
R_{\pi_{1}}(X_{1},T_{1})^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
or
R_{\pi_{2}}(X_{2},T_{2})^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
is empty, then the map

f:R_{\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2}}(X,T)\to R_{\pi_{1}}(X_{1},T_{1})\times_{R(\Sigma,S)}R_{\pi_{2}}(X_{2},T_{2})

as described in Lemma
2.32
is a continuous bijection of compact Hausdorff spaces.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
R_{\pi_{1}}(X_{1},T_{1})^{\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)}}
be empty. As a result,
\Stab(\rho_{1})=\Stab(\rho_{1}|_{(\Sigma,S)})
and so
\Stab(\rho_{1})\backslash\Stab(\rho_{1}|_{(\Sigma,S)})\cong\{I\}
where
I\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
is the identity. Then by Lemma
2.32
,

f^{-1}(([\rho_{1}],[\rho_{2}]))\cong\Stab(\rho_{1})\backslash\Stab(\rho_{1}|_{(\Sigma,S)})/\Stab(\rho_{2})\cong\{I\}/\Stab(\rho_{2})\cong\{I\}

Thus the fiber of
f
over any point is always a point, finishing the lemma.
∎

As a result of this, many results will be simplified if the character varieties involved have no representations with stabilizer
(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathrm{U(1)})
. Fortunately, the following result of Herald and Kirk ensures that this condition can be achieved with holonomy perturbations.

3.2.

Definition 3.9

Remark

There are two useful decompositions of the tangle sum

First there is

Taking this perspective will allow us to compare

Lemma 3.22

N

Proof.

\Stab(\rho|_{T_{1}})=\Stab(\rho|_{T_{2}})=\mathrm{U(1)}

3.20

3.19

Remark
.

In order to condense already lengthy calculations and reduce symbolic clutter, at times we will abuse notation and use the name of elements in

In order to calculate the image of

under a particular representation

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}(a\overline{c}x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}(-e^{-\theta\textbf{k}}x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}(-\cos\theta x+\sin\theta\textbf{k}x)

\displaystyle=-\cos\theta x+\sin\theta\operatorname{Im}(\textbf{k}x)

\displaystyle=-\cos\theta(\sin\alpha\cos\beta\textbf{i}+\sin\alpha\sin\beta\textbf{j}+\cos\alpha\textbf{k})+\sin\theta(-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\textbf{i}+\sin\alpha\cos\beta\textbf{j})

\displaystyle=(-\sin\alpha\cos\beta\cos\theta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\sin\theta)\textbf{i}+(\sin\alpha\cos\beta\sin\theta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\cos\theta)\textbf{j}

\displaystyle\phantom{==}-\cos\alpha\cos\theta\textbf{k}

\displaystyle=-\sin\alpha\cos(\theta-\beta)\textbf{i}+\sin\alpha\sin(\theta-\beta)\textbf{j}-\cos\alpha\cos\theta\textbf{k}

Recall that for
Q\in C(\textbf{i})
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
e^{\nu Q}=\cos\nu+Q\sin\nu
. Thus, in order to calculate
p=e^{t\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)}
, we must rewrite it so the exponent is a scalar times a unit traceless quaternion. Letting

n:=|t\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|

, we can rewrite our equation as
p=e^{n\frac{t\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)}{n}}
where
\frac{t\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)}{n}\in C(\textbf{i})
and
n\in\mathbb{R}
as desired. Then we can calculate
n
for our representation
\rho=\Gamma(\gamma,\theta,\alpha,\beta)
:

(4.1.3)

\displaystyle n:=|t\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{\sin^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}(\theta-\beta)+\sin^{2}\alpha\sin^{2}(\theta-\beta)+\cos^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}\theta}

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{(1-\cos^{2}\alpha)+\cos^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}\theta}

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}\alpha(\cos^{2}\theta-1)}

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\alpha\sin^{2}\theta}

Note that if
t>0
, then
0\leq n\leq t
. Putting these calculations together, we can find the image of
p
under
\rho=\Gamma(\gamma,\theta,\alpha,\beta)
.

p=\cos n+\sinc(n)t[-\sin\alpha\cos(\theta-\beta)\textbf{i}+\sin\alpha\sin(\theta-\beta)\textbf{j}-\cos\alpha\cos\theta\textbf{k}]

where

\sinc(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sin n}{n}&n\neq 0\\
1&n=0\end{cases}

From this, we can calculate

(4.1.4)

\displaystyle\overline{pa}p=

\displaystyle[-\sinc(n)t\sin\alpha\cos(\beta-\theta)-\cos n\textbf{i}+\sinc(n)t\cos\alpha\cos\theta\textbf{j}-\sinc(n)t\sin\alpha\sin(\theta-\beta)\textbf{k}]p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}(\beta-\theta)-\cos^{2}n]\textbf{i}+

\displaystyle 2[\cos n\sinc(n)t\cos\alpha\cos\theta-\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha\sin(\theta-\beta)\cos(\beta-\theta)]\textbf{j}+

\displaystyle 2[-\cos n\sinc(n)t\sin\alpha\sin(\theta-\beta)+\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cos\theta\cos(\beta-\theta)]\textbf{k}.

Plugging this into Equation
4.1.2
gives

(4.1.5)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(\gamma,\theta,\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\cos\gamma\left[2\cos n\sinc(n)t\left[\sin^{2}\alpha\sin\beta\sin(\theta-\beta)-\cos^{2}\alpha\cos\theta\right]+\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{\cos\gamma[[}\left.2\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\beta\cos\alpha\sin\theta\cos(\beta-\theta)\right]+

\displaystyle\sin\gamma\left[\cos\alpha\cos^{2}n-2\cos n\sinc(n)t\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\beta\sin(\theta-\beta)+\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{\sin\gamma[[}\left.\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\cos\alpha\sin^{2}\alpha\cos(\beta-\theta)\cos(\beta+\theta)\right]

We can break this complicated expression up into more manageable pieces by defining:

(4.1.6)

\displaystyle F(t,\theta,\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle 2\cos n\sinc(n)t\left[\sin^{2}\alpha\sin\beta\sin(\theta-\beta)-\cos^{2}\alpha\cos\theta\right]+

\displaystyle 2\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\beta\cos\alpha\sin\theta\cos(\beta-\theta)

(4.1.7)

\displaystyle G(t,\theta,\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\cos\alpha\cos^{2}n-2\cos n\sinc(n)t\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\beta\sin(\theta-\beta)+

\displaystyle\sinc^{2}(n)t^{2}\cos\alpha\sin^{2}\alpha\cos(\beta-\theta)\cos(\beta+\theta)

so that now

\Phi_{t}(\gamma,\theta,\alpha,\beta)=\cos\gamma F(t,\theta,\alpha,\beta)+\sin\gamma G(t,\theta,\alpha,\beta).

\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\,\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)&-1&2\,\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)\\

T
in the boundary of

b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are the bounding cochains of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
satisfying Conjecture
5.2
. Since we are going to add the earring to
T_{1}
, Lemma
5.3
implies that
b_{1}=0

HF((R^{\natural}(T_{1}),0),(R_{D}(T_{2}),b_{2}))=I^{\natural}(K)
. Calculating the reduced Khovanov homology and Alexander polynomials for
K
gives the bounds
l(K)=u(K)=7
and thus
\rank(I^{\natural}(K))=7
. So to prove the theorem we need to show that
b_{2}\neq 0
, which can be done by showing
HF((R^{\natural}(T_{1}),0),(R_{D_{t}}(T_{2}),0))\neq 7
. To do this, we first compute the images of
R^{\natural}(T_{1})
and
R_{D_{t}}(T_{2})
in the pillowcase using Theorems
3.19
and
4.22
, which is shown in Figure

\rank(CF((R^{\natural}(T_{1}),0),(R_{D}(T_{2}),0)))=9
. Next we must identify all immersed bigons which give the differentials of the chain complex. There are two such bigons which are shown in Figure

. Note that the vertices of these bigons are distinct, so taking the homology of our chain complex gives
\rank(HF_{1}(K))=HF((R^{\natural}(T_{1}),0),(R_{D}(T_{2}),0))=5
. Thus,
b_{2}\neq 0
