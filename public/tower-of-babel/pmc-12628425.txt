Seroprevalence and genetic characterization of Toxoplasma gondii in hunted wild boars (Sus scrofa) from Croatia
Authors: Lea Grbavac, Filip Dámek, Sandra Thoumire, Aurélien Mercier, Karine Passebosc-Faure, Nika Konstantinović, Marta Kiš, Željko Mihaljević, Tatjana Živičnjak, Radu Blaga, Delphine Le Roux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractToxoplasmosis is a food- and waterborne zoonosis of a great importance, ranked as the fourth most important foodborne parasitosis in the world. The objective of this study was to determine the seroprevalence and molecular prevalence of Toxoplasma gondii in wild boar (Sus scrofa) and to identify genotypes circulating in Croatia. A total of 103 wild boars from four hunting areas in Croatia were screened. Cardiac fluid samples were tested for anti-T. gondii antibodies by MAT, while heart samples were tested for T. gondii DNA using qPCR. The seroprevalence in the cardiac fluid reached 54.4%, while 19.4% of the heart samples were positive by qPCR. The highest seroprevalence was detected in the hunting ground Visocica with 95.6%. Partial microsatellite genotyping was achieved for 2/20 qPCR-positive heart samples suggesting type II strain of T. gondii. Relatively high seroprevalence and detection of T. gondii DNA in tested samples highlights the risk of human infection through consumption of undercooked meat. Further studies focusing on parasite detection in wild boar meat, especially across different climates, are needed to understand regional differences. Expanding research to other wildlife species is also important to clarify their role in the spread of T. gondii.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-025-08590-1.Keywords: Toxoplasma gondii, Wild boar, Foodborne zoonosis, MAT, Seroprevalence, QPCR

Introduction

Toxoplasmosis is a food- and waterborne zoonosis of a great importance, ranked as the fourth most important foodborne parasitosis in the world (FAO, WHO 2014). It is caused by an obligate intracellular parasite, Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii), capable of infecting all warm-blooded animals and humans as intermediate hosts, while domestic cats and wild felids serve as its definitive hosts (Dubey 2009). Although the infection with T. gondii in humans is mostly asymptomatic or benign, it represents a major concern for vulnerable groups, particularly the developing fetus in congenital cases and immunocompromised patients, in whom toxoplasmosis may result in severe and sometimes life-threatening manifestations, either following primary infection or reactivation (Sobanski et al. 2013; Abbas et al. 2020; Lima et al. 2021; Dubey et al. 2021). It is estimated that about one-third of the world’s population is chronically infected with T. gondii, supported by the finding of the reported global seroprevalence in pregnant women of 33.8% (Rostami et al. 2020). Insufficiently heat-treated tissue of animals containing viable tissue cysts of T. gondii, and food and water contaminated with sporulated oocysts are considered the most common sources of infection for humans (Almeria and Dubey 2021). Data from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) indicate that meat infections account for approximately 60% of all T.gondii infections in humans in Europe, suggesting that the main sources of infection are insufficiently heat-treated pork, beef and lamb (Koutsoumanis et al. 2018).

The numbers of wild boar (Sus scrofa) soared in recent decades, becoming one of the most common ungulate in Europe, and a popular hunted game species (Vicente et al., 2019, Massei et al. 2015). Similarly, in Croatia, an increase in the wild boar population has been observed over the last two decades, accompanied by a continuous annual increase in the number of hunted animals (Gavran et al. 2019). Wild boar hunting has a long tradition in Croatia and has recently become an attractive activity for tourists as well. Game meat, including wild boar meat is considered a delicacy in many European countries, mainly due to its nutritional value and culinary appeal (Niewiadomska et al. 2020; Ciobanu et al. 2022; Sampels et al. 2023). Meanwhile, wild boar meat is recognized as a relevant public health problem when the meat is infected with viable T. gondii (EFSA, 2007, Rostami et al. 2017).

A recent meta-analysis of T. gondii seroprevalence in wild boar estimated that 26% of the wild boar population in Europe is positive (Rostami et al. 2017). Genotyping studies of wild boar population in various European countries have shown the presence of different T. gondii genotypes (Fernández-Escobar et al. 2022).

Even though numerous studies have been conducted on the presence of T. gondii among wild boar in Europe, studies performed in Croatia are scarce. To date, only one research has been carried out on 92 meat juice samples from wild boar, and T. gondii antibodies were detected in 55.4% of the tested animals (Kiš et al. 2021). The objectives of this study were to determine the seroprevalence of T. gondii in wild boar in Croatia, to detect T. gondii DNA in heart tissue, and to identify genotypes circulating in wild boar population in Croatia. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study providing DNA detection and molecular characterization of T. gondii isolates from wild boar in Croatia.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Wild boar hearts were collected from May 2021 to January 2022 during the regular annual harvest in four hunting areas in Croatia (Fig. 1). Heart samples were recovered from wild boar carcasses from authorised hunt. A total of 103 wild boar hearts were collected and placed in labeled clean plastic bags, frozen at −20 °C, and delivered to the Parasitology and Parasitic Diseases with Clinic Unit, Zagreb, Croatia, where they remained stored at −20 °C until further processing. A set of data from each wild boar (i.e. sex, age group, date of sampling and location) were recorded by the hunters. The animals included in the current study were divided into three groups based on their age: piglets (less than 6 months old), yearlings (6–12 months old) and adults (over 12 months old), as reported by Roqueplo et al. (2017).

Locations of the hunting areas included in the study, with the nearest populated areas visualised, created using the software QGIS (QGIS Geographic Information System, version 3.16.12)

Serological examination

After thawing each heart sample, 500 µl of cardiac fluid was collected from each collection bag using a sterile pipette and transferred into a labeled 1.5 ml Safe-Lock tube (Eppendorf, Austria). Once collected, all cardiac fluid samples were stored at −20 °C, then sent frozen to the BIPAR Joint Research Unit (JRU), Animal Health Laboratory, Anses, Maisons-Alfort, France for serological analysis.

All cardiac fluid samples were analyzed for the presence of anti-T. gondii antibodies using the modified agglutination test (MAT) as described by Villena et al. (2012). Cardiac fluid samples were thawed and centrifuged at 1500 x g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and transferred to a new 1.5 ml Safe-Lock tube (Eppendorf, Austria). Antigen (Rh strain) for this study was provided by the National Reference Centre for Toxoplasmosis in Reims, France (Villena et al. 2012). The specific anti-T. gondii antibody titre in the cardiac fluid samples were tested by using a dilution series ranging from 1:6 to 1:48 in the MAT. Each plate contained both a positive and a negative control. All samples that reacted at a dilution of ≥ 1:6 were considered positive, in accordance with other studies (Djokic et al. 2016; Roqueplo et al. 2017).

Molecular analysis

Digestion of heart samples

All 103 heart samples were cleaned of fat and connective tissue, and fifty grams per heart sample were cut into small pieces before being briefly blended with a handheld blender. Subsequently, the heart samples were digested, using a 1% trypsin (Gibco™ Trypsin 1:250 powder, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) diluted in saline solution (Sodium Chloride 9 mg/ml solution for infusion, Fresenius Kabi, Germany) and placed on a heated shaking plate 37 °C at 200 rpm for 90 min, according to the protocol decribed by Opsteegh et al. (2016). Each digested sample was filtered through gauze into two 50 ml centrifuge tubes (LLG-Centrifuge tubes, LLG Labware, Germany), and centrifuged at + 4 °C at 1800 x g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was washed twice in 15 ml of 0.9% NaCl (Sodium Chloride 9 mg/ml solution for infusion, Fresenius Kabi, Germany) before combining both pellets into a single centrifuge tube. An aliquote equal to approximately 1 ml of the pellet was transferred using a sterile pippette into a sterile 1.5 ml Safe-Lock tube (Eppendorf, Austria), frozen at −20 °C, and sent to BIPAR JRU, Animal Health Laboratory, Anses, Maisons-Alfort, France for further processing.

DNA extraction

The DNA was extracted from 80 mg of heart pellet using a commercial kit (NucleoSpin DNA RapidLyse; Macherey-Nagel, Germany). Following the DNA extraction step, all DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until assayed. Each extraction procedure included one positive (meat spiked with T. gondii tachyzoites), one negative control (meat confirmed negative for T. gondii) and blank control (sterile distilled water).

Quantitative PCR

For detection and quantification of T. gondii, a multiplex quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay was performed, targeting the B1 gene and 529 RE, using primers and probes from Homan et al. (2000) and Lass et al. (2012), respectively. In addition, a previously described internal amplification control (IAC) (Slana et al. 2008) was used for monitoring the potential inhibition of PCR reactions.

Amplification and fluorescence detection were performed in a 96-well-plate setting using a LightCycler 480Real-Time PCR instrument (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Each twenty-five microlitres of qPCR reaction mixture contained 12.5 µl of 2X Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara Bio Europe, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, France), 0.42 µM of each primer (TgonF, TgonR, Tox-9 F, Tox-11R, IACF and IACR), 0.25 µM of each probe (TgonProbe, Tox-TP1 and IACProbe), 10 µl of template DNA, 0.06 µl of IAC DNA and Milli-Q® water (Table 1). The reaction mixture was subjected to initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 45 amplification cycles consisting of a denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 s, and a combined annealing and extension step at 60 °C for 40 s. Following the DNA amplification, the samples were cooled down to 40 °C. Fluorescence signal was measured at the end of each extension step at 533–580 nm for Tox-TP1 probe, 465–510 nm for TgonProbe and 618–660 nm for IACProbe. Each sample was tested in triplicate, including positive and negative control and distilled water on each plate. Samples with a Cp value ≤ 40 for both targets or only one that showed a smooth exponential-amplification curve were considered positive, while samples without a visible exponential amplification curve but with a positive IAC-PCR were scored negative. For the samples without a visible IAC-PCR signal, DNA extraction was repeated as follows - the extracted DNA was purified using ethanol precipitation as described by (Zeugin and Hartley 1985), resuspended in 50 µl of Milli-Q water and retested by qPCR.

For quantification and evaluation of the efficiency of the assay, a standard curve was prepared using a 10-fold serial dilutions of T. gondii DNA extracted from known numbers of oocysts of type II strain ranging from 1 × 103 to 1 × 10− 1.

Samples which tested positive by qPCR were visualised on an agarose (SeaKem® LE Agarose, Lonza, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) gel (3%, 75 V, 6 h) containing ethidium bromide (Ethidium Bromide, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), using a UV transilluminator to confirm the amplicon sizes (Online resource 1).

Genotyping of T. gondii

All positive samples were sent for genotyping to the Epidemiology – Strains Pole of the National Reference Centre for Toxoplasmosis (CNR) in Limoges, France. They were genotyped using microsatellite typing method in a single multiplex PCR assays described by Ajzenberg et al. (2010) and using the guidelines provided by Joeres et al. (2024). Among the 15 loci analyzed, eight were “typing” markers (TUB2, W35, TgM-A, B18, B17, M33, IV.1, XI.1) showing low levels of polymorphism and minimal intra-lineage variation, whereas the remaining seven were highly polymorphic “fingerprinting” markers with substantial intra-lineage diversity (M48, M102, N83, N82, AA, N61, N60). Simplex PCRs were carried out using the same procedure when the full 15 microsatellite genotyping was incomplete. For simplex PCRs the annealing temperature(s) specific to each microsatellite marker’s primer pair was used, as previously described (Ajzenberg et al. 2010). In addition, the volume corresponding to the 14 pairs of microsatellite marker primers not added to the simplex PCR master mix was replaced with an equivalent volume of distilled water. The Toxoplasma reference strain ME49 (Type II) was used as positive control for allele size calibration. Distilled water (no DNA template) was used as the negative control.

Statistical analysis

The overall seroprevalence of the specific anti-T. gondii antibodies was calculated as the percentage of positive samples among all 103 tested animals. The significance of differences in prevalence among the tested animals in terms of sex (male, female), age (three categories: piglets, yearlings, adults), and location (four hunting grounds) was assessed using a chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test. Statistical data analysis was performed using the software program STATA 13.1 (StataCorp. 2016. Stata Statistical Software: Release 13.1, College Station, Texas, USA) and the results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. A map of Croatia was created using the software QGIS (QGIS Geographic Information System, version 3.16.12) to visualise hunting grounds (Fig. 1).

Results

In this study, the presence of T. gondii IgG antibodies was investigated in cardiac fluid samples using the MAT. The overall seroprevalence in these samples was found to be 54.4% (56/103, 95% CI: 44.7–63.8%). Anti-T. gondii antibodies were detected in 52.7% (29/55, 95% CI: 28.8–66.3%) female boars, while a seroprevalence of 56.2% (27/48, 95% CI: 41.2–70.5%) was found in males. Seroprevalences by age were as follows: 45.9% (11/24, 95% CI: 25.6–67.2%) in piglets, 58.1% (25/43, 95% CI: 42.1–73.0%) in yearlings, and 55.6% (20/36, 95% CI: 38.1–72.1%) in adults. A statistically significant difference was observed only between the seroprevalence rates of T. gondii from the four hunting areas (p = 0.001). The highest seroprevalence was recorded in the hunting ground Visočica with 95.6% (22/23, 95% CI: 78.0–99.9.0.9%), while in other hunting areas it ranged from 38.9% (7/18, 95% CI: 17.3–64.2%) in Novo Glinsko Selo, 43.3% (13/30, 95% CI: 25.5–62.6%) in Spacva to 43.7% (14/32, 95% CI: 26.4–62.3%) in Sumbar (Table 2). A majority of animals showed titres between 1/24 and 1/48 (Table 3). Real-time PCR analysis of wild boar hearts revealed the presence of T. gondii in 19.4% (20/103; 95% CI: 12.3–28.4%) samples. No statistical difference was found between molecular prevalence and sex, age and hunting grounds (Table 2). An inhibition was detected in two samples. For these two samples, the DNA extraction, ethanol precipitation, and qPCR were repeated and one out of the two, showed a positive result. Partial genotyping was achieved for 2/20 qPCR-positive heart samples (Online Resource 2). Of the two genotyped samples, seven markers were successfuly amplified in one sample, and five markers in the other, including four typing markers (B18, M33, TgM-A and W35) for these two individuals. Based on these last four markers the two partially genotyped samples could correspond to strains of T. gondii belonging to the type II lineage (Table 4).

Discussion

In the present study, a high presence of IgG antibodies against T. gondii was detected in cardiac fluid samples of wild boar from four hunting grounds in Croatia using the MAT (54.4%, 56/103). The same seroprevalence (55.4%) was previously revealed by ELISA in the meat juice samples of wild boar diaphragms from Karlovac County, Croatia (Kiš et al. 2021). These results are in agreement with seroprevalence rates reported in other European countries using different serological tests, including Romania (56.6%, ELISA) (Grema et al. 2015), France (55%, MAT) (Richomme et al. 2010), Italy (53.1%, ELISA) (Villa et al. 2023), Sweden (50%, ELISA) (Wallander et al. 2014) and Poland (48%, ELISA) (Puchalska et al. 2021). The highest seroprevalence in wild boar in Europe was reported in Slovenia (62%, ELISA) (Bandelj et al. 2021), while significantly lower rates were reported in Switzerland (6.7%, ELISA) (Berger-Schoch et al. 2011) and Ukraine (7.7%, ELISA) (Galat et al. 2024). These variations in seroprevalence based on the detection of IgG antibodies against T. gondii in wild boar across Europe can mainly be explained by the use of different serological diagnostic techniques, the type of serological matrix, and the choice of cut-off value.

In our study, MAT was used due to its previously demonstrated higher sensitivity compared to other serological tests in pigs, and as a species-independent test, it is considered a gold standard for detecting antibodies against T. gondii in animals and meat (Forbes et al. 2012; Gamble et al. 2005; Villena et al. 2012). A recent systematic review on the seroprevalence of T. gondii found that cardiac fluid and serum samples are five times less likely to test positive for T. gondii antibodies compared to meat juice (Dámek et al. 2023b). This discrepancy could be due to lower consistent levels of specific antibodies compared to serum or their variations depending on the type of muscle tissue, with the heart being the richest in antibodies compared to other muscles (Wallander et al. 2015; Coelho et al. 2015; Viana et al. 2020). However, due to the lack of studies comparing different sample matrices, definitive conclusions are difficult to draw (Dámek et al. 2023b). In our study, only cardiac fluid was selected as the sample matrix for MAT, due to the technical challenges associated with obtaining blood in an appropriate manner from wild boar carcasses, therefore we can not rule out an underestimation of seroprevalence, despite high antibody titres found in most of MAT positive samples.

Indeed, among seropositive wild boar in this study, the majority (57.1%, 32/56) had high antibody titres (≥ 1:24). This finding contrasts with a study carried out in France on meat juice samples that used the same cut off value (1:6) and where only a small proportion (6.5%, 55/841) of wild boar showed titres at or above this level (Roqueplo et al. 2017). Altogether, what particularly stands out are the high antibody levels (≥ 1:24) found in most piglets. This high titre observed in young animals could be due to recent postnatal horizontal infection, as wild boar piglets begin to consume solid food in addition to their mother’s milk at a very early age, which increases their risk of early parasite exposure (Arnold et al. 2019; Dubey et al. 2020). Another explanation could be colostrally acquired antibodies, but these usually only persist for the first 1–3 weeks of life and decrease shortly after weaning, thus making them unlikely to be the cause of the high titres observed in this study (García-Bocanegra et al. 2010). Transplacental transmission of T. gondii could constitute another probability, but since it occurs rarely, it is considered highly unlikely (Calero-Bernal et al. 2013; Dubey et al. 2020). Future studies involving a larger sample of wild boar piglets may help clarify the factors contributing to the high antibody titres we observed at a young age.

In the present study no correlation was found between antibody titre, seroprevalence, and age. This finding is consistent with some studies that found no significant age-related differences in T. gondii seroprevalence in wild boar, challenging the conventional assumption of lifelong antibody persistance and T. gondii infection in hosts (Opsteegh et al. 2011; Jokelainen et al. 2015; Rentería-Solís et al. 2024). On the contrary, several studies have reported an association between seropositivity and the age of wild boars (Rostami et al. 2017; Bandelj et al. 2021; Lizana et al. 2021; Sini et al. 2024; Galat et al. 2024). Opsteegh et al. (2011) suggested the possibility that certain intermediate hosts may eliminate the infection over time, indicating that tissue cysts may not endure throughout the host’s lifetime. In our study, the lack of a correlation between seroprevalence and age in wild boars might be ascribed to the limited sample size (103 animals), also because wild boars are more prone to infection with T. gondii due to their natural habitat than domestic pigs, in which a positive correlation between serostatus and age is typically observed (Olsen et al. 2020; Castillo-Cuenca et al. 2021; Betić et al. 2022). Extensive seroprevalence research that includes all age categories of wild boars may clarify parasite dynamics in wildlife and improve insights into the immune response of juvenile animals following infection.

The hypothesis of this study was that wild boar would have a similar probability of exposure to T. gondii in all hunting areas due to their comparable distance from nearby settlements, where domestic cats are likely to have similar opportunity to contaminate soil and water with oocysts, which serve as main sources of infection for wild boar (Fig. 1). However, a statistically significant difference in prevalence between hunting grounds was observed (p = 0.001). Interestingly, the Visocica hunting ground had an exceptionally high seroprevalence (95.6%). One possible explanation could be the high number of seropositive adult wild boar sampled in this area, despite no consistent age-related differences in seroprevalence being observed across the study. Another possible explanation could be the geographical location of the Visocica hunting ground, which is situated at higher altitude than the other hunting grounds in this study, and is partly located on the Velebit mountain, characterized by a hilly-montane terrain with mild temperatures and wetter climate. These conditions favour the sporulation and survival of T. gondii oocysts throughout the year. Another possible reason for high seroprevalence in wild boar in Visocica could be the higher soil contamination with T. gondii oocysts due to the presence of wild cats (Felis sylvestris) and lynxes (Lynx lynx), as the Velebit mountain is their natural habitat (Shapiro et al. 2019). All these factors together could contribute to a higher seroprevalence of T. gondii in wild boars in this particular hunting ground.

The molecular detection rate of T. gondii in wild boar heart tissue in our study was 19.4% (20/103). This finding indicates a lower prevalence compared to several other European studies, such as those conducted in Sardinia by Sini et al. (2024), which reported a rate of 37.1%, and in Southern Italy by Santoro et al. (2019) and by Sgroi et al. (2020), with rates of 28.3% and 22.6%, respectively. Conversely, our detection rate was higher than the 12% reported by Stollberg et al. (2021) in Germany. These differences highlight the geographical heterogeneity of T. gondii prevalence among wild boar populations, likely influenced by factors such as differences in host (wild boar population) density across regions, environmental contamination and climatic conditions. In addition, the irregular distribution of tissue cysts within the host, variations in sample sizes, the amount of tissue used for analysis, the use of different digestion methods and the different molecular detection techniques used may influence detection sensitivity.

Majority of seropositive samples (41/56) in this study tested negative by qPCR, probably due to the limited amount of heart tissue used for testing. The 50 g subjected to digestion often did not represent the entire heart, leaving some of the heart muscle unprocessed and possibly reducing chance of detecting T. gondii DNA. In addition, DNA extraction was not performed on the whole pellet, which may have further decreased the probability of parasite detection. Conversely, five samples in our study were negative by MAT but positive by qPCR, which may be attributed to the lack of sensitivity of MAT in cases of recent infection or low parasitic load in which low antibody concentration could be undetected. Furthermore, the type of the matrix used in this study could also provide a good explanation, as cardiac fluid contain lower antibody concentration compared to serum and may be under the threshold of MAT sensitivity (Wallander et al. 2015; Djokic et al. 2016). Very high Cp-values in four of these five samples (Online Resource 1) indicate a low DNA content and possibly a low parasite load, which could support the explanation of generally low antibody concentration as suggested in a study conducted on experimentally infected sheep (Opsteegh et al. 2010). A similar positive correlation between antibody level and the presence of T. gondii DNA was also observed in a study on feral raccoons in Germany (Engel et al. 2023). In wild boar, one study reported a correlation between seropositivity and parasite detection (Stollberg et al. 2021), while other studies found no such agreement (Slany et al. 2016; Sini et al. 2024). These differences in the level of concordance between serostatus and T. gondii DNA detection may be due to different serological and molecular detection methods applied. However, our study was not specifically designed to compare direct and indirect methods of T. gondii detection.

Genotyping of T. gondii was attempted in twenty samples of wild boar hearts, but was only successful in two samples, probably due to low DNA concentrations as indicated by the high Cp-values (Online Resource 1). Partial genotyping of two samples suggests the circulation of strains belonging to the type II lineage among Croatian wild boars, which are consistent with the majority of strains previously found in wild boars from France and Italy (Richomme et al. 2009; Papini et al. 2018; Gazzonis et al. 2018) and, more generally, with the predominant lineage circulating in Europe in domestic and wild animals and humans (Galal et al. 2019; Fernández-Escobar et al. 2022). Although this interpretation is reasonable in the context of European Toxoplasma diversity, caution is warranted since only four of the eight typing microsatellite markers were successfully amplified. The strains could alternatively correspond to recombinant genotypes, such as type II/III hybrids, which have previously been reported in both wildlife and the human population in Serbia. Notably, the type III lineage is the second most prevalent in Europe after type II (Fernández-Escobar et al. 2022).

Wild boar, apart from ingesting soil and water contaminated with sporulated oocyst from felid feces, can also acquire T. gondii by ingesting infected rodents or other small animals, driven by their natural scavenging and predatory instincts. This behaviour exposes wild boars to a variety of T. gondii strains circulating within the wildlife ecosystem. Consequently, wild boars may serve as indicators for the contamination of the environment with T. gondii oocysts and might also provide a good overview of the genetic diversity of strains circulating in their habitat.

Wild boar hunting has a long tradition in Croatia, and the growing wild population has led to an increase in the number of animals hunted. In the 2021/2022 hunting season, the number of wild boar hunted increased by 31.7% (52 381 animals) compared to 2020/2021 season (39 778 animals), according to data from the Croatian Ministry of Agriculture. The consumption of insufficiently heat-treated wild boar meat may pose a risk of transmission of T. gondii to humans. The data presented in this study and the recent increase in wild boar hunting suggest that the risk of T. gondii transmission to humans via consumption of wild boar meat might have increased, although reliable data on the different ways of wild boar meat preparation and consumption is still lacking. It is well known that T. gondii tissue cysts do not survive freezing below − 12 °C (Kuticic and Wikerhauser 1996), cooking or roasting (Dubey et al., 1990; El-Nawawi et al. 2008). More recent studies have shown that they are also susceptible to curing salt, even in homemade preparations, and do not withstand the fermentation process (Hill et al. 2018; Fredericks et al. 2019; Dámek et al. 2023a). These observations indicate that the risk of infection from consuming such products is considered low, but the traditional practice of tasting the raw meat mixture after seasoning in home production of cured wild boar meat products poses a considerable risk of T. gondii infection for humans and should be considered.

Conclusions

This study identified a relatively high seroprevalence of T. gondii in wild boar in Croatia, highlighting the potential risk of human infection through the consumption of insufficiently heat-treated meat or the tasting of raw sausage mixture during traditional cured meat production. To accurately assess the risk, future investigations focusing on serology and direct detection of the parasite in wild boar meat intended for human consumption are necessary. Variations in seroprevalence observed in one hunting ground, possibly linked to climatic differences, calls for broader geographic sampling. While the findings suggest possible circulation of the type II strain in Croatia’s sylvatic environment, the limited genotyping success due to low DNA concentrations highlights the need for future studies with larger sample sizes to improve parasite detection and confirm the presence of the type II strain. Investigations in other wildlife species, such as carnivores, are also needed to elucidate their role in the dynamics of T. gondii within Croatian wildlife.
