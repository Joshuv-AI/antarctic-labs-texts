Local electrostatics governs tryptophan oxidation and enables rational stability engineering in antibodies
Authors: Sunidhi Lenka, Shrenik Mehta, Nicole Stephens, Franziska Seeger, Wei-Ching Liang, Andrew Watkins, Jonathan Zarzar, Negar Jafari, Rhyan Puno, Caleigh Azumaya, Ryan Kelly, Shuang Wu, Cecilia Chiu, Meredith Hazen, Yan Wu, Flaviyan Jerome Irudayanathan, Sreedhara Alavattam, Robert Kelley, Saeed Izadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOxidation of tryptophan (Trp) residues in therapeutic antibody complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) can impair binding affinity, stability, and developability, yet the molecular determinants of site-specific susceptibility remain incompletely understood. Here, we present a structure-informed machine learning framework trained on Trp oxidation profiles from 187 monoclonal antibodies to identify the physicochemical drivers of oxidation risk. Beyond solvent accessibility, the dominant known predictor, we identify local residue-level electrostatic potential (Epot) as a strong independent modulator: negatively charged microenvironments markedly increase oxidation susceptibility. A two-parameter model combining only solvent accessibility and Epot achieves 79% classification accuracy, approaching the 84% performance of full-feature ensemble models, and correctly classifies all 10 CDR Trp sites in a blind validation panel of eight clinical-stage IgG1 antibodies. Leveraging the long-range nature of electrostatic effects, we show that targeted distal charge-altering mutations, without direct modification of the oxidation-prone Trp, reduce oxidation rates by approximately 50% in four of five re-engineered antibodies, with binding affinity preserved in two. In a clinically relevant anti-CD33 antibody where the critical CDR Trp oxidizes at 97% and Trp-to-Phe substitution abolishes binding, iterative electrostatic optimization yielded variants with up to ~50% oxidation reduction, including one single-point-mutation variant achieving 27% oxidation reduction at only 1.4-fold affinity cost, substantially outperforming direct Trp substitution. Redox replica-exchange molecular dynamics simulations provide mechanistic support, revealing an 88 mV increase in Trp reduction potential upon distal charge-reversing mutations. Together, these results establish local electrostatics as a predictive, tunable, and mechanistically grounded handle for rational antibody engineering, and provide a practical framework for simultaneously optimizing oxidation stability and antigen-binding function.KEYWORDS: Machine learning, monoclonal antibodies, multi-property optimization, oxidation

Introduction

Oxidation is a major degradation pathway in proteins with far-reaching implications in physiological functions and pathological conditions.1–9 Oxidative damage is thought to contribute to aging2 and the development of diseases such as Alzheimer’s,3,4 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,5 and cataracts by increasing protein susceptibility to proteolysis6,7 and reducing enzymatic activity.8,9 In therapeutic antibodies, oxidative damage can compromise structural integrity and function, potentially leading to adverse outcomes including loss of binding affinity,10–14 aggregation,13,15,16 fragmentation,17,18 color change of drug substance,19,20 and altered immunogenicity.21 Understanding the determinants and underlying mechanisms of oxidation and the associated risks is critical for assessing its role in disease progression and ensuring the long-term chemical and physical stability of therapeutic antibodies.22–24

Among the amino acids prone to oxidation, methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) stand out as particularly susceptible in antibody therapeutics.13,23,25,26 Met oxidation has been extensively studied, with its susceptibility closely tied to solvent-accessible surface area (SASA).27–29 In contrast, Trp oxidation is less well understood yet arguably more consequential in therapeutic contexts12,14,25 for two key reasons. First, Trp oxidation involves multiple reaction pathways and produces a variety of oxidative products, including oxindolealanine, hydroxytryptophan, kynurenine, and N-formylkynurenine (Figure 1a). This complexity makes predicting and characterizing Trp oxidation far more challenging.14,19 Second, Trp plays a critical role in protein–protein and hydrophobic core interactions,30–32 making it difficult to substitute without significantly compromising binding. Trp’s side chain features a highly accessible hydrophobic surface, a strong potential for cation–π interactions, and an indole N-H group capable of hydrogen bonding. In fact, the unique properties make Trp more prevalent,33 and highly surface exposed,25,34 in the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of antibodies compared to other proteins.

Computational models ranging from quantum mechanical calculations35 to molecular dynamics (MD) simulations29,36 and machine learning (ML) approaches27,37 have been used to probe the mechanisms of protein oxidation and to develop predictive models. Among the key factors influencing oxidation susceptibility, the SASA of Trp and Met has been consistently identified as a critical determinant.35–40 Structural parameters such as dihedral angles,37 torsional properties,37 loop flexibility,35 and proximity to aromatic residues37,41,42 have also been investigated, though to a lesser extent.

Design strategies to mitigate or prevent Trp oxidation are as critical as predictive efforts, but they remain relatively limited. One common approach involves substituting oxidation-prone Trp residues with other aromatic amino acids, such as phenylalanine or tyrosine.12,43 Nevertheless, due to the distinct physicochemical properties of Trp, its removal is often impractical, as it can adversely affect antigen binding44,45 and specificity.46 Therefore, rational engineering strategies that preserve Trp within the binding site while mitigating its susceptibility to oxidation are highly desirable.

Here, we use experimentally measured CDR Trp oxidation rates from 187 mAbs to train structure-informed ML models and derive actionable design rules. We confirm SASA as a primary driver of oxidation risk and identify local residue-level electrostatic potential (Epot) as a second, independent determinant. We show that a simple two-parameter model using only SASA and local electrostatics captures much of the predictive power of high-dimensional ML models and generalizes to a blind panel of clinical-stage antibodies. Finally, we demonstrate that local electrostatics can be modulated through distal charge-altering mutations to reduce Trp oxidation while retaining antigen binding, and we provide mechanistic support from redox replica-exchange MD simulations. Together, these results establish local electrostatics as an underappreciated but mechanistically grounded and practically actionable determinant of Trp oxidation risk in therapeutic antibodies.

Results

Distribution and frequency of CDR tryptophan oxidation

We analyzed 264 CDR Trp residues across 187 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) exposed to oxidative stress. Using a 35% oxidation threshold,38 55 residues (21%) were classified as susceptible. Oxidation frequency varied by loop (Figure 1b): H3 showed the highest rate (40%), while L1 and L2 showed the lowest (10%). Given H3‘s central role in antigen binding,47–49 this enrichment is particularly noteworthy and underscores the potential impact of Trp oxidation on antibody stability and function.

Structural determinants of tryptophan oxidation susceptibility

Feature selection was guided by prior work on protein oxidation35–42 and supplemented with hypotheses specific to Trp. Structural descriptors included solvent exposure (e.g., SASA), flexibility, local hydrophobicity (e.g., residue-level spatial aggregation propensity (SAP) scores50), backbone and side-chain geometry, and residue-level Epot,51 calculated via the Poisson-Boltzmann equation52 (Figure 1c). A full list of features is provided in Supplemental Table S1. Features were averaged over MD trajectories starting from Fab models. Hierarchical clustering revealed 11 feature clusters falling into six categories: SASA, electrostatics, hydrophobicity, flexibility, backbone geometry, and dihedral angles (Figure 2a).

Using Random Forest (RF) feature importance analysis and SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values (Figure 2b), we identified key structural and environmental features associated with Trp oxidation susceptibility. As expected, solvent accessibility, captured by features such as n_res_water_5, SASA_Loop, and SASA_res, emerged as the most influential factor. This is consistent with prior findings, as Trp residues buried within the protein structure are shielded from 2,2’-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH)-derived radicals and therefore less prone to oxidation.

Notably, Epot was the second most important feature category, despite being relatively unexplored in the context of Trp oxidation. SHAP analysis showed that more negative Epot features (e.g., Epot_NEG, Epot_SUM) correlate with higher oxidation risk, while positive Epot values are protective. Other features also contributed meaningfully, though to a lesser extent. Local clustering of hydrophobic and aromatic residues, captured by metrics such as nb_seq_score_2 and SAP scores were associated with elevated oxidation susceptibility. Additionally, greater local flexibility correlated with increased oxidation risk. These observations are in line with previous studies highlighting the role of loop dynamics35 and neighboring aromatic interactions37,41,42 in modulating oxidation behavior.

A two-parameter model captures most predictive power of high-dimensional classifiers

To better understand the individual predictive contributions of key features and to reduce model complexity, we evaluated a simplified two-parameter model based on the top-ranked descriptors: SASA and local electrostatics (Epot). We compared the performance of this minimal model against that of full-feature RF and Gradient Boosting (GB) classifiers, which were trained using all 50 features and evaluated via 10-fold cross-validation (Table 1). The high-feature models achieved an accuracy of approximately 84%, with high specificity (96% for RF and 94% for GB), but limited sensitivity (40% and 45%, respectively), reflecting a tendency to underpredict oxidation-prone residues (i.e., a high false negative rate).

We next examined the predictive utility of the top features individually (Figure 2c and Table 1). A correlation analysis between SASA and observed oxidation frequency revealed a positive relationship, indicating that Trp residues with greater solvent exposures are more likely to undergo oxidation. To investigate this further, we categorized Trp residues as either buried or exposed based on their SASA values. Guided by the correlation plot, we empirically defined buried Trp residues as those with SASA ≤ 50 Å2, a threshold consistent with the relative solvent accessibility (RSA) cutoffs of <20–25% reported in prior studies.53

Consistent with expectations, buried Trp residues exhibited a strong protective effect: 96 of 101 were correctly classified as non-oxidized (true negatives). This aligns with the mechanistic understanding that burial limits access to AAPH-derived radicals. However, exposure alone was not sufficient to predict oxidation. Among the 163 exposed Trp sites (SASA > 50 Å2), only 50 were oxidized, corresponding to a specificity of just 46%. This indicates that, while solvent exposure is a necessary condition, additional factors also play a critical role in determining oxidation susceptibility.

The second most important feature, Epot, exhibited a negative correlation with oxidation rate. Specifically, approximately 85% of Trp residues situated in a positively charged environment (Epot > 0) were correctly classified as non-reactive (146 of 171 cases; Figure 2c, Table 1). As a single-feature classifier, Epot outperformed SASA, achieving higher overall accuracy (67% vs. 55%) and greater specificity. This suggests that a locally positive electrostatic environment may offer a protective effect against oxidation. However, Epot showed lower sensitivity than SASA, indicating it is less effective at detecting oxidation-prone residues.

Combining SASA and Epot substantially improved predictive performance over either feature alone. As shown in Figure 2d, 85 of 109 (78%) exposed Trp residues in a positively charged environment fell below the oxidation rate threshold, compared to only 28 of 54 (52%) of exposed sites in a negatively charged environment. The resulting two-feature classification model achieved an overall accuracy of 79%, closely approaching the performance of the full-feature RF and GB models (84%), while using only a fraction of the input complexity (Table 1).

Prospective validation in clinical IgG1 antibodies

To assess the generalizability of our two-feature model beyond the internal dataset, we conducted a blind prediction study on a set of clinical-stage antibodies. We curated a panel of 629 clinical-stage IgG1 molecules from a 2022 snapshot of TheraSAbDab,54 extracting variable domain sequences. For each antibody, static structure models of the Fab domains were generated using DeepAb55 (see Methods). SASA and Epot, the top predictive features identified earlier, were calculated for all Trp residues located within the CDR loops. To guide selection for experimental validation, we divided the plot into four quadrants using SASA = 50 Å2 and Epot = 0 kT/e as empirical thresholds. From each quadrant, representative antibodies were randomly selected, with particular emphasis on candidates predicted to be at high oxidation risk, i.e., those with Trp residues in highly exposed (SASA > 50 Å2) and negatively charged (Epot < 0 kT/e) environments, marked as red points in Figure 3a. The sequences for the 8 selected clinical antibodies are provided in Supplemental Table S2.

A total of eight antibodies, all sharing the same IgG1 constant framework but with distinct variable regions, were expressed and subjected to AAPH oxidative stress under standardized conditions. Trp oxidation levels were then experimentally quantified and compared against our a priori in-silico predictions. The clinical antibodies were further assessed using a charge neutral AAPH analog (Supplemental Figure S1).

Figure 3b summarizes the SASA and Epot values, predicted oxidation risks, and experimentally measured Trp oxidation rates for 10 sites across these eight antibodies. Notably, our model correctly classified all 10 Trp residues. In particular, the four residues with high SASA and negative Epot values exhibited substantial oxidation (51.34% to 93.35%), in line with our predictions. These included MabA (H3 loop), MabB, MabC, and MabD, all of which contained CDR Trp residues predicted to be unstable due to their high exposure and unfavorable electrostatic environment.

Conversely, Trp residues in positively charged or buried environments were oxidation-resistant. For example, MabE and MabF displayed high SASA but were located in regions with positive Epot values, and both showed minimal oxidation. Similarly, MabA contained a second Trp residue with borderline SASA (44.9 Å2) and mildly negative Epot, which also remained stable after AAPH treatment.

These results demonstrate that a simple two-feature model, using only SASA and local electrostatics, can prospectively predict Trp oxidation outcomes in external datasets. The successful blind prediction across this panel of clinical-stage antibodies provides promising evidence for the generalizability of the model within the scope of the tested cases.

Electrostatic modulation via distal point mutations reduces Trp oxidation risk

Given the strong influence of local electrostatics on predicting Trp oxidation risk, we investigated whether modulating the strength and sign of the Epot around susceptible Trp residues through point mutations would affect oxidation rates. To test this, we analyzed a subset of five antibodies with CDR Trp residues within a negative Epot and oxidation rate > 35%. To mitigate the oxidation risk, we introduced point mutations by substituting nearby acidic residues with basic residues. Specifically, single, double and triple D→R/K mutations were applied to shift the local Epot around the Trp side-chain from negative to positive. Note that our designs focused exclusively on removing or mitigating the oxidation risk, without necessarily optimizing or considering the impact on binding affinity.

Table 2 and Figure 4a summarize the calculated electrostatic descriptors (Epot), SASA, and oxidation rates for the original antibodies and their engineered variants. In Mab1, W91 in the L3 loop has highly negative Epot (−1.55 kT/e) and a SASA of 58.38 Å2, resulting in a high oxidation rate of 70.6%. To counteract this, we identified three Asp residues in the H3 loop and mutated them to Arg (D97R, D99R & D101R). This triple mutation shifted the side-chain Epot to a mildly positive value (0.69 kT/e) and reduced SASA (28.5 Å2). The impact on SASA was likely due to increased packing and conformational adjustments. Through these mutations, the oxidation rate decreased by 46%, lowering to 38.1% in the triple variant Mab1.RRR. It should be noted that while Mab1.RRR represents a partial success, achieving a 46% reduction in oxidation rate, the oxidation level (38.1%) remains marginally above the 35% threshold, and the triple mutation incurred a 60-fold affinity cost, illustrating that electrostatic modulation without binding-aware design can yield incomplete outcomes, motivating the dual-optimization strategy applied subsequently to the anti-CD33 antibody (results below).

In Mab2, a similar approach was taken to reduce the oxidation rate of W58 in the H2 loop, initially at 45.6%. Three D→R mutations in the H3 loop were introduced (D100cR, D100dR, D101R), shifting the local Epot significantly from −1.52 kT/e to 1.65 kT/e. Unlike in Mab1, the SASA for this variant remained high (66.04 Å2 compared to 80.53 Å2 for the wild type), and the mutations were positioned at distances ranging from 10.5 Å to 18.6 Å from the Trp site (Figure 4b). Despite the high SASA in the variant Mab2.RRR (Figure 4c), the oxidation rate decreased from 45.6% to 28.3% (below the cutoff).

For Mab3, we introduced a single D98R mutation, which significantly altered the Epot, but had minimal impact on SASA. The reduction in the oxidation rate of W100b in H3 was marginal, with only a ~3% decrease, suggesting that altering electrostatics alone was insufficient in this case. We speculate that the extremely high SASA (close to RSA of 90% or 197 Å2) may be the dominant factor, overshadowing the effects of changes in Epot and limiting the impact on oxidation.

In contrast, W100a in Mab4‘s H3 loop exhibited a pronounced response to electrostatic modulation. A single D100K mutation (Mab4.K) reduced the oxidation rate by more than 50%, from 86.2% to 41.7%. A double mutation (D95R and D101R) nearly eliminated the oxidation risk, lowering the rate to just 1.3%. Notably, this double mutation (Mab4.RR) also increased SASA by 68% compared to the wild type (RSA = 49% or SASA = 107.85 Å2), suggesting that for intermediate RSA values, Epot becomes the dominant factor in oxidation susceptibility. Interestingly, the Mab4.KR double mutation shifted the side-chain Epot to a small negative value (−0.24 kT/e), compared to Mab4 Epot. This modification was still highly effective in lowering the oxidation rate by approximately 80%, reducing it to 16.6% though the oxidation is higher than Mab4.RR where the side chain Epot is positive.

In Mab5, a single D31A mutation reduced the oxidation rate of W33 (H1) from slightly above the threshold (40.7%) to just below it (34.7%). The modest effect of D31A alone suggested that the adjacent Y32 residue partially shielded W33 from electrostatic perturbation. Combining D31A with Y32A amplified the positive shift in local Epot and further reduced oxidation by 54%, to 18.7%, illustrating the synergistic contribution of aromatic clustering and charge modulation consistent with the third-ranked feature cluster in our ML model. Overall, these results confirm that targeted electrostatic mutations can effectively reduce Trp oxidation, with synergistic gains when aromatic shielding is also disrupted.

Introducing Trp into a negative electrostatic environment drives high oxidation

In addition to engineering strategies aimed at mitigating Trp oxidation, we sought to test whether we could intentionally induce oxidation susceptibility by introducing a Trp residue into an unfavorable local electrostatic environment. Specifically, we aimed to validate whether placing a Trp residue within a negatively charged, solvent-exposed surface patch would be sufficient to trigger high oxidation rates, independent of native sequence or structural context.

To test this, we identified residue R100a in the H3 loop of Mab2, a site located at the center of a strongly negative Epot (Figure 4d). We chose Arg as the starting residue due to its structural similarity to Trp in terms of bulky size and SASA, as well as its inherent positive charge, making it an ideal candidate to assess the effect of charge reversal.

We introduced a single point mutation, R100aW, generating the variant Mab2.W (Figure 4e and Table 2). The resulting Trp residue (W100a) had a high solvent-accessible surface area (SASA = 220.81 Å2) and a strongly negative Epot (Epot = −2.22 kT/e). Experimental evaluation of this variant under AAPH-induced oxidative stress revealed an extremely high oxidation rate of 96.8%, confirming that the engineered Trp residue was indeed highly susceptible to oxidation.

This result provides a clean positive control for the entire predictive framework: SASA and local electrostatics are sufficient, independent of native sequence context or structural motifs, to drive Trp oxidation. Critically, the outcome was predicted a priori from Epot and SASA alone, before experimental measurement.

Oxidation-reducing mutations retain binding in half of engineered variants

Given the success of electrostatic point mutations in reducing Trp oxidation, we next evaluated whether these modifications affected antigen binding. Importantly, the original designs were made without any consideration of potential impact on binding affinity; the sole objective was to mitigate oxidation risk by modulating local electrostatics.

Despite this, binding was retained in 4 of 8 engineered variants (50%) (Table 2), a notable outcome consistent with the long-range nature of electrostatic effects, which can be tuned at positions with greater sequence tolerance away from direct contact residues.

Notably, Mab4.K and the Mab5 variants (Mab5.A and Mab5.AA) demonstrated substantial reductions in oxidation while maintaining strong binding to their respective targets. Mab3.R also retained binding affinity, although it showed only marginal reduction in oxidation risk. These results suggest that certain electrostatic mutations, even when applied to CDR-adjacent or framework regions, can be tolerated without compromising antigen recognition.

Redox MD simulations mechanistically link local electrostatics to Trp oxidation propensity

To elucidate the mechanistic basis behind the differential oxidation propensity of Trp residues in distinct electrostatic environments, we performed redox potential MD simulations using the redox replica exchange method.56 Two antibody systems were selected for comparison: Mab4, which contains a Trp residue located in a region of negative Epot, and Mab4.RR, a variant in which two Asp residues are substituted with two Arg residues, resulting in a positively charged environment. This charge reversal leads to a 98% reduction in observed Trp oxidation in Mab4.RR, making it an ideal model system for probing the impact of local electrostatics on redox behavior.

In both systems, the Trp radical was modeled at the nitrogen atom of the indole ring. While radical formation is mechanistically more favored at the C2 or C3 carbon positions, we chose the nitrogen site due to the availability of corresponding electrochemical data and associated free energy changes (ΔG) required to compute reduction potentials. Our goal was not to pinpoint the exact radical species, but to assess whether any Trp radical formation would exhibit a shift in redox potential under different electrostatic conditions. The observed 88 mV difference in standard reduction potential between Mab4 and Mab4.RR corresponds to a roughly 2 kcal/mol change in the equilibrium constant for electron transfer at physiological temperatures (ΔΔG ~ nFΔEº ~ 8.5 kJ/mol, or 3.4 kBT at 310 K), consistent with the 98% reduction in experimentally observed oxidation rate in Mab4.RR and supporting the conclusion that the negatively charged environment in Mab4 meaningfully lowers the energetic barrier to Trp oxidation.

Redox replica exchange MD simulations were conducted by titrating the Trp residue (TRX 100a) over a redox potential range of 900–1250 mV. The fraction of reduced species at each potential was used to determine the standard reduction potential (Eº) of Trp in each system (Figure 5a,b). The results revealed that the Trp in Mab4 exhibited an Eº that was 88 mV lower than in Mab4.RR. Notably, the Eº of Mab4.RR closely matched that of a capped tryptophan control, indicating that the electrostatic environment in Mab4 leads to a significantly more oxidizable Trp residue. This 88 mV difference corresponds to a ~ 1.5 pH unit shift in redox potential (Figure 5b), underscoring the substantial influence of local electrostatics on Trp oxidation susceptibility.

(a) Calculated redox potential (Eº) for Trp100a in Mab4 and its electrostatically engineered variant Mab4.RR. The lower Eº in Mab4 indicates higher susceptibility to oxidation, consistent with its negatively charged local environment. (b) Redox titration curves for Trp100a in Mab4 (blue) and Mab4.RR (red), fitted using the Nernst equation. These findings provide direct evidence that electrostatic modulation can significantly alter the redox potential of Trp residues in antibody frameworks, with potential implications for predicting and mitigating site-specific oxidation risks in therapeutic antibodies.

Dual optimization of Trp stability and binding affinity in an anti-CD33 antibody

Given the promising results from oxidation-reducing variants that retained binding, we next explored whether it was possible to simultaneously optimize both Trp oxidation resistance and antigen-binding affinity. These efforts focused on a clinically relevant antibody targeting CD33, where a critical tryptophan residue (W96) in the CDR H3 loop was identified as highly oxidation-prone and essential for binding (Figure 6).

W96 is situated at the VH-VL interface and plays a central role in antigen recognition. Structural analysis using a Fab-CD33 antigen cryogenic electron microscopy (CryoEM) complex (PDB ID: 9MKG, generated in this work; see Methods and Supplemental Figure S2 a,b) revealed that W96 engages in a cation-π interaction with K52, as well as stabilizing hydrogen bonds with D101 and surrounding residues, including R94 (Figure 6a). This intricate network suggests that both the aromatic nature and precise orientation of W96 are critical for maintaining high-affinity binding. However, W96 is located near a negatively charged surface (Figure 6b), rendering it highly susceptible to oxidation. Under AAPH-induced oxidative stress, W96 exhibited a 97% oxidation rate, which led to a dramatic > 500-fold reduction in binding affinity. A conventional W96F substitution resulted in complete loss of function, with a > 1000-fold decrease in affinity, highlighting the limitations of such traditional mitigation strategies in this case.

To further evaluate the applicability of our model, we pursued a rational dual-optimization strategy aimed at reducing the oxidation risk of W96 while preserving binding. Notably, this optimization was performed without access to the CryoEM Fab-antigen complex at the time, highlighting the generalizability of our approach for early-stage discovery programs where such structures are often unavailable.

The optimization was carried out over three iterative design rounds: 1) Global charge tuning, to increase overall isoelectric point (pI) and improve chemical stability; 2) Local targeting, where residues within 10 Å of W96 were mutated to modulate the local Epot; and 3) Combinatorial refinement, based on binding and expression results from prior rounds.

As shown in Figure 6c, these charge-altering mutations were effective in raising the side-chain Epot of W96, which correlated strongly with reduced oxidation rates. Figure 6d summarizes the top 13 variants, including Epot, oxidation reduction relative to the lead candidate, and their impact on binding (reported as fold change in KD). While some variants exhibited trade-offs between stability and affinity, two emerged as particularly promising.

Variant S11, featuring a single point mutation, achieved a 27.2% reduction in W96 oxidation with only a 1.4-fold reduction in binding affinity. To contextualize this result: W96 oxidizes at 97% in the wild-type lead, and direct W96F substitution abolishes binding with a greater than 1000-fold loss in affinity. Against this baseline, S11 represents a practically meaningful outcome – substantially reduced oxidation liability with near-complete retention of antigen recognition – and illustrates the advantage of electrostatic tuning over direct residue replacement at functionally critical sites. Variant S13, with additional mutations, showed a 49.9% decrease in oxidation at the cost of a 20.7-fold reduction in affinity, still significantly better than the >1000-fold loss observed with W96F. These data demonstrate that it is possible to tune the local electrostatics around a critical Trp residue to reduce its oxidation risk, while retaining functional binding through careful, structure-guided design.

Discussion

Tryptophan oxidation is among the most consequential degradation pathways in therapeutic antibody development, capable of disrupting antigen binding, promoting aggregation, and altering immunogenicity at sites that are frequently essential for function. Yet, the molecular determinants of site-specific oxidation susceptibility have remained incompletely understood, limiting the rational design of oxidation-resistant antibodies.

Across a diverse panel of 187 antibodies, structure-informed ML classifiers confirmed SASA as the primary driver of CDR Trp oxidation and identified local residue-level electrostatics (Epot) as a strong, independent modulator, with negative microenvironments markedly increasing oxidation likelihood. A simplified two-parameter model incorporating only these descriptors achieved 79% classification accuracy, approaching the 84% of full-feature ML models, and correctly classified all 10 CDR Trp sites across eight clinical-stage antibodies in prospective blind testing. Despite substantial variation in sequence, target, and molecular context across this panel, the model’s perfect classification reinforces SASA and Epot as robust, broadly applicable descriptors. We note the validation set, while correctly classified in full, is modest in size and enriched for high-risk cases, and larger prospective studies will be important to establish the full breadth of applicability.

The long-range nature of electrostatic effects provides a clear rationale for mitigating oxidation through targeted point mutations. By introducing one to three charge-altering mutations near oxidation-prone Trp residues, we reduced oxidation rates by an average of ~50% in four of five internal antibodies, while maintaining binding affinity in two of these cases. Notably, several beneficial mutations were located in CDRs distinct from the oxidizing Trp or at framework sites not in direct contact with the residue, underscoring the ability of electrostatics to modulate oxidation risk over nanometer length scales. These results suggest that electrostatic tuning can be deployed in regions with greater sequence tolerance, alleviating the need to alter critical binding residues directly.

We further embedded these principles in a multi-parameter design strategy aimed at co-optimizing oxidation resistance and binding affinity in a clinically relevant anti-CD33 antibody. This antibody contains a Trp96 residue in CDR H3 that is essential for antigen recognition, but exhibits ~97% oxidation under AAPH stress. Conventional mitigation via Trp-to-Phe substitution resulted in catastrophic loss of function, with a > 1000-fold reduction in affinity, highlighting the limitations of simple aromatic substitution in such contexts. By contrast, charge-altering mutations designed to increase the local Epot around W96 reduced its oxidation by up to ~50% while retaining binding, with some variants achieving ~27% reduction in oxidation at only a 1.4-fold penalty in KD. These data demonstrate that local electrostatics can be tuned to mitigate oxidation at functionally critical Trp sites while preserving antigen binding substantially better than Trp removal.

To elucidate the mechanistic basis for these observations, we performed redox replica-exchange MD simulations on Mab4 and its Mab4.RR variant, which differ by two Asp-to-Arg mutations that flip the local environment from negative to positive and reduce observed oxidation by 98%. These simulations revealed an 88 mV increase in the standard reduction potential (Eº) of the Trp residue in Mab4.RR, corresponding to a ~ 1.5 pH unit shift in redox potential, with the Eº in Mab4.RR closely matching that of a capped tryptophan control. This provides direct evidence that the negatively charged environment in Mab4 stabilizes the oxidized state and lowers the energetic barrier to Trp oxidation. A plausible mechanistic interpretation is that electron loss from Trp – the initiating step of oxidation – is thermodynamically stabilized by negative electrostatics and destabilized by positive ones, consistent with prior work on redox-active Trp residues in azurin and related systems,57–59 and with studies linking local polarity to singlet oxygen reactivity at the indole ring.60,61

While the current models rely on MD-derived descriptors, the underlying design rule is simple: exposed Trp residues in negatively charged patches represent the highest risk sites and primary targets for electrostatic tuning. This rule can be integrated into existing in silico developability workflows using static homology models or modest conformational sampling, without requiring full-scale MD for every candidate. Incorporating structural data from Fab-antigen complexes in future work could enable finer control over affinity-preserving mutations, allowing electrostatic tuning to explicitly respect binding geometries – though the successful CD33 optimization performed here without complex structure illustrates the approach’s utility even in early-stage discovery.

Several limitations merit discussion. First, the ML classifiers show modest sensitivity (40–47%), reflecting a tendency to underpredict oxidation-prone residues. This is partly a consequence of the class imbalance inherent in the dataset, where oxidation-susceptible Trp residues represent approximately 21% of all CDR Trp sites. Class imbalance correction strategies such as SMOTE or cost-sensitive learning could be explored in future work as larger curated datasets become available. Importantly, the two-parameter rule-based model was not trained or optimized on this dataset, and therefore class imbalance correction is not applicable to its evaluation; its validity rests instead on the mechanistic grounding of its two descriptors in Trp redox chemistry, blind prospective classification of all 10 CDR Trp sites across eight clinical-stage antibodies, and experimental confirmation through targeted re-engineering of oxidation-prone residues in multiple internal antibodies.

Second, Asp-to-Arg and Asp-to-Lys substitutions are not necessarily purely electrostatic perturbations; they might simultaneously alter side-chain charge, steric bulk, and local backbone conformation, as evidenced by the SASA changes observed in variants such as Mab1.RRR and Mab4.RR. These confounding factors complicate strict attribution of the observed oxidation changes to electrostatics alone. The strongest mechanistic evidence for an electrostatic-specific effect comes from the redox replica-exchange MD simulations, which directly link the local electrostatic environment to the Trp reduction potential in a physics-based framework independent of mutational phenotypes, and from the distal mutation results in Mab2.RRR, where mutations located 10.5–18.6Å from the Trp residue reduced oxidation without direct steric contact with the Trp side chain. Future work employing isosteric charge-neutralizing substitutions (e.g., Asp→Asn) could help further decouple electrostatic from steric contributions.

Third, this study focused on AAPH-induced oxidation. Comparative experiments using a charge-neutral AAPH analog across eight antibodies yielded broadly consistent oxidation profiles (Supplemental Figure S1), and, while this comparison is not powered to rule out reagent-specific artifacts definitively, it argues against a strong localization effect. The more direct and reagent-independent evidence comes from the redox replica-exchange MD simulations, which compute the standard reduction potential of Trp entirely from the protein electrostatic field without invoking AAPH chemistry, confirming that the observed susceptibility differences arise from intrinsic Trp redox energetics rather than from reagent behavior.

Additionally, the charge-altering mutations introduced in this study (Asp-to-Arg and Asp-to-Lys substitutions) shift local and global electrostatic properties of the antibody, which could in principle influence colloidal stability, viscosity, or immunogenicity, all critical developability attributes. A systematic evaluation of these endpoints was beyond the scope of this work, but should be considered as part of downstream lead optimization in a drug development context.

In summary, local side-chain electrostatics, alongside SASA, is a key and previously underappreciated determinant of Trp oxidation risk in therapeutic antibodies. It is predictive, tunable through distal charge-altering mutations, and mechanistically grounded in Trp redox energetics. Together, these findings provide a practical framework for rational antibody engineering that enables simultaneous optimization of chemical stability and antigen-binding function-two objectives that have historically been difficult to pursue in tandem.

Materials and methods

Trp oxidation dataset: antibody panel and stress protocol

The internal dataset has 187 mAbs (light chain and heavy chain combination). These mabs were oxidized at pH 5.5 using AAPH for 16 h at 37°C and the CDR Trp oxidation was reported with respect to a non-spiked control. A total of 264 CDR Trp residues were present in the 187 mabs, with 209 CDR Trp not oxidized and 55 data points being oxidized. A cutoff threshold of 35% oxidation rate was used for this classification. The 35% oxidation threshold was adopted from Sharma et al.38 The 187-antibody internal panel, which to our knowledge represents the largest published Trp oxidation dataset in the biopharmaceutical development field, originates from multiple independent discovery campaigns spanning a range of therapeutic targets and both fully human and humanized frameworks, predominantly IgG1 with a minority of IgG4 and engineered variants. The 264 CDR Trp sites are distributed across all six CDR loops, providing broad structural and sequence coverage. A precise target enumeration or formal sequence diversity analysis is not feasible, as the panel was assembled across close to 20 programs over an extended period. Further, the external blind validation on eight clinical-stage antibodies from TheraSAbDab,54 sharing no sequence or target overlap with the training set, classified prospectively and correctly across all 10 CDR Trp sites, provides a more direct and rigorous test of generalizability than internal diversity metrics alone. Clinical-stage and engineered mAbs were oxidized under the same AAPH conditions. Clinical antibodies were additionally tested with a charge-neutral AAPH analog under identical conditions (Supplemental Figure S1).

Fab structure modeling and MD simulations

We constructed the variable domain (Fv) structure from the antibody sequences using the DeepAb55 modeling technique. To generate the full Fab structures, an internal VMD62 script was used to graft the Fvs onto the constant region of the Herceptin Fab by aligning their elbow loops. The decision to generate the Fab domain, rather than the Fv alone, was made to account for any potential electrostatic contributions from the Fab’s constant region. The resulting Fab structures were then subjected to MD simulations. The Fab structure was solvated and parameterized using the FF14SB force field63 and TIP3P64 water model in a 10 Å truncated octahedron box. Following relaxation protocol, 500 ns of MD simulation was performed. Further, 1000 snapshots were extracted every 0.5 ns for feature analysis. A systematic comparison of MD-derived versus static structure descriptors for electrostatic and surface features was reported by Park and Izadi.51 The full protocol is described in the SI.

Computation of structural and physicochemical descriptors from MD trajectories

For each MD simulation frame, we extracted 50 features to describe the Trp oxidation propensity, as shown in Supplemental Table S1. Feature selection in this study was guided by prior literature on protein oxidation mechanisms rather than data-driven wrapper methods, which we acknowledge as a potential source of selection bias. For each antibody in our dataset, we computed 50 residue-level structural features, capturing various characteristics such as solvent accessibility (e.g., SASA, SASA_loop, n_loop_water_5), flexibility (e.g., loop_rmsd, res_rmsd), local hydrophobicity (e.g., SAP scores), backbone dihedral angles (φ,ψ,χ) angles of neighboring residues), local electrostatic potentials (e.g., Epot), and interactions with neighboring polar, nonpolar, and aromatic residues. Detailed protocol is provided in the SI.

Machine learning classification and feature importance analysis

Feature relationships were assessed by hierarchical clustering. Dendrogram plot was generated using Spearman rank correlation matrix and single linkage clustering to visualize feature relationships. For quantifying feature importance to Trp oxidation rate, RF regressor (1000 estimators) was trained with standardized features using a 5-fold cross-validation split, with SHAP values computed on the test set using shap. TreeExplainer. Further GB and RF classifiers were used to predict oxidation rates as a binary classification problem. For classification, oxidation rates larger than 35% were used to create two classes (reactive vs non-reactive). Detailed ML protocol is explained in SI.

Antigen binding kinetics by surface plasmon resonance

All surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements were conducted on a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). Anti-human IgG antibody was immobilized on a CM5 chip according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The mabs in question were then captured on the chip (1 μg/mL, 60 sec) and affinity for the respective antigen determined using single cycle kinetics. The sensograms were analyzed using the manufacturer’s software and fit to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to calculate the kinetic and binding parameters. All antibody variants were confirmed to be greater than 95% monomer by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) under native conditions prior to SPR measurements. The full protocol is described in SI.

Trp reduction potential calculations by redox replica-exchange MD

The Mab4 WT and Mab4.RR variants were used for redox replica exchange simulations, with the tryptophan (TRX) residue parametrized as titratable in two states (Trp and Trp.+). The atomic charges for the tryptophan residue can be found in Supplemental Table S3. Redox MD was performed on a capped tryptophan dipeptide, and the Trp 100a in Mab4 was defined as the titratable residue. Simulations included 8 redox windows from 900 mV to 1250 mV, with replica exchange runs of 60 ns, 10,000 exchange attempts, and solvent relaxation after each exchange. Full methods are provided in the SI.

Binding affinity measurement of engineered anti-CD33 variants by SPR

The binding affinity of protein and protein interaction was determined by SPR technology (Biacore 8K+, Cytiva). Briefly, each antibody variant was captured by Protein A sensor chip (Series S) on the different flow cell to achieve approximately 150 response units (RU), followed by the injection of five-fold serial dilutions of human CD33 protein (R&D Systems; 0.16 nM to 100 nM) in HBS-EP buffer. Sensorgrams were processed with reference and blank subtraction and analyzed using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to calculate the kinetic and binding parameters. The detailed protocol is provided in SI.

Iterative electrostatic engineering for co-optimization of oxidation resistance and binding affinity

Internal lead candidates were engineered in three iterative steps with distinct optimization objectives. Initially, a structurally-aware sequence optimization approach65 was adapted to identify acidic framework residues (Asp/Glu) for substitution with basic residues (Arg/Lys) in tolerated positions, generating 24 candidate sequences for small-scale expression and affinity measurements. Next, mutations were introduced to Ala, Lys, and Arg around the oxidizing Trp residue to increase affinity, identifying single mutations, light chain double mutations, and combinations of heavy chain double and triple mutations. In the final step, beneficial mutations from both previous stages were combined in silico, producing 23,915 candidate sequences. Active learning66 was used to select 90 sequences with desired properties, which were tested for improved affinity and oxidation resistance, demonstrating the effectiveness of multi-property optimization via active learning-assisted combinatorial mutagenesis. The detailed protocol is provided in SI.

Cryo-EM structure determination of the 15G15.3 Fab-CD33 complex

Cryo-EM data was collected using a Titan Krios G3i with a K3 camera, capturing over 18,000 movies at varying tilts. The data were processed with cryoSPARC, including motion correction, CTF estimation, and 2D classification, resulting in a 3D reconstruction at 3.5 Å resolution. Model building was done using UCSF Chimera67 and the human CD33 PDB structure, followed by refinement in COOT68 and Phenix.69 The final model was validated and refined to 3.5 Å resolution (Supplementary Table S4). Full details of the method are provided in the SI.
