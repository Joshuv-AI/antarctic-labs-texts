METTL3-mediated m6A methylation to promote mandibular condyle fracture repair via TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway
Authors: Zhitao Yao, Guodong Lv, Wei An, Maimaitituxun, Jin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMandibular condylar fractures are common maxillofacial injuries. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), one of the most abundant eukaryotic mRNA modifications, has not yet been clearly elucidated in terms of its specific role in condylar fracture repair. A mandibular condyle fracture rat model was established. Micro-CT scans and biochemical indicators were measured at 1- and 5 weeks post-fracture. The expression levels of m6A methylation-related enzymes were analyzed. METTL3 knockdown and overexpression models were constructed to evaluate structural healing via micro-CT, biomechanical properties, osteogenesis/chondrogenesis-related proteins, and the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. BMSCs were isolated for in vitro analyses, including cell viability, apoptosis, osteogenic differentiation, signaling pathway protein expression, and MeRIP-seq analysis. MeRIP-qPCR, RNA pull-down, and mRNA stability assays were used to verify METTL3-mediated m6A methylation targets. Compared to the 1-week group, the biochemical indicators in the 5-week group significantly increased, accompanied by upregulated METTL3 and downregulated FTO expression. METTL3 knockdown delayed fracture repair, disrupted bone structure, reduced osteogenic markers, and enhanced chondrogenic differentiation. Conversely, METTL3 overexpression significantly promoted bone healing and osteogenic differentiation. In vitro, METTL3 silencing reduced BMSC viability and inhibited osteogenic potential, along with suppression of TGF-β/Smad and RhoA/BMP9 signaling pathways. MeRIP-seq revealed extensive changes in m6A methylation peaks after METTL3 knockdown, identifying IGF-BP3 as a key target. MeRIP and RNA pull-down assays confirmed IGF-BP3 as a METTL3-mediated m6A modification target. METTL3 enhances IGF-BP3 mRNA stability through m6A methylation, subsequently activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway to promote BMSC osteogenic differentiation and condylar fracture repair. This study highlights the critical role of METTL3-mediated m6A modification in mandibular condyle fracture repair and provides potential molecular targets for bone tissue engineering and clinical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51178-7.Keywords: Mandibular condylar fractures, N6-methyladenosine, METTL3, TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, IGF-BP3Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular biology

Introduction

Mandibular condylar fracture is one of the most common maxillofacial bone injuries, accounting for 20%–72% of all mandibular fractures1. Due to the complex anatomical structure of the condyle, improper management of such fractures can easily result in masticatory dysfunction, facial asymmetry, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, severely impacting patients’ quality of life2. Therefore, effective treatment and repair of condylar fractures has long been a major focus in the field of maxillofacial surgery. However, current clinical approaches are largely limited to surgical fixation and conventional rehabilitation strategies3, with a lack of effective molecular-targeted interventions to promote fracture healing and bone regeneration. Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying condylar fracture repair and identifying key regulatory factors and signaling pathways holds significant theoretical and clinical value.

Fracture healing involves a series of complex biological phases, including osteoblast activation, endochondral ossification, and remodeling of the bone matrix4. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), as key osteogenic progenitor cells, play a central role in bone repair and tissue regeneration. Precise regulation of their proliferation, differentiation, and function is crucial for determining the speed and quality of bone healing5,6. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway plays a core role in bone regeneration by promoting the phosphorylation of downstream Smad proteins, thereby enhancing the expression of osteogenic genes and facilitating bone tissue regeneration7,8. However, the upstream molecular mechanisms governing this pathway remain poorly understood.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most abundant modification of mRNA in mammals and plays vital roles in regulating RNA splicing, stability, translation efficiency, and cellular functions. Recently, increasing attention has been paid to its role in BMSC osteogenic differentiation and tissue repair9,10. The dynamic regulation of m6A methylation is mediated by a group of enzymes: the methyltransferase complex—comprising METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP—catalyzes m6A addition, while the demethylases FTO and ALKBH5 remove the modification, together forming a finely tuned methylation regulatory network11. Previous studies have shown that METTL3, as the core component of the methyltransferase complex, plays a crucial role in promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and modulating chondrocyte function12,13. However, the specific mechanisms and targets of m6A modification in mandibular condylar fracture repair remain largely unexplored.

This study aims to systematically investigate the expression dynamics and biological function of the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 during mandibular condylar fracture repair. Through the establishment of METTL3 knockdown and overexpression models, combined with micro-CT imaging, mechanical testing, cell proliferation and apoptosis assays, osteogenic differentiation analysis, MeRIP-seq high-throughput sequencing, RNA pull-down, and mRNA stability assays, this study seeks to uncover the key role of m6A modification in condylar fracture repair. We hope that the findings of this study will provide novel theoretical insights and potential therapeutic strategies for fracture repair, and offer a scientific foundation for improving clinical outcomes in patients with condylar fractures.

Materials and methods

Establishment of animal model

Healthy male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing 250–300 g were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xinjiang Medical University. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC-20220442-18). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Furthermore, this study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Rats were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled conditions (temperature 22–25 °C, relative humidity 50–60%, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. After a 7-day acclimatization period, the animals were randomly divided into experimental groups.

Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). Under sterile conditions, a 1.5 cm arc-shaped incision was made at the left mandibular angle. The skin and muscle were bluntly dissected to expose the condylar structure. A standardized horizontal fracture line was created at the condylar neck. In the Sham group, only the skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised to expose the condyle and mandibular angle region without inducing a fracture. Postoperative intramuscular injection of penicillin (2 × 10⁵ U/rat/day) was administered to prevent infection. Serum and mandibular bone samples were collected at 1 week (1w) and 5 weeks (5w) postoperatively.

The fracture rats were further randomized into the following groups: Fracture group, Fracture + sh-NC group, Fracture + sh-METTL3 group, Fracture + OE-NC group, and Fracture + OE-METTL3 group. Mandibular bone samples were collected at 5 weeks post-surgery. Detailed information regarding the lentiviral constructs is provided as follows: Lentiviral particles (sh-METTL3, OE-METTL3, and their respective controls) were synthesized and packaged by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). For METTL3 overexpression, the full-length rat METTL3 coding sequence was cloned into the pLenti-CMV-MCS-3FLAG-EGFP-Puro vector backbone driven by a CMV promoter. For METTL3 knockdown, the synthesized shRNA targeting METTL3 (sequence: 5’-GAGTTGATTGAGGTAAAGCGAGG-3’) was inserted into the pGLV3-U6-EGFP-Puro vector under the control of the U6 promoter. The final viral titer was concentrated to ≥ 1 × 10⁹ TU/mL. High-titer lentivirus (10 µL, 1 × 10⁹ TU/mL) was administered via tail vein injection 3 days before the fracture procedure. Rats were euthanized via an overdose of inhalation anesthesia (5% isoflurane), followed by disinfection.

Micro-CT imaging and biomechanical testing

To assess bone healing of the mandibular condyle after fracture, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was performed on the left condyle. Mandibles were harvested and soft tissues were removed. The condylar fracture region was scanned at 180° rotation, with a voltage of 65 kV, current of 200 µA, resolution of 9 μm, and exposure time of 300 ms. The reconstructed 3D images were analyzed using Dataviewer software to observe fracture morphology and healing.

In the present study, the 1-week and 5-week time points were selected to represent an early healing stage and a later reparative stage, respectively, in order to characterize the temporal progression of condylar fracture repair. To further evaluate the biomechanical properties of the newly formed bone, maximum load (maximum fracture force) testing was performed on the condylar region at 5-week. Fresh condylar samples were secured using custom-made molds to stabilize the distal mandible, ensuring that the load was applied perpendicularly to the fracture surface. Compression testing was conducted using a displacement-controlled mode with a constant loading speed of 0.05 mm/s applied to the top of the condyle. Bone compressive strength was calculated from the load-displacement curve, with maximum load corresponding to peak stress.

Serum biochemical index detection

Postoperative blood samples were collected via orbital bleeding, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum for ELISA analysis. Serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D₃ (25-HVD₃), procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), bone alkaline phosphatase (BALP), and calcium ions (Ca²⁺) were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of BMSCs and experimental grouping

Eight-week-old healthy male SD rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), disinfected, and euthanized. Bilateral femurs and tibias were rapidly isolated. Under sterile conditions, both epiphyses were removed to expose the bone marrow cavity. The cavity was flushed with 5 mL sterile PBS containing 2% FBS, and the flushing solution was collected. After centrifugation at 400 g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were resuspended in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Third-passage BMSCs were used for subsequent experiments.

BMSCs were divided into five groups: Control, sh-NC, sh-METTL3, OE-NC, and OE-METTL3. Lentiviral transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was set to 30, and 8 µg/mL polybrene was added to enhance infection efficiency. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were harvested 72 h after transfection for total RNA extraction. METTL3 mRNA expression was measured by RT-qPCR to verify knockdown or overexpression efficiency.

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biosharp, China). BMSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well, with three replicates per group. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Mean OD values were used to reflect cell viability and generate proliferation curves.

Cell apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, USA). BMSCs were gently digested with 0.25% trypsin (without EDTA), collected, and washed twice with PBS. Approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1× Binding Buffer, then incubated with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. After adding 400 µL 1× Binding Buffer, cells were immediately analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.6 software.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

BMSCs from each group were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵/mL. After cell attachment, osteogenic induction was initiated using commercial osteogenic differentiation medium (Cronda, China), with medium refreshed every 3 days. Cells were cultured for 21 days for Alizarin Red S staining and 7 days for ALP staining and activity assays.

For Alizarin Red S staining, cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Cells were stained with 0.1% Alizarin Red S solution (Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. After removing the staining solution, cells were washed three times with PBS and imaged under an inverted microscope. Grayscale analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

For ALP staining, cells were similarly washed and fixed. Staining was performed using the BCIP/NBT color development kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and photographed. ImageJ was used to quantify ALP staining intensity.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from condylar fracture tissues and BMSCs using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan) to synthesize cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara) on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (USA). Primers were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), as listed in Table S1. β-actin was used as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting assay

Condyles from the fracture region were harvested at 5 weeks post-surgery, minced, and ground in liquid nitrogen. Tissues or cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime). Total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts (30 µg) of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (1:1000, Abcam) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed three times with TBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:5000, CST) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher) and imaged using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and target protein expression was normalized to β-actin.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing

BMSCs from the sh-NC and sh-METTL3 groups (n = 3 biological replicates per group) were collected, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and a simpliNano spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare). A total of 30 µg of RNA per sample was used for mRNA enrichment using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The enriched mRNA was then fragmented randomly in a fragmentation buffer containing magnesium ions at 94 °C. The fragmented RNA (~ 100 nt) was incubated with an m6A-specific antibody (Synaptic Systems) at 4 °C for 2 h.

Immunoprecipitated RNA and corresponding input RNA were used for library preparation with the Ovation SoLo RNA-Seq System Kit (NuGEN). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) strategy. Raw sequencing data (fastq format) were processed using fastp (v0.19.11) to generate clean reads.

Clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (Rnor_6.0) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Peak calling for m6A-enriched regions was performed using exomePeak (v2.16.0). Identified peaks were annotated, and genes with peaks in exon regions were considered peak-associated genes. Differential m6A peaks were defined as those with FDR ≤ 0.05.

For gene expression analysis, clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and featureCounts (v1.6.5) was used for quantification. Gene expression was represented as FPKM and read counts. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.30.1), and genes with p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Correlation between differential m6A peaks and DEGs was analyzed and visualized using quadrant plots to assess the relationship between m6A modification changes and gene expression fold changes. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the GOseq R package.

MeRIP-qPCR validation, RNA pull-down assay, and mRNA stability analysis

Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs and fragmented into 100–200 nt using RNA Fragmentation Buffer. For MeRIP-qPCR, 300 µg of RNA was used for m6A immunoprecipitation, with 10 µg reserved as the input control. RNA was incubated with anti-m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, #202003) and Protein A/G magnetic beads for 4 h at 4 °C. m6A-enriched RNA was eluted, purified, and reverse transcribed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). The enrichment level of IGF-BP3 mRNA was quantified by qPCR, normalized to input.

For the RNA pull-down assay, biotin-labeled RNA probes for IGF-BP3 (Probe-IGF-BP3: UUGAGGUCUUGGUCCACACUACAG; Probe-NC: AACUCCAGAACCAGGUGUGAUGUC) were synthesized by Hippo Bio (China). Each 1 µg probe was incubated with 50 µL magnetic beads in binding buffer for 30 min. BMSCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, Beyotime) on ice for 30 min to extract total protein. The lysates were incubated with the probe-conjugated beads at 4 °C with gentle rotation for 4 h. After washing 3 times, the bound proteins were eluted by boiling for 5 min and analyzed by Western blot to detect METTL3 enrichment in the IGF-BP3 probe group.

For mRNA stability analysis, BMSCs were treated with Actinomycin D (10 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich), and cells were collected at 0 h, 4 h, and 8 h. Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed. The relative mRNA levels of IGF-BP3 were determined using RT-qPCR, with β-actin as the internal control. The relative expression at each time point was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.​.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate or more. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). One-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA) was used to compare differences between groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Dynamic changes in bone formation markers and m6A methyltransferases during condylar fracture repair

To characterize the healing course of the condylar fracture model, we compared post-fracture changes at 1 and 5 weeks. Micro-CT imaging showed a distinct fracture line and limited mineralized tissue formation at 1 week, consistent with an early healing stage. In contrast, at 5 weeks, the fracture region exhibited substantial new bone formation, improved structural continuity, and partial reconstruction of the condylar contour, indicating progression to a relatively advanced reparative stage rather than definitive complete healing (Fig. 1A). Serum biochemical analysis further supported this temporal healing process. Compared with the 1-week group, serum levels of 25-HVD₃, P1NP, BALP, and Ca²⁺ were significantly increased at 5 weeks, suggesting enhanced bone formation activity during later-stage repair (Fig. 1B).

Red arrows indicate the condylar fracture region, corresponding to the original fracture line and adjacent reparative bone area. (B) Serum levels of 25-HVD3, P1NP, BALP, and Ca2+ detected by ELISA. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001.

RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses were performed to evaluate the expression of representative m6A-related regulators, including METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, FTO, and ALKBH5, in condylar bone tissue at different stages after fracture (Fig. 2A and B; Figure S1). At the mRNA and protein level, METTL3 was markedly decreased at 1 week and partially restored at 5 weeks, whereas FTO was significantly increased at 1 week after fracture and then decreased at 5 weeks. In contrast, Mettl14 and Wtap showed relatively stable mRNA expression but decreased protein levels in fracture tissues, whereas ALKBH5 displayed a transient increase at the mRNA level without a corresponding significant change at the protein level. These findings indicating a possible upward trend in m6A modification levels during bone repair and its potential role in regulating bone regeneration. Based on the magnitude of change and the subsequent functional validation, METTL3 was selected as the primary focus for further mechanistic investigation.

METTL3 promotes condylar fracture repair and bone regeneration via TGF-β/Smad signaling

To clarify the role of METTL3 in fracture repair, METTL3 knockdown and overexpression models were established (Figure S2), and their effects on bone morphology and biomechanics were evaluated. Micro-CT analysis (Fig. 3A) showed that the condylar surface remained rough in the fracture, sh-NC, and OE-NC groups. In the sh-METTL3 group, bone continuity was poor, with sparse and fractured trabeculae. In contrast, the OE-METTL3 group exhibited smooth bone surfaces, dense trabeculae, and well-maintained structural continuity, suggesting enhanced bone reconstruction. To further determine whether structural repair at 5 weeks was accompanied by restoration of tissue-level mechanical function, we performed maximum load testing in the condylar region. Compared with the fracture and sh-NC groups, the OE-METTL3 group exhibited significantly improved maximum load, whereas the sh-METTL3 group showed further deterioration in mechanical strength (Fig. 3B and C). These findings indicate that the reparative changes observed at 5 weeks were associated with partial biomechanical recovery, and METTL3 enhances biomechanical performance of the regenerating bone.

METTL3 regulates bone structure and mechanical properties of the mandibular condyle. (A) Micro-CT 3D reconstructions of fracture repair. Red arrows indicate the condylar fracture region, corresponding to the original fracture line and adjacent reparative bone area. (B) Biomechanical testing and (C) maximum load analysis. **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001.

Western blot analysis (Fig. 4A, Figure S3A) demonstrated that expression of osteogenic markers ALP, OCN, and Collagen I was significantly reduced in the fracture group compared to the sham group, and further decreased in the sh-METTL3 group. Conversely, their expression was significantly upregulated in the OE-METTL3 group. Similarly, chondrogenic markers Col2α1, Aggrecan, and SOX-9 were markedly downregulated in the fracture and sh-METTL3 groups but upregulated in the OE-METTL3 group (Fig. 4B, Figure S3B). Furthermore, expression of TGF-β, p-Smad2/Smad2, and p-Smad3/Smad3 was significantly decreased in the fracture group and further reduced in the sh-METTL3 group, but significantly elevated in the OE-METTL3 group (Fig. 4C, Figure S3C), suggesting that the TGF-β/Smad pathway serves as a key mediator of METTL3-regulated bone reconstruction.

METTL3 regulates BMSC activity, apoptosis, and osteogenic potential

We further investigated the regulatory effects of METTL3 on BMSC function at the cellular level. RT-qPCR analysis showed that METTL3 mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the sh-METTL3 group compared to sh-NC, and significantly increased in the OE-METTL3 group compared to OE-NC (Fig. 5A). CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 5B) indicated that cell proliferation was significantly inhibited in the sh-METTL3 group, while markedly enhanced in the OE-METTL3 group. Flow cytometry (Fig. 5C) showed increased apoptosis in the sh-METTL3 group and reduced apoptosis in the OE-METTL3 group, suggesting that METTL3 protects BMSC viability.

METTL3 regulates BMSC expression, proliferation, and apoptosis. (A) RT-qPCR analysis of METTL3 expression in sh-METTL3 and OE-METTL3 groups. (B) Cell proliferation assessed by CCK-8 assay. (C) Apoptosis analysis by Annexin V/PI dual-staining flow cytometry. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001.

Alizarin Red S staining revealed significantly fewer mineralized nodules in the sh-METTL3 group compared to sh-NC, and significantly more in the OE-METTL3 group compared to OE-NC (Fig. 6A). Similarly, ALP staining demonstrated reduced ALP activity in the sh-METTL3 group and increased ALP levels in the OE-METTL3 group, suggesting that METTL3 promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Fig. 6B). Western blot analysis (Fig. 7A, Figure S4A) showed that RhoA and BMP9 were significantly downregulated in the sh-METTL3 group, and significantly upregulated in the OE-METTL3 group. Further analysis of osteogenesis-related signaling (Fig. 7B, Figure S4B) indicated that TGF-β and downstream p-Smad2/Smad3 levels were decreased in the sh-METTL3 group but elevated in the OE-METTL3 group, confirming that METTL3 regulates osteogenesis via the BMP/TGF-β/Smad pathway.

METTL3 modulates expression of osteogenesis-related signaling proteins. (A) Western blot detection of RhoA and BMP9. (B) Western blot detection of TGF-β and p-Smad2/Smad3. Original blots are presented in Figure S4. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001.

Effect of METTL3 knockdown on m6A modification

To evaluate how METTL3 knockdown affects m6A methylation of cellular mRNA, we first assessed the global distribution of m6A signals. Compared to the sh-NC group (15,255 peaks), the sh-METTL3 group exhibited a global reduction in mRNA m6A modification levels (13,367 peaks; Table S2). A total of 11,504 differential m6A peaks were identified (Fig. 8A), corresponding to 7,427 genes. Distribution analysis showed that these differential peaks were mainly located in the 3′UTR and CDS regions (Fig. 8B).

METTL3 knockdown leads to changes in m6A methylation and mRNA expression. (A) Differential m6A peaks identified by MeRIP-seq between sh-METTL3 and sh-NC groups. (B) Distribution of differential m6A peaks across mRNA elements. (C) DEGs identified by RNA-seq between sh-METTL3 and sh-NC groups. (D) Quadrant plot showing genes with concurrent m6A and expression changes. (E) GO enrichment analysis for biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. (F) m6A modification level of IGF-BP3 decreased in the sh-METTL3 group.

RNA-seq analysis after METTL3 knockdown identified 1,458 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 652 upregulated and 806 downregulated genes (Fig. 8C, Table S3). A quadrant plot (Fig. 8D) showed that 767 genes exhibited both changes in m6A modification and expression levels. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these 767 genes were primarily involved in ossification, osteoblast differentiation, and regulation of ossification (Fig. 8E). Among these ossification-related candidates (Table S4), IGF-BP3 was prioritized for further investigation because it showed coordinated decreases in both m6A peak enrichment and mRNA expression after METTL3 knockdown, peak localization primarily in the 3’UTR/CDS regions (indicative of mRNA stability regulation), and was closely linked to the osteogenic processes highlighted by the enrichment analysis (Fig. 8F). In addition, because our downstream protein data implicated the TGF-β/Smad pathway during METTL3-mediated osteogenic regulation, IGF-BP3 was considered a biologically relevant candidate for mechanistic validation.

METTL3 enhances IGF-BP3 mRNA stability via m6A modification

MeRIP-qPCR results (Fig. 9A) confirmed that METTL3 knockdown significantly reduced the m6A enrichment of IGF-BP3 mRNA, while overexpression significantly increased it. RNA pull-down assay (Fig. 9B, Figure S5) demonstrated that the negative control probe did not enrich METTL3 protein, whereas the IGF-BP3-specific probe effectively pulled down METTL3, suggesting a direct interaction. mRNA stability assays (Fig. 9C) showed that IGF-BP3 mRNA levels and stability were reduced in the sh-METTL3 group, but elevated and stabilized in the OE-METTL3 group.

Discussion

This study investigated the critical role of METTL3-mediated m6A methylation in the repair of mandibular condylar fractures. Our findings suggest that METTL3 activates the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, potentially through enhanced m6A methylation of IGF-BP3 mRNA, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and bone regeneration in the fractured condyle. These results not only reveal a novel regulatory mechanism of m6A methylation in bone tissue regeneration but also provide a promising molecular target and theoretical foundation for future fracture therapies.

As one of the most prevalent epigenetic modifications of eukaryotic mRNA, m6A has been shown to play a vital role in fracture repair14. However, its specific functions during condylar fracture repair remain largely uncharacterized. In this study, analysis of fracture models at different postoperative time points revealed a progressive upregulation of the methyltransferase METTL3 and a downregulation of the demethylase FTO, suggesting a dynamic elevation of m6A modification associated with the bone healing process15. Previous reports have demonstrated the osteoinductive role of METTL3 in stem cell differentiation16, and our results are consistent with these findings, further reinforcing the importance of METTL3 in skeletal repair. Notably, although several m6A-related regulators were profiled in this study, their expression patterns were not entirely uniform. For example, Mettl14 and Wtap showed relatively stable transcript levels but reduced protein expression, whereas ALKBH5 exhibited a transient mRNA increase without an obvious protein-level alteration. This discrepancy suggests that post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation may also contribute to the dynamic remodeling of the m6A machinery during fracture repair.

Using both in vivo and in vitro METTL3 knockdown and overexpression models, we confirmed the positive role of METTL3 in promoting bone healing and enhancing the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs17. Silencing METTL3 impaired bone structural integrity, reduced biomechanical strength, and suppressed the expression of osteogenic markers such as ALP, OCN, and Collagen I, while overexpression of METTL3 facilitated bone repair and increased marker expression. These results align with previous findings indicating the essential role of METTL3 in BMSC osteogenesis18.

An additional finding of interest was that METTL3 overexpression increased not only osteogenic markers but also chondrogenesis-related markers, including Col2α1, Aggrecan, and SOX-9, in condylar fracture tissues collected at 5 weeks after surgery. This pattern suggests that METTL3 may promote a broader osteochondral reparative response rather than osteogenesis alone. Because mandibular condylar fracture repair is characterized by complex tissue remodeling involving both bone- and cartilage-related reparative events4,19, the increased expression of chondrogenic markers in the OE-METTL3 group may reflect enhanced reparative tissue maturation at the fracture site. Notably, TGF-β, p-Smad2/Smad2, and p-Smad3/Smad3 were simultaneously elevated in the OE-METTL3 group, indicating that activation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway may be associated with this phenotype. This interpretation is biologically plausible because TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling is a recognized regulator of cartilage development and chondrogenesis and has been reported to cooperate with SOX9 in controlling chondrocyte-specific markers such as type II collagen and aggrecan20,21. In addition, prior studies have suggested that METTL3 is involved in mesenchymal stem cell chondrogenic differentiation, supporting the biological plausibility that METTL3 may contribute to coordinated osteochondral repair22. However, the present study did not directly test whether TGF-β signaling is required for the METTL3-mediated increase in chondrogenesis-related markers. Therefore, although our data support a possible involvement of TGF-β signaling in the coordinated osteochondral response, further experiments using pathway-specific inhibition or rescue approaches will be required to establish direct causality.

Another point that deserves discussion is the potential involvement of BMP signaling in METTL3-mediated osteogenic regulation. Compared with the TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway, the canonical BMP-Smad1/5/9 axis is often considered a major signaling route in bone formation23,24. In the present study, we observed that BMP9 expression was reduced after METTL3 knockdown and increased after METTL3 overexpression in BMSCs, indicating that BMP-related osteogenic signaling may also be responsive to METTL3. Nevertheless, because we did not directly examine Smad1/5/9 phosphorylation, our data do not allow a definitive conclusion regarding activation of the canonical BMP-Smad1/5/9 pathway. Therefore, while the present findings suggest that BMP-associated signaling may contribute to the observed osteogenic phenotype, the mechanistic evidence in this study more directly supports the involvement of the TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway. Future studies incorporating direct assessment of p-Smad1/5/9, as well as pathway-specific inhibition or rescue approaches, will be necessary to determine the relative contributions of BMP-Smad1/5/9 and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling in METTL3-mediated fracture repair.

Mechanistically, MeRIP-seq analysis enabled us to comprehensively identify m6A methylation changes following METTL3 knockdown, revealing that many differentially methylated genes are enriched in osteogenic pathways, with IGF-BP3 emerging as a key regulatory target. IGF-BP3 is an important binding protein that modulates insulin-like growth factor activity, and plays roles in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and bone formation25.

Our MeRIP-qPCR and RNA pull-down assays confirmed that METTL3 directly enhances IGF-BP3 mRNA stability via m6A methylation, consistent with classical models where m6A affects gene expression by regulating RNA stability26. These results also highlight IGF-BP3 as a direct downstream effector of METTL3.

Importantly, this study further demonstrated that TGF-β/Smad signaling acts as a key mediator in the METTL3-driven bone regeneration process. This pathway is a well-established regulator of bone formation, fracture repair, and skeletal tissue remodeling27–29. METTL3 overexpression significantly enhanced the activity of this signaling cascade, suggesting its role in facilitating bone healing through TGF-β/Smad activation. Notably, our data support previous findings that IGF-BP3 can activate the TGF-β/Smad pathway30, implying that METTL3 may enhance IGF-BP3 mRNA stability via m6A, thereby activating downstream TGF-β/Smad signaling.

Furthermore, a clearer interpretation of the present model also requires consideration of the site-specific biology of mandibular fracture repair. The mandible, including the mandibular condyle, belongs to the craniofacial skeleton and is largely derived from the cranial neural crest, whereas long bones such as the tibia arise predominantly from mesoderm-derived progenitors. These distinct embryonic origins are known to influence progenitor-cell identity, osteogenic programs, and regenerative behavior in craniofacial versus appendicular skeletal tissues31,32. In this context, the use of a mandibular condylar fracture model is justified because our study was designed to investigate METTL3 function specifically in mandibular condylar fracture repair, rather than to model long-bone healing in general. Moreover, mandibular fracture repair is characterized by complex osteochondral remodeling, and previous studies have shown that cartilage-related reparative events and chondrocyte-to-osteoblast transformation contribute to healing in the mandible and condyle33. Therefore, the present model is appropriate for studying METTL3 in a clinically relevant craniofacial repair setting.

Despite these promising findings, this study has some limitations. First, clinical samples were not included, which limits the translational applicability of the conclusions. Second, although IGF-BP3 was identified as a key target based on its dramatic fold changes and pathway relevance, several other critical osteogenic candidates also exhibited significant alterations in m6A modification patterns. The potential competitive interactions among these mRNAs for METTL3 binding, or their collective contribution to the overall bone regeneration network, remain to be elucidated in future studies. Then, the mechanisms by which METTL3 expression is regulated in the fracture microenvironment or under biomechanical stress are still unclear and warrant further investigation to fully define the intricate m6A regulatory networks involved in bone regeneration. Additionally, while our findings firmly establish the therapeutic potential of targeting m6A modification for mandibular fracture repair, future comparative studies are warranted to determine whether these molecular dynamics are universally applicable to mesoderm-derived long bone healing or if they are intricately tied to the neural crest phenotype. Finally, while a marked structural reconstruction and improved mechanical properties at 5 weeks, the present data do not justify an unequivocal conclusion that complete healing or full temporomandibular joint functional restoration had been achieved. Therefore, the 5-week endpoint should be interpreted more cautiously as a late reparative stage with partial biomechanical recovery. Future studies incorporating dedicated functional assessments, such as mandibular motion analysis, masticatory performance, or temporomandibular joint functional evaluation, will be needed to define the relationship between structural repair and true functional restoration more comprehensively.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated the essential biological functions and molecular mechanisms of METTL3-mediated m6A methylation in mandibular condylar fracture repair. Specifically, we demonstrated that METTL3 promotes BMSC osteogenic differentiation and bone regeneration by enhancing IGF-BP3 mRNA stability and activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. These findings offer significant theoretical insights and identify a potential therapeutic target for future craniomaxillofacial bone regeneration strategies and clinical interventions.
