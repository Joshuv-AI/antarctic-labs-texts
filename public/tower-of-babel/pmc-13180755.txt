Prevalence and predisposing factors of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Dawuro Zone, Southwest Ethiopia: an institution-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Getachew Teshome, Berhanu Effa, Asrat Chernet, Temesgen Weldemedhin, Mengistu Geribo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hookworm infection and anemia remain major public health problems in developing countries, particularly among pregnant women in resource-limited settings. These conditions are associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including increased morbidity, mortality, and poor pregnancy outcomes. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Dawuro Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2022 to March 2023 among pregnant women attending antenatal clinics in selected public health facilities. A total of 367 participants were recruited using systematic random sampling. Data on socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires. Stool specimens were collected from each participant and examined for hookworm infection using direct wet mount and test tube flotation techniques. Hemoglobin concentration was measured using a portable HemoSmart analyzer. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with hookworm infection and anemia. A total of 367 pregnant women participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 100%. The prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia was 10.6% and 31.9%, respectively, with a mean hemoglobin concentration of 11.33 ± 1.92 g/dL. Factors significantly associated with hookworm infection and anemia included walking barefoot (AOR = 2.89; 95% CI: 1.27–6.60), poor sanitation practices (AOR = 2.89; 95% CI: 1.24–6.74), inadequate iron supplementation (AOR = 4.59; 95% CI: 1.8–11.5), and low household income (AOR = 7.0; 95% CI: 3.27–14.98). Hookworm infection and anemia were common among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Dawuro Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. These conditions were associated with behavioral and socioeconomic factors such as lack of footwear, poor sanitation practices, inadequate iron supplementation, and low household income. Strengthening early antenatal care attendance, improving sanitation and hygiene practices, and ensuring adequate nutritional supplementation may help reduce the burden of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08675-5.

Introduction

Hookworm infection, caused by the nematode parasites Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, remains a significant public health problem in many low- and middle-income countries. Transmission usually occurs through skin penetration by infective filariform larvae present in contaminated soil, particularly among individuals who walk barefoot (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2013). The infection is highly prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions due to favorable climatic conditions and inadequate sanitation facilities (Echazú et al. 2015).

Although hookworm infection is often asymptomatic, it can lead to iron deficiency anemia, malnutrition, and other health complications, especially among vulnerable populations such as pregnant women (Bartsch et al. 2016). During pregnancy, anemia poses serious health risks for both the mother and the fetus. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anemia in pregnancy as a hemoglobin (Hb) level below 11 g/dL (World Health Organization (WHO) 2014). Reduced hemoglobin levels impair oxygen transport in the body and increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal mortality (Steer 2000).

The burden of anemia is particularly high in sub-Saharan Africa, where nutritional deficiencies, infectious diseases, and parasitic infections are common contributing factors (World Health Organization (WHO) 2011). Globally, it is estimated that approximately 44 million pregnant women are infected with hookworms, leading to significant health consequences and economic losses (Brooker et al. 2008). According to WHO estimates, about 2 billion people worldwide are affected by anemia based on established hemoglobin thresholds (< 10.5 g/dL during the second trimester of pregnancy, < 11 g/dL during the first and third trimesters, and in children under five years of age, < 11.5 g/dL in preschool children, < 12 g/dL in adolescents and non-pregnant women, and < 13 g/dL in men) (World Health Organization 2008; WHO/UNICEF/UNU 2001).

In Ethiopia, intestinal helminth infections remain common, particularly in rural areas where sanitation conditions are poor and access to health services may be limited. Although deworming has been incorporated into antenatal care (ANC) services as part of national maternal health programs, its utilization remains suboptimal, particularly among rural, less educated, and low-income women.

In the Dawuro Zone of Southwest Ethiopia, information regarding the prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia and to identify associated factors among pregnant women attending antenatal care services in the area. The findings of this study may help inform targeted public health interventions aimed at improving maternal health outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study area, period, and study design

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2022 to March 2023 in Dawuro Zone, located approximately 515 km southwest of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The zone is bordered by Gamo Gofa to the south, Konta to the west, the Gojeb River and Oromia Region to the north, Hadiya and Kembata Tembaro zones to the northeast, and Wolaita Zone and the Omo River to the east and southeast. Dawuro Zone consists of 10 woredas and two administrative towns, with the majority of the population residing in rural areas. The zone is characterized by diverse topography, including mountains, plateaus, lowlands, and gorges. Agriculture is the primary livelihood of the population, with maize, enset, sorghum, and root crops serving as major agricultural products, alongside livestock rearing. Health services in the zone include one general hospital, two primary hospitals, 21 health centers, and 178 health posts, as well as several private clinics and drug stores serving both rural and urban populations. Data on socio-demographic characteristics and clinical factors were collected using a structured questionnaire. Laboratory investigations were conducted to measure hemoglobin levels and identify intestinal parasitic infections from stool samples.

Study population, sample size determination, and sampling technique

The source population comprised all pregnant women attending ANC clinics in Dawuro Zone. The study population included pregnant women who attended ANC services at the selected health facilities during the study period and consented to participate in the study. Pregnant women who had received anti-helminthic treatment within the previous month or had experienced recent blood loss were excluded from the study. The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a 32% prevalence of hookworm infection based on a previous study conducted in Dembecha District, Northwest Ethiopia (Misikir et al. 2020). After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 367 participants. Health facilities were selected using simple random sampling, including one general hospital, one district hospital, and three health centers. The total sample size was proportionally allocated to each facility based on the number of pregnant women attending ANC services. Study participants were selected using systematic random sampling. The sampling interval (K) was calculated by dividing the total number of ANC attendees by the required sample size. The first participant was selected randomly using the lottery method, followed by every Kth pregnant woman attending the ANC clinic until the required sample size was reached.

Study variables

The dependent variables were prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia. Independent variables encompassed socio-demographic factors (age, marital status, educational status, residence, occupation, household size and income, etc.), obstetric-related factors (gestational age, gravidity, parity, history of abortion, first pregnancy, etc.), hygiene and environmental factors (absence of a private latrine, practice of open defecation, lack of a latrine lid, absence of handwashing facilities, nail hygiene, walking barefoot, waste disposal system).

Data collection methods

After obtaining written informed consent, socio-demographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered in the local language by trained nurses and midwives. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code, which was used on both the questionnaire and the laboratory request form to ensure confidentiality and accurate data linkage.

Stool sample collection and parasitological examination

Approximately 20 g of fresh stool was collected from each participant using clean, dry, leak-proof, labeled containers. The samples were examined for intestinal parasites using direct wet mount and test tube flotation techniques.

Direct wet mount

Approximately 2 mg of fresh stool was emulsified with a drop of physiological saline (0.85%) on a clean glass slide. The preparation was covered with a coverslip and examined under a light microscope using 10 × magnifications for the detection of hookworm ova and other intestinal parasites.

Test tube flotation technique

In this technique, 2 g of stool was mixed with 30 mL of saturated sodium chloride flotation solution (density = 1.20). The fecal suspension was filtered through a wire mesh to remove large debris and transferred into a clean test tube. Additional flotation solution was added until a positive meniscus formed at the top of the tube. A coverslip was gently placed on the surface of the tube and allowed to stand for 10 min to enable parasite eggs to float and adhere to the coverslip. After incubation, the coverslip was carefully removed and placed on a glass slide, then examined under a light microscope using a 10 × objective lens for the detection of hookworm eggs and other intestinal parasites (WHO 1991). Participants who tested positive for intestinal parasites using either diagnostic method were provided appropriate treatment according to WHO guidelines (WHO. 1987).

Blood sample collection and hematological examination

Capillary blood samples were collected aseptically from the fingertip of each participant and analyzed using a HemoSmart hemoglobinometer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After calibration of the device, a drop of blood (1 µL) was applied to the test strip, and hemoglobin levels were measured in g/dL. Hematocrit (HCT) values were also recorded as percentages. Results were obtained within five minutes. In addition, thick and thin blood smears were prepared on clean glass slides. Thin blood films were fixed with absolute methanol for 30 s, drained, and air-dried. Both smears were stained with 10% Giemsa solution for 10 min, rinsed with buffered water, air-dried, and examined under a light microscope using 100 × oil immersion for the detection of hemoparasites. Red blood cell morphology was evaluated on thin blood films to assess the morphological type of anemia. Participants diagnosed with anemia and/or malaria infection received treatment according to WHO treatment guidelines (Health et al. 1991; World Health Organization 2023). Information regarding human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status was obtained from participants’ medical records, and a history of chronic illness was assessed through self-report. Participants receiving medical treatment were counseled on preventive measures and scheduled for follow-up visits as necessary.

Data analysis and interpretation

Data were checked for completeness and consistency before entry into Epidata version 3.1, and then exported to SPSS version 26 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was initially performed to identify potential factors associated with hookworm infection and anemia. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding factors. The strength of associations was expressed using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data quality assurance and ethical considerations

Data collectors received training on the study objectives, questionnaire content, and data collection procedures prior to the commencement of the study. Daily supervision was conducted to ensure completeness and consistency of the collected data. All laboratory procedures were performed according to standard operating procedures (SOPs). For quality assurance, 10% of stool samples were randomly selected and re-examined blindly by experienced medical laboratory technologists. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Wolaita Sodo University (Ref. No: WSU 41/30/314). Official letters of permission were obtained from the Dawuro Zone Health Department and the respective woreda health offices. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants before data collection. Confidentiality of participant information was strictly maintained throughout the study. This study was not registered as a clinical trial, as it involved observational data collection without interventional procedures.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 367 pregnant women attending antenatal care services were included in the study, yielding a response rate of 100%. The participants ranged in age from 18 to 44 years, with a mean age of 25.25 ± 4.82 years. The majority of participants were aged 20–24 years (38.0%), followed by 25–29 years (30.5%), while 20.0% were aged above 30 years. Most of the respondents were in their second trimester (47.4%), followed by third trimester (43.1%), and first trimester (9.5%). The average family size was 4.13 ± 1.55, with 60.5% of participants reporting a family size of five or more members. Regarding occupation, housewives constituted the largest group (41.4%), followed by government employees (25.1%) and farmers (17.2%). In terms of educational status, 93.5% of participants were literate, while 6.5% had no formal education. Prevalence of intestinal parasites

Among the 367 pregnant women who provided stool samples for parasitological examination, 139 (37.9%) were found to be infected with at least one intestinal parasite species. A total of five intestinal parasite species were identified. The most common parasite detected was Giardia lamblia (16.6%), Entamoeba histolytica (5.7%), hookworm infection (10.6%), Ascaris lumbricoides (2.7%), and Trichuris trichiura (2.2%). All detected infections were single infections, and no mixed infections were observed among the study participants. The presence of intestinal parasitic infections was significantly associated with anemia (p < 0.05). Severity of anemia and associated factors

The distribution of anemia severity among pregnant women showed that moderate anemia was the most prevalent (18.5%), followed by mild anemia (10.6%) and severe anemia (3.3%). These findings align with WHO reports indicating that mild to moderate anemia is highly prevalent among pregnant women in low-income settings, primarily due to poor nutritional status and parasitic infections, including hookworm infestation. Gravidity was significantly associated with anemia severity (p = 0.030). Primi gravida women had a higher proportion of moderate anemia (6.8%) compared with multigravida women, reflecting the increased physiological demands of a first pregnancy, compounded by inadequate iron stores and suboptimal prenatal micronutrient supplementation. Parity was also significantly associated with anemia severity (p = 0.005). Women with parity of two or more predominantly exhibited moderate anemia, suggesting that repeated pregnancies without adequate birth spacing and nutritional replenishment may progressively deplete maternal iron reserves. Analysis of red blood cell (RBC) morphology demonstrated that normocytic–normochromic anemia was the predominant pattern across all severity categories. This pattern is commonly associated with anemia of chronic disease and early-stage iron deficiency. Factors associated with the prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia

In the bivariate analysis, several variables showed a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) with the prevalence of hookworm infection and anemia. These variables included socio-demographic factors such as educational status, occupation, residence, family size, and family income. Environmental and sanitation-related factors were also significant, including the availability of latrines, type of latrine, patterns of latrine use, presence of latrine lids, and the availability of handwashing facilities near latrines. In addition, personal hygiene practices, particularly the habit of wearing shoes, and clinical as well as obstetric factors—including history of blood loss, birth interval, antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, gravidity, parity, pregnancy trimester, place of delivery, history of malaria infection in the previous year, and iron and folate supplementation—were significantly associated in the bivariate analysis. However, in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, only a limited number of variables remained significantly associated with hookworm infection. Specifically, the absence of latrine access, lack of handwashing facilities near latrines, and the habitual practice of walking barefoot were independently associated with an increased prevalence of hookworm infection.

Regarding anemia, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors. These included low family income (< 3000 Ethiopian Birr), fewer ANC visits, advanced gestational age (particularly during the second and third trimesters), higher gravidity and parity, home delivery, a history of blood loss, short birth intervals, and inadequate iron and folate supplementation (P < 0.05). Furthermore, a strong association was observed between hookworm infection, blood loss, and anemia. Pregnant women infected with hookworm were more likely to experience blood loss leading to anemia [AOR = 8.72, 95% CI (2.22–34.18)]. Similarly, the habit of walking barefoot significantly increased the likelihood of hookworm infection and related anemia [AOR = 2.89, 95% CI (1.27–6.61)], supporting the well-established role of skin penetration in hookworm transmission. Pregnant women who did not receive adequate iron and folate supplementation were 4.6 times more likely to develop anemia compared with those who received sufficient supplementation [AOR = 4.59, 95% CI (1.83–11.53)]. Discussion

The present study assessed the prevalence and associated factors of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women attending antenatal care in the Dawuro Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. The findings revealed a notable prevalence of hookworm infection (10.6%) and anemia (31.9%), highlighting a significant public health concern in the study area. A total of five intestinal parasites were identified in the participants’ stool samples: trophozoite stages of Giardia lamblia (16.6%), trophozoite stages of Entamoeba histolytica (5.7%), ova of hookworm (10.6%), ova of Ascaris lumbricoides (2.7%), and ova of Trichuris trichiura (2.2%). These parasitic infections were significantly associated with the prevalence of anemia among the study participants (P < 0.05).

Our study confirms a strong association between hookworm infection and anemia, which is consistent with findings from East Nepal, where helminthic infection was significantly associated with anemia (46.5%) among pregnant women (Sheh and Ansari 2001). Similarly, research conducted in Kenya demonstrated that a higher hookworm burden during pregnancy correlates with an increased risk of moderate to severe anemia (Kanegeni 2019). This is particularly evident during the first ANC visit, which aligns with our finding, that a lower number of ANC visits significantly increased the odds of hookworm–anemia co-infection (AOR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.04–4.8). This supports the hypothesis that inadequate early ANC limits the timely diagnosis and treatment of parasitic infections.

Several studies have reported a higher prevalence of hookworm infection than that observed in the present study, including Nigeria (35.8%) (Alli et al. 2011), Ghana (45%) (Humphries et al. 2011), Uganda (45%) (Mpairwe et al. 2014), Vietnam (78.15%) (Sant et al. 2008), Andhra Pradesh (78.14%) (Kudaravalli et al. 2011), Dabat Primary Hospital and Debark General Hospital in Northern Ethiopia (21.4%) (Alula 2024), Dembecha district in Northwest Ethiopia (32%) (Misikir et al. 2020), Southwest Ethiopia (59.3%) (Yosef et al. 2024), Mecha district in Oromia (14.2%) (Feleke and Jember 2018) and West Gojjam (18.8%) (Hailu et al. 2020). Similarly, studies conducted among pregnant women in East Nepal (58.9%) (Sheh and Ansari 2001), Nairobi, Kenya (36.2%) (Kanegeni 2019), South India (51%) (Saraswathi et al. 2013), Southeast Nigeria (40.4%) (Solomon et al. 2013), South Africa (57.3%) (Hoque et al. 2007), West Algeria (40.08%) (Demmouche et al. 2010), and West Arsi Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia (38.6%) (Niguse et al. 2013), reported higher prevalence rates of anemia than the present study. These variations may be attributed to differences in geographic location, the time periods during which the studies were conducted, and variations in the socio-demographic and economic characteristics of the study populations.

Conversely, studies conducted in some parts of Ethiopia reported lower prevalence rates of hookworm infection among pregnant women than those observed in the present study, including Northwest Ethiopia (5.5%) (Feleke and Jember 2018), Hossana (7.0%) (Tesfaye et al. 2015), Addis Ababa (1.3%) (Intestinal 2015), Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital (1.3%) (Derso et al. 2016), Yirgalem General Hospital (2.7%) (Buchala et al. 2022), Gondar Hospital (4.7%) (Kumera et al. 2018) and Kenya (3.9%) (Wekesa et al. 2014). The relatively higher prevalence of hookworm infection in the current study area may be attributed to the low socioeconomic and educational status of the participants, the absence of regular shoe-wearing habits, and limited awareness regarding the transmission, prevention, and control of hookworm infection. However, our findings are comparable to those reported in studies conducted in Uganda (11%) (Felister et al. 2020), Southern Nigeria (11.4%) (James et al. 2015), Wondo Genet (11.2%) (Bolka and Gebremedhin 2019) and Kasoa Polyclinic in Ghana (10.3%) (Abaka-Yawson et al. 2020). This similarity may be due to comparable geographic settings, socio-demographic characteristics, and levels of awareness regarding the prevention and control of hookworm infection.

Key socio-demographic factors such as low educational status, rural residence, large family size, and low household income were significantly associated with the co-occurrence of hookworm infection and anemia. These findings are consistent with studies from Eastern Wollega, Oromia, and Western Ethiopia, which reported that illiteracy, low income, and inadequate sanitation infrastructure significantly contributed to parasitic infections and anemia (Mihiret et al. 2017; Ejeta et al. 2014). Our adjusted analysis further confirmed that pregnant women from households earning less than 3000 ETB per month were seven times more likely to develop hookworm infection and anemia (AOR = 7.0, 95% CI: 3.27–14.98). The role of education and place of residence was also notable. Women with no formal education had higher odds of infection, consistent with studies conducted in Nepal and Eastern Wollega (Sheh and Ansari 2001; Mihiret et al. 2017), which reported higher intestinal parasite prevalence among illiterate mothers. Although education was not statistically significant in the adjusted analysis, the crude association and supporting literature suggest that education plays an important role in disease prevention through improved knowledge and hygiene practices. Environmental sanitation factors were also significant. The absence of improved latrines, non-use of latrine facilities, and lack of handwashing facilities were strongly associated with co-infection. This finding aligns with evidence from Eastern Wollega and Northern Tigray (Mihiret et al. 2017; Brehe et al. 2018), where open defecation and the absence of latrine covers increased exposure to helminth infections. In our study, the absence of handwashing facilities was associated with a 3.2-fold higher likelihood of infection (AOR = 3.21, 95% CI: 0.50–20.4), highlighting the critical importance of WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene) interventions. Regarding maternal behavior, not wearing shoes was significantly associated with higher odds of hookworm infection (AOR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.27–6.6), reflecting the fecal–soil–skin transmission route of hookworm. This finding is supported by several studies conducted across Sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria and Ethiopia (Solomon et al. 2013; Alemu and Umeta 2015), which have linked barefoot walking with soil-transmitted helminth infections.

Several obstetric factors also influenced infection risk. Pregnant women in their third trimester were significantly more likely to experience co-infection (AOR = 4.59, 95% CI: 1.33–15.8). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Northwest Ethiopia (Feleke and Jember 2018), South Africa (Hoque et al. 2007), and West Algeria (Demmouche et al. 2010), where the prevalence of anemia peaked during the later stages of pregnancy. In addition, multigravida, birth intervals of less than two years, and home delivery were associated with an increased likelihood of infection. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Western Ethiopia and Nigeria (Ejeta et al. 2014; Solomon et al. 2013), which linked these factors to nutritional depletion and limited access to health services.

Our findings also highlight the importance of nutritional supplementation, particularly iron and folate, in preventing anemia. Pregnant women who inadequately supplemented with iron and folate had significantly higher odds of anemia (AOR = 4.59, 95% CI: 1.8–11.5). Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Northwest Ethiopia (Feleke and Jember 2018) and Northern Tigray (Brehe et al. 2018). However, consistent with results from Gondar University Hospital (Kumera et al. 2018) and India (Saraswathi et al. 2013), supplementation alone may not be sufficient to prevent anemia in some cases, underscoring the need for comprehensive interventions that integrate nutritional, parasitic, and behavioral strategies.

Interestingly, a history of malaria infection and blood loss was also associated with an increased risk of anemia. Although malaria infection within the past year did not remain statistically significant after adjustment (p > 0.05), the presence of Plasmodium falciparum parasites was significantly associated with anemia (AOR = 4.62, 95% CI: 1.64–11.4). This suggests that co-infection exacerbates the severity of anemia, a pattern that has also been reported in several studies across Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia (Alemu and Umeta 2015).

Conclusion

Hookworm infection and anemia were common among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Dawuro Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. These conditions were associated with behavioral and socioeconomic factors such as lack of footwear, poor sanitation practices, inadequate iron supplementation, and low household income. Strengthening early antenatal care attendance, improving sanitation and hygiene practices, and ensuring adequate nutritional supplementation may help reduce the burden of hookworm infection and anemia among pregnant women.

Limitations of the study

The relatively short study period and small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the cross-sectional study design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between intestinal parasitic infections and anemia. Parasite burden was not assessed because stool examinations were performed using qualitative diagnostic methods, which do not quantify egg counts. As a result, the intensity of hookworm infection could not be determined, and the relationship between infection intensity and anemia severity could not be evaluated. Although intestinal parasites were identified, the number of participants with multiple parasitic infections was insufficient to permit meaningful stratified analysis. Consequently, the effect of multi-parasitic infections on the severity of anemia could not be assessed. Additionally, some behavioral variables were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias.
