Complex DNA Synthesis Sequences
Boaz Moav, Ryan Gabrys, Eitan Yaakobi

Abstract
DNA-based storage offers unprecedented density and durability, but its scalability is fundamentally limited by the efficiency of parallel strand synthesis. Existing methods either allow unconstrained nucleotide additions to individual strands, such as enzymatic synthesis, or enforce identical additions across many strands, such as photolithographic synthesis. We introduce and analyze a hybrid synthesis framework that generalizes both approaches: in each cycle, a nucleotide is selected from a restricted subset and incorporated in parallel. This model gives rise to a new notion of a complex synthesis sequence . Building on this framework, we extend the information rate definition of Lenz et al. and analyze an analog of the deletion ball, defined and studied in this setting, deriving tight expressions for the maximal information rate and its asymptotic behavior. These results bridge the theoretical gap between constrained models and the idealized setting in which every nucleotide is always available. For the case of known strands, we design a dynamic programming algorithm that computes an optimal complex synthesis sequence, highlighting structural similarities to the shortest common supersequence problem. We also define a distinct two-dimensional array model with synthesis constraints over the rows, which extends previous synthesis models in the literature and captures new structural limitations in large-scale strand arrays. Additionally, we develop a dynamic programming algorithm for this problem as well. Our results establish a new and comprehensive theoretical framework for constrained DNA, subsuming prior models and setting the stage for future advances in the field.

Introduction

DNA-based data storage has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional storage technologies, driven by advances in DNA synthesis and sequencing
[
1
,
2
]
.
It offers orders-of-magnitude higher information density and long-term stability without power.
However, the synthesis stage remains expensive and time-consuming, motivating continued research.

A DNA storage system comprises three components:
a DNA synthesizer that produces the encoded strands, a storage medium for preserving them, and a DNA sequencer that reads and converts them back to digital form.
External encoding/decoding ensures reliable reconstruction even in the presence of errors.
While many parts of the pipeline have been optimized
[
3

4
,
5

, less attention has been given to the synthesis stage, which is the focus of this work.

Current DNA synthesis machines rely on parallel strand creation and typically follow two paradigms.
In the first method, the machine can add any of the four nucleotides in each cycle (e.g., photon-directed multiplexed enzymatic synthesis
[
6
]
).
In contrast, in the second method, nucleotides are added according to a predetermined synthesis sequence: in each cycle, a designated nucleotide is appended only to selected strands, implying that every synthesized strand must be a subsequence of this predefined (e.g., phosphoramidite chemistry
[
7

[

Prior work on the second paradigm analyzed the information density per cycle

By contrast, the first paradigm yields a trivial upper bound on the information rate since all strands can extend every cycle. This creates a large theoretical gap, which we address by studying information rates when multiple nucleotides are available per cycle.

Our framework generalizes the

Recent experimental advances in large-scale, light-directed, and enzymatic DNA synthesis have revealed that spatial control across dense arrays is inherently limited by optical and chemical coupling between neighboring sites. The works of
[
14
,
8
]
identified scattering, diffraction, and optical-flare effects as the dominant sources of sequence errors in earlier light-directed systems, motivating their redesign of the photochemical reaction cell, in which two substrates must be positioned approximately 60-100
\mu
m apart to minimize reflections. These findings demonstrate that maintaining synthesis fidelity depends on restricting simultaneous activations within shared optical or chemical regions of the array.

Motivated by these experimentally observed constraints, we model an abstract DNA synthesis process on an
m\times n
array, in which at most
\rho
strands per row advance during each cycle, while multiple columns may proceed concurrently. This abstraction captures the effective resource coupling observed in the experimental system of Sack et al.
[
14
]
and provides a theoretical framework for analyzing the efficiency limits of spatially constrained parallel synthesis.

Recall from Figure
1
that the primary challenge involved in the complex synthesis process was determining which of the
w
possible nucleotides to assign to a collection of, say,
k
strands, where we are allowed to assign any of the
w
nucleotides to as many of the
k
strands as we would like.
In
Section

V
, we initiate the study of the setting where we can assign each of the
w
nucleotides to at most a certain number of programmed strands.
As a starting point, in this paper, we begin with the setting where
k=2
,
w=1
, and each nucleotide from the synthesis strand can be assigned to at most a single strand.

Even in this simple setting, the problem does not have a straightforward answer, and many of the techniques developed previously appear incompatible under the new framework. For this setting, we design a dynamic-programming algorithm and outline open questions that may be addressed in future research.

The distinction between the fully parallel setting and the array model lies in how much parallelism is permitted per cycle.
In the standard and complex synthesis settings, strands do not interfere with one another: in each cycle, any strand whose next base lies in the available set (of size
w
) may advance.
By contrast, the array model enforces row-level coupling. That is, at most one strand per row may advance in a given cycle.

For a concrete comparison, let
w=1
, the periodic synthesis sequence
(ACGTACGT\ldots)
, and target strands
ACG
and
CGT
.
Then, in the fully parallel model, both are completed in four cycles.
Under the array constraint, if the two strands share a row, only one may advance per cycle; with the fixed
ACGT
phase, the shortest completion takes seven cycles (e.g., synthesize
CGT
using cycles 2-4, and
ACG
using cycles 1,6, and 7), because in the second cycle one cannot synthesize
C
in both strands.
This added coupling fundamentally changes the optimization compared with the fully parallel case.

.

II-B

Problem Statement

Extending the model introduced in
[
9
]
, consider a system where
k
DNA strands
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\Sigma_{q}^{*}
are synthesized in parallel.
In our model, synthesis proceeds by choosing a fixed complex synthesis sequence
{\bm{s}}=({\bm{\psi}}_{1},{\bm{\psi}}_{2},\ldots)\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}
.
In each cycle, for each DNA strand
{\bm{x}}_{j}
, we may either append one nucleotide from
{\bm{\psi}}_{i}
to the strand
{\bm{x}}_{j}
or do nothing.
A strand
{\bm{x}}
can be synthesized by
{\bm{s}}
if and only if
{\bm{x}}
is a sub-instance of
{\bm{s}}
.

For sequences
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}
and
{\bm{x}}\in\Sigma_{q}^{*}
, define
{\bm{s}}_{i:j}\coloneqq({\bm{\psi}}_{i},{\bm{\psi}}_{i+1},\ldots,{\bm{\psi}}_{j})
and
{\bm{x}}_{i:j}\coloneqq(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1},\ldots,\sigma_{j})
as contiguous subsequences.
Let
N^{n}({\bm{s}},\tau)
denote the number of distinct sequences over
\Sigma_{q}^{n}
that can be synthesized by
{\bm{s}}_{1:\tau}
; let
N({\bm{s}},\tau)
denote the number over
\Sigma_{q}^{*}
.
The following lemma is immediate.

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}
, and let
\tau
be a positive integer. Then,

\displaystyle N^{n}({\bm{s}},\tau)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{1:\tau},\tau-n)|

\displaystyle N({\bm{s}},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\tau}|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{1:\tau},i)|

Proof:

Every
{\bm{y}}\in N^{n}({\bm{s}},\tau)
is, by definition, a length-
n
sub-instance of
{\bm{s}}_{1:\tau}
, hence
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},\tau-n)
.
The converse is analogous.
Summing over
n
yields the second identity.
∎

Definition 5
.

Let
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}
. We define the
information rates
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{q,w}({\bm{s}},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log_{2}\left(N^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}({\bm{s}},t)\right)}{t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{q,w}({\bm{s}})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log_{2}\left(N({\bm{s}},t)\right)}{t}\,.

Then, denote

f(q,w,\alpha)\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}}\mathcal{R}_{q,w}({\bm{s}},\alpha).

And similarly,

f(q,w)\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}}\mathcal{R}_{q,w}({\bm{s}}).

We now state the problems we address.

Problem 1
.

Compute
f(q,w,\alpha)
and
f(q,w)
, and find semi-infinite sequences
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{*}
that attain these values.

III
presents our results for
1
.

5
coincides with
[
9
]
;
[

solves

.
For

,
f(q,q)=\log_{2}(q)
, because all strands advance every cycle; this serves as a trivial upper bound.

fixes the complex synthesis sequence.

II-C

Previous Work

The problem of efficient synthesis was first introduced in
[
9
]
, where the goal was to determine the maximum number of nucleotides that can be encoded across multiple strands given
t
synthesis cycles.
In particular,
[
9
]
solved
1
for the case
w=1
.
In that work, the authors proved that for
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, the sequence that maximizes the rate is the periodic sequence
{\bm{p}}_{q}
, i.e.,

{\bm{p}}_{q}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{q},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{q},\ldots).

Moreover, the authors derived the exact values of
f(q,1)
and
f(q,1,\alpha)
, as stated in
[
9
, Theorem 3]
and
[
15
, Proposition 6.7]
.
Their main results can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 1

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
q
be an integer and
0\leq\alpha<1
. Then,

f(q,1)=-\log_{2}(x_{q})\,,

where
x_{q}
is the unique positive solution to
\sum_{i=1}^{q}x^{i}=1
. Also,

\displaystyle f(q,1,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\alpha\log_{2}(q)&\,\alpha<\frac{2}{q+1}\,,\\
\alpha\log_{2}\left(\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{q}i\cdot(x_{q,\alpha})^{i-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)&\,\frac{2}{q+1}<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

where
x_{q,\alpha}
is the unique solution to
\sum_{i=1}^{q}(1-\alpha i)x^{i}=0
, on the interval
0<x<1
.

In addition,
[
9
]
presented an explicit code construction for the periodic synthesis sequence
{\bm{p}}_{q}
.
They showed that any strand of length
n-1
can be encoded into a sequence of length
n
, synthesizable in
\left\lfloor\frac{q+1}{2}n\right\rfloor
cycles.
Subsequent works expanded on these results.
In
[
10
]
, new codes were proposed that achieve a high information rate, approaching the capacity.
The authors used the same periodic synthesis sequence of length
n
, and introduced coding schemes for any
0<\rho<1
, to synthesize strands of length
\rho n
.
In
[
12
]
, the authors generalized the synthesis codes by introducing a family of low-rate codes, and in
[
16
]
the same authors analyzed the redundancy and information rate of such constructions for asymptotically large codeword lengths.
In parallel,
[
11
]
considered an alternative setting in which shortmers were used as synthesis symbols.

Also, in
[
17
]
the authors introduced the problem of synthesis in batches, i.e., to partition the sequences to be synthesized into batches of equal size, and synthesize each batch separately, to minimize the number of synthesis cycles.
The authors presented bounds on the number of cycles that are required to synthesize all the batches, using an optimal partition of the sequences beforehand.
In
[
18
]
, the authors gave a novel approach to optimize the bounds of
[
17
]
with a polynomial-time concurrent zero-knowledge proof protocol.

In
[
19
]
, a single-indel-correcting code was presented, which is also limited by the number of synthesis cycles needed to synthesize each codeword, i.e., each strand. In other words, the authors constructed an explicit VT-code, which has only codewords that can be synthesized in
T
cycles for a fixed
T
. Additionally, in
[
20
]
, the authors improved the code of
[
19
]
and also presented another code construction, which satisfies the run-length-limited constraint as well. Moreover, in
[
21
]
, the authors presented codes and explicit constructions for correcting errors that are the result of a synthesis defect, i.e., when the synthesis machine does not add the required nucleotide in a given cycle to any of the strands.
Also, in
[
22
]
the authors proposed a code that can be synthesized in
T
synthesis cycles while maintaining the
\ell
-RLL and
\epsilon
-balanced constraints. They provided an explicit algorithm for encoding and decoding sequences of length
n
in a runtime complexity of
O(n^{4}\ell^{2})
, and a capacity-achieving redundancy. The authors also provided an extension to handle indel errors with an additional redundancy.

These prior works fully characterize the case
w=1
; in the next section, we broaden the scope to
w>1
and establish the information-theoretic limits of the complex synthesis model.

III-A

The Size of the Sub-instance Ball

In this subsection, we study
\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},t)
, the
t

{\bm{s}}=({\bm{\psi}}_{1},{\bm{\psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{\psi}}_{n})\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
. We generalize definitions and results from
[
9
]
to handle complex sequences.
For
\sigma\in\Sigma_{q}
and
{\mathbf{A}}\subseteq\Sigma_{q}^{*}
, define
\sigma\circ{\mathbf{A}}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}=(\sigma,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\colon\allowbreak(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in{\mathbf{A}}\}
.

Definition 7
.

Let
\Sigma_{{\bm{s}},t}=\cup_{i=1}^{t+1}{\bm{\psi}}_{i}
, i.e., the set of all symbols from
\Sigma_{q}
appearing in the first
t+1
complex symbols of
{\bm{s}}
.
For
\sigma\in\Sigma_{{\bm{s}},t}
, let

denote the index of the first complex symbol

in
{\bm{s}}
that contains

.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0<n,t\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
.
Then,

\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},t)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{{\bm{s}},t}}\sigma\circ\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{j_{\sigma}+1:n},t-j_{\sigma}+1).

Consequently,

|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},t)|\,\,=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{{\bm{s}},t}}|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{j_{\sigma}+1:n},t-j_{\sigma}+1)|.

Proof:

This result follows immediately from the same arguments as
[
23
, Lemma 1]
.
∎

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
) that the size of the single-deletion ball of
{\bm{x}}\in\Sigma_{q}
equals the number of runs in
{\bm{x}}
.
The following theorem generalizes this result to complex sequences.
Recall from
Definition

4
that
r({\bm{s}})
denotes the vector of complex runs of
{\bm{s}}
, and
r_{k}({\bm{s}})
denotes the number of type-
k
complex runs, i.e., the entries of
r({\bm{s}})
equal
k
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
n>1
and
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|=w^{n-2}\,\sum_{k=1}^{w}k\,r_{k}({\bm{s}})\,.

Proof:

Since
t=1
,
Lemma

2
implies the following recursion:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|

\displaystyle=|{\bm{\psi}}_{1}|\cdot|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{2:n},1)|+|{\bm{\psi}}_{2}\setminus{\bm{\psi}}_{1}|\cdot|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{3:n},0)|

\displaystyle=w\cdot|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{2:n},1)|+(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}\cdot|\mathcal{I}({\bm{s}}_{3:n})|

\displaystyle=w\cdot|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}}_{2:n},1)|+(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}\cdot w^{n-2}\,.

(1)

Thus, we provide a proof using induction on
n
.
For
n=2
,
{\bm{s}}=({\bm{\psi}}_{1},{\bm{\psi}}_{2})
. Then by
Lemma

2
,
|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|=|{\bm{\psi}}_{1}|+|{\bm{\psi}}_{2}\setminus{\bm{\psi}}_{1}|=w+(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}
.
Since
w^{n-2}=1
, and
r_{k}({\bm{s}})=0
except possibly for
k=w
and
k=(r(s))_{2}
, the expression
w^{n-2}\sum_{k=1}^{w}kr_{k}({\bm{s}})
equals
w+(r(s))_{2}
, as required.

We proceed by induction on
n
.
Using (
1
) and the inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|

\displaystyle=w\left(w^{n-3}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{w}k\cdot r_{k}({\bm{s}}_{2:n})\right)+(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}\cdot w^{n-2}

\displaystyle=w^{n-2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{w}kr_{k}({\bm{s}}_{2:n})+(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}\right).

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{w}k\cdot r_{k}({\bm{s}})-\sum_{k=1}^{w}k\cdot r_{k}({\bm{s}}_{2:n})=(r({\bm{s}}))_{2}
, the claim follows, which completes the proof.
∎

Note that for

, the value of

for a (traditional) sequence

is exactly the number of runs in
{\bm{x}}
, and thus

2
coincides with the result for the single-deletion ball when

.

Example 4

Using the notation from

Claim 2
.

Let

,
q
and

be positive integers, and let
{\bm{s}}\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
. Then,

1.

w^{n-1}\leq|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|

2.

If

, then
|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|\leq w^{n-1}\cdot n
, and the sequences
{\bm{s}}^{(1)}=({\bm{\psi}}_{1},{\bm{\psi}}_{2},\ldots)\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
, such that

for all

, are the only sequences that attain the maximum of the single-sub-instance ball.

3.

If
w>\left\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\right\rfloor
, then
|\mathcal{D}({\bm{s}},1)|\leq w^{n-1}+w^{n-2}(n-1)(q-w)
, and the sequences
{\bm{s}}^{(2)}=({\bm{\psi}}_{1},{\bm{\psi}}_{2},\ldots)\in\Psi_{q,w}^{n}
, such that
|{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\cap{\bm{\psi}}_{i+1}|=2w-q
for all

, are the only sequences that attain the maximum of the single-sub-instance ball.

Proof:

Since
—
D
(
s
,1)— = w^n-2 ⋅∑_k=1^wk⋅r_k(

),
and if

, the maximum is obtained if

for, hence

and
r_{k}({\bm{s}})=0
for all

, i.e., the maximal value in each entry of

.
Then, we note that
{\bm{s}}^{(1)}
is the only structure of the complex sequences for which it holds that

.

Otherwise, if
w>\left\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\right\rfloor
, the maximum is obtained when

, since one cannot have two adjacent symbols with an empty intersection, and

is the minimal intersection size.

It is obtained by choosing the symbol

to contain the

, and thus leave

The minimum is obtained if

and

for all

.
Sequences of the form

are the only complex sequence for which it holds that

\displaystyle c_{i}=\{

Example 5
.

Let

\displaystyle\Sigma_{6}=\{\sigma_{0}\!=0,\sigma_{1}\!=1,\sigma_{2}\!=2,\sigma_{3}\!=3,\sigma_{4}\!=4,\sigma_{5}\!=5\}\,,

-sub-instance ball of the periodic complex sequence.

Lemma 3
.

Let

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}({\bm{c}}_{n},t)|

\displaystyle=w\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\mathcal{D}({\bm{c}}_{n-i},t-i+1)|

\displaystyle+(q-w\cdot m)\cdot|\mathcal{D}({\bm{c}}_{n-m-1},t-m)|.

Proof:

Using
Lemma

2
, it holds that

(

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\bigcup_{\sigma\in c_{i}}\sigma\circ\mathcal{D}(({\bm{c}}_{n})_{(i+1):n},t-i+1)\right)

\displaystyle\cup\left(\bigcup_{\sigma\in(c_{m+1}\setminus c_{1})}\sigma\circ\mathcal{D}(({\bm{c}}_{n})_{(m+2):n},t-m)\right).

Note that if

, then

In this section, we address
2
by introducing in

IV-A

Dynamic Programming Approach

Our approach is a generalization of the well-studied problem of computing the shortest common supersequence (SCS)
[
26
]
.
Let
{\bm{x}}[j]
be the
j
-th entry of
{\bm{x}}
, and set
{\bm{x}}[0]=\varepsilon
, i.e., the empty word. For contiguous substrings, use
{\bm{x}}[i\colon j]
.
Next, we need to introduce new notations, based on
[
26
]
, to establish the recursion behind the dynamic programming approach, which is presented in Claim
4
. For
{\bm{\psi}}\in\Psi_{q,w}
,
{\bm{x}}\in\Sigma_{q}^{*}
, and
i\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}},i)=\begin{cases}i-1,&\text{if }{\bm{x}}[i]\in{\bm{\psi}},\\
i,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

And assume
g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}},0)=0
by convention. For example,

\displaystyle g(\{A,C\},(A,C,G),2)=1,

\displaystyle g(\{A,C\},(A,C,G),3)=3.

For brevity, let
g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},i_{1\to k})
represent the following sequence,
(g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}}_{1},i_{1}),g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}}_{2},i_{2}),\ldots,g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}}_{k},i_{k}))
.
Lastly, denote

L[i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]\coloneqq|SCCS({\bm{x}}_{1}[1\!:\!i_{1}],{\bm{x}}_{2}[1\!:\!i_{2}],\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k}[1\!:\!i_{k}])|,

where
{\bm{x}}_{j}[1:0]
is the empty string.

Claim 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k}\}\subseteq\Sigma_{q}^{*}
be sequences of lengths
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
, respectively. For all
0\leq i_{j}\leq n_{j}
, let
i_{j}^{\prime}=\max\{0,i_{j}-1\}
and
U=\{{\bm{x}}_{j}[i_{j}]\colon i_{j}>0\}_{j=1}^{k}
.
Also, let
V=\{{\bm{\psi}}\in\Psi_{q,w}\colon U\cap{\bm{\psi}}\neq\emptyset\}
represent the valid symbols.
Then,
L[i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }i_{1}=\dots=i_{k}=0\\
L[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{k}^{\prime}]\!+\!1&\text{ if}\,\left|U\right|\!\leq\!w\\
\min_{{\bm{\psi}}\in V}\{L[g({\bm{\psi}},\!{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},\!i_{1\to k})]\}\!+\!1&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where the minimum in the last case is taken over
{\bm{\psi}}\in\Psi_{q,w}
such that there exists
{\bm{x}}_{j}\in\mathcal{X}
for which
{\bm{x}}_{j}[i_{j}]\in{\bm{\psi}}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that we only consider
{\bm{\psi}}\in V
because, without it,
g({\bm{\psi}},{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},i_{1\to k})
would return
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
, creating a circular argument and making this case undefined.

One way to interpret the term
L[g({\bm{\psi}},\!{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},\!i_{1\to k})]
in the previous claim is as follows. For every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
, we form a new set, which we refer to as
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, as follows: (i) If
{\bm{x}}
does not end with a symbol in
{\bm{\psi}}
, add
{\bm{x}}
to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, (ii) If
{\bm{x}}
ends with a symbol from
{\bm{\psi}}
, remove the symbol and add the resulting sequence to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
.
Then, the quantity
L[g({\bm{\psi}},\!{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},\!i_{1\to k})]
is the length of the complex sequence that results from concatenating the SCCS of the sequences in
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
{\bm{\psi}}
.

Algorithm

1
finds an SCCS of
k
sequences and its length.
During every iteration of the algorithm, it finds an SCCS of
{\bm{x}}_{1}[1\colon i_{1}],{\bm{x}}_{2}[1\colon i_{2}],\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k}[1\colon i_{k}]
, following the recursion established in
4
, so that there are two cases to consider:

1.

There exists
{\bm{\psi}}\in\Psi_{q,w}
, such that
U\subseteq{\bm{\psi}}
.
In this case, an SCCS can be formed by appending the symbol
{\bm{\psi}}
to an SCCS of the sequences
{\bm{x}}_{1}[1\colon i_{1}^{\prime}],{\bm{x}}_{2}[1\colon i_{2}^{\prime}],\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k}[1\colon i_{k}^{\prime}]
.

2.

The algorithm searches for the complex symbol
{\bm{\psi}}
that minimizes
L[g({\bm{\psi}},\!{\bm{x}}_{1\to k},\!i_{1\to k})]
.
At least one index strictly decreases; hence, the recursion terminates.

Finally, for
U
, let
{\bm{\psi}}_{U}\in\Psi_{q,w}
be an arbitrary symbol such that
U\subseteq{\bm{\psi}}_{U}
.

Efficient Synthesis for Two-Dimensional Strand Arrays with Row Constraints

In this section, we introduce another theoretical synthesis model.
Suppose you need to synthesize strands arranged in an
m\times n
matrix, where each cell represents a strand.
Per cycle, at most one strand per row may advance; multiple strands in the same column may advance in the same cycle.
That is, in each clock cycle, the synthesis machine generates one symbol according to its predefined (not complex) synthesis sequence
{\bm{s}}\in\Sigma_{q}^{*}
.
In each cycle, at most one strand per row may advance; thus, up to
m
strands (one per row) can advance simultaneously, possibly in the same or different columns.

In this work, we wish to choose the machine’s synthesis sequence optimally for a fixed set of strands; that is, what is the optimal way to program the synthesis machine and determine which base to synthesize in each cycle?

Let

Relation to the SCS Problem

If
n=1
, the problem was solved in
[
9
]
as the SCS of all the strands.
We wish to connect the problem of the optimal synthesis sequence in the array model to an SCS problem.

Let
R_{k}=\{{\bm{x}}_{k,1},{\bm{x}}_{k,2},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{k,n}\}
be the sequences in the
k
-th row.
Denote by
\mathcal{W}_{k}
the interleavings of
R_{k}
, i.e., the set of all merges that preserve the within-strand orders.
Note that each
w\in\mathcal{W}
is of length
nL
, the sum of the lengths of all the sequences in
R_{k}
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
T^{*}
be the length of a shortest synthesis sequence for an
m\times n
array of sequences.
Then,

T^{*}=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}_{2},\\
\ldots,w_{m}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}\end{subarray}}\left|SCS(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{m})\right|

Proof:

Take any synthesis sequence
{\bm{s}}
(the emitted symbols).
For each row
k
, let
S_{k}
be the subsequence of
{\bm{s}}
consisting of the cycles in which row k advanced.
Because a row advances at most one strand per cycle and must respect each strand’s order,
S_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
.
Also,
{\bm{s}}
is a common supersequence of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
.
Hence,
|{\bm{s}}|\geq SCS(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})
, and

\displaystyle SCS(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})\geq\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}_{2},\\
\ldots,w_{m}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}\end{subarray}}\left|SCS(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\right|.

Since it is true for any
{\bm{s}}
, and
T^{*}
is obtained by minimizing over all
{\bm{s}}
, it yields the stated lower bound.

Conversely, fix any choice
w_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
for all
k
, and let
{\bm{s}}
be a shortest common supersequence of
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
.
Clearly,
{\bm{s}}
is a valid synthesis sequence: on each symbol
r
of
{\bm{s}}
, in each row
k
advance the row’s pointer in
w_{k}
if its next symbol equals
r
, and realize that advance by moving exactly one of that row’s strands that contributes that next symbol in
w_{k}
.
This never gets stuck (since
s
is a supersequence of every
w_{k}
), and respects the “at most per row per cycle” rule.
Thus,
|{\bm{s}}|
cycles suffice and
T^{*}\leq|s|
by its definition, including the RHS in the theorem.
Together with the lower bound, equality holds.
∎

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

We aimed to find
f(q,w)
and
f(q,w,\alpha)
, i.e., the maximum information rate for synthesis over
\Psi_{q,w}
, and to identify the sequences that achieve it.
Moreover, we intended to compute the SCCS to find the shortest complex synthesis sequence for a given set of
k
sequences to synthesize.
We extended Lenz’s results
[
9
]
for
w=1
by completely solving
f(q,w)
and
f(q,w,\alpha)
and found the complex synthesis sequences that achieve the maximum information rate.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis of the information rate under the complex synthesis model and also the first analysis of the sub-instance ball as a mathematical structure.

Also, we provided an efficient algorithm for finding the SCCS for a given set of sequences, as well as a simple approximation algorithm for cases where the number of given sequences is large; however, this can be extended to additional approximation algorithms in future work.

Furthermore, for the complex synthesis model, further research can be conducted on synthesis coding and addressing error correction, whether caused by synthesis defects or at any other step during the process of DNA synthesis, storage, or sequencing.

Finally, there is more research to be done on the two-dimensional array synthesis model. One example is the scenario in which the synthesis machine works according to the alternating sequence, the strands are assigned to the cells in the
m\times n
array, and some questions one can ask are as follows.
What is the optimal way to program the synthesis machine and determine which base to synthesize in each cycle? How to encode the strands such that the maximum synthesis time is minimized?
