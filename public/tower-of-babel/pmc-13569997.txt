Association between the ratio of uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (UHR) and the abnormal risk of sarcopenia: Evidence from two large population-based surveys and interpretable machine learning-driven sarcopenia screening
Authors: Yunnong Luo, Aisheng Wang, Minfei Wu, Yang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia is a geriatric syndrome linked to nutritional intake, chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. While uric acid (UA) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) are recognized biomarkers, their specific relationship with sarcopenia remains debated. The uric acid-to-HDL-C ratio (UHR) has emerged as a novel, integrated biomarker. This study investigates the UHR-sarcopenia association using representative US and South Korean populations and develops an interpretable machine learning screening framework.ObjectivesTo investigate the association between UHR and sarcopenia in two nationally representative populations and to develop and externally validate an interpretable machine-learning model for sarcopenia screening.DesignA population-based cross-sectional study using NHANES 2011–2018 as the primary cohort and KNHANES 2024 as the external validation cohort.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 7,314 participants from NHANES (2011–2018) and 3,274 from KNHANES (2024). The UHR-sarcopenia relationship was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, smooth curve fitting, and subgroup analyses. To develop a screening model for prevalent sarcopenia, feature selection was performed using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm. Machine learning models were trained on the NHANES cohort, interpreted using SHAP values, and rigorously validated externally using the KNHANES dataset.ResultsHigher UHR was significantly associated with greater odds of prevalent sarcopenia. In fully adjusted models, participants in the highest UHR quartile had higher odds of sarcopenia than those in the lowest quartile in NHANES (OR 2.014, 95% CI 1.552–2.625; P<0.001) and KNHANES (OR 1.658, 95% CI 1.183–2.342; P=0.004). Among seven machine-learning models, LightGBM demonstrated the most balanced performance, achieving an AUC of 0.810 in NHANES and maintaining good discrimination during external validation in KNHANES (AUC 0.823).ConclusionsElevated UHR is significantly associated with sarcopenia. The UHR-integrated LightGBM model demonstrates robust discriminative capacity, serving as a practical and interpretable tool for sarcopenia screening in clinical practice.Keywords: uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, sarcopenia, NHANES, KNHANES, machine learning

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive musculoskeletal disorder characterized by an accelerated loss of muscle mass and function with advancing age. 1 It is closely associated with adverse health outcomes in older populations, predisposing individuals to an increased risk of fractures, physical disability, and elevated mortality. 2 The global prevalence of sarcopenia is estimated to range from 10% to 27%, a figure that continues to rise steadily in tandem with the rapid aging of the global population.3,4 Consequently, the escalating healthcare expenditures allocated for the prevention and management of sarcopenia—across community, perioperative, and general clinical settings—impose a substantial personal, societal, and economic burden. 5

Therefore, elucidating the pathogenic factors underlying sarcopenia is of paramount importance, as it deepens our mechanistic understanding and facilitates the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies from an epidemiological perspective.6,7 Accordingly, the present study focuses on identifying factors and biomarkers associated with prevalent sarcopenia to inform the development of early interventions, thereby mitigating the long-term adverse health consequences of muscle loss.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the pathogenesis of sarcopenia is multifactorial, encompassing genetic predisposition, malnutrition, and lifestyle factors, alongside chronic comorbidities such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and stroke.8–13 Currently, various conventional biomarkers—including triglycerides, glucose, creatinine, platelet count, and lymphocyte count—have been extensively investigated to characterize their associations with the development of sarcopenia.14,15 Notably, uric acid, a key byproduct of purine metabolism, serves as a pivotal nutritional and metabolic biomarker frequently implicated in chronic inflammation and oxidative stress; it has been identified as a risk factor for adverse outcomes including dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and metabolic syndrome, thereby highlighting its broad systemic impact on health. 16 Emerging evidence indicates a similarly close relationship between UA and sarcopenia, with elevated UA levels have been reported to be associated with sarcopenia. 17 Conversely, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol is widely recognized as a protective factor against chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, owing to its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, as well as its capacity for reverse cholesterol transport.18,19 Building upon these mechanistic insights, researchers have proposed UHR as a novel metabolic biomarker, demonstrating its significant associations with various disorders, including depression, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes.20–22 As a composite index, UHR integrates the combined effects of elevated UA and depleted HDL-C levels, thereby offering a more sensitive and accurate reflection of an individual’s systemic metabolic and nutritional status than either parameter alone. 23 Given its established efficacy as an integrated metabolic and nutritional indicator, investigating the specific role of UHR in the context of sarcopenia is both biologically plausible and highly warranted. 24

Machine learning (ML) possesses a robust capacity to process large-scale, complex, and heterogeneous datasets, offering a synthesis of comprehensiveness and predictive accuracy that traditional statistical approaches often lack. 25 Previous studies have demonstrated that ML models utilizing basic demographic and baseline clinical variables can effectively screen for conditions such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease within the general population. 26 Building upon this premise, the present study aims to develop and validate an interpretable ML framework that seamlessly integrates demographic profiles with the UHR to accurately identify prevalent sarcopenia.

Therefore, the present study conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional analysis to investigate the association between the UHR and sarcopenia in the general population. We hypothesized that an elevated UHR is associated with an increased prevalence of sarcopenia, potentially reflecting underlying metabolic perturbations and inflammatory dysregulation. Elucidating this relationship could yield novel insights into the metabolic and inflammatory pathways implicated in sarcopenia, thereby identifying potential biomarkers for early detection or novel therapeutic targets.

Methods

Participants of study

The present study is a population-based cross-sectional analysis utilizing data derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) databases. Both NHANES and KNHANES are continuous national survey programs primarily designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the general populations in the United States and South Korea, respectively. To ensure national representativeness, both surveys employ a rigorous stratified, multistage probability sampling design, collecting comprehensive data through structured interviews, standardized physical examinations, and detailed laboratory assessments. 27 Comprehensive details regarding the study protocols, methodologies, and data collection procedures are publicly accessible via their respective official websites (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes and https://biobank.nih.go.kr).

Data for this study were retrieved from the 2011–2018 NHANES and 2024 KNHANES datasets. The specific inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) availability of complete data on appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), body mass index (BMI), UA, and HDL-C; (2) complete information regarding covariates, including age, sex, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, stroke, malignant tumors, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and diabetes mellitus. The detailed participant selection workflow is illustrated in Figure 1.

The participant selection flow-chart.

The NHANES study protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board (ERB), and the KNHANES study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in both surveys. This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified data; therefore, no additional ethical approval was required. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. 28

Measurement of UHR

The primary exposure variable, UHR, was derived from laboratory biochemical parameters obtained from both the NHANES and KNHANES databases. Serum HDL-C levels were quantified using direct immunoassay or precipitation methods, whereas UA concentrations were evaluated via a timed endpoint method. Specifically, the UHR was mathematically calculated by dividing the UA concentration (mg/dL) by the HDL-C concentration (mg/dL), and the resultant ratio was expressed as a percentage. 29

Detection and definitions of sarcopenia

The diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in the present study were established in accordance with the consensus guidelines of the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health Sarcopenia Project. 5 Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was calculated as the sum of bone-free lean mass in all four extremities, derived from whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans. 30 Subsequently, ASM was adjusted based on BMI. According to the standards of the National Institutes of Health Foundation (FNIH) for sarcopenia, low muscle mass is defined as a ratio of male ASM to BMI less than 0.789, and for females, it is less than 0.512. This was applied simultaneously to the NHANES and KNHANES datasets in this study.31,32

Muscle strength and physical performance were not incorporated into the primary outcome definition because harmonized functional measurements were not available throughout the entire study period. Specifically, handgrip strength was available only in the 2011–2014 NHANES cycles, whereas comparable gait-speed measurements were not available across the 2011–2018 cycles. Restricting the analysis to participants with available muscle-function measurements would therefore have substantially reduced the study period and sample size and would have limited the consistency of the cross-cohort analysis. Accordingly, the present operational definition primarily captures the low-muscle-mass component of the broader sarcopenia phenotype and should not be interpreted as equivalent to a comprehensive clinical diagnosis incorporating muscle function.33,34

Assessment of covariates

Based on established associations with the UHR and sarcopenia in prior literature, several key covariates were identified a priori for subsequent analyses, including age, sex, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, stroke, malignant tumors, CVD and diabetes mellitus. In the two cohorts of NHANES and KNHANES, adults over the age of 20 were selected, smoking status was categorized via questionnaire responses into three groups: never smokers (<100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former smokers (≥100 cigarettes in a lifetime but not currently smoking), and current smokers (≥100 cigarettes in a lifetime and currently smoking). Alcohol consumption was dichotomized as drinkers and non-drinkers based on whether participants had consumed more than 12 alcoholic drinks over the past year. Hypertension was defined by either a self-reported physician diagnosis or a calculated average of three separate blood pressure measurements yielding a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes mellitus was identified through a self-reported physician diagnosis or a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥ 6.5%.35,36 CVD was comprehensively defined based on self-reported history or having been informed by a healthcare professional of a diagnosis of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, or myocardial infarction. 37 Detailed protocols regarding questionnaire administration, specimen collection, processing, quality assurance, and monitoring are publicly accessible on the respective official websites of NHANES and KNHANES.

Statistical analysis

In this study, survey-weighted statistical analyses were utilized to describe the baseline characteristics of participants with and without sarcopenia. Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and weighted percentages. Differences between groups were evaluated using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and Welch two-sample t-test for continuous variables. To investigate the independent association between the UHR and sarcopenia, three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, with results presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and marital status. Model 3, the fully adjusted model, incorporated all remaining covariates based on Model 2. Furthermore, the UHR was categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group, to conduct tests for linear trends (P for trend). Exploratory subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed in both the NHANES and KNHANES cohorts to assess potential heterogeneity in the association between UHR and sarcopenia across predefined strata. Additionally, smooth curve fitting and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to explore potential non-linear dose-response relationships between the UHR and sarcopenia. To quantify the incremental predictive value of UHR relative to conventional clinical variables, we additionally constructed four basic clinical logistic regression prediction models, including models that incorporate common clinical variables such as age and gender, and models that add UA and HDL to these bases. Subsequently, by incorporating UHR into the basic clinical models, we compared the discriminatory capabilities of the four models using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and further evaluated the calibration and clinical practicability of the models through calibration curves and DCA curves to assess the incremental value of UHR. Finally, to develop a model for screening prevalent sarcopenia and to evaluate the contribution of UHR to model classification, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and the Boruta algorithm were applied for feature selection within the NHANES dataset, and the selected features were subsequently incorporated into ML model development. The original dataset was randomly partitioned into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Seven ML algorithms including K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), Random Forest (RF), LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost were trained and systematically compared using consistent evaluation metrics. and a commonly used and reasonable method for hyperparameter optimization was adopted (Supplemental Table S1)).38,39 SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to interpret the selected LightGBM model by quantifying the contribution of individual features to model predictions and were interpreted as model-derived feature contributions. Global feature importance was summarized using the mean absolute SHAP value for each predictor. Furthermore, the 2024 KNHANES dataset was used for external validation of model discrimination. Meanwhile, in order to verify the stability of the model, we conducted a sensitivity analysis based on it (Supplementary Materials). Lastly, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed by calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF < 10 indicating the absence of significant collinearity.

All statistical analyses were performed using DecisionLinnc software (version 1.0). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05 for all tests. 40 To ensure the analytical accuracy and robustness of our findings, all results underwent multiple validation steps.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 summarizes the weighted baseline demographic, anthropometric, and laboratory characteristics of the NHANES participants. Among the 7,314 participants included in the final analysis, 702 were diagnosed with sarcopenia. The mean age of the cohort was 41.53 ± 11.36 years, comprising 3,762 (51.44%) females. Notably, participants in the sarcopenia group exhibited significantly elevated UHR levels compared to those in the non-sarcopenia group (13.37 ± 7.83 vs. 11.69 ± 5.37, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the two groups demonstrated significant differences across the vast majority of baseline covariates, including age, race, marital status, and the prevalences of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, cancer, and CVD (all P < 0.05). Table 2 delineates the baseline profiles of the KNHANES external validation cohort. Of the 3,274 enrolled participants, 441 were identified as having sarcopenia. The mean age of this validation cohort was 60.59 ± 12.00 years, with 1,849 (56.48%) female participants. Consistent with the NHANES findings, UHR levels were substantially higher in the sarcopenic individuals compared to their non-sarcopenic counterparts (12.05 ± 3.31 vs. 11.02 ± 3.26).

Association between UHR and sarcopenia

Table 3 delineates the potential association between the UHR and sarcopenia assessed via multivariable logistic regression analyses. A significant positive association between the UHR and sarcopenia was consistently observed across all three analytical models: the unadjusted Model 1 (OR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.031–1.056, P < 0.001 for NHANES; OR = 1.092, 95% CI: 1.061–1.123, P < 0.001 for KNHANES), the partially adjusted Model 2 incorporating basic demographics (age, sex, and marital status) (OR = 1.040, 95% CI: 1.027–1.054, P < 0.001 for NHANES; OR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.011–1.077, P < 0.001 for KNHANES), and the fully adjusted Model 3 incorporating all covariates (OR = 1.034, 95% CI: 1.020–1.048, P < 0.001 for NHANES; OR = 1.038, 95% CI: 1.004–1.071, P < 0.001 for KNHANES). To further elucidate the complex relationship between the UHR and sarcopenia and to provide more clinically interpretable outcomes, the continuous UHR variable was converted into categorical quartiles, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. In the NHANES cohort, compared to the reference group, participants in the highest UHR quartile (Q4) exhibited a 135.7% (P < 0.001), 119.6% (P < 0.001), and 101.4% (P < 0.001) higher higher odds of prevalent sarcopenia in Models 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Similarly, in the KNHANES cohort, the highest UHR quartile was associated with a 126.4% (P < 0.001), 75.2% (P < 0.001), and 65.8% (P < 0.001) increased higher odds of prevalent sarcopenia across the three corresponding models. Trend tests conducted across the three models revealed highly significant linear trends in the unadjusted model (P for trend < 0.001); however, this statistical significance was attenuated in the adjusted models. Additionally, VIF values for all included covariates ranged from 1.028 to 1.379, remaining well below the threshold of 10, thereby indicating the absence of multicollinearity.

Association between the UHR and Sarcopenia by multiple logistic models.

To further delve into the underlying relationship between the UHR and sarcopenia, restricted cubic spline (RCS) fitting was employed (Figure 2), accompanied by a threshold effect analysis to elucidate their potential non-linear association (Table 4). In the KNHANES dataset, a significant non-linear dose-response relationship was consistently observed across the unadjusted Model 1, the partially adjusted Model 2, and the fully adjusted Model 3 (all P for non-linearity < 0.001). An inflection point was identified at a UHR of 9.259, revealing distinctly different magnitudes of the UHR–sarcopenia association on either side of this threshold. Specifically, in the KNHANES cohort, threshold-effect analysis identified an inflection point at UHR = 9.259. Below this point, each one-unit increase in UHR was associated with higher odds of prevalent sarcopenia (OR = 1.345, 95% CI: 1.117–1.618, P = 0.002), whereas above 9.259, further increases in UHR were not significantly associated with additional increases in the odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.004, 95% CI: 0.965–1.045, P = 0.828; likelihood-ratio P = 0.003). A similar change in slope was suggested in NHANES, but the estimated inflection point differed (8.605), and the evidence for non-linearity was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted RCS model (P for non-linearity = 0.078). Therefore, the exact KNHANES-derived threshold of 9.259 was not consistently replicated across cohorts.

Association between UHR and sarcopenia in different subgroups

To rigorously validate the robustness of the association between the UHR and sarcopenia across diverse subpopulations, stratified subgroup analyses were performed within the NHANES (Figure 3) and KHANES (Supplemental Materials Figure 2) dataset based on categorical covariates, encompassing sex, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, cancer, and CVD. Furthermore, interaction tests were conducted to explore potential heterogeneity across subgroups. The association between UHR and sarcopenia was generally consistent across most strata; however, evidence of heterogeneity was observed according to hypertension and diabetes status. The association was stronger among participants without hypertension (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11) than among those with hypertension (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.97–1.05; P for interaction = 0.030). Similarly, UHR was positively associated with sarcopenia among participants without diabetes (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.08), whereas the association was attenuated among those with diabetes (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.92–1.03; P for interaction = 0.005). Corresponding subgroup analyses in KNHANES showed no significant interaction for hypertension (OR = 1.05 vs. 1.06; P for interaction = 0.889) or diabetes (OR = 1.06 vs. 1.01; P for interaction = 0.156). Therefore, the heterogeneity observed in NHANES was not replicated in KNHANES and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Predictive value of UHR

Compared with the basic clinical model, in the NHANES dataset, the inclusion of UHR significantly improved the discrimination performance in predicting sarcopenia, with the AUC increasing from 0.632 to 0.663 (Figure 4(a)). Similar improvements were also observed in the external validation dataset of KNHANES, with the AUC increasing from 0.766 to 0.779 ((Figure 4(d))). Moreover, in the analysis of calibration curves and DCA curves, the model with UHR showed an increase in net clinical benefit. These results indicate that the predictive information provided by UHR exceeds the incremental value that can be offered by conventional clinical variables or UA or HDL alone.

Feature selection

Prior to model development, LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm were applied to the prespecified candidate variables as complementary feature-screening approaches. Both methods retained all candidate features under the applied selection criteria; therefore, no variables were excluded from subsequent model development (Figure 5). Given the relatively limited and clinically prespecified feature set, the absence of feature elimination was interpreted as indicating that neither method identified a sufficiently redundant or uninformative variable for exclusion, rather than as evidence that all variables had equivalent predictive importance. The complete candidate feature set was therefore carried forward into the machine learning analyses.

Development of the machine learning model

In the present study, seven widely utilized machine learning algorithms were developed. The predictive performance of these models was comprehensively evaluated by plotting Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves (Figure 6) and calculating a consistent set of evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and the AUC values (Table 5). The specific AUC values for each model were as follows: KNN (0.767), LR (0.663), NB (0.642), RF (0.678), LightGBM (0.810), XGBoost (0.792), and CatBoost (0.791). Consequently, the LightGBM and XGBoost models achieved the highest discriminative ability. A more detailed evaluation of the LightGBM model revealed an accuracy of 93.3%, a precision of 94.4%, and an MCC of 0.529. Overall, LightGBM achieved the highest AUC among the seven evaluated models and showed competitive performance across the remaining metrics. Particularly, its recall rate and F1 score were relatively low, indicating that under the set threshold, a considerable number of sarcopenia patients were not correctly identified as positive. Therefore, in addition to the conventional accuracy rate, PR-AUC and balanced accuracy rate were also adopted to evaluate the model performance. The results showed that the balanced accuracy rates of XGBoost and LightGBM were 0.660 and 0.664 respectively, and the PR-AUC values were 0.552 and 0.561 respectively. Given that its ROC-AUC was the highest and it performed well in several complementary metrics, LightGBM was selected as the model for subsequent model interpretation based on SHAP and external validation. The SHAP analysis demonstrated that UHR made a substantial contribution to model classification, with UHR ranking among the most influential features for distinguishing participants with and without prevalent sarcopenia (Figure 7). Higher UHR values were generally associated with model outputs favoring classification as prevalent sarcopenia. SHAP analysis showed that UHR had the largest mean absolute SHAP value among the included predictors (mean |SHAP| = 0.671), followed by age (0.521), indicating that these variables made the largest average contributions to the LightGBM model predictions. The SHAP beeswarm plot further illustrated the direction and distribution of these feature contributions across individual participants (Figure 7). SHAP dependence analysis suggested a non-linear relationship between UHR and model output. No clear age-dependent pattern was observed, whereas some descriptive heterogeneity was evident between men and women.

External validation

To rigorously assess its real-world applicability, external validation of the optimal machine learning model—initially developed within the NHANES cohort—was conducted utilizing the independent KNHANES dataset. External validation of the LightGBM model developed in the NHANES cohort was performed using the independent KNHANES dataset. The model maintained good discriminative performance in KNHANES, with an AUC of 0.823, the calibration slope is 1 (95%CI: 0.882-1.113), the calibration intercept is approximately 0 (95%CI: -0. 201-0.126), and the Brier score is 0.102 (Figure 8). These findings support the transportability of the model for cross-sectional sarcopenia screening in an independent population, although further prospective validation is required before clinical implementation.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the present study is among the first to investigate the association between UHR and prevalent sarcopenia using two large population-based datasets. We observed a consistent positive association between higher UHR and prevalent sarcopenia across multivariable regression, restricted cubic spline, threshold, and subgroup analyses. Similar associations were observed in both NHANES and KNHANES, supporting the reproducibility of the epidemiological association across two populations. In addition, the machine learning analyses showed that UHR contributed substantially to the discrimination of prevalent sarcopenia, and the LightGBM model maintained good discriminative performance during external validation in KNHANES. An important consideration in interpreting the machine learning results is the class imbalance in the NHANES cohort, in which participants with sarcopenia represented a relatively small proportion of the study population. Under such conditions, overall accuracy can be disproportionately influenced by correct classification of the majority non-sarcopenia class and may therefore overstate performance for identifying the minority class. Although LightGBM achieved the highest ROC-AUC among the evaluated models, its relatively modest sensitivity at the applied classification threshold indicates that a substantial proportion of participants with sarcopenia were not classified as positive. Accordingly, we expanded model evaluation to include PR-AUC and balanced accuracy in addition to ROC-AUC, precision, recall, F1-score, and MCC, and overall accuracy was no longer interpreted in isolation. 41 Notably, substantial differences existed between the NHANES development cohort and the KNHANES external validation cohort. Participants in KNHANES were considerably older than those in NHANES (mean age, 60.59 vs. 41.53 years), and the prevalence of major comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes, also differed between the two populations. Despite these differences in demographic and clinical characteristics, the LightGBM model retained good discrimination in KNHANES, suggesting a degree of transportability across the two populations. However, external validation in a single South Korean cohort does not establish universal generalizability. Differences in ethnicity, healthcare settings, disease prevalence, lifestyle, and population characteristics may influence both discrimination and calibration. Therefore, further validation in independent European, other Asian, and ethnically diverse populations is required before broader clinical implementation. 42 Therefore, the current model should be regarded as an exploratory complementary classification tool rather than a stand-alone clinical screening instrument. 43 Importantly, however, the consistency of these findings across datasets and analytical approaches should not be interpreted as evidence that elevated UHR precedes or causes sarcopenia. Because both UHR and sarcopenia were assessed within a cross-sectional framework, the temporal direction of their relationship cannot be determined, and reverse causation remains possible. Simultaneously, a distinct non-linear dose-response relationship was identified across the unadjusted, partially adjusted, and the highly credible fully adjusted RCS models, 44 a finding further corroborated by smooth curve fitting. Building upon this established non-linearity, a threshold effect analysis was utilized to further decode their complex interrelationship. Exploratory subgroup analyses in NHANES suggested that the UHR–sarcopenia association might vary according to hypertension and diabetes status, with a stronger positive association observed among participants without these conditions. Hypertension and diabetes share several metabolic and vascular abnormalities relevant to both UHR and muscle health, including insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. One possible explanation is that, in individuals with established metabolic disease, the substantial background metabolic and inflammatory burden may attenuate the incremental information captured by UHR. Conversely, among individuals without established hypertension or diabetes, UHR may more clearly reflect underlying metabolic disturbances associated with sarcopenia. However, from a clinical perspective, the current research results do not support the use of different UHR thresholds or interpretation rules based on hypertension or diabetes status. Although the NHANES study indicated the existence of subgroup heterogeneity, no such situation was found in the KNHANES, suggesting that specific interpretations for comorbidities are premature. Therefore, at present, UHR should be interpreted in conjunction with the overall metabolic and clinical context, rather than using subgroup-specific thresholds.45,46 Furthermore, following rigorous feature selection via LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm, all predetermined covariates were retained for subsequent machine learning analyses, preliminarily underscoring the substantial diagnostic value of race, age, and the UHR for sarcopenia. Following feature selection using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm, the selected variables were incorporated into the machine learning analyses. Among the seven evaluated algorithms, LightGBM demonstrated the most balanced discriminative performance. SHAP analysis identified UHR as the feature with the largest mean absolute SHAP value, followed by age. These findings reflect model-derived feature contributions.47–49 The SHAP dependence analysis further suggested that the contribution of UHR to model prediction was non-linear. Although some sex-related heterogeneity was visually observed, particularly at moderate-to-high UHR levels, these patterns reflect the internal behavior of the fitted model. Importantly, however, feature importance derived from SHAP analysis should not be interpreted as evidence that UHR is a causal determinant of sarcopenia. Because both UHR and sarcopenia were assessed within a cross-sectional framework, the model distinguishes prevalent sarcopenia rather than predicting its future occurrence.

The UHR reflects the physiological balance between UA and HDL-C. Previous investigations exploring the independent associations of UA and HDL-C with sarcopenia have yielded equivocal and highly debated findings. Generally, UA is recognized as a prominent surrogate marker for nutritional status, inflammation, and oxidative stress; it is mechanistically capable of promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, through multiple pathways. 47 Conversely, HDL-C is traditionally recognized as a protective, anti-inflammatory factor capable of reducing the circulating levels of C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, and leukocyte counts. 48 Specifically, while several studies have documented a significant positive association between elevated UA levels and sarcopenia,49,50 contradictory evidence suggests that UA may actually exert a protective effect against muscle depletion. 51 A similar paradox exists for HDL-C: although Huang et al. identified this pivotal metabolic biomarker as a critical protective factor against sarcopenia, 52 other researchers have reported diametrically opposed conclusions, demonstrating a significant positive association between HDL-C and the presence of sarcopenia. 53 These inconsistencies suggest that UA or HDL-C considered individually may incompletely characterize the metabolic milieu associated with sarcopenia. In contrast, UHR integrates the relative information conveyed by UA and HDL-C into a single composite index, thereby simultaneously reflecting higher UA burden and lower HDL-C levels. Thus, the potential advantage of UHR lies in its ability to provide a parsimonious representation of two complementary metabolic components rather than relying on either biomarker in isolation. Importantly, our additional comparative analyses directly evaluated the predictive performance of UHR against UA alone, HDL-C alone, and UA and HDL-C entered as separate predictors, and provide further support for the potential value of UHR as an integrated and readily obtainable biomarker for sarcopenia risk assessment.

From a clinical perspective, the KNHANES-derived inflection point of UHR = 9.259 may provide an exploratory reference for risk assessment rather than a diagnostic or treatment cutoff. Below this point, increasing UHR was associated with a steeper increase in the odds of sarcopenia, whereas the association plateaued thereafter. UHR values around this range may therefore prompt closer assessment of muscle health and modifiable metabolic or lifestyle factors, although the present cross-sectional study cannot determine whether UHR-guided intervention or monitoring improves outcomes. Importantly, the exact threshold was not replicated in NHANES, where a different inflection point (8.605) was estimated and non-linearity was not significant after full adjustment. This between-cohort discrepancy suggests that the estimated inflection points may be population-specific rather than universally applicable clinical cutoffs. Therefore, prospective validation in independent populations is required before a specific UHR threshold can be recommended for clinical risk stratification or decision-making. Furthermore, by comparing the basic clinical model with the logistic regression model incorporating UHR, we demonstrated that UHR provided more favorable predictive information beyond the conventional clinical variables, further supporting its predictive value in the risk of sarcopenia. More broadly, incorporation of UHR into the machine learning framework suggests that UHR may provide complementary information for sarcopenia risk assessment. Individuals with elevated UHR may warrant closer evaluation of muscle health, nutritional status, and potentially modifiable lifestyle factors. However, neither the identified UHR threshold nor the current prediction model should be used alone to guide treatment decisions, and prospective studies are needed to determine whether UHR-informed monitoring or intervention improves clinical outcomes. Further prospective validation is required before UHR can be incorporated into routine sarcopenia screening. Future longitudinal cohort studies should include repeated assessments of UHR and sarcopenia-related measures, such as appendicular skeletal muscle mass and sarcopenia status, to determine whether changes in UHR precede the development or progression of sarcopenia. Such longitudinal data would help clarify the temporal sequence of the observed association and reduce concerns regarding reverse causation. In addition, Mendelian randomization and other genetically informed causal inference approaches may provide complementary evidence by evaluating whether genetically predicted variation in uric acid, HDL-C, or related metabolic traits is associated with sarcopenia.54–56 These approaches may be less susceptible to conventional confounding and reverse causation, although their validity depends on the underlying instrumental-variable assumptions. Therefore, longitudinal observational studies and genetically informed analyses should be regarded as complementary approaches for clarifying the temporal and potentially causal relationship between UHR-related metabolic traits and sarcopenia. Finally, class imbalance may have affected model performance because sarcopenia represented a minority class. Although LightGBM maintained good discrimination in the external KNHANES cohort, its modest sensitivity indicates limited identification of positive cases. External validation does not exclude potential overfitting. Therefore, the model should not be considered a stand-alone screening tool, and future studies should evaluate class-sensitive learning, threshold optimization, and prospective validation.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, data were meticulously selected from two high-quality, large-scale national databases (NHANES and KNHANES) under stringent inclusion criteria, thereby ensuring robust statistical power and broad generalizability of our findings. Second, we employed a multifaceted analytical approach—incorporating multivariable logistic regression, RCS, and subgroup analyses—to comprehensively investigate the association between the UHR and sarcopenia. This rigorous methodology not only unveiled a potential non-linear dose-response relationship but also ensured the reliability of our results through the sequential adjustment of confounding covariates across different models. More importantly, we integrated advanced machine learning frameworks, executing robust feature selection within the NHANES dataset and identifying the optimal predictive model via a consistent set of evaluation metrics. Furthermore, SHAP analysis visually underscored the paramount predictive importance of the UHR, while successful external validation using the independent KNHANES cohort definitively proved the robustness of the constructed model. Collectively, these rigorous methodological steps support the clinical positioning of the UHR as a reliable biomarker for sarcopenia prediction.

Nevertheless, the present study is subject to several inherent limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design of the present study does not allow us to establish the temporal sequence or causal direction of the association between UHR and sarcopenia. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as an association with prevalent sarcopenia rather than evidence that elevated UHR precedes or contributes causally to the development of sarcopenia. Reverse causation represents an important alternative explanation. Individuals with established sarcopenia may experience reduced habitual physical activity, altered dietary intake and nutritional status, changes in body composition, and metabolic or inflammatory disturbances. These changes could, in turn, influence purine metabolism and serum uric acid concentrations, as well as lipid metabolism and HDL-C levels, thereby affecting UHR. Consequently, an elevated UHR may partly represent a metabolic correlate or consequence of prevalent sarcopenia rather than an antecedent causal exposure. In addition, UHR and sarcopenia may share common upstream metabolic or inflammatory determinants, which could also contribute to the observed association. Because these potential pathways cannot be distinguished using cross-sectional data, prospective longitudinal studies with repeated measurements of UHR and muscle-related outcomes are needed to clarify temporal ordering. Future Mendelian randomization or other genetically informed causal inference approaches may provide complementary evidence regarding whether the observed association reflects a causal relationship.57,58 Second, sarcopenia was operationally defined using the FNIH ASM/BMI criterion, whereas muscle strength and physical performance were not incorporated. This approach was selected to maintain a harmonized definition across the full NHANES 2011–2018 period and the independent KNHANES cohort, because handgrip strength was available only for the 2011–2014 NHANES cycles and comparable functional measurements were not consistently available across the entire study period. Similar FNIH ASM/BMI-based definitions have been applied in previous population-based NHANES studies. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this operational definition primarily captures the low-muscle-mass component of sarcopenia and may not fully represent the broader clinical phenotype characterized by impaired muscle strength and/or physical performance. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted in the context of this operational definition, and future studies incorporating harmonized measures of muscle mass, handgrip strength, and physical performance are warranted.59–61 Third, although our multivariable models rigorously adjusted for a comprehensive array of clinical and demographic covariates, the potential for unmeasured or residual confounding remains. Thus, we cannot definitively exclude the influence of extraneous variables on the observed relationship. For instance, UA metabolism exhibits well-documented sexual dimorphism due to hormonal influences, typically resulting in higher baseline UA levels in males than in females. Such inherent biological variations could subtly modify the dynamic relationship between the UHR and sarcopenia. 62 In addition, the estimated UHR inflection points differed between NHANES and KNHANES, limiting the generalizability of a single threshold for clinical use.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study comprehensively investigated the association between the UHR and sarcopenia, demonstrating that an elevated UHR serves as a significant risk indicator for this condition and highlighting its potential clinical utility in risk assessment. Nevertheless, given the inherent cross-sectional nature of our design and the possible influence of residual confounding, these findings must be interpreted with caution. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between UHR and sarcopenia, while Mendelian randomization and other causal inference approaches may provide complementary evidence regarding causality.
