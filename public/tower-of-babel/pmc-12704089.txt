Evolving strategies in cardiovascular disease prevention: 2025 American society for preventive cardiology highlights☆
Authors: Annalisa Filtz, Michael D. Shapiro, Paul K Whelton, Ann Marie Navar, Fatima Rodriguez, Seth S Martin, Martha Gulati, Ron Blankstein, Paul M Ridker, Deepak L Bhatt, Clyde W Yancy, David J Maron, Leandro Slipczuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The 2025 American Society for Preventive Cardiology meeting highlighted evolving strategies in cardiovascular disease prevention, spanning risk models based on traditional risk factors, emerging biomarkers, novel therapeutics, and digital health innovations. Key discussions addressed lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and inflammation as a causal risk factor, their clinical management, and readiness for targeted therapies; optimal systolic blood pressure targets informed by recent randomized controlled trials; and ongoing debate regarding apolipoprotein B versus low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) as the primary lipid target. Advances in digital health emphasized prevention through artificial intelligence, health equity in technology, and the growing role of wearables. Imaging emerged as a central theme, with sessions highlighting its role in risk assessment, monitoring treatment response, and refining prevention strategies, especially in young adults. Sessions on women’s cardiovascular health underscored female-specific risk enhancers and limitations of current risk prediction models. Additional debates addressed the use of polygenic risk scores in young adults and strategies for universal screening with LDL-C, hsCRP, and Lp(a). Heart failure prevention was highlighted as a critical frontier, with emphasis on stage-based risk stratification, early identification of subclinical disease, and targeted interventions to avert progression to symptomatic heart failure. Updates on renal denervation reaffirmed its safety, efficacy, and durability as an adjunctive therapy in resistant hypertension. Collectively, these highlights emphasize the rapid evolution of preventive cardiology, integrating precision risk stratification, digital tools, and novel therapies into routine care.

Introduction

With >600 participants and over 50 distinguished faculty speakers, the 2025 American Society for Preventive Cardiology (ASPC) meeting marked a milestone for the society. The week opened with a dedicated Training Academy that welcomed 15 early-career professionals, underscoring ASPC’s commitment to fostering the next generation of leaders in preventive cardiology. The program featured the 4th Women’s Cardiovascular Health Symposium, a joint session with the European Atherosclerosis Society, and, for the first time, live patient stories that brought a compelling personal perspective to the discussions (Fig. 1).Fig. 1American Society for Preventive Cardiology 2025 summary. This review summarizes key highlights from ASPC 2025, including: (1) updated perspectives on lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and inflammation as targets for emerging therapies; (2) expert insights on evolving blood pressure (BP) targets and the role of renal denervation; (3) debates presenting expert perspective on apolipoprotein B (apoB) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) as primary lipid targets; (4) digital health strategies including wearables, mobile health, and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven opportunistic prevention; (5) unique considerations in cardiovascular risk assessment for women and younger adults; (6) the role of imaging and biomarker screening in modern prevention; and (7) heart failure (HF) prevention through stage-based risk stratification and targeted interventions to prevent progression to symptomatic disease (Central Illustration). Together, these topics reflect a rapidly evolving landscape in which preventive cardiology is increasingly precise, technology-enabled, and patient-centered.

Lipoprotein(a): myths, misconceptions, and management and emerging therapies

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a genetically determined, highly atherogenic lipoprotein that is an independent causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and calcific aortic valve stenosis. Despite increasing recognition, its integration into routine preventive cardiology testing remains inconsistent, largely due to persistent misconceptions regarding testing, interpretation, and clinical actionability in the absence of currently approved targeted therapies [1,2].

Recent epidemiologic studies, Mendelian randomization analyses, emerging randomized controlled trial data on investigational therapies, and updated international guidelines provide a strong foundation for the clinical utility of measuring Lp(a). Collectively, these findings establish that Lp(a) assessment is not merely an academic exercise but an essential component of modern preventive cardiology. It informs comprehensive risk assessment, guides therapeutic intensity, supports family screening, and readiness for the integration of emerging Lp(a)-lowering therapies currently in late-phase clinical development.

Several principles underpin contemporary recommendations. First, universal screening is increasingly endorsed, as approximately 20 % of adults have Lp(a) concentrations associated with elevated risk of ASCVD or calcific AV stenosis [[3], [4], [5]]. Second, although levels are largely genetically determined, recent data from population-based cohorts and clinical trial demonstrate greater intra-individual variability, supporting serial measurement in selected clinical scenarios [6]. Third, accurate Lp(a) assessment requires molar units (nmol/L) using standardized assays, since conversion between nmol/L and mg/dL is inaccurate due to apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] isoform size heterogeneity [7]. However, mass assays are generally reliable and can be used in most clinical settings [8]. Fourth, absolute concentration values of Lp(a) —not race-, ethnicity-, or sex-specific percentiles— are associated to ASCVD risk [9,10]. Fifth, assessment of both apoB and Lp(a) is necessary to comprehensively quantify atherogenic lipoprotein-related risk, as Lp(a) confers residual risk independent of apoB [11,12]. Six, elevated Lp(a) remains an important driver of ASCVD risk even when LDL-C is optimally controlled, highlighting the need for risk mitigation strategies beyond LDL-C lowering alone [[13], [14], [15]]. Notably, individuals with elevated Lp(a) may also derive greater benefit from PCSK9 inhibitors, which can further reduce LDL-C and overall ASCVD risk [16,17]. Nevertheless, optimization of other cardiovascular risk factors—such as blood pressure, glycemia, and lifestyle—significantly reduces overall ASCVD risk, even in the presence of elevated Lp(a) [18]. Seven, selected individuals with elevated Lp(a), particularly those with genetically high risk, may derive net cardiovascular benefit from low-dose aspirin, based on genetic, observational, and post-hoc analyses of RCT data [19]. Eight, elevated Lp(a) should be regarded as clinically actionable today. Determination of optimal antihypertensive systolic blood pressure (SBP) is informed by observational associations between SBP and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and by experience in RCTs, especially randomization to different SBP treatment targets.

Observational cohort and clinical linkage studies have identified a striking log-linear association between SBP, from 90 to 180 mmHg, and various CVD outcomes. Eight RCTs have compared outcomes in groups randomized to an SBP <130 mmHg versus a higher SBP and five RCTs in groups randomized to an SBP <120 vs. <140 mmHg [21,22]. Forest plots demonstrated an almost universal pattern of CVD and all-cause mortality benefit for the groups randomized to lower vs. higher SBP treatment targets [22]. Major CVD events (composite of stroke, coronary heart disease, HF, and CVD mortality) were significantly reduced by about 20 % in the groups randomized to an SBP treatment target of <130 mmHg vs. a higher SBP and in those randomized to an SBP <120 vs. <140 mmHg [22]. There was a corresponding reduction in all-cause mortality of about 10 % for both treatment comparisons. In addition, each component of the CVD composite was reduced in those randomized to lower vs. higher SBP treatment targets. Reports of hypotension, syncope, falls, electrolyte disturbance, and acute kidney injury were significantly more common in those randomized to lower vs. higher SBP treatment targets. However, adverse event reports were relatively uncommon, with only one case of hypotension (the most common adverse outcome) for >500 patients randomized to an SBP <130 mmHg and for >600 patients randomized to <120 mmHg. The corresponding numbers for falls and electrolyte disturbance were approximately 3000 and 2000, respectively.

The recently released 2025 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC) BP Guideline recommends an SBP treatment goal of <130 mmHg, with encouragement to achieve an SBP <120 mmHg [23].

Although there is disagreement regarding whether apoB should replace LDL-C as the primary therapeutic target at the population level, several areas of consensus have emerged. First, therapeutic intensification is warranted in patients who achieve LDL-C goals but retain elevated apoB levels. Second, clinical trials should incorporate apoB measurement to generate the evidence needed to guide implementation of clinical practice. Finally, regardless of the specific biomarker used, more efforts are necessary to improve gaps in lipid testing and improve lipid management in at-risk patients.

Opportunistic and inclusive prevention: AI and health techquity

CVD remains the leading cause of death worldwide, yet many opportunities for prevention are overlooked. AI and digital health innovations have the potential to strengthen preventive cardiology by enabling opportunistic screening and incorporating principles of health equity into technology design.

Opportunistic prevention utilizes pre-existing clinical data, such as routine chest computed tomography (CT) scans, to assess cardiovascular risk without additional testing. The NOTIFY-1 project showed that notification of patients and clinicians of incidental coronary artery calcium (CAC) detected on prior non-gated chest CT scans significantly increased statin initiation and persistence. These findings highlight the potential of AI-enabled workflows to translate incidental imaging results into meaningful improvements in preventive care [28].

Beyond CAC detection, a broad range of digital strategies—including remote blood pressure monitoring, AI-assisted risk prediction, mobile applications, and wearable devices—can extend preventive interventions beyond traditional clinical settings [[29], [30], [31]], Fig. 2. These tools hold promise for democratizing access and improving adherence; however, their benefits are not automatically equitable, and proposed digital interventions need to be rigorously tested and monitored in clinical settings.Fig. 2Digital Innovation in Prevention. The principle of health “techquity” serves as a framework to ensure digital health solutions do not widen existing disparities [32]. Key challenges include uneven access to digital tools, underrepresentation of diverse populations in training datasets, and variability in digital literacy. Strategies to address these issues include validation of algorithms across demographic subgroups, co-design of interventions with patients and communities, culturally tailored communication, and systematic monitoring for bias and unintended consequences.

In summary, opportunistic and inclusive approaches have the potential to shift preventive cardiology from a reactive to a proactive paradigm. By leveraging data embedded in routine clinical workflows and implementing digital solutions with equity as a core principle, preventive interventions can be delivered earlier, more effectively, and more broadly. This framework is particularly relevant for populations historically underserved in cardiovascular care, ensuring that advances in AI and digital health are accessible to all patients.

Wearables & mobile health technologies

The integration of wearable and mobile health technologies represents a promising new frontier in preventive cardiology. These digital health tools are increasingly being recognized for potential to empower patients and clinicians, improve patient outcomes and the care experience, while improving costs and access to care [33]. These technologies offer a pathway to more personalized, proactive, and scalable cardiovascular care. A role for digital health interventions is emerging across the care continuum, spanning primordial to primary to secondary prevention [34].

Wearables provide continuous data streams of metrics such as step count, sleep duration, heart rate, and heart rhythm, while smartphone applications enable behavioral interventions that include personalized goal setting and medication adherence reminders. Digital health technologies may also be combined with community engagement approaches to enhance behavioral change across diverse populations and care settings [35]. Additionally, realizing the potential of wearables and mobile health in clinical care will require data visibility within clinical workflows, with interpretable summaries of data that are linked to evidence-based actions [36].

Growing evidence supports digital health interventions. A recent real-world study in primary prevention showed that a mobile coaching program improved blood pressure, cholesterol, and weight[37]. In the secondary prevention setting, an RCT of tablet-based mobile cardiac rehabilitation intervention in older adults with coronary artery disease was presented. The intervention incorporated remote monitoring, asynchronous exercise, and weekly exercise therapist calls [38]. Although the intervention did not significantly increase 6-minute walk distance or other outcomes compared with usual care, where patients could receive traditional center-based cardiac rehabilitation, the intervention group achieved similar results with a trend towards benefit. Additional clinical trials in this area are ongoing [39].

Despite progress, challenges remain. Reimbursement structures are not yet optimized for digital health adoption, underscoring the need for policy innovation to match technological advances [40]. Moreover, additional robust evidence from RCTs and real-world implementation studies are needed to establish the benefits and harms, cost-effectiveness, and scalability of digital health interventions (Fig. 2). Training programs must prepare the next generation of clinician-innovators to harness these opportunities responsibly [41].

Looking forward, technology is expected to augment rather than replace the clinician’s role in preventive cardiology. Striking the right balance between digital tools and human touch will be essential. Wearable and mobile health solutions hold the potential to transform preventive cardiology from reactive, episodic encounters to proactive, continuous care.

Assessing cardiovascular risk in women throughout the lifespan

Cardiovascular risk assessment is a critical step in CVD prevention. Identifying an individual’s short- and long-term risk of CVD has been incorporated into the AHA/ACC guideline for the primary prevention of CVD prevention [42]. Although risk assessment has traditionally focused on ASCVD, the AHA recently introduced the Predicting Risk of cardiovascular EVENTs (PREVENT) score, which expands risk assessment to include both ASCVD and HF [43]. Although the PREVENT risk score was derived from a more diverse and contemporary population, and can be applied at younger ages, all risk assessment tools have significant limitations [44]. Notably, 14–27 % of patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome have no standard modifiable risk factors, also known as “SMuRFless.”[[45], [46], [47]] In women, CVD risk scores are heavily age-dependent, limiting the detection of younger individuals at elevated risk [48].

Many women are SMuRFless and would be categorized as low risk. Female-specific risk enhancers, although not incorporated into conventional risk scores, can refine risk stratification and guide clinical decision-making. Relevant female-specific risk enhancers should be evaluated across women’s lifespan (Fig. 3). These include age at menarche, polycystic ovarian syndrome, the presence of adverse pregnancy outcomes (i.e. gestational hypertension, preeclampsia and eclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm delivery, and small for gestational age birth) along with infertility and miscarriages. Additional considerations include functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, premature menopause, premature ovarian insufficiency, and exposure to exogenous hormones, including oral contraceptive and menopausal hormone therapy [49,50]. Additional conditions disproportionately affecting women—such as systemic inflammation, autoimmune disorders, and breast cancer—also serve as risk enhancers for CVD [49,50]. Analyses from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study (ARIC) and Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) cohorts indicate that the presence of three or more risk enhancers confers predictive value comparable to a CAC score ≥100; however, female-specific risk enhancers were not included in these studies [51].Fig. 3Female specific and female predominant risk enhancers across the lifespan.Female specific and female predominant risk enhancers that are associated with increased cardiovascular risk that need to be considered beyond traditional, modifiable cardiac risk factors in cardiovascular disease risk assessment. Population-derived risk assessment tools are inherently imperfect when applied to the individual, particularly in women. Enhancing precision in risk stratification will require tools capable of identifying high-risk women at earlier ages. Routine primary prevention assessment may be optimized by focusing on key biomarkers: LDL-C, Lp(a), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), which independently predict cardiovascular risk in both sexes [[52], [53], [54]]. Universal screening of these biomarkers could establish a foundation for more accurate early risk assessment in women.

Imaging and polygenic risk score in young individuals

A featured session at ASPC 2025 focused on risk assessment in young adults. This is a timely topic, giving the growing recognition by the preventive cardiology community that prevention efforts need to start earlier in life [55,56]. Nevertheless, an important challenge in the field is that risk scores often underestimate risk in young individuals [57,58]. Two complementary approaches to address this limitation are cardiovascular imaging and polygenic risk scores (PRS).

The role of imaging in guiding prevention is based on the observation that atherosclerosis is widely prevalent among young asymptomatic adults (Table 4) and is associated with a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events [59,60]. Plaque detection provides incremental prognostic value beyond traditional risk factors, risk scores, and PRS [61]. The direct visualization of plaque establishes the presence of disease, the strongest predictor of future events. While routine imaging of unselected young individuals for the presence of atherosclerosis is not recommended, the yield of imaging could be increased by considering the presence of risk factors and data from PRS. Highlighting this approach, Dr. Milena Petranovic (Mass General Brigham) presented data at ASPC showing that the use of PRS can improve the yield of identifying the presence and severity of plaque on coronary CT angiography (CCTA).

A key concept to the effective use of imaging in young adults is that the presence of any plaque signifies increased risk and should trigger treatment intensification. But a normal scan at a very young age may not be reassuring in the long term. Thus, imaging is most helpful when there is uncertainty about patient risk, particularly if the information derived from such testing has a high likelihood of impacting treatment decisions.

Serial anatomic imaging is the best way to monitor response to therapy for patients with atherosclerosis, arguments for and against

Summary

A formal debate evaluated the clinical utility of serial anatomic imaging for monitoring therapeutic response. Audience support for this proposition increased from 45 % before the session to 55 % afterward.

The pro argument highlighted the limitations of conventional risk assessment models, which may both overestimate and underestimate cardiovascular risk, and proposed that anatomic imaging provides superior prognostic accuracy. Data were presented showing that on non-contrast CT, a > 15 % increase in CAC score over two years was associated with higher rates of acute myocardial infarction compared with smaller increases [67]. Observational studies of the effect of various pharmacologic interventions on plaque regression—particularly noncalcified plaque—on serial CCTA were presented [[68], [69], [70]]. Additional data demonstrated an association between plaque progression and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [71]. Examples of AI-enabled quantification of plaque characteristics were highlighted. The pro perspective concluded that changes in atherosclerosis are highly correlated with outcomes. CCTA is a non-invasive imaging modality with low cost and low risk, and serial assessment can identify patients in whom current therapies are not sufficient, thereby guiding the addition of non-statin and anti-inflammatory treatments.

The con perspective argued that although baseline imaging is extremely valuable to better assess risk for events in an asymptomatic population and motivate patients and clinicians to initiate and adhere to lifestyle and pharmacological interventions, serial imaging with CCTA has several limitations [28]. First, once a clinically significant atherosclerotic burden is defined, aggressive therapy should be initiated. A blinded serial CCTA trial was presented that demonstrated that showing a CAC scan with a score >0 but <400 once to a patient slowed progression of plaque compared with not showing a CAC scan [64]. Data were presented that showed a clear correlation between plaque regression/progression and achieved LDL-C in randomized controlled trials [72]. Data were presented that showed radiation from medical tests and procedures is associated with an increased risk of cancer [73,74]. Furthermore, no RCTs have been completed to demonstrate that anatomic imaging of asymptomatic patients improves outcomes (let alone serial imaging), therefore guidelines do not recommend CCTA or vascular ultrasound screening for subclinical atherosclerosis and insurance does not cover these tests (with rare exception), making them cost prohibitive for the majority of patients. Universal screening for LDL-C, CRP, and Lp(a)

Preventive cardiologists universally screen their patients for LDL-C, a practice justified by the central role of LDL-C as the most important modifiable cardiovascular risk factor after smoking cessation and blood pressure control [75]. However, nearly half of all patients destined to suffer a major adverse atherosclerotic event present with cholesterol levels well within the normal range. Furthermore, the five major risk factors – hyperlipidemia, hypertension, smoking, diabetes, and obesity – account for only 50 to 60 % of all incident vascular events [76]. Recognition that inflammation, quantified by hsCRP, and Lp(a) are independent causal determinants of atherosclerosis underscores the need for universal assessment of LDL-C, hsCRP, and Lp(a) as a foundation of contemporary risk stratification [77].

In the primary prevention setting, the US Women’s Health Study and the European EPIC—Norfolk Cohort included over 45,000 participants. Both demonstrate that a single baseline measure of LDL-C, hsCRP, and Lp(a), provides robust, independent, and additive prediction of lifetime cardiovascular risk (Fig. 5) [52,53]. Risks increased in a stepwise manner with the number of elevated biomarkers. This allowed for early identification of individuals who would otherwise remain classified as low risk until later decades by conventional 10-year risk scores. Moreover, it has recently been shown that “SMuRFless but inflamed” individuals – those lacking standard modifiable risk factors yet exhibiting elevated hsCRP – clearly have increased lifetime cardiovascular risk. Beyond primary prevention, the concept of simultaneously measuring LDL-C, hsCRP, and Lp(a) has repeatedly been validated in contemporary secondary prevention cohorts, both in statin-treated and statin-naïve populations [[79], [80], [81], [82], [83]]. Integration of hsCRP and Lp(a) in addition to LDL-C provides fundamental insights on individual risk and guides therapeutic selection.

In patients with residual cholesterol risk, intensification of LDL-C lowering remains appropriate, whereas residual inflammatory risk identified by hsCRP elevation may be addressed with anti-inflammatory therapy such as low-dose colchicine, recently approved by FDA for secondary prevention [78,[84], [85], [86]]. For those with substantially elevated Lp(a), outcome data from ongoing phase 3 trials are awaited to establish whether selective Lp(a) lowering reduces cardiovascular events.

Finally, the integration of imaging tests with modifiable biomarkers is overdue. Imaging tests such as CAC score identify which patients warrant treatment. However, biological biomarkers are needed to determine what therapeutic approach is most appropriate. Universal screening of LDL-C, hsCRP, and Lp(a) – the three most important modifiable cardiovascular biomarker risk factors – provides critical information that complement imaging-based risk assessment. This approach refines characterization of residual risk and guides targeted interventions beyond lifestyle modification and statin therapy [77].

Renal denervation

Hypertension is an important modifiable risk factor for stroke, HF, myocardial infarction, kidney disease, and cardiovascular mortality. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP) elevations independently increase risk, with SBP exerting the greater effect, irrespective of whether hypertension is defined by the ≥140/90 mm Hg or ≥130/80 mm Hg threshold [87]. Renal artery denervation has been investigated as an adjunctive therapy for patients with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension despite lifestyle modification and pharmacologic therapy with at times conflicting results.

Initial evidence from the open-label SYMPLICITY HTN-2 trial reported substantial reductions in BP following radiofrequency ablation of renal sympathetic nerves [88]. In contrast, the sham-controlled SYMPLICITY HTN-3 did not meet its primary efficacy endpoint at 6 months but establish the procedure’s safety [89]. Extended follow-up to 36 months demonstrated significant and durable reductions in both office and ambulatory SBP in the renal denervation arm, with no evidence of delayed adverse events [90]. These observations prompted refinements in patient selection, procedural technique, and trial design, leading to the SPYRAL HTN—OFF MED and SPYRAL HTN—ON MED. Both trials demonstrated BP lowering in patients both off and on background antihypertensive therapy [[91], [92], [93]].

Ultrasound-based renal denervation trials confirmed these findings. The RADIANCE-HTN SOLO, RADIANCE-HTN TRIO, and RADIANCE II trials showed reductions in BP across diverse populations, including in resistant hypertension [[94], [95], [96]]. Importantly, medium-term follow-up has confirmed the durability of this effect [97].

Alcohol-mediated renal denervation is being studied as an alternative sympathetic-targeting modality. The TARGET OFF-MED trial in untreated hypertensive patients showed safe delivery of alcohol mediated renal denervation, but no significant 24-hour SBP difference at 8 weeks. At 12 months with medication escalation, renal denervation achieved similar office BP versus sham, but with a significantly lower medication burden [98]. TARGET BP I trial extended these findings to patients on background therapy, showing modest 24-hour ambulatory SBP reduction at 3 months, without significant differences between groups in office BP or in major adverse events [99].

Evidence synthesis from systematic reviews and meta-analyses has reinforced these trial-level observations. A pooled analysis of 10 trials (almost 2500 patients) confirmed significant reductions in both office and ambulatory BP across sham-controlled studies, regardless of device type or medication status [100].

Overall, evidence supports renal denervation as a safe, durable, and modestly effective adjunct in hypertension not controlled with lifestyle and maximal medical therapy. Renal denervation provides incremental, sustained BP lowering, with approximately a 5 mmHg reduction in office SBP noted in sham-controlled randomized trials, with larger reductions noted in observational data. It is assumed that this reduction in blood pressure would translate into substantial reductions in cardiovascular events over time, though this remains to be proven. Prevention of heart failure

Despite advances in clinical science and the development of effective therapies that have altered the natural history of HF, without any equivocation, the best therapy remains the only intervention with an unlimited impact on morbidity and mortality – prevention, Fig. 7.Fig. 7Spectrum of Heart Failure Prevention: Targeting Early Stages to Delay Symptomatic Disease. cMRI, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging; GLS, global longitudinal strain; HF, heart failure; LV, left ventricle.Fig. 7

Spectrum of Heart Failure Prevention: Targeting Early Stages to Delay Symptomatic Disease. cMRI, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging; GLS, global longitudinal strain; HF, heart failure; LV, left ventricle.

Following the introduction of the new HF staging system in 2002, studies highlighted the striking divergence in outcomes across the spectrum—from individuals without risk factors, to those with risk factors or asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction, to patients with symptomatic disease or recurrent hospitalizations. The magnitude of these differences underscores that prevention must remain central to altering the natural history of HF [101]. The pivotal transition is clearly asymptomatic to symptomatic ventricular dysfunction with important pathobiological implications regarding neurohumoral activation and ventricular remodeling. With this as the most important theorem addressing disease progression, the steps necessary to prevent the onset of left ventricular dysfunction take primacy.

The intersection of prevention strategies with the Stages of HF is key as different strategies are pertinent for Stage A (risk factors only) and Stage B (asymptomatic LV dysfunction). Though not formally incorporated in the ACC/AHA hierarchal staging scheme, theoretically Stage B2, i.e., asymptomatic LV dysfunction with risk enhancement, is similarly relevant and again infers a separate suite of interventions. Risk enhancers include biomarkers, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, global longitudinal strain by echocardiography, early fibrosis by cMRI, and other known to be important prognostic indicators [102].

Among modifiable risk factors, hypertension is the most prominent contributor to HF. Multiple outcomes-based hypertension trials and especially the landmark SPRINT trial demonstrated a remarkable decrement in the incidence of HF with assiduous control of BP in those at increased cardiovascular disease risk [103]. Administration of sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors in patients with type II diabetes also attenuates the incidence of HF by nearly 30 % [104]. The new anti-obesogenic drugs substantially reduce MACE, making it reasonable —though yet to be proven— to expect a lower incidence of HF [105]. General themes addressing heart healthy lifestyle now archived as Life’s Essential 8 (American Heart Association) carry sufficient protection from CVD events that again, a reduction in the incidence of HF is the expectation.

Of great enthusiasm is the creation, validation and now promulgation of not only the CVD PREVENT Risk Calculator but especially the PREVENT HF Risk Calculator. Appropriate use of this calculator calibrates risk from <10 % lifetime to ∼35 % lifetime risk. This more precise assessment of risk offers the ability to gauge those at highest risk and thus reap greatest benefit from disease modifying therapies [106].

The future resides in part at the precipice of clinical implementation of more widely available genetic risk assessment in the identification of inherited dilated cardiomyopathies (DCM). Approximately 20 % of new onset DCM is genetically determined, and offspring with pathogenic variants carry ∼40 % risk. Interestingly, in those with apparent non-genetic causes of DCM, including alcohol and exposure to cardiotoxins, 40–50 % harbor pathogenic variants. Finally, 90 % of the genetic factors associated with DCM are sourced from just 14 known genes [107].

Exciting new data suggest even greater risk assessment and calibration using data science. Previously un-recognized data available in CAC scanning combined with easily available clinical risk factors exceeds the risk prediction of PREVENT HF (AUC 0.844 vs. 0.777, respectively) [108].

Collectively, the notable progress in the management of all phenotypes of clinically symptomatic HF (Stage C & D) is now paralleled by the opportunities for risk assessment, prediction and intervention for Stages A and B. Preventing HF is a clinical reality and is appropriate for all physicians and care providers to actualize without hesitation.

Conclusion

The 2025 ASPC meeting highlights a transformative period in preventive cardiology. Emerging biomarkers, advanced imaging, and digital health technologies are improving risk assessment and guiding tailored interventions.
