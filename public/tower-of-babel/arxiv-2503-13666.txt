Countably compact inverse semigroups and Nyikos’ problem
Serhii Bardyla

Abstract
A regular separable first-countable countably compact space is called a Nyikos space. In this paper, we give a partial solution to an old problem of Nyikos by showing that each locally compact Nyikos inverse topological semigroup is compact. Also, we show that a topological semigroup S S that contains a dense inverse subsemigroup is a topological inverse semigroup, provided (i) S S is compact, or (ii) S S is countably compact and sequential. The latter result solves a problem of Banakh and Pastukhova and provides the automatic continuity of inversion in certain compact-like inverse semigroups.

Theorem 1.8

Compact-like semigroups with dense inverse subsemigroups and the automatic continuity of inversion

Let
A
be a subset of a space
X
. By
\operatorname{cl}_{X}(A)
or simply
\overline{A}
we denote the closure of
A
in
X
.
Let

s(A)=\{y\in X\colon\hbox{ }\exists\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq A\hbox{ such that }\lim_{n\in\omega}x_{n}=y\}.

The
sequential closure

\operatorname{cls}(A)
of
A
is defined recursively as follows. Let
\operatorname{cls}^{0}(A)=A
and assume that for each ordinal
\xi<\alpha\leq\omega_{1}
the set
\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(A)
is defined. Then
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(A)=\bigcup_{\xi\in\alpha}\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(A)
if
\alpha
is limit and
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(A)=s(\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A))
if
\alpha=\gamma+1
. Finally, put
\operatorname{cls}(A)=\operatorname{cls}^{\omega_{1}}(A)
.
Recall that a space
X
is sequential if and only if
\overline{A}=\operatorname{cls}(A)
for every
A\subseteq X
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a subsemigroup of a topological semigroup
S
. Then
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(A)
is a subsemigroup of
S
for each
\alpha\leq\omega_{1}
.

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{cls}^{0}(A)=A
is a subsemigroup of
S
. Assume that for each
\xi<\delta\leq\omega_{1}
,
\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(A)
is a semigroup. If
\delta
is limit, then
\operatorname{cls}^{\delta}(A)=\bigcup_{\xi<\delta}\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(A)
is a subsemigroup of
S
, being an ascending union of semigroups. Suppose that
\delta=\gamma+1
for some
\gamma<\omega_{1}
. Then
\operatorname{cls}^{\delta}(A)=s(\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A))
. Pick any points
x,y\in\operatorname{cls}^{\delta}(A)
and sequences
\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A)
and
\{y_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A)
such that
x=\lim_{n\in\omega}x_{n}
and
y=\lim_{n\in\omega}y_{n}
.
Since
\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A)
is a semigroup, we get that the sequence
\{x_{n}y_{n}:n\in\omega\}
is contained in
\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(A)
.
Since
S
is a topological semigroup, the sequence
\{x_{n}y_{n}:n\in\omega\}
converges to
xy
. Thus
xy\in\operatorname{cls}^{\delta}(A)
, witnessing that
\operatorname{cls}^{\delta}(A)
is a subsemigroup of
S
. Hence
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(A)
is a subsemigroup of
S
for each
\alpha\leq\omega_{1}
, as required.
∎

The following problem appears in
[
6
, Problem 2.2]
.

Problem 2.2

(Banakh, Pastukhova)
.

Assume that
S
is a compact topological semigroup that contains a dense inverse subsemigroup. Is
S
an inverse semigroup?

For a semigroup
S
by
E(S)
we denote the set of all idempotents of
S
.
A semigroup
S
is called
regular
if for each
x\in S
there exists
y\in S
such that
xyx=x
. Note that every inverse semigroup is regular. A regular semigroup
S
is inverse if an only if
E(S)
is a semilattice (see
[
32
, Theorem 5.1.1]
). A filter
\mathcal{F}
on a space
X

converges
to a point
x\in X
if
\mathcal{F}
contains all neighborhoods of
x
. For a subset
F
of an inverse semigroup
S
let
F^{-1}=\{x^{-1}:x\in F\}
.
The following proposition is useful for detecting inverse semigroups among topological semigroups with a dense inverse subsemigroup.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
S
be a topological semigroup with a dense inverse subsemigroup
X
and for every
y\in S\setminus X
there exists an ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}
convergent to
y
such that
X\in\mathcal{F}
and the ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
generated by the family
\{F^{-1}:X\supseteq F\in\mathcal{F}\}
converges to some element of
S
. Then
S
is an inverse semigroup.

Proof.

First let us show that the semigroup
S
is regular. Fix any
y\in S\setminus X
. By assumption, there exists an ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}
on
S
convergent to
y
such that
X\in\mathcal{F}
and the ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
converges to some element
z\in S
. Consider a neighborhood
U
of the point
w=yzy
. By the continuity of the semigroup operation in
S
, there exist neighborhoods
V(y)
and
V(z)
of
y
and
z
, respectively, such that
V(y)V(z)V(y)\subseteq U
. Then there exist
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{1}\subseteq V(y)\cap X
and
F_{2}^{-1}\subseteq V(z)\cap X
. Then for
F=F_{1}\cap F_{2}
we get
F\subseteq FF^{-1}F\subseteq U
. Since the neighborhood
U
of
w
is arbitrarily chosen, the filter
\mathcal{F}
converges to
w
. Since the space
S
is Hausdorff, any filter on
S
converges to at most one point, implying that
y=w=yzy
. Hence
S
is a regular semigroup.

To prove that
S
is an inverse semigroup it suffices to show that idempotents of
S
commute. For this it is enough to show that
E(S)=\overline{E(X)}
, as
E(X)
is a commutative semigroup. Fix an idempotent
e\in S\setminus X
. By assumption, there exists an ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}
convergent to
e
such that
X\in\mathcal{F}
and the ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
converges to some point
f\in S
.
Similarly as above one can check that
fef=f
and
e=efe
.
Since
ee=e
, the continuity of the semigroup operation implies that for every neighborhood
U
of
e
there exists a neighborhood
V
of
e
such that
VV\subseteq U
. Pick any
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subseteq V
and observe that
FF\subseteq U
.
Consequently, the filter
\mathcal{F}^{2}
generated by the family
\{FF:F\in\mathcal{F}\}
converges to
e
.
Fix a neighborhood
U
of
f
. Since
f=fef
and
S
is a topological semigroup, there exist neighborhoods
V(f)
and
V(e)
of
f
and
e
, respectively, such that
V(f)V(e)V(f)\subseteq U
. Since the filter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
converges to
f
and the filter
\mathcal{F}^{2}
converges to
e
, there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subseteq X
,
F^{-1}\subseteq V(f)
and
FF\subseteq V(e)
. Fix any
x\in F
. Observe that

x^{-1}xxx^{-1}\in F^{-1}FFF^{-1}\subseteq V(f)V(e)V(f)\subseteq U.

Since
X
is an inverse semigroup, the elements
x^{-1}x
and
xx^{-1}
are idempotents. Since
E(X)
is a subsemigroup of
X
, the element
x^{-1}xxx^{-1}
is also an idempotent. As the neighborhood
U
of
f
is arbitrarily chosen, we get
f\in\overline{E(X)}
. Since
S
is a Hausdorff topological semigroup,
f
is an idempotent. Let
\mathcal{F}^{-2}
be the filter generated by the family
\{FF:F\in\mathcal{F}^{-1}\}
. Similarly as above one can check that
\mathcal{F}^{-2}
converges to
f
. Since
S
is a topological semigroup and
e=efe
, for any neighborhood
W
of
e
there exist neighborhoods
V(f)
and
V(e)
of
f
and
e
, respectively, such that
V(e)V(f)V(e)\subseteq W
. As the filter
\mathcal{F}^{-2}
converges to
f
and the filter
\mathcal{F}
converges to
e
, there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subseteq X\cap V(e)
and
(FF)^{-1}=F^{-1}F^{-1}\subseteq V(f)
. Then for any
x\in F
we have

E(X)\ni xx^{-1}x^{-1}x\in FF^{-1}F^{-1}F\subseteq V(e)V(f)V(e)\subseteq W.

Thus
e\in\overline{E(X)}
witnessing that
E(S)=\overline{E(X)}
, as required. Hence
S
is an inverse semigroup.

A space

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

(i) Let
S
be a compact topological semigroup containing a dense inverse subsemigroup
X
.
Fix any
y\in S\setminus X
and consider an ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}
on
S
that contains the family
\{U\cap X:U
is a neighborhood of
y\}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{F}
converges to
y
. By the compactness of
S
, the ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
generated by the family
\{F^{-1}:X\supseteq F\in\mathcal{F}\}
converges to some point of

. Proposition
2.3
implies that
S
is an inverse topological semigroup. By
[
29
, Proposition 1.6.7]
, inversion is automatically continuous in compact inverse topological semigroups. Hence

is a topological inverse semigroup.

(ii) Since the space
S
is sequential we get that

S=\overline{X}=\operatorname{cls}(X)=\operatorname{cls}^{\omega_{1}}(X)=\bigcup_{\xi<\omega_{1}}\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(X).

First let us show that
S
is an inverse semigroup. Note that
\operatorname{cls}^{0}(X)=X
is an inverse subsemigroup of
S
.
Assume that
\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(X)
is an inverse subsemigroup of
S
for every
\xi<\alpha\leq\omega_{1}
. If the ordinal
\alpha
is limit, then

\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)=\bigcup_{\xi<\alpha}\operatorname{cls}^{\xi}(X)
is an inverse semigroup, being an ascending union of inverse semigroups.
Suppose that
\alpha=\gamma+1
for some
\gamma<\omega_{1}
. Lemma
2.1
implies that
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)=s(\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X))
is a subsemigroup of
S
that contains densely the inverse semigroup
\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X)
.
Fix any
x\in\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)\setminus\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X)
and a sequence
\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X)
convergent to
x
.
By
[
23
, Theorem 3.10.31]
, each Hausdorff countably compact sequential space is sequentially compact. Thus the sequence
\{x_{n}^{-1}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X)
contains a convergent in

\{x^{-1}_{n_{k}}:k\in\omega\}
. Set
y=\lim_{k\in\omega}x^{-1}_{n_{k}}
and note that
y\in s(\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X))=\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)
. Fix any free ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}
on
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)
which contains the set
\{x_{n_{k}}:k\in\omega\}
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}
converges to
x
, and the ultrafilter
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
generated by the family
\{F^{-1}\colon\operatorname{cls}^{\gamma}(X)\supseteq F\in\mathcal{F}\}
converges to
y
. Proposition
2.3
implies that
\operatorname{cls}^{\alpha}(X)
is an inverse semigroup. Hence

, inversion is automatically continuous on every countably compact sequential inverse topological semigroup.

S

A semigroup
S
endowed with a topology is called
semitopological
if for every
s\in S
the shifts
l_{s}:x\mapsto sx
and
r_{s}:x\mapsto xs
are continuous in
S
. It is clear that every topological semigroup is semitopological.

Proposition 2.8
.

Each inverse topologically periodic semitopological semigroup
S
is Clifford.

Proof.

Fix an element
x\in S
and a neighborhood
U
of
x
. Since
X
is topologically periodic, there exists a positive integer
n\geq 2
such that
x^{n}\in U
. Then

x^{n}=x^{2}x^{-2}x^{n}\in U\cap(x^{2}x^{-2})U,

and

x^{n}=x^{n}x^{-2}x^{2}\in U(x^{-2}x^{2})\cap U.

It follows that
V\cap(x^{2}x^{-2})V\neq\varnothing
and
V(x^{-2}x^{2})\cap V\neq\varnothing
for each neighborhood
V
of
x
. Since
X
is a Hausdorff semitopological semigroup, we obtain
x^{2}x^{-2}x=x=xx^{-2}x^{2}
. It follows that
x^{-1}x^{2}x^{-2}=x^{-1}=x^{-2}x^{2}x^{-1}
.
Then

\displaystyle x^{-1}x=x^{-1}x^{2}x^{-2}x^{2}x^{-2}x

\displaystyle=x^{-1}x^{2}x^{-2}x=

\displaystyle=(x^{-1}x)(xx^{-1})(x^{-1}x)=(xx^{-1})(x^{-1}x).

In the formula above, the second equality follows from the fact that
x^{2}x^{-2}
is an idempotent, whereas the last equality holds as idempotents commute in
S
.
Similarly

\displaystyle xx^{-1}=xx^{-2}x^{2}x^{-2}x^{2}x^{-1}

\displaystyle=xx^{-2}x^{2}x^{-1}=

\displaystyle=(xx^{-1})(x^{-1}x)(xx^{-1})=(xx^{-1})(x^{-1}x).

Hence
xx^{-1}=x^{-1}x
witnessing that the semigroup
S
is Clifford.
∎

By
\beta X
we denote the Stone-Čech compactification of a Tychonoff space
X
(see
[
23
, Chapter 3.6]
). The following result appears in
[
4
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Theorem 2.9

(Banakh, Dimitrova, Gutik)
.

Let

be a Tychonoff topological semigroup such that

is pseudocompact. Then the semigroup operation of
S
extends to a continuous semigroup operation on
\beta S
.

We are in a position to prove Theorem
1.8
. We need to show that inversion is continuous in any inverse topological semigroup
S
provided (i)
S{\times}S
is pseudocompact, or (ii)

is regular, topologically periodic and

is countably compact.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

(i) By Theorem
2.9
,
\beta S
is a compact topological semigroup that contains

as a dense inverse subsemigroup. Theorem
1.6
implies that
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup. Then the continuity of inversion in

.

(ii) The proof follows from Proposition

and Theorem

.
∎

3
Chains in topological semilattices

Recall that each semilattice possesses the natural partial order
\leq
defined by
a\leq b
if and only if
ab=ba=a
. Further, if a semilattice is treated as a poset, then by default it is assumed to carry the natural partial order.
For an element
x
of a semilattice
X
put
{\downarrow}x=\{y\in X\colon y\leq x\}
and
{\uparrow}x=\{y\in X\colon x\leq y\}
. Note that if
X
is a semitopological semilattice, then for every
x\in X
the sets
{\downarrow}x
and
{\uparrow}x
are closed.
A semitopological semilattice
X
is called
chain-compact
if all maximal chains in
X
are compact. The following two theorems follow from
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Banakh, Bardyla)
.

For a semitopological semilattice
X
the following is equivalent:

(1)

X
is chain-compact;

(2)

each nonempty chain
L\subseteq X
has
\inf L\in\overline{L}
and
\sup L\in\overline{L}
;

(3)

no closed chain in
X
is topologically isomorphic to an infinite regular cardinal endowed with the order topology and the semilattice operation of minimum or maximum.

Theorem 3.2

(Banakh, Bardyla)
.

If
X
is a chain-compact subsemilattice of a topological semigroup
Y
, then
X
is closed in
Y
.

The following fact is well known.

Proposition 3.3

(Folklore)
.

The closure of a chain in a semitopological semilattice is a chain.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
. If
\overline{L}
is compact, then there exists
z=\sup L\in\overline{L}
such that for each neighborhood
U
of
z
the set
L\setminus U
is countable.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
,
\overline{L}
is a compact chain. Theorem
3.1
(2) applied to
\overline{L}
implies that
\overline{L}
contains the supremum of
L
which we denote by
z
. It is clear that
z=\sup L^{\prime}
for each uncountable subset
L^{\prime}
of
L
. By Theorem
3.1
(2),
z\in\overline{L^{\prime}}
for each uncountable subset
L^{\prime}
of
L
. It follows that for each neighborhood
U
of
z
the set
L\setminus U
is countable, as required.
∎

Dually, one can prove the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be a semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\max)
. If
\overline{L}
is compact, then there exists
z=\inf L\in\overline{L}
such that for each neighborhood
U
of
z
the set
L\setminus U
is countable.

Recall that a space
X
is called
countably tight
if for every
A\subseteq X
and
x\in\overline{A}
there exists a countable subset
B\subseteq A
such that
x\in\overline{B}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be a countably tight semitopological semilattice and
L
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
or
(\omega_{1},\max)
. Then
\overline{L}
is not compact.

Proof.

Let
L
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
. If
\overline{L}
is compact, then Lemma
3.4
yields
z=\sup L\in\overline{L}
. The countable tightness of
X
implies that there exists a countable subset
A
of
L
such that
z\in\overline{A}
. Then there exists
l\in L
such that
A\subseteq{\downarrow}l
. Since
X
is a semitopological semilattice,

is a neighborhood of

disjoint with

, a contradiction.
In case when

is isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\max)
, the proof is similar.
∎

We are in a position to prove Theorem
1.5
. We need to show that for a Tychonoff Nyikos topological semilattice
X
the following conditions are equivalent: (i)
X
is compact; (ii) no chain in
X
is isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
or
(\omega_{1},\max)
; (iii) no chain in
X
is topologically isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
or
(\omega_{1},\max)
equipped with the order topology.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Since each first-countable space is countably tight, the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows from Lemma
3.6
. The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is obvious.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Since
X
is first-countable, for any cardinal
\kappa>\omega_{1}
there is no chain in
X
that is topologically isomorphic to
(\kappa,\min)
or
(\kappa,\max)
endowed with the order topology. Since
X
is countably compact, no closed chain in
X
is topologically isomorphic to the discrete semilattices
(\omega,\min)
and
(\omega,\max)
. By assumption, no chain in
X
is topologically isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
or
(\omega_{1},\max)
endowed with the order topology. Theorem
3.1
(3) implies that the semilattice
X
is chain-compact. By
[
23
, Corollary 3.10.15]
,
X{\times}X
is countable compact and thus pseudocompact. By Theorem
2.9
,
\beta X
is a topological semigroup that contains

as a dense subsemilattice. Theorem
3.2
implies that
X
is closed in
\beta X
and thus

is compact.
∎

The rest of this section contains technical results on chains in topological semilattices. They will be used to prove the compactness of locally compact Nyikos topological semilattices (see Theorem
4.12
), which is a milestone in the proof of Main Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\alpha
be an ordinal and
Y
be a semilattice. Then the following assertions hold:

(1)

if
h:(\alpha,\min)\rightarrow Y
is a homomorphism, then
h(\alpha)
is isomorphic to
(\beta,\min)
for some
\beta\leq\alpha
;

(2)

if
h:(\alpha,\max)\rightarrow Y
is a homomorphism, then
h(\alpha)
is isomorphic to
(\beta,\max)
for some
\beta\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

(1) Consider the subsemilattice
A=\{\min(h^{-1}(x)):x\in h(\alpha)\}
of
(\alpha,\min)
. Since
A\subseteq\alpha
, there exists an ordinal
\beta\leq\alpha
such that
A
is isomorphic to
(\beta,\min)
. It is straightforward to check that
h(\alpha)
is isomorphic to
A
and thus to
(\beta,\min)
.

The proof of (2) is analogous.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X
be a countably compact semitopological semilattice,
x\in X
, and
C
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega,\min)
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

For each neighborhood
U
of
x
the set
C\setminus U
is finite;

(2)

x\in\overline{C}
;

(3)

x=\sup C
.

Proof.

The implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) is obvious.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): Assume that
x\in\overline{C}
. Fix any
c\in C
and neighborhood
U
of
x
. Note that the set
{\uparrow}c\cap U
is nonempty, witnessing that
c\in cU
. Since
U
was arbitrarily chosen and
X
is a Hausdorff semitopological semilattice, we get that
cx=c
. Since the point
c
was chosen arbitrarily, we obtain that
c\leq x
for all
c\in C
, i.e.
x
is an upper bound of
C
. Assume that
y
is another upper bound of
C
. Taking into account that
x\in\overline{C}
, we have
U\cap yU\neq\varnothing
for each neighborhood
U
of
x
. Since
X
is a Hausdorff semitopological semilattice,
x=yx
. Hence
x=\sup C
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): To derive a contradiction, assume that there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that the set
D=C\setminus U
is infinite. It is clear that
D
is a cofinal subset of
C
isomorphic to
(\omega,\min)
. In particular, we have
\sup D=x
. By the countable compactness of
X
, there exists
y\in\overline{D}
. Repeating the arguments above, we obtain
y=\sup D
. Hence
y=x
, but
x\notin\overline{D}
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
X
be a semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\alpha,\min)
for some ordinal
\alpha
. If
\overline{L}
does not contain maximum, then
L
is cofinal in
\overline{L}
and
\overline{L}
is isomorphic to
(\alpha,\min)
.

Proof.

Let
L=\{l_{\xi}:\xi\in\alpha\}
, where
l_{\xi}\leq l_{\eta}
if and only if
\xi\leq\eta
. By Proposition
3.3
,
\overline{L}
is a chain. Fix any
x\in\overline{L}
. If
L\subseteq{\downarrow}x
, then
\overline{L}\subseteq{\downarrow}x
, as
{\downarrow}x
is closed in
X
. But then
x
is the maximum of
\overline{L}
, a contradiction. So, for each
x\in\overline{L}
, the set
L\setminus{\downarrow}x
is nonempty. It follows that the ordinal
\alpha
is limit and
L
is cofinal in
\overline{L}
.
For every
x\in\overline{L}\setminus L
let
\delta(x)=\min\{\xi\in\alpha:x\leq l_{\xi}\}
. Since
L
is cofinal in
\overline{L}
, the ordinals
\delta(x)
,
x\in\overline{L}\setminus L
, are well defined.

Seeking a contradiction, assume that there exists an infinite decreasing chain
\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\overline{L}
. The chain
L
, being isomorphic to an ordinal, contains only finite decreasing chains. Hence we lose no generality assuming that
\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\overline{L}\setminus L
. Observe that
\{\delta(x_{n}):n\in\omega\}
is a nonincreasing chain in
\alpha
. Thus there exists
k\in\omega
such that
\delta(x_{n})=\delta(x_{m})
for each
n,m\geq k
. Then the set
W=X\setminus({\uparrow}x_{k}\cup{\downarrow}x_{k+2})
is a neighborhood of
x_{k+1}
which is disjoint with
L
. The obtained contradiction implies that
\overline{L}
contains only finite decreasing chains. Hence
\overline{L}
is isomorphic to
(\theta,\min)
for some ordinal
\theta
. Fix the order isomorphism
\psi:\overline{L}\rightarrow\theta
. It is easy to see that
\psi
can be defined recursively as follows:
\psi(l_{0})=0
and for each
y\in\overline{L}
,

\psi(y)=\begin{cases}\psi(x)+1,&\hbox{if }\exists x<y\hbox{ such that }\{z\in\overline{L}:x<z<y\}=\varnothing;\\
\sup\{\psi(x):x<y\},&\hbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
\psi(l_{\xi})\geq\xi
for all
\xi\in\alpha
, we get
\alpha\leq\theta
.

Claim 3.10
.

\psi(l_{\xi})\leq\xi+1
for every
\xi<\alpha
.

Proof.

Assume that
\psi(l_{\delta})<\delta+1
for all
\delta<\xi
. There are two cases to consider:

(i)

\xi=\mu+1
for some ordinal
\mu<\alpha
;

(ii)

\xi
is a limit ordinal.

In case (i) we have
\psi(l_{\mu})\leq\mu+1=\xi
. Observe that the set
A=\overline{L}\setminus({\uparrow}l_{\xi}\cup{\downarrow}l_{\mu})
is open in
\overline{L}
and disjoint with
L
. Therefore
A=\varnothing
. Hence there exists no
z\in\overline{L}
such that
l_{\mu}<z<l_{\xi}
. The definition of
\psi
implies that
\psi(l_{\xi})=\psi(l_{\mu})+1\leq\xi+1
.

Consider case (ii). Let
\pi
be the supremum of the set
\{l_{\delta}:\delta<\xi\}
in
\overline{L}
. It is clear that
\pi\leq l_{\xi}
. Since the set
\{l_{\delta}:\delta<\xi\}
is cofinal in
\{x\in\overline{L}:x<\pi\}
and
\psi
is an order isomorphism, we get that

\psi(\pi)=\sup\{\psi(l_{\delta}):\delta<\xi\}\leq\sup\{\delta+1:\delta<\xi\}=\xi,

where the inequality above follows from the inductive assumption. So, if
\pi=l_{\xi}
, then we are done. Assume that
\pi<l_{\xi}
. Observe that the set
\overline{L}\setminus({\downarrow}\pi\cup{\uparrow}l_{\xi})
is empty, as it is open in
\overline{L}
and disjoint with
L
. Hence there exists no
z\in\overline{L}
such that
\pi<z<l_{\xi}
. Then

\psi(l_{\xi})=\psi(\pi)+1\leq\xi+1
.
∎

Since the ordinal
\alpha
is limit,
\alpha=\sup\{\xi+1:\xi<\alpha\}
. Observe that
\psi(L)
is cofinal in
\theta
, as
\psi
is an order isomoprhism. Then
Claim
3.10
yields
\theta\leq\sup\{\xi+1:\xi<\alpha\}=\alpha
. Thus
\theta=\alpha
, as required.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
X
be a countably compact countably tight semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
. Then
\overline{L}
is topologically isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
endowed with the order topology, and
L
is cofinal in
\overline{L}
.

Proof.

Fix any
x\in\overline{L}\setminus L
. Since
X
is countably tight, there exists a countable subset
A
of
L
such that
x\in\overline{L}
. It is clear that there exists
l\in L
such that
A\subseteq{\downarrow}l
. Since
X
is a semitopological semillatice, the set
{\downarrow}l
is closed and hence
x\in\overline{A}\subseteq{\downarrow}l
. It follows that for each
x\in\overline{L}
there exists
l\in L
with
x\leq l
. Thus,
\overline{L}
does not contain maximum. Lemma
3.9
implies that
\overline{L}
is isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
and
L
is cofinal in
\overline{L}
.
Let
\overline{L}=\{y_{\xi}:\xi<\omega_{1}\}
, where
y_{\xi}\leq y_{\delta}
if and only if
\xi\leq\delta
. Fix any
\xi<\omega_{1}
. Clearly, for each
\delta<\xi
the set

\{y_{\alpha}:\delta<\alpha<{\xi+1}\}=\overline{L}\setminus({\downarrow}y_{\delta}\cup{\uparrow}y_{\xi+1})

is open in
\overline{L}
. Hence the order topology on
\overline{L}
is contained in the original one. To derive a contradiction, assume that the order topology on
\overline{L}
is strictly coarser than the original one. Then, taking into account that for each successor ordinal
\theta<\omega_{1}
the point
y_{\theta}
is isolated in both topologies, there exists a limit ordinal
\xi<\omega_{1}
and a neighborhood
U
of
y_{\xi}
such that for any
\delta<\xi
the set
\{y_{\alpha}:\delta<\alpha<\xi+1\}\setminus U
is not empty. Since
\xi
is countable, there exists a cofinal in
{\downarrow}y_{\xi}\setminus\{y_{\xi}\}
subset
\{\pi_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\overline{L}\setminus U
. But this contradicts Lemma
3.8
, as
y_{\xi}=\sup\{\pi_{n}:n\in\omega\}
, but
y_{\xi}\notin\overline{\{\pi_{n}:n\in\omega\}}
.
∎

4
Nyikos semilattices

The aim of this section is to show that each locally compact Nyikos topological semilattice is compact (see Theorem
4.12
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Each separable countably compact semitopological semilattice possesses the minimum.

Proof.

Let
D
be a countable dense subset of a countably compact semitopological semilattice
X
. Recall that for each
x\in X
the set
{\downarrow}x
is closed. Since
X
is countably compact and the family
\{{\downarrow}x:x\in X\}
has the finite intersection property,
Z=\bigcap_{d\in D}{\downarrow}d\neq\varnothing
. Pick any
z\in Z
and note that
zX=z\overline{D}\subseteq\overline{zD}=\overline{\{z\}}=\{z\}
. Hence
z
is the minimum of
X
.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

Let
X
be a semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain. Denote
I_{L}=\{x\in X:|xL|<|L|\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every semilattice
X
and chain
L\subseteq X
the set
I_{L}
is either empty or an ideal.

Proof.

Suppose that
I_{L}\neq\varnothing
. Fix any
x\in X
and
y\in I_{L}
. Since
|yL|<|L|
, we get
|xyL|=|x(yL)|\leq|yL|<|L|
. Hence
xy\in I_{L}
, witnessing that
I_{L}
is an ideal.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a countably tight semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
. Then
I_{L}
is a closed ideal.

Proof.

Observe that
L\subseteq I_{L}
and hence
I_{L}\neq\varnothing
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
I_{L}
is an ideal.
Let
L=\{l_{\xi}:\xi<\omega_{1}\}
, where
l_{\xi}\leq l_{\beta}
if and only if
\xi\leq\beta
. Fix any adherent point
z
of
I_{L}
. Since
X
is countably tight, there exists a countable subset
B\subseteq I_{L}
such that
z\in\overline{B}
.
For each
b\in B
there exists an ordinal
\alpha_{b}<\omega_{1}
such that
bl_{\xi}=bl_{\alpha_{b}}
for all
\xi\geq\alpha_{b}
. Let
\alpha=\sup\{\alpha_{b}:b\in B\}
. Then for each
\xi\geq\alpha
and
b\in B
we have
bl_{\xi}=bl_{\alpha}
. Since
z\in\overline{B}
, for each neighborhood
U
of
z
and
\xi\geq\alpha
we have
Ul_{\alpha}\cap Ul_{\xi}\neq\varnothing
. Taking into account that
X
is a Hausdorff semitopological semilattice, we conclude that
zl_{\alpha}=zl_{\xi}
for all
\xi\geq\alpha
. It follows that
|zL|<\omega_{1}
and hence
z\in I_{L}
.
∎

Similarly one can show the following.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
be a countably tight semitopological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\max)
such that
I_{L}\neq\varnothing
. Then
I_{L}
is a closed ideal.

Note that the order topologies on semilattices

and

coincide. The following result follows from
[

, Theorem 1]

Theorem 4.6

(Banakh, Bonnet, Kubiś)
.

Let
X
be a separable topological semilattice that contains a subspace

homeomorphic to
\omega_{1}
endowed with the order topology. Then
|X\setminus L|>\omega
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X
be a Nyikos topological semilattice and
L\subseteq X
be a chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
. Then there exists
x\in L
such that
x\in\overline{X\setminus I_{L}}
.

Proof.

Let
L=\{l_{\alpha}:\alpha<\omega_{1}\}\subseteq X
, where
l_{\alpha}\leq l_{\beta}
if and only if
\alpha\leq\beta
. It is clear that
L\subseteq I_{L}
.
Fix any
x\in I_{L}
.
Since
xL
is countable, there exists
\delta<\omega_{1}
such that
xl_{\xi}=xl_{\delta}
for each
\xi\geq\delta
. By Lemma
3.11
, the chain
\overline{L}
is topologically isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
equipped with the order topology, and contains

as a cofinal subset. Hence
xl_{\delta}=xa
for each

, implying that the set

is countable. It follows that
I_{L}=I_{\overline{L}}
. Since
X
is separable, it contains a countable dense subsemilattice

. Seeking a contradiction, assume that there exists an open set

such that

.
Consider the subsemilattice

of

generated by
\overline{L}\cup(D\cap U)

Since

we obtain that the set

is countable. Since

is dense in

and

. It follows that
\overline{L}\subseteq\overline{U\cap D}

is dense in the topological semilattice

.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Nyikos topological semilattice. Then

contains no chain isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\min)
.

Proof.

To derive a contradiction assume that
X
contains a chain
L=\{l_{\alpha}:\alpha<\omega_{1}\}

l_{\alpha}\leq l_{\beta}
if and only if
\alpha\leq\beta
. By
[

is countable compact and thus pseudocompact. Theorem
2.9
implies that
\beta X
is a compact topological semigroup. Since
X
is dense in
\beta X
, we obtain that
\beta X
is a semilattice. By Lemma

, there exists

.
Lemma
4.7
implies that there exists
l_{\alpha}\in L
such that for each neighborhood

of
l_{\alpha}
the set

contains an element
d_{U}
.
Since

of

such that

is compact.
The continuity of the semilattice operation in

yields neighborhoods

of
l_{\alpha}
and
V
of

such that

.
By Lemma
3.4
, there exists an ordinal

such that

. Note that

is isomorphic to

5
Proof of the main result and final remarks

A partially ordered space
(X,\leq)
endowed with a topology is called a
pospace
if
\leq
is a closed subset of
X{\times}X
.
Each inverse semigroup
S
carries the natural partial order
\leq
defined by
x\leq y
if and only if
x=xx^{-1}y
.
The Green’s relations
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
on an inverse semigroup
S
are defined as follows:

(i)

(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}
if and only if
x^{-1}x=y^{-1}y
;

(ii)

(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
if and only if
xx^{-1}=yy^{-1}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{R}
;

(iv)

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{L}
.

For more about Green’s relations on inverse semigroups see
[
37
, Chapter 3.2]
.
The following two results can be considered folklore.

Proposition 5.1

(Folklore)
.

Every nonempty compact subset of a pospace contains a minimal element.

Proposition 5.2

(Folklore)
.

If
S
is a topological inverse semigroup, then each two
\mathcal{H}
-classes within the one
\mathcal{D}
-class are homeomorphic.

In fact, Proposition
5.2
follows from the proof of
[
32
, Lemma 2.2.3]
. We are in a position to prove Main Theorem
1.4
, which states that every locally compact Nyikos inverse topological semigroup is compact.

Proof of Main Theorem
1.4
.

Consider a locally compact Nyikos inverse topological semigroup
S
. By Corollary
1.7
,
S
is a topological inverse semigroup. Note that the semilattice of idempotents
E(S)
is a retract of
S
under the continuous map
\pi:x\mapsto xx^{-1}
. It follows that
E(S)
is a locally compact Nyikos topological semilattice. Theorem
4.12
implies that
E(S)
is compact. By Theorem
1.6
,
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup. We claim that
E(S)=E(\beta S)
. Since
E(S)
is compact, it suffices to show that
E(S)
is dense in
E(\beta S)
. For this fix any
e\in E(\beta S)
and its neighborhood
U
. Since
ee^{-1}=e
and
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup, there exists a neighborhood
V
of
e
such that
VV^{-1}\subseteq U
. Since
S
is dense in
\beta S
, there exists
s\in S\cap V
. It follows that
ss^{-1}\in U\cap E(S)
. Since the neighborhood
U
is chosen arbitrarily, we get that
e\in\operatorname{cl}_{\beta S}(E(S))
. Hence
E(S)
is dense in
E(\beta S)
, which implies
E(S)=E(\beta S)
, as claimed.

Seeking a contradiction, assume that
\beta S\setminus S\neq\varnothing
. Since
S
is locally compact, the remainder
\beta S\setminus S
is closed and, consequently, compact. As
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup,
[
38
, Proposition 3.8]
implies that
\beta S
is a pospace with respect to the natural partial order. By Proposition
5.1
,
\beta S\setminus S
contains a minimal element
h
. Let
e=hh^{-1}
and
f=h^{-1}h
. Since
E(S)=E(\beta S)
, we have
e,f\in S
.
Let

T=\{x\in S:xx^{-1}\leq e\hbox{ and }x^{-1}x\leq f\}.

Fix a neighborhood
U
of
h
. Since
ehf=h
, the continuity of the semigroup operation in
\beta S
yields a neighborhood
V
of
h
such that
eVf\subseteq U
. Observe that for every
x\in S\cap V
we have
exf\in T\cap U
. Since
U
was chosen arbitrarily,
h\in\operatorname{cl}_{\beta S}(T)
.

Since
S
is a topological inverse semigroup, the subgroups
H_{e}=\{x\in S\colon xx^{-1}=e=x^{-1}x\}
and
H_{f}=\{x\in S\colon xx^{-1}=f=x^{-1}x\}
are closed in
S
. Theorem
1.3
implies that each closed subgroup of
S
is compact. Thus, the sets
H_{e}
and
H_{f}
are compact. Observe that
H_{e}
is the
\mathcal{H}
-class of the element
e
and
H_{f}
is the
\mathcal{H}
-class of the element
f
.
Consider the
\mathcal{H}
-class
H_{e,f}:=\{x\in S\colon xx^{-1}=e\hbox{ and }x^{-1}x=f\}
. If
H_{e,f}\neq\varnothing
, then
[
37
, Proposition 5]
implies that
H_{e,f}
lies in the same
\mathcal{D}
-class with
H_{e}
and
H_{f}
. By Proposition
5.2
, the set
H_{e,f}
is compact. Thus
h\in\operatorname{cl}_{\beta S}(T\setminus H_{e,f})
. Two cases are possible:

(i)

h\in\operatorname{cl}_{\beta S}(\{x\in S:xx^{-1}<e\hbox{ and }x^{-1}x\leq f\})
;

(ii)

h\in\operatorname{cl}_{\beta S}(\{x\in S:xx^{-1}\leq e\hbox{ and }x^{-1}x<f\})
.

(i) Since
e=hh^{-1}
and
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup, for each neighborhood
U
of
e
there exists a neighborhood
V
of
h
such that
VV^{-1}\subseteq U
. By assumption, there is
x\in V
such that
xx^{-1}<e
and
xx^{-1}\in U
. It follows that
e\in\operatorname{cl}_{S}({\downarrow}e\setminus\{e\})
. Since
S
is first-countable, there exists a sequence
\{e_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq{\downarrow}e\setminus\{e\}
that converges to

. Since
eh=h
we get that the sequence
\{e_{n}h:n\in\omega\}
converges to

. Note that

for each

, as otherwise

for some

, which contradicts the choice of

. Since
e_{n}hh^{-1}e_{n}h=e_{n}h
, we get that
e_{n}h\leq h
with respect to the natural partial order on
\beta S
. Since
h
is a minimal element in
\beta S\setminus S
, we get that
e_{n}h\in S
for all

. It follows that
\{e_{n}h:n\in\omega\}
is an infinite closed discrete subset of

, which contradicts the countable compactness of

.

(ii) Since
f=h^{-1}h
and
\beta S
is a topological inverse semigroup, for each neighborhood
U
of

there exists a neighborhood
V
of
h
such that
V^{-1}V\subseteq U
. By assumption, there is
x\in V
such that
f>x^{-1}x\in U
. It follows that
f\in\operatorname{cl}_{S}({\downarrow}f\setminus\{f\})
. Since
X
is first-countable, there exists a sequence
\{f_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq{\downarrow}f\setminus\{f\}
that converges to
f
. Since

we get that the sequence

converges to

. Similarly as above it can be checked that

and

for every

. Since

is a minimal element in
\beta S\setminus S
, we get that

for all

. It follows that
\{hf_{n}:n\in\omega\}
is an infinite closed discrete subset of

, which contradicts the countable compactness of

.

The obtained contradictions imply

.
∎

A
band
is a semigroup consisting of idempotents. A semigroup
S
is called
simple
if
S
contains no proper two-sided ideals or, in other words,
SxS=S
for any
x\in S
.
The following example shows that Main Theorem
1.4
does not generalize over simple bands.

Example 5.3
.

Let
X
be one of the consistent examples of a locally compact noncompact Nyikos space discussed in the introduction. Let
X_{1}
be the space
X
endowed with the left zero operation, i.e.,
xy=x
for each
x,y\in X
. It is easy to see that this operation is continuous and associative, that is,
X_{1}
is a topological semigroup. Let
X_{2}
be the space
X
endowed with the right zero operation, i.e.,
xy=y
for each
x,y\in X
. Similarly, one can check that
X_{2}
is a topological semigroup.
Taking into account
[
23
, Corollary 3.10.15]
, it is easy to check that the finite product of Nyikos spaces remains Nyikos. Hence the direct product
S:=X_{1}{\times}X_{2}
is a noncompact Nyikos topological semigroup. Note that
(a,b)(a,b)=(a,b)
for every
(a,b)\in S
, which makes
S
a band. Fix
(a,b)\in S
. Then for any
(c,d)\in S
we have
(c,d)=(c,a)(a,b)(b,d)\in S(a,b)S
, witnessing that
S=S(a,b)S
. Since the point
(a,b)
was chosen arbitrarily, the band
S
is simple.

The
Ostaszewski space
was constructed under (
\diamondsuit
) in
[
44
]
. It is a Nyikos space and possesses (among others) the following properties: locally compact, hereditary separable, each compact subspace is countable.
The results of this paper establish another peculiar property of the Ostaszewski space.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
S
be the Ostaszewski space endowed with a continuous semigroup operation. Then each inverse subsemigroup of
S
is countable.

Proof.

Let
Y
be an inverse subsemigroup of
S
. Note that
\overline{Y}
is a locally compact first-countable countably compact topological subsemigroup of
S
. Since
S
is hereditary separable,
\overline{Y}
is a locally compact Nyikos topological semigroup. Corollary
1.7
implies that
\overline{Y}
is a topological inverse semigroup. By Main Theorem
1.4
,
\overline{Y}
is compact. Since each compact subspace of the Ostaszewski space is countable, we get

. Hence
|Y|\leq\omega
, as required.
∎
