Antibiotic use in pediatric dental infections: knowledge and awareness levels of dentists
Authors: Elif Esra Ozmen, Tuğçe Nur Sahin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics are crucial in treating infections; however, their unnecessary and incorrect use can lead to numerous health issues, notably antibiotic resistance. Evaluating the accuracy and awareness of antibiotic prescription practices among dentists, who play a significant role in antibiotic use, is critical in developing effective health policies and professional training programs. This study assesses dentists’ knowledge levels and practice habits regarding antibiotic use. Using a structured questionnaire, the research employed a cross-sectional design and was conducted with 202 general dentists, 98 pediatric dentists, and 43 oral and maxillofacial surgeons. Data were analyzed using demographic characteristics, antibiotic prescription practices, and scenario-based questions measuring knowledge levels. The results revealed that while most participants were generally knowledgeable about antibiotic use, inconsistencies in prescription practices were evident. Notably, unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions were identified in some cases, contributing to significant health issues such as antibiotic resistance. This study emphasizes the necessity of promoting conscious antibiotic use by evaluating dentists’ practices. Therefore, the urgent need for developing professional training programs and strengthening strategies to combat antibiotic resistance is paramount.Keywords: Antibiotics, Pediatric, Dentistry, Antibiotic resistance, Dental infection controlSubject terms: Drug discovery, Risk factors, Paediatric research

Introduction

Antibiotics are vital drugs used in treating bacterial infections and are considered one of the cornerstones of modern medicine1. Although many antibiotics used in medicine today can be entirely synthetic or semi-synthetic compounds, they are naturally produced by bacteria or fungi. They are thus referred to as chemotherapeutic agents. Among these, the antibiotics most suitable for dental issues are β-lactams, macrolides, lincosamides, nitroimidazoles, and tetracyclines2. However, the indiscriminate and unnecessary use of antibiotics has become a growing public health concern worldwide. This practice leads to the development of antibiotic resistance and complicates the treatment of infections. Antibiotic resistance imposes significant financial burdens on healthcare systems and increases the complexity of patient management3.

Antibiotics are frequently prescribed in dental practice, especially for treating dental infections in pediatric patients. While this practice is crucial in managing acute diseases, the unnecessary use and improper dosing of antibiotics reduce treatment efficacy and contribute to the spread of resistant bacterial strains4. Therefore, assessing dentists’ knowledge and awareness levels regarding antibiotic use is essential5.

Dentists’ knowledge and awareness regarding antibiotic use directly impact treatment outcomes and patient safety. Correct and appropriate antibiotic use ensures effective infection treatment. In contrast, incorrect and unnecessary use can lead to the spread of antibiotic resistance6. Additionally, appropriate antibiotic use reduces treatment costs and shortens patient recovery times. Therefore, conscious antibiotic use by dentists is critically essential for both individual patient health and public health7.

Considering this information, this study’s primary aim is to investigate dentists’ knowledge and awareness levels regarding antibiotic use. The study will also evaluate dentists’ antibiotic prescription habits and adherence to current clinical guidelines. The findings of this study will contribute to the development of educational programs for dentists and promote more conscious antibiotic use.

A study conducted in Türkiye found that dentists’ prescribing behavior is influenced by factors such as clinical efficacy, safety, patient compliance, promotional materials, support for attending conferences, frequency of visits by pharmaceutical representatives, and the similarity of drug names to active ingredients8. The study planned to use a survey-based descriptive cross-sectional study to evaluate the knowledge and attitudes of dentists in Jordan. It also evaluated the usual way dentists prescribe antibiotics and the factors affecting this. The results of this study indicate that there are situations where dentists cannot be rational in writing prescriptions and that education is critical to prevent this situation9. A global study evaluated dental antibiotic prescribing patterns in Australia, the United Kingdom, and North America (the United States and British Columbia, Canada). Treatment prescriptions written by dentists in 2017 were examined. In 2017, dentists in the United States had the highest antibiotic prescribing rate per 1,000 people, while Australia had the lowest rate. Penicillin-class antibiotics, notably amoxicillin, were the most prescribed antibiotics for all countries. The second most common agents prescribed were clindamycin in the United States and British Columbia (Canada) and metronidazole in Australia and the United Kingdom. It has been concluded that physicians are affected by many factors when prescribing antibiotics10.

The aim of this study is to assess the knowledge and awareness levels of dentists in Türkiye regarding antibiotic use in pediatric dental infections. Additionally, the study will examine dentists’ antibiotic prescribing habits in pediatric patients and their adherence to current clinical guidelines. The findings of this study will contribute to the development of educational programs for dentists and promote more informed antibiotic use in pediatric dental care.

Methods

Protocol

This cross-sectional study evaluated dentists’ knowledge and awareness levels regarding antibiotic use. The study was designed as survey research using open-ended and multiple-choice questions. It was approved by the Local Scientific Medical Research Ethics Committee of Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University Faculty of Medicine on 26.03.2024, approval number 03-2024/08. Written consent was obtained from the participants, and the Declaration of Helsinki conducted the research. The surveys were distributed to participants both electronically and in print. To address the potential risk of compromising participant anonymity and increasing response errors due to the distribution of surveys both electronically and in print, we implemented the use of unique identifiers. In this process, participants were asked to use a self-selected identifier, such as a nickname or a special code, that would be distinct from those of other participants when completing the survey. These identifiers helped ensure that each participant was uniquely recognized. For instance, if a participant completed the survey both electronically and in print, the unique identifier allowed us to identify that both responses came from the same individual. As a result, during data analysis, such duplicate responses were detected and treated as a single entry, and any repeated responses were excluded from the dataset. This approach helped maintain anonymity while enhancing the data’s accuracy and reliability. Participants completed the surveys anonymously, and the research team securely collected the data. The data collection process took place between June 2024 and July 2024.

The survey forms were prepared using Google Forms (Google, Inc., 2017, California, USA). Before the survey questions, a brief description of the study’s nature and purpose was provided, along with a consent form, in Sect. 1. Participants were assured of the study’s voluntary nature and the confidentiality of their responses. Individuals who selected the option “I do not wish to participate” were excluded from the study. After reviewing the literature, relevant studies and guidelines, a 23-question survey was prepared9,11,12.

A pilot study was conducted with five randomly selected volunteer dentists to evaluate the face and content validity. Respondents used nicknames instead of their real names to ensure data accuracy and confidentiality and were asked to use the same nickname for a follow-up test two weeks later. Data from the pilot study were not included in the final analysis. Subsequently, the validated questions were reviewed by one oral surgeon and two pediatric dentists using a 5-point Likert scale evaluation form sent via email. All questions were revised based on expert feedback and finalized accordingly. The pilot study assessed the comprehensibility of the survey questions, ensuring that participants understood the questions correctly and could provide meaningful responses. It also tested the clarity of the language used in the questions and facilitated revisions where necessary. The pilot study provided insights into the effectiveness of the data collection process. The challenges identified during the pilot study helped improve the processes in the main study. Additionally, the pilot study allowed us to evaluate participants’ reactions to the length and scope of the survey. It enabled us to test the analyzability of the data obtained and whether meaningful results could be produced. Feedback from participants also guided us in refining the survey questions and data collection methods.

A self-administered, online 23-question survey consisting of five sections (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5) was distributed to 360 general and specialist dentists with the support of the Turkish Society of Pediatric Dentistry and the Society of Pediatric Dentistry via email and messages. Seventeen dentists declined to participate, resulting in a study group of 343 general dentists, pediatric dentists, and oral surgeons. No personal information, including email addresses, was requested from the participants. Demographic data, professional experience, and details about the institutions where the participants worked were collected. The study included actively practicing general and specialist dentists under age 65.

The survey form comprised the following sections:

Demographic Information: Gender, age, professional title, and duration of clinical experience of the participants.

Antibiotic Use: Frequency of antibiotic prescriptions, types, and prescribed antibiotic dosages.

Awareness and Knowledge Level: Questions about antibiotic resistance mechanisms, adherence to antibiotic use guidelines, and the consequences of indiscriminate antibiotic use.

Practice Habits: Reasons for prescribing antibiotics and information provided to patients regarding antibiotic use.

Sample size

The population of the study consists of General Dentists, Pediatric Dentists, and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons working in Türkiye. According to the 2022 press release data from the Istanbul Chamber of Dentists, the total number of dentists working in Türkiye is 43,199. Using the simple random sampling method, it was calculated that a minimum of 96 participants would be needed for a 10% sampling error, and at least 381 participants for a 5% sampling error. These calculations were made using the sampling calculation method developed by Yazicioglu and Erdogan13.

Statistics

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation) were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the participants and the survey results. Additionally, group differences were examined using the chi-square test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the participants in the study, 95 were male (27.69%) and 248 were female (72.30%). The study included 202 general dentists (58.9%), 98 pediatric dentists (28.6%), and 43 oral and maxillofacial surgeons (12.5%). Among the participating dentists, 154 had five years or less of experience, while 189 had more than five years of professional experience. The participants reported working in various settings: 109 in university hospitals, 89 in private clinics, 113 in oral and dental health centers, and 32 in their own clinics (Table 1). According to the data obtained from Table 1, the majority of dentists work in large healthcare institutions such as university hospitals (31.8%) and oral and dental health centers (32.9%). However, a significant portion also chooses to practice in private clinics (25.9%) or their own clinics (9.3%). This diversity in work environments suggests that dentists’ professional experiences and specializations may vary depending on their workplace.

The antibiotic prescription habits for pediatric dental infections are shown in Table 2. The majority of the participating dentists demonstrated a high level of knowledge about the consequences of indiscriminate antibiotic use, providing reassurance to the audience about the dentists’ awareness. Of the participants, 94.9% identified antibiotic resistance, 84.8% identified disruption of gut flora, 76.9% identified kidney dysfunction, and 76% identified liver dysfunction as a consequence of improper antibiotic use. Additionally, 56.9% of the participants always ask about the patient’s previous and/or current antibiotic use before prescribing antibiotics. Another 28.6% ask often, 8% ask sometimes, 5.9% are unsure, and 0.6% never ask (Table 3).

Of the participating dentists, 49.1% reported seeing 0–3 pediatric patients per full working day. When prescribing antibiotics, 55.3% always discuss the appropriate dose and method of use with the patient and/or their caregiver. In children diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, 84.4% of the dentists indicated that antibiotics are unnecessary. Similarly, 91.5% of the dentists stated that antibiotics are not required for children with necrotic pulp without systemic infection symptoms. For children diagnosed with acute apical periodontitis with percussion sensitivity and widened periodontal space, 50.1% of the dentists considered antibiotics necessary. Conversely, for children with chronic apical periodontitis with a fistula and periapical radiolucency, 85% of the dentists indicated that antibiotics are not required. For children with localized intraoral fluctuating swelling and acute apical abscesses without systemic disease, 58.7% of the dentists considered antibiotics necessary. Among children with localized fluctuating swelling and immunological dysfunction, 88.9% of the dentists deemed antibiotics necessary. Finally, for children with fever, lymphadenopathy, and trismus, 94.7% of the dentists considered antibiotics necessary (Table 4).

Dentists with more than 5 years of experience recommended using antibiotics more frequently in certain situations than those with less than 5 years of experience. Significant differences were observed in the use of antibiotics, particularly in cases of chronic apical periodontitis and acute apical periodontitis (p < 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated the knowledge levels and practice habits regarding antibiotic use among dentists across Türkiye, revealing essential findings. The participants were considerably diverse in terms of demographic characteristics, professional experiences, and work environments, which enhanced the generalizability of the findings. Of the participants in the study, 95 were male (27.69%) and 248 were female (72.30%). The study included 202 general dentists (58.9%), 98 pediatric dentists (28.6%), and 43 oral and maxillofacial surgeons (12.5%). Among the participating dentists, 154 had five years or less of experience, while 189 had more than five years of professional experience. The participants reported working in various settings: 109 in university hospitals, 89 in private clinics, 113 in oral and dental health centers, and 32 in their own clinics. Most dentists work in large healthcare institutions such as university hospitals (31.8%) and oral and dental health centers (32.9%). However, a significant portion also chooses to practice in private clinics (25.9%) or their clinics (9.3%). This diversity in work environments suggests that dentists’ professional experiences and specializations may vary depending on their workplace. Additionally, it is believed that there is a significant relationship between the work setting and factors such as professional development, patient relationships, and practice management. These findings underscore the importance of considering work environments when designing professional training and development programs for dentists. The inclusion of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (OMFS) in our sample was intentional due to their significant role in managing complex dental and maxillofacial conditions, which often involve pediatric patients. Although OMFS practitioners are not exclusively dedicated to pediatric care, they frequently encounter pediatric cases, particularly those involving trauma, congenital abnormalities, or complicated infections that require surgical intervention. Given their expertise in managing severe and complex conditions, it was crucial to include OMFS in the study to provide a comprehensive understanding of antibiotic prescribing practices across different specialties that interact with pediatric populations. Their inclusion allows for a more holistic analysis of antibiotic use in pediatric dental infections, reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of care required for this patient group.

49.1% of the participants reported seeing 0–3 pediatric patients during a typical workday. This indicates that a large portion of dentists deal with a relatively small number of pediatric patients in their daily practice. However, 11.4% of the participants see more than 15 pediatric patients per day. This suggests that dentists’ daily workloads and the pediatric patient populations they serve vary widely. For those who see more pediatric patients, applying antibiotic guidelines more rigorously may be necessary.

Antibiotics are among the most consumed and misused drug groups. In developing countries, unnecessary antibiotic consumption is prevalent14. Increased antibiotic consumption outside of proper indications can be defined as over-prescription, improper antibiotic use, prescribing drugs not aligned with clinical guidelines, and patients not adhering to the recommended treatment15. Inappropriate antibiotic consumption can increase the risk of antibiotic resistance and decrease the efficacy of antibiotics used to treat bacterial infections14. Consequently, it can lead to a significant public health issue with the rapid spread of resistant bacteria within communities16. Most dentists (47.2%) prescribe antibiotics to less than 5% of their pediatric dental infection patients. This percentage suggests that dentists generally limit antibiotic use and prefer to prescribe antibiotics only when necessary. However, while 1.2% reported never prescribing antibiotics, 3.8% indicated that they prescribe antibiotics to 75–100% of their patients. These findings suggest that while most dentists are cautious in their use of antibiotics, a small group may be at risk of unnecessary antibiotic use. Therefore, more vital guidelines and educational programs may be necessary to optimize antibiotic usage.

Our findings indicate that most participants stated that they prescribe antibiotics only when necessary and by current guidelines (67.8%). However, 29.2% did not confirm this and mentioned that patient demands, and inadequate appointment times influenced their decisions to prescribe antibiotics. This finding suggests a potential inconsistency in the adherence to clinical guidelines for antibiotic use, which could be a significant issue in dental practice. The fact that 29.2% of respondents reported not following the guidelines highlights the importance of educational and awareness-raising efforts. This situation highlights potential risks contributing to the spread of antibiotic resistance due to indiscriminate antibiotic use17. Literature indicates that unnecessary antibiotic use due to patient demands is not uncommon18. Similarly, in our study, 40% of the dentists who believed they did not prescribe antibiotics according to proper guidelines cited patient demand as a reason. Additionally, 23% cited inadequate appointment times as a contributing factor. This finding suggests that patient demands and time pressures may significantly impact dentists’ antibiotic prescribing decisions. This situation highlights the need to address patient education and time management in clinical practice.

Additionally, most participants (56.9%) responded positively to the question, “Do you always or mostly discuss the appropriate dose and method of antibiotic use with the patient and/or their caregiver when prescribing antibiotics?”. Despite this, it is crucial to always inquire about the patient’s current medications. These responses suggest that while dentists consider patient history, which is an essential step in preventing antibiotic resistance, the issue of prescribing antibiotics due to patient demands persists. 56.9% of the participants reported consistently asking about the patient’s current and past antibiotic use before prescribing antibiotics. Additionally, 28.6% said they do this often, 8% sometimes, and 5.9% were unsure. Only 0.6% indicated that they never ask. These data suggest that many dentists consider the patient’s antibiotic history before prescribing. However, given that not all dentists practice this consistently, there may be a need for greater emphasis on reviewing a patient’s history. This could be a critical step in preventing unnecessary antibiotic use.

According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, Türkiye has the highest antibiotic use frequency in Europe19. This indicates that antibiotics, which hold a significant place in our country’s medication consumption, are likely being used indiscriminately. In this study, regarding the frequency of antibiotic prescriptions, 47.2% of the participants reported prescribing antibiotics to less than 5% of their daily patients. However, 52.8% indicated prescribing antibiotics at a higher rate. This finding reflects a tendency towards unnecessary antibiotic use, which can increase the risk of resistance development. In recent years, efforts have been made globally and in our country to control excessive and irrational antibiotic use to minimize its adverse effects20,21.

One of this study’s main objectives was to raise awareness about the unnecessary use of antibiotics. Information about the excessive use of antibiotics is available in our country and many countries worldwide. For example, a study conducted in the United Kingdom found that 80% of antibiotics prescribed to treat odontogenic diseases were unnecessary22. Another study in the United States found that antibiotics were inappropriately used for prophylaxis in 80% of cases23. In 2015, the WHO launched the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance and published the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance24. This plan aims to prevent widespread and incorrect antibiotic use in developed and developing countries.

Odontogenic infections are a group of diseases caused by microorganisms in the dental and periodontal biofilm originating from the hard tissues of the teeth or the periodontium. If untreated, they can lead to severe local or systemic complications. Odontogenic infections can stem from dental caries, trauma, pulpitis, periapical periodontitis, endo-periodontal lesions, pericoronitis, and periodontal disease. The most common cause of these infections is periapical periodontitis, often from severe caries, and the most affected teeth are the mandibular molars25. Despite the high incidence of odontogenic infections, there is no specific criterion for prescribing antibiotics in this field. Many dental pain cases arise from pulp infections requiring a dentist’s surgical intervention rather than antibiotic therapy24.

This study investigated dentists’ antibiotic prescribing habits in various clinical scenarios. The responses indicate that dentists adopt different approaches to antibiotic use. For example, while 84.4% of the respondents deemed antibiotics unnecessary for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, 10% considered them necessary. According to the literature, excessive antibiotic use is every day in cases requiring endodontic treatment, such as irreversible pulpitis without systemic conditions like fever or lymphadenopathy, particularly among children and adolescents26. 84.4% of the participants indicated that antibiotics are not necessary in this case, reflecting a general tendency against using antibiotics in treating symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. Additionally, 91.5% of the participants believe that antibiotics are unnecessary in necrotic pulp without symptoms, suggesting an awareness that antibiotics should only be used when specific indications are present. In another scenario, 50.1% of the participants’ thought antibiotics were necessary, while 42.8% did not, highlighting a difference in decision-making among dentists for this type of case. Furthermore, 85% of the dentists stated that antibiotics are unnecessary in localized apical abscess without systemic disease, whereas 12% believed they were. This indicates that the use of antibiotics in localized apical abscesses is generally not preferred. Finally, 94.7% of the participants noted that antibiotics are necessary in cases with more complex infections, showing that antibiotic use is common in such scenarios.

For acute apical periodontitis, 50.1% of the dentists believed antibiotics were necessary, while 42.8% did not, highlighting inconsistencies in clinical decision-making and a need for further education27. In cases of chronic apical periodontitis with a fistula and periapical radiolucency, 12% of the dentists recommended antibiotics, while 85% did not. The higher percentage of less experienced general dentists (with less than five years of practice) recommending antibiotics suggests that inexperience might contribute to inappropriate prescribing. The chi-square analysis provided valuable insights into the impact of socio-professional characteristics on antibiotic prescribing habits among dentists. Notably, significant differences were identified in specific clinical scenarios, such as “Chronic Apical Periodontitis with Fistula” and “Localized Fluctuating Swelling with Fever and Lymphadenopathy,” where general dentists with more than five years of experience demonstrated statistically significant differences in their prescribing behaviors compared to those with less than five years of experience. These findings suggest that the level of experience directly influences decision-making in these particular clinical contexts, with more experienced dentists adopting different approaches. Conversely, no significant differences were observed in other clinical scenarios or among different specialty groups (pediatric dentists and oral and maxillofacial surgeons), indicating that socio-professional characteristics may have a lesser impact on antibiotic prescribing practices outside of these specific situations. The analysis highlights the critical role of clinical experience in the decision-making process.

For children with localized intraoral fluctuating swelling and acute apical abscesses without systemic disease, 58.7% of the dentists considered antibiotics necessary, while 37% did not. This discrepancy underscores the need for consistent clinical guidelines. However, most dentists appropriately considered antibiotics essential for children with localized fluctuating swelling, pain, lymphadenopathy, and immunological dysfunction or trismus, aligning with the literature24.

When asked about the preferred route of antibiotic administration for a medically healthy 4-year-old with cellulitis, 37.2% of the dentists recommended an oral single antibiotic. For therapeutic purposes (not prevention) in children without drug allergies, 65.2% preferred Amoxicillin + Clavulanic Acid, while 22.7% preferred Amoxicillin28. Although the type, combination, and dosage of antibiotics depend on the severity of the infection and the dominant bacterial species, some studies align with the participants’ preference for the combination of Amoxicillin + Clavulanic Acid over Amoxicillin alone29. Regarding antibiotic choice, 65.2% of the participants preferred the amoxicillin + clavulanic acid combination for children, a widely recommended combination in the literature. This finding suggests that many dentists adhere to current clinical guidelines, although different antibiotic options may be used in some instances. There may be a need for greater standardization and guidance regarding antibiotic selection.

For children with penicillin allergies requiring systemic antibiotics for odontogenic infections, the most prescribed antibiotics were Clarithromycin 250 mg (22.4%) and Clindamycin 150 mg (21.5%). These choices reflect careful consideration of patient safety and efficacy. However, further guidance on alternative antibiotics could enhance standardization in clinical practice. Compared with the literature, Clarithromycin and Azithromycin are acceptable choices30.

When prescribing penicillin, most dentists (45.7%) recommended a 1-week course, while 31% advised patients to continue until the medication was finished. This finding indicates that dentists have varying practices regarding the duration of antibiotic use. Better standardization of treatment duration could be an essential step in combating antibiotic resistance. According to the literature, the typical duration of antibiotic use after dental procedures is 5–7 days31. Patients should be advised to complete the antibiotic course even if symptoms resolve to prevent resistance28.

Dentists were well-informed regarding the consequences of indiscriminate antibiotic use, with 94.9% identifying antibiotic resistance as the most common consequence. The literature indicates that Ampicillin, tetracyclines, and metronidazole have the highest resistance rates, while azithromycin and ciprofloxacin have the lowest32,33. This resistance increases with repeated antibiotic cycles, leading to microorganism selection34. However, there were gaps in knowledge about some consequences of indiscriminate antibiotic use. For example, only 60% of the participants knew that antibiotic resistance could weaken the immune system, and 77% knew it could cause liver function impairment.

Studies indicate that individuals do not use antibiotics without consulting a doctor or pharmacist, which is mainly attributed to the health policies in our country35. It is observed that antibiotic prescriptions for prophylactic or therapeutic purposes in dentistry constitute approximately 10% of antibiotic prescriptions worldwide36. Besides the dentists’ approach, patients not adhering correctly to the prescribed antimicrobial treatment can exacerbate the excessive and incorrect use of antibiotics in dentistry37. In the decision-making process regarding antibiotic use, most participants (55.3%) stated that they always discuss the appropriate dose and method of use with the patient or their caregiver. Additionally, 31% said that they do this most of the time. However, 13.2% indicated that they are unsure or only sometimes have these discussions. These findings suggest that most dentists effectively communicate antibiotic usage instructions to patients and caregivers. However, considering this practice is not consistently followed, there may be a need to emphasize patient education and communication skills. This approach represents a positive step towards correct use and patient education. However, it was observed that there is hesitation in some cases about not prescribing antibiotics, which can lead to inconsistencies in clinical practices.

Unlike adult patients, pediatric patients have more expansive bone marrow spaces and immature facial skeletal bones, which facilitate the rapid spread of dental infections, necessitating early intervention38. Similar studies indicate that pediatric dentists are the specialists who recommend antibiotics the least in rational drug use26. Our study also found that pediatric dentists and oral surgeons are less likely to recommend antibiotics for chronic infections without systemic conditions than general dentists. Among general dentists, experienced practitioners showed a similar approach to specialists, highlighting the importance of experience. Specialists’ success can be attributed to the knowledge acquired during specialty training and the experience gained while treating children and adolescents over a certain period.

The findings provide a basis for developing educational programs for dentists and strengthening professional knowledge and practices. Most participants (94.9%) indicated that indiscriminate antibiotic use leads to antibiotic resistance, demonstrating a high level of awareness among dentists. Additionally, 84.8% were aware of the disruption of gut flora, 76.9% recognized kidney dysfunction, and 76% identified liver dysfunction as other adverse consequences. However, only 62.3% were aware of the weakening of the immune system, and 60.8% acknowledged the development of allergies as potential outcomes. Only 1.5% of respondents knew the possible consequences of indiscriminate antibiotic use. These findings suggest that dentists are generally aware of antibiotic resistance and other side effects. However, awareness of certain side effects, such as the weakening of the immune system and the development of allergies, may be lower. This indicates a need for further education and awareness-raising efforts on these topics.

There are some limitations to this study. For instance, the sample of dentists who participated was selected from specific clinics, which may not fully represent the general population of dentists. This limits the generalizability of the study’s results. Additionally, the data obtained from the survey form may be subjective and based on the participants’ perceptions, which can lead to deviations from objective assessments. Response rates and instances of incomplete responses can also affect data integrity and the accuracy of analyses.

The study’s strengths include using a structured survey form, which provided a comprehensive data set. Researchers collected extensive information on antibiotic use, offering robust data for analysis. Moreover, reaching a large sample of 343 dentists working in different clinics across Türkiye increases the external generalizability of the study. Detailed statistical analyses were conducted on the data, and an ethics committee approved the study by the Declaration of Helsinki. This supports the reliability and scientific quality of the study.

In conclusion, this study has significantly contributed by evaluating dentists’ knowledge levels and practice habits regarding antibiotic use. Our findings underscore the need to strengthen educational programs for dentists and promote more conscious antibiotic use. Such initiatives can be essential to individual patients and overall public health.
