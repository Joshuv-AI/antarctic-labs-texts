Development and validation of a predictive model for early postoperative pain after cesarean section
Authors: Pu Xu, Hui Huang, Guangyin Qiu, Hui Cheng, Li Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify independent risk factors for early postoperative pain following cesarean section and to develop a reliable nomogram prediction model to facilitate clinical risk assessment and individualized pain management. We retrospectively collected clinical data from women who underwent cesarean section at our hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. Early postoperative pain was assessed within 24 hours after surgery using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and the highest NRS score (resting or activity-related) during the first 24 hours was used as the primary outcome measure. Moderate-to-severe pain was defined as NRS > 3 or the need for additional analgesia. Patients were categorized into pain and no-pain groups based on the presence of moderate-to-severe pain. Univariate logistic regression was performed to screen potential risk factors, followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors. A nomogram prediction model was then constructed based on the final multivariate results. Model performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, and clinical utility was examined through decision curve analysis. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling. A total of 768 patients were included, of whom 587 (76.43%) experienced early postoperative pain. Multivariate logistic regression identified 4 independent predictors: diabetes mellitus (OR = 1.763, 95% CI: 1.072–3.874), history of previous surgery (OR = 2.825, 95% CI: 1.152–4.678), absence of intraoperative NSAID use (OR = 3.164, 95% CI: 1.198–9.476), and intraoperative blood transfusion (OR = 2.124, 95% CI: 1.182–7.635). The nomogram demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.804 in the training cohort and 0.770 in the validation cohort, and showed favorable calibration and clinical utility. Early postoperative pain after cesarean section is influenced by multiple perioperative factors. The nomogram developed in this study provides an effective tool for individualized risk prediction, enabling clinicians to identify high-risk patients and implement targeted preventive strategies. This model may contribute to optimizing postoperative pain management and enhancing maternal recovery.

1. Introduction

Cesarean section is the most common procedure in obstetrics and gynecology.[1,2] In China, along with the opening of the second and third child policy, the cesarean section rate in China has been increasing year by year.[3] Some studies have reported that the cesarean section rate in China has increased from 28.8% in 2008 to 36.7% in 2018.[4] The degree of pain after cesarean delivery is generally moderate-to severe, and the source of pain is mainly caused by the following reasons. First, abdominal incision pain, especially after tissue swelling the incision pain is more obvious.[5] Secondly, paroxysmal severe pain in the lower abdomen is triggered after contractions, which mostly occurs 1 to 2 days after surgery.[6] Also, gastrointestinal dysfunction leads to intestinal distension and spasm and triggers dull pain.[7] If acute postoperative pain is poorly controlled, not only does it interfere with early mobility out of bed and mother-infant contact, but the incidence of chronic pain after cesarean delivery is significantly increased.[8,9] It is important to understand the factors influencing early pain after cesarean delivery and to actively correct reversible triggers at an early stage. Many relevant studies have focused on the risk factors for chronic pain after cesarean delivery.[10–12] Existing studies lack accurate prediction models for early pain after cesarean delivery. Nomograms have been widely used in clinical research for individualized risk prediction and have demonstrated good predictive performance in obstetric-related outcomes. Therefore, there is a clear need for a clinically applicable and individualized prediction tool that can identify women at high-risk of early postoperative pain after cesarean section.[13–15] The aim of the present study was to investigate the independent risk factors for pain in the early postoperative period after cesarean section and to develop a nomogram prediction model accordingly.

2. Information and methods

2.1. Data sources and data collection

This study retrospectively analyzed the data of hospitalized cesarean deliveries from January 2022 to December 2023 in our hospital. The study was retrospective and complied with the relevant standards of the Hospital Ethics Committee.

2.2. Inclusion exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: all women who underwent cesarean section; data were complete and the results of the follow-up visits were available. Exclusion criteria included women who underwent cesarean section under general anesthesia; women who were transferred to cesarean section during labor analgesia; and those with incomplete clinical data. Patients converted from labor analgesia were excluded because prior exposure to neuraxial analgesics and prolonged analgesic interventions during labor could significantly influence postoperative pain perception and analgesic requirements, thereby introducing heterogeneity in pain assessment.

2.3. Collection of related variables

Relevant information of women who underwent cesarean section was collected, including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus, hyperemesis gravidarum, marriage, children, surgical history, anesthesia mode, type of surgery, intraoperative use of dexamethasone, intraoperative use of dexmedetomidine, intraoperative use of opioid, intraoperative use of NSAIDs, duration of the operation, amount of intraoperative hemorrhage and intraoperative blood transfusion. The numerical rating scale (NRS) was used according to the postoperative pain score at 24 h. The NRS uses a total of 11 numbers from 0 to 10 to indicate the degree of pain, with 0 being no-pain and 10 being the most pain the woman can imagine. The resting score refers to the resting state pain score; the active pain score refers to the pain score when turning over at 90° or moving out of bed. Postoperative pain was assessed within 24 hours after surgery using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). Both resting pain and activity-related pain were routinely evaluated; however, the highest NRS score (either resting or activity-related) during the 24-hour postoperative period was selected as the primary outcome measure in this study. This approach more accurately reflects the overall pain experience of the patient. A score > 3 on the NRS (either resting or activity-related) or the need for additional analgesia was defined as “inadequate pain control” or moderate-to-severe pain. Accordingly, patients were classified into the inadequate pain control group (NRS > 3 or requiring additional analgesia) and the no inadequate pain control group (NRS ≤ 3 and not requiring additional analgesia).[16]

Continuous variables such as age, BMI, and surgical duration were analyzed as continuous variables in the regression model. To account for potential non relationships, we employed restricted cubic splines to model these variables more accurately. This approach provides a more robust statistical analysis, avoids the loss of information associated with arbitrary categorization, and better reflects the clinical variability of these factors. The categorical approach has been acknowledged as a limitation in the study due to its reduced statistical power.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The collected data were randomly divided into modeling group (70%) and validation group (30%) in the ratio of 7:3 in R (4.3.2) software. In the modeling group, SPSS26.0 software was used for 1-way analysis of the differences between the 2 groups. Continuous variables such as age, BMI, and surgical duration were analyzed as continuous variables using restricted cubic splines to capture potential nonlinear relationships. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Variables with P < .05 screened by univariate analysis were included in multifactorial logistic regression analysis, and variables with P < .05 in multifactorial logistic regression analysis were identified as independent risk factors for early pain after cesarean delivery. The independent risk factors selected in the multifactorial logistic regression analysis were plotted on a nomogram in R software, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted and the area under the curve was calculated in the modeling and validation groups, and the calibration curves and decision curves were plotted to assess the performance of the model.

3. Results

3.1. General information

A total of 768 women who underwent cesarean section were included in this study, and a total of 587 patients experienced early postoperative pain, with an incidence rate of 76.43%. According to the ratio of 7:3, 538 and 230 patients were randomly divided into modeling group and validation group.

3.2. Independent risk factors for early postoperative pain after cesarean delivery

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed exclusively in the training cohort to screen candidate predictors prior to multivariate modeling.

In the modeling group, 16 variables were analyzed by univariate logistic regression analysis, and the results showed that 9 variables were potential risk factors for early pain after cesarean delivery, including BMI, diabetes mellitus, hyperemesis gravidarum, surgical history, intraoperative use of dexmedetomidine, intraoperative use of opioids, intraoperative use of NSAIDs medication, intraoperative bleeding, and intraoperative blood transfusion (Table 1). A multifactorial logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the presence of diabetes mellitus (OR,1.763; 95% CI, 1.072 to 3.874), history of surgery (OR,2.825; 95% CI, 1.152 to 4.678), no intraoperative use of medication with NSAIDs (OR,3.164; 95% CI, 1.198 to 9.476) and intraoperative blood transfusion (OR, 2.124; 95% CI,1.182~7.635) were independent risk factors for early postoperative pain after cesarean delivery (Table 2).

3.3. Nomogram development and validation

A nomogram was developed using the screened independent risk factors and used to predict the risk of early pain after cesarean delivery (Fig. 1). The nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariate model, and its point assignments reflect the relative strength of association between each predictor and the outcome. ROC curves were then plotted for the modeling and validation groups and the area under the corresponding curves were calculated to be 0.804 and 0.770 (Figure 2A, B). In addition, the calibration curves are plotted, indicating that the nomogram predicts the risk in good agreement with the actual occurrence of the risk, and has a good predictive ability (Fig. 2C, D). Also decision making showed that nomogram had good predictive ability (Figure 2E, F).

4. Discussion

It has been found that having diabetes mellitus, history of surgery,nonuse of intraoperative NSAIDs medication and intraoperative blood transfusion are risk factors for early post cesarean section pain. In this study, we used predictors that are common and easily recognized in clinical practice and developed this nomogram prediction model based on the 4 independent risk factors identified and after model validation it was determined that the presently developed model has good predictive ability.

In this study, diabetes mellitus was identified as an independent risk factor for early postoperative pain after cesarean section. Rather than indicating a direct causal mechanism, this association may reflect altered pain perception and increased vulnerability to perioperative stress in patients with metabolic dysregulation. Previous studies[17–19] have suggested that individuals with diabetes may exhibit heightened pain sensitivity and impaired analgesic responses, potentially related to hyperglycemia-associated inflammatory and neuroimmune alterations. In addition, perioperative factors commonly encountered in women with gestational diabetes mellitus, such as glycemic fluctuations, anesthetic and analgesic management, perioperative fasting, and fluid therapy, may further influence postoperative recovery and pain experience.[20] Therefore, diabetes mellitus should be regarded as a clinically relevant marker of increased risk for early postoperative pain rather than a definitive mechanistic cause. These findings highlight the importance of careful perioperative glucose management and individualized analgesic strategies in this population.[21]

In this study, a history of surgery was found to be a risk factor for early postoperative pain after cesarean delivery. A study[22] suggests that a history of preoperative surgery may lead to intra adhesions, and the pulling and damage to the tissues during surgery may exacerbate postoperative pain. In addition, patients with a history of surgery may be more sensitive to pain, and the postoperative inflammatory response and release of nerve growth factor may lead to increased nerve sensitivity.[23] Therefore, for women with a history of surgery, patients should be thoroughly assessed before surgery, including pain thresholds, previous pain experience, and psychological status, in order to develop an individualized pain management plan. Appropriate anesthetic techniques and analgesia should be used during cesarean section and pain control should be initiated as early as possible in the postoperative period.

In this study, the nonuse of intraoperative NSAIDs medication was found to be a risk factor for early postoperative pain after cesarean delivery. Intraoperative use of NSAIDs is an important method of analgesia for cesarean section recommended by the European Society for Regional Anesthesia and Pain Management and the Society of Obstetric Anesthesiologists, and intraoperative administration is the “gold standard” for postoperative analgesia after cesarean section.[24] The results of this study also suggest that the use of NSAIDs is a recommended analgesic measure. It has been suggested that NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin synthesis, an important mediator of inflammation, pain and fever, by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme activity.[25] Therefore, failure to use NSAIDs may result in poor postoperative pain control and increased patient discomfort and pain-related stress. Therefore, before cesarean section, patients should be evaluated for contraindications to the use of NSAIDs. In the absence of contraindications, a multimodal analgesic strategy combining NSAIDs with other analgesic medications is used intraoperatively and postoperatively to achieve better analgesia and reduce the side effects of a single drug.[26]

In this study, intraoperative blood transfusion was identified as an independent risk factor for early postoperative pain after cesarean section. Importantly, blood transfusion itself is unlikely to be a direct biological cause of postoperative pain. Instead, it more plausibly serves as a surrogate marker for surgical complexity, significant intraoperative blood loss, and extensive tissue trauma.[27] These factors are commonly associated with longer operative duration, heightened inflammatory responses, and increased postoperative pain burden. In addition, patients requiring blood transfusion may experience greater perioperative physiological stress and require more intensive intraoperative interventions, which together may contribute to higher pain scores in the early postoperative period.[28] Therefore, the observed association between intraoperative blood transfusion and early postoperative pain likely reflects the overall severity of the surgical procedure rather than a direct causal effect of transfusion on pain perception.[29]

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design is inherently subject to potential bias and unmeasured confounding. Second, this was a single-center study conducted in a tertiary care hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Third, early postoperative pain was defined based on activity-related NRS scores rather than resting pain, which may not fully capture all dimensions of postoperative pain experience. In addition, several continuous variables were categorized using predefined cutoff values to facilitate clinical interpretability, which may have resulted in some loss of information and reduced statistical efficiency. The relatively wide confidence intervals observed for certain predictors may be attributable to the low prevalence of specific exposures, such as intraoperative blood transfusion and nonuse of NSAIDs, potentially affecting the precision of the estimates. Finally, as this prediction model was developed and internally validated in a single cohort, external validation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies is warranted to further confirm its robustness and clinical applicability.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified diabetes mellitus, history of surgery, nonuse of intraoperative NSAIDs, and intraoperative blood transfusion as factors associated with early postoperative pain after cesarean delivery. Based on these factors, a nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized risk estimation in the early postoperative period. However, this model is based on internal validation from a single-center cohort and should be regarded as a preliminary tool. Further prospective, multicenter studies with refined outcome definitions and continuous variable analysis are required to confirm its predictive value and clinical utility before widespread clinical use.
