The effect of virgin coconut oil (VCO) on cardiovascular disease risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yang Zhang, Yilingrui Wang, Lin Li, Xiaoya Hong, Hui Xia, Mohammad Hassan Sohouli, Zhiwei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic disorders remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Virgin coconut oil (VCO), rich in medium-chain triglycerides and phenolic compounds, has been proposed to confer metabolic benefits through lipid modulation, anti-inflammatory activity, and antioxidant effects. However, clinical evidence on its efficacy is mixed. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the impact of VCO on key cardiometabolic risk factors in adults.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in five database from inception to August 2025. RCTs assessing VCO supplementation in adults were included if they reported anthropometric indices, lipid profiles, glycemic markers, blood pressure, or inflammatory outcomes. Data were pooled using a random-effects model.ResultsFourteen RCTs with 1049 participants from 11 countries, with intervention durations ranging from 2 to 24 weeks, met inclusion criteria. Pooled analyses showed that VCO significantly reduced triglycerides (WMD: − 12.12 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 23.14 to − 1.09) and increased high density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) (WMD: 7.91 mg/dL; 95% CI: 4.41 to 11.40). No significant changes were observed for other lipid markers, fasting blood sugar (FBS), anthropometric measures, blood pressure, or CRP. Subgroup analyses indicated greater TG reduction in short-term interventions (≤ 8 weeks) and among individuals with metabolic disorders, and a significant TC increase in participants under 50 years. Assessment of publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s test indicated no significant evidence of bias.ConclusionsVCO supplementation may improve specific lipid parameters, particularly TG and HDL-C, while showing limited effects on other cardiometabolic outcomes. Larger, longer-term trials are warranted to clarify its role in cardiometabolic risk management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02019-6.Keywords: Virgin coconut oil, Cardiometabolic risk, Meta-analysis, Triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Cardiometabolic disorders, including obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance, constitute major contributors to global morbidity and mortality, exerting a heavy burden on healthcare systems [1–4]. The pursuit of nutritional strategies that can modulate these risk factors has gained momentum in recent years [5–7].

Virgin coconut oil (VCO) stands out among dietary fats due to its rich content of medium-chain triglycerides and bioactive phenolic compounds, which may impart antioxidant and anti-inflammatory benefits that differ from those offered by conventional refined oils [1, 5, 8–10]. VCO differs fundamentally from refined coconut oil in its extraction and compositional profile [11]. VCO is obtained from fresh coconut meat through mechanical or natural cold-pressing methods, without exposure to chemical refining, bleaching, or deodorizing [12]. This gentle extraction process preserves naturally occurring antioxidants, polyphenols, and vitamins that are largely lost during the high-heat processing of refined coconut oil [13, 14]. In contrast, refined coconut oil is typically produced from dried copra and subjected to chemical purification, which significantly reduces its content of phenolic compounds and other bioactive constituents. Therefore, the potential health effects of VCO may not be comparable to those of refined coconut oil, justifying the exclusive focus on virgin coconut oil in this review. Meta-analyses have shown that coconut oil can increase both HDL-C and LDL-C compared to non-tropical vegetable oils, whereas refined versions may lack these potentially beneficial phytonutrients [15–18].

Emerging evidence suggests that VCO may decrease triglycerides and fasting blood sugar while increasing HDL-C, although effects on parameters such as body weight, BMI, total cholesterol, and inflammatory markers remain inconsistent and subject to substantial heterogeneity [19–22]. These uncertainties underscore the need to isolate the effects of virgin (unrefined) coconut oil from those of refined or other vegetable oils like sesame oil, which may confound metabolic outcomes [23, 24].

However, most prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not clearly distinguished between virgin (unrefined) coconut oil and refined coconut oil or other tropical and non-tropical oils. This lack of distinction creates uncertainty about whether the observed metabolic effects are attributable to VCO itself or to other oil types. Therefore, a research gap remains in determining the specific cardiometabolic effects of VCO. The present systematic review and meta-analysis was designed to address this gap by including only randomized controlled trials that directly examined VCO supplementation compared with placebo or other oils. The present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the pooled effects of VCO supplementation on key cardiometabolic risk factors, while conducting subgroup and sensitivity analyses to explore heterogeneity and effect modifiers.

Methods

Information sources and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [25]. A comprehensive literature search was carried out from database inception through August 2025 (final search date) in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL), with no language or publication date restrictions. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree terms, and free-text keywords related to virgin coconut oil (e.g., “virgin coconut oil,” “VCO,” “Cocos nucifera”), cardiovascular and metabolic health outcomes (e.g., “lipid profile,” “triglycerides,” “HDL cholesterol,” “fasting blood glucose,” “blood pressure,” “waist circumference”), and inflammatory markers (e.g., “C-reactive protein,” “CRP,” “interleukin-6,” “IL-6”), as well as relevant metabolic disorders (e.g., “metabolic syndrome,” “obesity,” “type 2 diabetes”)(see Supplementary Table 1). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to combine intervention-related and outcome-related terms. Additionally, reference lists of all eligible articles and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify further studies.

Exclusion criteria were: non-randomized studies, observational designs, animal or in vitro studies, studies without a control group, interventions combining VCO with other active compounds where the independent effect of VCO could not be isolated, and non-original publications such as reviews, protocols, or editorials.

Study selection, data extraction, and data items

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts of selected records were retrieved and assessed against inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extracted included: First author, year of publication, and country of study; Participant characteristics: health status, age, BMI, and sample size per group; Intervention details: VCO dose, form, and duration; Comparator type; Baseline and post-intervention means and standard deviations (SD) for relevant outcomes.

When outcomes were reported as standard errors, confidence intervals, or p-values, SDs were calculated using standard formula. If necessary, corresponding authors were contacted for missing data. For trials with multiple follow-up points, data from the longest duration were extracted for analysis.

Risk of bias assessment and certainty assessment

Methodological quality of included trials was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool [26]. Domains assessed included: Randomization process, Deviations from intended interventions, Missing outcome data, Measurement of outcomes, and Selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias. Two reviewers independently conducted the assessment, with disagreements resolved by consensus or a third reviewer. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [27].

Effect measures, data synthesis, and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA version 14.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA). Continuous outcomes were pooled as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Missing SDs were estimated from reported standard errors, confidence intervals, or p-values using standard formulas [28, 29]. A random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was employed to account for between-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics, with I² interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), substantial (50–75%), or considerable (>75%) [30]. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted based on: : Intervention duration (≤ 8 weeks vs. >8 weeks), Participant metabolic status (with metabolic disorder vs. whitout metabolic disorder), and age of participants (< 50 vs. ≥50). Sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out method assessed the robustness of pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plot visualization and Egger’s regression test, with trim-and-fill methods applied if asymmetry was suspected [31]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Study selection

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 2154 records were identified through database searching. After removing duplicates, 1654 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 1626 were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Twenty-eight full-text articles were then assessed in detail, and eight were excluded due to reasons such as insufficient data (n = 2), incompatible study design (n = 2), irrelevant outcomes (n = 8), absence of an appropriate control group (n = 1), or combining VCO with other interventions (n = 1). Ultimately, 14 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis.

A total of 14 RCTs published between 2015 and 2024 were included in this meta-analysis, representing a diverse set of populations across 11 countries, including Iran, the Philippines, Brazil, India, Sri Lanka, the United Kingdom, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and the United States. Study participants ranged from healthy young adults to those with metabolic syndrome, central obesity, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, post-acute coronary syndrome, mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s disease, and COVID-19 infection. Mean participant ages spanned from 21 years in healthy volunteers to 74 years in Alzheimer’s disease patients. Sample sizes ranged from 14 to 184 participants across study arms. Most studies followed a parallel-group design (n = 12), with only two employing a crossover design. Intervention durations varied from 2 to 24 weeks, with the majority lasting 4 to 12 weeks. VCO supplementation protocols differed in dose and form, ranging from 10 capsules/day or 100 mg/day capsules to 50 g/day of oil, or body weight–adjusted doses such as 1.2 mL/kg/day. Comparators included placebo (e.g., carboxymethylcellulose solution), standard diets without VCO, or alternative fat sources such as soybean oil, butter, safflower oil, red palm olein, or canola oil. Baseline BMI values ranged from ~ 20.8 kg/m² in healthy young volunteers to > 33 kg/m² in individuals with obesity, indicating a wide spectrum of cardiometabolic risk profiles. Several trials involved co-interventions such as standardized meals or pharmacologic agents (e.g., atorvastatin) to reflect real-world treatment contexts.

Risk of bias assessment (Based on Table 1)

Overall, the methodological quality of the included RCTs was moderate. Random sequence generation was judged to be low risk in all studies, indicating adequate randomization procedures. One study (Vogel et al., 2020) was rated as high risk of bias overall due to issues with allocation concealment, blinding, and incomplete data. Conversely, several trials (e.g., M. Alejandria 2024, Maiti 2024, Fernando 2023, Teng 2024, Setyawati 2023) achieved a low overall risk of bias rating. In summary, while randomization processes were robust across studies, potential biases related to blinding and incomplete reporting warrant cautious interpretation of findings.

The overall GRADE quality assessment for the meta-analysis was rated as very good (score = 9), supporting a moderate-to-high level of confidence in the pooled estimates.

Meta-analysis

Glucose metabolism outcomes

The meta-analysis indicated that VCO consumption did not significantly reduce serum FBS levels compared with placebo (WMD: − 1.91 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 5.95 to 2.13; P = 0.353), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 52.3%) (Fig. 2).

Anthropometric measurements

In terms of anthropometric indices, the analysis indicated no meaningful differences between VCO and control groups regarding body weight (WMD: 0.13 kg; 95% CI: − 1.75 to 2.01; Pvalue = 0.892; I²=0.0%) and BMI (WMD: − 0.57 kg/m²; 95% CI: − 1.81 to 0.67; Pvalue = 0.367; I²=68.7%). Similarly, WC and Fat mass showed comparable patterns, with WMDs of − 0.23 cm and − 0.08% respectively, though these reductions did not reach significance (Pvalue = 0.894 WC and Pvalue = 0.925 Fat mass), and low heterogeneity was noted for both variables [WC (I²=0.0%) and Fat mass (I²=0.0%)] (Fig. 3).

Forest plots from the meta‐analysis of clinical trials investigating the effects virgin coconut oil on (a) weight (kg), (b) BMI (kg/m2), (c) WC (cm), and (d) Fat mass (%). WMD: weighted mean

Lipid profile parameters

TG levels were significantly lowered following consumption with VCO, with a WMD of −12.12 mg/dL (95% CI: −23.14 to −1.09; Pvalue = 0.031; I²=64.3%). Along with these results, intake of VCO increased HDL levels compared to placebo (WMD: 7.91 mg/dL; 95% CI: 4.41 to 11.40; Pvalue < 0.001; I²=90.2%). However, the effects of VCO on other lipid markers were generally not statistically significant for LDL-C (WMD: 6.10 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 0.91 to 13.11; Pvalue = 0.088; I²=80.9%) and TC (WMD: 6.77 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 1.90 to 15.44; Pvalue = 0.126; I²=79.8%) (Fig. 4).

Inflammation and blood pressure indicators

Following VCO consumption, no significant changes were found in SBP (WMD: 0.53 mmHg; 95% CI: −1.92, 2.98; Pvalue = 0.671; I2 = 20.5%), and DBP (WMD: 0.21 mmHg; 95% CI: −1.81, 2.22; Pvalue = 0.842; I2 = 0.0%) as well as C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (WMD: − 0.75 mg/L; 95% CI: − 2.29 to 0.80; Pvalue = 0.343), despite low heterogeneity (I²=0.0%) (Fig. 5).

Subgroups analysis

Subgroup analysis indicated that virgin coconut oil (VCO) supplementation significantly reduced triglyceride (TG) levels in interventions < 8 weeks (WMD: − 20.31 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 37.23 to − 3.39) and in participants with metabolic conditions (WMD: − 14.76 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 25.88 to − 3.64). HDL cholesterol increased significantly across most subgroups, particularly in short-term interventions and in those without metabolic conditions. Total cholesterol (TC) significantly increased only in participants < 50 years (WMD: 13.65 mg/dL; 95% CI: 2.50 to 24.79).

Heterogeneity varied, with low inconsistency in some TG and HDL subgroups and high variability for several TC and LDL comparisons. These patterns suggest that participant health status, age, and intervention duration may influence the metabolic effects of VCO and partly explain heterogeneity in the main analyses(Supplementary Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out method was applied to assess the influence of each individual study on the pooled effect size. The findings remained robust after sequential elimination of studies (Supplemental Figs. 1–4).

Publication bias

A visual review of the funnel plots indicated no apparent asymmetry, suggesting a low likelihood of publication bias concerning the effects of VCO interventions across the evaluated outcomes. This visual observation was further corroborated by Egger’s regression test, which revealed no statistically significant evidence of publication bias for any of the parameters analyzed. The P-values were as follows: weight (P = 0.453), BMI (P = 0.881), WC (P = 0.327), fat mass (P = 0.624), LDL cholesterol (P = 0.903), HDL cholesterol (P = 0.542), TC (P = 0.807), TG (P = 0.464), FBS (P = 0.138), CRP (P = 0.624), SBP (P = 0.174), and DBP (P = 1.00). Supplementary Figs. 5 through 8 present the corresponding funnel plots. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method revealed no missing studies, further supporting the robustness of the publication bias analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized available randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence on the effects of VCO on cardiometabolic risk factors. The pooled results suggest that VCO significantly reduces TG concentrations and increases HDL-C, while showing no meaningful effects on FBS, anthropometric indices, TC, LDL-C, blood pressure, or CRP. Subgroup analyses indicated heterogeneity in effects, with TG reduction more pronounced in interventions lasting less than eight weeks and in participants with metabolic disorders, and a significant TC increase in those aged under 50 years. These findings provide nuanced insights into the metabolic impact of VCO, especially in the context of prior evidence on coconut oil and other dietary fats.

In the current analysis, VCO did not significantly reduce FBS compared with placebo, suggests variability in population characteristics and intervention protocols. These findings are consistent with prior meta-analyses that have reported negligible effects of coconut oil on glycemic indices in non-diabetic individuals [32, 33]. For example, Neelakantan et al. (2019) found no significant change in fasting glucose when coconut oil replaced other dietary fats in healthy or overweight adults [32]. Mechanistically, medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) in VCO are absorbed directly into the portal circulation and undergo rapid β-oxidation, which in ketogenic diets can improve insulin sensitivity and glycemic control [34]. However, most included trials in our analysis were conducted under isocaloric or ad libitum conditions, limiting the potential for metabolic shifts seen in low-carbohydrate or ketogenic contexts [35]. Moreover, the inclusion of metabolically healthy participants likely attenuated observable effects, as baseline glycemia was already within normal range. By contrast, in small-scale RCTs involving type 2 diabetes patients, modest improvements in glycemic markers have been reported with MCT supplementation [36], suggesting that VCO may have a more noticeable effect in hyperglycemic populations—a hypothesis that warrants targeted investigation.

Our pooled analysis showed no meaningful differences between VCO and control groups in body weight, BMI, WC, or fat mass. These results mirror those from previous systematic reviews, which found minimal or clinically irrelevant weight changes with coconut oil supplementation [37, 38]. For example, a recent meta-analysis by Abdollahi et al. concluded that any weight reduction associated with coconut oil intake was likely due to calorie displacement rather than intrinsic metabolic effects [38]. From a mechanistic perspective, MCTs have been shown to increase postprandial thermogenesis and fat oxidation compared with long-chain triglycerides [35], potentially leading to small weight reductions over time. However, these effects are typically observed in tightly controlled feeding studies and often dissipate in free-living conditions where total caloric intake is not restricted [39]. Moreover, several trials included in our analysis added VCO to existing diets rather than substituting it for other fats, which could increase total caloric intake and negate any thermogenic advantage. The low heterogeneity for WC and fat mass suggests consistent null effects across studies, strengthening the conclusion that VCO supplementation alone is unlikely to induce meaningful changes in body composition without concurrent energy restriction or structured lifestyle interventions.

The most robust findings of this meta-analysis were a significant TG reduction and a marked HDL-C increase. These results are in line with earlier reports indicating that coconut oil, particularly VCO, tends to raise HDL-C and may modestly lower TG, especially in individuals with elevated baseline values [32, 33]. The TG-lowering effect observed here was particularly strong in short-term interventions (< 8 weeks) and in participants with metabolic disorders, echoing findings from MCT oil studies in which rapid alterations in hepatic lipid metabolism lead to early reductions in circulating TG [34, 40]. MCTs enhance fatty acid oxidation and decrease hepatic de novo lipogenesis, thereby lowering VLDL-TG production [41]. The additional presence of antioxidant phenolics in VCO may attenuate lipid peroxidation and improve lipid handling [23, 42]. The consistent HDL-C elevation across most subgroups aligns with metabolic effects of lauric acid, which increases HDL-C through upregulation of apolipoprotein A1 synthesis and reduced cholesteryl ester transfer protein activity [33]. The ~ 8 mg/dL increase observed in our analysis is clinically notable, exceeding the HDL-C change reported in some meta-analyses that pooled both refined and virgin coconut oils [32, 33]. However, the clinical relevance of isolated HDL-C elevation is debated, as recent Mendelian randomization and intervention studies have questioned whether simply raising HDL-C reduces cardiovascular events [40]. For LDL-C and TC, pooled effects were not statistically significant overall. This variability likely reflects differences in comparator oils: replacing polyunsaturated vegetable oils with VCO tends to increase LDL-C, whereas replacing butter or other saturated fats may lower it [41]. Notably, our subgroup analysis revealed a significant TC increase in younger participants (< 50 years), potentially due to higher dietary cholesterol sensitivity or differing dietary patterns in this group. From a clinical perspective, the TG reduction of approximately 12 mg/dL observed in our pooled analysis, while statistically significant, is relatively modest compared with reductions typically achieved with pharmacological lipid-lowering agents. However, even modest TG lowering has been associated with reduced cardiovascular risk in high-risk populations, particularly when baseline TG levels are elevated. The present findings are in line with other recent meta-analyses evaluating functional plant oils and nutraceuticals with cardiometabolic benefits. For instance, Vahedi et al. (2025) [43]demonstrated that sesame oil supplementation significantly improved triglyceride and HDL-C levels in individuals with metabolic disorders, supporting the lipid-modulating potential of bioactive plant oils. Similarly, a study reported that sesame oil favorably influenced metabolic biomarkers, including lipid and glycemic parameters, across clinical populations [44]. Moreover, Atefi et al. (2021) [45]found that barberry supplementation significantly reduced both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, highlighting the broader role of plant-derived bioactive compounds in cardiometabolic regulation. These results, together with our current findings on virgin coconut oil, underscore that natural oils rich in phenolic compounds and medium-chain fatty acids may exert beneficial effects on lipid metabolism and cardiovascular risk factors through complementary mechanisms.

No significant change in CRP was observed overall. This finding is consistent with previous RCTs showing little to no effect of VCO on systemic inflammation in healthy adults [46]. In vitro and animal studies have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects of VCO phenolics via inhibition of NF-κB activation, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity [36, 47–50]. However, translating these molecular effects to measurable changes in systemic biomarkers may require longer interventions, higher phenolic intake, or populations with elevated baseline inflammation [51, 52]. Interestingly, some subgroup analyses in our dataset suggested reductions in CRP in older participants or specific intervention types, indicating that anti-inflammatory effects may be more detectable in high-risk or high-inflammatory-burden groups. Future trials should stratify by baseline inflammatory status to better assess these relationships.

VCO supplementation had no significant effect on systolic or diastolic blood pressure, consistent with earlier reviews of coconut oil interventions [5, 53]. While phenolic compounds in VCO could theoretically improve endothelial function and vascular tone, the modest magnitude of dietary fat–related effects on blood pressure generally requires prolonged exposure and may depend on concurrent weight loss or sodium reduction [54, 55]. Given the short duration of most included trials, the absence of blood pressure changes is not unexpected.

The considerable heterogeneity observed for LDL-C and HDL-C (I² up to 90%) may be explained by several factors. First, the choice of comparator oils varied across trials: replacing polyunsaturated oils (e.g., soybean, safflower, or canola oil) with VCO generally increased LDL-C, whereas replacing animal fats (e.g., butter) had the opposite effect. Second, participant characteristics, including age, baseline lipid status, and presence of metabolic disorders, likely influenced lipid responses. Third, differences in intervention dose (ranging from 10 capsules/day to 50 g/day) and duration (2–24 weeks) may have contributed to inconsistent findings. These variations underscore the need for standardized trial designs to better isolate the effects of VCO on lipid outcomes.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of focusing specifically on virgin coconut oil and conducting detailed subgroup analyses, several limitations warrant consideration. The number of high-quality, long-duration RCTs remains small, with many studies limited by small sample sizes, unclear blinding, and variable comparator oils. Dietary intake outside the intervention was often not strictly controlled, making it difficult to isolate the effects of VCO substitution versus addition. Furthermore, while we attempted to include only virgin coconut oil, some trials did not clearly specify processing methods, potentially introducing variability in phenolic content and bioactive compound levels. Another limitation of our review is that we were unable to perform a dose-response analysis of VCO supplementation. The included trials applied highly heterogeneous dosing strategies (ranging from fixed daily amounts such as 10–50 g/day to body weight–adjusted regimens), and the limited number of studies reporting comparable doses precluded formal meta-regression. As such, it remains unclear whether higher or prolonged doses of VCO may yield different cardiometabolic effects. Future trials with standardized dose categories are needed to clarify potential dose-response relationships. Another limitation of this review is that it was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO. Although prospective registration is generally encouraged, we decided against it due to reported concerns about potential misuse of registered titles and the lengthy approval process, which may delay timely completion of reviews. Nonetheless, all steps of our work were conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 recommendations to minimize risk of bias and ensure transparency. A key limitation of this review is that most of the included RCTs were of short duration, generally less than 12 weeks. This considerably limits the ability to extrapolate our findings to longer-term cardiometabolic outcomes or to assess sustained safety and efficacy of VCO supplementation.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, the present findings suggest that virgin coconut oil supplementation could be considered as an adjunct dietary strategy for patients with metabolic disorders, particularly those presenting with hypertriglyceridemia or low HDL-C. The significant reduction in TG and elevation of HDL-C observed in short-term interventions highlight a potential role of VCO in lipid management when integrated into an overall balanced diet. However, given the neutral or inconsistent effects on LDL-C, total cholesterol, glycemic control, body weight, and blood pressure, VCO should not replace established lipid-lowering or cardiometabolic therapies. Clinicians should also be cautious in recommending VCO to the general population until more long-term safety and efficacy data become available. In practice, VCO may be most appropriate for targeted use in individuals with specific lipid abnormalities, ideally as part of a comprehensive lifestyle and dietary modification plan, rather than as a universal dietary recommendation. These findings also underscore the importance of considering the type of fat that VCO replaces in the diet. When VCO substitutes for animal-derived saturated fats such as butter, the overall effect may be favorable, with modest reductions in TG and increases in HDL-C that could be beneficial for lipid management. By contrast, replacing unsaturated vegetable oils (such as olive, soybean, or canola oils) with VCO is unlikely to confer additional benefit and may even increase LDL-C and total cholesterol levels.

Conclusion

In summary, VCO supplementation appears to significantly reduce TG and raise HDL-C, with greater benefits observed in short-term interventions and among individuals with metabolic disorders. However, effects on other cardiometabolic parameters are minimal or inconsistent, and the long-term cardiovascular implications of HDL-C elevation in the absence of LDL-C reduction remain unclear.
