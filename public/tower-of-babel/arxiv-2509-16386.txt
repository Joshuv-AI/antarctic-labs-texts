Stokes’ theorem as an entropy-extremizing duality
Daniel Lazarev

Abstract
. Given a manifold ℳ ⊂ ℝ n \mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} , we consider all subsets of ℳ \mathcal{M} that satisfy the generalized Stokes’ theorem and show that ∂ ℳ \partial\mathcal{M} maximizes the associated information theoretic entropy functional. This provides an information theoretic characterization of the duality expressed by Stokes’ theorem, whereby the boundary of a manifold is the ‘least informative’ subset of the manifold satisfying the Stokes relation.



August 10, 2026

Entropy has been used to recast and strengthen many foundational results in geometry and analysis, including the isoperimetric inequality, the Brunn-Minkowski inequality, and Young’s inequality
[
DCT91
,
CT91
]
. Beyond reproving known results, an entropic perspective can uncover new structure. For example, the celebrated entropy monotonicity result of Artstein, Ball, Barthe and Naor
[
ABBN04
]
shows that entropy not only characterizes the Gaussian extremizer in the central limit theorem, but also increases monotonically along the sequence of normalized sums of i.i.d. random variables, thus suggesting a deeper gradient flow structure toward Gaussianity
[
JKO98
,
Vil09
]
.

In this note, we show that Stokes’ theorem admits a natural reinterpretation as an entropy-extremizing duality. Given a differential form
\omega
on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we identify
\partial\mathcal{M}
as the maximum entropy representative among all subsets of
\mathcal{M}
that encode the same integral information about
{\operatorname{d}}\omega
. In this formulation, entropy maximization serves as a variational principle that selects the canonical boundary dual to an exact form. This perspective provides a conceptual bridge between geometric dualities and information-theoretic optimality, suggesting a general framework in which classical dualities—such as those arising from convex analysis, Hodge theory, or functional inequalities—may be understood via entropic extremality
[
AMS04
,
AAM09
,
HK22
]
. This entropy-based approach may also be extended beyond Stokes’ theorem, hinting at a more general entropic calculus over cohomological or variational structures.

The generalized Stokes’ theorem is given by

Theorem 1

(Stokes’ Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an orientable manifold of dimension
n
with boundary
\partial\mathcal{M}
, and let
\omega
be an
(n-1)
-form, with exterior derivative
{\operatorname{d}}\omega
. Then

(1)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}{\operatorname{d}}\omega=\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\omega\;.

The proof is constructive and follows, at its core, from the fundamental theorem of calculus and the definition of the derivative (see, for example,
[
GP74
]
).
In general, however, there could be other subsets of
\mathcal{M}
that satisfy (
1
).
We define the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{M}
that satisfy the Stokes relation for a given
\omega
,

(2)

\mathcal{B}_{\omega}:=\left\{B\subset\mathcal{M}:\int_{B}\omega=\int_{\mathcal{M}}{\operatorname{d}}\omega\right\}.

Of course, since it must integrate with respect to the
(n-1)
-form,
\omega
, every
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional subset of
\mathcal{M}
.
Our main result, given in Theorem
3
below, shows that among all sets
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
satisfying the Stokes relation,
\int_{B}\omega=\int_{\mathcal{M}}{\operatorname{d}}\omega
,
\partial\mathcal{M}
is the only one that maximizes entropy.
From an information theoretic perspective, this means
\partial\mathcal{M}
serves as the only subset of
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the constraint given by the Stokes relation but assuming no more information, or satisfying no additional constraints, other than that one.

Given any
n
-form,
\alpha
, we write its integral over
\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)
, the
n
-ball centered at
x\in\mathcal{M}
of radius
\varepsilon
,

\alpha(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)):=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)}|\alpha|={\operatorname{vol}}\big(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)\big)\;\bar{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)),

where
\bar{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))
is the average of
|\alpha|
over
\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)
, and where the integration is over the absolute value of the differential form to avoid sign issues.

We can then define an
(
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
)
-ball
,

\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon_{\alpha})
by choosing a radius
\varepsilon_{\alpha}>0
such that

(3)

{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon_{\alpha}))=\alpha(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)).

In essence,
\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon_{\alpha})
is larger where there is more mass as given by
\alpha
at
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Notice that
\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon_{\alpha})=\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)
for
\alpha=1\,d{\operatorname{vol}}
, and more generally, if
\alpha=c^{n}\,d{\operatorname{vol}}
, for some constant
c
, then
\varepsilon_{\alpha}=c\varepsilon
.

We first define a distribution function based on the above construction:

Definition 2
.

Given a manifold and form,
(\mathcal{A},\alpha)
, both of dimension
n
, we define the
density function
of
\alpha
on
\mathcal{A}
by,

(4)

\rho_{\alpha,\mathcal{A}}(x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon_{\alpha}))}{{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))\;\alpha(\mathcal{A})}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\alpha(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))}{{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))\;\alpha(\mathcal{A})}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\bar{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))}{\alpha(\mathcal{A})}\;,

provided the given
x\in\mathcal{A}
is not a singularity and
\alpha(\mathcal{A})\neq 0
.
Then the
entropy of
(\mathcal{A},\alpha)
, by,

(5)

S(\alpha,\mathcal{A}):=-\int_{\mathcal{A}}\rho_{\alpha}\log(\rho_{\alpha})\;d{\operatorname{vol}}\;.

If the manifold on which
\rho_{\alpha,\mathcal{M}}
is defined is clear, we will also write
\rho_{\alpha}:=\rho_{\alpha,\mathcal{M}}
, and we will similarly omit the subscripted dimension from the volume form if clear from context.

For example, for
\alpha=c\,d{\operatorname{vol}}
,

\rho_{c\,d{\operatorname{vol}}}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{c\,{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))}{{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))\,c\,{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathcal{M})}=\dfrac{1}{{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathcal{M})},

whose entropy is
S(c^{n}d{\operatorname{vol}},\mathcal{M})=\log{\operatorname{vol}}(\mathcal{M})
. Thus, forms with constant value for all points serve as a sort of ‘uniform distribution’. This is in keeping with the standard definition of entropy of measures (generalizing both discrete and continuous entropies), where by generalizing the definition to include the entropy of any finite measure (not necessarily a probability measure), one could check that the entropy of a constant measure always attains the maximum value (that of the uniform distribution) regardless of the value of the constant weight
[
Laz23
]
.

Theorem 3
.

With the notation as in Theorem (
1
) and formula (
2
),

(6)

S(\omega,B)\leq S(\omega,\partial\mathcal{M}),

with equality if and only if
B=\partial\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
, define
\operatorname{int}(B):=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{int_{JB}}(B_{i})
, where
\operatorname{int_{JB}}(\cdot)
is the Jordan-Brouwer interior of a set and
\{B_{i}\}
is the set of connected components of
B
.
Then

\int_{B}|\omega|=\sum_{i}\bigg|\int_{B_{i}}\omega\;\bigg|=\sum_{i}\bigg|\int_{\operatorname{int}(B_{i})}{\operatorname{d}}\omega\bigg|=\int_{\operatorname{int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|\,.

Consider small (closed) cubes
\sigma_{i}\subset\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of side length
\varepsilon
, for some
\varepsilon>0
. For small enough
\varepsilon
,
\int_{\sigma_{i}}{\operatorname{d}}\omega\approx c_{i}\int_{\sigma_{i}}d{\operatorname{vol}}
, where
c_{i}
is a constant.
Consider the sum of such cubes such that

\int_{{\rm int}(B)}{\operatorname{d}}\omega\approx\sum_{i}c_{i}{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\sigma_{i})
.

For all
x\in\sigma_{i}
, and small enough
\varepsilon>0
so that

\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon)\subset\sigma_{i}

\rho_{{\operatorname{d}}\omega,{\rm int}(B)}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{|c_{i}|{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))}{{\operatorname{vol}}_{n}(\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,\varepsilon))\;\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}=\dfrac{|c_{i}|}{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|},

More specifically, if
x\in\partial\sigma_{i}\subset\sigma_{i}
(since
\sigma_{i}
is a closed cube), and small enough
\varepsilon>0
so that

\mathbb{B}^{n-1}(x,\varepsilon)\subset\partial\sigma_{i}
, we have

\rho_{\omega,B}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{|c_{i}|{\operatorname{vol}}_{n-1}(\mathbb{B}^{n-1}(x,\varepsilon))}{{\operatorname{vol}}_{n-1}(\mathbb{B}^{n-1}(x,\varepsilon))\;\int_{B}|\omega|}=\dfrac{|c_{i}|}{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}=\rho_{{\operatorname{d}}\omega,{\rm int}(B)}(x).

The entropy of
\rho_{\omega}:=\rho_{\omega,B}
is then,

S(\omega,B)=-\sum_{i}\dfrac{|c_{i}|}{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}\log\left(\dfrac{|c_{i}|}{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}\right)=\log\prod_{i}\left(\dfrac{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}{|c_{i}|}\right)^{\dfrac{|c_{i}|}{\int_{{\rm int}(B)}|{\operatorname{d}}\omega|}}

In the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
limit, we get a formula for the entropy of
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
:

(7)

S(\omega,B)=\log G_{\omega}({\rm int}(B)),

where
G_{\omega}
is the weighted geometric mean determined by
\omega
, which can be expressed as a product integral.
Since
G_{\omega}
is a monotone increasing set function, and for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
,
{\rm int}(B)\subseteq{\rm int}(\partial\mathcal{M})
, enlarging the domain from
{\rm int}(B)
to
{\rm int}(\partial\mathcal{M})
cannot decrease entropy, with equality only when
B=\partial\mathcal{M}
.
∎

Example 4
.

Given positive constants,
a,b,c,r
, let
X=[-2a,2a]\times[-b,b]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}c,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(x,y)\in[-a,a]\times[-b,b]\\
c/r,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(x,y)\in[a,2a]\times[-b,b]\\
-c/r,\,\,\,\,\,\,(x,y)\in[-2a,-a]\times[-b,b]\end{cases}.

Let
Y=[-a,a]\times[-b,b]
, so
Z_{Y}:=\int_{Y}f(x,y)\,\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y=4abc
,
and

Z_{X}:=\int_{X}|f(x,y)|\,\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y=4abc+4abc/r
.
Then,
\rho_{Y}=1/(4ab)
, so
S(f,Y)=\log(4ab)
. Similarly,

\rho_{X}=\dfrac{|f|}{Z_{X}}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{4ab+4ab/r},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(x,y)\in Y\\
\dfrac{1}{4ab+4abr},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(x,y)\in X\setminus Y\end{cases},

and

S(f,X)=\dfrac{1}{1+1/r}\log\left(4ab+\dfrac{4ab}{r}\right)+\dfrac{1}{1+r}\log\left(4ab+4abr\right).

Then,

S(f,X)-S(f,Y)=\dfrac{1}{1+1/r}\log\left(1+\dfrac{1}{r}\right)+\dfrac{1}{1+r}\log\left(1+r\right)\geq 0,

since
r>0
, so
S(f,Y)\leq S(f,X)
even though
\int_{X}f(x,y)\,\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y=\int_{Y}f(x,y)\,\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y
.

Interestingly, the same does not hold for the mean. The mean of
|f|
over
Y
is given by
\langle f\rangle_{Y}:=\int_{Y}|f|\,p_{Y}\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y=c
, while
\langle f\rangle_{X}:=\int_{X}|f|\,p_{X}\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y=\dfrac{1}{1+1/r}\cdot c+\dfrac{1}{1+r}\cdot\dfrac{c}{r}
. Note that
\langle f\rangle_{Y}=\langle f\rangle_{X}
for
r=1
, but
\langle f\rangle_{Y}\geq\langle f\rangle_{X}
for
r>1
and
\langle f\rangle_{Y}\leq\langle f\rangle_{X}
for
r<1
. The same is true for generalized means of all orders,
\langle f\rangle_{Y}^{p}:=(\int_{Y}|f|^{p}\,p_{Y}\,{\operatorname{d}}x\,{\operatorname{d}}y)^{1/p}
,
p>1
.

Example 5
.

Define
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the annulus with inner radius
r_{i}
and outer radius
r_{o}
, and form
\omega=r\,{\operatorname{d}}\theta
with counterclockwise orientation.
Then
\int_{\partial M}\omega=2\pi(r_{o}-r_{i})=\int_{M}{\operatorname{d}}\omega
. Other than
\partial M
, the only other candidate
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
is such that
\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_{B}\omega=2\pi r_{B}
, that is, the circle with counterclockwise orientation with radius
r_{B}=r_{o}-r_{i}
.

The normalization for
\partial M
is then,
\int_{\partial M}|\omega|=2\pi(r_{o}+r_{i})
, so

(8)

\rho_{\omega,\partial M}(x)=\begin{cases}r_{o}/(2\pi(r_{o}+r_{i})),\;\;\;\;\;x\in\partial M_{o}\\
r_{i}/(2\pi(r_{o}+r_{i})),\;\;\;\;\;x\in\partial M_{i}\end{cases}\;\;.

Then

S(\omega,\partial M)=\dfrac{r_{i}^{2}}{r_{o}+r_{i}}\log\left(2\pi\frac{r_{o}}{r_{i}}+2\pi\right)+\dfrac{r_{o}^{2}}{r_{o}+r_{i}}\log\left(2\pi\frac{r_{i}}{r_{o}}+2\pi\right).

On the other hand,
\int_{B}|\omega|=2\pi r_{B}
, so
\rho_{\omega,B}=1/(2\pi)
,
so

S(\omega,B)=r_{B}\log(2\pi).

Noticing that
r_{B}=(r_{o}^{2}-r_{i}^{2})/(r_{o}+r_{i})
, we have

S(\omega,\partial M)-S(\omega,B)=\dfrac{r_{i}^{2}}{r_{o}+r_{i}}\log\left((2\pi)^{2}\frac{r_{o}}{r_{i}}+(2\pi)^{2}\right)+\dfrac{r_{o}^{2}}{r_{o}+r_{i}}\log\left(\frac{r_{i}}{r_{o}}+1\right)>0.

The entropy-extremizing perspective for Stokes’ theorem presented here suggests a broader framework of
entropy-extremizing dualities
. In this note we have focused on subsets

B\subset\mathcal{M}
satisfying the Stokes relation, and shown that the canonical boundary

\partial\mathcal{M}
uniquely maximizes entropy.

Instead of the space of submanifolds satisfying the Stokes relation, we can similarly consider the space of operators that globally satisfy the Stokes relation: Given
\omega\in\Omega^{n-1}(\mathcal{M})
, one may consider the alternative
operators
\delta:\Omega^{n-1}(\mathcal{M})\to\Omega^{n}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\delta\omega\;=\;\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\omega\;,

that is, operators indistinguishable from the exterior derivative
{\operatorname{d}}
when integrated over
\mathcal{M}
.
Preliminary investigations indicate that among all such
\delta
, entropy is similarly maximized uniquely
when
\delta={\operatorname{d}}
.

These observations point toward a more comprehensive theory of entropy-extremizing dualities, potentially
encompassing classical dualities from convex analysis, Hodge theory, and functional inequalities. A systematic development of this framework is the focus of future work.
