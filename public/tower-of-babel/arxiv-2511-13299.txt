1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the construction and study of free objects in
the category of Banach
f\!
-algebras. Our main motivation comes from the recent developments in the framework of free Banach lattices, with the aim of exploring the limitations of this constructions in the more general setting of Banach
f\!
-algebras, as every Banach lattice equipped with the identically zero multiplication becomes a Banach
f\!
-algebra. Our goal is to provide the first steps in the foundation of free Banach
f\!
-algebras and open up new research directions on the interaction between Banach spaces and Banach
f\!
-algebras.

The reader might wonder why (Banach)
f\!
-algebras? why free objects? After addressing these questions, we outline the contents of the paper, its main results, and the technical and conceptual difficulties they involve.

1.1.
Why (Banach)
f\!
-algebras?

The notions of
f\!
-algebra and
f\!
-ring were introduced by
Birkhoff and Pierce back in
[
20
]
. Since then, these structures have
been extensively studied from an algebraic point of view. A
comprehensive account of this line of research can be found in the
survey
[
34
]
and its more than 150 references.

But why were these notions introduced in the first place? To answer this question, let us consider the framework of vector lattices consisting of real-valued functions defined on a set. Most such spaces
naturally carry a multiplication operation (tipically, the pointwise
product) under which the positive cone remains closed. Vector lattice algebras provide an abstraction of this situation. However, Birkhoff and Pierce soon realized that the general
theory of vector lattice algebras was not particularly tractable, as a further condition was needed to properly abstract the behavior of function spaces. This missing ingredient was the
f\!
-algebra condition: if
x\wedge y=0
and
z\geq 0
,
then

(zx)\wedge y=0=(xz)\wedge y.

This property encapsulates the idea that pointwise product of functions preserves its
supports. The mathematical fact that led
Birkhoff and Pierce to this particular condition is the following:
a vector lattice algebra is the subdirect product
of totally ordered algebras if and only if it satisfies the
f\!
-algebra condition. As a consequence, one of the most celebrated results
in the early theory of
f\!
-algebras was obtained: every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra is
associative and commutative (even if associativity is not assumed as
part of the definition).

The first application of
f\!
-algebras to Operator Theory
is due to Bigard and Keimel
[
15
]
and
to Conrad and Diem
[
27
]
, independently: the set of
orthomorphisms (order bounded operators that preserve bands) on an arbitrary vector lattice, equipped with the usual order and with composition as product, forms an
f\!
-algebra. This observation
gave rise to an intensive development of the theory of
f\!
-algebras from an
operator-theoretic perspective. The works of Huijsmans

[
38
,
38
,
40
]
, de
Pagter

[
30
,
41
,
42
,
59
]
,
Bernau

[
13
,
12
,
14
]
,
van Rooij
[
25
]
, and Wickstead

[
68
,
71
]
,
among others, greatly contributed to this progress. More recent
developments and applications include:
the study of averaging and Reynolds operators

[
22
]
, the construction of vector
lattice powers
[
24
]
,
\mathbb{L}
-functional
analysis
[
49
]
,
\mathbb{L}
-valued integration

[
47
]
, tensor products

[
26
,
44
]
, free objects

[
48
,
46
]
, and the parametrization of all
f\!
-algebra products on AL and AM-spaces
[
56
]
.

Banach
f\!
-algebras were first investigated in the 1980s by Martignon

[
54
]
and Scheffold

[
63
,
62
,
64
]
. It is remarkable how
little attention these objects have received, given that Banach
lattices have been extensively investigated and that Banach
f\!
-algebras are
particularly well behaved. First of all, one should note that every Banach lattice equipped with the
identically zero multiplication becomes a Banach
f\!
-algebra in a standard (yet somehow trivial) way. Also recall that every Banach
f\!
-algebra with identity is lattice-algebra isomorphic to a space
of continuous functions—a fact that directly connects with the original motivation
of modelling spaces of functions.

A relevant property in the research of
f\!
-algebras is concerned with the existence of divisors of zero: an
f\!
-algebra is semiprime if and only if
x^{2}=0
implies
x=0
. In our research, we will soon face the following elementary question: is the
completion of a normed semiprime
f\!
-algebra also semiprime? This innocent-looking question turned out to be surprisingly subtle. Although it is not difficult to verify that the completion of a normed
f\!
-algebra is a Banach

f\!
-algebra, the answer to the question above will be negative: in
Example

5.3
we will construct a normed
semiprime
f\!
-algebra whose completion is not semiprime. Remarkably, the construction
uses the fact that the free normed
f\!
-algebra generated by
\ell_{1}
is
semiprime, providing a direct application of free objects to answer such a basic question.

1.2.
Why free objects?

The previous paragraph justifies how free Banach
f\!
-algebras can be a useful tool to tackle certain questions. From a more general perspective, it is natural to assume that in order to understand a given category, one must understand its free objects.
Free groups, algebras, modules, lattices, and Boolean algebras, among others, play a
central role throughout algebra and order theory. In topology, the Stone–Čech
compactification and the completion of a metric or uniform space can
also be viewed as instances of free constructions. A modern and increasingly
powerful approach in Functional Analysis is to study free objects in
categories related to Banach spaces. Lipschitz free Banach spaces,
holomorphic free Banach spaces, and free Banach lattices are notable
examples
[
32
]
. It is therefore natural to attempt extending the theory of
Banach
f\!
-algebras in this direction.

The main inspiration for developing free Banach
f\!
-algebras comes from the
theory of free Banach lattices. The free Banach lattice generated by a
set was first introduced in
[
60
]
. Although this foundational work
established several key properties, many questions remained open, as
the authors lacked an explicit description of the norm in such objects.
This gap was later filled in
[
6
]
, where both an explicit construction and
a norm were provided for the more general case of the free Banach lattice
generated by a Banach space. This breakthrough resolved several of the
open problems from
[
60
]
and also answered a question posed by J. Diestel
concerning weakly compactly generated Banach lattices. Following these
two seminal papers, the theory of free Banach lattices has flourished,
with significant developments in various directions (for instance, free complex
Banach lattices
[
37
]
,
free Banach lattices generated by a lattice

[
7
,
3
]
and free Banach lattices
satisfying convexity conditions
[
45
]
) and applications in the study of
norm-attaining lattice homomorphisms
[
17
,
28
]
, relations between linear and
lattice embeddings
[
4
]
, constructions of push-outs
[
8
]
and complemented subspaces of Banach lattices

[
36
]
.
The study of free Banach lattices themselves has also become
a subject of great interest

[
58
,
9
,
31
,
5
,
29
]
, as they serve as the canonical tool for
understanding the interaction between Banach spaces and Banach lattices.

The study of free Banach
f\!
-algebras represents a further step in this
direction. Although these objects (and more generally, free Banach lattice
algebras) have been considered before

[
46
,
67
]
, no successful construction is yet
known of a free object where the norm, lattice, and algebraic
structures interact. The main obstacle lies in the presence
of a product, which renders most known techniques ineffective. Indeed,
the existing methods for free Banach lattices often rely on the fact that equations in a vector lattice are
positively homogeneous—something that no longer holds in
f\!
-algebras.
New approaches are therefore required. In what follows, we outline the
nature of these difficulties, the techniques we have employed, and the
results we have obtained.

1.3.
Outline and main results

The core of the paper lies in Sections
4

and
5
. In
Section

4
we describe the
abstract construction of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by a Banach
space
E
, and try to make this construction as explicit as possible. More
precisely, in
Section

2
we start from the explicit description of the free
Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
as a sublattice-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{E^{*}}

and work our
way to the free normed and Banach
f\!
-algebras using a known
general argument. This argument, however, does not provide anything
close to an explicit description of the objects, because one of the steps in
the construction
involves quotienting out
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
by the kernel of a maximal seminorm
that is not given explicitly.

Using a novel structure theorem for Banach
f\!
-algebras developed in

Section

3
, we are able to compute this kernel:
it consists of the functions that vanish on the
unit ball
B_{E^{*}}
(
4.10
). This elegant result is
particularly useful, as this
set is precisely the kernel of the restriction map
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})
. Quotienting it out we obtain an injective lattice-algebraic
representation of the free normed
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
inside

C(B_{E^{*}})
(
4.11
).

The reader familiar with free Banach lattices may not be surprised. After all, it is a known fact that the free Banach lattice generated by the Banach space
E
can be represented
inside
C(B_{E^{*}})
. Yet several new difficulties arise in our setting: The elements of the free vector
lattice are positively homogeneous, so the restriction map
\operatorname{FVL}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})
is already injective. In contrast, the elements of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
are not necessarily
homogeneous, and therefore
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
cannot be represented inside

C(B_{E^{*}})
via this map. It is a remarkable coincidence that, in
constructing
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, we quotient out exactly the kernel of this
non-injective restriction map.

The representation of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
inside
C(B_{E^{*}})
has immediate applications. For instance, the free normed

f\!
-algebra generated by a Banach space is always semiprime
(
4.15
). A much more substantial application is the
description, up to isomorphism, of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by
a finite-dimensional Banach space
E
. This space is isomorphic to

C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})
equipped with pointwise order and multiplication given by

(f\star g)(r,u)=rf(r,u)g(r,u),

where
(r,u)\in[0,1]\times S_{E^{*}}
and
f,g\in C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})
, together with the map
\eta\colon E\to C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})
defined by
\eta(x)(r,u)=u(x)
for
x\in E

(
4.16
). This characterization has important consequences:
the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by a finite-dimensional Banach space
is semiprime (
4.22
), and Banach spaces of the
same dimension generate isomorphic free Banach
f\!
-algebras
(
4.24
). It is worth noting here that unlike in the case of free Banach
lattices, the latter result is not immediate from the definition,
as free Banach
f\!
-algebras allow only the extension of contractive operators. Indeed, a
contractive isomorphism between finite-dimensional Banach spaces need
not extend to an isomorphism between the free Banach
f\!
-algebras they
generate (
Example

6.9
).

The proof of the above theorem relies heavily on the study of formal
expressions involving the lattice, linear and multiplication operations. These expressions, called LLA expressions, are analyzed in

Section

2
. The main result,
2.2
, states
that every LLA expression that vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
must also vanish on
every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra. This result, already known in the context of
universal algebras, is here reproved in a language more familiar to
functional analysts. As part of the proof, we establish analytic
properties of LLA expressions (
2.6
) that will later
prove useful in several other results. Using these facts, we obtain an
explicit description of the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra generated by a set
(
2.8
) and study its properties
(Sections
2.2
and
2.3
).

Returning to free Banach
f\!
-algebras, note that the above characterization
for finite-dimensional Banach spaces provides only limited information
about the free norm. One of the most striking features of the free
Banach lattice is that its norm can be computed by considering only
operators with range in
\ell_{1}^{n}
. In
4.29
we
prove an analogous result: the norm in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}^{n}]
can be
computed by restricting to operators into finite-dimensional and semiprime Banach

f\!
-algebras. After some additional results, we extend this to show that the
same holds for
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[L_{1}(\mu)]
for any measure
\mu

(
4.34
).

The representation of the free normed
f\!
-algebra in
C(B_{E^{*}})
plays a
central role in understanding this space. It is then natural to ask:
is the extension of this map to the completion (that is, to the free
Banach
f\!
-algebra) also injective?
Section

5
is devoted to
this question. When the answer is affirmative, we say that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]

is representable in
C(B_{E^{*}})
. This property is significant,
since many results about the free Banach lattice rely only on the fact
that it embeds into
C(B_{E^{*}})
, and one may expect similar
phenomena for the free Banach
f\!
-algebra. However, the question has no
immediate answer, as the representation of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
in
C(B_{E^{*}})

is not an isomorphic embedding.

5.1
clarifies this issue by showing
that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is representable in
C(B_{E^{*}})
if and only if it is
semiprime. The next natural question is then whether
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
itself
is semiprime. We know that
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
is, but the completion of a
semiprime normed
f\!
-algebra need not be semiprime
(
Example

5.3
). In general, it remains open whether
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]

is semiprime, though the results of
Section

4
show that it is
semiprime whenever
E
is finite-dimensional or
E=L_{1}(\mu)
for an
arbitrary measure
\mu
.

Section

6
examines the lattice and algebraic properties of
the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by a Banach space. As we mentioned above, much
more can be said when these objects are representable in

C(B_{E^{*}})
(that is, when they are semiprime). It is remarkable
that, although the free vector lattice generated by a Banach space
E
is order dense in
the corresponding free Banach lattice only when
E
is finite dimensional, the free normed
f\!
-algebra is always order dense in the
corresponding free Banach
f\!
-algebra whenever the latter is semiprime
(
6.6
). We also analyze the behavior of an
operator
T\colon E\to F
with respect to its natural extension

\bar{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]

(
6.10
). These properties are subtler than
in the free Banach lattice setting. As mentioned earlier, a
contractive isomorphism
T\colon E\to F
need not extend to an
isomorphism
\bar{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]
, even when
E
and
F

are finite-dimensional (
Example

6.9
).

For isometries, however, the situation is more accessible. Isometric
Banach spaces have lattice-algebra isometric free Banach

-algebras, and
in
Section

7
we prove the converse, provided that the
underlying Banach spaces have smooth duals (
7.3
).
Finally,
Section

8
is devoted to the construction of free
(Archimedean, normed, Banach)
f\!
-algebras with an algebraic identity. The
discussion of these objects is considerably simpler, since every

f\!
-algebra with a norm-one identity is lattice-algebra isometric to

Preliminaries

For the basic facts regarding vector and Banach lattices we refer the
reader to

[

,
50
,
1
]
.
A concise introduction to

-algebras can be found in

Let

containing

. If

is just a vector

the same vector lattice equipped with the identically

The free vector lattice generated by a set

(respectively, by a vector space

) will be denoted by
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
(respectively,
\operatorname{FVL}[E]
).
The free Banach lattice generated by a Banach space

2.
Free Archimedean
f
-algebras

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a set. The
free (Archimedean)
f\!
-algebra generated
by
S
is an (Archimedean)
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
together with a map

\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
such that, for every
(Archimedean)

f\!
-algebra
A
and every map
T\colon S\to A
, there exists a
unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to A

satisfying
\hat{T}\circ\delta=T
.

The existence of free
f\!
-algebras has been long known in the field of
universal algebras
[
48
, Section 5.2]
: it follows from the fact that
f\!
-algebras form an
equationable class
[
18
, Theorem VII.8]
. Archimedean

f\!
-algebras, however, do not form an equationable class
[
46
, Section 8]
.
Henriksen and Isbell showed in

[
35
]
that every LLA expression (that
is, every expression involving finitely many variables, linear and
lattice operations, and a product) that vanishes on the reals, also
vanishes on every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra. It then follows from a standard argument due to
Birkhoff
[
19
]
that the free
Archimedean
f\!
-algebra exists.

In
Section

2.1
we revisit the result of Henriksen and Isbell through
completely new lens. Their proof and formulation depended heavily on
universal algebra language and techniques that may be unfamiliar to
the modern analyst. Here we shall use a more operator-centric
approach together with a representation theorem
due to Henriksen and Johnson
[
33
]
.

Admittedly, this may not seem very original. Yet this approach will be
used in
Section

3
to show that a refined version of the
result holds for Banach
f\!
-algebras. This result will, in turn, be central
in the construction of free Banach
f\!
-algebras, the main topic of this
paper.

In
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
the lattice and algebraic
properties, respectively, of the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra are explored.
This work is the analogue of what Baker
[
11
]
and Bleier

[
21
]
did for free vector lattices
The reader familiar with free vector lattices
will find the properties of free Archimedean

f\!
-algebras surprisingly familiar and, at the same time, different enough
to be of interest and to deserve a separate study.

Even though free
f\!
-algebras date back to 1956, there are still several
open conjectures regarding free (Archimedean)
f\!
-algebras (see

[
48
]
for a survey on the topic). Some of these
have received recent attention, especially the Birkhoff-Pierce
conjecture
(see for instance
[
52
,
53
,
66
,
51
]
). Also, free
f\!
-algebras play a prominent role in the
study of semialgebraic geometry (see
[
48
, Section 5]
for
an introduction to the topic and further references).

2.1.
Construction of free Archimedean
f\!
-algebras

Informally, a lattice-linear-algebraic (LLA) expression is any
expression constructed using finitely many variables, the lattice,
linear and multiplication operations. An LLA expression is said to vanish
on a subset
B
of an
f\!
-algebra if “evaluating” the expression at arbitrary
elements of
B
always gives zero. This section is devoted to proving
the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA expression. If
\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
,
then it also vanishes on every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra.

Once this result is established, it will follow from a standard
argument that the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra generated by a set
S
is
the vector lattice algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
generated by the
evaluations
\delta_{s}
, where
\delta_{s}(x)=x(s)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
and
s\in S
.
But before proceeding to the proof of
2.2
, a more
precise definition of LLA expressions is needed.

2.1.1.
LLA expressions

Let
\Omega
be a set of formal operations and let
T
be a set of
formal variables. We are going to describe the procedure to construct formal
expressions on the variables of
T
using the operations from

\Omega
. The definition of such expressions is inductive on the
complexity of the expression. Define every element of
T
and the
0-ary operations
to be expressions of complexity
1
. If
\omega\in\Omega
is a

k
-ary operation, for some
k\geq 1
, and
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k}
are expressions of complexity
n-1
, then
\omega(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k})
is an expression of complexity
n
. By construction, every formal
expression is a word in the alphabet
T\cup\Omega

containing only finitely many elements from
T
. The
fact that at most
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in T
appear in
\Phi
is made
explicit by writing
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
. Denote by

\mathcal{E}(T,\Omega)
the set of formal expressions on the
variables of
T
and using the operations from
\Omega
. The formal
operations with words in
\mathcal{E}(T,\Omega)
make it into an

\Omega
-algebra (see
[
46
, Section 3]
for a more
detailed introduction to the language of universal algebras).

Let
A
be an
\Omega
-algebra. Given a map
\phi\colon T\to A
it
extends, by induction on complexity, to a unique
\Omega
-algebra
homomorphism
\tilde{\phi}\colon\mathcal{E}(T,\Omega)\to A
. If

\phi(t)=a_{t}
, denote
\tilde{\phi}(\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}])
by

\Phi(a_{t_{1}},\ldots,a_{t_{n}})
.

When
\Omega
consists of a 0-ary operation
0
, a unary operation

\lambda
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and two binary operations

+
and
\vee
, the elements of
\mathcal{E}(T,\Omega)
are called

lattice-linear (LL) expressions
in
T
. Note that, for this
particular
\Omega
,
every vector lattice is an
\Omega
-algebra. If
\Omega
contains an
additional binary operation
\cdot
, the elements of

\mathcal{E}(T,\Omega)
are called
lattice-linear-algebraic (LLA)
expressions
. In this case, every vector lattice algebra is an
\Omega
-algebra. Observe that every LL expression can also be regarded as an
LLA expression where
\cdot
does not appear. Conversely, from every
LLA expression
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
one can produce an LL expression

\Phi_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
in the following way: repeat the same steps
as in the inductive construction of
\Phi
except that, every time the
product operation is performed, the resulting expression is set to

0
. This way, only operations
0
,
\lambda
,
+
and
\vee
appear
in
\Phi_{0}
, which is therefore a lattice-linear expression. An LLA
expression
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
is said to
vanish
in a
subset
B
of a vector lattice algebra
A
if
\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
for every
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in B
.

2.1.2.
2.2
for
f\!
-algebras with identity

The proof of
2.2
is divided into several steps: first,
we show the result is true when an identity is present; then we show
it is true when the product is identically zero; and finally we prove
the general case.

The case with identity follows easily from a representation theorem
due to Henriksen and Johnson
[
33
]
. Given a compact Hausdorff space
K
, denote by
C^{\infty}(K)
the set of
continuous functions from
K
into
[-\infty,\infty]
, the
two-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}
, that are real-valued on an
open dense set. Given
f\in C^{\infty}(K)
, denote by

R(f)
the set of points at which
f
is real-valued.

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(K)
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The functions

\lambda f
,
f\vee g
and
f\wedge g
, defined pointwise, belong to

C^{\infty}(K)
. If there are functions
h,k\in C^{\infty}(K)

satisfying
h(x)=f(x)+g(x)
and
k(x)=f(x)g(x)
for all
x\in R(f)\cap R(g)
,
then
h
and
k
are called the
sum
and
product
of
f

and
g
, and are denoted
f+g
and
fg
, respectively. Since
R(f)\cap R(g)
is dense
in
K
, these operations are uniquely determined. But they may not
exist. A subset
A
of
C^{\infty}(K)
closed under these operations (meaning that the sum and product
of any two elements in
A
always exist and belong to
A
) has an
f\!
-algebra structure. The main
result of Henriksen and Johnson
[
33
, Theorem
2.3]
is that every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity is
lattice-algebra isomorphic to an
f\!
-algebra in
C^{\infty}(K)
, for an appropriate
K
, with the
identity going to the constant one function in
C^{\infty}(K)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA expression. If
\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
,
then it also vanishes on every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity.

Proof.

Identify
A
with an
f\!
-algebra in
C^{\infty}(K)
, for a certain compact
Hausdorff
K
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
. By definition of the
operations in
C^{\infty}(K)
:

\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})(t)=\Phi(a_{1}(t),\ldots,a_{n}(t))=0\quad\text{for every }t\in R(a_{1})\cap\cdots\cap R(a_{n}).

Since
R(a_{1})\cap\cdots\cap R(a_{n})
is dense in
K
, it follows
that
\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
.
∎

2.1.3.
2.2
for
f\!
-algebras with the zero product

It is a well-known fact that every lattice-linear expression vanishing on
\mathbb{R}

must also vanish on every vector lattice (see, for instance,

[
11
]
). This will be key in showing that

2.2
holds for
f\!
-algebras with the zero product. The
proof of this fact is done in two steps: first, it is
shown that an LLA expression vanishing on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
must vanish on
every vector lattice with the zero product
(
2.4
); then it is shown that every LLA
expression vanishing on
\mathbb{R}
must also vanish on
\mathbb{R}_{0}

(
2.5
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA expression and let
X
be a
vector lattice. If
\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
, then it also vanishes
on
X_{0}
.

Proof.

At the core of this proof is the fact that
\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\Phi_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X_{0}
. To show this, proceed by induction on
k
, the
complexity of
\Phi
. If
k=1
, then
\Phi=\Phi_{0}
and the result
is clear. Suppose it is true for LLA expressions of complexity up to

k
, and let
\Phi
have complexity
k+1
. If either

\Phi=\Psi+\lambda\Upsilon
or
\Phi=\Psi\vee\Upsilon
for some LLA
expressions
\Psi
and
\Upsilon
of complexity up to
k
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\Phi_{0}=\Psi_{0}+\lambda\Upsilon_{0}
or
\Phi_{0}=\Psi_{0}\vee\Upsilon_{0}
, and the desired result follows from applying
the induction hypothesis to
\Psi
and
\Upsilon
.
If instead
\Phi=\Psi\Upsilon
, then by definition
\Phi_{0}=0

and, since the product in
X_{0}
is identically zero, also

\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\Psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\Upsilon(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0.

In particular, this applies to
\mathbb{R}_{0}
. Hence
\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=0
for
every
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}
. Since
\Phi_{0}
is
a
lattice-linear expression that vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
, it must also vanish
on every vector lattice. It
follows that
0=\Phi_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA expression. If
\Phi
vanishes on a
neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
, then it also vanishes on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
.

Proof.

Note that
\Phi=\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
naturally determines a
continuous function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\mapsto\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
. The
same is true of
\Phi_{0}
. We are going to prove that

(1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

holds for every
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

by induction on the complexity of
\Phi
.

If
\Phi
has complexity
1
, then (
1
) is clear.
Assume that the result is true if
\Phi
has complexity up to
k-1
.
Let
\Phi
have complexity
k
. There exist LLA expressions
\Psi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
and
\Upsilon[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
of complexity
up to
k-1
such that either:

(i)

\Phi=\lambda\Psi+\Upsilon
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, in which case
\Phi_{0}=\lambda\Psi_{0}+\Upsilon_{0}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})+\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lambda\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lambda\Psi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})+\Upsilon_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}).

(ii)

\Phi=\Psi\vee\Upsilon
, in which case
\Phi_{0}=\Psi_{0}\vee\Upsilon_{0}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})\vee\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\vee\bigg(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\vee\Upsilon_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}).

(iii)

\Phi=\Psi\cdot\Upsilon
, in which case
\Phi_{0}=0

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})\cdot\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\bigg(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\Upsilon(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})\bigg)=0,

where in the last equality we are using that
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\mapsto\Upsilon(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
defines a continuous
function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that
\Upsilon(0,\ldots,0)=0
by
construction.

Now suppose there exists a
\delta>0
such that
\Phi(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})=0
for every
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in[-\delta,\delta]^{n}
. Then the limit in (
1
) must vanish, and
therefore
\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=0
for
every

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. It was shown in
the proof of
2.4
that, when evaluated in
\mathbb{R}_{0}
,

\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=0
. Hence
\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
.
∎

Equation (
1
) will be of great use in later sections.
For convenience, we collect it in a separate lemma and show that the
limit is uniform on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For every LLA expression
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
and
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(\varepsilon\lambda_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\lambda_{n})}{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}).

Moreover, the limit is uniform on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the following elementary fact:
Let
K
be a compact metric space and let
(f_{n})
and
(g_{n})
be
sequences of real-valued continuous functions defined on
K
such
that
f_{n}\to f
and
g_{n}\to g
uniformly, for some
f,g\in C(K)
.
Let
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. Then
h\circ(f_{n}\times g_{n})\to h\circ(f\times g)
uniformly, where
f\times g\colon K\times K\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined by
(f\times g)(k_{1},k_{2})=(f(k_{1}),g(k_{2}))
,
(k_{1},k_{2})\in K\times K
.

Let us check this. By keeping only a tail of the sequences, we may
assume that
\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty},\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}<1
hold for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
[a,b]
be an interval in

\mathbb{R}
containing the images of
f
and
g
. Then

f_{n}(K),g_{n}(K)\subseteq[a-1,b+1]
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
. Being
[a-1,b+1]^{2}
a compact
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the continuous function
h
is uniformly
continuous on it.
Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
\delta>0
be such that
|h(x,y)-h(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|<\varepsilon

whenever
|x-x^{\prime}|,|y-y^{\prime}|<\delta
and
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in[a-1,b+1]
.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty},\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}<\delta
whenever
n\geq N
. It follows that for every
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
:

|h(f_{n}(k_{1}),g_{n}(k_{2}))-h(f(k_{1}),f(k_{2}))|<\varepsilon

whenever
n\geq N
. This proves the fact.

Now let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact subset. We will show that
the limit is uniform on
K
by induction on the complexity of
\Phi
. If

k=1
, then
\Phi=\Phi_{0}
and the limit is uniform on
K
. Suppose the
limit is uniform on
K
whenever the LLA expression has complexity up to
k
.
Let
\Phi
be a LLA expression of complexity
k+1
. Then there
exist LLA expressions
\Psi
and
\Upsilon
of complexity up to
k
, and
a continuous function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=h(\Psi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}),\Upsilon(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})).

Of course, this continuous function
h
can only be a linear function,
the supremum or the multiplication. Let
(r_{m})
be an arbitrary
sequence of positive real numbers decreasing to zero. If
h
is
positively homogeneous (i.e., if it is a linear function or the
supremum), then

\frac{\Phi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}=h\bigg(\frac{\Psi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}},\frac{\Upsilon(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}\bigg).

By induction hypothesis

\bigg(\frac{\Psi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}\bigg)_{m}\text{ and }\bigg(\frac{\Upsilon(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}\bigg)_{m}

converge to
\Psi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and

\Upsilon_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, respectively, uniformly
on
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in K
. By the initial
observation, the sequence

\bigg(\frac{\Phi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}\bigg)_{m}

also converges to
h(\Psi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}),\Upsilon_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}))=\Phi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
uniformly on
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in K
.

It remains to check the case when
h
is the multiplication. In
this case,
\Phi_{0}=0
. The sequence

(\Upsilon(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n}))_{m}

converges to zero uniformly on
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in K
. Indeed,
\Psi
, when seen as a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is
continuous. This means that, fixed an
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
a
\delta>0
such that
|\Upsilon(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})|<\varepsilon

whenever
\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|x_{i}|<\delta
. Let

M=\max_{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in K}|\lambda_{i}|.

Then
|\Upsilon(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})|<\varepsilon

as long as
|r_{m}|<\delta/M
. This proves the claim.

In the expression

\frac{\Phi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}=\frac{\Psi(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})}{r_{m}}\Upsilon(r_{m}\lambda_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\lambda_{n})

the left term of the product converges to
\Psi_{0}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
while the right term converges to
0
, both uniformly on

K
. Again by the initial observation, it follows that the product
converges uniformly to
0
.
∎

The desired result now follows directly from the previous lemmas.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA and let
X
be a vector lattice. If
\Phi

vanishes on a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
, then it also vanishes on
X_{0}
.

2.1.4.
2.2
for Archimedean
f\!
-algebras

The only ingredient left to cook
2.2
are
orthomorphisms. A positive linear map
\pi\colon X\to X
on a vector lattice
X
is called a
positive orthomorphism
if
a\wedge b=0
in
X

implies
\pi(a)\wedge b=0
. An
orthomorphism
is the difference
of two positive orthomorphisms. The set of orthomorphisms on
X
is
denoted by
\operatorname{Orth}(X)
. This set, with the usual operator order and
composition, is an
f\!
-algebra with identity. When
X
is
Archimedean, so is
\operatorname{Orth}(X)
(see
[
39
, Section 4]
).

In an
f\!
-algebra
A
every multiplication map is an orthomorphism. The map

L\colon A\to\operatorname{Orth}(A)
defined by
L(a)b=ab
for
a,b\in A
is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism. Its kernel is the
annihilator of

A
:

N(A)=\{\,x\in A:xy=0\text{ for all }y\in A\,\}.

The annihilator is both an algebraic ideal and a band. Moreover, it
coincides with the set of nilpotent elements, which in turn is just

N(A)=\{\,x\in A:x^{2}=0\,\}.

The proof of these facts can be found in
[
39
, Section 3]
.

Proof of
2.2
.

The product in an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
A
extends to its order
completion, making it an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra (see
[
39
, Section
7]
). Since
A
lattice-algebra embeds in its order
completion, it suffices
to prove the result for order complete Archimedean
f\!
-algebras.

From now on, assume
A
is order complete. In this case, the
annihilator
N(A)
is a
projection band. Let
P\colon A\to A
be the band projection onto

N(A)
, and let
P^{d}
be the band projection onto its disjoint
complement,
N(A)^{d}
. The

f\!
-algebra condition implies that the product is band
preserving, so
P^{d}xP^{d}y\in N(A)^{d}
for every
x,y\in A
.
From the identity

xy=(Px+P^{d}x)(Py+P^{d}y)=P^{d}xP^{d}y

follows both that
P(xy)=0=PxPy
and

P^{d}(xy)=P^{d}xP^{d}y
. This shows that
P
and
P^{d}
are
lattice-algebra homomorphisms from
A
onto the
f\!
-algebras
N(A)
and

N(A)^{d}
, respectively.

Thus, given
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
,

\displaystyle\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=P(\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))+P^{d}(\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Phi(Pa_{1},\ldots,Pa_{n})+\Phi(P^{d}a_{1},\ldots,P^{d}a_{n}),

and we just need to show that
\Phi(Pa_{1},\ldots,Pa_{n})=0
in

N(A)
and
\Phi(P^{d}a_{1},\ldots,P^{d}a_{n})=0
in
N(A)^{d}
.
Since the product in
N(A)
is identically zero, the first follows
immediately from
2.7
.

It is direct to check that
N(N(A)^{d})=\{0\}
, so that the map

L\colon N(A)^{d}\to\operatorname{Orth}(N(A)^{d})
is an injective
lattice-algebra homomorphism. But
\operatorname{Orth}(N(A)^{d})
is an
Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity; from
2.3
it follows that

L(\Phi(P^{d}a_{1},\ldots,P^{d}a_{n}))=\Phi(LP^{d}a_{1},\ldots,LP^{d}a_{n})=0.

Since
L
is injective,
\Phi(P^{d}a_{1},\ldots,P^{d}a_{n})=0
, and
this finishes the proof.
∎

2.1.5.
The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra generated by a set

Now that
2.2
has been established, we can construct
the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra generated by a set
S
. A word on
notation: when we say that a concrete space with a concrete map is

the free object
we mean that they satisfy the required
universal property. This is reasonable because any other pair
satisfying the universal property will be isomorphic to the given one.
Once we have an explicit construction, we will always identify the
free object with that particular realization, and consequently denote
both of them the same way.

The function space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
, equipped with pointwise
linear operations, order and product, becomes an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra. For every
s\in S
, define
\delta_{s}\colon\mathbb{R}^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\delta_{s}(x)=x(s)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
. Denote
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)=\operatorname{VLA}\{\,\delta_{s}:s\in S\,\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
, and define the map
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
by
\delta(s)=\delta_{s}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra over a set
S
is
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
together
with the map
\delta
.

Proof.

This fact follows from
2.2
using a standard
argument due to Birkhoff
[
19
]
. For
convenience of the reader, we sketch it here.

Let
A
be an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra, and let
T\colon S\to A
be a
map. For every
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
there exists an LLA expression

\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
such that
f=\Phi(\delta_{s_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{s_{n}})
for some
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S
. Define

\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to A
by
\hat{T}(f)=\Phi(Ts_{1},\ldots,Ts_{n})
. We need to check that
T
is indeed well-defined. Suppose
that also
f=\Psi(\delta_{t_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{t_{m}})
for some
other LLA expression
\Psi
and some
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in S
.
Then for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
:

\Phi(x(s_{1}),\ldots,x(s_{n}))=f(x)=\Psi(x(t_{1}),\ldots,x(t_{m})).

Since
(x(s_{1}),\ldots,x(s_{n}),x(t_{1}),\ldots,x(t_{m}))
takes all
possible values in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
as
x
ranges through
\mathbb{R}^{S}
, it
follows that the LLA expression
\Phi-\Psi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
.
By
2.2
, it must also vanish on
A
. In
particular,
\Phi(Ts_{1},\ldots,Ts_{n})=\Psi(Tt_{1},\ldots,Tt_{m})
.
Hence
\hat{T}
is well-defined, and
it is then clear from the definition
that it is the unique lattice-algebra homomorphism satisfying

\hat{T}\delta=T
.
∎

2.2.
Lattice properties

In this section, we consider the properties of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
as a vector
lattice and compare them with
those of the free vector lattice
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
.

Before proceeding to the study of such properties, a preliminary
result on the simplification of LLA expressions in
f\!
-algebras is needed.

Recall that, whenever
A
is a real algebra, and
B\subseteq A
is a
subset,
\mathbb{R}[B]
denotes the subalgebra of
A
generated by
B
. In other words,
\mathbb{R}[B]

contains the polynomials in the elements of
B
with no constant term.
Whenever
X
is a vector lattice, and
Y\subseteq X
is a subset,

\Lat Y
denotes the sublattice generated by
Y
. It is standard
that every element
x\in\Lat Y
can be written as

x=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}z_{j},

where
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\barespn Y
.

A
d
-algebra

A
is a
vector lattice algebra in which
a(b\vee c)=(ab)\vee(ac)
and
(b\vee c)a=(ba)\vee(ca)
hold for all
b,c\in A
and
a\in A_{+}
. Every
f\!
-algebra is
in particular a
d
-algebra.

Proposition 2.9
.

If
A
is a
d
-algebra and
S\subseteq A
is such that
A=\operatorname{VLA}(S)
, then

A=\Lat(\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]).

Proof.

The proof consists in showing that
B=\Lat(\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S])
is a sublattice-algebra; since it contains
S
, and

A=\operatorname{VLA}(S)
, it will then follow that
A=B
.
Obviously,
B
is a sublattice, so it is only left to show that

B
is closed under taking products. Observe that, given
x\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
, separating its positive and negative coefficients one can
write
x=x_{p}-x_{n}
, where

x_{p},x_{n}\geq 0
and
x_{p},x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
. Now let
y\in B
, say

y=\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}\quad\text{with
}u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S].

Using the
d
-algebra condition:

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=(x_{p}-x_{n})\bigg(\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle=x_{p}\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}-x_{n}\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}-x_{p}\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}+x_{n}\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}x_{p}u_{i}-\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}x_{n}u_{i}-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}x_{p}v_{j}+\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}x_{n}v_{j}.

Since
x_{p}u_{i},x_{p}v_{j},x_{n}u_{i},x_{n}u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
, it follows that
xy\in B
.

Now suppose
x=\bigvee_{k=1}^{p}w_{k}
for some
w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
. The element
x
can be rewritten as

x=w_{1,p}\vee\bigvee_{k=2}^{p}(w_{k}+w_{1,n})-w_{1,n}=w-w_{1,n},

where
w=w_{1,p}\vee\bigvee_{k=2}^{p}(w_{k}+w_{1,n})\in B
is positive
because
w_{1,p}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=w\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}-w\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}-w_{1,n}y

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}wu_{i}-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}wv_{j}-w_{1,n}y,

where
wu_{i},wv_{j},w_{1,n}y\in B
because we have already
checked that the product of an element of
\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
by an
element of
B
is in
B
. Finally, suppose that
x
is an
arbitrary
element of
B
, say
x=\bigvee_{k=1}^{p}w_{k}-\bigvee_{l=1}^{p}z_{l}
for some
w_{k},z_{l}\in\mathbb{R}[s_{+},s_{-}:s\in S]
. Then

xy=\bigg(\bigvee_{k=1}^{p}w_{k}\bigg)y-\bigg(\bigvee_{k=1}^{p}z_{k}\bigg)y\in B

because each of the terms is an element of
B
by the previous case.
So
B
is closed under multiplication, and therefore is a
sublattice-algebra. Since it contains
S
, it must be
B=A
.
∎

Recall that in
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
,
\delta_{s}
is a weak unit for every
s\in S
, and

\operatorname{FVL}(S)
has a strong unit if and only if
S
is finite (in which
case
\sup_{s\in S}|\delta_{s}|
is a strong unit). In
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)

the situation is as follows.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
S
be a non-empty set.

(i)

The element
\delta_{s}
is a weak unit of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
for every
s\in S
.

(ii)

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
does not have a strong unit.

Proof.

(i)

Suppose
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
were such that
|f|\wedge|\delta_{s}|=0
. Write
f=\Phi(\delta_{s},\delta_{s_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{s_{n}})
, for a certain LLA
expression
\Phi
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S
. By
evaluating the expression
|f|\wedge|\delta_{s}|=0
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
it follows that
0=f(x)=\Phi(x(s),x(s_{1}),\ldots,x(s_{n}))
whenever
x(s)\neq 0
. This
implies that the continuous function

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathbb{R}^{n+1}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})&\longmapsto&\Phi(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\\
\end{array}

vanishes as long as
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
. Hence it must be
identically zero, and therefore
f=0
by

2.2
.

(ii)

Suppose
e\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is a strong unit. The
space of real sequences
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
equipped with
pointwise linear operations, order and product is an
Archimedean
f\!
-algebra. Fix

s_{0}\in S
and let
T\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
be defined
by
Ts_{0}=(n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
Ts=0
if
s\neq s_{0}
. Denote

a=Ts_{0}
. By construction,
\hat{T}(e)\in\operatorname{VLA}\{a\}=\Lat\{\mathbb{R}[a]\}
. More precisely,

\hat{T}(e)\in\Lat\{a,a^{2},\ldots,a^{k}\}
, for a certain

k\in\mathbb{N}
. Being finitely generated,
a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{k}
is a strong unit of
\Lat\{a,a^{2},\ldots,a^{k}\}
,
so
\hat{T}(e)\leq\lambda(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{k})
for a certain
\lambda
. And since
a\leq a^{2}\leq\cdots\leq a^{k}
, one arrives at
\hat{T}(e)\leq k\lambda a^{k}
.
By assumption,
e
is a strong unit, so there exists
\mu>0

such that
|\delta_{s_{0}}|^{k+1}\leq\mu e
. Evaluating
\hat{T}

at both sides of the inequality yields
a^{k+1}\leq k\lambda\mu a^{k}
or, equivalently,
a\leq k\lambda\mu
. This contradicts the fact that
a
is unbounded.∎

If the set
S
has more than one element, then
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
does not have
non-trivial projection bands. In particular, it
does not have atoms and it is not
\sigma
-order complete. This is based on a
topological argument (see
[
21
]
) which also
works for
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
. We sketch it below, after introducing the
necessary notation.

Definition 2.11
.

The
support
of
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
is defined to be the set

\supp(f)=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}:f(x)\neq 0\,\}.

For a subset
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
its
support
is
defined as

\supp(F)=\bigcup_{f\in F}\supp(f).

Equip
\mathbb{R}^{S}
with the product topology. Then every element of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is a continuous function and therefore its support is an open set.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
S
be a set with more than one element. Then
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
does
not have non-trivial projection bands. In particular, it does not
have atoms and it is not
\sigma
-order complete.

Proof.

Suppose
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)=B\oplus B^{d}
, with
B
a non-trivial projection
band. Denote by
P
the projection onto
B
, and by
P^{d}
its
complementary projection. The first step is to show that

\supp(B)\cup\supp(B^{d})=\mathbb{R}^{S}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,
x\neq 0
. Choose
s\in S
such that
x(s)\neq 0
. Since

0\neq|\delta_{s}|(x)=|P\delta_{s}|(x)+|P^{d}\delta_{s}|(x)
,
either
|P\delta_{s}|(x)\neq 0
or
|P^{d}\delta_{s}|(x)\neq 0
. In
any case,
x\in\supp(B)\cup\supp(B^{d})
. This proves

\supp(B)\cup\supp(B^{d})=\mathbb{R}^{S}\setminus\{0\}
.

The second step is to show that
\supp(B)\cap\supp(B^{d})=\emptyset
. If
x\in\supp(B)\cap\supp(B^{d})
, there exist positive
f\in B
and
g\in B^{d}

such that
f(x)>0
and
g(x)>0
. Then
(f\wedge g)(x)=f(x)\wedge g(x)>0
, which contradicts the fact that
f\wedge g\in B\cap B^{d}=\{0\}
.

We have thus shown that
\supp(B)
and
\supp(B^{d})
are a
disconnection of
\mathbb{R}^{S}\setminus\{0\}
. This is absurd, since

|S|>1
.
∎

When the set
S
has cardinality one,
\operatorname{FVL}(1)
can be identified with

\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and therefore it is both order complete and atomic. For
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)
the
situation is not so straightforward.
Recall that
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)=\operatorname{VLA}\{I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}
, where
I(x)=x
for
all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.13
.

(i)

The unique decomposition into non-trivial projection
bands of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)
is
\operatorname{VLA}\{I_{+}\}\oplus\operatorname{VLA}\{I_{-}\}
.

(ii)

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)
has no atoms.

(iii)

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)
is not
\sigma
-order complete.

Proof.

(i)

Let
B_{+}=\operatorname{VLA}\{I_{+}\}
and
B_{-}=\operatorname{VLA}\{I_{-}\}
. It suffices
to check that
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
are subspaces with

B_{+}\subseteq B_{-}^{d}
satisfying
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(I)=B_{+}\oplus B_{-}
as vector spaces (see
[
55
, Lemma
1.2.8]
). Certainly,
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
are
subspaces. Since
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
have disjoint supports,

B_{+}\cap B_{-}=\{0\}
and
B_{+}\subseteq B_{-}^{d}
.
According to
2.9
, every
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)
can be written as

f=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}[p_{i}^{+}(I_{+})+p_{i}^{-}(I_{-})]-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}[q_{j}^{+}(I_{+})+q_{j}^{-}(I_{-})],

where
p_{i}^{+},p_{i}^{-},q_{j}^{+},q_{j}^{-}
are polynomials.
Again, since the supports of
I_{+}
and
I_{-}
are disjoint,

f
can be rewritten as

f=\bigg[\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{+}(I_{+})-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}q_{j}^{+}(I_{+})\bigg]+\bigg[\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{-}(I_{-})-\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}q_{j}^{-}(I_{-})\bigg].

This proves
f\in B_{+}+B_{-}
.

If
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(1)=B\oplus B^{d}
, where
B
is a non-trivial
band projection, then, as in
2.12
,

\supp(B)
and
\supp(B^{d})
form a disconnection of

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. So we may assume

\supp(B)=(0,\infty)
. Then
I_{+}\in B
and, since band
projections are closed under multiplication,

B_{+}\subseteq B
. It follows easily that
B=B_{+}
, and
similarly
B^{d}=B_{-}
.

(ii)

Let
a>0
, say
a=\Phi(I)
for some LLA expression

\Phi
. It is not difficult to
show, by induction on the complexity of
\Phi
, that there
exists an
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(x)}{x^{m}}=\infty.

On the other hand

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{x^{m}\wedge\Phi(x)}{x^{m}}=1,

so it cannot be the case that
(I_{+})^{m}\wedge a
is a
scalar multiple of

. Hence,
a
is not an atom.

(iii)

Even though the sequence
f_{n}=(I_{+})^{n}\wedge I_{+}
is
bounded below by
0
, it does not have an infimum. Indeed,
suppose
f
is a positive lower bound. Then
f(t)=0
for
t\in[0,1)
and, since
f
is continuous, there must exist

\delta>0
such that
0\leq f(t)\leq 1/2
for
t\in[1,1+\delta]
. Choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
big enough so
as to have

1/2<(1+\delta)^{N}-(1+\delta).

By continuity, there exists
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
such that

1/2<(1+\delta^{\prime})^{N}-(1+\delta^{\prime})<1.

It then follows that
[((I_{+})^{N}-I_{+})_{+}\wedge I_{+}]\vee f
is
a lower bound for
(f_{n})
that is strictly bigger than

. Hence
(f_{n})
does not have an infimum.∎

Remark 2.14
.

The free vector lattice
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
has the
countable chain
condition
: a disjoint collection in
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
is at most
countable. The reason is purely topological: the supports of a disjoint collection of functions form a
disjoint collection of open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and such a collection
is at most countable (see
[
61
]
). The very same argument also
shows that
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
has the countable chain condition.

Remark 2.15
.

Let
T
be a subset of
S
. The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(T)
may be viewed as the
sublattice-algebra of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
generated by
\delta_{t}
for
t\in T
. The unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
P\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
determined by
P\delta_{s}=\delta_{s}
if
s\in T
and
P\delta_{s}=0
if
s\not\in T
, defines a projection onto
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(T)
.

Similarly, the free vector lattice
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
may be viewed as the sublattice of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)

generated by
\delta_{s}
for
s\in S
. Equip
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
with
the zero product, which as usual we denote by
{\operatorname{FVL}(S)}_{0}
. The map
\delta\colon S\to{\operatorname{FVL}(S)}_{0}

extends to a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
P\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to{\operatorname{FVL}(S)}_{0}
. Forgetting
about the product on
{\operatorname{FVL}(S)}_{0}
, the map

P\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to\operatorname{FVL}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)

is a lattice homomorphic projection. The explicit expression of
this projection is

(Pf)(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(tx)}{t},\quad\text{where }f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S).

Indeed, suppose
f=\Phi(\delta_{s_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{s_{n}})
,
for a some LLA expression
\Phi
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S
. By

2.6
,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(tx)}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(tx(x_{1}),\ldots,tx(s_{n}))}{t}=\Phi_{0}(\delta_{s_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{s_{n}})(x).

So the limit exists and, since
\Phi_{0}
is a lattice-linear

Pf\in\operatorname{FVL}(S)
. From the formula defining

it is
immediate to check that the map

is a lattice
homomorphic projection extending
\delta
. By uniqueness, it must

For lattice-linear expressions, there is continuity with respect to
the expressions in the following sense: if
(\Phi_{k}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}])_{k}
is a sequence of lattice-linear expressions that converges
uniformly to
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
when seen as functions in

C([-1,1]^{n})
, then
\Phi_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
converges in norm to

\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in a Banach
lattice

. This is no longer true for LLA expressions.

Example 2.16
.

Consider the sequence of LLA expressions

\Phi_{k}[t]=t\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{t^{2j}}{(2j)!}\bigg)^{2}-t\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{t^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}\bigg)^{2}.

For every

:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Phi_{k}(x)=x\cosh^{2}x-x\sinh^{2}x=x

and this limit is uniform on
[-1,1]
. In other words, if
\Phi[t]=t
,
and we see
\Phi_{k}
and
\Phi
as elements in

,
\Phi_{k}\to\Phi
in the uniform norm. But, in
\mathbb{R}_{0}
(or any other

whereas

for every

. Hence
\Phi_{k}(x)

.

2.3.
Algebraic properties

In this section the algebraic properties of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
are considered.
Recall that an
f\!
-algebra

is
semiprime
if
N(A)=\{0\}
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
S
be a set.

(i)

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is semiprime.

(ii)

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
does not have an identity element.

Proof.

The result is trivial when
S
is empty. For the rest of the
proof, assume that
S
is non-empty.

(i)

Suppose
f\in N(\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S))
, and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be
different from zero. There exists
s\in S
such that

x(s)\neq 0
. Then
0=(f\delta_{s})(x)=f(x)x(s)
, so

f(x)=0
. This shows that
f=0
.

(ii)

Suppose
1\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
were an identity. Fix
s_{0}\in S

and define
T\colon S\to C[-1,1]
by
Ts=0
for
s\neq s_{0}
and
(Ts_{0})(t)=t_{+}
for
t\in[-1,1]
. Then

\hat{T}(1)=\hat{T}(1)^{2}
. Since

[-1,1]
is connected,
\hat{T}(1)
is either
0
or

\mathbb{1}
, where
\mathbb{1}
is the constant one
function. But
\hat{T}(1)\hat{T}(\delta_{s_{0}})=\hat{T}(\delta_{s_{0}})=Ts_{0}\neq 0
, so

\hat{T}(1)=\mathbb{1}
. This means that
\mathbb{1}\in\hat{T}(\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S))=\operatorname{VLA}\{Ts_{0}\}
. This is impossible because every element of
\operatorname{VLA}\{Ts_{0}\}
has support contained
in
[0,1]
.∎

It is natural to wonder whether a chain condition for the product
exists. Namely, if
F\subseteq\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is an uncountable family, is
it true that there exist
f,g\in F
such that
fg\neq 0
? To answer
this question, it is useful to reformulate semiprimeness in terms of
what Scheffold
[
63
]
calls
f^{*}\!
-algebras: a vector lattice
algebra
A
is called an
f^{*}\!
-algebra when

ab=0\text{ if and only if }|a|\wedge|b|=0\text{ for all }a,b\in A.

The following is part of
[
16
, Theoreme
9.3.1]
.

Lemma 2.18
.

An
f\!
-algebra
A
is an
f^{*}\!
-algebra if and only if it is semiprime.

Remark 2.19
.

In particular,
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is an
f^{*}\!
-algebra. Therefore,
families
F\subseteq\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
such that
ab=0
for all distinct
a,b\in F
are the same as families of pairwise disjoint elements,
and therefore must be countable.

3.
A structure theorem for Banach
f
-algebras

Before getting to free Banach
f\!
-algebras, the main topic of this paper,
we need to introduce a new tool in the theory of Banach
f\!
-algebras.
Recall that every vector lattice
X
, when equipped with the
identically
0
product, becomes an
f\!
-algebra. We are denoting this
f\!
-algebra by
X_{0}
. In particular, when
X
is a Banach lattice,
X_{0}
is a
Banach
f\!
-algebra. Another basic example of Banach
f\!
-algebra is the space

C(K)
of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space
K
, with
the linear operations, order and product defined pointwise. Given
Banach
f\!
-algebras
A
and
B
their direct sum
A\oplus_{\infty}B
,
equipped with the maximum norm and coordinatewise operations, is also a
Banach
f\!
-algebra.

The next theorem shows that every Banach
f\!
-algebra can be seen as a (not
necessarily closed) sublattice-algebra of

X_{0}\oplus_{\infty}C(K)
, for appropriate
X
and
K
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every Banach
f\!
-algebra
A
there exist a Banach lattice
X
,
a compact Hausdorff space
K
and a contractive injective lattice-algebra
homomorphism
R\colon A\to X_{0}\oplus_{\infty}C(K)
.

The approach to the proof will be very similar to that of

2.2
. First we consider the case that
A
has trivial
annihilator. The following was already proved in
[
54
, Proposition
1.7]
. Since the proof uses some ideas that are worth
keeping in mind, we sketch it below.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every Banach
f\!
-algebra
A
with trivial
annihilator there exists a contractive injective lattice-algebra
homomorphism
A\to C(K)
, for a certain compact Hausdorff space

K
.

Proof.

Since
A
is a Banach lattice,
\operatorname{Orth}(A)
coincides with the center

\mathcal{Z}(A)
of
A
(see
[
1
, Theorem
3.29]
). The map
L\colon A\to\mathcal{Z}(A)

sending
a\in A
to
L_{a}
, the operator of left multiplication by

a
, is an injective lattice-algebra homomorphism (see the discussion in

Section

2.1.4
). The center

\mathcal{Z}(A)
, being a Banach
f\!
-algebra with strong unit
I
, is
lattice-algebra isometric to
C(K)
for some compact Hausdorff
space
K
(see
[
54
, Proposition 1.4]
).
∎

The map in previous lemma need not be an embedding, as the following
example shows.

Example 3.3
.

Let
(x_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a sequence such that
x_{n}\geq 1
and

x_{n}\to\infty
. Consider the space of sequences

A=\{\,(a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:a_{n}x_{n}\to 0\,\}.

This is a sublattice of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
that is closed under the
coordinatewise product. Equipped with the norm

\|(a_{n})\|=\sup_{n}|a_{n}|x_{n}

it becomes a Banach lattice. Since

\|ab\|=\sup_{n}|a_{n}||b_{n}|x_{n}\leq\sup_{n}|a_{n}||b_{n}|x_{n}^{2}\leq\|a\|\|b\|,

A
is a Banach
f\!
-algebra. The sequence
(e_{n}/x_{n})
is such that

\|e_{n}/x_{n}\|=1
. If
a\in A
is in the unit ball (that is,

|a_{n}|\leq 1/x_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
), then

\|e_{n}/x_{n}a\|=\frac{1}{x_{n}}\|a_{n}e_{n}\|=|a_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{x_{n}}

which tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore
\|L_{e_{n}/x_{n}}\|

tends to zero while
\|e_{n}/x_{n}\|=1
.

Proof of
3.1
.

By embedding
A
in its bidual (which is again a Banach

f\!
-algebra when equipped with the Arens product, see
[
62
]
), we may assume
A
is order complete.
Let
P\colon A\to A
denote the band projection onto
its annihilator
N=N(A)
,
and let
P^{d}
denote the band projection onto its disjoint
complement. Both
P
and
P^{d}
are
multiplicative (see the proof of
2.2
). In particular, both
N
and
N^{d}

are Banach
f\!
-algebras on their own, with the structure inherited from

A
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}T\colon&A&\longrightarrow&N\oplus_{\infty}N^{d}\\
&f&\longmapsto&(Pf,P^{d}f)\\
\end{array}

defines an injective lattice-algebra homomorphism.
Since both
P
and
P^{d}
are contractive, so is
T
.
Note that
N
is a Banach lattice with the zero product.
On the other hand,
N^{d}
is a Banach
f\!
-algebra with trivial
annihilator. By
3.2
there exists an injective and contractive lattice-algebra
homomorphism
S\colon N^{d}\to C(K)
. The composition

R=(I_{N}\oplus S)T\colon A\to N\oplus_{\infty}C(K)

defines the desired injective and contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

(i)

In
3.1
,
R
need not be an
embedding, as
Example

3.3
shows.

(ii)

Passing to the bidual in the proof of
3.1
is not
superfluous, for the annihilator need not be a projection
band when the Banach
f\!
-algebra is not order complete. For instance, consider in the Banach lattice

C[0,1]
the element

p(t)=(2t-1)\chi_{[1/2,1]},\quad t\in[0,1],

and define, for
f,g\in C[0,1]
, the product
f\star g=fgp
, where juxtaposition denotes the usual pointwise
product. It is clear that
C[0,1]
with this product
becomes a Banach
f\!
-algebra, with annihilator

N=\{\,f\in C[0,1]:\supp(f)\subseteq[0,1/2]\,\}.

This is certainly a band but it is
not a projection band.

(iii)

3.1
is not true for general
f\!
-algebras, even
if they are Archimedean and
uniformly complete. For instance,
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the
pointwise order and product is an Archimedean and
uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra with trivial annihilator, yet
there is no positive map from
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
to a
space of continuous functions. Indeed, if
R\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to C(K)
were positive, where
K
is some compact
Hausdorff space, then
the sequence
x=(n/\|Re_{n}\|_{\infty})_{n}
would satisfy

Rx\geq\frac{n}{\|Re_{n}\|_{\infty}}Re_{n}

so that
\|Rx\|_{\infty}\geq n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This
is a contradiction.

(iv)

However,
3.1
does hold for normed

f\!
-algebras: just embed them in their norm completion, which
is a Banach
f\!
-algebra (see
[
44
, Proposition
2.1]
).

(v)

Given a Banach
f\!
-algebra
A
and a map
R\colon A\to X_{0}\oplus_{\infty}C(K)
as in
3.1
, we shall denote
Ra=(a_{N},a_{C})
for

a\in A
. Note that the maps
a\mapsto a_{N}
and
a\mapsto a_{C}
are
also contractive lattice-algebra homomorphisms.

The following theorem is a refinement of
2.2
for
normed
f\!
-algebras.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Phi
be an LLA expression. If
\Phi

vanishes on
[-1,1]
, then it also vanishes on the unit ball of
every normed
f\!
-algebra.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
vanishes on a neighbourhood of
0
, it must vanish
on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
(
2.5
). Since
\Phi
vanishes on

\mathbb{R}_{0}
, it must also vanish on every
vector lattice with the zero product (
2.4
).
Let
A
be a Banach
f\!
-algebra, and let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
be such
that
\|a_{i}\|\leq 1
.
Let
R\colon A\to X_{0}\oplus_{\infty}C(K)
be a map as in
3.1
.
Then

\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})_{N}=\Phi((a_{1})_{N},\ldots,(a_{n})_{N})=0.

Since
R
is contractive,
\|(a_{i})_{C}\|_{\infty}\leq\|Ra_{i}\|\leq\|a_{i}\|\leq 1
, so
|(a_{i})_{C}(t)|\leq 1
for every
t\in K
. By assumption:

\Phi((a_{1})_{C}(t),\ldots,(a_{n})_{C}(t))=0\quad\text{for every }t\in K.

This yields
0=\Phi((a_{1})_{C},\ldots(a_{n})_{C})=\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})_{C}
.
Thus
R(\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))=(0,0)
. Injectivity of

implies

\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Previous result is meaningless for LL expressions: if an
LL expression vanishes on
[-1,1]
, then by positive homogenity it
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
. This is no longer
true for LLA expressions since, in general, they are not
positively homogeneous. For instance,

\Phi[t]=(t_{+}^{2}-t_{+})_{+}

is an LLA expression that vanishes on
[-1,1]
but not on
\mathbb{R}
. By
previous theorem, the identity
(x_{+}^{2}-x_{+})_{+}=0
holds whenever
x
is
contained in the unit ball of a normed
f\!
-algebra.

4.
The free Banach
f
-algebra
generated by a Banach space

Given a Banach space
E
, the free Banach lattice generated by
E
is
a Banach lattice
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
together with a linear isometric embedding

\phi_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FBL}[E]
such that for every bounded linear
operator
T\colon E\to X
into a Banach lattice
X
, there exists a
unique lattice homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FBL}[E]\to X
such that

\hat{T}\circ\phi_{E}=T
and
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
. This object was first
introduced in the case
E=\ell_{1}(S)
by B. de Pagter and A. Wickstead

[
60
]
and for general Banach spaces
E
by A. Avilés, J. Rodríguez and P. Tradacete
[
6
]
.
Free Banach lattices have been used to settle open questions and
have proven to be key to a proper understanding of Banach lattices
(see
[
58
]
).

The notion of a free Banach lattice algebra has also been
considered before
[
69
,
46
]
.
Through abstract arguments from universal algebras, it is possible to
show that such an object exists. Yet no explicit construction is
known. In fact, it is not clear at all whether such an explicit
description is even possible.

The goal of this section is to study free objects in the much better
behaved category of Banach
f\!
-algebras and to get as close as we can to an
explicit description of these spaces. We shall see that, even though a
large theory of free Banach lattices is known, free Banach
f\!
-algebras are
still challenging to understand. New ideas are required, since the introduction of a product invalidates arguments that rely on positive homogeneity. In fact, a first subtlety due to the lack of
positive homogeneity is already present in the definition of free
Banach
f\!
-algebra.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. The
free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated
by
E
is a Banach
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
together with a linear isometric
embedding
\eta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
such that, for every Banach

f\!
-algebra
A
and every contractive operator
T\colon E\to A
, there exists a
unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to A

satisfying
\hat{T}\circ\eta_{E}=T
and
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
.

Remark 4.2
.

The universal property defining
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
only extends
contractive operators. The reason for this is that non-contractive
maps cannot, in general, be extended to bounded algebra
homomorphisms. For example, if the map

T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
Tx=2x
, extended to a lattice-algebra
homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\mathbb{R}]\to\mathbb{R}
, then

\hat{T}(\eta_{\mathbb{R}}(1)^{n})=T(1)^{n}=2^{n}

while
\|\eta_{\mathbb{R}}(1)^{n}\|\leq\|\eta_{\mathbb{R}}(1)\|^{n}\leq 1
. This
would imply that
\hat{T}
is unbounded. Another way to see this
obstruction is that, while scaling a lattice homomorphism by a
positive number gives another lattice homomorphism, scaling an
algebra homomorphism does not give an algebra homomorphism (unless
the scalar is
1
).

Remark 4.3
.

Let
S
be a set. We shall denote
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}(S)]
by
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}(S)

and call it the
free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by
S
. The
reason for this is that for every Banach
f\!
-algebra
A
and every map

T\colon S\to A
satisfying
\|T\|=\sup_{s\in S}\|Ts\|\leq 1

there exists a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}(S)\to A
with
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
satisfying
\hat{T}\eta_{e_{s}}=Ts
.

The existence of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by a Banach space follows from that of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)

using abstract general arguments that are already known. The details
of this construction are carried out in

Section

4.1
. But, as we have anticipated, these arguments do not provide an explicit description of the space. In

Section

4.2
we give a more explicit and useful description of free
Banach
f\!
-algebras using
3.5
. In

Section

4.3
a
characterization, up to isomorphism, of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by a finite-dimensional Banach space is derived. Finally,

Section

4.4
is devoted to the study of the free norm; it will
be particularly useful when we study the properties of free Banach

f\!
-algebras.

Although the following objects are not central, it is
convenient to have them at hand, since
they appear as intermediate steps in the construction.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
E
be a vector space. The
free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra generated by
E
is an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
together with a
linear map
\delta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
such that, for every
Archimedean

f\!
-algebra
A
and every linear map
T\colon E\to A
, there exists a
unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]\to A

satisfying
\hat{T}\circ\delta_{E}=T
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
E
be a normed space. The
free normed
f\!
-algebra generated
by
E
is a normed
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
together with a linear isometric
embedding
\eta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
such that, for every
normed

f\!
-algebra
A
and every contractive operator
T\colon E\to A
, there exists a
unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to A

satisfying
\hat{T}\circ\eta_{E}=T
and
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
.

The reason to have that much notation is the following: through the
universal properties, it is not difficult to check that
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
is
nothing more than
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
, where
S
is a Hamel basis of
E
. We will
equip
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
with an appropriate seminorm, and quotient out by its kernel
to obtain a norm. This will yield
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. It is easy to check,
again using universal properties, that
the completion of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
is precisely
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
. With these
notations we are aware, at each step of the process, which universal
property each object has. Furthermore, we will also
exhibit an explicit description of these free objects.

4.1.
Abstract construction

We shall follow the previous outline to show that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
exists. In
doing so, we follow closely the expositions of V. Troitsky

[
65
]
and M. de Jeu
[
46
]
.
The construction of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
that we use is essentially the
same, with the appropriate adaptations, as that of
[
65
]
for
the free vector lattice over a vector space.

Let
E
be a vector space, and let
E^{\#}
be its algebraic dual. The
space of functions
\mathbb{R}^{E^{\#}}
, equipped with pointwise linear
operations, order and product, is an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra. For
every
x\in E
, define
\delta_{x}\colon E^{\#}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\delta_{x}(\omega)=\omega(x)
for all
\omega\in E^{\#}
. Denote
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]=\operatorname{VLA}\{\,\delta_{x}:x\in E\,\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{E^{\#}}
, and
define the map
\delta\colon E\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
by
\delta(x)=\delta_{x}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra over a vector space
E
is
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]

together with the map
\delta
.

Proof.

Being a sublattice-algebra of the Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
\mathbb{R}^{E^{\#}}
,
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
is again an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra.
Moreover, the map
\delta
is linear. Indeed, if
x,y\in E
,
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\omega\in E^{\#}
, then

\delta_{\lambda x+\mu y}(\omega)=\omega(\lambda x+\mu y)=\lambda\omega(x)+\mu\omega(y)=(\lambda\delta_{x}+\mu\delta_{y})(\omega).

Let
A
be an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra and let
T\colon E\to A
be a
linear map. For
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
, say
f=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})
for some LLA expresion
\Phi
and

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
, define
\hat{T}(f)=\Phi(Tx_{1},\ldots,Tx_{n})
. As in the proof of
2.8
, the only non-trivial
part is to check that
\hat{T}
is well-defined. For this it
suffices to check that, if

\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})=0
in
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
, then

\Phi(Tx_{1},\ldots,Tx_{n})=0
in
A
.

Let
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
be a basis of the span of
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Write, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

x_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{ij}z_{j}\quad\text{for certain }\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}.

Define the LLA expression

\Psi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}]=\Phi\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{1j}t_{j},\ldots,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{nj}t_{j}\bigg].

By the linearity of
\delta
,

0=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})=\Psi(\delta_{z_{1}},\ldots\delta_{z_{m}}),

which implies

\Psi(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{m}))=0

for every
\omega\in E^{\#}
. Since
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
are
linearly independent,
(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{m}))
ranges
through
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
\omega
ranges through
E^{\#}
. By
2.2
,

\Psi
vanishes on
A
. Using the linearity of
T
it follows
that

\Phi(Tx_{1},\ldots,Tx_{n})=\Phi\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{1j}Tz_{j},\ldots,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{nj}Tz_{j}\bigg)=\Psi(Tz_{1},\ldots,Tz_{m})=0.\qed

Remark 4.7
.

Note that when
E
is a normed space, the previous construction can
be carried out in
\mathbb{R}^{E^{*}}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{E^{\#}}
. But in
contrast with the free vector lattice over a vector
space, now we cannot go one step further and
restrict the elements of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
to
B_{E^{*}}
so as to obtain
a representation of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
in
C(B_{E^{*}},w^{*})
. The reason
for this is that LL expressions are positively homogeneous, while
LLA expressions need not be (for instance, in
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[\mathbb{R}]
the
non-zero element
((\delta_{1})_{+}^{2}-(\delta_{1})_{+})_{+}
vanishes on

B_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}=[-1,1]
, see Remark
3.6
). Despite
this, it is remarkable that in
Sections

4.2
and
5
we will still be able to represent

\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
and, in some cases,
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
inside
C(B_{E^{*}})
.

Next we proceed to the abstract construction of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
for a normed
vector space
E
. In
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
(regarded as the sublattice-algebra of

\mathbb{R}^{E^{*}}
generated by
\{\,\delta_{x}:x\in E\,\}
), let
\mathcal{N}
be the set
of lattice seminorms
\nu
that are submultiplicative and satisfy
\nu(\delta_{x})\leq\|x\|
for all
x\in E
. This set is not empty,
for if
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
,
then
\nu_{x^{*}}(f)=|f(x^{*})|
defines an element of

\mathcal{N}
.

Let
\rho(f)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}}\nu(f)
for every
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
. We claim
that
\rho
defines a submultiplicative lattice seminorm on
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
First, we need to show that the quantity
\rho(f)
is actually finite.
If
f=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})
, for a certain
LLA expression
\Phi
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
, it is not difficult
to check, by induction on the complexity of
\Phi
, that there exists
a polynomial
p(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
with

p(0,\ldots,0)=0
such that

(2)

|f|\leq p(|\delta_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\delta_{x_{n}}|).

Then for every
\nu\in\mathcal{N}
:

\nu(|f|)\leq\nu(p(|\delta_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\delta_{x_{n}}|))\leq p(\nu(|\delta_{x_{1}}|),\ldots,\nu(|\delta_{x_{n}}|))\leq p(\|x_{1}\|,\ldots,\|x_{n}\|),

where in the second inequality we have used the triangle inequality
and that
\nu
is
submultiplicative, and in both the second and the third inequalities
that the coefficients of
p
are positive. So
\rho(f)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}}\nu(f)\leq p(\|x_{1}\|,\ldots,\|x_{n}\|)
is indeed finite. That
\rho(f)
is a submultiplicative
lattice seminorm follows from the definition.

The kernel of this seminorm

\ker\rho=\{\,f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]:\rho(f)=0\,\}

is an order and algebraic ideal. An elementary argument shows that the
quotient

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]/\ker\rho
is again an
f\!
-algebra (see
[
23
, Proposition
3.2]
). Denote this quotient by
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, denote by

q_{E}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
the quotient map, and set
\eta_{E}=q_{E}\delta_{E}
. Then
\rho
induces a norm in
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
that
makes it into a normed
f\!
-algebra. Moreover, the map
\eta_{E}
is
contractive with respect to this norm (in fact, it is an isometry; see
below). Denote the norm simply by
\|{\cdot}\|
and let
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
be the completion
of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
with respect to this norm.
Abusing the notation, we shall denote also by
\eta_{E}
the
composition of this map with the canonical embedding
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

The free normed
f\!
-algebra generated by a normed space
E
is
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]

together with the map
\eta_{E}
. The free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated
by a Banach space
E
is
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
together with the map
\eta_{E}
.

Proof.

We need to check that
\eta_{E}
is a linear isometric embedding
and that
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, together with
\eta_{E}
, satisfy
the universal property defining the free normed
f\!
-algebra generated by
E
.
Let
(A,\|{\cdot}\|_{A})
be a normed
f\!
-algebra, and let
T\colon E\to A
be a contractive operator. Let
\tilde{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]\to A
be the unique lattice-algebra homomorphism satisfying
\tilde{T}\delta_{E}=T
. It is direct to check that, since
\|T\|\leq 1
,

f\mapsto\|\tilde{T}f\|_{A}/\|T\|
defines an element of

\mathcal{N}
.
This implies that
\|\tilde{T}f\|_{A}\leq\|T\|\rho(f)
for all
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Hence there exists a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism

\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(E)/\ker\rho\to A
satisfying

\hat{T}q_{E}=\tilde{T}
and
\|\hat{T}\|\leq\|T\|
. This implies
\hat{T}\eta_{E}=\hat{T}q_{E}\delta_{E}=\tilde{T}\delta_{E}=T
and, since
\eta_{E}

is contractive, also
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
.

It only remains to show that
\eta_{E}
is an isometric embedding.
Let
T\colon E\to\ell_{\infty}(B_{E^{*}})
be the the standard
isometric embedding
Tx=(x^{*}(x))_{x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}}
. Since

\ell_{\infty}(B_{E^{*}})
, with coordinatewise order and product, is a Banach
f\!
-algebra, there exists a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\ell_{\infty}(B_{E^{*}})
satisfying
\|\hat{T}\|=1
and
\hat{T}\eta_{E}=T
. Using that
\eta_{E}
is contractive it follows that

\|x\|=\|Tx\|\leq\|\hat{T}\|\|\eta_{E}x\|=\|\eta_{E}x\|\leq\|x\|

holds for all
x\in E
. Therefore
\|\eta_{E}x\|=\|x\|
.

It is direct that the completion of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, namely

\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
, together with
\eta_{E}
, is the free Banach
f\!
-algebra generated by
E
.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Let
Z
be the closure of
\Lat\{\,\eta_{x}:x\in E\,\}
in

\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Let
\phi_{E}\colon E\to Z
be
\eta_{E}
with the codomain restricted to
Z
. Then
Z
, together with

\phi_{E}
, is the free Banach lattice
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
. Indeed, let
X
be a Banach
lattice, and let
T\colon E\to X
be an operator. Equip
X
with
the zero product, so that it becomes a Banach
f\!
-algebra. Then
S=T/\|T\|

extends to a lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{S}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to X_{0}
satisfying
\hat{S}\eta_{E}=S
and
\|\hat{S}\|=1
. Let

\hat{T}\colon Z\to X
be
the restriction of the lattice homomorphism
\|T\|\hat{S}
to

Z
. Then
\hat{T}
is a lattice homomorphism
satisfying
\hat{T}\phi_{E}=T
. In particular,
\|T\|\leq\|\hat{T}\|\leq\|T\|\|\hat{S}\|=\|T\|
, so
\|\hat{T}\|=\|T\|
. An immediate
consequence of this is that the
free norm in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
, when restricted to
Z
, is the norm of the free
Banach lattice, for which an explicit formula was found in

[
6
]
. From now on,
Z
will be
denoted as
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
.

Moreover,
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
is contractively complemented in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
by a
lattice homomorphism. Indeed, the
canonical inclusion
\eta_{E}\colon E\to{\operatorname{FBL}[E]}_{0}
extends to a
unique contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism
P\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to{\operatorname{FBL}[E]}_{0}
. Forget about the zero product in
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
, and
consider
P
merely as a lattice homomorphism. The restriction

P|_{\operatorname{FBL}[E]}\colon\operatorname{FBL}[E]\to\operatorname{FBL}[E]
is a lattice homomorphism of
norm one satisfying
P\eta_{E}=\eta_{E}
. By uniqueness,
P|_{\operatorname{FBL}[E]}
is the identity on
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
. Hence the composition of
P
with
the canonical inclusion
\operatorname{FBL}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is a projection of

\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
onto
\operatorname{FBL}[E]
.

4.2.
Explicit description of the
kernel

Thus far we have shown, using only general arguments, that
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]

and
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
exist for a general Banach space
E
. But since we have
quotiented out
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
(which we know explicitly) by a mysterious
ideal
\ker\rho
, we have no explicit description for
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
Fortunately, we can use
3.5
to describe this
ideal.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. Let
\rho
be the greatest
submultiplicative lattice seminorm on
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
such that
\rho(\delta_{x})\leq\|x\|
for all
x\in E
. Then

\ker\rho=\{\,f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]:f|_{B_{E^{*}}}=0\,\}.

Proof.

For every
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
,

\nu_{x^{*}}(f)=|f(x^{*})|
defines a lattice seminorm that is
submultiplicative and such that
\nu_{x^{*}}(\delta_{x})\leq\|x\|
for all

x\in E
. If
f\in\ker\rho
, then

|f(x^{*})|=\nu_{x^{*}}(f)\leq\rho(f)=0.

Hence every function in
\ker\rho
vanishes on the unit ball.

Conversely, suppose
f\in\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
is such that
f|_{B_{E^{*}}}=0
.
Write
f=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})
for a
certain LLA expression
\Phi
and some
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
;
by changing
\Phi
as necessary, we may assume that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are linearly independent and
\|x_{i}\|\leq 1
(see the proof
of
4.6
). The goal is to show that

q_{E}f=\Phi(q_{E}\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,q_{E}\delta_{x_{n}})=\Phi(\eta_{E}(x_{1}),\ldots,\eta_{E}(x_{n}))

is zero in
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. By
3.1
there exists an injective and
contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism
R\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to X_{0}\oplus_{\infty}C(K)
, for a certain Banach lattice
X
and
a certain compact Hausdorff space
K
. Denote by
R_{1}
(resp.
R_{2}
) the composition of
R
with
the projection onto the first (resp. second) coordinate. We will
show that
R_{i}(q_{E}f)=0
for
i=1,2
.

For every
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
:

0=f(x^{*})=\Phi(x^{*}(x_{1}),\ldots,x^{*}(x_{n})).

We claim that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
(x^{*}(x_{1}),\ldots,x^{*}(x_{n}))
takes all values in

[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{n}
as
x^{*}
ranges through

B_{E^{*}}
. Indeed, set
F=\barespn\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, and let

\|{\cdot}\|_{1}
denote the
\ell_{1}^{n}
-norm in
F
. Since
F
is
finite-dimensional, there exists
C>0
such that
\|{\cdot}\|_{1}\leq C\|{\cdot}\|
, where
\|{\cdot}\|
stands for the
norm in
E
. Define
\varepsilon=1/C
, and let
x^{*}\colon F\to\mathbb{R}
be a linear functional satisfying
(x^{*}(x_{1}),\ldots,x^{*}(x_{n}))\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{n}
. If

x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
is an arbitrary element of
F
,
then

|x^{*}(x)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}||x^{*}(x_{i})|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|_{1}\leq\|x\|.

This shows that
x^{*}\in B_{F^{*}}
. Use Hahn–Banach to extend
this functional to an element
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
. Since
(x^{*}(x_{1}),\ldots,x^{*}(x_{n}))
can be chosen arbitrarily in
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{n}
, the desired claim follows.
It is a consequence of this claim and
2.7
that
\Phi

vanishes on every Banach lattice with the zero product; in
particular, it vanishes on
X_{0}
, and therefore
R_{1}(q_{E}(f))=0
.

Since
R_{2}
is a
contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism,
R_{2}=\hat{T}

for a certain contractive operator
T\colon E\to C(K)
satisfying

R_{2}\eta_{E}=T
. For
t\in K
, denote by
\phi_{t}\in C(K)^{*}
the
evaluation functional at
t
. Recall that
\phi_{t}
is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism of norm one. Compute:

\displaystyle R_{2}(q_{E}(f))(t)

\displaystyle=\Phi(R_{2}(\eta_{E}(x_{1}))(t),\ldots,R_{2}(\eta_{E}(x_{n}))(t))

\displaystyle=\Phi((Tx_{1})(t),\ldots,(Tx_{n})(t))

\displaystyle=\Phi((T^{*}\phi_{t})(x_{1}),\ldots,(T^{*}\phi_{t})(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}}(T^{*}\phi_{t}),\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}}(T^{*}\phi_{t}))

\displaystyle=\Phi(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n}})(T^{*}\phi_{t})

\displaystyle=f(T^{*}\phi_{t})=0

since
\|T^{*}\phi_{t}\|\leq 1
and
f
vanishes on the unit ball.
This shows that
R_{2}(q_{E}(f))=0
. Hence
R(q_{E}(f))=0
and, since
R

is injective,
q_{E}(f)=0
.
∎

Since
\delta_{x}\colon E^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is weak
∗
continuous, so is every
element of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Therefore there is a restriction map:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]&\longrightarrow&C(B_{E^{*}},w^{*})\\
&f&\longmapsto&f|_{B_{E^{*}}}\\
\end{array}.

Operations in
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
are computed pointwise, so this is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism. However, as noted in

4.7
, this map is not injective, so
we cannot view the elements of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
as continuous functions on
the unit ball of the dual.

It is remarkable that this map does induce an injective
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\iota\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})

because, according to
4.10
, its kernel is precisely
\ker\rho
. Note that, by definition of the norm in
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
,
\iota
is
contractive. We have thus found an explicit lattice-algebraic
representation of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. There exists a unique injective and
contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism

\iota_{E}\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})

satisfying
(\iota_{E}\eta_{x})(x^{*})=x^{*}(x)
for all
x\in E
and

x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
.

From now on,
\iota_{E}
(or simply
\iota
when
E
is clear from the
context) will denote the map in the above proposition. The following
is a first application of this representation result.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
E
be a Banach space and let
f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. There exist
\lambda>0
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
such that

|f|\leq\lambda|\eta_{x_{1}}|\vee\cdots\vee|\eta_{x_{n}}|.

Proof.

As already mentioned in
Section

4.1
, there exists a polynomial
p(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
with
p(0,\ldots,0)=0
such that
|f|\leq p(|\eta_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\eta_{x_{n}}|)
. Without loss of generality,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in B_{E}
. It follows
that for every
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
:

\displaystyle\iota(|f|)(x^{*})

\displaystyle\leq p(|x^{*}(x_{1})|,\ldots,|x^{*}(x_{n})|)

\displaystyle\leq p(1,\ldots,1)\,|x^{*}(x_{1})|\vee\cdots\vee|x^{*}(x_{n})|

\displaystyle=p(1,\ldots,1)\iota(|\eta_{x_{1}}|\vee\cdots\vee|\eta_{x_{n}}|)(x^{*}),

where in the second inequality we have used the fact that

|x^{*}(x_{i})|\leq 1
. Since
\iota
is an injective lattice
homomorphism, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

Let

be a finite-dimensional Banach space. There exists a
finite subset
F\subseteq S_{E}
for which

is a strong unit in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
and
such that the norm it induces

is equivalent to

Proof.

We shall first show that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
has a strong unit

. Denote by
\Fin(S_{E})
the family of finite subsets of

S_{E}
. For every
F\in\Fin(S_{E})
, define the element

e_{F}=\sup_{x\in F}|\eta_{x}|\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. In fact, this element
belongs to
\operatorname{FVL}[E]
, where we are identifying
\operatorname{FVL}[E]
inside

\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
as in
4.9
. Keep in mind that, with this
identification, the norm of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, when restricted to
\operatorname{FVL}[E]

we can refer to the free norm on
\operatorname{FVL}[E]
without ambiguity.

Let
\iota\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})

be the injective lattice-algebra homomorphism from

4.11
. For every
x^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}
, the net

(\iota(e_{F})(x^{*}))_{F\in\Fin(S_{E})}
converges to
\|x^{*}\|_{E^{*}}
. Since
E
is

\|{\cdot}\|_{E^{*}}
is a continuous
function on
B_{E^{*}}
(with respect to the weak
∗
topology). Moreover,
(e_{F})_{F\in\Fin(S_{E})}

is an increasing net. By Dini’s theorem,
\iota(e_{F})
converges
uniformly to
\|{\cdot}\|_{E^{*}}
in
C(B_{E^{*}})
.

Recall from
[
58
, Section 9.1]
that, because

E
is finite-dimensional, the free norm is equivalent to the
uniform norm on
\iota(\operatorname{FVL}[E])
; that is,
\iota
is an
embedding when restricted to
\operatorname{FVL}[E]
. We have shown above that the net

(\iota(e_{F}))
is Cauchy in the uniform norm. Hence the net

(e_{F})
is Cauchy in the free norm.

Let
e\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
be the limit of
(e_{F})
. Since
(e_{F})
is an
increasing net,

. We are
going to show that

, is the whole

(and therefore that

is a strong unit). For this, we

and

for every

. Hence

.

\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\subseteq I_{e}

. We have thus shown that

\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
, is a submultiplicative

. By construction of the free norm,

on
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

On the other hand,
|f|\leq\|f\|_{e}e
holds for every

\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Now if

is also a Cauchy sequence in the norm

converges

. Hence,

is a strong unit, and that the norm

is
equivalent to the free norm on
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

To finish the proof, it only remains to show that there exists a

of the desired form equivalent to

. For this, it

.

does not play any special role in the proof; any scalar in

. Hence

for all

. Taking
the limit in

, it follows that

.

is finite-dimensional,

S_{E^{*}}
is a compact metric space under the metric induced by
the norm. Moreover,
S_{E^{*}}
can be embedded isometrically as a compact subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
f_{0}
is, by
2.6
, the uniform limit of
continuous functions. It is, therefore, continuous. Furthermore,
since
S_{E^{*}}
is compact,
f_{0}
is uniformly continuous on

S_{E^{*}}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
\delta>0
such that

|f_{0}(u)-f_{0}(u^{\prime})|<\varepsilon/2
whenever
\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{E^{*}}<\delta
and
such that
|f(r_{n}u)r_{n}^{-1}-f_{0}(u)|<\varepsilon/2
whenever
0\leq r_{n}<\delta
and
u\in S_{E^{*}}
(recall the limit
defining
f_{0}(u)
is uniform). For
n\in\mathbb{N}

big enough we will have
r_{n},\|u-u_{n}\|<\delta
, and then,
whenever
r_{n}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle|Sf(r_{n},u_{n})-f_{0}(u)|

\displaystyle=|f(r_{n}u_{n})r_{n}^{-1}-f_{0}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(r_{n}u_{n})r_{n}^{-1}-f_{0}(u_{n})|+|f_{0}(u_{n})-f_{0}(u)|<\varepsilon.

When
r_{n}=0
,
Sf(r_{n},u_{n})=f_{0}(u_{n})
and

|f_{0}(u_{n})-f_{0}(u)|<\varepsilon/2
.
This proves that
Sf(r_{n},u_{n})\to f_{0}(u)=Sf(0,u)
, and therefore that
Sf

is a continuous function.

It is clear
from the definition that
S
is a lattice homomorphism. It is also
an algebra homomorphism because, for
r\neq 0
,

S(fg)(r,u)=\iota(fg)(ru)r^{-1}=rSf(r,u)Sg(r,u)=(Sf\star Sg)(r,u).

The equality also holds for general
(r,u)\in[0,1]\times S_{E^{*}}
by continuity. Certainly,
(S\eta_{x})(r,u)=u(x)=(Tx)(r,u)
. Hence
Sf=\hat{T}f
for all
f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
∎

With the explicit expression we can now show that the map
\hat{T}
is
injective on
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
E
be a finite-dimensional Banach space, and let
\hat{T}
be
as in
4.19
. If, for some
f,g\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
,

\hat{T}f\leq\hat{T}g
, then
f\leq g
.

Proof.

Let us first check that
\hat{T}
is injective on
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
Suppose
\hat{T}f=0
for some
f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Then, for every

u\in S_{E^{*}}
and
0<r\leq 1
,

0=(\hat{T}f)(r,u)=\iota(f)(ru)/r.

This implies that
\iota(f)
vanishes on
B_{E^{*}}\setminus\{0\}
.
Since every element of
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
vanishes also at
0
, it follows
that
\iota(f)=0
. Injectivity of
\iota
implies that
f=0
. Hence the map
\hat{T}|_{\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]}\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\hat{T}(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E])
is a lattice isomorphism. In particular, if

\hat{T}f\leq\hat{T}g
, then by applying the inverse it follows
that
f\leq g
.
∎

Proof of
4.16
.

Let
e^{\prime}=\sup_{x\in F}|\eta_{x}|\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
be as in
4.17
, where

F\in\Fin(S_{E})
, and let

\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})
be as in

4.19
. First note that

(\hat{T}e^{\prime})(r,u)=\sup_{x\in F}|u(x)|\geq 1/2

(see the end of the proof of
4.17
).
Therefore
\hat{T}e^{\prime}
is a strong unit in
C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})
. We are going to show that

\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}=\|f\|_{e^{\prime}}
for all
f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

Since
|f|\leq\|f\|_{e^{\prime}}e^{\prime}
, applying
\hat{T}
to this inequality
yields immediately
\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}\leq\|f\|_{e^{\prime}}
.
Similarly,
\hat{T}|f|=|\hat{T}f|\leq\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}\hat{T}e^{\prime}
and from

4.20
it follows that
|f|\leq\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}e^{\prime}
. Hence also
\|f\|_{e^{\prime}}\leq\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}
.

Since the free norm is equivalent to
\|{\cdot}\|_{e^{\prime}}
and the
uniform norm is equivalent to
\|{\cdot}\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}
, the
equality
\|\hat{T}f\|_{\hat{T}e^{\prime}}=\|f\|_{e^{\prime}}
must hold for all

f\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
. In particular,
\hat{T}
is injective and its
image is closed under the uniform norm.

To complete the proof it only remains to show that
\hat{T}
is
surjective. This will follow as a consequence of the Stone–Weierstrass
theorem once we show that the image of
\hat{T}
contains the
constant one function and separates points.

Define, as in the proof of
4.17
, the element
e_{F}=\sup_{x\in F}|\eta_{x}|
for every
F\in\Fin(S_{E})
. We
already showed in the lemma that
(e_{F})
converges to a certain

in
the free norm. Note that

(\hat{T}e_{F})(r,u)=\sup_{x\in F}|Tx|(r,u)=\sup_{x\in F}|u(x)|

converges to
1
. Therefore

\hat{T}e=\lim_{F}\hat{T}e_{F}=\mathbb{1}
, where
\mathbb{1}
denotes the
constant one function.

Let
(r,u),(r^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in[0,1]\times S_{E^{*}}
. If
u\neq u^{\prime}
,
then there exists
x\in S_{E}
such that
u(x)\neq u^{\prime}(x)
. In other
words:

(\hat{T}\eta_{x})(r,u)=u(x)\neq u^{\prime}(x)=(\hat{T}\eta_{x})(r,u).

If instead

, then we have

(\hat{T}e^{2})(r,u)=(\hat{T}e\star\hat{T}e)(r,u)=r

and therefore
(\hat{T}e^{2})(r,u)\neq(\hat{T}e^{2})(r^{\prime},u^{\prime})
. Thus
the image of

separates points of

. From the Stone–Weierstrass

\hat{T}
is surjective.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

The representation
\iota\colon\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})
is never
an embedding. Indeed, when
E
is infinite-dimensional, the norm
of the free Banach lattice cannot be equivalent to an
AM-norm (this is a consequence of
[
58
, Proposition 9.30]
).
Suppose that
\iota
were an embedding for some
finite-dimensional
E
; we will show
that this contradicts
4.16
. In this case, the image
of
\iota
would be a closed sublattice-algebra of
J_{0}
, the
closed (lattice and algebraic) ideal of

C(B_{E^{*}})
formed by the functions that vanish at
0
. It is immediate to check that

\iota(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E])
separates the points of
B_{E^{*}}
, and that
for every
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
different from zero there exists

such that

. As a
consequence of the

\iota(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E])=J_{0}
. But
J_{0}
does
not have a strong order unit; this contradicts

4.16
.

As we shall see in
Section

5
, determining whether

\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is semiprime or not is very important and yet far from
trivial.

Corollary 4.22
.

If
E
is a finite-dimensional Banach space, then
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is
semiprime.

Proof.

By
4.16
,
we only need to check that the product (
3
) is
semiprime. Suppose
f\star f=0
. Then

0=(f\star f)(r,u)=rf(r,u)^{2}

for all
(r,u)\in[0,1]\times S_{E^{*}}
. This means that

vanishes on the dense set

. By continuity,

.
∎

In the next result,
S_{\infty}^{n}
denotes the unit sphere of
\ell_{\infty}^{n}
.

Corollary 4.23
.

Let

be a Banach space of dimension

. The free Banach
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is lattice-algebra isomorphic to

with pointwise order, and product

where
(r,u)\in[0,1]\times S_{\infty}^{n}
and

Identify, by taking coordinates,

and

with
their homeomorphic copies in

. The map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\phi\colon&[0,1]\times S_{E^{*}}&\longrightarrow&[0,1]\times S_{\infty}^{n}\\
&(r,u)&\longmapsto&(r,u/\|u\|_{\infty})\\
\end{array}

is a homeomorphism. Consider the associated composition operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}T\colon&C([0,1]\times S_{\infty}^{n})&\longrightarrow&C([0,1]\times S_{E^{*}})\\
&f&\longmapsto&f\circ\phi\\

\displaystyle T(f\star g)(r,u)

.
∎

-algebra generated by a

4.4.
On the free norm

It follows from the abstract construction in
Section

4.1
that the norm in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is the supremum of the lattice
seminorms that are submultiplicative and that make the canonical map
\eta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
contractive. There is an equivalent way of
defining this norm: for every
f\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
,
\|f\|
is the
smallest number such that, whenever
A
is a Banach

f\!
-algebra and
T\colon E\to A
is a contractive operator,

\|\hat{T}f\|\leq\|f\|
. Moreover, if
f\in\operatorname{FBL}[E]
, then

\|f\|=\sup\{\,\|\hat{T}f\|\;:\;T\colon E\to\ell_{1}^{n}\text{ with }\|T\|\leq 1\,\}.

This important fact was first proved in

[
6
]
and is at the center of the
theory of free Banach lattices. For this reason, in this section we
try to reach a similar result for free Banach
f\!
-algebras. Only
considering operators that arrive at
\ell_{1}^{n}
is too ambitious;
instead, we will take the supremum over all operators that arrive at
finite-dimensional semiprime
f\!
-algebras. Although the semiprimeness
condition may look artificial at first, it will turn out to be
very useful in
Section

5
.

Definition 4.27
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. For every
f\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
, define

\tau_{E}(f)
to be the least positive number such that, if
A
is a
semiprime finite-dimensional Banach
f\!
-algebra, and

T\colon E\to A
is a contractive operator, then
\tau_{E}(f)\geq\|\hat{T}f\|
.
When there is no ambiguity on the underlying Banach space,
\tau_{E}
is simply
denoted by
\tau
.

Remark 4.28
.

Note that
\tau(f)
is finite since
\|\hat{T}f\|\leq\|f\|
for every contractive operator
T\colon E\to A
. In
particular,
\tau(f)\leq\|f\|
. It is not difficult to check from
the definition that
f\mapsto\tau(f)
defines a
submultiplicative lattice seminorm on
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
.

It will be very valuable for the discussion of

Section

5
(and in particular for

5.4
) to determine when
\tau
defines a norm on
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
(i.e., when
\tau(f)=0
implies

f=0
). The following result is an important step in this direction.

Theorem 4.29
.

The norm in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}^{n}]
is
\tau
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The main computation of the theorem is encapsulated in the next
lemma. Since it is quite technical, let us explain the ideas that
motivate it. From
4.16
we know that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}^{n}]

is isomorphic to
C(K)
, for a certain compact Hausdorff space
K
,
with a product of the form
f\star g=wfg
, where
w\in C(K)_{+}
and
juxtaposition denotes the pointwise product. (We are not being
explicit with the values of
K
and
w
on purpose; the argument will
not depend on this.) Let
f=\Phi(\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}})\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
, for a certain LLA expression

\Phi
. Approximate the generators

\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}}
and the weight
w
by simple
functions (i.e., functions that take only finitely many values).
Disjointify the family of characteristic functions involved in these
simple functions. Then their span will be a finite-dimensional
sublattice
A
that contains discrete approximations of
\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}}
. Define a product in this space that consists in
multiplying pointwise by the discrete approximation of
w
. If the
approximations are good enough, this new product will be almost
submultiplicative for the free norm, and
\Phi
, when evaluated at
the discrete approximations of the
\eta_{i}
, will be very close to

f
. This is the statement of the lemma. Afterwards, in the proof of the theorem, we will use this
finite-dimensional
f\!
-algebra

, with an appropriate norm, to construct
an operator
T\colon\ell_{1}^{n}\to A
for which
\|\hat{T}f\|_{A}
is
very close to the free norm of
f
. One caveat though: since
K
is not sufficiently disconnected, we may not be
able to approximate the functions by simple functions. For this reason
we shall work in
B(K)
, the Banach lattice of bounded
Borel-measurable functions on
K
.

Lemma 4.30
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space, and let
C(K)
be equipped
with the usual lattice structure and a certain product
\star
that makes
it a Banach
f\!
-algebra. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C(K)
, and let
f=\Phi(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
for some LLA expression
\Phi
. Fix

\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
. Then there exists a finite-dimensional sublattice

of

, together with a semiprime
f\!
-algebra product
\circ
in

, and

(f_{1})_{d},\ldots,(f_{n})_{d}\in A

|(f_{i})_{d}|\leq|f_{i}|
and, if

(where now
\Phi

), then

.
Moreover,

Denote by
\star

with the

is a Banach

-algebra, there exists a weight

, such that
(f\star g)(t)=w(t)f(t)g(t)
for all

(see
[
63
, Korollar
1.4]
).

Consider the bidual
C(K)^{**}

equipped with the Arens product. This is again a Banach
f\!
-algebra (see
[
62
]
), and
we have a lattice-algebra isometry
C(K)\to C(K)^{**}
.
Consider the Banach lattice
B(K)
of bounded Borel-measurable
functions on
K
.
Note that
C(K)\subseteq B(K)\subseteq C(K)^{**}
in a natural way,
since every bounded Borel-measurable function can be integrated against an
element of
M(K)=C(K)^{*}
. We are going to show that
B(K)
is a
subalgebra of
C(K)^{**}
, thus becoming a Banach
f\!
-algebra. Even
better, the Arens product of

is
(F\star G)(t)=w(t)F(t)G(t)
because, if

and

,
then

\left<\mu f,g\right>=\left<\mu,f\star g\right>=\int_{K}g(t)w(t)f(t)d\mu(t)

implies

, and

\left<G\mu,f\right>=\left<G,\mu f\right>=\int_{K}G(t)d(\mu f)(t)=\int_{K}f(t)w(t)G(t)d\mu(t)

implies
d(G\mu)(t)=w(t)G(t)d\mu(t)
. Therefore

\left<F\star G,\mu\right>=\left<F,G\mu\right>=\int_{K}F(t)w(t)G(t)d\mu(t)

and, since this holds for all
\mu\in M(K)
, it follows that

(F\star G)(t)=w(t)F(t)G(t)
for all
t\in K
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. For convenience, we shall assume that

\|f_{1}\|_{\infty},\ldots,\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
; this can always be
achieved changing
\Phi
if necessary. Let
0=c_{0}<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{N}<c_{N+1}=1+\delta
be
a partition of the interval
[0,1+\delta]
with
\max_{i}|c_{i+1}-c_{i}|<\delta
, this

small enough and to be
determined later. (We use
1+\delta
instead of
1
to force

c_{N}>1
, since our functions can take the value
1
.)
Consider the algebra
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
K
generated by

\{c_{i}\leq(f_{s})_{\sigma}<c_{i+1}\}\text{ for }s=1,\ldots,n,i=0,\ldots,N,\sigma\in\{+,-\}\text{ and }

\{c_{i}\leq w<c_{i+1}\}\text{ for }i=0,\ldots,N.

Note that this algebra is contained in the Borel sets of

. Also, since it is finitely generated,

is

.

This observation implies that

\chi_{\{c_{i}\leq(f_{s})_{\sigma}<c_{i+1}\}}=\sum_{j\in J(s,\sigma,i)}a_{j},

for certain

similarly one defines

so that

\chi_{\{c_{i}\leq w<c_{i+1}\}}=\sum_{j\in J(w,i)}a_{j}.

Since

,
we have

(f_{s})_{\sigma}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}(f_{s})_{\sigma}\sum_{j\in J(s,\sigma,i)}a_{j},\quad w=\sum_{i=0}^{N}w\sum_{j\in J(w,i)}a_{j}.

Let
A=\barespn\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\}
. We are going to define
elements of
A
that approximate
(f_{s})_{\sigma}
and

. For
every
s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\sigma\in\{+,-\}
, and

In other words,

. More precisely,

Similarly, for

. Define

Since the

is a sublattice of

.

Fix

5.

Recall from

L(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E])
. Since

is dense in

, by continuity they must
also coincide on
L(\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E])

\phi_{t}(I)=1=\phi_{s}(I)
. Since
L(\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E])
and

and
therefore that

.

Being

compact,

is actually an embedding. That is,

is homeomorphic to its image
\psi(K)\subseteq B_{E^{*}}
.
Note that
(L\eta)(x)=\iota(\eta_{x})\circ\psi
. In other
words: the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}S\colon&E&\longrightarrow&C(\psi(K))\\
&x&\longmapsto&\iota(\eta_{x})|_{\psi(K)}\\
\end{array}

extends to a lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{S}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(\psi(K))
which is injective. Indeed,

, where

denotes the composition

is precisely
C_{\psi^{-1}}\circ L
.

Consider the natural restriction map

, which is also a contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism. The diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(\psi(K))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(B_{E^{*}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{\iota}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}

commutes, because

Corollary 5.2
.

If

is a finite-dimensional Banach space, then
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is
representable.

Proof.

This is a consequence of

Even though
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
is semiprime for every Banach space
E
, it
is not clear at all whether its completion
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is also
semiprime. In general, it is not true that the completion of a
semiprime normed
f\!
-algebra is semiprime. To give an example of this
rather elementary fact turns out to be surprisingly difficult. The
only example we have found makes in fact use of free Banach
f\!
-algebras.

Example 5.3
.

Consider the Banach
f\!
-algebra
B=\mathbb{R}_{0}\oplus_{\infty}\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}]
.
Denote by
e_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, the canonical basis of
\ell_{1}
, and
denote by
e_{0}=(1,0)\in B
. Let
A=\operatorname{VLA}\{(1,\eta_{e_{n}/n}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq B
. It is clear that
A
is a normed
f\!
-algebra, with the
norm inherited from
B
. Its completion is its closure in
B
, and
it contains
e_{0}
since
\|\eta_{e_{n}/n}\|=\|e_{n}/n\|_{1}=1/n
tends
to zero. Hence its completion is not semiprime, because
e_{0}^{2}=0
.

The difficult part is to show that
A
is actually semiprime.
Suppose
a\in A
is such that
a^{2}=(0,0)
. By definition, there
exists an LLA expression
\Phi[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
such that

a=\Phi((1,\eta_{e_{1}}),\ldots,(1,\eta_{e_{n}/n}))
. Since the
operations are computed coordinatewise:

(0,0)=a^{2}=(\Phi(1,\ldots,1)^{2},\Phi(\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}/n})^{2}).

We are going to show that
\Phi
vanishes on

[-1/n,1/n]^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fix
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in[-1/n,1/n]^{n}
. The element

x^{*}=(\lambda_{1},2\lambda_{2},\ldots,n\lambda_{n},0,0,\ldots)\in B_{\ell_{\infty}}

seen as a contractive functional on
\ell_{1}
extends to a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\widehat{x^{*}}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}
. Therefore

0=\widehat{x^{*}}(\Phi(\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}/n})^{2})=\Phi(x^{*}(e_{1}),\ldots,x^{*}(e_{n})/n)^{2}=\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})^{2}.

It follows that
\Phi(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=0
. Hence

\Phi
vanishes on
[-1/n,1/n]^{n}
. By
2.7
,

\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
. Therefore

a=(\Phi(1,\ldots,1),\Phi(\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}/n}))=(0,\Phi(\eta_{e_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{e_{n}/n})).

This shows that
a
lives only in
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}]
. But since the
latter space is semiprime (see
4.15
), it must
be
a=0
. Hence

is semiprime, but its completion is not.

The previous example illustrates why determining whether the
completion of a semiprime normed
f\!
-algebra is again semiprime or not is far
from trivial. The next proposition shows how
\tau
can be very
useful in that matter.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. Suppose
\tau
defines a norm on
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
. Then
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is semiprime.

Proof.

This proof is based on the observation that every semiprime
finite-dimensional
f\!
-algebra

has an
identity. Indeed, if
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is a basis of pairwise
disjoint elements of
A
, then
e_{i}^{2}=w_{i}e_{i}
for some
w_{i}\geq 0
,
and since
A
is semiprime,
w_{i}>0
. The identity is
the element
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}/w_{i}
.

Let
f\in N(\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E])
. Let
A
be a semiprime finite-dimensional Banach

f\!
-algebra and let

be a
contractive operator. Since is a
sublattice-algebra of

, in particular is semiprime,
and by the initial observation has an identity. Let

be in the
preimage of the identity. Then

\hat{T}f=\hat{T}f\hat{T}g=\hat{T}(fg)=0.

Since
T
and
A
were arbitrary,
\tau(f)=0
, and since we are
assuming
\tau
defines a norm on
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
,
f=0
.
∎

In this section we investigate the lattice and algebraic properties of the

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. Neither
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
nor
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
have an
approximate identity.

Proof.

Suppose
(h_{\alpha})
were an approximate identity in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
Let
x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
be a non-zero functional. Consider it as
an operator
x^{*}\colon E\to\mathbb{R}_{0}
, and extend it to a contractive
lattice-algebra homomorphism

\widehat{x^{*}}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\mathbb{R}_{0}.

Let
x\in E
be such that
x^{*}(x)\neq 0
. Since
\eta_{x}h_{\alpha}\to\eta_{x}
in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
, it follows
that

\widehat{x^{*}}(\eta_{x})\widehat{x^{*}}(h_{\alpha})\to\widehat{x^{*}}(\eta_{x})=x^{*}(x)\neq 0.

This is impossible
because in
\mathbb{R}_{0}
the product of any two elements is 0. The same
argument works for
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
.
∎

The following two properties are analogous
to
[
58
, Propositions 9.1 and 9.4]
. In this
context, however, one has to
be careful with the fact that we do not know whether
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is
representable in

or not.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
E
be a Banach space.

(i)

E
is finite-dimensional if and only if
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
has a
strong unit.

(ii)

E
is separable if and only if
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
has a quasi-interior point.

Proof.

(i)

When
E
is finite-dimensional, it was shown in

4.16
that
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
has a strong unit.
Conversely, suppose
e\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is a strong unit but

\dim E=\infty
. Scaling
e
, we may assume that
|\eta_{x}|\leq e
for all
x\in B_{E}
. Let

f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
be such that
\|e-f\|<1/2
. Then
\|\hat{\iota}e-\hat{\iota}f\|_{\infty}<1/2
.
Since
f\in\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
,
\hat{\iota}f=\Phi(\iota(\eta_{x_{1}}),\ldots,\iota(\eta_{x_{n}}))
for some LLA expression
\Phi
and some

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
. We are assuming
E
to be
infinite-dimensional, so there exists
x^{*}\in S_{E^{*}}
that
vanishes on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Hence
(\hat{\iota}f)(x^{*})=0
. For every
x\in B_{E}
:

\displaystyle|x^{*}(x)|

\displaystyle=\iota(|\eta_{x}|)(x^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\hat{\iota}e(x^{*})

\displaystyle=|(\hat{\iota}e)(x^{*})-(\hat{\iota}f)(x^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\iota}e-\hat{\iota}{f}\|_{\infty}<1/2.

This contradicts the fact that
\|x^{*}\|=1
.

(ii)

Suppose
E
is separable. Then
\eta(E)
is
separable and, since
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is the closed
sublattice-algebra generated by
\eta(E)
, it is also
separable. In particular, it has a quasi-interior point.

Conversely, suppose
e\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is a quasi-interior

x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
is such that

\hat{\iota}e(x^{*})=0
, then
\hat{\iota}f(x^{*})=0
for all

. Since

is dense in
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
,
it follows that
\hat{\iota}f(x^{*})=0
for all

f\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
. In particular,
x^{*}(x)=\hat{\iota}\eta_{x}(x^{*})=0
for all
x\in E
. This shows that
\hat{\iota}e
only
vanishes at
0
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
U_{n}=\{\,x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}:\hat{\iota}e(x^{*})<1/n\,\}
. Then
U_{n}
is a weak
∗
open
subset of

and
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_{n}=\{0\}
. For every

, let

be a
finite set such that

contains

V_{n}=\{\,x^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}:|x^{*}(x)|<1\text{ for all }x\in A_{n}\,\}.

Let

be the closed span of
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
. We claim that

. Indeed, if

were not

, then there
would exist some

vanishing on

. On
the other hand, since
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}V_{n}=\{0\}
, there
exists some

x^{*}\not\in V_{n}
, that is,

|x^{*}(x)|\geq 1
for some

. Since

, this is a contradiction.∎

In the previous section, we insisted that the representability of
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
E
be a Banach space for which
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]

(i)

For every

(ii)

(iii)

Even though the free vector lattice is not order dense in
the free Banach lattice unless the base space is

f\!

Showing this requires first the following technical fact.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
E
be a Banach space and let
U\subseteq B_{E^{*}}
be a
weak
∗
open set. Then there exists an element
f\in\iota(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E])
,
f>0
,
such that
\supp f\subseteq U
and
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Proof.

For every
a<b
, the support of the real function
x\mapsto[x_{+}(x_{+}-a)(x_{+}-b)]_{-}
is contained in
[a,b]\cap[0,\infty)
. Define, depending on the interval
(a,b)
, the LLA
expression

B_{(a,b)}[t]=\begin{cases}[t_{+}(t_{+}-a)(t_{+}-b)]_{-}&\text{if }b>0,\\
[t_{-}(t_{-}+a)(t_{-}+b)]_{-}&\text{if }b\leq 0.\end{cases}

Note that
x\mapsto B_{(a,b)}(x)
defines a non-zero real
function whose support is
[a,b]\cap[0,\infty)
if
b>0
and

[a,b]
otherwise. Normalize it so as to have
0\leq B_{(a,b)}(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Making

U
smaller, if necessary, we may assume that there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in B_{E}
linearly independent,
\delta>0
and
x^{*}\in E^{*}
with
\|x^{*}\|<1
such that

U=\{\,y^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}:|y^{*}(x_{i})-x^{*}(x_{i})|<\delta\text{
for }i=1,\ldots,n\,\}.

For a fixed
0<\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta
to be determined later,
define

f=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}B_{(x^{*}(x_{i})-\delta^{\prime}/2,x^{*}(x_{i})+\delta^{\prime}/2)}(\eta_{x_{i}}).

The most difficult part is to show that

f\neq 0
. For this, define a
functional
y^{*}
on
F=\barespn\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
in the
following way. If
|x^{*}(x_{i})|\geq\delta^{\prime}/2
, then set

\varepsilon_{i}=0
and
y^{*}(x_{i})=x^{*}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
. If instead
-\delta^{\prime}/2<x^{*}(x_{i})<\delta^{\prime}/2
, then there exists
0\leq\varepsilon_{i}<\delta^{\prime}/2
such that

x^{*}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}>0
; in this case, set

y^{*}(x_{i})=x^{*}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
.

We are going to show that, for
\delta^{\prime}
small enough,

\|y^{*}\|\leq 1
. Let
C>0
be such that
\|{\cdot}\|_{\infty}\leq C\|{\cdot}\|
in
F
, where
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\|_{\infty}=\max\{|\lambda_{i}|:1\leq i\leq n\}
. For
x\in B_{F}
,
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
, compute

\displaystyle|y^{*}(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(x^{*}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|x^{*}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|x^{*}\|\|x\|+\|x\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|(\|x^{*}\|+Cn\delta^{\prime}/2).

Recall that
\|x^{*}\|<1
was fixed at the beginning, and that

and
C
are determined by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, which were also chosen
before
\delta^{\prime}
. Thus we may set
\delta^{\prime}
small enough so as
to have
\|y^{*}\|\leq 1
. Use Hahn–Banach to extend it to a
functional
y^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
. This functional satisfies

B_{(x^{*}(x_{i})-\delta^{\prime}/2,x^{*}(x_{i})+\delta^{\prime}/2)}(y^{*}(x_{i}))>0

for every

, and so
f(y^{*})>0
. This shows that

f\neq 0
. Now we check the remaining properties of
f
. By
construction, for every
z^{*}\in B_{E^{*}}
,
f(z^{*})\geq 0
, and

f(z^{*})\neq 0
implies
z^{*}(x_{i})\in(x^{*}(x_{i})-\delta/2,x^{*}(x_{i})+\delta/2)
for

. Therefore

and
\supp f\subseteq U
.
∎

Let

Let

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
E
and
F
be Banach spaces for which
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
and
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]

are semiprime. Let
T\colon E\to F
be a contractive operator.
Then the
extension operator
\bar{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]
satisfies

\hat{\iota}_{F}(\bar{T}f)=\hat{\iota}_{E}(f)\circ T^{*}
.

Proof.

The composition operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}C_{T^{*}}\colon&\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]&\longrightarrow&C(B_{F^{*}})\\
&f&\longmapsto&\hat{\iota}_{E}(f)\circ T^{*}\\
\end{array}

is a lattice-algebra homomorphism. Since

C_{T^{*}}(\eta_{x})=\iota_{F}(\eta_{Tx})
,
C_{T^{*}}
maps
\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[E]
to

\iota_{F}(\operatorname{FN{\it f}\!A}[F])
contractively, so the range of
C_{T^{*}}
is contained
in
\hat{\iota}_{F}(\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F])
. By uniqueness,

C_{T^{*}}=\hat{\iota}_{F}\circ\bar{T}
.
∎

Right after the corresponding lemma for free Banach lattices, in
[

it is shown that an operator between Banach spaces

is
injective, onto or has dense range if and only if the induced operator
between the free Banach lattices
\bar{T}\colon\operatorname{FBL}[E]\to\operatorname{FBL}[F]
is
injective, onto or has dense range, respectively. The following example shows that for free Banach
f\!
-algebras the situation is different.

Example 6.9

Let
T\colon\ell_{1}^{2}\to\ell_{\infty}^{2}
be the formal identity.
Identify both

and
B_{\ell_{\infty}^{2}}

the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Identify also
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}^{2}]
and

\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{\infty}^{2}]

and

through
\hat{\iota}_{\ell_{1}^{2}}

and
\hat{\iota}_{\ell_{\infty}^{2}}
, respectively

\bar{T}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{1}^{2}]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[\ell_{\infty}^{2}]
is, according to

\bar{T}f=f|_{B_{\ell_{1}^{2}}}

7.
Isometries between free Banach

f
-algebras

It is direct from the universal property that two isometric Banach spaces have isometric free Banach
f\!
-algebras. Do isometric free
Banach
f\!
-algebras correspond to isometric Banach spaces? The goal of this
section is to show that they do when the Banach spaces have smooth
dual. The proof follows the same steps as
[
58
, Theorem
10.18]
, but one has to proceed with more care: every
isometry between the free Banach
f\!
-algebras induces a
w^{*}
-continuous
function between the unit balls of
the duals that need not be positively homogeneous. Therefore this
function cannot be extended to the whole dual, and the results
in
[
43
]
cannot be applied directly.
Instead, a refinement of the result in that paper is needed.

Recall that a Banach space
E
is said to be
smooth
if for
every
x\in X
there exists a unique
f_{x}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that

f_{x}(x)=\|x\|
. This functional
f_{x}
is called the
support
functional
at
x
and is given by

f_{x}(y)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|x+ty\|-\|x\|}{t}.

The quantity
[x,y]=\|y\|f_{y}(x)
defines a semi-inner product with

\|x\|=[x,x]^{1/2}
. We say that
x

is orthogonal to

y
, and
denote it by
x\perp y
, if
[y,x]=0
. Note that orthogonality is right
additive. Moreover, Birkhoff–James orthogonality coincides with this
notion of orthogonality; that is,

x\perp y\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|\text{ for
all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth normed spaces and
let
f\colon B_{X}\to B_{Y}
be a surjective mapping satisfying

|[f(x),f(y)]|=|[x,y]|\quad\text{for all }x,y\in B_{X}.

Then there exist a linear surjective isometry
U\colon X\to Y

and a map
\sigma\colon B_{X}\to\{-1,1\}
such that
f=\sigma U|_{B_{X}}
.

Proof.

The first step is to show that, for every
x\in B_{X}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda x\in B_{X}
, there exists a scalar

\gamma=\gamma(\lambda,x)
with
|\gamma|=|\lambda|
such
that
f(\lambda x)=\gamma f(x)
. This
\gamma
is defined by the
identity

\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(\lambda x)-\xi f(x)\|=\|f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)\|.

Note in particular that
\|f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)\|\leq\|f(\lambda x)\|\leq 1
. By definition

\|f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)+\mu f(x)\|\geq\|f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)\|

holds for every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, so by Birkhoff–James orthogonality,
f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)\perp f(x)
. Since
f
is
surjective and
f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)\in B_{Y}
, there exists
z\in B_{X}
such that
f(z)=f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)
. From
f(z)\perp f(x)
we get
z\perp x
and then

z\perp\lambda x
and
f(z)\perp f(\lambda x)
. Using that
the semi-inner product is right additive we conclude that

f(z)\perp(f(\lambda x)-\gamma f(x)).

But the right hand side is precisely
f(z)
.
Thus
f(z)=0
and
f(\lambda x)=\gamma f(x)
. Moreover,

|\lambda|\|x\|=\|\lambda x\|=\|f(\lambda x)\|=\|\gamma f(x)\|=|\gamma|\|f(x)\|=|\gamma|\|x\|

implies
|\lambda|=|\gamma|
.

Next, let
x,y\in B_{X}
be linearly independent and such that
x+y\in B_{X}
. We want to show
that
f(x+y)=\alpha f(x)+\beta f(y)
, where
\alpha=\alpha(x,y)
,
\beta=\beta(x,y)
and
|\alpha|=1=|\beta|
. As
before, define
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
by the relation

\min_{\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(x+y)-\xi f(x)-\eta f(y)\|=\|f(x+y)-\alpha f(x)-\beta f(y)\|

and note that
f(x+y)-\alpha f(x)-\beta f(y)\in B_{Y}
.
Then

f(x+y)-\alpha f(x)-\beta f(y)\perp f(x)\quad\text{and}\quad f(x+y)-\alpha f(x)-\beta f(y)\perp f(y).

Take
z\in B_{X}
such that
f(z)=f(x+y)-\alpha f(x)-\beta f(y)
.
Then
f(z)\perp f(x)
implies
z\perp x
,
f(z)\perp f(y)
implies

z\perp y
and the right additivity of orthogonality implies

z\perp x+y
and
f(z)\perp f(x+y)
. Hence
f(z)\perp f(z)
and

f(z)=0
. Let us show that
|\alpha|=1
. Let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\|x+\lambda_{0}y\|=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\|x+\lambda y\|.

Once again,
x+\lambda_{0}y\in B_{X}
.
Then
x+\lambda_{0}y\perp y
and
x+\lambda_{0}y\not\perp x
, for
otherwise
x+\lambda_{0}y=0
, contradicting the fact that
x
and

y
are linearly independent. Denote
w=x+\lambda_{0}y
. Since

w\perp y
we also have
f(w)\perp f(y)
. Then

[f(x+y),f(w)]=\alpha[f(x),f(w)]
and

|\alpha||[x,w]|=|\alpha||[f(x),f(w)]|=|[f(x+y),f(w)]|=|[x+y,w]|=|[x,w]|.

Since
[x,w]\neq 0
, it follows
|\alpha|=1
. Similarly,

|\beta|=1
.

Let us consider the projective spaces
\mathbb{P}X
and
\mathbb{P}Y
obtained as respective quotients of
X
and
Y
by identifying proportional vectors. Note that the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\tilde{f}\colon&\mathbb{P}X&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}Y\\
&\left<x\right>&\longmapsto&\left<f(x/\|x\|)\right>\\
\end{array}

is well-defined and surjective. We want to check that it satisfies
the assumptions of the Fundamental Theorem of Projective Geometry
(see
[
43
, Theorem 2.1]
) whenever
\dim X\geq 3
.

First we need to check its image is not contained in a projective
line. Let
x\in S_{X}
be a unit vector. Since
\dim X\geq 3
we can
choose unit vectors
y\in\ker f_{x}
and
z\in\ker f_{x}\cap\ker f_{y}
. From
x\perp y
,
x\perp z
and
y\perp z
it follows that

x,y,z
are linearly independent. Then
f(x),f(y),f(z)
are
unit vectors such that
f(x)\perp f(y)
,
f(x)\perp f(z)
and

f(y)\perp f(z)
. As before we conclude that they are linearly
independent. So the image of
f
is not contained in a
two-dimensional subspace, thus the image of
\tilde{f}
is not
contained in a projective line.

Second we need to check that
\tilde{f}
preserves projective
lines. Suppose
c=\lambda a+\mu b
with
a,b,c\neq 0
. Without
loss of generality we may assume
a,b\in S_{X}
. Set

M=\max\{|\lambda|,|\mu|,\|c\|\}
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\left<c\right>)

\displaystyle=\left<f(\frac{c}{\|c\|})\right>

\displaystyle=\left<f(\frac{M}{\|c\|}\frac{c}{M})\right>

\displaystyle=\left<f(\frac{\lambda}{M}a+\frac{\mu}{M}b)\right>

where in the third equality we have used that both
c/M
and

c/\|c\|
belong to
B_{X}
. After the previous computation, using
the properties of
f
together with the fact that
a,b,\lambda/Ma,\mu/Mb\in B_{X}
, it follows that
\tilde{f}(\left<c\right>)
is contained in the projective line that passes through

\left<a\right>
and
\left<b\right>
.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Projective Geometry there exists a
bijective linear map
A\colon X\to Y
such that

\tilde{f}(\left<x\right>)=\left<Ax\right>\quad\text{for all }x\in X.

This means in particular that for every
x\in S_{X}
there exists a
nonzero
\lambda(x)\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(x)=\lambda(x)Ax
. More
generally, for nonzero
x\in B_{X}
, since
f(x/\|x\|)=\gamma f(x)
, one also has
f(x)=\lambda(x)Ax
for some
\lambda(x)\neq 0
. Note that, if
x\in B_{X}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
are nonzero, and

\mu x\in B_{X}
, then

\gamma\lambda(x)Ax=\gamma f(x)=f(\mu x)=\lambda(\mu x)\mu Ax

and therefore

|\lambda(\mu x)|=|\gamma/\mu||\lambda(x)|=|\lambda(x)|.

Let
x,y\in B_{X}
be linearly independent and such that
x+y\in B_{X}
. Then

\lambda(x+y)A(x+y)=f(x+y)=\alpha f(x)+\beta f(y)=\alpha\lambda(x)Ax+\beta\lambda(y)Ay

and since
Ax
and
Ay
are linearly independent, it follows that

|\lambda(x)|=|\alpha\lambda(x)|=|\lambda(x+y)|=|\beta\lambda(y)|=|\lambda(y)|.

In conclusion,
|\lambda(x)|
is constantly
|\lambda|
. By
letting
\sigma(x)=\lambda(x)/|\lambda|
and
U=|\lambda|A

one gets the desired result for
\dim X\geq 3
.

For
\dim X=2
, let
x_{0},y_{0}\in X
be such that
x_{0}\perp y_{0}

and
\|x_{0}\|=\|y_{0}\|=1/2
. For every

\mu
with
\|x_{0}+\mu y_{0}\|\leq 1
(in particular, for every
\mu\in[-1,1]
), there exist
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x_{0}+\mu y_{0})=\omega_{1}f(x_{0})+\omega_{2}f(y_{0})

with
|\omega_{1}|=1
and
|\omega_{2}|=|\mu|
.

Define
\omega(\mu)=\omega_{1}
and
h(\mu)=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
.
Once we show that
h(\mu)=\mu h(1)
for all
\mu\in[-1,1]
, most of the work will be done.
For every
\lambda,\mu\in[-1,1]
with
\|x_{0}+(\lambda+\mu)y_{0}\|\leq 1
we have

f(x_{0}+(\lambda+\mu)y_{0})=\omega(\lambda+\mu)(f(x_{0})+h(\lambda+\mu)f(y_{0}))

but also

\displaystyle f(x_{0}+(\lambda+\mu)y_{0})

\displaystyle=f((x_{0}+\lambda y_{0})+\mu y_{0})

\displaystyle=\omega_{4}^{\prime}f(x_{0}+\lambda y_{0})+\omega_{3}f(y_{0})

\displaystyle=\omega_{4}f(x_{0})+\omega_{4}h(\lambda)f(y_{0})+\omega_{3}f(y_{0})

for certain real numbers
\omega_{3},\omega_{4}^{\prime},\omega_{4}
with

|\omega_{3}|=|\mu|
,
|\omega_{4}^{\prime}|=1
and
|\omega_{4}|=1
. Since

f(x_{0})
and
f(y_{0})
are linearly independent,
\omega_{4}=\omega(\lambda+\mu)
and

\omega(\lambda+\mu)h(\lambda)+\omega_{3}=\omega(\lambda+\mu)h(\lambda+\mu)

which implies

(5)

h(\lambda+\mu)=h(\lambda)+\frac{\omega_{3}}{\omega}.

Taking absolute values, we have

|\lambda+\mu|=\left|h(\lambda)+\frac{\omega_{3}}{\omega}\right|.

Since
|\omega_{3}/\omega|=|\mu|
, if
\mu\neq 0
, then we can divide by
|\omega_{3}/\omega|
to get

\left|\frac{\lambda}{\mu}+1\right|=\left|h(\lambda)\frac{\omega}{\omega_{3}}+1\right|.

Using that
|h(\lambda)|=|\lambda|
it easily follows

(6)

\frac{\lambda}{\mu}=h(\lambda)\frac{\omega}{\omega_{3}}.

Hence, if also
\lambda\neq 0
, then we can put together
(
5
) and (
6
) to get

h(\lambda+\mu)=h(\lambda)\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right).

Fix
0\leq\eta<1
. In this case,
\|x_{0}+\eta y_{0}\|\leq 1
. We may set
\lambda=1
and
\mu=\eta-1
in
previous equation to get
h(\eta)=\eta h(1)
. Similarly,
\lambda=-1
and
\mu=1-\eta
yield
h(-\eta)=\eta h(-1)
. Finally,
setting
\lambda=1/2
and
\mu=-1
yields
h(-1/2)=h(1/2)
,
which in turn implies
h(-1)=h(1)
. Hence
h(\mu)=\mu h(1)

holds for all
\mu\in[-1,1]
; that is,

f(x_{0}+\mu y_{0})=\omega(\mu)(f(x_{0})+\mu h(1)f(y_{0})),

where
|\omega(\mu)|=1=|h(1)|
.

By the same argument, for every
\lambda\in[-1,1]
there exist signs
\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)
and
g(1)
such that

f(\lambda x_{0}+y_{0})=\omega^{\prime}(\lambda)(\lambda g(1)f(x_{0})+f(y_{0})).

Since

\omega^{\prime}(1)(g(1)f(x_{0})+f(y_{0}))=f(x_{0}+y_{0})=\omega(1)(f(x_{0})+h(1)f(y_{0}))

we conclude that
h(1)g(1)=1
, that is,
h(1)=g(1)
.

Define a linear map
U\colon X\to Y
by
Ux_{0}=f(x_{0})
and
Uy_{0}=h(1)f(y_{0})
. We are going to
show that
f
coincides with
U|_{B_{X}}
up to a sign. Every
element of the unit ball can be written uniquely as
\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}
for certain
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose first that

|\lambda|\geq|\mu|
. If
\lambda=0
, then
f(0)=0=U(0)
, so
assume also
\lambda\neq 0
. Then
x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0}\in B_{X}
,
and therefore there exists a
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\gamma|=|\lambda|
such that

\displaystyle f(\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma f(x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma\omega(\mu/\lambda)(f(x_{0})+\mu/\lambda h(1)f(y_{0}))

\displaystyle=\gamma\omega U(x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\omega U(\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}).

Hence
f
coincides with
U
at
\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}
up to a sign

\omega\gamma/\lambda
. Similarly, if
|\mu|>|\lambda|
, then
there exists a
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\delta|=|\mu|
such
that

\displaystyle f(\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta f(\lambda/\mu x_{0}+y_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta\omega^{\prime}(\lambda/\mu)(\lambda/\mu g(1)f(x_{0})+f(y_{0}))

\displaystyle=\delta\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\mu}g(1)(\lambda f(x_{0})+\mu h(1)f(y_{0}))

\displaystyle=\delta\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\mu}g(1)U(\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}),

and again
f
coincides with
U
at
\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}
up to a
sign
\delta\omega^{\prime}/\mu g(1)
.

It only remains to check that
U
preserves the norm. An element
of
X
can be written uniquely as
\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}
.
Suppose first that
|\lambda|>|\mu|
, so as to have
\|x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0}\|\leq 1
. In this case,

\displaystyle\|\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0}\|

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0}\|

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|f(x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0})\|

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|U(x_{0}+\mu/\lambda y_{0})\|

\displaystyle=\|U(\lambda x_{0}+\mu y_{0})\|.

The case
|\mu|\geq|\lambda|
is completely analogous. This
completes the proof when
\dim X=2
.

When
\dim X=1
, fix a unit vector
x_{0}\in X
, and define a linear
map
U\colon X\to Y
by
U(\lambda x_{0})=\lambda f(x_{0})
. This map
clearly preserves the norm. For every
\lambda\in[-1,1]
,

\lambda\neq 0
, there
exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\gamma|=|\lambda|
such that

f(\lambda x_{0})=\gamma f(x_{0})=\gamma/\lambda U(\lambda x_{0}).

Hence,
f
coincides with
U
at
\lambda x_{0}
up to a sign
\gamma/\lambda
.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

The previous theorem is still true if
f\colon\delta B_{X}\to\delta B_{Y}
, for some
\delta>0
, and
f(\delta B_{X})=\delta B_{Y}
. Indeed, let
X_{\delta}
(resp.
Y_{\delta}
) be the
space
X
(resp.
Y
) with the norm scaled by
1/\delta
.
These are still smooth normed spaces satisfying
B_{X_{\delta}}=\delta B_{X}
and
B_{Y_{\delta}}=\delta B_{Y}
. Moreover, one can
easily check that the semi-inner products are related by

[x,y]_{X_{\delta}}=[x,y]_{X}/\delta^{2}
, and the same is true for
Y
and

Y_{\delta}
. Hence
f\colon B_{X_{\delta}}\to B_{Y_{\delta}}
is a
surjective mapping that preserves the semi-inner product. By
previous theorem, there exist a linear surjective isometry

U\colon X_{\delta}\to Y_{\delta}
and a map
\sigma\colon B_{X_{\delta}}\to\{-1,1\}
such that
f=\sigma U|_{B_{X_{\delta}}}
. It is then clear that
U\colon X\to Y
is still a linear
surjective isometry, and that
f=\sigma U|_{\delta B_{X}}
.

With this we can now state and prove our main theorem in this section. The proof
follows closely that of
[
58
, Theorem 10.18]
.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
E
and
F
be Banach spaces with smooth dual. An operator

T\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]
is a surjective lattice-algebra
isometry if and only if
T=\bar{V}
, for some surjective isometry

V\colon E\to F
. In particular,
E
and
F
are isometric if and
only if
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
and
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]
are lattice-algebra isometric.

Proof.

First we are going to construct a map
\phi_{T}\colon B_{F^{*}}\to B_{E^{*}}
. Let
y^{*}\in B_{F^{*}}
. Extend
y^{*}\colon F\to\mathbb{R}

to a unique contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism

\widehat{y^{*}}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]\to\mathbb{R}
. Define
\phi_{T}(y^{*})=T^{*}(\widehat{y^{*}})\circ\eta_{E}
(that is,

\widehat{\phi_{T}(y^{*})}=T^{*}(\widehat{y^{*}})
).

We will use the
lattice-algebraic homomorphisms
\hat{\iota}_{E}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to C(B_{E^{*}})
and
\hat{\iota}_{F}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]\to C(B_{F^{*}})
to express
\phi_{T}
in
another way. Note that, if
g\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]
, then
\widehat{y^{*}}(g)=\hat{\iota}_{F}(g)(y^{*})
. Indeed,
one can easily check that
g\mapsto\hat{\iota}_{F}(g)(y^{*})

is a contractive lattice-algebra homomorphism extending

y^{*}
. If
f\in\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
and
y^{*}\in B_{F^{*}}
, then

(\hat{\iota}_{E}(f)\circ\phi_{T})(y^{*})=\hat{\iota}_{E}(f)(\phi_{T}(y^{*}))=\widehat{\phi_{T}(y^{*})}(f)=T^{*}(\widehat{y^{*}})(f)=\hat{\iota}_{F}(Tf)(y^{*}).

Thus
\hat{\iota}_{F}(Tf)=\hat{\iota}_{E}(f)\circ\phi_{T}
. Plugging in
f=\eta_{E}(x)
yields
\phi_{T}(y^{*})(x)=\hat{\iota}_{F}(T\eta_{E}(x))(y^{*})
for all
y^{*}\in B_{F^{*}}
.
In particular, if
(y_{\alpha}^{*})
is a net that weak
∗

converges to
y^{*}
in
B_{F^{*}}
, then

\phi_{T}(y_{\alpha}^{*})(x)=\hat{\iota}_{F}(T\eta_{E}(x))(y_{\alpha}^{*})\to\hat{\iota}_{F}(T\eta_{E}(x))(y^{*})=\phi_{T}(y^{*})(x).

It follows that
\phi_{T}
is weak
∗
to weak
∗
continuous.
It is also direct to check that
\phi_{T}
is invertible with
\phi_{T}^{-1}=\phi_{T^{-1}}
.

Suppose that
B_{E^{*}}
and
B_{F^{*}}
are equipped with the
corresponding semi-inner products. We are going to show that
\phi_{T}
preserves the semi-inner product when restricted to

\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}
. According to
[
58
, Lemma
10.19]
, showing that
\phi_{T}
preserves the
semi-inner product is equivalent to showing that

\max_{\pm}\|\phi_{T}(x^{*})\pm\phi_{T}(y^{*})\|=\max_{\pm}\|x^{*}\pm y^{*}\|

holds for every
x^{*},y^{*}\in\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}
. Consider the
operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}S\colon&F&\longrightarrow&\ell_{1}^{2}\\
&y&\longmapsto&(x^{*}(y),y^{*}(y))\\
\end{array},

where
\ell_{1}^{2}
is equipped with the pointwise product.
It is direct to check that
\|S\|=\max_{\pm}\|x^{*}\pm y^{*}\|\leq 1
.
One can also check that its extension
\hat{S}\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[F]\to\ell_{1}^{2}
is
\hat{S}(f)=(\hat{\iota}_{F}(f)(x^{*}),\hat{\iota}_{F}(f)(y^{*}))
. Then
\hat{S}T\colon\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]\to\ell_{1}^{2}
is a lattice-algebra homomorphism with

\|\hat{S}T\|=\|\hat{S}\|
. Moreover, the universal property
of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra implies
\|\hat{S}T\eta_{E}\|=\|\hat{S}T\|
. Unfolding the definitions:

\hat{S}T(\eta_{E}(x))=(\hat{\iota}_{F}((T\eta_{E})(x))(x^{*}),\hat{\iota}_{F}((T\eta_{E})(x))(y^{*}))=(\phi_{T}(x^{*})(x),\phi_{T}(y^{*})(x)).

By direct computation,
\|\hat{S}T\eta_{E}\|=\max_{\pm}\|\phi_{T}(x^{*})\pm\phi_{T}(y^{*})\|
, and this coincides with

\|S\|=\max_{\pm}\|x^{*}\pm y^{*}\|
.

For the same reasons,
\phi_{T^{-1}}
preserves the semi-inner
product when restricted to
\frac{1}{2}B_{E^{*}}
. This implies
that

\phi_{T}|_{\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}}\colon\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}\to\frac{1}{2}B_{E^{*}}
is surjective.
Hence we can apply
7.1
and the remark after it to
this map: there exists a linear surjective isometry

U\colon F^{*}\to E^{*}
and a map
\sigma\colon\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}\to\{-1,1\}
such that
\phi_{T}=\sigma U|_{\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}}
. We claim that

\sigma
is continuous on
\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}\setminus\{0\}
for the norm
topology. Suppose it was not. Then there would exist a convergent
sequence
y_{k}^{*}\to y^{*}
in
\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}\setminus\{0\}
such
that
\sigma(y_{k}^{*})
does not converge to
\sigma(y^{*})
.
To fix ideas, suppose
\sigma(y^{*})=-1
(the other case is
identical). Then there exists a subsequence of
y_{k}^{*}
, which we
denote the same way, for which
\sigma(y_{k}^{*})=1
. Note that

Uy_{k}^{*}\to Uy^{*}
. At the same time, since norm convergence
implies weak
∗
convergence,
Uy_{k}^{*}=\phi_{T}(y_{k}^{*})\to\phi_{T}(y^{*})=-Uy^{*}
. Hence
Uy^{*}=0
, a contradiction with the
fact that
y^{*}\neq 0
.

When
\dim F>1
,
\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}\setminus\{0\}
is connected, and
therefore
\sigma
, being continuous, must be constant. Absorbing

\sigma
in
U
, we have
\phi_{T}=U|_{\frac{1}{2}B_{F^{*}}}
, and therefore
that
U
is weak
∗
continuous on bounded sets. This implies that

U
is an adjoint operator; its pre-adjoint
V
is an isometry
such that
T=\bar{V}
(the details are the same as in the end of the
proof of
[
58
, Theorem 10.18]
). When
\dim F=1
, but
\dim E>1
, we can use the same argument with
\phi_{T^{-1}}
.

It only remains to consider the case
\dim E=\dim F=1
. Recall
from
4.16
that, in this case,
\operatorname{FB{\it f}\!A}[E]
is isomorphic
to

C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})
with the supremum norm, pointwise
lattice structure, and product

(f\star g)(r,u)=rf(r,u)g(r,u)

for every
f,g\in C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})
and
(r,u)\in[0,1]\times\{-1,1\}
. The canonical map
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\to C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})
is determined by
\eta(1)(r,u)=u
. Suppose

T\colon C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})\to C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})

is a surjective lattice-algebra isometry. Being a lattice
isometry, it must be of the form
Tf=f\circ\phi
, where
\phi\colon[0,1]\times\{-1,1\}\to[0,1]\times\{-1,1\}
is a
homeomorphism (
[
1
, Theorem 4.25]
).
Put
\phi(r,u)=(\phi_{1}(r,u),\phi_{2}(r,u))
.
Since
T
is also an algebra homomorphism,

T(fg)(r,u)=\phi_{1}(r,u)f(\phi(r,u))g(\phi(r,u))

must be equal to

(TfTg)(r,u)=rf(\phi(r,u))g(\phi(r,u))

for every
f,g\in C([0,1]\times\{-1,1\})
. Thus
\phi_{1}(r,u)=r
. Being
\phi_{2}
continuous, it
must be constant on connected components. That is, there exists

\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
such that
\phi_{2}(r,u)=\sigma u
. The isometry

\begin{array}[]{cccc}V\colon&\mathbb{R}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&x&\longmapsto&\sigma x\\
\end{array}

is such that

T(\eta_{x})(r,u)=\eta_{x}(\phi(r,u))=x\phi_{2}(r,u)=x\sigma u=\eta_{Vx}(r,u).

Hence
T=\bar{V}
, as wanted.
∎

8.
Free Banach
f
-algebras with identity

In this final section we discuss free objects in the category of
(Archimedean, normed, Banach)
f\!
-algebras with an algebraic identity. We
always assume that the homomorphisms in these categories preserve
the identity.

First recall (for instance, from
[
39
]
) some general facts about an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
A
with identity
1
:

(i)

1\geq 0
,

(ii)

1
is a weak order unit,

(iii)

A
is semiprime,

(iv)

A
is lattice-algebra isomorphic to
\operatorname{Orth}(A)
.

From
(i)
it follows that, if
A
is a Banach
f\!
-algebra with
identity, it can always be renormed so as to make the identity have
norm one (
[
67
, Theorem 2.2]
). From now on,
we shall assume that Banach
f\!
-algebras have identities of norm one.
In this case, every Banach
f\!
-algebra is lattice-algebra
isometric to
C(K)
, for some compact Hausdorff space
K
(see

[
57
, Theorem 3.4]
). Hence, the category of Banach

f\!
-algebras with identity is precisely that of
C(K)
-spaces, where the
morphisms are lattice-algebra homomorphisms preserving the
algebraic identity. This, in turn, is the category of AM-spaces with
unit whose morphisms are the lattice homomorphisms that preserve the
norming unit of the AM-space.

Let us now define precisely the object we are trying to construct.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
E
be a vector space. The
free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity generated by
E
is an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]
with identity
1
together with a
linear map
\delta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]
such that, for every
Archimedean

f\!
-algebra
A
with identity
1_{A}
and every linear map
T\colon E\to A
, there exists a
unique lattice-algebra homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]\to A

satisfying
\hat{T}(1)=1_{A}
and
\hat{T}\delta_{E}=T
.

To construct the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra we used LLA expressions.
These were formal expressions obtained from finitely many formal
variables using: a 0-ary operation
0
, a unary operation
\lambda

for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and three binary operations
+
,
\vee

and
\cdot
. If we add an additional 0-ary operation
1
, we obtain
expressions that can be evaluated at any vector lattice algebra with
identity. These we shall call
1
-LLA expressions
.

The following is a simple consequence of the representation theorem by
Henriksen and Johnson. The proof is essentially the same as that of

2.3
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\Phi
be a
1
-LLA expression. If
\Phi
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
,
then it also vanishes on every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity.

With this result, the construction of
\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]
follows the same
steps as that of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}[E]
presented in
4.6
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
E
be a vector space. The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with
identity generated by
E
is

\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]=\operatorname{VLA}(\{\,\delta_{x}:x\in E\,\}\cup\{1\})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{E^{\#}},

where
1(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in E^{\#}
, together with
the map
\delta_{E}\colon E\to\operatorname{FAFA}^{1}[E]
,
\delta_{E}(x)=\delta_{x}
.

At this point we could proceed as in
Section

4.1
to obtain an abstract
construction of the free Banach
f\!
-algebra with identity. But, as noted above,
this object is the same as the free AM-space with unit, which was
already considered in
[
45
, Theorem 5.4]
.

Theorem 8.4

(
[
45
, Theorem 5.4]
)
.

Let
E
be a Banach space. Let
\phi_{E}\colon E\to C(B_{E^{*}})

be the natural embedding. For every compact Hausdorff space
K

and every norm one operator
T\colon E\to C(K)
there exists a
unique lattice homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon C(B_{E^{*}})\to C(K)

satisfying
\hat{T}\circ\phi_{E}=T
and

\hat{T}\mathbb{1}_{B_{E^{*}}}=\mathbb{1}_{K}
. Moreover,
\|\hat{T}\|=1

and
\hat{T}
is an algebra homomorphism.

The free Banach
f\!
-algebra with identity generated by
E
is not only
contained in
C(B_{E^{*}})
, but is in fact the whole
C(B_{E^{*}})
,
and the free norm is the supremum norm. Note also that, if we want to
preserve the norm, we can only extend norm one operators, since every
extension has norm one (because it sends the identity to the
identity).
