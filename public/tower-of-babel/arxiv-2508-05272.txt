Conditional validity and a fast approximation formula of full conformal prediction sets
Nicolai Amann

Abstract
Prediction sets based on full conformal prediction have seen an increasing interest in statistical learning due to their universal marginal coverage guarantees. However, practitioners have refrained from using it in applications for two reasons: Firstly, it comes at very high computational costs, exceeding even that of cross-validation. Secondly, an applicant is typically not interested in a marginal coverage guarantee which averages over all possible (but not available) training data sets, but rather in a guarantee conditional on the specific training data. This work tackles these problems by, firstly, showing that full conformal prediction sets are conditionally conservative given the training data if the conformity score is stochastically bounded and satisfies a stability condition. Secondly, we propose an approximation for the full conformal prediction set that has asymptotically the same training conditional coverage as full conformal prediction under the stability assumption derived before, and can be computed more easily. Furthermore, we show that under the stability assumption, n n -fold cross-conformal prediction also has the same asymptotic training conditional coverage guarantees as full conformal prediction. If the conformity score is defined as the out-of-sample prediction error, our approximation of the full conformal set coincides with the symmetrized Jackknife. We conclude that for this conformity score, if based on a stable prediction algorithm, full-conformal, n n -fold cross-conformal, the Jackknife+, our approximation formula, and hence also the Jackknife, all yield the same asymptotic training conditional coverage guarantees.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

In the past years, uncertainty quantification and predictive inference have seen an increased interest since in modern data science one is often confronted with a high-dimensional framework, for which many of the classic results established in statistics no longer hold. Prediction intervals based on fitted errors or even the bootstrap may fail if the number of explanatory variables is not negligible compared to the number of observations (cf.
Mammen 1996
,
Bickel & Freedman 1983
,
El & Purdom 2018
).
One possible approach is given by
k
-fold cross-validation, its special case, the Jackknife, and its variant CV+, which was recently introduced by
Barber et al. 2021
.
However, these procedures may not even provide marginally valid prediction intervals in general: In fact,
Barber et al. 2021
showed the existence of non-stable algorithms such that the marginal coverage probability of the Jackknife and Jackknife+ intervals is essentially
0
and
1-2\alpha
for the Jackknife+ (where
1-\alpha
was the target).

An alternative is given by conformal prediction, which provides a universal marginal coverage guarantee as long as the data are exchangeable (cf.
Vovk et al. 2005
). While the split conformal method lacks efficiency because the predictor is trained only on a subset of the data (cf.
Steinberger & Leeb 2023
for a comparison with the Jackknife), a different picture arises for full conformal prediction, which uses all available training data in the fitting and calibration step.
However, practitioners have refrained from using the latter method for two reasons: Firstly, the prediction sets are computationally very expensive, even exceeding the Jackknife’s computational costs. Secondly, a practitioner is typically interested in a conditional coverage guarantee given its training data rather than a marginal coverage guarantee, which averages over all possible (but unavailable) training data sets.

Bian & Barber 2023
showed that full conformal prediction is not training conditionally conservative for all conformity scores.
Given this negative result, we might be willing to restrict our analysis of the training conditionally conservativeness of full conformal prediction sets to a large class of conformity scores of practical interest.

Another alternative is cross-conformal prediction, which typically comes with lower computational costs than full conformal prediction. However, due to its similarity to the Jackknife+ for one specific conformity score, the negative results for the coverage probabilities of the Jackknife+ immediately carry over to cross-conformal prediction (cf. Appendix B.2.1 in
Barber et al. 2021
). It is therefore an open question whether cross-conformal prediction might be training conditionally conservative/valid for, at least, some typical conformity scores of interest.

1.2
Contributions

This work tackles the two significant challenges of full conformal prediction: Firstly, we show that prediction sets based on full conformal prediction are training conditionally conservative in large samples if the conformity score is stochastically bounded and satisfies a stability condition (cf.
Theorem

3.5
). Secondly, we propose an approximation of full conformal prediction sets that satisfies the same asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees as the original version given the stability condition (cf.
Theorem

4.2
). This new variant has lower computational costs since the quantiles need not be recalculated for every
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, we can also show that under some regularity assumptions, the expected length of the set difference between these two methods vanishes asymptotically (cf.
Proposition

A.3
). Thus, the two prediction sets are closely related despite their different computational costs.

We demonstrate its usefulness by applying it to two typical conformity scores:
Suppose the conformity score is a unimodal absolute in-sample prediction error. In that case, the shortcut formula can be approximated up to a deviation of
\varepsilon
by a bisection algorithm that needs
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon))
steps (cf.
Algorithm

3
and
Proposition

B.1
). Furthermore, we show that for some particular prediction errors, the shortcut formula needs no model refits at all, thereby having a computational efficiency comparable to sample splitting.
If the conformity score is the absolute out-of-sample prediction error, our approximation formula coincides with the Jackknife, which provides a connection between the Jackknife and full-conformal prediction (cf.
Corollary

4.8
).

Besides introducing a shortcut formula for full conformal prediction, this work comes with some additional results that might be interesting for their own sake.
We show that the actual training conditional coverage probability of full conformal prediction sets also avoids
overshooting
the nominal level if the conformity score is stable and fulfills a continuity assumption (cf.
Theorem

3.9
).
Furthermore, we extend our results to
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction sets by showing that they have the same asymptotic training conditional coverage guarantees as full conformal if the underlying conformity score is stable, thereby providing another computationally attractive alternative to full conformal prediction (cf.
Corollary

5.3
).
However, since the computational costs of our approximation formula are not larger than those of
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction in general, and substantially lower for some special cases, we consider the computational benefits of
n
-fold cross-conformal to be somewhat limited.

As a side result, we conclude that, if the conformity score is based on the out-of-sample prediction error of a stable algorithm, full-conformal prediction also provides the same asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees as
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction, the Jackknife, and the Jackknife+ (cf.
Corollary

5.4
). Furthermore, we give a weaker version of this result for the conformity score based on the in-sample prediction error of a stable algorithm under the additional assumption that the distribution of the feature vector is nonatomic (cf.
Theorem

A.5
).

2.2
The goal

The

of this paper is to create a prediction set

Ideally, we want our prediction set to be training conditionally valid in large samples, that is,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS_{\alpha}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\right|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0\text{ for all }\alpha\in[0,1].

(1)

However, even for large samples, the coverage probability of any non-randomized prediction set may not be close to its target level for every

if the distribution of

is non-continuous. For example, if

is Dirac-distributed, no other coverage probability than

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS_{\alpha}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)>\alpha+\varepsilon_{n}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{n}

(2)

where
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a null-sequence,
and a guarantee of the form (
1
) for distributions fulfilling a continuity condition.

We emphasize that the coverage guarantees should hold conditionally on the training data rather than the new feature vector
x_{n+1}
.

1

In the following, the terms

Our

3
Full conformal prediction

The prediction sets we are considering are based on full conformal prediction. To define them, we need the concept of a conformity score and augmented data.

For each
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
let the conformity score
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{m}:\mathcal{Z}_{n}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

Note that we here use the original definition of the conformity score

Example 3.2
.

For

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{in}((y,x),\mathcal{T}_{n})=|y-\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}(x,\mathcal{T}_{n}\cup(y,x))|.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{out}((y,x),\mathcal{T}_{n})=|y-\mathcal{A}_{n,n}(x,\mathcal{T}_{n})|.

For any family of conformity scores

Definition 3.4

We call a family of conformity scores

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}_{n}(t_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathcal{C}_{n-1}(t_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash n})|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Assume

we extend the results of

to the case of general stable conformity scores and, additionally, show that the prediction sets are not only conditionally conservative in large samples but also do not overshoot the nominal level under a continuity assumption (cf.
Theorem

3.9
). In particular, the latter statement has been an open question even for the special case treated in
Liang & Barber 2025
.

4
A shortcut formula

As shown in
Section

3
, full conformal prediction sets are conditionally conservative in large samples if based on stable and stochastically bounded conformity scores. Given the negative result of
Bian & Barber 2023
(cf. Theorem
2
therein), we cannot hope that these conditional coverage guarantees can be extended to every arbitrary, potentially non-stable, conformity score. Thus, we will restrict our further analysis to stable conformity scores.

Nevertheless,
Section

3
does not solve the main problem arising in practice: Since the model has to be refitted for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}
, it comes with high computational costs.
As we will see in
Section

4.1.3
and
Section

4.2
, the main challenge for several typical conformity scores lies in calculating
\hat{F}_{y}
while the calculation of the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})
can often be done efficiently by exploiting the structure of the conformity score. In such a scenario, we propose the following modification of full-conformal prediction sets:

Definition 4.1
.

For a
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
we define the
\delta
-distorted shortcut prediction sets based on the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}
as

\displaystyle PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}:\mathcal{C}_{n}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})\leq\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta\},

where
\hat{G}
is the empirical distribution function (ecdf) of the non-augmented conformity scores
(\mathcal{C}_{n-1}(t_{i},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i}))_{i=1}^{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{G}(t)=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{[\mathcal{C}_{n-1}(t_{i},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i}),\infty)}(t).

The main advantage of using the modification lies in the fact that the function
\hat{G}
is independent of the point
y
and therefore needs to be computed only once.
We stress that now
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
violates the invariance principle of full conformal and therefore might not be marginally valid for
any
distribution and conformity score. However, in practice this marginal coverage guarantee might be useless for the statistician if the prediction set is not conditionally valid (at least in large samples) as in such a setting the actual coverage probability for the available training data can essentially be
0
(and the probability of getting such a training set is approximately
\alpha>0
).

We showed in
Section

3
that stable conformity scores come with a conditional coverage guarantee in large samples (under the minor additional assumption of the stochastic boundedness of the conformity score).
In such a setting, the shortcut formula of
Definition

4.1
possesses the same conditional coverage guarantee in large samples as the following result shows.

Theorem 4.2

(Asymptotic equivalence between the shortcut and the original full conformal sets)
.

Assume
\mathfrak{C}
is stable.

(i)

General case:
Then, the following statements are equivalent:

G1)

For all
\delta>0
, the
\delta
-inflated full conformal prediction sets are asymptotically uniformly conditionally conservative, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\geq\varepsilon\right)=0\text{ for all }\varepsilon>0.

G2)

For all
\delta>0
the
\delta
-inflated shortcut sets
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
as in
Definition

4.1
are asymptotically uniformly conditionally conservative, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\geq\varepsilon\right)=0\text{ for all }\varepsilon>0.

(ii)

Continuous case:
If, additionally, the continuous case assumption CC is fulfilled, then the following statements are equivalent:

C1)

The original, non-inflated full conformal prediction sets are
asymptotically uniformly conditionally valid, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\right|\right)=0.

C2)

The non-inflated shortcut sets
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)
are
asymptotically uniformly conditionally valid, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\right|\right)=0.

We will abbreviate the asymptotic equivalence results of
Theorem

4.2
by simply writing
PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)\leftrightharpoons PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)
and use this notation in
Section

5
. In fact, a slightly stronger equivalence statement than
Theorem

4.2
can be made, which, under some regularity conditions, imply that the expected length of the set difference between
PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)
and
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)
vanishes asymptotically (cf.
Proposition

A.3
for the full statement).

Combining
Theorem

3.5
and
Theorem

3.9
with
Theorem

4.2
immediately gives the following result:

Corollary 4.3

(Asymptotic conservativeness/validity of the shortcut sets)
.

Assume
\mathfrak{C}
is stable and
(\mathcal{C}_{n}(t_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is stochastically bounded.

(i)

General case:
Then, for any
\delta>0
the
\delta
-inflated shortcut prediction sets
are asymptotically uniformly conditionally conservative, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\geq\varepsilon\right)=0\text{ for all }\varepsilon>0.

(ii)

Continuous case:
If, additionally, the continuous case assumption CC is fulfilled, then the non-inflated shortcut prediction sets are
asymptotically uniformly conditionally valid, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(0)\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)-\alpha\right|\right)=0.

To sum it up, we have shown that for stable conformity scores, the asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees of the shortcut formula coincide with the ones for the original full conformal prediction sets. Thus, in practice, one can use the shortcut formula for stable conformity scores due to its computational advantages compared to full conformal prediction without losing the conditional coverage guarantees in large samples.

In particular,
Corollary

4.3
shows that for all
\delta>0
the
\delta
-inflated shortcut sets based on stable conformity scores
\mathfrak{C}
are asymptotically marginally conservative, that is, for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(y_{n+1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta))\leq\alpha.

Furthermore, in the continuous case, this statement remains true for the non-inflated shortcut sets.

In the following, we demonstrate the computational advantages of the shortcut formula for several typical conformity scores.

4.1
In-sample prediction error

We start with the conformity score
\mathcal{C}^{in}_{n}
defined as the absolute in-sample prediction error based on a prediction algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and assume for simplicity
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\mathbb{R}
throughout this section.
Then,
\hat{G}
is the ecdf of the
fitted
errors, and therefore
\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)
is simply the
\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil
-th smallest fitted error.
In this scenario, the quantile
\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)
can be computed efficiently, since the model has to be fitted only once. Thus, it remains to calculate the in-sample prediction error
|y-\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n}\cup(y,x_{n+1}))|
for different values of
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}
.

4.1.1
Affine predictors

Assume the prediction algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is affine in the feature vector
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},y)
, that is,
\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{y})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})^{T}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},y)+g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}),

for measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)p}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then, our shortcut formula can be computed without any refitting:
To see this, note that the conformity score can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{in}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})=|y\underbrace{(1-f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})^{T}e_{n+1})}_{=:a(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}-\underbrace{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},0)^{T}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}_{=:b(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})}|.

We then have

\displaystyle PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\left[b(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})\pm\dfrac{\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta}{a(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})}\right]&\text{if }a(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})\neq 0\\
\mathcal{Y}_{n}&\!\begin{aligned} &\text{if }a(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})=0\text{ and }\\
&|b(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})|\leq\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta\end{aligned}\\
\emptyset&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Since the model need not be refitted for the calculation of
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, it comes with similar computational costs as sample splitting.

Example 4.4
.

Typical examples of predictors that are linear in the feature vector are the Ridge and the OLS.

4.1.2
kNN

For the
k
-th nearest neighbor algorithm with
k\geq 2
, we can do a similar trick:
If the distribution of
x_{n+1}
is nonatomic, then the in-sample prediction error of the
k
-th nearest neighbor prediction coincides with the (scaled) out-of-sample prediction error of the
(k-1)
-th nearest neighbor prediction almost surely:

\displaystyle\left|y-\mathcal{A}^{kNN}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{y})\right|=\frac{k-1}{k}\left|y-\mathcal{A}^{(k-1)NN}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})\right|,

where
\mathcal{A}^{(k-1)NN}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})
denotes the
(k-1)
-th nearest neighbor (out-of-sample) prediction for
x_{n+1}
based on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)=\left[\mathcal{A}^{(k-1)NN}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})\pm\frac{k}{k-1}\left(\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta\right)\right]\text{ a.s.}

Furthermore, note that the conformity score is stable whenever
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\frac{k}{n}=0
.

4.1.3
Unimodal conformity scores

This section demonstrates the usefulness of the new shortcut formula by providing an explicit, exponentially fast algorithm for unimodal conformity scores, in the sense that it can compute
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
up to a deviation of
\varepsilon
in
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon))
steps.

Definition 4.5
.

We call a conformity score unimodal, if for almost all
x_{n+1}
and training data
\mathcal{T}_{n}
there exists a point
m=m(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
such that the function
y\mapsto\mathcal{C}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})
is strictly decreasing on
(-\infty,m]
and strictly increasing on
[m,\infty)
.

Example 4.6
.

A conformity score is unimodal whenever the function
y\mapsto\mathcal{C}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})
is strictly convex and fulfills
\lim_{|y|\to\infty}\mathcal{C}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})=\infty
for almost all
x_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

The unimodality condition not only ensures that the prediction set of the shortcut method is a (possibly unbounded) interval, but more importantly, allows an exponentially fast approximation of it with a bisection algorithm. Starting with one point
m
inside, one point
L
left (but outside), and one point
U
right (but outside) the prediction set allows to approximate the interval exponentially fast by halving the space between the inner point and the outer point in each step (see
Algorithm

1
in
Appendix

B
). However, this approach assumes that one can find these three points
L,m,U
.
Algorithm

3
in
Appendix

B
provides a procedure that includes this preliminary step without losing its computational efficiency (cf.
Proposition

B.1
for a precise statement).

4.2
Out-of-sample conformity score

The shortcut formula of
definition

4.1
not only provides a fast approximation for unimodal conformity scores, but also shows a connection to the Jackknife:
To see this, we consider the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{out}
based on the absolute out-of-sample prediction error of an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.
In this case, the values
\mathcal{C}(t_{i},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i})
coincide with the absolute value of the leave-one-out residuals
|\hat{u}_{i}|:=|y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(x_{i},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i})|
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
Thus,
\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)
is just the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the ecdf of the absolute leave-one-out residuals. To sum it up, in this special case, the shortcut formula of
Definition

4.1
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}:|y-\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})|\leq\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})+[-\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\delta,\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)+\delta].

This is just the formula for the
\delta
-inflated prediction interval of the symmetrized Jackknife (cf.
Barber et al. 2021
,
Amann et al. 2025
).
Before summarizing these findings, we need the definition of asymptotic out-of-sample stable prediction algorithms.

Definition 4.7
.

We call a prediction algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that is symmetric with respect to a permutation of the training data
6
6

6

A prediction algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is called symmetric if it does not change by a permutation of the training data, that is, if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, all training data
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
and every permutation
\pi:\{1,\ldots,n\}
it fulfills
\mathcal{A}(x,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))=\mathcal{A}(x,(t_{\pi(1)},\ldots,t_{\pi(n)}))
.

asymptotically out-of-sample stable (cf.
Amann et al. 2025
) if it fulfills

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n,n}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{n,n-1}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash n})\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Corollary 4.8
.

For the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{out}
, the shortcut formula coincides with the symmetric Jackknife.
If the symmetric prediction algorithm used for the Jackknife is asymptotically out-of-sample stable as in
Definition

4.7
,
then
\delta
-inflated prediction intervals based on the symmetrized Jackknife are asymptotically conditionally equivalent to full conformal prediction sets based on the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{out}
in the sense of
Theorem

4.2
.

Remark 4.9
.

Assume the conditions of
Corollary

4.8
hold and the prediction error
y_{n+1}-\mathcal{A}_{n,n}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{T}_{n})
is bounded in probability.
Then the assumptions of
Corollary

4.3
are fulfilled. Therefore, the symmetrized Jackknife is asymptotically uniformly conditionally conservative in the general case and asymptotically uniformly conditionally valid in the continuous case. This result follows from the fact that
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
coincides with the symmetrized Jackknife prediction intervals and is in line with the literature (cf. Proposition B.1 of
Amann et al. 2025
).

Discussion

5.1
Cross-conformal prediction

Another computationally beneficial alternative to full conformal prediction is cross-conformal prediction. We here investigate its conditional coverage probability in large samples.

Definition 5.1

(
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction sets)
.

For
\delta\geq 0
we define the
\delta
-inflated
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction set based on the training data
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and a new feature vector
x_{n+1}
as

\displaystyle PS^{\text{cc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)=\left\{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}:1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{\mathcal{C}_{n}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i})\leq\mathcal{C}_{n-1}(t_{i},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\backslash i})+\delta\}>\alpha(n+1)\right\}.

(8)

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume
\mathfrak{C}
is stable. Then, prediction sets based on
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction are asymptotically equivalent to the shortcut sets in the sense that
Theorem

4.2
holds with
PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)
replaced by
PS^{\text{cc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)
.

Combining
Proposition

5.2
with
Theorem

4.2
immediately implies the following result.

Corollary 5.3

(Equivalence of cross-conformal and full conformal under stability)
.

Assume
\mathfrak{C}
is stable. Then, prediction sets based on
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction are asymptotically equivalent to full conformal prediction sets in the sense that
Theorem

4.2
holds with
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)
replaced by
PS^{\text{cc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)
.

If the conformity score is bounded in probability, then
\delta
-inflated prediction sets based on cross-conformal prediction are asymptotically uniformly conditionally conservative for
\delta>0
in the sense of
Theorem

3.5
. Furthermore, under the additional Assumption CC, the non-inflated cross-conformal prediction sets are asymptotically uniformly conditionally valid in the sense of
Theorem

3.9
.

Corollary

5.3
shows that
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction comes with the same asymptotic coverage guarantees as full conformal prediction when based on stable conformity scores and is therefore another computationally advantageous alternative to full conformal prediction.
However, the major advantage of our shortcut formula is that it needs no more model refits than
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction in general and can be approximated much more efficiently for unimodal conformity scores (cf.
Proposition

B.1
).

5.2
Asymptotic equivalence under stability

We summarize our findings combined with existing results for the Jackknife versions in the following statement.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
PS^{1}(\cdot)\leftrightharpoons PS^{2}(\cdot)
denote the asymptotic equivalence between two methods of creating prediction sets
PS^{1}(\cdot)
and
PS^{2}(\cdot)
in the sense of

4.2
.

a)

If
\mathfrak{C}
is stable, we have

\displaystyle PS^{\text{fc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)\leftrightharpoons PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot)\leftrightharpoons PS^{\text{cc}}_{n,\cdot}(\cdot),

denotes the
n
-fold cross-conformal method.

(

\mathcal{A}

, we have

\displaystyle PI^{sJ}_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)\leftrightharpoons PI^{sJ+}_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot),

where
PI^{sJ}_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)
and

denote prediction sets based on the symmetric Jackknife and the symmetric Jackknife+, respectively, with prediction algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.

Assume the conformity score

5.3
Comparison with the literature

A similar version of
Theorem

3.5
has appeared in the literature.
In fact,
Liang & Barber 2025
showed that
\delta
-inflated full conformal prediction sets (with
\delta>0
) are asymptotically conditionally conservative for
fixed

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, if the conformity score
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{in}
is used (cf. Theorem
3.7
therein).

Theorem

3.5
is a generalization in several ways:
Firstly, the special case of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{in}
is contained in our more general framework, allowing for a huge class of conformity scores.
8
8

8

Note that our stability condition requires
|y_{n+1}-\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1})|-|y_{n+1}-\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash n})|
to converge to
0
in this special case. A sufficient condition for this is
|\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1})-\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash n})|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0
. Thus, our stability condition is comparable to, but slightly weaker than, the one in
Liang & Barber 2025
.

Secondly, we can prove a stronger coverage guarantee which holds uniformly over all possible
\alpha
rather than pointwise, which allows to choose
\alpha
depending on the training data at the minor cost of additionally assuming a stochastically bounded conformity score.
Thirdly, we can show that the non-inflated prediction sets are asymptotically conditionally valid in the continuous case, while
Liang & Barber 2025
focused on the case of
\delta
-inflated prediction sets only (with
\delta>0
).

Furthermore,
Ndiaye 2022
proposed a shortcut formula for full conformal prediction sets that is slightly different from ours.
However, to provide valid coverage guarantees for the shortcut formulas provided therein, the conformity scores must fulfill a worst-case stability condition, which implies that out-of-sample prediction errors can be accurately approximated by in-sample prediction errors. Under this condition, even the naive prediction interval based on fitted errors is conditionally conservative in large samples (cf. Proposition B.
2
in
Amann et al. 2025
). However, this worst-case stability condition typically fails to hold in the high-dimensional regime where the dimension of the feature vector
p
is not negligible compared to the number of observations
n
, which is a common setting in data science.

6
Summary

The present paper shows that inflated full conformal prediction sets are asymptotically conditionally conservative if the sequence of conformity scores is stable and bounded in probability. Our results are based on finite-sample PAC bounds that make no assumption about the distribution
P_{n}
of the feature-response pair.
In the asymptotic framework, we
allow
P_{n}
to depend on
n
and therefore include the challenging high-dimensional framework as a special case.
Furthermore, we showed that under a continuity assumption, the non-inflated full conformal prediction sets are even asymptotically conditionally valid, i.e., their training conditional coverage probability converges to the nominal level in expectation rather than just not undershooting it.

Furthermore, we propose a shortcut formula for full conformal prediction sets that provides the same asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees for stable conformity scores. We demonstrate the computational advantages of the shortcut formula for the conformity score defined as the absolute in-sample prediction error for several cases of interest. If the conformity score is defined as the absolute out-of-sample prediction error, it coincides with the symmetrized Jackknife, thereby providing a connection between the Jackknife and full conformal prediction. Additionally, we showed that for general stable conformity scores,
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction sets satisfy the same asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees as full conformal prediction.
Thus, if the conformity score is defined as the absolute out-of-sample prediction error based on a stable prediction algorithm, full conformal prediction,
n
-fold cross-conformal prediction, the Jackknife, and the Jackknife+ satisfy the same conditional coverage probabilities in large samples.

The Lévy gauge

Our proofs heavily rely on the Lévy gauge, a technique which was introduced in
Amann et al. 2025
. For convenience, we will repeat the definition and provide some properties we will use in this section.

Definition C.1

(Lévy gauge)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
and
G:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be cumulative distribution functions and
\delta\geq 0
.
We then define the Lévy gauge between
F
and
G
with tolerance parameter
\delta
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)=\inf\{\varepsilon\geq 0:F(t-\delta)-\varepsilon\leq G(t)\leq F(t+\delta)+\varepsilon\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(9)

For comparison, we present the definition of the Lévy metric
L(F,G)
between
F
and
G
:

\displaystyle L(F,G)=\inf\{\varepsilon\geq 0:F(t-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\leq G(t)\leq F(t+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

The Lévy gauge uses an externally chosen tolerance level
\delta\geq 0
while the amount of shifting of
t
is only internally given in the definition of the Lévy metric.
In the next lemma, we list some properties of the Lévy gauge:

Lemma C.2

(Lemma C.2 of
Amann et al. 2025
)
.

Let
F
and
G
be cumulative distribution functions and
\delta\geq 0
.
Then the following results hold:

a)

The infimum in
equation

9
is attained. In particular, this yields

\displaystyle F(t-\delta)-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)\leq G(t)\leq F(t+\delta)+\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

b)

The Lévy gauge is symmetric
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)=\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(G,F)
.

c)

The function
\delta\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)
is nonincreasing and continuous from the right on
[0,\infty)
.
In particular, we have
0\leq\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)\leq\mathcal{L}_{0}(F,G)=\|F-G\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

d)

The Lévy gauge can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\max(F(t)-G(t+\delta),G(t)-F(t+\delta)).

(11)

e)

Let
H
be another distribution function and
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
We then have the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta+\varepsilon}(F,H)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)+\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(G,H).

(12)

f)

The Lévy gauge is connected to the Lévy metric as follows:

\displaystyle\min(\delta,\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G))\leq L(F,G)\leq\max(\delta,\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G))\text{ for every }\delta\geq 0.

(13)

g)

c>0

and

Then the Lévy gauge fulfills the following scaling invariance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(F,G)=\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta}{c}}(F(c\cdot),G(c\cdot)),

(14)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}{\,\mathbb{E}}(F^{2}-Fk_{i})+\dfrac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j:i\neq j}{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}(k_{j}-F)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}E(k_{i}^{2}-k_{i}F).

We will deal with the three terms in the last equality separately.
Starting with the first one, we stress the fact that

{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}F)={\,\mathbb{E}}(\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)F)={\,\mathbb{E}}(F^{2})
.
Now, let

. We then have

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,n+1}k_{n+1,i})

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(k_{i,n+1}\|\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash i,\tilde{n+1}\leftrightarrow n+1}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)\right).

We would like to emphasize that both

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(Fk_{n+1})={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)+{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)\right).

We claim that the last summand can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)\right))

(20)

\displaystyle\leq{\,\mathbb{E}}(M\left|\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)\right|)\leq M{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{n+1}-k_{n+1,i}|)=M\Delta.

\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)

t_{n+1}
and

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right))

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right)),

where we exchanged
t_{i}
with
t_{n_{1}}
in the first equality and replaced
t_{n+1}
by an i.i.d. copy
\tilde{t}_{n+1}
in the second equality.
With the same argument as in
equation

20

we have

\displaystyle\left|{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,i}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{i}\leftrightarrow i}\right))-{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))\right|\leq M\Delta.

Putting the pieces together, we end up with

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(F^{2})\leq 2M\Delta+{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))=2M\Delta+{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}F),

(21)

where we used the fact that
F
is independent of
t_{n+1}
in the last equality.

We now turn to
{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}(k_{j}-F)
for

.
For this, we again use the argumentation as in

20
to conclude that

\displaystyle\left|{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,j}k_{j})-{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}k_{j})\right|\leq M\Delta.

Furthermore, we observe that

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,j}k_{j})={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{j}\|\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash j}\right)={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{j}\|\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash j}\right)).

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{j}\|\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\backslash j}\right))={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}F).

To sum it up, we have shown that

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}k_{j})\leq M\Delta+{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i,j}k_{j})=M\Delta+{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}F).

(22)

Next, we turn to the third term and start with the case
i=n+1
.
For this, we note that for any

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}F)

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)))\geq{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,j}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{j}\leftrightarrow j}\right))-M\Delta

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{j}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1,j}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\tilde{j}\leftrightarrow j}\right))-M\Delta\geq{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{j}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right))-2M\Delta

\displaystyle={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{j}F)-2M\Delta,

where we used an inequality as in
equation

20

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}^{2}-k_{n+1}F)\leq{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{n+1}^{2})-{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{j}F)+2M\Delta={\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{j}^{2}-k_{j}F)+2M\Delta.

i<n+1
(by setting

Next, we take any

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}^{2}-k_{i}F)

\displaystyle\leq{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}\max(0,k_{i}-F))\leq M{\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{i}-F))

\displaystyle=M{\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{i,n+1}-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right),0)).

\displaystyle M{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\max(k_{i,n+1}-\mathbb{E}\left(k_{n+1}\|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right),0)\right)

\displaystyle\leq M{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\max(k_{i,n+1}-k_{n+1}),0\right)

\displaystyle\leq M{\,\mathbb{E}}\left(\max(k_{i}-k_{n+1},0)+|k_{i,n+1}-k_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq M\Delta+M{\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{i}-k_{n+1},0).

However, since the data are i.i.d. the term

has the same distribution as
k_{j}-k_{l}

\displaystyle M{\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{i}-k_{n+1},0)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{M}{2}\left({\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{i}-k_{n+1},0)+{\,\mathbb{E}}(\max(k_{n+1}-k_{n+i},0)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{M}{2}{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{i}-k_{n+1}|)=\dfrac{M}{2}{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{1}-k_{2}|).

To sum it up, we have shown that

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}(k_{i}^{2}-k_{i}F)\leq M\Delta+\dfrac{M}{2}{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{1}-k_{2}|).

Combining the inequality of the preceding display with
equation

21

equation

22
we conclude

\displaystyle{\,\mathbb{E}}((F-\hat{F})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2n}{n+1}M\Delta+\dfrac{n}{n+1}M\Delta+\dfrac{2M\Delta}{(n+1)^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{n+1}\left(M\Delta+\dfrac{M}{2}{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{1}-k_{2}|)\right)

\displaystyle=M\Delta\dfrac{3n^{2}+4n+3}{(n+1)^{2}}+\dfrac{M}{2(n+1)}{\,\mathbb{E}}(|k_{1}-k_{2}|),

which finishes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle+\dfrac{C_{2}}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}{\,\mathbb{E}}(\min(2K+3\delta,|X_{2}-Y_{2}|))

Next, we proof
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq PI
. First, we note that, due to the unimodality,
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
is a (possibly unbounded) interval and distinguish several cases:
If
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)=\emptyset
, then
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq PI
is trivially fulfilled.
If
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\supseteq[-2^{K},2^{K}]
, then
\mathcal{C}_{n}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})\leq\hat{G}^{-1}(1-\alpha)
for all
y\in[-2^{K},2^{K}]
. In particular, in
Algorithm

3
we have
\max(c_{1},c_{2})\leq b
and hence the algorithm gives
PI=\mathbb{R}
, trivially fulfilling
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq PI
.
If
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(-2^{K},\infty)
and
2^{K}\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, we have
c_{2}\leq b<c_{1}
. In this case,
Algorithm

1
computes a
u\in[-2^{K},2^{K}]
such that
u\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
. In particular, we
u<y
for all
y\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
and therefore
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq[u,\infty)=PI
.
The case
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(-\infty,2^{K})
and
-2^{K}\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
can be shown analogously.
If
\emptyset\neq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(-\infty,-2^{K})
, the conformity score is nondecreasing on
[-2^{K},2^{K}]
and therefore
m=-2^{K}
. In this case, we have
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(-\infty,-2^{K}+\varepsilon)=PI
.
The case
\emptyset\neq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(2^{K},\infty)
can be proven analogously.

It remains to deal with the case
\emptyset\neq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq[-2^{K},2^{K}]
.
If
Algorithm

2
gives the output
m=-2^{K}
, then, by the unimodality we conclude

\mathcal{C}_{n}((y,x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})>\mathcal{C}_{n}((-2^{K},x_{n+1}),\mathcal{T}_{n})>b

for all
y\geq-2^{K}+\varepsilon
. To put it in other words, we have
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(-2^{K},-2^{K}+\varepsilon)\subseteq PI=(-\infty,-2^{K}+\varepsilon)
.
The case
m=2^{K}
can be shown analogously.

Now, if
|m|<2^{K}
, we conclude that the minimum of the conformity score is indeed located in
(-2^{K},2^{K})
.
If
m\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, we also have
M\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
and, by
Algorithm

2
the minimum of the conformity score is inside the interval
(\min(m,M),\max(m,M))
. Consequently, we have
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq(\min(m,M),\max(m,M))
. Furthermore, we have
\lambda(PI\backslash PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta))\leq\lambda(PI)\leq\varepsilon
.

In the last remaining case we have
m\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq[-2^{K},2^{K}]
and
|m|<2^{K}
.
In this situation,
Algorithm

1
calculates points
l_{1}\in(-2^{K},m)
and
u_{1}\in(m,2^{K})
, such that
l_{1},u_{1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
.
Since
m\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, this shows that
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq PI
.

It remains to show that in the case
\emptyset\neq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq[-2^{K}+\varepsilon,2^{K}-\varepsilon]
we have
[L+\varepsilon,U-\varepsilon]\subseteq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)\subseteq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
.
In this case, the minimum of the conformity score is located inside of
[-2^{K}+\varepsilon,2^{K}-\varepsilon]
and therefore
|m|<2^{K}
.
In the case where
m\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, we have
\lambda(PI)\leq\varepsilon
as shown before and hence
[L+\varepsilon,U-\varepsilon]=\emptyset\subseteq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
trivially.
In the case where
m\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
, the bisection algorithm provides
l_{1}
and
l_{0}
, such that
l_{1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
and
l_{0}\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
with
l_{0}\leq l_{1}+\varepsilon
. Similarly, we have
u_{0}\in PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
and
u_{1}\notin PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
with
u_{0}\geq u_{1}-\varepsilon
.
Thus, the set
[l_{0},u_{0}]
is a subset of
PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
(even in the case
l_{0}>u_{0}
).
As a consequence we have
[L+\varepsilon,U-\varepsilon]\subseteq PS^{\text{sc}}_{n,\alpha}(\delta)
(even in the case
U-L<2\varepsilon
).
∎
