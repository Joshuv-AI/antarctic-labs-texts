Economic Disadvantage During Childhood, Obesity, and Diabetes Across Three Birth Cohorts of Older Mexicans
Authors: Jesús-Daniel Zazueta-Borboa, Rafael Samper-Ternent, Rebeca Wong, Neil Mehta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDiabetes prevalence has increased markedly in Mexico. We examined the individual and joint contributions of economic disadvantage during childhood (EDDC) and elevated body weight on diabetes prevalence in 3 cohorts of Mexican adults.MethodsData on those 60–69 years old from the 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959 birth cohorts in Waves 1 (2001), 3 (2012), and 5 (2018) of the Mexican Health and Aging Study were used. EDDC was defined as the absence of a toilet in the household before age 10. Body mass status was defined using self-reported perceived body image at age 50. Diabetes was based on respondent reports. Supplementary analyses using HbA1c as a criterion for diabetes were conducted. A regression-decomposition approach was implemented. Logistic regression models included adjustments for sociodemographic characteristics and access to medical care.ResultsDiabetes prevalence was 23% overall and 11%, 25%, and 26% in the 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959 cohorts, respectively. EDDC declined across successive cohorts, whereas the prevalence of overweight/obesity at age 50 increased. EDDC and overweight/obesity were associated with higher odds of reporting diabetes. A scenario that eliminates disadvantaged EDDC reduced diabetes prevalence by 11% in a pooled sample, while eliminating overweight/obesity reduced it by 30%. Overweight/obesity explained 42% of the rise in diabetes prevalence between the 1930–1939 and 1950–1959 cohorts. Improvement in EDDC explained 18% of the rise in diabetes prevalence between 1930–1939 and 1950–1959 cohorts.DiscussionHigh body weight across Mexican birth cohorts seemed to offset the potential benefits from improvements in childhood conditions on adult diabetes risk.

Early childhood conditions are important predictors of health and mortality at older ages, even after accounting for later-life socioeconomic status and behaviors (Beltrán-Sánchez et al., 2022; Hayward & Gorman, 2004; Mackenbach, 2012; Monteverde et al., 2020). Several pathways linking early life conditions with older-aged health have been proposed. The Barker or “fetal origins” hypothesis focuses on gestational exposures, postulating that factors such as suboptimal nutrition, stress, and unhealthy lifestyles during pregnancy alter gene expression and cellular function in the offspring, leading to insulin resistance, obesity, and hypertension in later life (Hanson et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2013). Other pathways emphasize experiences during early childhood, including those related to nutritional deprivations, exposures to environmental contaminants, and psychosocial stress. These exposures are postulated to result in epigenetic changes, modifications to the microbiome, high allostatic load, and early insulin resistance, predisposing individuals to later-life chronic conditions (D’Amico et al., 2022; Gingras et al., 2018; González-Barranco et al., 2003; Peter Memiah et al., 2022).

Numerous population-based studies have found that adverse conditions during childhood (economic disadvantage during childhood: EDDC) are associated with higher diabetes risk during older adulthood (Beltrán-Sánchez et al., 2015; Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2013). Although the full mechanisms remain unclear, adverse EDDC is associated with early pancreatic beta cell dysfunction, higher levels of insulin resistance, and epigenetic changes (Jiang et al., 2013). In Mexico, as in several other Latin American countries, there have been significant improvements in EDDC beginning in the 1940s as evidenced through rising household incomes, increased sanitation, improved childhood vaccination and nutrition programs, and expansion of schooling (Barba Solano 2015; García Guerrero, 2014). These improvements, along with more widespread social and economic advances, have contributed to the rapid rise in Mexican life expectancy that occurred during the second half of the twentieth century (García Guerrero, 2014).

Although we would expect improvements in EDDC to have a beneficial effect on diabetes risk later in life, improvements in EDDC in Mexico, as elsewhere, have been accompanied by increasing levels of obesity associated with substantial changes in diet and physical activity that accompanied economic development (Marrón-Ponce et al., 2019). Mexican birth cohorts that were the first to experience substantial improvements in EDDCs—namely, those born after 1940—also have experienced high levels of obesity throughout life. The net effect of improving EDDCs and rising obesity on diabetes and adult health more generally is not known. In this study, we evaluate the individual and joint effects of EDDC improvements and rising obesity on the prevalence of diabetes across three Mexican birth cohorts (1930s, 1940s, 1950s) who experienced significant changes in economic conditions and obesity over their lifetimes.

Improvements in Economic Disadvantage During Childhood in Mexico After 1940

During the second half of the twentieth century, a combination of economic advances and public health interventions improved living conditions for most Mexicans, resulting in substantial advances in health and nutrition among children (García Guerrero, 2014; Sepúlveda et al., 2006). One of the most important changes took place in 1943 with the creation of the national Mexican healthcare system (Moreno Jaimes & Flamand, 2016). Although the system was rolled out nationally, urban areas benefitted more than rural ones (Barba Solano 2015; Sepúlveda et al., 2006). Along with increasing the reach of modern healthcare, large-scale vaccination and nutrition campaigns were implemented in both rural and urban areas, which have been credited with reducing infant mortality throughout the country (Moreno Jaimes & Flamand, 2016; Sepúlveda et al., 2006).

Between the 1940s and 1960s, Mexico experienced significant growth in manufacturing, exports, and Gross Domestic Product (Moreno Brid & Ros, 2004), which resulted in rising household incomes and an increase in the proportion of households having access to running water and toilets. In addition, between 1959 and 1975, important federal policy reforms of education took place, aiming to increase enrollments at the primary and lower-secondary levels through an expansion of school infrastructure, an increase in teacher training, and incentives to raise school attendance. Consequently, the number of schools increased, especially in urban areas (Urbina, 2018).

The main beneficiaries of these changes were cohorts born after 1940 (Beltrán-Sánchez et al., 2022). Cohorts born in the 1930s, for example, had higher levels of EDDC, higher infant and child mortality, lower educational attainment, and less access to formal medical care compared to those born in the 1940s or later (Beltrán-Sánchez et al., 2022; Payne & Wong, 2019; Wong, Michaels-Obregón, Palloni, Gutiérrez-Robledo et al., 2015). We would expect improving EDDCs to have a positive effect on health across the life course. As in other populations, studies of Mexico have found that socioeconomic status during childhood is inversely associated with mortality after age 50 (Saenz & Wong, 2015), rates of diabetes (Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019; Gonzalez-Samano & Villarreal, 2024; Kohler & Soldo, 2005), and disability (Huang et al., 2011). Socioeconomic disadvantages during childhood have also been found to be associated with poorer self-rated health during adulthood (Ruiz-Pantoja & Ham-Chande, 2007). These findings generally hold after controlling for socioeconomic status during adulthood, an important confounder of the relationship between EDDC and later-life health (Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019).

Rising Obesity

Some of the same economic forces that led to improvements in EDDC also set the stage for rising obesity levels. In Mexico, as in many countries, economic growth has been accompanied by rising obesity (Talukdar et al., 2020). The Mexican government began monitoring the national prevalence of adult obesity in 2000 through the Mexican National Health and Nutrition Survey. In 2000, the national prevalence of obesity was estimated at 24% for the population aged 20 years and older, whereas in 2018 it was 36% (Barquera et al., 2020). Little is known about obesity trends prior to 2000; however, studies on earlier periods indicate that, in 1992, the prevalence of obesity was 21% among people aged 20–69 years (Arroyo et al., 2000). Obesity has risen in both poor and high-income regions and is widespread throughout urban and rural populations (Barquera & Rivera, 2020). Obesity levels are higher among women compared to men. The causes of this epidemic continue to be studied, but much research points to drastic changes in eating habits driven by rising incomes, increased availability of cheap high-calorie processed foods, and marketing campaigns by the food industry (Marrón-Ponce et al., 2019; Soto-Estrada et al., 2018). Sugar consumption in particular has increased dramatically: in 1990, 8% of total daily calories consumed were from sugar, compared to 15% in 2013 (Soto-Estrada et al., 2018). To date, national policy responses have been weak (Barquera & Rivera, 2020).

Not surprisingly, Mexico has experienced rising levels of chronic diseases associated with obesity. Most concerning is the rapid rise in diabetes levels. In 2000, the percentage of Mexican adults aged 50 and older with diabetes was 12.7%; by 2021, it was 24.2% (López Sánchez et al., 2022). Previous studies using data from the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) have reported an age-adjusted diabetes prevalence of 12% (2001), 16% (2003), and 19% (2015) among individuals aged 50 and older (Gonzalez-Gonzalez et al., 2017). The two leading causes of death among older men and women in Mexico in 2019—cardiovascular disease and diabetes—are both associated with obesity (INEGI, 2023a). Using vital records to identify the cause of death, one study found that diabetes rose from causing 54 deaths per 100,000 persons for men and 58 deaths per 100,000 for women in 1998, to 74 for men and 63 among women in 2019 (INEGI, 2023b).

Current Study

Although obesity and lifestyle changes have been implicated in the rise in diabetes (Barquera & Rivera, 2020), their effect relative to improved living conditions has not been evaluated. We assess the individual and joint contributions of disadvantaged EDDC and obesity to diabetes prevalence in three birth cohorts of Mexicans (those born in 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959) when the cohorts were ages 60–69 years. We provide estimates of the contributions of the two factors for (a) all cohorts jointly, (b) each birth cohort separately, and (c) the difference in diabetes prevalence across birth cohorts. We hypothesize that improvement in EDDC over time has reduced diabetes risk, but that this beneficial effect has been surpassed by the effect of rising obesity.

Method

Data

We used data from MHAS, a national, longitudinal, and household-based panel study of adults (ages 50+) and their spouses. The MHAS contains a rich set of information related to the health, economic circumstances, and well-being of older Mexicans. It is one of the “partner” studies of the U.S. Health and Retirement Study aimed at monitoring aging and health globally (https://g2aging.org/). The baseline survey was conducted in 2001 (n = 15,186). Follow-up interviews were conducted in 2003, 2012, 2015, and 2018. To refresh the sample and represent the population age 50+ in Mexico, MHAS added new participants aged 50–61 in 2012 and participants aged 50–55 in 2018. In total, 21,123 individuals participated in the study between 2001 and 2018. There were also 5,324 deaths among participants during 2001–2018. Follow-up rates were generally high in comparison with other similar surveys (Orozco Rocha et al., 2018; Rebeca Wong et al., 2015). We selected individuals aged 60–69 from three different birth cohorts (1930–1939, 1940–1949, 1950–1959) from Wave 1 (2001), Wave 3 (2012), and Wave 5 (2018), respectively, to perform a cross-sectional analysis by selected cohorts. Our pooled analytical sample size was n = 7,921. Supplementary Table S1 provides detailed information on the construction of the analytical sample and the number of direct and proxy interviews (Supplementary Table S2).

Our main analysis is based on self-reported diabetes status. Previous studies using MHAS estimate that 18% of all individuals with diabetes are undiagnosed (Kumar et al., 2016); thus, reliance on self-reported status yields an underestimation of the actual prevalence of diabetes. We performed a supplementary analysis using a 2012 subsample of MHAS respondents ages 50–79 (n = 2,086) who had their blood drawn. For this subsample, we ascertained diabetes status by hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels. We analyzed a sample of n = 1,408 respondents with HbA1c levels and complete information on the covariates used for the analysis. We were unable to investigate cohort differences in the biomarker subsample due to small sample sizes. Supplementary Table S3 provides a diagram of the sample selection for this supplementary biomarker analysis.

Measures

Diabetes status

Diabetes was measured by respondents reporting yes to a question asking whether a “doctor or medical personnel” has diagnosed them with diabetes. In the first (baseline) interview of each participant, the question asks about “ever” being diagnosed and, in the follow-up interviews, the question asks about being diagnosed “since the last” interview. We used diabetes status information at the first wave respondents reached ages 60–69 years during 2001–2018.

Covariates

EDDC was assessed by asking about the presence of a toilet in the household before age 10 years. This measure of childhood socioeconomic disadvantage has been used in previous research to identify the socioeconomic status of the household when the respondent was a child (Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019; Saenz & Wong, 2015). The measure has been associated with higher mortality and worse health outcomes at older ages (Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019; Saenz & Wong, 2015). The question was asked only during the initial wave in which a respondent joined the survey.

We use a retrospective question asking about body mass status at age 50 to ascertain past body mass status for use in the regression models. The use of an earlier-in-life measure of body mass status (i.e., pertaining to an age earlier than the age at the time of the survey) reduces the effects of reverse causation bias in the obesity-health relationship and may better capture long-term health effects of body mass relative to body mass status at the time of the survey (Mehta et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2020; Stokes & Preston, 2016). We use a question asking about body figure at age 50 years, which was asked only in 2015: “Now please think about when you were 50 years old. Which figure best represents you at that age?” Supplementary Figure S1 provides the body figures that were shown to the participants (Stunkard et al., 1983). For women, we used the following body mass status classifications: underweight (image a), normal weight (b–d), overweight (e, f), and obese (g–i). For men, the classifications were underweight (image j), normal weight (k–m), overweight (n, o), and obese (p–r). In addition to body shape status at age 50, we present descriptive statistics for standard body mass index (BMI) classifications (based on self-reported weight and height) at the time of the first survey when respondents were ages 60–69. We excluded those who were underweight in the analytical sample, given that this category represented less than 5% of the pooled sample any of the cohort samples.

We adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics including sex (female/male), age (continuous, in years), urban/rural residence, and years of formal education (0 years, 1 to 6 years, and 7 years or more). We additionally included access to medical attention (yes/no) as a covariate due to the recent increase in access to medical services in Mexico and its association with diagnosis and treatment of diabetes (Beltrán-Sánchez et al., 2015; Parker et al., 2018). We used these characteristics as reported also in the first wave in which participants were ages 60–69.

Analytical Approach

We applied a two-step procedure. First, we estimated logistic regression models predicting the probability of self-reporting diabetes as a function of EDDC, self-reported body image at age 50, and sociodemographic characteristics on the pooled sample. We estimated two models. Model 1 included a main effect for birth cohort along with the other covariates. Model 2 added to Model 1 an interaction between birth cohort and body mass status at age 50. Supplementary Table S5 presents logistic regression results for each cohort separately. Second, using the regression estimates of Model 2, we performed a counterfactual analysis to identify the proportion of self-reported diabetes prevalence attributable to EDDC, higher body mass status at age 50 (overweight/obesity), and both risk factors combined. We followed a similar approach as previous studies (Elo et al., 2017). Using the coefficients in equation (1), we predicted the prevalence of diabetes by taking the mean of the individual-level predictions using the actual levels of the covariates. This mean represents the “observed” prevalence of self-reported diabetes. Next, three counterfactual prevalence estimates were calculated. The first one sets EDDC = 0 for all respondents, which represents the elimination of EDDC from the population. The difference between the observed prevalence and this counterfactual prevalence indicates the amount of diabetes prevalence attributable to EDDC. The second counterfactual was based on assuming that everyone in the sample had normal weight status at age 50 (i.e., setting obesity and overweight to 0, given that individuals with normal weight had a lower prevalence of self-reported diabetes compared to those with overweight and obesity), and the third was based on eliminating both risk factors, EDDC and overweight/obesity at age 50, simultaneously. Given the multiplicative nature of the model (additive on the log scale), the effects observed in the first and second counterfactual did not sum to the joint effect of the two risk factors in the third counterfactual. The counterfactual exercise was conducted on the pooled sample and for each birth cohort separately, although Models 1 and 2 were estimated only on the pooled sample.

We did not include sample weights in the analysis, given that the weights in MHAS represent the population at each cross-section, but not at other points in time.

Biomarker subsample analysis

Analysis of the biomarker subsample proceeded similarly to that of the main analysis in that we implemented a logistic regression model in the form of Model 1 with diabetes status as the outcome. This analysis aimed to address how sensitive results were to the underestimation of self-reported diabetes in our analysis. Individuals ages 50–79 in the 2012 Wave were included and we did not control for cohort. We applied the same three counterfactual scenarios as described above to identify the individual and joint contributions of EDDC and body mass status to diabetes prevalence. A criterion of HbA1c >6.5 or a report of taking medication to control their diabetes was used to define diabetes status. We also used BMI at the time of survey to identify body mass status (normal, overweight, obese), because the retrospective question on body shape at age 50 years was not available for most respondents.

Results

Descriptive Results

In the pooled sample, the prevalence of self-reported diabetes was 23.0% (Table 1). The prevalence was 11.1%, 25.4%, and 26.2% in the 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959 cohorts, respectively. The share of female participants was 55.8%, and the mean age was 63.9 years; this number was similar across birth cohorts. In the pooled sample, 39.9% of individuals lived in rural areas, with similar percentage across cohorts. The share of people with 7 years or more of formal education increased across cohorts, from 16.7% (1930–1939 cohort) to 26.3% (1940–1949 cohort) and 36.8% (1950–1959 cohort). EDDC disadvantage was prevalent and declined over cohorts: 75.1%, 70.9%, and 66.6% in the 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959 cohorts, respectively. Approximately half of the pooled sample was overweight or obese at age 50. The prevalence of normal weight at age 50 declined across the cohorts with 54.7%, 51.6%, and 47.2% in the 1930–1939, 1940–1949, and 1950–1959 cohorts, respectively. Overweight increased across cohorts, but obesity was relatively stable, ranging between 8.8% (1940–1949) and 11.0% (1950–1959). At ages 60–69, there were slight declines in normal weight, and slight increases in obesity across the successive cohorts. Nearly 34.8% of those in the 1930–1939 birth cohort had no access to medical attention at ages 60–69, compared to only 9.3% of those in birth cohort 1950–1959.

Regression Results

Table 2 shows the regression results from the main analytical sample. Model 1 indicates that female (vs male), older age (vs younger), living in an urban area (vs rural), and lower education (vs high) were associated with higher odds of reporting diabetes. Notably, disadvantaged EDDC was associated with higher odds of reporting diabetes (odds ratio [OR]: 1.26; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.11, 1.44). Relative to normal body mass status, overweight (OR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.82, 2.29) and obesity (OR: 3.52; 95% CI: 2.96, 4.17) at age 50 years were associated with higher odds of reporting diabetes. Those without access to medical care had lower odds of reporting diabetes than those with access to medical attention (OR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.49, 0.70). Reported diabetes increased across the cohorts, with higher odds in those in the 1940–1949 (OR: 2.52; 95% CI: 2.09, 3.04) and 1950–1959 (OR: 2.76; 95% CI: 2.27, 3.34) cohorts relative to the 1930–1939 cohort. Model 2 includes the interaction between cohort and body mass status, and the interaction between cohort and EDDC. These results indicate that the odds ratios associated with overweight and obesity were higher in the 1940–1949 and 1950–1959 cohorts relative to the 1930–1939 cohort. However, only the interaction between overweight and the 1950–1959 cohort was significant at the p = .05 threshold (OR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.11, 2.44 for the interaction term), whereas the interaction term between cohort and EDDC was not statistically significant. Regression results by cohort can be found in Supplementary Table S5.

Each birth cohort followed a similar pattern in that the elimination of each risk factor resulted in a lower predicted self-reported diabetes prevalence. Biomarker Subsample Results

Descriptive characteristics of the biomarker subsample are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S4. Using the HbA1c >6.5 criterion or reports of taking diabetes medications, the total prevalence of diabetes was 36.9% (Table 1). In the biomarker subsample, 46.3% of individuals with diabetes were undiagnosed, a variable we present for descriptive purposes and one not used in the regression models. Of this sample, 64.3% had an EDDC disadvantage and 27.6% was obese. Results from the logistic regression model are shown in Supplementary Table S6. EDDC disadvantage was associated with higher odds of having diabetes (OR: 1.28; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.65), a difference not statistically significant at the p = .05 level, yet similar in magnitude to the OR for EDDC disadvantage in Model 1 of Table 2. Obesity was associated with more than twice the odds of diabetes relative to normal BMI (OR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.57, 2.83). Table 4 shows results from the counterfactual scenarios. Under the counterfactual of eliminating EDDC disadvantage, the probability of having diabetes fell by 0.04 points or 9.7% (95% CI: −0.7%, 19.4%) of the original amount. Under the counterfactual scenario of eliminating both eliminating overweight and obesity at age 60, the probability declined by 0.07 or 20.2% (95% CI: 10.5%, 30.2%). Eliminating both risk factors simultaneously resulted in an explanatory effect of 0.11 or 29.0% (95% CI: 16.1%, 41.8%) of the prevalence of diabetes.

Discussion

We evaluated the potentially countervailing effects of rising obesity, but improving economic conditions in childhood, on diabetes status in three birth cohorts of Mexicans when these cohorts were ages 60–69 years. We found that, for the cohorts combined, the estimated explanatory effects of overweight/obesity on diabetes prevalence were larger than that of disadvantaged EDDC. In this pooled sample, we found that eliminating disadvantaged EDDC hypothetically would lead to about an 11.1% reduction in the prevalence of self-reported diabetes. In contrast, eliminating overweight/obesity would hypothetically reduce diabetes prevalence by one-third. Moreover, rising overweight/obesity was a main explanation for the rise in diabetes prevalence across cohorts, explaining more than 40% of the increase in diabetes prevalence between the 1930–1939 and 1950–1959 birth cohorts. Our results were robust in using an objective biomarker of diabetes status, in that overweight/obesity had a larger estimated effect than eliminating disadvantaged EDDC.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Previous studies on older Mexicans have analyzed the association between EDDC and diabetes or obesity and diabetes but, to our knowledge, no study has compared the role of the two risk factors. Our results are similar to those of previous studies in Mexico regarding the association between EDDC and diabetes at older ages in Mexico (Carrillo-Vega et al., 2019; Gonzalez-Samano & Villarreal, 2024; Ruiz-Pantoja & Ham-Chande, 2007). Ruiz-Pantoja and Ham-Chande (2007) found that early childhood condition is an important determinant of self-reported diabetes later in life. Carrillo-Vega et al. (2019) showed that reported overweight and obesity are important determinants of the likelihood of self-reported diabetes. Gonzalez-Samano and Villarreal (2024) showed that individuals who experienced EDDC and poverty during adulthood reported higher chances of being diagnosed with diabetes. These studies combined multiple birth cohorts and age groups in their analysis, while we compared people at the same age range (60–69) from different birth cohorts, enabling us to capture the differences across birth cohorts and disentangle the effect of EDDC and obesity across them. The importance of cohort comparisons hinges on the fast pace of change that the Mexican population underwent across successive cohorts born in the second half of the 20th century. Indeed, this pattern is likely to be observed in other middle-income countries in Latin America and other regions of the world.

Moreover, our contribution compared to the above-mentioned studies is that we adopted a life course perspective on socioeconomic conditions by including EDDC and socioeconomic status at the moment of the interview, also including weight status by using body mass figure when the individual was 50 years old. Previous studies adopted a life course perspective only by controlling for EDDC while considering socioeconomic and weight status at the time of the interview. Our approach allows us to go a step further by considering the changing nature of socioeconomic and body mass status over the life course and their changes across cohorts.

Implications

Older adults in Mexico have benefited from significant societal-wide improvements in education, healthcare, and general socioeconomic conditions over the last three decades. These improvements were accompanied by increasing life expectancy and general health status of the population (García Guerrero, 2014). Substantial improvements in EDDC also occurred and, according to existing theories, we would expect these improvements to improve the metabolic health of the population in old age. Our findings do indicate that a more advantaged EDDC is associated with lower diabetes risk net of educational attainment and access to medical care, supporting existing evidence on the importance of EDDC on metabolic health throughout life.

Nonetheless, the potentially beneficial effects of improved EDDC have been masked by rising obesity, which has occurred simultaneously with and is partially a result of the broader socioeconomic improvements in Mexico. The increase in obesity that we observed and the increase in the risk of self-reported diabetes are in line with what has been described as the nutrition transition model (Popkin & Ng, 2022). This model suggests that large shifts in diet and physical activity patterns change nutritional outcomes, and those lifestyle changes can be related to major demographic and socioeconomic changes (Turrell & Vandevijvere, 2015). In the last 40 years, Mexican diets shifted from fresh and unprocessed food, to ultraprocessed food with high sugar, fat, and salt (Marrón-Ponce et al., 2019). In 2019, 23.1% of the Mexican population’s total dietary energy came from ultraprocessed food products. Indeed, Marrón-Ponce et al. (2019) reported that the increase in consumption of ultraprocessed food is not explained by individual sociodemographic characteristics, but rather by macroeconomic, environmental, and sociocultural factors.

The changes in dietary habits and food exposure help explain why, despite the observed improvement in childhood economic conditions and the increase in education across successive birth cohorts, the prevalence of overweight and obesity continued to increase. Unlike in high-income countries where there is a clear gradient of obesity prevalence across socioeconomic status (Kagenaar et al., 2022), in Mexico the increase in obesity has occurred across all socioeconomic groups; in fact, in 2018, people with high socioeconomic status experienced larger changes toward overweight and obesity compared to people with low socioeconomic status (Barquera & Rivera, 2020).

From the counterfactual analysis, we observed that, even after controlling for EDDC, overweight, and obesity, 60% of the difference in diabetes across cohorts remained to be explained in the pooled sample (Table 3). In addition, when comparing the contribution of those two factors combined, 55% remained to be explained (Supplementary Table S8). Although multiple factors might explain the cohort differences, we consider that a part of this might be explained by the important changes in access to medical insurance (period effect) between the cohorts under study. Access to medical care may result in differences in the prevalence of self-reported diabetes between cohorts, given that a large fraction of individuals aged 60–69 from cohort 1930 to 1939 had no access to medical insurance at the time of the interview (2001). Conversely, previous, older cohorts benefited from the introduction of the medical insurance program called Seguro Popular that started after 2003, especially those living in rural areas or who worked in the informal sector (Parker et al., 2018). This factor would serve to explain the increase in diabetes between cohort 1930–1939 versus cohort 1940–1949, because those individuals from cohort 1940–1949 were interviewed in 2012. For this cohort, the fraction without medical insurance was 12.9% compared to 34.8% of those from the birth cohort 1930–1939.

Strengths and Limitations

For this study, we adopted a cohort perspective that allowed us to observe change in the prevalence of self-reported diabetes across cohorts; previous studies using MHAS reported an increase in self-reported diabetes across different waves, but combined multiple age groups. We are the first to document this alarming increase in diabetes across cohorts among MHAS participants, particularly among those from more recent cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, most of the information on the individuals from birth cohort 1930 to 1939 was obtained in Wave 1 (2001) or 2 (2003), but in order to include information on body figures at age 50, we selected individuals from the cohort 1930 to 1939 who survived until Wave 5 in 2015, because the questions regarding body figures at age 50 were asked in Wave 5 (2015). From previous research (Saenz & Wong, 2015) we know that, during the period 2001–2012, individuals with adverse socioeconomic conditions were more likely to die. Thus, our strategy results in more selected individuals from that cohort. This can introduce selection bias because we are analyzing the healthiest individuals from that cohort, which would partially explain the lower levels of self-reported diabetes next to the important increase in people with medical insurance due to the introduction of Seguro Popular discussed before. In this manuscript, however, we focus on the effect of EDDC, overweight, and obesity across successive birth cohorts, and even if we included the healthiest from the cohort 1930 to 1939, the effect of EDDC, overweight, and obesity is similar to what we observed in the model for other birth cohorts and for the pooled sample.

Another important limitation is that the dependent variable we used was self-reported diabetes. We partially overcome this limitation, however, by including in the diabetes group not only the individuals who self-reported diabetes but also those who reported taking medication to control their diabetes, either medication or insulin shots. In addition, to gauge the sensitivity of our results to this limitation, we replicate the analysis using the MHAS subsample with biomarkers, to acknowledge the underreporting of diabetes that has been previously mentioned (Kumar et al., 2016) and the effect of EDDC, overweight, and obesity in this subsample.

Being diagnosed with diabetes also requires access to and utilization of health services. We expect that increases in health services over time will lead to higher diagnosis levels and these could bias our estimates of differences in cohorts. We did adjust for a binary variable that captures access to medical attention, but this variable may be insufficient to capture the myriad dimensions of healthcare access and utilization relevant to being diagnosed with diabetes. In sensitivity analyses, we found that excluding this variable had a small effect on the cohort differences in the odds of reporting diabetes as shown in Model 1 in Table 1 (see Supplementary Appendix S10). Further work is needed to identify the role of medical care in this population, both in terms of the risk of developing diabetes and the prevention of diabetes-related sequelae.

Conclusion

The increase in overweight and obesity among more recent birth cohorts of older adults in Mexico appears to have offset the progress in reducing the socioeconomic disadvantages during childhood experienced by these cohorts. Thus, in addition to eliminating socioeconomic disparities during childhood, it is important to reduce overweight and obesity in middle-age among adults in order to reduce the prevalence of diabetes in old age in Mexico. As we highlighted, reducing early childhood conditions can lead to a decline of 11.4% (95% CI: 5.25%, 17.6%) in the probability of self-reported diabetes, but eliminating overweight and obesity at age 50 can lead to a decline of 30.4% (95% CI: 26.6%, 34.3%). Eliminating both would reduce the probability of self-reported diabetes at age 60–69 by 39.5 (95% CI: 33.5, 45.4%). Policies based on evidence that uses a life course perspective and focuses on better life conditions for children, along with interventions to improve health behaviors among Mexican adults, are likely to influence health in old age and reduce mortality by the main causes of death in this population.
