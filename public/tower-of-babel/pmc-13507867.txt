Lanthionine Ketenamine Derivative Enhances Early Functional Recovery Following Skeletal Muscle Ischemia‐Reperfusion Injury Through Modulation of Autophagy and Oxidative Stress
Authors: Valentina Barrera, Jose H. Treviño, Travis T. Denton, Blake B. Rasmussen, Christopher Rathbone, Yulieth Soto, Maria A. Gonzalez Porras, Hugo Giambini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschemia‐reperfusion injury (IRI) induces skeletal muscle damage through oxidative stress, impaired autophagy, and satellite cell (SC) dysfunction, ultimately compromising regenerative capacity and functional recovery. We investigated whether the naturally occurring autophagy‐stimulating compound 2‐n‐hexyl lanthionine ketenamine phosphonate (2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P) enhances SC resilience and promotes early regenerative responses following IRI. Primary SCs isolated from Sprague Dawley rats (96% Pax‐7+ purity) were treated with 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P or rapamycin. Autophagic flux was assessed using CYTO‐ID flow cytometry and Western blot analysis of LC3‐I and LC3‐II, and viability was assessed under baseline and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)‐induced oxidative stress conditions. In parallel, a proof‐of‐concept hindlimb IRI model (3‐h tourniquet) evaluated early functional recovery and transcriptional changes. In vitro, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P significantly increased autophagy flux and enhanced SC viability across concentrations and treatment durations, whereas rapamycin reduced proliferation. Under 500 µM H2O2, which decreased viability by ~63%, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P restored cell survival toward control levels, demonstrating robust protection against oxidative stress. In vivo, treated animals demonstrated improved grip strength recovery between days 2 and 7 post‐injury compared to PBS‐treated controls. Molecular analyzes revealed significant upregulation of autophagy regulators (Beclin‐1, ULK‐1) and antioxidant marker SOD‐1, with coordinated upward trends in additional autophagy (ATG‐7, LC3, p62) and myogenic genes (MyoG, MHC). Collectively, these findings suggest that 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P enhances SC survival by modulating autophagy and reinforcing antioxidant defenses, resulting in improved early functional recovery following IRI. These results identify 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P modulation of autophagy and oxidative stress as a promising therapeutic strategy for limiting IRI‐induced skeletal muscle damage.

Introduction

Traumatic injuries frequently cause severe limb damage, with muscle pathology often arising from acute ischemia‐reperfusion injury (IRI) secondary to vascular disruption, emergency tourniquet application, or acute compartment syndrome [1, 2]. IRI progresses through distinct stages: an initial period of ischemia leading to cellular energy depletion and ion imbalance, followed by reperfusion, which triggers swelling, inflammation, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [2]. These processes collectively exacerbate muscle damage, ultimately contributing to atrophy and weakness [2]. Previous studies have demonstrated promising outcomes in mitigating IRI using strategies such as thermomodulation [3], controlled reperfusion [1], and pharmacological approaches targeting ROS [4]. Moreover, transplantation of muscle progenitor cells (MPCs) at later stages of IRI has been shown to enhance functional recovery [5]. However, major challenges remain as prophylactic interventions are rarely feasible, immediate post‐injury treatments are uncommon [5], and the delayed use of MPCs requires intermediary cell sourcing steps, limiting their timely and effective clinical application.

Satellite cells (SCs), resident stem cells within muscles, play a crucial role in muscle growth and repair [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. They activate upon trauma, following a myogenic program that supports muscle regeneration [12, 13, 14]. Their function depends on efficient autophagy, a cellular degradation process essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Studies in various stem cell types have established that autophagy regulates their quiescence, activation, differentiation, and self‐renewal [11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Following injury, autophagy is dynamically regulated during differentiation to support myotube formation [18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Suppression of autophagy due to injury can impair skeletal muscle recovery, leading to atrophy, myofiber degeneration, reduced force production, and myopathies [15, 18, 26]. Importantly, IRI disrupts autophagosome clearance (i.e., autophagy flux), partly through ROS‐mediated mechanisms, contributing to cell death [27]. Enhancing autophagy and protecting against ROS post‐injury via novel therapeutics could potentially improve muscle regeneration and function.

Traditional pharmacological approaches, such as rapamycin and/or its derivatives, can upregulate autophagy but also inhibit protein synthesis pathways critical for muscle repair. Lanthionine ketenimine (LK), a sulfur amino acid metabolite found in mammalian brain tissue at low concentrations, possesses potent neuroprotective, neurotrophic, and anti‐neuroinflammatory properties [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Both LK and lanthionine ketenamine ethyl ester (LKE, Figure 1) have demonstrated the ability to slow motor decline in a mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [33]. Importantly, LK derivatives, such as the lanthionine ketenamine (ester) phosphonate (ester)s (LK(E)‐P(E)s, Figure 1), have been shown to activate autophagy in neural cells [34, 35]. However, the role of LK phosphonate analogs in skeletal muscle stem cells remains largely unexplored. Given the central role of SCs in orchestrating muscle repair, evaluating the direct cellular effects of these compounds on autophagy regulation and oxidative stress resistance is a critical first step toward therapeutic translation. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to determine whether the autophagy‐stimulating compound 2‐n‐hexyl‐LKE‐P enhances autophagy flux and improves SC viability under oxidative stress conditions in‐vitro. As a secondary, exploratory objective, we used a proof‐of‐concept in‐vivo rat model of skeletal muscle IRI to evaluate whether these molecular findings were associated with early functional and transcriptional responses.

Lanthionine ketenamine (ester) (LKE) and its 2‐substituted phosphonate analogs, the lanthionine ketenamine (ester) phosphonate (ester)s (LK(E)‐P(E) and the 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P. The latter was used in the current study.

Methods

Animals

This study has been conducted in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act and the Implementing Animal Welfare Regulations in accordance with the principles of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at UT San Antonio. Twelve 6‐month‐old male Sprague Dawley rats were obtained from Envigo LLC (Indianapolis, IN). Six animals were assigned to in‐vitro experiments and six to in‐vivo evaluations. Rats were housed at 21°C, maintained on a 12:12 h light‐dark cycle, and given ad libitum access to food and water.

Satellite Cell Isolation

Hindlimb and shoulder muscles were used for skeletal muscle precursor cell (i.e., satellite cell ‐SC‐) isolation based on the procedure described by Allen et al. [36] and with modifications [7, 37, 38]. Briefly, muscles from both hindlimbs (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and gastrocnemius) and shoulders (supraspinatus and infraspinatus) were isolated following removal of skin and fascia. Hindlimb and shoulder muscles were then minced, separately, followed by enzymatic digestion (Protease (1.25 mg/mL); Sigma Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO), differential centrifugation and filtration steps, to obtain a pellet that was resuspended in Growth Media (GM; 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco; Carlsbad, CA), 1% pen/strep (10,000 U/ml Penicillin + 10,000 ug/ml Streptomycin) (Gibco; Carlsbad, CA), 0.2% MycoZap (Lonza; Houston, TX), Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Gibco; Carlsbad, CA) with 1 g/L d‐glucose, l‐glutamine, and 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate). The cell suspensions were pre‐plated first for 2 h and then for 24 h on a tissue culture‐treated 150‐mm‐diameter plate. After 24 h of pre‐plating, the non‐adherent cells were seeded onto 150‐mm‐diameter matrigel‐coated (0.1 mg/ml; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) plates in GM. The cells were cultured for 3‐4 days, and GM was changed every 48 h until the cells reached ∼80%–90% confluency.

Satellite Cell Purity Verification

Flow cytometry was performed to determine the purity/% yield of SCs. First, SCs were cultured for 3 days in GM, then trypsinized and separated into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing ~1 × 106 cells per tube. SCs viability was determined using the Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend; San Diego, CA) at 1:500 dilution and following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. After viability staining, SCs were washed with 1X phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 1500g for 5‐min to remove any remaining viability dye. SCs were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA) for 5‐min at room temperature, and washed twice with PBS followed by centrifugation for 5‐min at 1500g between each wash. SCs were then permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X‐100 (ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA) for 5‐min at room temperature, washed, blocked (10% goat serum; Catalog #: G9023, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 30‐min at 4°C, and then incubated with the primary rabbit anti‐Pax‐7 antibody (SD083‐04; ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA) at a 1:50 dilution for 1 h at 4°C. Cells were then washed twice, probed with a goat anti‐rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) secondary antibody (#A11008, ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA)) at 1:1000 dilution for 1 h at 4°C, washed, resuspended in PBS, and then analyzed using a FACS Celesta flow cytometer equipped with a High Throughput Sampler (HTS) (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA).

Pharmacological Drugs

Pharmacological compounds were reconstituted with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA). Stock solutions were diluted to the desired concentrations in culture media, maintaining a final DMSO concentration of 0.1%. Consistent with prior studies on skeletal muscle cells [39, 40, 41], rapamycin (MilliporeSigma, St Louis, MO) was added to culture media at a final concentration of 100 nM. 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P was synthesized using standard Michaelis‐Arbuzov reaction conditions as previously described [42]. The chemical structure of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P was confirmed by 1H, 13C, and 31P NMR, and liquid chromatography‐tandem UV spectrophotometry‐high‐resolution mass spectrometry (LC/UV/HRMS). Following the approach of Shen et al. [42], a concentration range of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P was prepared (0.01 µM, 0.1 µM, 1 µM, 5 µM, 10 µM, 100 µM, and 1 mM).

Cell Viability and Proliferation

To evaluate cell viability and proliferation, MTS assays (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay; Promega; Madison, WI) were performed. Briefly, hindlimb cells were cultured until reaching ∼80%–90% confluency, then trypsinized and seeded in Matrigel‐coated 96‐well plates. After a 4 h attachment period, GM was replaced with fresh media containing rapamycin or various concentrations of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P for 24 h and 72 h prior to analyzes. Cell viability/proliferation was assessed by measuring absorbance at 490 nm using a plate reader (Synergy 2 Multi‐Mode Microplate Reader; BioTek, Winooski, VT), with background values from media‐only wells subtracted from treated wells. Cell number was quantified for each condition, and values were normalized to the control group and expressed as relative fold change. A total of 1 well/rat (n = 6 total) were used for each group.

Hydrogen Peroxide‐Induced Oxidative Stress

After exposure to different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; 100 µM, 200 µM, 500 µM; ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) (n = 3 replicate wells per rat per concentration), MTS assays were performed to determine the appropriate concentration of H2O2 to use to evaluate hindlimb SC viability under oxidative stress. Based on the H2O2 concentration that significantly reduced viability (500 µM), the protective effects of the pharmacological compounds were subsequently assessed using that concentration. A total of 1 well per rat (n = 6 total) were used for each group. Table 1 summarizes the treatment modalities, which included simultaneous administration of pharmacological compounds with H2O2, as well as pre‐treatment with compounds for 24 h prior to additional treatment and H2O2 exposure. Viability data were normalized to control samples without H2O2 and expressed as relative fold change.

Autophagy Flux Assessment

Autophagy flux was evaluated using two different methods. First, similar to previous studies [43, 44], a commercially available kit (CYTO‐ID Autophagy Detection Kit; Enzo Life Sciences Inc., Farmingdale, NY) was used. This kit employs a dye that selectively labels autophagic compartments, enabling their quantification. Hindlimb‐derived cells were seeded in Matrigel‐coated T‐25 flasks and allowed to adhere for 24 h, after which the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the following experimental treatments: control, rapamycin (positive control, 100 nM), and 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P (0.1 mM and 1 mM). After 68 h of incubation, cells were treated with chloroquine (CQ; 10 µM), a lysosomal inhibitor, for an additional 4 h. After treatment, cells were trypsinized, collected by centrifugation, washed once in PBS, and resuspended in a PBS‐CYTO‐ID dye solution. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. At the end of this period, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) for 20 min. Cells were then washed three additional times with PBS and resuspended in PBS for imaging to prevent degradation of autophagic vacuoles. An imaging flow cytometer (ImageStream X Mark II; Cytek Bioscience, Seattle, WA) was used to quantify fluorescence intensity.

Second, western blot analysis was performed on shoulder‐derived cells. SCs were seeded onto Matrigel‐coated 100‐mm culture dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh growth medium containing the following experimental treatments: control, rapamycin (100 nM; positive control), or 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P (0.1 μM, 0.1 mM, or 1 mM), and the cells were incubated for 48 h. To inhibit lysosomal degradation and assess autophagic flux, bafilomycin‐A1 (10 nM) was added to the culture medium 4 h before the end of the treatment period. Parallel cultures were processed without bafilomycin. Following treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed with Cell Lytic Cell Lysis Reagent (Cat. No. C2978, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA‐free (Cat. No. 78441, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 15,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C until analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat. No. 23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Western Blot Analysis

Equal amounts of protein (25 μg) were mixed with 4× Protein Loading Buffer (Cat. No. D30927‐01, LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) supplemented with β‐mercaptoethanol, denatured at 95°C for 5 min, and separated on 4%–15% Mini‐PROTEAN TGX Stain‐Free precast gels (Cat. No. 4568084, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using 1× Tris/Glycine/SDS running buffer (Cat. No. 1610732, Bio‐Rad). Proteins were transferred onto Trans‐Blot Turbo Mini 0.2‐μm nitrocellulose membranes (Cat. No. 1704158, Bio‐Rad) using a Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio‐Rad) for 7 min. Membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBST) and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Cat. No. 37525, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted 1:1000 in TBST: rabbit anti‐LC3 (Cat. No. L8918, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and rabbit anti‐β‐actin (Clone 13E5, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with IRDye 680RD Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cat. No. 926‐68071, LI‐COR Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were subsequently washed in TBST, rinsed in TBS, and imaged using the Odyssey Fc Imaging System (LI‐COR Biosciences). Band intensities were quantified using Image Studio Software (LI‐COR Biosciences), and all proteins of interest were normalized to β‐actin.

In‐Vivo Ischemia‐Reperfusion Injury (IRI) Model

Prior to injury, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and received a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine‐HCl (1.2 mg/kg) for analgesia. Rats were placed in the supine position and the left hind limb was elevated to reduce blood flow. After 5 min, a tourniquet was applied to the proximal hind left limb and maintained at 250 mmHg for 3 h to induce ischemia (Figure 2). Reperfusion was initiated by deflating the tourniquet and lowering the limb. The contralateral right limb served as a non‐injured control. Animals were randomized into two groups (n = 3 per group; Group 1: PBS, Group 2: Treatment). Both groups received intraperitoneal (IP) and intramuscular (IM) injections into the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles of the left injured leg. The treatment group received 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P (100 mg/kg IP, 10 mg/kg IM), while the control group received equivalent volumes of PBS. Following treatment, animals were allowed to recover from anesthesia and received carprofen (5 mg/kg, subcutaneous) for analgesia for three consecutive days. Treatments were repeated after 72 h (IP and IM), and animals were euthanized 7 days after the initial injury using CO2 asphyxiation.

Schematic of the ischemia‐reperfusion injury (IRI) model and treatment. Rats were positioned supine with the left hind limb elevated, and a tourniquet (250 mmHg) applied for 3 h. Animals were randomized into control (PBS) or treatment (2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P: 100 mg/kg intraperitoneal [IP] and 10 mg/kg intramuscular [IM]) groups (n = 3 each). Injections were administered at surgery and 72 h later IP and IM (tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles). Contralateral limbs served as non‐injured controls.

Grip Strength Test

Grip strength of the injured limbs was assessed using the BIO‐GS4 device (Bioseb, Pinellas Park, FL; force capacity 2500 g; resolution 1 g/0.01 N), with each animal undergoing four consecutive grip strength measurements with rest intervals between trials at three time points: prior to IRI, 2‐days, and 7‐days post‐injury. Grip strength outcomes were normalized to each animal's body weight.

Histology

To confirm the extent of injury in our rat IRI model, the gastrocnemius muscles were harvested from both hind limbs of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P‐ and PBS‐treated animals following euthanasia. Each muscle was bisected, with one half designated for quantitative real‐time PCR and the other for histological analysis. The tissue designated for histological analyzes was then frozen in 2‐methylbutane pre‐cooled with liquid nitrogen (approximately −150°C). Frozen tissues were stored at −80°C until processing. Transverse cryosections were prepared using a cryostat and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Representative slides were selected and examined under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at 5× magnification (Figure 3).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Gene expression of myogenic, ROS, and autophagy‐related markers was evaluated in gastrocnemius tissues previously collected on animals treated with either 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P or PBS. Muscles were dissected and homogenized in TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA), with reagent volume adjusted according to tissue weight (1 mL TRIzol per 100 mg of tissue) to ensure efficient lysis. RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration was measured using a Take3 Micro‐Volume Plate (BioTek, Winooski, VT). Due to the high yield, samples were diluted and normalized to 100 ng for cDNA synthesis. cDNA was synthesized using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad, Hercules, CA) on a T100 Thermal Cycler (BioRad, Hercules, CA). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (BioRad, Hercules, CA) using pre‐designed primers (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) (Table 2). Gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with 18S rRNA serving as the housekeeping gene. Genes evaluated included: myogenic markers (MyoD, MyoG, and MHC), ROS‐related markers (SOD‐1 and NRF‐2), and autophagy markers (Beclin‐1, ULK‐1, ATG‐7, LC3, and p62). Six technical replicates were performed per gene for each rat, and the mean value was used for further analysis. Expression levels from the injured limb were normalized to those of the contralateral uninjured limb and are reported as relative fold change.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SE. One‐way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison tests was used to evaluate differences in (1) viability with multiple treatment groups compared to control and rapamycin, (2) viability under oxidative stress, including pre‐treated samples, and (3) autophagy flux measurements. All analyzes were performed on the raw data. Cell‐based assay data were normalized to their respective controls and expressed as fold‐change. Unpaired t‐tests were used for in‐vivo analyzes, including PCR of myogenic, autophagy, and ROS‐related genes, and grip strength measurements. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0).

Results

2‐n‐Hexyl LKE‐P Enhances Satellite Cell Viability While Rapamycin Suppresses Proliferation

The purity of SCs following isolation was approximately 96% of Pax‐7+ cells, confirming high isolation efficiency (Figure 4). Under 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P or rapamycin treatment, rapamycin significantly reduced viability compared to control (p < 0.01) (Figure 5). In contrast, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P significantly increased viability relative to control, irrespective of the concentration or treatment duration (p < 0.05). Moreover, viability was significantly higher with 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P compared to rapamycin (p < 0.0001), again independent of concentration.

2‐n‐Hexyl LKE‐P Protects Satellite Cells From H2O2‐Induced Oxidative Stress

Treatment with 100 µM and 200 µM H2O2 increased SC number compared to control, although this difference did not reach statistical significance, suggesting these concentrations were not overtly toxic under our experimental conditions. In contrast, 500 µM H2O2 caused a significant reduction in cell number when compared to the untreated control, and to the 100 µM and 200 µM H2O2‐treated groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A). When normalized to control, 500 µM H2O2 reduced viability by ~63%. Across all doses and treatment conditions, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P improved viability compared to the control (untreated) with H2O2 and rapamycin groups, suggesting a protective effect (Figure 6B). Interestingly, rapamycin consistently showed reduced viability compared to the control with H2O2 and the untreated control (p < 0.05). After a 24 h of recovery from treatment, viability in the 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P‐treated groups increased to values similar to the untreated control, with all doses—except 0.01 µM and 10 µM—showing significantly better outcomes than rapamycin (p < 0.05). Importantly, after recovery, treatment with 0.1 µM and 100 µM showed a trend toward higher values compared to the untreated control group (not significant), and significantly outperformed the H2O2‐treated control group.

2‐n‐Hexyl LKE‐P Stimulates Autophagy Flux in Satellite Cells

Rapamycin significantly increased autophagy flux compared to control (p < 0.01) (Figure 7). Similarly, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P increased flux at both concentrations tested compared to control (1 mM, p < 0.001), with 1 mM 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P inducing greater flux than 0.1 mM (p < 0.01). Figure 8A shows a representative Western blot of LC3‐I, LC3‐II, and β‐actin from cultured SCs treated with various concentrations of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P, or rapamycin (Rap), in the presence (+Baf) or absence (−Baf) of bafilomycin‐A1 (Baf). As expected, treatment with bafilomycin resulted in LC3‐II accumulation in control cells (Figure 8B), confirming effective inhibition of lysosomal degradation. 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P treatment in the presence of bafilomycin further increased LC3‐II accumulation, with the largest response observed at the 0.1 mM concentration (Figure 8B–C). Similarly, LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratios were generally higher in the 0.1 and 1 mM LKE groups than in control cells under lysosomal inhibition (Figure 8D). To further assess autophagic activity, LC3‐II accumulation following lysosomal inhibition (ΔLC3‐II) was calculated by comparing paired bafilomycin‐treated and untreated samples (Figure 8E). After 48 h of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P (0.1 mM) treatment, autophagic flux was greater than the untreated control group. Collectively, these findings suggest that 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P enhances autophagosome formation, particularly at the 0.1 mM concentration.

2‐n‐Hexyl LKE‐P Enhances Grip Strength Recovery Following IRI

While body weight decreased, differences between pre‐injury and 2‐ or 7‐days post‐IRI were not significant (Figure 9A). IRI caused a significant reduction in grip strength at both 2 days post‐injury (~65% for PBS, ~77% for 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P; p < 0.05 vs. pre‐injury) and 7 days post‐injury (~67% for PBS, ~63% for 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P) (Figure 9B). While PBS‐treated animals did not recover between 2 and 7 days, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P‐treated animals demonstrated a trend toward improved functional recovery, showing approximately 60% higher grip strength by day 7 versus day 2 (p < 0.01), although strength remained below pre‐injury levels.

2‐n‐Hexyl LKE‐P Promotes Myogenesis, Antioxidant Defense, and Autophagy in an In‐Vivo IRI Model

Expression of MyoD, MyoG, and MHC ‐ key markers of SC activation, differentiation, and regeneration ‐ was higher in 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P‐treated groups, particularly MyoG and MHC, although differences were not statistically significant (Figure 10). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P on autophagy regulation, oxidative stress resilience, and regenerative signaling in the context of skeletal muscle injury. Our primary and most robust findings demonstrated that 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P modulates autophagy flux, improves cellular viability, and confers protection under oxidative stress conditions in‐vitro. These findings are particularly relevant given that (1) the effects occurred without the suppression of proliferation observed with rapamycin, (2) satellite cells (SCs) are central mediators of skeletal muscle regeneration [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 45], and (3) autophagy plays a critical role in maintaining stem cell function [11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Autophagy is essential for maintaining SC homeostasis by regulating quiescence, activation, and differentiation processes [11, 22, 23], and its impairment leads to defective regeneration and muscle degeneration [15, 18, 25, 26, 46]. In our study, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P significantly increased autophagy flux, similar to rapamycin, but without the reduction in cell viability observed with rapamycin treatment. This distinction is important as rapamycin induces autophagy primarily through mTORC1 inhibition, a pathway that, while promoting catabolic clearance, simultaneously suppresses protein synthesis and myogenic signaling critical for regeneration [23, 47, 48]. The preservation and enhancement of SC viability with 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P suggests that autophagy can be stimulated without compromising anabolic or proliferative capacity, potentially through alternative or more balanced regulatory mechanisms. Positive modulation of autophagic flux was further confirmed by the accumulation of LC3‐II following 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P treatment during lysosomal inhibition, with the most consistent response observed at the 0.1 mM concentration. Because bafilomycin blocks autophagosome degradation, increased LC3‐II accumulation following treatment reflects continued autophagosome formation despite inhibition of lysosomal clearance. Although no significant difference was observed in autophagic flux, the overall biological response remained consistent with activation of the autophagy pathway.

The protective effects of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P under oxidative stress further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. IRI is characterized by excessive ROS production during reperfusion [2, 27, 49, 50], which not only damages cellular components but also disrupts autophagosome‐lysosome fusion, impairing autophagic flux [27, 51, 52]. In our experiments, exposure to 500 µM H2O2 significantly reduced SC viability, replicating the oxidative burden observed during reperfusion. Treatment with 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P improved viability across doses and treatment groups, including recovery conditions. These findings suggest that pharmacological enhancement of autophagy may restore cellular homeostasis under oxidative stress, consistent with prior work demonstrating that enhancing autophagy mitigates IRI‐related tissue injury [49, 50, 53].

Our in‐vitro findings are further supported by our secondary, exploratory proof‐of‐concept in‐vivo rat model of IRI. Specifically, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P treatment in‐vivo increased the expression of the upstream autophagy regulators Beclin‐1 and ULK1 [54, 55, 56], suggesting the activation of autophagosome formation. In addition, ATG‐7, LC3, and p62 also exhibited upward trends. These findings are consistent with our in‐vitro observations, in which 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P treatment increased LC3‐II accumulation in cultured SCs, providing complementary downstream evidence supporting modulation of the autophagic pathway. Collectively, these findings suggest coordinated regulation autophagy‐associated signaling rather than isolated changes in individual autophagy markers. These findings align with studies showing that autophagy is protective during ischemia but becomes dysregulated during reperfusion [50, 57], and that restoration of autophagic activity reduces tissue damage [50]. Complementing autophagy activation, antioxidant defense pathways were enhanced in treated animals. SOD‐1 was significantly increased and NRF‐2 showed an upward trend. NRF‐2 serves as a master regulator of antioxidant gene expression [58, 59], while SOD‐1 catalyzes superoxide detoxification [58, 60, 61]. The simultaneous upregulation of autophagy and antioxidant signaling is biologically coherent, as redox balance is essential for maintaining effective autophagic flux [27, 52]. Thus, 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P may exert a dual protective role by enhancing autophagic clearance and reinforcing ROS detoxification pathways.

Markers of myogenesis (MyoD, MyoG, MHC) demonstrated upward trends, particularly MyoG and MHC, which reflect differentiation and fiber maturation [12, 13, 14]. Although these changes did not reach statistical significance, their coordinated directionality suggests engagement of regenerative pathways. Given that autophagy is required for proper myogenic differentiation [18, 19, 62, 63], the observed molecular profile is consistent with a pro‐regenerative cellular environment. Functionally, grip strength remained below pre‐injury levels at 7 days; however, treated animals demonstrated improvement between days 2 and 7, whereas PBS‐treated animals did not. While limited by sample size, this trend parallels the molecular signatures observed and suggests that early enhancement of autophagy and antioxidant defenses may accelerate recovery. Previous literature demonstrates that autophagy induction can mitigate IRI in multiple tissues [49, 50, 53, 64, 65, 66, 67], yet its role is highly context‐dependent [57]. Excessive or dysregulated autophagy may exacerbate injury, whereas controlled enhancement appears protective [50, 57]. Our findings extend this body of work by demonstrating that modulation of autophagy in skeletal muscle SCs can improve cellular resilience and viability. Targeting stem cell‐specific autophagy may therefore represent a refined therapeutic strategy compared to global mTOR inhibition.

This study has several limitations. Although our in‐vivo and in‐vitro findings provide complementary evidence supporting modulation of the autophagy pathway through flow cytometry and Western blot analysis, additional studies incorporating larger numbers of biological replicates and complementary approaches (e.g., immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence) are needed to further characterize autophagic activity and strengthen the mechanistic conclusions. Our in‐vivo study was exploratory with a short‐term evaluation window (7‐days post‐injury), restricting our ability to assess long‐term regenerative outcomes. In addition, dose optimization and comprehensive histological analyzes were not performed. Accordingly, the in‐vivo findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, and future studies should address these limitations to further validate the role of 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P as a potential therapeutic compound.

Conclusion

The convergence of increased autophagy flux, enhanced oxidative stress resilience, upregulated antioxidant signaling, and supportive transcriptional changes provides mechanistic evidence that 2‐n‐hexyl LKE‐P promotes a pro‐survival and pro‐regenerative milieu in satellite cells. These findings provide a biologically grounded rationale for expanded in‐vivo studies to determine whether stem cell‐targeted autophagy modulation can meaningfully improve long‐term muscle regeneration following IRI.
