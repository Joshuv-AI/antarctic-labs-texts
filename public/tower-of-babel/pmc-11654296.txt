Exploring machine learning algorithms to predict not using modern family planning methods among reproductive age women in East Africa
Authors: Tirualem Zeleke Yehuala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of the modern family planning method provides chances for women to reach optimal child spacing, increase quality of life, increase economic status, achieve the desired family size, and prevent unsafe abortions and maternal and perinatal deaths. However, use modern family planning is low among reproductive age women in East Africa. Therefore, this study aimed at predicting not using modern family planning and identifying its associated factors among reproductive age women in east Africa using machine learning methods.MethodsThe data set was obtained from a demographic health survey in East Africa. This study used data on weighted sample of 92,564 women who were in their reproductive age. Python software was utilized for data processing, and machine learning models such as Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), XGB (Extreme Gradient Boosting), SVM (Support Victor Machine), and K-Nearest Neighbor Decision Tree (DT) were implemented. In this work, we evaluated the predictive models’ performance using performance assessment criteria such as accuracy, precision, recall, and the AUC curve.ResultIn this study, 92,564 reproductive women’s was used in the final analysis. Among the proposed machine learning models, XGB performed best overall in the proposed classifier, with an accuracy of 98.7%, precision of 99.8%, recall of 98%, 99.9% AUC score, had high specificity 98%, sensitivity 99.8%, and low error rate discovery was 0.013%. The most significant determinants of not using modern family planning in East Africa: lack of education, age 25–29, living in a rural area, being single, not knowing how to use a contraceptive, and smoking cigarette.ConclusionExtreme Gradient Boosting proved effective in predicting not using modern family planning, offering valuable insights for interventions and policy decisions in East Africa. Machine learning could help achieve early prediction and intervention on reproductive women’s not using modern family planning. This leads to a recommendation for policy direction to reduce child and maternal mortality and improve living standards in east Africa.

Introduction

The components of modern family planning include pills, injectable, intrauterine contraceptive devices, vaginal rings, implants, condoms, female sterilization, and male sterilization [1]. Modern family planning methods have many benefits like birth spacing, prevents unintended pregnancies, reduces perinatal, infant, and maternal mortalities [2]. Moreover, social benefits Family planning is an improvement in education for all children and birth-related complications as it provides adequate time for a mother to recover from her previous pregnancy sufferings [3].

Worldwide, in 2030, 1.19 billion women are expected to require modern family planning methods. Furthermore, 1.1 billion people need modern family planning. 874 million people will be using modern family planning in 2021 [4]. To achieve a predicted decline in fertility by 2025, the UN estimates that the prevalence of contraception needs to be 67% in developing nations and 66–75% in developed nations [5]. However, many women do not adopt modern family planning methods for many reasons. There was a great disparity in the uptake of modern FP between developing and developed countries. The uptake of modern contraceptive methods among married reproductive-age women in developing countries was 45%, while it was 70% in developed countries. Globally, there were also regional differences in the uptake of modern FP, like 56% in Latin America, 74% in East Asia, 34% in South Asia, and 14% in Africa [6]. Therefore, modern contraceptive utilization in Africa is very low [4].

In sub-Saharan African countries, the projected prevalence of not utilizing family planning will remain high in 2030 [7]. The East African region had a disproportionally high rate of not using modern family planning methods [8]. In Uganda, 70% of women did not use modern family planning methods in 2020. In Ethiopia, 64% of married women did not use modern family planning [5] and in Kenya, 40%; in Madagascar, 60% of reproductive women’s families did not use modern family planning [5, 9, 10].

The consequences of not using modern family planning are a decline in economic status, reduced education, a lack of quality of life, and increased maternal and perinatal mortality [11, 12].

Many scholars reported distance from health facilities, fear of side effects, religious factors, cultural beliefs, lack of awareness, low levels of education, community norms and stigma, and the and the availability of family planning services [13], monthly income [14], educational, delivery place [15, 16], number of living children, husband’s disapproval of family planning [17], marital status, having positive attitudes family planning [18], and Husband influence was associated with low uptake of modern family planning methods [19, 20].

Even though many studies are conducted using classical statistical analysis techniques, they do not discover hidden knowledge and cannot find predictive patterns and rules for evidence-based decision-making [20, 21]. Additionally, many studies currently recommend that artificial intelligence, data mining, and machine learning algorithms can predict health care service delivery, like the uptake of modern family planning, using different machine learning algorithms [22, 23]. Machine learning algorithms help predict problems and their determinants by using large datasets that help public health decision-making [24].

This study aimed to identify novel insights and predict not using modern family planning, using the recent East Africa (2019–2022) DHS dataset, by applying machine learning methods to model buildings, and SHAP values to identify important determinants. No review exists focusing on the prediction of not using modern family planning through the use of machine learning in the most recent East African DHS data set.

As a result, machine learning is important to identify the important factors and early predict not using family planning. Predicting not using family planning is important to fill the gap in meeting the need for family planning, which is essential to reducing financial barriers, maternal and child mortality, and societal pressure. This leads to a recommendation that policymakers or program managers can apply based on the prediction and do an intervention like awareness creation, improving health education, enhancing speed and quality of public services, and improving policy design for health education.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in east African countries using the DHS dataset, representing five nations. Geographically, east Africa is a sub-region of Africa that includes 20 internationally known countries, among which Ethiopia, Kenya, Madagascar, Tanzania, and Rwanda conducted the study.

Data sources

This study utilized measured DHS program data, accessed online, comprising the Women's Record (IR). Among 20 East African countries, 5 DHS datasets were eligible for analysis since the rest of the East African countries had no recorded family planning and we did not get the DHS dataset in the years 2019–2022, as shown in Table 1.

Population, and eligibility criteria

In this study, women in east Africa aged 15 to 49 years and all sexually active, married, or living in unions during the survey period who were in the selected enumeration areas at the time of DHS data collection were the study populations and included in this study. Women who were sterilized, infertile, or not sexually active were not included in the study population.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

This study used a weighted sample of 92,564 women aged 15 to 49 years across five east African countries using the recent DHS dataset. A two-stage stratified cluster sampling technique was used to select study participants. In the first step, a stratified sample of enumeration areas (EAs) was selected at random; in the second stage, households were selected using systematic random sampling in the selected EAs. In each selected household, women were interviewed with an individual questionnaire.

Study variables and measurements

In this investigation, the outcome variables were not used in modern family planning. In the DHS survey, the mother was asked, “Did she use modern family planning. Those mothers who said that they had were coded as ‘Yes’ and those who responded no or not using modern family planning were coded as ‘No’.

According to the literature review, the predictors for not using modern family planning include use of family planning, place of residence, knowledge about family planning, marital status and working status of women, women’s education, wealth index, age, country, and ever-terminated pregnancy.

Data analysis procedure

Data processing is a machine learning method that converts unprocessed data into a comprehensible format [25]. Machine learning (ML) algorithms were implemented in Python 3.10.2 using Jupiter Notebook, and some basic packages like Panda, Scikit-Learn, Imblearn, Numpy, and Seaborne were utilized for gathering data, preparing data, discretizing data, transforming data, and choosing, training, and evaluating models. Data prepossessing

In this study, we employed data preprocessing to eliminate missing values, noisy data, and outliers since it was done in its intended form. In this study, we used four major methods of data processing: data cleaning, data integration, data transformation, and discretizing.

Data cleaning

As data cleaning entails finding and fixing mistakes, inconsistencies, and inaccuracies in data, it is crucial for improved machine learning model performance [26]. In this study, we employed data cleaning, which typically includes missing value imputation, to remove noise and outliers. Raw data is always missing, and that data cannot be sent through modeling, building, or testing [27]. In this study, before data processing, 11 features were missing; among those, 10 were categorical, and one feature was continued and imputed by the median and mean values, respectively. One of the simplest and most straightforward methods for imputing missing values for categorical variables is mode imputation [28].

Feature selection

In this study, there are many features in the dataset, including over a thousand features. However, not all of these features are always relevant; therefore, feature selection is essential since unnecessary features during model training cause us to degrade the model’s overall accuracy, increase its complexity, limit its capacity to be generalized, and bias the model [29, 30]. We used wrapper feature selection-based machine learning algorithms and shape values to select relevant features for model building. Wrapper is essential for quickly determining how variables interact and for determining which feature subset is best for the intended machine learning method [31].

Class balancing

Machine learning algorithms are prone to bias toward the majority class when given an unbalanced data set [32, 33]. To avoid machine learning models biased toward the majority class (using family planning in this study), the training data was balanced using the synthetic minority oversampling technique. We employed SMOTE oversampling by creating synthetic examples (new observations) that resemble the minority class by interpolating between minority classes samples in the feature space rather than creating exact copies of existing examples.

Data transformation

On the other hand, data transformation entails transforming the data into a format that is appropriate for analysis; this may entail changing the data’s types, scaling, normalizing, or renaming [34]. In this study, we employed one-hot-Encoding techniques implemented using Python to encode categorical to dummy variables, with each category as a separate variable coded as 0 or 1 to indicate not utilizing family planning and utilizing family planning, respectively. Additionally, we scale the dataset before building the model to make it look normal and suitable for analysis and easy model training.

Data discretization and integration

With discretization, the range of an attribute (its values) is divided into intervals to minimize the number of values for a given continuous attribute [35]. In this study, we performed data discretization, which involves converting continuous variables into discrete features in accordance with DHS criteria to limit outlier influence and reduce noise, to make the data easier to understand and analyze. Mother-age variables in this study were continued to be discrete in intervals according to DHS guidelines. In this study we used five east Africa DHS dataset, we integrate based on identification variables, Sort both data files by the identification variables, and Determine the base (primary) file finally merge by using python software.

Model selection

In this study, the dependent variables were subjected to a binary classification since not utilizing family planning was a categorical variable with two categories, such as “using family planning” and “not using family planning.” Model selection: after the data has been prepared and divided into training and testing tests, suitable models are selected to perform the training. Since the outcome variable was categorical, the task was a classification task, and appropriate classifiers needed to be selected to conduct the prediction. The classification algorithms used for this analysis were Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), XGB (Extreme Gradient Boosting), SVM (Support Victor Machine), and K-Nearest Neighbor Decision Tree (DT) classifiers.

Model training

Finally, the whole data was split into training and testing by randomly assigning 74,051 (80%) of the data for model training and 18,513 (20%) for testing the trained model. But a tenfold cross-validation method on the training data was used for training the models. Tenfold divides all observations into 10 equal-sized groups of samples called folds, and 9 folds are used to train the model, and then 1 fold is used for testing 10 times repeatedly. Hence, the ten-fold cross-validation performance measure is the average of the values computed in this loop.

Model evaluation

In this study, the performance of predictive models was evaluated by testing a dataset. We divided the data into hold-out method training (80%) and test (20%) sets and repeated this process five times. Then, the performance of the trained models was evaluated using the test set based on the criteria of accuracy score, ROC curve, and precision (P), recall (R), and F-measure as follows:

We used a 10-fold cross-validation procedure on the training set to determine the ideal values of the hyper-parameters for Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), XGB (Extreme Gradient Boosting), SVM (Support Victor Machine), and K-Nearest Neighbor Decision Tree (DT). Once the greatest value of the receiver operating characteristic was established, hyper parameters were selected. Five times, we went through this process using various training and test divisions.

Supervised ML Classifier results

The goal of this study was to predict not using family planning and identify important determinants of not using family planning among reproductive women’s. Supervised machine learning algorithms were considered, such as Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), XGB (Extreme Gradient Boosting), SVM (Support Victor Machine), and K-Nearest Neighbor Decision Tree (DT), in order to achieve the goal. The model trained using the hold-out method selected supervised machine learning algorithms using both balanced and unbalanced training data, and. accuracy, AUC, precision, recall, and F-measure are the parameters used to evaluate the performance of the model. The predicted XGB model correctly predicted 11,981 (49%) women who had utilizing family planning and 12,052 (49%) women who had not using family planning, and XGB model incorrectly predict 16(0.8%) women’s not using family planning, and 301(1.2%). Discussion

This study used a machine learning model for predicting not using family planning and its determinants among reproductive-age women in east Africa that can be used for intervention. This study implemented machine learning to predict the non-use of modern family planning among East African women. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) model exhibited superior with accuracy (98.7%), precision (99.8%), AUC ROC curve (99.9%), and recall (98%). Key determinants included education, age, rural residence, marital status, contraceptive knowledge, and smoking. Such models aid evidence-based decision-making and uncover hidden patterns in data. Our results conflict with those made in Bangladesh, which indicated that neural networks (NN) were applied to predict contraceptive practice. The neural network model was highly significant for predicting contraceptive practice, with an accuracy of 79.34% [39]. However, Iqramul Haq has not used extreme gradient boosting in their study, and this might be a result of the disparities in socioeconomic status, culture, way of life, and study area.

This study showed that mothers who were married were more likely to use family planning compared with those who were not married. Married or in-union women of reproductive age who have awareness of pregnancy and have knowledge of family planning. These findings are supported by similar findings in low- and middle-income countries [40].

Regarding women’s working status, those who are employed may have more access to information and resources related to family planning compared to those who are not working. Working women may also have more autonomy and decision-making power in their households, which can influence their ability to make choices about when to have children and how many to have. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in South Asia, Ethiopia [41, 42].

Similarly, lack of family planning knowledge was one of the determinants of not using family planning. Women who are aware of different contraceptive methods and their effectiveness are more likely to use them to prevent unintended pregnancies. This finding supported the study conducted in Ethiopia and Cameroon [5, 43]. Lack of knowledge about contraceptives can lead to unplanned pregnancies and larger family sizes. This result was supported by findings from a study conducted in Pakistan [44]. It also agrees with the results of other research conducted in Indonesia. It suggests that when a household grows in size, women and other family members who may have a say in reproductive matters may grow increasingly persuaded of the benefits of family planning techniques [45].

Like wisely, it has been demonstrated that women who are educated are more likely to have knowledge of family planning than those who are not educated. This could be due to the fact that women with a primary education may be unaware of where contraceptive methods can be found and when to use them. This result is also consistent with research carried out in Ethiopia [42, 46].

Similarly, residency, which is another major predictor of not using family planning, when it comes to healthcare resources, urban women could have easier access than rural women, including family planning clinics and information. Due to unequal access to resources, rural women may have fewer alternatives for contraception and be less able to make educated decisions about family planning. The study conducted in Ethiopia provided evidence in favor of this conclusion [18].

In this study, religion and faith influence the use of family planning; in comparison, Islamic religion does not use family planning. This study was support by study [47, 48], religion contend that family planning is an outside plot by the West to limit the expansion and power of the Islamic world.

A machine learning model is important for evidence-based decision-making about not using family planning and identifying important determinants of not using family planning. Additionally, machine learning models can find hidden patterns in data, which makes it possible to extrapolate such patterns to new, previously undetected data.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study used the classification machine learning method compared, identified, and helped recognize specific risk factors related to not using modern family planning among reproductive age women in east Africa countries that can be used as intervention targets. We proposed six machine learning classifier models such as the RF, DT, SVM, XGB, k-nearest neighbor, and logistic regression, XGB was the best predictive model with an accuracy of 98.7%, a precision of 99.8%, an AUC ROC curve of 99.9%, and a recall of 98% in this study.

The results show living in a rural, being single, not knowing how to use a contraceptive, age 25–29, lack of education, and smoking cigarettes were the most important predictors of not using family planning in East Africa. On the other hand, among women who were of reproductive age, the use of family planning was significantly correlated with living in an urban area, being married, being exposed to radio, being wealthy or poor, and having had a pregnancy terminated is the most predictor for using modern family planning.

This study also revealed that women’s educational level is also a key determinant of not using family planning practices. Women with lower levels of education may have no knowledge about contraceptives and reproductive health, as well as no opportunities for economic independence and decision-making power within their families. XGBoost proved effective in predicting non-use of modern family planning, offering valuable insights for interventions and policy decisions in East Africa. Therefore, policymakers should consider the research’s findings and develop a plan for boosting modern family planning in East Africa based on the variables that have been found to be significant.

Strength and limitation

This study had some limitations. First, DHS does not include behavioral factors like attitude variables and knowledge factors.
