A Characterization of Curves that Lie on a Totally Umbilical Surface of a Space Form
Rafael López

Abstract
. We give necessary and sufficient conditions on the curvature and the torsion of a regular curve of the space forms ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} and 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} to be contained in a totally umbilical surface. In case that the curve has constant torsion, we obtain the value of the curvature of the curve. Also numerical pictures of these curves are shown.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
53A04, 53A05, 53B20

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

In this paper, we investigate the following problem:

Given a surface
S
in a
3
-dimensional space
M^{3}
, how to characterize a regular curve
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to M^{3}
to be contained in
S
in terms of the curvature and the torsion of
\alpha
?

Although the formulation of this problem is simple, surprisingly its solution, if any, is hard and only in a few cases of surfaces
S
a satisfactory answer is known. A regular curve
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to M^{3}
, which it is not a geodesic, has defined its curvature
\kappa
and its torsion
\tau
. If the ambient space
M^{3}
has constant curvature, both quantities
\kappa
and
\tau
determine the curve
\alpha
up to a rigid motion of
M^{3}
. For general manifolds
M^{3}
the uniqueness modulo congruences is valid once is prescribed initial conditions on the position and the Frenet frame
[
2
,
3
]
.

Let now
S
be a given surface in
M^{3}
. If the curve
\alpha
is contained in
S
, that is,
\alpha(I)\subset S
, there is not an
a priori
condition on
\kappa
and
\tau
that characterizes the fact that the image of
\alpha
lies contained in
S
because the notions of
\kappa
and
\tau
are intrinsic of the curve.

An interesting application of the above problem is in computer vision
[
10
,
11
,
13
,
15
,
17
]
. Suppose that we have a spatial curve in Euclidean space of which we have sufficient information data with respect to the position of its points. From these data, we are able to compute the curvature and the torsion of the curve. The problems asks whether such a curve is contained on a given surface
S
of Euclidean space. As one can notice, the problem is difficult because the data corresponds to a one-dimensional object, whereas the object we are looking for is two-dimensional.

Without loss of generality, we will assume in this paper that all curves are parametrized by arc length, being
s
is the arc length parameter. We also assume sufficiently regularity of the curves, at least
C^{4}
, although one may demand something weaker that
C^{4}
by choosing an appropriate frame different than the Frenet frame.

Following with the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, two cases of surfaces
S
have a known answer to the problem:

(1)

S
is a plane
. Then
\alpha
lies contained in
S
if and only if
\tau=0
.

(2)

S
is a sphere
. Suppose that
R
is the radius of the sphere. If
\alpha
lies contained in
S
with
\tau\not=0
, then

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=R^{2},

(1.1)

where the prime
(^{\prime})
stands for the derivative with respect to
s
. If
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
, then the converse is true. If the radius of the sphere is unknown, then
\alpha
lies contained in some sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
if and only if

\frac{\tau}{\kappa}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}=0.

(1.2)

Notice that Eq. (
1.1
) is a third order differential equation in the coordinates of the curve whereas (
1.2
) is of fourth order. Equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) appear in many textbooks of differential geometry: see for example, Ex. 13, Sect. 1.5, in
[
9
]
. Since there is more than one way to prove (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), we review different approaches given in the literature. For example, in
[
25
, p. 32]
, Struik exploits the idea of contact between curves and surfaces to obtain (
1.1
) but to prove (
1.2
) Struik assumes that
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
as in
[
9
]
. Kühnel in
[
18
, Th. 2. 10]
also uses the idea of contact to characterize with (
1.2
) spherical curves by using that if a curve
\alpha
has a contact of order
k
with a surface
S=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:G({\bf x})=0\}
then all derivatives of
s\mapsto G(\alpha(s))
up to order
k
vanish (see also
[
16
, Lem. 19.2]
). Yet another proof using order of contact is given by da Silva
[
5
, Th. 2.1]
where the author applies the theory of contact as Kühnel did but using Bishop frames. Other question related with (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) is that one assumes that the torsion
\tau
does not have zeros. A characterization that does not require the torsion to be non-zero can be found in
[
27
]
. Extensions of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) when the ambient space is the Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{3}
and the surface
S
is the pseudo-sphere or the hyperbolic plane appear in
[
4
,
12
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

Other simple surface
S
where we can pose the problem in Euclidean space is a circular cylinder,
S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x^{2}+y^{2}=R^{2}\}
,
R>0
. However, the problem is far to have a successful answer. In
[
15
]
the authors give necessary conditions for
\alpha
to be included in
S
in terms of a resultant of two polynomials whose coefficients are expressed by
\kappa
,
\tau
and their first and second derivatives. More recently, in
[
24
]
the authors derive some conditions when
\alpha
is regarded as a curve at constant separation from the axis of the cylinder. Then they obtain a differential equation involving
\kappa
,
\tau
and the angle
\xi
between the separation vector from the axis and the normal vector of
\alpha
: see Props. 4 and 5 in
[
24
]
.

A different approach to the initial problem is using different notions of curvatures. Bishop, in a remarkable paper
[
1
]
, introduced a new frame for a spatial curve
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In the new frame
\{T=\alpha^{\prime},N_{1},N_{2}\}
the key property is that
T
and
N_{i}^{\prime}
are parallel,
i=1,2
. In the corresponding Bishop equations, two new curvatures
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are defined. Comparing with Frenet frames, Bishop frames exist even if the curve has points with
\kappa=0
. Bishop characterized curves contained in spheres by means of a linear relation of
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
. Generalizations of this result have been obtained when
S
is a geodesic sphere of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
\mathbb{H}^{3}

[
7
]
or a pseudosphere of the Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{3}

[
4
]
.

In this paper we consider the initial problem when
M^{3}
is the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or the
3
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
S
is a totally umbilical surface. More precisely, we extend the characterizations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) for all totally umbilical surfaces of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. To distinguish with the use of Bishop frames given in
[
4
,
7
]
, we will follow the same ideas of
[
9
]
using Frenet frames and requiring that
\tau
has not zeros. Notice that
\tau\not=0
is an open condition so if
\tau(s_{0})\not=0
at some point
s_{0}
, then
\tau\not=0
in a subinterval of
\alpha
around
s_{0}
.

Let
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
be the space form of constant sectional curvature
c\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Let
S\subset\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
be a totally umbilical surface. If
c=0
, then
S
is a plane or a round sphere and the problem has been solved as it has been previously explained. If
c=\pm 1
and when
S
is a geodesic sphere, equation (
1.2
) appeared in
[
7
, Thm. 4]
(see Rem.
2.1
below). In the present paper and for the use of Frenet frames, we use the hyperquadric model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and Eq. (
1.1
) will be extended for all totally umbilical surfaces. The value of
R
will be substituted in terms of the mean curvature
H
of
S
.

We consider the hyperquadric model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. In the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with standard coordinates
p=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
, define the metric

\langle,\rangle=dx_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}+dx_{3}^{2}+c\,dx_{4}^{2}.

(1.3)

Consider the hyperquadric
\mathcal{H}=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\langle p,p\rangle=c\}
. In case that
c=-1
, we only take the component of
\mathcal{H}
where the
x_{4}
-coordinate is positive. Then
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
is isometric to
\mathcal{H}
endowed with the induced metric
\langle,\rangle
from
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Notice that if
c=1
, then
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{S}^{3}
is the round sphere in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
of radius
1
and centered at the origin.

The totally umbilical surfaces of
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
are the intersection of affine hyperplanes of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
\mathcal{H}
, that is,

U_{\vec{a},\sigma}=\{p\in\mathcal{H}:\langle p,\vec{a}\rangle=\sigma\},

where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
\vec{a}\not=0
, with
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\epsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}

[
23
]
. If
c=1
, then necessarily
\epsilon=1
and
\sigma\in(-1,1)
. We give the classification of the totally umbilical surfaces.
For distinguishing if necessary, we add a superscript
()^{h}
and
()^{s}
to the notation of
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
depending if the surface is contained in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, respectively.

Consider the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. In this case
c=-1
and the metric (
1.3
) is Lorentzian. The classification of the totally umbilical surfaces depends on the causal character of the affine hyperplane:

(1)

totally geodesic planes:
\epsilon=1
,
\sigma=0
;

(2)

equidistant surfaces:
\epsilon=1
,
\sigma\not=0
;

(3)

horospheres:
\epsilon=0
,
\sigma\not=0
;

(4)

geodesic spheres:
\epsilon=-1
,
|\sigma|>1
.

See Fig.
1
, left. A unit normal vector field on
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
is

\xi(p)=-\lambda(\vec{a}+\sigma p),\quad\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+\sigma^{2}}}.

If
A
is the shape operator on
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
, then
A\xi=\lambda\sigma\cdot{\rm id}
, which proves that
U^{h}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is totally umbilical. Notice that the mean curvature of
U^{h}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is

H=\lambda\sigma=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\epsilon+\sigma^{2}}}

(1.4)

and the extrinsic curvature is
K_{ex}=\sigma^{2}/(\epsilon+\sigma^{2})
. The Gauss equation implies that the intrinsic curvature
K
is constant, namely,
K=K_{ext}-1=-\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon+\sigma^{2}}
.

Left: Totally umbilical surfaces in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}

\mathbb{S}^{3}
: totally geodesic spheres (blue solid curve) and geodesic spheres (red dotted curve).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
be a totally umbilical surface of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Let
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{3}
be a regular curve.

(1)

Case
\sigma=0
. If
\alpha
is contained in
U_{\vec{a},0}^{h}
then
\tau=0
.

(2)

Case
\sigma\not=0
. If
\alpha
is contained in
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
with
\tau\not=0
, then

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=\frac{1}{H^{2}},

(1.5)

where
H
is the mean curvature of
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
.

Reciprocally, if
\alpha
has constant zero torsion
\tau=0
then
\alpha
is contained in a totally geodesic plane. In case that
\tau\not=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
, if

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=C>0

(1.6)

for some constant
C
, then
\alpha
is contained in a totally umbilical surface of mean curvature
H
, where
C=1/H^{2}
: an equidistant surface if
C>1
, a horosphere if
C=1
or a geodesic sphere if
C<1
.

If it is unknown the type of totally umbilical surface, the analogous result to (
1.2
) is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{3}
be a regular curve. Then
\alpha
is contained in a totally geodesic plane if and only if
\tau=0
. If
\tau\not=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
, then
\alpha
is contained in a totally non-geodesic umbilical surface if and only if

\frac{\tau}{\kappa}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}=0.

(1.7)

Consider now the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. The totally umbilical surfaces
U^{s}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
are totally geodesic spheres (or great spheres) if
\sigma=0
or geodesic spheres if
\sigma\not=0
. In the latter case, the radius of the sphere is
R=\sqrt{1-\sigma^{2}}
. See Fig.
1
, right. Now the unit normal vector of
U^{s}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is
\xi(p)=(a-\sigma p)/\sqrt{1-\sigma^{2}}
and
A\xi=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\sigma^{2}}}\cdot{\rm id}
. The mean curvature is
H=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\sigma^{2}}}
and the intrinsic curvature is
K=\frac{1}{1-\sigma^{2}}
.

(1)

Case

.

(2)

Case

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=\frac{1}{H^{2}},

.

Theorem 1.4
.

[

Let
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{3}
be a regular curve. Then
\alpha
is contained in a totally geodesic sphere if and only if
\tau=0
. If
\tau\not=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
, then

\frac{\tau}{\kappa}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}=0

In all these results, the dimension of the ambient space is
3
. For higher dimensions and in arbitrary manifolds
M^{n+1}
,
the generalization of the Bishop frame, the so-called rotation minimizing frame (RM frame in short), also is useful in characterizations of curves. A RM frame along a curve
\alpha
is a frame
\{T,N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}\}
where
T
and
\nabla_{T}N_{i}
are parallel. In
[
7
, Thm. 3]
, the authors prove that a regular curve
\alpha
lies on a geodesic hypersphere of radius
R
of
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
(resp.
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
) if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\kappa_{i}+\coth(R)=0
(resp.
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\kappa_{i}+\cot(R)=0
). Here
a_{i}
are real constants and
\kappa_{i}
are the curvatures of the RM frame. In the present paper, and using the same ideas, we extend this result to curves contained in totally umbilical hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{M}^{n+1}(c)
,
c\not=0
(for geodesic hyperspheres, it is the same result that
[
7
]
). Notice that now the regularity that we demand is weaker than
C^{4}
, and
C^{2}
is enough.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{M}^{n+1}(c)
be a
C^{2}
curve,
c\in\{-1,1\}
. If
\alpha
is contained in a totally umbilical hypersurface
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
, then there is a linear relation

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\kappa_{i}+\sigma=0,

(1.11)

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, are real constants. Reciprocally, if (
1.11
) holds, then
\alpha
is contained in a totally umbilical hypersurface of
\mathbb{M}^{n+1}(c)
.

Identity (
1.11
) is valid for curves included in totally umbilical hypersurfaces
\Sigma
of
any
manifold
M^{n+1}
where, up to a constant in (
1.11
), it holds
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\kappa_{i}(s)-H(\alpha(s))=0
and
H
is the mean curvature of
\Sigma
: see
[
8
, Thm. 1]
. Thus the novelty here is that we prove the converse when
M^{n+1}
is a space form.

Proof of these results will be done in Sect.
2
. After each one of the proofs, we will compare the techniques and the results employed with that of the existing literature to show what is actually new in this paper. In the last Section
3
we investigate those curves of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
contained in totally umbilical surfaces with constant torsion. We will derive in Thm.
3.1
the expression of the curvature and some numerical pictures of these curves are shown.

2.
Proof of the results

Let
\alpha\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
be a regular (non-geodesic) curve parametrized by arc length. Let
\kappa>0
and
\tau
be the curvature and the torsion of
\alpha
, respectively. Associated to
\alpha
there is a Frenet frame
\{T,N,B\}
whose Frenet equations are

\begin{split}\nabla_{T}T&=\kappa N,\\
\nabla_{T}N&=-\kappa T+\tau B,\\
\nabla_{T}B&=-\tau N.\end{split}

Here
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
. If
\nabla^{0}
is the Levi-Civita connection of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with respect to the metric
\langle,\rangle
, then
\nabla
is
\nabla^{0}
are related by the identity

\nabla^{0}_{X}Y=\nabla_{X}Y-c\,\langle X,Y\rangle p,

(2.1)

where
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{M}^{3}(c))
are tangent vectors at
p\in\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Necessity
. Since
\alpha
is contained in
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}^{h}
, then
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle=\sigma
for all
s\in I
. Differentiating with respect to
s
, we have
\langle T,\vec{a}\rangle=0
. Differentiating again and using the Frenet equations together (
2.1
), we have

\langle\nabla_{T}T+\langle T,T\rangle\alpha,\vec{a}\rangle=0,

that is

\kappa\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle+\sigma=0.

(2.2)

We distinguish two cases.

(1)

Case
\sigma=0
. This corresponds when
U_{\vec{a},0}
is a totally geodesic plane, in particular,
\epsilon=1
. Since
\kappa\not=0
, then (
2.2
) yields
\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle=0
and differentiating with respect to
s
we have
\tau\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle=0
. This implies
\tau=0
identically in
I
because if
\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle=0
, using that
\{T,N,B,\alpha\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{4},\langle,\rangle)
, we would have
1=\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=-\langle\alpha,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}=0
, a contradiction.

(2)

Case
\sigma\not=0
. Differentiating (
2.2
) together the second Frenet equation and the fact that
\langle T,\vec{a}\rangle=0
, we get

\kappa^{\prime}\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle+\kappa\tau\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle=0.

(2.3)

On the other hand, because
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\epsilon
and the fact that
\{T,N,B,\alpha\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{4},\langle,\rangle)
, we deduce from identities (
2.2
) and (
2.3
)

\begin{split}\epsilon&=\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}+\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}-\langle\alpha,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}\\
&=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{2}}-\sigma^{2}.\end{split}

This implies

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=1+\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma^{2}}.

This identity together (
1.4
) is just (
1.5
).

Sufficiency
. We distinguish two cases according to
\tau
.

(1)

Case
\tau=0
. Then
\nabla_{T}B=0
, hence
\nabla_{T}^{0}B=0
by (
2.1
). This proves that
B=B(s)
is a constant function. Let
\vec{a}=B
, in particular,
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\langle B,B\rangle=1
. Since the derivative of the function
s\mapsto\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle
is
\langle T,\vec{a}\rangle=0
, then the function
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle
is constant. Because
\vec{a}=B
is a tangent vector of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, then
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle=0
for all
s\in I
, proving that
\alpha
is contained in the totally geodesic plane
U_{\vec{a},0}
.

(2)

Case
\tau\not=0
. Define the function
\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{4}
by

\beta(s)=\alpha(s)+\frac{1}{\kappa(s)}N(s)-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}(s)}{\kappa(s)^{2}\tau(s)}B(s).

(2.4)

Using the Frenet equations, we have

\frac{d}{ds}\beta(s)=\left(\frac{\tau}{\kappa}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}\right)B.

(2.5)

The derivative of (
1.6
) yields

\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\tau\kappa^{2}}\left(\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}-\frac{\tau}{\kappa}\right)=0.

(2.6)

Since
\kappa^{\prime}=0
, the parenthesis must vanish, hence (
2.5
) leads to
\frac{d}{ds}\beta(s)=0
for all
s\in I
. This proves that
\beta
is a constant function. Let
\beta(s)=\vec{b}
for some vector
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Multiplying (
2.4
) by
\alpha
and because
\alpha
is orthogonal to
N
and
B
, we have
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{b}\rangle=-1
for all
s\in I
. Moreover, using (
1.6
) and (
2.4
) we get

\langle\vec{b},\vec{b}\rangle=-1+\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=-1+C.

If
C=1
, then
\langle\vec{b},\vec{b}\rangle=0
, and
\alpha
is contained in the horosphere
U^{h}_{\vec{b},-1}
. If
C\not=1
, let
\vec{a}=\vec{b}/\sqrt{|C-1|}
. Then
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=1
if
C>1
and
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=-1
if
C<1
. In both cases,
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle=-1/\sqrt{|C-1|}:=\sigma
. Thus
\alpha
is contained in the equidistant surface
U^{h}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
if
C>1
or in the geodesic sphere
U^{h}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
if
C<1
. Finally, once we know that
\alpha
is contained in a totally umbilical surface, the value of
C
is given by (
1.5
), that is,
C=1/H^{2}
.

Equation (
1.5
) can be also obtained from the computations done in
[
8
]
and that the authors did not mention. Indeed, in Remark 1 of
[
8
]
the authors employ Frenet frames for curves in a generic Riemannian
3
-manifold
M^{3}
. Following the calculations and notations of
[
8
]
, let
\alpha
be a curve contained in a totally umbilical surface
S\subset M^{3}
. Let
\xi
and
H
be the unit normal and the mean curvature of
S
, respectively. Since
S
is totally umbilical, then the tangential component of
\nabla_{T}\xi
vanishes. Thus
\nabla_{T}\xi=c_{1}N+c_{2}B
. Then
c_{1}=H/\kappa
,
c_{2}^{\prime}=-\tau c_{1}
and
c_{1}^{\prime}=\tau c_{2}
. The same computation of
[
8
, Rem. 1]
gives

1=\langle\xi,\xi\rangle=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=\left(\frac{H}{\kappa}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{H}{\kappa}\right)^{\prime 2}.

Since

is constant because

is a totally umbilical surface, and assuming

\frac{1}{H^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^{\prime 2}.

This identity coincides with (
1.5
).

Necessity

. Differentiating twice again, we have

and

\kappa^{\prime}\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle+\kappa\tau\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle=0.

The case
\sigma=0

Thus

\begin{split}1&=\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}+\langle B,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}+\langle\alpha,\vec{a}\rangle^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\\

This gives (

2.4.
Proof of Theorems
1.5

First we recall that RM frames along regular curves are frames of type
\{T,N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}\}
, where
T
and
\nabla_{T}N_{i}
are parallel. Therefore, the RM equations are

\begin{split}\nabla_{T}T&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}N_{i},\\
\nabla_{T}N_{i}&=-\kappa_{i}N_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq n.\end{split}

(2.7)

The functions
\kappa_{i}
are called the curvatures associated to the RM frame.

As we pointed out in the Introduction, the first part of the statement was done in
[
8
]
. For the sake of completeness, we recall it. Since
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle=\sigma
, the derivative with respect to
s
gives
\langle T,\vec{a}\rangle=0
. Differentiating with respect to
s
again and using (
2.1
) and (
2.7
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}\langle N_{i},\vec{a}\rangle+\sigma=0.

(2.8)

On the other hand, from (
2.7
) one gets

\frac{d}{ds}\langle N_{i},\vec{a}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{T}N_{i},\vec{a}\rangle=-\kappa_{i}\langle T,\vec{a}\rangle=0.

Thus
\langle N_{i},\vec{a}\rangle
is a constant function
a_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. This together (
2.8
) proves (
1.11
).

We now prove the converse. Define
\beta(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}N_{i}(s)-\sigma\alpha(s)
. Then (
2.7
) implies

\begin{split}\beta^{\prime}(s)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\nabla_{T}N_{i}(s)-\sigma T(s)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\kappa_{i}(s)T(s)-\sigma T(s)\\
&=0.\end{split}

Thus there is
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}=\mathbb{M}^{n+1}(c)
such that
\beta(s)=\vec{a}
for all
s\in I
, that is,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}N_{i}(s)-\sigma\alpha(s)=\vec{a}.

In particular, if we multiply by
\alpha(s)
we obtain
\langle\alpha(s),\vec{a}\rangle=-c\sigma
. This proves that
\alpha
is contained in the totally umbilical hypersurface
U_{\vec{a},-c\sigma}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
1.5
shows how powerful RM frames are in comparison with the Frenet frame. Indeed, from Remark 2 of
[
7
]
we have a taste of how complicated characterizing spherical curves in higher dimensions can get. For curves in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, it is possible to find a generic equation characterizing spherical curves using in Frenet frames following Thm. 5.1 of
[
6
]
which generalizes the given one in Rem. 2 of
[
8
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

Equation (
1.11
) can be interpreted as model-dependent because of
\sigma
in the right hand-side. However, we can renormalize the linear relation (
1.11
) such that
a_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}^{2}=1
. In such a case, the constants
a_{i}
can be interpreted as the coordinates of the unit normal of the hypersurface along the curve whereas

changes to be the mean curvature of the totally umbilical hypersurface. Notice that in

, the value of
\coth(R)
(resp.

) is the mean curvature of the geodesic hypersphere of radius
R
in

(resp.
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
): see also
[
8
, Thm. 1]
.

3.
Curves with constant torsion contained in totally umbilical surfaces

In this section we study curves in a totally umbilical surface of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with constant torsion. If instead of curves with constant torsion we ask for those curves with constant curvature
\kappa
, then the curves are known. If the curve is included in a totally umbilical surface
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
with constant mean curvature
H
then
\kappa=H+\kappa_{g}
, where
\kappa_{g}
is the geodesic curvature of the curve. Thus we deduce that
\kappa
is constant if and only if
\kappa_{g}
is constant. The curves of constant geodesic curvature in
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
are known because
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is isometric to a space form. If the ambient space is
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, then it is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}(c)
and a sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(c)
in case of a horosphere, geodesic plane or equidistant surface, and geodesic sphere, respectively. If
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is included in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, then it is isometric to a sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(c)
.

Suppose now that
\alpha
is contained in a totally umbilical surface
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
of
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
and that the torsion
\tau
of
\alpha
is constant. When the ambient space is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, spherical curves with constant torsion are studied in
[
14
]
and parametrizations of such curves are obtained in terms of hypergeometric functions. We extend these results in the context of totally umbilical surfaces of space forms. We will discard the case
\tau=0
identically because then
\alpha
is included in a totally geodesic surface and in such a case, the curvature is an arbitrary function. Assume the case that the torsion
\tau
is a non-zero constant and we will derive explicitly the curvature
\kappa
of the curve. We will exclude the particular case that
\kappa
is constant which it has been previously considered.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
be a totally umbilical surface of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. If
\alpha
is a regular curve contained in
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
with constant non-zero torsion
\tau
and
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
, then the curvature of
\alpha
is

\kappa(s)=\frac{|H|}{\sin(\tau s+a)},\quad a\in\mathbb{R},

(3.1)

where
H
is the mean curvature of
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
.

It will always be understood that the domain of
\alpha
is maximal in order to have well defined functions and that the curvature
\kappa
is positive.

Proof.

Let
\tau\not=0
be a real number. Since the torsion is non-zero, then
U_{\vec{a},\sigma}
is not a totally geodesic surface, in particular,
H\not=0
. Then Eqs. (
1.5
) and (
1.8
) can be written as

\tau^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^{2}+{\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^{\prime}}^{2}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{H^{2}}.

Let
w(s)=1/\kappa(s)
. Notice that
w
is not a constant function because
\kappa^{\prime}\not=0
. Then the above equation reads as
\tau^{2}w^{2}+w^{\prime 2}=\tau^{2}/H^{2}
whose solution is

w(s)=\frac{1}{|H|}\sin(\tau s+a)
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
. This gives (
3.1
).
∎

Once we know
\kappa
and
\tau
, the curve
\alpha
is determined up to rigid motions of
\mathbb{M}^{3}(c)
. A further step would be to get an explicit parametrization of the curve
\alpha
. This will involve the solution of (
3.1
) together the fact that
\alpha
is parametrized by arc-length. Such as it occurs in
[
14
]
, hypergeometric functions are used. However, our interest in this section is to give numerical pictures of curves with constant torsion contained in totally umbilical surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We will employ the software Mathematica to make these figures
[
26
]
.

We begin with curves in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. In order to visualize the curve, we will show the picture in the upper-halfspace model
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
where
\alpha
will be a curve in the
3
-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
instead of
\mathcal{H}
. The numerical computations will be done first in the hyperquadric model
\mathcal{H}
and finally, the isometry

\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+},\quad\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{3}+x_{4}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{3}+x_{4}},\frac{1}{x_{3}+x_{4}}\right)

between both models of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
will give us the desired picture.

In the example that we show in this section, we take a horosphere of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
as the totally umbilical surface. After an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, a horosphere in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
is a horizontal plane. Thus
\alpha
will be a curve contained in a (horizontal) plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which we identify with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider in
\mathcal{H}
the horosphere
U^{h}_{\vec{a},1}
, where
\vec{a}=(0,0,1,-1)
. The equations of
U^{h}_{\vec{a},1}
are
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-x_{4}^{2}=1
and
x_{3}+x_{4}=1
. This horosphere, via
\Phi
, is the horizontal plane of equation
z=1
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
, where
(x,y,z)
are the standard coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
.
Let
\tau
be the a non-zero real number which will be the torsion of the curve that we are looking for. Since
|H|=1
, then (
3.1
) gives
\kappa(s)=\frac{1}{\sin(\tau s+a)}
. Take
a=0
. Let

\alpha(s)=(x_{1}(s),x_{2}(s),x_{3}(s),1-x_{3}(s))

(3.2)

be a curve in
\mathbb{H}^{3}=\mathcal{H}
parametrized by arc-length with torsion
\tau
. This gives two equations, namely,

\begin{split}x_{1}(s)^{2}+x_{2}(s)^{2}+2x_{3}(s)&=0,\quad\mbox{because }\alpha(s)\in\mathbb{H}^{3},\\
x_{1}^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x_{2}^{\prime}(s)^{2}&=1,\quad\mbox{because }|\alpha^{\prime}(s)|=1.\end{split}

From the second equation, there is a smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
such that

\left\{\begin{split}x_{1}^{\prime}(s)&=\cos\theta(s),\\
x_{2}^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\theta(s).\end{split}\right.

(3.3)

We now calculate the curvature of
\alpha
. We know that
\kappa(s)=|\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)|
. From (
2.1
), we have

\begin{split}\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)&=\nabla^{0}_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)-\langle\alpha^{\prime}(s),\alpha^{\prime}(s)\rangle\alpha(s)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}(s)-\alpha(s)\\
&=(x_{1}^{\prime\prime}(s)-x_{1}(s),x_{2}^{\prime\prime}(s)-x_{2}(s),x_{3}^{\prime\prime}(s)-x_{3}(s),-x_{3}^{\prime\prime}(s)+x_{3}(s)-1).\end{split}

Using both equations (
3.3
), we obtain

\kappa(s)=|\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)|=\sqrt{1+\theta^{\prime}(s)^{2}}.

(3.4)

Knowing that
\kappa(s)=1/\sin(\tau s)
, the above equation reduces into
\theta^{\prime}(s)=\pm\cot(\tau s)
. The solution of this equation is

\theta(s)=\frac{1}{\tau}\log\sin(\tau s)
after choices of integration constants. Finally the curve
\alpha
given in (
3.2
) is determined by the ODE system

\begin{split}x_{1}^{\prime}(s)&=\cos(\frac{1}{\tau}\log\sin(\tau s)),\\
x_{2}^{\prime}(s)&=\sin(\frac{1}{\tau}\log\sin(\tau s)).\end{split}

Once we know
x_{1}(s)
and
x_{2}(s)
, the curve
\alpha
given in (
3.2
) is completely determined by the identities

x_{3}(s)=-\frac{x_{1}(s)^{2}+x_{2}(s)^{2}}{2},\quad x_{4}(s)=1+\frac{x_{1}(s)^{2}+x_{2}(s)^{2}}{2}.

We show in Fig.
2
two curves of constant torsions
\tau=1
and
\tau=2
for given initial conditions. Here we are identifying the horosphere
z=1
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since
\kappa(s)=1/\sin(\tau s)
, the domain of the curve is
(0,\frac{\pi}{\tau})
. In particular, as
s\to 0
and
s\to\frac{\pi}{\tau}
, we have
\kappa\to\infty
. This explains that the curve in Fig.
2
spirals very fast close to the end points of the curve.

We now present an example in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. For the geodesic sphere
U^{s}_{\vec{a},\sigma}
, let

and
\sigma=1/2
, in particular,

. Then

with the sphere

\mathbb{S}^{2}(R)=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=R^{2}\}
of radius

in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
thanks to the map

Again, let
\tau
be a non-zero real number. By choosing
a=0
, then the expression (
3.1
) gives
\kappa(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}\sin(\tau s)}
, where we are assuming
\sin(\tau s)>0
in order to ensure that
\kappa
is positive.

Let
\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s),z(s))
be a curve in
\mathbb{S}^{2}(R)
parametrized by arc-length. Since
\alpha(s)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}(R)
for all
s\in I
, we parametrize
\alpha
by

\alpha(s)=R(\cos\varphi(s)\cos\theta(s),\cos\varphi(s)\sin\theta(s),\sin\varphi(s)).

(3.5)

The fact that
\alpha
is parametrized by arc-length writes as

\varphi^{\prime 2}+\theta^{\prime 2}(\cos\varphi)^{2}=\frac{4}{3}.

(3.6)

For the computation of
\kappa
we know
\kappa=|\nabla_{T}T|
. From (

), we have

\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)=\nabla^{0}_{\alpha^{\prime}(s)}\alpha^{\prime}(s)+\langle\alpha^{\prime}(s),\alpha^{\prime}(s)\rangle\alpha(s)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}(s)+\alpha(s).

Then equation

&-\theta^{\prime 2}\left(\varphi^{\prime 2}(\cos(2\varphi)-3)+\cos(2\varphi)+1\right)+\theta^{\prime 4}(\cos\varphi)^{2}+\varphi^{\prime 4}-2\varphi^{\prime 2}+1\\
&=\frac{4}{9(\sin(\tau s))^{2}}.\end{split}
