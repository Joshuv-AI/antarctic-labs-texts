Correspondence: Cancer incidence and mortality after a first-ever venous thrombosis in northern Sweden
Authors: Minh-Hoang Tran, Kim-Huong Truong-Nguyen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe congratulate Hägg et al. on their study investigating cancer incidence and mortality following first-ever venous thromboembolism (VTE), which provides valuable insights into VTE as a potential marker for underlying malignancies. However, we highlight concerns regarding healthy user bias, the unclear follow-up duration, and inconsistent adjustment in the statistical analysis. We also suggest the use of the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to address competing risks, and the accurate reporting of sex-gender terminology. Lastly, we advise caution in concluding a high incidence of cancer following first-ever VTE without pre-VTE data for comparison and recommend acknowledging potential surveillance bias when interpreting the higher cancer detection rate within 6 months of VTE diagnosis.Keywords: venous thromboembolism, cancer, neoplasm, mortality

Dear Editor,

First, we would like to congratulate Hägg et al. on their study entitled “Cancer incidence and mortality after a first-ever venous thrombosis: a cohort study in northern Sweden,” published in the Thrombosis Journal [1]. Their findings provide valuable insights into the epidemiology of cancer following venous thromboembolism (VTE), highlighting the importance of considering VTE as a possible marker for underlying malignancies in clinical practice. However, we have some comments about their study design, statistical analysis, and data interpretation/reporting.

For the study design, given the nature of health examination programs where healthier individuals are more likely to seek preventive care, there is a risk of healthy user bias in this study setting [2]. The authors should address this issue and confirm whether this effect can significantly distort the findings and clinical implications. The authors also did not specify the outcome(s) determining the follow-up. This led to the unclearly defined duration of follow-up, as some participants were stopped being followed after cancer diagnosis despite another outcome of interest (mortality). The authors should clarify this to avoid misunderstanding.

For the statistical analysis, there seems to be an inconsistent adjustment between the exposure and the 2 outcomes. The authors investigated the total effect of first-ever VTE on cancer but only demonstrated the direct effect of first-ever VTE on mortality by conditioning on cancer (a potential mediator on the pathway from first-ever VTE to mortality). Thus, the reported findings might be incomparable to each other. Additionally, as competing risks (mortality versus cancer) may arise from the time-to-event analytical approach in this study, the authors should consider other methods to address this, such as the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model [3].

For the data interpretation/reporting, as the focus of this study seems to be on the biological association between first-ever VTE and cancer or mortality, sex-gender reporting terminology should be accurately used to avoid confusion [4]. In this case, the 2 major biological sexes should be referred to as “female” and “male”, not interchangeably with “woman” and “man” as reported in this study. More importantly, concluding the high incidence of subsequent cancer following a first-ever VTE might be inappropriate, as there are no data on the pre-VTE period for comparison. This conclusion also implicitly hypothesises a causal relationship where first-ever VTE can trigger carcinogenesis and lead to detectable cancer within 6 months of VTE diagnosis, which may be unusual considering the average growth rate of tumour cells. Based on the current findings and the risk of surveillance bias, the authors should only confirm the higher rate of cancer detection within 6 months of VTE diagnosis, as compared with the period after that.
