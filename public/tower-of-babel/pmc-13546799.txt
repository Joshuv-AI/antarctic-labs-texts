Implant size distribution and mid-term outcomes of transfibular total ankle arthroplasty in a Japanese cohort
Authors: Koichiro Yano, Katsunori Ikari, Takumi Koseki, Ayako Tominaga, Ken Okazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTotal ankle arthroplasty (TAA) is a widely accepted alternative to ankle arthrodesis for end-stage ankle arthritis. Preserving ankle motion is particularly relevant in Japanese populations, in which kneeling and squatting are integral to routine activities. However, Japanese individuals generally have smaller bone morphology than Western patients, raising concerns regarding the suitability of implant size variations originally designed for Western populations. This study aimed to examine implant size distribution, clinical and radiological outcomes, and implant survival rate following transfibular TAA in a Japanese cohort.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 50 patients (51 ankles) who underwent transfibular TAA between March 2019 and October 2022 with three or more more years of follow-up (mean 4.3 years (3.0 to 6.0, SD 0.9)). Clinical outcomes were evaluated preoperatively and at three, six, 12, and 24 months postoperatively, and at final follow-up using the Self-Administered Foot Evaluation Questionnaire (SAFE-Q) and ankle range of motion. Radiological assessment comprised the lateral distal tibial angle, anterior distal tibial angle, tibiotalar angle, and tibiotalar ratio. Complications and implant survival were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, with implant removal as the endpoint.ResultsSmaller implant sizes predominated, with size 2 (33.3%) and size 3 (37.3%) used most frequently; no patients required size 5 or 6. All SAFE-Q domains improved significantly from baseline, with mean changes exceeding the minimal clinically important difference. Range of motion increased by three months postoperatively and subsequently stabilized. Radiological parameters demonstrated satisfactory alignment and reduced coronal variance. Delayed wound healing occurred in 15.7% and medial malleolar fractures in 9.8%; all cases achieved bone union without additional intervention. Implant survival rate was 98.0% at three years and 95.5% at four years.ConclusionTransfibular TAA produced favourable mid-term clinical, radiological, and survival outcomes in Japanese patients. The available implant size range adequately accommodated the smaller ankle morphology typical of Japanese patients. These findings may inform implant development and preoperative planning for populations with similar anatomical characteristics.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(9):1163–1171.

Introduction

Ankle arthritis is a debilitating condition that markedly reduces patients’ quality of life through pain, functional limitations, and socioeconomic burden. Osteoarthritis (OA) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are the primary aetiologies, and surgical treatment is often required when conservative measures fail. Tibiotalar arthrodesis was historically the gold standard for severe ankle arthritis management; however, it frequently caused altered gait mechanics, adjacent joint degeneration, and persistent pain, prompting the development and growing use of total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) as an alternative.1 In East Asian cultures, daily tasks such as kneeling or squatting remain common, especially in Japan, where the traditional seiza posture is widely practised.2,3 These positions require substantial ankle mobility, underscoring the importance of joint preservation in this population. East Asian patients also tend to have smaller ankles than Western patients.2 The transfibular TAA approach offers distinct advantages, including direct visualization of the ankle’s centre of rotation, limited bone resection, and the ability to adjust fibular length. Fibular shortening in varus cases and lengthening in valgus cases enables appropriate repositioning of the fibula, contributing to improved ankle stability. In particular, the fixed-bearing design and cementless fixation of the transfibular implant were considered preferable to the cemented mobile-bearing prosthesis previously used at our institution, which demonstrated high rates of radiological failure including talar component subsidence (28.9%) and tibial component migration (21.1%) in a RA cohort.4 Although this approach was introduced in Western countries and existing clinical data arise solely from Western populations,5-18 it remains unclear whether current implant sizes are appropriate for Japanese patients. Moreover, clinical outcomes in this population have not been examined. This study aimed to identify commonly used implant sizes in Japanese patients and evaluate mid-term outcomes of transfibular TAA.

Methods

This study included patients who underwent transfibular TAA using the Trabecular Metal Total Ankle (TM Ankle; Zimmer Biomet, USA) at our institution from March 2019 to October 2022. Exclusion criteria were postoperative follow-up less than three years, insufficient preoperative data, and conversion from arthrodesis to TAA. Two orthopaedic surgeons specializing in foot and ankle surgery (KY, KI) performed all procedures; neither contributed to TM Ankle system development. Postoperatively, the ankle was immobilized in a splint. Partial weightbearing with an ankle brace was initiated on postoperative day 10, and full weightbearing was permitted by postoperative day 21. The institutional review board approved this study (approval number: 181006), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patient demographic characteristics are summarized in Table I.

Implant size

The implant used in this study is a fixed-bearing prosthesis in which the tibial component and polyethylene insert are integrated. The implant is available in six sizes (1 to 6). Tibial and talar components must match, as there is no compatibility between different sizes. We investigated the implant sizes used in the cases included in this study.

Clinical examinations

Clinical evaluations were conducted preoperatively and postoperatively at three, six, 12, and 24 months, and at final follow-up. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the Self-Administered Foot Evaluation Questionnaire (SAFE-Q),19,20 developed by the Japanese Society for Surgery of the Foot. The SAFE-Q comprises 34 items distributed across five subscales: pain and pain-related, physical functioning and daily living, social functioning, shoe-related, and general health and wellbeing. Passive ankle range of motion (ROM) was also evaluated at the same timepoints.

Radiological examinations

Radiological assessments were conducted preoperatively and at the latest follow-up to evaluate implant positioning, alignment, subsidence, and radiolucency. Standard weightbearing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs were obtained. Following the methodology of Maccario et al,14 we measured the anatomical lateral distal tibial angle (normal value 90° (SD 5°)), anatomical anterior distal tibial angle (normal value 85° (SD 5°)), tibiotalar angle (TTA; normal value 90° (SD 5°)), and tibiotalar ratio (TTR; normal range 27% to 42%) during the preoperative evaluation. Postoperatively, alignment of the tibial and talar components was assessed using their articular surfaces to ensure consistency with preoperative angular measurements. The α angle (normal value 90° (SD 2°)) represented the lateral distal tibial angle, the β angle (normal value 85° (SD 2°)) corresponded to the anterior distal tibial angle, and the γ angle indicated the sagittal placement of the talar component relative to the long axis of the talus (normal value 20° (SD 2°)). TTA and TTR adhered to preoperative measurement techniques. Additionally, radiolucency at the latest follow-up was evaluated. Radiolucency was defined as a radiolucent line ≥ 2 mm around the entire bone-implant interface of either the tibial or talar component.

Complications

Postoperative complications and revision procedures were documented and classified according to the Canadian Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society Reoperation Coding System (CROCS).21 Delayed wound healing was defined as failure of wound closure within two weeks. Fibular nonunion was defined as the absence of cortical bone bridging across the osteotomy site in all three planes on CT at 12 months. Tibial component subsidence was defined as displacement ≥ 2 mm or angular deviation ≥ 5°, and talar component subsidence was defined as vertical displacement ≥ 2 mm.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and reported as mean with SD, whereas categorical variables were summarized as n (%). Preoperative and postoperative continuous variables were compared using paired t-tests, and categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The distribution-based minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was calculated as 50% of the SD of the change between preoperative and latest follow-up scores.22 Variability in TTA and TTR from preoperative to latest follow-up measurements was assessed using the Morgan–Pitman test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to estimate prosthesis survival. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power (v. 3.1.9.6, University of Düsseldorf, Germany) based on sample size, an effect size of 0.5, and an α error probability of 0.05. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (v. 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Results

Initially, 62 patients (64 ankles) were included in this study, all of whom were Japanese. We excluded nine patients (nine ankles) who were lost to follow-up before the three-year postoperative period, three patients (three ankles) with incomplete preoperative clinical evaluations, and one patient (one ankle) who had undergone conversion from ankle arthrodesis to TAA. Consequently, 50 patients (51 ankles) were analyzed (Figure 1). A post-hoc power analysis indicated a statistical power of 0.97, based on an effect size of 0.5, an α error probability of 0.05, and the final sample size. The mean follow-up duration was 4.3 years (3.0 to 6.0, SD 0.9). In the coronal plane, using alignment deviations of > 5° to define varus or valgus deformities, 25 ankles (49.0%) demonstrated preoperative varus deformity. As concurrent procedures, Achilles tendon lengthening was performed in ten cases (19.6%), and peroneal tendon lengthening was performed in one (2.0%).

Implant size

The implant sizes used were size 1 in nine ankles (17.6%), size 2 in 17 ankles (33.3%), size 3 in 19 ankles (37.3%), and size 4 in six ankles (11.8%). No patients required size 5 or 6 implants (Figure 2). Even in the patient with the smallest bone size, appropriate coverage was achieved with a size 1 implant without implant overhang (Figure 3).

Clinical outcomes

All SAFE-Q subscales demonstrated significant improvement compared with preoperative values at three, six, 12, and 24 months postoperatively and at the latest follow-up. The latest follow-up scores for all five subscales exceeded MCID thresholds (Figure 4 and Table II). For ROM, dorsiflexion showed no significant change at any postoperative timepoint relative to baseline, whereas plantarflexion significantly improved from six months postoperatively through to the final evaluation. Total ROM also increased significantly from three months postoperatively to the final follow-up (Figure 5 and Table III).

Radiological outcomes

Preoperatively, the lateral distal tibial angle was 94.4° (SD 6.0°), and the α angle improved significantly to 90.4° (SD 2.8°) at the latest follow-up (p < 0.001, paired t-test). The preoperative anterior distal tibial angle was 77.5° (SD 6.3°), and the β angle increased significantly to 83.9° (SD 11.6°) at the latest follow-up (p = 0.002, paired t-test). At the latest follow-up, the γ angle measured 17.0° (SD 6.1°). The Morgan–Pitman test showed markedly reduced TTA variance at the latest follow-up (F = 35.4, p < 0.001) compared with preoperative values (Figure 6). For TTR, no significant change in variance was identified between the preoperative and latest follow-up assessments (F = 3.0, p = 0.088, Morgan–Pitman test). Radiolucency was observed in one case (2.0%).

Complications

The complications classified according to CROCS are summarized in Table IV. Delayed wound healing occurred in eight ankles (15.7%). Of these, six ankles healed with continued wound care, while one case developed a superficial infection that resolved after removal of the fibular plate. Another case developed a deep infection treated with irrigation, debridement, and insert exchange 1.5 months postoperatively, which achieved temporary control; however, subsequent tibial component loosening required revision of the tibial component one year and nine months after the initial procedure. One additional case without delayed wound healing underwent isolated tibial component revision three years and six months after the primary surgery due to tibial component subsidence. Superficial infections at the lateral incision site and at the proximal tibial pin insertion site were each observed in one case and resolved with short-course antibiotic therapy. Perioperative medial malleolar fractures, identified intraoperatively or in the immediate postoperative period, occurred in five ankles (9.8%). None required alteration of the rehabilitation protocol, and bone union was achieved in all cases. Medial pain persisted in two cases, and medial gutter debridement was performed two years and ten months and three years and two months after the primary surgery, respectively. Superficial peroneal nerve injury occurred in one patient (2.0%) and improved with mirogabalin besilate therapy. No fibular nonunion was identified. The mean time to fibular union was 7.0 months (2 to 12, SD 3.1). The implant survival rate, using implant removal as the endpoint and calculated with the Kaplan–Meier method, was 98.0% at three years and 95.5% at four years (Figure 7).

Discussion

This is the first study to describe implant size distribution and mid-term outcomes of transfibular TAA in Japanese patients. The findings indicated a tendency towards smaller implant sizes. DeVries et al11 reported a mean implant size of 4 (2 to 6, SD 1), reflecting the more frequent use of larger components in lateral-approach TAA performed mainly in Western patients. Moreover, the manufacturer’s post-market clinical follow-up report of 121 ankles conducted exclusively in Western patients demonstrated that size 1 to 6 implants were used in 2%, 7%, 29%, 37%, 19%, and 7% of cases, respectively, with size 4 being the most common.23 Notably, sizes 3 and above accounted for 91% of all implants, indicating that Western populations more frequently require larger components compared with our Japanese cohort. Patient-reported outcomes improved significantly after surgery, and ankle ROM also demonstrated meaningful postoperative gains. Radiological assessment confirmed that satisfactory alignment was achieved, and implant survival at a mean follow-up of 4.3 years remained favourable.

Previous research has reported satisfactory short- to mid-term results of transfibular TAA in Western populations,5-18,23 and the present findings showed similarly favourable outcomes in Japanese patients. Mid-term studies with a minimum follow-up of five years have reported implant survival rates of 92.1% to 100%, with consistent improvements in patient-reported outcomes and radiological alignment.10,12-14 Earlier studies primarily included patients with OA, with relatively low proportions of patients with RA (0% to 34.7%).5-10,14,16-18,23 In contrast, this study involved a higher proportion of patients with RA (62.7%), yet clinical results, radiological findings, and implant survival remained comparable to prior reports. Notably, the inclusion of patients with RA did not lead to an increased risk of infection or component loosening.

The present study showed that smaller implant sizes were predominantly used in Japanese patients undergoing transfibular TAA. This observation aligns with a previous anatomical report indicating that the ankle joints of East Asian individuals are generally smaller than those of Western populations.2 The absence of size 5 and 6 components in this cohort further underscores population-specific morphological differences. Even in the patient with the smallest bone dimensions, a size 1 implant provided adequate coverage without overhang (Figure 3), confirming that the available size range was appropriate for the anatomical characteristics of Japanese patients. Clinically, accurate preoperative templating and meticulous intraoperative sizing are essential. Oversizing may cause cortical impingement or soft-tissue tension, while undersizing can result in insufficient bone coverage and early loosening.

With regard to PROMs, all five subscales demonstrated significant improvement beginning at three months postoperatively, and these gains were sustained through the final follow-up. The results were comparable to those reported in Western studies, although direct comparison is limited by differences in the PROM instruments used; the majority of prior studies employed the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society score,5,7,9,14,15,18 visual analogue scale for pain,5-7,10,12,14-16,18 or the Short Form Health Survey (SF-12),10,12,14,18 rather than the SAFE-Q used in the present study. Furthermore, the differences between preoperative and final follow-up scores exceeded the MCID, indicating clinically meaningful improvement for the patients.

Ankle ROM increased significantly at three months postoperatively and remained improved through the final follow-up, consistent with outcomes reported in Western cohorts.5,6,14-16,18 Daily activities for many Japanese populations, such as sitting in the seiza position or squatting, require substantial ankle mobility, which underscores the clinical relevance of postoperative ROM gains in this population.

Regarding radiological alignment, all parameters demonstrated satisfactory correction, aligning with previous reports.5-7,10,11,13,14,16,18 Preoperatively, both varus and valgus deformities were present, with a higher proportion of varus deformity (49.0%) compared with previous studies (7.9% to 41.6%),10,13,16-18 likely reflecting the greater prevalence of varus alignment among Japanese patients.24 Despite this, all ankles achieved neutral convergence postoperatively with significantly reduced variability. For sagittal alignment, assessed using the TTR, the Morgan–Pitman test did not detect a significant change in variance (F = 3.0, p = 0.088), indicating that the spread of sagittal alignment values was maintained throughout the follow-up period.

In this study, the incidence of delayed wound healing was 15.7%, higher than previously reported rates of 1.0% to 10.0%.5,9,11,14,17,18 The elevated rate of delayed wound healing likely reflects the higher proportion of patients with RA in this cohort, a population known to be at increased risk of impaired wound healing due to immunosuppressive therapy and underlying vasculopathy. Additionally, our use of a stricter definition – failure of wound closure within two weeks – may have captured cases that would not have been classified as delayed wound healing in prior studies using less stringent criteria. One case progressed to deep infection, underscoring the importance of meticulous wound care. Perioperative medial malleolar fractures, identified intraoperatively or in the immediate postoperative period, occurred in 9.8% of ankles (RA: two ankles (6.3%); post-traumatic OA: three ankles (27.3%)), also higher than prior reports (0% to 3.3%),5-8,11,14-17 which may reflect the greater number of patients with preoperative varus deformity. Nonetheless, all fractures achieved bone union without additional fixation or delays in rehabilitation, and thus were not considered a major concern. Other complications, including infection, radiolucency, tibial component subsidence, and medial pain, were comparable to those previously described.5-18,23 No cases of fibular nonunion or talar component subsidence were identified. Implant survival, using implant removal as the endpoint, was 98.0% at three years and 95.5% at four years, consistent with published results.6,8-10,13,14,18,23

This study has several limitations. It was a single-centre, retrospective investigation, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, this study was not designed as a comparative investigation and did not include a control group, such as a Western cohort or patients treated with alternative surgical approaches. Therefore, the purpose of this study was not to demonstrate implant size suitability or superiority, but rather to describe the real-world distribution of implant sizes and mid-term outcomes in a Japanese cohort undergoing transfibular TAA. Periprosthetic cyst formation was not systematically evaluated, as CT was not performed routinely at follow-up; this represents another limitation of the present study. Additionally, the follow-up period reflects mid-term outcomes; long-term survivorship and late complications remain unclear. Therefore, continued follow-up and multicentre prospective studies are needed to confirm the long-term durability and clinical effectiveness of transfibular TAA in Japanese patients.

In summary, transfibular TAA demonstrated favourable mid-term clinical, radiological, and survivorship outcomes in Japanese patients. Smaller implant sizes were predominantly used, and the available size range adequately accommodated the ankle morphology typical of Japanese patients. These findings offer meaningful guidance for optimizing preoperative planning and implant selection.

Take home message

- Transfibular total ankle arthroplasty is safe and effective for Japanese patients, with favourable mid-term clinical, radiological, and survivorship outcomes comparable to those reported in Western cohorts.

- Implant sizes 1 to 4 are sufficient to cover the full range of ankle morphology in Japanese patients; sizes 5 and 6 were not required in any case. Surgeons operating on Japanese or similarly small-statured populations should anticipate predominant use of sizes 2 and 3, and plan preoperative templating accordingly.

- These findings provide practical, population-specific guidance for implant selection in patients with smaller ankle anatomy.
