An injectable, leadless bioelectronic interface for battery-free wireless peripheral neuromodulation
Authors: Mohamed Elsherif, Salma Mansour, Zhansaya Makhambetova, Fotoon Aldhanhani, Batoul Khlaifat, Mahmoud Elbeh, Vega Pradana Rachim, Sohmyung Ha, Hicham Abada, Juan S. Barajas-Gamboa, Carlos M. Abril Vega, Khalil B. Ramadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Injectable bioelectronics offer a minimally invasive approach to peripheral nerve stimulation but remain limited by onboard energy storage and fragile leads. Here, we present SEED, a leadless, battery-free bioelectronic interface engineered for percutaneous delivery through a standard 14-gauge needle. SEED (Stimulating Electrode for Electroceutical Delivery) operates in the magnetoquasistatic regime using low-frequency (65 kilohertz) resonant inductive coupling, externalizing waveform generation and control, enabling programmable neuromodulation without onboard active electronics. A spiral-helix electrode geometry promotes longitudinal nerve engagement while limiting off-target field spread. Benchtop and ex vivo characterization demonstrates precise, programmable control of stimulation frequency, pulse width, and amplitude under physiologically relevant conditions. In vivo validation in a rat sciatic nerve model confirms frequency-locked motor responses and graded neural recruitment following percutaneous deployment. SEED exhibits strong radiopacity and acoustic contrast, supporting compatibility with ultrasound and computed tomography for image-guided neuromodulation. This platform provides a scalable pathway toward minimally invasive bioelectronic therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) is a nonpharmacological approach to treating chronic pain, restoring motor function, and modulating systemic physiological activity (1–4). By delivering spatially focal electrical pulses to specific neural targets, PNS can selectively recruit or inhibit peripheral pathways while bypassing the systemic side effects associated with traditional pharmacotherapy (5). Despite its clinical utility in restoring sensory feedback and promoting neuroplasticity, a fundamental trade-off between procedural invasiveness and stimulation precision hinders widespread adoption of these technologies (6–8).

Current clinical PNS technologies largely fall into two delivery paradigms, each associated with fundamental limitations. Noninvasive transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) offers ease of use but is plagued by poor depth selectivity (9–12). To reach deep-seated nerves, TENS requires high current amplitudes that often cause nonspecific muscle activation and cutaneous discomfort, severely limiting patient compliance and therapeutic accuracy. On the other hand, fully implantable stimulators provide deep, focal activation but necessitate invasive surgical procedures for the placement of pulse generators and lead systems (13). These interventions can require general anesthesia, specialized operating room environments, and long recovery times while remaining susceptible to long-term complications such as lead migration, infection, and the eventual depletion of onboard batteries (14). In response, recent work has explored battery-free wireless powering strategies for implantable bioelectronics, including magnetoelectric, ultrasonic, and inductive coupling–based approaches, each offering distinct trade-offs in penetration depth, alignment tolerance, and form factor constraints (15–19).

Injectable, battery-free bioelectronics present a promising alternative that combines the focal precision of implanted devices with the minimal invasiveness of a needle-based injection (20). By leveraging resonant inductive coupling, these miniaturized systems can be powered wirelessly through biological tissue, enabling an unlimited operational lifetime without the bulk of internal energy storage (21, 22). When engineered into a needle-compatible form factor, such devices can be deployed percutaneously to target nerves, marking a critical shift toward minimally invasive neuromodulation.

Here, we present SEED (Stimulating Electrode for Electroceutical Delivery), a battery-free, injectable bioelectronic interface that overcomes the energy storage bottleneck through a geometry-optimized inductive coupling architecture. Unlike prior miniaturized stimulators that rely on complex integrated circuits or onboard energy storage, SEED uses a minimalist, fully passive design inspired by the form factor of clinically deployed RFID (radio-frequency identification) animal identification tags. This approach enables a device diameter of 1.4 mm, compatible with a standard 14-gauge hypodermic needle, while supporting the delivery of stimulation currents sufficient for deep-seated peripheral nerve activation. Operating at a low-frequency carrier (65 kHz) in the magnetoquasistatic regime, the system enables external waveform-based control while maintaining low tissue absorption and high tolerance to angular misalignment between the external transmitter (Tx) and the injectable SEED. We used electric-field modeling to optimize electrode geometries within the constrained injectable form factor. We then validate the SEED platform through benchtop characterization, ex vivo testing in conductive biological media, and in vivo stimulation of the rat sciatic nerve, establishing its stability, radiopacity, and compatibility with image-guided, minimally invasive neuromodulation workflows.

RESULTS

SEED design, assembly, and encapsulation

SEED is a battery-free, injectable bioelectronic interface with a cylindrical form factor compatible with percutaneous delivery (Fig. 1, A and B). The device measures 1.4 mm in diameter and 9 mm in length for a total volume of ~14 mm3 (Fig. 2, A and B). This form factor enables delivery through a standard 14-gauge needle without surgical implantation. The SEED form factor is similar to commercially deployed injectable animal identification microchips, as well as metallic fiducial markers routinely used in image-guided procedures (Fig. 2A).

SEED architecture integrates all functional components within a millimeter-scale footprint. The internal circuitry consists of a solenoidal receiver (Rx) coil for wireless power harvesting, a tuning capacitor for resonance alignment, and a micro–light-emitting diode (μLED) for visual indication of device activation (Fig. 2C). The neural interface is formed by a bipolar spiral-helix configuration comprising two electrically isolated platinum-iridium (Pt─Ir) electrodes wrapped along the SEED body (Fig. 2, A and B). The polymeric enclosure incorporates microgrooves that guide electrode wrapping and maintain consistent electrode spacing along the SEED surface. The spatial organization of these components within the SEED device is illustrated in the exploded schematic shown in Fig. 2D.

We fabricated custom polymeric scaffolds using high-resolution microstereolithography for reliable assembly of submillimeter components, including 0201-size surface-mount devices (Fig. 2, E to J). These scaffolds incorporate microscale cavities for component placement and epoxy-retention features that localize low-temperature soldering adhesive during electrical interconnection, ensuring mechanical stability during assembly.

Following circuit assembly, the device was enclosed within a printed polymeric shell and sealed using medical-grade epoxy (Fig. 2, K to P). The polymeric enclosure incorporates microscale grooves (50 μm) that guide electrode wrapping and ensure consistent interelectrode spacing along the SEED surface. The structural integrity and surface morphology of the encapsulated SEED were evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Low- and high-magnification SEM imaging revealed a uniform cylindrical profile without visible surface cracks, voids, or delamination (Fig. 2, N to P).

Electrode-geometry selection via electric-field modeling

Electrode geometry influences electric-field orientation and spatial confinement in miniaturized neuromodulation interfaces, considerations that are particularly relevant for injectable devices where postdeployment orientation relative to the target nerve cannot be actively controlled. To guide electrode selection within the geometric constraints of the millimeter-scale SEED device, we performed a comparative electric-field analysis across several electrode topologies compatible with a cylindrical injectable architecture (Fig. 3). The simulations were intended to evaluate geometry-dependent differences in field orientation and spatial confinement rather than to predict neural activation thresholds or in vivo stimulation efficacy. While neural depolarization is fundamentally governed by spatial variations in extracellular potential, we used electric-field distributions as a comparative design-guidance framework to evaluate how electrode geometries shape the directionality and spatial confinement of stimulation fields relative to a target nerve.

We identified five electrode topologies compatible with an injectable, millimeter-scale cylindrical device, representing configurations that can be fabricated and deployed without compromising injectability (Fig. 3B). These included (i) hemispherical end-cap electrodes, (ii) coplanar bipolar pad electrodes, (iii) dual circumferential ring electrodes, (iv) longitudinal strip electrodes, and (v) a spiral-helix configuration integrated along the SEED surface. All configurations shared identical SEED dimensions, electrode materials, and applied differential potentials to isolate the influence of electrode geometry on the resulting electric-field distributions.

Finite-element simulations were conducted in an anatomically realistic tissue environment to enable a direct comparison of geometry-dependent field behavior. Electric-field profiles were extracted along anatomically defined longitudinal and transverse paths to examine nerve-aligned field components and radial field spread into the surrounding tissue (Fig. 3, C to G). The analysis focused on relative differences in field orientation and spatial confinement rather than absolute stimulation thresholds.

Across the evaluated configurations, electrode geometry strongly influenced electric-field directionality (Fig. 3, H to J). The spiral-helix configuration produced a peak longitudinal electric-field magnitude of ~143 V m−1 along the nerve axis compared to 65 to 85 V m−1 for the other geometries under identical applied potentials (Fig. 3I). In the radial direction, the helix geometry exhibited more rapid transverse field attenuation, with the electric field decaying to 50% of its peak value within ~4.8 mm compared to ~6 to 8.5 mm for alternative configurations (Fig. 3J). In contrast, end-cap, circumferential, and coplanar geometries generated fields dominated by radial or end-focused components that extended farther into surrounding tissue (Fig. 3, H to J). On the basis of these geometry-dependent differences in longitudinal field orientation and amplitude and transverse confinement and attenuation, the spiral-helix configuration was selected as a practical electrode geometry for the injectable SEED architecture.

Wireless powering of SEED

We next characterized the wireless powering architecture enabling the battery-free operation of SEED. We used a resonant inductive wireless power-transfer scheme consisting of an external Tx coil and a miniaturized Rx coil integrated within the injectable SEED. The Tx coil (7 cm in diameter, 18-gauge Litz wire) was driven by a low–output-impedance (~50 mΩ) radio-frequency (RF) amplifier and configured in series with a tuning capacitor to form a high–quality factor LC resonant circuit, generating a time-varying magnetic field for wireless energy transfer (Fig. 4A). We selected a carrier frequency of 65 kHz to ensure operation within the magnetoquasistatic regime, minimizing electromagnetic attenuation in biological tissue while supporting inductive coupling at clinically relevant depths. To ensure stable operation, the system was designed to operate at least an order of magnitude below the self-resonant frequencies of both the Tx and Rx coils.

The electromagnetic response of both coils was characterized over a frequency range of 10 kHz to 8 MHz (figs. S1 and S2). The Tx coil exhibited predominantly inductive behavior, with a phase angle approaching +90° across the measured spectrum. The measured inductance varied with frequency from 84.1 to 279 μH (microhenrys), and reflection-coefficient (S11) measurements revealed a self-resonant frequency at 2.32 GHz, well above the operating regime (fig. S1, A to G). When paired with a 70-nF series capacitor, the Tx coil displayed a pronounced resonance minimum (−40.3 dB) at 65 kHz, confirming accurate tuning to the target operating frequency (fig. S1G). The dc resistance of the Tx coil was measured to be 0.23 Ω (fig. S1H).

The miniaturized Rx coil exhibited a higher dc resistance (26.1 Ω) because of its dense winding geometry and small form factor (fig. S2A). Frequency-dependent characterization revealed a self-resonant transition at 2.615 MHz, indicated by a phase crossing of −2.1° and a corresponding change in apparent inductance, consistent with a transition to capacitive behavior (fig. S2, B to D). At the operating frequency of 65 kHz, the Rx coil remained strongly inductive (L ≈ 324 μH) (fig. S2D). We paired the Rx coil with an 18-nF parallel capacitor to achieve resonance alignment, maximizing magnetic flux linkage and enabling stable power delivery to both the onboard red μLED indicator and stimulation electrodes (fig. S2, E and F).

To further quantify the performance of the wireless power-transfer link, we experimentally measured the transmitted power (Pin), received power (Pout), and the corresponding power transfer efficiency (PTE) under representative operating conditions. The transmitted power was calculated from the root-mean-square (RMS) Tx voltage and current measured at the amplifier output, while the received power was determined from the RMS voltage measured across a 1-kΩ resistive load connected to the SEED electrodes. Across the tested input conditions, the transmitted power ranged from 0.14 to 4.38 W, while the received power ranged from 0.007 to 0.146 W, corresponding to a wireless link efficiency between 3.7 and 5.2% with an average efficiency of ~4.5% (Table 1). These values are consistent with reported efficiencies for millimeter-scale inductively powered implants, which typically achieve PTEs of a few percent (23).

Building on the demonstrated power-transfer capability of the inductive link, we amplitude modulated the inductive carrier using a low-frequency envelope to support neural stimulation at physiologically relevant frequencies (Fig. 4B). Specifically, we modulated the 65-kHz carrier within the range of 10 to 100 Hz, enabling efficient wireless power transfer while delivering low-frequency stimulation waveforms suitable for peripheral nerve activation.

Precision benchtop characterization of electrical stimulation output

We next quantitatively characterized the electrical output delivered by SEED under benchtop conditions using externally programmed stimulation waveforms to assess amplitude scaling, load dependence, and temporal programmability (Fig. 4, C to I). Stimulation waveforms were defined at the Tx and delivered wirelessly to SEED using an amplitude-modulated (AM) carrier. Electrical output was evaluated under controlled benchtop loading conditions using resistive loads (1 kΩ) to enable reproducible characterization of the electrical output of the SEED device.

We first quantified the relationship between Tx input amplitude and the induced electrical output at the Rx (Fig. 4, C to G). Increasing Tx input from 300 to 600 mVpp linearly increased the induced peak voltage and load current from 0.62 to 2.92 mA (Fig. 4G). We then evaluated system performance across a range of load impedances consistent with values commonly reported for biological tissue and electrode-tissue interfaces (Fig. 4H). When the load resistance was varied from 1 to 10 kΩ under fixed Tx input conditions, the induced current decreased with increasing load resistance, while the induced peak voltage increased and approached saturation beyond 4.7 kΩ (Fig. 4H).

We next varied the modulation pulse width at a fixed carrier frequency and modulation frequency to assess temporal modulation capability (Fig. 4I). Varying the pulse width from 0.15 to 1.8 ms increased the induced peak voltage from 1.2 to 5.36 V, reflecting the envelope-controlled duration of effective conduction during AM operation (Fig. 4I). These measurements establish that stimulation amplitude and temporal parameters can be independently programmed through external control of the transmitted waveform.

Characterization of wireless power-transfer robustness and misalignment tolerance

We evaluated the robustness of wireless power transfer under deployment-relevant conditions by characterizing the performance of the 65-kHz inductive link across lateral, angular, and axial misalignment configurations between the Tx and Rx coils (Fig. 4, J to N). These measurements define the spatial operating envelope of wireless power transfer. When the Rx coil was parallel to the Tx, induced peak voltage (Vp) decreased rapidly with increasing lateral offset, whereas orthogonal (90°) and tilted (70°) orientations exhibited a more gradual decay (Fig. 4, J to L). For these nonparallel configurations, functional power transfer was maintained for lateral offsets up to ~0.4R, where R denotes the radius of the Tx coil (Fig. 4, K and L). Vp decreased with increasing angular deviation, with ~33% of the peak voltage retained at a tilt angle of 60°, and functional operation was maintained for angular deviations up to ~75° (Fig. 4M). Vp decreased monotonically with increasing axial distance, while functional stimulation levels were maintained up to an axial separation of ~3.5 cm (z/R ≈ 1), defining the effective operating depth of the wireless link under the tested conditions (Fig. 4N). Together, these measurements delineate the spatial tolerance of the wireless power-transfer system under misalignment conditions relevant to percutaneous deployment.

Ex vivo functional validation in biological tissue

We conducted ex vivo experiments using freshly dissected rat tissue maintained in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to evaluate device performance in biologically relevant environments (Fig. 5, A to D). These experiments assessed whether the wirelessly powered, fully passive architecture maintains stable and programmable electrical output when operating through conductive biological media. We powered SEED using a 65-kHz carrier with low-frequency amplitude modulation. At a fixed Tx input amplitude of 700 mVpp and a modulation frequency of 33 Hz, the stimulation pulse width was varied from 0.3 to 1.8 ms. Under these conditions, the induced peak voltage measured in tissue increased monotonically from 1.2 to 2.7 V with increasing pulse width (Fig. 5, B to D). The measured ex vivo waveforms exhibited trends consistent with those obtained under benchtop resistive loading, indicating stable operation of the inductive link and Rx circuitry in the presence of conductive biological tissue. These results establish that externally programmed stimulation parameters are preserved during ex vivo operation under tissue-relevant electrical loading conditions.

Percutaneous injectability and image-guided deployment

We evaluated the percutaneous delivery of SEED using a standard 14-gauge curved needle by injecting SEED into a tissue-mimicking agarose phantom (2%, w/v) (Fig. 5, E to I). SEED traversed the needle lumen without mechanical resistance, enabling controlled delivery and intact deployment across repeated injection cycles. We assessed functionality after deployment into agarose phantom and found that 65-kHz carrier frequency produced reproducible illumination of the integrated red μLED, indicating preserved electrical continuity and effective inductive coupling after needle transit (Fig. 5, F and G). SEED remained operational during repeated activation cycles while embedded in the hydrated phantom for up to 60 days, indicating the preserved wireless activation and electrical continuity of the encapsulated device.

Image-guided deployment and postinjection localization were evaluated using clinical-standard ultrasound imaging. During percutaneous insertion, SEED exhibited a strong hyperechoic signature, enabling real-time visualization of the needle trajectory and SEED as it exited the distal needle tip (Fig. 5H and movie S1). Following deployment, SEED remained readily identifiable within the phantom, demonstrating stable positioning and high acoustic contrast (Fig. 5H). Additional imaging in a chicken-leg tissue model further confirmed the reliable ultrasound visibility of the injected SEED under heterogeneous tissue conditions (Fig. 5I).

In vivo percutaneous deployment and functional neuromodulation

We evaluated in vivo deployability and functional performance in anesthetized rats following the percutaneous delivery of SEED using a standard 14-gauge curved needle (Fig. 5, J and K). After injection into the hindlimb, wireless coupling was immediately confirmed by visible transcutaneous illumination of the integrated red μLED during repeated activation cycles, indicating preserved electrical continuity and effective inductive powering under in vivo conditions (Fig. 5J and movie S2). We visualized the placement of the injected SEED using micro–computed tomography (μCT) (Fig. 5, L and M). SEED exhibited strong radiopacity, with both the solenoidal Rx coil and helical Pt─Ir electrodes clearly resolved, enabling visualization of device position relative to the surrounding anatomy.

We next assessed whether an implanted SEED could modulate peripheral nerves using a rat sciatic nerve model. Following placement adjacent to the sciatic nerve, we delivered stimulation using externally programmed AM waveforms (Fig. 5, N to P). Varying the modulation frequency between 1 and 2 Hz at a fixed Tx input of 150 mVpp induced leg-twitch responses at the applied stimulation frequency, with displacement traces temporally aligned to the stimulation waveform (Fig. 5, N and O). Stimulation intensity was independently modulated by adjusting the Tx input amplitude. Reducing the Tx voltage from 150 to 125 mVpp at a constant modulation frequency of 2 Hz resulted in a corresponding decrease in leg-twitch displacement amplitude while preserving the stimulation frequency (Fig. 5P). These in vivo measurements demonstrate percutaneous deployment, wireless activation through intact tissue, externally programmed stimulation, and postimplantation localization of SEED under physiological conditions.

DISCUSSION

SEED represents a shift in the design paradigm of injectable bioelectronics, moving away from complex, battery-dependent implants toward a minimalist, geometry-driven, wirelessly powered architecture. By decoupling energy harvesting and stimulation control from implanted hardware, we address a central limitation that has constrained the miniaturization, deployability, and experimental accessibility of peripheral nerve stimulators. Rather than addressing electrode design, wireless power transfer, and injectability in isolation, SEED brings these elements together within a single injectable architecture. Together, the benchtop characterization, ex vivo tissue validation, injectability and imaging studies, and in vivo functional experiments define a coherent design space for minimally invasive neuromodulation using fully passive, needle-injectable devices.

We leveraged electric-field simulations as a design-guidance framework for interpreting electrode-geometry selection under the stringent constraints of a millimeter-scale, needle-injectable device. Rather than serving as predictors of absolute stimulation thresholds, the simulations helped contextualize how electrode geometry can influence field orientation and spatial confinement in scenarios where precise postdeployment positioning cannot be guaranteed. Because the orientation of an injectable device relative to the target nerve cannot be controlled after deployment, this geometry-guided approach provides a practical design strategy for preserving nerve-aligned stimulation components within a needle-injectable architecture. In current clinical peripheral neuromodulation systems, electrical stimulation is commonly delivered using cuff electrodes, percutaneous leads with longitudinal contacts, or paddle- and ribbon-style electrodes that are positioned adjacent to or wrapped around target nerves. These interfaces rely on controlled surgical or semisurgical placement and mechanical stabilization to preserve the electrode orientation and proximity to the nerve over time. While such approaches enable effective longitudinal activation, their physical size, reliance on leads, and deployment complexity limit their compatibility with millimeter-scale, needle-injectable devices, where electrode placement precision and postdeployment orientation cannot be actively controlled.

From this perspective, the spiral-helix electrode functions as a geometry-based analog to the longitudinal contact arrangements used in conventional neuromodulation tools, including cuff electrodes, ribbon electrodes, and longitudinal lead contacts. Whereas clinical systems rely on controlled placement and fixation to achieve nerve-aligned activation, the helical topology distributes the stimulation interface along the SEED body, providing a passive means of maintaining nerve-aligned field components in an injectable form factor. This geometry-driven strategy is particularly relevant for needle-deployed devices, where stimulation efficacy must be preserved through structural design rather than through mechanical anchoring or implanted control electronics.

Inductive wireless powering is widely used in implantable devices; however, many existing systems rely on planar or disk-shaped Rx coils that are optimized for controlled alignment with external Txs and are typically integrated into larger, surgically implanted form factors. In contrast, the elongated solenoidal Rx used here is specifically tailored to the geometric constraints of a millimeter-scale, needle-injectable device, enabling magnetic flux capture along the SEED length rather than across a planar surface. Operating in the magnetoquasistatic regime, this architecture emphasizes inductive dominance and tolerance to geometric variability rather than precise coil alignment, a consideration that becomes increasingly important as device dimensions shrink and postdeployment orientation cannot be controlled. The incorporation of a high-permeability ferrite core further supports efficient flux coupling within the constrained Rx volume, contributing to robust power transfer despite the angular and positional offsets inherent to injectable deployment. By externalizing waveform generation and modulation to the Tx, SEED maintains a minimal implanted footprint while preserving the real-time programmability of stimulation frequency, pulse width, and amplitude without onboard active electronics.

The robustness measurements further reveal how this architecture accommodates the realities of injectable deployment. The observed tolerance to lateral displacement, angular misalignment, and axial separation reflects the interaction between Tx field geometry and the ferrite-core Rx, enabling effective coupling even under nonideal orientations. The ferrite core enabled meaningful flux capture even at large angular deviations, mitigating the sharp coupling losses that typically limit air-core miniaturized Rxs. These features collectively define a spatial operating envelope well suited to injectable devices, which cannot be actively oriented or repositioned following deployment and must remain functional despite postinjection tissue motion.

Recent advances in peripheral neuromodulation have produced a wide range of wireless and minimally invasive stimulation platforms, including RF-powered implants, ultrasonic-powered microdevices, flexible or mesh-based electronics, and syringe-injectable systems (20–22, 26). While many of these approaches successfully eliminate onboard batteries and reduce surgical burden, they often rely on higher-frequency power transfer, mechanically unconstrained form factors, or integrated active circuitry, introducing trade-offs in penetration depth, alignment tolerance, deployment precision, or long-term positional stability (19, 20, 26, 27). Early RF-powered systems typically required surgically fixed Rx geometries to preserve coupling efficiency, whereas later miniaturized designs often traded geometric stability or predictable field orientation for reduced size or mechanical compliance (20, 22, 26, 27). The principal advance of SEED is the integration of a needle-injectable form factor, a fully passive stimulation architecture without onboard electronics, and low-frequency magnetoquasistatic inductive powering within a millimeter-scale peripheral neuromodulation device.

In contrast to these systems, SEED externalizes signal generation and control while maintaining a fully passive implant architecture whose stimulation geometry is preserved following injection. This design supports stable neuromodulation under conditions that challenge many existing systems, including angular misalignment, depth variability, and tissue motion, and bridges the gap between noninvasive stimulation methods with limited depth selectivity and surgically implanted systems that require permanent hardware placement.

The stimulation waveform in SEED consists of a sinusoidal carrier gated by a square envelope and can be approximated, because of diode-based rectification in the circuit, as a partially rectified signal at the electrode interface (24). Under this approximation, the effective charge per pulse is reduced relative to a conventional square waveform by a factor of ~1/π (~0.32) for the same peak voltage and pulse width. Consequently, higher applied voltages may be required to achieve comparable neural activation, reflecting a trade-off between passive device simplicity and waveform controllability (28, 29).

SEED is compatible with clinically relevant imaging modalities that support minimally invasive deployment and postimplantation localization. Ultrasound imaging enables real-time visualization during percutaneous insertion through its strong hyperechoic signature, while μCT provides high-resolution confirmation of device position relative to surrounding anatomical structures. Prior work in ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve interventions and stimulation approaches highlights the importance of spatial targeting for effective neuromodulation and supports the use of imaging to guide device placement (30–32). Such electrodes are typically positioned within a few millimeters of the target nerve, with sheath-level targeting on the order of ~2 mm and broader functional targeting with 0.5 to 3 cm depending on the application (30–32). These procedures are commonly performed using 18- to 22-gauge needles under real-time ultrasound guidance. Other percutaneous implantation procedures such as prostate brachytherapy report millimeter-scale variability in seed placement and treatment margins, typically 3 to 6 mm (33, 34). Effective PNS does not require direct electrode-nerve contact and can be achieved when electrodes are positioned within a few millimeters of the nerve, depending on stimulation amplitude and electrode configuration (25, 28, 31, 32). An advantage of an actuation device such as SEED over fiducial markers or brachytherapy seeds is that functional testing can be performed in real time during positioning and output can be measured using electroneurography or electromyography. Within this framework, SEED can be positioned in real time before release using ultrasound guidance, and the needle trajectory can be adjusted to achieve appropriate proximity to the target nerve. This approach is consistent with established clinical workflows for percutaneous placement of injectable implants, in which positioning is finalized before device release and supports controlled, image-guided targeting without requiring postdeployment repositioning.

Several considerations remain for extended or chronic use of the SEED platform. The present implementation uses monophasic stimulation, which is appropriate for acute and exploratory studies but may require charge-balancing strategies for long-term operation. Potential approaches include passive charge-balancing networks or electrode geometries engineered to minimize net dc offset. Long-term positional stability also remains an important consideration for injectable devices; future iterations may incorporate surface texturing, compliant coatings, or bioresorbable anchoring features to mitigate migration while preserving injectability. At the system level, integration with compact wearable Txs and support for multidevice operation represent natural extensions of the current platform.

In summary, SEED establishes a class of injectable, wirelessly powered bioelectronic interfaces that integrate rational electrode-geometry selection, robust low-frequency wireless powering, and percutaneous deployability within a unified design framework. By demonstrating that effective PNS can be achieved using a fully passive, needle-injectable device, this work expands the design space of minimally invasive neuromodulation and provides a scalable foundation for future bioelectronic therapies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

SEED fabrication and encapsulation

A stereolithography-based three-dimensional (3D) printing system (MicroArch S240, Boston Micro Fabrication) with a nominal resolution of 10 μm was used to fabricate custom polymeric molds for electrical interconnection of miniature circuit components. The molds were designed to enable reliable placement and bonding of 0201-size surface-mount components, including the tuning capacitor and red μLED. Each mold incorporated microscale traps, channels, and component housings to facilitate precise alignment and controlled deposition of low-temperature solder epoxy.

During assembly, electrical interconnections were formed using a low-temperature solder epoxy (Chip Quik, TS391LT10), which was applied within the mold-defined cavities to mechanically secure and electrically bond the components. The solder epoxy was cured using localized hot-air heating from a precision soldering station. All assembly steps were performed under a stereomicroscope to ensure accurate component placement and electrical continuity.

The Rx coil was custom fabricated (KUK Electronic AG, Switzerland) and consisted of ~250 turns of 50-μm-diameter copper wire wound around a ferrite core with a diameter of 0.7 mm, resulting in an overall coil diameter of ~1.1 mm. For circuit assembly, the tuning capacitor (18 nF) was connected in parallel with the Rx coil, and the red μLED indicator was connected in series to provide visual confirmation of device activation.

The assembled electronic components were inserted into a 3D-printed polymeric enclosure with an inner diameter of 1.2 mm, a wall thickness of ~100 μm, and a length of 8 mm. After verification of electrical continuity, the open end of the enclosure was sealed using medical-grade epoxy (EPO-Tek 301, Epoxy Technology Inc.) to provide hermetic insulation and mechanical stability. The fully encapsulated device measured ~1.4 mm in diameter and 9 mm in length, corresponding to a total volume of ~14 mm3.

Before in vivo experimentation, the encapsulated SEED devices were sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 min, followed by rinsing with sterile PBS and air-drying under aseptic conditions. This encapsulation and sterilization protocol yielded a mechanically robust, electrically insulated device capable of prolonged operation in hydrated biological environments.

Electrode-geometry selection via human-scale electric-field simulations

Human-scale electric-field simulations were performed as a design-guidance study to inform electrode-geometry selection within the geometric constraints of a needle-injectable, millimeter-scale device. The simulations were intended to enable comparative, geometry-dependent analysis of electric-field orientation and spatial confinement, rather than to predict neural activation thresholds or in vivo efficacy.

Simulations were conducted using Sim4Life’s electro-ohmic quasistatic solver (version 8.2.0, ZMT Zurich MedTech AG). The Yoon-Sun version 4.0b female anatomical model, which is derived from magnetic resonance and computed tomography imaging, provides anatomically realistic representations of soft tissues, vasculature, and peripheral nerves. SEED was positioned within the forearm region adjacent to the ulnar nerve and surrounding vasculature, consistent with the anatomical placement illustrated in Fig. 3A. Electrical material properties for all tissues were assigned according to IT’IS Tissue Database version 4.2 (Table 3).

Five electrode topologies compatible with needle injection were evaluated while holding all other parameters constant: (i) dual hemispherical end-cap electrodes, (ii) coplanar bipolar pad electrodes, (iii) dual circumferential ring electrodes, (iv) longitudinal cross-strip electrodes, and (v) spiral-helix electrodes distributed along the SEED surface (Fig. 3B).

For all configurations, a 1-V differential potential was applied between the electrodes to enable a relative comparison of electric-field distributions. The outer boundary of the computational domain was assigned a Neumann boundary condition corresponding to zero normal electric-field flux. To enable a physiologically relevant comparison of field behavior, electric-field magnitude (|E|) profiles were extracted along anatomically defined sampling paths relative to SEED and the adjacent nerve (Fig. 3H). Extracted field profiles were used to compare relative field orientation, spatial confinement, and decay behavior across electrode geometries (Fig. 3, H to J).

Measurement of wireless PTE

Wireless PTE of the SEED inductive link was experimentally quantified under representative operating conditions. The external Tx coil was driven by a function generator producing a sinusoidal carrier at 65 kHz. The signal was amplified using a low–output-impedance RF power amplifier and delivered to the series-resonant Tx coil. The transmitted power (Pin) was determined from the RMS voltage and current measured at the output terminals of the RF amplifier. The Tx voltage was measured using a digital oscilloscope (Rigol DS1104Z Plus, UltraVision, Rigol Technologies, China) with the probe connected directly to the amplifier output terminals. The Tx current was obtained using the current monitor output provided by the RF amplifier. This monitor output generates a voltage proportional to the output current, allowing the current waveform to be measured using the oscilloscope according to the calibration factor specified in the amplifier manual. Analysis and comparison of waveforms

The stimulation waveform used in SEED differs from the charge-controlled square pulses commonly used in conventional neurostimulation systems. In the SEED architecture, neural stimulation is generated using a low-frequency square modulation envelope applied to a sinusoidal RF carrier (65 kHz), enabling wireless energy transfer while defining stimulation timing without active electronics in the implant. To provide a quantitative comparison, we analyzed the effective charge delivered by the AM waveform relative to a conventional square pulse under identical electrical conditions. For a square pulse with peak voltage Vp, load R, and pulse width Tp, the delivered charge isQsquare≈VpR Tp(4)

Accordingly, the charge per pulse can be approximated asQAM≈VpπR Tp(6)yieldingQAMQsquare≈1π≈0.32(7)

This analysis indicates that for the same peak voltage and pulse width, the AM waveform delivers a reduced effective charge compared to a conventional square pulse. This reduction in effective charge implies that for a given pulse width, a higher applied voltage may be required for the AM waveform to reach activation thresholds comparable to those of conventional square-pulse stimulation, as stimulation thresholds depend on both waveform shape and effective charge delivery (28, 29). Reported thresholds for extracellular PNS using conventional square pulses are typically on the order of ~1 to 10 μC, which is consistent with the effective stimulation regime achieved by SEED (28). This reflects a trade-off between device simplicity and waveform controllability in the passive SEED architecture.

Benchtop testing of SEED

Benchtop experiments were performed to characterize the electrical response of SEED under controlled wireless powering conditions. The external Tx coil was driven by a function generator producing a sinusoidal carrier at 65 kHz with low-frequency amplitude modulation. The transmitted signal was amplified using a low–output-impedance RF amplifier and delivered to the series-resonant Tx coil.

To quantify the relationship between Tx input and Rx output, the input amplitude of the function generator was varied from 300 to 600 mVpp in 100-mVpp increments while maintaining a fixed carrier frequency (65 kHz), modulation frequency (14 Hz), and modulation pulse width (25 ms). The SEED electrodes were connected across a resistive load, and the induced voltage was measured using a digital oscilloscope.

To evaluate the effect of load impedance, the resistive load connected to the SEED electrodes was varied from 1 to 10 kΩ under fixed Tx input amplitude and fixed carrier and modulation frequencies (65 kHz and 14 Hz, respectively). The induced voltage across each load was recorded, and the corresponding current was calculated from the measured voltage and known resistance.

Temporal modulation capability was assessed by varying the modulation pulse width while maintaining constant carrier frequency (65 kHz), modulation frequency (14 Hz), and Tx input amplitude. Induced voltage measurements were performed with the SEED electrodes connected across a 4.7-kΩ resistive load, and stimulation current was calculated accordingly. All electrical measurements were acquired using a digital oscilloscope (Rigol DS1104Z Plus, UltraVision, Rigol Technologies, China).

Ex vivo functional testing in biological tissue

Ex vivo functional testing was performed using freshly dissected rat stomach tissue to evaluate device operation under biologically relevant electrical loading conditions. Tissue samples were harvested immediately after euthanasia and immersed in 1× PBS to maintain physiological ionic conductivity throughout the experiment.

SEED was placed directly on the surface of the dissected tissue, with the stimulation electrodes in contact with the hydrated tissue interface. Wireless powering was achieved using a 65-kHz carrier frequency with low-frequency amplitude modulation generated by the external Tx. During testing, the Tx input amplitude was fixed, and stimulation parameters were defined by externally programmed modulation waveforms.

To assess temporal programmability under ex vivo conditions, the modulation pulse width was varied while maintaining constant carrier frequency and modulation frequency. The induced voltage waveform delivered to the tissue was measured using a digital oscilloscope connected across the stimulation electrodes. All measurements were performed while the tissue remained fully hydrated in PBS.

Electrical recordings obtained under ex vivo conditions were compared to benchtop measurements acquired using resistive loads to verify consistency in waveform shape and amplitude under conductive biological loading. No tissue damage or gross changes in tissue appearance were observed during the course of ex vivo testing.

Percutaneous injectability and agarose phantom testing

The percutaneous injectability and postdeployment functionality of SEED were evaluated using a tissue-mimicking agarose phantom. Agarose phantoms were prepared at 2 wt % in 1× PBS and cast into rectangular molds to provide a hydrated, mechanically compliant environment with physiological ionic conductivity.

SEED was delivered into the agarose phantom using a standard 14-gauge curved hypodermic needle. SEED was loaded into the needle lumen before insertion and expelled into the phantom by advancing the plunger under manual control. Injection was performed at room temperature, and the SEED position within the phantom was visually confirmed following deployment.

Postinjection electrical functionality was assessed by wirelessly powering the injected device using a 65-kHz carrier frequency with low-frequency amplitude modulation generated by the external Tx. Successful operation was confirmed by illumination of the integrated red μLED during repeated activation cycles. Devices were cycled on and off multiple times to verify the reproducibility of wireless activation after needle transit and embedding in the hydrated phantom.

To assess encapsulation robustness under prolonged tissue-like exposure, injected devices were maintained within the agarose phantom for up to 60 days. During this period, devices were periodically wirelessly powered to confirm continued electrical operation and structural integrity.

Ultrasound imaging was used to evaluate the image-guided visualization and postdeployment localization of the injected SEED. Imaging was performed using a clinical ultrasound system (Siemens ACUSON X300, Siemens Healthineers, Germany). SEED was imaged during needle insertion to visualize the trajectory and during postinjection placement within the agarose phantom. Additional ultrasound imaging was conducted with SEED embedded in a chicken-leg tissue model to assess acoustic visibility under heterogeneous tissue conditions.

In vivo percutaneous deployment and functional neuromodulation

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of New York University Abu Dhabi and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (protocol number: 21-0007A4). Adult female Sprague-Dawley rats (10 to 12 months old) were used for all experiments and housed under a 12-hour light-dark cycle with food and water provided ad libitum. Animals were anesthetized using inhalational isoflurane and placed in a prone position on a temperature-controlled surgical platform throughout the procedure.

SEED was delivered percutaneously using a standard 14-gauge curved hypodermic needle. The needle was inserted into the hindlimb and advanced toward the sciatic nerve region under anatomical guidance. SEED was expelled from the needle lumen by advancing the plunger and positioned adjacent to the sciatic nerve without surgical exposure. Following deployment, the needle was withdrawn, and the injection site was visually inspected.

Wireless activation of the implanted SEED was achieved using an external Tx operating at a 65-kHz carrier frequency with low-frequency amplitude modulation. Device operation was verified during the procedure by visible transcutaneous illumination of the integrated red μLED. Stimulation waveforms were defined externally at the Tx and delivered wirelessly to the implanted device.

Functional neuromodulation was assessed by monitoring hindlimb motion during stimulation. Leg movement was recorded using a high-speed camera positioned orthogonally to the limb, and video recordings were acquired during stimulation at defined modulation frequencies and Tx input amplitudes. Displacement traces were extracted offline from the recorded videos for subsequent analysis.

Following completion of functional testing, device localization was assessed using μCT imaging. Imaging was performed to visualize the position of the implanted SEED relative to the surrounding anatomy and to confirm device retention at the deployment site.
