Evaluating sugarcane irrigation water for sustainable enhanced oil recovery and efficient waste management
Authors: Shamim Miraei, Maysam Mohammadzadeh-Shirazi, Azim Kalantariasl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal water scarcity is driving the oil and gas sector to explore unconventional water sources for enhanced oil recovery (EOR). This study introduces sugarcane irrigation water as a sustainable alternative injection fluid. Two water samples were tested against five crude oils to assess emulsion formation, interfacial tension (IFT), wettability alteration, and organic deposition. Emulsion stability ranged from 37 to 82% after 3 h at 80 °C, while IFT decreased from 21.4 to 9.7 mN m⁻¹. Contact angle reductions of up to 36° indicated a partial shift from oil-wet to neutral-wet conditions. Phase separation varied widely (0–100%), depending on crude type and water–oil mixing ratio, with enhanced emulsion stability correlating qualitatively with lower IFT. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that sugarcane irrigation water can function effectively as an EOR injection brine, linking agricultural return flows to sustainable petroleum operations without compromising recovery. These results highlight the dual advantage of repurposing agricultural drainage and expand the portfolio of unconventional injection waters by demonstrating physicochemical compatibility with crude oils.Keywords: Sugarcane irrigation water, Enhanced oil recovery (EOR), Sustainable water solutions, Emulsion formation, Environmental sustainability, Agricultural water reuse in oil recoverySubject terms: Chemical engineering, Environmental chemistry, Environmental impact

Introduction

The stages of oil production from the reservoir are divided into three primary, secondary, and tertiary (or enhanced recovery) production periods. In the primary stage, only natural reservoir energy is utilized for production, and in the secondary production stage, water and gas are injected to maintain reservoir pressure. Water injection, due to its ease, low cost, and satisfactory efficiency, has been recognized as one of the common and practical methods for pressure maintenance over many years. After the primary and secondary stages of reservoir production, a considerable amount of oil still remains, various fluid injections are performed to activate enhanced oil recovery mechanisms and improve the production4. Various fluid injection methods, including the injection of smart water (seawater, low-salinity water, carbonate water, etc.), are used to activate enhanced oil recovery (EOR) mechanisms and improve production.

Water injection is a widely employed method in reservoir pressure maintenance and enhanced oil recovery. However, it can also have adverse effects, including emulsion formation, reservoir deposition, and reduced fluid mobility within the reservoir9. Emulsion formation during water injection in reservoirs plays a dual role, offering both benefits and challenges. On the positive side, emulsions can improve sweep efficiency by increasing the viscosity of the injected water, reducing its mobility, and preventing it from bypassing oil-rich zones. They also enhance mobility control by blocking high-permeability channels (thief zones), forcing water into less permeable regions containing residual oil, and facilitating a piston-like oil displacement toward production wells. However, uncontrolled emulsion formation can cause significant operational issues, including flow assurance problems due to increased flow resistance, formation damage from pore blockages near wellbores, and processing challenges at surface facilities where separating emulsions into oil and water phases is costly and complex. Balancing the advantages of emulsions with their potential drawbacks is essential for maximizing oil recovery and minimizing operational risks in water injection projects19.

Assessing injection water quality is vital to maintain reservoir performance. Key steps include controlling solids, analyzing ionic composition, and preventing deposits to ensure optimal injection rates with minimal pressure loss3. Previous studies have extensively examined factors contributing to reservoir damage during water injection and their impact on well injectivity. Variables such as temperature, pressure, pH variations, and water composition have been identified as key contributors. For instance, as water flows through the wellbore and reservoir, temperature increases can alter the solubility of dissolved materials, leading to salt precipitation in pipelines or reservoir rock. Additionally, suspended solid particles in injection water can cause formation damage, as particle size significantly influences pore throat plugging and permeability reduction2.

Hessi et al. studied the water quality from Prudhoe Bay, Alaska, and found that the presence of solid particles and oil droplets in injection water (ranging from 0.5 ppm to 11.5 ppm, with an average of 3.1 ppm) during water flooding could block reservoir channels, reducing injectivity8. Similarly, a water flooding study in the North Sea revealed that solid particles caused greater reservoir damage compared to oil droplets24. Furthermore, the compatibility of injection water with formation water plays a critical role. For instance, Pearls et al. noted that salts in the aqueous phase stabilize emulsions by increasing ionic strength, which enhances compound adsorption on surfaces21. Conversely, water absorption by reservoir rocks can lead to swelling, reducing pore sizes and permeability. The formation of emulsions and organic deposits due to incompatibility between water and oil samples is a significant challenge17. Yuan and Tad simulated water injection into carbonate reservoirs and observed that SO4²⁻rich solutions caused CaSO4 deposition, reducing the permeability of porous media15. Similarly, Thompson et al. reported that crude oil devoid of waxes and solid particles does not form stable emulsions22, while Lee demonstrated that crude oils lacking polar components also exhibit low emulsion stability. Interfacial tension (IFT) further influences fluid flow and emulsion formation, affecting wettability and water permeability13.

Government regulations have limited the use of freshwater sources for EOR operations, and seawater is available only for offshore projects. So, for onshore EOR projects, produced water is the preferred water source6. The increasing global population and climate change have disrupted the balance between water supply and demand, necessitating the exploration of unconventional water sources. Irrigation water use includes water that is applied by an irrigation system to sustain plant growth in agricultural and horticultural practices. Agricultural wastewater harms the environment through nutrient runoff, causing eutrophication and water pollution. Chemical contaminants threaten groundwater and biodiversity, while soil salinization reduces land productivity. Managing wastewater sustainably is crucial to protecting ecosystems and mitigating climate impacts. Agricultural irrigation water, particularly from sugarcane fields, represents a promising option due to its volume and low salinity. A significant portion of irrigation water not consumed by plants is discharged, providing an opportunity for reuse. With primary treatment, this water can be reused for irrigation or returned to drinking water sources. It also has potential as injection water for oilfields.

Most previous research on enhanced oil recovery (EOR) has focused on conventional injection waters such as seawater, produced water, or chemically engineered low-salinity brines. Seminal works, such as11, demonstrated the potential of low-salinity flooding in sandstone reservoirs, while more recent studies7,23 have investigated the roles of ion exchange, fines migration, and mineral reactions in carbonate systems. These studies established the mechanistic basis for low-salinity EOR but remain tied to industrial and natural water sources that often compete with agricultural and municipal needs.

Although water reuse has gained increasing attention, most recent work has focused on treated produced water or municipal wastewater as injection alternatives, leaving agricultural return flows virtually unexplored. Despite being widely available in regions with large-scale farming, drainage waters such as those from sugarcane irrigation have not been systematically evaluated for their suitability in EOR. This represents a significant knowledge gap, given their moderate salinity, unique ionic composition, and potential for dual use in both agriculture and petroleum operations.

This study addresses that gap by, for the first time, introducing sugarcane irrigation water as an EOR agent and evaluating its physicochemical compatibility with crude oils. The work systematically examines emulsion stability, interfacial tension, wettability alteration, and oil–water interactions under reservoir-relevant conditions. Compared with earlier efforts on seawater and tailored brines, this study not only expands the portfolio of unconventional injection waters but also contributes to global sustainability goals by promoting circular water use, reducing dependence on freshwater, and minimizing the environmental footprint of petroleum operations.

Materials and methods

Materials

Samples were transferred immediately to pre-cleaned high-density polyethylene bottles, stored at 4 °C, and analyzed/used within 14 days to minimize biological or chemical changes. Prior to experiments, waters were filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters to remove suspended solids. No chemical adjustment (e.g., pH or salinity modification) was performed. These procedures follow standard protocols for water sample preservation and dissolved-fraction definition.

Elevated concentrations of divalent cations such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ can enhance surface charge interactions, thereby influencing wettability alteration and emulsion stability. Anions such as SO₄²⁻ and Cl⁻ contribute to scaling potential and mineral deposition, which can impact the overall efficiency of EOR processes.

Methodology

Emulsion stability test procedure

Emulsion stability between irrigation water and crude oil was evaluated following the ASTM D3707 framework. Water and oil samples were preheated to 80 °C for 20 min. Equal volumes (40 mL each) were combined in clean beakers and stirred at 1500 rpm for 15 min using a mechanical electric stirrer. The mixing speed was selected based on literature showing finer droplet formation and enhanced water-in-oil emulsion stability at higher stirring rates (Ghannam 2005; Elleuch et al. 2008; Solè et al. 2010).

After mixing, emulsions were transferred to graduated cylinders and incubated in a convection oven at 80 °C. Separated water volumes were recorded at 5, 10, 15, 30 min; 1, 2, 3, 24, 48, and 72 h. Phase separation (%) was calculated as:

Each oil–water pair was tested in triplicate (n = 3) and reported as mean ± SD. Organic deposits were assessed by filtering emulsions on Whatman paper and inspecting visually and via optical microscopy. Microphotographs were captured using a Dino-Lite digital microscope across at least five fields of view per sample (Fig. 1).

Schematic illustration of the compatibility test procedure.

Interfacial tension (IFT) test procedure

IFT between crude oil and irrigation water was measured at 25 °C and 1 atm using the pendant-drop method. Droplet shapes were captured with a Dino-Lite digital microscope and analyzed in ImageJ using Young–Laplace fitting. Densities were measured independently (Table 2). Three independent drops were analyzed per oil–water pair (n = 3) and reported as mean ± SD in mN m⁻¹. This approach follows ISO 19403-3 guidance (Fig. 2).

Detailed procedure for the surface tension measurement.

Wettability alteration test procedure

Contact angles were measured in the oil-on-rock-in-water configuration at 25 °C and 1 atm. Carbonate thin sections were initially water-wet, then aged for 21 days at 80 °C in crude oil to establish oil-wet conditions. Contact angles were measured at 1 day, 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 weeks, 4 weeks, and 5 weeks while sections were immersed in sugarcane irrigation water.

The setup used a vertically mounted syringe pump and U-shaped needle to form droplets that detached naturally onto the rock surface submerged in water. Three droplets per thin section, three sections per condition (n = 9) were analyzed and reported as mean ± SD. Image analysis was performed in ImageJ. Interpretation follows oil–water–rock conventions: < 90° water-wet, ≈ 90° neutral-wet, > 90° oil-wet; shifts toward neutral-wet or reduced oil-wetness are reported as appropriate (Fig. 3).

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the potential of sugarcane irrigation water for enhanced oil recovery, a few limitations should be noted. IFT measurements were conducted at 25 °C, while emulsion tests were performed at 80 °C, so correlations are indicative rather than exact. Contact-angle measurements may not fully replicate in-situ reservoir conditions, and coreflood, injectivity, corrosion, or microbial assessments were not included. Despite these points, the results are robust and offer a strong foundation for future studies under more field-relevant conditions.

Crude oils with higher asphaltene and resin content (e.g., crude oil E) form more stable emulsions due to enhanced surface activity. Higher water content disrupts the oil-water balance, leading to emulsification.

Based on the results, it can be concluded that the lowest IFT is associated with sugarcane irrigation water 2 in contact with crude oil E, while the highest IFT is observed with sugarcane irrigation water 2 in contact with crude oil D. Crude oils with more polar components reduce IFT due to stronger interfacial interactions. The presence of ions such as Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ alters IFT by modulating ionic strength. Additionally, surfactants naturally present in crude oils further reduce interfacial tension.

Interfacial tension values (Table 6) showed deviations below 0.6 mN·m⁻¹, which aligns well with previously reported ranges for pendant drop tensiometry under controlled laboratory conditions11 (Wang et al. 2021).

The results indicate a significant inverse relationship between emulsion stability and interfacial tension (IFT). Specifically, as emulsion stability increases, IFT decreases. This observation can be attributed to the enhanced distribution of surfactant molecules at the interface, which reduces interfacial energy and prevents phase separation. A reduction in IFT not only improves emulsion stability but also highlights the efficiency of interfacial film formation, a critical factor in the thermodynamic and kinetic stabilization of emulsions. These findings emphasize the pivotal role of interfacial phenomena in determining the long-term stability of emulsions, particularly in applications where stability is crucial.

Additionally, lowering IFT can improve oil recovery by reducing capillary forces, facilitating the mobilization of trapped oil, and promoting its dispersion into the aqueous phase as emulsions. The presence of active ions in brine can significantly influence both the oil-brine IFT and contact angle (CA), causing them to vary over time.

Wettability alteration results

Mechanistic insights into emulsion stability, IFT, and wettability alteration

The observed correlations between interfacial tension (IFT), emulsion stability, and wettability alteration can be further explained by the chemical and physical interactions at the oil–water interface. Crude oils contain polar components such as asphaltenes, resins, and naturally occurring surfactants, which adsorb at the oil–water interface and reduce interfacial energy, thereby stabilizing emulsions5,14. Our results indicate that crude oils with higher asphaltene and resin content, such as Crude Oil E, form more stable emulsions due to enhanced surface activity and stronger adsorption at the interface.

The presence of divalent cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) and monovalent ions (Na⁺, K⁺) in sugarcane irrigation water further modulates interfacial phenomena. Divalent cations can bridge negatively charged functional groups in polar oil fractions, enhancing emulsion stability and promoting wettability alteration towards a more water-wet state20. Monovalent ions mainly influence the ionic strength of the aqueous phase, which can screen electrostatic repulsion between droplets and thus affect emulsion coalescence and droplet size distribution. Sulfate and chloride ions also play a role in scaling potential and may indirectly influence emulsion behavior through mineral deposition at the interface.

Mechanical and thermodynamic factors are equally important. Emulsions are stabilized not only by chemical adsorption but also by the formation of viscoelastic interfacial films, which prevent droplet coalescence10. The reduction in viscosity observed after emulsion formation is largely due to mechanical dispersion of oil aggregates, while increases in density are associated with water droplets trapped within the emulsion (Table 4). Ionic exchange between the water phase and crude oil can further modify fluid properties, influencing both emulsion stability and wettability (Table 5).

Although direct measurement of surfactant activity and functional group interactions was beyond the scope of this study, the SARA analysis combined with observed IFT and emulsion behaviors provides indirect evidence of their mechanistic roles. Future work may include FTIR, NMR, or surface tensiometry to quantify these interactions and further validate the proposed mechanisms1614.

Overall, these mechanistic insights provide a more comprehensive understanding of how sugarcane irrigation water interacts with crude oils, highlighting the chemical and physical bases for the observed correlations between IFT, emulsion stability, and wettability alteration, which are critical for effective EOR applications.

Conclusion

This study introduces sugarcane irrigation water as a novel, sustainable candidate for enhanced oil recovery, demonstrating its ability to form stable emulsions, reduce interfacial tension, alter wettability, and interact chemically with crude oil. The observed effects are highly dependent on the specific chemistry of both the crude oil and the irrigation water, highlighting the importance of tailored compatibility assessments for each reservoir.

Practical implications

Sugarcane irrigation water offers a cost-effective, environmentally friendly alternative for EOR, reducing dependence on synthetic chemicals and mitigating ecological impacts, particularly in water-scarce regions. Its application, however, requires careful assessment of compatibility with specific crude oils and produced waters to prevent reservoir damage or deposition. Future work should optimize injection strategies for various reservoir conditions, evaluate IFT under realistic temperatures, and assess long-term environmental consequences.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore sugarcane irrigation water for EOR, providing a promising pathway for sustainable, eco-conscious oil recovery.

It should be noted that IFT measurements were performed at 25 °C due to instrumentation constraints, whereas emulsion stability was evaluated at 80 °C. Therefore, the reported correlation between IFT and emulsion behavior is qualitative, and future work should address IFT determination under reservoir-relevant temperatures to strengthen this link.
