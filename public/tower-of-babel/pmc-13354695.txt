CAR T-cell Therapy for Extramedullary Multiple Myeloma: from Early Response to Durable Disease Control
Authors: Ziyi Yang, Mi Zhou, Yi Xiao
Published: 2026

Comment

Extramedullary disease (EMD) in multiple myeloma should be treated as a biologically high-risk state, not merely as an anatomical variant of relapse. The clinically important distinction is between paraskeletal disease, which extends from bone into adjacent soft tissue, and true extraosseous EMD, which is discontinuous from bone and reflects haematogenous dissemination. This distinction matters in the CAR T-cell era because true extraosseous EMD marks marrow escape, clonal evolution, altered adhesion and homing programmes, and an extramedullary immune niche that can differ substantially from marrow disease. When these entities are pooled, the field risks overestimating the durability of cell therapy in the subgroup that most needs improvement [1].

BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy has changed the therapeutic landscape of relapsed or refractory myeloma by producing rapid cytoreduction and deep remissions after a single cellular product. In EMD, however, the central question is no longer whether CAR T cells can induce a response. The more important question is why early responses so often fail to become durable disease control. This shift in emphasis is clinically important. Patients with active extraosseous EMD receiving standard-of-care CAR T-cell therapy have lower response rates and shorter progression-free and overall survival than patients without EMD [2]. A multicentre idecabtagene vicleucel analysis reached a similar conclusion, showing that responses occur but are less durable in patients with EMD [3]. These data argue against excluding patients with EMD from CAR T-cell therapy; instead, they identify EMD as a stringent test of whether a cellular therapy pathway can convert early tumour killing into sustained systemic control.

Several mechanisms explain this durability gap. First, EMD is spatially heterogeneous. A patient may have marrow disease, paraskeletal lesions and true extraosseous deposits that differ in antigen density, proliferative kinetics and local immune composition. Marrow minimal residual disease negativity therefore cannot be assumed to represent whole-body disease control. Second, CAR T-cell exposure is not uniform across anatomical sites. Adequate expansion in blood does not guarantee trafficking into bulky, poorly vascularised or organ-based lesions. Third, single-antigen targeting remains vulnerable to antigenic diversification. BCMA-positive lesions may respond while BCMA-low or immune-excluded lesions persist. Fourth, the extramedullary microenvironment itself can limit cellular immunity. In patients receiving anti-BCMA CAR T-cell-based therapy, extramedullary lesions have shown weaker remission than intramedullary disease and may be enriched for exhausted CD8 + T cells and immunosuppressive macrophage populations [4]. These findings place EMD at the intersection of tumour-intrinsic aggressiveness and lesion-level immune resistance.

The practical consequence is that EMD should become a prespecified stratification and monitoring variable in future CAR T-cell trials. At baseline, studies should distinguish paraskeletal disease from true extraosseous EMD and report lesion number, organ site, bulky disease, previous local therapy, high-risk cytogenetics and, where feasible, molecular features of extramedullary lesions. This change is immediately translatable because it requires better trial design rather than a new technology. It would also clarify whether poor outcome is driven by lesion biology, tumour burden, antigen loss, inadequate trafficking or post-infusion management.

The second near-term priority is imaging-integrated assessment. Conventional myeloma response criteria and marrow MRD are insufficient when relapse can occur outside the marrow without a parallel rise in serum markers. PET/CT or whole-body MRI should therefore be incorporated at baseline, early after infusion and during surveillance for patients with EMD. Response should be recorded at both patient and lesion level, including metabolic clearance, residual focal activity, new lesion formation and discordance between marrow and extramedullary sites. This approach would prevent overinterpretation of serological or marrow responses and would identify oligoprogressive or isolated extraosseous relapse earlier.

The third priority is rational bridging and post-infusion management. For patients with bulky, symptomatic or organ-threatening lesions, local radiotherapy or short-course systemic cytoreduction may create a safer and more favourable starting point for infusion. Bridging should be chosen to reduce tumour burden and inflammatory risk while preserving T-cell fitness after leukapheresis. After infusion, passive observation may be insufficient for true extraosseous EMD. Risk-adapted strategies, including local treatment of residual lesions, early treatment at imaging progression, and clinical-trial testing of post-CAR T-cell consolidation with immunomodulatory drugs or bispecific antibodies, deserve prospective evaluation. These approaches should not be presented as established maintenance therapy, but they address the specific clinical problem that early remission is often not durable.

Next-generation engineering remains important, but it should be positioned realistically. Dual-target or tandem CAR T cells, alternative targets such as GPRC5D, humanised binders, optimised costimulation and armoured constructs may reduce antigen escape, improve persistence or counteract immunosuppressive niches. Early BCMA/GPRC5D dual-target CAR T-cell therapy has shown encouraging activity in relapsed or refractory myeloma with EMD [5]. Nevertheless, multi-targeting alone cannot solve target-independent barriers such as poor trafficking, incomplete lesion infiltration, macrophage-rich microenvironments and delayed detection of extramedullary relapse. Engineering innovation should therefore be tested within EMD-enriched and EMD-stratified trials that include imaging endpoints and explicit post-infusion pathways.

In this framework, CAR T-cell therapy is not a marginal option for EMD; it is a potent but incomplete platform. Its value lies in rapid cytoreduction and the possibility of deep remission, while its limitation is the inconsistent translation of that remission into durable control. The field should move from asking whether EMD responds to asking how each patient with EMD should be classified, bridged, imaged, consolidated and surveilled. For true extraosseous EMD, durable benefit will probably require an integrated pathway: precise anatomical and biological classification, lesion-level imaging response, controlled bridging, early recognition of spatial relapse, and prospective testing of dual-target or sequential immune strategies. Mechanisms and Barriers of CAR T-cell Therapy in Extramedullary Multiple Myeloma (EMD) CAR-T therapy begins with collection and genetic engineering of patient T cells to express a chimeric antigen receptor targeting myeloma-associated antigens, most commonly BCMA. After expansion and infusion, CAR-T cells traffic through the circulation, infiltrate extramedullary lesions, recognize antigen-positive myeloma cells, form immune synapses, and become activated. Activated CAR-T cells release cytotoxic molecules such as perforin and granzyme, induce tumor-cell apoptosis, and amplify antitumor immunity. However, therapeutic efficacy may be limited by high tumor burden, antigen heterogeneity, poor lesion infiltration, immunosuppressive microenvironments, T-cell exhaustion, and antigen escape
