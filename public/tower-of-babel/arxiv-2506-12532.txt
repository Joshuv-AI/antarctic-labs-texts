Bayesian inference for the learning rate in Generalised Bayesian Inference
Jeong Eun Lee, Sitong Liu, Geoff K. Nicholls

Abstract
In Generalised Bayesian Inference (GBI), the learning rate and hyperparameters of the loss must be estimated. However, these inference-hyperparameters can’t be estimated jointly with the other parameters by giving them a prior, as we discuss. Several methods for estimating the learning rate have been given which elicit and minimise a loss based on the goals of the overall inference (in our case, prediction of new data). However, in some settings there exists an unknown “true” learning rate about which it is meaningful to have prior belief and it is then possible to use Bayesian inference with held out data to get a posterior for the learning rate. We give conditions under which this posterior concentrates on the optimal rate and suggest hyperparameter estimators derived from this posterior. The new framework supports joint estimation and uncertainty quatification for inference hyperparameters. Experiments show that the resulting GBI-posteriors out-perform Bayesian inference on simulated test data and select optimal or near optimal hyperparameter values in a large real problem of text analysis. Generalised Bayesian inference is particularly useful for combining multiple data sets and most of our examples belong to that setting. As a side note we give asymptotic results for some of the special “multi-modular” Generalised Bayes posteriors, which we use in our examples.

1
Introduction

Generalised Bayesian Inference (GBI) with a Gibbs posterior
(
Chernozhukov and Hong 2003
;
Zhang 2006
;
Jiang and Tanner 2008
;
Bissiri et al. 2016
;
Winter et al. 2023
)
gives a general framework for updating belief in parametric models which has proven useful in many settings. In GBI the usual parametric observation model
p({\mathbf{x}}|\phi)
for data
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
given a parameter
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}}
is replaced by a loss
\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})
and the update from prior
\pi(\phi)
to posterior becomes

\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})\propto\exp(-\eta\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}}))\pi(\phi),

(1)

where
s=(\eta,\beta)
.
The hyperparameter
\eta>0
is called the learning rate
as it controls the rate at which information about informing
\phi
accumulates as data is gathered. In some settings the loss has its own hyperparameters
\beta
which are distinct from
\phi
. We call
\eta
and
\beta
inference hyperparameters (as opposed to model hyperparameters).

The inference hyperparameters
\eta
and
\beta
must be chosen to support the goals of the inference. Several different methods for estimating them have been given, reflecting different goals. See
Wu and Martin 2023
for an overview.
Popular methods include matching the frequentist coverage probability
(
Styring et al. 2017
;
Winter et al. 2023
)
, matching the Hyvarinen score
(
Yonekura and Sugasawa 2023
)
and matching the asymptotic covariance using the Fisher information
(
Lyddon et al. 2019
;
Holmes and Walker 2017
;
Frazier and Nott 2024
)
. Methods related to cross validation and sequential prediction
(
Grünwald 2012
;
Grünwald and van Ommen 2017
;
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
)
have been explored, and
Carmona et al. 2024
minimises the Posterior Mean Squared Error in a kind of supervised classification task.

We are sometimes asked, “why not treat the hyperparameters
as extra variables alongside
\phi
, give them a prior
\rho(\eta,\beta)
and estimate everything together using the
{\mathbf{x}}
data”? Bayes theorem gives a posterior for
\eta,\beta
and
\phi
proportional to

\rho(\eta,\beta)\pi(\phi)\frac{\exp(-\eta\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}}))}{c(\phi,\eta,\beta)}

where
c(\phi,\eta,\beta)
normalises the “likelihood”,
\exp(-\eta\ell_{\beta})
over
{\mathbf{x}}
. This appears because Bayes rule
holds for a normalised observation model but leaves us with the wrong
\phi
dependence. Could we simply accept the new
\phi
-dependence?
Miller and Dunson 2018
observe that this is just model elaboration - the whole point of GBI is that adds a degree of freedom to model elaboration by removing the requirement that
\exp(-\eta\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}}))
be normalised. Perhaps the belief update for
\eta
and
\beta
given above is itself Generalised Bayes, which doesn’t require a normalised observation model? This would be GBI with a joint loss for
\eta,\beta
and
\phi
equal to
\eta\,\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})
which is not justified by any inferential objective for
\eta
. This idea is wrong however you look at it.

One straightforward approach is to choose
\eta
and
\beta
to maximise a utility evaluated on held-out calibration data. Methods based on LOOCV estimates of the ELPPD (for example,
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
, who use the WAIC) and SafeBayes (
Grünwald and van Ommen 2017
, who use sequential prediction) are related. We make go a step further and use calibration data in a GBI belief update for
\eta
and
\beta
. In this setting the loss for
\eta
and
\beta
is justified by a clear inferential objective, which is to maximise the posterior probability for an infinite and unobserved population of test data.

One area in which GBI has found applications is handling misspecification in models for multiple heterogeneous data sets. In this setting the modules making up the overall model have different losses each with their own learning rate.
Liu and Goudie 2024
and
Carmona and Nicholls 2022
treat multi-modular problems of this kind.
We choose to illustrate Bayesian estimation of inference hyperparameters using a belief update called Semi-Modular-Inference (SMI) which is tailored for multi-modular models. The SMI-posterior proves to be an instance of a Sequential-Gibbs posterior
(
Winter et al. 2023
)
. This connection hadn’t been made in the literature. It is useful as it brings the considerable body of theory given in
Winter et al. 2023
to bear on SMI-posteriors.

2
Bayesian Inference for Inference Hyperparameters

When we define a loss
\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})
for use in GBI, we often want to keep the parametric link
p({\mathbf{x}}|\phi)
between data and parameter but adjust the belief update to allow for misspecification. Applications such as
Rigon et al. 2023
directly constructing a loss without an observation model seem rather rare.
Let
x
be a generic component of
{\mathbf{x}}
. Denote by
p^{*}(x)
the true generative model for the data. We suppose components are conditionally independent given
p^{*}
(with a block structure we detail below). One way to define the loss is to penalise departures from
p^{*}
using a suitable divergence
D(p^{*}(\cdot)||p(\cdot|\phi))
and use the data to estimate
D
as a function of
\phi
.
Jewson et al. 2018
and
Jewson and Rossell 2022
discuss some of the factors involved in choosing
D
and this is discussed in a wider class of generalisations of Bayesian inference in
Knoblauch et al. 2022
.

We seek a divergence we can estimate, in which
p^{*}
doesn’t need to be evaluated but only simulated. One way to achieve this is to make the divergence
D
a Bregman divergence,

D_{f}(p^{*}||p)=\int f(p^{*}(x))dx-\int f(p(x|\phi))dx-\int f^{\prime}(p(x|\phi))(p^{*}(x)-p(x|\phi))dx,

where
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly-convex continuously-differentiable function and

f^{\prime}
is its derivative. The general “Bregman” loss function
\ell_{f}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})
, which depends on the parameterisation of
f
, is an unbiased estimate of
D_{f}
. Up to terms not depending on
\phi
,

\ell_{f}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})=n\mathbb{E}_{x|\phi}(f^{\prime}(p(x|\phi)))-n\int f(p(x|\phi))dx-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{\prime}(p(x_{i}|\phi))\,.

(2)

When
f(p)=p\log(p)-p
the loss is the negative log-likelihood and (
1
) is the
power posterior
,
\pi_{\eta}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})\propto p({\mathbf{x}}|\phi)^{\eta}\pi(\phi)
. If
f(p;\beta)=(p^{\beta}-1)/\beta(\beta-1)
then
\ell_{\beta}
in (
2
) is the
\beta
-loss
(
Basu et al. 1998
;
Minami and Shinto 2002
;
Stummer and Vajda 2012
)
up to constants not depending on
\phi
,

\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{\beta-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(x_{i}|\phi)^{\beta-1}+\frac{n}{\beta}\int p(x|\phi)^{\beta}dx.

(3)

We call the posterior in (
1
) the
\beta
-loss posterior
when
\ell_{\beta}(\phi;{\mathbf{x}})
is the
\beta
-loss.
In applications the integrals are evaluated by quadrature or simulation. Inference with the
\beta
-loss posterior is robust to outliers
(
Knoblauch et al. 2022
;
Jewson et al. 2024
)
. It interpolates the Itakura–Saito loss (
\beta=0
), the negative log-likelihood (
\beta=1
) and the L2-norm divergence (
\beta=2
). In this paper we consider the power posterior and the
\beta
-loss posterior.

Suppose the goal is to predict a “block” of test data
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{m}),\ z\sim p^{*}
using the model
p(z|\phi)
and that the loss for this operation is
l(s;z,{\mathbf{x}})=-\log(p_{s}(z|{\mathbf{x}}))
, where

p_{s}(z|{\mathbf{x}})=\int p(z|\phi)\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})d\phi

is the posterior predictive probability for the test data. The risk,

\tilde{l}(s;{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p^{*}}(l(s;z,{\mathbf{x}}))\,,

(4)

is the (negative of the) Expected Log Joint Posterior Predictive or ELJPD
(
Cooper et al. 2024
)
, reducing to the Expected Log Pointwise Posterior Predictive or ELPPD when
m=1

(
Vehtari et al. 2017
)
.
What exactly makes a block is discussed in
Cooper et al. 2024
. In our case, we assume that if
z
and
z^{\prime}
are two blocks of test data then
z
and
z^{\prime}
are independent given
p^{*}
and also independent in the fitted model
p(z,z^{\prime}|\phi)=p(z|\phi)p(z^{\prime}|\phi)
. This simplifies asymptotic analysis. We find in our experiments that asymptotic properties of hyperparameter estimates are robust to some correlation between blocks. Since
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}
and
z
all have the same distribution we assume the same block structure in the training
{\mathbf{x}}
and calibration data
{\mathbf{y}}
.

In this paper we show that
s
may be estimated using held-out data and another layer of GBI targeting
s
.
Suppose we have in addition to
{\mathbf{x}}
a “calibration data set” of
J
blocks of
m
samples,
{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}=(y_{(1)},\dots,y_{(J)})
with
y_{(j)}=(y_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,y_{jm})
for
j=1,\dots,J
distributed according to
p^{*}
(obtained for example by splitting
{\mathbf{x}}
into training and calibration data).
We can estimate a loss off these data in two different ways, leading to the
pooled
and
product
posteriors. In the product case (which is our main interest),

l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})=-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log(p_{s}(y_{(j)}|{\mathbf{x}}))\,,

(5)

and in the pooled case, with loss
l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,Jm)},{\mathbf{x}})
, all the calibration data is taken as a single block.

Suppose that for fixed
{\mathbf{x}}
there is a unique
s^{*}=(\eta^{*},\beta^{*})
minimising the risk in (
4
) associated with prediction of a block
z
of test data. These “pseudo optimal” hyperparameters depend on the training data
{\mathbf{x}}
as they should: they adjust the predictive distribution determined by the misspecified observation model to better match the distribution in the sample population
z\sim p^{*}
; since
n
is finite, the posterior predictive need not be given by a Bayesian belief update even if the models are well specified. If
s^{*}
exists then we have data
{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}
and a loss

l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
informing
s
given
{\mathbf{y}}
, so we can use a second round of GBI, taking a prior
\rho(s)
on the hyperparameters and defining a posterior

\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})\propto\rho(s)\prod_{j=1}^{J}p_{s}(y_{(j)}|{\mathbf{x}})\,,\ s\in\Omega_{s}.

(6)

Here
\Omega_{s}
is the space of possible
(\eta,\beta)
-values. This is often low dimensional. The power posterior has a learning rate so
\Omega_{s}=[0,1]
and if (
1
) is the
\beta
-loss posterior with a learning rate then

\Omega_{s}=[0,1]\times[0,\infty)
. One setting where the dimension of
\eta
can get larger is when the likelihood is a product over multiple data sets and each factor has its own learning rate.
Carmona and Nicholls 2022
gives variational methods for scalable inference in this setting.

When we use (
6
) to estimate
s
we can work with the joint posterior

\rho(s,\phi_{(1:J)}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})\propto\rho(s)\prod_{j=1}^{J}p(y_{(j)}|\phi_{(j)})\pi_{s}(\phi_{(j)}|{\mathbf{x}})\,.

(7)

This can be sampled using a nested MCMC algorithm given in Appendix
A
. However, as the dimension of
s
is typically just one or two, the block-predictive functions
p_{s}(y_{(j)}|{\mathbf{x}})
can be simply estimated, using MCMC samples
\phi^{(t)}\sim\pi_{s}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}}),\ t=1,\dots,T
, at points on a lattice of
s
values and all further calculations carried out using quadrature.

2.1
Hyperparameter estimation and evaluation criteria

In practice the calibration data is obtained by splitting the data into training
{\mathbf{x}}
and calibration
{\mathbf{y}}
. This is discussed further in Appendix
F
. Once we have an estimate
\hat{s}
for
s
we pool the training and calibration data sets,
throw away the sampled
\phi_{1:J}
in (
7
), and go forward with
\pi_{\hat{s}}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
using all the data. We are assuming that if
\hat{s}
adjusts the predictive to make
p_{\hat{s}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}})
match the distribution of
{\mathbf{y}}
then
\hat{s}
will adjust
p_{\hat{s}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
to match the distribution of the unseen test data
z
. We see support for this in several experiments Appendices
E.2.1
and
E.2.2
.

We consider three
s
-estimators: the posterior mean
\hat{s}_{(J,m)}=E_{s\sim\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})}(s)
, the loss-minimiser
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}=\arg\min_{s}l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
and an estimator

\widehat{s}_{KL(J,m)}
minimising the KL divergence between
\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
and
\pi_{s^{*}}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
. This is defined in Appendix
C.1
where we give a Monte Carlo estimator. In Appendix
C.2
we show using a normal approximation to the posterior that the KL-estimate for
\eta
in the power posterior is approximately the harmonic mean
\widehat{\eta}_{KL(J,m)}\simeq 1/E_{\eta\sim\rho(\cdot|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})}(1/\eta)
.

When we compare different methods for estimating
s
and updating belief in Sections
5.1
,
5.2
and Appendix
E.6
, we compute their risk ratio
R(\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2})
estimated on test data (which wouldn’t be available in practice). If we have
J^{(z)}
blocks of test data then

R(\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{\{z_{j,1:m}\}^{J^{(z)}}_{j=1}\sim p^{*}}\left[\frac{p_{\hat{s}_{1}}(\{z_{j,1:m}\}^{J^{(z)}}_{j=1}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}{p_{\hat{s}_{2}}(\{z_{j,1:m}\}^{J^{(z)}}_{j=1}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]\,.

(8)

The expectations in (
8
) are estimated using Monte Carlo. If
R(\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2})>1
then
\hat{s}_{1}
has lower risk. In the unsupervised classification experiment in Section
5.3
we use the true class assignments and the Brier score defined in (
22
).

2.2
Related literature

We categorize methods for conducting Bayesian analysis on misspecified models into three groups. The first group comprises methods that apply bootstrapping within a Bayesian framework, such as Weighted Likelihood Bootstrap
(
Newton 1991
;
Newton and Raftery 1994
;
Lyddon et al. 2019
)
and BayesBag
(
Bühlmann 2014
;
Huggins and Miller 2021
)
. The second group includes the tempered likelihood, resulting in power posteriors
Walker and Hjort 2001
;
Grünwald 2012
;
Miller and Dunson 2018
. The third group generalises the second and involves replacing the likelihood with a more general loss function that mediates data and parameters, including approaches like PAC-Bayes
(
Germain et al. 2016
;
Zhang 2006
;
McAllester 1998
;
Shawe-Taylor and Williamson 1997
)
, Gibbs posteriors
(
Zhang 2006
;
Jiang and Tanner 2008
;
Martin and Syring 2022
)
, and Generalized Bayes
(
Bissiri et al. 2016
;
Grünwald and van Ommen 2017
)
. We focus on the power posterior, but also consider more general losses which are empirical estimates of the Bregman divergence, so our work falls in the second and third groups.

Shen et al. 2023
estimate
\eta
in a “power prior” setting
(
Carvalho and Ibrahim 2021
)
with many of the same elements but different goals. We have training data
{\mathbf{x}}
, calibration data
{\mathbf{y}}
and test data
z
. These all have some unknown true distribution
p^{*}
and we use the same observation model
p(\cdot|\phi)
with a common parameter
\phi
. The Gibbs posterior is used to help treat misspecification in the observation model. In
Shen et al. 2023
,
{\mathbf{x}}\sim p(\cdot|\phi_{x})
is a well-specified model for the “old” data set which they use to make a prior for the parameters of a “new” data set
{\mathbf{y}}\sim p(\cdot|\phi_{y})
. For example,
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
measure a treatment response in two drug trials involving adults and children respectively and
\phi_{x}
and
\phi_{y}
are model parameters for the two data sets. Misspecification arises if
\phi_{x}
differs significantly from
\phi_{y}
. As this is a concern, the authors construct a prior for
\phi_{y}
by down-weighting the information from the old data, taking

\pi(\phi_{y},\eta|{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\propto\rho(\eta)p({\mathbf{y}}|\phi_{y})\pi_{\eta}(\phi_{y}|{\mathbf{x}}).

This joint posterior is the posterior in (
7
) when
J=1
(the pooled case). The authors call
\pi_{\eta}(\phi_{y}|{\mathbf{x}})
the “power prior” for
\phi_{y}
.

Shen et al. 2023
give an analysis of the asymptotic behavior of
\eta
as
n
and
m
(the size of
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
when
J=1
) grow at fixed
n/m
, considering the case where the true and fitted observation models for
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are in the same parametric family of GLMs with possibly different parameters. They show
\eta\to 0
if the true parameters differ.
By contrast, in the following section we give the asymptotic behavior of
\eta
and
\beta
as
m
and
J
grow at fixed
n
, as this is the limit in which the “true”
\eta^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
are defined.

2.3
Asymptotic distribution of hyperparameters

The posterior for the hyperparameters
s=(\eta,\beta)
in (
6
) may concentrate as
we gather more calibration data
{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}
. This can occur in two ways: the block-size
m
may grow at fixed
J
;
the number of blocks
J
may grow at fixed
m
.

Assumption 1
.

(i) There exists

\bar{s}_{(J,m)}=\{s\in\Omega_{s};\nabla_{s}l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})=0\},

(ii)
\frac{1}{J}l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\xrightarrow{a.s.}\widetilde{l}(s,{\mathbf{x}})
with
\widetilde{l}(s,{\mathbf{x}})
the risk defined in (
4
) and (iii) there exists

\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}=\{s\in\Omega_{s};\nabla_{s}\widetilde{l}(s;{\mathbf{x}})=0\}.

Here
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
is equal to the true
s^{*}
(ie, the optimal hyperparamters defined on test data) which we want to estimate. If we have a generalised Bayes posterior for
s
which concentrates on
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
then the posterior for
s
concentrates on the truth.

We begin by fixing
J=1
and taking
m\to\infty
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
J=1
, let
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)}
be
m
iid observations and Assumption
1
(i) holds. Assume that for any
s
, both
\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})
and

\log p({\mathbf{y}}|\phi)
are
C^{\infty}
in
\phi
. Assume there exists
\bar{\phi}=\{\phi;\nabla_{\phi}\log p({\mathbf{y}}|\phi)=0\}
, the Hessian
H_{\bar{\phi}}:=-\frac{1}{m}\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log p({\mathbf{y}}|\bar{\phi})
is finite and positive definite and there exists
\widetilde{\phi}=\{\phi;\nabla_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p^{*}}[\log p(z|\phi)]=0\}
.
For sufficiently large
m
,
\bar{s}_{(1,m)}\approx\argmin_{s}-\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})
. If the pseudo optimal
\widetilde{s}_{(1,\infty)}=\argmin_{s}-\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})
is an unique minima and
\sup_{s}|\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})-\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})|\xrightarrow{p}0
, then
\bar{s}_{(1,m)}\xrightarrow{p}\widetilde{s}_{(1,\infty)}
.

Proof.

The Laplace approximation for
\exp(-l(s;{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}}))
at large
m
is

\displaystyle\exp(-l(s;{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})(2\pi)^{d_{\phi}/2}\exp(\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log p(y_{i}|\bar{\phi}))}{m^{d_{\phi}/2}\sqrt{|H_{\bar{\phi}}|}}

(9)

\displaystyle\propto\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})\qquad\mbox{(in $s$-dependence).}

(10)

The proof for the consistency minima for
s
follows Thm 5.7 in
van der Vaart 2007
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that
J=1
and the conditions of Theorem
1
hold. The posterior density for
s
in (
6
) is
\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\propto\exp(-l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)},{\mathbf{x}}))\rho(s)
. It follows from Theorem
1

that, for sufficiently large
m
,

\rho(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\approx\dfrac{\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})\rho(s)}{\rule[-2.39996pt]{0.0pt}{12.0pt}\int\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})\rho(s)ds}.

The important point here is that the
s
-posterior for the pooled loss doesn’t concentrate with
m
but has a limiting distribution which is only as concentrated as
\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})\rho(s)
. We now fix
m\geq 1
and take
J\to\infty
and show that the posterior for
s
does concentrate at
\tilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
. Moreover, as we show in Appendix
B
, the loss minimisers
\tilde{s}_{\infty,m}
and
\tilde{s}_{1,\infty}
need not coincide.

Assumption 2
.

The Hessian
H_{s}=\nabla^{2}_{s}\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)};{\mathbf{x}})
is positive definite and it also holds that
\sup_{J}\sup_{s\in\Omega_{s}}\sup|\nabla^{3}_{s}\frac{1}{J}l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})|<\infty
.

Under these assumptions, it may be shown using the sandwich estimator (Ch 5,
van der Vaart 2007
) that
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}
concentrates around
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
,

\sqrt{J}(\bar{s}_{(J,m)}-\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)})\to N(0,H_{s}^{-1}{\text{V}ar}_{p^{*}}(\nabla_{s}\ell(\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)};z,{\mathbf{x}}))H_{s}^{-1})\,.

(11)

The asymptotic distribution in (
11
) assumes conditional independence of data in blocks. Having linked
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}
and
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
we now link
s
and
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}
to get the asymptotic posterior for
s
in the multiple-block case.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions

and

\widetilde{l}(s;{\mathbf{x}})>\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)};{\mathbf{x}})

\liminf_{J}\inf_{s\in K_{s}\setminus\{\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}\}}(\frac{1}{J}\ell(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})-\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)};{\mathbf{x}}))>0.

If
\rho(s)
is continuous and strictly positive at
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
and
s\sim\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
then

\sqrt{J}(s-\bar{s}_{(J,m)})\xrightarrow{t.v.}N(0,H_{s}^{-1})

with
H_{s}=\nabla^{2}_{s}\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)};{\mathbf{x}})
the Hession defined in Assumption
2
.

The proof for Theorem
2
follows the same path as Corollary
2
via Theorems
3
and

3
Generalised Bayes for multi-modular models

3.1
Related work in muti-modular settings

Multi-modular Bayesian inference with model misspecification has distinctive characteristics compared to misspecification in single module settings.
Liu et al. 2009
provided an early analysis on “modularization.” Methods like Markov melding
(
Goudie et al. 2019
)
and Bayesian melding
(
Poole and Raftery 2000
)
address conflicts between priors across modules.
Liu and Goudie 2025
gives a formal definition of a module. In this paper, we assume that modules are identified as either misspecified or well-specified, which might be the result of an initial Bayesian analysis of the overall model.

Cut-model
inference
(
Plummer 2015
)
has proven very effective in this setting. It can be thought of as a kind of sequential imputation procedure, in which the uncertainty for a shared parameter is estimated from a first well-specified module and the distribution passed as a prior for the shared parameter in a second misspecified module. This is not Bayesian inference, as information from the second module does not inform the shared parameter. An early form of Cut model inference has been available in WinBUGS
(
Spiegelhalter et al. 2014
)
and cut models have found many applications
(
Blangiardo et al. 2011
;
Finucane et al. 2016
;
Styring et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Nicholson et al. 2022
;
Teh et al. 2021
;
Lunn et al. 2013
;
Kaizar 2015
;
Zigler and Dominici 2014
)
.
Jacob et al. 2017
provides an overview of modularized Bayesian analysis, including Cut-models, from the perspective of statistical decision theory, while
Pompe and Jacob 2021
discusses their asymptotic properties.
Frazier and Nott 2024
considered general losses and prove a BVM-type result for the generalized cut posterior. Nested MCMC
(
Plummer 2015
;
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
)
is commonly used to fit Cut models. Recent advancements include Laplace
(
Frazier and Nott 2024
)
and variational approximations
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, unbiased MCMC
(
Jacob et al. 2020
)
and a computationally efficient variant of nested MCMC
(
Liu and Goudie 2020
)
.

Cutting feedback from the second module into the first leads to a bias-variance trade-off. For example, if the parameters of a well-specified module lack sufficient “local” information, incorporating limited information from misspecified modules can reduce uncertainty without introducing significant bias.
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
address this issue using Semi-Modular Inference (SMI). In this framework, a loss with a learninig rate is designed to control the flow of information from a misspecified module, while the loss for a well-specified module is just the negative log-likelihood. This gives a sequence of posteriors parameterised by the learning rate and interpolating the conventional posterior and the cut posterior. Various ways to parameterise the sequence have been suggested
(
Nicholls et al. 2022
;
Nott et al. 2024
)
and choices of loss include tempered likelihood
(
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
)
and tempered marginal likelihood
(
Nicholls et al. 2022
;
Frazier and Nott 2024
)
. SMI has proven effective in applications
(
Liu and Goudie 2024
;
Styring et al. 2022
;
Carmona et al. 2024
)
involving misspecified models for high dimensional latent variables.

The asymptotic properties of Gibbs posterior in the single module case, including posterior consistency and a Bernstein von Mises (BVM) type result, have been established in
(
Martin and Syring 2022
;
Miller 2021
)
.
Frazier and Nott 2024
show similar results for the generalized cut posterior. Recently,
Winter et al. 2023
presented a sequential Gibbs posterior and its asymptotic properties, extending
(
Miller 2021
)
. Their setup in fact includes SMI posteriors, though they do not discuss the connection. We make this explicit.

5.3
Estimation of Sense Change using the Power Posterior

In this section, we illustrate Bayesian estimation of
\eta
in
a power posterior of the form (
1
) for a challenging problem with a single module (so SMI isn’t needed) of real data. The data is an english text corpus, described in
Zafar and Nicholls 2024a
, spanning
T=10
twenty-year blocks of time from 1810 to 2010. The task is to cluster instances of a target word according to their meaning, taking into account changes in meaning over time. For example, the target word “bug” has four main meanings in the corpus (associated with insect, bacteria or virus, software error and secret listening device). Each instance of the target word is extracted, together with the text in a small window either side. These small blocks of text are called “snippets”. The target word is used in different senses in different snippets; the probability for any given context-word to appear alongside the target word will be different in snippets with different meanings. Our task is to cluster the snippets by the sense of their target word using the other words in the snippet to identify the sense. We use the Embedded Diachronic Sense Change (EDiSC) model introduced in
Zafar and Nicholls 2024a
.

EDiSC is a bag-of-words model resembling Latent Dirichlet Allocation. It is a good example because it shows our methods are tractable on a problem which is already fairly challenging: the data are sparse, there is a time series structure, the parameter space is moderately high dimensional and it is hard to get good MCMC convergence. It is, however, typical of the kind of problem where Generalised Bayes is helpful: it has many latent variables (the unknown true snippet sense-labels) and the fitted model is mildly misspecified (labels are all conditionally independent in the model, which is implausible for real text).
Zafar and Nicholls 2024b

showed that Generalised Bayes did lead to improved prediction of sense labels. They selected the learning rate by adjusting
\eta
so that the posterior predictive distribution matched the data. We will choose the learning rate using Bayesian inference and held out data.

See Appendix
E.4
for details of the model and numerical values for
K,N,V
and
L
below. The number of clusters
K
is fixed and there are
N
snippets
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{N})
in the full data set. If
{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of all the
V=|{\mathcal{V}}|
words in the corpus then each snippet
\bar{x}_{d}\subset{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of
L
words appearing in the text just before and after the
d
’th instance of the target word. For
d\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
z_{d}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
is a parameter giving the cluster assignment for snippet

. The power posterior is given in (
E.6

6
Discussion

We have given a straightforward Bayesian estimator for inference hyperparameters
s=(\eta,\beta)
including the learning rate and loss hyperparameters. The “likelihood” for
s
is the posterior predictive,

p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}|{\mathbf{x}})
and we used simple uniform priors.
We distinguished between pooled and product losses in (
5
). In the former we have a single predictive distribution for all the data
{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,Jm)}
and in the latter
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}|{\mathbf{x}})
is defined as the product of predictive distributions for each of the blocks
y_{(j)},\ j=1,\dots,J
. We saw that, under regularity conditions, a posterior using the product-loss concentrates as
J\to\infty
at fixed
m
on the optimal hyperparameters for predicting unseen test data. In contrast the pooled loss with
J=1
and
m\to\infty
converges to a diffuse distribution. Both these behaviors were visible in experiments (see Figures
2
and
6
and the examples in Appendix
E.2
). Where we had more than one inference hyperparameter (in
(\eta,\beta)
-SMI) we estimated them jointly.

We trialed three estimators for
s
: the mode, mean and KL-minimiser. Looking across all our experiments, the mean performs slightly better than the mode, unless the optimal value is on the boundary of
\Omega_{s}
. The KL-minimiser performed worst. We gave theory and experiments for several different loss functions, working mainly with multi-modular problems and SMI (the exception is the example in Section
5.3
which is simply the power posterior with a single data set). We saw that
\gamma
-SMI (with a marginal likelihood loss) and
\eta
-SMI (essentially the power posterior) converge to equality at large training data sample size. There were always clear gains (in risk) from moving away from Bayes and usually gains over a Cut-model belief update.
(\eta,\beta)
-SMI performed well in the State Space model example in Section
5.2
, and was comparable to
\eta
-SMI even in settings where the optimal
\beta\simeq 1
, so that
(\eta,\beta)
-SMI is “over-parameterised” compared to
\eta
-SMI.

In our experiments with real data (in Section
5.3
and Appendix
E.6
) we split a larger data set
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
into
training
{\mathbf{x}}
and calibration
{\mathbf{y}}
components. The sample size in these problems is relatively large (in the sense that the data are strongly informative) so although the calibration data make up a small fraction of the whole,
{\mathbf{y}}
is nevertheless large enough to inform
s
(for example,
J
ranges from
30
to
60
in Section
5.3
). We discuss some of the issues involved in splitting an original data set in Appendix
F
.

Working with the posterior predictive
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}|{\mathbf{x}})
comes at a cost, as it must be estimated at each
s
value of interest. This is of course far easier than estimating a marginal like
p_{s}({\mathbf{x}})
, at least if
n
(the number of samples in
{\mathbf{x}}
) is large. The main drivers of complexity are the dimension of
s
, how rapidly MCMC targeting
\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})
converges and whether we have a pooled or product-loss posterior. One or two inference hyperparameters is often all that’s needed in practical work in GBI and in that case evaluation on a lattice of
s
values is straightforward. When
s
has higher dimension our MCMC scheme (Appendix
A
) targeting
\rho(s,\phi_{(1:J)}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
works well if
\pi_{s}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}})
is easily to sample.
However, if we have a product loss with many blocks then we need to run
J
MCMC chains targeting
\pi_{s}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}})
to equilibrium for each MCMC step varying
s
. This may be impractical. For example, the posterior for
s
in Figure
A.1
(bottom right corner) has a product loss with
J=40
and dimension of
\phi=(\varphi,\theta_{M})
equal
41
so the nested MCMC has dimension around
1600
in the “inner” MCMC chains. However, in Figure
6
(bottom right corner) we use smoothing, as
J=1000
is out of reach. A variational approach like
Battaglia and Nicholls 2024
, which amortises over hyperparameters, may be needed.

A
Sampling hyperparameters using smoothing and MCMC

We propose two methods to sample from
\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})
. These exploit the two representations in (
6
) and (
7
).

Smoothing
This works well when the dimension of
s
is low (one or two, which is actually typical). For the first method, we evaluate
p^{(s)}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
at a few candidate
s
values and use some smoothing method, for example, spline, so that we are able to evaluate
p^{(s)}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
at any
s
value. Treating
p^{(s)}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
as the likelihood for
s
and with a prior
\rho(s)
, it is possible to do Bayesian inference for
s
. Marginalisation over parameters for
p^{(s)}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
is often numerically approximated. For example, the Gaussian quadrature in
D.4
.

MCMC
This still works if the dimension of
s
is larger, though it is slow for the product-loss. In the second method, we target
\rho(s,\phi|{\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{x}})
and we propose a nested MCMC algorithm. We first illustrate how the algorithm works for product loss, i.e. we target
\rho(s,\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
. The version for pooled loss is to set
J^{\prime}=1
and
m^{\prime}=Jm
.

The joint distribution of
(s,\phi)
using product loss is

\displaystyle\pi(s,\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{J,m};{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\propto\rho(s)\prod_{j=1}^{J}p(y_{(j)}|\phi_{j})\frac{\pi(\phi_{j})p_{s}({\mathbf{x}}|\phi_{j})}{p_{s}({\mathbf{x}})}.

(A.1)

Let a proposal distribution
q(s^{\prime}|s)
be given for the hyperparameter. The following algorithm is ergodic for
\pi(s,\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{J,m};{\mathbf{x}})
in the limit that its parameter
I
is taken to infinity.

Let
X_{t}=(s,\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{J})
.

1.

Sample
s^{\prime}\sim q(\cdot|s)
;

2.

For
j=1,\dots,J
,
run an MCMC chain
\tilde{\Phi}_{j,i},\ i=0,1,\dots I
with
\Phi_{j,0}=\phi_{j}
targeting

\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi^{\prime}|{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{\pi(\phi^{\prime})p_{s^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}|\phi^{\prime})}{p_{s^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})}

and set
\phi_{j}^{\prime}=\tilde{\Phi}_{j,I}
(with
I
large enough so
\phi_{j}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{x}})
to a good approximation);

3.

Accept
\phi^{\prime}_{j}
with probability

\displaystyle\alpha(s^{\prime},\phi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\phi_{J}^{\prime}|s,\phi)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(s^{\prime},\phi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\phi_{J}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})q(s|s^{\prime})\pi_{s}(\phi_{1}|{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\dots\cdot\pi_{s}(\phi_{J}|{\mathbf{x}})}{\pi(s,\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{J}|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)};{\mathbf{x}})q(s^{\prime}|s)\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi^{\prime}|{\mathbf{x}})\dots\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi_{J}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{x}})}\right\}\qquad\mbox{(use Equation~\ref{eq:s phi joint prod})}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\frac{\rho(s^{\prime})q(s|s^{\prime})\prod_{j=1}^{J}p({\mathbf{y}}_{j,1:m}|\phi_{j}^{\prime})}{\rho(s)q(s^{\prime}|s)\prod_{j=1}^{J}p({\mathbf{y}}_{j,1:m}^{\prime}|\phi_{j})}\right\}

set
X_{t+1}=(s^{\prime},\phi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\phi_{J}^{\prime})
and otherwise set
X_{t+1}=(s,\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{J})
.

B
Pooling and Blocking Pseudo-optimal hyperparameters differ

In this Appendix we show that the loss minimisers
\tilde{s}_{\infty,m}
and
\tilde{s}_{1,\infty}
need not coincide. Theorem
1
shows the large
m
behaviour of
l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
and the loss minimizer for a single calibration dataset. We assume that
m
is sufficiently large enough for the Laplace approximation in Theorem
1
and show the behaviour of
l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
for large
J
.

Theorem B.1
.

Suppose the assumptions in Theorem
1
hold for each
j
-th calibration dataset. There exists
\bar{\phi}_{j}=\{\phi;\nabla_{\phi}\frac{-1}{m}\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log p({\mathbf{y}}_{j,i}|\phi)=0\}
,
j=1,...,J
. For sufficiently large
m
,
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}\approx\argmin_{s}\frac{1}{J}\sum^{J}_{j=1}-\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}_{j}|{\mathbf{x}})
and the conditional pseudo optimal
s
is

\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}=\argmin_{s}-\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{2}Tr(V_{\widetilde{\phi}}\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}}))

(B.2)

where
V_{\widetilde{\phi}}=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[\nabla^{2}_{\phi}l(d^{\prime},\widetilde{\phi})]^{-1}{\text{V}ar}_{p^{*}}(\nabla_{\phi}l(d^{\prime},\widetilde{\phi}))\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[\nabla^{2}_{\phi}l(d^{\prime},\widetilde{\phi})]^{-1}
.

Proof.

Using the Laplace method, an approximation of an average loss is simplified

\frac{1}{J}l(s;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\approx\frac{1}{J}\sum^{J}_{j=1}\left(-\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}_{j}|{\mathbf{x}})-\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log p({\mathbf{y}}_{j,i}|\bar{\phi}_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\log|H_{\bar{\phi}_{j}}|\right)

and the empirical loss minimizer is the value minimizing the first term. Taking the limit on
J
for given
m
, the pseudo optimal
s
is

\widetilde{s}_{\infty,m}\approx\argmin_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\phi}\sim N(\widetilde{\phi},V_{\widetilde{\phi}})}[-\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})]
where

V_{\widetilde{\phi}}=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[\nabla^{2}_{\phi}-\log p(z|\widetilde{\phi})]^{-1}{\text{V}ar}_{p^{*}}(\nabla_{\phi}-\log p(z|\widetilde{\phi}))\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[\nabla^{2}_{\phi}-\log p(z|\widetilde{\phi})]^{-1}
. The distribution for
\bar{\phi}
is simply the sandwich estimator. Using a second order Taylor expansion around
\widetilde{\phi}
,

\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})\approx\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})+(\widetilde{\phi}-\bar{\phi})^{T}\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\phi}-\bar{\phi})^{T}(\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}}))(\widetilde{\phi}-\bar{\phi})\,,

and we find the expectation over
\bar{\phi}
. Since
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\phi}}[(\widetilde{\phi}-\bar{\phi})]=0
, the limiting loss is

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\phi}}[\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})]\approx\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}Tr(V_{\widetilde{\phi}}\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}}))\,.

∎

The pseudo-optimal
s
for single and
J
calibration datasets are different by the adjustment term (second term in the approximation of
\mathbb{E}[\log\pi_{s}(\bar{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})]
). As
J,m\to\infty
, the adjustment term becomes negligible and
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,\infty)}=\widetilde{s}_{(1,\infty)}
. If both
J
and
m
are sufficiently large enough or
\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})
is smooth enough such that
\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log\pi_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}|{\mathbf{x}})
is almost constant with
s
then
\bar{s}_{(J,m)}\approx\bar{s}_{(1,m)}
. Even if the calibration data size increases, the loss minimizers do not necessarily converge to the same pseudo-optimal value. Maximizing
{\rm ELJPD}_{m,{\mathbf{x}},s}
is equivalent to minimizing
\widetilde{l}(s;{\mathbf{x}})
which is
\tilde{s}_{\infty,m}
.

Corollary B.1
.

Suppose that
\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})=N(\phi;\mu_{s},V_{s})
. With Theorems
1
,
\widetilde{s}_{(1,\infty)}=\argmin_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}-\mu_{s})^{T}V_{s}^{-1}(\widetilde{\phi}-\mu_{s})+\log|V_{s}|
. With Theorem
B.1
,
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}=\argmin_{s}(\widetilde{\phi}-\mu_{s})^{T}V_{s}^{-1}(\widetilde{\phi}-\mu_{s})+\log|V_{s}|+Tr(V_{\widetilde{\phi}}V_{s}^{-1})
.

Proof.

The pseudo optimal loss-hyperparameters are obtained by substituting
N(\phi;\mu_{s},V_{s})
to
\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{x}})
for
\widetilde{s}_{(1,\infty)}
in Theorem
1
and
\widetilde{s}_{(\infty,m)}
in Theorem
B.1
.
∎

C
The KL-estimator

C.1
\hat{s}_{KL}
definition and estimation

In this section, we define
\widehat{s}_{KL(J,m)}
and give an estimation procedure when we cant simply use the harmonic mean approximation, such as for
(\eta,\beta)
-SMI. It works reasonably well when the dimension of
s
is low (as it usually is). The estimator is
\widehat{s}_{KL(J,m)}=\arg\min_{s^{\prime}}D^{(KL)}_{s,s^{\prime}}
where

\displaystyle D^{(KL)}_{s,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}[KL(\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\|\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})]

(C.3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}[\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\log\frac{\pi_{s}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}{\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}\right)]

(C.4)

We use Monte Carlo to estimate
\bar{s}_{KL}
.

1. Simulate
T
samples from
\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
, denoted as
s_{1},\dots,s_{T}
.

2. For each
s_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
:

Simulate
J
samples from
\pi_{s_{t}}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
, denoted as
\phi_{1,t},\dots,\phi_{J,t}
.

3. The expected KL divergence in (
C.3
) evaluated at a given
s^{\prime}
value is approximated by:

\widehat{KL}(s^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{\pi_{s_{t}}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}{\pi_{s^{\prime}}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}
.

4. Smooth
\widehat{KL}(s^{\prime})
and find
\hat{s}_{KL(J,m)}=\argmin_{s^{\prime}}\widehat{KL}(s^{\prime})
.

For example, to estimate
\hat{b}_{KL}
in Fig.
E.6
, we took
T=400
and
J=1000
.

C.2
\widehat{\eta}_{KL}
approximation with harmonic mean

For simplicity in notation, we use the same notations in Section 4.2. Suppose that
p^{(\eta)}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\propto p(\phi)e^{-\eta\ell(\phi;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}
for some loss function
\ell
. For example, the negative log-likelihood for
\ell
gives the generalized Bayes inference. For a large sample
n^{\prime}=n+Jm
, the
\eta
-SMI posterior is normally approximated by
N(\bar{\phi},\frac{1}{\eta n^{\prime}}H^{-1})
where
\overline{\phi}=\argmin_{\phi}\ell(\phi;{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})
and
H=\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{\phi})
where
\widetilde{\phi}
is the pseudo truth. Plugging the normal approximation to (
C.3
) and taking the derivative with respect to
\eta^{\prime}
, we get the harmonic mean

\displaystyle\widehat{\eta}_{KL(J,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\eta^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim p(\eta|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}[KL(p^{(\eta)}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})\|p^{(\eta^{\prime})}(\phi|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\argmin_{\eta^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim p(\eta|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\log\frac{\eta}{\eta^{\prime}}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{\eta}\right)

The minimizer takes a form of the harmonic mean
\widehat{\eta}_{hm(J,m)}
,

\widehat{\eta}_{hm(J,m)}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim p(\eta|{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)},{\mathbf{x}})}[1/\eta]}\,.

(C.5)

For the state space model, even if the number of samples is large,

D.2
Detailed treatment of SMI as sequential Gibbs

We present the large
n
behaviours of SMI-posteriors described in Section
3.2
, applying the asymptotic properties of the sequential Gibbs posterior in Section
4
established by
Winter et al. 2023
in a straightforward manner.
A probability density function and distribution are denoted by
p
and
P
, respectively. The true generative density for
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are denoted by
p^{*}_{x_{1}}
and
p^{*}_{x_{2}}
respectively.

The SMI-posteriors have the same conditional update for
\theta
given
\varphi
but different loss functions for
\varphi
. Given
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell(\theta;\varphi,{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{-1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi,\theta)
,
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell(\theta|\varphi,{\mathbf{x}})\xrightarrow{a.s.}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{2}}}[\log p(x_{2}|\varphi,\theta)]
. We assume that Assumptions
D.1
(ii) and
D.2
(ii) hold for
\ell(\theta;\varphi,{\mathbf{x}})
then, the conditional posterior convergence is obtained.

We now focus on the empirical loss functions for
\varphi
, which is often formed as a marginal loss function. In this case almost sure convergence in Assumption
3
need not hold. Assuming that
\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}n_{1}/n_{2}
,
Corollary
D.1
shows that posterior consistency is still holds if the loss functions converge in probability.

Corollary D.1
.

Suppose Assumption
3
does not hold but
\ell_{s}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})\to\widetilde{\ell}_{s}(\varphi)
in probability and
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell(\theta|\varphi,{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\to\widetilde{\ell}(\theta|\varphi)
in probability for every
\varphi\in\Omega_{\varphi}
. If the prior probabilities over
N_{\varphi,\epsilon}
and
N_{\theta,\epsilon}
are positive for all
\epsilon>0
and Assumption
D.1
holds then
P^{(s)}(d((\varphi,\theta),(\widetilde{\varphi}_{s},\widetilde{\theta}_{\widetilde{\varphi}_{s}}))<\epsilon|{\mathbf{x}})\to 1
in probability.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of Theorem
3
given in
Winter et al. 2023
, except that the integrand in the denominator of Equation A.3 goes to infinity in probability. Tracing this change in the mode of convergence back through their proof gives the stated result.
∎

A marginalised loss function is approximated by Laplace’s method, and the approximation is known to be no worse than
O(n_{2}^{-1})

(
Wong 1989
;
Schillings et al. 2020
)
. Recently,
Bilodeau et al. 2023
presented the tight lower error bound for some well-known probability models. We use this in Appendix
D.3
to establish some background results. The convergence property of Laplace’s method is written for

in Theorem
D.1
in Appendix
D.3
and the asymptotic behaviour of

is given in Corollary
D.4
. Corollary
D.2
shows the posterior consistency for the
\gamma
-SMI posterior.

Corollary D.2
.

If Assumption
D.3
holds for
p(x_{2}|\varphi,\theta)
, a marginalized loss converges in probability;

\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell_{\gamma}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})\xrightarrow{p}-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{2}}}[\log p(x_{2}|\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})]-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{1}}}[\log p(x_{1}|\varphi)]
. If Assumption
D.1
(i) holds substituting
\ell_{(s)}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
with
\ell_{\gamma}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
, and there is a positive prior probability in a neighborhood of
(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma},\widetilde{\theta}_{\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma}})
, then
P^{(\gamma)}(d((\varphi,\theta),(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma},\widetilde{\theta}_{\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma}}))<\epsilon|{\mathbf{x}})\to 1
in probability.

Proof.

Following Corollary
D.4
,
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{2}}}[\log p(x_{2}|\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})]
and
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{1}|\varphi)\xhookrightarrow{a.s.}\alpha\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{1}}}[\log p(x_{1}|\varphi)]
. This is sufficient to show the convergence of
\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell_{\gamma}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
in probability. Following Corollary
D.1
, the
\gamma
-SMI posterior consistency is obtained.
∎

Corollary D.3
.

Substituting
\ell_{(s)}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
with
\ell_{\eta}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
, if Assumption

(i) holds and there is a positive prior probability in a neighborhood of
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\eta}

P^{(\eta)}(d(\varphi,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\eta})<\epsilon|{\mathbf{x}})\to 1
in probability.
Let
\varphi^{(\eta)}\sim\pi_{\eta}(\varphi|{\mathbf{x}})
and
\varphi^{(\gamma)}\sim\pi_{\gamma}(\varphi|{\mathbf{x}})

\eta=\gamma

P(d(\varphi^{(\eta)},\varphi^{(\gamma)})<\epsilon)\to 1

Proof.

Under Assumption
D.3
,
p_{\eta}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)
is approximated by Laplace’s method and

is approximated by

\frac{1}{n_{2}}\widehat{\ell_{\eta}}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{1}|\varphi)-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\log\dfrac{p(\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})(2\pi)^{d_{\theta}/2}\exp(-\eta\ell(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2}))}{n_{2}^{d_{\theta}/2}\sqrt{|\eta\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\ell(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})|}}

where

which equals to the limiting loss in Corollary
D.2
if

\frac{1}{n_{2}}\ell_{\gamma}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})\xrightarrow{p}-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{1}}}[\log p(x_{1}|\varphi)]-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{2}}}[\log p(x_{2}|\varphi,\theta^{*}_{\varphi})]=\lim\limits_{n_{2}\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\widehat{\ell_{\eta}}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})\,.

D.3
Marginalized loss

Suppose that
r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})=-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{2,i}|\varphi,\theta)
. Assuming iid samples,
r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\to\widetilde{r}(\theta,\varphi)
almost surely where
\widetilde{r}(\theta,\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}_{x_{2}}}[-\log p(x_{2}|\varphi,\theta)]
. Let
\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi}
be the minimizer of
\widetilde{r}(\theta,\varphi)
for a given
\varphi
. Using the Laplace method, we approximate
p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)
and present the asymptotic behaviour.

Assumption D.3
.

We assume that for any
\varphi\in\Omega_{\varphi}
, (i) Both
p(\theta)
and
r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
are
C^{\infty}
. (ii) There exists an
\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}\in\Omega_{\varphi}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
,
\delta_{\epsilon}>0
where
\delta_{\epsilon}=\inf\{r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})-r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2}):\theta\in\Omega_{\theta}\text{ and }\|\theta-\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}\|\geq\epsilon\}
. (iii) The Hessian
H_{\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}}:=\nabla^{2}_{\theta}r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
is positive and finite.

The above assumption implies that
\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}
is the unique minimizer of
r(\theta,\varphi;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
for a given
\varphi
.

Theorem D.1
.

Under the Assumption
D.3
, as
n\to\infty
we have

p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)=\int p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi,\theta)p(\theta)d\theta=\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}(1+O(n^{-1}))

where

\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}=\dfrac{\pi(\overline{\theta}_{\varphi})(2\pi)^{d_{\theta}/2}\exp(-nr(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2}))}{n^{d_{\theta}/2}\sqrt{|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})|}}\,.

Proof.

Theorem
D.1
is a straightforward variant of Section IX in
Wong 1989
, and we omit the proof as it is identical. With the Assumption
D.3
,
p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)
has an asymptotic expansion of the form,
p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)=e^{-nr(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})}(\sum^{k^{\prime}}_{k=0}c_{k}n^{-k}+O(n^{-d_{\theta}/2-k^{\prime}}))
where
k^{\prime}
is some positive integer and
c_{0}=p(\overline{\theta}_{\varphi})(2\pi)^{d_{\theta}/2}|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}r(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})|^{1/2}
.
∎

The Laplace approximation
\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}
in Theorem
D.1
is still valid under weaker conditions that
p(\theta)
is continuous and
r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
is continuous second-order partial derivatives in the neighbourhood of
\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}

(
Wong 1989
)
. Another variant of the Laplace approximation result is presented in
Schillings et al. 2020
.

Corollary D.4
.

Under Assumption
D.3
, for large
n
,
\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}\xrightarrow{\text{p}}p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)
and,
\frac{-1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)\xrightarrow{p}\widetilde{r}(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})
where
\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi}
is the minimizer of
\widetilde{r}(\theta,\varphi)
for a given
\varphi
.

Proof.

If Assumption
D.3
holds, for large
n
we have that
\frac{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)-\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}}{\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}}=O(n^{-1})
. There exists
c^{\prime}<\infty
such that
|p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)-\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}|<\frac{c^{\prime}\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}}{n_{2}}
. There exists
\epsilon
such that
\lim_{n_{2}\to\infty}P\left(\frac{c^{\prime}\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}}{n_{2}}>\epsilon\right)=0
then
\lim_{n_{2}\to\infty}P(|p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)-\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}|>\epsilon)=0
and this is sufficient to show
\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}\xrightarrow{\text{p}}p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)
. The loss convergence
r(\varphi,\theta;{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\xrightarrow{a.s.}\widetilde{r}(\varphi,\theta)
yields the minimizer convergence,
\overline{\theta}_{\varphi}\to\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi}
.

The risk approximation is

\frac{-1}{n_{2}}\log\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}=-\frac{\log(\pi(\overline{\theta}_{\varphi})(2\pi)^{d_{\theta}/2})}{n_{2}}+\frac{d_{\theta}\log n_{2}}{2n_{2}}+r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})+\frac{1}{2n_{2}}\log|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})|

and as

, all terms become negligible except
r(\varphi,\overline{\theta}_{\varphi};{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
. Then
\lim_{n_{2}\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n_{2}}\log\widehat{p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)}=\widetilde{r}(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})
. By the transitivity,
\frac{-1}{n_{2}}\log p({\mathbf{x}}_{2}|\varphi)\xrightarrow{p}\widetilde{r}(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{\varphi})
.
∎

D.4
Computation for marginalized loss

The marginalized loss has the effect of marginalizing unison parameters or unwanted parameters. For example, in the
\gamma
-SMI posterior, the module-specific parameter
\tilde{\theta}
in the belief update for
\varphi
is marginalized. Such integral is not always solved analytically and is often numerically estimated. There is a wide range of works on marginal likelihood methods, and these methods are straightforwardly adapted to marginalized loss estimation.
Frazier and Nott 2024
used the marginalized marginal likelihood identity
(
Chib 1995
)
with the Laplace approximated posterior distribution.

We use the adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature (AGHQ) method to estimate a marginalized loss. Gaussian quadrature is a well-known numerical method that integrates the product of the
d
-dimensional Gaussian density and any polynomial of total order
2k-1
or less for a carefully chosen number of quadrature points,
k
. An integral is approximated by a weighted sum of
d
-dimensional integrands at quadrature points that points and weights are generated from the family quadrature rules. The relative rate of AGHQ is known to be
O(n^{-\lfloor(k+2)/3\rfloor})

(
Liu and Pierce 1994
;
Jin and Andersson 2020
)
. Recently
Blair and Tang 2024
showed that this relative rate still holds for approximating the marginal likelihood. The Laplace approximation is a special case of AGHQ with one quadrature point for AGH, and the relative error is known to be
O(n^{-1})

(
Kass and Steffey 1989
)
. We used the R-package
adhq

(
Stringer 2021
)
to approximate a marginalized loss using the AGHQ.

E.2.1
Risk comparison for Normal-mixture experiment

When we learn the hyperparameters
s
, we split the data into training and calibration data sets. We don’t in practice have the test data to which the fitted belief update will ultimately be applied. In this section we ask whether we gain anything over simply using the Bayesian or Cut-model belief update on the combined training and calibration data. We also investigate how performance depends on the split fraction into training and test data.

We split the available data
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}=\{\overline{x}_{1,1:n_{1}},\overline{x}_{2,1:n_{2}}\}
into a training dataset
{\mathbf{x}}
and calibration dataset
{\mathbf{y}}
. Let
0<k<1
represent the proportion of the training data
{\mathbf{x}}
within
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
, with the calibration dataset comprising
(1-k)
of
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
. Given
k
, the training dataset is defined as
{\mathbf{x}}=({\mathbf{x}}_{1},x_{2})
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\bar{x}_{1,1:kn_{1}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}=\bar{x}_{2,1:kn_{2}}\}
. We calibrate by learning to predict
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
-module data only, so the calibration dataset, partitioned into blocks of length
m
and given by
{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}
, is the complement of
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
within
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}_{1}
. Here
{\mathbf{y}}_{(J,m)}=(y_{(1)},\dots,y_{(J)})
with
y_{(j)}=(y_{j,1},\dots,y_{j,m})
has
J
blocks with
J=(1-k)n_{1}/m
. We generally fix
m
small compared to
(1-k)n_{1}
for the values of the split proportion
k
we choose to consider. However, if
J=1
(for “pooling”) we take
m=(1-k)n_{1}
.

We examine the posterior prediction quality and risk using the test data for
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
-module, since calibration targeted prediction of
{\mathbf{y}}_{1}
. The block size
m
taken for the test data is the same as that for the calibration data (we knew when we calibrated what the prediction task would be). However, the number of blocks
J^{(z)}
in the test data may differ from that in the calibration data
J
. Since we only have test data for the
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
module, denote by
{\mathbf{z}}_{(J^{(z)},m)}=(z_{(1)},\dots,z_{(J^{(z)})})
with
z_{(j)}=(z_{j,1},\dots,z_{j,m})
the test data. Let
n_{t}=J^{(z)}m
be the number of samples in the test data. The optimal
s
value
for predicting the test data using all the training and calibration data
will be

\bar{s}_{(J^{(z)},m)}=\argmin_{s}l(s;{\mathbf{z}}_{(J^{(z)},m)},\overline{{\mathbf{x}}})
(when we have
J^{(z)}
blocks of test data each of size
m
) and
\bar{s}_{(1,n_{t})}=\argmin_{s}l(s;{\mathbf{z}}_{(1,J^{(z)}m)},\overline{{\mathbf{x}}})
(in the pooled case with all the data in one block).

Let
m=3
,
n=60
,
n_{t}=n_{1}=30
be the data set size parameters before splitting. We generate 100 realizations of
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
. For each
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
, we estimate the risk ratio
R(s,s_{o})
, defined in (
8
), using 30 test datasets across three levels of misspecification
\rho^{*}=0.1,0.9,0.99
and split proportions
k=(0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8)
. The reference value
s_{o}
corresponding to the cut (
\gamma_{o}=\eta_{o}=b_{o}=0
) or conventional (
\gamma_{o}=\eta_{o}=b_{o}=1
) posterior is considered.

E.9

\beta

s
-estimators MAP and posterior mean with Cut and Bayes. The dependence on the train/calibrate split fraction is shown on the

-axis.

indicates the SMI-variant/estimator pair predicts with lower risk than the comparator. See Figures

E.12
and

E.17
in Appendix

\beta
and

\beta

\gamma

\rho^{*}=0.1,0.9,0.99

E.4.1
EDiSC model details

The EDiSC model infers evolving word meanings over time in an unsupervised, bag-of-words framework, using context words to determine the sense of a target word. For a detailed introduction of the model and notation, we refer the reader to
Zafar and Nicholls 2024a

and
Zafar and Nicholls 2024b
. Here, we summarize the key components for the current paper. The training data
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for a target word consists of
n
snippets, each a set of about
L
words
x_{d}\subset{\mathcal{V}}
from a vocabulary
{\mathcal{V}}
from just before and after the target word, with
V={\mathcal{V}}
possible words (
|x_{d}|
may be slightly less than
L
as stopwords and very low frequency words are filtered out) and spans
T
discrete contiguous time
periods and
G
text genres. Each snippet
x_{d}
,
d=1,\ldots,n
, belongs to time period
\tau_{d}
and genre
\gamma_{d}
. In the generative model, for each
x_{d}
, we first sample its sense
z_{d}
, and then sample context words given the sense. Sense
z_{d}
is sampled from a multinomial sense-prevalence distribution
\tilde{\phi}^{\gamma_{d},\tau_{d}}
over
K
senses (indexed by genre and time) so that

p(z_{d}=k|\tilde{\phi}^{\gamma_{d},\tau_{d}})=\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{\gamma_{d},\tau_{d}}\kern 5.0pt\text{for all senses }k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.

Given
z_{d}
, at each position
i
in
x_{d}
, context words
x_{d,i}
are sampled independently from a multinomial distribution
\tilde{\psi}^{z_{d},\tau_{d}}
over the
V
-sized lemmatized vocabulary (indexed by sense and time) so that

p(x_{d,i}=v|z_{d},\tilde{\psi}^{z_{d},\tau_{d}})=\tilde{\psi}^{z_{d},\tau_{d}}_{v}\kern 5.0pt\text{for all words }v\in\{1,\ldots,V\}.

The inferential task is to learn
\tilde{\phi}
and
\tilde{\psi}
given
{\mathbf{x}}
and correctly group the snippets into clusters by meaning.

We use the notation
(z_{d},\gamma_{d},\tau_{d})
for snippet-specific sense-genre-time triples, and
(k,g,t)
for their generic equivalents.
Let
\tilde{\phi}^{g,t}=\mathrm{softmax}(\phi^{g,t})
and
\tilde{\psi}^{k,t}=\mathrm{softmax}(\psi^{k,t})
. The real-valued arrays
\phi
and
\psi
evolve over time
t=1,\dots,T
so their priors (given in
Zafar and Nicholls 2024a
) model that stochastic process. The likelihood model is

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(z_{d}=k|\phi)\,p(x_{d}|z_{d}=k,\psi)

(E.6)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\phi}^{\gamma_{d},\tau_{d}}_{k}\prod_{w\in x_{d}}\tilde{\psi}^{k,\tau_{d}}_{w}.

Snippets
{\mathbf{x}}
are observed data, whereas sense assignments
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
are missing data. The generalized Bayesian posterior is

\pi_{\eta}(\phi,\psi\mid{\mathbf{x}})\propto\pi(\phi,\psi)p({\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi,\psi)^{\eta}

by raising the likelihood to a power of
\eta
.

E.6
Epidemiological data

The model has two modules: in each population
i=1,\dots,n
,
n=13
, a Poisson response for the number of cancer cases
{\mathbf{x}}_{2,i}
in
T_{i}
women-years of followup, and a Binomial model for the number
{\mathbf{x}}_{1,i}
of women infected with HPV in a sample of size
N_{i}
from the
i
’th population;
{\mathbf{x}}_{2,i}\sim Poisson(T_{i}\exp(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\phi_{i}))
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2,i}\sim Binomial(N_{i},\phi_{i})
. The
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
-module is known to be a suspect, and the
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
-module is well-specified. The shared parameter is
\varphi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})
and the
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
-module specific parameter (see Figure
1
) is
\theta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
following the two modular setup in Section 2. The uniform prior is assigned for each
\phi
,
\phi_{i}\sim U[0,1]
,
i=1,\dots,n
and
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\sim N(0,10^{3})
.

To fit our purpose, the data from a Binomial model
({\mathbf{x}}_{1,i},N_{i})
is represented by Bernoulli trials
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{1,i,j}\}^{N_{i}}_{j=1}
;
{\mathbf{x}}_{1,i,j}\sim Bernoullie(\phi_{i})
for
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
. This is to expand the data and divide it into training, calibration, and test datasets. The 1st, 3rd, 4th, 7th and 9th populations are selected because their empirical probabilities are not too close to 0, and there are enough samples per population. For each of those populations,
50\%
of
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{1,i,j}\}^{N_{i}}_{j=1}
,
i\in\{1,3,4,7,9\}
is used as the training data,
25\%
as the calibration data
{\mathbf{y}}_{(1,m)}
, and
25\%
as the test data
{\mathbf{z}}_{(1,n_{t})}
. The
{\mathbf{x}}_{1,i}
data for the other populations (
i\in\{2,5,6,7,8,10,11,12,13\}
) and all data from the Poisson model (
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
) are used as the training data.

With limited samples, there is a danger of losing information by partitioning Bernoulli samples and a single calibration dataset is considered. We randomly partitioned data of the selected populations, estimated a posterior mean denoted by
\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)}
and compared the
\eta
-SMI posterior predictive for the test data. This was repeated 100 times, and the result is summarized in Figure
E.21
. The posterior mean estimate (
\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)}
) tends to be smaller than 0.5 on average, and the relative risk shows that the cut posterior results in better prediction on average, which previous studies showed
Carmona and Nicholls 2020
.

For the
\gamma
-SMI posteriors, the marginalized loss
l_{\gamma}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}})
is estimated using the AGHQ method with
k=5
quadrature points. The
\eta
-SMI posterior was simulated using the R-package,
rstan

(
Carpenter et al. 2017
)
and
2\times 10^{4}
posterior samples (after taking the burn-in of
10^{4}
) are used to estimate
\ell_{(\eta)}(\varphi,\theta^{\prime};{\mathbf{x}}_{1},N,{\mathbf{x}}_{2},T)
and
R({\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)},\eta_{o}})
in Figure
E.21
.
The
\gamma
-SMI posterior was simulated using the R-package,
adaptMCMC

(
Scheidegger 2024
)
implementing the adaptive Metropolis algorithm
(
Vihola 2012
)
. The marginalized loss was estimated using the adaptive Gaussian Hermite quadrature method with 5 quadrature points using the R-package
aghq

(
Stringer 2021
)
and
2\times 10^{5}
posterior samples thinned by 10 are used to estimate
\ell_{(\gamma)}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}}_{1},N,{\mathbf{x}}_{2},T)
in Figure
E.21
.

The right plot in Figure
E.21
compares the logged negative loss functions for
\eta
and
\gamma
from one simulation and they are very similar. Consequently they performs similarly in
\eta
and
\gamma
estimation and predicting the test data;
\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)}=0.366
,
\widehat{\gamma}_{(1,m)}=0.364
,
R(\widehat{\gamma}_{(1,m)},0)=0.178
,
R(\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)},0)=0.195
,
R(\widehat{\gamma}_{(1,m)},1)=0.982
and
R(\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)},1)=1.078
. If we use the MLE we get
\bar{\eta}_{(1,m)}=\bar{\gamma}_{(1,m)}=0
,
R(\bar{\gamma}_{(1,m)},0)=R(\bar{\eta}_{(1,m)},0)=1
,
R(\widehat{\gamma}_{(1,m)},1)=R(\widehat{\eta}_{(1,m)},1)=0.001
so this is a case where the MLE/MAP estimate does better than the posterior mean. This is because the minimum loss value is moderately strongly favored by loss and sits on the boundary at
\eta/\gamma=0
.

Remarks on the train/calibrate split

Although performance is fairly flat with
0<k<1
in most cases in the examples in Appendix
E.2.1
and
E.2.2
, performance on small calibration data sets (
k=0.8
) is generally weaker
(the boxes are flat or slope down with
k
in Figures
2
,
6
and the examples in Appendix
E.2
).
However, the total number of data points split between
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
in this example is small, just
n_{1}=30
, so the number of data points in the calibration data at
k=0.8
is
Jm=(1-k)n_{1}=6
, which is very small. When we split larger data sets in Sections
5.2
,
5.3
and Appendix
E.6
, calibration data sets which are large, but a small fraction of the total, don’t seem to show these problems.

Interestingly, very small training data sets with
k=0.2
perform well.
What is happening here is presumably that it only takes a small data set
{\mathbf{x}}
to inform
\phi
through
\exp(-\ell_{s}(\varphi;{\mathbf{x}}))
in (
12
) so
\pi_{s}(\varphi|{\mathbf{x}})
has the right
\varphi
-dependence even when the number of points in
{\mathbf{x}}
is small. However,

p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
is not very informative of
s
unless the number of blocks is reasonably large. When
k=0.2
,
J=(1-k)n_{1}/m
with
m=3
here so
J=8
and when
k=0.8
we have
J=2
which leaves
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
fairly flat in
s
. It seems that in strongly misspecified settings we need at least
6
to
8
blocks of calibration data for
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
to be reasonably informative of
s
.
Again, for the larger real data sets in Sections
5.3
and Appendix
E.6
, the number of blocks in
{\mathbf{y}}
is always large. For example, in Section
5.3
,
J
ranges from
20
to
59
blocks (see Table
E1
) so even though
k\simeq 0.7
we get very good concentration for
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})
and hence
\rho(s|{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})
in Figure
7
.

Finally, if we took
k=0
we would be calibrating using the marginal likelihood
p_{s}({\mathbf{y}})
for
{\mathbf{y}}
. This is equivalent to estimating
s
using
\rho(s,\varphi|{\mathbf{y}})\propto\rho(s)\exp(-\ell_{s}(\varphi;{\mathbf{y}}))
. We were critical of this in the introduction. The problem is that
\ell_{s}(\varphi;{\mathbf{y}})
is a loss for
\varphi
, and can’t be used as a loss for
s
as it doesnt express any meaningful inferential goal in the belief update for
s
. If we take
k=1
then we simply get the prior for
s
and no belief update.
