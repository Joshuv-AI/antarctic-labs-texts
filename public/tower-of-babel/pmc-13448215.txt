Development and analysis of a seven-level common- ground switched-capacitor inverter topology
Authors: Adil Sarwar, Samina Parween, Mohammad Zaid, Mohammed A AlAqil, Md Shafiul Alam, Mohammad Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a novel Seven-Level Common-Ground Switched-Capacitor Boost Inverter (7L-CG-SCBI) topology with high efficiency, voltage boosting feature, and reduced leakage current for renewable energy applications. The proposed inverter employs a single DC source, three switched capacitors, eleven unidirectional switches, and one bidirectional switch to generate a seven-level output voltage with a gain of three. The capacitors achieve self-balancing without additional sensors or complex control, ensuring stable operation and reduced design complexity. The common ground structure effectively eliminates leakage currents, enhancing system safety and grid compatibility. A comprehensive analysis covering operating principles, modulation strategy, loss evaluation, and reliability assessment using a Markov model is presented. The simulation results in MATLAB/Simulink and experimental validation confirm the inverter’s higher efficiency (96.13%), low total harmonic distortion, and reliable performance of the inverter under varying load and frequency conditions. The compact configuration, self-balancing feature, and high reliability make the proposed 7L-CG-SCBI a promising solution for photovoltaic and other renewable energy systems.Keywords: Switched-capacitor multilevel inverter (SC-MLI), Common-ground topology, Voltage boosting, Renewable energy systems, Leakage current elimination, Reliability analysis, Clean energy conversion, Sustainable power electronicsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Multilevel inverters (MLIs) are being increasingly used in industries because of their excellent performance in power conversion systems. They have the ability to enhance power quality and minimizing total harmonic distortion (THD). It makes them a preferred choice for many applications like renewable energy system, electric vehicles, grid integration etc.1–3 . Conventional MLI inverter topologies like neutral-point clamped (NPC), flying capacitor (FC), and cascaded H-bridge (CHB) inverters, have commonly been used in industry and research in the last few decades. These inverters help lower the voltage stress on switches, reduce the need for large filters, and improve electromagnetic interference (EMI)4 . However, there are various issues associated with conventional MLIs. For example, maintaining voltage balance in FC and NPC topologies is a significant challenge. It often requires complex balancing circuits and control algorithms5 . On the other hand, the switched capacitor multilevel inverter (SC-MLI) based topologies are gaining attention as a better alternative to conventional MLIs. It can balance its capacitor voltage automatically and generate a boosted output voltage without requiring transformers. Because of these advantages, SC-MLIs are well suited for renewable energy system6,7. SC-MLI topologies have several advantages over conventional inverters, including low leakage current and high efficiency, especially in transformerless configurations. The absence of transformers reduces overall costs and eliminates safety concerns related to leakage currents, which can distort the output current and cause electromagnetic interference. Moreover, the ability of SC- MLIs to achieve higher output levels and reduce harmonic distortion simplifies filter design and makes them a viable option for grid-tied applications8.

To address the challenges of reducing control complexity and minimizing component count, several innovative MLI topologies have been proposed for single-phase applications. Proposed seven-level CG-SCBI

Circuit topology

The proposed seven-level common ground switched capacitor boost inverter (7L-CG-SCBI) topology is illustrated in Fig. 2. The circuit utilizes only one DC power source, twelve switches (S1 to S12), consisting of eleven unidirectional switches, one bidirectional switch, and three switched capacitors (C1, C2, and C3). Capacitors are used for voltage boosting and level formation. Capacitors C2 and C3 are linked in series with each other and positioned parallel to the input DC voltage (Vdc), whereas C1 is connected in parallel to the input supply. This configuration allows capacitor C1 to charge up to the input voltage Vdc without requiring any sensors or additional circuitry.

Operating principle

1) DecimalComplex Level 0:

Figures 3 (a), (b), and (c) show three switching states that generate a 0 level. Each configuration involves different combinations of closed and open switches, which leads to the charging of capacitors in specific ways. In the first state, switches S2, S5, S10, and S12 are closed; during the second state, capacitor C1 is charged to Vdc, with switches S1, S2, S5, S10, and S12 closed; and in the third state, switches S3, S4, S8, and S11 are closed, creating distinct charging paths for the capacitors while maintaining the overall system at a balanced 0 level.

Switching Path of the proposed topology illustrating the load current flow paths for various voltage levels: (a–c) zero voltage levels, (d–h) positive voltage levels, and (i–o) negative voltage levels.

2) DecimalComplex Level + Vdc:

Figure 3(d) illustrates the switching states used to generate the + Vdc output level, with switches S1, S2, S4, S5, S7, S8, S9, and S11 being in a conducting state. In this configuration, the C1 and C2 capacitors are charged to Vdc.

3) DecimalComplex Level + 2Vdc:

Figure 3(e) illustrates the switching condition where switches S1, S3, S7, S8, and S12 are deactivated, whereas switches S2, S4, S5, S6, S9, S10, and S11 remain in the conducting state. As a result, capacitors C1, C2, and C3 discharge. Another way to achieve a 2Vdc output, as shown in Fig. 2(f), is by deactivating switches S3, S7, S8, and S12 while keeping switches S1, S2 S4, S5, S6, S9, S10, and S11 conducting. Under this condition, capacitor C1 charges while C2 and C3 are discharging.

4) DecimalComplex Level + 3 Vdc:

The maximum voltage output for this mode, as illustrated in Fig. 3(g), is generated when all three capacitors discharge in series with the load. This occurs when switches S1, S3, S5, S8, S9, and S12 are deactivated, whereas the remaining switches S2, S4, S6, S7, S10, and S11 are actively conducting. In this configuration, capacitors C2 and C3 are charged.

In this case, C1 and C3 are discharged.

In the proposed topology, efficiency degradation at high power is mainly caused by: (i) increased conduction losses due to higher load current flowing through a larger number of series-connected semiconductor devices and switched-capacitor paths, (ii) elevated switching losses arising from higher current stress during turn-on and turn-off transitions, and (iii) increased capacitor-related losses, including ESR losses and ripple-current-induced dissipation, which become more pronounced at higher power levels. To evaluate reliability, the failure rate (λ), which represents the frequency of failures over time, is has been used. Evaluating the reliability of system components is essential for estimating how long component can operate before failure occurs. Markov modeling approach has been used for reliability analysis. This method involves representing the system in various operational states and observing how it transitions between them over time.

As shown in Fig. 8, the system is represented via three distinct operational states:

Markov three-state model.

III. State 1 is the healthy state where the system operates normally, producing all expected output voltage levels.

IV. State 2 is the degraded state where the system is partially functional. Although not fully operational, the system continues to deliver energy.

V. State 3 is the failed state where the system is nonfunctional and unable to produce any voltage at the output, Therefore no power is delivered.

The transitions between these states have been defined via Markov transition rates, denoted by λij, which describe the likelihood of moving from one state to another over time. λsk represents the failure rate of the kth switch entering a faulty condition.

Conclusion

This paper presented a novel 7 L-CG-SCBI topology which can be used for renewable energy application. The proposed topology combines the benefits of switched-capacitor and common-ground structures to achieve voltage boosting, leakage current elimination, and self-balanced capacitor voltages using a single DC source. It generates seven voltage levels employing only twelve switches and three capacitors. The common-ground arrangement ensures the complete elimination of leakage current thereby enhancing safety and makes the design highly suitable for transformerless photovoltaic (PV) and other renewable energy systems. All capacitors have identical ratings and share equal voltage stresses, which simplifies design, improves reliability, and eases maintenance. Simulation and experimental results confirm the validity of the proposed inverter. A peak efficiency of 96.13%, low total harmonic distortion, and robust operation under both resistive and inductive loads as well as variable-frequency conditions was achieved. Comparative analysis demonstrated that the proposed 7 L-CG- SCBI outperforms several existing topologies in terms of component count, voltage gain, and reliability while maintaining simplicity and stability.
