Robot motion skill learning method based on focused reward transformer
Authors: Borui Nan, Xinyi Zheng, Wanru Gong, Lanyue Bi, Xiaoyv Zhu, Xiaoqing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the field of robotics control, prevailing research is progressively leveraging more sophisticated deep learning networks to enhance learning outcomes in specific domains of robotics motion control. Notably, the Decision Transformer (DT), a prominent model in motion control learning, predominantly focuses on long-term expected returns and often overlooks the critical significance of immediate action values, which are essential for the adaptive behaviors observed in higher animals. Addressing this gap, this paper introduces the Focused Reward Transformer (FRT) . This innovative training network combines guided attention mechanisms with a significant emphasis on reward processing. The FRT aims to optimize robot motion control by dynamically balancing long-term predictions with immediate environmental cues. The effectiveness of the FRT was rigorously evaluated by detailed simulations on the MuJoCo platform, using three different tasks: Hopper, Walker2d, and Halfcheetah. These tasks broadly represent the challenges inherent to robotics dynamics. Our experimental results demonstrate a substantial improvement in the performance of control tasks, the ability to validate FRT, refine robotic control, and skillfully combine in-time context responses with the long-term planning of strategies. This confirms the potential of FRT to advance the field of robotic control by introducing more nuanced approaches to learning and decision-making processes.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a machine learning method that develops decision-making strategies by interacting with the environment. It is widely used in the field of robot motion control1–3. In RL, the fundamental goal is to design a strategy that maximizes the sum of expected returns in the Markov Decision Process (MDP)4. While traditional RL approaches excel in simulated environments, they often face challenges in balancing short-term actions with long-term goals, particularly in real-world robotic applications where dynamic constraints and safety requirements impose critical trade-offs. Offline RL eliminates interactions with the environment during training and relies on fixed datasets to derive effective strategies5. This approach is particularly critical in domains where each interaction with the environment is prohibitively expensive or fraught with substantial risk, such as finance6, healthcare7 and recommendation systems8, where trial-and-error learning is impractical.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)9, Long Short-Term Memory Net-works (LSTMs)10, and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs)11 process sequential information by handling elements one at a time. However, their ability to manage long sequential data is limited due to their sequential processing nature. To address this limitation, the attention mechanism was introduced12, allowing models to selectively focus on different parts of the input data while generating outputs, thus capturing long-range dependencies more effectively13,14.

The Transformer architecture, proposed by Vaswani et al.12, effectively addresses the challenges of sequential data modeling. It features an encoder-decoder structure that employs a self-attention mechanism, enabling parallel processing of relationships between all words in an input sequence. This parallelizable nature allows the Transformer to leverage advanced computational hardware for training large-scale models12,15. The Decision Transformer (DT) architecture16 effectively applies the Transformer framework to offline RL tasks. It employs a target-conditional strategy that transforms offline RL into a supervised learning problem, thereby circumventing self-guided stability issues related to long-term credit allocation17–19. DT treats the sum of future rewards, or return-to-go (RTG), as the target and learns strategies conditioned on both RTG and state. As such, it is categorized as a reward-conditioning method20.The Transformer architecture is widely applied in various fields, including image classification21,22, object detection23,24, semantic segmentation, natural language processing12,19,25, image processing26, and video understanding27.

While DT excels in long-term return estimation, recent studies highlight its critical limitation: it often prioritizes global return optimization at the expense of immediate action valuation28. This imbalance can lead to suboptimal decisions in dynamic environments where real-time feedback is essential-a challenge analogous to biological systems that continuously integrate instantaneous sensory inputs with long-term behavioral goals. For instance, in robotic locomotion, organisms instinctively adjust gait patterns based on terrain feedback while maintaining trajectory objectives-a capability yet to be fully replicated in artificial agents.

This study addresses a pivotal challenge in robotic control: balancing immediate action efficacy with long-term goal optimization in dynamic, uncertain environments. Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) models, including decision transformers (DT), often struggle with this trade-off, particularly in scenarios requiring rapid adaptation, such as uneven terrain navigation or real-time medical decision support systems. To address this issue, we propose a new unified decision-making framework that integrates immediate and long-term rewards. By focusing on two key areas for improvement, we arrive at the Focused Reward Transformer (FRT), which includes the following modules:The Reward-Integrated Transformer (RIT) : This mechanism improves the traditional Decision Transformer by integrating an additional reward sequence into the input embedding layer.The Reward-Driven Transformer (RDT) : This mechanism integrates dedicated reward to process mechanisms.The Focused Reward Transformer (FRT) : This mechanism optimizes the drawbacks of RDT by selectively integrating reward-specific sequences into the attention mechanism and effectively balances responses to immediate feedback with long-term planning.Our experiments utilize the Mujoco suite29 and the D4RL dataset30 to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed strategy for complex robotic tasks. The experiment was divided into three main groups, each focusing on a different robot simulation: “Hopper”, “Walker2d” and “HalfCheetah”. Our experimental framework includes a systematic evaluation process. In each environment, we deploy all the algorithms simultaneously to allow for a comprehensive evaluation of their performance in terms of inference accuracy, action execution errors, and training time. Our strategy effectively manages the complex dynamics and motor challenges that reflect real-world scenarios.

Methods

Overview

In this study, we propose a series of improved decision transformer (DT) frameworks by focusing on reward processing, including the Reward Integration Transformer (RIT), the Reward Driven Transformer (RDT) and the Focused Reward Transformer (FRT). These frameworks aim to integrate immediate reward signals into action selection strategies while preserving the long-term memory and planning capabilities by focusing and setting an attentional weight on current reward outcomes. The goal is to balance responsiveness to immediate feedback with long-term strategic rewards.

The three models are based on same backbone and constantly adjust their structural details, eventually forming the FRT model that provides the best performance in complex reinforcement learning scenarios. In the following sections, we’ll discuss each model and highlight changes and improvements in attention mechanisms and reward integration specifically.

Model 1: reward-integrated transformer (RIT)

The Reward-Integrated Transformer (RIT) model aims to improve the traditional Decision Transformer by integrating an additional reward sequence into the input embedding layer during the decision-making procedure. This allows the self-attention mechanism to consider immediate rewards along with the return-to-go (R), state (s), and action (a) sequences, therefore enhancing the model’s understanding of the current environment and its importance in the whole simulation. What’s more, this new structure makes our model able to introduce both innediate rewards and long-term return signals at the same time. Results

Experimental setup

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the ability of decision transformer models to improve robotic motion skill learning in a simulated environment. Through the use of high-fidelity simulation platforms like MuJoCo (Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact), we aim to assess how effectively our models enhance task performance over time. By leveraging task scores, we seek to demonstrate how our proposed approach leads to improvements in motion control skills, simulating a realistic learning process for robotic systems.

MuJoCo provides a robust platform for simulating complex robotic tasks, making it an ideal environment for testing control strategies. In this study, we use MuJoCo to evaluate the performance of our decision transformer models in enhancing robotic motion control. The goal is to simulate the robot’s ability to learn and refine its motion skills, with task scores serving as the key metric to assess the impact of our method.

We employed datasets generated by D4RL for the medium-dataset of Hopper, Walker2d, and HalfCheetah within the MuJoCo gym environment, whose parameters could be seen in Table S1 of the supplementary information. Post-training tests were conducted in these corresponding environments. Table 1 outlines the key dynamics features and gym dataset details for each task. This diversity allows a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s ability to manage complex dynamics and motor control in simulations closely resembling real-world conditions.

In our setup, as shown in Table S1 of the supplementary information, training iterations are followed by evaluation phases every 2,000 iterations. During these evaluations, the model’s performance is averaged over 100 consecutive trials. Summary

After integrating the experimental data, we transitioned from conducting isolated analyses to performing a comprehensive evaluation, including DT FRT, its ablated version RDT, and RIT.

As shown in Table 3, RIT outperforms FRT when only normalization is considered. However, when average rewards in the closing phase are included in the analysis, FRT demonstrates superior performance in terms of average post-stabilization scores and shows less volatility across all three environments, resulting in the best overall performance. Additionally, the instantaneous distribution of individual scores, as illustrated in Fig. 7, provides a more intuitive visualization of FRT’s advantages. This is further supported by the distribution of confidence intervals displayed in Fig. 8.

In summary, the combination of these factors confirms that FRT is the highest-performing model overall.

Discussion

This study proposes a framework that uses attention mechanisms to balance immediate and long-term rewards, aiming to address the limitations of traditional decision transformers, especially in scenarios that require immediate action optimisation, such as robot motion control. Meanwhile, we evaluated the performance of FRT and its ablated versions, RDT and RIT. The results show that FRT achieves higher average post-reward stability and higher system stability in different environments.

The progression from RIT to RDT and finally to FRT represents a systematic enhancement of the Decision Transformer framework. FRT begins by integrating immediate rewards, dynamically adjusts attention based on these rewards, and ultimately refines the attention mechanism to selectively focus on essential reward sequences. This process achieves an optimal balance between immediate and long-term objectives, making FRT the most effective model. It demonstrates superior adaptability and decision-making accuracy in complex reinforcement learning environments.

Beyond robotics, FRT’s principles of reward balancing hold promise in fields such as healthcare and finance. In healthcare, FRT could optimize decision-making systems that balance immediate treatment outcomes with long-term patient health. In finance, it may improve portfolio management and algorithmic trading by balancing short-term financial decisions with long-term strategies, potentially leading to more adaptable and precise decision-making systems.

The results strongly support FRT’s effectiveness in controlled simulations, but several limitations should be noted. First, the findings are based on simulations, which may not fully reflect the complexities of real-world robotic systems. Thus, while FRT performs well in balancing short-term and long-term rewards in simulated tasks, its application in dynamic, uncertain, and constrained real-world environments remains uncertain.

Another limitation is the computational complexity of FRT, which, despite promising results, demands significant resources. This could hinder its scalability in resource-limited environments. Future work should focus on optimizing FRT’s computational efficiency, such as by simplifying the model, exploring more efficient attention mechanisms (e.g., sparse or linear attention), or leveraging techniques like model pruning or knowledge distillation. Additionally, using hardware acceleration or exploring transfer learning may help mitigate resource demands while maintaining performance.

Finally, improving sensory capabilities in robots could enhance interaction with the environment and skill acquisition, expanding the real-world applicability of FRT without compromising performance.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates FRT’s effectiveness in complex reinforcement learning tasks and lays the groundwork for advancing Decision Transformer models in robotics. It highlights the need for further exploration of robust, scalable models that can balance diverse reward structures, thereby driving innovation in autonomous robotic learning and control.
