Hydrogel microneedles functioning in pathological surroundings: where soft materials overcome hard scars
Authors: Kun Lei, Zimeng Xu, Dianhao Gong, Weiyi Wang, Jie Cheng, Junbo Li, Xinchang Pang, Zhiqiang Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathological scarring is a manifestation of abnormal skin wound healing characterized by fibrosis, resulting from excessive fibroblast activation and abnormal deposition of the extracellular matrix, Currently, the main treatment options, such as corticosteroid injections or ointments, cryotherapy, laser therapy and radiation therapy, face some challenges in their application. For example, long-term corticosteroid injections can lead to side effects of skin atrophy and telangiectasia; topical ointments have limited penetration. Cryotherapy lacks precision and damages the surrounding skin, leading to pigmentation abnormalities. Laser treatment requires multiple sessions with long treatment period and costly fee. Radiation therapy has a very limited scope of application, and even low-dose radiation can still damage surrounding tissues. Hydrogels microneedles (HMNs) represent a novel transdermal drug delivery platform. The microneedle structure enables them to penetrate the stratum corneum, facilitating precise drug delivery, and the materials used to prepare HMNs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, thereby reducing the risk of skin irritation and allergic reactions. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive hydrogels have also demonstrated significant potential and distinct advantages in the field of scar treatment. However, a comprehensive report on HMNs for scar treatment are rarely conducted. This review provides a systematic overview of HMNs technology for pathological scar treatment, summarizing recent research advances in this area. Firstly, the design and fabrication of HMNs is discussed about material preparation methods and numerous smart responsive HMNs are detailedly demonstrated. Secondly, various action mechanisms involved in scar treatment are outlined. Subsequently, the current status of clinical application research in scar treatment are summarized. Finally, the current bottlenecks in HMNs technology regarding drug loading capacity and penetration depth are highlighted, and future directions such as smart responsiveness, personalised therapy and the integration with artificial intelligence (AI) to facilitate the clinical translation of HMNs technology are outlined and envisaged.Keywords: Hydrogel microneedles, Responsive hydrogels, Scar treatment, Drug delivery, Transdermal drug delivery

Introduction

Pathological scars (PSs) are fibrotic disorders caused by abnormal wound healing, referring to abnormal reparative responses during the healing process of skin injuries, and the clinical manifestations include excessive collagen deposition and persistent inflammation [1,2]. Based on clinical, epidemiological, and histopathological classifications, they are primarily composed of keloids and hypertrophic scars (HS) [3,4]. As a relatively common scar, HS are scar tissues that grow only within the boundaries of the original wound and do not infiltrate or spread into the surrounding normal skin. They most commonly appear in areas of high tension or joint movement, such as the area in front of the sternum, which typically present the raised, itchy lesions. Over time, as the inflammation subsides, the scars gradually soften, flatten, and fade in color [1,3]. Keloids are scar tissues that break through the boundaries of the original injury site and grow into surrounding normal skin in an infiltrative, crab-leg-like, or nodular manner [5]. They can occur anywhere and such in cases of deep skin injury, they can be observed in areas of stress concentration. Inflammation may lessen but does not disappear, and the scars will persist [[6], [7], [8]]. Both types of scars will protrude above the surface of the skin [[9], [10], [11]]. Currently, the main methods for treating PSs include corticosteroid injections or ointments, cryotherapy, laser treatment, and radiotherapy [[12], [13], [14]]. Corticosteroid injections can reduce the recurrence rate and are compatible with combination therapy, but long-term injections can easily cause skin atrophy, lead to capillary dilation, and cause significant pain [15]. Cryotherapy is safe and causes minimal pain, but it can damage the surrounding skin, leading to hypopigmentation, and there is no standardised treatment duration [16]. Laser scar treatment is highly targeted and precise, but it requires multiple sessions and is expensive [17]. Radiation therapy has a very limited range of applications and may cause hyperpigmentation and telangiectasia [6,18].

Therefore, there is an urgent need for a more efficient, minimally invasive, and targeted new therapy. The new local drug delivery system has attracted attention due to its low cost and few side effects. Especially in recent years, with the development and extensive research of microneedle technology in the field of local drug delivery, as a minimally invasive, efficient, and highly targeted transdermal drug delivery method, microneedle drug delivery can deliver drugs precisely to the targeted site, and achieve precise local sustained-release treatment, showing great prospects in the treatment of PSs [[19], [20], [21]]. In 1976, Gerstel and Place, based on a deep understanding of traditional intradermal injection techniques, systematically proposed the concept of microneedle transdermal drug delivery for the first time and applied for a patent for it (Fig. 1) [35]. In 1998, Henry et al. successfully used reactive ion etching (RIE) microfabrication technology to fabricate silicon-based microneedle arrays, which were applied for the first time in transdermal drug delivery [22]. However, transdermal drug delivery faces significant challenges in overcoming the strong barrier function of the skin's stratum corneum, particularly for macromolecular drugs, in which traditional formulations are often unable to address. Prausnitz et al. demonstrated the fabrication of metal microneedle arrays in 2004, conducted in vitro skin penetration experiments, and validated the feasibility of transdermal insulin delivery and blood glucose reduction in animal models of diabetes. These were the first-generation solid microneedles that enabled the creation of physical microchannels [23]. However, these microneedles (MNs) cannot eliminate the need for a second procedure or the potential biosafety risks. Consequently, Miyano et al. developed the first soluble microneedle in 2005, demonstrating a safe, biodegradable, and easy-to-use sugar microneedle transdermal drug delivery system that effectively addressed the risks of fracture and residue associated with earlier microneedles, as well as the need for additional equipment [24].

Hydrogel dressings are widely used in topical drug delivery and the treatment of acute and chronic wounds, hard-to-heal wounds and HS [36]. They maintain a moist wound environment, conform to all types of uneven skin surfaces and reduce systemic side effects of the medication. However, they also have certain limitations, including a sudden release of drugs, an obstacle for penetrating deeply into scars, and the challenge to balance breathability with water resistance [37]. When the unique structural advantages of hydrogels are combined with microneedle technology, they can well address the above shortcomings. It cannot be denied that the emergence of hydrogel materials has significantly advanced the developments of microneedles [38]. Hydrogels with a three-dimensional network structure, are characterized by high water retention, good elasticity, excellent biocompatibility and flexibly adjustable structural properties, making them an ideal substrate for the preparation of microneedles [[39], [40], [41]]. They not only address the shortcomings (such as the poor skin penetration) of conventional hydrogel dressings, but also retain their inherent advantages in drug delivery and wound healing. Donnelly team developed a hydrogel-based microneedle array using cross-linked polymers in 2012 and validated its efficacy, safety, and application potential in the field of transdermal drug delivery through systematic in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo experiments [25]. In recent years, with advances in drug delivery technology, hydrogels have evolved from traditional types to smart hydrogels, enabling more precise and intelligent controlled drug release [42,43]. In 2016, Hardy et al. first developed a light-responsive hydrogel microneedle array. In vitro experiments demonstrated that, upon activation by 365-nm ultraviolet light, the microneedle array could release drugs on demand over a period of 160 h, delivering up to three doses. These smart HMNs can form a dynamic drug reservoir beneath under the skin and exhibit responsive drug release behavior in response to changes in the local microenvironment [26]. In the same year, Lee et al. developed a graphene-based electrochemical device equipped with thermosensitive microneedles, creating an integrated smart patch for diabetes monitoring and treatment. This patch enables real-time monitoring of glucose levels in sweat and triggers the transdermal release of metformin.

Experiments on diabetic mice confirmed its effectiveness in lowering blood glucose levels [27]. In 2018, the core-shell soluble layered microneedles (LMNs) were first reported, which maintained good mechanical properties even at 60% relative humidity and achieved a transdermal drug delivery efficiency of nearly 90% within 10 s. These microneedles outperformed conventional homogeneous HA microneedles, and addressed the issues of insufficient strength and difficult insertion associated with traditional soluble microneedles under high-humidity conditions, while reducing drug residue and controlling the delivery time [28]. Han et al. used 3D printing to modify the shape of microneedles, creating biomimetic microneedles with reverse-curved barbs to enhance their adhesion in 2020 [29]. Maher Amer et al. developed a light-responsive hydrogel microneedle array composed of a blend of polyvinyl alcohol and N-isopropylacrylamide conjugated with pyran. These microneedles can adhere to the ocular delivery site upon swelling and shrink upon exposure to light, enabling sustained ocular drug delivery without the need for external adhesives [30]. In 2023, inspired by the predatory behavior of the blue-ringed octopus, a biomimetic microneedle patch with physicochemical synergies was developed composed of silk fibroin-pluronic F127 (Silk-Fp)-poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm), the patch achieves a strong adhesion of over 10 kPa on moist tissue surfaces. The thermosensitive microneedles can detect body temperature to enable a dual-mode drug delivery system comprising early active drug injection, followed by sustained-release [31]. In 2024, Zhou et al. utilized a method involving rolling microneedles (RMNs) to prepare multi-structured templates, creating meta microfluidic microneedle chip (MMN) based on a photonic crystal membrane (PC). This device enables real-time monitoring of inflammatory factors in mouse wounds, and the MXene conductive layer facilitates motion sensing around the wound. MMN patches loaded with human epidermal growth factor significantly promoted skin wound healing in mice, representing a multifunctional composite hydrogel microneedle that integrates photonic crystal sensing capabilities with an MXene conductive layer [32]. Recently, inspired by wood, Hu et al. developed microneedles with anisotropic pores (APC-MNs) using unidirectional freeze-casting technology. This approach addressed the issues of random pore distribution and low transport efficiency associated with traditional porous microneedles, while also providing dual functionality for both biofluid sampling and drug delivery [33]. In 2026, a programmable, biodegradable electrochemical microneedle sensor array fabricated using 3D printing technology was innovatively developed, which can be applied to deep-seated organs to perform real-time, spatial monitoring of multiple biochemical parameters. Meanwhile, it also features electro-programmable self-destruction and biodegradation, demonstrating both efficacy and biocompatibility for monitoring the health of deep-seated organs during the perioperative period [34].

As a novel drug delivery and tissue intervention vehicle that combines biocompatibility with minimally invasive transdermal drug delivery system capabilities, HMNs offer a fresh approach to the clinical treatment and repair of PSs [25]. Using a minimally invasive approach, it penetrates the stratum corneum barrier of scar tissue to directly target fibrotic lesions in the dermis. Its physical penetration breaks down the abnormally cross-linked network of collagen fibers in the scar, thereby activating the skin's endogenous repair pathways [44]. Furthermore, the inherent swelling and sustained-release properties of hydrogels enable the targeted and sustained delivery of active ingredients with anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, thereby precisely regulating the scar microenvironment and inhibiting excessive fibroblast proliferation and abnormal collagen deposition. Furthermore, HMNs can effectively bypass the skin barrier inherent in scar tissue and deliver anti-scar medications precisely to the target site with significantly higher efficiency than traditional topical formulations, showing great promise for the treatment of PSs, such as PSs and keloids [45]. Furthermore, by designing smart responsive HMNs, an on-demand drug delivery can be achieved, enabling more precise scar treatment. However, though there are various methods and strategies to fabricate HMNs for for treating scars, such microneedle-based scar therapies using HMNs technology and how HMNs thrive and conquer PSs in complex pathological conditions are rarely roundly summarized and illustrated.

Here, this review provides a comprehensive overview of the field of hydrogel microneedle therapy for scar treatment and summarizes recent research advances in this area (Fig. 2). First, the design and construction strategies of HMNs are described, with a focus on the materials and preparation methods, especially for smart responsive microneedles. Second, various mechanisms of scar treatment are introduced, such as photodynamic therapy, topical medication. Then, the current status of clinical application research in scar treatment is summarized. Finally, the technical challenges facing HMNs are outlined in scar treatment, as well as their future development directions and clinical translation. Finally, we anticipate how mechanistic structures, innovative technologies, emerging diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, and the integration with artificial intelligence (AI) may reframe the future directions in the area. Together, hydrogel microneedle materials and technologies enable innovative approaches in skin disease medicine and patient-specific therapies.

Materials and fabrication strategies of hydrogel microneedles

HMNs are microneedles made of hydrogel material; during their preparation, a polymer solution is cured in some manner to form a gel structure with sufficient mechanical strength. As a novel medical delivery system that integrates biomaterials science with micro- and nanoengineering, its core advantage stems from the functional synergy between the inherent properties of hydrogel matrix and the microneedle structure. Hydrogels are three-dimensional network structures formed by cross-linking the hydrophilic polymers, endowing them with excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and high load-carrying capacity. They are also capable of absorbing and retaining large amounts of water without dissolving, which enables the stable encapsulation of various active ingredients, including small-molecule drugs and biomacromolecules [[46], [47], [48]]. The minimally invasive design of microneedle array enables precise penetration of the skin's stratum corneum-the primary barrier to traditional transdermal delivery, allowing for the targeted delivery and controlled release of active ingredients while avoiding the pain and risk of cross-infection associated with invasive injections. Since the stratum corneum in scarred skin is thicker than in other skin types, HMN requires higher mechanical strength. The functionality of HMNs is highly dependent on their matrix material and the choice of material determines the overall performances of microneedles, while the preparation methods provide a key pathway for performance optimisation. Through a targeted chemical modification of hydrogel matrix, optimisation of microneedle geometric parameters, and continuous improvement of the preparation process, it is possible to achieve multidimensional control over the mechanical properties, degradation rates, drug release kinetics, and biocompatibility of microneedles.

This section primarily introduces the materials used to prepare HMNs, including natural polymers and synthetic polymers. The natural polymers are usually biocompatible, enzymatic-degradable, mechanically weak and prone to rapid degradation, such as chitosan (CS) [49], gelatine [50] and hyaluronic acid (HA) [51]. Synthetic polymers usually possess stable structures, controllable mechanical properties, high purity and non-biodegradability, such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) [52] and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) [53]. Besides, the cross-linking and preparation methods for HMNs are also systematacially demonstrated.

Composition and action mechanism

Needle-tip substrate material

Chitosan (CS)

CS is a natural cationic polyelectrolyte primarily derived from shrimp and crab shells, as well as fungal cell walls. It is produced through the deacetylation of chitin [49,[54], [55], [56], [57]]. Besides, it also exhibits excellent film-forming properties, antibacterial activity, adsorption capacity, and gel-forming ability. Methods for preparing CS include chemical alkali extraction and enzymatic hydrolysis, pollution-free process-as well as new techniques such as microwave irradiation, which can enhance production efficiency [[58], [59], [60]].

Although pure CS hydrogels retain the inherent bioactivity and hydrophilic properties of CS, they lack sufficient mechanical strength and are prone to rupture and breakage in practical applications such as under mechanical stress or when penetrating the skin [61]. CS microneedles can be modified through chemical modification or composite modification with other materials to enhance the physicochemical properties. Ahmad et al. fabricated a modified thiolated chitosan (TCS) by using thioglycolic acid [62]. The TCS-based microneedles achieved a skin penetration rate of 84%, with no microneedle breakage, and were able to penetrate the dermis.

Hyaluronic acid (HA)

HA is a natural linear polysaccharide with high hydrophilicity, and non-immunogenicity, which is widely found in the human synovial fluid, eye vitreous humor, and skin [51,[63], [64], [65]]. HA is easily modified by using functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on molecular chains of natural HA to construct a three-dimensional network structure, hence improving physical and chemical properties while retaining the original biocompatibility and degradability [66].

Nettles et al. modified hyaluronic acid with a molecular weight of approximately 1,000,000 Da using methacrylic anhydride to prepare a 1.5% (w/v) hyaluronic acid methacryloyl (HAMA) solution, which was then cross-linked under visible light (514 nm) to form HA-MA hydrogel, characterized by a stable three-dimensional network structure. Through repeated precipitation and dialysis, toxic byproducts and unreacted materials were effectively removed. Furthermore, the material exhibits good biocompatibility, good mechanical properties, and adjustable degradability, making it particularly suitable for microneedle fabrication [67]. In 2025, a study by Wu et al. confirmed that by optimizing the HAMA concentration (10% w/v) and UV crosslinking time (20 min), the HA microneedles demonstrated sufficient mechanical strength (withstanding 1.5 N of pressure) to penetrate the skin's stratum corneum, as shown in mouse experiments. Insertion experiments further demonstrated that the microneedles formed a complete array and left clear pores in the skin, proving their effective drug delivery capability and showing no significant toxicity to the body [68]. Meng et al. developed a soluble microneedle array for the treatment of HS, using hyaluronic acid/(2-hydroxypropyl) -β-cyclodextrin (HA/HP-β-CD) as the matrix, which was loaded with miR-141-3p-functionalized exosomes. HP-β-CD regulates the release rate, while HA delays carrier degradation; furthermore, HA endogenous nature reduces immunogenicity, and HP-β-CD exhibits low toxicity. HP-β-CD also enhances the mechanical strength of the HA microneedles to facilitate penetration of hypertrophic scar tissue. These microneedles enable painless transdermal delivery and sustained release of exosomes, significantly improving scar thickness, collagen alignment, and fibroblast distribution, thereby achieving excellent anti-scarring effects through the regulation of target pathways [69].

Gelatin

Gelatin is a derivative obtained by partially hydrolyzing collagen; its primary sources are connective tissues such as the skin and bones of animals like cattle and pigs. During the conversion of collagen into gelatin, the triple-helix structure is disrupted and the molecular chains unfold, transforming it from a substance that can only swell into one that is soluble in water, thereby making it easier to process and handle [50,70]. Hydrogels prepared from pure gelatin suffer from suboptimal physicochemical properties, which can be modified using both physical and chemical methods to improve their properties. Through physical modification, the mechanical, structural, and morphological properties of gelatin can be easily optimized, resulting in compressible, tough, flexible, adhesive, and stretchable characteristics [71,72].

GelMA was synthesized by reacting gelatin with methacrylic anhydride (MA) under mild conditions. This process grafts methacrylate groups onto the amino and hydroxyl groups of the gelatin molecules, endowing the material with photo-crosslinking capability while retaining the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequence, which promotes cell proliferation and migration. This not only addresses gelatin tendency to dissolve at body temperature, expanding its potential as a cell carrier, but also preserves gelatin biochemical functions [[73], [74], [75]]. Furthermore, GelMA exhibits excellent thermosensitive gelling properties and degradability, allowing its mechanical and biological properties to be tuned by adjusting the GelMA concentration, the degree of methacryloyl substitution, the initiator concentration, and UV conditions [76,77].

Silk fibroin (SF)

SF is a natural polymeric fibrous protein extracted from silk, primarily composed of heavy chains (390 kDa), light chains (26 kDa), and P25 protein [78]. SF hydrogels are biomaterials prepared using SF as the matrix material through specific cross-linking processes [79]. The formation of SF hydrogels depends on their transition from a random coiled conformation to a β-sheet structure (sol-gel transition). Their preparation methods are primarily divided into two categories: physical cross-linking and chemical cross-linking [80]. The use of sericin to prepare microneedles avoids the risks of prolonged skin irritation and residue. Structural stability can be optimized through physical or chemical cross-linking, while the sol-gel transformation properties of SF enable drug loading and sustained release. Furthermore, material modification can impart smart properties such as glucose responsiveness, pH responsiveness, or electro-responsiveness, allowing drug release to be precisely regulated in response to physiological signals within the body or external stimuli [81]. Yin et al. used a mixture of small-molecule reagents (such as 2-ethoxyethanol, ECS) to inhibit excessive crystallization of SF (thereby reducing the formation of β-folds), enabling the microneedles to maintain their rigidity in a dry state for skin penetration. Upon contact with tissue fluid, the microneedles absorbed water and swelled through a randomly curled structure, forming a porous network that facilitated drug diffusion [82].

Sodium alginate (SA)

SA is a natural anionic polyelectrolyte extracted from brown algae such as kelp and sargassum. It consists of M and G blocks linked by 1,4-glycosidic bonds, with the M/G ratio directly determining its gelling and mechanical properties. It undergoes a sol-gel transition by forming a cross-linked structure via calcium ions [[83], [84], [85]]. However, traditional calcium-ion-crosslinked alginate hydrogels suffer from drawbacks such as molding shrinkage, weak mechanical properties and poor lubricity [86]. By combining a pre-concentrated sodium alginate precursor solution with DPPC liposomes, the LipoGel biomimetic lubricating composite hydrogel has been constructed to enhance both mechanical and interfacial lubrication properties whilst retaining excellent biocompatibility, making it suitable for applications such as artificial cartilage, implant lubrication and drug-eluting dressings [87]. Furthermore, alginate hydrogels possess excellent cell encapsulation capacity and biocompatibility, and can be used to prepare cell patches for wound repair. By encapsulating ARPE-1 engineered cells (secreting reparative factors) within a calcium alginate matrix and combining this with a PDMS support layer, the patch is able to retain cells and provide a sustained local release of cytokines. In full-thickness wound models in mice and pigs, this patch modulates gene expression in immune cells, activates skin development and collagen remodeling pathways, and accelerates wound healing, thereby offering a new approach to in situ cellular therapy for chronic wounds [88].

Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)

As a synthetic polymeric material, PVP is a biodegradable polymer formed by the polymerization of vinylpyrrolidone monomers and polyvinylpyrrolidone. It not only has good hydrophilicity but also can dissolve well in a variety of polar solvents, while exhibiting excellent binding properties and the ability to stabilize suspensions and emulsions [[89], [90], [91]]. High-molecular-weight PVP provides stronger mechanical support, making it suitable for microneedle penetration of the skin [92]. Based on this property, Prausnitz et al. optimized a highly water-soluble microneedle patch composed of PVP and sodium bicarbonate. This microneedle patch dissolves extremely rapidly with only the application time of 10 s, thereby enabling rapid drug delivery, while maintaining relatively stable performance during storage under ambient conditions [93]. Zhang et al. selected PVP as the matrix material for soluble microneedles due to its excellent water solubility and film-forming properties, ensuring the mechanical strength of the needle tips and rapid tissue penetration. After the microneedles penetrate the gingiva, the PVP matrix dissolves within approximately 10 min, enabling painless, minimally invasive drug particle delivery and leaving the drug-loaded polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) particles in situ for sustained release [94].

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)

As a widely applied synthetic hydrophilic polymer, PVA stands out for its unique structural tunability and flexible fabrication pathways that support diverse biomedical hydrogel construction. [52,95,96]. Hydrogels microneedles made from PVA can effectively penetrate the skin stratum corneum, avoiding the problems of breakage and insufficient penetration depth commonly associated with traditional micro-needles. At the same time, they act gently on the skin, causing no severe irritation or inflammatory reactions, and are suitable for people with different skin types [97]. Freeze-thaw cycles are the most common method for forming PVA hydrogels, but the hydrogels produced by this method have relatively low strength [98,99]. Zhang et al. prepared the hydrogel using three freeze-thaw cycles, forming a basic network through hydrogen bonding. The needle tip was then immersed in a 1 M Na2SO4 solution for 24 h; the salting-out effect enhanced the needle tip's strength, ensuring its penetration into the skin [100]. Ahmed et al. prepared PVA-based hydrogels using a triple strategy involving freeze-thaw, densification, and cross-linking. Through hydrogen bonding and metal-oxygen coordination, the resulting hydrogels exhibit ultra-high strength, toughness, dynamic reversibility, and fatigue resistance [101].

Polyethylene glycol (PEG)

PEG exhibits excellent biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, and prolonged circulation time by inhibiting clearance. Besides, it is highly stable and possesses chemical properties to allow for flexible modification [[102], [103], [104]]. These characteristics enable PEG to be widely used in various forms, such as blocks, solid films, and hydrogels (as a matrix for controlled drug delivery and as a cell delivery carrier to promote tissue regeneration) [105,106]. PEG modification includes three categories: physical conjugation, chemical conjugation, and self-assembly. PEG and its functional derivatives can serve as excellent base materials and modifiers for the fabrication of microneedles. PEG-based polymers are biodegradable and processable. Their degradation products are small-molecule ethylene glycol units that can be metabolized by the human body, posing no risk of tissue toxicity or residual contamination [[107], [108], [109]]. When preparing microneedles using polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) as the core raw material, the cross-linking polymerization process triggered by ultraviolet light allows for precise control over the morphological uniformity and aspect ratio of the microneedles. The resulting PEGDA microneedles can penetrate the skin stratum corneum [110]. Moreover, PEGDA microneedles exhibit excellent loading capacity for active peptide drugs, and effectively preserve drug activity and enable targeted transdermal delivery, which have demonstrated excellent therapeutic outcomes in the treatment of keloids [111].

Composite materials

Zerbinati et al. investigated a novel composite hydrogel developed by cross-linking HA with PEG. Through chemical characterization, rheological analysis, and histological validation, it demonstrated that the composite hydrogel exhibits good biocompatibility and excellent chemical and mechanical properties, and that the PEG cross-linking technique prolongs the hydrogel degradation time in vivo [112]. Niu et al. developed a novel hydrogel material using SF and PVA for the delivery of hydrophobic drugs and tissue engineering. SF can form covalent disulfide bonds through horseradish peroxidase (HRP)/hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) catalysis, constructing the three-dimensional network framework of hydrogel and providing a fundamental scaffold. The hydrophilic groups (hydroxyl groups) of PVA form hydrogen bonds with water or SF chains, while the hydrophobic groups (polyethylene chains) provide binding sites for the drug. The SF network ensures long-term stability, and the slow dissolution of PVA chains enables controlled degradation. This approach overcomes the performance limitations of single materials and achieves the integration of biological functions with material properties [113]. Zheng et al. utilized HA as the drug-loaded hydrogel tip and PVA as the flexible support matrix, loading ε-poly-L-lysine (EPL) to prepare bilayer peelable composite hydrogel microneedles. By leveraging the rapid dissolution of HA upon contact with interstitial fluid within 90 s to release EPL, the composite hydrogel microneedles achieved targeted antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects against acne [114].

Brief summary

The choice of tip material for HMNs is a key factor determining their mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and drug-loading capacity. GelMA, HA and SF, which are derived from natural sources, exhibit excellent bioactivity and degradation behavior to well accelerate tissue regeneration. However, their inherent mechanical properties are insufficient, requiring cross-linking or composite modification to enhance structural stability. SA offers significant advantages in terms of injective molding, but its resistance to enzymatic degradation in vivo limits its use in long-term implants. Synthetic polymers such as PVA and PEG possess a wider range of adjustable mechanical properties and greater structural stability, but they usually suffer from the drawbacks of being biologically inert and difficult to degrade [[115], [116], [117]]. In this section, numerous hydrogel materials for preparing HMNs, including natural polymers such as CS, gelatin and HA, as well as synthetic polymers such as PVA and PVP, are in detail presented and their structural features, properties, and modification methods are systematacially demonstrated. Properties and applications of needle-tip substrate materials.

The function of the backing layer

The backing layer of HMNs serves as the key structural component and connects the microneedle array to the external force-application device. Its primary functions include the mechanical support supply, drug delivery regulation, and ensured biocompatibility [151]. Moreover, it endows the microneedle array with sufficient mechanical strength to prevent the microneedles from bending or breaking during skin penetration, while its elasticity or adhesiveness allows it to adhere closely to the skin, thereby reducing drug leakage [152].

For the selection of materials, the backing layer must not only balance mechanical properties and biocompatibility, but also be chosen based on the intended application scenario. Backing layer materials with excellent biocompatibility and degradability are suitable for short-term drug delivery scenarios. Materials with greater mechanical stability and controllable degradation rates are more suitable for sustained release applications or scenarios requiring high-intensity penetration [153]. Xu et al. prepared a bilayer microneedle patch loaded with young fibroblast-derived exosomes (Y-EXO) using a spray-freeze-drying method. The base layer utilized PVA as the core material, which formed a bilayer structure with the HAMA needle tips through UV cross-linking, with the junction exhibiting an ultra-thin, easily separable morphology. The PVA backing layer possesses a flexible film-like structure that can conform to any skin contour and provides stable mechanical support for the 800-μm-tall needle tips, thus ensuring effective penetration from the epidermis to the dermis [154].

Some backing layers can serve as an extended carrier for drug reservoirs, and by controlling the material swelling rate or degradation characteristics, they enable sustained drug release while also isolate external contaminants and reduce the risk of skin infection. Shao et al. used water-soluble HA as the substrate to fabricate the backing layer, constructing a reservoir structure filled with a mixture of iron powder-based self-heating materials to provide the material basis for the patch's self-heating function. The outer layer of the backing layer is encapsulated in a composite structure consisting of a hydrophobic PE film and a porous PU film, which can isolate external water and oxygen. Upon removal of the PE film, the inner porous PU membrane allows air to slowly permeate into the reservoir within the backing layer, triggering a mild exothermic oxidation reaction of the iron powder to achieve on-demand activation of the self-heating effect. The entire backing layer integrates reservoir, self-heating, protective, heat-conducting, and drug-delivery functions [155]. Fan et al. prepared a multifunctional microneedle patch using silk fibroin‐methacryloyl (SilMA) as the substrate loaded with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). The mechanical stability and biocompatibility of SilMA enable it to serve as a wound dressing. The incorporated AgNPs block the invasion of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli into the wound, imparting antimicrobial properties to the entire substrate [156]. Hu et al. used the zwitterionic poly (sulfobetaine methacrylate) (SBMA) as a matrix, incorporating asiaticoside (AS) and hair particles (HMP) to form an AS-HMP/SBMA composite system capable of providing mechanical support to the needle tip. Under near-infrared light (NIR) irradiation, the HMP in the backing layer generates a mild thermal effect that inhibits bacterial growth at the wound site and enhances local vascular permeability. Furthermore, centella asiatica glycosides and HMP exhibit a synergistic effect, promoting granulation tissue growth and collagen deposition to accelerate skin regeneration [157].

Preparation of hydrogel microneedles

Mechanisms of hydrogel formation

In the preparation of HMNs, the cross-linking method determines whether the microneedles can achieve key functions such as skin barrier penetration, stable drug loading, and targeted controlled release. Therefore, the cross-linking method influences the performance of HMNs in biomedical applications such as drug delivery, tissue repair, and biosensing. The core structure of HMNs consists of a three-dimensional network framework formed by cross-linking hydrophilic polymer chains [46]. This framework can retain a large amount of water, mimicking the microenvironment of natural tissues, and be engineered to respond specifically to external stimuli (such as temperature, pH, enzymes, and glucose) by adjusting the cross-linking method. The appropriate selection of cross-linking methods is a key prerequisite for achieving a synergistic balance between the three-dimensional network structure, physicochemical properties, and biological functions. From the perspective of the fundamental principles, the cross-linking of HMNs primarily falls into two categories: physical cross-linking and chemical cross-linking [158,159]. These two methods form network structures through different action mechanisms, providing different unique advantages and limitations, and are capable of meeting diverse preparation requirements and application scenarios.

Physical cross-linking

Physical crosslinking of hydrogels is a process that constructs a three-dimensional network structure through non-covalent interactions. Without the use of chemical crosslinking agents, this approach helps reduce the toxicity of the system while offering both biosafety and dynamic responsiveness, making it widely applicable in fields such as biomedicine and flexible materials. The core cross-linking mechanisms primarily include hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic association, cyclic freeze-thawing, and electrostatic interactions [160,161], corresponding to distinct material systems and performance characteristics [162,163].

As a weak chemical bond, hydrogen bonding is one of the most common mechanisms and can be formed by electrostatic interactions between atoms with high electronegativity and hydrogen atoms, thus serving as a physical cross-linking mechanism based on noncovalent interactions [164]. Two or more polymers or materials with compatible molecular structures can spontaneously self-assemble into a three-dimensional network structure through intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions (Fig. 3A) [165]. Hydrogen bonding is also a key noncovalent interaction that is ubiquitous in the physically cross-linked networks of HMNs. Natural polymers such as chitosan and gelatin rely on intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds to gel with good biocompatibility under mild conditions [170].

The hydrophobic association mechanism primarily involves the interactions between hydrophobic groups on polymer chains in an aqueous environment [171]. Gong et al. prepared HMNs for vaginal wound repair by combining hydrogen bonding in PVA with hydrophobic interactions formed by the β-sheet structure of SF. Hydrogen bonding served as the primary physical cross-linking force, while hydrophobic interactions provided stability, enhancing the mechanical stability of the material in a hydrated state and slowing the rate at which water disrupts the hydrogen bonds between PVA and SF (Fig. 3B). Force-displacement testing of the microneedle array validated the mechanical properties of the material and cross-linking method (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, significant anti-inflammatory therapeutic effects were achieved in vivo, with a marked inhibitory effect on the expression of IL-6 as a key pro-inflammatory factor in vaginal wounds (Fig. 3D) [166].

In the freeze-thaw cycle process, the polymer solution freezes at low temperatures, causing water molecules to form ice crystals. The polymer chains are displaced from the ice crystal regions and aggregate into physical cross-linking points. Upon subsequent thawing, the polymer chains redissolve and form strong hydrogen bonding interactions, further stabilizing the cross-linked structure (Fig. 3E).) Through multiple freeze-thaw cycles, a stable three-dimensional network structure can gradually form, thereby enabling the preparation of hydrogels [167]. The primary polymers include polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and gelatin to form freezing-thawing gels. Chen et al. applied this method to develop the hydrogel microneedles for the treatment of diabetic wounds. A PVA/HA mixture loaded with Au2Pd3 nanozymes was injected into a mold, frozen at −20°C for 12 h, then thawed at room temperature for 4 h, and this cycle was repeated three times to form HMNs. Under near-infrared light at 808 nm, the nanozymes generate localized high temperatures to kill bacteria and accelerate enzymatic catalytic reactions (Fig. 3F), thereby lowering blood glucose levels and promoting wound healing [168].

Electrostatic interaction is a method for constructing hydrogel networks by utilizing the electrostatic attraction between charged molecules or groups. Through the charge attraction between cationic and anionic components, it is possible to achieve molecular chain cross-linking, phase separation control, or structural stabilization [172]. Ouyang et al. utilized the complementary charge interactions between cationic CS and anionic Sargassum polysaccharide (SFP) to achieve physical crosslinking, thereby constructing HMNs with high mechanical strength and excellent adhesion properties to plastics, glass, and pig skins (Fig. 3G) [169]. These microneedles also exhibit high hygroscopicity and pH-responsive drug release capabilities. What's more, physical cross-linking is a reversible process and cross-linking sites can dissociate and reform again. In short, due to good biocompatibility, mild, solvent-free conditions without chemical crosslinkers or photoinitiators and reversible nature, physical crosslinking methods to construct HMNs can allow for controlled drug release, biodegradability, and responsiveness to physiological stimuli such as pH, temperature, or ionic strength. These corresponding formed microneedles are ideal for transdermal delivery of sensitive biomacromolecules (e.g., peptides, proteins, or vaccines), on-demand therapy triggered by skin microenvironment changes, and minimally invasive biosensing where avoidance of chemical residues is critical.

Chemical cross-linking

Chemical cross-linking is the process of linking hydrophilic polymer chains via covalent bonds to form a three-dimensional network structure [173]. Compared to physical crosslinking, the covalent bonds formed by chemical crosslinking are stronger and enable the hydrogel to maintain structural integrity even under repeated swelling, mechanical stress, or in complex environments. This overcomes the drawback of physically crosslinked hydrogels, which are prone to network dissociation due to external factors such as temperature and pH [174]. In the preparation of HMNs, the primary methods include radical polymerization crosslinking, Schiff-base reaction crosslinking, and boric acid ester bond [175,176].

In chemical cross-linking, radical polymerization is a commonly used method for preparing HMNs, in which covalent bonds are formed between polymer chains through radical reactions, resulting in a three-dimensional network structure [177]. Lin and colleagues prepared microporous HMNs for scar treatment. By triggering the radical polymerization reaction of the acrylate double bonds (C=C) in PEGDA molecules via UV irradiation, PLGA was uniformly dispersed within the cross-linked network, jointly forming a covalently cross-linked network. The CaCO3 particles within it occupied space during microneedle fabrication with a following acid etching, thus resulting in microneedles with a microporous structure (Fig. 4A). This enhanced the mechanical strength and degradability of the microneedles. Furthermore, quantitative analysis of scar area via H&E staining after 14 days of scar treatment demonstrated a significant therapeutic effect (Fig. 4B) [178].

The Schiff-base reaction is a condensation reaction between an aldehyde or ketone and a primary or secondary amine under acidic or neutral conditions, resulting in the formation of a Schiff-base compound containing an imine bond (C=N) [181]. Lu et al. synthesized two functionalized HA derivatives based on HA through chemical modification, and then used them as the two reactive monomers for cross-linking. An equimolar mixture of HA was modified with hydrazide groups (HA-ADH), which contains a branched hydrazine group (-NH-NH2), and HA-QA-ALD contains a quaternary ammonium groups (QA) and an aldehyde group (-CHO). Then, the hydrazide groups of HA-ADH reacted with the aldehyde groups of HA-QA-ALD via a Schiffer-base reaction to form imine bonds (-CH=N-NH-). Through the covalent bonding of these imine bonds, the two HA derivatives cross-linked with each other, ultimately forming three-dimensional networked HA-QA HMNs (Fig. 4C), and as evidenced by the force-displacement curves of the microneedles, the microneedles formed via Schiff-base crosslinking exhibit excellent mechanical properties and can effectively penetrate the epidermal layer of the skin (Fig. 4D) [179].

Covalent cross-linking via ester bonds is achieved through esterification or ester exchange reactions and is a type of condensation reaction. In an esterification reaction, a compound containing a carboxyl group (-COOH) reacts with a compound containing a hydroxyl group (-OH) under specific conditions; water molecules are eliminated to form an ester bond (-COO-), thereby linking different molecular chains via covalent bonds [177]. Chen et al. used citric acid (CA) as a crosslinking agent and upon heating, CA molecules undergo intramolecular dehydration to form cyclic anhydrides, which rapidly reacts with the hydroxyl groups (-OH) on the polysaccharide chains of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) to form monoester bonds, thereby constructing a three-dimensional hydrogel network (Fig. 4E). The esterification reaction was promoted by temperature control, and the resulting CA/CMC solution was poured into a mold to form the HMNs after multiple centrifugations to ensure a full filling of the solution. FTIR spectroscopy of the prepared microneedles revealed a characteristic peak at 1710-1730 cm−1, which directly corresponds to the carbonyl stretching vibration of the ester bond, confirming the occurrence of the esterification reaction (Fig. 4F) [180].

Chemical cross-linking enhances flexibility and spatiotemporal precision during the gelation process and helps stabilize the hydrogel matrix, thus endowing the hydrogel microneedles with robust mechanical strength and stable network structures. Besides, this strategy allows precise control over the crosslinking density, enabling tunable drug release kinetics and excellent shape fidelity even for high-aspect-ratio microneedles, and meanwhile, the covalent bonds can resist premature degradation, ensuring prolonged and predictable delivery of therapeutics. Based on the advantages, chemically crosslinked HMNs are particularly suitable for delivery of small-molecule drugs, antibiotics, or growth factors requiring sustained release, as well as for applications demanding high mechanical insertion efficiency, such as on thick or tough skin. Although chemical crosslinking typically involves crosslinkers or photoinitiators that may require careful removal and reasonable option to maintain biocompatibility, this method is widely adopted because of its robustness and versatility in controlled-release systems [182].

Methods for preparing hydrogel microneedles

The performance of HMNs depends not only on material selection but also on the fabrication process, and the choice of process is inextricably linked to the used materials to form the hydrogel [183]. The choice of preparation method must consider the structural characteristics, functional requirements, mass production conditions, and application scenarios of the target microneedles, while also balance key factors such as material compatibility, production cost, and operational complexity. Currently, the primary methods for preparing HMNs include micro-molding, 3D printing, and in-situ formation techniques.

Micro-molding

The micro-molding method involves a pouring process of prepared hydrogel solution into a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold and a filling process via centrifugation or vacuum to remove air bubbles from the liquid, thereby preventing the formation of voids in the microneedles. This ensures that the hydrogel solution completely saturates the fine cavities of the mold, guaranteeing the accurate replication of structures such as the needle tips and shafts [25]. Finally, after curing and drying, the microneedles are obtained (Fig. 5A).

Mold preparation is a critical step in determining the precision of microneedle morphology. First, silicon-based or metal master molds are fabricated using micro-and nanofabrication techniques such as photolithography and femtosecond laser processing to etch predetermined structures, such as conical or pyramidal shapes-into the microneedle arrays [187]. PDMS is then poured onto the surface of the master mold, cured, and peeled off to obtain a reusable negative mold with excellent elasticity. To prevent distortion of the microneedle tips during fabrication, it is common practice to add the hydrogel precursor solution in multiple stages and remove air bubbles from the mold by evacuating the air or centrifuging the mixture in a bench-top centrifuge. The micro-molding method for fabricating microneedles is well-established and offers low production costs, consistent performance, and ease of scaling up production [23]. Making it the current mainstream approach for microneedle fabrication. By following the structural replication-material forming approach, the biocompatibility of hydrogels can be combined with the minimally invasive properties of microneedles.

Zhao et al. proposed a novel strategy for the treatment of HS based on supramolecular self-assembly. By leveraging host-guest interactions between cucurbituril-7 (CB [7]), dihydroartemisinin (DHA), and gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), the CB [7]‐AuNCs‐DHA nanoparticles (CAD) supramolecular nanoparticles were constructed, and then were loaded into GelMA HMNs via micromolding for transdermal delivery. By synergistically inducing ferrocytosis and apoptosis, the treatment overcomes the apoptosis resistance of hypertrophic scar fibroblasts. In a rabbit ear model, treatment for just three weeks significantly improved scar appearance and reduced scar thickness, while demonstrating good biocompatibility [188].

3D printing technology

Micro-molding imposes extremely high demands on physical molds and prolonged use can lead to mold wear, thus resulting in reduced precision of the produced needle tips. In contrast, 3D printing technology enables the rapid and high-precision fabrication of MN prototypes, including complex geometries and sharp needle tips, thereby minimizing structural changes caused by mold wear [189] and providing an efficient and precise solution for the preparation of functional hydrogel-based microneedle devices. Concretely, 3D printing technology is based on the principle of layer-by-layer printing and additive manufacturing. By directly controlling the size, shape, and array configuration of microneedles through digital models, it eliminates the need for molds and significantly shortens the research and development cycle [190]. Common 3D printing methods include digital light processing (DLP) and direct ink writing (DIW) [191].

Digital Light Processing (DLP) printing uses a digital micromirror device (DMD) to project UV light in specific patterns, causing the photoresin to undergo a polymerization reaction in the corresponding areas, thereby curing layer by layer to form a three-dimensional structure. Zhang et al. mixed HAMA with a GelMA solution and added the photoinitiator lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP). Using a digital micromirror device chip, a 405-nm UV light was projected onto the surface of photosensitive bio-ink in a cross-sectional pattern corresponding to a predefined 3D model, causing the ink to form a photopolymerize layer by layer and ultimately stack into a complete MN patch structure. Direct printing driven by digital models avoids some issues such as needle tip blunting and breakage caused by insufficient precision during mold demolding (Fig. 5B) [184].

Direct Ink Write (DIW) printing involves a mixing of a polymer solution with nano-silica particles to form a composite ink that exhibits yield stress behavior [192]. The composite ink is extruded from a nozzle via a pneumatic extrusion system, with the nozzle moving along a predetermined path. When the nozzle approaches the substrate, the ink is extruded and adheres to the substrate. Subsequently, the nozzle is lifted upward, causing the ink to form a liquid bridge via capillary action. The liquid bridge continuously contracts until the neck ruptures, forming a microneedle structure. Li et al. abandoned the typical layer-by-layer deposition of conventional 3D printing. Instead, they extruded the nano-composite ink from a sub-millimeter nozzle via a single-step pneumatic extrusion process. Combined with a vertically controlled pulling motion, they utilized the rheological properties of ink to form microneedles with tips approximately 5 μm in diameter. This approach achieved a resolution of two orders of magnitude higher than the nozzle size, and individual microneedles could be formed in a single step, significantly improving fabrication efficiency (Fig. 5C) [185].

3D printing enables precise, layer-by-layer construction of HMNs with complex geometries (e.g., pyramidal, conical, taper angle, or barbed structures) and customized arrays (e.g., density of microneedles) based on the physiological characteristics of different administration sites, which are difficult to obtain by traditional molding methods. Besides, this technology provides exceptional design flexibility, rapid prototyping, and high reproducibility, allowing on-demand tuning of needle height, spacing, and mechanical properties without the need for physical molds. It also can facilitate the integration of multiple materials or drug payloads within a single microneedle patch, thereby further enhancing the efficiency and safety of transdermal drug delivery. Hence, this technology demonstrates tremendous potential for applications in the fields of personalised medicine, precision therapy, fabrication of microneedles with gradient or multi-layered drug release profiles, and rapid iteration of wearable microdevices for biosensing or vaccination campaigns.

In-situ molding technology

In-situ molding technology refers to a preparation method in which hydrogel precursor materials are directly applied to the skin or a carrier surface, where they gel in situ upon exposure to an external stimuli to form microneedle structures [193]. With its core advantages of on-demand shaping and precise adaptation, this approach has become a key direction in hydrogel microneedle fabrication. By combining the inherent properties of the material with process control, it enables the morphological construction and functional activation of microneedles in specific scenarios, significantly enhancing transdermal drug delivery and biosensing performance. Furthermore, it provides a method for preparing hydrogels with specific spatial configurations and functional characteristics without the need for additional shaping or post-processing [194].

Hydrogels formed in situ through a sol-gel transition can dynamically conform to biological surfaces, resolving the mechanical mismatch between rigid electronic devices and flexible biological tissues, and significantly improving contact performance and signal acquisition quality.

[195]. Zheng et al. developed a core technology to address the shortcomings of first-generation cryomicroneedles-specifically, including their reliance on cold-chain storage and transportation and the inability to load cells in situ. Using a porous hydrogel microneedle scaffold that can be stored and transported at room temperature as a carrier, they employed a one-step impregnation method to load a cell suspension containing a low-toxicity cryoprotectant via capillary forces. Upon freezing, the scaffold solidifies in situ into cryomicroneedles, which is very suitable for intradermal delivery. This technology prevents cell damage and contamination during long-distance transport, eliminates reliance on the cold chain, allows for flexible loading of autologous or allogeneic cells, and enables the effective intradermal delivery of various therapeutic cells, thus representing a technological breakthrough and an enhancement in clinical applicability (Fig. 5D) [186].

Stimuli-responsive hydrogel microneedles

Stimuli-responsive microneedles can achieve controlled drug release in response to specific physiological conditions (e.g., hyperglycemia or acidic environments) or external stimuli (e.g., light and heat). Based on the nature of the trigger, stimuli-responsive mechanisms can be broadly classified into two categories: endogenous responses and exogenous responses [[196], [197], [198], [199]].

Endogenous responses

Endogenous responses depend on the intrinsic characteristics of the body physiological microenvironment. The common endogenous stimuli signals primarily include pH [200], reactive oxygen species (ROS) [201,202], glucose [203,204], and other physiology-related signals [205,206]. This section primarily introduces the action mechanisms, signal recognition, and transduction processes of endogenous stimuli responses, as well as their typical applications and regulatory principles in skin disease field.

pH-responsiveness

pH-responsive hydrogels are polymer networks capable of sensing changes in environmental pH, primarily through the protonation and deprotonation of ionizable groups in the polymer chains and pH-responsive dynamic covalent bonds (e.g., Schiff-base bond) [207]. These hydrogels hold particular value in the field of targeted drug delivery. pH-responsive polymers functionalized with acidic groups behave as anionic polymers after deprotonation under alkaline conditions. Polymers containing basic groups behave as cationic polymers once protonated under acidic conditions (Fig. 6A) [208]. Significant differences in the pH environment exist across different parts of the body (such as the stomach, intestines, and tumor tissues), which provides natural conditions for pH-triggered release.

Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework-8 (ZIF-8) is a metal-organic framework (MOF) formed by the self-assembly of divalent zinc ions (Zn2+) and 2-methylimidazole ligands via Zn-N coordination bonds. It features a zeolite-like topological structure, high crystallinity, high specific surface area, and a uniform porous structure [216,217]. ZIF-8 exhibits pH-responsive degradability and under neutral or weakly alkaline conditions, the coordination bonds remain stable and the framework intact. Under weakly acidic conditions, the imidazole ligands undergo protonation, and the Zn-N coordination is disrupted, which leads to rapid framework degradation and the release of the loaded drug, enabling precise response to acidic pathological microenvironments such as tumors and infected wounds to achieve smart controlled release [218]. Jimo et al. prepared pH-responsive Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP)-loaded astragaloside IV (As) ZIF-8 nanoparticle microneedle patches (BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs) for wound healing. These patches utilise pH changes during different stages of wound healing to achieve controlled release of As, and the synergistic therapeutic effects (Fig. 6B), effectively promoting wound healing and strongly inhibiting scar formation. Within 48 h of drug release, the release rate reached 76% under acidic conditions, but the value is only 25% under neutral conditions (Fig. 6C) [209].

The Schiff-base bond is a pH-responsive dynamic covalent bond formed by the dehydration condensation of an amino group and an aldehyde group (Fig. 6D), which can undergo protonation and cleavage in a weakly acidic environment, leading to the responsive drug release [210]. Jiang et al. developed a three-in-one core-shell bilayer pH-responsive microneedle for wound healing. The core is loaded with lactoferrin-based nanoparticles (LFT NPs) and covalently conjugated with the porphyrin sonosensitizer and its positive charge allows it to target negatively charged bacteria, and upon ultrasound, the ROS is effectively triggered and generated for rapid bactericidal action and biofilm disruption. The tip layer consists of a gel network formed by a Schiff-base bonds (-CH=N-) cross-linking between methacrylated-modified chitosan (CSMA) and spermidine derivative (SPDTA) loaded with the anti-inflammatory agent spermidine (SPD), exhibiting an adjustable pH-responsiveness. In the acidic stage of a wound, the Schiff-base bonds rapidly break down, and SPD is released to achieve sequential immune regulation and anti-inflammatory effects. In a neutral environment, the Schiff-base bonds stay stable, and the hydrogel exhibits a relatively slow degradation accompanied by sustained slow drug release (Fig. 6E). [211].

ZIF-8 is a crystalline nano-MOF carrier formed by the coordination of Zn2+ with 2-methylimidazole and it is structurally stable under neutral conditions (pH = 7.4). In the weakly acidic environment of a wound (pH 5.5-6.5), the Zn-N coordination bond breaks irreversibly, enabling pH-targeted drug release. The released zinc ions exert antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and wound-regenerative effects, and can also synergise with phototherapy to inhibit bacterial growth. The drawbacks are that the nanopowder is difficult to mold on its own, and excessive zinc release under acidic conditions can easily induce cytotoxicity. The Schiff base is a dynamic, reversible covalent bond formed by the condensation of an aldehyde group and an amino group. The Schiff base-crosslinked hyaluronic acid hydrogel is structurally most stable in a weakly acidic environment (pH 3-4). Upon exposure to alkali, it hydrolyses into a sol, which can be restored to a gel upon re-acidification. It offers the advantages of being injectable, rapidly gel-forming in situ and self-healing, making it suitable as a wound support matrix. However, the Schiff base-crosslinked hydrogel lacks inherent anti-infective and tissue repair capabilities, and its hydrolysis products exhibit a certain level of cytotoxicity.

Glucose-responsiveness

Glucose-responsive hydrogels undergo reversible structural phase transitions in response to changes in glucose concentration [219]. Currently, there are three main approaches to achieve the responsive mechanism: glucose oxidase (GOx), phenylboronic acid (PBA), and Concanavalin A (Con A) [204].

GOx is a natural enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of glucose into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide (Fig. 6F), whilst simultaneously altering the local osmotic pressure. This process contains three mechanisms: (1) regulating the ionisation of macromolecules and electrostatic interactions by acidification; (2) cleavaging the chemical bonds or restructuring the dynamic coordination cross-links by hydrogen peroxide oxidation; (3) solute concentration gradients-induced water absorption and swelling, which can alter the intermolecular forces and cross-linking density of the hydrogel, ultimately resulting in structural changes such as shrinkage and hardening, or swelling and degradation [203,212,219]. Xuan et al. developed a lysozyme amyloid fibrils ceria nanozymes (Lys-AFs-Ceria)-enhanced microneedle system that co-loads GOx into a polyphenol hydrogel. Through a cascade of catalytic synergies with superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), this system can effectively mediate glucose consumption, thereby achieving hypoglycemia, alleviating hypoxia, and reducing local glucose concentrations (Fig. 6G) [213].

PBA is a class of boron-containing compounds with unique responsiveness, which can form a cyclic phenylboronic ester, combined with the ortho-dihydroxy groups of glucose. This reaction proceeds spontaneously under mild conditions, forming a three-dimensional network hydrogel (Fig. 6H) [214], In high-glucose environments, competitive binding leads to a decrease in crosslinking density and network relaxation, triggering drug release; in low-glucose conditions, the network re-crosslinks, thus enabling closed-loop regulation [220]. Besides, as a dual responsive functional group, glucose-responsive PBA is typically combined with pH responsiveness. Yu et al. developed a PBA-based glucose-responsive microneedle insulin patch by introducing a dimethylamino ethyl acrylate (DMAEA) basic unit. The complex is stabilized by the electrostatic attraction between the protonated groups of DMAEA and the boronate ester. Under physiological pH conditions, PBA forms a cyclic boronate ester (Fig. 6I). When blood glucose levels rise, PBA reversibly binds to the cis-1,2-diol of glucose, forming a negatively charged glucose-boronate ester complex. This synergistic effect significantly increases the negative charge density of the polymer matrix, and weakens the electrostatic interactions between the matrix and insulin, thus promoting rapid insulin release [215]. Con A is a tetrameric metalloprotein isolated from sword beans, and specifically binds to glucose [221]. Glucose can competitively bind to Con A, leading to the release of insulin [222]. However, due to the immunogenicity of Con A, it may trigger adverse immune reactions and therefore, it is rarely used.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsiveness

As a typical and important endogenous response, ROS-responsive hydrogels are smart materials capable of sensing and responding to changes in ROS concentration, thus attracting extensive attention in designing and constructing stimul-responsive hydrogel materials and microneedles. By introducing ROS-sensitive chemical bonds into the hydrogel network, oxidative cleavage occurs under high ROS conditions, and the gel network can undergo a disintegration, swelling, or hydrophilic/hydrophobic transition process, thereby triggering drug release [201]. Common ROS-sensitive dynamic covalent bonds include thioether bonds, Thioketal (TK) bonds, PBA, and diselenium bonds [202,223].

A thioether bond is a chemical bond formed by a sulfur atom linked to two carbon atoms via single bonds; it is the sulfur-containing analogue of an ether bond and is easily oxidized by ROS to form a sulfinyl or sulfonyl group under oxidative conditions (Fig. 7A) [224]. Maria et al. developed a thiol-based ROS-responsive functional monomer using ethylene glycol/thioether acrylate (EG) and prepared hydrogels via 4D printing. In the presence of ROS, the thioether groups are oxidized to form sulfoxides and sulfones, enabling a transition from hydrophobic to hydrophilic molecules without the need for chemical bond cleavage. The oxidation-induced swelling of the thioether groups significantly enhances the drug release rate, with the release amount increasing by a factor of 2 compared with non-oxidized conditions (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the needle-like printed structure increases the drug release amount by a factor of 9, allowing for the regulation of sustained-release effects [225].

TK is a chemical bond formed when two sulfur atoms are linked to a carbon backbone. In environments with high levels of ROS, oxidative cleavage occurs, triggering carrier dissociation and drug release [202]. Zhang et al. utilized the TK bond as a ROS-responsive moiety to construct a ROS-responsive Asiatic acid/PEG2000-TK-PLGA5000 nanoparticles loaded dissolving microneedles (AA/PTP MN). This system achieves highly effective treatment of HS by remodeling the pathological microenvironment of the scar. The high-level ROS environment in HS can cause the TK bond to break, leading to the disintegration of the nanoparticles and the release of asiatic acid (AA). The in vitro ROS scavenging experiments using human hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HSFb) demonstrated that both free AA and AA/PTP nanoparticles effectively scavenged ROS, and the antioxidant effect of AA/PTP nanoparticles was far superior to that of free AA at the same concentration, exhibiting a clear dose-dependent relationship (Fig. 7C) [226].

PBA can also respond to ROS, but the mechanism differs fundamentally from that of glucose response. PBA can undergo reversible condensation with the cis-epiglycol form of glucose to form a cyclic boronate ester; the intramolecular Ar-B backbone remains intact, and the compound can be dissociated and restored to its original state by removing glucose or acidifying the solution, making it suitable for glucose-responsive insulin delivery systems. In contrast, reactive oxygen species such as hydrogen peroxide can undergo an irreversible oxidative deboronation reaction with PBA. Following a nucleophilic attack by the hydroperoxide ion on the boron atom, intramolecular aryl migration and rearrangement permanently sever the Ar-B covalent bond, causing the PBA backbone to decompose completely into phenol and boronic acid. This process is irreversible, endowing the material with the ability to degrade irreversibly in tumor and inflammatory microenvironments, as well as with lesion-targeted drug delivery properties [229,230].

As a multi-responsive functional group, phenylboronic acid can also respond to ROS, and unlike the mechanism for glucose response, the mechanism for ROS response in phenylboronic acid involves the cleavage of the boronate bond [229]. Zhang et al. used HA loaded with doxycycline (DOX) as the backing layer, and utilized aldehyde-modified hyaluronic acid (AHA) cross-linked with 3-aminophenylboronic acid (3-APBA) to form AHA-PBA, which was then cross-linked with PVA to create the hydrogel needle tip loaded with resveratrol (RES). The boronic acid group reacts with the ortho-hydroxyl group on PVA to form a dynamic boronate bond, resulting in the formation ROS-responsive hydrogel microneedles. At the site of a wound infection, large amounts of ROS (H2O2) are produced and causes the boronate bond to cleave, hence leading to the degradation of the hydrogel network and triggering the responsive release of RES to achieve antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 7D) [227].

A diselenide bond is a type of dynamic covalent bond formed by two selenium atoms linked via a covalent bond; under the oxidative action of ROS, the single bond between the two selenium atoms is oxidized and broken [231]. ROS-responsive hydrogels constructed based on diselenide bonds can maintain a stable three-dimensional network structure under normal physiological microenvironments. However, in lesion areas with high ROS levels, the selective cleavage of diselenide bonds, mediated by excessive H2O2 and hydroxyl radicals in the microenvironment, triggers the dynamic dissociation or degradation of the hydrogel network structure, thereby enabling the controlled release of loaded drugs, growth factors, or active ingredients [232]. Zhou et al. developed soluble microneedle patches by combining selenium-functionalized hyaluronic acid (SeHA) with shikonin-loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (SFN) loaded with purpurin, and then applied them to treating the psoriasis. This system can respond to the high ROS microenvironment in psoriatic lesions, and cause preferential cleavage of diselenium bonds in molecular chains, thereby achieving the ROS scavenging and controlled drug release (Fig. 7E) [228].

Exogenous responses

Exogenous responses, on the other hand, depend on externally applied physical or chemical stimuli. Common regulatory methods include light stimulation [233], electrical and magnetic stimulation [234,235], ultrasonic stimulation [236], and various other external intervention techniques [206,237]. This section provides a systematic discussion of the basic types, operating principles, and typical applications of exogenous stimuli responses, while outlining the response mechanisms and regulatory characteristics of different exogenous signals.

Light-responsiveness

Photo-responsive hydrogels are dynamic polymer networks that undergo spatially and temporally controllable changes in response to light stimulation [233]. Photo-responsive HMNs primarily include two types: photothermal-response (PTT) and photodynamic-response (PDT) [238].

PTT material can convert light energy into thermal energy through the photothermal conversion effect upon exposure to external light. As the temperature rises, thermosensitive structural changes occur, thereby enabling drug release [239]. Luan et al. designed a near-infrared-responsive bilayer hydrogel microneedle loaded with triptolide (TP) and paeoniflorin (Pae), and black phosphorus (BP) for the treatment of early-stage skin lesions in systemic sclerosis (SSc). The upper layer of the needle tip is loaded with TP and Pae, while the lower layer is loaded with BP. Upon irradiation with near-infrared light, BP absorbs the light and generates thermal energy (Fig. 8A), causing the change of hydrogel structure and drug release [240].

PDT refers to a hydrogel microneedle matrix loaded with a photosensitizer; upon irradiation with light of a specific wavelength, it generates ROS through a photodynamic reaction, thereby achieving therapeutic effects such as bactericidal, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory actions, while simultaneously triggering structural changes in the hydrogel to facilitate drug release [[245], [246], [247]]. Xie et al. addressed the issue of biofilm infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-positive bacteria in chronic diabetic wounds by preparing a microneedle patch loaded with the benzoxaborole (BOB)-functionalized photosensitizer TPI-BOB. Under white light irradiation, the photosensitizer generates a large amount of ROS (Fig. 8B), thereby killing and eliminating Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [241]. When applied to scar repair, the core mechanism of PDT relies on the synergistic action of photosensitizer-mediated photochemical reactions and the body's biological regulatory pathways, enabling the targeted inhibition of excessive scar tissue proliferation whilst simultaneously remodeling the tissue microenvironment in the affected area [248].

Electro/magnetically responsiveness

Electro-responsive hydrogels are smart polymeric materials that undergo rapid, reversible changes in volume, shape, mechanical strength, or permeability when exposed to an external electric field and they can primarily convert electrical energy into chemical or mechanical energy [249].

Electro-responsive microneedles are commonly used in wearable devices, and self-powered triboelectric generation is one of the typical power supply mechanisms in such devices. Conductive polymer-composite electro-responsive hydrogels serve as the core functional material system. These hydrogels incorporate conductive polymers into the hydrogel matrix, and can undergo redox reactions upon electrical stimulation, thereby influencing the structural behavior of the hydrogel network [250]. Zhang et al. designed self-powered triboelectric-responsive microneedles for the treatment and repair of intervertebral disc degeneration. By introducing conductive polymers such as polypyrrole (PPy) into the hydrogel network, the hydrogel is endowed with reversible electronic conductivity and electrochemical redox capabilities, generating different voltages under varying friction frequencies during movement (Fig. 8C). During the human movement, skin friction generates voltage, causing changes in the conjugated structure, charge distribution, ion migration, and electrostatic interactions of the conductive polymers. These changes alter the hydrogel network, leading to the release of exosomes that repair the intervertebral disc [242].

Magneto responsive hydrogels are constructed by incorporating magnetic materials into a biocompatible hydrogel matrix, allowing for remote control of their shape, mechanical strength, drug release, or bioelectrical signals via an external magnetic field [235]. Zhang et al. developed an orally administered magneto responsive microneedle robot for the delivery of macromolecules in the intestine. The hard magnetic particles (NdFeB) were incorporated into the microneedle substrate, and under the influence of a specific magnetic field, the microneedles can rotate in response to changes in the magnetic field (Fig. 8D). The microneedle robot can propel the needle tips into the small intestinal mucosa. After the connecting layer degrades, the magnetic substrate is guided by the magnetic field and is completely expelled from the body, leaving no residue behind. It is non-toxic and does not obstruct the intestines. This design has been demonstrated to effectively lower blood glucose levels in diabetic minipigs following oral insulin administration [243].

Ultrasonic-responsiveness

As intelligent HMNs that have emerged in recent years, ultrasonic-responsive HMNs can undergo reversible or irreversible changes in structure, mechanical properties, or chemical properties in response to external ultrasonic stimulation [251]. Xiang et al. developed an ultrasound-triggered soluble hyaluronic acid microneedle patch for the non-invasive treatment of propionibacterium acnes-induced acne. Zinc porphyrin-based metal-organic framework was combined with zinc oxide (ZnO) to form a ZnTCPPZnO heterostructure with highly efficient interfacial charge transfer. Once in vitro ultrasonic stimulation, the heterojunction significantly reduces the activation energy of oxygen, efficiently generating singlet oxygen (1O2), with antibacterial efficiencies of up to 99.73% and 99.64% against Propionibacterium acnes (P.acnes) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Concurrently, the slow release of zinc ions from the material upregulates genes related to DNA replication and zinc homeostasis in fibroblasts, promoting skin repair and the resolution of inflammation [252]. Xue et al. developed a wearable flexible ultrasound microneedle patch (wf-UMP) for synergistic immunotherapy of tumors, which integrates a lead-free flexible ultrasonic array, a bioadhesive hydrogel, and soluble microneedles loaded with piezoelectric nanoparticles. Under ultrasonic stimulation, 0.95K0.48Na0.52Nb0.97Sb0.03O30.05Bi0.5Na0.5ZrO3-0.2% Fe2O3 (mKNNs) can generate high-voltage electric fields efficiently producing ROS to achieve a killing of sonocatalytic tumor (Fig. 8E), induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), and simultaneously remodel the tumor immune microenvironment. When combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors, this system not only suppresses primary tumors, but also triggers distant effects and long-term immune memory, effectively inhibiting distant metastases and recurrence [244].

Briedf summary

Smart responsive HMNs can detect and respond multiple signals, including pH, temperature, light, ROS, and glucose, and achieve controllable structural and functional transformations [250]. Due to their precise recognition of environmental changes and adaptive regulatory capabilities, these materials demonstrate immense application potential in fields such as biomedicine, sensing and detection, and smart drug delivery. Different response modes exhibit significant differences in triggering conditions, kinetic behavior, and structural reversibility. Therapy mechanism of hydrogel microneedle for scar treatment

Currently, as a novel minimally invasive delivery platform, HMNs have demonstrated significant and wide applications across multiple fields due to their adjustable structure and customizable functions. Their key physicochemical properties, including swelling capacity, mechanical strength, degradation rate, and drug-loading capacity, directly determine their specific application potential in areas such as tissue engineering, transdermal drug delivery, biosensing, and wound healing. In the treatment of skin scars, this technology has also demonstrated significant therapeutic effects adn the mechanisms primarily involve the creation of physical microchannels, targeted drug delivery, modulation of the scar microenvironment, and enhancement of synergistic therapy. In this section, the four kinds of therapy methods, their features, and mechanisms are illustrated.

Physical and mechanical mechanisms

Collagen fibers in scar tissue are arranged in parallel and their spacing between collagen bundles in normal scars and HS is smaller than that in normal skin. This abnormal collagen structure forms a dense fibrous network, which not only reduces tissue elasticity but also impedes the penetration and exchange of drug molecules and nutrients [273,274]. Mechanical stimulation can modulate the formation of scar and the mechanical stress at the wound site is a key driver of scar formation, which can effectively reduce the scarring by stress shielding [275]. Besides, mechanical tension can regulate the metabolic reprogramming via yes-associated protein 1 (YAP) and transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) transcription factors, promote glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, thereby providing energy and raw materials for the proliferation and matrix synthesis of scar tissue [276]. After microneedle patches penetrate scar tissue, physical mechanical forces activate signaling molecules on the cell membrane, and then initiate multiple signaling pathways that regulate intracellular transcription factors, thereby inhibiting excessive collagen deposition and promoting normal extracellular matrix remodeling to achieve a scar repair (Fig. 9A) [19]. Under mechanical force, hydrogel microneedles can precisely penetrate the scar epidermis and superficial dermis, and directly disrupt the dense collagen network to form numerous microchannel structures within the scar tissue with diameters ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers. By precisely puncturing the skin, they induce the repair and collagen regeneration. With the physical mechanical effects of microchannel formation and localized micro-injury stimulation, the skin scars can be effectively treated. These microchannels not only provide physical pathways for subsequent substance exchange, significantly increasing the permeability of scar tissue, but also break the mechanical equilibrium of the local tissue, creating space for subsequent collagen remodeling [280,281]. Furthermore, the micro-injuries created by HMNs constitute a controlled trauma that activates the natural wound healing pathways of local tissues. These micro-injury signals stimulate the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, and induce moderate infiltration of inflammatory cells, which release various cytokines to initiate the repair process.

Drug delivery mechanisms

In addition to physical and mechanical means, the targeted drug delivery mechanisms have also important effect to facilitate skin scar repair for HMNs. Traditional topical anti-scar medications struggle to penetrate the dense barrier of scar tissue, resulting in extremely low bioavailability. While local injection therapy can increase drug concentration, and it is associated with significant trauma, uneven drug distribution, and a high risk of adverse reactions [282]. As a novel drug delivery vehicle, HMNs can load the anti-scar active ingredients into a hydrogel matrix. After the needle penetrates the scar tissue, the swelling and degradation properties of hydrogels within HMNs enable the slow release and local accumulation of the drug, effectively addressing the shortcomings of traditional administration methods [283]. Besides, the drug delivery way of HMNs is painless, safe, and convenient. They can serve as an alternative to traditional corticosteroid injections, providing a novel minimally invasive drug delivery approach for the treatment of HS. Anti-scarring drugs and bioactive factors delivered via HMNs primarily include corticosteroids, anticancer drugs, and active ingredients from traditional Chinese medicine.

Corticosteroids are commonly used anti-scarring agents in clinical practice and can promote scar resolution and symptom relief through multiple mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory effects, immune modulation, and inhibition of fibroblast proliferation [284]. Triamcinolone acetonide (TAC) and compound betamethasone (CB) are the most commonly used corticosteroids in clinical practice. However, clinical use of TAC may be associated with adverse reactions such as telangiectasia, skin atrophy, and pigmentary changes [285], while CB may cause pain at the injection site, subcutaneous atrophy, hypopigmentation or depigmentation (especially when injected into the lesion), and a risk of local infection. HMNs can deliver these drugs precisely to the dermal layer of the scar, preventing waste of the medication in the epidermal layer and reducing the incidence of adverse reactions such as skin atrophy and telangiectasia.

Anticancer drugs primarily target fibroblasts by inhibiting DNA synthesis, blocking cell proliferation, or interfering with cell differentiation, thereby suppressing excessive wound healing and scar hyperplasia. For example, 5-fluorouracil, bleomycin, and certain antimitotic agents can effectively curb the excessive proliferation of fibroblasts in scar tissue by inhibiting cellular DNA synthesis and division, demonstrating significant efficacy in the treatment of HS and keloids [[286], [287], [288]].

The active ingredients of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) represent an important research direction in the field of scar treatment. Their multi-target, multi-pathway mechanisms of action align closely with the complex pathological processes of scar formation, demonstrating unique application potential. Traditional Chinese medicine can inhibit scar formation by reducing inflammatory responses, suppressing fibroblast proliferation, inducing fibroblast apoptosis and autophagy, promoting extracellular matrix degradation, reducing angiogenesis, and inhibiting the cutaneous nervous system (Fig. 9B) [277]. Compared to chemical drugs, active compounds in Traditional Chinese medicine exhibit higher safety profiles and lower rates of adverse reactions, making them more suitable for long-term topical applications. They offer significant advantages, particularly in preventive interventions during the early stages of scar formation. Currently, research on the anti-scarring effects of active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicine has gradually expanded from in vitro experiments on single components to in vivo validation of compound formulations and the development of novel delivery systems. For example, loading asiaticoside (AS) and Tanshinone IIA onto carriers such as hydrogels, microspheres, and liposomes can significantly enhance their transdermal absorption and targeting, further improving anti-scarring efficacy [289].

Mechanisms of bioactivity regulation

Exosomes can serve as the core bioactive units in the field of cell-free therapy for PSs. By targeting and regulating cellular behavior and molecular pathways in the scar microenvironment, they can reverse the pathological inflammatory-fibrotic repair process at its root, thereby offering a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of refractory scars such as HS and keloids [290]. HMNs can treat PSs by loading them with bioactive factors such as exosomes. This approach primarily involves the exogenous introduction of bioactive units via a minimally invasive delivery method to precisely intervene in the cellular biological behavior and molecular signaling pathways within the scar microenvironment, thereby breaking the pathological inflammation-fibrosis repair cycle and guiding scar tissue toward physiological remodeling [291]. HMNs can precisely encapsulate anti-scar biofactors and cell-derived active carriers within the three-dimensional hydrogel network through gentle encapsulation, blending, or secondary loading. By utilizing the dermal channels formed by the minimally invasive puncture of the microneedle array, these active units are delivered to pathological targets within the scar, bypassing the barrier of the stratum corneum [292].

Lu et al. employed a core-shell bilayer structure, with the shell loaded with the anti-inflammatory small molecule mangiferin and the core loaded with human mesenchymal stromal cell (hMSC)-derived exosome (Fig. 9C). This differentiated drug delivery design utilizes the rapid release of mangiferin to achieve early anti-inflammatory effects, while the sustained-release of exosomes promotes angiogenesis and cell proliferation. In vitro experiments confirmed that this system induces macrophage polarization and enhances the formation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) tubes and cell migration. In vivo studies using a full-thickness skin defect model in mice demonstrated that it accelerates wound closure, promotes re-epithelialization, and significantly downregulates the expression of scar-related markers such as α-smooth muscle actin(α-SMA) and transforming growth factor-β(TGF-β), thereby achieving a scar-free skin regeneration [278].

The bioactive regulation mechanism of HMNs intervenes in the pathological process of scar formation at molecular, cellular, and tissue levels, effectively inhibiting fibrosis and promoting tissue remodeling. Chen et al. developed a core-shell structured layered HMN with a top layer of SA loaded with CaCO3 nanoparticles containing naringin (Nar) and a bottom layer of GelMA containing hypoxia-induced exosomes (EXO-H) derived from human umbilical vein endothelial cells. These microneedles can penetrate the eschar of burn wounds, and Nar exerts anti-inflammatory and ROS-scavenging effects through sustained release, while EXO-H promotes angiogenesis, thus achieving scar-free healing in a rat burn model [293].

Mechanisms of microenvironmental regulation

The development of scar tissue is closely related to changes in the local microenvironment and some factors such as reduced skin hydration, abnormal oxygen partial pressure, and pH disturbances collectively, promote the abnormal activation of fibroblasts and excessive collagen deposition [294,295]. HMNs can precisely modulate the local microenvironment of scars through their inherent properties and drug-delivery capabilities, thereby providing optimal conditions for tissue repair. Hydrogels possess excellent water-absorbing and water-retaining properties and upon insertion into scar tissue, they increase the local tissue hydration through swelling, thus improving the dry and hardened physical characteristics of the scar, while providing sufficient moisture for cellular metabolism and promoting tissue repair [296].

An adequate supply of oxygen can suppress the expression of hypoxia-inducible factors, and reduce fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. The microchannels formed by microneedles can promote gas exchange between scar tissue and the external environment, thereby increasing local oxygen partial pressure [297]. Yang et al. used HMNs loaded with responsive drugs to scavenge excess ROS and deplete abnormally expressed matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby restoring the homeostasis of the pathological microenvironment (Fig. 9D) [279]. Additionally, the drugs induced apoptosis in scar fibroblasts, hence reducing abnormal collagen deposition. Some functional hydrogels can also alter the pH of scar tissue by modulating matrix components; for example, an acidic microenvironment can inhibit fibroblast activity, while the pH-responsive degradation properties of hydrogels enable targeted drug release under specific pH conditions, further optimizing the effects of microenvironment regulation [298]. This endogenous therapy achieved through microenvironment regulation provides new therapeutic targets for scar treatment.

Brief summary

HMNs can treat the scars through four differemt mechanisms, including physical and mechanical remodeling, drug delivery, regulation of biological activity, and modulation of the local microenvironment. For practical applications, they are not completely independent of each other; instead, several mechanisms often work together simultaneously. Physical and mechanical remodeling can be combined with drug delivery and regulation of biological activity to achieve synergistic effects across multiple mechanisms, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Table 3 systematacially summarizes and compares the various mechanisms underlying hydrogel-based scar treatment. The action mechanism of HMNs in scar treatment is characterized by multidimensional and synergistic effects. By initiating the repair process through physical and mechanical stimulation, utilizing efficient drug delivery as the core therapeutic approach, and optimizing repair conditions through microenvironmental regulation, this method offers a safer and more effective novel therapeutic strategy for clinical scar treatment.

The application of hydrogel microneedles in scar treatment

Treatment of HS

HS are a type of raised, thickened scar that remains confined within the boundaries of the original wound. They typically develop after skin injury, such as burns, surgery, or trauma, resulting from an overproduction of collagen during the wound healing process. The formation mechanism involves the excessive fibroblast proliferation, dysregulated inflammation, and increased expression of growth factors like TGF-β, promoting collagen deposition while reducing matrix degradation. Zhang et al. utilized silk protein microneedles to treat HS through mechanical intervention and the reparative effect was largely dependent on geometric parameters. An optimal needle length of 1000 μm enabled complete penetration of the dermis whilst minimizing the inflammatory response, whereas a needle length of 500 μm failed to penetrate the dermis, and a length of 1500 μm triggered an inflammatory response, thereby compromising the therapeutic outcome. Within an array ranging from 5×5 to 15×15/cm2, as microneedle density increased, the dispersion of tissue stress improved, leading to greater scar improvement. However, when the density exceeded 20×20/cm2, therapeutic efficacy actually diminished due to localized stress accumulation at the needle tips. The pyramid-shaped needle tip provides uniform and gentle mechanical stimulation, whilst a methanol annealing treatment increases the microneedles’ compressive strength to over 0.65 N, meeting the 0.5 N penetration threshold and supporting a 30-day remodeling process under controlled degradation conditions. By adjusting the size and density of the microneedles, this structure inhibits the integrin-FAK signaling cascade, reduces the contractile stress exerted by fibroblasts, lowers the levels of pro-fibrotic factors, and creates a low-stress scar microenvironment [281].

In 2023, Chen and his team developed a GelMA/PEGDA double-network hydrogel microneedle patch (CB-HMNP) loaded with a CB and the dual-network structure provides the microneedles with sufficient mechanical strength and reduces the rate of biodegradation. In vitro experiments confirmed that the patch significantly inhibited the proliferation of human hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (hHSFs) and exhibited good biocompatibility. Animal studies demonstrated that after three administrations, the best therapeutic results were achieved using microneedles 775 μm in length with an 18 μm-thin tip, arranged in a 10×10 equidistant array with a single-needle insertion force of 0.47 N. CB-HMNP reduced the scar elevation index (SEI), the collagen I/III ratio, and transforming growth factor-β1(TGF-β1) protein expression, with therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of conventional CB injections [308]. Xie et al. developed a hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel microneedle loaded with bleomycin. Using a two-step casting method, they produced a microneedle array with sufficient mechanical strength capable of penetrating pig skin to a depth of over 300 μm. The microneedles dissolved almost completely within 10 min in mouse skin, releasing a cumulative 52% of bleomycin, The skin fully recovered within 3 h with no significant irritation. The microneedles maintained stable drug activity after 6 weeks of storage at room temperature. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the bleomycin not only inhibited the proliferation of human hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (hHSFs) and downregulated TGF-β1 secretion, but also the synergistic effect of high-concentration HA further enhanced the anti-scarring effect [309].

Salvianolic acids possesses well-established antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and free radical scavenging properties, protecting cells from various forms of damage and exhibiting anti-fibrotic effects [310]. Inspired by the characteristics of the thorns on the kapok tree, Mbituyimana developed a hyaluronic acid-soluble microneedle system loaded with salicylic acid B (Sal-B), addressing the issues of poor skin permeability and insufficient stability of Sal-B. The microneedles, prepared using a micro-molding method, can penetrate the skin painlessly and dissolve in the dermis, enabling sustained drug release over 12 h. The formulation retains its activity even after 6 months of storage at room temperature. In a rabbit ear hypertrophic scar model, after five administrations, it significantly reduced the scar elevation index, decreased collagen deposition, and downregulated the expression of fibrosis-related proteins (TGF-β1, STAT3, etc.), demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared to traditional topical creams [311]. Zhang et al. developed a centella asiatica acid/PEG-TK-PLGA nanoparticles-loaded HA microneedles to repair the hypertrophic scar. There is an optimal range for microneedles with an 800 μm fine conical tip, a 10×10 array, and a 700 μm needle spacing. The fineness of the tip, the uniformity of the array and the mechanical insertion force can exert a sustained positive influence on scar oxidative stress, inflammation and collagen remodeling. Microneedles that do not meet these geometric parameters will result in superficial drug retention and failure of targeted drug delivery, ultimately significantly reducing the capacity for scar-free repair. When ROS levels are high in the scar tissue, the TK bond breaks to release AA, which scavenges ROS, reduces inflammation, inhibits the proliferation of abnormal fibroblasts and keratinocytes, and reduces excessive collagen deposition, thereby remodeling the pathological microenvironment of the scar and ultimately achieving scar-free repair of HS [226].

Various types of HMNs possess different action mechanisms, efficacy, safety and clinical applications in scar models. AA/PTP ROS-responsive arbutin microneedles can simultaneously regulate oxidative stress, inflammatory responses and collagen fibrosis due to their ability to deliver drugs directly to the lesion site. They demonstrate the best overall performance across key indicators, such as the scar elevation index, collagen remodeling and repair of the pathological microenvironment, which are the only system capable of improving the high ROS microenvironment within scars. High-density fibrin drug-free microneedles rely on the integrin-FAK mechanical pathway to reduce scar stiffness and their efficacy is comparable to that of the former. However, they fail to alleviate redness or oxidative stress and as they have no drug-related side effects, they are suitable for individuals with drug allergies. Compound betamethasone hydrogel microneedles can rapidly inhibit fibroblast proliferation through the sustained-release of corticosteroids, but long-term use carries risks of skin thinning and pigmentation abnormalities. Sal-B biomimetic microneedles offer good biocompatibility and high formulation storage stability, yet lack a targeted drug delivery design, resulting in limited therapeutic efficacy. Bleomycin microneedles exhibit the strongest anti-fibrotic effect, but their high cytotoxicity makes them prone to causing depigmentation and tissue atrophy, limiting their use to refractory severe scars. Overall, microenvironment-responsive smart drug-loaded microneedles simultaneously address the three core challenges of transdermal penetration, off-target drug delivery and multi-pathological disorders, making them the greatest potential technology for the minimally invasive treatment of HS. Other types of microneedles, including mechanical microneedles, corticosteroid microneedles, natural medicine microneedles and cytotoxic drug microneedles, may be selected on a tiered, differentiated basis according to the severity of the scar, the patient's tolerance and the conditions of use.

Treatment of keloids

As a stubborn scar, keloid is raised, firm scars that extend beyond the original wound boundaries and rarely regress spontaneously. They result from an excessive and prolonged wound healing response, driven by persistent fibroblast activation, overproduction of collagen (especially type I and III), and dysregulation of growth factors such as TGF-β. Different from HS that keep within the wound margins and may partially flatten over time, keloids grow invasively into adjacent healthy skin and often recur after surgical excision. Additionally, keloids are more common in darker-skinned individuals and can develop months after even minor skin injuries, which are not usually sensitive to early intervention. Xue et al. developed a HMN based on PEGDA and loaded with the anticancer drug 5-FU. With a 940 ± 12 μm in length of needle is, its low-resistance conical tip design, in conjunction with a critical effective insertion force of 0.05 N, work synergistically to form a complete microchannel in the skin, enhancing drug accumulation in the dermis and effectively inhibiting the proliferation of scar fibroblasts. In vitro experiments demonstrated they can effectively inhibit the proliferation of keloid fibroblasts with the advantages of low cost and self-administration [312]. Gou et al. prepared glabridin-loaded dissolving microneedles (Gla-MN) with a 800 μm length and a 0.062 N of effective penetration force. In a New Zealand rabbit ear keloid model, Gla-MN demonstrated excellent skin penetration, penetrating at least 4 to 5 layers of parafilm, and the skin had largely returned to normal 4 h after puncture. Through efficient transdermal drug delivery and targeted inhibition of the PI3K/Akt and TGF-β1/α-SMA signaling pathways, the treatment significantly reduced scar thickness and improved scar hardness and erythema, with efficacy superior to that of a simple glabridin solution [313].

AS downregulates the expression of TGF-β1, reduces type I collagen deposition, and promotes the reverse differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, thereby softening HS [264]. Due to the poor water solubility and low skin permeability of AS, AS-PNS nanocrystals (AS-PNS-NCs) utilise total ginseng saponins (PNS) as a stabiliser, which are then loaded onto PVP-K90-based degradable microneedles (AS-PNS-NCs-DMNs). Through the dissolution of 500 μm tetrahedral PVP-based nanocrystal microneedles, a skin penetration channel is established through the synergistic action of the shallow, appropriately sized needle length, the low-resistance conical tip configuration, and a critical penetration force of 0.21 N, downregulating TGF-β1 and α-SMA expression to reduce excessive collagen deposition, thereby significantly reducing scar thickness and improving collagen organisation [314].

Micro-needling alone can mildly inhibit scar hyperplasia and the mechanism involves the microneedle-deriven change of the local mechanical microenvironment of the scar, thereby blocking the mechanical transmission of fibrotic signals by fibroblasts. However, without the synergistic effect of medication, its efficacy is significantly weaker than that of drug-loaded micro-needles. All three microneedle systems all achieve inhibition of scar fibroblasts via minimally invasive transdermal delivery, improve adherence to home treatment and incur significantly lower costs than outpatient injections. However, there are marked differences in their core mechanisms. PEGDA-loaded 5-FU non-swelling microneedles possess high mechanical strength and provide sustained-release over 12 h; they have only been validated in vitro and, whilst relying on the potent cell proliferation-inhibiting effects of the chemotherapeutic agent, carry a risk of chemical irritation. HA-loaded glabridin fully dissolvable microneedles are gentle and leave no residue with rapid 24-h drug release; they regulate fibrosis via a single pathway, have a short intervention period of only 12 days, and offer limited improvement for mild, newly formed scars. PVP-loaded centella asiatica glycoside-Panax notoginseng saponin nanocrystal-dissolving microneedles utilise the synergistic effects of two natural active ingredients to downregulate TGF-β1/α-SMA via multiple pathways and inhibit fibroblast migration. In a 30-day long-term rabbit ear model, they demonstrated the optimal improvement in scar thickness, collagen deposition and fibrotic proteins, though the preparation process is more complex. Meanwhile, puncture with blank microneedles can mildly suppress scarring via mechanical signals. The three types of microneedles are respectively suited to hard/old scars, mild new facial scars, and moderate-to-severe HS or post-surgical scar prevention scenarios; however, at present, there is a lack of human clinical data to support their efficacy.

Combination with other therapies

HMNs can not only be used as a standalone treatment but can also be combined with various traditional therapeutic methods to amplify therapeutic effects through synergistic action. When used in combination with topical medications, the microchannels formed by HMNs can penetrate the skin stratum corneum barrier, and significantly enhance the penetration depth and absorption efficiency of subsequent topical treatments, thereby addressing the challenge that traditional topical medications face in penetrating the skin. When silicone gel is applied following microneedle pretreatment, the drug concentration in scar tissue can increase by 3-5 times, resulting in more pronounced effects in softening scars and improving their appearance [315].

In PDT for HS, poor transdermal delivery of photosensitizers, the need for specific excitation, and cell-protective autophagy reduce the treatment efficacy [316,317]. Microneedles can deliver photosensitizers to the dermal layer of the scar, where they are activated by light of a specific wavelength to treat the scar. Chen designed a functionalized hydrogel microneedle patch (MNP) loaded with the photosensitizer metal-organic framework (MOF) and the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ). Based on a gelatin-starch matrix, this functionalized MNP possesses sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate the scar barrier, enabling precise drug delivery to deep layers of the lesion. Under visible light irradiation, MOFs efficiently generate ROS to induce apoptosis in scar fibroblasts, while CQ inhibits protective autophagy by blocking the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes and elevates ROS levels. The synergistic action of these two components downregulates the expression of type I collagen and TGF-β1 [318].

The core of gene therapy for scars lies in precisely regulating key pathways involved in scar formation at the molecular level, reducing fibroblast proliferation, and thereby blocking or reversing the formation and progression of PSs at their root [319]. HMNs can be combined with gene therapy for scar treatment. These microneedles can penetrate the skin stratum corneum barrier to deliver gene therapy components to the dermal layer of the scar. By silencing pro-fibrotic genes or overexpressing pro-repair genes, they directly inhibit myofibroblast differentiation, regulate collagen metabolic balance, and simultaneously modulate the secretion of inflammatory factors and angiogenesis.

Early intervention for scars

The formation of PSs is associated with excessive activation of the inflammatory response during the early stages of wound healing, abnormal fibroblast proliferation, and disorganized deposition of collagen fibers [320,321]. The inflammatory and proliferative phases of wound healing represent critical intervention windows for preventing pathological scar formation. In the early stages of wound repair, HMNs can non-invasively penetrate the skin stratum corneum via micron-scale needles and form microchannels in the dermis. This approach not only improves local microcirculation at the wound site, but also promotes the drainage of inflammatory exudates through physical microchannels, thereby alleviating excessive early inflammatory responses at their source. Additionally, leveraging the degradable, swellable, and controlled release properties of hydrogels, active ingredients such as anti-fibrotic drugs, gene therapies, cytokines, or stem cells can be precisely delivered to the site of tissue damage, enabling targeted regulation during the early stages of healing. Clinical validation and barriers

In recent years, a number of single-blind, randomised, controlled clinical trials, including those with scar-based and autologous controls, have gradually established a comprehensive body of clinical evidence, validating the potential for clinical translation across multiple dimensions, ranging from small-molecule chemotherapeutic agents and glucocorticoids to targeted nucleic acid preparations.

A single-blind, randomised, controlled clinical trial was conducted to investigate, for the first time, the treatment of keloids with 5-fluorouracil-loaded dissolvable microneedles. The study compared the efficacy and safety of weekly microneedle patches versus monthly intralesional 5-FU injections over 12 weeks, with follow-up until week 24. Injections resulted in faster short-term scar reduction and a more significant decrease in scar volume at 12 weeks; however, there was no statistically significant difference in the degree of scar improvement between the two groups at the 3-month follow-up after treatment cessation. Pain levels were significantly lower throughout the microneedle treatment course, with only transient, mild hyperpigmentation and superficial desquamation occurring, both of which resolved spontaneously; there were no adverse reactions such as persistent severe pain or ulcers associated with the injections [328]. A single-blind, self-controlled clinical trial utilized triamcinolone hyaluronic acid soluble microneedles with high and low drug-loading doses; participants self-administered the patches daily for 30 days as an intervention for keloids. The high-dose microneedles achieved more pronounced reduction in scar volume, but some recurrence of scarring was observed following discontinuation of treatment [329]. Lim et al. conducted a randomised, single-blind, split-scar clinical trial-the world's first human trial of siRNA-loaded soluble microneedles for post-operative scarring-comparing the effects of an 8-week intervention with siRNA microneedles versus standard clinical silicone patches on surgical incisions two weeks post-operation; siRNA microneedles can suppress the expression of the pro-fibrotic SPARC protein; at 60 days, the reduction in scar volume was significantly greater than that achieved with silicone patches, demonstrating a stronger anti-fibrotic effect [330]. A double-blind, controlled clinical trial comparing triamcinolone-loaded dissolvable microneedles with sham microneedles, with three interventions at two-week intervals, assessed efficacy in HS and keloids in a stratified analysis. TAC microneedles significantly reduced the volume of HS and improved scar scores, whilst placebo microneedles had no substantial scar-reducing effect; however, for dense keloids, only subjective scar scores improved, and the reduction in scar volume was not statistically significant. Histological analysis confirmed that 1500 μm microneedles can only reach the superficial dermis and are unable to penetrate the deep fibrotic tissue of keloids [331].

Although a comprehensive theoretical framework and ample experimental data on hydrogel microneedles have been established in the laboratory, the transition from laboratory samples to large-scale commercialisation still faces obstacles in various areas, including production, regulation, clinical trials and the market. Over the past decade, the annual compound growth rate of global publications on hydrogel microneedles has been 14.2 percent; yet only 6 percent of research has progressed to human clinical trials, whilst 94 percent remains at the in vitro, cellular and animal trial stages [332,333]. Regulations governing aesthetic medicine and medical devices vary significantly between countries, requiring hydrogel formulations to be re-optimized to meet regional standards. The journey from prototype through clinical trials to profitability for hydrogel microneedles demands sustained, substantial financial investment, which most laboratories are unable to sustain over the long term without revenue. Research laboratories possess only basic formulation development capabilities and lack partnerships with clinical institutions or Contract Research Organisations (CROs), resulting in an extremely lengthy full registration and validation cycle [334,335].

Conclusion and perspective

This review provides a systematic overview of research progress in the use of hydrogel microneedles for scar treatment, covering the selection of materials, preparation methods and the principles of smart responsive hydrogels, as well as the mechanisms and applications of hydrogel microneedles in scar treatment.

The formation of scars is essentially a pathological proliferation of tissue resulting from a prolonged imbalance in the post-traumatic immune microenvironment and an abnormally polarised repair process. The core pathological features manifest as excessive inflammatory infiltration in the early stages of injury, persistent polarization of macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype, abnormal activation of fibroblasts and excessive collagen deposition, as well as disruption of the immune-cell-matrix signaling axis. Owing to their minimally invasive and painless transdermal delivery properties, versatile drug-loading capacity, and ideal biocompatibility, hydrogel microneedles have gradually emerged as a novel, cutting-edge intervention strategy in the field of targeted intervention and repair of PSs, opening up entirely new avenues of research and practical application to overcome the technical limitations of traditional clinical scar treatments. Compared to traditional treatment modalities, hydrogel microneedles offer distinct advantages in remodeling abnormal scar tissue structure, accelerating skin tissue regeneration and repair, reducing scar hyperpigmentation, and improving skin texture irregularities. This multi-mechanism, synergistic composite treatment approach effectively overcomes the limitations of monotherapy, significantly enhancing the overall efficacy of scar repair and its clinical value.

Currently, with the convergence of biomaterials science, micro- and nano-fabrication techniques, and biomedical engineering, the pace of research and development about smart, responsive hydrogel microneedles continues to accelerate. However, the clinical translation of these functionalized microneedle carriers for scar treatment still faces multiple practical challenges and technical barriers. On the one hand, it is difficult to reconcile the inherent swelling characteristics of hydrogel substrates with their mechanical performance limitations. The long-standing challenge of dynamically balancing the mechanical strength of the microneedle, its skin penetration capability, and the swelling rate of the matrix persists. Furthermore, formulations designed for high drug loading are highly prone to causing issues such as softening and fracture of the needle tip, which directly limits the actual penetration efficacy of the microneedles, On the other hand, the development of composite drug delivery systems still has shortcomings. Macromolecular active substances such as anti-inflammatory proteins, immunomodulatory peptides and functional growth factors commonly suffer from insufficient encapsulation efficiency, poor storage stability and pronounced burst release effects; technologies for sustained-release and time-controlled drug delivery still require further optimisation. Besides, PSs exhibit significant individual heterogeneity; differences in the site of onset, stage of the disease, severity, and patient constitution directly alter the density of scar tissue and the characteristics of the microenvironment, greatly increasing the difficulty of formulating standardised treatment protocols. Furthermore, the large-scale batch production of medical-grade hydrogel microneedles, as well as the processes for sterilisation and standardised quality control systems, have not yet been fully established. During sterilisation, issues such as structural damage of the needles, inactivation of active drugs, and deterioration of the materials’ physicochemical properties are prone to occur. Moreover, key indicators, including long-term biosafety, long-term reparative efficacy, and the risk of skin sensitization, still require a validation and refinement through large-scale, long-term, multi-centre clinical trials.

In this new era of rapid development in digital healthcare and smart biomaterials, the deep integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technology is emerging as the key driver for overcoming bottlenecks in the research and development of hydrogel microneedles and accelerating the implementation of precision scar treatment, thereby significantly enhancing the relevance, innovation and clinical guidance value of this field of research. Leveraging cutting-edge technologies such as AI-driven big data analysis, machine learning, finite element simulation and intelligent algorithm optimisation, it is possible to achieve high-throughput screening and precise formulation optimisation of hydrogel matrices. By using AI models to predict the mechanical strength, swelling properties, degradation cycles and drug-loading potential of different material combinations, the optimal material system suited to scar puncture requirements can be rapidly identified, significantly shortening the research and development cycle and reducing the costs associated with experimental trial and error. Utilizing AI-driven finite element simulation technology, the system can accurately simulate the mechanical process of microneedle penetration through the skin, the force-deformation behavior of the needle tip, and the penetration and diffusion pathways of the drug within the dermis, thereby providing quantitative evidence for the refined design of microneedle array morphology, needle tip structure, and arrangement density. Furthermore, by integrating artificial intelligence image recognition with multi-omics data analysis, we can accurately analyse individualised pathological parameters, such as the morphological characteristics, degree of fibrosis and inflammation levels-of different patients’ scars. This enables the intelligent matching of microneedle drug formulations, dosages and intervention cycles, thereby facilitating the rapid development of personalised treatment plans.

In the future, the convergence of hydrogel microneedles and artificial intelligence will drive the continuous advancement in this field across several key areas (Fig. 10): environmentally intelligent responses, synergistic combination of multiple therapies, AI-driven personalised precision diagnosis and treatment, and expansion into diverse disease scenarios and so on. In terms of intelligent response, leveraging AI-assisted molecular design, we will precisely develop hydrogel microneedles that respond to pH, reactive oxygen species, specific proteases and the inflammatory microenvironment. This will enable the intelligent recognition of signals from the pathological microenvironment and the on-demand release of drugs, thereby maximising the efficiency of targeted therapy. In the realm of multi-modal synergy, AI algorithms are utilized to optimise drug combination ratios and combination strategies. Microneedle drug delivery technology is seamlessly integrated with physical modalities such as photothermal therapy, photodynamic therapy and ultrasound-guided intervention to establish a multi-modal synergistic repair system, thereby addressing the challenge of treating severe, intractable scarring. Leveraging 3D scanning and imaging, AI-driven modelling, and customised 3D printing technology, we can precisely replicate the contours and curved surfaces of scars to construct bespoke, irregularly shaped microneedle arrays, thereby truly implementing a personalised, precision diagnosis and treatment model of “one type per person, one formula per scar”. Furthermore, with the support of artificial intelligence, the scope of hydrogel microneedle applications will be further expanded from skin scar repair to the targeted intervention of systemic fibrotic diseases such as scleroderma and organ fibrosis. With the deep convergence of material modification technologies, micro- and nano-manufacturing processes, artificial intelligence and digital technologies with clinical medicine, the comprehensive performance, mass production processes and safety evaluation systems of hydrogel microneedles will be further improved. The pace of clinical translation will accelerate, ultimately establishing them as a core component of a comprehensive, end-to-end management system for PSs, providing scar patients with safer, more efficient, minimally invasive, convenient and personalised treatment options.
