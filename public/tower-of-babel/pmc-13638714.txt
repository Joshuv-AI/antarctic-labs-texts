The influence of stenosis severity and geometric orientation on atherosclerotic progression: A fluid-structure interaction study in the carotid artery
Authors: Harshawardhan Patil, Alexandrina Untaroiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis remains the leading global cause of mortality, and its progression is strongly influenced by the local hemodynamic environment. Low Wall Shear Stress (WSS) is widely recognized as a promoter of atherosclerosis, yet the combined influence of stenosis severity and its geometric orientation on disease progression in anatomically realistic geometries has not been systematically examined. This study assesses the risk of atherosclerotic progression in a carotid artery using one-way Fluid–Structure Interaction (FSI). Four stenosis levels were simulated (0, 30, 60, and 80%), along with three angular orientations of the stenotic apex relative to the ICA wall. Blood flow was modeled using the k-ω SST turbulence model, and arterial walls were represented using a Mooney–Rivlin hyperelastic formulation. For 0% and 30% stenosed cases, regions of low Time-Averaged WSS (TAWSS <= 0.4 Pa) appeared in the ICA. In contrast, the 60% and 80% stenoses produced markedly elevated TAWSS at the stenosis throat, ranging from 20–60 Pa. Arterial wall deformation of the 0% and 30% cases was observed around 0.8 mm, while the 60% and 80% stenoses exhibited prominent foci of high deformation upto 2.1 mm. The presence of disturbed flow dynamics and their resulting atheroprogression risk was assessed through the interplay of Reynolds number, nondimensional WSS, nondimensional Turbulent Kinetic Energy (TKE) and the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI). At 30% severity, the upstream-tilted stenosis intensified early-stage progression indicators, whereas at 60% severity, the downstream-tilted orientation increased plaque vulnerability. The study demonstrates a comparative framework to rank relative risk of atheroprogression using the disturbed flow signatures and wall mechanics observed across the 8 geometries.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is an irreversible, chronic disease affecting millions of people worldwide and the leading cause of death globally. In 2019, 55.5 million new cases were registered globally, with an estimated 17.9 million deaths worldwide [1, 2]. It is characterized as a geometrically focal, complex disease dependent on the histology of arterial tissue, hemodynamic forces at the arterial wall, and multi-modal biomarkers [1, 3, 4]. There is a broad consensus that Wall Shear Stress (WSS) is one of the most significant biomechanical parameters for the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic stenoses [5, 6, 7, 8].

With the advent of numerical methods for a wide range of physical phenomena, evaluation of arterial biomechanics numerically has emerged as a viable alternative [1, 3, 9]. Varghese et al. [10] investigated pulsatile flow for idealized stenotic geometries with four Reynolds-Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) turbulence models, and found that the low Reynolds k - ω turbulence model showed better agreement with experimental results by [11, 12]. For larger arteries with a high shear rate, it is also demonstrated that a Newtonian rheology model for blood is acceptable [13, 14, 15, 16].

Geometrical differences in arterial curvature and undulations directly influence flow dynamics, and may differentially favor disease progression [17]. Given the frequent occurrence of stenoses in the carotid artery, this study focuses on Carotid Artery Stenoses (CAS) [18]. Kaazempur-Mofrad et al. [19] showed a correlation between intimal thickening and plaque location with low and oscillatory wall shear stress. Katranas et al. [8] analyzed whether WSS correlates with plaque morphology, curvature and stiffness, and found that plaque segments had lower WSS than adjacent normal segments.

Younis et al. [20] investigated geometric differences such as bulb size, bifurcation angle, curvature for insights on atherogenesis. They found that geometry is the dominant determinant of the local hemodynamic environment compared to inflow waveform or viscosity model. Notably, they concluded that regions of high cyclic strain and low, oscillatory shear may colocalize.

Fluid Structure Interactions (FSI) have also gained prominence in demonstrating the influence of wall mechanics on the hemodynamic stresses and plaque progression, such as in [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Shakya et al. [25] analyzed the effect of stenosis severity in the context of 2-way FSI by comparing 6 levels of stenosis at 4 anatomically distinct sites within the carotid artery. They concluded that while studying hemodynamic response of arteries with multiple stenoses, the effect of one stenosis on another should also be considered [25].

Yang et al. [26] conducted an in-vivo validation study for 2-way FSI, for 14 patients with a total of 32 scan pairs. While they concurred that plaque initiation and early-stage progression evolves in low WSS regions, they argued that the more advanced stages of plaque progression correlate positively with WSS and negatively with plaque wall stress. Their study challenges the accepted mechanism of low WSS driven atheroprogression for advanced stenosis stages.

Holzgapfel et al. [24] reviewed the state-of-the-art for patient-specific CFD and FSI simulations. They note that the most quoted rupture threshold is 300 kPa, however, there is a need to develop a more location and tissue-specific threshold, and a proper failure criterion. Lopes et al. [23] noted that it may be insufficient to assume isotropic linear elasticity of arterial walls due to their non-homogeneous and visco-elastic nature, noting that the preferred choice for capturing mechanical behavior of both healthy and stenosed vessels was hyperelastic models [23].

The works of [21, 22] observe that rigid-wall models overestimate wall shear stresses. Lopes et al. [22] observed that while the effect of varying the arterial stiffness may be minimal, omitting compliance entirely leads to overestimation of velocity magnitudes and shear stresses. Moradicheghamahi et al. (2019) [21] report upto 20% higher maximum shear stress for their rigid wall case. In light of this, it is acknowledged that our study, which employs a 1-way FSI approach, sits at the upper bound of physiologic athero-promotive hemodynamics and wall mechanics.

Given the review of the literature above, it is also pertinent to note that, the influence of stenosis severity in simultaneity with change in orientation of the stenosis in anatomical geometries remains unexplored to the best of our knowledge. In this study, we bridge that gap by exploring the controlled effect of stenosis severity and its geometric orientation on athero-promoting hemodynamics and related wall mechanics. We perform a 1-way FSI analysis on a patient-specific carotid artery with a comparison between 0(healthy), 30, 60 and 80% stenosed cases. 3 levels of angular orientations are considered for the 30% and 60% cases [27]. In addition to elucidating established metrics such as TAWSS and OSI, we also elucidate the interplay between Reynolds Number (Re), nondimensional WSS and non-dimensional Turbulent Kinetic Energy to shed light on the occurrence of persistent flow disturbances.

The ICA and ECA diverge from a small region known as the carotid sinus. The model is a hybrid Reynolds-Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) formulation that blends near-wall accuracy with numerical stability under freestream conditions. It introduces a shear stress limiter to improve predictions for adverse pressure gradients and flow separation regions. For more information on its formulation, the reader is referred to [32].

An incompressible, Newtonian rheology model for blood is used with a constant density of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 1060\ \text [kg /m^{3}]$$\end{document} and constant viscosity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu = 0.0035\ \text [kg/ms]$$\end{document}. As stated in literature, the Newtonian assumption is acceptable for large diameter arteries like the carotid artery [13, 14, 15, 16, 21]. Inlet turbulence internsity was assumed at 5% while outlet turbulence intensity was assumed at 10% for both outlets. The solution numerical methods employed are first-order upwind spatial discretization for momentum, second-order upwind spatial discretization for k and ω , second-order for pressure, and first-order implicit transient formulation.

Velocity boundary conditions are obtained from pulsed Doppler measurements by Augst et al. (2003) [33], and have been used by [25]. The flow profiles mentioned in Fig. 4a are imposed on the CCA inlet, and ECA-ICA outlets. The threshold convergence for the RMS Scaled residuals of continuity, x, y, z velocities, k, ω are set to 1e-6. The time-stepping regime was divided into 2 portions, with a step size of 0.00385 sec from t=0.0 sec to t=0.4 sec (end of systole), followed by a step size of 0.01818 sec from t=0.4 sec to t=1.0 sec (end of diastole). A total of 137 time steps were thus executed over 1 cardiac cycle. The dual time-stepping regime is justified due to the presence of high temporal acceleration and momentum fluxes during the systole, meaning that a finer step size is required to accurately capture the inertial and viscous forces at play. During the diastolic phase, the temporal driving gradients are much gentler, allowing a larger timestep whilst maintaining convergence and stability.

For the simulation of the 80% stenosis case, it is essential to note that the mass flow rate drops considerably at high stenosis levels [34], and imposing the same velocity boundary conditions taken from a healthy subject’s Doppler ultrasound onto a severely stenosed case will lead to non-physiologic values. Therefore, in order to maintain simulation realism, the CCA and ICA velocity profiles are scaled by a factor of 0.5 [34]. It is also assumed that the drop in CCA mass flow rate is equal to the drop in the ICA mass flow rate. The ECA mass flow rate is accordingly adjusted to ensure mass continuity.

Methods for FEA analyses

The FEA simulations were performed in ANSYS Mechanical 2024 R1 with a transient structural solver. Imported pressure from the CFD simulation were sampled at every 10 time steps of the CFD simulation and applied to the inner walls of the artery shell as one of the boundary conditions for the FEA simulation. Discussion

The Discussion section is organized into 6 parts. "Velocity contours" section entails discussion on the velocity contours, highlighting the nonlinear flow dynamics involved. "Time-averaged wall shear stress" section talks about Time-Averaged Wall Shear Stress (TAWSS) and identifies low TAWSS regions, prone to be susceptible to atheroprogression. "Interplay between Re, WSS and TKE" section elucidates how the interplay between Re, nondimensional WSS (NWSS) and nondimensional TKE (NTKE) reveals insights on disturbed flow dynamics, while Oscillatory shear index (OSI) section discusses the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI). "Arterial wall deformation and principal stresses" section includes the structural dynamics observed for the 8 cases and its implications on disease progression. "Qualitative assessment of disease progression" section ultimately coheres the discussion in the previous sections to build a qualitative framework of relative risk of atheroprogression. "Limitations" section addresses the shortcomings of this study.

Velocity contours

Figure 5 reveals the velocity contours for each case at peak systole (t = 0.173 sec). The velocity profiles in the CCA across cases indicate a broad region of uniform speed flow (plug flow) as the flow profile is still developing. There is a hotspot of slow, recirculating flow at the distal ICA wall for the unstenosed case (Fig. 5b). The flow impinging at the sinus from the CCA can be seen reorienting predominantly toward the proximal (inner) wall in the ICA and ECA, leaving the distal outer wall with comparatively low momentum exchange. The broadening of the vessel’s local diameter at the base of the distal ICA is also seen to influence flow separation. The distal ICA wall will hence have much higher particle residence time, potentially facilitating LDL migration across the intimal layer, which is responsible for atherogenesis. There is also the presence of a shear layer (light blue-green interface) seen in the 0% and 30% cases. This may indicate the presence of turbulence, primarily because of the widening of conduit area slightly prior to the bifurcation. It is observed that even for healthy arteries, geometric features have a significant influence on flow dynamics and at least intermittent turbulence may be present.

Comparing Fig. 5b–d shows that for the 30% stenosis, flow separation has occurred for the AOA-1 and AOA+1 cases upstream of the stenosis, while for the AOA 0 case, there is a brief reattachment at the apex. The largest recirculation pockets around the stenosis are seen for the 30% AOA+1 case. Figure 5e–g show that for the 60% stenosis, all 3 AOA cases behave like a converging throat upstream of the apex, with flow separation immediately downstream of it. For all the 3 cases for 60%, we see that the increased jet velocity encounters a rapidly widening area post the apex, leading to rapid dissipation of the inertial core and a transformation to a large recirculating pocket in the downstream. It is seen that the 60% AOA 0 case indicates the largest recirculation pocket. For the 80% case (Fig. 5h), most of the flow can be seen reorienting into the ECA, leaving a quiescent, recirculating pocket of flow in the post-stenosis ICA region on the distal side and a stenotic jet on the proximal side. Despite a 50% drop in inlet velocity profile for this case (as stated in "Methods for CFD analyses" section ), we see that the peak velocity is 1.49 m/s, almost 2.5 times that of the unstenosed case’s CCA velocity.

Time-averaged wall shear stress

Figure 7 represents the Time-Averaged Wall Shear Stress (TAWSS) averaged over the cardiac cycle, as defined in Eq. 4. The first millimeter of the inlet and outlets has been hidden from view due to numerical artifacts. Figure 7a indicates that a significantly large region of the unstenosed distal ICA experiences sustained low values of shear stresses (TAWSS <= 0.4 Pa). A circumferential region of the CCA upstream of the bifurcation, also experiences similarly low TAWSS. These regions have been correlated in the literature as being susceptible to atherogenesis [1, 3, 4, 5, 19, 20]. As reported in several studies, low hemodynamic shear stresses are associated with disorganized endothelial cell shape, inhibition of certain vasoregulatory enzymes, higher oxidative stress, higher inflammatory mediators and lower anti-inflammatory mediators. It is also associated with decreased thrombo-resistance and increased lipid uptake by the endothelial wall [1, 3, 19].

Figure 7b–d reveal that much of the distal ICA wall of the 30% stenosed geometry cases experiences sustained low values of TAWSS similar to the unstenosed case. This suggests that atheroprogression through further plaque thickening is highly likely for the 30% cases. Figure 7e–g show that the 60% stenosed geometries experience wide bands of higher TAWSS across the stenoses’ apex. The 60% AOA 0 case has a max. TAWSS of 26 Pa, the 60% AOA-1 case has a max. TAWSS of 24 Pa, while the 60% AOA+1 case has a max. TAWSS of 20 Pa. The observed maxima can be explained due to the orientation of the acting shear area. When flow impinges on the stenosis, in the AOA-1 case and AOA 0 cases, the shear stress acting area would be oriented cross-stream into the direction of the proximal ICA wall, which will stretch the endothelium further towards the proximal side of the ICA. In contrast, the AOA+1 case stretches the acting shear area cross-stream away from the bulk flow and into the distal ICA wall. It is seen that the 80% stenosis case (Fig. 7h) receives a max. TAWSS of 60 Pa even at 50% damping of CCA and ICA flow rates. Similar high values of TAWSS have been reported in Figure 9 of [41]. Such consistent high shear regions are likely to promote faster endothelial erosion. These findings concur with the work of [6, 26, 41, 42, 43], indicating that high TAWSS is observed at the stenosis throat in advanced stages. It is also emphasized that the the risk of adverse cardiovascular events is higher at severe stenoses [7, 44]. Yang et al. [26] also argue that a positive correlation exists between high TAWSS and advanced-stage progression. However, the exact physiological mechanism behind this remains poorly understood.

Interplay between Re, WSS and TKE

The top row in Fig. 8 presents the relationship between NTKE and NWSS versus Re for the section planes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _i$$\end{document}. The flow of physical time is marked by the direction of the arrows shown. It is striking from Fig. 8 (top left) that the relationship for 0% and 30% cases is hysteretic. NTKE is low and nearly constant in early systole, but steeply rises as Re starts decreasing from peak systole onward. This rapid rise is followed to an oscillating pattern of TKE throughout the diastole. Disturbed flow dynamics have been known to favor atheroprogression [45, 46]. The presence of shear layers and recirculation zones discussed in "Velocity contours" section means that TKE can influence energy exchange in the direction of the wall. Furthermore, given that these fluctuations in NTKE are basically temporal fluctuations in space-averaged turbulence (Eq. 6), they modulate the near-wall momentum transport and therefore contribute to the observed cyclic fluctuations in the space-averaged wall shear stress.

In milder stenosis cases, the conduit diameter is large enough for a complex interplay between NTKE and oscillatory viscous effects. This interplay is being seen in Fig. 8 (bottom left), with all 4 cases exhibiting a viscous, oscillatory dominance during post systole, marked by a higher NWSS on Re-descent than during the Re-ascent stage. The area enclosed by this counter-clockwise loop reflects the relative duration and magnitude of this delayed response, and may be indicative of the persistence of flow disturbances or unsteadiness-induced instabilities.

For the more severe cases of stenosis, we see that though there a hysteretic relationship between NTKE and Re, the small conduit diameter means that inertial forces overpower in this region. Viscous effects do not play a major role, and NWSS loading stays nearly linear with Re. Hence, the flow disturbed flow characteristics were not persistent throughout the cycle for the high stenoses cases. However, the risk of MACE events for more severe stenosis still remains high. Furthermore, high TAWSS combined with flatter NWSS curves may still influence endothelial injury or plaque destabilization [7, 44].

Oscillatory shear index (OSI)

To lend further support to the above discussion, we present contours of Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI) in Fig. 9. It can be seen that areas of high OSI are markedly present in the ICA distal wall for the unstenosed case, and throughout the surface area of the stenoses for the 30% cases. the 30% AOA-1 case is seen to exhibit the highest OSI with a wide band across the stenosis. It is closely followed by the AOA 0 and AOA+1 cases. The observed behavior correlates with the expected flow behavior around obstructions. The AOA-1 case, with its apex oriented into the flow, promotes flow detachment and possible vortex formation at its apex. In contrast, the AOA 0 and AOA+1 cases may delay it because of their geometric orientation.

For the 60% cases, we observe that OSI signatures is minimal. This means that the local shear patterns exhibit minimal deviation from the global flow regime prescribed by the boundary conditions. Furthermore, it is worth noting that the unsteadiness elucidated in the NWSS versus Re curves correlate well with OSI across all 8 cases. This indicates that they may be helpful in assessing athero-promoting flow signatures as well. While OSI remains the metric of choice in identifying regions of oscillatory shear, it may not necessarily capture the underlying coupling between turbulence diffusion and wall shear. The NTKE and NWSS curves at a section are therefore presented as a complementary viewpoint that reveals how geometry-dependent turbulence fluctuations modulate the shear fluctuations in time. They may be particularly helpful in cases where similar OSI values can arise from different unsteady mechanisms.

Arterial wall deformation and principal stresses

Figure 9 reveals insights about the maximum deformation of the artery walls from the FEA simulation. It is noted that the maximum deformations of the geometries occur at different times. The deformation waveform (not shown) exhibits some transient oscillations, due to a combination of supports, inertia, damping effect of the structure, as well as nonlinear stiffness due to the hyperelasticity. They may also be indicative of excitations near the natural frequency the system, which has been reported in prior literature [47].

Furthermore, by confining the structural simulation to the luminal boundary, we isolate the geometric effects of the stenosis on the wall mechanics. A prominent focus is observed in the posterior of the CCA (not shown) that is a hotspot of high deformation. The max. deformation is 0.86 mm for the unstenosed case. It is caused mainly by the flow reoriented backwards from the bifurcation mixing with the flow coming from the inlet. For the 30% cases (Fig. 9b–d), the maximum deformation is seen at the center of the stenosis. For the 60% cases (Fig. 9e–g), the region of high deformation on the stenosis is larger. Hotspots of high deformation may increase risk of an intraplaque hemorrhage with repeated cardiac cycles [48, 49].

For the 60% AOA 0 case, a peak deformation of 1.8 mm is observed on one side of the stenosis. Whereas, the AOA-1 case displayed two foci of deformation around 1.6 mm on both sides of the stenosis. The 60% AOA+1 case represents the largest hotspot of deformation at 2.1 mm. This is best explained by the combination of flow impingement, inertia and damping effect exhibited by that orientation of stenosis This may increase the risk of intraplaque hemorrhage for the 60% cases. The 80% case is also similarly vulnerable to intraplaque hemorrhage.

The maximum strain is occurring at the systolic peak due to accurate interpolated pressure alignment. Figure 10 reveals the maximum principal cyclic stress at peak systole for the 8 geometries. It is seen that 5 out of 8 (Fig. 10a–d,f) geometries exhibit a maximum principal stress of 0.14 MPa. The carotid sinus and the stenosis edges where it meets the artery experience the most principal stress. This may be due to the shoulders of the plaque acting as geometric inflection points. The 60% AOA 0 case and the AOA+1 case (Fig. 10e,g) experience a maximum principal stress of 0.18 MPa, which closely matches the plot of Teng et al. Figure 6D for a 3D fully coupled FSI simulation [35]. It is to be noted that the 80% case experiences a maximum principal stress of 0.23 MPa. This is close to the established 0.3 MPa rupture threshold. However, as noted by [24], additional work needs to be done to unravel the failure modes and refine the threshold with location and tissue-specific insights.

Limitations

The most prominent limitation of the study was that we incorporated 1-way FSI, not 2-way FSI. 2-way FSI was attempted, however, due to the fluid and structural densities being similar, and the material model being highly compliant and nonlinear, numerical instabilities with the current framework prevented a converged solution. Another limitation which was also declared in "Methods for FEA analyses" section is that the parameter set of the Mooney–Rivlin solid may be non-unique due to lack of an all mode deformation dataset. Lastly, the strength of the qualitative assessment of the relative risk of progression is limited by the large increments of stenotic severity employed.

Conclusion

This study investigated the combined influence of stenosis severity and orientation on the hemodynamics and wall that affect atheroprogression. It is concluded that our 1-way FSI model sits at the upper-bound of physiologic athero-promotive reality, by virtue of the validation of the velocity, TAWSS, cyclic strain and max. principal cyclic tress metrics against published experimental and computational works. Low TAWSS zones which are known to be favorable to atheroprogression, were identified. Hysteretic signatures of NWSS with Re yield insights in the persistence of flow disturbances. It was observed that the 0% and 30% geometries exhibited the highest amounts of oscillatory shear while 60% and 80% geometries recorded minimal oscillatory shear. The prevalence of the hysteretic signatures of NWSS correlated well with the regions of high OSI. The structural simulations revealed regions at risk of intraplaque hemorrhage or rupture. The effect of angular orientation yielded mixed results. For the milder stenoses cases, the AOA-1 case exhibited higher amounts of disturbed flow signatures and hence, may be at more risk of progression relative to the AOA 0 and AOA+1 case. In contrast, for the advanced stenosis cases, the AOA+1 seemed most at risk of destabilization compared to the AOA 0 and AOA-1 cases. Finally, the 8 geometries were ranked for atheroprogression risk based on the hemodynamics and wall mechanics investigated. In summary, the study highlights the influence of geometric features like stenosis severity and orientation in shaping the relative risk of atheroprogression.
