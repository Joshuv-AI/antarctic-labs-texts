Comparison of calcium hydroxide removal efficiency of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, phytic acid and N-acetylcysteine using sonic activation: a micro-CT study
Authors: Ahmet Tekdemir, Mügem Aslı Ekici, Berkan Çelikten, Bağdagül Helvacıoğlu Kıvanç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to compare the efficacy of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), phytic acid (IP6), and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) with sonic activation on the removal efficiency of calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) from root canals with micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis.MethodsThirty-two extracted human mandibular first premolars were used in this study. The teeth were randomly divided into 4 groups based on the chelating agents to be used to remove Ca(OH)2 from the root canals (n=8). The root canals were prepared with the ProTaper rotary system up to X5. The specimens were scanned using micro-CT after preparation. Calcium hydroxide paste was delivered into root canals using a syringe system (SURE-Paste) combined with a lentulo spiral. All teeth were scanned using micro-CT to determine the filling volume. After 7 days, Ca(OH)2 was removed from the root canals. EDTA was used with syringe irrigation for the first group while, EDTA, IP6, and NAC were used with sonic activation at other groups, respectively, to remove Ca(OH)2. Teeth were scanned using micro-CT to calculate the volume and the percentage of Ca(OH)2 remnants. Data were analyzed statistically by one-way ANOVA and Duncan’s tests (α=0.05).ResultsNone of the chelating agents and irrigation techniques completely removed Ca(OH)2. The percentage of the remnant volume of the Ca(OH)2 was significantly different between the groups (p < 0.05). The EDTA with syringe irrigation group had higher Ca(OH)2 remnant volume values compared to other groups (p < 0.05). The use of the EDTA, IP6, and NAC with sonic activation did not significantly affect the percentage of remaining Ca(OH)2 (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe calcium hydroxide removal efficiencies of all three chelating agents were similar. IP6 and NAC can be used as alternatives to EDTA for Ca(OH)2 removal from the root canals with sonic activation.Keywords: Acetylcysteine, Calcium hydroxide, Chelating agents, Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, Phytic acid

Background

Intracanal medicaments should be completely removed from the root canals to achieve proper root canal filling [1]. Removal of intracanal medicaments, especially calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) from root canals, can be obtained by various systems and techniques [2]. Previous studies have shown that irrigation with the sonic activation technique is effective in removing calcium hydroxide from root canals and improves root canal disinfection [3, 4].

Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is one of the most common and effective chelating agents used to remove Ca(OH)2 from root canals. However, EDTA has a strong demineralization effect and has the potential to cause excessive dentin erosion. Additionally, EDTA causes expansion of dentinal tubules, softening of dentin, and denaturation of collagen fibers [5, 6]. Due to these disadvantages of EDTA, many solutions have been investigated in recent years in the search for a more efficient and biocompatible chelation agent. Phytic acid (IP6) and N-acetylcysteine ​​(NAC) are chelating agents that are being investigated for their biocompatibility and smear layer removal effectiveness [7–9].

The IP6 solution can be extracted from rice at low cost and has multiple negative charges. This makes it an effective chelator of cations such as Ca+2, Mg+2, and Fe+2 [10]. The high negative charge density of IP6 facilitates its chelating activity. IP6, which has the ability to remove the smear layer, has been proposed as an alternative chelating material for irrigation of root canals. IP6 is also biocompatible with osteoblast-like cells [11]. Nassar et al. [8] stated that IP6 effectively removed the smear layer with minimal negative effects on osteoblast cells. They also stated that the coronal dentin surface irrigated with 1% IP6 contained wider dentinal tubules and was cleaner than when irrigated with EDTA. However, IP6 studies on the effectiveness of removing Ca(OH)2 from root canals are still insufficient.

NAC is an antioxidant material and the acetylated form of the amino acid cysteine ​​ [12], which has the closed formula C5H9NO3S. It is used in clinical medicine for the detoxification of heavy metals such as mercury and lead. NAC is also known as the precursor of glutathione, which serves as the main molecule in the intracellular antioxidant system [13, 14]. Additionally, NAC directly neutralizes free radicals by releasing a hydrogen atom from its thiol group [15].

The antioxidant and antibacterial properties of NAC has been extensively studied, and it has also been identified to posses a potential for smear layer removal in dentistry [16, 17]. There are limited studies on the removal of the smear layer and Ca(OH)2 from root canals as a chelating agent in dentistry [9]. However, no date based on the compare the effectiveness of NAC, Ip6, and EDTA after activation in removing calcium hydroxide were found in the literature review. Furthermore, the current study evaluates the calcium hydroxide removal effectiveness of these irrigating solutions volumetrically using micro-CT.

In this study, it was aimed to compare the removal effectiveness of Ca(OH)2 using different chelating agents (EDTA, IP6, and NAC) by using sonic activation with micro-CT analysis. The null hypothesis tested is that there is no difference between the effectiveness of the irrigation solution used for calcium hydroxide removal after sonic activation.

Methods

The study was started after receiving the ethics committee report (Gazi University Faculty of Dentistry Clinical Research Ethics Committee approval report numbered 2020.24/6). Thirty-two freshly extracted lower first premolar teeth were used in this study. Teeth were obtained from Gazi University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery. Lower first premolars, extracted for periodontal and orthodontic reasons, were used. Calculus, debris, and soft tissue remnants were removed with a periodontal curette, and the teeth were disinfected in a 5.25% NaOCl solution for 2h. The teeth were kept at room temperature under 100% humidity until used. Teeth were examined with a loupe (Admetec, Haifa, Israel) under 2.7X magnification, followed by radiographs taken from the mesiodistal and buccolingual axes. Following these investigations, teeth with caries, cracks, fractures on the root surface, internal and external resorption, unclosed root apices, pulp stone and calcification in the root canal, lateral root canals, and more than one root canal, and previous root canal fillings were excluded from the study.

Existing literature information was taken as a source for effect size calculation [18]. For the sample size calculation (G*Power, for Mac, Version 3.1.9.4), it was decided that the statistical power was 80%, the effect size was 25%, and the statistical significance level was 0.05. According to these calculations, it was found that a total of 24 teeth were required, with 6 teeth in each subgroup. To prevent the statistical power from decreasing and to consider the possibility of unforeseen negative situations that may arise during the study, studying on 32 lower first premolar human teeth was decided.

The endodontic access cavities of the selected teeth were prepared using a #012 round bur (Hager and Meisinger GmbH, Germany) under water cooling in accordance with the cavity principles. The working length in root canals was recorded as 1 mm shorter than the distance between the point where the ISO #10 type K file (Diadent, Burnaby, BC, Canada) was seen at the root apex and the top of the tubercle. After performing a glide path with an ISO #15 K-type file (Diadent, Burnaby, BC, Canada), the root canals were prepared with the ProTaper Next rotary instrument system files from X1 to X5 (Dentsply Tulsa Dental Specialties, Tulsa, OK, USA) and the X-Smart endodontic motor (Dentsply, Tulsa, OK, USA), in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions for use. After each canal file was used, the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 5% NaOCl (Microvem, Sakarya, Turkey) using a 27 G side-hole endodontic needle with a closed tip. After the preparation phase of the samples was completed, 5 mL of 17% EDTA (Kemiger, Ankara, Turkey), 5 mL of 5% NaOCl, and 2 mL of distilled water were applied to each root canal as the final irrigation protocol, respectively. Then, the root canals were dried with paper points (Pearl Endo, Ardadent L.T.D., Ankara, Turkey).

After the irrigation phase, SURE-Paste (Sure Endo, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea), which contains Ca(OH)2 paste in the syringe, was applied to the root canals by positioning the application tip 1 mm short of the working length. After the paste was injected, the black belted lentulo spiral (#40) (Diadent, Burnaby, BC, Canada), which was positioned 1 mm short of the working length, was used in a clockwise rotation with a low-speed hand-piece (KaVo, Biberach-Riss, Germany) with 300 rpm speed and operated with back-and-forth movements for 10 s. To observe Ca(OH)2 distribution homogeneously within the root canals, two radiographs were taken from each sample in the mesiodistal and buccolingual directions. Ca(OH)2 was reapplied to samples that did not show homogeneous distribution. Then the cavities were closed with temporary filling material (Nucavfil, FSP Dental, UK). The samples were kept at 37o under 100% humidity for 1 week.

Teeth (n=32) were randomly divided into 4 different groups, with 8 teeth in each group, based on the irrigation solutions to be used to remove Ca(OH)2 from the root canals and the irrigation activation method (n=8). After the temporary filling material was removed, the root canals were prepared for 10s at the working length with the ProTaper Next rotary instrument system file coded X5 (Dentsply Tulsa Dental Specialties, Tulsa, OK, USA) and the X-Smart endodontic motor (Dentsply, Tulsa, OK, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for use.

In all groups except EDTA group, the chelation agent was activated with the EndoActivator at 10,000 cycles/min for 60s with activation tip of the EndoActivator 25.04 which was placed in the root canals 1 mm short of the working length. Finally, the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of distilled water in all groups. NaOCl and sterile distilled water were not activated in any way during experimental procedures.

EDTA group

Irrigation was done with the traditional syringe technique by a 27 G side-hole endodontic needle with a closed tip. The needle was positioned 1mm short of the working length and moved back and forth throughout the irrigation. Ca(OH)2 was removed from the root canals by applying 5 mL of 5% NaOCl followed by 5mL of 17% EDTA.

EDTA+SA group

5mL of 5% NaOCl followed by 5mL of 17% EDTA was used to remove Ca(OH)2. The chelation agent was activated with the EndoActivator.

IP6+SA group

IP6 (Hangzhou Lianzheng Chemical, Zhejiang, China), prepared at 50% concentration by its manufacturer, was mixed with distilled water and converted into the 1% form, which is used in endodontics. 5mL of 5% NaOCl followed by 5mL of 1% IP6 solution was used to remove Ca(OH)2. The chelation agent was activated with the EndoActivator.

NAC+SA group

NAC (Sisco Research Laboratories, Mumbai, India), produced in pure powder form by its manufacturer, was turned into a solution with a concentration of 200mg/ml by adding 0.2 g to each 1mL of distilled water as a result of the literature review [19]. 5mL of 5% NaOCl followed by 5mL of 200mg/mL NAC solution was used to remove Ca(OH)2. The chelation agent was activated with the EndoActivator.

The samples in each group were scanned with a micro-CT (Bruker micro-CT SkyScan 1275, Kontich, Belgium) a total of 3 times: after shaping the root canals, after placing Ca(OH)2 in the root canals, and after removing Ca(OH)2 from the root canals.

The samples were placed in groups on the rotating platform of the micro-CT to be scanned. The parameters of the X-ray source were set as 8 W, 80 Kv, and 125 µA. A 1.0 mm thick Al filter was used in the scanning. Images were taken three times from each angle. Each image was combined with a 20 ms exposure and made into a single image. Thus, the noise rate in the image was reduced. Other parameters of the device were adjusted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each scan took 40 min.

As a result of the scans, raw images with a pixel size of 25 μm were obtained. NRecon (SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) software was used to reconstruct the raw images. This reconstruction software allowed combining the raw images obtained from the scans and examining the internal structure of the samples. The obtained images were transferred to the Data Viewer program (SkyScan), which allowed the samples to be examined in all three spatial planes. CTAn (v. 1.23.3 Bruker microCT Systems, Kontich, Belgium) software was used for the selection of ROIs and the diameter was kept constant across the samples. The global threshold used for the segmentation and the threshold parameters were kept the same for all the samples. The data for each sample were examined and analyzed by two endodontists who were blinded to the experimental groups.

Data were analyzed with a one-way ANOVA test. For multiple group comparisons, Duncan’s test was used as a post-hoc test. Firstly, the homogeneity of variances, which was a prerequisite for parametric tests, was checked with the Levene test. It was determined that the assumption of homogeneity in variances was met. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to check the normality assumption, and it was determined that normality was achieved. When examining differences between groups, the significance value was determined as p < 0.05. IBM SPSS 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to analyze the data of volume of the root canal after preparation and before and after removing Ca(OH)2.

Discussion

In this study, it was aimed to compare the removal effectiveness of Ca(OH)2 using different chelating agents (EDTA, IP6, and NAC) with micro-CT analysis. The null hypothesis that there is no difference between the effectiveness of the irrigation solution used for calcium hydroxide removal after sonic activation was accepted. In the literature, many studies investigating the removal of Ca(OH)2 from root canals have shown that many different applications were preferred [20, 21]. In the present study, the group in which EDTA was formed without sonic activation was used for comparison with the activated groups, and many studies similar to this are available in the literature. Because, EDTA is the most commonly used chelating agent, it was identified like as a control group. This may be considered a limitation of present study. In their independent studies, Wiseman et al. [21] and Chawla & Kumar [20] used sonic activation as an irrigation technique to remove Ca(OH)2 from root canals. In both studies, different concentrations of NaOCl and EDTA solutions were applied to the root canals, respectively, and sonic activation was performed for 60 s. In this study, the chelation agent in the relevant group was activated, in accordance with the studies of Wiseman [21] and Chawla & Kumar [20]. This protocol aimed to provide standard irrigation in all groups. Besides, Tomson et al. [22] reported that Ca(OH)2 cannot be completely removed by using EDTA alone without activating it. To increase the effectiveness of irrigation solutions applied to the root canal system, the usage of these solutions with irrigation activation techniques is recommended [23]. Sonic activation systems have been shown to be highly efficient in disinfecting root canals [4].

In different experimental stages of this study, the samples were scanned 3 times with micro-CT. In the first micro-CT evaluation, standardization of the samples was achieved since their preparation volumes of the samples were similar to each other. In the second micro-CT evaluation, the initial volume of Ca(OH)2 applied to the root canals was calculated. When these data were compared with the preparation volumes, findings regarding the filling quality of Ca(OH)2 were obtained. It was observed that Ca(OH)2 applied with a combination of syringes and lentulo spirals filled an average of 90.92% of the root canals, regardless of the groups. This rate was found to be higher than the rates in Smutkeeree et al.‘s [24] study comparing the Ca(OH)2 filling quality of syringe and lentulo systems. The high Ca(OH)2 filling quality of the samples in our study may be due to the fact that the root canals were checked with radiographs and poor-quality fillings were repeated. After the removal of Ca(OH)2 from the root canals, the final volume of Ca(OH)2 was calculated with the third micro-CT scan. In our study, it was observed that the chelation agents (EDTA, IP6, and NAC) used with sonic activation had similar effectiveness in removing Ca(OH)2 from the root canals.

Nassar et al. [11] stated that IP6 had biostatic and biocidal activities against various microbial strains, including Gram (+) and Gram (-) bacteria, as well as C. albicans and E. faecalis. Nassar et al. [8] stated that IP6 had minimal effect on osteoblast cells and effectively removed the smear layer. In a study comparing the effect of IP6 on the bond strength of resins to dentin with EDTA, it was stated that IP6 had similar chelating effectiveness as EDTA [11]. Afshan et al. [25] stated that 1% IP6 had a less erosive effect on root dentin than 17% EDTA. Additionally, Nikhil et al. [26] reported that the effect of 1% IP6 on microhardness was less than that of 17% EDTA. Sumathi [27] investigated the effectiveness of EDTA, IP6, and etidronic acid in removing the smear layer and their effects on dentin erosion. As a result of the study, it was stated that IP6 caused less erosion on the root canal wall than other materials, and its effectiveness in removing the smear layer was similar to other materials. Eymirli et al.7 compared the removal efficiencies of Ca(OH)2 and triple antibiotic paste with laser-activated EDTA and IP6 and stated that there was no significant difference between the efficiencies of the materials as a result of the study. In the study of Falakaloğlu et al. [28], the Ca(OH)2 removal activities of EDTA and IP6 used with syringe irrigation were compared, and the activities of the materials were found to be similar. These findings were compatible with this study.

Raghu et al. [29] compared the effectiveness of chelating agents activated with PUI on removal of Ca(OH)2 and showed there was no statistically difference between chelating agents. Vineeta et al. [30] and Bajaj et al. [31] evaluated to the removal effect of EDTA and chitosan after ultrasonic activation for aqueous-based and oil-based Ca(OH)2 and showed there was no statistically difference between chelating agents for aqueous-based Ca(OH)2. In present study, SURE paste which is water soluble Ca(OH)2 paste, was used and there was no statistically difference between chelating agents after sonic activation. Results of these studies is compatible with present study. Neelakantan et al. [32] evaluated the removal efficiency of Ca(OH)2 with different irrigation solutions and activation techniques and reported that EDTA was statistically significantly more effective after activation. Ma et al. [33] used NaOCl and EDTA with sonic, ultrasonic activation and conventional syringe irrigation for removing of Ca(OH)2 from root canals and compared data with mico-ct. They suggested that there was no statistically significant difference between the activation groups but activation groups were statistically more effective than conventional syringe irrigation.

In recent study, chelating agents (EDTA and poly (AA-co-AA)) with passive ultrasonic activation was not increased effectiveness of Ca(OH)2 removal [34]. This result contradicts the current findings. Such difference could be explained by distinctness of the evaluation methods and chelating agents used between studies. Mehra et al. [35] compared the Ca(OH)2 removal efficacy of chelating agents activated with Endoactivator and suggested that EDTA was the least effective solution. The results’ differences from the current study may be due to differences in irrigation techniques (e.g., needle type, final irrigation procedure), as well as the use of CBCT. Wang et al. [36] evaluated the calcium hydroxide removal effectiveness of different irrigation activation methods using photography and microscopy. They used equal amounts of NaOCl, EDTA, and distilled water in the final irrigation, and found that activation with an endoactivator was significantly more effective than conventional syringe irrigation, but found no statistically significant difference between the activation methods used.

There were many studies on the antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities of NAC in endodontics [12, 37–40], there were limited data about its chelating efficiency. Rajakamuran et al. [9] compared the chelation feature of NAC with EDTA and stated that EDTA and NAC were equally effective in removing the smear layer. They also stated that NAC caused significantly less reduction in dentin microhardness than EDTA [9]. Data in previous studies regarding Ca(OH)2 removal efficiency of NAC as a chelating agent was insufficient. In this study, the Ca(OH)2 removal efficiency of NAC was found to be similar to other irrigation solutions used.

Ca(OH)2 could not be completely removed from the root canals in any sample either with the sonic activation technique or the syringe irrigation in this study. These findings were consistent with Taşdemir et al. [41] and Khaleel et al. [42]. When the groups in the sonic activation technique was used to remove Ca(OH)2 were compared with the group without activation, it was seen that sonic activation was more effective, regardless of the chelation agent. These findings were consistent with many previous studies [2, 41–46].

Conclusions

No difference was found between EDTA, IP6, and NAC used with sonic irrigation in terms of Ca(OH)2 removal efficiency from root canals. These results in that IP6 and NAC can be used as alternatives to EDTA. From a clinical perspective, this result may indicate that it would be advantageous to use a chelating agent that can remove Ca(OH)2 with minimal adverse effects on dentin and activating these solutions will be effective. New studies are needed in the search for alternative chelation agents to EDTA.
