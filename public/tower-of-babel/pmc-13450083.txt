Low sRAGE Level is Associated with Increased Future Exacerbation Risk in Mild-to-Moderate COPD and Patients with Chronic Bronchitis Symptoms
Authors: Yutian Zhang, Bi Ran, Hao Wang, Dianmei Zhou, Kai Zi, Zijian Zeng, Xi Yan, Tao Wang, Lei Chen, Tao Ye, Paul Jones, Jun Chen, Fuqiang Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of patients at high-risk for exacerbation in mild-to-moderate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), particularly those with chronic bronchitis (CB) phenotype exhibiting worse outcomes, is critical for slowing lung function decline and reducing the subsequent healthcare burden. While the soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE) correlates with emphysema severity in COPD, in mild-to-moderate COPD or patients with CB symptoms, no prospective investigation has evaluated the association between sRAGE and exacerbation risk.MethodsSub-cohort data from the COMPASS study, a prospective study in China, were analyzed. Associations between sRAGE levels and exacerbations were assessed treating sRAGE as a continuous variable and categorized into tertile. Negative binomial regression and cox regression were used to assess exacerbation rate and onset time. Subsequent analyses of lung function, COPD Assessment Tool (CAT) score and high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) parameters were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models based on a cutoff determined by Akaike information criteria (AIC).ResultsIn the COMPASS sub-cohort, 201 patients had mild-to-moderate COPD and 230 individuals reported CB symptoms. Continuous sRAGE level was not associated with exacerbation risks, but with earlier onset time. In addition, participants in the lowest sRAGE tertile exhibited significant shorter time to first exacerbation in both populations. The lowest tertile was also associated with increased exacerbation risks in patients with CB and showed a borderline significant association in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. No significant differences in lung function decline, HRCT changes, or CAT score progression were observed between the low- and high-sRAGE groups.ConclusionLower sRAGE level may be associated with an increased exacerbation risk in mild-to-moderate COPD and in patients with CB symptoms. Blood sRAGE is a candidate biomarker for identifying subgroups at higher exacerbation risk in populations at high risk of disease progression.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT04853225; GSK study code 208630.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the third leading cause of death globally, imposes substantial economic and social burden.1 Current therapies recommended by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (GOLD) fail to prevent accelerated lung function decline driven by persistent inflammation and irreversible airway remodeling,2 highlighting the critical need for early intervention in mild-to-moderate COPD (GOLD 1–2) or even pre-COPD.2,3

Exacerbations of COPD (ECOPD) increase local and systemic inflammation and accelerate disease progression. Importantly, exacerbations in mild-to-moderate COPD, which constitute >80% of all COPD cases and remain largely asymptomatic and undiagnosed,4 drive a greater rate of lung function decline than advanced COPD.5 On the other hand, chronic bronchitis (CB), defined as chronic cough and sputum production for at least 3 months per year over 2 consecutive years, is prevalent in 27–35% of patients with COPD6,7 and independently worsens clinical outcomes by accelerating lung function decline,8 increasing exacerbation frequency, and elevating mortality in COPD,9,10 regardless of airflow limitation (FEV1/FVC ≥ 70%). Therefore, identifying patients at high risk of exacerbations among those with mild-to-moderate COPD or CB symptoms, both of whom show accelerated disease progression, is critical for timely intervention to prevent accelerated lung function decline and reduce subsequent healthcare burdens. Previous studies have shown that prior exacerbation history remains the strongest clinical predictor of future COPD exacerbations.11 Although several circulating biomarkers, such as fibrinogen, C-reactive protein, surfactant protein D, have been linked to exacerbation risk, biomarker evidence remains relatively limited in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD and CB symptoms, highlighting the need to for further studies in these less-studied cohorts.12

The soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE) is a truncated variant of the transmembrane receptor RAGE (encoded by the AGER gene), which lacks transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains. sRAGE is produced either via alternative splicing or proteolytic cleavage, and circulates in the bloodstream as a decoy receptor, sequestering pro-inflammatory ligands and attenuating the inflammation response.13 It serves as an important biomarker in various lung diseases such as lung injury,14,15 asthma,16,17 and COPD.18

sRAGE has been identified as a biomarker for COPD severity, particularly the emphysema phenotype. Smith et al reported reduced circulating sRAGE levels in patients with COPD, which was negatively correlated with the severity of airflow limitation.18 Mendelian randomization studies further suggested a causal protective role of sRAGE in lung function.19 Lower circulating sRAGE levels are associated with increased emphysema severity,20 especially HRCT-defined centrilobular emphysema presence, emphysema progression and severity, but not associated with HRCT-defined airway wall thickness or paraseptal emphysema.21 Notably, the AGER rs2070600 (Gly82Ser) polymorphism is associated with lower plasma sRAGE levels in TESRA and ECLIPSE, but not associated with DLCO% predicted or lung density.13 The AGER rs2070600 polymorphism exhibits inter-population variation in allele frequency, with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 21.9% in East Asian populations compared to 7.6% in Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry, suggesting this population-specific genetic architecture might contribute to inter-ethnic differences in baseline sRAGE levels between Asian and European cohorts.22 Taken together, these findings indicate that circulating sRAGE levels may partly reflect emphysema-related structural changes.

Nevertheless, the association between sRAGE and disease progression and clinical outcomes in COPD remains controversial across different studies. Low sRAGE level correlated with accelerated emphysema progression and predicted annual FEV1 decline in the ECLIPSE study, but not in the COPDGene, SPIROMICS, or SCCOR.23,24 Similarly, although sRAGE was linked to severe exacerbations in COPDGene, this finding was absent in SPIROMICS and SUMMIT post-hoc analyses.12,25 However, these studies predominantly enrolled patients with moderate-to-very-severe COPD. Given the clinical importance of early risk stratification, evidence remains limited in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD or CB symptoms. To date, no prospective investigation has evaluated the association between sRAGE and clinical outcomes in studies exclusively focused on these populations.

Our study aimed to assess the association between sRAGE levels and clinical outcomes in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD and individuals with CB symptoms, using data from the COMPASS study.26

Materials and Methods

Study Design

COMPASS was a multicenter, prospective, non-interventional study to investigate the progression and endotypes of COPD in China. The study protocol was described in detail elsewhere.26 Briefly, between April 2020 and June 2024, approximately 2000 participants were recruited, including never smokers, CB patients with no airflow limitation, and COPD patients across GOLD grades 1 to 4. CB was defined as chronic mucus hypersecretion (≥ 3 months of cough and sputum production annually over the past 2 years) and a baseline post-bronchodilator (post-BD) FEV1/FVC ratio ≥ 70%. A sub-cohort of approximately 400 participants was predefined in the study protocol and underwent blood biomarker assessments (IP-10, sRAGE, CC16, HbA1c) and HRCT scans. All participants were followed for 2.5 years. For the present analysis, we integrated the baseline and follow-up data of this prespecified sub-cohort, including blood sRAGE levels, exacerbations, spirometry, and HRCT quantitative parameters.

Population

This study aimed to assess the association between sRAGE level and clinical outcomes in two populations from the COMPASS sub-cohort: (1) patients with mild-to-moderate COPD (GOLD 1 and 2), with and without CB, and (2) patients with CB symptoms, with and without airflow limitation, as illustrated in Figure 1. Outcomes

The primary outcome is the association between sRAGE levels and exacerbation rate, and time to first exacerbation during follow-up. An exacerbation is defined as the occurrence of an episode of increased cough (with or without phlegm), shortness of breath or chest symptoms (chest discomfort or tightness) which lasted 48 h or more and interfered with the subject’s usual activities.26 The subjects were asked for a history of moderate/severe exacerbations at each clinic visit, including exacerbation visits. Additionally, subjects were contacted by phone every 3 months by the study staff and asked about their exacerbation details for the previous 3 months. Secondary outcomes include changes in lung function (including FEV1, FVC, and MMEF etc)., COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score, and HRCT-derived parameters (including a. %LAA−950, indicating percentage of emphysema; b. Pi10, a parameter of airway wall thickness; and c. DPMgas_trapping, indicating percentage of air trapping) from the baseline. sRAGE level was measured at the central laboratory by a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems, Catalog Number: SRG00) using a Tecan SunriseTM microplate reader.

Statistical Analysis

For primary outcomes, sRAGE was first analyzed as a log-transformed continuous variable considering its skewed distribution. Second, sRAGE was categorized using tertile cutoffs, in accordance with the cutoffs in SUMMIT biomarker study.25 Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were then used to assess potential non-linear associations between sRAGE and exacerbation-related outcomes. Finally, to further characterize low-sRAGE subgroups within each population, percentile-based cutoffs from the 5th to 50th percentiles were compared using Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the cutoff with the lowest AIC was selected for subsequent analyses.

Exacerbation rates were analyzed using negative binomial regression models to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs). Time to first exacerbation was analyzed using Cox regression models, with the proportional hazards assumption assessed before model fitting. Continuous outcomes (eg, FEV1, CAT score, and HRCT parameters) were assessed using linear mixed-effect models.

Statistical models were specified according to outcome type. For exacerbation outcomes, model 1 was adjusted for baseline age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status (never, former, or current smoker), post-BD FEV1% predicted, and exacerbation history 12 months prior to enrollment (the established clinical predictor of future exacerbations). Model 2 further added inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, and HRCT-defined emphysema (defined as the percentage of low attenuation lung voxels with a density < −950 Hounsfield units [%LAA−950] ≥ 5%) to account for treatment exposure and parenchymal structural damage. Separate supplementary models additionally adjusted for blood eosinophil counts (associated with higher exacerbation risk)27 and biomass exposure (a great risk factor of COPD especially in low- and middle-income countries, LMICs)28 to rule out potential confounding. Zero-inflated negative binomial models were performed additionally for exacerbations, accounting for excess zero events given that most patients experienced no exacerbations during follow-up.

For all other clinical outcomes, Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, ICS use, and post-BD FEV1% predicted. Model2 further adjusted for exacerbation history and emphysema. Missing data were managed using the available case method. Random effects incorporated participant-specific random intercepts and slopes to account for potential autocorrelation in repeated measures.

Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

When analyzed as a continuous variable, the association between sRAGE and exacerbation risk did not reach statistical significance in either population, yet lower sRAGE levels were consistently associated with shorter time to first exacerbation across both populations (Table 2). We next examined associations by sRAGE tertiles, aligned with the analytical cutoffs of the SUMMIT biomarker study25 (e-Table 1). In patients with mild-to-moderate COPD, the lowest tertile showed a non-significant trend toward higher exacerbation risk compared with the middle tertile (P = 0.065) and the combined middle and highest tertiles (P = 0.071). However, the lowest tertile was significantly associated with shorter time to first exacerbation. Among patients with CB symptoms, lower sRAGE tertiles were significantly associated with both increased exacerbation rate and earlier time to first exacerbation (Table 3 and Figure 2). RCS analyses suggested a trend toward higher IRR (Incidence Rate Ratio) and HR (Hazard Ratio) at lower sRAGE levels in both populations (Figure 3). To identify cutoff values for subsequent analyses, we further evaluated a series of percentile-based cutoffs (5% to 50% at 5% intervals) using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) in the two populations. Exacerbations

Among patients with mild-to-moderate COPD (population 1), 41 of 201 (20.4%) participants experienced at least one exacerbation. Individuals with lower sRAGE level experienced a significantly higher rate of exacerbations than those with higher sRAGE level after covariates adjustment (model1, IRR 2.462, 95% CI [1.076 to 5.690], P = 0.023, Table 5). In patients with CB symptoms (population 2), 38 of 230 (16.5%) participants had at least one exacerbation, and the IRR between the lower sRAGE group versus higher sRAGE group was 3.281 after covariates adjustment (model 1, IRR 3.281, 95% CI [1.233 to 9.033], P = 0.011, Table 5). This significance was consistent after further adjustment (model2, Table 5). Furthermore, lower sRAGE level was significantly associated with earlier onset of exacerbations in both populations after adjusting using both model1 and model2 (Table 5 and e-Figure 1). HRCT Parameters

Participants underwent HRCT scans at baseline and at the 2.5-year follow-up. Among mild-to-moderate COPD patients, the annual rate of change in %LAA−950 was slightly higher in the high sRAGE group than in the low sRAGE group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.21, Table 6). Similarly, the rates of change in Pi10 and DPMgas_trapping were not significantly different between the groups. These nonsignificant findings were consistent in patients with CB with and without airflow limitation (Table 6).

CAT Score

The CAT score remained relatively stable over 2.5 years of follow-up, and the rate of change in the score was not significantly different between the low and high sRAGE groups for both populations. (Table 6).

Discussion

Our analysis of the prospective COMPASS sub-cohort demonstrated that lower circulating sRAGE levels were associated with increased exacerbation risk and shorter time to first exacerbation across patients with mild-to-moderate COPD (GOLD 1 and 2) and those with CB symptoms regardless of airflow limitation. This association remained significant after adjustment for prior exacerbation history, the established clinical predictor of future events despite inherent recall bias. These findings suggest that sRAGE might serve as a candidate objective biomarker for identifying subgroups with higher exacerbation risk in early-stage COPD at high risk of disease progression. However, the prognostic value of sRAGE warrants further independent validation in larger, multi-ethnic cohorts.

Our findings differ with the null results from the SUMMIT trial post-hoc biomarker study, in which no significant association was observed between sRAGE levels and exacerbations, hospitalizations, or FEV1 decline.25 Notably, while the tertile cutoffs were lower in the SUMMIT biomarker study than in our cohort (e-Table 3), this discrepancy remained when using a comparable tertile-based analytical approach, ruling out cutoff selection as a primary explanation. Although the SUMMIT trial has larger sample size (biomarker study n=1673), two population differences likely explain this divergence. First, the SUMMIT trial enrolled patients with moderate COPD (mean FEV1 approximately 60% predicted), reflecting greater baseline disease severity compared with our cohort of milder disease and CB phenotypes (mean FEV1 approximately 75–82% predicted). Second, cardiovascular burden is independently associated with both exacerbation risk and circulating sRAGE levels,29,30 the SUMMIT cohort was characterized for increased cardiovascular risk,25 and had a correspondingly higher exacerbation rate (36.6%, 604/1649) than our mild-to-moderate COPD (20.4%) and CB (16.9%) cohorts; The cardiovascular confounding may dilute respiratory-specific biomarker signals and contribute to the observed discrepant findings. However, consistent with SUMMIT, we observed no association between sRAGE and longitudinal changes in lung function or HRCT-derived structural parameters. These divergent associations for exacerbation endpoints, with consistently null results for functional and structural outcomes, indicate the discrepancy might stem from population heterogeneity rather than methodological variation, and sRAGE might primarily track structural lung damage in more advanced stage, whereas its link to exacerbation risk is context-dependent. Taken together, these findings indicate that the association of sRAGE and exacerbations may differ across patient subgroups, and null findings from SUMMIT’s high-cardiovascular-risk moderate COPD cohort should be extrapolated with caution to milder disease stages or airway-predominant phenotypes.

In our study, no significant association was observed between low sRAGE levels and 2.5-year progression of FEV1 decline, HRCT-defined emphysema progression (%LAA−950), airway remodeling (Pi10), or small airway gas trapping (DPMgas_trapping). These results align with findings in COPDGene, SPIROMICS, SUMMIT and Pittsburgh COPD SCCOR, but contrast with results from the ECLIPSE cohort, where lower sRAGE was linked to both faster FEV1 decline and greater emphysema progression (defined by PD15). This discrepancy may also reflect differences in disease severity because ECLIPSE enrolled patients with more advanced COPD (mean FEV1 approximately 50% predicted), whereas the other cohorts comprised milder disease. Patients with severe airflow limitation typically exhibit lower sRAGE levels with established structural lung damage, which may enhance the association of the biomarker with disease progression. Restricted to patients with mild-to-moderate COPD and CB (with or without airflow limitation), our analysis further found no association of sRAGE levels and progressions of lung function, emphysema and airway remodeling. These findings may be due to limited statistical power, the relatively short 2.5-year follow-up period, and COVID-19 related public health measures that likely attenuated natural COPD progression. Alternatively, sRAGE may lack sufficient sensitivity for early-stage structural or functional changes, where the degree of emphysema is insufficient to cause detectable change of sRAGE level. Notably, baseline sRAGE levels did not differ significantly by GOLD grade, smoking status, emphysema status, or prior exacerbation history. This finding indicates sRAGE has limited utility as a cross-sectional marker of disease severity or phenotype in this mild-to-moderate COPD and CB cohort, consistent with its reduced sensitivity to early-stage structural lung changes. To our knowledge, no prior studies have evaluated sRAGE in relation to CAT score changes, which we found to be non-significant. Notably, we observed a slower decline of MMEF and MMEF% predicted in the lower sRAGE group, which might require replication in larger cohorts. Collectively, these findings indicate that sRAGE may be more relevant with higher exacerbation risk than disease progression in early stage of COPD.

Beyond patients with mild-to-moderate COPD, we also evaluated the aasoociation in patients with CB symptoms (with and without airflow limitation). CB represents a major phenotype affecting approximately 30% of COPD patients and is associated with lung function decline,8 increased exacerbation risk, and reduced quality of life, even in the absence of airflow obstruction.9,10 This establishes CB not merely as a COPD-associated phenotype but also as a critical presence of pre-COPD at a high risk of disease progression. Although sputum mucin, particularly MUC5AC, have been linked to CB-related symptoms, exacerbations, and lung-function decline,31,32 circulating biomarkers for outcome stratification in symptom-defined CB populations remain poorly defined, especially during the pre-COPD phase. In our study, lower sRAGE level was associated with a higher exacerbation risk and shorter time to first exacerbation in patients with CB (with FEV1% predicted ≥ 50%), irrespective of airflow limitation, suggesting that sRAGE may help identify a subgroup of CB patients at increased risk of exacerbations, including those who have not yet developed spirometry-defined COPD. However, further validation in larger and independent cohorts is needed before interpreting these findings in clinical settings.

While sRAGE has traditionally been linked to emphysema severity, our analyses demonstrated that the association between low sRAGE level and increased exacerbation risk in patients with CB, even after adjustment for HRCT-defined emphysema. These findings indicate an alternative pathophysiological role of sRAGE in airway inflammation, particularly in the CB phenotype characterized by mucus hypersecretion and neutrophilic inflammation. We hypothesize, as a speculative mechanism, that low sRAGE levels reflect impaired clearance of RAGE ligands, which could drive epithelial RAGE activation, subsequent neutrophilic inflammation, MUC5AC overproduction, and increased susceptibility to infection-induced exacerbations. This is supported by transient early elevation of sRAGE during acute exacerbations and initial smoke exposure,33–35 with systemic levels declining progressively with disease severity, indicating a dual role of sRAGE as both a protective decoy receptor and an indicator of ongoing inflammatory processes in the lung parenchyma as well as in the airways. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the potential specific mechanisms.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate the association between blood biomarker sRAGE and clinical outcomes exclusively in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD within an East Asian population, demonstrating that lower sRAGE was associated with a higher exacerbation risk in mild-to-moderate COPD and CB patients. However, limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may introduce potential instability into our multivariable regression models. This may also result in model inflation and increased type I errors. Second, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, during which changes in social distancing and public health infection control measures may have reduced the overall incidence of exacerbations and lowered the reporting rates of moderate exacerbations. Third, AGER rs2070600 (Gly82Ser) genotyping was unavailable in this cohort. This polymorphism exhibits inter-ethnic allele frequency variation that affects baseline circulating sRAGE levels, but consistent evidence from TESRA, ECLIPSE20 and Chinese Han populations36 shows no association with DLCO% predicted, lung density, or impaired lung function. This indicates the polymorphism modulates baseline sRAGE expression but not its biological role in COPD, and is thus unlikely to substantially confound our core findings. Fourth, due to unavailable data, the associations between sRAGE and clinical outcomes were not adjusted for renal function (which modulates sRAGE clearance) or blood draw time since last cigarette (which affects sRAGE level acutely).37 In addition, patients with CB symptoms, particularly those who have not developed irreversible airflow limitation, were not analyzed separately in this study due to limited sample size (N = 97) and extremely low events during 2.5-year follow-up. The association between sRAGE and clinical outcomes in CB patients should be addressed in larger cohorts with enough follow-up time. Finally, although the COMPASS was prospectively designed, this post-hoc analysis remains observational and is therefore subject to potential bias, including selection bias and residual confounding. Future studies are needed to explore the potential prognostic roles of sRAGE across COPD severities and phenotypes in larger cohorts.

Conclusions

Lower sRAGE level was associated with increased exacerbation risk and shorter time to first exacerbation in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD and CB symptoms. In patients with mild-to-moderate COPD and CB, low baseline sRAGE level may be a candidate biomarker for identifying patients who may require closer clinical monitoring.
