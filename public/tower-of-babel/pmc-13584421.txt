Age-related differences in pediatric anaphylaxis: a single-center experience
Authors: Enes Çelik, Hande Yüksel Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric anaphylaxis is a potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction with age-related differences in triggers and clinical manifestations. Real-life data are important to improve recognition, risk assessment, and management. This study aimed to evaluate the demographic and clinical characteristics, triggers, management, and factors associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis in children, with particular emphasis on age-related differences.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted at a pediatric allergy clinic between April 1, 2023, and July 1, 2025. Potential cases among children aged 0–18 years were identified using anaphylaxis-related ICD-10 codes and were subsequently re-evaluated according to the World Allergy Organization 2020 and European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology 2021 diagnostic criteria. Demographic characteristics, triggers, clinical manifestations, treatment, clinical outcomes, and laboratory findings were analyzed.ResultsA total of 116 patients were included; 45 (38.8%) were female, and the mean age was 9.7 ± 5.9 years. Anaphylaxis most commonly occurred at home (66.4%), and oral exposure was the most frequent route of allergen exposure (67.2%). Foods were the most common triggers (42.2%), followed by drugs (37.1%) and Hymenoptera venom (12.9%). Tree nuts, cow’s milk, and hen’s egg were the leading food triggers. Food-induced anaphylaxis was significantly more frequent in children aged 0–5 years, whereas drug-induced reactions were more common in older children. Cardiovascular involvement was more frequent in patients aged ≥ 12 years and in Hymenoptera venom- and drug-induced reactions, whereas neurological involvement was more frequent in children aged 0–5 years. Hymenoptera venom-induced reactions had the shortest onset time (15 min [IQR, 5–30]). Intramuscular epinephrine was administered to 94.0% of patients. Moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis was observed in 64 patients. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, a history of food allergy was independently associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis (OR, 5.08; 95% CI, 1.32–19.52; p = 0.018).ConclusionClinical manifestations and triggers of pediatric anaphylaxis varied according to age. Food-induced reactions were more common in younger children, whereas drug-induced reactions increased with age. A history of food allergy may indicate an increased risk for moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07619-3.

Background

Anaphylaxis is a systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset and the potential to progress to life-threatening or fatal outcomes [1]. The reported incidence of anaphylaxis in children varies widely worldwide, ranging from 1 to 761 cases per 100,000 person-years [2]. Despite its potentially life-threatening nature and increasing incidence, pediatric anaphylaxis may remain underdiagnosed, particularly in emergency care settings, which can delay appropriate recognition and treatment [1, 3].

IgE-mediated anaphylaxis is regarded as the classical and most common mechanism, whereas non–IgE-mediated reactions may occur through immunologic or non-immunologic pathways [4]. The triggers of anaphylaxis vary by age and geographical region, but foods, drugs, and Hymenoptera venom are consistently among the leading causes [5]. In children, foods are prominent triggers, particularly cow’s milk, peanuts, and tree nuts, although the implicated allergens may differ according to regional dietary habits [6]. Antibiotics and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the main causes of drug-induced anaphylaxis [7].

Anaphylaxis most commonly involves the skin or mucous membranes, followed by respiratory and gastrointestinal manifestations. However, up to 10% of patients may present without cutaneous findings, which can contribute to misdiagnosis or delayed recognition. Prompt intramuscular (IM) epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis, and delayed administration has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality [8].

Importantly, pediatric anaphylaxis is not a uniform clinical entity across childhood. Recent studies have demonstrated age-related differences in both triggers and clinical manifestations [9–13]. Food-induced anaphylaxis is particularly prominent in infants and younger children, whereas drug-induced anaphylaxis becomes relatively more frequent with increasing age [9–12]. Age-related differences have also been reported in organ-system involvement and reaction severity; cardiovascular involvement has been observed more frequently in adolescents in some cohorts, while older age has been associated with greater anaphylaxis severity in others [9, 10]. More recent evidence further suggests that age itself may be an important determinant of the clinical phenotype of pediatric anaphylaxis, particularly with respect to respiratory manifestations [13]. Potential risk factors for severe pediatric anaphylaxis have also been investigated, including age, trigger-related factors, and underlying allergic diseases; however, reported associations have varied across studies [12, 14]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of considering age when evaluating the likely trigger, clinical presentation, and severity of anaphylaxis in children.

Despite growing evidence of age-related heterogeneity, findings across pediatric studies are not uniform, and differences in study populations, age-group definitions, trigger distributions, and severity assessments make direct comparisons difficult [9–13]. Furthermore, the distribution of anaphylaxis triggers may vary across populations, supporting the value of regional real-world data [5, 9–12]. Studies evaluating age-related patterns together with clinical characteristics, management, and factors associated with reaction severity may therefore provide additional information for age-specific recognition and risk assessment in pediatric anaphylaxis.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the demographic and clinical characteristics, triggers, management, and factors associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis in children, with particular emphasis on age-related differences.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Division of Pediatric Allergy and Immunology, Ankara Atatürk Sanatorium Training and Research Hospital, Ankara, Türkiye, between April 1, 2023, and July 1, 2025. Children aged 0–18 years who were evaluated for suspected anaphylaxis during the study period were screened. Potential anaphylaxis cases were identified from electronic medical records of the Pediatric Allergy and Immunology Division using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes T78.0, T78.2, T80.5, and T88.6, which correspond to anaphylactic reaction or shock. All pediatric patients presenting to our hospital with suspected anaphylaxis are routinely evaluated by the Pediatric Allergy and Immunology Division either at the time of initial presentation or during the observation period. Accordingly, the study period was defined according to the date of the anaphylaxis event, which also corresponded to the date of evaluation by our division.

All patients identified through these ICD-10 codes were subsequently re-evaluated according to the World Allergy Organization (WAO) 2020 [4] and European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) 2021 [15] anaphylaxis diagnostic criteria. This step was necessary because, in Türkiye, an anaphylaxis-related ICD-10 code is required for issuing an epinephrine auto-injector report; therefore, some patients without a documented anaphylactic episode who were considered at high risk may also have been registered under these codes. Patients who fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis according to either the WAO 2020 or EAACI 2021 criteria and had sufficient clinical data available in their medical records were included. Patients who did not meet either diagnostic criterion or had incomplete medical records were excluded. For patients who experienced more than one anaphylaxis event during the study period, only the first event was included in the analysis. Anaphylaxis events occurring before the study period were not included in the analysis. Demographic and clinical characteristics, suspected triggers, treatment details, and laboratory findings were obtained from the medical records. Clinical manifestations were categorized according to the organ system involved. Neurological involvement was defined as the presence of persistent crying, altered consciousness, irritability, or paresthesia. Cardiovascular involvement included hypotension, lightheadedness, syncope, chest pain, or palpitations [16]. Suspected triggers were assigned primarily on the basis of clinical history and the temporal relationship between exposure and symptom onset, with allergological testing used as supportive evidence when available. Clinical manifestations, severity, and acute treatment of the index anaphylaxis episode were derived from documentation of the acute event, whereas allergological evaluation data were obtained predominantly from post-anaphylaxis follow-up records.

Anaphylaxis severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe according to the EAACI position paper on the management of anaphylaxis in childhood by Muraro et al. [17]. This grading system was selected because it provides a symptom-based framework applicable to pediatric anaphylaxis and could be consistently applied to the clinical information available in retrospective medical records. Although newer grading systems have been proposed, a standardized and internationally accepted severity scoring system for anaphylaxis remains an unmet need [1]. Severity was assigned based on the most severe clinical manifestation documented during the reaction. For comparative analyses, patients were categorized into mild and moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis groups.

Allergological evaluation

Allergological evaluation was performed as part of routine clinical care when clinically indicated and was not conducted specifically for the purposes of this study. These assessments were performed predominantly during post-anaphylaxis follow-up, and the available results were retrospectively extracted from the medical records. Skin prick testing (SPT) and/or measurement of allergen-specific IgE levels were performed in selected patients according to clinical indication and were directed either toward the suspected culprit agent of anaphylaxis or toward general atopic assessment in patients with concomitant allergic symptoms. SPT was performed using standardized allergen extracts (Lofarma, Milan, Italy), and a positive result was defined as a wheal diameter ≥ 3 mm greater than that of the negative control. Total and allergen-specific IgE levels were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay system (IMMULITE 2000 XPI, Siemens, Germany), and allergen-specific IgE levels ≥ 0.35 kUA/L were considered positive.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons of continuous variables among more than two independent groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc correction or the Kruskal–Wallis test with post hoc pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni correction was used, depending on data distribution. For comparisons between two independent groups, continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Monte Carlo exact test, as appropriate.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis. Candidate variables for the multivariable logistic regression model were selected based on findings from univariable analyses together with clinical relevance. Variables with a p value < 0.20 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion; IV/IM trigger exposure (p = 0.201) was additionally retained because its p value was close to the prespecified threshold and because of its potential clinical relevance to anaphylaxis severity. Given the sample size and the number of moderate-to-severe events, the number of variables included in the final model was restricted to reduce the risk of overfitting. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a p value > 0.05 indicating adequate fit. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for variables included in the final multivariable model, and graphical representations were prepared using GraphPad Prism software. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Ankara Atatürk Sanatorium Training and Research Hospital (Approval No: 2024-BÇEK/376). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because of the retrospective design and the use of anonymized patient data.

Results

A total of 195 patients were evaluated for anaphylaxis during the study period; after exclusion of 79 patients who did not meet the diagnostic criteria or had missing data, 116 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among them, 52 had mild anaphylaxis and 64 had moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis.

Flow diagram showing patient selection and classification according to anaphylaxis severity

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population according to age group are shown in Table 1. Of the 116 patients, 45 (38.8%) were female, and the mean age was 9.7 ± 5.9 years. A family history of atopy was present in 30 patients (25.9%), and 60 patients (51.7%) had a history of at least one atopic disease. Anaphylaxis most commonly occurred at home (66.4%), and oral exposure was the most frequent route of allergen exposure (67.2%). A known allergy to the culprit trigger was present in 16.4% of patients. Symptoms developed within the first 30 min in 51.7% of patients, and 79.3% received medical treatment within 1 h. Exercise was documented as a cofactor in 2 patients (1.7%). The majority of patients had no prior history of anaphylaxis; only six patients had experienced an anaphylaxis event before the study period. These previous events were not included in the analysis. Although an epinephrine auto-injector was available in 4 patients, it was used during the episode in only one patient.

When patients were compared according to age group, allergic rhinitis was significantly less frequent in the 0–5-year age group than in older children (p = 0.017). Atopic dermatitis and food allergy were more common in the 0–5-year age group in the overall comparison; however, pairwise comparisons did not reveal statistically significant differences between individual age groups. Other demographic and clinical characteristics were similar among age groups.

The distribution of anaphylaxis triggers according to age group is shown in Table 2. Foods were the most common triggers (42.2%) and were significantly more frequent in children aged 0–5 years than in older children (68.4%; p < 0.001). Tree nuts, cow’s milk, and hen’s egg were the leading food triggers. Cow’s milk and hen’s egg were significantly more frequent in children aged 0–5 years (both p < 0.001). Drugs were the second most common triggers (37.1%) and were significantly less frequent in children aged 0–5 years than in older age groups (15.8%; p = 0.003). Antibiotics were the most common drug trigger, followed by NSAIDs. Hymenoptera venom accounted for 12.9% of reactions and tended to be more frequent in older children, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.057). The other triggers category comprised five patients. In three patients, anaphylaxis was temporally associated with inhalational exposure to cats, with sensitization to cat allergens supported by positive SPT and/or allergen-specific IgE results. In the remaining two patients, anaphylaxis occurred in temporal association with skin contact with multiple topical creams/products; however, allergological testing for the individual products or their components could not be performed, and a specific causative agent could not be identified. Idiopathic anaphylaxis was observed in 4 (3.4%) patients, respectively.

Clinical manifestations and organ system involvement according to age group are presented in Table 3. The most common manifestations were urticaria (83.6%) and angioedema (49.1%). Compared with children aged 0–5 years, patients aged ≥ 12 years more frequently had pruritus, dyspnea, and lightheadedness (p = 0.025, p = 0.010, and p = 0.003, respectively), whereas cough and irritability were more common in children aged 0–5 years (p = 0.010 and p = 0.002, respectively). Throat tightness was more frequently reported in patients aged ≥ 12 years, and persistent crying was more frequent in children aged 0–5 years (p = 0.031 and p = 0.014, respectively); however, post hoc pairwise comparisons were not significant for these manifestations.

Additional analyses showed that the median time to symptom onset differed significantly according to anaphylaxis trigger (p < 0.001). The shortest onset time was observed in Hymenoptera venom-induced reactions (15 min [IQR, 5–30]), followed by drug-induced reactions (30 min [IQR, 10–90]), food-induced reactions (60 min [IQR, 20–120]), and reactions due to other triggers (180 min [IQR, 90–300]). In pairwise comparisons, symptom onset was significantly earlier in Hymenoptera venom-induced reactions than in food-induced and other trigger-induced reactions, while drug-induced reactions had a significantly shorter onset time than reactions due to other triggers. Cardiovascular involvement also differed significantly according to trigger and was more frequent in Hymenoptera venom- and drug-induced reactions than in food-induced reactions (53.3%, 39.5%, and 12.2%, respectively; p = 0.006). No significant differences were observed among trigger groups in terms of skin/mucosal, respiratory, gastrointestinal, or neurological involvement.

Management, clinical outcomes, and laboratory findings according to anaphylaxis severity are shown in Table 4. IM epinephrine was administered to 94.0% of patients. Although the overall rate of IM epinephrine administration did not differ significantly between severity groups, the need for two or more doses was higher in the moderate-to-severe group (p = 0.019). Systemic corticosteroids and antihistamines were administered to 96.6% and 88.8% of patients, respectively, without significant differences between groups. Intravenous (IV) fluid therapy, oxygen therapy, and inhaled β2-agonist treatment were significantly more frequent in patients with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis (p = 0.001, p = 0.010, and p = 0.001, respectively). Observation time was also longer in the moderate-to-severe group (p < 0.001). Overall, 94.0% of patients were admitted to the hospital, 3.4% required intensive care unit admission, 2.6% developed biphasic anaphylaxis, and 1.7% had refractory anaphylaxis. Eosinophil count and total IgE levels did not differ significantly between the severity groups.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study provides real-life data on pediatric anaphylaxis evaluated in a pediatric allergy clinic, with a particular focus on age-related differences and clinical severity. Reactions most commonly occurred at home and after oral exposure. Foods were the leading triggers, especially in children aged 0–5 years, whereas drugs were the second most common triggers and became more frequent in older children. Cutaneous and mucosal findings were the most common clinical manifestations, and symptom patterns differed across age groups. In addition, a history of food allergy was independently associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis.

Anaphylaxis was more common in boys, consistent with the pediatric literature [2]. Most reactions occurred at home, and oral exposure was the most frequent route of trigger exposure. Similarly, previous studies have reported that pediatric anaphylaxis most commonly occurs at home and that triggers are most often encountered through the oral route [10, 18]. This pattern may be explained by the predominance of food-related anaphylaxis in childhood, as food exposures most commonly occur in the home environment and through the oral route.

Tree nuts, cow’s milk, and hen’s egg were the leading food triggers of anaphylaxis in our cohort. Data from the European Anaphylaxis Registry [19] showed that foods were the most common elicitors of pediatric anaphylaxis, with tree nuts, peanuts, cow’s milk, and hen’s egg among the leading food triggers. Food trigger patterns may vary according to geographic region, dietary habits, and cultural differences in food consumption [5]. In line with this, previous pediatric studies from Türkiye have reported some variation in the ranking of food triggers; however, nuts, cow’s milk, and hen’s egg are consistently among the most frequently identified causes of food-induced anaphylaxis [9, 10, 20–23]. In the present cohort, cow’s milk and hen’s egg were significantly more frequent in children aged 0–5 years, consistent with previous reports indicating that milk and egg are the predominant triggers of food-induced anaphylaxis in infants and young children [11, 24]. Similarly, Klim et al. recently reported cow’s milk and hen’s egg as the leading triggers of food-induced anaphylaxis in children younger than 2 years [25]. They also highlighted the challenges of recognizing anaphylaxis in very young children, particularly because nonverbal children may be unable to communicate certain symptoms. In that cohort, only approximately one in ten patients who received medication from healthcare professionals were treated with epinephrine, despite a substantial proportion of severe reactions. Difficulties in recognizing age-specific or nonverbal manifestations may contribute to delayed recognition and treatment. In our cohort, irritability and persistent crying were more frequently observed in younger children, further emphasizing the importance of age-specific recognition to facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

Notably, sesame and other seeds were the fourth most common food trigger category in our cohort, ranking higher than peanut. This finding is consistent with previous data from Türkiye, where seed allergies constitute a prominent component of the pediatric food allergy spectrum. In a Turkish pediatric cohort of patients with IgE-mediated food allergy, seed allergy was reported in 24.7% of patients, with sesame being the most common seed allergen, whereas peanut allergy was reported in 14.9% [26]. The clinical relevance of sesame is further supported by a recent pediatric study from Türkiye, in which 46.4% of children with confirmed sesame allergy had experienced at least one anaphylactic reaction [27]. These findings, together with our results, highlight the regional importance of sesame and other seeds as clinically relevant food allergens in Turkish children.

Drugs were the second most common triggers of anaphylaxis. Among drug-related reactions, antibiotics were the leading triggers, followed by NSAIDs. In the literature, drugs have been reported as the second or third most common trigger of pediatric anaphylaxis, depending on the study population, age distribution, and clinical setting [10, 18–21, 23, 28]. Consistent with the present findings, antibiotics and NSAIDs are generally among the most frequently implicated drug groups [9, 10, 20, 29, 30]. Drug-induced anaphylaxis was more frequent in children aged ≥ 6 years, whereas food-induced anaphylaxis predominated in children aged 0–5 years. This age-related distribution is also in line with previous reports showing that food-related reactions are more common in younger children, while drug-related reactions become more frequent with increasing age [7, 10, 31, 32].

In our study, cutaneous and mucosal manifestations were the most frequently involved system, followed by gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous pediatric anaphylaxis studies reporting cutaneous and mucosal involvement as the most common clinical presentation. Respiratory and gastrointestinal manifestations are generally reported as the second and third most frequently involved systems, although their relative frequencies vary across studies [10, 19, 20, 23, 33]. While no significant age-related difference was found for the three most commonly involved systems, cardiovascular involvement was more frequent in patients aged ≥ 12 years, whereas neurological manifestations were more frequent in children aged 0–5 years. Similarly, previous studies have reported more frequent cardiovascular symptoms in older children, while neurological or behavioral findings may be more prominent in infants and younger children [10, 18, 22]. In the European Anaphylaxis Registry [19], vomiting and cough were more prevalent in younger children, whereas subjective symptoms such as nausea, throat tightness, and dizziness were more common later in childhood. Consistent with this age-related pattern, patients aged ≥ 12 years in our study more frequently presented with dyspnea, pruritus, and lightheadedness, whereas cough and irritability were more common in children aged 0–5 years. Nausea and hypotension also tended to increase with age, while vomiting and reduced alertness tended to decrease. These findings may partly reflect age-related differences in symptom expression and recognition: younger children more often present with objective or behavioral signs, whereas older children and adolescents can more clearly report subjective symptoms such as dyspnea, nausea, chest discomfort, and lightheadedness.

Hymenoptera venom ranked third among triggers but was associated with the shortest symptom onset time. Cardiovascular involvement also differed significantly according to trigger type and was more frequent in Hymenoptera venom- and drug-induced reactions than in food-induced reactions. Consistent with these findings, Serbes et al. [18] reported that venom-induced reactions had the highest rate of rapid symptom onset. This may be explained by the route and kinetics of exposure, as Hymenoptera stings and some drug exposures, particularly IV or IM administrations, may result in more rapid systemic exposure than food allergens, which require gastrointestinal digestion and absorption.

IM epinephrine was administered to 94.0% of patients. In contrast, Soyak Aytekin et al. [34], using nationwide Ministry of Health data from Türkiye, reported a substantially lower epinephrine administration rate. The higher rate observed in our cohort may partly reflect the characteristics of our readily accessible hospital-based setting and the specialized pediatric allergy care environment, where regular anaphylaxis training is provided to healthcare professionals. The potential importance of education and professional training is supported by a recent nationwide study from Türkiye, in which 86.5% of primary care physicians correctly identified epinephrine as the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis, and higher levels of professional training were associated with significantly better knowledge scores [35]. However, because the retrospective records did not allow reliable differentiation between medications administered before hospital arrival and those administered during or after hospital evaluation, the observed epinephrine administration rate should not be interpreted as specifically reflecting prehospital or first-contact management. Therefore, direct comparison with nationwide real-life data should be made cautiously.

A history of food allergy was independently associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis in our cohort. Previous pediatric studies have reported variable predictors of severe anaphylaxis. Older age at reaction onset has been associated with greater severity in some cohorts [12], whereas another large pediatric study identified a history of drug allergy, but not trigger type or overall atopic history, as an independent predictor of severe anaphylaxis [14]. These differences may reflect heterogeneity in study populations, trigger distributions, and severity definitions. Although no fatal reactions occurred in our cohort, previous reviews have suggested that patients with known food allergy, particularly peanut or tree nut allergy, may be at increased risk of fatal anaphylaxis [6]. Our findings add to this evidence by suggesting that a history of food allergy may be associated with an increased likelihood of moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis in children. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective, single-center design and the relatively wide confidence interval. Further studies in larger, multicenter pediatric cohorts are needed to clarify the role of food allergy history in predicting anaphylaxis severity.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting in a pediatric allergy clinic may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, serum tryptase was not included in the analysis because it was not routinely measured in all patients and the timing of measurement was not standardized, precluding a reliable assessment of its association with clinical severity. In contrast, total IgE was retained only as a descriptive marker of the allergic profile of the cohort and was not considered a biomarker of anaphylaxis severity. Third, the relatively small overall sample size and limited numbers in some age- and trigger-based subgroups may have reduced statistical power for subgroup comparisons and restricted the number of covariates that could be included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Therefore, residual confounding by variables not included in the model cannot be excluded. In addition, the association between a history of food allergy and moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively wide confidence interval of the effect estimate. Fourth, anaphylaxis severity was classified using the Muraro et al. [17] grading system. Because currently available severity grading systems are not fully interchangeable, application of a different or more recent grading framework might have resulted in reclassification of some episodes and influenced severity-based comparisons and regression analyses. Fifth, allergological evaluation was not uniformly performed in all patients and was guided by clinical indication; therefore, some suspected triggers could not be objectively confirmed, and trigger attribution in these cases relied primarily on clinical history and the temporal relationship between exposure and symptom onset. Food triggers were also recorded in grouped categories, preventing evaluation of the distribution of individual allergens within categories such as tree nuts, seeds, and legumes. Finally, the retrospective records did not allow reliable differentiation between medications administered before hospital arrival and those administered during or after hospital evaluation, limiting interpretation of treatment timing and setting.

Conclusions

Pediatric anaphylaxis showed age-related differences in triggers and clinical manifestations. Food-induced anaphylaxis predominated in younger children, whereas drug-induced reactions became more frequent with increasing age. A history of food allergy was independently associated with moderate-to-severe anaphylaxis. These findings support the importance of considering age-related patterns and individual clinical characteristics when assessing pediatric anaphylaxis.
