Pharmacologic reversion of Merkel cell carcinoma via CBP/p300 inhibition
Authors: Joseph L. Collura, Kuan Cheok Lei, Mitalee Chandra, Thibault Kervarrec, Hyun Jung Park, Mazdak Dalkoohi, Jürgen C. Becker, Yuan Chang, Patrick S. Moore, Masahiro Shuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe provide the example of cancer cell reversion to quiescent neuronal cells by CBP/p300 small molecule inhibitors using human Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC), a cancer addicted to expression of the Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV) T antigen oncogene. This work defines the dependence of the Merkel cell-like cancer lineage and tumorigenicity on the CBP/p300-mediated viral and cellular gene control.

Human Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine skin cancer associated with immunosuppression and ultraviolet exposure. The majority of MCC is caused by Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV) (1) which, when clonally integrated, causes cancer cell transformation through expression of virus-encoded large T (LT) and small T (ST) oncoproteins (2). Once integrated, LT is also invariably mutated in cancer cells (3). This cancer signature mutation causes deletion of the C-terminus of the LT oncoprotein that ablates its DNA helicase activity. In contrast to virus-positive MCC, which has a low mutation burden, virus-negative MCC has a high mutational load that phenocopies viral oncogenesis (4, 5). Regardless of viral status, all MCC types (both virus-positive and virus-negative) express Merkel cell neuroendocrine markers such as chromogranin A, CK20, and ATOH1 (6–8).

MCV-positive MCC cells depend on continued expression of viral T antigens (2). In a coculture system with keratinocytes, short-hairpin (sh)RNA-mediated knockdown of T antigens in some MCC cell lines causes reversion of the cancer cells to a differentiated, nontransformed neuronal cell phenotype (9). The differentiation of MCV-positive MCC cells requires cocultured keratinocytes, suggesting that the tumor tissue microenvironment contributes to this phenotypic reversion. Like normal Merkel cells, MCC tumors express high levels of Merkel cell lineage transcription factors ATOH1 and SOX2, which are important for embryonic Merkel cell development (10–12). MCV LT activates SOX2, a cellular oncogene, through LT inhibition of pRb pocket proteins (9). This promotes ATOH1 gene expression and MCC cell proliferation, consistent with Sox2-Atoh1 as a Merkel cell lineage-specific oncogenic pathway (9). In a transgenic mouse model, Atoh1 coexpression with MCV ST and LT in cytokeratin (CK)5-positive basal epidermal cells induced neuroendocrine tumors that closely resemble human MCC (13). In one clinical study, a rare human MCV-positive MCC tumor was described with clonal virus integration throughout the cancer but loss of T antigen gene expression in one portion (14). In this tumor, cells retaining T antigen gene expression showed typical features and expression patterns (CK20, Sox2, and Ki-67 positivity) of MCC, while cells in areas that lost T antigen expression also lost CK20, Sox2, and Ki-67 and differentiated into cells with neurofilament-positive neurofibrils. Taken together, these observations support a model in which T antigen sustains a dedifferentiation, proliferative state through the Sox2-Atoh1 pathway.

In this study, we examined the potential for CREB-binding protein (CBP)/p300 small-molecule inhibitors to promote MCC cell differentiation and examined downstream stem cell signaling pathway genes during reversion of the neuroendocrine phenotype. Mouse cancer reversion by knockout of an oncogene or a restoration of a tumor suppressor protein is well documented (15–17); however, in these cases, cancer cells undergo apoptotic cell death, leading to loss of the tumor rather than true phenotypic reversion. Furthermore, tumor reversion has not been described either following treatment with a small molecule inhibitor or in naturally occurring human cancers. The CBP/p300 transcriptional coactivator has been shown to regulate MCV T antigen expression in MCV-positive MCC cells (18). Here, we show that some patient-derived MCV-positive MCC cell lines undergo cell cycle arrest and acquire a differentiated neuronal phenotype following treatment with the CBP/p300 active site inhibitor A-485 (19) and a bromo-domain proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) dCBP-1 (20). This model underscores the pivotal role of stem cell signaling to a human cancer and allows better characterization of viral and lineage-specific carcinogenesis.

Results

CBP/p300 Inhibition Induces Neuronal Differentiation.

MCV pan T antigen knockdown in MCC cells by a short-hairpin RNA (shpanT) induces neurite-like projections positive for neurofilament L (NFL) (9) in the presence of keratinocytes (Fig. 1 A and B). CBP and p300 histone acetyltransferase (HAT) inhibitors suppress MCV T antigen gene expression from both replicating MCV episomes and integrated MCV genome in human MCC cells (18, 21). Two CBP/p300 inhibitors, A-485 (5 µM) and dCBP-1 (100 nM), were used to examine the effect of CBP/p300 inhibition on MCV-positive MCC cell lines MS-1, MKL-2, CVG-1, BroLi, MKL-1, and WaGa (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig S1 A–D). A-485 is a catalytic active site inhibitor of CBP/p300 acetyltransferase (19) while dCBP-1 is a PROTAC consisting of the bromodomain inhibitor GNE-781 linked to pomalidomide (20). Chemical CBP/p300 inhibition induced similar morphological changes to those induced by MCV pan T antigen knockdown only in the presence of cocultured keratinocytes (Fig. 1A). No morphological changes were seen in the absence of cocultured keratinocytes, consistent with the critical role of keratinocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Treatment of MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, and BroLi cell lines resulted in ~90% of cells undergoing transition from a rounded cell body to a spindle-like cell shape. Simultaneously, cell bodies developed NFL-positive neurite-like projections, which was localized to perinuclear cuffs in mock-treated cells (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). These cellular changes were concurrent with marked reduction in T antigen gene expression (Fig. 2 A and B). This differentiation was cell line dependent: MKL-1 and WaGa, which have highest levels of LT protein expression in mock-treated cells (Fig. 2B), were resistant to these CBP/p300-dependent changes.

CBP/p300 Directly Regulate T Antigen Expression.

qRT-PCR was used to measure MCV T antigen mRNA expression after CBP/p300 inhibition (Fig. 2A). All cell lines tested (MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, and WaGa) showed significant loss of T antigen mRNA (Fig. 2A). ST mRNA was also reduced except for WaGa cells treated with A-485 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). LT and ST viral oncoprotein expression was attenuated in a dose-dependent fashion with A-485 and dCBP-1 treatment except for ST protein in WaGa cells treated with A-485 (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). This was associated with concurrent loss of histone H3 lysine 18 acetylation (H3K18ac), a direct target for CBP/p300 (22). Sox2 and Atoh1 protein levels also diminished in these cell lines after treatment, except for MKL-1 cells (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). In this cell line, Sox2 levels were maintained after A-485 treatment regardless of the dose but were markedly reduced with dCBP-1 treatment >20 nM. A standard dosing of 5 µM A-485 or 100 nM dCBP-1 was used in subsequent experiments since these drug concentrations nearly ablated H3K18ac modification in all cell lines.

Chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR analysis for the MCV T antigen promoter in CVG-1 cells identified CBP and p300 binding to the MCV promoter region (−155/+1) (Fig. 2C). In addition, ChIP for H3K18ac was positive for this promoter region, confirming it to be a site for CBP/p300 HAT activity. T antigen promoter assays, using a luciferase reporter stably transduced into MCC cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E), found that the activity of 156 bp region (−155/+1) of the T antigen promoter was significantly inhibited by CBP/p300 inhibitors.

MCC Cells Enter a Quiescent State Upon Loss of CBP/p300 Acetyltransferase Activity.

The effect of CBP/p300 inhibitors on MCV-positive MCC cell growth was determined using resazurin cell proliferation assays. UISO (MCV-negative MCC), BJ (foreskin fibroblast), NCI-H69 and NCI-H146 (neuroendocrine small cell lung cancer), U87 and U251 (glioma), U2OS (osteosarcoma), and adenovirus E1A-immortalized 293 cell lines were examined as controls to compare the sensitivity of MCV-positive MCC cell proliferation to the CBP/p300 inhibitors (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Proliferation was ablated for all MCV-positive (MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, WaGa) and MCV-negative (UISO) MCC cell lines tested by A-485 and dCBP-1 without significant cell death. BJ cell proliferation, however, was less sensitive to A-485 and dCBP-1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). NCI-H69 proliferation was more resistant to CBP/p300 inhibition, whereas NCI-H146 proliferation was inhibited completely (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). U2OS cells were moderately sensitive to A-485 but not dCBP-1, and proliferation of other cell lines U87, U251, and 293 showed resistance to both 5 µM A-485 and 100 nM dCBP-1 although H3K18ac was decreased by both drugs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). No increase was seen in PARP cleavage (cPARP) among MCV-positive MCC cells after A-485 treatment, regardless of inhibitor dose. In contrast, p27Kip1, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor (CKI) expressed in G0/G1 cell cycle (23, 24), was elevated in all treated MCV-positive cells (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). This was confirmed by transducing a nonfunctional mVenus-p27Kip1 reporter (25) into MCV-positive MCC cells to measure p27Kip1 stabilization (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). CBP/p300 inhibition increased p27Kip1 reporter stabilization, indicative for G0/G1 arrest, and cell colonies formed clump-like aggregates. Cell cycle profile analysis confirmed A-485 and dCBP-1-induced reduction in the cycling cell populations (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Small increases in sub2N fractions, without cPARP expression, after treatment are consistent with the inhibitors promoting nonapoptotic cell death(2) in a small fraction of MCV-positive cells.

CBP/p300 Inhibitors Downregulate Merkel Cell/MCC-Associated Genes in MCC.

Total RNA sequencing was performed on repeats of CVG-1 cells treated with 5 µM A-485 or 100 nM dCBP-1 (Datasets S1–S3) (26). ~70% of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) coordinately increased or decreased after A-485/dCBP-1 treatment compared to mock-treated cells (Fig. 4A). Gene set variation analysis on the DEGs identified significant downregulation of mTORC1 signaling genes, G2/M checkpoint genes, MYC target genes, and E2F target genes (Fig. 4B). Suppression of gene expression after CBP/p300 inhibition was highest for pathway genes known to be activated by MCV ST (mTORC1, G2/M checkpoint, and Myc) (27–29) and LT (E2F) (3, 30, 31), consistent with A-485 and dCBP-1 deactivating the MCV T antigen gene locus. Genes involved in interferon alpha response and heme metabolism were significantly upregulated by CBP/p300 inhibition (Fig. 4B).

Merkel cell (MC)-related transcription factors ATOH1, DACH1, GFI1, HES6, INSM1, ISL1, LHX3, MYT1, NHLH1, POU4F2, and SOX2 have been identified in animal studies (32). Most of these factors decreased >2-fold after treatment (Fig. 4C). Three MC transcription factors, MYT1, NHLH1, and POU4F2, were not significantly altered (< 2-fold change), and ASCL1 expression was not detected in CVG-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). These results were confirmed in 5 MCC cell lines tested by qRT-PCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A−D). KRT20 and KRT8 genes encoding MC-associated keratins have increased expression in Merkel cells and MCC (7, 33). CHGA (chromogranin) and SYP (synaptophysin) encoded proteins are also associated with MCC but can be commonly expressed in neuronal tissues. KRT20 and KRT8 expression significantly decreased, while CHGA was unchanged and SYP was increased, after treatment (Fig. 4D). qRT-PCR confirmed KRT20 expression reduction with treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). GSEA using GO Biological Process gene sets showed an upregulation of neuronal-associated genes (dorsal–ventral neural tube patterning, positive regulation of neurotransmitter secretion (dCBP-1 alone), consistent with CBP/p300 inhibition causing reversion of the MCC cancer cell phenotype to a differentiated neuronal phenotype (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C and Datasets S4 and S5). This upregulation was accompanied by the coordinated upregulation of intracellular transport genes (intraciliary transport, intraciliary anterograde/retrograde transport (A-485 alone), endosome-to-lysosome transport, autophagosome organization), which are critical for axonal vesicle trafficking and synaptic signaling (34–36) (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Treatment also caused marked downregulation of DNA replication, chromosome segregation, and nuclear division pathway genes, consistent with cell cycle arrest of the differentiating neuronal cells (Fig. 3 A and D).

CBP/p300 Inhibition Defines a Unique Gene-regulatory Program Independent of T Antigen Function.

Due to the similar neuronal differentiation phenotypes induced by pan T antigen knockdown and CBP/p300 inhibitor treatment, we compared the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CVG-1 cells obtained by CBP/p300 inhibitors with those obtained by pan T antigen knockdown (9) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A and Dataset S6). We focused on the DEGs that are commonly up- or downregulated by A-485 and dCBP-1 treatment as canonical targets of CBP/p300. A greater number of genes were found to be downregulated by both CBP/p300 inhibition and pan T antigen knockdown, consistent with the gene activator function of CBP/p300 and viral T antigen. Of the genes downregulated by pan T antigen knockdown, more than half of the genes (56.6%, 896 out of 1582) were commonly downregulated by both methods of CBP/p300 inhibition. On the other hand, those same 896 genes only account for 15.7% (896 out of 5703) of the total genes commonly downregulated between both drug treatments. Compared to the downregulated genes, upregulated genes were less concordant between CBP/p300 inhibition and pan T antigen knockdown. We performed a Gene Ontology analysis to identify functional gene sets that are uniquely associated with CBP/p300 inhibition, pan T antigen knockdown, or both (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B and Dataset S6). Downregulated DEGs common between pan T antigen knockdown and CBP/p300 inhibitor treatment (896 genes) were enriched with the genes associated with chromosome segregation, DNA replication, and cell cycle checkpoint, consistent with loss of cell proliferation. No functional gene set was enriched in the upregulated common DEGs (91 genes). The DEGs that were uniquely upregulated by CBP/p300 inhibition (1,651 genes that were common between A-485 and dCBP-1) contained genes for cilium organization and assembly, which is critical for neuronal development (34). However, the DEGs that were uniquely downregulated (4,807 genes) contained genes for ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, RNA splicing, and RNA biogenesis. The DEGs uniquely upregulated by T antigen inhibition (426 genes) were enriched with genes associated with epithelial cell morphogenesis and proliferation. The DEGs exclusively downregulated by T antigen inhibition (585 genes) included genes associated with amino acid metabolism.

Neuroblastic Differentiation in MCC Aligns With CBP/p300 Inhibition-Driven Gene Programs.

Rare cases of MCC with loss of MCV T antigen gene expression in a portion of the tumor tissue have been described as harboring a neuroblastic tumor component (14). IHC staining on a combined MCC case harboring a neuroblastic component for LT, Neurofilaments, and Sox2 revealed loss of LT and Sox2 in the neuroblastic component and relocalization of neurofilament proteins to neurite structures in the tissue (Fig. 5A), as well as the definitive border between each component marked by expression of LT or change in neurofilaments localization (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Spatial transcriptomics has been used to describe relative gene expression for the T antigen-negative (nonproliferating, neuronal phenotype) and T-antigen positive (proliferating MCC phenotype) tumor cells. Comparison of gene expression for the CBP/p300 inhibitor-treated CVG-1 cells to this dataset revealed 621 genes upregulated in the neuroblastic tissue component were also upregulated in CVG-1 cells treated with A-485 (42 genes), dCBP-1 (102 genes), or both (477 genes) (Fig. 5B and Dataset S7). Using spatial transcriptomic data from selected spots of combined MCC tissue to represent classical and neuroblastic MCC (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B) cross-referenced with RNA sequencing data obtained for CBP/p300 inhibition, a large overlap of genes lost in the neuroblastic component overlapped with CBP/p300 inhibition (Fig. 5C). Many genes increased in the neuroblastic component were also concordantly increased with CBP/p300 inhibition, but a portion of DEGs were also discordant seeing no change or decrease with CBP/p300 inhibition. Of the top 6 DEGs upregulated in neuroblastic tumor component and expressed in CVG-1, 4 genes (SYT11, CNTN1, MAP6, and NDRG4) were significantly induced by CBP/300 inhibition in CVG-1 (Fig. 5D). These genes are highly expressed in neuronal tissues (37–39). Of 2194 upregulated neuroblastic MCC component DEGs, 67 genes are neural lineage related (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C) and 19 of these genes are upregulated by both CBP/p300 inhibitors in CVG-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Unbiased gene ontology analysis of the 477 genes that were concordantly upregulated following treatment with A-485, dCBP-1, and the neuroblastic component revealed that 7 of the top 10 significantly overrepresented biological processes are related to synaptic signaling and the nervous system (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). To compare the transcriptional changes in the neuroblastic component with those induced by pan T antigen knockdown, we examined at the overlap of DEGs in these two populations. We observed notable differences with only 127 (4.8%) commonly upregulated genes and 115 (6.7%) commonly downregulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7F). Among the DEGs, 43.9% (147 of 262) were concordantly downregulated in both the neuroblastic component and following pan T antigen knockdown, whereas only 5.8% (127 of 2,194) were concordantly upregulated. The neuroblastic component shared a larger number of upregulated DEGs with CBP/p300 inhibitor treatment (21.7%, 477 of 2194 genes in Fig. 5B) than with pan T antigen knockdown (5.8%).

Discussion

Many tumors depend on a limited number, or even a single oncogenic driver to sustain their malignant phenotype, despite carrying numerous additional mutations that contribute to tumorigenicity (40). This phenomenon, known as “oncogenic addiction”, is frequently seen in virus-driven cancers where viral oncoproteins hijack key cellular pathways (41). For example, targeting HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins impedes HPV-positive cervical cancer and oropharyngeal cancer cell growth (42). Viral oncoproteins being the primary drivers in virus-induced cancers make them logical targets for prevention and therapy. In MCV-positive MCC, we previously showed that shRNA-mediated knockdown of the viral T antigen not only stops cell division but also induces a quiescent neuronal phenotype accompanied by a loss of both epithelial and neuroendocrine Merkel cell markers such as Sox2, Atoh1, and KRT20 (2, 9, 43). In this study, we demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CBP/p300 coactivators similarly suppresses viral T antigen oncogene expression and downstream Sox2-Atoh1 signaling, efficiently converting MCC cells into a differentiated neuronal phenotype with neurite outgrowth.

Compared to other cell lines, MCV-positive MCC cell proliferation is extremely sensitive to CBP/p300 inhibitors, resulting in a complete ablation of their growth activity. Our results also suggest that CBP/p300 control unique cellular targets beyond viral T antigen oncogenic targets. Gene Ontology analysis identified that CBP/p300 inhibition decreases RNA splicing genes and ribosomal biogenesis genes and increases cilium organization/assembly genes, which are essential for neuronal differentiation and function (44). It is possible that, in addition to activating viral T antigen oncogene, CBP/p300 also promotes carcinogenesis by affecting splicing and ribosomal biogenesis and it inhibits cilium formation, preventing neuronal differentiation. Further functional studies are needed to demonstrate the contribution of CBP/p300-specific targets to MCC oncogenesis and manifestation of Merkel cell phenotype.

Among six MCV-positive MCC cell lines tested, two (MKL-1 and WaGa) resisted neurite formation by the CBP/p300 inhibitor treatment, although cell proliferation was ablated in all cell lines. The lack of a general effect on all MCC cell lines tested could be due to the natural differences between each cell line because of accrued changes leading to transformation, such as differences in the levels of T antigen (Fig. 2B), or unaltered Sox2 expression (a critical inhibitor of neuronal differentiation) in MKL-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Another possibility is that the wild-type p53 function, which is retained in MKL-1 and WaGa cells, inhibits neuronal differentiation (45). Nonetheless, by mimicking the shRNA inhibition of MCV T antigen, CBP/p300 inhibition may allow a model of chemical toggling between the transformed Merkel cell-like phenotype and nontransformed neuronal phenotype.

Such a phenotypic conversion has been reported to spontaneously occur in vivo in MCC. Albeit rare, some MCV-positive MCC tumors exhibit regions with a classic neuroendocrine phenotype alongside areas with a neuroblastic appearance (Fig. 5A) (14). Despite sharing identical clonal integration of MCV DNA, these neuroblastic regions lack both T antigen expression and Merkel cell markers as well as no mitotic activity. These findings align with our in vitro data showing that small molecule-mediated CBP/p300 inhibition suppresses T antigen and Sox2 expression, efficiently converting MCC cells to a quiescent neuronal phenotype in a keratinocyte coculture model. This phenotypic conversion appears to be specifically controlled by keratinocytes, a primary component of the epidermis that is connected to sensory neuron fibers. Studies have shown that neurite outgrowth is stimulated when neurons are cocultured with keratinocytes (46). A recent spatial transcriptomic study in MCC tissues suggest that epidermal MCCs display epithelial gene expression pattern compared with dermal MCC (47), suggesting the epidermal microenvironment affecting cellular phenotype, presumably via secretory factor and/or direct cell-to-cell interaction.

Both Merkel cell identity and tumorigenicity in MCV-positive MCC are under epigenetic regulation. MCV LT activates EZH2 with both histone methyltransferase-dependent and -independent roles in tumorigenesis (48–50). The ST antigen induces LSD1/KDM1A expression via a MycL-EP400 transcriptional activator complex (27). LSD1 has been shown to play a critical role in reprograming Merkel cells into Merkel cell-like tumors downstream of the ST oncoprotein, locking the cells in a stem cell state by inhibiting neuronal gene expression through Atoh1 (51, 52). CBP/p300 inhibition may unlock this epigenetically repressed stem cell–like state and revert transformed cells to a quiescent neuronal phenotype by downregulating Atoh1. CBP-mediated hyperacetylation of chromatin superenhancers plays a crucial role in tissue-specific gene expresssion, such as in pancreatic beta cells (53). CBP/p300 activity is also required for chondrocyte-specific gene expression, controlling cartilage tissue-specific gene expression and chondrocyte differentiation (54). CBP/p300 inhibition also downregulates lineage-specific transcription factors like the microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) in melanocytes, affecting cell differentiation, survival, and proliferation (55). The CBP/p300 inhibitor A-485 used in this study targets lineage-specific tumors including melanoma, several hematological malignancies, and androgen receptor-positive prostate cancer (19, 55). These findings suggest that CBP/p300 function is not broadly epigenetic but is instead tailored to regulate lineage- and tissue-specific gene expression programs in both normal cells and cancer cells.

While we show that CBP/p300 directly regulates the MCV promoter, the detailed mechanism of this regulation remains unclear. CBP and p300 do not bind DNA directly but are recruited by sequence-specific transcription factors (56). ChIP-qPCR and promoter reporter assays indicated that the T antigen promoter (from −155 to +1) is under direct CBP and p300 control. Identifying the host transcription factor(s) that recruit CBP/p300 to this region and their binding element will be critical for understanding sustained T antigen expression in MCC (57). The similarity in transcriptional profiles between CBP/p300-inhibited MCC cells and neuroblastic tumor components of MCC tumors further support a model in which CBP/p300 sustain the aggressive neuroendocrine phenotype by maintaining T antigen protein expression (9, 11). Additional in vivo studies are needed to evaluate whether CBP/p300 inhibitors can reverse MCC in tissue settings that resemble the native tumor microenvironment.

Our data demonstrate that both Merkel cell lineage identity and tumorigenicity driven by MCV are regulated by CBP/p300. Small molecule inhibition of these coactivators reverts MCC cells from a proliferative tumorigenic state to a quiescent neuronal phenotype. Recent updates to the Hallmarks of Cancer include “unlocking phenotypic plasticity” and “nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming” (58). Virus-driven MCC, with its low mutation burden, provides a unique model to explore how epigenetic mechanisms govern lineage-specific carcinogenesis and alter cancer cell plasticity.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Chemicals.

MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, BroLi, WaGa, UISO, NCI-H146, and NCI-H69 cell lines were maintained in RPMI medium (Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR) and nonessential amino acids (Corning). BJ fibroblasts (ATCC, CRL-2522), U87, U251, U2OS, 293, and 293FT cells (Invitrogen) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (Corning) with 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR) and nonessential amino acids (Corning). Cells were kept at 37 °C and 5% CO2. CBP/p300 inhibitors A-485 (Selleckchem) and dCBP-1 (MedChemExpress) were prepared as 10 mM and 100 μM stocks in DMSO, respectively; mock treatments contained 0.1% DMSO. All cell lines were screened for Mycoplasma every two months.

Plasmids, Transfection and Lentiviruses.

The pCDH-EF1-mVenus-p27K− vector, a kind gift from Mohamed Bentires-Alj (Addgene #176651) was used to generate lentivirus coexpressing mVenus-p27Kip1 and puromycin resistance gene. MCC cell lines (MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, and CVG-1) that stably express mVenus-p27Kip1– virus were established by selecting mVenus-p27Kip1–-transduced cells with 1 μg/mL puromycin. For lentivirus production, 293FT cells (Invitrogen) were cotransfected with lentivirus vector and packaging vectors pCMV VSV-G (Addgene #8454) and pCD/NL-BH*DDD (Addgene #17531) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Cell culture supernatant containing infectious lentivirus was harvested at day 3 post-transfection, and 500 μL of supernatant was used for infection.

Cell Proliferation Assays.

MCV-positive MCC cell lines (3 × 104 of MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, and WaGa), MCV-negative UISO cell line (1 × 104), small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma cell lines (8 × 103 of NCI-H146 and NCI-H69), glioblastoma cell lines (4 × 103 of U87 and U251), osteosarcoma U2OS cell line (4 × 103), adenovirus E1A-immortalized embryonic kidney 293 cell lines (3 × 103) and foreskin BJ cell line (6 × 103) were seeded in a 96-well plate with triplicates for each measurement time in 200 µL of medium containing mock, 5 µM A-485, or 100 nM dCBP-1. On days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9, 95 µL of medium was removed and 8 µL of Resazurin (Cell Signaling) was added. Measurements took place 4 h after addition. Minimum of 2 repeats were performed.

Differentiation Assays.

First, 6 × 104 MKL-1, MKL-2, MS-1, CVG-1, BroLi, and WaGa were pretreated with DMSO, 5 μM A-485, or 100 nM dCBP-1 containing keratinocyte serum-free medium (17005042, ThermoFisher) for 3 d. Then, 6 × 103 keratinocytes were seeded in a 24-well plate on the last day of MCC cell pretreatment. The day after keratinocyte seeding, the MCC cells were added to the keratinocyte-containing wells in their original medium. Differentiation was taken up to 3 d, when cells were imaged or fixed and underwent immunofluorescent analysis.

Antibodies.

Primary antibodies used for immunoblots include Sox2 (Proteintech Cat# 66411-1-Ig, RRID:AB_2881783), Atoh1 (Proteintech Cat# 21215-1-AP, RRID:AB_10733126), E2F-1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc-251, RRID:AB_627476), PARP (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9532, RRID:AB_659884), Cleaved PARP (ASP214) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 5625, RRID:AB_10699459), p27 Kip1 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 3686, RRID:AB_2077850), Total H3 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 14269, RRID:AB_2756816), H3K18ac (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 13998, RRID:AB_2783723), and Hsc70 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc-7298, RRID:AB_627761). Neurofilament-L antibody (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2837, RRID:AB_823575) and Rabbit IgG (Proteintech Cat# 30000-0-AP, RRID:AB_2819035) were used for immunofluorescence.

Immunoblot.

Harvested cells from each experiment were suspended in SDS lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, and 1%SDS, pH 8.0) and sonicated until genomic DNA was fully sheared to solubilize nuclear proteins. The protein was then quantified using BSA (bovine serum albumin) standards on a Biotek Synergy 2 machine. After quantification, samples were mixed with 4× LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) plus 5% beta-mercaptoethanol and resolved by SDS-PAGE gel with percentages adjusted to detect the target protein of interest. Precision Plus Protein (Bio-Rad) was used as a ladder for all immunoblots. After running the gel, protein was transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane overnight using a wet transfer buffer at 35 V. Membranes were blocked (5% skim milk in TBS), incubated with primary antibodies (5% milk in TBS, overnight at 4 °C or 5 h at room temperature). Secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences Cat# 925-32210, RRID:AB_2687825, LI-COR Biosciences Cat# 925-68070, RRID:AB_2651128, LI-COR Biosciences Cat# 925-32211, RRID:AB_2651127, LI-COR Biosciences Cat# 925-68070, RRID:AB_2651128) were diluted with 5% skim milk in TBS at 1:10,000, and membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the absence of light. Between the primary and second antibody reactions, membranes were washed 3 times in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20.

Quantitative Real-Time Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT-PCR).

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol (Invitrogen). DNase treatment and reverse transcription were performed using the iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). qRT-PCR was performed using the ΔΔCT method. 18 s rRNA was used as a control. All primer sequences used can be found in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation qPCR.

ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP kit (9003; Cell Signaling Technology) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For 10 min, 8 × 106 CVG-1 cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature. Cross-linking was quenched with the addition of 125 mM glycine for 5 min at room temperature and followed by digestion and sonication to fragment the chromatin. Fragmented chromatin was analyzed on agarose gels to confirm proper digestion and formation of chromatin unit ladder. Chromatin was then incubated with CBP (7425; Cell Signaling Technology), p300 (54062; Cell Signaling Technology), H3 (4620; Cell Signaling Technology), or a negative control IgG (2729; Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Chromatin and primary antibody were then incubated with protein G magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation and subsequently eluted and used as a template for qRT-PCR with specific primers. Primers can be found in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Cell Cycle Analysis.

MCV-positive MCC cells treated with or without CBP/p300 inhibitors for 4 d were dispersed with Accutase (Sigma) after PBS wash. Cells were fixed with 10% buffered formalin for 6 min, followed by permeabilization in 0.1% Triton-X in PBS for 5 min. After cells were washed once with PBS containing 1% BSA, 50 µg/mL propidium iodide and 40 µg/mL RNaseA and incubated for 1 h, they were analyzed on an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (Beckton Dickinson).

RNA Sequencing and Sequencing Analysis.

RNA sequencing was performed for CVG-1 cells treated with 5 μM A-485 and 100 nM dCBP-1. Cells were harvested after 6 d of treatment and total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen). Samples were then aliquoted and sent to Azenta for QC and sequencing. Reads from six libraries, representing duplicates for each condition (mock, A-485-treated, dCBP-1-treated), were trimmed by Trimmomatic v.0.38 with default setting for paired-end reads, followed by alignment with STAR v.2.7.10b using human genome reference GRCh38.p14. The aligned reads were quantified by featureCounts from SubRead v.1.5.1. The quantified expression was analyzed in R statistical environment v.4.3.1. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 1.40.2, differentially expressed genes were defined by having Benjamini and Hochberg-adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1. Gene set enrichment analysis between conditions was performed by fgsea v.1.26.0 on hallmark gene signature obtained from Molecular Signature Database and tumor transcriptional metaprograms from Gavish et al. (59). Per-sample gene set enrichment was calculated by gene set variation analysis (GSVA) v.1.48.3. Gene ontology over-representation analysis was performed on the concordantly up-/downregulated genes between CBP/p300 inhibitor treatments and pan T antigen knockdown in CVG-1 cells using clusterProfiler v.4.14.6; overrepresented terms were visualized in descending order of gene ratio, i.e., number of genes in the query gene set found in a particular GO biological process term divided by the total number of genes in the query gene set.

Immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemistry.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described (9), substituting 5% BSA for blocking rather than 10% FBS. DAPI was used at 0.2 μg/mL for 4 min. A combined MCV-positive MCC tumor with neuroblastic regions was described previously and was processed for hematoxylin phloxine saffron staining (HPS) and immunohistochemical staining as described in (14).

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical significances of other experiments were analyzed using student’s t-tests. Differences with an associated P < 0.05 were considered to be significant. Level of significance is denoted by asterisks. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
