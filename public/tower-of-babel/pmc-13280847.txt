Recycling Zn Casing Waste into a ZnO–Cu-Based Electrocatalyst for Selective CO2 Electroreduction to Ethylene
Authors: Pitchapa Pittayavinai, Teerapat Inudom, Jitti Kasemchainan, Sirilux Poompradub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The fabrication of a nanostructured electrocatalyst was derived from zinc–carbon (Zn–C) battery waste for the electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2RR). The zinc casing was converted into zinc oxide (ZnO) nanorods via hydrothermal oxidation and decorated with Cu2O and CuO nanoparticles (NPs) using drop casting to form a ZnO–CuI,II casing electrocatalyst. Electrochemical CO2RR was conducted via chronoamperometric electrolysis at potentials from −1.70 to 0.00 V. The ZnO–CuI,II casing electrocatalyst exhibited selective formation of ethylene (C2H4) with a Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 5.62% at – 1.30 V vs. Ag/AgCl, along with minor production of CH4, CO, and H2. After regeneration, CH4, CO, and H2 were still detected, but C2H4 was absent due to nanoparticle agglomeration and detachment, as shown by scanning electron microscopy. This work highlights a sustainable route for transforming battery waste into functional electrocatalysts for hydrocarbon production via CO2 electroreduction.

Introduction

Recently, the United Nations Global E-waste Monitor reported that there was 2.21 million tons of electronic garbage in 2023, of which only about 20% is recycled through organized and regulated channels. , The ever-growing accumulation of electronic waste (e-waste), estimated to reach 74.7 million tons by 2030, posed significant environmental and health risks due to the leaching of toxic heavy metals from improperly discarded materials. − Therefore, the ‘reuse, reduce, and recycle (3R)’ principal is a suitable set of methods to manage the electronic garbage.

Among the various components of e-waste, zinc–carbon (Zn–C) batteries contribute significantly to global battery waste. The production of Zn–C batteries totals about 225,000 tons per year, and they cannot be recharged or replaced and so are discarded as waste after use. , A Zn–C battery generally consists of a cathode mix (56.42% of manganese dioxide (MnO2), carbon, and electrolyte), a steel can (13.84%), a zinc (Zn) casing (11.58%), a carbon rod (7.65%), plastic film (3.33%), and iron top and bottom (2.53%). , The main components in the cathode mix can be recycled and applied in many applications, such as rechargeable Zn-ion batteries recycled from the extraction of MnO2 and carbon. In addition, a solid adsorbent for dye adsorption is obtained by separating MnO2 from the black mass in the cathode mix. − The Zn powder can be recycled as an electrocatalyst. However, the Zn casing is rarely recycled and remains an underutilized resource.

Recycling waste Zn casings into functional materials presents a sustainable and economically attractive approach for energy-related applications. − In particular, Zn-based materials have garnered attention as electrocatalysts for the electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2RR), due to the cost-effective manner compared to gold and silver and inherent ability to catalyze the conversion of CO2 to CO. ,,, Especially, the oxidation of Zn enhances the activity for CO conversion. , Unfortunately, Zn and the oxidation of Zn to zinc oxide (ZnO) cannot produce value-added hydrocarbons (HCs), such as methane (CH4) and ethylene (C2H4), because it requires a high energy to adsorb CO onto the electrocatalyst surface for activating the C–C bond formation. , In contrast, copper (Cu)-based electrocatalysts are uniquely capable of facilitating multi-electron CO2RR pathways, enabling the selective production of hydrocarbons. − The Cu2O and CuO nanostructures have been shown to stabilize Cu+/Cu2+ species and enhance selectivity toward C2 products. However, Cu-based catalysts often suffer from poor structural stability and H2 evolution competition. Recent advances in electrocatalysts have highlighted that the strategic combination and precise engineering of metal sites within structured frameworks can significantly enhance electrocatalytic performance and selectivity. For instance, Fe2O3 nanoparticles encapsulated in the pores of nickel-2,3,6,7,10,11-hexaaminotriphenylene (Ni-HITP), as well as Ni catalysts with a combined N- and C-coordination structure and precisely tailored multi-metal sites, have been shown to facilitate the synthesis of urea and C2 products. , Furthermore, integrating these advanced metal centers into specialized electrolyzer systems, such as those utilizing molecular sieve membranes or solid-state electrolytes, enables efficient CO2 and flue gas reduction to high-value chemicals like HCOOH and pure urea under optimized local environments. To address this, recent studies have demonstrated that combining Cu with ZnO can enhance catalyst durability and selectivity by suppressing hydrogen evolution and promoting C–C coupling. − The CO2 conversion efficiency is then increased and can easily yield HC products. Accordingly, there is a high opportunity to use waste Zn casings as an electrocatalyst in the CO2RR because the main composition of the Zn casing is Zn.

Herein, the production of HC products in the CO2RR was investigated using Zn casing waste from a Zn–C battery. The production of C2H4 was mainly focused on because it is an important monomer for polyethylene production. The Zn casing was first converted to ZnO nanostructures via hydrothermal oxidation and subsequently decorated with Cu2O and CuO nanoparticles (NPs) through drop-casting. The obtained ZnO–CuI,II casing was then characterized by attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The Faradaic efficiency (FE) was measured at various potentials (−1.70 to 0.00 V) using Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. In order to investigate the effect of the electrocatalyst in the CO2RR, a comparison between the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II, which consists of ZnO, Cu2O, and CuO NPs on carbon paper, was performed. Regeneration of the catalyst was used to determine the stability of the ZnO–CuI,II casing relative to that for nZnO–CuI,II. Finally, the proposed catalytic mechanism of the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II was proposed and illustrated the effect of the different preparation methods. This work underscores the untapped potential of e-waste-derived materials as viable electrocatalysts for the hydrocarbon production from CO2.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

The spent Zn–C batteries (1.5 V, size AA) were sourced from discarded electronic gadgets, and the Zn casing was manually separated from the battery cover and used as the raw material. ZnO, CuO, and Cu2O were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. Acetone (C3H6O), potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3), ethanol (C2H5OH), isopropyl alcohol (C3H7OH), sodium chloride (NaCl), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were supplied by Qrec Chemical Ltd., New Zealand. Ethylene glycol (EG: C2H6O2) was supplied from KEMAUS Chemical, Australia. Ammonium persulfate (APS: (NH4)2S2O8) was purchased from Merck, Germany. Super C65 nano carbon black was purchased from MSE Supplies, USA, in order to improve the conductivity of the electrode. Carbon paper was supplied from MSE Supplies, USA. Nafion 117 (Dupont) was employed as a proton-exchange membrane. High-purity CO2 gas (99% v/v) was supplied by Labgaz Co. Ltd. All chemicals were used as received, without further purification.

Preparation of ZnO from the Waste Zn Casing via Hydrothermal Treatment

The extracted Zn casing was ultrasonically cleaned in acetone for 30 min, followed by sonication in distilled water for 30 min, and then air-dried. The cleaned Zn strips (1.0 × 0.5 cm) were immersed in a 1:1 mixture of 2 M NaOH and 0.4 M APS in a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 150 °C for 3 h. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting solid was washed, dried, and designated as hydrothermally derived ZnO (ZnO casing).

Fabrication of Cu2O and CuO NPs-Modified ZnO Casing

Cu2O and CuO NPs were dispersed in ethanol (molar ratio 2:1) and sonicated for 1 h. Separately, carbon black (20 mg) was dispersed in ethylene glycol (50 mL) and sonicated for 1 h. The two suspensions were combined and stirred for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The obtained powder was separated from the liquid and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The obtained powder was redispersed in isopropyl alcohol and drop-cast onto the ZnO casing surface. The sample was subsequently annealed at 150 °C for 30 min to yield the Cu2O/CuO-decorated ZnO casing, denoted as ZnO–CuI,II casing.

The electrodes of ZnO, Cu2O, and CuO NPs on carbon paper were prepared to compare against the ZnO–CuI,II casing. The ZnO, Cu2O, and CuO NPs at a mole ratio of ZnO (4):Cu2O (2):CuO (1) were sonicated for 1 h. Carbon black was also prepared by sonication with EG for 1 h. Next, both suspensions were then mixed together for 1 h and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min, and the powder was separated from the liquid and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The dried powder was then suspended by stirring in isopropyl alcohol and dropped onto the carbon paper to yield the nZnO–CuI,II electrode. Characterization

The elemental composition of the Zn casing was analyzed using X-ray fluorescent spectrometry (XRF: S8 Tiger, Bruker, USA) equipped with a sequential-type spectrometer and analyzer crystal for X-ray dispersion. The surface functional groups of Zn casing, ZnO casing, and ZnO–CuI,II casing were evaluated by ATR-FTIR (Thermo Ficher, Nicolet iS5, USA) analysis, performed at a resolution of 4 cm–1 with 64 scans. The oxidation state of the metal cations in the ZnO–CuI, II casing was determined by XPS (Kratos Analytical Axis Ultra, UK) using a monochromatic Al–Kα X-ray source. The surface morphology was examined by SEM (JEOL, JSM-6840LV, USA), operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, followed by platinum sputter-coating. The crystal structure of each sample was examined by XRD (Rigaku, SmartLab, USA), measured over a 2θ range of 20–90° and a scanning step of 0.01.

Electrochemical CO2 Reduction

The FE was determined via hydrogen-cell measurement (H-cell) in a standard three-electrode cell (Figure ) by using 0.1 M KHCO3 as the electrolyte. The active geometric surface area of the working electrode was 0.5 cm2. Ag/AgCl (in a 3 M NaCl solution) was used as the reference electrode, and a Pt wire was used as the counter electrode. A Nafion 117 proton exchange membrane was placed between the cathode and anode compartments to prevent the products from being reoxidized. Next, a 0.1 M KHCO3 solution was applied and purged with 99.99% (v/v) CO2 for 20 min prior to electrochemical measurements to achieve near-saturation conditions, and then cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed over a potential range from −2.00 to 1.00 V at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. The stabilization of the current response was monitored prior to recording the CV curves to ensure steady-state conditions. Finally, chronoamperometric electrolysis was examined under a potential range from −1.70 to 0.00 V. The potential range for chronoamperometric electrolysis was specifically chosen on the basis of the active CO2 reduction region identified in the CV. The gaseous products were identified using gas chromatography (GC: Shimadzu GC-2014) with a thermal conductivity detector and helium (He) as the carrier gas.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Electrocatalysts

The composition of the Zn casing was analyzed by XRF and found to be composed of Zn (88.80%), manganese (0.40%), chlorine (10.69%), and other elements (0.11%). The main component of the Zn casing is Zn0. Characterization of the samples by ATR-FTIR revealed no peak of ZnO in Zn casing spectra (Figure a), but after hydrothermal treatment, the ZnO casing displayed the ZnO band at wavenumbers of 612 and 1091 cm–1 (Figure b). To improve the FE of the CO2RR, Cu2O and CuO NPs were deposited onto the ZnO casing to form the ZnO–CuI,II casing. The ATR-FTIR results of the ZnO–CuI,II casing (Figure c) revealed peaks at 612 and 1091 cm–1, corresponding to the ZnO band, and at 875 and 1437 cm–1, corresponding to Cu–O and CuO bands, respectively. , Unfortunately, ATR-FTIR analysis cannot distinguish between the oxidation states of Cu2O and CuO. Therefore, XPS was used to determine the oxidation state of copper, with deconvoluted XPS spectra shown in Figure .

Representative ATR-FTIR spectra of the (a) Zn casing, (b) ZnO casing, and (c) ZnO– CuI,II casing.

Deconvoluted Cu 2p XPS spectra for the ZnO–CuI,II casing.

The XPS spectra of the ZnO–CuI,II casing showed the peaks of Cu2O (Cu+) at binding energies (BEs) of 933.05 and 952.72 eV corresponding to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 of Cu 2p, respectively. The BE of CuO (Cu2+) was also seen at 934.49 eV in Cu 2p3/2 and 954.76 eV in Cu 2p1/2. , Accordingly, it was concluded that both Cu2O and CuO were successfully coated onto the ZnO casing. Note that the satellites peaks at BEs of 944.00 and 960.00 eV occurred from multiplet splitting, plasmon loss, and shakeup processes in the transition metal oxides. The coexistence of Cu+ and Cu2+ species as evidenced by the XPS analysis may play a vital role in enhancing the electrochemical performance. The mixed-valence state of Cu+ and Cu2+ species is proposed to promote the C–C coupling process by stabilizing *CO intermediates and enhancing the active surface area to obtain C2H4. However, there were other reasons for the discrepancies in the proportions of Cu­(I) and Cu­(II) between the XPS results and the initial precursor ratio. Exposure to the environment during or after synthesis may have resulted in oxidation or reduction. Furthermore, the identified Cu species might not accurately reflect the material’s bulk composition because XPS is a surface-sensitive technique.

The morphology of the electrocatalysts was examined by SEM. A flower-like structure was found in the case of the Zn casing (Figure a), and after hydrothermal treatment, the obtained ZnO casing showed a structural change from a flower-like structure to nanorods (NRs) (Figure b). The cross-sectional view of the ZnO–CuI,II casing confirmed that Cu2O and CuO were deposited onto the structural ZnO NRs (Figure c). The branch structure originated from the ZnO NRs with the deposition of Cu2O and CuO NPs. The elemental composition derived from the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis also confirmed the presence of Cu and Zn in the ZnO–CuI,II casing. The presence of Cu2O and CuO NPs may increase the available surface area for reaction with CO2, resulting in increased CO2 adsorption and reaction with CO2 to convert it to CO. Then, the high surface area of the electrocatalyst from the Cu2O and CuO NPs would more easily convert CO to value-added HCs.

Representative SEM images of the (a) Zn casing surface, (b) ZnO casing surface, and (c) ZnO–CuI,II casing surface (cross section); plus (d) the EDX of the ZnO–CuI,II casing.

Finally, the crystal structure of the ZnO–CuI,II casing was confirmed by XRD analysis (Figure ). The XRD patterns of the ZnO–CuI,II casing and the ZnO casing (inset Figure ) were clearly different. The diffraction angles of the ZnO casing appeared at 31.70 °, 34.22 °, 37.93 °, 54.25 °, 62.74 °, and 70.02 °, corresponding to the ZnO planes of (100), (101), (102), (103), (200), and (112), respectively, whereas, the diffraction angles of the ZnO–CuI,II casing were at 34.50 °, 42.40 °, 73.70 ° and 77.57 °, corresponding to the Cu2O planes at (111), (200), (311), and (222), respectively, in accord with JCPDS 75–1531. The diffraction angles of CuO were at 31.50 °, 48.72 °, 56.30 °, and 63.59 °, corresponding to planes of (110), (2̅02), (202), and (1̅13), respectively, in accord with JCPDS 48–1548. The other prominent peaks could be the result of impurities from the Zn casing which was characterized by the elemental compositions from XRF, as described earlier.

Representative XRD pattern of the ZnO–CuI,II casing.

Cyclic Voltammogram (CV) of the ZnO–CuI, II Casing and nZnO–CuI, II

The redox reaction in the CO2RR was evaluated using CV under nitrogen- (N2: 99% (v/v)) and CO2-saturated KHCO3 solutions within a potential range from −2.00 V to +1.00 V vs. Ag/AgCl (Figure ). Under the N2-saturated KHCO3 solution, the CV exhibited a broad anodic peak between −0.25 V and +1.00 V vs. Ag/AgCl (Figure a), with the highest current density observed at – 0.25 V vs. Ag/AgCl, corresponding to the oxidation of Cu+ to Cu2+. Notably, no cathodic peak was detected, indicating the absence of significant reduction processes under these conditions. This lack of cathodic activity likely raised from the absence of a reducible species in the N2-saturated KHCO3 solution, as N2 was electrochemically inert and did not participate in electron transfer at the examined potentials. On the other hand, the ZnO–CuI,II casing displayed clear redox features under the CO2-saturated in KHCO3 solution. An anodic peak centered at – 0.25 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) again indicated the Cu+ oxidation reaction, while a cathodic feature spanning from −1.00 V to +1.00 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) suggested the involvement of CO2 reduction processes. These results imply that CO2 plays a role in facilitating or stabilizing redox activity on the electrode surface. Interestingly, the anodic signal corresponding to the oxidation of Zn to Zn2+ at – 0.86 V vs. Ag/AgCl was absent, which may be attributed to the complete surface coverage of the ZnO casing by Cu2O and CuO NPs, as confirmed by SEM analysis (Figure c). This suggests that ZnO did not directly participate in the CO2 reduction process. Instead, the Cu-based species were the predominant active sites. The cathodic peak of the ZnO–CuI,II casing displayed only Zn2+ to Zn at a potential of −0.86 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), and the peaks for Cu2+ to Cu+ (−0.25 V vs. Ag/AgCl) disappeared because both Cu2+ and Cu+ could oxidize an electron from Zn, which has a higher reduction potential than both Cu2+ and Cu+. In addition, as reported previously, Cu species play a crucial role in modulating electron transfer and enhancing H2 evolution reaction (HER) pathways during CO2RR. ,

Representative cyclic voltammograms of the (a) ZnO–CuI,II casing and (b) nZnO–CuI,II. The condition for CO2RR was stable in the 3rd cycle, using a scan rate of 50 mV/s with a potential range from −2.00 to 1.00 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) in a 0.1 M KHCO3 solution.

The effect of Zn morphology on redox behavior was evaluated by preparing the comparative electrode denoted as nZnO–CuI,II. The nZnO–CuI,II was fabricated using ZnO NPs while maintaining the Cu2O and CuO content equivalent to that in the ZnO–CuI,II casing. The extended CV of nZnO-CuI,II, displayed in Figure b, exhibited distinct anodic peaks at – 0.86 V and +0.34 V vs. Ag/AgCl, corresponding to the oxidation of Zn to Zn2+ and Cu+ to Cu2+, respectively. In the cathodic region, the reduction peaks assigned to Cu2+ to Cu+ (−0.25 V vs. Ag/AgCl) and Zn2+ to Zn (−1.00 V vs. Ag/AgCl) were also observed. The observed shift in both anodic and cathodic peak positions, compared to the ZnO–CuI,II casing, may be attributed to several factors, including differences in electrocatalyst–electrolyte interaction, charge transfer kinetics, and double-layer capacitance effects. Additionally, variations in the redox peak potentials may arise from scan rate-dependent pseudocapacitive behavior and the influence of negative capacitance, as previously reported. ,

Electrochemical Analysis of CO2RR

Chronoamperometric electrolysis was conducted over the potential range from −1.70 to 0.00 V vs. Ag/AgCl to evaluate the Faradaic efficiency (FE) of CO2RR products. The ideal potential for the production of C2H4 was found to be −1.30 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), which was identified as the optimal potential for C2H4 production, as shown in Table S1 (see Supporting Information). In this study, the main desired product is C2H4, as it can be used in many applications, such as polymer production, ripening fruits and vegetables, or antifreeze production. ,, The FE values for CO2RR products are summarized in Table , obtained from various electrocatalysts (ZnO casing, ZnO–CuI,II casing, nZnO, nCuI,II, and nZnO–CuI,II). When using the ZnO casing as the electrocatalyst, the minor products and their FE were CO (0.16%), H2 (4.59%), and CH4 (0.49%). These results are consistent with previous studies, indicating that ZnO primarily facilitates the formation of *CO and *CHO intermediates, which can lead to CH4 production. However, ZnO exhibits a relatively low binding energy (BE) for *CO, thereby hindering the stabilization required for C–C coupling and subsequent C2H4 formation. ,,, Similarly, the use of nZnO as the electrocatalyst yielded low C–C coupling activity, producing CO (4.24%), H2 (12.90%), and CH4 (1.03%). These findings further support the notion that ZnO alone is insufficient to drive efficient C2H4 under the tested conditions.

On the other hand, incorporating different metals with Cu-based structures has emerged as an effective strategy for tuning adsorption energies and binding affinities of key intermediates, thereby enhancing product selectivity. In particular, C2H4 formation is favored through pathways involving C–C coupling or *CO dimerization. , The Cu-based electrocatalysts could establish C–C bonds to produce C2H4 with an enhanced active surface area and increased residence time of *CO intermediates due to the complicated transport processes in/near structured catalysts during the CO2RR. ,− Especially, Cu2O and CuO encouraged HC production by improving the active sites, such as providing induced undercoordinated sites, grain boundaries, and rough surfaces. , This effect was corroborated by the performance of the nCuI, II electrocatalyst, which yielded C2H4 with an FE of 2.55%, along with CO (2.93%) and H2 (5.82%).

Therefore, adding Cu2O and CuO to both ZnO-based supports greatly increased the formation of C2H4 during the CO2RR. The ZnO–CuI,II casing achieved the highest C2H4 FE of 5.62%. This result was in agreement with the previous reports that the presence of ZnO in ZnO-decorated Cu2O nanocubes or the Cu–Zn catalyst via the sputtering and electrochemical cyclic voltammetry treatment could effectively promote the CO adsorption to form C2 products such as C2H4 and C2H5OH. ,

While the nZnO–CuI,II electrocatalyst yielded a slightly lower FE of 4.82%. This discrepancy could be explained by differences in the electrode structure, metal–support interactions, and preparation techniques. Significantly, the ZnO–CuI,II casing exhibited minimal formation of CO (0.09%), H2 (6.10%), and CH4 (0.06%), suggesting that most of the *CO intermediates were further reduced through C–C coupling pathways rather than being desorbed as CO. This behavior is consistent with the known function of Cu-based species in promoting *CO dimerization and adsorption, which improves the selectivity of the C2H4 product, whereas nZnO–CuI,II achieved high FEs of 3.74% for CO, 13.85% for H2, and 0.32% for CH4 due to its high surface area, facilitating an enhanced reactivity with CO2. Additional multi-carbon products such as methanol (CH3OH), ethanol (C2H5OH), or ethane (C2H6) may have been formed, as suggested by Todorova et al. However, gaseous product quantification in this study was limited, and no further analysis of minor or liquid-phase products was conducted.

It is noted that the FE of C2H4 in this study was lower than that of state-of-the-art Cu-based catalysts (typically ∼ 17–34%, and up to >50–80% under optimized flow-cell conditions). , Cu–Zn and Cu/ZnO systems have also demonstrated a higher FE of C2H4 (∼40–51%) due to strong interfacial effects. , Generally, the relatively low selectivity is attributed to the H-cell configuration, competing HER, and incomplete interface optimization. , Nevertheless, the ZnO–CuI,II casing in this study exhibited the highest FE of C2H4 (5.62%) among the studied samples, while Zn foil, ZnO, and ZnO/CuO-based electrocatalysts as references showed negligible activity (0–4.10%). This trend confirms that Cu incorporation into ZnO could promote C–C coupling, supporting the role of Cu–ZnO interfaces.

Regeneration of the ZnO–CuI, II Casing and nZnO–CuI, II

In order to confirm the stability of the electrocatalyst after the CO2RR, the regeneration ability of the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II was studied at a potential of −1.30 V, with the results summarized in Table . Moreover, SEM analyses of the electrocatalyst before and after CO2RR are shown in Figure . In the second CO2RR, both the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II could not produce C2H4. Before using the CO2RR, the morphology of the ZnO–CuI,II casing displayed Cu2O and CuO NPs covering the ZnO NRs (Figure a). After the CO2RR, the morphology of the ZnO–CuI,II casing was significantly changed, with the Cu2O and CuO NPs being lost from the ZnO NRs (Figure b); therefore, in the second CO2RR, the ZnO NRs directly produced CO, H2, and CH4. The rapid decline in C2H4 selectivity after regeneration indicates that the structural stability of the active Cu species remains a critical limitation of the present catalyst. On the basis of the observed Cu NP detachment and agglomeration, future efforts should focus on improving the anchoring strength of Cu species on the ZnO support through stronger metal–support interactions or deposition methods that yield more uniformly dispersed and strongly bound Cu sites. Accordingly, additional long-term measurements were not pursued as the target products were no longer detected after the regeneration process, indicating a significant loss of catalytic selectivity.

Representative SEM cross-sectional images of the (a, b) ZnO–CuI,II casing (a) before and (b) after CO2RR and (c, d) nZnO–CuI,II (c) before and (d) after CO2RR.

The nZnO–CuI,II electrocatalyst was prepared from ZnO, Cu2O, and CuO NPs in a spherical shape and deposited onto carbon paper (Figure c). After the CO2RR, the electrocatalyst tended to aggregate (Figure d), and therefore, nZnO–CuI,II could continue to react with CO2 to produce the desired products, except for C2H4. nZnO–CuI,II had higher FEs for CO and CH4 than the ZnO–CuI,II casing because nZnO–CuI,II was composed of NPs with a higher surface area compared to the ZnO–CuI,II casing.

Proposed Mechanism of Electrochemical CO2RR Catalysis by the ZnO–CuI,II Casing and nZnO–CuI,II

A plausible mechanism for the electrochemical CO2RR over the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II electrocatalysts was schematically illustrated in Figure . For the ZnO–CuI,II casing catalyst (Figure a), the ZnO nanorod surface was uniformly coated with Cu2O and CuO layers, which may create a confined ZnO–Cu interfacial environment and increase the residence time of CO-related intermediates at the catalyst surface. Previous studies had reported that Cu exhibited a moderate binding strength toward adsorbed *CO, enabling *CO to undergo further H+/e– transfer and couple with another *CO to form multi-carbon products (C2+). In contrast, Zn showed relatively weak *CO binding, so *CO tended to desorb more readily and CO was produced with high selectivity. Based on earlier reports on Cu–Zn composite electrocatalysts, , it is therefore reasonable to hypothesize that CO or CO-related intermediates generated on Zn-containing sites may transfer to adjacent Cu sites, where the higher *CO surface coverage and more favorable *CO binding on Cu could facilitate C–C coupling and promote C2H4 formation.

Schematic illustration of the proposed mechanism for the electrochemical CO2RR catalysis by the (a) ZnO–CuI,II casing and (b) nZnO–CuI,II electrocatalysts.

The proposed mechanism for nZnO–CuI,II is illustrated in Figure b. The ZnO, Cu2O, and CuO nanoparticles were dispersed onto carbon paper. Although nanoparticulate systems typically offer a higher electrochemically active surface area, in this case, the spatial distribution of the catalyst may have been nonuniform, leading to inefficient activation of *CO species and suboptimal C2H4 formation. Furthermore, CuO nanoparticles may catalyze HER through interaction with H+ present in the electrolyte, resulting in enhanced H2 production. This competitive reaction could further reduce the FE of C2 products.

Conclusions

The Zn casing was successfully utilized as an electrocatalyst in CO2RR. The branched morphology of the ZnO NRs with the deposited Cu2O and CuO NPs enhanced the catalytic surface area and resulted in the production of valuable HCs, such as C2H4. The different preparation methods between the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II affected the FE for C2H4. The ZnO–CuI,II casing accomplished a higher FE for C2H4 than nZnO–CuI,II. Although the nZnO–CuI,II NPs were dispersed on the carbon paper, their incomplete distribution hindered the full activation of C2H4, preventing a high FE. The SEM micrographs also confirmed the different morphologies between the ZnO–CuI,II casing and nZnO–CuI,II. The proposed mechanisms for the electrochemical CO2RR revealed that the different structural features of the electrocatalysts potentially impacted product formation. However, C2H4 production could not be sustained in the second CO2RR cycle due to the loss of the Cu2O and CuO NPs from ZnO NRs. In summary, the ZnO–CuI,II casing serves as an effective electrocatalyst for CO2RR, not only reducing electronic waste but also creating a new electrocatalyst from recycled materials.
