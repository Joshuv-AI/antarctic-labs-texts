Stable Single-Pass Electrochemical Oxidation of Trace 1,4-Dioxane in Groundwater via Multistage Reactors with Current Pulsing
Authors: Shasha Yang, Leslie Arrazolo, Fabian S. Menges, Ian H. Billinge, Kwan Lam Yip, Jae-Hong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical oxidation (EO) can destroy a broad range of water contaminants, but single-stage, single-pass reactors often require high energy input and low water flux to achieve meaningful removal, limiting practical deployment. These constraints are acute for 1,4-dioxane, a persistent groundwater contaminant and potential human carcinogen, where long-term operation in low-conductivity water matrices is further hindered by anode degradation, cathodic scaling, and formation of harmful byproducts. Here, we develop and validate a scalable strategy that combines a multistage, flow-through EO reactor with on/off current pulsing using commercially available Ti4O7 mesh anodes. In a laboratory electrolyte (50 mM NaClO4), 1,4-dioxane degradation in a single-stage cell followed pseudo-first-order kinetics (k = 0.103 min–1), and staging substantially increased single-pass removal across practical fluxes. In synthetic groundwater, pulsed operation markedly suppressed mineral scaling and reduced perchlorate byproduct formation by 44–82% relative to direct-current electrolysis while improving operational stability. Integrating staging with pulsing delivered the most durable performance: a four-stage reactor operated at 15 s on/15 s off maintained stable treatment in groundwater at an environmentally relevant 1,4-dioxane concentration (0.1 mg/L), achieving 60–80% single-pass removal (average ∼70%) over 20 days with minimal voltage rise. These results demonstrate that coupling reactor staging with pulsed electrolysis mitigates key failure modes and byproduct risks, providing a practical design and operating framework for long-lasting EO as point-of-use/point-of-entry treatment of trace contaminants in groundwater.

Introduction

As the pressure on centralized water and wastewater infrastructure increases, distributed treatment schemes are increasingly considered a sustainable alternative. Realizing effective decentralized treatment, however, still requires innovations that deliver high contaminant removal under practical hydraulic and water-quality conditions. Among emerging contaminants, 1,4-dioxane remains particularly challenging because of its persistence and high mobility in aquatic environments. Historically, 1,4-dioxane was used as a solvent stabilizer for methylene chloride in industrial processes, and it is also present as a byproduct/impurity in personal care products at levels that can reach hundreds of ppm. , As a result, 1,4-dioxane is now widely detected in groundwater, a major human exposure pathway. Epidemiological evidence has led to its classification as a potential human carcinogen. − In response to these health concerns, several US states have adopted stringent drinking-water standards; for example, New York State established a maximum contaminant level of 1 μg/L in 2020. Conventional filtration and adsorption processes remove 1,4-dioxane poorly, because of its small molecular size, neutral charge, and hydrophilicity. Conventional advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) such as UV/H2O2 can degrade 1,4-dioxane, − but their implementation in distributed systems is often constrained by practical requirements for chemical handling, energy delivery, and process control.

Electrochemical oxidation (EO) is a distinctive AOP that has been widely demonstrated for degrading pharmaceuticals, , organic dyes, , and carboxylic acids , in water. EO can oxidize pollutants without addition of chemical reagents through anodically generated oxidants and direct electron-transfer pathways. A typical EO setup consists of a power supply, an anode, a cathode, and a supporting electrolyte and can operate at ambient temperature and pressure. These attributes make EO attractive for decentralized treatment because of its modular design, operational simplicity, scalability, and potential for automation.

To move beyond batch operation, increasing attention has focused on flow-through EO reactors in which contaminated water passes through porous electrodes, thereby enhancing mass transfer by thinning the diffusion layer. − Most flow-through studies to date have employed a single-stage electrochemical cell (one anode/cathode pair) operated in either recirculation or a single-pass mode. Single-pass operation is particularly relevant to pump-and-treat applications, yet removal efficiency in single-pass, single-stage systems typically decreases as water flux increases because hydraulic retention time becomes too short for effective oxidation. , Increasing the current density can partially compensate for reduced residence time, but doing so elevates anodic potentials and accelerates oxidative stress, leading to electrode degradation and eventual deactivation. In realistic water matrices, continuous direct-current (DC) electrolysis also promotes mineral deposition (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+) on the cathode under an applied electric field, which increases cell resistance and compromises electrode integrity. Collectively, constraints, such as flux-dependent performance loss, anode oxidation, and cathode scaling, represent key barriers to deploying EO as a robust distributed treatment technology.

Here, we address these coupled limitations in treatment efficiency and electrode longevity for single-pass EO, using commercially available electrodes, and focusing on reactor and operational design. Multistage flow-through configurations, in which water sequentially passes through multiple identical cells, have been reported to improve contaminant removal through multistep reaction sequences. However, multistage EO has rarely been evaluated in realistic groundwater matrices with the explicit objective of extending electrode service life. In parallel, pulsed electrolysis has been studied as an alternative operational strategy for electrochemical water treatment, in part by periodically relaxing concentration and potential gradients near the electrode surface that develop under DC operation. Despite these advances, the synergistic combination of multistage reactor design and on/off pulsed electrolysis has not been explored as an integrated strategy for stable, long-term EO performance.

In this study, we develop and evaluate a multistage, flow-through EO system operated under on/off current pulsing for the long-term treatment of 1,4-dioxane in a low-conductivity, groundwater-relevant matrix at environmentally relevant concentrations. We specifically test the hypothesis that distributing the electrochemical load across stages while introducing periodic resting can (i) sustain high single-pass removal at practical water fluxes, (ii) mitigate anodic oxidation and cathodic scaling that drive deactivation, and (iii) suppress undesired parallel reactions, including formation of toxic byproducts. By establishing performance–stability relationships under realistic conditions, this work provides a practical design and operating framework for deploying EO as a safer and more durable distributed treatment technology.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

All chemicals were used as received. 1,4-Dioxane (C4H8O2), 1,4-dioxane-d8 (C4D8O2), sodium chlorate (NaClO3), sodium perchlorate (NaClO4), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), magnesium chloride (MgCl2), and calcium chloride (CaCl2) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). Mesh-type Ti4O7 anodes and Ti cathodes were obtained from QuanVerge Inc. (NV, USA). According to the vendor, the Ti4O7 mesh was fabricated by using a proprietary coating method on the Ti mesh (certified grade 1).

Electrode Characterization and Analytical Methods

Pristine Ti4O7 was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab, The Woodlands, TX, USA) upon receipt to confirm the phase purity. The XRD instrument includes a Cu Kα source with a beam energy of 8.04 keV, corresponding to an X-ray wavelength of 1.5406 Å. Ti4O7 electrodes before and after long-term experiments (e.g., 57 or 240 h) were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI VersaProbe III, Chanhassen, MN, USA) to quantify changes in the Ti oxidation state. For tests in laboratory water (50 mM NaClO4), 1,4-dioxane was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, USA) with an XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Trace-level 1,4-dioxane in groundwater-matrix tests was analyzed by a gas chromatograph coupled with a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (GCMS, TQ8050 NX, Shimadzu Co., Japan) equipped with an AOC-6000 autosampler and a DB 624UI column (30 m length, 0.25 mm i.d., 1.4 μm film thickness, Agilent). 1,4-Dioxane-d8 was used as the isotopically labeled internal standard. Sample preparation and 1,4-dioxane analysis by GCMS are described in Text S1. Inorganic disinfection byproducts including chlorate (ClO3 –) and perchlorate (ClO4 –) generated during EO were quantified by ion chromatography (940 Professional IC Vario, Metrohm, Switzerland) using an A Supp 19–250/4.0 mm column. The eluent consisted of 8.0 mM sodium carbonate, 0.25 mM sodium bicarbonate, and 45% (v/v) HPLC-grade acetonitrile. Calcium and magnesium from the cathode scale dissolved in 5% HNO3 solution were quantified by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, 5800 Vertical Dual View, Agilent Technologies, USA).

Single- and Multistage Single-Pass Electrochemical Cell

Single- to four-stage electrochemical reactors were assembled by using commercially available mesh Ti4O7 (anode) and Ti (cathode) electrodes installed in the acrylic reactor (Figure S1). For the single-stage configuration (denoted “1 stage”), one pair of Ti4O7 mesh and Ti mesh electrodes (2 × 2 cm each) were placed in a reactor with a volume of 2.4 mL. The two-stage configuration (“2 stage”) contained two identical anode/cathode pairs arranged as anode/cathode/anode/cathode, and the reactor volume was doubled to 4.8 mL. The same electrode layout was extended to the three-stage (“3 stage”) and four-stage (“4 stage”) systems. All electrodes were powered by a potentiostat (BioLogic Science Instruments, France).

A feed solution containing 1,4-dioxane (8.8 mg/L) in laboratory water (50 mM NaClO4, 8230 μS/cm) or synthetic groundwater (composition provided in Table S1, 2539 μS/cm) was pumped upward through the reactor (bottom to top). The influent concentration of 8.8 mg/L was selected to evaluate the degradation kinetics and system performance. For tests under more environmentally relevant conditions, 0.1 mg/L 1,4-dioxane was spiked into the synthetic groundwater. The flow rate was controlled using a peristaltic pump, and all experiments were conducted in a single-pass mode. Effluent samples were collected directly at the reactor outlet. Cathodic scaling was quantified by measuring the mass of Mg and Ca dissolved from the cathode into 5% HNO3. After each test, the cathode was air-dried at room temperature for 30 min and sonicated in 5% HNO3 for 10 min. The resulting solution was then analyzed by ICP-OES for dissolved Ca and Mg.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of the Ti4O7 Mesh Electrode

We first confirmed that the as-received Ti4O7 electrode exhibited purity and performance comparable to those of research-grade Ti4O7 electrodes reported for water treatment applications. XRD analysis confirmed that Ti4O7 was the dominant TiO x phase on the electrode surface (Figure S2a). The diffraction patterns aligned well with the reference pattern for Magnéli-phase Ti4O7 (JCPDS #00-050-0787), indicating high chemical purity. XPS results further showed that Ti­(IV) was the dominant Ti valence state with 3.52% Ti­(III) sites and 80.39% lattice oxygen (Figure S2b,c). The Ti valence states in the obtained electrode were consistent with literature reports for Magnéli Ti4O7. , In the following discussion, changes in the percentages of Ti­(III) sites and lattice oxygen in different spent anodes are compared as indicators of anode oxidation.

We then performed cyclic voltammetry in 100 mM NaClO4 to characterize the electrochemical properties of the Ti4O7 electrode (Figure S3a). The as-received Ti4O7 electrode exhibited a relatively high oxygen evolution potential of ∼2.3 V RHE, which is favorable for EO water treatment. This onset potential was comparable to those reported for other Ti4O7 anodes (2.2–2.7 V RHE). − We further conducted cyclic voltammetry within a non-Faradaic potential window (open-circuit potential ±0.05 V) at scan rates of 5–100 mV/s (Figure S3b) to determine an electric double-layer capacitance of 10.19 mF (Figure S3c). The electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) was then estimated as ∼170 cm2 using an average specific capacitance of 60 μF/cm2 for oxide electrodes. The roughness factor (ECSA divided by the geometric surface area of ∼6.7 cm2) was 25, indicating a large accessible surface area and the high availability of active sites.

Single-Stage vs Multistage Electrochemical Cell in Lab Water

We assembled electrodes in EO systems configured with one to four stages (Figure a) and evaluated each configuration for 1,4-dioxane removal. For these initial tests, we used a relatively high 1,4-dioxane concentration (8.8 mg/L, 100 μM) in 50 mM NaClO4. All experiments were conducted in single-pass, flow-through mode at water fluxes of 12–720 L/m2·h (Text S2), and the same current density (7 mA/cm2) was applied to each stage in all systems.

(a) Setup of electrochemical cell with different stages. (b) Removal of 1,4-dioxane by electrochemical cell with 1–4 stages at various water flux at a current density of 7 mA/cm2 per anode. (c) Comparison of experimental 1,4-dioxane removal and estimated removal from pseudo-first-order kinetics by 1–4 stage system at various water flux. (d) Energy consumption by 1–4 stage system at various water flux at a current density of 7 mA/cm2. (e) Removal of 1,4-dioxane and (f) energy consumption by 1–4 stage cell at various water flux at a current of 48 mA (corresponding to current density per anode of 7, 5.25, 3.5, and 1.75 mA/cm2 for the 1-, 2-, 3-, and 4-stage systems, respectively). All tests were conducted in 50 mM NaClO4 (pH: 6.41) electrolyte spiked with 8.8 mg/L 1,4-dioxane. Number of experimental replicates: n = 2.

As water flux increased, 1,4-dioxane removal decreased (Figure b), consistent with a shorter hydraulic retention time (HRT) and thus reduced the time for 1,4-dioxane to be degraded (Text S2). Adding an excess radical quencher (MeOH) decreased the 1,4-dioxane removal rate to ∼30 from ∼70% (Figure S4). Incomplete reaction inhibition suggests that 1,4-dioxane oxidation results from both electrochemically generated hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and direct electron transfer at the anode surface. Figure b also shows that multistage operation substantially improved 1,4-dioxane removal across the tested flux range. For example, at 72 L/m2·h, the four-stage system achieved 86% removal, whereas the single-stage system achieved 46%. This trend is kinetically expected because one pass through a four-stage cell is equivalent to four consecutive passes through a single-stage cell (Figure S5a).

Degradation of 1,4-dioxane in the single-stage cell at different HRTs followed pseudo-first-order kinetics with a rate constant of k = 0.103 min–1 (Figure S5b). For an n-stage cell, assuming identical stages with the same rate constant, the effluent concentration (C n ) can be predicted by 1 Cn=C0e−nkt where C 0 is the 1,4-dioxane concentration in the influent, and t is the HRT calculated by cell volume (mL) divided by flow rate (mL/min). This model provides a rapid design estimate for the number of stages needed to meet a target removal efficiency for a given water flux and current density. For example, at 36 L/m2·h and 7 mA/cm2, achieving 99.9% removal would require seven stages; additional stages (≥8) would yield diminishing returns and approach complete removal. Similar predictions can be made for changes in current density (higher removal at higher current density; Figure S6a,b) using rate constants obtained from the fitted kinetics (Figure S6c).

We next compared experimental multistage performance with model predictions (Figure c). With an increasing water flux (i.e., decreasing HRT), the experimental removal exceeded that predicted from the single-stage kinetics. We attribute this enhancement to improved mass transfer of 1,4-dioxane to the anode/electrolyte double layer at higher flux, , an effect likely amplified by sequential placement of mesh electrodes that provides extra turbulence. Thus, while the kinetic model provides a conservative baseline, the practical performance of the multistage, pass-through system can outperform theoretical estimates at higher flux.

We further explored the transition between kinetic and mass-transfer limited regions with increasing water flux in single- and multistage systems. We normalized the removal rates from Figure b as the total mass removed per time per electrode area (g/h·m2). As shown in Figure S7, in the low-flux region, 1,4-dioxane oxidation was kinetically limited and the available electrode area in multistage systems was underutilized, resulting in low normalized removal rates. As water flux increased, the normalized removal rate increased substantially with additional electrode stages, confirming that multistage stacking synergizes with enhanced mass transport to improve overall treatment capacity. These observations collectively demonstrate that while a single-stage configuration may be more area-efficient when treating at low flow rates, multistage stacking is essential for overcoming mass transfer limitations and allowing enhanced removal at high throughput.

This improved removal, however, came at the expense of higher energy consumption when the same current density was applied at every stage. Electrical energy consumption (EEO) was defined as the electrical energy (kWh) required to reduce the 1,4-dioxane concentration by 1 order of magnitude in 1 m3 of water (Text S2). As shown in Figure d, when each electrode was operated at 7 mA/cm2, EEO varied strongly with the number of stages, even at the same flux; at lower flux (higher HRT and higher overall removal), EEO increased with increasing stage number. We therefore evaluated an alternative operating mode in which the same total current (48 mA) was applied to all systems (equal to the current used for the single-stage system, 7 mA/cm2). Under this condition, the current density per anode was 7, 5.25, 3.5, and 1.75 mA/cm2 for the 1-, 2-, 3-, and 4-stage systems, respectively. Despite the lower per-electrode current density in the multistage configurations, the sequential reaction scheme (Figure S5a) still enabled increased 1,4-dioxane removal (Figure e). Importantly, multistage operation under constant total current reduced EEO across the tested flux range (Figure f), lowering energy consumption by approximately 50% relative to operation at 7 mA/cm2 per stage. The lower anodic potential at reduced current (Figure S3a) corresponded to lower cell voltage, likely suppressing parasitic reactions (e.g., oxygen evolution and bubble formation) and improving current utilization, collectively contributing to a lower energy demand.

Multistage System in Synthetic Groundwater Matrix

We further examined the lifetime of the as-received electrodes by conducting long-term, flow-through treatment of synthetic groundwater (Table S1) spiked with 1,4-dioxane (8.8 mg/L). We first evaluated a single-stage cell operated at a total current of 48 mA (7 mA/cm2). As shown in Figure , the 1,4-dioxane removal efficiency rapidly declined with the treatment time, concomitant with an increase in cell voltage. After ∼57 h, the cell voltage exceeded the potentiostat limit (10 V), and the system deactivated, resulting in complete loss of 1,4-dioxane destruction capability. This deactivation was accompanied by both anode oxidation and cathode scaling. Anode oxidation was confirmed by XPS, which showed a 55% decrease in the number of Ti­(III) sites and a 33% decrease in the level of lattice oxygen relative to the fresh anode (Figure S8a,b). In parallel, a thick white deposit formed on the spent cathode, consisting of Mg­(OH)2, MgCO3, and CaCO3 precipitates (Figure S8c,d). Together, these results indicate that the observed deactivation at ∼ 57 h resulted from concurrent failures at both the anode and the cathode.

Synthetic groundwater spiked with 8.8 mg/L 1,4-dioxane was pumped through the cells at a water flux of 36 L/m2·h. A constant current of 48 mA (corresponding to current density per anode of 7 and 1.75 mA/cm2 for the 1- and 4-stage systems, respectively) was applied. Number of experimental replicates: n = 2.

We hypothesized that, under the same total current input, distributing the load across a multistage system would reduce the electrochemical burden on each electrode relative to a single-stage configuration, thereby extending service lifetime. Consistent with this hypothesis, the four-stage system maintained substantially higher 1,4-dioxane removal (50–80%) for 240 h while consuming, on average, ∼50% of the electrical energy required by the single-stage system (data not shown; Figure ). However, despite the improved stability and reduced energy consumption, the 1,4-dioxane removal efficiency in the four-stage system still gradually decreased from 78 to 47% over the 240-h treatment period.

Pulsed Electrolysis

In parallel with the multistage configuration examined above, we evaluated a pulsed electrolysis scheme to mitigate the efficiency loss observed during long-term treatment of synthetic groundwater. Recent studies have shown that periodic potential pulsing can enhance CO2 reduction by suppressing the competing hydrogen evolution reaction. , Periodic current pulsing has also been reported to repeatedly renew the electrical double layer (EDL) and reduce energy consumption during electrochemical treatment of municipal solid-waste leachate.

We first applied periodic pulsing to a single-stage system for single-pass treatment of the same synthetic groundwater at a water flux of 36 L/m2·h. As shown in the inset of Figure S9a, a current of 48 mA was applied during the pulse-on period, while 0 mA (a non-Faradaic condition) was applied during pulse-off. For the initial screening, the pulse-on duration was set equal to the pulse-off duration. Effluent samples were collected after 1 h once a steady state was reached. Although the system was periodically off (50% of the total operating time), 1,4-dioxane removal increased from 29 to ∼40% as the pulse-on time increased from 1 to 15 s and then approached a plateau at longer pulse-on times (20 and 30 s; Figure S9a). Because electrode-deactivation processes can develop under longer electrolysis periodse.g., EDL expansion that slows diffusion of reactants such as 1,4-dioxane to the anode surfacewe selected 15 s as the pulse-on duration for subsequent experiments.

We then varied the duty ratio, defined as the fraction of pulse-on time within each cycle, from 100% (continuous electrolysis) to 25% (15 s on/45 s off) by increasing the pulse-off duration: 2 dutyratio=tonton+toff×100% where t on is the anodic on-time and t off is the resting off-time. As expected, the 1,4-dioxane removal rate decreased with a decreasing duty ratio due to the shorter effective electrolysis time (Figure a). Duty ratio also strongly influenced the formation of perchlorate (ClO4 –), a disinfection byproduct produced via oxidation of chloride, which is ubiquitous in groundwater. , At the chloride concentration used in this study (6 mM), Cl– also inhibited 1,4-dioxane removal through the competition for active sites on the anode surface (Figure S4). Notably, at duty ratios of 25–75%, pulsed electrolysis produced 44–82% less perchlorate than continuous electrolysis (Figure a). It is noteworthy that this reduction was substantially larger than the corresponding decrease in the level of 1,4-dioxane removal.

We attribute the strong dependency of Cl– oxidation on pulsing to its multistep reaction pathway involving ClO– and ClO3 – intermediates prior to formation of ClO4 – (Figure b). ,, During pulse-off, chloride-oxidation reactions are halted, allowing the intermediates to be transported away from the electrode surface. Consequently, when the current is reapplied, ClO4 – does not immediately form due to the absence of accumulated precursors such as ClO3 –. The concurrent decrease in ClO3 – formation further supports that pulsing slows the chain reactions from Cl– to ClO4 – (Figure S9b). We note that the chloride concentration in our synthetic groundwater (6 mM) is at the upper end of reported groundwater Cl– levels. Therefore, perchlorate formation should be substantially lower for most waters containing lower chloride concentrations. On/off pulsing also markedly mitigated cathodic scaling. For example, a duty ratio of 50% reduced scaling by 82% (Figure a). Inorganic scale on the cathode surface is driven by the gradual accumulation of OH– generated from the hydrogen evolution reaction at the cathode. We hypothesize that pulse-off periods interrupt the OH– buildup and allow the release of Ca2+ and Mg2+ enriched near the cathode surface, analogous to the mechanism proposed above for suppressing perchlorate formation (Figure b). To test this hypothesis, we directly measured the pH near the cathode surface under both DC and pulsed operation. As illustrated in Figure S9c, under DC conditions, the cathode surface pH increased rapidly to and remained at ∼14 throughout the electrolysis. In contrast, during on/off pulsed operation, the pH at the cathode surface rose more gradually and decreased to ∼12 during pulse-off. These findings indicate that pulsed operation mitigates extreme local alkalization at the cathode surface, thereby suppressing Ca/Mg carbonate or hydroxide precipitation.

Finally, we conducted a long-term test on a single-stage system under the same conditions as those in Figure , but with pulsed operation. A pulse-on time of 15 s and a 50% duty ratio (15 s on/15 s off) was selected because it reduced ClO4 – formation and scaling by >50% while maintaining a relatively high 1,4-dioxane removal rate. As shown in Figure c, the single-stage system operated for at least 60 h with a stable 1,4-dioxane removal efficiency of ∼30%, whereas complete deactivation occurred at ∼57 h under continuous electrolysis. This improved stability was consistent with less severe anode oxidation, as reflected by smaller decreases in Ti­(III) sites (49% decrease) and lattice oxygen (29% decrease) after 60 h of pulsed treatment (Figure S10a,b). Collectively, these results suggest that periodic pulsing can be advantageous, because the benefits of substantially suppressing parallel side reactions (toxic byproduct formation and cathodic scaling) and extending anode lifetime can outweigh the moderate loss in 1,4-dioxane removal efficiency.

Long-Term Treatment of 1,4-Dioxane by Pulsed Multistage System

We have shown above that, under continuous DC operation, the multistage system provided a longer service life than the single-stage system. However, continuous DC potential application imposed oxidative stress and promoted cathode scaling, resulting in a gradual decline in 1,4-dioxane removal. Although pulsed electrolysis, an alternative strategy to enhance the service life, delayed deactivation of the single-stage system, it did not fully prevent the time-dependent decrease in removal efficiency, likely because the current per electrode remained relatively high in the single-stage configuration. To improve long-term stability, we therefore proposeas a new approach, combining the multistage design with pulsed electrolysis. This strategy lowers the potential burden on each electrode while introducing periodic resting, which is expected to mitigate anode oxidation and cathode scaling and, thereby, stabilize treatment performance.

To test this concept, we conducted a long-term experiment treating 1,4-dioxane (8.8 mg/L) in synthetic groundwater using a four-stage system operated with 15 s on/15 s off pulsing at a pulse-on current of 48 mA (Figure a). After 240 h, the 1,4-dioxane removal efficiency decreased by only 6%, accompanied by a modest increase in the cell voltage of ∼1.5 V (Figure b). Consistent with Figure a, switching from continuous electrolysis to pulsed operation slightly reduced 1,4-dioxane removal (by ∼18%). Nevertheless, compared with the four-stage system under continuous electrolysiswhere the removal efficiency decreased by ∼30% over 240 h, the pulsed strategy markedly improved stability, consistent with the benefits of periodic resting and slower cathodic scale accumulation. Moreover, under the same pulsing conditions, the four-stage system maintained a stable and higher removal efficiency (∼60%) than the single-stage system under DC operation (∼30%). XPS analysis of the spent anode surface further indicated the lowest extent of oxidation among the tested conditions, with a 45% decrease in Ti­(III) sites and a 15% decrease in lattice oxygen (Figure S10c,d).

To evaluate performance under more realistic conditions, we spiked a trace level of 1,4-dioxane (0.1 mg/L; at the high end of concentrations reported in groundwater) into the synthetic groundwater matrix. Control experiments performed in 50 mM NaClO4 electrolytevarying the number of stages (1–4), water flux (36–720 L/m2·h), and initial 1,4-dioxane concentration (0.1–50 mg/L)confirmed a substantial increase in removal efficiency as the influent 1,4-dioxane concentration decreased (Figure S11), attributable to reduced competition for a limited •OH flux and for reactive sites at the anode surface. Figure c shows the single-pass treatment of trace 1,4-dioxane in the groundwater matrix using the pulsed scheme over 20 days of continuous operation. Throughout the 20-day test, the system maintained stable 1,4-dioxane removal between 60 and 80%, with an average of ∼70%. The cell voltage also remained nearly constant, increasing by only ∼0.8 V over the entire period, further indicating stable long-term operation.

Cost Analysis

We further conducted a cost analysis comparing a conventional single-stage system operated under the DC mode with the proposed four-stage system operated under pulsed conditions (Figure S12). The electrode material cost for the 1-stage configuration was $5.33, whereas the 4-stage system required four times the electrode area, resulting in an electrode material cost of $21.32. Despite the higher upfront electrode cost, pulsed operation substantially reduced the electrical energy demand of the 4-stage system. The specific electrical energy consumption of the 1-stage system over 57 h of operation was estimated as 29.86 kWh/m3. In contrast, the four-stage system operated for 240 h (approximately four times longer) with an estimated electrical energy consumption of 6.6 kWh/m3, attributable to periodic resting during pulsed electrolysis. Using an electricity rate of $0.30/kWh, the corresponding electricity costs were $8.96/m3 for the 1-stage system and $1.98/m3 for the 4-stage pulsed system. Overall, the multistage pulsed configuration enabled substantially longer operation (≥4 times) while increasing the combined electrode-plus-electricity cost by only ∼1.5 times, highlighting its potential as a cost-effective and durable EO strategy for groundwater treatment. We further extended the electricity cost estimation to a household-scale deployment of the proposed 4-stage system. According to the US Geological Survey, the average residential water use in the United States is around 82 gallons per person per day. For a four-person house, operation of such a four-stage pulsed EO system would incur an additional electricity cost of approximately $2.46 per day. Collectively, these cost estimations demonstrate the feasibility of so-designed multistage pulsed EO system for decentralized water treatment application (e.g., point-of-entry or point-of-use).

It is important to note that the cost analysis presented here was intended as a preliminary comparison based on bench-scale treatability tests, with the aim to highlight the potential operational benefits of the proposed reactor configuration. The present study demonstrated that the number of stages affects the reactor utilization efficiency and that the pulsing duty ratio directly influences average power consumption and oxidant generation dynamics. These relationships define the design space that ultimately governs the most cost-effective operating conditions for a given application. A comprehensive lifecycle economic assessment should also include factors such as maintenance frequency, cleaning procedures, and electrode replacement costs. Such a complete techno-economic analysis would be more meaningful when performed under pilot- or full-scale long-duration operation in field-relevant conditions, which will be the focus of our future work.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a multistage, flow-through EO reactor incorporating commercially available Ti4O7 mesh electrodes for the destruction of 1,4-dioxane in groundwater-relevant conditions. Prior EO studies have demonstrated effective contaminant degradation, but long-term behavior is often inferred from short-duration tests − or evaluated in simplified, high-conductivity electrolytes that do not capture performance losses and side reactions that emerge in realistic matrices. − Here, by operating in single-pass mode and extending continuous treatment from hours to weeks, we directly quantified the coupled limitations that constrain practical EO deployment (flux-dependent removal losses, progressive electrode deactivation, cathodic scaling, and perchlorate formation) and established engineering strategies to mitigate them.

We showed that reactor staging provided a scalable route to increase single-pass removal without increasing the electrochemical burden on a single electrode pair. At the same total current input as a conventional single-stage reactor, multistage operation improved 1,4-dioxane degradation through multistep reaction sequences while lowering energy consumption and extending service life in synthetic groundwater. Second, we demonstrated that on/off pulsed electrolysis is an effective operational lever to suppress failure pathways and improve water-quality outcomes. Pulsing substantially reduced perchlorate formation and cathode scaling relative to continuous DC operation, indicating that periodic resting interrupts multistep chloride-oxidation pathways and limits the accumulation of scaling precursors near the cathode. Importantly, integrating these two approaches, multistage architecture plus pulsed operation, yielded the most durable performance, enabling stable single-pass treatment of trace 1,4-dioxane in a low-conductivity groundwater matrix for 20 days with an average removal of ∼ 70% and minimal voltage rise. Collectively, these results establish a practical design–operation framework that moves EO closer to point-of-entry or point-of-use deployment for persistent trace organics.

Further work is warranted to translate this approach to field implementation. Reducing anode–cathode spacing and optimizing hydraulic design could decrease ohmic losses and further improve energy efficiency, particularly in low-conductivity waters. Electrode materials and coatings with higher stability under elevated anodic potentials should also be evaluated for long-duration operation, where polarization may increase over time. In addition, future studies should quantify performance in diverse real groundwaters (varying chloride, alkalinity, natural organic matter, and hardness), develop predictive models that couple kinetics with mass transfer and byproduct pathways under pulsing, and assess scale-up considerations, such as modular stacking, power-management strategies, and maintenance protocols.
