Insect vectors have personality: first evidence with the tiger mosquito Aedes albopictus
Authors: Giulia Cordeschi, Valentina Mastrantonio, Chiara De Nicola, Valentina Lucchesi, Romeo Bellini, Daniele Canestrelli, Daniele Porretta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, our view of inter-individual diversity in insects has been deeply impacted by the discovery that they exhibit individual personalities, expressed as consistent individual differences in behavioral tendencies across time and contexts. Personality traits, such as boldness, exploration, and activity, have been documented in several species and linked to variation in multiple life history traits with consequences for insect population ecology. However, personalities remain largely unexplored in mosquito vectors. Here, we investigated the presence of personality traits in the tiger mosquito Aedes albopictus larvae. A total of 41 larvae (16 males, 18 females, 7 unsexed) were individually tested under laboratory conditions. Activity was measured by time spent in locomotion in the housing trays, exploration by the area covered in a novel environment, and boldness by the latency to re-emerge after a simulated aerial threat. All behavioral traits showed significant repeatability through time, with no significant effect of sex. Moreover, boldness, activity, and exploration were significantly correlated, with more active larvae being bolder and more exploratory. Overall, our results reveal the occurrence of personalities in mosquito vector larvae. We propose that incorporating personality into mosquito research could offer new insights into their biology and ecology, with potential implications for vector control strategies and the management of mosquito-borne diseases.Keywords: Animal personality, Repeatability, Mosquitoes, Vector ecology, Aedes albopictusSubject terms: Behavioural ecology, Entomology, Animal behaviour

Introduction

Animal personality refers to consistent interindividual variation in behavioral traits across time and contexts1,2 and has become a central topic in behavioral ecology over the past two decades1,3–8. Personality traits include boldness, exploration, activity, aggressiveness, and sociability, which are often correlated, resulting in behavioral syndromes3,6,9,10. A growing body of evidence shows that personalities can influence ecological and evolutionary processes by affecting population persistence and demography, local adaptation, individual dispersal and species interactions3,6,10–17.

While much of the research has focused on vertebrates, an increasing number of studies have begun to explore personalities in invertebrates, including insects18–22. The occurrence of personality has been documented in several insect taxa challenging the view that they have stereotyped behaviors19,23–29. As in vertebrates, it is now clear that personality can significantly shape insect population ecology and evolutionary dynamics22,29. For example, in the field cricket Gryllus integer, males from populations exposed to higher predation pressure exhibit reduced boldness, suggesting local adaptation30. In the firebug Pyrrhocoris apterus, bolder and more exploratory individuals showed a greater tendency to disperse, and were more likely to host parasites, highlighting the role of personality in shaping dispersal dynamics and species interactions31,32.

Despite the importance of personality for insect population ecology, the potential occurrence of personality traits in mosquito species remains unexplored. Mosquitoes (Diptera: Culicidae) comprise about 3,500 recognized species and occur in practically every region of the world except Antarctica33–35. Many mosquito species belonging to the Anopheles, Aedes and Culex genera are vectors of pathogens that cause major diseases in humans and domestic animals, such as malaria, filariasis, encephalitis, yellow fever, and dengue36. Like all holometabolous insects, mosquitoes undergo complete metamorphosis with immature stages (larvae and pupae) developing in water. The larval stage is a key step in the mosquito life cycle, as larvae accumulate food reserves necessary to tide them through the intense process of metamorphosis37. Furthermore, conditions experienced during the larval stage can have lasting effects on adult traits and, ultimately, on pathogen transmission potential via carry-over effects38.

Behavioral studies on mosquito larvae have shown a range of behaviors linked to foraging, predator avoidance, and movement39–44. These behaviors vary not only within species due to biotic and abiotic environmental conditions39,45–50, but also among species, populations and sexes39,44,51,52. For example, differences in feeding strategy were observed among Aedes, Culex and Anopheles species, with Aedes and Culex typically browsing (i.e. removing microorganisms from surfaces using their mouth parts), whereas Anopheles favours filtering microorganisms from the water column53. In Aedes triseriatus, differences between populations in patterns of activity and positioning were observed54, as well as an intersexual difference in antipredator behaviour, with males showing a significant shift away from risky behaviour (thrashing and browsing)47. These findings highlight the complexity and ecological relevance of mosquito larval behavior. However, most studies have focused on average trends rather than consistent individual variation.

To begin addressing this gap, we investigated personality traits in the larvae of the tiger mosquito Aedes (Stegomyia) albopictus (Skuse, 1894). This species is native to temperate and tropical areas of Asia and has spread and become established over the last few decades through all continents except Antarctica55. Due to its rapid expansion and capacity to act as a vector of several human arboviruses, such as dengue and chikungunya, Ae. albopictus represents a major global threat to public health56,57. Here, we specifically aimed to characterize larval personality traits of activity, exploration, and boldness and assess whether these traits are correlated.

Results

Aedes albopictus larvae were individually characterized for activity, exploration, and boldness, testing individuals twice on two consecutive days. Personality traits are defined as repeatable inter-individual behavioural differences across time or contexts1.We found a high inter-individual diversity in all tested behaviors. For activity (i.e., the general level of activity of an individual), we measured the percentage of time each larva spent actively moving, i.e. performing thrashing behavior. Thrashing time ranged from 92.6% to 5.2% in the first trial and from 94.7% to 6.1% in the second trial (Fig. 1). For exploration (i.e., an individual’s reaction to a new situation), we assessed the spatial coverage by larvae in a novel environment by using an experimental arena divided into 2 × 2 cells. We found that the number of cells crossed by individuals at least once ranged from 12 to 111 in the first trial and from 7 to 80 in the second trial (Fig. 1). For boldness (i.e., the propensity to risk-taking behaviors), we measured the latency to re-emerge from deeper water following a threatening aerial stimulus. It ranged from 24.63s to 370.02s in the first trial and from 18.53s to 515.85s in the second trial (Fig. 1).

All behavioral traits measured in Ae. albopictus larvae showed significant repeatability across trials, indicating that inter-individual differences were stable and consistent over time. Generalized linear mixed effect (GLMM)-based repeatability models showed that all behaviors were significantly repeatable, with no significant effect of sex (Table 1). In addition, our results showed that boldness, activity, and exploration were significantly correlated. Indeed, thrashing time and space coverage in the novel environments were positively correlated (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients 0.44, p = 0.003). In contrast, the latter was negatively correlated with latency to re-emerge (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients − 0.35, p = 0.027) (Table 2), which means that more active individuals were also more explorative and bolder.

Discussion

Previous studies have highlighted the behavioral complexity of mosquito larvae, showing variation across species, populations and sexes44,47,54,58. However, no studies had previously investigated consistent inter-individual behavioral differences. Here, we investigated the presence of personality traits in the mosquito Aedes albopictus and we found that larvae exhibit consistent individual differences along three behavioural axes: activity, boldness and exploration. We observed repeatability values which are comparable to the average behavioral repeatability reported by Bell et al. (2009)59 across more than 750 species for traits related to activity, antipredator response, and exploration. Our experimental design was well-controlled for the environment, while it did not account for the genetic background of the experimental larvae (single- or multiple-mother origin). If Ae. albopictus larvae used have originated from a single female, the observed repeatability could be affected by both the genetic background and the shared environment. In contrast, if the larvae came from different females, the observed repeatability would be primarily affected by the shared environment. Although it is unlikely that the experimental larvae have originated from a single mother (see methods), explicitly testing for behavioral repeatability of individuals from different mothers and environments would allow to overcome this potential limitation.

The occurrence of personalities in mosquito larvae is especially relevant, because larval stage is a key stage in the mosquito life cycle. Research on animal personality showed that activity and boldness often correlate with ecological traits such as foraging efficiency, growth rate and survival6,11,12,19,60. For example, more active and exploratory individuals generally encounter more food, leading to greater energy intake and faster growth61. Similarly, bolder individuals may face higher predation risk but benefit from greater resource acquisition10,22. Therefore, in mosquitoes, variation in larval personality can lead to differential resource exploitation and adaptation, ultimately influencing larval populations. Furthermore, since early life experience affects later stages through carry-over effects, larval personalities might directly affect adult traits. For example, the larval ability to acquire and store nutrients has been shown to affect female fertility and vector competence38,62–64. In the yellow fever mosquito Aedes aegypti, poor larval nutrition reduces adult size and fecundity63,65. Likewise in Anopheles stephensi, larval food availability influences body size, survival, gonotrophic cycle length, and biting rate, directly affecting malaria transmission potential38. Interestingly, in animal species with complex life cycles, including holometabolous insects, personality traits and behavioural syndromes can also remain consistent through metamorphosis26,66. For instance, in the lady beetle Eriopis connexa, ontogenetic consistency between larvae and adults has been documented for activity, aggressiveness, boldness, and exploration67. In mosquitoes, whether personality expressed during the larval stage persists into adulthood warrants further study as this could shape relevant adult traits such as fecundity and dispersal68,69, finally affecting vectorial capacity.

Investigating the occurrence of personality can also be relevant for mosquito control. Mosquitoes indeed are important disease vectors36 and the larval stage is often a primary target of chemical and biological control strategies70–73. The occurrence of larval personality may affect the success of these mosquito control methods. Indeed, personality traits could influence the likelihood of insecticide exposure and survival. For instance, in the maize weevil (Sitophilus zeamais), more active individuals survived better under insecticide pressure by reaching untreated areas since they cover greater distances11. Also, the individual personality might determine the efficacy of the predator in controlling vector populations since their effectiveness could depend on prey behavior, as observed in several animal species28,74–77.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence of personality traits in a mosquito species, revealing consistent individual differences in Ae. albopictus larvae. Extending this research to natural populations will be crucial to capture the frequency of behavioural types under field conditions, which could be underestimated in captive animals and to assess the ecological relevance of larval personality59,78. Furthermore, addressing the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to personality will help clarifying how they interact in shaping consistent individual behavioural variation79,80. Overall, these results by adding a new dimension of intra-specific diversity in mosquito larvae contribute to filling a gap in mosquito biology and ecology and lay the groundwork for future research.

Methods

Aedes albopictus individuals used in this study came from the mosquito mass colonies of Centro Agricoltura Ambiente (CAA) “Giorgio Nicoli” (Crevalcore, Bologna, Italy). The colony was originated from field samples collected in Emilia-Romagna in 2020 and maintained under standard laboratory conditions (28 ± 1 °C, 80 ± 5% RH, L:D 14:10 h). The experimental larvae have originated from egg-laying paper where about 10,000 females oviposited. After egg hatching, first-instar larvae were transferred individually to plastic trays (12 × 12 × 7 cm) filled with 200 ml of tap water and maintained in a thermostatic chamber with standard conditions of temperature (26 ± 1 °C), relative humidity (70%), and photoperiod (16: 8 h light: dark). Experimental larvae were fed ad libitum with cat food. All behavioral tests were conducted when individuals reached the third larval stage.

All Ae. albopictus larvae (N = 41) were individually characterized in activity, exploration, and boldness personality axes1 (Fig. 2). Activity (i.e., the general level of activity of an individual) was assessed by measuring time spent in locomotion in the housing trays. Each third-instar larva was video-recorded individually for 10 min in the housing tray using a Canon TG-6 camera. We measured the percentage of time the larvae spend actively moving, i.e., the thrashing behavior (Fig. 2A). During thrashing, the larva moves through the water column by energetic lateral flexions of the body40,81. This behavior is the wriggling motion mosquito larvae make when they swim.

Schematic representation of the tests conducted on Aedes albopictus larvae. Experimental arena used to investigate activity, exploration, and boldness (graphic design by Alessandra Spanò).

Exploration (i.e., an individual’s reaction to a new situation) was assessed as the spatial coverage in a novel environment1. Larvae were placed individually in new plastic trays (i.e. novel environment) (40 × 30 × 12 cm) filled with 1000 ml of tap water. Thus, larvae were video-recorded for 10 min using a Canon TG-6 camera. The arena was divided into a grid of 300 cells (2 × 2 cm), applied for the extraction of data (Fig. 2B). The exploration was measured by computing the number of different cells crossed by individuals at least once during the 10 min of recording.

Boldness (i.e., the propensity to risk-taking behaviors) was assessed as the latency to re-emerge following a threatening aerial stimulus. Mosquito larvae resting at the water surface respond to aerial threats such as a shadow by diving into deeper water, and then swim back to the surface to breath40,48,82. Larvae were placed individually in new plastic trays (5 × 5 × 10 cm). After allowing the larva to acclimate for 10 min, we applied a shadow stimulus using a custom-made robotic arm to automatically deliver and standardize the stimulus («Arduino Uno»; http://www.arduino.cc). This system eliminates any potential confounding effects associated with manual stimulus delivery. The stimulus consisted of a black cardboard (5 × 5) horizontally presented around 10 cm above their housing trays moved by a system composed of a servomotor associated with a control board. The movement of the paper caused a passing shadow on the water, which induced the larva to dive (Fig. 2C). We recorded larval diving behavior at 30 frames/s with an Olympus TG-6 camera in front of the experimental tray. Diving tests were carried out in a lightbox with a single LED light (intensity of 1400 lx) situated above the experimental tray. Recording started when the mechanical stimulus was delivered and ended when the larvae returned to the surface. The latency to re-emerge following the stimulus was measured for each larva. All larvae were tested individually twice on two consecutive days between 8 AM and 1 PM. A single operator (G.C.) manually scored videos using the software BORIS (Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software), to extract the percentage of time larvae spend actively moving (activity), the number of different zones crossed by individuals (exploration), and the latency to re-emerge following the stimulus (boldness).

After the experiments, 7 larvae were removed for transcriptomic analyses, while all remaining larvae were kept in the housing containers to develop to the adult stage; once adults, they were sexed based on morphology (16 males and 18 females). The number of animals and repeated measures per individual provides sufficient power to estimate moderate levels of repeatability83. We used Generalized linear mixed effect (GLMM)- based repeatability models (rpt in rptR package)84,85 to test the repeatability of each behavioural trait. In each model, we entered behavioral traits singly as dependent variables and trials (two for each individual) and sex as fixed effects. Despite no morphological sex-related differences can be determined ad the larval stage, the sex of individual was included in the analysis to check for a potential effect37. We applied ANOVA using Wald chi-square tests to determine the main effect of fixed factors. We tested whether random intercepts explained a significant portion of the variation observed - whether individual variation in activity, exploration and risk-taking was repeatable - including ID (individual identities) as a random factor83. We assumed Gaussian error distribution for activity and diving behaviors. We log-transformed diving behavior after visual inspection of model residuals. We applied Poisson error distribution with log link function to exploration (count data, number of cells crossed by the larva). A parametric bootstrapping method (number of iterations = 1000) was used to calculate the confidence interval (CI) and the likelihood ratio test to estimate the p-value of the repeatability distribution. Finally, we tested correlation among behavioral traits using Spearman’s rank correlation method implemented in rcorr function of the Hmisc Rpackage, using values from the first trial because of the individual repeatability. Data were analyzed in R version 4.1.2.
