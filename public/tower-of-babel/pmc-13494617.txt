Mature oocyte retrieval without an exogenous trigger during random-start stimulation in a very early intrauterine pregnancy: a case report
Authors: Mohammad Saaed ElFarran, Karolina Palinska-Rudzka, Raj Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe a rare case of mature oocyte retrieval without an exogenous trigger during random-start controlled ovarian stimulation (RS-COS) coinciding with a very early intrauterine pregnancy.DesignCase report.SubjectA 32-year-old para 1 patient with inflammatory breast cancer (grade 3, T4N2M0, ER/PR negative, HER2 3+, Ki-67 60%).ExposureLuteal-phase RS-COS using a gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist protocol with human menopausal gonadotropin (hMG) 300 IU daily. Egg retrieval was performed on day 12 after stimulation.Main Outcome MeasuresNumber and maturity of oocytes retrieved; occurrence of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS).ResultsOn day 10 of stimulation, 25 follicles measuring ≥12 mm were seen, of which 18 were in the right ovary. A gestational sac was seen in the uterine cavity. The serum human chorionic gonadotropin level was 2,563 IU/L. Oocyte retrieval from the right ovary was performed without administering a trigger. Nine metaphase II oocytes were retrieved and vitrified; mild clinical OHSS occurred.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that it may be possible to perform COS in early pregnancy and be able to retrieve mature oocytes without the use of an exogenous trigger. To our knowledge, there are no previous published reports of multiple metaphase II oocyte retrieval without an exogenous trigger during RS-COS.

Introduction

This is a case report on the potential effect of exposure to pregnancy-induced human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in the context of successful ovarian stimulation for fertility preservation.

Case report

A 32-year-old para 1 patient presented with a right-sided breast mass and was diagnosed with inflammatory breast cancer (grade 3 invasive ductal carcinoma; T4N2M0; ER/PR-negative; HER2 3+; Ki-67 60%). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin, trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and docetaxel was planned. Fertility preservation (FP) was discussed urgently at a tertiary center. The patient provided written informed consent for FP and for publication of anonymized clinical details. To minimize Zika risk associated with recent partner travel to an endemic area, the couple opted for oocyte rather than embryo cryopreservation. The patient did not disclose a possibility of conception at this stage. Baseline body mass index was 34.5 kg/m2 and antimüllerian hormone (AMH) 3.48 ng/mL. A serum pregnancy test performed 3 days before stimulation was <1 IU/L.

Luteal-phase RS-COS was commenced with human menopausal gonadotropin 300 IU daily. Serial ultrasounds tracked follicular growth; on stimulation day 10, ultrasound identified an early intrauterine gestational sac and subsequent serum β-hCG measured 2,563 IU/L. On stimulation day 12, follicle-tracking ultrasound identified an early intrauterine gestational sac; serum β-hCG was 2,563 IU/L. After multidisciplinary discussion (reproductive medicine, oncology, and early-pregnancy services), options of cycle cancellation vs. proceeding to retrieval without a trigger were reviewed and patient was keen on proceeding. In addition, the patient was complaining of abdominal distention and bloating, so ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval (USOR) was further considered for symptomatic relief. On abdominal ultrasound on the day of USOR, the right ovary measured approximately 12 × 12 cm with an estimated 35 recruited follicles overall (20 right and 15 left); 25 follicles were ≥12 mm (right ovary: 18; ≥12 mm). Nine follicles measured 12–16 mm (7 right, 2 left). Sixteen follicles measured 17–22 mm (11 right and 5 left). Free fluid was present in the pouch of Douglas not observed on the scan at the start of random-start controlled ovarian stimulation (RS-COS) with no new corpora lutea seen, suggesting hyperstimulation rather than ovulation. Serum β-hCG level measured 5,973 IU/L. The left ovary on scan done on the day of USOR was not accessible vaginally.

We decided to proceed to oocyte retrieval to obtain oocytes for FP. In view of the high follicle number and the presence of an ongoing early pregnancy, no exogenous trigger (neither hCG nor gonadotropin-releasing hormone [GnRH]-agonist) was administered. The oocyte retrieval was limited to the right ovary to reduce excessive manipulation of the anatomically difficult-to-access left ovary, to provide symptomatic relief given the markedly enlarged ovaries, and, in part, to preserve corpus luteum function while pregnancy options were considered. Ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval resulted in the collection of 9 metaphase II (MII) oocytes. Interestingly, no immature oocytes were retrieved. The patient received prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin in the form of 5,000 units of dalteparin subcutaneously every 24 hours for 4 weeks and a 7-day course of 0.5 mg oral cabergoline daily starting the day of USOR, alongside standard ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) precautions and outpatient follow-up. On day 4 after retrieval, blood tests showed normal hematocrit (42%) with normal electrolytes, liver and renal function, and coagulation profile (Table 1). Based on ultrasound findings, bloods tests, and symptoms, mild OHSS developed as per the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists guidelines and no worsening of OHSS blood tests or symptoms were reported. Discussion

Fertility preservation is a key component of comprehensive care in reproductive-age women diagnosed with malignancy. The RS-COS enables timely FP without delaying cancer treatment and occasionally achieves a number of MII oocytes comparable with conventional start (1). However, coincident early pregnancy can modify pituitary-ovarian dynamics e.g., progesterone rise, luteinizing hormone (LH) suppression, complicate triggering, and has been associated with failed retrieval and OHSS in case reports (2).

Pregnancy changes the endocrine profile in many ways relevant to stimulation. Rising hCG maintains luteinization and progesterone production in the first trimester of pregnancy. The COS cycle coinciding with early pregnancy might lead to premature luteinization across the cohort of stimulated follicles, with early progesterone production and potential effects on oocyte competence, resulting in variable MII oocyte numbers in RS-COS (3).

Reassuringly, evidence refers to the comparable livebirth rates of progestin-primed COS compared with COS while using GnRH antagonist to prevent ovulation (4). Furthermore, COS performed after early abortion with still-positive but falling β-hCG has yielded acceptable MII oocyte numbers (5, 6). This could be attributed to the luteotropic activity of hCG, which, in low doses, is thought to aid in the maturation of oocytes (6).

The addition of low-dose hCG to COS regimens has shown a good safety profile (7). This is evidenced by data showing comparable livebirth rates and good-quality embryo numbers when replacing follicle-stimulating hormone with low-dose hCG to complete COS cycles (7). Moreover, evidence shows that hCG produced in early pregnancy may functionally substitute for LH at the shared luteinizing hormone/choriogonadotropin receptor (8). Compared with pituitary LH (short half-life), circulating hCG provides sustained receptor occupancy and downstream cyclic adenosine monophosphate signaling, plausibly sufficient, when rising, to accomplish final oocyte maturation even without an exogenous trigger. Although evidence is needed to support the former statement, in our setting, steadily increasing endogenous β-hCG offered a biologically credible explanation for retrieval of multiple MII oocytes despite withholding a trigger.

This case illustrates 2 dilemmas in clinical practice. The first relates to whether oocyte retrieval should be performed in a situation where the follicles have been exposed to high levels of hCG for a few days. We concluded that there was no evidence of ovulation, because the same number of intact follicles were observed on ultrasound throughout stimulation and no corpora lutea were seen on ultrasound. Hence, we expected to obtain oocytes from the retrieval. Had we observed evidence of ovulation then this would have argued against a decision to perform the procedure.

The second clinical question relates to the use or nonuse of a trigger for final follicular maturation before egg retrieval. In this context, the patient displayed serum hCG levels in excess of those typically seen after an injection of hCG used as trigger (9). Hence, we judged that exogenous hCG was unlikely to add a further luteotropic drive. A GnRH-agonist trigger was not used because an adequate pituitary LH surge was considered less likely because of early-pregnancy progesterone/estradiol feedback, and any additional pharmacologic perturbation of early gestation was avoided while pregnancy continuation was under consideration.

Throughout the treatment, we had obtained consent from the couple based on extensive discussions on the outcomes and risks. We ensured that the couple were aware of the scarce literature on ovarian response with ongoing pregnancy. The patient was aware of the risks and management of OHSS according to the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists guidelines, poor ovarian response, and delays in receiving chemotherapy after her FP cycle (10).

What distinguishes this case is the retrieval of multiple MII oocytes and no immature oocytes without any exogenous trigger in the setting of a very early intrauterine pregnancy with rising endogenous β-hCG, and subsequent lack of OHSS symptoms despite anticipated high risk. Although hCG is known to activate LH receptors to induce final oocyte maturation, prior published reports of RS-COS in coincident pregnancy have occasionally shown immature cohorts after GnRH-agonist trigger or cycles undertaken after termination with low residual hCG (Table 2). Although a negative serum hCG is now incorporated into safe start of RS-COS protocols in oncology patients, retesting should be undertaken without delay when clinically indicated (e.g., unexpected progesterone elevation, atypical bleeding, or uncertainty about cycle phase), recognizing that very early pregnancies may be below assay detection at the start of COS.

In cases of COS coinciding with pregnancy, counseling should include the potential impact of endogenous hCG on maturation and the heightened risk of severe OHSS. After USOR, high-risk OHSS measures and monitoring should be applied. Luteal support may be considered on an individual basis while multidisciplinary teams appraise oncological timelines and pregnancy decisions.

Lastly, we make a plea for prospective data from centers reporting on the management of incidental pregnancy detected during COS. This may help define the range of endogenous hCG compatible with successful retrieval of mature oocytes and report the future quality of embryos, clinical pregnancy rates, and live birth rates using oocytes retrieved from those COS cycles.

In a very early intrauterine pregnancy that was below the detection threshold at the start of RS-COS, endogenous hCG may have provided sufficient luteinizing hormone/choriogonadotropin receptor stimulation to permit retrieval of multiple MII oocytes without the administration of an exogenous ovulatory trigger; this observation does not imply elimination of OHSS risk.

Management should be individualized and multidisciplinary. Treatment should prioritize patient safety, avoid exacerbating OHSS, and incorporate oncological considerations with clear patient counseling.
