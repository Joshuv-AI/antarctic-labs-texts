Autonomous demon exploiting heat and information at the trajectory level
Juliette Monsel, Matteo Acciai, Rafael Sánchez, Janine Splettstoesser

Abstract
We propose an electronic bipartite system consisting of a working substance, in which a refrigeration process is implemented, and of a nonthermal resource region, containing a combination of different thermal baths. In the working substance, heat is extracted from the coldest of two electronic reservoirs (refrigeration) via heat- and particle transport through a quantum dot. This quantum dot of the working substance is capacitively coupled to the resource region. In such a setup, a finite cooling power can be obtained in the working substance, while the energy exchange with the resource region exactly cancels out on average. At the same time, information is always exchanged, even on average, due to the capacitive coupling between the two parts of the bipartite system. The proposed system therefore implements an autonomous demon with fully vanishing heat extraction from the resource. Unlike macroscopic machines, nanoscale machines exhibit large fluctuations in performance, so precision becomes an important performance quantifier. We give a comprehensive description of the thermodynamic performance of the proposed autonomous demon in terms of stochastic trajectories and of full counting statistics and demonstrate that the precision of the cooling power strongly depends on the operation principle of the device. More specifically, the interplay of information flow and counter-balancing heat flows dramatically impacts the trade-off between cooling power, efficiency, and precision. We expect this insight to be of relevance for guiding the design of energy-conversion processes exploiting nonthermal resources.

II.1
Triple-dot setup

For each dot, we consider a single level with energy
\epsilon_{i}
, where

The Hamiltonian describing the three quantum dots is therefore given by

\hat{H}=\sum_{i=\text{H},\text{W},\text{C}}\epsilon_{i}\hat{n}_{i}+U_{\text{H}}\hat{n}_{\text{H}}\hat{n}_{\text{W}}+U_{\text{C}}\hat{n}_{\text{C}}\hat{n}_{\text{W}}+U\hat{n}_{\text{H}}\hat{n}_{\text{C}},

(1)

where we have denoted the electron number operator in dot

(a)
, is very large with respect to temperatures,

]
and was shown to merely have a quantitative effect, while not hindering the operation of the demon.

Each resource dot is furthermore tunnel-coupled to a single terminal, that is a large fermionic reservoir characterized by its temperature

and chemical potential

, with

. We choose

The working-substance dot W, in the lower part of the setup, is tunnel-coupled to two terminals, called the left (L) and right (R) reservoirs, with respective temperatures
T_{\text{L}}
,

and chemical potentials
\mu_{\text{L}}
,

The condition of zero energy exchange between resource and working substance is achieved by fixing one of the model parameters, see Sec.

For simplicity, we assume that the tunnel rates

and

of the upper terminals are energy independent.
However, it is crucial in this work to have energy-dependent tunnel rates for the left and right reservoirs. Given that the energy at which the electrons tunnel in and out of the dot W is fully determined by the occupations
h
and

of the upper dots, we denote the lower tunnel rates
\Gamma_{\text{L}/\text{R}}^{hc}

Since the only possibly relevant potential

II.2.3
Information flow

We now split the system into a lower part (W), the working substance, and an upper part (D), the demon, see Fig.
1
(a)
, where the working substance is shown with a light-green background. While we are interested in a situation where there is no heat or energy exchange between these two subsystems on average, the two systems are however never independent of each other due to the capacitive coupling between the dots. This dependence of the two components of this bipartite system can be cast in terms of
mutual information

The mutual information between W and D reads

I_{\text{W}:\text{D}}=\sum_{h,w,c}\bar{p}_{hwc}\log\left(\frac{\bar{p}_{hwc}}{\bar{p}^{\text{W}}_{w}\bar{p}^{\text{D}}_{hc}}\right),

(10)

with
\bar{p}^{\text{W}}_{w}=\sum_{h,c}\bar{p}_{hwc}
and
\bar{p}^{\text{D}}_{hc}=\sum_{w}\bar{p}_{hwc}
. We emphasize that as soon as there is interdot Coulomb interaction and W and D are out of equilibrium with respect to each other, there is mutual information between the working substance and the resource---even in cases where there is clearly no feedback involved in the refrigeration or work extraction process

3

3

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{W}}

\eta_{\text{info}}=\frac{\beta_{\text{L}}J_{\text{L}}^{Q}+\beta_{\text{R}}J_{\text{R}}^{Q}}{J^{I}}.

(13)

Due to the local second laws of Eq. (

), one finds that in general

1\geq\eta_{\text{info}}\geq\eta_{\text{global}},

(14)

since the information efficiency does not account for effects of the local entropy production as it could in general arise from mechanisms like local Joule heating or Fourier heat conduction from the hot to the cold part of the resource.

II.2.4
Cooling-power fluctuations from full counting statistics

A main quantity of interest in this paper, in addition to the average currents in the device, is the precision of the produced cooling power, or, in other words, the absence of cooling power fluctuations. We compute these fluctuations using full counting statistics (FCS).

We here consider the case
\mu_{\text{R}}=0
, such that the cooling power
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}=J^{Q}_{\text{R}}
is equal to the energy current out of the right reservoir,
J_{\text{R}}^{E}
. The (zero-frequency) noise in the cooling power is therefore given by
\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\!\langle E_{\text{R}}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}
where
\langle\!\langle E_{\text{R}}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle
is the variance (second cumulant) of the energy
E_{\text{R}}
transferred from the right reservoir into dot W. Similarly, the energy current defined in Eq. (
5
) corresponds to the derivative of the first cumulant,
J^{E}_{\text{R}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\!\langle E_{\text{R}}\rangle\!\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}
.

In order to calculate this variance, we define a counting field
\xi
for the energy flowing out of the right reservoir. Then, the master equation (
2
) including the counting fields becomes
[
75
,
119
]

\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}(t,\xi)=W(\xi)\mathbf{P}(t,\xi),

(15)

with
W(\xi)=W_{\text{C}}+W_{\text{H}}+W_{\text{L}}+W_{\text{R}}(\xi)
and
[W_{\text{R}}(\xi)]_{hwc}^{h^{\prime}w^{\prime}c^{\prime}}=[W_{\text{R}}]_{hwc}^{h^{\prime}w^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\text{e}^{i\Delta E_{hwc}^{h^{\prime}w^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\xi}
(see Eq. (
3
)).
From this, one finds the moment-generating function as
\mathcal{G}(t,\xi)=\sum_{hwc}[\mathbf{P}(t,\xi)]_{hwc}
.
As the next step, the
k
-th cumulant of the energy is obtained by differentiating the cumulant-generating function
\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=\log(\mathcal{G}(t,\xi))
,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle E_{\text{R}}^{k}\rangle\!\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=(-i\partial_{\xi})^{k}\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)|_{\xi=0}.

(16)

In the long-time limit (of interest for our steady-state analysis), the cumulant-generating function can be approximated by
\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)\simeq\lambda(\xi)t

[
119
]
, where
\lambda(\xi)
is the eigenvalue of
W(\xi)
such that
\lambda(\xi)\rightarrow 0
for
\xi\to 0
while all the other eigenvalues have negative real parts in this limit, due to the uniqueness of the steady state in the case of interest here.
Then, the noise in the cooling power is given by

\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}=-\partial^{2}_{\xi}{\lambda(\xi)}|_{\xi=0}\ .

(17)

More precisely, we do the following to obtain the cumulants analytically. Starting from the moment-generating function in the long-time limit
[
120
,
75
]
, we take the Laplace transform,

\mathcal{G}(z,\xi)=\sum_{hwc}\left[\frac{1}{z\mathds{1}-W(\xi)}\bar{\mathbf{P}}\right]_{hwc},

(18)

where
\bar{\mathbf{P}}
is the steady state of the master equation (
2
) (i.e. for
\xi=0
). Then, the eigenvalue
\lambda(\xi)
is the pole
z_{0}
of
\mathcal{G}(z,\xi)
close to
\xi=0
.
Using the Taylor expansion of
z_{0}
around
\xi=0
,

z_{0}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}\frac{(i\xi)^{n}}{n!},

(19)

in the denominator of
\mathcal{G}(z,\xi)
[Eq. (
18
)], we determine the coefficients
c_{n}
by proceeding order by order. In this way, one obtains
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}=c_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}=c_{2}
.

In order to analyze the performance accounting for the trade-off between cooling power, efficiency, and precision, we define the performance quantifier

X_{\mathrm{TUR,global/info}}=2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{global/info}}}{1-\eta_{\mathrm{global/info}}}\frac{T_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}}\eta_{\text{Carnot}}

(20)

inspired by the thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TUR)
[
121
,
122
,
123
]
see explanations in Appendix
B
. We have denoted
\eta_{\text{Carnot}}=T_{\mathrm{R}}/(T_{\mathrm{L}}-T_{\mathrm{R}})
the Carnot efficiency of a standard refrigerator operating between temperatures
T_{\mathrm{R}}
and
T_{\mathrm{L}}
.
In particular, under demon condition,
J_{\mathrm{in}}^{Q}=0
, and at zero potential bias,
\mu_{\text{R}}=0
,
X_{\mathrm{TUR,global}}
corresponds exactly to the TUR for the three-dot system
[
123
]
. Therefore
X_{\mathrm{TUR,global}}
is bounded by 1, like the standard TUR for refrigerators, however, we cannot make a similar statement about
X_{\mathrm{TUR,info}}
, see details in Appendix
B
.
Instead, since
\dot{\Sigma}=\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{W}}+\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{D}}
, from the local second laws (
12
), and using the expressions of the efficiencies, given in Eqs. (
8
) and (
13
), we find that in general

X_{\mathrm{TUR,info}}\geq X_{\mathrm{TUR,global}}.

(21)

In Sec.
III.3
, we will use both quantifiers to analyze the performance of the triple-dot system operating as a “demonic” refrigerator.

II.3
Trajectory analysis of the dynamics

To get further insight into how the device operates, it is interesting to look at the trajectories corresponding to the Markovian stochastic process described by the master equation (
2
). Indeed, we can define the thermodynamic quantities we are interested in at the trajectory level. Furthermore, we can use our insight on the stochastic dynamics to tune the parameters of the system to favor trajectories with useful outcomes —here specifically transporting heat out of the right reservoir.

II.3.1
Trajectories for the entire triple-dot system

We consider trajectories in the triple-dot state space
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},...,\gamma_{M})

\gamma_{m}=h_{m}w_{m}c_{m}

\tilde{\gamma}

\tau_{\text{th}}

The probability of trajectory

the probability of the transition
\gamma_{m-1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{m}}\gamma_{m}

\alpha_{m}=\text{C},\text{H},\text{L},\text{R}
indicates which reservoir is responsible for the transition
4
4

4

Strictly, we only need the
\alpha_{m}
notation for dot W since it is the only dot connected to two different reservoirs, which can induce transitions.

\alpha_{m}=0

The transition probability is given by

P_{\alpha_{m}}[\gamma_{m}|\gamma_{m-1}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[W_{\alpha_{m}}]^{\gamma_{m-1}}_{\gamma_{m}}\delta t&\text{if $\gamma_{m}\neq\gamma_{m-1}$}\\

\end{array}\right..

(23)

With this definition, if the transition

P_{\alpha_{m}}[\gamma_{m}|\gamma_{m-1}]=0
. Furthermore, we have
P_{\alpha_{m}}[\gamma_{m}|\gamma_{m}]\leq 1
and,
\forall\gamma_{m-1}

\sum_{\gamma_{m}}\sum_{\alpha_{m}}P_{\alpha_{m}}[\gamma_{m}|\gamma_{m-1}]=1

At the single-trajectory level, the entropy production is given by

(24)

where
P(\tilde{\gamma})
is the probability that the triple-dot system follows the time-reversed trajectory
5
5

5

Note that we do not need to modify the probability function to the probability of the time-reversed system because we are looking at steady state and constant external parameters.
,
\tilde{\gamma}
.
The entropy production can be split into

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\bar{p}(\gamma_{0})}{\bar{p}(\gamma_{M})}\right),

(25)

are respectively the stochastic entropy variation of the dots and of reservoir
\alpha

II.3.2
Trajectories for the bipartite system: demon and working substance

In the same spirit as in Sec.

III
Triple-dot refrigerator

We are interested in the refrigerator operating mode of the device shown in Fig.
1

T_{\text{R}}<T_{\text{L}}
and define
\bar{T}=(T_{\text{L}}+T_{\text{R}})/2

\delta T=T_{\text{L}}-T_{\text{R}}>0

\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}\equiv J^{Q}_{\text{R}}

III.1
Operation cycles acting in parallel

From now on, we focus on the special case depicted in Fig.
1
(a)
, in which trajectories in state space can occur following the scheme of Fig.
1
(b)
.
Concretely, this means that we only allow for a certain set of tunneling transitions, which is selected by a fully asymmetric energy dependence of tunnel rates. We assume that tunneling from and to dot W can occur from the right reservoir only when one of the dots H and C in the resource region is occupied, namely
\Gamma_{\text{R}}^{00}=0
and
\Gamma_{\text{R}}^{10},\Gamma_{\text{R}}^{01}\neq 0
. At the same time, we assume that tunneling from and to dot W due to the left reservoir can occur only when dots H and C are empty, namely
\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{10}=\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{01}=0
and
\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{00}\neq 0
. The non-vanishing transition rates are found from Fermi’s golden rule and given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{w\pm}_{\text{C}/\text{H}}&=\Gamma_{\text{C}/\text{H}}f_{\text{C}/\text{H}}\bm{(}\pm(\epsilon_{\text{C}/\text{H}}+wU_{\text{C}/\text{H}})\bm{)},\\
\Gamma^{hc\pm}_{\text{L}/\text{R}}&=\Gamma_{\text{L}/\text{R}}^{hc}f_{\text{L}/\text{R}}\bm{(}\pm(\epsilon_{\text{W}}+hU_{\text{H}}+cU_{\text{C}})\bm{)},\end{aligned}

(29)

see also Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
).
Here, we have furthermore used that the Coulomb interaction between dot H and C is infinitely large, meaning that simultaneous occupation of the two resource dots is excluded. This assumption implies that heat exchange between reservoirs H and C can occur only via dot W and not directly within the resource region, i.e., Fourier heat flow is weak within the resource region.

This fact together with the tunnel-rate asymmetry described in this section, implies that the operation of the triple-dot device is largely based on heat- and information exchange with the two dots H and C—and hence with the two reservoirs H and C—separately. Therefore, the device exhibits two operation cycles involving a single resource reservoir acting
in parallel
to achieve cooling in the absence of average heat flow between the working substance and the resource region, as will be explained in the next section, Sec.
III.2
.

This situation is in contrast to Ref.
[
42
]
, where the nonequilibrium character of the combined resource region was explicitly apparent.
Here, we instead make a parameter choice which rather allows us to focus on the interplay of heat-driven
[
61

62

and information-driven
[
21
,
23
]

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{M}}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\langle\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C})M_{\mathcal{C}}(\gamma)+\nu_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})M_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}(\gamma)\rangle_{\gamma}.

(33)

We have denoted
M_{\mathcal{C}}(\gamma)
the number of cycles
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
in trajectory
\gamma
which are equivalent to basic cycle
\mathcal{C}
after removing the self-retracing parts. The cycle probability
\pi(\mathcal{C})
tells us how often a cycle
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
equivalent to basic cycle
\mathcal{C}
occurs, therefore we can write
\langle M_{\mathcal{C}}(\gamma)\rangle_{\gamma}=\pi(\mathcal{C})\langle M_{\text{cycles}}(\gamma)\rangle_{\gamma}
, where
M_{\text{cycles}}(\gamma)
is the total number of cycles from
000
back to
000
of any kind in trajectory
\gamma
, including purely self-retracing ones. From Eq. (
33
), we thus get

\displaystyle J^{\nu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C})\pi(\mathcal{C})+\nu_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})\pi(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})\right]\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{\langle M_{\text{cycles}}(\gamma)\rangle_{\gamma}}{t_{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C})\pi(\mathcal{C})+\nu_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})\pi(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})\right],

(34)

where the time
\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}
is the average duration of any cycle from
000
to
000
, namely the average time it takes the system to come back to state
000
.
Identifying the terms in Eq. (
34
) with Eq. (
30
), we finally obtain

r_{\mathcal{C}}=\frac{\pi(\mathcal{C})}{\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}}.

(35)

The average cycle time
\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}
is given by the recurrence time from state
000
. This recurrence time, in turn, is equal to the average holding time in
000
, namely
1/(\Gamma_{\text{C}}^{0+}+\Gamma_{\text{H}}^{0+}+\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{00+})
, divided by the steady-state occupation of state
000

[
129
]
. We therefore obtain the explicit expression for the average cycle time
\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}
in terms of tunnel rates and of the steady-state master equation solution as

\bar{t}_{\text{cycle}}=\frac{1}{\bar{p}_{000}(\Gamma_{\text{C}}^{0+}+\Gamma_{\text{H}}^{0+}+\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{00+})}.

(36)

We find that the analytical expression of
\pi(\mathcal{C})
obtained from Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
) is in excellent agreement with our results from numerical simulations, as we discuss in Secs.
III.3
and
III.4
.

We now use the relation between rates
r_{\mathcal{C}}
and probabilities
\pi(\mathcal{C})
given in Eq. (
35
) to simplify the general current expression and to thereby highlight the role of time-reversed cycles. Using furthermore the fact that
\nu_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})=-\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C})
and the fluctuation relation (
24
), we find

J^{\nu}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}},\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}},\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}\text{C}}}\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C})r_{\mathcal{C}}\left[1-\text{e}^{-\Sigma(\mathcal{C})}\right].

(37)

III.2.3
Average currents for cycles acting in parallel

With these compact expressions for all currents of interest, we are now able to give a more precise meaning to the concept of “cycles acting in parallel” outlined Sec.
III.1
by showing that all currents can be split into contributions where the working substance interacts separately with the cold and hot resource reservoirs. To that aim, we introduce the fundamental set of cycles
\{\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}},\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}\}

[
128
]
(see Appendix
A.2
for details). Any basic cycle
\mathcal{C}
, as defined in Sec.
III.2.1
, can be constructed from this fundamental set as

\mathcal{C}=\sigma_{\text{C}}\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}\oplus\sigma_{\text{H}}\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}},

(38)

with
\sigma_{\alpha}=-1,0,1
and
\alpha=\text{C},\text{H}
. The symbol
\oplus
means the union of the two cycles where any transition they have in common has been removed
[
128
]
, for instance,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{C}}=-\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}
or
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}\text{C}}=-\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
.
This decomposition allows us to express the generic thermodynamic quantity
X(\mathcal{C})
in terms of contributions of the cold and hot cycles,

X(\mathcal{C})=\sigma_{\text{C}}X(\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}})+\sigma_{\text{H}}X(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}),

(39)

with, in particular,
X(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}\text{C}})=X(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}})-X(\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}})
and
X(\tilde{\mathcal{C}})=-X(\mathcal{C})
. Using the decomposition (
38
) for each basic cycle in Eq. (
30
), we can write

\displaystyle J^{\nu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}})F^{\text{C}}+\nu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}})F^{\text{H}},

(40)

where we have defined the fluxes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F^{\text{C}}=\,&r_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}}+r_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}\text{C}}}-r_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{C}}}-r_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}\text{C}}}=-\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{C}}}{U_{\text{C}}},\\
F^{\text{H}}=\,&r_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}}+r_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}\text{C}}}-r_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}}}-r_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}\text{C}}}=-\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{H}}}{U_{\text{H}}}.\end{aligned}

(41)

This means that one can rewrite any average current in terms of the heat currents out of the hot and cold resource reservoirs. Equation (
40
) is particularly insightful for
J^{Q}_{\text{R}}
since it allows splitting the cooling power into
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\text{C}}+\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\text{H}}
, with

\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\alpha}=Q_{\text{R}}(\mathcal{C}_{\alpha})F^{\alpha},

(42)

such that we can see whether reservoir
\alpha=\text{C},\text{H}
from the demon part contributes to the cooling,
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\alpha}>0
, or is detrimental to it,
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\alpha}<0
.
For the particle current, equation (
40
) becomes

J^{N}=\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{C}}}{U_{\text{C}}}+\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{H}}}{U_{\text{H}}}.

(43)

This coincides with the result from Ref.
[
42
, Eq. (13)]
, which was obtained, unlike here, in the absence of temperature and voltage biases between the left and right reservoirs but for possibly nonzero tunnel rates
\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{10}
,
\Gamma_{\text{L}}^{01}
and
\Gamma_{\text{R}}^{00}
.

We can also write the heat current out of the right reservoir, namely the cooling power, as

J^{Q}_{\text{R}}=-(\epsilon_{\text{W}}+\bar{U})J^{N}-\delta U\left(\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{H}}}{U_{\text{H}}}-\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{C}}}{U_{\text{C}}}\right),

(44)

with
\bar{U}=(U_{\text{H}}+U_{\text{C}})/2
and
\delta U=(U_{\text{H}}-U_{\text{C}})/2
. Interestingly, the heat current out of the left reservoir

J^{Q}_{\text{L}}=\epsilon_{\text{W}}J^{N}

(45)

is always proportional to the particle current, also referred to as “tightly coupled”
[
130
,
131
,
132
]
, due to the choice of
\Gamma^{10\pm}_{\text{L}}=\Gamma^{01\pm}_{\text{L}}=0
(electrons exchanged between contact L and dot W all have the same energy,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{W}}
). This is in contrast to the cooling power, which—in addition to a tightly coupled component—also has a term proportional to
\delta U
that is independent of the particle current.

Since we are specifically interested in the performances of the device operating under the demon condition,
J^{Q}_{\text{in}}=0
, it is interesting to express this condition as a condition for the interaction energies in terms of the fluxes of the fundamental cycles. We find

\frac{U_{\text{H}}}{U_{\text{C}}}=-\frac{F^{\text{C}}}{F^{\text{H}}}.

(46)

While this is an insightful relation, showing how the parameter choice needs to connect to the occurrence rates, see Eq. (
41
), in the following, we fix the demon condition by a choice of single-level energies (which are here implicit in the fluxes on the right-hand side of the equation).

From Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
), we get further insights into the interaction energies required for a refrigerator working under demon conditions. We note that imposing the demon condition
J_{\mathrm{in}}^{Q}=J^{Q}_{\text{R}}+J^{Q}_{\text{L}}=0
for
\delta U=0
,
\bar{U}\neq 0
necessarily results in a vanishing average particle current in the working substance,
J^{N}=0
. This means that the cooling power vanishes in that case, as can also be seen in Fig.
3
at
U_{\text{H}}/U_{\text{C}}=1
.

III.3
Steady-state performance and precision

We start by presenting the performance of the device working as a refrigerator while exploiting a resource in the absence of heat extraction from the resource, based on the steady-state transport properties presented in Sec.
II.2

J^{Q}_{\text{in}}=0

This can be achieved in different ways using some demon parameters as knobs, while the others are fixed. We have here decided to adjust the single-level energy of dot H,
\epsilon_{\text{H}}

Typically, one of the most relevant performance quantifiers is the cooling power

III.4
Distinguishing operation based on basic cycles

The final important task is to identify in which way the working principles of the two optimal cases (I) and (II) differ, leading to very different performances with respect to the precision of the refrigeration process.

The addition energies of the three quantum dots in cases (I) and (II) result in the following situation, depicted in Figs.
4
(a)
and
4
(d)
.
In case (I) the single-level energy of dot W is positive; a positive cooling power
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}=J^{Q}_{\text{R}}=-J^{Q}_{\text{L}}
, see Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
), thus needs to go along with a negative particle current,
J^{N}<0
, cf. Fig.
4
(b)
, meaning that cooling is mediated by electron-like excitations.
By contrast, in case (II) the single-level energy of dot W is negative; a positive cooling power
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}>0
thus needs to go along with
J^{N}>0
, cf. Fig.
4
(e)
, meaning that cooling is mediated by hole-like excitations

7
7

7

When analyzing the two components of the heat current
J^{Q}_{\text{R}}
of Eq. (
44
) instead, we find that the tightly coupled and the non-tightly coupled parts have opposite signs in both cases (I) and (II). For (I) both contributions have the same order of magnitude, with a positive tight-coupling contribution. For (II), the tight-coupling contribution is negative but much smaller than the non-tight-coupling one (
-\epsilon_{\text{W}}\sim\bar{U}
).
.

More important for the operation principle of the device is the contribution of the hot resource reservoir to the cooling, quantified by
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\mathrm{H}}
, see Eq. (
42
) as well as Figs.
4
(b)
and
4
(e)
. Indeed, in case (I), reservoir H acts detrimentally to the cooling, with
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{\mathrm{H}}<0
. Its main role in the device operation is therefore to provide the heat flow
J^{Q}_{\text{H}}
to suppress the average heat flow
J^{Q}_{\text{in}}
from the demon to the working substance. Consequently, the cold resource reservoir is the only one to drive the cooling process, in the same manner as described in Ref.
[
21
]
for a double-dot setup with a single-reservoir resource. Conversely, in case (II), both resource reservoirs contribute positively to the cooling process,
collaborating
hence in parallel. While this consideration concerns an effect of the cooling power contribution from each resource contact (namely the decomposition in terms of cycles of the fundamental set, as given in Eq. (
40
)), also the contribution of the different basic cycles, as introduced in Sec.
III.2.1
, to the operation plays an important role as discussed in the following.

Indeed, an interesting aspect distinguishing cases (I) and (II) is the information exchange as compared to the heat exchange going along with the different cycles.
The basis to understand the importance of the acquired information is the positions of the addition energies of the resource dots, shown in Figs.
4
(a)
and
4
(d)
. In case (I), the Coulomb interaction of dot H is large. As a result of this and of the position of the single-level energies, both dots H and C hence “see” both the electron-like and the hole-like parts of the distributions of the reservoirs they are tunnel-coupled to.
The two addition energies are thus aligned with very different parts of the distributions, making both of them effectively cold (note that
U_{\text{H}}\approx 3T_{\text{H}}
).
This is in contrast to case (II), where the addition energies of dot H communicate only with (mostly empty) hole-like excitations of reservoir H and dot C only communicates with electron-like excitations of reservoir C. Whether dot W is occupied or not hence has a smaller influence on the tunnel rates of dot H.
This is visible particularly clearly in the information exchanged with the basic cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
:
I(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}})\ll I(\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}})
in case (II), while
I(\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}})\simeq I(\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}})
in case (I); see Fig.
4
and also Fig.
8
in Appendix
E
to compare the information (as well as other thermodynamic quantities) at the cycle level.
Furthermore, in case (I) both
\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
, which have the largest positive cooling power of the three basic cycles, go along with information received by the demon, see Fig.
4
(c)
. Interestingly in case (II), while cycles
\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}\text{C}}
exploit an information flow into the demon, this is not the case for cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
, whose contribution to the cooling power is negative, see Fig.
4
(f)
. The beneficial working principle of cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
is hence more similar to the one of an absorption refrigerator, using heat for a useful task, such as in the double-dot heat engines of Refs.
[
61
,
62
]
, see also a qubit implementation
[
133
]
.

A further important difference between the two cases, which has an influence on the fluctuations of the cooling power is the following.
When analyzing the probability with which basic cycles
\mathcal{C}
occur compared to their time-reversed
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, we note that in case (I), the more probable
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}\text{C}}
contribute with a negative cooling power. They are hence counterproductive to the result of cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}
, which is more probable than
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{C}}
and contributes with a large positive cooling power.
The situation is very different in case (II). Here the more probable of the pairs of basic cycles
\mathcal{C}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
(
\mathcal{C}_{\text{C}}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\text{H}}
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{H}\text{C}}
in this case), are all beneficial for the refrigeration process. It is clear that this reduces significantly the fluctuations in the cooling power.

IV
Conclusions

We have provided a detailed analysis of an information-based refrigerator exploiting capacitive coupling between three quantum dots, inspired by previous proposals and realizations of quantum-dot-based Maxwell demons
[
21
,
24
]
and heat engines
[
61
,
62
]
.

We showed that this triple-dot refrigerator can work as an ideal autonomous Maxwell demon generating finite cooling power in the working substance, while not extracting any heat on average from the resource region.
We showed furthermore that the precision of this refrigerator and the trade-off between precision, cooling power, and efficiency, strongly depends on the underlying parameter regimes, in particular on the ratio of interaction energies with different parts of the resource region.

Our detailed analysis based on steady-state master equation dynamics, full counting statistics, as well as trajectory-resolved dynamics allowed us to identify that refrigerator realizations mostly exploiting information suffer from reduced precision compared to those realizations which exploit both information and heat engine aspects.

We expect these insights to be important for the design of energy-converting devices, which exploit generic types of resources that are not characterized by one unique temperature.

]

Appendix B
Thermodynamic uncertainty relation

Since we are studying the steady state of a Markov jump process, the thermodynamic uncertainty relation holds
[
123
]
, namely

\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}\dot{\Sigma}\geq 2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{2}.

(53)

Based on this relation, we define the coefficient

x_{\text{TUR, global}}=\frac{2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{2}}{\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}\dot{\Sigma}},

(54)

which is therefore bounded by 1. Using the expression
\eta_{\text{global}}
, Eq. (
8
), we get

\frac{\eta_{\text{global}}}{1-\eta_{\text{global}}}=\frac{\beta_{\text{L}}J^{Q}_{\text{L}}+\beta_{\text{R}}J^{Q}_{\text{R}}}{\dot{\Sigma}},

(55)

such that

x_{\text{TUR, global}}=\frac{2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}}{\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}}\frac{\eta_{\text{global}}}{1-\eta_{\text{global}}}\frac{J^{Q}_{\text{R}}}{\beta_{\text{L}}J^{Q}_{\text{L}}+\beta_{\text{R}}J^{Q}_{\text{R}}},

(56)

since
\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}=J^{Q}_{\text{R}}
. We are interested here in the case where there is no potential bias,
\mu_{\text{R}}=0
, and the demon condition is fulfilled, therefore
J^{Q}_{\text{L}}=-J^{Q}_{\text{R}}
. With this, we find

x_{\text{TUR, global}}=\frac{2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}}{\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}}\frac{\eta_{\text{global}}}{1-\eta_{\text{global}}}\frac{T_{\mathrm{L}}T_{\mathrm{R}}}{T_{\mathrm{L}}-T_{\mathrm{R}}},

(57)

which is our motivation for the definition of the performance quantifier
X_{\text{TUR, global}}
given in Eq. (
20
).

By analogy, we define

x_{\text{TUR, info}}={2\mathcal{P}_{\text{cool}}^{2}}/{\mathcal{S}_{\text{cool}}\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{W}}}
,
which becomes
X_{\text{TUR, info}}
at
\mu_{\text{R}}=0
,
J^{Q}_{\text{in}}=0
. However, the reduced dynamics of dot W alone cannot be described by a Markov jump process due to the capacitive couplings to dots C and H. Therefore, the TUR does generally not hold and
x_{\text{TUR, info}}
is not bounded by 1. Nevertheless, we observe in our plots (Figs.
3
(d)
and
7
(e)
) that
X_{\text{TUR, info}}\leq 1
for our choice of parameters, such that there is no TUR violation for the reduced system.

Also note that at the demon condition and
\mu_{\text{R}}=0
, the global efficiency from Eq. (
8
) can be expressed as

\eta_{\text{global}}=\frac{\beta_{\text{R}}-\beta_{\text{L}}}{\beta_{\text{C}}-\beta_{\text{H}}}\frac{\epsilon_{\text{W}}(U_{\text{H}}-U_{\text{C}})}{U_{\text{C}}U_{\text{H}}}\leq 1,

(58)

showing that the efficiency increases with the temperature difference
\delta T
between the working substance reservoirs but decreases when increasing the temperature difference between the resource reservoirs. However, note that there is a maximum value of
\delta T
above which the demon condition can no longer be achieved.
