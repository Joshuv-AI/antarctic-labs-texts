Background

Medication errors represent a significant global healthcare challenge, with potentially severe consequences for patient safety. Traditional paper-based prescribing have long been associated with critical risks, including illegible prescriptions, incorrect dosages, and incomplete medication instructions [1, 2].

A 2018 systematic review of prescription errors in primary care settings revealed that over half of written prescriptions contain mistakes, frequently involving unclear drug names or dosage information that can lead to dispensing errors and patient harm [3]. Additionally, studies have highlighted the prevalence of such errors across different healthcare settings. A study in various hospitals across the United States reported that prescribing errors occur in 0.4% to 1.9% of all medication orders and cause harm in about 1% of all patients [4]. An Iranian study of transcription errors in a teaching hospital found that 21% of handwritten prescriptions contained at least one error, with approximately 4% of these errors having potential clinical significance [5].

Electronic prescribing (E-prescribing) have been introduced to address these paper-based challenges. As an information technology solution to reduce medication errors, E-prescribing allow physicians to directly input orders electronically instead of using paper and handwriting. By ensuring legible orders, E-prescribing improves prescription quality [6]. It also assists physicians with proper order entry by providing drug dosages and sequences, displaying laboratory data, and selecting/differentiating laboratory interventions [6, 7].

Studies have demonstrated potential benefits, including reducing medical errors by up to 90% and minimizing incomplete or illegible orders [8, 9]. However, the transition to electronic systems is not without challenges. While addressing traditional prescription errors, E-prescribing introduces complex technological and human-factor challenges. These may include potential system design limitations, user interaction complexities and technical constraints that could compromise patient safety [10–13].

To identify and prevent E-prescribing errors, investigating the underlying causes is important. Previous research has revealed multiple potential sources of errors. A study investigating factors in E-prescribing errors revealed issues such as inappropriate drug choices, default doses, alert fatigue, and remote ordering as causes [14]. Another study identified system design, usability, and performance issues as causes of error [15]. A comprehensive review revealed that the major factors were small screen size, drop-down selection errors, misinterpretation of defined system words, and poor work processes. Other issues included automobile drug suggestions, preset orders, order entry delays, and alert overload [16].

Despite increasing E-prescribing adoption across healthcare settings, comprehensive understanding of the underlying error mechanisms remains limited. Existing research predominantly focuses on quantitative error tracking, which provides insufficient insight into the nuanced factors contributing to these errors. This qualitative study addresses this critical gap by exploring E-prescribing errors through the perspectives of healthcare professionals in Iranian Social Security Organization outpatient centers. The findings will provide valuable insights for healthcare organizations seeking to optimize E-prescribing and minimize potential risks associated with electronic medication ordering.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative content analysis study was conducted across outpatient centers of the Social Security Organization in Yazd and Kashan, Iran. The E-prescribing has been implemented in all outpatient centers under the Social Security Organization in these cities since 2015. A total of 8 outpatient centers (5 in Yazd and 3 in Kashan) were included in this study. The context and prescribing workflow overview in this system, were presented in supplementary file 1.

Participants and sampling

We used purposive sampling to recruit physicians, pharmacists, and pharmacy technicians with at least 6 months of experience via the E-prescribing. The eligibility criteria included having a professional doctorate degree (for physicians/pharmacists) or a postgraduate degree (for pharmacy technicians).

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in person at participants’ outpatient centers by two members of the research team (ShA, RSh) between September 2022 and February 2022. The interviews lasted 15–30 min and continued until thematic saturation was reached. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim on the day they were conducted. The research team familiarized themselves with the data through repeated review of recordings and transcripts. The interview guide was presented in supplementary file 2.

Data analysis

The data analysis followed a systematic qualitative content analysis approach using the algorithm proposed by Graneheim and Lundman [17]. The analysis process involved the following comprehensive steps:

Initial data immersion

Two primary researchers (ShA and RSh) independently read all transcripts multiple times to develop an initial understanding of the interview content and identify preliminary potential codes and themes.

Coding process

Meaningful units related to E-prescribing challenges and errors were extracted. Initial codes representing participant experiences were developed, then condensed and abstracted into potential themes.

Expert collaboration

Two additional methodology experts with healthcare research backgrounds were consulted: a senior qualitative research methodologist from Kashan University of Medical Sciences and a healthcare informatics expert with extensive experience in qualitative research. These experts independently reviewed the coding process, provided external validation and insights to minimize potential research team bias, and participated in collaborative discussions to refine theme interpretation.

Thematic development

Codes were categorized based on similarities and differences. The codes were then reviewed within the context of the original transcripts. Abstract themes that comprehensively reflected participant experiences were developed, ensuring that the themes accurately represented the research participants’ perspectives.

Verification and validation

Coded transcripts were shared with participants to confirm accurate representation. Member checking was conducted to validate theme interpretations. An audit trail of analytical decisions was maintained.

Iteration and saturation

The analysis continued concurrently with data collection, allowing for iterative refinement of emerging themes until thematic saturation was achieved.

Rigor

Several steps were taken to strengthen credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability per published criteria. Coded transcripts were shared with participants to ensure accurate representation. The analysis involved collaboration with additional methodology experts. The standards of reporting for qualitative research were followed. This study was conducted and reported according to the 32-item COREQ (Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research) checklist.

Ethics

This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences Research Council (IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1400.037). Informed consent was obtained prior to the interviews. To ensure participant confidentiality and data security, all audio and textual data were stored on encrypted computers accessible only to the research team. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code, and audio recordings were deleted after transcription and participant verification. The data will be securely stored for 5 years post-study before being permanently deleted.

This qualitative study explored the complex landscape of E-prescribing errors by analyzing the multifaceted factors contributing to medication mistakes in healthcare settings. Before delving into the primary thematic findings, it is crucial to understand the contextual backdrop that motivated the transition to electronic prescribing systems.

Contextualizing the need for E-prescribing: challenges in paper-based prescribing

The transition to electronic prescribing was motivated by significant challenges inherent in paper-based prescribing. Participants identified several critical limitations that compromised patient safety and healthcare efficiency.

Participants encountered numerous issues with paper-based prescribing that ultimately motivated the adoption of E-prescribing. Physicians consistently lacked comprehensive access to patient medication histories, frequently resulting in inappropriate or contraindicated medication orders. Illegible handwriting emerged as a critical problem, leading to misinterpreted directions, incorrect dispensing, and compromised patient safety.

Additional paper prescribing difficulties encompassed maintaining updated records, patients’ challenges in recalling complex medication regimens, and inherently inefficient workflows. As one physician candidly described:In paper prescribing, we lacked access to patients’ comprehensive medical histories. The illegibility of handwriting created significant obstacles, particularly when prescribing medications for our predominantly chronic patients who either did not have their medications with them or could not recall their specific names.

In paper prescribing, we lacked access to patients’ comprehensive medical histories. The illegibility of handwriting created significant obstacles, particularly when prescribing medications for our predominantly chronic patients who either did not have their medications with them or could not recall their specific names.

Maintaining accurate and up-to-date medication dosage information in paper records posed another substantial challenge. Compounding this issue, patients’ inability to recall or effectively communicate details about their prescribed medications made it exceedingly difficult for physicians to safely order new treatments. Another physician elaborated:Many patients were unaware of their medication names and would instead attempt to describe the pill by its shape or color. This approach created considerable challenges in understanding the exact medications they were already taking, making it difficult to avoid potential interactions or contradictions when prescribing new drugs.

Many patients were unaware of their medication names and would instead attempt to describe the pill by its shape or color. This approach created considerable challenges in understanding the exact medications they were already taking, making it difficult to avoid potential interactions or contradictions when prescribing new drugs.

During the transition to E-prescribing, participants initially experienced declining efficiency and productivity. However, they observed that performance and workflow gradually improved as they gained system experience and developed necessary skills. While acknowledging certain benefits, users continued to encounter difficulties integrating these technologies into their clinical environments and practices.

Of particular concern was the context of medication distribution in central pharmacies of social security centers, where medications are provided free of charge. This system raised significant apprehensions about potential medication misuse and the repetitive issuance of similar prescriptions for individual patients. Moreover, the phenomenon of patients consulting multiple physicians in a single day and receiving similar medications without cross-physician awareness was identified as a critical potential issue.

Our analysis revealed four major themes related to E-prescribing errors: system, human, organizational, and medication-related factors.

System factors

The E-prescribing system presented several technical challenges that could potentially lead to medication errors.

The autocomplete function for drug names was identified as a particularly problematic feature that could facilitate selection mistakes. As one physician elaborated:When typing, I often enter only a few initial letters, and a dropdown menu appears. It’s easy to click on the wrong option, so I typically try to type more letters to ensure only one choice appears in the dropdown menu. This helps me avoid accidentally selecting a different medication, such as prescribing amlodipine instead of amiloride.

It’s easy to click on the wrong option, so I typically try to type more letters to ensure only one choice appears in the dropdown menu. This helps me avoid accidentally selecting a different medication, such as prescribing amlodipine instead of amiloride.

Inadequate dose options: A pharmacist explained that patients frequently required manual instructions when the electronic system lacked the necessary format for medication selection.

Insufficient clinical decision support: The system critically lacked comprehensive clinical guidelines, drug interaction alerts, and prescribing protocols. As one interviewee candidly noted:The current system completely lacks the capability to assist physicians in making informed drug prescription decisions. There’s a notable absence of clinical knowledge and guidelines. The system provides no feature to determine which protocol to follow, resulting in what often feels like experimental prescribing.

The current system completely lacks the capability to assist physicians in making informed drug prescription decisions. There’s a notable absence of clinical knowledge and guidelines. The system provides no feature to determine which protocol to follow, resulting in what often feels like experimental prescribing.

The interviewees also advocated that more clinical intelligence be incorporated into the system, such as alerts on the basis of patient risk factors and additional checks for inappropriate medications. As one concluded, “In my opinion, improving some features of the prescription system, such as adding alerts on the basis of the patient’s condition and regarding incorrect drug selection, can prevent medication errors.”

Another physician shared a specific incident highlighting the system’s shortcomings:

“I personally experienced a situation where I selected a drug in the system, and while it was highlighted, I mistakenly prescribed a hospital-grade antibiotic instead of a simple one. The system failed to provide a warning about this critical distinction.” To prevent prescribing errors, the interviewees emphasized the importance of distinguishing inpatient versus outpatient medications through separate codes in the system design.

Label printing challenges: Pharmacy technicians encountered significant issues with label printing workflows. The combination of small text size and the omission of drug quantities on labels created substantial confusion in identifying and dispensing correct medications.

“The label printer’s small size creates difficulties in accurately documenting the medications entered by physicians. Critically, the quantity of drugs is frequently not specified,” reported one pharmacy technician.” However, the interviewees highlighted areas for improvement in label printing and management. Suggestions included bolder fonts, more visible patient details, clear demonstrations of drug forms, and adding images of medications to labels.

Inadequate medication instructions: Incomplete or unclear label instructions further compounded patient misunderstanding. Without sufficiently detailed directions specifying doses and frequencies, patients struggled to comprehend their medication regimens correctly.

Technical disruptions: System outages and technical issues caused significant operational interruptions, further compromising the reliability of the E-prescribing process.

Design limitations: The system’s lack of user-friendliness and the failure to involve key stakeholders in its design were identified as fundamental contributors to potential medication errors. These systemic deficiencies underscore the critical need for comprehensive redesign and improvement of the E-prescribing system to enhance patient safety and healthcare provider efficiency. Regular meetings between developers and prescribers were advised to enable direct reporting of system issues and errors needing correction or support. As one prescriber said, “We do not have support for working with the system, and we don’t know whom to provide our feedback.”

Human factors

Mental and physical conditions significantly influenced prescribing accuracy, with factors such as fatigue, thirst, illness, and irritability playing critical roles during medication prescription. One prescriber candidly described the impact of exhaustion:Fatigue creates a substantial difference in our performance. It directly affects our concentration. During extremely challenging shifts with overwhelming chaos, we must exert extra mental effort to maintain accuracy and focus.

Fatigue creates a substantial difference in our performance. It directly affects our concentration. During extremely challenging shifts with overwhelming chaos, we must exert extra mental effort to maintain accuracy and focus.

The E-prescribing was observed to negatively impact physician-patient interactions. As one physician noted:Once I complete the prescription in the system, I immediately press the exit button and ask the patient to leave. Essentially, the visual and interpersonal communication between physician and patient has dramatically diminished.

Once I complete the prescription in the system, I immediately press the exit button and ask the patient to leave. Essentially, the visual and interpersonal communication between physician and patient has dramatically diminished.

Several human-related factors contributed to potential medication errors:

Knowledge gaps: Insufficient clinical knowledge among physicians.

Professional hierarchy: A reluctance to seek clarification from senior colleagues due to perceived professional barriers.

Complex system requirements: The E-prescribing imposed intricate procedural demands, including the mandatory entry of specific numeric codes for medication orders. Pharmacy staff were required to input identical codes when processing prescriptions. A pharmacist highlighted a critical system limitation regarding code entry:The system deletes prescriptions after only two incorrect code attempts. This creates a significant risk. If staff have already printed labels and dispensed medications to patients, and the prescription is subsequently deleted from the inventory, there becomes no mechanism to accurately record that dispensing.

If staff have already printed labels and dispensed medications to patients, and the prescription is subsequently deleted from the inventory, there becomes no mechanism to accurately record that dispensing.

Organizational factors

Insufficient staffing levels, an aging workforce, inexperienced personnel, and inadequate training emerged as significant organizational challenges. Interviewees reported negative experiences with training, describing it as too brief to provide a comprehensive understanding of the system. New staff members were more prone to errors compared to their more experienced colleagues. One pharmacy technician highlighted the challenges of an aging workforce, stating:When an older individual is responsible for prescription folding, their natural fatigue and exhaustion increase. They may become accustomed to reading labels with similar fonts, which can lead to potential mistakes.

When an older individual is responsible for prescription folding, their natural fatigue and exhaustion increase. They may become accustomed to reading labels with similar fonts, which can lead to potential mistakes.

The complexity of multiple non-integrated platforms further compounded operational difficulties. As one staff member explained: “I am familiar with one system, but when a patient arrives with health insurance or alternative coverage, I am unsure how to proceed due to my limited experience with other insurance platforms.”

Limited technical support was another critical concern. A prescriber emphasized this issue, noting: “We lack adequate system support and are uncertain about whom to contact with our feedback or concerns.”

Medication factors

Similar drug names presented a significant risk for medication errors. As one physician explained: “Due to spelling similarities, if one does not carefully examine the complete name of medications, drugs like metoprolol and metronidazole could be mistakenly entered. Moreover, with the large number of medications, it is possible to erroneously enter 100 instead of 10.”

Complex medication regimens and multiple drug formulations further increased the potential for errors. Another healthcare professional noted: “The complexity of a patient’s clinical condition was identified as a critical source of potential mistakes. As the variety of prescribed drugs increases, the number of prescriptions also rises, consequently heightening the likelihood of dangerous drug interactions.” To address selection errors, the interviewees suggested reorganizing drug listings and modifying search functionality during selection.

Discussion

This study revealed that the factors contributing to E-prescribing errors are complex and interconnected, involving multiple stakeholders across the healthcare system. Our findings identified four main categories of error sources: system, human, organizational, and medication-related factors. In the system domain, technical challenges such as problematic autocomplete features for drug names emerged as significant contributors to selection errors. Human factors, particularly prescriber fatigue and reduced physician-patient interaction time, were found to compromise prescribing accuracy. At the organizational level, insufficient training programs and inadequate staffing levels hindered effective system utilization. Medication-related factors, including look-alike drug names and complex dosing regimens, further increased error risks. These findings align with previous research. Nanji et al. reviewed outpatient computer-generated prescriptions and reported that a substantial proportion contained errors, with error rates varying markedly across prescribing systems, indicating that system functionality and implementation processes can influence prescribing safety [18]. Similarly, Mozaffar et al.identified safety threats linked to suboptimal system design, workarounds, and inadequate implementation/training, supporting our findings on the key role of system and organizational factors in E-prescribing errors [19].

Results demonstrated that working in chaotic, high-volume healthcare environments presents significant challenges that substantially increase the risk of medical errors, particularly in medication prescribing processes. A study by Alshahrani et al. reported that heavy workloads, time constraints, environmental distractions, and multitasking have consistently been identified as primary contributors to E-prescribing and medication administration mistakes [14]. The contextual environment of prescribing plays a critical role in patient safety, as evidence indicates that noisy, crowded clinical settings can significantly impair prescribers’ concentration and decision-making capabilities [20]. Westbrook et al. specifically highlighted that impact of interruptions, multitasking and poor sleep on prescribing errors among emergency physicians. In their observational study of 28 clinicians, they identified 208 prescribing errors across 239 medication orders, with 60% of orders containing at least one error [21]. These findings underscore the urgent need for systemic redesign to support healthcare professionals working under high-pressure conditions. Implementing strategies such as noise reduction in clinical environments, creating dedicated quiet zones, improving workflow management, and developing user-centric technological interfaces could potentially mitigate usability-related errors and enhance overall patient safety.

Clinical knowledge about drugs, dosages, patient factors, and medication interactions represents a critical determinant of prescribing accuracy, with substantial evidence highlighting its pivotal role in medication error prevention [22]. Numerous studies have demonstrated divergent perspectives on the impact of clinical knowledge on prescribing errors. Lewis et al. found that junior doctors’ prescribing errors were often related to practical knowledge gaps (especially dosing) and failures to consider contraindications or drug interactions, within broader workload and cultural pressures [23]. Similarly, Dornan et al. conducted an in-depth investigation into prescribing errors among foundation trainees, revealing that many errors were linked to deficiencies in practical prescribing education and workplace support, and suggesting that targeted educational interventions may help reduce prescribing errors by addressing these gaps [22]. Comprehensive error mitigation strategies should focus on implementing integrated, technology-enhanced continuous medical education and decision support systems that facilitate ongoing professional development, knowledge assessment, and interdisciplinary collaboration [24]. A systematic review demonstrated that such integrated approaches can significantly improve clinical decision-making and reduce medical errors [25]. While clinical knowledge remains fundamental in preventing medication errors, it cannot be viewed in isolation. The most effective error reduction strategies must integrate deep clinical understanding, technological support, and adaptive learning environments. Healthcare institutions must recognize that prescribing safety is a complex interplay of individual knowledge, systemic support, and continuous professional development.

Inadequate training on E-prescribing and insufficient education on error correction procedures were identified as significant contributors to medication errors. Interviewees consistently reported dissatisfaction with their training experiences, characterizing the sessions as cursory and inadequate, with an unrealistic expectation that prescribers would master the system through self-learning. While healthcare facilities must ensure comprehensive users training before system implementation, the relationship between training and error reduction is complex. Leung et al. demonstrated that traditional training and feedback mechanisms alone showed limited impact on E-prescribing accuracy, suggesting that system design improvements may be more effective in modifying user behavior than educational interventions [26]. Furthermore, Lowe et al. found that even well-trained users made significant errors when operating under high work pressure and in distracting environments, indicating that training cannot fully compensate for systemic design issues [27]. These findings suggest a dual approach: providing comprehensive technical training during the pilot phase, with ongoing refresher sessions, and implementing robust system design improvements that minimize the cognitive burden on users, particularly in high-stress situations.

This study identified two factors contributing to medication errors: the absence of prescription protocols or guidelines for prescribers and limited drug interaction alert features in E-prescribing. These finding are consistent with prior research. Various studies have shown that integrating protocols and advanced alerts significantly reduces mistakes [28–30]. However, the effectiveness of drug interaction alerts presents a more complex picture. While Nanji et al. highlighted inadequate alerts as a key driver of prescribing errors [18], subsequent studies have shown that excessive alerting can lead to “alert fatigue,” where clinicians begin to ignore alerts due to frequent exposure to low-priority warnings [31, 32]. Therefore, E-prescribing must not only integrate up-to-date prescribing protocols/guidelines for convenient prescriber access but also implement drug interaction alerts in an intelligent manner that balances safety and usability. Achieving this balance requires both the design of clinically relevant and meaningful alerts and sufficient prescriber training on the effective utilization of these protocols and alert systems.

E-prescribing errors present significant patient safety concern, with several distinct but interrelated causative factors. This study identified three primary error categories: look-alike drug name confusion, spelling errors, and numeric inaccuracies in dosage calculations. Supporting the presence of both selection-related and dosing-related problems in E-prescribing, Westbrook et al. observed E-prescribing errors and reported that the most common error types were wrong drug quantity (40%), wrong duration of therapy (21%), wrong dosing directions (19%), and wrong dosage formulation (11%) [33]. These findings align with broader research demonstrating that numeric errors in dosage calculations persist across different E-prescribing platforms and healthcare settings. To address these challenges, a multi-faceted intervention approach is essential [34]. This should include implementing intelligent system design features such as tall-man lettering and real-time drug name alerts to reduce selection errors [35], incorporating automated dose calculation tools and range checks to prevent numeric mistakes, providing structured training programs for prescribers [36], and establishing clear communication protocols between pharmacists and physicians for prescription verification [37].

The study identified poor user interface design and insufficient stakeholder engagement as significant contributors to prescribing errors. While this finding aligns with existing literature, the relationship between system design and error prevention warrants deeper examination. Alshahrani et al. reported that E-prescribing errors are multifactorial and socio-technical; thus, technical design fixes alone may have limited impact [14]. Similarly, Mozaffar et al. reported that addressing user needs and simplifying processes in system design can reduce mistakes. These findings emphasize the critical role of human factors engineering in system development [19]. Moreover, comparative studies have shown that systems co-designed with clinicians demonstrate significant improvements in both usability and safety outcomes. For instance, Russ et al. reported that user-centered design led to a 50% reduction in the median number of prescribing errors [38], while Marcilly et al. found that clinician-informed alert systems doubled alert acceptance rates compared to traditional designs [39]. The evidence suggests that successful E-prescribing should prioritize physician and pharmacist workflows through user-centered design principles, implement intuitive interfaces based on cognitive task analysis, and establish continuous feedback mechanisms for iterative improvement. This multi-faceted approach has been shown to enhance both system adoption rates and patient safety outcomes.

The results emphasize the critical importance of implementing comprehensive E-prescription that integrate all insurance providers. This finding aligns with extends previous research in several key ways. Khoja et al. demonstrated that fragmented E-prescription led to increase in prescription duplications and in medication discrepancies across different healthcare settings [40]. Similarly Furthermore, another study found that integrated E-prescription platforms reduced medication errors compared to non-integrated systems, particularly in preventing drug-drug interactions and allergic reactions [41]. To achieve nationwide integration, standardization of electronic health records (EHR) within the national E-prescription framework is essential. This standardization serves multiple purposes: it enables consistent prescription capabilities across healthcare institution, ensures seamless access to patient medication histories, and facilitates accurate real-time information sharing between providers. Therefore, we recommend establishing robust infrastructure for integrated national E-prescription through strategic collaboration between insurance entities and the Ministry of Health, with particular emphasis on standardized data protocols and interoperability frameworks.

Implications for research and practice

This qualitative investigation corroborates existing literature on the complex interplay between technological, environmental, clinical and human factors in E-prescribing errors. Given the increasing adoption of E-prescribing implementing a comprehensive sociotechnical approach that addresses user needs and perspectives is crucial for enhancing patient safety. Based on our findings, we propose several key recommendations:

E-prescribing developers must address design-related error sources, particularly problematic autocomplete features, look-alike drug name confusion, and alert fatigue issues, healthcare organizations should implement comprehensive, continuous training programs that focus on both technical competency and clinical knowledge enhancement, end users should actively participate in system updates and improvements through structured feedback mechanisms and usability testing, and healthcare institutions should foster an environment where staff freely identify and report system safety risks, particularly in high-pressure clinical settings.

Future research directions and practical implications include:

Conducting quantitative studies to measure the effectiveness of proposed interventions in reducing E-prescribing error rates, investigating the impact of environmental factors and workflow disruptions on system use and error rates, developing and testing standardized national E-prescribing protocols and guidelines, implementing integrated decision support systems that balance safety and usability, establishing dedicated user support centers with continuous assistance capabilities and examining the relationship between workload, system design, and error patterns in different clinical settings.

Limitations of the study

While this represents the first qualitative investigation examining factors contributing to E- prescribing errors among various grades of medical and non-medical prescribers in Iran. several limitations warrant consideration. The relatively small size of 10 participants may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Additionally, conducting the study exclusively within Iranian Social Security Organization outpatient centers may restrict the applicability of the results to other healthcare settings or countries. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, particularly regarding the generalizability of specific error patterns and intervention effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study reveals that both clinician and non-clinician prescribers encounter similar E-prescribing challenges, with errors stemming from multiple interacting factors including system design, clinical knowledge, environmental conditions, and organizational processes. While interventions should prioritize improving system design and enhancing user training, our findings emphasize the critical importance of addressing the broader sociotechnical context, including workflow integration, environmental factors, and organizational support systems. Success in reducing E-prescribing errors requires a balanced approach that considers both technical and human factors while ensuring system usability and patient safety remain paramount. The implementation of standardized protocols, integrated decision support systems, and continuous professional development programs will be crucial in achieving these objectives.
