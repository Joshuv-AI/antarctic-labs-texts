Gynaecological follow-up of adolescent and young adult cancer survivors: a comparative cohort study
Authors: Octavia Pingault, Aurélie Moskal, Clara Bouvard, Cécile Payet, Anaïs Havet, Cécile Faure-Conter, Marie Viprey, Christine Rousset-Jablonski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess whether gynaecological follow-ups among adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer survivors are less compliant with recommendations than those without a history of AYA cancer.DesignA retrospective national cohort study with exposed–unexposed design was conducted using the French National Health Data System.SettingThe exposed group included AYAs who underwent cancer treatment in France between 2012 and 2014 and was matched in a 1:1 ratio with the unexposed group of AYAs without cancer or chronic disease.ParticipantsA total of 7312 AYAs initiated cancer treatment during the specified period. After applying exclusion criteria, 3663 AYAs were matched to unexposed AYAs.Primary and secondary outcomes measuresThe primary outcome was compliance with gynaecological follow-up recommendations, including gynaecological consultation, sexually transmitted infections screening and cervical cancer screening. Secondary outcomes involved assessing each component of the follow-up, along with human papillomavirus vaccination coverage.ResultsExposed AYAs were significantly more likely to comply with gynaecological follow-up guidelines, with a compliance rate of 23.2%, compared with 16.5% among unexposed AYAs (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.70; p<0.001). When each component of the primary outcome was analysed separately, compliance rates remained significantly higher among those exposed than among those unexposed AYAs. Regarding human papillomavirus vaccination, exposed AYAs displayed higher coverage, with a significantly greater proportion having initiated a catch-up schedule (28.1% vs 18.5%).ConclusionAYA cancer survivors were more likely to have gynaecological follow-up compliant with recommendations, including gynaecological consultations, sexually transmitted infections and cervical cancer screenings.Keywords: Adolescent, Human Papillomavirus Viruses, Cancer, Papillomavirus Vaccines, Sexually Transmitted Disease

Introduction

Adolescent and young adults (AYAs), defined as individuals aged 15–25 years in France, represent a distinct population with unique healthcare needs. Each year, approximately 2150 AYAs are newly diagnosed with cancer in France, more than half of whom are female.1 2 Owing to advances in diagnosis and treatment, the 5-year overall survival for AYA patients with cancer significantly improved from 2000 to 2015.2 This increased survival has led to new concerns, particularly regarding the quality of gynaecological care of these survivors.

AYAs face specific gynaecological challenges, including increased risk of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and human papillomavirus (HPV) exposure. To address these issues, the French National Authority for Health has introduced recommendations for gynaecological follow-up, which include annual consultation,3 vaginal swab STI screenings for those aged 15–25,4 HPV vaccination5 and cervical cancer screening starting at age 25.6

Although guidelines suggest that STI and cervical screening for AYA cancer survivors should not differ from the general AYA population, several studies have shown inadequate adherence to these recommendations, with lower participation in screening and preventive health activities.7 8 Many survivors reported barriers to attending follow-up medical appointments, including fear of receiving another cancer diagnosis, wanting to move on with life and a lack of perceived need due to an absence of symptoms.9 Although HPV vaccination rates in France are notably low compared with those in other European countries,10 AYA cancer survivors may be more aware of cancer prevention efforts. However, to date, no study has evaluated the compliance of AYA cancer survivors with gynaecological follow-up recommendations in France. It was therefore assumed that gynaecological follow-up for AYA cancer survivors would be lower.

The aim of this study was to compare compliance with gynaecological follow-up recommendations between AYA cancer survivors and AYAs with no history of cancer using data from the French National Health Data System (SNDS).

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data statement.

Study design

A retrospective exposed–unexposed cohort study was conducted using data from the French SNDS. The exposed group consisted of AYAs who began active cancer treatment between 2012 and 2014, with the entry date defined as the first recorded date of cancer management for each individual. To account for the duration of active cancer treatment, a 2-year interval was applied to establish the index date. This exposed cohort was matched 1:1 with an unexposed AYA population (ie, individuals with no history of cancer or chronic disease) at the index date. Gynaecological follow-up data were then assessed and compared between the two matched cohorts over a 5-year period (online supplemental figure 1).

Data source

Data were obtained from the SNDS, the French national claims database that covers 99% of the population. This database includes pseudonymised data on socio-demographic characteristics, long-term disease status, medical diagnoses according to the International Classification of Disease, 10th Revision and information on reimbursements for outpatient and inpatient care. The study used data spanning from 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2021.

Population

The exposed group included all French female AYAs who began active cancer treatment between 2012 and 2014 at ages 15–25. Cancer therapeutic management was captured in both inpatient and outpatient settings. AYAs with presumed benign tumours were excluded. Active cancer treatment was defined as surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy or administration of any anticancer drug (online supplemental appendix A). Systemic treatment included chemotherapy and anticancer drugs, such as hormonotherapy, immunotherapy and others (radiopharmaceuticals, photosensitisers and chimeric antigen receptor T cells). Combined treatment was defined by systemic treatment and surgery and/or radiotherapy.

AYAs with prevalent cancers and those who died between the entry and the follow-up end dates were excluded.

The unexposed population was selected from the same cohort of females aged 15–25 years between 2012 and 2014. Individuals previously treated for cancer, undergoing cancer care during follow-up, those with chronic diseases or those who had died were excluded.

Exposed AYAs were matched 1:1 to controls based on age at the index date, department of residence and complementary solidarity health insurance status. Complementary solidarity health insurance status is allowed for socio-economically disadvantaged people and provides 100% coverage of healthcare costs. We considered it as a proxy of social deprivation.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was compliance with gynaecological follow-up recommendations over the 5 year follow-up period. This was defined by a combined criterion including (i) at least one annual gynaecological consultation, (ii) at least one STI screening for AYAs under 25 years during follow-up and (iii) at least one cervical cancer screening for AYAs who turned 25 during follow-up.3 4 6

Gynaecological consultation was recorded using three criteria: (i) inpatient or outpatient procedures or consultations performed by a gynaecologist or midwife, (ii) gynaecological procedures and (iii) dispensations of gynaecological treatments prescribed by a gynaecologist, midwife or general practitioner (online supplemental appendix B). STI screenings were identified through biological procedure codes for chlamydia, using immunological methods, culture or molecular hybridisation (online supplemental appendix C). For cervical cancer screening, both cervical cytology and HPV tests were included (online supplemental appendix C).

Other measures

According to French guidelines, cervical screening with cytology should be performed at age 25, followed by screening every 3 years after two consecutive annual negative tests (ie, three smear tests are recommended between age 25 and 30). For individuals over 30, screening should be performed using HPV tests, according to the 2020 guidelines.6 The completion of the three smear tests between ages 25 and 30 was compared between the two cohorts. Additionally, the initial HPV test was analysed for AYAs who turned 30 during the 2020–2021 follow-up period.

HPV vaccination was also assessed. In France, the first HPV vaccination is recommended between 11 and 14 (two doses), with ‘catch-up’ vaccination (CUV) advised for those aged 15–19.5 The study focused on CUV among AYAs aged 17–19 at the index date, as they were still eligible for CUV during the 5 years of follow-up.5 Data on initial vaccination were gathered to identify AYAs requiring CUV. Completion of the initial vaccination entailed two injections; otherwise, CUV required three doses to complete the schedule. All vaccine types (bivalent, quadrivalent and nonavalent) were included in the analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were presented as means (SDs) and frequencies (percentages). Associations between the exposed/unexposed groups and compliance with gynaecological follow-up recommendations were assessed by logistic regression with a robust variance estimator to account for subject matching. Results are expressed as ORs with 95% CIs and p values. Association between compliance with gynaecological follow-up recommendations and cancer treatment type was assessed among AYA survivors using χ2 test, with a significance set at p<0.05.

A subgroup analysis was performed for AYAs aged 25 at the index date to evaluate the completion of the three recommended cervical smear tests. A sequence index plot was generated, and sequence similarity was calculated using Ward’s hierarchical clustering method.

A Sankey diagram was generated to illustrate the pathways and proportions of HPV vaccination status (no vaccination, incomplete vaccination and complete vaccination), first for the initial vaccination and then for CUV, across three key time points: entry date, index date and follow-up end date.

Data analysis was performed with SAS (version 8.3; 2019–2020 SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, US) and R statistical software (version 4.1.1, R Core Team).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Study population

Between 2012 and 2014, a total of 6 128 061 female AYAs were identified, including 7312 individuals who started active cancer treatment between the ages of 15 and 25. After excluding cases with prevalent cancers, presumed benign tumours and deaths before the end of the follow-up period, 3664 AYAs were eligible for inclusion in the exposed cohort. For the unexposed cohort, 5 371 939 AYAs met the eligibility criteria. The matching process resulted in a final study population of 3663 AYAs in each group (online supplemental figure 2).

Description of cancer management

Two-thirds of the AYAs treated for cancer were aged 20–25, with a mean age of 21.5 years at the onset of cancer management. The most common cancer type was thyroid cancer (21.2%), followed by Hodgkin lymphoma (19%). Haematological malignancies were more frequent among adolescents aged 15–19 years (37.8%). In terms of cancer treatment, 34.7% of AYAs underwent surgery alone, 20.1% underwent surgery and radiotherapy or radiotherapy alone, 17.6% received systemic treatment and 27.6% received combined treatment (table 1). Gynaecological follow-up

Compliance with gynaecological follow-up

Exposed AYAs were significantly more likely to comply with recommended gynaecological follow-up compared with unexposed AYAs (23.2% vs 16.5%, OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.70, p<0.001) (table 2).

Gynaecological consultation

Exposed AYAs were significantly more likely to receive at least one annual gynaecological consultation, with a compliance rate of 39.1% compared with 28.3% among unexposed AYAs (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.47 to 1.79; p<0.001) (table 2).

Sexually transmitted infection screening

Exposed AYAs had a higher likelihood of having undergone at least one STI screening before turning 25 (24.8% vs 21.1%, OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.41; p=0.01). Across the entire study population, 31.1% received at least one screening (table 2).

Cervical cancer screening

Exposed AYAs were significantly more likely to initiate cervical cancer screening at the recommended age of 25 years (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.54 to 1.91; p<0.001), with 68.1% undergoing at least one smear test compared with 55.4% of unexposed AYAs. The mean age at the first smear test was 26.3 years for the exposed group and 26.6 years for unexposed AYAs. For the three smear tests recommended between 25 and 30 years, a greater proportion of exposed AYAs completed the entire series (13.8% vs 11.7%). Among those eligible for cervical screening, 64.7% underwent at least one smear test (exposed, 69.2%; unexposed, 60.2%). For HPV testing, 2.7% of exposed AYAs followed the updated recommendation, compared with 1.8% of the unexposed group (table 2).

Compliance with gynaecological follow-up according to the cancer treatment type

Adherence to the recommended gynaecological follow-up differed significantly among AYA survivors depending on the type of cancer treatment received (p<0.001), including gynaecological consultation, STI screening and cervical cancer screening. AYAs who underwent surgery alone were the most compliant (26.4%), whereas those who received only systemic treatment were the least compliant (17.4%).

Human papillomavirus vaccination

A total of 453 AYAs from each group were eligible for the CUV during cancer management or the follow-up period. Exposed AYAs had a higher completion rate of the initial HPV vaccination (15.9%) compared with unexposed AYAs (11.7%). Among those who either did not complete the initial vaccination or had an incomplete schedule, exposed individuals were significantly more likely to initiate CUV (28.1%) than the unexposed group (18.5%) (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.41; p<0.01). Discussion

AYA cancer survivors were significantly more likely to have adhered to gynaecological follow-up current recommendations than their cancer-free peers, including gynaecological consultation, STI screening and cervical cancer screening. Despite this increased adherence, overall compliance remained low.

Previous studies have highlighted the limited access to gynaecological care for AYA cancer survivors, particularly noting a lack of long-term follow-up.11 12 However, adherence to gynaecological consultation had not been specifically studied in France. Cancer survivors may have more frequent medical visits because of their heightened medical concerns such as managing adverse gynaecological effects of cancer therapies, fertility issues, endocrine dysfunctions and contraception, which may explain their higher rates of follow-up.13 14 Nevertheless, AYAs who received systemic treatment were the least compliant among survivors. The burden of treatment received may be such that AYA survivors avoided any further medical care once they were cured. Alternatively, the follow-up care required may have been so intensive that gynaecological care was overlooked.

To date, no studies have addressed post-cancer STI screening. In this study, STI screening prevalence among AYAs exceeded the rates reported by the French Public Health Agency (7% in 2016 and 12% in 2021 for individuals aged 15–25).15 Screening is recommended for sexually active AYAs, and given the minimum age of 17 years in this study, it is likely that a higher proportion of participants were sexually active, leading to increased screening rates. Regarding the observed disparity between exposed and unexposed AYAs, cancer survivors may experience delays in reaching sexual milestones, which could partially mitigate this finding.16

The literature on cervical cancer screening reports conflicting findings. Although some suggest that cancer survivors are less likely to undergo screening,17 the majority of studies, including our results, highlight that cancer survivors are more likely to adhere to HPV smear screening recommendations.18 19 This may be due to their increased risk for cervical dysplasia and neoplasms.20 In this study, the rate of unexposed AYAs aged 25 years at the index date who had at least one smear test aligned with the national coverage rate (65.5% for individuals aged 25–29 in 2015–2017).21

Our findings on HPV vaccination differ from recent studies, which reported significantly lower initiation rates among survivors.7 22 In France, vaccination coverage reached 22.8% in 2018, whereas the present study revealed a slightly lower rate of 19.4%.23 This discrepancy may be due to the fact that vaccinations administered in non-hospital settings or during public campaigns may not have been recorded in the SNDS database. Moreover, some AYAs in our study cohort were already in the recommended age range for HPV vaccination before it was introduced in July 2007, making them ineligible for vaccination.

To our knowledge, this is the first study in France comparing gynaecological follow-up between AYA cancer survivors and cancer-free controls. The national scope of this study enabled a comprehensive and representative analysis of French AYAs, as recorded in national health registries.1 2 One notable strength of this study is the consideration of various healthcare professionals, including general practitioners, who are increasingly involved in providing primary gynaecological care.24 Additionally, using data from the SNDS provided a more exhaustive and accurate dataset, free from the potential recall biases. Regarding socio-demographic factors, to minimise confusion bias, we matched AYAs of the two groups of comparison on age, department of residence as a proxy of care accessibility and complementary solidarity health insurance status, which is allowed for socio-economically disadvantaged people and which provides 100% coverage of healthcare costs, as a proxy of social deprivation.

There are limitations inherent to the use of the SNDS, as the data were not initially collected for research purposes and may thus contain random or systematic measurement errors.25 First, the sexual activity status of AYAs was not available, which may have led to an underestimation of compliance rates for sexually active individuals. Nonetheless, as the included AYAs were at least 17 years old at the beginning of the follow-up period—close to the median age of the first sexual intercourse in France (17.6 years)—the extent of underestimation is minimised.26 However, it would also have been interesting to collect data on younger AYAs, as their behaviour may differ in response to gynaecological prevention initiatives. Further analysis of this age group would inform future approaches, including the need for specific consultation in this area. Another limitation relates to the exposure of AYAs to cancer and the possibility of recurrence. Despite the 2-year interval used to exclude prevalent cancers, uncertainty remains about cancers before this timeframe. If it was ultimately a recurrence, heightened medical surveillance could have resulted in an overestimation of follow-up adherence. Similarly, other chronic diseases among exposed AYAs were not taken into account, which may have introduced a similar bias. Additionally, the last 2 years of follow-up overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to disparities in healthcare access.27 Despite this, this study adapted by encompassing teleconsultations in its assessment of gynaecological consultations. Finally, in the absence of laboratory test results in the SNDS, it was impossible to determine whether the screenings conducted were abnormal and, thus, whether subsequent ones were control smears (deviating from standard follow-up recommendations). Other confounding factors such as lifestyle, ethnicity and religion28 29 were also not available in this medico-administrative database.

Various strategies could be proposed to improve AYAs adherence with primary care gynaecological screenings and prevention, as written information reports, digital tools for monitoring or networks of healthcare professionals to facilitate referrals.30 31 In addition, cancer survivors may face heightened risk of persistent HPV infection and cancer because of treatment-related immunosuppression, which should prompt increased vaccination and screening.20 32 The integration of gynaecological management in AYA cancer survivors’ long-term follow-up programmes may also be considered.33

This retrospective nationwide study revealed that AYAs treated for cancer are more likely to adhere to gynaecological follow-up recommendations; however, the overall compliance rates remain relatively low, indicating an opportunity for improving care for young women.
