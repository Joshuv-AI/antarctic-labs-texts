Lipoblastoma of the head and neck in children: a single-institution study
Authors: Yanzhen Li, Ge Zhang, Nian Sun, Qiaoyin Liu, Xuexi Zhang, Zhiyong Liu, Jialu Wang, Xin Ni, Shengcai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLipoblastoma is a rare benign tumour, with a higher recurrence rate in the head and neck region owing to complex anatomy. The study summarizes the clinical features, surgical risks, and complications of paediatric lipoblastoma in various anatomical domains of the head and neck, aiming to improve diagnosis and management.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on children who were treated for lipoblastoma of the head and neck between 2016 and 2023. Data assessed included location, demographics, clinical presentation, imaging, treatment, complications, recurrence, and follow-up.ResultsA total of 32 patients with lipoblastoma were retrospectively analyzed. These lipoblastomas located in the face (n = 2, 6.3%), nuchal region (n = 3, 9.4%), anterior neck (n = 6, 18.8%), lateral neck (n = 1, 3.1%), parapharyngeal region (n = 6, 18.8%), cervicomediastinal region (n = 4, 12.5%), and cervicoscapular-axillary region (n = 10, 31.3%). Complete resection was achieved in twenty-four cases. Residual lesions were confirmed in eight patients (25.0%). During a mean follow-up of 64.2 months, residual lesions regressed in two patients, remained stable in four, and progressed in two parapharyngeal cases that required secondary surgery via a transcervical approach. No recurrence was observed after complete resection.ConclusionA location-based approach aids in understanding surgical risk and prognosis in pediatric head and neck lipoblastoma. Given the benign nature and favorable outcomes, maximal safe resection with preservation of critical neurovascular structures is recommended. Complete excision may be technically challenging in deep head and neck spaces, and careful surgical planning is essential.

Introduction

Lipoblastoma is a rare benign tumour arising from embryonic white fat, occurring almost exclusively in infants and young children[1]. These tumors most commonly occur in the limbs and trunk, while neck involvement is rare. Surgical excision is curative; however, recurrence rates ranging from 14 to 24% have been reported [2, 3].

It has been suggested that recurrence rates may be higher in the head and neck region owing to the complex anatomical structures. Lipoblastomas are broadly classified into encapsulated (localized) and diffuse (infiltrative) types, which may differ in biological behaviour and prognosis. Data correlating anatomical location, growth pattern, surgical strategy, and long-term outcome in the head and neck remain limited.

Focusing on these anatomical challenges, the present study provides a location-based analysis of pediatric head and neck lipoblastoma with long-term follow-up. Rather than emphasizing cohort size, we aim to provide incremental insights into residual disease behavior, location-specific surgical risks, and implications for surgical approach selection and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This is a single-center retrospective case series study. It was conducted at the Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University, National Center for Children's Health. Through the medical record system search, patients aged 0 ~ 18 years with pathologically confirmed lipoblastoma arising in the head and neck region between January 2016 and December 2023 were included. Patients with prior surgical treatment elsewhere were excluded. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children's Hospital (No. [2025]-E-020-R).

Diagnostic workup

All patients underwent preoperative imaging, including ultrasonography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and/or computed tomography (CT), to evaluate tumor size, location, extent, and relationship to adjacent structures. Preoperative fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was not routinely undertaken. Definitive diagnosis was established by postoperative histopathologic examination. Immunohistochemistry was performed when necessary to exclude other adipocytic or soft tissue tumors.

Surgical management and definitions

All patients underwent surgical treatment under general anesthesia. Surgical approach selection was based on tumor location, extent, and proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Complete resection (CR) was defined as macroscopic complete tumor removal at surgery, with no evidence of residual disease on early postoperative imaging. Incomplete resection (ICR) was defined as the presence of gross residual tumor during surgery, microscopic residual disease at the surgical margin, or radiological evidence of residual disease on early postoperative imaging. Recurrence was defined as tumor reappearance after documented CR, whereas residual disease progression referred to growth of known residual tumor during follow-up.

Follow-up

Postoperative follow-up was performed using ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The initial assessment was conducted within 1–3 months after surgery. For those without residual disease, follow-up was scheduled annually. For patients with residual lesions, follow-up was performed every 6–12 months.

Results

Demographics

A total of 34 cases were pathologically confirmed as lipoblastoma, among which 2 cases were lost to follow-up, resulting in 32 cases ultimately included in the analysis. The study cohort included 11 males (34.4%) and 21 females (65.6%), with a median age of 3.1 years extending from 0.4 to 10.1 years, and 18 cases under 3 years. These lipoblastomas located in the face (n = 2, 6.3%), nuchal region (n = 3, 9.4%), anterior neck (n = 6, 18.8%), lateral neck (n = 1, 3.1%), parapharyngeal region (n = 6, 18.8%), cervicomediastinal region (n = 4, 12.5%), and cervicoscapular-axillary region (n = 10, 31.3%) (Fig. 1). The majority of patients presented with an isolated, asymptomatic mass that was soft on exam. Three patients presented with sleep snoring, one case among them accompanied by secretory otitis media. Radiographic findings

A total of thirty patients underwent preoperative ultrasonography, five underwent computed tomography (CT), and twenty-nine underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted sequences with routine fat suppression were included in all MRI examinations. Based on previous classification criteria and imaging examinations, lesions located in the facial, anterior cervical, and lateral cervical regions were classified as circumscribed, whereas those involving the parapharyngeal space, cervicomediastinal region, or cervicoscapular–axillary region were considered diffuse. However, in one case involving a facial lipoblastoma, preoperative physical examination revealed adhesion between the mass and the skin. Intraoperatively, the tumor exhibited ill-defined borders, adhesions to surrounding tissues, and involvement of the skin. We consider it more accurate to classify this case as the diffuse type. Among the three lesions in the nuchal region, two were subcutaneous and classified as circumscribed, while one located on the muscular layer and extended to the vertebral body, thus classified as diffuse type.

On ultrasonography, twenty-two lesions presented with high or moderately high echogenicity, four with moderate echogenicity, and two with low echogenicity. Two lesions located in the cervical mediastinum demonstrated mixed high and low echogenicity. Relatively abundant intratumoral vascularity was observed in three cases, whereas the remaining lesions showed poor or minimal blood flow signals. Band-like hyperechoic septations were identified in twenty-four cases (80.0%).

Computed tomography (CT) typically demonstrated well-defined, smoothly marginated, low-attenuation masses. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed well-circumscribed, round or ovoid, and frequently lobulated lesions. In most cases (24/29, 82.8%), the tumors appeared classic hyperintense on both T1- and T2-weighted images (Fig. 2). Eleven lipoblastomas demonstrated heterogeneous signal characteristics (Fig. 3). On fat-suppressed sequences, seventeen lesions (58.6%) showed low signal intensity (Fig. 2C), nine exhibited heterogeneous signal intensity (Fig. 3C), and two appeared predominantly hyperintense. Strand-like linear septations were observed on both T1- and T2-weighted images in twenty-three patients (79.3%) (Fig. 2). Notably, one case of cervicomediastinal lipoblastoma demonstrated two distinct signal patterns between the cervical and mediastinal portions of the tumor (Fig. 4). Surgery and follow-up

Twenty-seven lesions were removed using a transcervical approach, and a nuchal lipoblastoma with extension into the spinal canal was completely resected following a laminotomy. A combined cervicothoracic approach was required for three tumors—two located in the cervicomediastinal region and one in the cervicoscapular–axillary region—due to the deep inferior extension of the lesions. In one exceptional case, a parapharyngeal lipoblastoma was found to invade the carotid artery. Following intraoperative vascular rupture, partial carotid resection and artificial vascular graft replacement were performed. Two parapharyngeal lipoblastomas were initially resected incompletely via a transoral approach. Complete resection was achieved in 24 cases. Incomplete resection occurred in eight cases, including one lesion in the lateral neck, five in the parapharyngeal space, and two in the cervicomediastinal region. In the two parapharyngeal cases approached transorally, the residual tumor measured more than 0.5 cm in maximal diameter, whereas all other remnants were minimal, with a maximal diameter of less than 0.5 cm based on both intraoperative assessment and initial postoperative imaging. Postoperative complications were observed in four patients, all of which were mild and resolved with conservative management (Table 1). The median follow-up duration was 64.2 months (range: 12.0–147.0 months). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasonography were the most frequently used imaging modalities during follow-up. In two parapharyngeal cases, residual tumor progressed following initial intraoral excision; both were successfully re-excised via a transcervical approach, highlighting the importance of adequate surgical exposure.

Pathologic examination

In this study, five pediatric patients underwent preoperative fine-needle aspiration biopsy. Among them, three cases were diagnosed as lipoblastoma, while the remaining two were identified as adipose-derived tumors. Gross pathological examination of the excised lesions most commonly revealed well-circumscribed, encapsulated, yellowish-pink adipose tissue. Microscopic evaluation demonstrated lesions composed of adipocytes at various stages of differentiation, ranging from primitive, stellate, and spindle-shaped mesenchymal cells to lipoblasts and mature adipose cells (Fig. 5A). Discussion

Lipoblastoma is a rare, benign, and rapidly growing tumor of infancy and early childhood that originates from embryonal white adipose tissue. It occurs almost exclusively in infants and young children, and is most often diagnosed within the first three years of life, with a slightly higher incidence in males [4–7]. In the present series, the mean patient age was 3.1 years, which is slightly higher than the average age reported in other series. In our cohort, the sex distribution differed from most previous reports, with a higher proportion of females. However, whether a true sex predilection exists in lipoblastoma requires confirmation in studies with larger sample sizes.

Most fat lobules are composed of mature adipocytes in the center, surrounded by immature mucinous cells. The ratio of the two is related to the degree of adipocyte differentiation. Because adipose tissue has lower acoustic attenuation than surrounding muscle and skin, it usually appears hyperechoic on ultrasound. Low-differentiated lipoblastomas with abundant myxoid components may present as heterogeneous or hypoechoic masses. Therefore, lipoblastoma typically appears as a hyperechoic mass on ultrasound, but may also present as hypoechoic, isoechoic, or mixed echogenicity [8, 9]. In this study, high or moderately high echogenicity was observed in twenty-two cases, and linear hyperechoic septa were identified in twenty-four cases, these two features were considered ultrasonographic findings of lipoblastoma.

On T1-weighted MRI, signal intensity varies with adipocyte maturity and the proportion of myxoid stroma. Immature lipoblasts and myxoid components exhibit intermediate to low signal intensity, whereas mature adipocytes show high signal intensity. T2-weighted signal intensity also varies, mainly according to myxoid and fibrous content, with myxoid stroma and lipoblasts showing high signal intensity. Low signal intensity on fat-suppressed sequences provides strong evidence of a lipomatous component [8, 9]. In this study, twenty-four lesions showed typical hyperintense on T1 and T2-weighted images, and seventeen showed signal loss on fat-suppressed sequences. Linear low-signal septa on T1- and T2-weighted images were observed in twenty-three lesions. Signal loss on fat-suppressed sequences and linear septation were regarded as characteristic MRI features of lipoblastoma (Fig. 2). Tumors in the anterior cervical and facial regions showed relatively homogeneous signals, whereas lesions in the deep cervical spaces and junctional regions often appear as heterogeneous signals. One cervicomediastinal lipoblastoma showed distinct signal characteristics between the cervical and mediastinal components (Fig. 3). Whether the tumors in the two anatomical regions derived from the same regional tumor cells with different degrees of differentiation, or from the two different anatomical regions needs further research.

Preoperative cytology or core needle biopsy was not routinely performed in this cohort because clinical presentation and imaging findings were generally sufficient to guide diagnosis and surgical planning in this age group. Recent pediatric studies indicate that most fat-containing soft tissue tumors in children are benign and may be classified based on clinical and imaging criteria, with biopsy reserved for lesions showing atypical features such as low fat content, prominent myxoid zones on imaging, or age ≥ 10 years. Although FNAC and core biopsy can be diagnostic in select cases, their routine use in infants and young children is limited by sampling error, risk of bleeding, and the need for general anesthesia, without clear evidence of improved diagnostic accuracy in typical presentations [10, 11]. FNAC was performed in five patients in this study, but in two cases it failed to reliably distinguish benign from malignant disease. These evidence limit the routine clinical utility of preoperative FNAC in pediatric head and neck lipoblastoma.

Lipoblastoma is commonly classified into two histological types: circumscribed lipoblastoma, which accounts for approximately 70% of cases and typically presents as a superficial, well-encapsulated lesion; and diffuse lipoblastomatosis, representing about 30% of cases, and by a deep-seated, poorly circumscribed lesion with an infiltrative growth pattern that may involve adjacent muscular structures [12, 13]. In this study, diffuse-type lesions were relatively more common. One facial lesion, although superficially located, lacked a complete capsule and exhibited prominent myxoid components with focal infiltration into the surrounding soft tissues and skin. Deep cervical lesions frequently adhered to major vessels, nerves, or vertebral structures and lacked a capsule, whereas tumor portions distant from critical structures were often encapsulated and more easily dissected. Accordingly, the feasibility of complete surgical excision and subsequent prognosis appear depend largely on both the anatomical location and the pathological characteristics of the tumor.

Complete surgical excision remains the definitive curative treatment when feasible. Reported recurrence rates range from 14 to 24%, mainly in diffuse lipoblastomatosis [14]. Recurrence may be more common in the head and neck because of complex anatomy [15–18]. However, many studies do not clearly distinguish true recurrence from regrowth of residual disease. In this series, the critical significance of recurrence versus residual progression in prognostic assessment was established. No recurrence occurred after complete excision. The residual rate was 25.0% (8/32), with the highest rate in parapharyngeal lipoblastomas, which frequently encased the carotid sheath and extended towasrd the vertebral foramen. Two patients with substantial residual tissue exhibited progression and required secondary surgery, whereas minimal remnants (with a maximum diameter of < 0.5 cm) remained stable.

Most authors advocate prioritizing functional preservation over radical excision because of the benign nature of lipoblastoma, the absence of malignant transformation, and the potential for spontaneous regression or maturation into lipoma [7]. Spontaneous regression has been reported [2], and maturation into benign lipoma after recurrence has also been described [19]. These findings support a strategy of maximal safe resection with preservation of neurovascular structures. In this series, patients with minimal residual disease showed favorable outcomes, and one recurrent parapharyngeal lesion demonstrated mature adipocytic differentiation on secondary excision. These findings support a function-preserving surgical philosophy rather than aggressive radical resection. Based on our observations, the volume of residual disease appears to influence clinical outcomes; however, given the limited sample size, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, and this will be a key focus of our future research.

With regard to surgical approach selection, a transcervical approach is recommended for parapharyngeal lipoblastomas to ensure adequate exposure of the lesion. For cervicomediastinal or cervicoscapular lipoblastomas, a combined cervicothoracic approach should be considered when necessary. The exceptional case involving carotid artery invasion leading to rupture and eventual reconstruction underscores the necessity of individualized decision-making and should not be considered representative of routine management. For lipoblastomas arising in different anatomical locations, site-specific risk assessment is required preoperatively [7, 17]. Considering that lipoblastomas have a tendency for local infiltration, particularly around vessels and nerves, and may even extend into the intervertebral foramina, we advocate for early detection and timely surgical excision.

Based on the data from this study, we propose the following treatment strategy framework: (1) Goal: To achieve maximal safe resection while preserving neurovascular function. (2) Approach Selection: For lipoblastomas located in the facial, nuchal, anterior cervical, and lateral cervical regions, the surgical approach is non-controversial; tumors in these areas are excised via facial or cervical incisions corresponding to the tumor site. For lipoblastomas in the parapharyngeal space, a transcervical approach is preferred to avoid substantial residual disease resulting from inadequate exposure via a transoral approach (as observed in two cases in this series that required secondary surgery). For lipoblastomas situated in the cervicomediastinal or cervicoscapular regions, a combined cervicothoracic approach should be prepared based on the inferior extent of the lesion. (3) Management of Residual Disease: If minimal residual lesions adherent to critical structures are encountered intraoperatively, they may be left in place. Our long-term follow-up indicates that such remnants mostly remain stable or even regress, thereby avoiding the risk of functional impairment associated with pursuing complete resection at all costs.

Histologically, lipoblastoma is predominantly composed of mature adipose tissue, interspersed with clusters of adipocytes at varying stages of differentiation and surrounded by a myxoid stroma. Myxoid morphology is relatively uncommon in lipoblastoma. Seven tumors in this series demonstrated abundant myxoid stroma and shared similar clinical and imaging features, including heterogeneous signals and increased vascularity. Intraoperatively, these lesions were partially encapsulated, adhered to adjacent structures, and frequently involved major vessels or nerves, increasing surgical complexity. These findings suggest that abundant myxoid stroma represents a distinct histologic pattern that may influence surgical planning and the risk of residual disease. However, given the limited number of cases and lack of molecular or long-term outcome validaion, no conclusions regarding a separate biological subtype can be drawn.

The differential diagnosis of pediatric adipocytic tumors includes lipoma, atypical lipomatous tumor, and rarely myxoid liposarcoma. In early childhood, lipoblastoma remains the most common entity. Molecular testing for PLAG1 rearrangement can support diagnosis. MDM2 positivity is a marker for liposarcoma. In this cohort, all cases that underwent MDM2 testing were negative. PLAG1 testing was available in a subset of cases and supported the pathological diagnosis of lipoblastoma; however, molecular testing was not systematically performed throughout the study period, representing a limitation.

As a retrospective, single-center study, this cohort is subject to selection and information bias. Although relatively large for a rare tumor, subgroup sample sizes remain limited, restricting statistical power. The absence of systematic molecular profiling further limits biological interpretation. Future prospective, multi-center studies incorporating standardized imaging protocols, molecular diagnostics, and long-term follow-up are needed to strengthen evidence-based management strategies for pediatric head and neck lipoblastoma.

Conclusion

Pediatric head and neck lipoblastoma demonstrates location-specific surgical challenges and variable resectability. Given the benign nature and favorable outcomes, maximal safe resection with preservation of neurovascular structures is recommended. Early diagnosis and careful surgical planning are essential, particularly for tumors arising in deep cervical spaces.
