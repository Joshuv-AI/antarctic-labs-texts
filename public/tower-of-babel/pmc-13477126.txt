Optimizing treatment of proximal thoracic aortic graft infections
Authors: Matthew A Thompson, Toshiki Fujiyoshi, Benjamin Kramer, Ashley M Lowry, Haytham Elgharably, Steven Gordon, Vidyasagar Kalahasti, Nabin Shrestha, Patrick Vargo, Eugene H Blackstone, Eric E Roselli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe treatment, assess outcomes, and identify risk factors for primary infection, reinfection, and death in patients with proximal thoracic aortic graft infection.MethodsFrom July 2007 to January 2018, 148 adults received treatment for graft infection of the aortic root (108 [73%]), ascending aorta (100 [68%]), and aortic arch (5 [3.4%]) at a median 2.7 years after implant. Median follow-up after diagnosis of graft infection was 2.8 years. Multiphase hazard modeling was used to determine instantaneous risk of infection and death. Multivariable regression and random forests were implemented to identify risk factors for time to infection, reinfection, and death.ResultsPatients presented with fever (108 [73%]), chest pain (66 [45%]), embolic ischemia (31 [21%]), or stroke (13 [8.8%]). One hundred forty (95%) patients underwent surgery, including 126 (85%) with complete graft excision and replacement, usually with an allograft. There were 3 (2%) hospital deaths. Seven-year freedom from reinfection was 79%. Graft excision and replacement with an allograft mitigated risk of reinfection, and history of intravenous drug use was a risk factor for reinfection. Survival at 1, 5, and 8 years was 84%, 72%, and 57%, respectively. Nonsurgical management, fungal pathogen, and stroke as a presenting symptom of graft infection were risk factors for early death.ConclusionsProximal aortic graft infections should be treated with prompt, tailored antibiotic therapy followed by complete graft excision. We recommend graft replacement with an allograft for its versatility as a conduit and its potential to mitigate reinfection.Key Words: allograft, graft salvage, reinfection, survival

Survival after diagnosis of proximal thoracic aortic graft infection.

Central Message

Proximal aortic graft infection is best treated by tailored antibiotics and graft excision with tissue conduit replacement. Incomplete resection and lifelong antibiotics are reasonable alternatives.

Perspective

Treatment guidelines for proximal thoracic aortic graft infections are lacking. We demonstrate excellent outcomes using a care path in which all patients receive prompt, tailored antibiotic therapy. Ideally, operative candidates should then undergo complete graft excision and tissue conduit replacement. When complete excision is not feasible, partial excision with lifelong antibiotics is feasible.

Graft infection occurs in 1% to 3% of patients with thoracic aortic grafts and may carry 25% to 75% mortality.1 Until the 2022 American Heart Association guidelines,2 there were no specific recommendations for managing aortic graft infections.3,4 Although open surgery with either in situ reconstruction or extra-anatomic bypass is reasonable for these patients, there is persistent debate regarding type of graft material used and application of tissue flaps.2

Diversity of patients prevents a 1-size-fits-all approach to treating thoracic aorta graft infection. Graft replacement with or without tissue flap coverage is appropriate for robust patients,5,6 those presenting in critical condition may undergo conservative surgery with extensive debridement without graft excision,1,7 and exceptionally high-risk patients may receive only medical treatment. Objectives of this study are to describe the treatment care path for proximal thoracic aortic graft infections at our high-volume aortic center, evaluate outcomes, and identify risk factors associated with time to primary infection, reinfection, and death.

Methods

Patients

From July 2007 to January 2018, 148 adults (124, 84% men; mean age 56 ± 13 years) were treated for suspected or diagnosed proximal thoracic aortic (root, ascending, arch) graft infection at Cleveland Clinic. Reinfections occurring during the study period were not considered new index events. Sixteen patients with thoracoabdominal aortic graft infections were excluded because of qualitative differences in the surgical treatment and outcomes of distal aortic graft infections.5

Definitions

Thoracic aortic graft infection was defined using validated criteria from the Management of Aortic Graft Infection Collaboration.8,9 Diagnostic criteria from 3 categories were classified as major or minor. Graft infection was suspected in the presence of a single major criterion only or ≥2 minor criteria from different categories. It was diagnosed in the presence of 1 major criterion plus any criterion (major or minor) from another category. Categories were clinical/surgical, radiological, and laboratory. Data

Data were abstracted from institutional quality registries, the electronic medical record and a prospectively maintained database. Use of these data for research was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (#18-579, approved May 14, 2018), with waiver of patient consent.

End Points

End points were thoracic aortic graft reinfection, reoperation for these reinfections, and death. Time 0 was the date of graft infection diagnosis. Cross-sectional follow-up data were supplemented with telephone interviews. Median follow-up was 2.8 years, with 25% followed >6 years and 10% followed >8.8 years (Figure E1).

Completeness of follow-up after treatment of proximal thoracic aortic graft infection.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and R software version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD or 15th, 50th (median), and 85th percentiles for skewed distributions. Categorical data are summarized by frequency and percent. Interval between graft implant to graft infection was assessed by the cumulative distribution function, probability density function, and a multiphase hazard model.10 These functions are accompanied by 68% confidence limits (±1 SE). Factors modulating each hazard phase were considered simultaneously in multivariable analysis (Table E1). Variable selection, with P > .05 for retention of variables in the model, utilized machine-learning bagging with 1000 bootstrap samples.11 Frequency of occurrence of variables found in the analysis indicated their reliability. Variables with ≥50% bootstrap reliability were retained.

In surgically treated patients, reinfection risk after primary graft infection was assessed nonparametrically by the Kaplan-Meier method and parametrically by a multiphase hazard model.10 Because the hazard model resolved a single hazard phase, random forest for survival was used to identify predictors of reinfection using covariates in Table E2 and 5000 regression trees with 10 variables per split. On-the-fly imputation was used to account for missing data.12 Variable importance was used to hierarchically order the variables in relation to predicted response.13 Partial dependency plots adjusted for all other covariates were used to visualize the relationships between outcome and covariates.14 Freedom from reoperation for reinfection was assessed non-parametrically by the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival after diagnosis of primary infection was assessed using nonparametric and parametric analyses. Factors influencing hazard phases were identified as described above (Table E2).11

Results

Primary Graft Infection

Of the 148 thoracic aortic graft infections, 108 (73%) were of the aortic root, 100 (68%) ascending aorta, and 5 (3.4%) aortic arch (Table 2). Regional involvement was not mutually exclusive: 60 (41%) were infections of both root and ascending grafts, and 1 (0.7%) of root, ascending, and arch grafts. Primary indication for initial graft implant was aortic valve endocarditis in 31 (21%) and graft infection in 5 (3.4%). Despite prior graft infection, these patients were included because they were treated at an outside institution with a prosthetic graft. Forty-four (30%) were infected mechanical or biologic composite valve grafts and 38 (26%) an infected allograft.

The most common presenting symptoms of primary infection were fever (108 [73%]), malaise (103 [70%]), and chest pain (66 [45%]) (Table 2). Infections were frequently complicated by periaortic abscess (100 [68%]), vegetations of aortic valve or graft (55 [37%]), or pseudoaneurysm (45 [30%]). A possible source of graft infection was identified in 57 (39%) patients who developed infections at other sites between graft implant and symptom onset: infection of bone (16 [11%]), dentition (15 [10%]), skin (14 [9.5%]), respiratory or genitourinary tracts (6 [4.1%]), or implantable cardiac electronic devices (3 [2.0%]). The leading causal pathogen was Staphylococcus aureus (41 [28%]) (Table E3).

Time From Initial Implant to Primary Infection

Patients presented with primary graft infection a median of 2.7 years (15th-85th percentile, 0.4-11 years) after implant. Cumulative incidence of time-to-infection was 32%, 64%, and 82% at 1-, 5-, and 10-years postimplant, respectively, with 1 taking place 27 years after implant (Figure 1, A). There were 2 distinct phases of time to infection: those occurring within 5 years and those later (Figure 1, B). Early-phase infections were more likely in patients with a biologic composite valve graft (P = .001) and endocarditis as the primary indication for original graft implant (P = .009) and less likely in patients who underwent aortic root replacement (P = .01) or had bicuspid aortic valves (P = .002). Graft implant performed at Cleveland Clinic (P = .001) was protective against late infection (Table E4).

Cumulative distribution of time to infection (A) and probability density of time to infection (B) after implanting a proximal thoracic aortic graft. 95% confidence limits denoted by dashed lines.

Treatment Strategy

All patients received tailored antibiotic therapy based on identified pathogen (Table E5). Two (1.4%) had rapid clinical and imaging improvement on antibiotic therapy and did not undergo surgery—1 was alive 9 years after treatment; the other died 4 years after discharge from sepsis attributed to chronic bacterial diskitis. Six patients (4.1%) deemed too debilitated to survive surgery were managed medically. Four died within 1 month of discharge on parenteral antibiotics. The other 2 are alive 7 and 12 years after discharge, having received 6 weeks of parenteral antibiotics followed by oral antibiotic suppression.

One hundred forty (95%) patients underwent surgery. Median interval from initiation of antibiotic therapy to surgery was 5 days (15th-85th percentile, 1.2-11 days). The infected graft was left in situ in 14 (9.5%). Four of these patients had an infected prosthetic aortic graft and underwent fungal thrombectomy (n = 1), emergency endovascular stenting of a dehisced suture line (n = 1), and irrigation and debridement (n = 2). The remaining 10 patients had an infected allograft from a prior surgery for endocarditis and underwent isolated bioprosthetic (n = 7) or mechanical (n = 1) aortic valve replacement or patch aortoplasty to correct anastomotic defects that precipitated large pseudoaneurysms (n = 2). One hundred twenty-six (85%) patients underwent graft excision and in situ replacement, usually with an allograft (Table 3). Tissue flap coverage was performed in 5 (3.6%) using omentum, muscle, or bovine pericardium.

Hospital Outcomes

There were 3 (2.0%) hospital deaths, all from the operative cohort. Two had penetrating chest wall abscesses—1 died from multiorgan failure after patch aortoplasty of an infected allograft pseudoaneurysm, and 1 died intraoperatively from florid disseminated intravascular coagulation post cardiopulmonary bypass. The third patient presented with multiple hemorrhagic strokes secondary to septic embolization. Postoperative complications included pacemaker implantation (13 out of 103 [13%]), tracheostomy (12 [8.1%]), and stroke (11 [7.4%]) (Table 3).

Reinfection and Reoperation for Infection

Seventeen patients (12%) experienced reinfection after surgery. Freedom from reinfection at 1, 5, and 7 years was 95%, 83%, and 79%, respectively (Figure 2, A and B). Repair strategy and history of intravenous drug use were the most important predictors of reinfection (Figure E2, A). Patients who underwent graft excision and replacement with an allograft or allograft/prosthetic graft combination had the highest freedom from reinfection, followed by excision and replacement with a prosthetic graft, then graft sparing (Figure 2, C). Patients with a history of intravenous drug use had lower freedom from reinfection (Figure 2, D). Eleven patients underwent reoperation for reinfection (Figure E2, B).

Survival

Survival 1, 5, and 8 years after diagnosis of aortic graft infection was 84%, 72%, and 57%, respectively, with 42 deaths (Figure 3, A). Hazard function analysis resolved 2 phases for instantaneous risk of death: an early, peaking phase within the immediate period following diagnosis, and a late, increasing phase, beginning 4 years later (Figure 3, B). Medical management without surgery (P < .001), embolic stroke as a presenting symptom (P = .004), and fungal pathogen (P = .005) were risk factors for early-phase death. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (P = .002) was a risk factor for late-phase death (Table E6).

Survival (A) and instantaneous risk of death (B) after diagnosis of proximal thoracic aortic graft infection. 95% confidence limits denoted by dashed lines.

Discussion

Key Findings

Proximal thoracic aortic graft infections tend to present within the first 3 years after implantation and are successfully treated with a combination of antibiotics and surgery, including graft excision with in situ reconstruction. Allografts decrease risk of reinfection compared with prosthetic conduits. Using this approach, patients can be treated with low operative mortality and occurrence of reinfection (Figure 4).

Diagnostic Considerations

Prompt diagnosis is critical for effective treatment of graft infection. A high index of suspicion for graft infection is required due to ambiguous presenting symptoms such as malaise and pain. Absence of fever should not eliminate graft infection from the differential because one-quarter of our patients were afebrile. Suspicion should be particularly high within 3 years of initial graft implant because this would have captured >50% of patients in our cohort.

Aortic valve infection is commonly associated with graft infections, and the graft often requires replacement with active valve endocarditis. Without clear aortic valve involvement, changes in periaortic collections on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging should raise suspicion. For this reason, it is important to get baseline postoperative imaging after aortic replacement. Beyond the early postoperative phase, metabolic imaging with positron emission tomography-computed tomography may help confirm the diagnosis.

Nonsurgical Management

Upon diagnosis, all patients should receive tailored antibiotic therapy, guided by an infectious disease specialist.2 Patients with early infection who experience rapid clinical improvement, have patent grafts without pseudoaneurysm or fistula, and no evidence of valve involvement may achieve graft sterilization with antibiotics alone, but this is rare.5,6,15,16 With this strategy, we recommend at least 6 weeks of parenteral antibiotics, close follow-up with an infectious disease specialist to tailor the oral suppression/prophylaxis regimen.2 This approach is only recommended for patients with a complete response to antibiotics, or are not surgical candidates.

Patients deemed unsuitable surgical candidates should be reassessed by the surgical team throughout their hospital stay. Mortality is high in these patients, even if they are discharged to a long-term acute care facility. However, some may be reconsidered later for definitive surgery if they recover.17

Surgical Management: Graft Replacement

Extensive debridement of infected or necrotic tissue, excision of graft, and in situ reconstruction with an allograft is our preferred approach. We use tissue flaps sparingly, usually in patients with aortoesophageal or aortobronchial fistulae to buttress the repair or in patients with significant dead space after evacuation of infected mediastinal hematomas. We occasionally use multiple allografts, bovine pericardial tube grafts, or a rifampin-soaked prosthetic graft for arch reconstruction when a single allograft is not long enough to reach the distal anastomosis. We use prosthetic graft without an allograft in rare situations: patients without signs of active infection intraoperatively, typically after extended preoperative parenteral antibiotics; earlier in the study timeframe, patients who required total arch replacement. Recently, we have been more aggressive about using all allografts and bovine pericardial tubes for arch repairs. Allografts may offer improved freedom from reinfection versus prosthetic material,18,19 although this remains a subject of debate. Comparisons of allografts and prosthetic implants are scant and rarely account for differences in patient risk profiles.20 Higher prevalence of root replacement, prosthetic valve endocarditis, and aortic abscess in patients who receive allografts, suggest a general bias toward allografts in patients with invasive endocarditis.20 This is consistent with our own practice but undermines comparison of allograft and prosthetic conduits.

The flexibility of allografts makes them optimally suited to reconstruct the left ventricular outflow tract or aortic anulus after extensive debridement. When tissue debridement disrupts the aorto-mitral curtain, the anterior mitral leaflet of the allograft can be used to close the defect and anchor the patient's anterior mitral anulus.21 When an arch repair is required, a tissue-only repair can be achieved in a few ways.

Surgical Management: Graft Sparing

Sparing the infected graft has limited application in managing proximal aortic graft infection. In our series, 10 out of 14 patients who underwent a graft-sparing approach already had an allograft from a prior surgery for aortic valve endocarditis. We recommend graft-sparing procedures when graft excision is not feasible due to a patient's comorbid state to improve control of active infection without the need for full cardiopulmonary bypass and reconstruction. Patients with deep sternal wound infection complicated by graft infection within 1 to 3 months of surgery may also be amenable to a graft-sparing approach with sternal debridement.1,7,22

The Cleveland Clinic Treatment Care Path

A flowchart summarizing our clinical decision-making process with these critically ill patients is demonstrated in Figure 5. Patients are initiated on broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, later tailored to the causal pathogen. Rarely, patients may continue with medical management alone. Otherwise, patients with physiologic reserve undergo surgical treatment with graft excision and allograft replacement, except in extenuating circumstances such as patients with severe sepsis or major comorbidities unable to tolerate complete excision, for whom graft sparing strategies may be pursued.

Limitations

As a tertiary care center, patients are referred to Cleveland Clinic at various time points in their treatment of graft infection. The cumulative incidence of infection after previous surgery was accelerated in our cohort due to 39 patients who had prior surgery for aortic valve endocarditis or prosthetic graft infection prior to presentation at our center. Limited availability of records from the referring hospital made it challenging to determine all events before presentation. Our median follow-up was only 2.8 years due to patients presenting from out of state with this unique problem and nearly 30% mortality by 5 years. Our analysis does not include how evolution of management techniques during the study period influenced outcomes.

Conclusions

Proximal thoracic aortic graft infections often present similarly to endocarditis, with fever and chest pain, but may have a more indolent course that requires a high index of suspicion and additional imaging for early diagnosis. We achieved excellent outcomes with an aggressive surgical approach comprising radical resection, excision of the infected prosthesis, and in situ reconstruction. Allograft conduits are preferable for their ability to fill space following debridement and for their potentially protective effect against reinfection. In patients who cannot tolerate graft excision and replacement, a graft salvage approach is feasible, but carries a greater risk of reinfection.
