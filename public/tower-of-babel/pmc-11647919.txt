Observations of educational tourism utilizing dark tourism—emphasizing human interactions within recovering communities
Authors: William D Y McMichael
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs Fukushima grapples with ongoing challenges related to reputational damage, it is becoming increasingly imperative to establish an effective means for global audiences to access, comprehend, and support the region's recovery efforts. To achieve this, Fukushima Prefecture has been strategically organizing educational tours tailored for international visitors, operating under the umbrella of ``Hope Tourism.'' These tours aim to bring about positive change by dispelling negative perceptions, offering a nuanced understanding of Fukushima's ongoing revitalization, and fostering connections between visitors and local residents. This study aims to contribute to the understanding of such tourism by providing an overview of Hope Tourism and addressing the reputational challenges faced by the prefecture. It will illustrate how experiences in Dark Tourism can effectively reshape a region's place identity, and serve as a proactive measure to alter perceptions of visitors to disaster-affected communities. Additionally, the study will analyze the learning outcomes of Hope Tourism experiences through the Fukushima Ambassadors Program, a short-term study abroad initiative provided by Fukushima University since 2012, which incorporates elements of Dark Tourism. Through these analyses, this study seeks to offer insights into the potential of educational tourism as a tool for fostering global support and understanding in the face of reputational challenges.

INTRODUCTION

The initiation of ocean discharges in August 2023 of water treated through the Advanced Liquid Processing System off the coast of Fukushima Prefecture served as a poignant reminder of the ongoing challenge of reputational damage confronting the region. Despite assurances from international organizations such as the International Atomic Energy Agency and support from various nations validating the safety of such discharges, formidable opposition from within Japan and neighboring countries resulted in the reinstatement of export restrictions of seafood from Fukushima and widespread global protests against the releases by civil groups. The persistent nature of this backlash poses a significant concern for the Fukushima fishing industry, which started its transition toward fully resumed operations in 2021 following a decade of trial fishing projects. While apprehensions regarding the environmental impact of the discharges may eventually diminish through continuous and transparent water quality testing, there remains a lasting sense of unease regarding more intricate challenges on the horizon. These challenges include issues associated with the transportation beyond the prefecture and potential reuse of over 14 million tons of soil generated from decontamination efforts. Of even greater concern are the procedures for the removal of spent fuel and molten fuel debris from the compromised nuclear reactors and the subsequent storage of such highly contaminated waste on-site. The complexity and potential risks associated with these aspects warrant careful consideration and proactive management strategies to mitigate further negative repercussions on the region’s environmental and socio-economic landscape.

As Fukushima grapples with such ongoing challenges, it becomes increasingly imperative to establish an effective means for global audiences to access, comprehend and support the region’s recovery efforts. Notably, researchers such as Sekiya [1] emphasize the importance of adopting a multifaceted approach, encompassing careful and accessible information dissemination beyond the specific water release issue to address the broader situation in Fukushima. Furthermore, the evaluation of the impact of such information dissemination is deemed crucial in mitigating reputational damage. To achieve this, the prefecture is strategically organizing educational tours tailored for international visitors, operating under the umbrella of ‘Hope Tourism’. These tours aim to bring about positive change by dispelling negative perceptions, offering a nuanced understanding of Fukushima’s ongoing revitalization and fostering connections between visitors and local residents. Aligned with the concept of Dark Tourism [3], Hope Tourism serves as a proactive measure to alter perceptions of visitors to disaster-affected communities. This study aims to contribute to the understanding of such tourism by providing an overview of Hope Tourism and addressing the reputational challenges faced by the prefecture. It will illustrate how experiences in Dark Tourism can effectively reshape a region’s place identity. HOPE TOURISM AND FUKUSHIMA’S REPUTATIONAL DAMAGE

Despite extensive government efforts to communicate the situation in Fukushima to the global audience, the negative image associated with the region has proven resistant to improvement on a worldwide scale. Ongoing concerns, particularly related to food safety and potential health risks from radiation persist as evidenced by a 2019 study is presented in Fig. 1. Conducted over 3 years on university students living in six different countries/regions, the study revealed heightened concerns about Fukushima’s food safety, particularly in countries significantly affected by the disasters, either geographically (e.g. China and South Korea) or politically (e.g. Germany). A 2022 survey across 10 nations conducted by Sekiya [1], similarly highlighted the increased anxiety regarding radioactive contamination in food products from Fukushima, especially in countries such as South Korea, Russia and the USA when compared to a similar study conducted in 2017. This suggests that governmental efforts to disseminate information about Fukushima’s food safety have not proven effective. Scholars have explored factors contributing to this persistent resistance, with Sekiya identifying distrust toward information provided by the national government and a lack of information accessibility as reasons for the anxiety. Kainuma [4] observed that the sophisticated and high-context nature of Fukushima’s issues has led to a decline in an accurate understanding and a loss of interest. McMichael [5] speculates that a ‘Nuclear Fear’ [6] rooted in the Cold War period was fueled by sensational media images at the time of the accident, contributing to a pervasive state of fear and doubt toward any progress made. Adding complexity to the situation, an excessive emphasis on safety can paradoxically lead to an increase in doubts and a loss of credibility of information. In 2023, Kikuchi [7] introduced the concept of ‘Fuhyo Kagaisha’ (perpetrators of reputational damage), a label assigned to political, civil and media groups/members who exploit Fukushima’s negative image for individual or political gain. Kikuchi’s analysis elucidated how fear mongering from such entities persist in disseminating a substantial volume of misinformation and stigmatization online, impeding the public’s understanding of the situation in Fukushima.

Consequently, balanced and transparent face-to-face communication from reliable sources has long been deemed an essential component of rebranding Fukushima’s image. Responding to such needs, the Fukushima government introduced Hope Tourism in 2016 as a potential means to disseminate credible information and cultivate support through hands-on tours. Initially part of an initiative to revive the domestic tourism industry in disaster-affected prefectures, the concept of Hope Tourism has today evolved into a tourism-based pedagogy for leveraging unique insights gained from Fukushima’s experience with the 11 March disasters. Through direct observation, dialog and experiential learning, Hope Tourism aims to instill a sense of hope among participants, who then bring about hope back to the regions they visit by spreading word about their situation globally. Hope Tourism incorporates three crucial input elements in its tours: ‘seeing’ (hands-on tours of disaster-affected areas), ‘hearing’ (dialogs with residents involved in recovery projects) and ‘feeling’ (the emotions felt by tourists exposed to revitalization efforts). Additionally, there are two crucial output elements: ‘applying’ (encouraging participants to apply their learnings from the tour to other global issues through reflection sessions held at the end of the tour) and ‘sharing’ (actively sharing experiences through social media and word of mouth). Originally targeting only domestic travelers, Hope Tourism has gained popularity in Japanese high schools, with over 9000 participants mainly on school trips from across Japan in 2022. Plans to train interpretation guides for inbound tourists began in 2018, aiming to leverage international tourism to the region as a tool to overcome reputational damage, with the 2020 Tokyo Olympics originally set as its initial start target. However, these plans were temporarily derailed by the COVID-19 pandemic. Today, the goal is to offer these tours commercially by the end of the fiscal year 2023.

For communities with existing tourism-based economies, where tourism contributed to identities and attachments before a disaster, a revival of tourism becomes a natural part of recovery strategies [8]. However, for communities like those in the Futaba region where Hope Tourism takes place, international tourism was non-existent before the disasters. Therefore, if not done carefully, the introduction of tourism-related changes in the socio-economic and physical landscape may hinder the already complicated processes of rebuilding a sense of place attachment for the reviving communities [8]. Hence, it is crucial for Hope Tourism to draw insights from successful instances of Dark Tourism in the past that have garnered local support and proved positive changes to the identity of the region through inbound tourism, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The forthcoming section of this paper will explore the key elements needed by examining the concept of Dark Tourism and how it leverages changes to the place identity of disaster-affected regions.

DARK TOURISM AND PLACE IDENTITY

The concept of Dark Tourism, introduced by Scottish tourism scholars John Lennon and Malcolm Foley in their co-authored book ‘Dark Tourism: The Attraction of Death and Disaster’ in 2000, designates tourism focused on visiting sites associated with negative legacies, such as death, disasters and atrocities resulting from historical tragedies. While the practice of visiting such sites is not novel, the conceptualization and systematic study of these experiences have enriched our understanding of visitor motivations, experiences and learning outcomes.

However, as scholarly attention to Dark Tourism expanded, nuanced perspectives beyond this framework have emerged. Research has delved into the multifaceted impact of Dark Tourism on disaster-affected regions, highlighting its role as a driver for economic recovery and its effective link to long-term destination image and branding recovery. For instance, Miller et al. [9] emphasize that the spotlight on the revival of affected areas during disasters enables tourism providers to repackage negative legacies under narratives such as ‘reconciliation’, ‘rebirth’, ‘new vitality’ and ‘recovery’. This process, conveyed by tourists, contributes to the revival of a destination brand with distinct originality compared to other regions. Lynch and Causevic [10] (termed this process as ‘Phoenix Tourism’, emphasizing tourism focused on recovery from disasters. They describe it as a phenomenon where tourism rises like a phoenix from the ashes of the negative history caused by disasters, with examples including Banda Aceh in Indonesia and New Orleans. Dunkley [11] and others argue that Dark Tourism enhances understanding, instigates a desire for further knowledge and promotes personal benefits, spanning emotional, cognitive and spiritual dimensions for visitors. These studies underscore how visitors’ interpretation of the ‘meaning’ associated with a place is actively influenced by intentional discourse and learning outcomes guided by tourism providers and guides acting as ‘gaze inducers’ [12].

Gaze inducers, a concept inspired by John Urry’s ‘tourist gaze’ from the 1990s, refer to stakeholders such as travel agencies, researchers, governments and NGOs who wield influence over the provision of Dark Tourism. Although controlling the sense and interpretation felt by visitors proves challenging due to the myriad ways visitors may feel about a location, a degree of control becomes feasible when there is a strong overarching narrative associated with the region or event. This is exemplified in cases such as Hiroshima’s Peace Tourism Initiative. Hiroshima City, promoting projects and facilities focusing on protecting, spreading and conveying the reality of the atomic bombing, launched the Peace Tourism Project in 2017 after former US President Obama’s visit in 2016 garnered heightened international interest. The project aimed to establish universal routes for international visitors to tour peace-related facilities beyond the iconic Atomic Bomb Dome and Peace Memorial Museum, constructing a framework for preserving memories and correlating the place identity of the city toward a theme of Peace. The implementation of this project is overseen by the Peace Tourism Promotion Council, a collaborative effort involving members from local NGOs, universities and interpreter guide organizations. Similarly, in New Orleans, post-disaster tours following Hurricane Katrina in 2005 initially focused on inspecting the damage and visiting emotionally charged locations. Despite global awareness efforts, these tours did not improve the region’s image and faced a backlash from affected communities, leading to access restrictions and regulations. To address this, major companies collaborated to provide tours with a more resident-based storyline, incorporating themes of regeneration and lessons learned aligned with Sustainable Development Goals by including visits to exhibits created by civil groups, outlining failures in disaster prevention and environmental protection by the government as the main causes of the tragedies. Guides, often with disaster experience, began to collaborate and play a crucial role in maintaining the authenticity of these messages, shifting the focus from tangible remnants to broader themes of recovery and lessons [13].

In this context, a crucial element becomes the facilitation of communication and cooperation between gaze inducers and local communities. White and Frew [14] caution against Dark Tourism held under a one-sided gaze, highlighting the potential for local friction and the selective presentation of facts influenced by the gaze-builder’s affiliations. Within the proposed conceptual framework, Hope Tourism is classified as a variant of Dark Tourism, aiming for tourists to develop place images associated with self-growth through the three-step process of seeing, listening and applying. Positioned in close proximity to other forms of Dark Tourism, this suggests that Hope Tourism in Fukushima may undergo a gradual evolution over time. It could transition toward Peace Tourism, dedicated to mourning and inheriting the sadness associated with historical tragedies. Alternatively, it may evolve toward Phoenix Tourism, emphasizing the transmission of lessons learned to the prevention of future disasters.

LEARNING OUTCOMES OF DARK TOURISM EXPERIENCES

In the subsequent segment, the study will delve into the learning outcomes of international tourists participating in such educational tours. Given the absence of large-scale Hope Tourism initiatives for international tourists, the analysis will focus on the Fukushima Ambassadors Program (hereinafter, referred to as the Ambassadors Program), a short-term study abroad initiative organized by the author. Launched in 2012, the Ambassadors Program is an 8-day study abroad experience available to students from partner universities of Fukushima University globally. Similar to Hope Tourism, the program aims to foster a profound understanding of Fukushima’s current challenges among international students. It achieves this by conducting educational tours in disaster-affected regions, aiming to cultivate heightened empathy with residents and equip participants with the knowledge and skills necessary to communicate Fukushima’s complexities globally. As of December 2023, the Ambassadors Program has been implemented 15 times, engaging 226 students from 16 universities in 8 countries. Additionally, over 600 students from Fukushima University have participated as volunteers or as part of a course requirement.

Within the Ambassadors Program framework, Fukushima University serves as the gaze inducer, actively cultivating close partnerships with various community groups to establish an organizational structure for program operations, as illustrated in Fig. 4. A central goal of the Ambassadors Program is to effectively disseminate accurate knowledge about the complex issues surrounding Fukushima’s revitalization. The program achieves this through a combination of lectures, site visits and project-based activities tailored to specific topics. The itinerary is organized into three sections: Fukushima’s past, present and future, encompassing both the positive (light) and negative (shadow) aspects of Fukushima’s revitalization. Tours commence with understanding the initial impacts of the disasters, progressing to current topics such as decommissioning and community reopening, and conclude with visits and opinion exchanges focusing on applying the gained knowledge to future 21st century issues. Much like Hope Tourism, the program places a significant emphasis on human interactions within recovering communities in the region.

Organizational structure of Fukushima Ambassadors Program [15].

To assess participants’ knowledge acquisition of Fukushima’s situation, the program administers a knowledge test comprising a total of 50 questions to international students before and after their participation. Figure 5 summarizes the results of tests conducted in 2017 and 2018. In the pre-program stage, participants from overseas exhibited lower correct answer rates for questions related to social issues in Fukushima (such as disaster-related deaths and reputational damage), and, as expected, their awareness of food safety was also low. However, post-program improvements in correct answer rates were particularly seen regarding questions about radiation safety and food safety. These results suggest that educational tourism can be an effective and reproducible means for participating students to comprehend complex themes.

Moreover, surveys conducted following the Ambassadors Program suggest that Fukushima’s place identity and destination image are effectively being revived. Both the follow-up surveys and the post-return surveys conducted in August 2018 targeting participants from 2012 to 2017 indicate that over 90% of international student participants (n = 102) responded affirmatively, stating that their image of Fukushima had changed. They indicated that their perceptions of the nuclear accident as an isolated event were transformed into a narrative centered around the vibrant human recovery process. Other transformations include impressions that contradicted the reported situation entirely, perceiving Fukushima as a place opposite to the envisioned ‘land of demise’, and realizing that the place was vastly different from the portrayed images. Many participants expressed that their initial negative impressions were overturned, and Fukushima was perceived as a land unfairly depicted by media. Additionally, some participants (15.56%) mentioned that their awareness now expanded beyond the nuclear accident to understand the magnitude of the compound disaster and its effects. Consequently, they developed an image of the location as significantly affected by the earthquake and tsunami, more so than they had perceived before participating in the program.

Open-ended responses in the same survey include statements such as, ‘Prior to participation, I possessed a scientific understanding of the incident. However, through the program, engaging in not only scientific but also emotional, social, psychological, and cultural analyses allowed me to perceive Fukushima holistically’ (Participant from program conducted in January 2014), ‘The image of Fukushima transformed from one of devastation caused by tsunami and nuclear power to that of hope, strength, and community’ (Participant from program conducted in August 2016) and ‘This program opened my eyes not only to the issues in Fukushima but also to what we can learn from Fukushima. It made me aware of larger issues such as the fragility of life and our roles in shaping society’ (Participant from program conducted in August 2019). These testimonials underscore a prevailing sentiment among participants who have developed an expansive perspective and heightened empathy toward the learning and challenges in Fukushima.

Finally, the Ambassadors Program demonstrates that international participants learning and working together can lead to extremely positive outcomes in their development of intercultural skills. Post-program evaluations, as depicted in Table 1, show an increase in their knowledge of society, customs and culture of other countries as well as an enhancement in critical thinking. International students reported an increase in their awareness of global issues such as the environment and poverty, ability to empathize with others and media literacy. These outcomes align with the OECD’s global competence model [16] that emphasizes the importance of addressing local, global and cross-cultural issues, understanding others’ perspectives and worldviews and recognizing the value of these perspectives.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study has delved into the background and effects of educational tourism initiatives designed to address Fukushima’s reputational challenges. By examining the Fukushima Ambassadors Program within the broader context of Hope Tourism, the study explored the potential of educational tourism in reshaping perceptions and fostering global support. The findings underscore the positive outcomes of such educational initiatives, revealing a notable shift in participants’ perceptions. Through Dark Tourism experiences, participants not only shed pre-conceived notions but also developed an expansive perspective and heightened empathy toward Fukushima’s challenges. The Ambassadors Program demonstrated that international collaboration, aimed at overcoming reputational damage, led to positive outcomes in participants development of intercultural skills, critical thinking and awareness of global issues.

As Fukushima continues to face intricate challenges on its path to recovery, the insights gleaned here offer valuable guidance for the strategic design of future Hope Tourism initiatives aimed at international tourists. These initiatives, that commenced in fiscal year 2023, can play a pivotal role in dispelling negative perceptions and fostering a more nuanced understanding of Fukushima’s ongoing revitalization. By leveraging the lessons learned from past Dark Tourism projects, Fukushima can actively shape its narrative, engage global audiences and navigate the complexities of reputational recovery. In doing so, Fukushima can emerge not only as a symbol of resilience but also as a model for regions confronting similar challenges worldwide.
