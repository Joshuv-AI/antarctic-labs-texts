Spatially resolved structure-function mapping of the anterior cruciate ligament-bone interface
Authors: Huizhi Wang, Qingqing Yang, Qinyi Shi, Kaixin He, Wenxin Feng, Li Li, Cheng-Kung Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo investigate the spatial distribution of structural features at the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-bone interface and examine their relationship with local tensile properties and stress distribution, thereby elucidating the structure-function relationship at this critical soft-hard tissue junction.MethodsHigh-resolution micro-CT was employed to obtain 3D imaging of 17 porcine ACL-femur interface specimens. Structural features were analyzed across different functional regions, specifically comparing the anteromedial (AM) and posterolateral (PL) bundles, as well as the direct and indirect insertion sites. Parameters assessed included macroscopic area, cortical tissue thickness, fibre angle, bone volume fraction (BVF), and degree of anisotropy (DA) in trabecular bone. Additionally, tensile tests were conducted on six porcine specimens to determine the elastic moduli of different interface regions. Finite element analysis was conducted to investigate stress distribution across the ACL-femur interface during the gait cycle. Spatial variations in structural features were then compared with local tensile properties and stress levels to elucidate the structure-function relationship.ResultsSignificant differences in structural features, tensile moduli, and peak stress were observed among the functional regions of the ACL-femur interface. The spatial distribution of structural features closely mirrored the patterns of mechanical properties and stress. Regions exhibiting higher tensile moduli and experiencing greater peak stress demonstrated increased cortical tissue thickness and BVF (indirect > direct). Furthermore, regions with larger fibre angles showed higher trabecular DA (PL > AM; direct > indirect). Overall, the differences between the direct and indirect regions were more pronounced than those between the AM and PL bundles.ConclusionThe mechanical function of the ACL-bone interface is strongly associated with the spatial organization of its structural features. The indirect region differs significantly from the direct region in both structural and mechanical characteristics, highlighting region-specific adaptations for load transfer at the ligament-bone junction.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(11):1006–1015.Keywords: Ligament-bone interface, Micro-structure, Tensile property, Stress, Spatial distribution, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), ligament, femur, mechanical properties, trabecular bone, Micro-CT, Finite element analysis, elastic moduli, Young’s moduli, knee joint

Introduction

Rupture of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the most prevalent sports-related injuries,1 particularly during high-risk manoeuvres such as jump landing and pivot cut.2 The injured ACL is often replaced with a tendon graft through a procedure known as ACL reconstruction (ACLR), which aims to restore joint functionality. However, the graft-bone interface remains the mechanically weakest point following surgery and is susceptible to failure under tensile loading.3,4 Moreover, individuals who have undergone ACLR are at a higher risk of developing knee osteoarthritis compared to those without ACL injury,5,6 potentially due in part to inadequate restoration of the native anatomical structures and their biomechanical roles at the ligament-bone interface. A thorough understanding of both the macro- and microstructural characteristics, along with their associated mechanical functions, is essential for advancing surgical techniques and biomaterials aimed at enhancing the reconstruction or regeneration of this complex interface.

Previous studies have identified a graded transition in the ligament-bone interface, comprising the ligament proper, uncalcified fibrocartilage (UFC), calcified fibrocartilage (CFC), subchondral bone, and trabecular bone.7,8 These transitional zones are thought to facilitate stress transfer from soft to hard tissues, minimizing stress concentrations at the interface.9-11 However, the 3D spatial distribution of these microstructures remains poorly characterized. On a macroscopic level, the ACL-bone interface includes distinct functional regions, such as the direct and indirect insertions,12 and the anteromedial (AM) and posterolateral (PL) bundles.13 The direct insertion connects the ligament mid-substance to the bone and features varying fibre orientations relative to the bone surface. In contrast, the indirect insertion is a fan-shaped extension of the direct interface, where fibres are typically anchored to the bone (Figure 1b). Several studies have investigated the mechanical contributions of the direct and indirect insertions under anterior tibial loading at different knee flexion angles. While some findings suggest that the direct insertion bears a greater proportion of the load,14,15 another study has reported higher forces in the indirect insertion, highlighting the lack of consensus regarding the functional distinction between these two regions.16 The AM and PL bundles, named according to the locations of their tibial bone insertions,17 serve different roles at varying knee flexion angles.18 Studies comparing conventional single-bundle (SB) ACL reconstruction with anatomical SB or double-bundle (DB, which covers both the AM and PL bundle insertions) reconstruction have demonstrated that anatomical techniques provide better translational and rotational knee stability, although no significant differences in joint kinematics or clinical scores were observed between anatomical SB and DB reconstructions.19-22 Moreover, studies suggest that rotating the graft during ACL reconstruction may reduce tibiofemoral separation, better match the ACL’s natural fibre orientation, and improve graft isometry without compromising knee biomechanics.23 These findings highlight the importance of restoring native anatomical structures. However, while most studies have characterized the multimodal properties of the ACL-bone interface as a whole,24-26 comprehensive quantitative comparisons of macro- and microstructural features, tensile properties, and stress distributions across its distinct functional regions remain limited. As a result, little is known about how the interface is structurally adapted to accommodate tensile loads along the entire interface.

In this study, high-resolution micro-CT was used to visualize the 3D structure of the ACL-bone interface. Macroscopic area, cortical tissue thickness, fibre angle, bone volume fraction (BVF), and degree of anisotropy (DA) in trabecular bone were quantified across different functional regions (AM vs PL; direct vs indirect). Tensile tests were conducted to determine the elastic moduli of these regions. Additionally, finite element analysis was performed to assess stress distribution across the ACL-bone interface during the gait cycle. Differences in structural features among different regions were then compared with the differences in local tensile moduli and peak stress to elucidate the structure-function relationship at the ligament-bone interface. It was hypothesized that significant regional differences would exist in these characteristics, and that variations in structural features would correlate with local mechanical properties. This study may provide critical insights for enhancing the reconstruction and regeneration of ligament-bone interfaces, thereby contributing to improved restoration of joint function and reduced postoperative complications following ACLR.

Methods

A total of 17 porcine knees (Duroc-Landrace-Yorkshire crossbred, castrated, one year old, male, right) were obtained from a licensed abattoir and dissected for micro-CT imaging. Only male pigs were used in order to avoid potential data variability caused by sex differences.27-29 A previous study has demonstrated that castrated male Duroc-Landrace-Yorkshire crossbred pigs have dimensions and morphology of the knee joint and ACL that are very similar to those of humans.30 The project proposal is in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Supplementary Material) and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (202101260). The soft-tissues outside the joint capsule were first removed, the capsule was then opened, and the medial femoral condyle was removed to disclose the ACL-bone interface. The outline of the whole interface and the boundary between the direct and indirect regions were marked manually using a marker pen (Figure 1b). The bone tissue was trimmed along the interface outline, after which the sample was immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stored at -20°C. The samples were thawed for 24 hours at room temperature before use.

To achieve high-contrast imaging of both ligament and bone, the samples underwent a series of processing procedures (see Supplementary Material for details). The samples were then scanned using a micro-CT system (Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa; Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) at a resolution of 25 μm, 0.4× optical magnification, and an exposure time of 1.2 s at 60 kV and 5 W. The images were reconstructed using the XMReconstructor provided with the scanner. Dragonfly (ORS, Canada) was used for the structural analysis.

The reconstructed 3D models of the interface samples were segmented into four functional regions based on prominent synovial membrane boundaries (Figure 1c): direct interface of the AM bundle (AM_D), indirect interface of the AM bundle (AM_InD), direct interface of the PL bundle (PL_D), and indirect interface of the PL bundle (PL_InD). The segmentation was validated by comparing with the manual dissection-based separation of the functional regions. The contours of each region were extracted, and the areas were measured in Dragonfly.

Histological analysis is considered the gold standard for observing 2D microstructural features of the ligament-bone interface.31 Micro-CT images were compared with histological sections to enable precise characterization of the microstructural features (Supplementary Material).

The entire interface was divided into a 10 × 10 coordinate grid, with lines plotted parallel and perpendicular to the distal margin line, which was defined by connecting the distal corners of the interface outline (Figure 2a). Each intersection point was projected onto the tide line (the boundary between the UFC and CFC zones) along a cross-sectional plane that was perpendicular to the tide line, for the purpose of measuring fibre angle and cortical tissue thickness.

Characterization of fibre angles. a) Schematic showing how the fibre angle was measured. b) to d) Distribution of anterior-posterior (AP) fibre angles in distinct functional regions. e) to g) Distribution of proximal-distal (PD) fibre angles in distinct functional regions.

Fibre angles in the anterior-posterior (AP) and proximal-distal (PD) directions were measured as the angle between the fibre orientation and the tangent to the tide line at each intersection point, along the AP and PD planes, respectively (Figure 2a).

Cortical tissue thickness (including the CFC and subchondral bone) was measured (Figure 3a) as the distance from the projected intersection point to the boundary between the subchondral bone and the trabecular bone.

Characterization of cortical tissue thickness, bone volume fraction (BVF), and degree of anisotropy (DA) in trabecular bone. a) Schematic showing how the cortical tissue thickness, BVF, and DA were measured. b) to d) Distribution of cortical tissue thickness in distinct functional regions. e) to g) Distribution of BVF in distinct functional regions. h) to j) Distribution of DA in distinct functional regions.

As shown in Figure 3a, four cylindrical volumes were inscribed within the four functional regions of the ACL-femur interface. In both the AP and PD planes passing through the central axis of each cylinder, the axis was adjusted to be perpendicular to the line connecting the points where the cylinder intersected the tide line. Along this axis, the base of each cylinder was positioned 2 mm above the boundary between the subchondral and trabecular bone. BVF and DA of the trabecular bone were quantified in each region using Dragonfly software (Comet Technologies, Canada).

Six porcine ACL-bone interface samples (Duroc-Landrace-Yorkshire crossbred, one year old, male, right) were prepared as cuboids with 5 × 5 square cross-sections (Figure 4a) for tensile testing. The tests were conducted using a miniature tensile machine (Orthotek Lab, China) (Figure 4b). The position of the bone portion within the clamp was adjusted to align the long axis of the ligament fibres with the direction of tensile loading, thus providing a more realistic representation of the mechanical properties of the entire structure.32 A 0.5 N preload was first applied, followed by 15 loading-unloading cycles between 0 and 0.15 mm at a rate of 0.0331 mm/s. Subsequently, the sample was stretched at the same rate until failure or until reaching the sensor’s maximum capacity of 50 N. The resulting load-displacement curve is shown in Figure 4c. Young’s moduli was calculated from the linear region of the curve using the measured cross-sectional area and initial length of each specimen.

Characterization of Young’s moduli in distinct functional regions using tensile testing. a) Sample preparation schematic. b) Tensile testing set-up. c) Representative force-displacement curve and method for calculating Young’s moduli. d) to f) Statistical comparison of Young’s moduli between functional regions.

To investigate stress distribution at the ACL-femur interface during walking, a finite element model of a porcine knee joint was developed and validated both anatomically and biomechanically (Supplementary Material) (Figure 5a). The ACL was divided into four bundles (AM_D, AM_InD, PL_D, PL_InD), with Young’s moduli assigned based on the tensile testing results. Anatomical dimensions within the model were measured and compared with micro-CT data, confirming that all values fall within the range of experimental measurements. A loading condition simulating porcine gait was applied,33 and peak von Mises stress in each functional region and stress distribution at the ACL-femur interface were recorded.

Finite element analysis of stress distribution at the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-femur interface during the gait cycle. a) Finite element model of a porcine knee joint. b) ACL tension at 30°, 60°, and 90° of knee flexion: model predictions versus published data.34 c) Stress distribution across the ACL-femur interface during the gait cycle. d) to f) Comparison of peak von Mises stress among distinct functional regions.

Statistical analysis

Pre-experimental measurements from three samples were used for a power analysis to estimate the required sample size using PASS 15 (PASS, Netherlands). A sample size of 17 was estimated to detect a 0.13 mm difference in cortical tissue thickness with α of 0.05 and power of 90%. The average value of all intersection points within each functional region was calculated to represent the overall value for this region. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Paired t-tests were conducted using SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM, USA) to evaluate statistical differences among distinct functional regions. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set. Post-hoc power analysis was performed to calculate the real power supporting the conclusions.

Results

Macroscopic area of distinct functional regions

The average areas of the distinct functional regions are shown in Figure 1d. The AM bundle occupied 18% more area than the PL bundle, while the direct region was 6% larger than the indirect region (Figures 1e and 1f). Among the four regions (AM_D, AM_InD, PL_D, PL_InD), the indirect region of the PL bundle had the smallest area.

Spatial distribution of fibre angle

Figures 2b and 2e show the distribution of fibre angles in the AP and PD directions, respectively. In the AP direction, the PL bundle exhibited a significantly larger fibre angle (oriented more posteriorly) than the AM bundle (p < 0.001, power = 1.000). In the PD direction, the direct interface showed a larger fibre angle (oriented more proximally) than the indirect interface (p = 0.003, power = 0.991).

Spatial distribution of cortical tissue thickness

A clear heterogeneous distribution of cortical tissue thickness was observed across the ACL-femur interface (Figure 3b). Statistical analysis showed that the thickness was significantly greater at the indirect interface compared to the direct interface (p = 0.002, power = 0.996) (Figure 3d). No significant difference was found between the AM and PL bundles (p = 0.854) (Figure 3c).

Spatial distribution of BVF and DA in trabecular bone

Figures 3e and 3h show the distributions of BVF and DA in trabecular bone across the four functional regions. BVF was significantly higher in the indirect region than in the direct region (p = 0.007, power = 0.823) (Figure 3g), while no significant difference was found between the AM and PL bundles (p = 0.106) (Figure 3f). DA was significantly greater in the direct region compared to the indirect region (p = 0.003, power = 0.948) (Figure 3j), and in the PL bundle compared to the AM bundle (p < 0.001, power = 1.000) (Figure 3i).

Tensile moduli of distinct functional regions

The average Young’s moduli of the AM_D, AM_InD, PL_D, and PL_InD regions were 7.21 MPa, 9.49 MPa, 6.34 MPa, and 14.46 MPa, respectively (Figure 4d). The indirect interface exhibited a significantly higher Young’s moduli than the direct interface, and the PL bundle showed a greater moduli than the AM bundle (Figures 4e and 4f).

Stress distribution at the ACL-femur interface during the gait cycle

Throughout the gait cycle, the peak von Mises stress was highest in the indirect interface of the PL bundle and lowest in the direct interface of the AM bundle (Figures 5c and 5d). Across the entire cycle, the indirect interface consistently exhibited higher peak stress than the direct interface, and the PL bundle showed greater peak stress than the AM bundle (Figures 5e and 5f).

Structure-function relationship across spatial regions

When comparing indirect and direct regions, peak stress and Young’s moduli were closely aligned with macroscopic area, cortical tissue thickness, and BVF (Figure 6a). Both parameters increased with cortical tissue thickness and BVF (indirect > direct), but decreased with macroscopic area. Differences between the AM and PL bundles were less pronounced than those between the indirect and direct interfaces.

AP, anterior-posterior; InD, indirect; PD, proximal-distal; PL, posterolateral.

The spatial distribution of fibre angles corresponded closely with the trabecular DA. In the PL bundle, fibres were oriented more posteriorly and showed greater trabecular DA compared to the AM bundle. Similarly, the direct interface exhibited more proximally oriented fibres and higher trabecular DA than the indirect interface. An integrated structure-function relationship at the ACL-femur interface is summarized in Figure 6c.

Discussion

This study found significant regional differences in structural features, tensile properties, and stress levels across the ACL-femur interface (AM vs PL; direct vs indirect). The spatial distribution of structural parameters closely mirrored the pattern of mechanical behaviour. Regions with higher tensile moduli and stress levels exhibited thicker cortical tissue, greater BVF (indirect > direct), and smaller macroscopic area. Regions with larger fibre angles also showed higher trabecular DA (PL > AM; direct > indirect). These multimodal distinctions were more pronounced between direct and indirect regions than between AM and PL bundles. Collectively, these findings highlight a robust structure-function relationship at the ligament-bone interface.

A consistent spatial pattern was observed across cortical tissue thickness, BVF, inverse macroscopic area, tensile moduli, and peak stress when comparing the direct and indirect interfaces. According to Wolff’s law,35 tissues adapt to mechanical loading through structural remodelling. Norman et al36 reported significantly thicker cortical tissue at the ACL femoral insertion than in adjacent regions of the intercondylar wall, likely due to the higher tensile stress experienced at the ligament enthesis. We hypothesize that the indirect interface experiences higher stress due to its smaller load-bearing area compared to the direct interface, which drives adaptive remodelling in cortical tissue thickness and BVF, ultimately resulting in increased tensile moduli. In addition, our findings are consistent with those of Luetkemeyer et al,37 who reported that the highest strain was located at the direct insertion site when the ACL was under tension, likely due to the lower stiffness of this region.

Fibre angle was found to be correlated with trabecular DA. The PL bundle exhibited a larger AP fibre angle than the AM bundle, reflecting a more posterior orientation. Similarly, fibres at the direct interface were more proximally oriented than those at the indirect interface. Given that ACL fibres originate at the femoral footprint and extend anteriorly and distally to insert the tibia, this fibre orientation suggests that the PL bundle and direct interface may experience more rotational loads than the AM bundle and the indirect interface. These loading conditions may drive opposing fibre orientation relative to the overall ACL direction. This aligns with a previous report indicating that fibres at the direct insertion adapt to mid-substance motion, whereas those at the indirect insertion remain fixed.30 Additionally, the AM bundle was reported to primarily restrict anterior-posterior tibial translation, while the PL bundle mainly restricts rotational instability.36 Furthermore, Skelley et al38 documented more uniform fibre alignment in the AM bundle than in the PL bundle, consistent with our observations. Suzuki et al39 proposed that ligament tension transmitted to bone may be reflected in the orientation of the underlying trabeculae, which may indicate the direction of force at the ligament-bone interface. Accordingly, regions with larger fibre angles may be subjected to more complex, multidirectional loading, resulting in greater trabecular DA. The above findings suggest that artificial ACL grafts should be designed to reflect regional differences in fibre angles, allowing greater rotational mobility in the PL and direct interfaces, while preserving more fixed, uniaxial alignment in the AM and indirect portions.

The observed structural and mechanical distinctions between the indirect and direct interfaces were more pronounced than those between the AM and PL bundles. While macroscopic comparisons between the AM and PL bundles at both the mid-substance40-42 and entheses43,44 levels have been widely investigated, the comparisons between the indirect and direct insertions remain rare. Current ACL reconstruction techniques often focus on restoring the AM and PL bundles at the direct interface, while the indirect interface is frequently overlooked due to its lack of direct connection to the ligament mid-substance.45 However, our findings suggest that the indirect interface may play a crucial role in load transfer and injury prevention, and should be adequately addressed during surgery. Specifically, techniques such as ACL stump-preserving repair or reconstruction may help to retain the indirect interface.46 In addition, future strategies could involve designing artificial grafts that incorporate the indirect region, developing tissue engineering approaches, or utilizing pharmacological therapies to reconstruct or regenerate this area, thereby better replicating the native anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of the ACL entheses and ultimately improving surgical outcomes.47 Furthermore, while most reconstruction strategies rely primarily on anatomical footprint for tunnel placement,48 this study highlights that optimal reconstruction must account not only for macroscopic anatomy but also for the underlying microstructure and mechanical function of each region. Advancements in high-resolution imaging and in vivo biomechanical assessment may enable more personalized surgical planning and facilitate the development of functionally guided reconstruction techniques.

In conclusion, the ACL-femur interface exhibits distinct spatial variation in structural and mechanical features across its functional regions, with the spatial distribution of structural features closely aligning with the regional mechanical behaviours. The differences between the direct and indirect regions are more pronounced than those between the AM and PL bundles, with the indirect interface exhibiting higher BVF, thicker cortical tissue, greater tensile moduli, and higher stress levels, suggesting that the indirect interface may play a critical role in load-bearing and injury prevention, and should be carefully reconstructed or regenerated to better restore joint function.
