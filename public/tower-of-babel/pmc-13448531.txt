Counting men, missing women: a qualitative exploration of barriers underlying sex disparity in death registration in selected districts of Bangladesh
Authors: Mrittika Barua, Protyasha Ghosh, Avijit Saha, Muhammad Abdul Mannan, Md Jahedul Islam, Shahrear Alam, Saima Mehjabeen, Syeda Tahmina Ahmed, Sayema Akter, Romain Santon, Anushka Mangharam, Moyeen Uddin, Nazrul Islam, Malabika Sarker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccurate and timely reporting of deaths and causes of death is crucial evidence for health policy and planning. However, in practice, there is a low level of completeness of the death registration process in low-income settings such as Bangladesh and more so among females than males. An in-depth exploration of the barriers to low registration among females is needed in Bangladesh, a country bound by unequal gender norms and practices.MethodsThe study relied on a qualitative approach and consisted of 33 key informant interviews with field-level implementers, policymakers and 13 focus group discussions among beneficiaries in eight subdistricts with low and high sex disparity.ResultsThe findings show that poor awareness about the registration process, inheritance-driven need and missing accurate supporting information hinder and delay death registration. Deep-rooted male-dominated inheritance practices contribute to lower registration of female deaths. This is further compounded by complex registration procedures, including the requirement for a pre-existing birth certificate and difficulties in obtaining or verifying supporting documents. In addition, awareness activities often emphasise inheritance-related benefits, unintentionally reinforcing the perception that death registration is more important for men than for women.ConclusionsPolicies need to be strengthened and adapted to improve death registration completeness by addressing the barriers. More gender-sensitive, stronger and context-appropriate policy reforms, along with better implementation and oversight, are needed to improve the completeness of female death registration.

Introduction

A well-established civil registration and vital statistics system produces timely data for mortality statistics.12 Accurate numbers of deaths and cause-of-death records are primary inputs for governance and development, as mortality statistics derived from these records can provide critical evidence for health policy and planning.3 Death records can ensure that administrative procedures accurately reflect current records.4 Using the data provided by death certificates (age, sex, cause of death) is also critical in allowing health officials and decision-makers to identify health threats and high-risk populations,5 in addition to providing information on potential hereditary health conditions to the family of the deceased.6 More recently, death registration data have proven beneficial when calculating excess mortality from the COVID-19 pandemic.6 Thus, the importance of recording deaths and completing death certificates cannot be understated.

Globally, there appear to be no disparities in the completion of death registration between the sexes, as shown by a multi-nation report based on 112 countries.7 However, the report presented female completeness as lower than that of males in 42 countries, and lowest in Myanmar, followed by India, Libya, Zambia and Nepal.7 This is aligned with Asia and the Pacific, where a disproportionate number of individuals, mainly women and children, do not have their deaths registered,6 thus leading to women being invisible or missing in the record. This aspect of being missed or invisible is guided by ‘The Missing Women Phenomenon’, coined by Nobel laureate Amartya Sen, who exclusively focuses on excess female deaths due to structural disadvantage (discrimination and negligence of women). We acknowledge that unregistered deaths do not necessarily suggest excess deaths. Sen’s formulation concerns the demographic consequences of gender discrimination, that is, excess female mortality, whereas the present finding concerns administrative consequences, that is, the systematic under-recording of female deaths. While these are analytically distinct phenomena, the underlying mechanism, that is, structural gender disadvantage determining whose life or death is counted, remains conceptually aligned with Sen. A closer look at the death registration in subcontinent countries such as Nepal (73% vs 65%), Myanmar (59% males vs 45% females) and India (80% vs 67%) shows huge discrepancies.7

One of the main reasons explaining the low proportion of completed death registrations, in general, is due to the lack of incentives for registering deaths in many Asian countries.6 The discrepancy in death registration between the sexes in Nepal has been attributed to the existing tradition of male-dominated property ownership, the provision of a social security allowance for widows and the legal requirement of a death registration certificate for transferring property ownership and accessing social security.8 In India, 59% of unregistered deaths are females7 and the higher proportion of death registrations among males has been linked to succession rights910 and a higher proportion of accidental deaths.11 Furthermore, there is a lower proportion of females employed in the formal sector and, hence, a perceived lower need to register their deaths.812 In addition, females tend to have a longer life expectancy than males, which also implies that there may be no one to register a woman’s death after the death of their husband in a single household.11 These factors, explored in the neighbouring country of India, may help in understanding the current situation of death registration in Bangladesh.

Bangladesh’s record-keeping for birth and death registrations (BDRs) is a recent development. Before the enactment of the BDR Act in 2004, the only relevant law was the Births, Deaths and Marriages Act of 1886.13 Under this law, recording deaths was not mandatory, resulting in the lack of a comprehensive record for death registrations. Following the BDR Act’s implementation in 2006, the Bangladesh government introduced the online Birth Registration Information System in 2010, which was upgraded to the Birth and Death Registration Information System (BDRIS) in 2020. In Bangladesh, death registration is free for 45 days after a death occurs, after which a late registration fee (less than US$1) applies. For online death registration, supporting documents such as the deceased’s national identity card, birth certificate and proof of death from a health centre, provider, graveyard authority or local government administrator are required. One must visit the registration office with these documents, where health assistants aid in filling out the online form using the information provided.

Between the launch of the online registration system in October 2010 and February 2014, the total number of deaths registered in Bangladesh reached 103 443 compared with the expected 761 000 deaths in 2013 alone.14 Further amendments to the act in 2013 led to the establishment of the Office of the Registrar General (ORG) in 2016, which is responsible for civil registration. In 2016, the government implemented a pilot project in Kaliganj district to strengthen the civil registration and vital statistics system, under which health assistants and family welfare assistants were trained as notification agents. The project led to an increase in BDR; the ‘Kaliganj model’ was scaled nationwide in 2020.

However, despite these initiatives, Bangladesh’s death registration completeness level was 45% in 2022, according to UNICEF.715 Nonetheless, there is limited published evidence on the disparity in death registration completion between males and females in Bangladesh and the underlying causes. Information on female mortality data is essential for governments to develop better public health policies for women. This knowledge is crucial for addressing the highly complex issues that leave some groups of women and girls behind in national development efforts. Therefore, the current research explores the barriers to death registration in areas with low and high sex disparity in death registration in Bangladesh.

Methods

Study design

The study design was qualitative and exploratory, as the purpose was to understand the underlying reasons for the sex disparity. The Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines were used to report sex and gender information throughout the article.16

Study site

Eight sites were selected based on data on death registration completeness for males and females, provided by the ORG (table 1), who categorised them as low or high according to national mortality data from 2022,17 the year the current study collected the data. According to this data, mortality was 56.4% for males and 43.6% for females (male mortality rate: 6.6 per 1000 population; female mortality rate: 5.1 per 1000 population). Therefore, subdistricts where female death registration completeness was less than 43.6% of total registered deaths were operationally considered lower female registration settings, indicating a high sex disparity in death registration. Consequently, areas with registration completeness above 43.6% were considered to have higher registration, thus a low disparity. We acknowledge that the threshold is derived from nationally registered deaths rather than from all deaths in the population, which may itself reflect the registration completeness problem under investigation. The classification of sites as high-disparity or low-disparity should be understood as an operational distinction rather than an absolute measure of differential registration behaviour or mortality.

Study locations with sex disparities

The selected districts were Dhaka (Dohar, Nawabganj and Dhaka North City Corporation (DNCC)), Thakurgaon (Ranisankail), Gaibandha (Palashbari), Satkhira (Shyamnagar), Cox’s Bazar (Kutubdia) and Sunamganj (Dharampash). These sites were also selected to represent urban, rural and hard-to-reach areas.

Study participants

The study participants were field-level implementers (registration staff, health assistants, village police), policymakers (senior officials involved in policy decisions from ORG) and adult community male and female members.

Sampling

First, one union/ward from each of the selected Upazila/city corporations was chosen randomly. While the key informants (field-level implementers and policymakers) were recruited using purposive sampling, the community representatives were recruited using the snowball method for the focus group discussions (FGDs) across all locations except DNCC, where convenience sampling was adopted. In DNCC, male visitors, who came for services at the ward council office (where BDRs are conducted), were approached for the FGD. The team had planned to recruit female participants in the same way, but not enough female visitors came to the office for the services. The purposive sampling criteria for the key informants were that the participant was involved in death registration implementation (staff) or decision-making (policymakers). Snowball sampling allowed participants with similar ages and socio-economic backgrounds to be included quickly. The field-level staff in the locations helped the team to identify one or two community members. The rest were recruited by snowball methods. Each FGD had six participants.

Data collection

A total of 33 key informant interviews (KIIs), averaging 60 min, were held at the respective offices to explore current death registration practices, associated challenges and perceived reasons for sex differences in registration.

FGDs were organised with community members to explore their knowledge of the death registration process, practices and challenges in the uptake of services. Considering time and other resources, one female FGD and one male FGD were planned in the selected eight subdistricts. This ensured that community voices were captured from areas with high and low sex disparity and that there was equal sex representation. The number of FGDs happened as planned except for in one location, DNCC, where only an FGD with male members was feasible. Therefore, one female and one male FGD were held in Dohar, Nawabganj, Ranisankail, Shyamnagar, Dharampash, Palashbari and Kutubdia, and one male FGD in DNCC, resulting in a total of 13 FGDs. During the FGDs, the participants’ ages and sexes were recorded. Each FGD took around 90 min. In DNCC, the FGD took place in a room at the ward council office. In the remaining locations, male FGDs took place in courtyards and female FGDs inside houses.

Each KII and FGD was conducted by a pair of researchers experienced in qualitative research based at the BRAC James P Grant School of Public Health of BRAC University, using semi-structured guidelines. The interview guides are provided in online supplemental file 1. In each pair, one conducted the interview while the other took notes. A total of eight researchers, four females and four males, collected the data. In each pair, both researchers were of the same sex and conducted FGDs with participants of the same sex. The researchers conducted daily debriefings with one another throughout data collection, during which they reflected on the process and reflexively assessed thematic repetition across interviews. No formal saturation criteria were applied; data collection ceased due to time and logistical constraints. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation.

Data analysis

The framework analysis method was applied to the collected data, comprising several steps.18 First, after verbatim transcription into Bangla, four researchers (PG, MAM, MJI, SA), who were part of the data collection team, each read at least two transcripts to familiarise themselves with the data. Second, they independently read the transcripts line by line and applied labels to relevant chunks of data (coding). The team took an open coding approach to ensure that important, relevant aspects of the data were not missed. Third, they met to discuss the application of the codes and resolved any disagreements, producing an analytical framework for coding the remaining transcripts. During this process, the team identified codes that could be grouped into categories and defined the codes and categories. Fourth, they applied the analytical framework to subsequent transcripts. New emerging codes were noted and discussed in meetings to refine the analytical framework. During this process, categories were also revisited and modified where necessary. Once all the transcripts were coded, the team generated code reports for each code, allowing them to review all the excerpts labelled with that code, identify patterns and note emerging themes and relationships. This process helped the researchers to determine how to organise the data display matrix, which was the next step and summarise the data for that code. They created a data display matrix in a Google Sheet, which allowed the team to work together in real time. They summarised the findings from the code report and put the relevant information, data sources and quotes in the data display matrix. Finally, they interpreted and discussed the charted data with other team members, noting ideas, differences between the cases and relationships between and within the codes and categories. The matrix also facilitated comparison of themes across sites and participant groups, enabling the team to assess the consistency and significance of findings. Using the data display matrix, the researchers discussed emerging themes and interpretations across several meetings.

To ensure data validity and trustworthiness, in addition to checking across researchers, responses from interviews and FGDs were triangulated.

Throughout the article, using the WHO19 definitions, ‘males’ and ‘females’ were used for sex-disaggregated data and ‘man’ and ‘woman’ were used when referring to roles and expectations that society ascribes to men and women in Bangladesh.

The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline was used to draft this manuscript, and the SRQR reporting checklist is included in online supplemental A.20

Reflexivity

The research team comprised public health researchers with prior experience in conducting qualitative research in settings similar to those studied. No team member had a prior relationship with the communities studied, nor prior involvement with the death registration system in Bangladesh. All researchers, being Bangladeshi, brought familiarity with the sociocultural context of the locations. Vital Strategies commissioned the research, contributed to the study’s conceptualisation and reviewed data collection tools. We acknowledge that this funder relationship may have introduced a pre-existing framing of death registration as a system to be strengthened, which could have influenced the formulation of research questions and tools for identifying barriers, rather than exploring a wider range of community perspectives. Gender matching between interviewers and FGD participants was applied to ensure participants felt comfortable discussing topics within their social context.

Results

The findings identified the following themes: limited awareness of the death registration process, an inheritance-driven need, missing supporting documents and a lack of fidelity in the registration process. These findings are elaborated below.

Limited awareness of the death registration process

Across all locations, limited awareness regarding the death registration process and criteria was a commonly reported barrier among the FGD participants. Although the participants, due to the selection procedure, may not represent the whole community, this theme emerged consistently across sites and participant groups. Although most FGD participants could not report the process, males, compared with females, more readily identified specific circumstances in which death registration would be necessary. Female participants, on the other hand, more often expressed general uncertainty about its purpose or necessity.

Now, the government disburses old age allowance, widow allowance, pregnancy allowance, and allowance for deserted and destitute women. If any such beneficiary passes away, he or she will be replaced by a family member who will continue to receive financial aid. For this, death registration is needed. (FGD 4, Male, Nawabganj, urban area, high sex disparity)

If we had known the benefits of death registration, we would have done it. But we do not know what its use. We know that birth registration is mandatory. You need the birth registration certificate everywhere. (FGD 11, Female, Sunamganj, hard-to-reach area, high sex disparity)

Those who are employed need to get their deaths registered. Women who have been housewives do not need to get their deaths registered. But if they have money in their bank account, then their death registrations need to be done. But this percentage (of women with bank account) is low. Here, women are mostly housewives. So, there is no question of pension. (FGD 2, Female, Dohar, urban area, high sex disparity)

Inheritance-driven need

People have been reported not to initiate the death registration process unless they need to inherit properties or money belonging to the deceased, a finding observed across all three locations. Death registration is a prerequisite for obtaining a ‘declaration of heirs’ (Warishnama) certificate. Key informants shared that the absence of any assets or money to pass on to their family after death discourages the family from registering the death. Oftentimes, men with assets and properties named after them, passed on to their children, lead to immediate registration of their deaths, which is not applicable to most women.

There is a financial reason why male deaths are registered more often. Suppose my mother is not inheriting my [maternal] grandfather’s properties. My mom is also not the inheritor of my father’s properties. My father’s assets are going to be divided among us brothers. For this, I would need the death and inheritor certificates from the union council. Only then will I be eligible for inheritance. This creates a need for men to register deaths [for inheritance]. For women, maybe, there is no such need. (KII 16, Implementor, Thakurgaon, rural area, high sex disparity)

The perceived lack of need for registering was also apparent during discussions with the community.

We don’t think that death registration is essential. We feel that the person has died, so why do it? But we do the registration when we need it for any work, like a death registration certificate for Warishan (inheritance). Until we have to do something, we don't do it willingly. We don’t understand its importance after someone’s death… (FGD 6, Male, Thakurgaon, rural area, high sex disparity)

More death registrations among males could also be due to the emphasis on inheritor certificates during outreach campaigns, which inadvertently invites more applications for male deaths, as hinted by implementers and community members.

We are getting more applications since we have been telling people that to get an inheritor (Warishan) certificate, you need to do death registration online (KII 9, Implementor, Nawabganj, urban area, high sex disparity)

During the campaigns, our ward members also informed us that we need a death certificate for the inheritor certificate; otherwise, how would they know that the person has died (FGD 13, Male, Kutubdia, hard-to-reach area, high sex disparity)

Often, inheritor certificates are issued without death registration certificates and accepted, thus reducing the need for and importance of death registration. This lack of consistency in the application process accounts for less systematic death registration.

There is a government policy that whenever an inheritor certificate needs to be provided, the death registration of the deceased person is mandatory. Photocopies of the inheritor’s NID card or birth certificate are also required for the inheritor certificate. Once these documents are provided, the death registration application can be accepted. But this is not happening in practice. The deaths are not being registered. People are getting a letter authorized by the Chairman stating the inheritor, which is being accepted but needs to stop. (KII 24, Implementor, Satkhira, rural area, low sex disparity)

Inheritance-driven need encourages the issue of fake supporting documents to speed up the process for death registration among males, often leading to a delay in registration or its incompletion due to incorrect information.

Someone came to me last week asking for a death registration certificate. He brought a document with him. He was young. I asked him where he lived and how the person had passed away. He could not tell me where the person passed away in location X. I asked, “Why have you come?” He said to apply [for death registration]. I told him, “Tell the inheritor (Warisan) to come since you cannot provide any information”. So, yesterday, another person came with the same document. I took that to my colleague, who issues the birth certificate. We cross-checked the birth certificate [of the deceased person] with additional documents and a certificate from a commissioner [already in the record] …My colleague said (looking at the new document), “Sir, this is a fraudulent document”. Plus, you see, he could not share any details with me! I told him (the person), “This should not happen. If you do fraudulent activities, you will be arrested…[…]…" Then that person left. You see, he has not yet returned! So, obviously, those were fake documents. (KII 3, Implementor, DNCC, urban area, low sex disparity)

Missing supporting information for registration

The requirement for various supporting documents and the complexity of collecting them discourage individuals from completing the death registration, and the verification process can be prolonged when the required documents or accurate information are missing. Although digital death registration has been mandatory since 2006, manual death registration continues in cases where a birth registration certificate has not already been provided. The software is designed to prevent the acceptance of death registrations if the individual’s birth registration has not been completed beforehand. As a result, deaths are primarily recorded manually in register books and later entered online upon request by beneficiaries, once all necessary information, including a birth certificate, has been provided. One implementer shared the software’s limitations.

Since 2006, we have been able to register both births and deaths online. However, we cannot register online those deaths that happened before 2006. In this case, we would have to use a private software where we put details of the death and later put that information on a letterhead issued by the Chairman (KII 9, Implementor, Nawabganj, urban area, high sex disparity)

One implementer particularly shared how religious ritual following death also delays the submission of essential documents for the deceased.

I have observed that whenever a Hindu comes for death registration, and we ask who died, we are told, “My father passed away”. That time, we say, “Okay, bring your father’s birth registration certificate and NID”. Then we are told, “When my father was cremated, we burned all his documents during cremation”. They do not keep anything with them. But we need the supporting documents. They wonder what the point of the certificate is when the person has already passed away. But when they need it, they come to us. (KII 14, Implementor, Thakurgaon, rural area, high sex disparity)

Providing supporting documents also becomes difficult when the information in the documents is not consistent with the details of the death. One such scenario arises when the death location does not match the burial location on the burial certificate, which hampers the registration process. For example, for someone who dies in Dhaka city but is buried in the village, the burial certificate issued in the village will not be accepted as proof of death since the location name would be different. One implementer shared the complexity of such differences.

We face hurdles in the case of those living in Dhaka but buried in their villages. They usually own land or properties in their villages. However, they think that the registration will be quicker and easier if they apply from Dhaka. But those from Barisal, Dinajpur, and Mymensingh do not want to go back there to do (death) registration. But they should only do it from there. They are also registered voters in that area. They have properties there. But they think doing it in Dhaka would be more useful, but that is not the case. The value is the same everywhere. (KII 3, Implementor, DNCC, urban area, low sex disparity)

Discussion

As this study draws on qualitative data from interviews and FGDs, the findings reflect participants’ perceived experiences and interpretations within their contexts. The barriers identified, therefore, should be understood as potential or possible explanatory mechanisms rather than causal relationships. Whether these mechanisms account for the observed sex disparity in registration completeness would require triangulation of quantitative data.

The current study identified three key barriers that appeared to be linked to the significant sex disparity. These are limited awareness of death registration, inheritance-driven needs and missing supporting documents to complete registration. Among these, the most significant barrier identified was the inheritance-driven need, mentioned by most participants. The findings indicated a reluctance or perceived lack of necessity to register the deaths of family members unless there was a question of inheritance or financial benefits. This aligns with previous studies in Bangladesh5 and neighbouring countries, such as India9 and Nepal.8 In Bangladesh, inheritance requires a warishan (inheritor) certificate, creating a systemic obstacle since men hold property rights. While such practices are legal, they are also supported by respective religions21 and unintentionally exclude women. Therefore, families often see no need to register female deaths, which may be associated with lower registration rates among women. This implies that gendered norms around inheritance, which predominantly favour men, may be associated with the lower registration completeness observed among females in the ORG data. In other words, while the disparity is indicated through sex disaggregated registration completion data, deep-rooted social norms may shape whether the women’s deaths will be recorded. The sex disparity in registration completeness in this regard may reflect gendered social norms and perceptions rather than a biological phenomenon.

Regarding inheritance, we also found that campaigns promote inheritance to encourage death registration, which unintentionally emphasises the inheritor certificate, thus deprioritising the registration of females’ deaths. The inheritance-focused campaign appears to limit death registration messaging to a specific group, which may be associated with the limited awareness of the need for death certificates reported by participants. The limited awareness of the registration process reported among participants seems to be connected to the lack of death registration, in the absence of a perceived need for inheritance.

However, current findings also show that death registration is rather complex, as it requires several supporting pieces of information. Missing information, for example, birth registration and burial certificate, where the location of death and burial need to be aligned, was identified by the implementers as a significant perceived barrier to completing death registration in the current research. Given that the country still struggles with achieving a hundred per cent birth registration completion, death registration would be even more challenging. In this scenario, the awareness campaigns should also focus strongly on the supporting information and documents required to complete the death registration.

Nonetheless, the death registration process must be simplified with extensive community mobilisation to encourage families. Future research should investigate mobilising campaigns to encourage and ensure female death registrations. One pilot study in Bangladesh revealed that community health workers notifying deaths through a mobile app during household visits and calls made to call centres by village police and family members notifying of deaths increased the number of death notifications,22 with greater contributions from the former. This suggests that active household visits from community health workers to disseminate information may be beneficial. It could also be helpful to involve health facilities where deaths occur to increase the reporting of deaths. Additionally, for deaths occurring at home, village police could be given the authority to directly apply for death registration online, as the service is free of cost for the first 45 days following a death, thus potentially reducing the sex discrepancies.

There are several limitations to the current study. First is the number of FGDs. Insights from female perspectives would have added to the perception of death registration in Dhaka city if the female FGD had been conducted. Although no significant differences were found between males and females in location types by sex disparity, findings from a female FGD in the DNCC area could have strengthened the overall findings for that location. Moreover, if a greater number of FGDs could be conducted, including beneficiaries who have registered deaths or failed to register, the findings would provide stronger, in-depth information, which could be sought on the experience. Second is the possibility of social desirability bias among the implementers, who may be inclined to favour the registration system, and among FGD participants, who may be inclined to conform to social norms. From a study design perspective, perhaps questions about implementation and barriers to service uptake (and enablers) could have shed light on some of the challenges from the implementers’ side. The third limitation is the limited number of geographic regions considered for the study. Inclusion of other regions could have provided richer contextual perspectives. The fourth limitation concerns the recruitment of FGDs through implementers, combined with snowball sampling, which may have introduced selection bias. It is possible that implementers approached participants who were more engaged or knowledgeable about the registration process than the broader community. This represents a potential threat to the credibility of the finding that poor awareness is a primary barrier, as the study population may have been systematically more aware than the general population.

Conclusion

The current research presents perceived barriers that may contribute to fewer registrations of female deaths. If unaddressed, these gaps may continue to hinder the completeness of mortality data and limit informed decisions regarding women’s health, needs and policies. A combination of poor awareness and an overemphasis on inheritance may contribute to the perceived lack of necessity for registering deaths, particularly among women. These findings thus call for stronger, more gender-sensitive and better-adapted policy reforms and closer regulation of their implementation.
