Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a mutation in the NF1 gene on chromosome 17, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 3000 live births [1]. Diagnosis is based on the NIH clinical criteria, which include café-au-lait macules, neurofibromas, Lisch nodules, and characteristic skeletal lesions [2]. Mandibular bone involvement in NF1 is well recognized and includes ramus defects, enlargement of the mandibular canal, and irregularity of the lower jaw contour [3]. Submucosal cleft palate is a rare association with NF1, in which the palatal mucosa appears intact but the underlying muscular layer is deficient [4]. This type of cleft palate may not be detectable on routine clinical examination and is often incidentally diagnosed on axial images [5]. Pseudo Class III malocclusion (functional Class III) is characterized by an anterior crossbite with a normal skeletal relationship that can be corrected upon mandibular retrusion. Its association with NF1 has rarely been reported. Additionally, tooth agenesis (missing teeth) has been described in NF1 patients, though it is not a common finding [6]. Most patients with NF1 develop cutaneous and ocular manifestations by adolescence; however, rare cases present with isolated skeletal findings [7]. This report describes monozygotic 13-year-old twins with suspected NF1 who presented to the orthodontic department with dental crowding and were found on panoramic radiography and CBCT to have a complete constellation of mandibular dysplastic changes along with a submucosal cleft palate, pseudo Class III malocclusion, and a missing tooth. The involvement of both monozygotic twins with identical findings strongly supports the genetic nature of the condition, even in the absence of classic cutaneous manifestations.

Case report

Monozygotic 13-year-old female twins (designated as Twin A and Twin B) were referred to the orthodontic department of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences with a chief complaint of dental crowding. Twin A presented with dental crowding in both arches. On clinical facial examination, she demonstrated symmetrical facial contours with no visible asymmetry, neurofibromas, or café-au-lait macules. No cutaneous or ocular stigmata of neurofibromatosis type 1 were identified. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no prior hospitalizations, surgeries, or chronic illnesses, and no history of head and neck trauma. Her developmental milestones were reportedly normal for her age. Twin B presented with identical clinical and radiographic findings to those of Twin A, with the exception of a supernumerary impacted maxillary molar which was additionally noted in Twin B. She also demonstrated no cutaneous or ocular stigmata of NF1 on physical examination, and her medical history was similarly unremarkable.

The surgical team recommended conservative management for the submucosal cleft palate, as the patients were asymptomatic with no speech difficulties or velopharyngeal insufficiency, and surgical intervention was deemed unnecessary at this stage. The patients were subsequently referred to a clinical geneticist for further evaluation and consideration of genetic testing. The orthodontic treatment plan was modified to address the dental crowding and pseudo Class III malocclusion while considering the underlying skeletal abnormalities. The patients are currently under regular follow-up with the orthodontic and genetic medicine departments for monitoring of any potential development of new manifestations.

Radiologic findings

On panoramic radiography

The panoramic radiographic findings were nearly identical in both twins (Figs. 1E and 2E). The mandibular ramus demonstrated marked dysplasia with significant thinning and elongation. The sigmoid notch and coronoid notch were widened in both horizontal and vertical dimensions, and the condylar head was elongated bilaterally. The mandibular canal was enlarged throughout its course, and the mental canal and mental foramen were also enlarged. Erosive changes and irregularity of the external mandibular contour were observed, particularly in the angle region. An impacted maxillary molar was noted in both twins, and a supernumerary impacted maxillary molar was additionally observed in Twin B. A missing premolar was noted in the mandible. Pseudo Class III malocclusion with anterior crossbite and normal skeletal relationship was evident.

On CBCT

Coronal CBCT sections revealed lateral bowing (lateral bulging) of the mandibular ramus (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Severe thinning of the cortical bone was observed at the ramus and angle regions (Fig. 4). The mandibular canal and mental foramen were enlarged (Fig. 3). Axial sections demonstrated a submucosal cleft palate in both twins, characterized by a defect in the muscular layer of the palate with intact mucosa (Fig. 5). No aggressive bone destruction or intraosseous soft tissue mass was observed in either patient.

Discussion

Neurofibromatosis type 1 produces a wide spectrum of skeletal and dental abnormalities, and the mandible is one of the most commonly affected facial bones [3,8]. Characteristic radiographic findings in the mandible include thinning and elongation of the ramus, enlargement of the sigmoid notch, widening of the mandibular canal, and erosive changes of the lower border [9]. These findings result from neurofibromatous infiltration along the inferior alveolar nerve and from chronic pressure effects of soft tissue neurofibromas [10]. Both of our patients demonstrated the complete constellation of these mandibular changes on panoramic radiography and CBCT (Figs. 1E, 2E, 3, and 4). The presence of a supernumerary impacted maxillary molar in one of the twins is also consistent with previous reports of an association between NF1 and supernumerary teeth [11]. Previous studies have shown that enlargement of the mandibular canal and mental foramen are key radiographic findings in NF1 patients [3,9].

Uniqueness of this case

What makes this case particularly noteworthy and distinct from previously reported cases is the involvement of both monozygotic twins with a complete constellation of NF1 radiographic findings, while both completely lack the classic cutaneous and ocular manifestations of NF1 at the age of 13 years. While isolated mandibular dysplasia has been reported in NF1 patients [3,8], the concurrent presence of submucosal cleft palate, pseudo Class III malocclusion, and mixed dental anomalies (simultaneous supernumerary and missing teeth) in both monozygotic twins without café-au-lait macules or Lisch nodules has not been previously described in the English-language literature. According to the NIH diagnostic criteria, the presence of 2 or more clinical features is required for a diagnosis of NF1 [2]. Both of our patients demonstrate at least 3 major criteria based on skeletal findings alone (characteristic osseous lesions), which strongly supports the diagnosis despite the absence of cutaneous manifestations.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of both monozygotic twins presenting with the complete constellation of mandibular dysplasia, submucosal cleft palate, pseudo Class III malocclusion, and mixed dental anomalies in patients without cutaneous or ocular findings, who also have a history of growth hormone therapy and bone fracture. The involvement of both monozygotic twins with identical findings makes this an exceptional report that strongly supports the genetic and heritable nature of this condition.

Clinical implications and lessons learned

This case provides several important clinical lessons for oral and maxillofacial radiologists, orthodontists, endocrinologists, and general dentists:

1.Unexplained dental crowding and malocclusion in patients, particularly in monozygotic twins, should not be dismissed as isolated dental problems. When radiographic evaluation reveals atypical findings such as ramus elongation, enlarged mandibular canal, or abnormal sigmoid notch morphology, a syndromic etiology such as NF1 should be considered, even in the absence of classical cutaneous manifestations. The involvement of both monozygotic twins with similar findings should be a red flag and mandate further evaluation.

The absence of café-au-lait macules and Lisch nodules in both 13-year-old patients is unusual. Most patients with NF1 meet at least 2 clinical criteria by this age [2]. Possible explanations include a mosaic form of NF1 (segmental NF1) or a mutation with incomplete penetrance [12]. Segmental NF1 is caused by postzygotic somatic mutations and may present with manifestations limited to one body region [5,12]. However, the involvement of both monozygotic twins with identical findings reduces the likelihood of segmental NF1 and more strongly supports a variant form with predominantly skeletal involvement or a mutation with incomplete penetrance. Additionally, the history of osteomalacia and bone fragility in these patients is consistent with the skeletal manifestations of NF1 [8].

The involvement of both monozygotic twins with identical findings strongly confirms the genetic nature of this condition and makes the diagnosis of NF1 highly probable, even in the absence of classic symptoms. Furthermore, while laboratory findings including CBC, calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, and vitamin D were within normal limits in our patients, normal laboratory values do not exclude NF1, as the diagnosis is based on clinical and radiographic criteria rather than blood tests.

The history of growth hormone therapy in both patients and the fracture in 1 twin leading to a diagnosis of osteomalacia is another important aspect to consider. While growth hormone therapy may be associated with increased bone fragility in some patients, the findings of bony dysplasia in both twins, particularly the characteristic mandibular involvement pattern of NF1, suggest that osteomalacia and bone fragility in these patients may be part of the skeletal manifestations of NF1 rather than merely a side effect of growth hormone therapy. Reduced bone mineral density and increased fracture risk are well-documented in NF1 [8,9] and may have been exacerbated by growth hormone therapy in these patients. The discontinuation of growth hormone therapy following the diagnosis of osteomalacia in 1 twin was a logical decision and may have prevented further fractures.

The most notable finding in our patients was the submucosal cleft palate (Fig. 5). Submucosal cleft palate is a rare association with NF1 [4,5]. In this type of cleft, the palatal mucosa appears intact, and the underlying muscular defect may not be detectable on routine clinical examination. Diagnosis is often made incidentally on axial images [4]. The detection of this condition in both of our patients was entirely incidental on CBCT, highlighting the critical role of cross-sectional imaging in the evaluation of orthodontic patients. This finding has significant clinical implications, as undiagnosed submucosal cleft palate may contribute to speech difficulties, velopharyngeal insufficiency, and middle ear problems if left unrecognized [4,5].

Our patients also presented with pseudo Class III malocclusion and a missing tooth. Pseudo Class III malocclusion in NF1 may result from mandibular elongation and forward positioning due to condylar elongation and ramus dysplasia [13]. Tooth agenesis (missing teeth) has been reported in NF1 patients, possibly related to neural crest cell involvement during odontogenesis [6,14]. The presence of a missing tooth alongside a supernumerary tooth in the same patient is particularly interesting and may reflect a generalized disturbance in dental development, which has been suggested in previous reports on craniofacial development in RASopathies [11].

The presence of mild hypertelorism in both of our patients is another finding that, while subtle, may be associated with NF1 and craniofacial dysplasias [8]. Hypertelorism has been previously described in patients with NF1 and may result from orbital dysplasia or involvement of the sphenoid wing [1]. This finding further supports the likelihood of an underlying syndromic condition in both of our patients.

Comparison with previous reports

A review of the literature reveals that while mandibular dysplasia is a well-recognized manifestation of NF1 [3,8,9], the specific combination of findings reported in our patients is unique. Friedrich et al. [3] described combined soft and hard tissue facial lesions in early childhood, but their cases typically presented with classic cutaneous findings. Muthusamy et al. [4] discussed diagnostic challenges in NF1 patients with dual diagnoses, but did not report a case with isolated skeletal findings in the absence of cutaneous lesions. Reis et al. [9] evaluated mandibular condyle dimensions in NF1 patients, but their study population included patients with established diagnoses based on cutaneous criteria.

Genetic testing for NF1 mutation was not performed in these cases at the time of this report, which is a limitation of our study. The patients were referred to a clinical geneticist and are currently under regular follow-up; however, genetic testing has not yet been conducted due to financial constraints. The diagnosis in these patients is therefore based on clinical and radiographic criteria, which are highly suggestive of NF1. Genetic testing remains planned for the future should funding become available. However, the striking radiographic findings, including the rare associations of submucosal cleft palate, pseudo Class III malocclusion, and mixed supernumerary and missing teeth in both monozygotic twins, make the diagnosis of NF1 highly probable.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that NF1 can present with isolated skeletal and dental manifestations in the maxillofacial region, even in the absence of classic cutaneous and ocular findings and with normal laboratory values. The involvement of both monozygotic twins with a complete constellation of radiographic findings strongly supports the genetic nature of this condition. The combination of mandibular ramus dysplasia, widening of the sigmoid notch, enlargement of the mandibular canal, lateral bowing of the ramus, submucosal cleft palate, pseudo Class III malocclusion, and mixed supernumerary and missing teeth on panoramic radiography and CBCT (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5) is highly suggestive of NF1. The history of growth hormone therapy and bone fracture in these patients further emphasizes the importance of thorough skeletal evaluation in patients with growth disorders. Oral and maxillofacial radiologists, orthodontists, and endocrinologists play a crucial role in the early recognition of these findings, which may represent the first and only clinical clues to an underlying syndromic condition. We recommend that clinicians maintain a high index of suspicion for NF1 in patients with unexplained dental crowding and malocclusion, particularly when associated with atypical radiographic findings and especially in monozygotic twins, and consider multidisciplinary management and genetic counseling for affected patients.
