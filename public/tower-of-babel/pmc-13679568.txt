Giant Cystic Lymphangioma of the Transverse Mesocolon in a 7-Year-Old Child: A Case Report

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Lymphatic malformations are benign developmental abnormalities of the lymphatic system. Most lymphangiomas are diagnosed in childhood, with approximately 90% recognized before 2 years of age, and the neck and axilla are the most frequent sites.1 Intra-abdominal lymphangiomas represent only a small proportion of cases and most often arise from mesenteric or retroperitoneal compartments; mesocolonic origin is distinctly uncommon.2–4

Clinical presentation is variable and depends on lesion size, location, and complications. Patients may be asymptomatic or may present with abdominal pain, distension, a palpable mass, bowel obstruction, hemorrhage, infection, or volvulus.3,5 Large cystic lesions can distort normal anatomy, making it difficult to establish their exact organ or mesenteric compartment of origin on imaging.3,6

The differential diagnosis of a large multiloculated intra-abdominal cystic mass in a child includes mesenteric or omental cysts, enteric duplication cysts, cystic teratoma, ovarian or other adnexal cysts, pancreatic pseudocyst, and other primary cystic mesenteric lesions.6 Histopathological examination is therefore important when imaging is not definitive. We report a giant cystic lymphangioma of the transverse mesocolon in a 7-year-old girl that mimicked an ovarian cyst preoperatively and was completely excised without bowel resection.

Case Presentation

A 7-year-old girl presented with a 3–4 week history of vomiting, abdominal pain, and progressive abdominal distension. Associated symptoms included poor appetite, abdominal fullness, generalized weakness, and irregular bowel movements.

She had no significant past medical or family history. Her parents were non-consanguineous, and her developmental and immunization history was reported as normal.

On examination, a large abdominal mass measuring approximately 12 × 10 x 12 cm was palpable, extending from the right flank to the pubic region. The mass was well defined, irregular, firm, non-tender, and mobile in the vertical direction. There was no hepatosplenomegaly. Bowel sounds were reduced and were more prominent on the left side. No lymphadenopathy or overlying skin changes were noted.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, renal function tests, liver function tests, and serum electrolytes, were within normal limits.

Abdominal and pelvic imaging (Figures 1–4) demonstrated a large multiloculated cystic mass occupying a substantial portion of the abdominal cavity. The lesion was initially interpreted as an ovarian cyst; however, the available preoperative imaging description did not confidently establish its precise organ or mesenteric compartment of origin. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5. At 8 months of follow-up, she remained asymptomatic and no recurrence had been observed.

Discussion

Cystic lymphangioma is a benign lymphatic malformation arising from abnormal development or sequestration of lymphatic channels. Although the lesion is strongly associated with early childhood, older children can be affected.1 Intra-abdominal lesions are uncommon and mesocolonic lesions are particularly rare.2,4,7

The present case is notable for the combination of age, size, location, and diagnostic mimicry. Our patient was 7 years old, had a clinically large lesion measuring approximately 12 × 10 x 12 cm, and the mass arose from the transverse mesocolon despite being interpreted preoperatively as ovarian in origin. Very large cystic masses may displace adjacent organs and alter normal anatomical relationships, which can make the site of origin difficult to determine radiologically.3,6

Published pediatric mesocolonic cases demonstrate similar diagnostic and operative variability. Yuen et al reported a 14-year-old girl with a large cystic mass in the transverse mesocolon that was completely excised.7 Mayer et al described a 10-year-old boy in whom a transverse mesocolonic cystic lymphangioma was discovered at laparotomy and required segmental colonic resection.8 More recently, Feng et al reported a 14-year-old boy with a right mesocolonic lymphangioma successfully treated by laparoscopic excision after CT localized the lesion to the mesocolon.6 These reports, together with the present case, illustrate that pediatric mesocolonic lymphangioma can occur beyond infancy and that the need for bowel resection depends on the lesion’s relationship to bowel and mesocolic structures rather than size alone.

Imaging is essential for characterizing a cystic abdominal mass and assessing its relationship to adjacent structures, but no single radiological feature is pathognomonic for lymphangioma. Typical descriptions include a well-defined uni- or multiloculated cystic lesion with septations, while the differential diagnosis remains broad.3,6 In the present case, the preoperative impression of an ovarian cyst underscores the importance of retaining a mesenteric or mesocolonic lesion in the differential diagnosis when the apparent site of origin is uncertain.

For symptomatic intra-abdominal lymphangioma, complete surgical excision is generally favored when it can be achieved safely. The operative approach should be individualized according to lesion size, location, and involvement of bowel or mesenteric vessels.4,6,9 Some mesocolonic lesions require intestinal resection to achieve complete removal, whereas others can be excised with bowel preservation.6,8 Historical series have shown very low recurrence after complete excision, while residual disease after incomplete removal is associated with a higher risk of persistence or recurrence.9 In our patient, complete excision was achieved without bowel resection and no recurrence was observed during 8 months of follow-up.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. It describes a single patient, and follow-up is limited to 8 months. In addition, the source imaging information available for this report did not include detailed radiological measurements, contrast-enhancement characteristics, or a complete mapping of the lesion’s relationship to the ovaries and mesocolic vessels. The operative record available for manuscript preparation likewise did not provide a more detailed vascular map. These details have therefore not been reconstructed retrospectively. Longer follow-up would further strengthen assessment of recurrence.

Conclusion

Giant cystic lymphangioma of the transverse mesocolon is an uncommon diagnostic consideration in children, including those beyond infancy. A large mesocolonic lesion may mimic an ovarian or other intra-abdominal cyst because its size can obscure the true site of origin. Mesocolonic lymphangioma should therefore remain in the differential diagnosis of a multiloculated cystic abdominal mass when imaging localization is uncertain. Surgical exploration can establish the anatomy and permit complete excision when technically feasible; histopathological examination provides definitive diagnosis. This case also demonstrates that complete excision may be possible without bowel resection when the lesion can be separated safely from adjacent bowel.
