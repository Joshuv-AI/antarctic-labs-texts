Systematic Comparison of High‐Fat Diet– and Streptozotocin‐Induced Prediabetic Obese C57BL/6J Mouse Models: A Sex‐Based Protocol Optimization Study
Authors: Min Xu, Chao Ke, Yangkun Liu, Wenying Shi, Yang Cao, Zhengyu Li, Yi Xiao, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsAnimal models of prediabetes (Pre‐DM) are essential for studying metabolic disease and testing therapies, yet high‐fat diet (HFD) and HFD‐plus‐streptozotocin (STZ) protocols vary in feeding duration, STZ dose, and diagnostic criteria and rarely account for sex. We compared five protocols in male and female C57BL/6J mice to find the best balance of duration, cost, and phenotypic stability and to characterize how HFD and HFD‐STZ differ in their effects on glucose and lipid metabolism.Materials and MethodsFive‐week‐old mice were stratified by sex and assigned to three HFD‐only groups (HFD‐12w, ‐16w, ‐20w) and two STZ groups of differing cumulative dose (HFD‐STZ‐L, ‐H). Body weight, food and energy intake, body fat percentage, fasting blood glucose (FBG), OGTT, ITT, fasting insulin, serum lipids, hepatic Oil Red O, histopathology, liver and kidney safety, and per‐animal cost were assessed.ResultsHFD induced obesity of equal magnitude in both sexes (non‐significant Sex × Diet interactions for body weight and fat mass). Glucose and hepatic‐lipid metabolism showed clear sex dimorphism: in males these deteriorated by Week 12, whereas females required 16 weeks to reach the Pre‐DM threshold, at a higher preset STZ dose. HFD‐STZ‐L matched HFD‐12w in both sexes within a shorter cycle (overall success 73.3%). Notably, STZ reversed the HFD lipid phenotype, synchronously reducing body fat, serum lipids, and hepatic fat in females.ConclusionsHFD‐STZ‐L is preferred for glucose and β‐cell studies in both sexes; HFD‐12w preserves the intact female obesity phenotype; HFD‐20w is preferred for MASLD studies. Biological sex must be incorporated into model design and phenotype interpretation.Keywords: C57BL/6J mice, glucose intolerance, high‐fat diet, insulin resistance, MASLD, model selection, obesity, prediabetes, sex differences, streptozotocin

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disease that poses a serious threat to global public health; prediabetes (Pre‐DM), as a transitional stage between normal blood glucose levels and T2DM, represents the most effective window of opportunity for intervention and reversal [1, 2]. The global prevalence of T2DM reached approximately 589 million in 2024 and is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [1]; approximately 5%–10% of individuals with prediabetes progress to T2DM annually [2], and in some regions, the prevalence of prediabetes has already surpassed that of T2DM itself [3]. Overweight or obesity (particularly central obesity) is an independent risk factor for prediabetes [4]; the combination of prediabetes and obesity represents a critical watershed in metabolic disease, signalling a significant increase in the risk of T2DM, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, microvascular complications, cancer, and cognitive impairment [5, 6].

Establishing reliable animal models of obese prediabetes is crucial for elucidating pathological mechanisms and evaluating therapeutic strategies. Inbred C57BL/6J mice, due to a defect in the nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase (Nnt) gene on chromosome 13, highly mimic the progressive evolution of human obesity and T2DM under high‐fat diet (HFD) stress [7, 8, 9, 10]. STZ, as a pancreatic β‐cell‐specific toxic agent, selectively enters β‐cells via GLUT2, induces alkylation‐mediated DNA damage and NAD+ depletion, and consequently causes β‐cell dysfunction and apoptosis [11, 12]. The combination of HFD and low‐dose STZ superimposes mild β‐cell damage onto HFD‐induced insulin resistance, effectively replicating the pathological features of prediabetes [13, 14]. However, current protocols are highly heterogeneous, with HFD durations ranging from 8 to 24 weeks, cumulative STZ doses ranging from 30 to 200 mg·kg−1, and inconsistent thresholds for defining Pre‐DM [13, 14, 15], making cross‐study comparisons and extrapolation of conclusions difficult.

Another key variable in metabolic diseases is biological sex. Clinical epidemiology indicates that the prevalence of T2DM in women is comparable to or even higher than that in men [16]; the metabolic and neurotoxic effects of high‐fat diets in mice are also sex‐dependent [17]; the mechanisms underlying metabolic syndrome may not be parallel between the sexes: oestrogen exerts significant regulatory effects on β‐cells, hepatic lipid metabolism, and fat distribution [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], and the intensity of stress required to reach the Pre‐DM threshold may also exhibit sex differences [15]. However, previous HFD‐STZ model studies have predominantly used male subjects, and directly extrapolating these protocols to females introduces systematic bias. Since 2016, the NIH has required biological sex to be treated as a core variable in basic research [26]; including both sexes in prediabetes obesity model studies and conducting direct comparisons is of great significance for improving research reproducibility and translational value.

Against this background, this study systematically compared five modelling protocols (HFD‐12w, HFD‐16w, HFD‐20w, HFD‐STZ‐L and HFD‐STZ‐H) in 5‐week‐old C57BL/6J male and female mice. The evaluation encompassed eight dimensions—obesity phenotype, glucose metabolism, blood lipids and hepatic lipids, liver and kidney safety, model consistency (coefficient of variation), maintenance of body fat percentage at the end of the study, and per‐animal cost. The aim was to identify the protocol with the optimal combination of duration, cost, and phenotypic stability; to characterize the distinct effects of HFD and HFD‐STZ on glucose and lipid metabolism; and to provide systematic recommendations regarding protocol selection based on sex.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

Five‐week‐old SPF‐grade C57BL/6J male and female mice were purchased from Hunan Slaike Jingda Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Changsha) and housed in the SPF‐grade containment facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (temperature 22°C–25°C, relative humidity 50%–60%, 12‐h light–dark cycle, lights on 08:00–20:00), with ad libitum food and water. Five mice of the same sex and treatment group were housed per cage. The study complied with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and relevant Chinese laws and regulations, and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (HNUCM21‐2501‐16).

Grouping and Establishment of the Prediabetic Obesity Model

After a 1‐week acclimation period, mice were stratified by sex and randomized using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel, then assigned to 5 groups: HFD‐12w, HFD‐16w, HFD‐20w (high‐fat diet [HFD] alone for 12, 16 and 20 weeks, respectively); HFD‐STZ‐L, and HFD‐STZ‐H (STZ injection administered after HFD feeding continued until the group mean exceeded the threshold of 1.20 times the mean of the combined ND group at the same age). Initial n = 8–10 for both females and males in each group.

The HFD was introduced gradually using a 3‐day transition method: Day 1 (D1)—100% ND; Day 2 (D2)—2/3 ND + 1/3 HFD; Day 3 (D3)—1/3 ND + 2/3 HFD; Day 4 (D4) onwards—100% HFD; the duration of HFD feeding is calculated from the start of 100% HFD (D4). The HFD is stored at −20°C; prior to feeding, it is left at room temperature until completely thawed, and is replaced every 3–4 days to prevent oxidation and spoilage. Diet: HFD (60% fat, 20% protein, 20% carbohydrates, 5.24 kcal·g−1, catalogue no. D12492) and ND (10% fat, 20% protein, 70% carbohydrates, 3.82 kcal·g−1, catalogue no. D12450J) were both purchased from Research Diets Inc. (New Brunswick, NJ, USA).

Streptozotocin (STZ; AmBeed Inc., catalogue number A667625) was administered as a single dose of 30 mg·kg−1 to achieve cumulative doses distributed according to the table below:

SexGroupSingle doseNumber of injectionsCumulative doseMaleHFD‐STZ‐L30 mg·kg−1 ×1 day30 mg·kg−1 MaleHFD‐STZ‐H30 mg·kg−1 ×3 days90 mg·kg−1 FemaleHFD‐STZ‐L30 mg·kg−1 ×3 days90 mg·kg−1 FemaleHFD‐STZ‐H30 mg·kg−1 ×6 days180 mg·kg−1

STZ was dissolved in 0.1 mol·L−1 sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5; Solarbio, catalogue no. C1013) in an ice bath under light‐protected conditions to a concentration of 10 mg·mL−1. The solution was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 3 μL·g−1 body weight (i.e., 30 mg·kg−1 per injection). The entire process, from preparation to the completion of the final injection, was completed within 30 min. For the first two injections, the animals were fasted overnight for 12 h (water was not restricted) the night before and injected the following morning. This brief pre‐injection fast is intended to lower blood glucose and thereby reduce competition between glucose and STZ for GLUT2‐mediated uptake into β‐cells, following established convention in STZ protocols; under the present conditions we have no direct evidence that fasting is strictly required for STZ efficacy (see Section 4.4). Starting with the third injection, fasting was discontinued prior to injection to avoid the stress and risk of hypoglycemia associated with prolonged fasting [27]; On the evening of the first injection, 10% sucrose (Hushi SCR, AR grade, catalogue number 10021463) was added to the drinking water to prepare a sugar solution for ad libitum consumption, to prevent STZ‐induced early hypoglycemia. Concurrently, the STZ control group (ND) was injected with an equal volume of sodium citrate buffer.

The rationale for the different cumulative doses in females and males: ① previous studies have consistently reported that female C57BL/6J mice are more resistant to STZ‐induced β‐cell toxicity, requiring approximately 2–3 times the cumulative dose to achieve a blood glucose effect equivalent to that in males [15, 27, 28]; ② preliminary experiments by our group showed that in males fed an HFD for 9 weeks, a cumulative dose of 180 mg·kg−1 (30 mg·kg−1 × 6 days) resulted in 100% crossing the T2DM threshold (Figure S2G–J); based on this, the upper limit for the STZ‐H group in males was set at 90 mg·kg−1. This study adopted an ‘equal phenotype’ rather than an ‘equal dose’ principle; potential confounders have been transparently disclosed in Section 4.4. Because the two sexes were not compared at an equal STZ dose, this study makes no independent claim of differential STZ sensitivity between the sexes; the unequal cumulative doses reflect a design parameter set a priori from prior literature.

Body Weight and Blood Glucose Monitoring, and Assessment of Obesity and Pre‐DM

Throughout the modelling process, after a weekly 6‐h photoperiod‐induced fast (08:00–14:00, water unrestricted), body weight was measured at the end of the fast (approximately 14:00), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured using a portable blood glucose meter (Omron GH83; upper measurement limit 33.3 mmol·L−1) which was used to dynamically track the time points at which body weight and blood glucose crossed thresholds; these longitudinal FBG data were included in the linear mixed‐effects model analysis in Section 2.8. Feed intake was recorded by cage (5 mice per cage shared a single measurement; statistical units were based on the cage): At each feed change (every 3–4 days), residual feed was weighed to calculate feed consumption, which was aggregated weekly to determine average daily feed intake; Average daily caloric intake = average daily food intake × 5.24 kcal·g−1 for HFD or 3.82 kcal·g−1 for ND. Body weight, feed, and fat pads were weighed using an electronic balance (Lichen LC‐YP3002B, accuracy 0.01 g). Body fat percentage was determined at the end of the study: During dissection, the weights of the perigonadal (gWAT), inguinal subcutaneous (iWAT), and perirenal (PWAT) white fat pads were measured. Body fat percentage = (total white fat pad weight/pre‐surgery body weight) × 100% (excluding brown fat).

The threshold for obesity was uniformly defined as body weight ≥ 1.20 × the mean body weight of the age‐matched ND group; the assessment time point was the body weight at the end of the modelling period for the HFD‐12w/16w/20w groups, and the body weight 1 week prior to STZ injection for the HFD‐STZ group (W11 for females, W9 for males). Prediabetes mellitus (Pre‐DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) were diagnosed according to the criteria for the diagnosis and classification of diabetes [29], using the upper threshold for fasting blood glucose (FBG) corresponding to the renal glucose threshold in rodents (13.9 mmol·L−1) [30] and the 2‐h OGTT glucose cutoff of 16.7 mmol·L−1 [29, 31]. At the individual level: each mouse's 0‐min FBG and 120‐min blood glucose from the OGTT were compared with the mean of the same‐sex ND group within the same treatment group; if either value was higher than the corresponding ND mean and both were below the T2DM threshold, the mouse was classified as Pre‐DM(OR logic); if either indicator reached or exceeded the T2DM threshold, the mouse was classified as having failed modelling. The final body fat retention rate served as a descriptive indicator: the proportion of mice with a body fat percentage at sampling ≥ the mean of the same‐sex ND group + 2 SD (5.9% for females, 6.82% for males). This was used solely to describe peripheral fat retention following STZ administration and was not included in the assessment of successful obesity modelling.

OGTT, ITT and AUC Calculations

All metabolic measurements were performed after a 6‐h daytime fast (08:00–14:00; water was not restricted), which aligns with the mice's feeding rhythm and is consistent with the recommended fasting duration for metabolic tolerance tests [32, 33, 34]. The administration and interpretation of the OGTT and ITT followed established protocols for assessing glucose homeostasis in mice [35, 36, 37]. OGTT: After fasting, body weight was measured and tail‐tip FBG was assessed (Omron GH83). Subsequently, a 20% glucose solution (2 g·kg−1, 10 μL·g−1, prepared by diluting a 50% glucose injection solution [Hubei Kelun] with saline) was administered via oral gavage. Blood samples were collected from the tail tip at 0, 15, 30,60, 90 and 120 min. ITT: After fasting, administer human insulin (NovoLog R, Novo Nordisk; 40 IU·mL−1, diluted with saline) at a dose of 0.75 U·kg−1 via intraperitoneal injection after fasting; blood samples were collected at the same time points. If blood glucose levels during the ITT were < 2.0 mmol·L−1 or obvious signs of hypoglycemia (lethargy, tremors, convulsions) occurred, the mouse was immediately treated by oral administration of 20% glucose; data from that mouse for that ITT session were excluded from the analysis. Water was withheld from the time of oral administration or injection until the end of blood sampling at 120 min; the interval between the OGTT and ITT was ≥ 5 days; the final FBG was measured at 0 min on the day of the OGTT. The area under the glucose curve (AUC) was calculated by the trapezoidal method: AUC = Σ i ½ × (G i + G i+1) × (t i+1 − t i ), where G i is the blood glucose concentration at time point ti; AUC is expressed in mmol·min·L−1. The ITT percentage decrease was calculated as ITT % decrease = (G 0 − G min)/G 0 × 100%, where G 0 is the fasting (0‐min) glucose and G min is the lowest glucose recorded during the ITT.

Serum Biochemistry and Insulin Assay

On the day of final sampling, after a 6‐h fast, approximately 500 μL of blood was collected from the retrobulbar venous plexus under deep isoflurane anaesthesia, followed by confirmation of death via cervical dislocation. Whole blood was centrifuged at 4°C at approximately 870 × g (3000 rpm, Thermo Fisher Fresco 21) for 15 min to separate serum, which was then stored at −80°C.

Fasting insulin levels were measured manually using a mouse insulin ELISA kit (CUSABIO, catalogue number CSB‐E05071m) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and readings were taken at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Huison MB‐530). Total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), uric acid (UA), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured on the Roche cobas 8000 fully automated biochemical analyser (c702 module; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany; regularly calibrated by the Hunan Provincial Institute of Metrology and Testing): TC, TG, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, BUN, CREA, and UA were measured using original Roche cobas reagents (enzymatic colorimetric method/colorimetric method); ALT (alanine substrate method) and AST (aspartate substrate method) were measured using reagents from BSBE (Beijing) compatible with the cobas c701/c702 (catalogue numbers 10 303 107 001 and 10 303 115 001, respectively). Detailed comparisons of the measured values for each parameter with the physiological reference ranges for C57BL/6J [38] are provided in Table S4.

Histopathology, Oil Red O Staining, and Quantitative Immunohistochemistry

Liver, pancreas, and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm using a microtome (Thermo Scientific HM 325) for haematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining to assess the morphology of liver, kidney, and pancreatic tissues and to count the number of islets. Fresh liver tissue was embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium and sectioned at 10 μm using a cryostat (Epredia CryoStar NX50; Epredia, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Sections were stained for 5 min at room temperature with 0.3% modified Oil Red O (Solarbio, catalogue no. G1261) and counterstained with haematoxylin. Pancreatic tissue was subjected to anti‐insulin immunohistochemistry: after inactivating endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2, heat‐mediated antigen retrieval was performed using Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0); the anti‐insulin primary antibody (clone EPR17359, Abcam ab181547, RRID:AB_2716761) was applied at a 1:6400 dilution overnight at 4°C; a universal two‐step polymer‐amplified detection system (Zhongshan Jinqiao ZSGB, catalogue no. PV‐9000, containing reaction enhancer and enzyme‐labelled secondary antibody) was used, followed by diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining and counterstaining with haematoxylin.

All sections were scanned using the TissueFAXS Plus whole‐slide scanning system (TissueGnostics GmbH, Vienna, Austria) at 200× magnification; quantification was performed using Fiji software: for liver Oil Red O staining, five fields of view (left, right, top, bottom, and centre) were selected from each section, and the percentage of positive area relative to the total tissue area was calculated, with the mean of the five fields taken; islet count was determined by counting HE‐stained whole pancreatic sections; the area of insulin‐positive β‐cells was calculated as the total area of insulin‐positive regions in whole sections stained by immunohistochemistry. Each quantification was performed on 6 animals per group, using 1 section per animal. The measurements were independently conducted by two researchers blinded to the group assignments, and the mean was calculated. When discrepancies exceeded 20%, the results were jointly reviewed.

Cost Calculation for Model Generation

Cost per mouse = Mouse purchase cost (¥29/mouse) + HFD feed cost + Cage cost + STZ injection cost. HFD feed cost = measured average food intake (females 1.866, males 2.165 g·d−1·mouse−1) × total feeding days × ¥2·g−1; cage housing cost is ¥0.92·mouse−1·d−1 (including housing, disinfection, and bedding); STZ injection cost is ¥2.5 per injection. The exchange rate is ¥6.81 = $1, and the euro is converted at a rate of €1 ≈ ¥7.80. These costs are based on the current fee schedule of the Laboratory Animal Center at Hunan University of Chinese Medicine for 2025–2026; due to significant differences between Europe and the United States, the cost‐effectiveness estimate is context‐dependent and the absolute amounts cannot be directly extrapolated; each institution should recalculate per‐animal cost from its own current local prices (mouse purchase, diet, housing, and STZ) before comparison (see Section 4.3 for details).

Data Inclusion Strategy and Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. There were no STZ‐related deaths or animal dropouts during the modelling period; all enrolled animals were included in the analysis. Except for individual ITT data points excluded due to hypoglycaemia treatment, outliers were not removed, and missing values were automatically handled by the model. Inclusion strategy: Section 3.1 (obesity phenotype analysis) included all enrolled animals; Sections 3.2, 3.4 (model phenotype descriptions) included only animals that developed obesity.

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 (NumPy v1.26, SciPy v1.17, statsmodels v0.14) and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Longitudinal data (body weight, food intake, caloric intake, and longitudinal FBG) were analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model (LMM, REML estimation; random intercept = per mouse/per cage), with fixed effects of Sex × Diet × Time. Cross‐sectional data were analysed using two‐way ANOVA (Sex × Diet) + Šídák simple effects (k = 2); one‐way ANOVA + Šídák (k = 3) was used for the three STZ groups or the three STZ‐vs‐HFD‐12w groups; a three‐way ANOVA (Sex × Diet × Cohort, Type III sum of squares, sum‐to‐zero coding) was used for the combined HFD‐12w/16w/20w groups; The HFD‐STZ group was excluded from the pooled ANOVA because cumulative doses were not comparable between females and males. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d (pooled SD; Table S3); correlations were calculated using Pearson's r, with 95% CIs derived via Fisher's z‐transformation. Significance symbols: In the main figure, ‘*’ indicates differences between diet/treatment groups within the same sex, and ‘#’ indicates differences between females and males within the same diet/treatment group; */# p < 0.05, **/## p < 0.01, ***/### p < 0.001, ****/#### p < 0.0001.

Results

HFD Induces Equivalent Obesity Phenotypes in Both Sexes of C57BL/6J Mice

To systematically compare the metabolic phenotypes of male and female mice under HFD feeding and the combined HFD‐STZ intervention regimen, 5‐week‐old male and female C57BL/6J mice were stratified by sex and randomly assigned to five groups: HFD‐12w, HFD‐16w, and HFD‐20w (three groups receiving sole HFD feeding), as well as HFD‐STZ‐L and HFD‐STZ‐H (two groups receiving injections of different cumulative doses of STZ following the conclusion of HFD feeding) (Figure 1A; see Section 2.2 for the specific protocol).

Under HFD feeding, body weight in both sexes of mice showed a sustained increase, with a clear divergence in growth trajectories compared to their respective ND groups (Figure 1B). A longitudinal mixed‐effects model for the HFD‐20w group revealed that the third‐order interaction term Sex × Diet × Week was not significant (p = 0.554; Table S1), indicating that the shape of the HFD‐induced weight gain trajectory was consistent between the two sexes; similarly, in the three‐way ANOVA combining the three HFD‐only groups, the Sex × Diet interaction was also not significant (p = 0.726; Table S1). The same was true for weight gain rates, where the three‐way Sex × Diet interaction was not significant (p = 0.286; Table S1; Figure S1). Therefore, the absolute difference in final body weight between females and males in the HFD‐20w group (females 31.8 vs. males 38.3 g) primarily stems from baseline sex differences rather than an asymmetric effect of the HFD between the sexes.

Data on food intake and caloric intake further indicate that both sexes reached a similar degree of caloric excess. The average daily food intake in grams was significantly lower in the HFD group than in the ND group (Figure 1C,D; p < 0.0001 for both females and males), consistent with compensatory food restriction caused by the high energy density of the HFD; however, because the energy density of the high‐fat diet (5.24 kcal·g−1) was much higher than that of the standard diet (3.82 kcal·g−1), average daily calorie intake remained significantly higher than that of the ND group (Figure 1E,F; p < 0.0001 for both females and males). The Sex × Diet interaction for food intake (grams) was significant (p < 0.001), but the relative increase in caloric intake compared to their respective NDs was consistent between the sexes (the Sex × Diet interaction for caloric intake was not significant, p = 0.094). That is, although females consumed fewer grams of food, they achieved a relative caloric surplus comparable to that of males due to the high energy density of the high‐fat diet.

The extent of body fat accumulation induced by the HFD was also comparable between the sexes (Figure 2C–E; three‐way ANOVA Sex × Diet interaction p = 0.230; Table S1). Absolute fat mass further supports this, with females in the HFD‐20w group (5.01 g) and males (4.96 g) being nearly identical (Cohen's d = +0.02; Figure S1). Morphologically, perirenal fat (PWAT), perigonadal fat (gWAT; males = periepididymal, females = periuterine), and inguinal fat (iWAT) were significantly enlarged in both male and female mice in the HFD‐20w group compared to the ND group (Figure 2F; representative images of the HFD‐12w, HFD‐16w, and HFD‐STZ groups are shown in Figure S1). Overall, the ‘lower body weight’ in females primarily resulted from a lower lean body mass baseline rather than reduced fat accumulation.

The first time the group mean exceeded the ND × 1.20 threshold for the corresponding age group was at W9 for males and W11 for females; the timing of STZ injection was set accordingly (the red dashed line in Figure 1B indicates the obesity threshold). After STZ injection, the final body weights of both male and female groups showed no significant difference compared to the HFD‐12w group of the same age (Figure 1H; one‐way ANOVA, p ≥ 0.079), indicating that STZ did not significantly alter body weight levels under the HFD background. This study used population mean (rather than individual 100% obesity rate) as the criterion for the timing of STZ injection to balance the establishment of an obesity background with the controllability of the total modelling period; see Section 3.4 for details on the individual‐level obesity rate and final modelling rate in the HFD‐STZ group. However, significant sex differences were observed in body fat percentage: female STZ‐L and STZ‐H groups showed significantly lower values compared to the HFD‐12w group (Figure 2B; p Šídák = 0.011*/0.001**) and were dose‐dependent (STZ‐H was further reduced compared to STZ‐L), whereas body fat percentages in all male groups were approximately 9%–10% with no significant differences between groups (ANOVA overall p = 0.51 ns), indicating that the STZ‐induced reduction in peripheral fat was significant in females but not apparent within the male dose range used in this study. Individual‐level analysis further confirmed this: using the mean of the same‐sex ND group plus 2 SD as the threshold (5.90% for females, 6.82% for males), the final body fat retention rates in female HFD‐STZ‐L and HFD‐STZ‐H groups were 66.7% (6/9) and 11.1% (1/9), respectively, while in males, these rates were 71.4% (5/7) and 100% (6/6), respectively (Table 3).

Sex Dimorphism in Glucose Metabolic Dysfunction and Assessment of Islet Morphology

In sharp contrast to the sex equivalence observed in obesity‐related dimensions, glucose metabolism exhibited clear sex dimorphism under HFD feeding. Longitudinal FBG monitoring from Weeks 0–20 revealed that FBG levels were systematically elevated in both sexes compared to the ND group, but neither reached the T2DM threshold (Figure 3A). In the HFD‐20w group, a significant third‐order interaction (Sex × Diet × Time) was observed in the longitudinal mixed‐effects model (p < 0.0001; Table S1), suggesting that not only did the magnitude of glucose metabolism differ between sexes, but the shape of the trajectory also differed. A three‐way ANOVA combining the three HFD‐only groups further revealed that the Sex × Diet interaction was significant for 2‐h blood glucose, ITT‐AUC, and ITT % decrease (p = 0.0004***/0.037*/0.006**; Table S1), whereas the Sex × Diet interaction for OGTT‐AUC and fasting insulin was not significant (p = 0.112/0.527).

In terms of time‐course progression, male glucose metabolism was already significantly and extensively impaired at the HFD‐12w time point, with OGTT‐AUC, 2‐h blood glucose, ITT‐AUC, and the percentage decrease in ITT all significantly higher than in the ND group (p Šídák = < 0.0001****/0.0002***/0.0013**/0.0035**); in contrast, during the same period, only OGTT‐AUC was significantly elevated in females (p Šídák = 0.0029**), while the remaining dynamic indicators did not reach statistical significance, indicating that glucose metabolism impairment in females was only in its early stages. By HFD‐16w, both female FBG and 2‐h blood glucose levels reached significant levels (p Šídák = 0.0094**/0.0006***); by HFD‐20w, FBG, 2‐h, and OGTT‐AUC were consistently significant in both sexes. Notably, female ITT‐AUC and ITT% decrease did not reach significance throughout the three cohorts (Figure 4N,P,R,X,Y), in contrast to male ITT‐AUC, which was significant at HFD‐12w and HFD‐20w, and the F versus M comparison within the HFD group was ##, indicating that female insulin sensitivity is generally preserved under HFD feeding. This sex‐specific difference is most clearly demonstrated by the stable marker ITT‐AUC, and its mechanism is closely related to the multifaceted protective effects of E2 (discussed in Section 4.2). As a static indicator, FBG generally lagged behind dynamic indicators: at 12 weeks, the HFD did not reach significance in either sex (males d = +0.41, females d = −0.07), and it was not until 16 weeks that both sexes reached significance successively. Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) appeared before impaired fasting glucose (IFG), and this classic prediabetic progression pattern was reproduced in this study.

At the mechanistic level, the two dynamic indicators were significantly positively correlated in both sexes within the HFD‐STZ cohort (combined ND + STZ‐L + STZ‐H groups) (OGTT‐AUC × ITT‐AUC: females r = +0.72, males r = +0.87; both p < 0.001; Figure S2A,B), suggesting that these two measures jointly reflect the same dimension of impairment in the insulin resistance–glucose disposal axis. Body fat percentage was also significantly positively correlated with OGTT‐AUC in the combined subgroup of the HFD‐12w/16w/20w groups for both sexes, with similar correlation strengths (female r = +0.67, male r = +0.62; all p < 0.0001; Figure S2C,D), suggesting that peripheral fat expansion driving glucose metabolism impairment is a mechanism shared by both sexes.

From HFD‐12w to HFD‐20w, glucose metabolism parameters generally plateaued under HFD intervention alone and did not deteriorate significantly over time (P_trend values for linear regression of all 6 glucose metabolism parameters in the HFD‐only subset were all > 0.10), indicating that HFD alone is insufficient to induce a natural progression from Pre‐DM to T2DM within 20 weeks, which forms the rationale for introducing β‐cell toxic injury (STZ) to establish a complete Pre‐DM/T2DM phenotypic spectrum. In this study, different cumulative STZ doses were set for the two sexes (females 90/180 vs. males 30/90 mg·kg−1), based on the fact that female C57BL/6J mice are reported to be less sensitive to STZ than males [15, 27, 28]; the upper limit of 90 mg·kg−1 for male STZ‐H was also directly supported by our group's preliminary experiments (all 5/5 subjects with complete data in the HFD‐STZ‐VH regimen with a cumulative dose of 180 mg·kg−1 crossed into the T2DM range; Figure S2G–J). The HFD‐STZ‐L group was equivalent to the age‐matched HFD‐12w group in both sexes, with all 6 glucose metabolism indices showing no significant differences (p Šídák ns) (Figures 3C,H and 4F,H). The phenotypes in both sexes replicated the characteristics of their respective HFD‐12w groups, but the HFD‐STZ‐L group achieved equivalent phenotypes in a shorter timeframe (10 weeks for males/12 weeks for females), and the conversion rate to the Pre‐DM state reached 100% in both sexes (see Section 3.4 for details). HFD‐STZ‐H exhibited high‐dose overshoot: females (cumulative 180 mg·kg−1) showed further increases in FBG, 2‐h glucose, OGTT‐AUC, and ITT‐AUC compared to HFD‐12w (p Šídák = 0.0094**/0.0017**/< 0.0001****/0.012*), with 2/9 crossing the FBG threshold of ≥ 13.9 and 4/9 crossing the 2‐h threshold of ≥ 16.7 mmol·L−1 (modelling rate 44.4%, 4/9); males exhibited further elevations in FBG and 2‐h blood glucose at just 90 mg·kg−1 (equivalent to the female HFD‐STZ‐L group) (p Šídák = 0.0034**/0.007**), with 4/5 crossing the FBG threshold and 2/5 crossing the 2‐h threshold (modelling rate 80%, 4/5), consistent with the previously reported direction of sex differences in STZ sensitivity. In males, OGTT‐AUC and ITT‐AUC did not increase significantly further in the HFD‐STZ‐H group compared to the STZ‐L and HFD‐12w groups (Figure 4F,H). This may be because AUC had already approached a ceiling in the high‐dose range; therefore, the determination of ‘overshoot’ relied on the proportion of individuals crossing the T2DM threshold rather than the group mean of AUC.

Insulin levels and pancreatic islet morphology remained generally stable. Fasting insulin levels did not differ significantly among the STZ groups (Figure 3L–P), and a three‐way ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of Diet on fasting insulin (p = 0.321; Table S1); the main effects of Diet on islet number and β‐cell area were near the threshold (p = 0.057/0.113; Figure 5A–J). Linear regression analysis revealed that in female HFD groups, islet number (slope = +1.25 islets·week−1, p = 0.024*) and β‐cell area (slope = +13.66 units·week−1, p = 0.045*) in the female HFD group increased significantly and positively with HFD duration, whereas no such trend was observed in males, suggesting a slow adaptive expansion of female β‐cells under prolonged HFD. Representative pancreatic HE and anti‐insulin IHC images are shown in Figure 5K,L. The stability of insulin and pancreatic morphology, in contrast to the impairment already shown by the concurrent OGTT/ITT results, aligns with the pathological characteristics where functional impairment predominates during the Pre‐DM stage, while morphological changes and β‐cell dedifferentiation—indicative of decompensation—are more common in the subsequent T2DM stage [39, 40, 41].

Sex Differences in Lipid Metabolic Disorders and Hepatic Steatosis

The serum lipid profile exhibited consistent, overlapping changes across all HFD‐fed groups. In the three HFD‐12w/16w/20w cohorts, total cholesterol (TC) was significantly elevated compared to the ND group (Figure 6A–E; p Šídák ≤ 0.007 for all female and male groups), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was also significantly elevated (Figure S3F–J; p Šídák ≤ 0.0002 for both female and male groups); serum triglycerides (TG) were significantly lower in the HFD‐12w and HFD‐20w groups compared to the ND group (Figure 6H,j; p Šídák ≤ 0.012 for both females and males), while the difference in HFD‐16w was not significant. A three‐way ANOVA revealed that the Sex × Diet interactions for TC, TG, and HDL‐C were all insignificant (Table S1), indicating that the magnitude of lipid changes induced by HFD was comparable between the sexes, consistent with the sex equivalence observed in obesity‐related dimensions. The increase in HDL‐C—a trend opposite to that observed in human clinical settings—reflects the species‐specific characteristic of C57BL/6J mice, which lack cholesterol ester transfer protein (CETP) and rely primarily on HDL as their lipid‐carrying particles [42]; the unexpected decrease in TG induced by the HFD is consistent with previous reports of adaptive remodelling of VLDL‐TG synthesis/secretion pathways in rodent HFD models [31, 43]. The overall response of LDL‐C was relatively weak (see Figure S3A–E for the complete panel).

Hepatic steatosis exhibited the strongest sex dimorphism induced by the HFD and was the metabolic parameter showing the greatest divergence in response between males and females in this study. The Oil Red O (ORO)–positive area in the liver was not significantly different (ns) in either female or male HFD‐12w groups (Figure 6 M); at 12 weeks, a hepatic lipid phenotype had not yet been established; starting from HFD‐16w, both female and male HFD groups showed a significant increase compared to the ND group, with the increase in males being significantly greater than that in females (Figure 6N,O): at HFD‐20w, males reached 38.17% (vs. ND 1.21%, ****), and females reached 22.77% (vs. ND 7.03%, ***). A three‐way ANOVA combining the three groups revealed highly significant interactions for both Sex × Diet and Diet × Cohort (F = 39.62/29.96, both p < 0.0001; Table S1), indicating that HFD‐induced hepatic lipid accumulation is not parallel between the sexes and is time‐dependent; the HFD‐20w group established a stable hepatic lipid accumulation phenotype in both sexes, with a significantly greater increase in males, consistent with early pathological features of MASLD [44], making it the preferred duration for MASLD‐related studies (representative liver ORO staining shown in Figure 6T).

At the mechanistic level, liver ORO and serum TG were significantly negatively correlated in both sexes across the combined subgroups of HFD‐12w, 16w, and 20w (females r = −0.51, males r = −0.52; all p ≤ 0.003; Figure 6P,Q), consistent with the lipid redistribution mechanism whereby ‘circulating TG is taken up by the liver and deposited as hepatic fat’ [43]. Body fat percentage was significantly positively correlated with hepatic ORO in both sexes, and the strength of this correlation was significantly greater in males than in females (females r = +0.76, males r = +0.89; both p < 0.0001; Figure 6R,S). This contrasts with the sex‐equivalent pattern observed for body fat percentage × OGTT‐AUC in Section 3.2—suggesting that peripheral fat expansion drives impaired glucose metabolism with equal intensity in both sexes, but drives hepatic fat accumulation significantly more in males than in females (mechanism detailed in Section 4.1). At the systemic level, Pearson correlation analysis of 14 core metabolic indicators within the single HFD‐20w group revealed 34 pairs with |r| > 0.7 in males (spanning peripheral fat, glucose metabolism, blood lipids, and hepatic fat; Figure S4B), whereas females exhibited 13 such pairs (Figure S4A). This indicates that, against the backdrop of prediabetic obesity, males exhibit a more synchronized cluster of metabolic syndrome features compared to females.

The HFD‐STZ group exhibited trends opposite to those of the HFD‐only group in terms of blood lipids and hepatic lipids, and the responses were highly asymmetric between females and males, consistent with the pattern observed in Section 3.1 regarding the reduction in body fat percentage in female HFD‐STZ animals. Female STZ‐L and STZ‐H groups exhibited significantly lower serum TC levels compared to the ND group (p Šídák = 0.0035**/0.0065**; Figure 6A), HDL‐C (p Šídák = 0.0005***/0.0052**; Figure S3F), and liver ORO (p Šídák < 0.0001****/0.0002***; Figure 6K), and all three parameters showed a synchronous significant decrease compared to HFD‐12w mice of the same age (Figure 6B,L; Figure S3G), indicating that the female HFD‐STZ model exhibits a phenomenon of ‘synchronous depletion of body fat, blood lipids, and liver fat’. In contrast, males exhibited a markedly different pattern: serum TC and HDL‐C were elevated compared to ND (TC p Šídák < 0.0001/0.0005; HDL‐C p Šídák ≤ 0.0006), with no significant difference compared to HFD‐12w; liver ORO did not reach significance compared to either ND or HFD‐12w (p Šídák ≥ 0.21). This indicates that lipid metabolism in males following STZ showed virtually no regression, consistent with the fact that the hepatic lipid phenotype had not yet been established in the HFD‐12w group itself. Notably, serum TG levels in the male STZ‐H group were abnormally elevated to 1.64 mmol/L (vs. male ND 0.90***; vs. HFD‐12w 0.71****; Figure 6F,G), suggesting an imbalance between VLDL‐TG synthesis and peripheral clearance under severe β‐cell damage (mechanism detailed in Section 4.1).

Safety Assessment and Cost–Benefit Analysis of the Model

Given that STZ exhibits non‐specific toxicity outside of β‐cells (including the liver and kidneys), and that long‐term feeding of a high‐fat diet (HFD) may cause damage to metabolism‐related organs, this study systematically evaluated biochemical and morphological indicators of liver and kidney function at the end point to verify that none of the treatment regimens introduced systemic target organ toxicity during the establishment of the prediabetes (Pre‐DM) model. Regarding liver and kidney safety, this study systematically evaluated five biochemical markers (ALT, AST, CREA, BUN, UA) against the physiological reference ranges for C57BL/6J [38]: among the 90 mean values (18 groups × 5 markers), only two were outside the normal range (marginal decrease in CREA in male STZ‐H, marginal increase in BUN in male HFD‐16w‐ND; see Table S4 for details). Overall, no consistent or progressive liver or kidney damage was observed. ALT and AST levels were mildly reduced or stable in most HFD/STZ groups, with no upward trend (Tables 1 and 2), consistent with the early pathological features of MASLD, where steatosis precedes hepatocyte injury [44]; BUN was significantly lower in multiple HFD/STZ groups compared to ND (Tables 1 and 2). UA exhibited clear sex dimorphism: male HFD‐STZ‐L, HFD‐STZ‐H and HFD‐20w groups all showed significantly higher levels than their respective ND controls (Table 1), consistent with HFD‐induced purine metabolism disorders [45]; in female HFD groups, UA levels showed no significant difference from ND (only female STZ‐L was elevated compared to the STZ control; Table 2), consistent with the renal protective mechanism of oestrogen inhibiting URAT1 [46]. Liver and kidney histology (Figure 7A: liver HE; Figure 7B: kidney HE) showed no obvious hepatocyte necrosis, fibrosis, or renal tubular atrophy, which was generally consistent with the biochemical findings; representative images of the remaining groups are shown in Figure S3L,M.

After confirming the safety of all modelling protocols, this study further conducted a systematic comparison of the five modelling protocols across eight dimensions: obesity modelling rate, maintenance of final body fat percentage, Pre‐DM modelling rate, over‐modelling (T2DM) rate, overall modelling rate, model consistency, modelling cycle, and cost per animal (Table 3). Under the optimization criteria adopted in this study, the HFD‐STZ‐L protocol (females: 11 weeks on HFD + 30 mg·kg−1 STZ × 3 days; males: 9 weeks on HFD + 30 mg·kg−1 STZ × 1 day;10–12‐week cycle) demonstrated the best overall performance in terms of overall model formation rate (73.3%), glucose metabolism CV% (< 10%), and cost per animal, making it the preferred choice for studies on glucose metabolism and pancreatic β‐cell mechanisms; its coefficient of variation for glucose metabolism is <10% (male FBG/2 h/OGTT‐AUC CV% were 4.8%/3.1%/8.1%, respectively; see Table S2), and it has the lowest nominal cost per animal (males ¥399, females ¥427; Table 3). It should be noted that the final body fat retention rate for female HFD‐STZ‐L is only 66.7%, and peripheral fat is partially stripped (Section 3.1), making them unsuitable for female studies requiring the preservation of a complete obesity phenotype—for such studies, female HFD‐12w mice are a more suitable choice. Their key advantages include the absence of STZ‐induced peripheral fat loss and a body fat percentage retention rate of 90.0%. Their cycle duration and cost are comparable to those of HFD‐STZ‐L, with only the overall modelling success rate (60.0%) being slightly lower than that of HFD‐STZ‐L (73.3%). The HFD‐20w model retains the complete hepatic lipid accumulation phenotype and is suitable for MASLD‐related studies; however, it entails higher sample loss due to a 50% male obesity modelling rate. The HFD‐STZ‐H model is not recommended as a Pre‐DM model due to its high modelling rate (44.4% in females, 80.0% in males); HFD‐16w is less cost‐effective than HFD‐12w and HFD‐20w and is therefore not recommended as a first choice. Discussion

In this study, we systematically established and compared Pre‐DM obesity modelling protocols in male and female C57BL/6J mice across two dimensions: sex and the effects of HFD duration and STZ administration. The three main findings are: HFD induces obese phenotypes of equal magnitude in both sexes, while glucose and lipid metabolism exhibit significant sex dimorphism; the HFD and HFD‐STZ models have opposite effects on glucose and lipid metabolism; and, based on this, we propose a sex‐stratified modelling protocol.

Sex Equivalence in Obesity Dimensions and Sex‐Specific Differences in Glucose and Lipid Metabolism and Their Mechanisms

The core finding of this study is that the metabolic effects induced by the HFD in both sexes exhibit completely different sex‐related patterns across different dimensions. Regarding obesity, the Sex × Diet interactions for final body weight, weight gain rate, body fat percentage, and absolute fat mass were all insignificant (Section 3.1); the absolute fat mass of HFD‐20w females and males was nearly equal. Although there were sex differences in food intake (grams), the degree of caloric excess was consistent between sexes—females achieved a relative caloric excess comparable to that of males with fewer grams of food, owing to the high energy density of the high‐fat diet. The lipid profile also showed sex equivalence: the Sex × Diet interactions for TC, TG, and HDL‐C were all insignificant (Section 3.3), consistent with the body weight dimension. The perception that ‘females are inherently resistant to HFD‐induced obesity’, formed from previous male‐biased studies, needs to be revised: females' ‘lower body weight’ primarily stems from a lower baseline lean body mass, rather than less fat accumulation; clinically, women with lower BMIs may also exhibit significant metabolic impairment.

In contrast to the sex equivalence observed in the obesity dimension, glucose metabolism and hepatic lipid metabolism exhibit clear sex dimorphism (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). In glucose metabolism, males showed earlier deterioration than females, and female ITT‐AUC and ITT % decrease did not reach statistical significance in the three HFD‐only cohorts throughout the study, indicating that insulin sensitivity is generally preserved in females under HFD feeding; the classic prediabetic progression pattern, in which impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) precedes impaired fasting glucose (IFG), was reproduced in this study and manifested within different time windows between the sexes. Regarding hepatic fat, the increase in males after 20 weeks on the HFD was significantly greater than in females, and the coupling strength between peripheral fat and hepatic fat was significantly stronger in males than in females. This contrasts with the sex‐equivalent relationship between body fat percentage and OGTT‐AUC in glucose metabolism—that is, while the driving force of peripheral fat expansion on glucose metabolism impairment is equivalent between sexes, the driving force on hepatic fat accumulation is significantly greater in males than in females, which is consistent with reports of distinct sex differences in MASLD in clinical and animal studies [22, 47]. At the systemic level, a 14‐variable correlation analysis revealed that after developing prediabetic obesity, males exhibited a more synchronized metabolic syndrome cluster compared to females.

The biological basis of sexual dimorphism is consistent with the multifaceted protective effects of oestrogen (E2). At the β‐cell level, E2 activates anti‐apoptotic proteins of the Bcl‐2 family via ERα/ERβ signalling, thereby inhibiting STZ‐induced DNA damage and oxidative stress [28, 48]; and attenuates oxidative stress‐induced β‐cell apoptosis via the GPER pathway [49]; enhances pancreatic antioxidant defences to neutralize STZ‐generated reactive oxygen species [50]; and reduces endoplasmic reticulum stress in β‐cells via the adaptive branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR) [51]. At the liver and peripheral fat levels, E2, via ERα, enhances PPARα‐mediated fatty acid β‐oxidation and inhibits SREBP‐1c‐mediated de novo lipid synthesis, thereby counteracting HFD‐induced excessive hepatic lipid accumulation [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]; E2 promotes subcutaneous fat storage rather than visceral fat deposition, thereby maintaining insulin sensitivity [20, 23]; and alleviates chronic inflammation in adipose tissue by inhibiting NF‐κB [24, 25], with chronic low‐grade inflammation itself being a key link between obesity and insulin resistance [52, 53]. At the renal level, E2 reduces uric acid reabsorption mediated by transporters such as URAT1. This mechanism is consistent with the observation in this study that UA levels were significantly elevated in the male HFD/STZ group but showed no significant changes in the female HFD groups [46]. Specifically, E2 downregulates hepatic lipid de novo synthesis via the ERα‐PPARα/SREBP‐1c axis, which aligns with the observation in Section 3.3 that ‘the coupling between peripheral fat and hepatic fat is significantly weaker in females than in males’ (r = +0.76 vs. +0.89)—suggesting that the female liver can partially ‘decouple’ from peripheral fat input; in contrast, the moderate correlation between body fat percentage and OGTT‐AUC in both sexes (r = +0.67 vs. +0.62) suggests that this regulatory mechanism does not exert an equivalent decoupling effect on glucose metabolism pathways, thereby echoing the asymmetric pattern of ‘liver‐fat dichotomy and glucose metabolism‐driven coupling of equal strength’ between the sexes.

The empirical evidence from this study comes primarily from the HFD‐duration dimension: under the same diet, the feeding duration required for females on a high‐fat diet (HFD) alone to reach a prediabetic state was 33% longer than that for males (16 vs. 12 weeks), consistent with this protective mechanism. The three‐fold higher cumulative STZ dose used in females (90 vs. 30 mg·kg−1) was set a priori from prior literature under the equal‐phenotype principle (Section 2.2); because the two sexes were not compared at an equal STZ dose, this dose difference should be regarded as a design parameter consistent with previous reports rather than an independently demonstrated sex dimorphism in STZ sensitivity (confounding discussed in Section 4.4). It is important to emphasize that while females required a longer HFD duration—and, under the equal‐phenotype strategy, a higher cumulative STZ dose—for their glucose metabolism and hepatic lipid damage to reach the threshold, this ‘slower’ progression is strictly limited to the glucose and hepatic lipid dimensions; in terms of obesity, the rate at which females develop the model and their absolute fat mass are identical to those of males. Therefore, the previous general impression that ‘females exhibit greater resistance to metabolic stress’ should be refined to ‘females require stronger metabolic stress to reach the Pre‐DM/MASLD threshold for glucose metabolism and hepatic lipid damage, but their modelling in the obesity dimension is not slower’, rather than the blanket assumption that ‘females are generally difficult to model’.

Differing Effects of HFD and HFD‐STZ Models on Glucose and Lipid Metabolism

Regarding glucose metabolism, STZ exacerbates the effects of HFD: HFD‐STZ‐L and HFD‐12w were equivalent in both sexes across six glucose metabolism indicators (p Šídák, all ns), but STZ‐L achieved equivalent phenotypes in a shorter timeframe (10 weeks for males/12 weeks for females) to achieve equivalent phenotypes while maintaining a 100% Pre‐DM modelling rate; HFD‐STZ‐H exhibited high‐dose overshoot, with males reaching an 80% overshoot modelling rate at a cumulative dose of only 90 mg·kg−1, while females required 180 mg·kg−1 before significant overshoot occurred.

In contrast to the pattern observed in glucose metabolism, where ‘STZ exacerbates HFD‐induced damage’, the effects of HFD and HFD‐STZ on lipid metabolism are opposite: HFD feeding causes simultaneous accumulation of blood lipids and hepatic lipids, whereas STZ induces a regression in lipid metabolism under continued HFD feeding, with highly asymmetric patterns observed between the sexes. Female HFD‐STZ‐L and HFD‐STZ‐H groups showed significantly lower serum TC, HDL‐C and ORO‐positive area in the liver compared to the ND group and age‐matched HFD‐12w group, and body fat percentage also decreased synchronously; that is, the female HFD‐STZ model exhibited a synchronous reduction in body fat, blood lipids, and hepatic lipids, with STZ's effects on females primarily manifesting in lipid metabolism. Lipid metabolism in males, however, differed markedly from that in females: since the male HFD‐12w group had not yet established a hepatic lipid phenotype, liver ORO levels showed no significant changes compared to the ND group or the HFD‐12w group following STZ injection, and overall lipid metabolism showed virtually no regression; in the male STZ‐H group, serum TG levels were abnormally elevated to 1.64 mmol/L, suggesting an imbalance between VLDL‐TG synthesis and peripheral clearance under conditions of severe β‐cell damage.

The mechanism underlying lipid metabolism regression in female STZ‐treated mice may involve the synergistic action of multiple pathways. STZ enters β‐cells via GLUT2 and induces insufficient insulin secretion through alkylated DNA damage; insulin deficiency and an elevated glucagon‐to‐insulin ratio enhance fat mobilization and fatty acid β‐oxidation, thereby altering the substrates available for hepatic esterification and peripheral tissue uptake [54]; The short‐term loss of appetite following STZ injection further reduces energy intake and exogenous lipid loading. It should be noted that the classic STZ type 1 diabetes model, in the absence of a high‐fat diet (HFD) background, typically exhibits elevated plasma triglycerides, and SREBP‐1c‐mediated de novo lipid synthesis in the liver is not necessarily downregulated [54, 55], The synchronous regression of lipid levels observed in female subjects in this study, while maintaining an HFD background, suggests that the primary cause is more likely a decrease in substrate supply and energy intake rather than a simple suppression of hepatic de novo synthesis. These mechanisms are, in principle, valid for both sexes; however, the more pronounced phenotypic changes in females may be due to higher baseline fat and blood lipid levels under HFD feeding, allowing for a greater range of variation; In contrast, liver lipid accumulation had not yet been established in male HFD‐12w animals, leaving insufficient room for the regression effect of STZ to manifest. Such bidirectional contrasts in lipid metabolism have previously been reported in STZ type 1 diabetes models [54, 55], but this study provides the first direct evidence of phenotypic differences in lipid metabolism between the two models in both sexes, obtained through parallel comparisons within the same experimental series while maintaining HFD feeding.

The differing effects on glucose and lipid metabolism provide direct guidance for model selection: the two models are complementary rather than interchangeable—the HFD‐20w model is suitable for MASLD/NASH research, while the HFD‐STZ‐L model is suitable for studies on glucose metabolism and β‐cell mechanisms. This aligns with the approach of previous studies that used HFD or HFD‐STZ models to evaluate the protective effects of dietary interventions, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, and other treatments on insulin resistance, hepatic lipid levels, or β‐cell function [56, 57, 58].

Comprehensive Evaluation of Modelling Protocols and Recommendations for Application

Based on the above findings, this study provides recommendations for modelling protocols stratified by sex (Table 3). The HFD‐STZ‐L model is the preferred choice for studying glucose metabolism and pancreatic β‐cell mechanisms in both sexes, as it has the shortest cycle (10 weeks for males/12 weeks for females) and the lowest cost per animal (¥399/€51 for males, females ¥427/€55), the lowest CV% for glucose metabolism (< 10%), and an overall model establishment rate of 73.3% for both females and males. Researchers using this protocol should be aware that peripheral fat in females is partially stripped (body fat percentage retention rate of 66.7%); therefore, this protocol is not suitable for studies in females requiring the preservation of a complete obesity phenotype. For such studies involving females, the HFD‐12w protocol is a more suitable alternative: its key advantages include the fact that peripheral fat is unaffected by STZ, a body fat retention rate of 90.0%, and a cycle duration and cost comparable to those of the HFD‐STZ‐L protocol, at the cost of a slightly lower overall modelling rate (60.0%) compared to the HFD‐STZ‐L protocol; In males, HFD‐12w can also serve as a short‐cycle Pre‐DM alternative in cost‐constrained scenarios (overall modelling rate of 70.0%). HFD‐20w establishes a stable hepatic lipid accumulation phenotype in both sexes (with a significantly greater increase in males) and is the preferred choice for MASLD/NASH‐related studies; however, it entails higher sample loss due to a 50% obesity modelling rate in males. HFD‐16w offers a lower cost‐effectiveness ratio than both HFD‐12w and HFD‐20w in both sexes; while its cost falls between the two, its phenotype is less typical than that of HFD‐20w and its cycle is not as short as that of HFD‐12w, so it is not recommended as the first choice. HFD‐STZ‐H is also not recommended as a Pre‐DM model due to its high modelling rates of 44.4% and 80.0% for females and males, respectively, and an overall modelling rate of only 40.7% and 14.7%.

Cost estimates are based on current fees charged by a laboratory animal center at a university in Central China for 2025–2026. Cage fees, feed prices, STZ prices, and labor cost structures vary significantly in Europe and the United States; the absolute amounts listed in this paper should not be directly extrapolated; however, the relative ranking of the five protocols and the ‘duration + cost + model consistency’ weighted framework remain valuable for cross‐regional reference, as the difference in duration (10 vs. 20 weeks) far outweighs regional price differences, and the relative cost advantage of the short‐duration HFD‐STZ‐L protocol is expected to be further amplified in Europe and the United States. Overall, the cost‐effectiveness conclusions are context‐dependent: the absolute figures should not be transferred directly across institutions, and each laboratory should recalculate per‐animal cost from its own current local prices before applying the relative ‘duration + cost + model‐consistency’ framework.

Limitations of the Study

This study has the following limitations. First, the unequal cumulative STZ doses between females and males (90/180 mg·kg−1 for females vs. 30/90 mg·kg−1 for males) stem from the ‘equal phenotype’ design principle (see Section 2.2 for details); the resulting dose confound may mean that certain sex‐related effects in the HFD‐STZ group partially reflect dose differences. Therefore, this study primarily relied on ‘within‐sex’ group performance when making model recommendations to minimize this effect. Accordingly, the sex‐related effects in the HFD‐STZ groups are not interpreted as a sex dimorphism in STZ sensitivity; any statement about the sex difference in STZ dose holds only within the equal‐phenotype dosing strategy and in line with prior reports. Second, the estrous cycles of female mice were not systematically monitored; oestrogen levels can fluctuate by several‐fold across different phases of the estrous cycle, which may contribute to intra‐group variability. This study partially balanced this by including n ≥ 7 females per group and conducting repeated sampling over multiple days. The results show that the CV% for FBG and 2‐h blood glucose in female HFD‐STZ‐L mice were 12.6% and 10.7%, respectively. Although higher than those in males (4.8% / 3.1%), they remained within acceptable ranges (< 15%). Combined with the partial balance achieved by having n ≥ 7 females per group and repeated sampling over multiple days, this suggests that the lack of estrous cycle monitoring had limited substantive impact on the core conclusions; future studies could confirm the estrous cycle phase via vaginal smears prior to OGTT in each female mouse. Third, this study did not directly assess β‐cell secretory reserve (e.g., GSIS, in vitro islet perfusion); the assertion that ‘functional impairment precedes morphological changes’ is supported only by the combined evidence of OGTT/ITT results and stable islet morphology. Whether the phase I/phase II insulin secretion kinetics of β‐cells are altered requires further direct experimental validation. Fourth, this study used only the C57BL/6J strain; the functional knockout mutation in the Nnt gene of this strain confers a particular susceptibility to HFD‐induced metabolic disorders [7, 8, 9, 59]. The generalizability of the study's conclusions to other strains, such as C57BL/6N and ICR, as well as to rat models, remains to be verified. Fifth, the limited overnight fast used before the initial STZ injections was based mainly on the mechanistic rationale of lowering blood glucose to reduce glucose–STZ competition for GLUT2 uptake, and on prior convention, rather than on direct verification that it is required; to further reduce animal stress and improve welfare, future work could systematically compare fasted versus non‐fasted protocols for STZ modelling efficiency and phenotypic consistency.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically established and compared Pre‐DM obesity modelling protocols in male and female C57BL/6J mice across two dimensions—a mixed‐sex design with five groups—examining the effects of HFD duration and the additive effect of STZ. We draw the following three conclusions.

First, HFD induces dimension‐selective metabolic dimorphism between the sexes: obesity and blood lipid levels are equivalent between the sexes, while glucose metabolism and hepatic lipid metabolism exhibit significant sex dimorphism. This pattern aligns with the multifaceted protective mechanisms of oestrogen—particularly the specific downregulation of hepatic lipid synthesis by the ERα‐PPARα/SREBP‐1c pathway—and provides experimental evidence from concurrent studies in both sexes to correct the general impression that ‘females are resistant to HFD’ and to develop sex‐stratified modelling protocols.

Second, the HFD and HFD‐STZ models exert opposite effects on glucose and lipid metabolism: STZ exacerbates HFD‐induced damage in glucose metabolism (achieving equivalent prediabetes in a short cycle), while in lipid metabolism, it causes a simultaneous regression of body fat, blood lipids, and hepatic fat in females; in males, however, these remain largely unchanged because hepatic fat has not yet been established after 12 weeks on the HFD. The two model types thus constitute a complementary rather than an alternative relationship, respectively addressing the research objectives of glucose metabolism and MASLD.

Third, based on the above findings, the following sex‐specific modelling protocols are recommended: HFD‐STZ‐L is the preferred model for both sexes in studies of glucose metabolism and β‐cell mechanisms (note that peripheral fat is partially removed in females); for studies in females requiring the preservation of a complete obesity phenotype, HFD‐12w is a suitable alternative; HFD‐20w is the preferred choice for MASLD/NASH‐related studies; HFD‐16w and HFD‐STZ‐H are not recommended due to cost‐effectiveness concerns and modelling issues. This study emphasizes the necessity of incorporating biological sex into the modelling of metabolic diseases and the interpretation of phenotypes, providing a basis for practical protocol selection in relevant basic and translational research.
