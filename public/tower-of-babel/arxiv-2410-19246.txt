Paths and Intersections: Characterization of Quasi-metrics in Directed Okamura-Seymour Instances
Yu Chen, Zihan Tan

Abstract
We study the following distance realization problem. Given a quasi-metric D D on a set T T of terminals, does there exist a directed Okamura-Seymour graph that realizes D D as the (directed) shortest-path distance metric on T T ? We show that, if we are further given the circular ordering of terminals lying on the boundary, then Monge property is a sufficient and necessary condition. This generalizes previous results for undirected Okamura-Seymour instances. With the circular ordering, we give a greedy algorithm for constructing a directed Okamura-Seymour instance that realizes the input quasi-metric. The algorithm takes the dual perspective concerning flows and routings, and is based on a new way of analyzing graph structures, by viewing graphs as paths and their intersections . We believe this new understanding is of independent interest and will prove useful in other problems in graph theory and graph algorithms. We also design an efficient algorithm for finding such a circular ordering that makes D D satisfy Monge property, if one exists. Combined with our result above, this gives an efficient algorithm for the distance realization problem.

Our Results

Preliminaries

By default, all graphs considered in this paper are
directed
edge-weighted graphs.

Let
G
be a plane graph (that is, a planar graph with its embedding). For a pair
u,v
of its vertices, we denote by

the

distance in

to

(that is, the minimum length of any directed path in

from

to

It is clear that

is a quasi-metric on the vertices of

Let

be a set of its vertices called

(G,T)
is an Okamura-Seymour instance, iff in the planar drawing of

We say that two pairs
(t_{1},t_{2}),(t_{3},t_{4})
of terminals

, iff
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}
are distinct terminals, and the order in which they appear on the boundary of the outer face is either
t_{1},t_{3},t_{2},t_{4}
or
t_{1},t_{4},t_{2},t_{3}
.

Demands and routings.

A

(G,T)
is a function
{{\mathcal{D}}}
that assigns to each ordered pair
(t,t^{\prime})
of terminals an integer
{{\mathcal{D}}}(t,t^{\prime})\geq 0
.
Alternatively, we can view a demand

as a collection of pairs of terminals in

(t,t^{\prime})

We say that
{{\mathcal{D}}}
is

(t,t^{\prime})

For an unweighted demand

of

in

of directed paths in

, such that all paths in

We say that

Partial quasi-metrics.

A
partial quasi-metric
is a function
D:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\left\{*\right\}
, such that

•

for all
t,t^{\prime}\in T

D(t,t^{\prime})=0
iff
t=t^{\prime}
; and

•

for all
t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in T
, such that none of
D(t,t^{\prime\prime}),D(t,t^{\prime}),D(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})
is
*

D(t,t^{\prime\prime})\leq D(t,t^{\prime})+D(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}).

Intuitively, partial quasi-metrics are quasi-metrics whose values are not yet completely determined, and for those values that are already determined, the triangle inequalities need to be satisfied.
For a graph
G

3
Preparation: Nests, Edge-weight LP, and Restricting Pairs

Let
D
be a quasi-metric on
T
and
\sigma
a circular ordering on
T
that satisfy the property in
Theorem

1
. We will construct an Okamura-Seymour instance whose induced shortest path distance metric on

is exactly

.
To be more specific, we will construct a specific type of Okamura-Seymour instances, called

, via a greedy approach, called

, that we introduce below.

3.1
Graph structure: nests and Path Insertion

Nests.

We say a directed Okamura-Seymour instance
(G,T)
is a
nest
, iff
G
consists of a collection
\Pi
of directed paths, such that

•

every path
\pi\in\Pi
has both endpoints in
T
;

•

the paths in
\Pi
are edge-disjoint; and

•

E(G)=\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi}E(\pi)
.

When the set
T
of terminals are clear from the context, we also say that
G
is a nest.

Path Insertion.

We now describe a generic process of constructing a nest, called
path insertion
. We start with an empty disc with all terminals in
T
lying on its boundary according to the ordering
\sigma
. The path insertion process performs iterations. In each iteration, we insert a directed path named
\pi_{t,t^{\prime}}
connecting an ordered pair
t,t^{\prime}
of terminals in
T
, and draw it as a directed curve
\gamma
within the disc connecting its endpoints, such that

•

\gamma
is a simple curve from
t
to
t^{\prime}
;

•

for every other curve
\gamma^{\prime}
inserted before, curves
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
intersect at a finite number (could be
0
, in which case they are disjoint) of points, called their
crossings
; and

•

at each crossing, the curves indeed cross each other (but not just touch and then depart).

At any time over the course of a path insertion process, a nest is obtained by viewing each crossing as a vertex and viewing each curve segment between a pair of its consecutive crossings as a directed edge.
See
Figure

3
for an illustration.

The path insertion process inserts
3
paths, shown in red, green, and purple, respectively.

(b)

The nest obtained from the path insertion process is a directed graph on

vertices.

We view the input quasi-metric
D
as given to us in a stream: we sequentially receive
|T|\cdot(|T|-1)
tuples, each consisting of an ordered pair
(t,t^{\prime})
and their value
D(t,t^{\prime})
. Equivalently, we are given a partial quasi-metric
D
which initially assigns
*
to all pairs, and then it gets refined entry by entry until we get the complete quasi-metric
D
.

We will run the path insertion process along with the stream. Upon receiving the value
D(t,t^{\prime})
for a pair
(t,t^{\prime})
, we insert the path
\pi_{t,t^{\prime}}
from
t
to
t^{\prime}
. Let

be the nest produced by all paths inserted so far, so

evolves with the stream. The goal is to ensure the following property:

P1.

at any time in the stream, there exists a way of setting the edge weights of

that makes it satisfy the current

; moreover, for every
(t,t^{\prime})
,
\pi_{t,t^{\prime}}
is the shortest

-

directed path in

Note that, if this property is satisfied, then at the end of the process, we obtain a nest

, such that there exists a way of setting its edge weights to satisfy the whole quasi-metric

3.2
Setting the edge weights: LP and its dual

For a nest
G
to satisfy a partial quasi-metric
D
, the existence of edge weights can be characterized by the following LP (a feasibility LP without any objective function). We say that a pair
(t,t^{\prime})
is
seen
if
D(t,t^{\prime})\neq*
(i.e., we have already received the pair
(t,t^{\prime})
and have inserted
\pi_{t,t^{\prime}}
in

).

(LP-Primal)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(\pi_{t,t^{\prime}})}x_{e}\leq D(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\forall\text{ seen }(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(P)}x_{e}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\forall\text{ seen }(t,t^{\prime}),\forall\text{ di-path }P\text{ from }t\text{ to }t^{\prime}

\displaystyle x_{e}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall e\in E(G)

By Farkas’ lemma, the feasibility of (LP-Primal) is directly related to the feasibility of its dual.
To exploit it, we need the following flow-related definitions.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the collection of paths in
G
with both endpoints in
T
. A
terminal flow

assigns each path

with a value

.
For each edge

the total amount of flow of

sent through

For a pair

of terminal flows, we say that

dominates

for all

.
For each pair

of terminals, we define

to

We define the

The following claim is an immediate corollary of Farkas’ lemma

Claim 3
.

Either

Claim 4

Upon receiving any pair

Proof.

For a pair of flows

We construct a graph

4.1

First we introduce some definitions.
Recall that

We say that a path

does not violate the capacity of

\big|\left\{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}\mid e\in E(Q)\right\}\big|\leq\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{C}}(t,t^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{1}[e\in\pi_{t,t^{\prime}}]
.

In other words, if

. Here we let

.

The structure of induction.

We will prove

Finding the Circular Ordering: Proof of
Theorem

2

In this section, we give an efficient algorithm for determining if the input quasi-metric
D
is realizable by some directed Okamura-Seymour instance. Specifically, the algorithm will decide if there exists a circular ordering
\sigma
, such that
D
satisfies Monge property with respect to
\sigma
, and computes one if it exists.

First, some intuitions of our algorithm. Imagine that we have somehow figured out that in the true ordering
\sigma
, terminals
a,b
appear consecutively. We can use this knowledge to discover the locations of other terminals in
\sigma
as follows. Consider a pair
c,d\in T
, and we compute
D(a,c)-D(b,c)
and
D(a,d)-D(b,d)
. If
D(a,c)-D(b,c)>D(a,d)-D(b,d)
, then by simply reorganizing terms we get

D(a,c)+D(b,d)>D(a,d)+D(b,c)
, and so
(a,d)
and
(b,c)
cannot cross due to Monge property, and so
a,b,c,d
appear in this order.
Similarly, if
D(a,c)-D(b,c)<D(a,d)-D(b,d)
, then we can derive that
a,b,d,c
must appear in this order. Therefore, we calculate
D(a,t)-D(b,t)
for all other terminals
t
, and if all these values are distinct, we can immediately find the true ordering
\sigma
.

The hard case is when many of the values
\left\{D(a,t)-D(b,t)\right\}_{t\in T}
are the same, and we are incapable of determining the relative ordering on these terminals. However, as long as not all these values are equal, we have still made progress by obtaining a refined ordering, for example we may now know that
a,b,c,d
must appear in this order, and between
d
and
a
there is another group of terminals. Then we can rely on
c,d
and compute the values
\left\{D(c,t)-D(d,t)\right\}
for terminals
t
in this group to further refine its ordering.
If at some point we cannot refine the ordering anymore, we will recurse on each group, try figuring out another consecutive pair (like the pair
(a,b)
we start with), and use this additional knowledge to further refine the ordering.

We now describe the algorithm in detail. We pick an arbitrary pair
a,b
of terminals, and run tests on them. The goal is to decide if they can appear consecutively on the boundary.
Throughout, we maintain a circularly ordered partition
\Sigma
of the terminals, starting with
\Sigma=(\left\{b\right\},T\setminus\left\{a,b\right\},\left\{a\right\})
(appearing clockwise in this order), and we will iteratively refine it. The sets in
\Sigma
are called
groups
, so initially there are three groups in
\Sigma
.

We will ensure that, at any point, for every four terminals
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}
such that (i) they belong to different groups in the current
\Sigma
; and (ii) their corresponding groups
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}
appear in
\Sigma
in this order, all Monge property inequalities on them are satisfied. After every iteration, we perform this test for all tuple of terminals, if some tuple does not satisfy Monge property, we terminate the algorithm and report
Fail
.

The tests will concern
3
groups and
4
terminals, where one group contains two terminals, denoted by
t_{1},t_{2}
, and others groups are singletons, denoted by
\left\{c\right\}
and
\left\{d\right\}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
\left\{c\right\},\left\{d\right\}
, and the group containing
t_{1},t_{2}
appear clockwise in this order.
The goal of this test is to try to decide the clockwise order on the four terminals, which is either
c,d,t_{1},t_{2}
or
c,d,t_{2},t_{1}
.

In all tests below, the criterion will imply that
c,d,t_{2},t_{1}
is the order. In other words, if we cut the circular ordering between
a
and
b
and obtain a linear ordering from
a
to
b
, then
t_{1}
appear before
t_{2}
in this ordering (i.e.,
t_{1}
is closer to

).
We also write

.
In particular, the criterion means that

should no longer be in the same group, and the old group containing them, which we denote by
S
, needs to be refined.

Test 1:
D(c,t_{1})+D(d,t_{2})<D(c,t_{2})+D(d,t_{1})
.

Reorganizing terms,
D(c,t_{1})-D(d,t_{1})<D(c,t_{2})-D(d,t_{2})
. For each terminal
t
in
S

and obtain an ordered partition
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
of

of terminals in
S

•

for indices

•

t,t^{\prime}

We then replace

Test 2:
D(t_{1},c)+D(t_{2},d)<D(t_{2},c)+D(t_{1},d)

Similar to Test 1, we can refine the group

\left\{D(t,c)-D(t,d)\right\}_{t\in S}

Test 3:

In this case, we refine the group

(S_{1},S^{\prime},S_{2})

For every

•

, then we put

;

•

in
S_{2}
;

•

in

.

Claim 11
.

For every
t\in S_{1}
,
t^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}\in S_{2}

Proof.

We prove

Since

and

D(c,t_{1})+D(t_{2},t)\geq D(c,t)+D(t_{2},t_{1}),\quad\text{and}\quad D(c,t_{1})+D(t_{2},t^{\prime})<D(t_{2},t_{1})+D(c,t^{\prime}).

Combined together,

D(c,t)-D(t_{2},t)\leq D(c,t_{1})-D(t_{2},t_{1})<D(c,t^{\prime})-D(t_{2},t^{\prime}),

which means
D(c,t)+D(t_{2},t^{\prime})<D(c,t^{\prime})+D(t_{2},t)
. By Monge property, the pairs

(t_{2},t^{\prime})

.
∎

Test 4:
D(t_{1},c)+D(d,t_{2})<D(d,c)+D(t_{1},t_{2})

Similar to Test 3, we refine the group

(S_{1},S^{\prime},S_{2})

and

We keep refining the ordered partition
\Sigma

If the algorithm never reports

We perform the above test on all pairs

Analysis of the algorithm

If the input quasi-metric

Therefore, it suffices to show that, given a valid ordering for each group in

it suffices to verify Monge property for any tuple

of
4

Case 1:

As we have constantly performed test for such tuple of

Case 2:

From our algorithm, we have created an instance on the group

Case 3:

,

,

Such a tuple will go through our four tests, and the fact that they pass the test (and

D(e,d)-D(e,c)=D(a,d)-D(a,c)=D(f,d)-D(f,c)
, and

On the other hand, since

D(c,e)-D(c,d)\geq D(a,e)-D(a,d)\geq D(f,e)-D(f,d)

Since the instance on

The Monge property on them is guaranteed.
