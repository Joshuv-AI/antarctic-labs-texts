Can social prescribing intervention reduce unplanned hospital usage in an ethnically diverse and deprived population: a quasi-experimental study using a dynamic staggered difference-in-differences approach
Authors: Bo Hou, Rachael H Moss, Mohammed Hammad, Sohail Abbas, Michael McCooe, John Wright, John Wildman, Trevor Sheldon, Josie Dickerson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of a social prescribing intervention (the Central Locality Integrated Care Service (CLICS)) on unplanned hospital usage in the city of Bradford.DesignA quasi-experimental study applying a dynamic staggered difference-in-differences (DID) analysis on a propensity matched cohort between 2019 and 2023, using data from the Connected Bradford dataset, a pseudonymised linked health dataset on the whole Bradford population.SettingCLICS was delivered within general practices in deprived and ethnically diverse inner-city areas of Bradford, Yorkshire, UK.ParticipantsIn total, 1304 CLICS patients were matched to 5216 control patients on key characteristics including ethnicity, deprivation, age, gender and health conditions.InterventionsA proactive social prescribing intervention that integrates clinical and non-clinical services, including an individualised approach to tailor support based on the patient’s needs, both within primary care services and by linking them to appropriate community-based assets/services.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the rate of unplanned hospital admissions and the secondary outcome was unplanned accident and emergency (A&E) attendances.ResultsCLICS patients were 2.1% (95% CI −3.8% to −0.4%, p=0.013) less likely to have an unplanned hospital admission and 2.4% (95% CI −4.6% to −0.2%, p=0.03) less likely to have an unplanned A&E attendance compared with the matched control. The DID analysis demonstrated a gradual increase in the association over time. Subanalyses revealed heterogeneity by ethnicity, the reduction in unplanned hospital admissions was observed only in patients of the Pakistani group, whereas the reduction in A&E attendances was statistically significant only in the white British group.ConclusionThe CLICS intervention was associated with a reduction in unplanned hospital usage. Social prescribing may be a valuable component of strategies to reduce health inequalities in unplanned hospital usage.

Introduction

Social prescribing is increasingly being advocated as part of personalised care and support. In England, the National Health Service (NHS) is investing significantly in referrals to social prescribing, planning, describing this as ‘the biggest investment in social prescribing by any national health system’.1 2

There are various models, but generally social prescribing is ‘an approach that connects people to activities, groups and services in their community to meet the practical, social and emotional needs that affect their health and wellbeing’.3 Social prescribing support is often delivered in primary care settings by the voluntary sector, local community and third sector services, which offer signposting to a service/activity, direct links to an activity and/or pairing with a link worker. The aim is to provide patients with the tools they need to address social causes of ill health, such as offering the opportunity to foster positive relationships and support with community groups.4

In England, the NHS has committed to social prescribing as part of the NHS ‘Long Term Plan’ with an aim of more than 900 000 referrals to social prescribing services by 2023/2024.2 NHS England stipulated the requirement for primary care to provide ‘proactive social prescribing’ to specifically target populations experiencing health inequalities.

Recent systematic reviews indicate that social prescribing improves physical and mental health and quality of life, as well as saving significant costs due to reduced numbers of general practitioner (GP) appointments and unplanned hospital admissions.3 5 However, the quality of this evidence is low, with studies limited by poor design such as small samples, lacking comparison groups, short follow-up and self-reported outcomes,6 7 leading to calls for more high-quality research to assess the effectiveness.8 Social prescribing is seen as an approach to help reduce health inequalities, but little research has been focused on communities with high levels of deprivation and there remains a notable gap in understanding its impact on minoritised ethnic communities.9

This paper reports the results of research which aimed to generate high-quality evidence on the impact of social prescribing on reducing health inequalities in an inner-city area of England with a highly ethnically diverse and deprived population. We focused on evaluating the impact on unplanned hospital use, (unexpected and urgent admissions alongside accident and emergency (A&E) admissions) which represent a major burden for patients, their families and health services.10 These are costly, create uncertainty for managers of health systems and can be unpleasant for patients and their families.11 Importantly, the rate and pattern of unplanned hospital admissions are a useful proxy indicator of health and health and social care inequalities.12 13 Based on previous findings, we hypothesise that participants who receive social prescribing will have less unplanned hospital usage than a matched population who did not receive the intervention.

This study is, as far as we are aware, the first to explore ethnic differences in the effects of social prescribing at a patient level on unplanned hospital admissions and A&E attendances; there was no prespecified hypothesis about the impact of social prescribing by different groups.

Methods

Study design and setting

A quasi-experimental study design using a dynamic staggered difference-in-differences (DID) analysis on matched cohorts was undertaken to evaluate the impact of a social prescribing intervention (delivered within usual practice) on unplanned hospital usage and health inequalities. In the absence of a randomised controlled trial, quasi-experimental methods using observational data are used to evaluate healthcare interventions and policies.14

Bradford is a large city in the North of England with high levels of deprivation and ethnic diversity.15 16 The 2021 census identified that high levels of deprivation in Bradford were almost double the rates of England deprivation levels.

The CLICS intervention

There is variability in the models of social prescribing that are described in the literature;17 to enable better understanding of the model delivered here, we describe this using the key design principles recommended by Oster et al.17

CLICS (Central Locality Integrated Care Service) was commissioned as a part of the reducing inequalities in communities (RIC) programme funded by the Bradford District and Craven Health and Care Partnership, formerly the Bradford City Clinical Commissioning Group (CCG). RIC was a multimillion pounds strategic health inequalities initiative targeting the most deprived inner-city areas of Bradford.18 CLICS was implemented in 25 inner-city GP practices (~150 000 patients) between September 2020 and March 2024.

CLICS functioned as a proactive, holistic case management service. The intervention used social prescribing principles,4 19 including an individualised approach to tailor support and applied an integrated approach, embedding community connectors within general practice settings. As a result, CLICS was able to use community-based assets to address wider determinants of health, including financial hardship, food insecurity, mental health and disability, alongside clinical service support to manage complex clinical needs, frailty assessments and medication reviews. These core components distinguish CLICS from traditional social prescribing models where link workers often sit outside the primary care team and may include light touch social prescribing such as signposting.

The CLICS team included, per 10 000 patients, 0.1 whole time equivalent (WTE) of a GP, 0.5 WTE of an advanced nurse practitioner, 0.5 WTE administrative support and 10 community connectors with knowledge of local assets.

On referral or proactive identification, patients underwent a comprehensive 45 min Holistic Needs Assessment with both the advanced nurse practitioner and a community connector. This assessment covered a wide range of needs including debt, social isolation, mental health, disability and clinical checks. Dependent on the patient’s needs they would then be assigned to the most appropriate pathway: primarily led by community connector; primarily led by the clinical advanced practitioner; or jointly led. All patients co-created a personalised care plan and their progress was reviewed at weekly multidisciplinary team meetings. The pathway was developed to provide multiple appointments by the same staff, so that time was available to build rapport with patients, to work with them to identify what was important to them and ultimately to provide the support they needed. Patients were discharged once their goals had been met or they withdrew from the service.18

Target population

There are two main cohorts included in CLICS:

Proactive: At-risk patients defined as carers, new asylum seekers, those with dementia and frailty diagnoses, anyone flagged as needing to shield during the pandemic and those flagged as ‘at risk’ during the pandemic. This was defined as having four or more out of the following 17 risk factors: 70 and over, body mass index 40 and above, chronic heart diseases, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, chronic respiratory diseases, cancer, diabetes, dementia, fall, frailty, end of life, hypertension, learning disabilities, spleen dysfunction, weak immune system and registered carer.

Reactive: Patients identified opportunistically by GP practices as in need, with no defined eligibility criteria.

Data

Patient-level primary and secondary care data from the Connected Bradford data resource were used. This dataset includes pseudonymised individual data on over 800 000 Bradford citizens linked across primary and secondary care, education and social care,19 20 with ethics and information governance approvals to be used as a research resource. We used a subset of the Connected Bradford dataset that included all patients registered with 1 of the 25 GP practices which received the intervention. A bespoke linked primary and secondary care patient-level dataset was built which included data from the CLICS intervention service.

The dataset included the period from 1 January 2019 to 31 August 2023, split into three periods:

The pre-intervention period (t<0) 1 January 2019 to 31 August 2020.

The pre-intervention period (t<0) 1 January 2019 to 31 August 2020.

The intervention period (t=0) 1 September 2020 to 30 June 2022.

The intervention period (t=0) 1 September 2020 to 30 June 2022.

The post-intervention period (t>0) 1 July 2022 to 31 August 2023.

The post-intervention period (t>0) 1 July 2022 to 31 August 2023.

Date of data extraction was December 2023.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was unplanned hospital admissions. The secondary outcome was unplanned A&E attendances. Unplanned hospital admissions identified by admission method codes in the Secondary Uses Service (SUS) data dictionary. Unplanned A&E attendances were identified as attendance categories according to the SUS data dictionary, excluding planned follow-up attendances.

Intervention and matched control group

The CLICS participants were identified using specific codes allocated to CLICS participants in the primary care data. Due to limited coding on pathways, we were not able to identify the specific pathways (eg, advanced practitioner and/or community connectors) that patients received.

To create a matched control group, we first identified a pool of adult patients (≥18 years) registered with 1 of the 25 GP practices participating in CLICS before 1 September 2020. We used a nearest neighbour matching method with a ratio of 1:4 (CLICS: control) based on: age, gender, ethnicity, 2019 Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) and the clinical risk factors eligibility criteria for CLICS. This ratio was based on a power calculation assuming we had a similar effect size as the Frome model—14% reduction in unplanned hospital admissions compared with the control group—we assumed 90% of the Frome model as it was done in a single practice whereas we have a diverse population.19 For best practice and to prevent issues around regression to the mean, we did not include outcomes in our matching process.21 This control group has received no intervention. Matching was conducted using the MatchIt (V.4.5.5) package in R.21

In total, 1304 CLICS patients were identified, from September 2020 to June 2022, who had received and been discharged from the intervention and 5216 matched control patients. Median length of treatment is around 3 months.

Statistical analysis

Data preparation and balance diagnostics

Data quality was assessed prior to analysis. There is no missing data in our analysis except for the 2019 IMD, as some CLICS patients had invalid residential postcodes in the primary care records. Missing IMD values were assigned a distinct ‘unknown’ category to preserve sample size and avoid exclusion bias. This was accounted for in the matching process using the large eligible population dataset we drew out control group from.

Baseline characteristics were compared between the intervention (CLICS) and control groups using independent t-tests for continuous variables and Pearson’s χ2 tests for categorical variables. Following nearest neighbour matching, balance was verified using standardised mean differences (SMD), with an SMD <0.1 considered indicative of negligible imbalance.

Dynamic Difference-in-Differences specification

The DID design is an established method that allows analysis of potential causal effects, with a treatment and control group.22 The Callaway and Sant’Anna dynamic staggered DID design was used for the estimation of multiple periods and variations in treatment timing.23 24 An advantage of this method compared with a standard two-way fixed effects (TWFE) estimator DID is that if there are heterogenous effects across treatment timing, the TWFE would potentially be biased due to patients receiving the treatment at different time points over several periods rather than at a single rollout time point. Further, this method is particularly suited to the evaluation of healthcare interventions and policies where there are limited measurable variables in the dataset and/or a lack of clear eligibility criteria.25

The dynamic staggered DID estimator used was specified as:

i=1, n, t=1, T, s=treatment group

yits denotes the outcome variable for the i-individual and the t-quarter in the treatment group. Parameters a and b capture person and quarter (person-quarter) fixed effects. The subscript p denotes the period relative to the quarter of treatment. We estimated eight leads and eight post-treatment effects (lags around) the treatment period, p=0. D is a dummy variable taken a value of 1 if the person has been treated in or before period p, and x is a vector of controls. The average treat effect on the treat is calculated from aggregating βp and γp estimates according to24 as an event study.

The DID estimates the average treatment effect on the treated sample (ATT) by aggregating ATT at groups level as an event study.23 In our analysis, never-treated units were used as controls. An ATT coefficient was calculated for the whole study period. Dynamic effects for each period of the CLICS intervention were estimated. For dynamic effects, we limited our analyses to the eight quarters before and eight quarters after the intervention time t=0. This allowed us to estimate post-discharge (from CLICS) impacts after about 2 years on the CLICS cohort treated in the full year (across four quarters).

Our outcomes were binary indicators (coded as 1 if the patient had at least one unplanned admission/attendance in the quarter, 0 otherwise). To account for this, and for the correlation of outcomes among patients within the same primary care practice, we estimate linear probability models using the Callaway and Sant’Anna estimator with robust SEs clustered at the GP practice level. While non-linear models (eg, logistic regression) ensure predictions remain within the (0, 1) interval, we are interested in the marginal effects26 and the Linear Probability Model (LPM) provides a more direct and transparent estimate of the ATT group, especially in DID designs, where interpreting interaction terms in non-linear models is prone to bias and complexity.27

To examine the variations in treatment effects by ethnic groups, the same analyses were repeated separately for the two largest ethnic groups in this population—white British and Pakistani.

Sensitivity analyses

To test the stability of our results, we repeated our main analysis on a balanced panel sample—data for patients who survived and stayed for the whole time—that excluded participants who died or moved out during the study period. The staggered DID method used here could allow for modelling of an unbalanced panel data.22 These are in the (onlinesupplemental tables 3 4).

Main analyses

Prior to the CLICS intervention period, the coefficients for DIDs between the CLICS and matched control groups showed small positive and statistically significant pre-trend difference (Pre-avg 0.4%, 95% CI 0.1% to 0.6%, p=0.001). This is especially true for the quarter prior to the intervention started Tm1 coefficient 2.7%, 95% CI 1.3% to 4.2%, p<0.001. After the CLICS intervention started, there is a gradual decrease in the rate of unplanned hospital admissions, consistent with the negative estimated coefficients. After four quarters, the CLICS group had a statistically significant lower rate of unplanned hospital admissions compared with the matched control group.

To assess the stability and robustness of the findings against potential attrition bias (eg, mortality or patients leaving the practice), we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a balanced panel design. This restricted the analytical cohort exclusively to individuals with complete data observations throughout the entire study period, excluding those who died or were lost to follow-up. The results of the balanced panel analysis (onlinesupplemental tables 3 4) remained consistent in both direction and magnitude with the main analysis. This confirmed that the observed reduction in unplanned admissions and A&E attendances was not an artefact of compositional changes in the cohort over time.

Discussion

This paper describes a quasi-experimental evaluation of a social prescribing intervention delivered as a part of standard practice in an inner-city setting with high levels of disadvantage and health inequalities. The study findings demonstrate that, for patients living in these deprived and ethnically diverse areas, receiving support from the social prescribing intervention CLICS was associated with a 2.1 percentage point reduction in unplanned hospital admissions and a 2.4 percentage point reduction in A&E attendances over a 2-year period. The dynamic DIDs demonstrated a gradual decrease in unplanned hospital admissions and A&E attendances over time with a larger association seen in the second-year after the intervention started. This suggests that the impact of social prescribing interventions may become stronger over-time and longer-term follow-up and evaluation of this cohort would be informative.

To estimate the absolute impact of this intervention, we used statistics from Hospital Episode Statistics for 2018/2019: Bradford city CCG had 19 230 unplanned hospital admissions,28 in an adult population of 105 000 which would translate to 385 fewer unplanned admissions per year in the adult population (2% reduction in unplanned hospital admissions) if CLICS was implemented. Assuming a £2800 per emergency admission,27 this would create a saving of £1 078 000 a year just on this. Additional savings would be made from avoided unplanned A&E attendances too and opportunity costs of time and efforts patients spent related to hospital visits.

These findings support those of previous studies, offering further robust evidence of the association of social prescribing on reduced hospital use of this intervention.7 19 24 29 The Frome study19 was the first large scale study in the UK that included a comparator group. It demonstrated an association of social prescribing with an average 14% reduction in the rate of unplanned hospital admissions compared with the control group. More recently, Wildman and Wildman19 30 used similar DiD methodologies to evaluate link worker social prescribing interventions for individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes in a city in North East England. They demonstrated that patients who received social prescribing had lower ‘non-elective care costs’ than those who had not engaged with social prescribing and also experienced improved glycated haemoglobin levels. Their research also reported a positive association between the level of patient engagement with social prescribing and reductions in subsequent healthcare admissions. The consistency of our results with these methodologically comparable studies strengthens the evidence that proactive, integrated social prescribing can effectively reduce acute hospital usage and costs.

While this study relies on routinely collected administrative data and cannot test causal pathways, the observed reduction in unplanned hospital usage may be explained by two broad mechanisms. First, by addressing the social determinants of ill health, the intervention likely improved patient resilience and reduced the physical triggers of acute admission. For example, resolving fuel poverty can prevent respiratory exacerbations, while addressing social isolation and home safety can reduce the risk of falls in frail elderly patients. Second, CLICS likely improved timely access to primary care. For older patients, particularly those with limited English proficiency or digital literacy, barriers to navigating appointment systems can lead to delayed presentation. Community Connectors functioned as a bridge, providing bilingual support and advocacy that enabled patients to access appropriate primary care services earlier. By resolving these issues upstream, the intervention may have prevented the escalation of minor ailments into conditions requiring urgent hospital admission.

Our estimated effect size was more modest than that in the Frome study. This may reflect methodological differences between the two studies including different populations, different timings of follow-up as well as variations in the social prescribing models being delivered.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we were able to use linked primary and secondary care data of those registered with a GP practice, which ultimately allowed us to identify and use a matched control group from the eligible population. Second, we used extensive individual level variables in routine data that enabled us to conduct a thorough matching procedure, reducing the influence of individual factors that may act as confounding variables. Third, the DID method suited our intervention particularly well as our interventions were gradually rolled out over several years and the dynamic staggered DID design prevents the use of forbidden comparisons between treatment and control groups.31

This is the first study reporting the dynamic effects of a social prescribing programme by ethnicity. The CLICS intervention was more effective in reducing unplanned hospital admissions for patients from the Pakistani heritage group. This might reflect differences in the characteristics of the white British and Pakistani heritage populations (by age, gender and chronic illness). The underlying causal mechanisms of how CLICS affects unplanned hospital usage for different populations requires further investigation.

There are, however, some important limitations. First, the CLICS intervention experienced some implementation challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic including changes to the eligibility criteria, meaning that some patient groups would not have been able to access CLICS for as long a duration as other patient cohorts. This may suggest the effect of the intervention is underestimated. The ‘community connector’ pathway of CLICS was also not delivered as planned during the COVID-19 lockdowns. We were not able to identify the community connector pathway of CLICS using routine data due to the impact of the pandemic on this pathway. Second, our results show the CLICS cohort had higher rates of unplanned hospital admissions and A&E attendances before intervention, this may suggest higher utilisation was the reason for referral to CLICS intervention and a regression to the mean issue and also may indicate that we did not capture all underlying risk factors in our matching. At a practical level, this may partly reflect the referral process conducted across GPs. Even though there is a difference in rates before intervention, the intervention reduces unplanned hospital admissions and A&E attendances, not simply back to a similar level as the controls but to a significantly lower level, suggesting that the intervention is having an effect.

Recommendations for research and practice

This study adds to the literature, offering a rigorous evaluation that demonstrates a clear association between social prescribing and unplanned hospital usage in a population experiencing health inequalities. Our evidence suggests that social prescribing can reduce unplanned hospital admissions and A&E attendances in an ethnically diverse and deprived population and may help to reduce health inequalities by providing additional community services designed to improve wider determinants of health. The current NHS long-term plan for social prescribing and for proactive social prescribing may be part of a solution to reducing health inequalities in the general population.

In order to understand more about the effective elements of these varying models of social prescribing, it is important that future studies describe their intervention in sufficient detail to enable comparison and identification of key effective components. Further exploration of potential differences in the effectiveness of different models on different communities is also needed.

Conclusion

This study adds to the evidence-base that social prescribing programmes are associated with a reduction in unplanned hospital admissions and unplanned A&E attendances in an ethnically diverse population living in deprived areas. Furthermore, our findings highlight important heterogeneity, suggesting that interventions may impact different ethnic groups differently. Proactive, integrated social prescribing models hold significant potential as a strategy to reduce health inequalities.
