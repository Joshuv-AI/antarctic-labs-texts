Online Multi-LLM Selection via Contextual Bandits under Unstructured Context Evolution
Manhin Poon, Xiangxiang Dai, Xutong Liu, Fang Kong, John C.S. Lui, Jinhang Zuo

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit diverse response behaviors, costs, and strengths, making it challenging to select the most suitable LLM for a given user query. We study the problem of adaptive multi-LLM selection in an online setting, where the learner interacts with users through multi-step query refinement and must choose LLMs sequentially without access to offline datasets or model internals. A key challenge arises from unstructured context evolution: the prompt dynamically changes in response to previous model outputs via a black-box process, which cannot be simulated, modeled, or learned. To address this, we propose the first contextual bandit framework for sequential LLM selection under unstructured prompt dynamics. We formalize a notion of myopic regret and develop a LinUCB-based algorithm that provably achieves sublinear regret without relying on future context prediction. We further introduce budget-aware and positionally-aware (favoring early-stage satisfaction) extensions to accommodate variable query costs and user preferences for early high-quality responses. Our algorithms are theoretically grounded and require no offline fine-tuning or dataset-specific training. Experiments on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that our methods outperform existing LLM routing strategies in both accuracy and cost-efficiency, validating the power of contextual bandits for real-time, adaptive LLM selection. Code is available at https://github.com/EntroShape/Online_LLM_Selection

Such multi-model setups are increasingly adopted in both academic research and industrial applications, where balancing performance and efficiency is critical

Existing approaches to multi-LLM routing

often rely on static policies, supervised learning on offline datasets, or single-step decision-making. However, real-world user interactions are often

. Users refine their queries based on prior answers, leading to multi-step interactions where the system can revise its LLM choices dynamically. For example, a user might ask a factual question, follow up with a clarification request, and then pose a deeper reasoning challenge. Each step introduces new contextual information—typically derived from the model’s earlier responses—which should influence subsequent model selection.

To support this type of dynamic, multi-step interaction, we propose a new framework for
online multi-LLM selection under unstructured context evolution
. In our setting, the system interacts with a user over a sequence of steps: at each step, it receives a prompt context—potentially incorporating previous model responses—and must select an LLM to generate the next reply. Crucially, the prompt evolves over time according to a black-box transformation that may include concatenation, rewriting, user edits, or proprietary formatting. Because this evolution process is unknown, non-differentiable, and potentially stochastic, conventional planning-based approaches such as reinforcement learning (RL) are ill-suited. In effect, modeling how a prompt evolves would itself require reasoning at the level of an LLM, rendering the transition dynamics unlearnable in practice.

To address these challenges—multi-LLM decision-making, the need for online adaptation, and unstructured context evolution—we adopt a contextual bandit framework. Rather than modeling full interaction trajectories, we decompose the process into a sequence of
myopic decisions
, where the system selects the best LLM for the current context without attempting to forecast how the prompt will evolve. This formulation is both feasible and effective: it allows us to sidestep modeling the unknown transition dynamics while still enabling continual improvement from user feedback. We formalize this learning objective using
myopic regret
, which quantifies the loss relative to the best possible LLM choice at each step.
Building on this formulation, we develop a LinUCB-based algorithm that maintains confidence-aware estimates of each model’s performance across varying prompt contexts. To handle practical deployment constraints, we further extend the algorithm to account for stochastic per-query costs and budget constraints, and introduce a heuristic that prioritizes high-quality responses early in the interaction to reflect user positional bias. Together, our framework offers a principled and scalable online solution for adaptive, cost-aware multi-LLM selection.

Overall, our work provides a principled and lightweight foundation for orchestrating multiple LLMs in open-ended, multi-step environments. By using contextual bandits under unstructured prompt evolution, we demonstrate that practical and scalable multi-LLM systems can be built without relying on offline training or complex environment simulation.

Motivation

Figure 1
:
Online multi-LLM selection with contextual bandits under unstructured context evolution.

Deploying a single LLM to handle diverse user queries often results in unnecessary trade-offs between cost, latency, and performance. In this paper, we advocate for a sequential, multi-model approach to LLM orchestration—where a system adaptively selects among multiple LLMs over multiple steps in response to user feedback. In this section, we outline three motivating observations: the promise of multi-LLM selection, the importance and challenge of evolving context, and the suitability of contextual bandits.

Why multi-LLM selection matters.

Modern LLMs exhibit diverse behaviors: they vary in factual accuracy, latency, and cost. As demonstrated in our experiments (Section
6
), no single model consistently dominates across all tasks. For instance, lightweight models like Mistral-Small-3.1 respond quickly and perform well on simple queries, while larger models like GPT-4.1 are better suited for complex reasoning. This motivates the use of
multi-LLM selection
—dynamically choosing which LLM to use for each query—to achieve better performance-cost trade-offs.

This strategy is increasingly adopted in production systems that serve diverse user queries. Recent works and industry practices have explored LLM routing and orchestration to reduce latency and API costs while maintaining output quality (e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
11
]
). In such systems, multiple LLMs are deployed as a shared model pool, and a routing policy determines which one to invoke. Using a high-cost model like GPT-4.1 indiscriminately is often unnecessary and inefficient—especially for trivial or repetitive queries. A well-designed selector can route easy queries to cheaper models while reserving more capable models for harder cases, yielding significant cost savings without sacrificing quality.

Why context matters and why it is unstructured.

A key design choice in our setting is to let each LLM selection depend not only on the original user query, but also on the responses of previously selected models. This results in an
evolving prompt context
, where the input to each LLM is dynamically constructed based on prior outputs. Through empirical observations (see Section
6
), we find that incorporating this context is crucial—later LLMs often perform better when they can “see” earlier attempts, as they may correct previous errors or build upon partial answers. This motivates our formulation of sequential multi-LLM selection with contextual adaptation.

However, modeling how the prompt evolves across steps is inherently difficult. The new context may be formed through a combination of LLM responses, prompt concatenation, user edits, or system-specific transformations. We formalize this process as a context evolution function
g
, which maps the current context and LLM output to the next-step prompt. Importantly,
g
is a
black-box function
—often non-differentiable, stochastic, or proprietary—and cannot be explicitly modeled or learned. In practice, predicting the next prompt would require simulating how an LLM response changes the prompt structure, which itself may require reasoning as complex as what the LLM performs.
This complexity makes multi-step planning intractable and violates the assumptions of RL, which depends on having a learnable or simulatable transition model. By contrast, our framework avoids this issue by adopting a myopic view: it selects the best LLM for the
current context
, without trying to predict future prompt evolution. This enables robust online decision-making even under unstructured and unpredictable context dynamics.

Why contextual bandits are the right tool.

Given the limitations of RL in this setting, we turn to contextual bandits as a lightweight yet principled alternative. Rather than modeling full trajectories, a contextual bandit approach selects the best LLM for each step based solely on the current prompt context. This
myopic decision-making
is both feasible and robust: it avoids the need to simulate or model
g
, while still adapting to evolving feedback over time.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, our framework supports multi-step interaction with users: at each step, the system observes a new prompt context, selects an LLM, receives feedback, and optionally evolves the context for the next step. We formalize the learning objective via
myopic regret
, which compares the agent’s chosen LLM to the best possible LLM for the current prompt context. This yields a theoretically tractable and practically meaningful measure of performance, even when full trajectory optimization is infeasible.

Contextual bandits are particularly attractive in this setting because they are lightweight, data-efficient, and robust to dynamic environments
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Unlike RL, they require no offline training or simulator access, and they adapt easily to new user populations, model APIs, and cost structures. This makes them well-suited for real-world multi-LLM systems, where queries are diverse, budgets vary, and models evolve rapidly. By combining theoretical rigor with practical feasibility, contextual bandits provide a compelling foundation for adaptive LLM routing in open-ended, real-time environments.

Problem Formulation

We consider an online sequential decision-making problem for adaptive multi-LLM selection, motivated by real-time user interactions with LLMs. Let
[K]=\{1,\dots,K\}
denote the set of available LLMs. Each LLM
k\in[K]
is associated with an unknown feature vector
\theta_{k}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, capturing its alignment with user preferences across different prompt contexts.
A learning agent interacts with users over
T
rounds. In each round
t\in[T]
, the agent receives a user query
Q_{t}
and engages in a multi-step adaptive interaction with the user, lasting at most
H
steps. Let
x_{t,1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the initial context vector derived from
Q_{t}
(e.g., an embedding or prompt representation).
At each step
h\in[H]
, the agent observes context
x_{t,h}
and selects an LLM
a_{t,h}\in[K]
to generate a response. Let
R_{t,h}
denote the output of LLM
a_{t,h}
when invoked on context
x_{t,h}
.
The user provides binary feedback
r_{t,h}\in\{0,1\}
, indicating satisfaction with the result. For analytical simplicity, we model the feedback as a linear function of the context and the selected LLM’s parameters corrupted by noise:

r_{t,h}=\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle+\epsilon_{t,h},

where
\epsilon_{t,h}
is a zero-mean sub-Gaussian noise term. This surrogate model enables the application of linear contextual bandit algorithms despite the discrete nature of the actual user feedback.
If
r_{t,h}=1
, the round ends. Otherwise, the agent proceeds to the next step, where the context is updated by incorporating the previous LLM response:

x_{t,h+1}=g(x_{t,h},a_{t,h},R_{t,h},r_{t,h}),

where
g
is a black-box function representing how the context evolves in response to the LLM’s output (e.g., through concatenation, rewriting, or augmentation).
Importantly, as discussed in
Section

2
,
g
is assumed to be unstructured and unlearnable: it may be stochastic, nonstationary, or governed by proprietary model behavior, and cannot be explicitly modeled or simulated.
This interaction continues for up to
H
steps or until the user is satisfied. The goal is to design an online decision policy that maximizes the expected cumulative user satisfaction over
T
rounds.

Myopic regret.

Due to the unstructured and unobservable nature of the context evolution function
g
, multi-step planning or reinforcement learning methods that rely on modeling or simulating future states are inapplicable. Instead, we adopt a myopic regret minimization framework, where the agent selects each LLM based solely on the current context, without modeling future transitions.
At step
h
of round
t
, define the instantaneous regret as:

\text{Reg}_{t,h}=\max_{k\in[K]}\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k}^{*}\rangle-\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle,

which quantifies the expected shortfall in user satisfaction due to a suboptimal LLM choice in the given context.
The total regret over
T
rounds is:

\mathcal{R}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{h=1}^{H_{t}}\text{Reg}_{t,h},

where
H_{t}\leq H
denotes the number of steps taken in round
t
.

Remark.

While it may seem natural to compare against the best full sequence of LLMs per query (i.e., sequence-wise regret), such analysis requires modeling or learning the transition function
g
, which is infeasible in practice. Moreover, the space of possible LLM trajectories is combinatorially large and highly nonstationary. Thus, sequence-wise regret is neither practically meaningful nor theoretically tractable in our setting.
By contrast, myopic regret minimization ensures robust performance at each decision point, without relying on assumptions about future context evolution, making it more suitable for real-time multi-LLM decision-making under unstructured prompt dynamics

In this section, we propose a LinUCB-based greedy algorithm for sequential LLM selection. As discussed in

Specifically, we adapt the LinUCB algorithm to this setting by maintaining a separate linear model for each LLM and selecting the arm with the highest upper confidence bound (UCB) at each step.

Budget-Aware Algorithms under Stochastic Costs

In real-time LLM selection settings, different models incur different costs due to latency, compute, or token-based pricing. Moreover, these costs can vary stochastically with user queries. Motivated by this, we extend our algorithm to handle per-query
random costs
, where each user specifies a budget and the learner must select LLMs adaptively within that budget while maximizing user satisfaction.

Problem setup.

In each round
t\in[T]
, the user provides a total budget
B_{t}>0
. At each step
h\in[H]
, when the learner selects arm
a_{t,h}
, it observes a reward
r_{t,h}\in\{0,1\}
and incurs a cost
c_{t,h,a_{t,h}}\in[0,C_{\max}]
drawn i.i.d. from a fixed but unknown distribution with mean
\mu_{a_{t,h}}
. The learner observes the cost only after querying the LLM. The cumulative cost must satisfy
\sum_{h=1}^{H_{t}}c_{t,h,a_{t,h}}\leq B_{t}
, where
H_{t}
is the number of steps taken in round
t
. The goal is to maximize cumulative user satisfaction under this per-round budget constraint.

5.1
Budget-Aware Greedy LinUCB Algorithm.

At each step, the algorithm computes:

•

Reward estimate:

\text{UCB}_{k}(x_{t,h})=\langle x_{t,h},\hat{\theta}_{k}\rangle+\alpha_{k}
, where
\hat{\theta}_{k}
is the LinUCB estimator and
\alpha_{k}
is the confidence width.

•

Cost estimate:

\hat{c}_{k}
is the empirical mean of observed costs for arm
k
, with confidence interval width
\beta_{k}=\sqrt{\log(2TK/\delta)/(2N_{k})}
, where
N_{k}
is the number of times arm
k
has been selected.

•

Score:
The algorithm computes

\text{Score}_{k}(x_{t,h})=\frac{\text{UCB}_{k}(x_{t,h})}{\max\{\hat{c}_{k}-\beta_{k},\varepsilon\}},

where
\varepsilon
is a small positive constant to avoid a denominator of 0, and selects the arm with the highest score whose upper-bound cost estimate
\hat{c}_{k}+\beta_{k}
fits within the remaining budget.

This two-level confidence mechanism ensures both optimism in reward and conservatism in cost, balancing exploration and budget feasibility. We now provide a regret bound comparing against a per-step myopic oracle that knows true reward and cost expectations.

Myopic regret definition.

In the presence of budget constraints and stochastic costs, it is infeasible to compare against a full-horizon optimal policy or to use standard cumulative reward as the regret benchmark. Instead, we adopt a
per-step myopic regret
formulation. At each step
(t,h)
, define the best feasible arm (oracle) as:

k^{*}_{t,h}=\arg\max_{k\in[K],\ \mu_{k}\leq b_{t,h}}\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{k}},

where
b_{t,h}
is the remaining budget before step
h
in round
t
. This oracle greedily selects the arm with the highest expected reward per unit cost that fits within the remaining budget. Let the algorithm’s selected arm be
a_{t,h}
. Then the per-step regret is:

\text{Reg}_{t,h}=\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k^{*}_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle-\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle,

and the total regret is:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{budget}}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{h=1}^{H_{t}}\text{Reg}_{t,h}.

Assumption 5

(Stochastic Cost)
.

For each arm
k\in[K]
, the cost
c_{t,h,k}
is i.i.d. with unknown mean
\mu_{k}\in(0,C_{\max}]
and sub-Gaussian noise.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions 1–5 and with probability at least
1-\delta
, the cumulative myopic regret of budget-aware greedy LinUCB algorithm satisfies

\mathcal{R}_{\text{budget}}(T)=\widetilde{O}\left(SL\cdot\sqrt{KdTH}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{C_{\max}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{T\log\left(\frac{TK}{\delta}\right)}\right).

This result extends classical LinUCB analysis to the setting with budget constraints and stochastic, unknown costs. The reward regret scales with
\sqrt{KdTH}
, while the additional cost regret grows with
\sqrt{T}
and is amplified by small mean costs
\mu_{k}
. Despite this challenge, the algorithm remains budget-feasible with high probability and achieves sublinear cumulative regret.

5.2
Positionally-Aware Knapsack Heuristic

The greedy budget-aware algorithm introduced earlier selects LLMs sequentially by maximizing a cost-normalized reward score. While effective in balancing reward and cost, this approach may overly prioritize cheap LLMs and delay the use of more capable ones until later in the interaction. In many real-world scenarios, however, users exhibit positional bias: they prefer to receive high-quality responses early and may disengage if early attempts fail. This motivates the need to consider
positional utility
, where earlier successful responses are more valuable in practice than later ones.

To better balance early accuracy and overall cost, we propose a positionally-aware knapsack heuristic in
Algorithm

2
. The key idea is to incorporate cost estimates and UCB reward scores into a dynamic selection procedure that prioritizes early deployment of high-confidence LLMs while respecting the per-query budget. At each step, the algorithm solves a 0-1 knapsack problem over the remaining LLM candidates, using UCB scores as reward estimates and empirical costs as weights. From the knapsack solution, it selects the arm with the highest UCB score, adds it to the candidate list, and updates the remaining budget. This process is repeated until no feasible arms remain or the budget is exhausted. By anchoring each decision within a budget-aware optimization, the method ensures that strong LLMs are selected as early as possible without exceeding cost constraints.

Remark.
This knapsack-based strategy explicitly targets positional utility by favoring the early use of high-performing models, rather than deferring them due to their cost. Unlike purely greedy cost-normalized approaches, which may over-prioritize cheap but weak models, the knapsack heuristic balances exploration and resource allocation more effectively. While the method is heuristic and lacks a formal regret bound, it aligns closely with practical deployment goals in interactive systems. As demonstrated in our experiments in , the algorithm delivers higher effective utility—especially when stronger models are costly and positional preferences matter.

7
Concluding Remarks

This work presents a contextual bandit framework for online multi-LLM selection under unstructured prompt evolution, offering a lightweight and theoretically grounded solution. While our algorithms demonstrate strong performance across diverse benchmarks, several limitations remain. First, we adopt a myopic decision strategy and do not model long-term interaction effects, which may limit performance in tasks requiring deep multi-step reasoning. Second, the framework assumes access to reliable user feedback, which may be noisy or delayed in practice. Third, we treat the LLM pool as fixed, whereas practical deployments may involve dynamic model availability or changing costs. Future work includes extending the framework to handle delayed and noisy feedback, incorporating non-myopic planning under partially learnable prompt dynamics, and optimizing LLM scheduling in dynamically evolving model pools.

Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

We decompose the total regret into two parts: (i) regret from suboptimal reward estimation, assuming true costs are known, and (ii) additional regret due to cost estimation errors.

Total Regret Decomposition.

At each step
(t,h)
, let the myopic oracle select

k^{*}_{t,h}=\arg\max_{k\in[K],\ \mu_{k}\leq b_{t,h}}\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{k}},

and let
a_{t,h}
be the algorithm’s selected arm. Then the per-step regret is

\text{Reg}_{t,h}=\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k^{*}_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}}\cdot\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}-\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{a_{t,h}}}\cdot\mu_{a_{t,h}}.

This can be decomposed as:

\text{Reg}_{t,h}=\left(\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k^{*}_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}}-\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{a_{t,h}}}\right)\cdot\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}+\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}-\mu_{a_{t,h}}}{\mu_{a_{t,h}}}\right).

For analysis, we upper bound this regret by two terms:

\text{Reg}_{t,h}\leq\underbrace{\left|\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{k^{*}_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}}-\frac{\langle x_{t,h},\theta_{a_{t,h}}^{*}\rangle}{\mu_{a_{t,h}}}\right|}_{\text{(A): reward estimation error under true costs}}\cdot\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}+\underbrace{\left|\frac{\mu_{k^{*}_{t,h}}-\mu_{a_{t,h}}}{\mu_{a_{t,h}}}\right|}_{\text{(B): cost estimation error}}.

(A) Reward regret.

If the cost estimates
\mu_{k}
were known, the algorithm reduces to a cost-weighted Greedy LinUCB variant. As in the standard LinUCB analysis, the cumulative regret from reward estimation over
T
rounds and
H
steps is:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{reward}}(T)=\widetilde{O}\left(SL\cdot\sqrt{KdTH}\right).

(B) Cost regret.

The algorithm uses an empirical estimate
\hat{c}_{k}
and confidence width
\beta_{k}
to compute the denominator in the score:

\text{Score}_{k}(x_{t,h})=\frac{\text{UCB}_{k}(x_{t,h})}{\hat{c}_{k}-\beta_{k}}.

With high probability, Hoeffding’s inequality ensures:

|\hat{c}_{k}-\mu_{k}|\leq\beta_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\log(2TK/\delta)}{2N_{k}}}.

This affects the ranking of arms. In the worst case, a suboptimal arm
k
with a slightly underestimated cost may be chosen over the optimal one, even if its reward is lower. Over
T
rounds, each such deviation contributes an additional regret. Assuming the maximum deviation in cost estimate affects selection for
O(\sqrt{T})
rounds and the worst-case reward difference is
O(1)
, we obtain:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{cost}}(T)=\widetilde{O}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{C_{\max}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{T\log\left(\frac{TK}{\delta}\right)}\right),

where the factor
\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{2}}
arises from the sensitivity of the score to cost estimation errors near small
\mu_{k}
.

Total regret.

Adding the two components:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{budget}}(T)=\mathcal{R}_{\text{reward}}(T)+\mathcal{R}_{\text{cost}}(T)=\widetilde{O}\left(SL\cdot\sqrt{KdTH}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{C_{\max}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{T\log\left(\frac{TK}{\delta}\right)}\right).\qed

Appendix B
Impact of Context

Our framework posits that leveraging evolving context—the output from previous LLM interactions—can enhance the performance of subsequent LLM selections. To specifically investigate this, we conducted a controlled experiment on 60 questions from the AIME dataset, comparing two strategies: Strategy 1, using Gemini-Flash as a standalone solver, and Strategy 2, a two-step sequential approach where Mistral-Small first attempts the problem, and its output (regardless of correctness) is then provided as context to Gemini-Flash for a second attempt. One example for positive and negative context impact is provided in appendix.

The results demonstrate a tangible benefit from context. Strategy 2 (Mistral
\rightarrow
Gemini w/ Context) achieved a 25% success rate, outperforming Strategy 1’s (Gemini-Only) 20% success rate. More tellingly, in 8 out of the 60 questions, Gemini-Only failed, Mistral’s initial attempt also failed, but Gemini with context from Mistral’s attempt subsequently succeeded. This directly indicates that the contextual information, even from an initially incorrect response, can provide valuable grounding, expose flawed reasoning paths to avoid, or offer a different starting point that enables the subsequent LLM to reach the correct solution. This aligns with the core motivation of our work: that adaptive, multi-step interactions where context evolves can lead to better outcomes than single-shot LLM applications.

However, the influence of context is not universally positive. In 3 instances, the Gemini-Only strategy succeeded, while the sequential Mistral
\rightarrow
Gemini approach failed. This suggests that poor or misleading initial context can sometimes derail a subsequent LLM that might have otherwise succeeded. Such cases underscore the complexity of context interaction and highlight a potential challenge in multi-LLM cascades. The example below demonstrates a scenario where the initial context might have had a detrimental effect.

Overall, while the sequential strategy incurred a higher average cost per question ($0.000712 for Strategy 2 vs. $0.000229 for Strategy 1) due to invoking two models in many cases, the net gain in accuracy (5% percentage points, with 8 direct context-driven successes) supports the hypothesis that context evolution is a valuable mechanism. This targeted experiment highlights the potential of contextual information to improve problem-solving in multi-LLM systems, providing a foundational rationale for the adaptive, context-aware selection mechanisms developed in this paper. The instances of negative impact also motivate further research into robustly determining when and how context should be passed or filtered.
