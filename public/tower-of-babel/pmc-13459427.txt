The efficacy of piezocision in accelerating leveling and alignment of anterior teeth in extraction and non-extraction treatments: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Abdalbaset T. Al-Bitar, Mohammad Y. Hajeer, Ahmad S. Burhan, Omar Ahmad Heshmeh, Ossama Aljabban, Khaldoun Darwich, Jehad M. Kara-Boulad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe need to shorten orthodontic treatment has led to the development of acceleration techniques, such as piezocision, a minimally invasive procedure that enhances tooth movement. Its effect on alignment, particularly in extraction versus non-extraction cases, remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the efficacy of piezocision in accelerating the leveling and alignment of anterior teeth, with particular attention to its effects within different orthodontic treatment protocols, as well as its impact on overall treatment duration and postoperative pain.Search methodsA thorough search was conducted up to December 2025 across the following databases: PubMed®, the Cochrane Library, Scopus®, Web of Science™, Embase®, and Google™ Scholar.Selection criteriaIncluded studies were only randomized, non-randomized, or cohort studies of patients with anterior crowding, comparing piezocision-assisted therapy to conventional treatment. Outcomes included alignment duration and overall treatment time, with some studies assessing postoperative pain as a secondary variable.Data collection and analysisTwo reviewers independently extracted data and assessed the risk of bias. Meta-analyses were performed using RevMan 5.4 with random-effects models, heterogeneity was evaluated, and evidence certainty was graded using standard frameworks.ResultsNine studies with 246 patients were included. Overall, piezocision reduced leveling and alignment time by 56.31 days (MD = -56.31; 95% CI: -74.00 to -38.63; P < 0.00001) with high heterogeneity (I² = 86%). Subgroup analysis showed no significant reduction in non-extraction cases (MD = -24.06 days; 95% CI: -51.54 to 3.42; P = 0.09; I²=25%; Tau²= 154.96; very low certainty), whereas extraction cases had a significant reduction of 79.8 days after sensitivity analysis (MD = -79.8; 95% CI: -85.21 to -74.38; P < 0.00001; I²=0%; Tau²= 0.00; high certainty). For overall treatment duration (3 studies), piezocision reduced time by 262.68 days (P = 0.002) but with low-certainty evidence and high heterogeneity (I²=93%). Evidence on postoperative pain was limited and of very low certainty.ConclusionsPiezocision effectively accelerates leveling and alignment in extraction treatments, while the benefits in non-extraction cases are limited. Overall treatment reduction shows a trend but remains uncertain. The procedure is well-tolerated, though evidence on pain is scarce. High-quality trials are needed to confirm clinical value.Trial registrationThe protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251231974).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08712-w.

Introduction

Due to the increasing demand for shorter orthodontic treatment duration among adult patients seeking improved function or aesthetics, various methods of accelerating tooth movement have been developed [1]. Extended treatment time affects several issues, including an increased likelihood of developing caries, gingival recession, and root resorption [2]. To handle these issues, attempts at acceleration have been made, which are commonly organized into four broad types: biological and pharmacological [3], biomechanical [4], physical [5, 6], and surgical approaches [7, 8]. In modern orthodontics, there have been many different types of surgical approaches with differing levels of invasiveness to improve overall treatment efficiency [1]. Surgically, corticotomy has proven to be the most effective clinically for accelerating tooth movement when used with conventional orthodontic treatment [8, 9]. This is based on the Regional Acceleratory Phenomenon (RAP), which is a biological response, as described by Frost [10]. More specifically, RAP from corticotomy results in increased bone metabolism and certain changes, such as temporary and localized demineralization, which enhance tooth movement. Although this has proven effective, the wider use of corticotomy in practice is still limited [11]. This limitation is because of its invasive nature, technical complexity, and potential discomfort for the patient, as it is performed with flap elevation, which has greater implications in terms of postoperative swelling, pain, and lower patient acceptance. All these factors reduce its acceptance by clinicians and patients alike [11].

The development of these corticotomy challenges has led to the exploration of minimally invasive options. One such option is piezocision, which has attracted research interest because of its positive effects on periodontal tissues and on the aesthetics and final results of orthodontic treatment [12]. Unlike corticotomy, piezocision makes smaller incisions with no flap elevation, which, in contrast to the recommended surgical morbidity, aims to achieve comparable treatment acceleration [13, 14]. Since its introduction, piezocision has attracted significant attention, leading to numerous studies on its effect on orthodontic tooth movement.

Recent evidence has shown that this minimally invasive technique can effectively speed up orthodontic treatment [1]. For instance, Lipani et al. reported the effectiveness of piezocision in accelerating canine retraction following premolar extractions [15]. Furthermore, a 2021 review confirmed its ability to accelerate the leveling and alignment of crowded anterior teeth [16]. That review is now over four years old, and given the growing interest in piezocision, it is likely that additional randomized clinical trials have been published since then. These newer studies are likely to either reinforce or refine the existing notion regarding the clinical value of piezocision. Although a recent review by Chen et al. [12] examined the effectiveness of piezocision in accelerating orthodontic alignment and leveling, it did not perform sensitivity or subgroup analyses (e.g., according to treatment protocol) to address the considerable heterogeneity observed across the included studies. Furthermore, the authors misclassified relatively high I² values as “moderate” heterogeneity, contrary to the thresholds established by Higgins et al. [17]. These limitations reduce the accuracy and reliability of their conclusions regarding the treatment protocol employed. Extraction and non-extraction treatments differ substantially in terms of space availability, biomechanical demands, and the severity of anterior crowding, which may influence the clinical relevance of acceleration techniques. Furthermore, the extraction procedure itself can influence the magnitude of the Regional Acceleratory Phenomenon (RAP), which may consequently enhance and increase the rate of tooth movement. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous systematic review has explicitly evaluated the effectiveness of piezocision on anterior leveling and alignment while systematically distinguishing between extraction and non-extraction protocols. Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effectiveness of piezocision in accelerating the leveling and alignment of anterior teeth by separately examining its effects within each protocol, to provide more clinically meaningful and protocol-specific evidence.

Materials & methods

Scoping search

Before finalizing the protocol for this systematic review, a PubMed scoping search was conducted to identify existing systematic reviews and evaluate their methodological approaches. Two reviews similar to the current study were identified; however, they synthesized evidence from various clinical cases collectively without a specific focus on the underlying treatment protocol. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the present review is the first to explicitly distinguish between extraction and non-extraction protocols. By separately examining these protocols, this study aims to address the observed clinical heterogeneity and provide more precise, protocol-specific evidence regarding the effectiveness of piezocision.

Protocol and registration

This review was registered in the PROESPERO database (ID: CRD420251231974) during its first phase. This review was conducted according to the criteria set out in the Cochrane Handbook [18], and was written according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [19].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study Design), to ensure a systematic and transparent selection process for included studies. Regarding the population under evaluation, participants should be over the age of 17, undergoing active orthodontic treatment, with crowded anterior teeth (Little’s Irregularity Index of at least 5 mm). Intervention: Piezocision-assisted corticotomy is performed to accelerate the tooth alignment process in conjunction with conventional orthodontic treatment. Comparison: Patients undergoing conventional orthodontic treatment without any acceleration method. Outcomes: The primary outcome studied was the time required to level and align crowded anterior teeth. Secondary outcome measures included pain or discomfort. Study design: The systematic review included randomized, non-randomized clinical trials and cohort studies, with no language or publication year restrictions, published up to December 2025. The search excluded all in vitro and animal studies, as well as cohorts, case-control studies, case reports and series, and review articles.

The primary search was conducted on August 23, 2025, by two reviewers (MATA, MYH) using the following databases: PubMed®, Web of Science™, Scopus®, Embase®, Google™ Scholar, and the Cochrane Library, without any time or language restrictions. A manual search of the reference lists of all included articles was also conducted to identify any additional relevant studies. The electronic search strategy used in the current systematic review is given in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

The article selection process was conducted in two stages. First, two reviewers (MATA and MYH) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the articles discovered through the electronic search. The same team subsequently evaluated the full texts of eligible articles. Any articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The reviewers resolved conflicts through discussion and, when necessary, consulted the third author (ASB) to reach a final consensus

Data collection process

The reviewers (MATA and MYH) extracted data from the included studies and arranged them in tables. This data included: author name, year of publication, study design, number of patients, and degree of crowding (with or without extraction), mean age, and outcomes. In case of disagreement, the third author (ASB) was consulted and discussed until consensus was reached.

Evaluation of the risk of bias in specific studies

The risk of bias in included studies was assessed based on Cochrane guidelines, using the RoB 2.0 tool for randomized trials [20] and the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies [21]. The two authors (MATA and MYH) conducted the assessment independently, and the results were then compared. If there was a discrepancy, a third reviewer (ASB) was consulted to make the final decision. For randomized studies, five domains were assessed and classified as low risk of bias, high risk of bias, or some concern: bias resulting from the randomization process, bias resulting from deviations from the planned interventions, bias resulting from missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of outcome, and bias in the selection of the result that was reported. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined as follows: low risk if all domains were rated as low risk, some concerns if at least one domain raised some concerns without any domain rated as high risk, and high risk if one or more domains were judged to be at high risk or if multiple domains had some concerns that significantly reduced confidence in the study’s findings. For non-randomized trials, the following domains were evaluated: confounding bias, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. The overall risk of bias for each study was then determined as follows: low risk if all domains were rated as low risk, moderate risk if all domains were rated as either low or moderate risk, serious risk if at least one domain was rated as serious but none as critical, critical risk if at least one domain was rated as critical, and no information if there was insufficient information in one or more key domains and no clear indication of serious or critical risk. Additionally, reporting bias (such as publication bias) was intended to be assessed using funnel plots; however, these were not generated because each meta-analysis included fewer than 10 studies, which limits the statistical power of such tests to distinguish real asymmetry from chance, as per the Cochrane Handbook recommendations [18].

Data synthesis

The meta-analysis was carried out using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4(Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Center, Cochrane Collaboration). Continuous outcomes were analyzed using the random-effects model with the inverse variance method, and results were expressed as mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was evaluated using a P-value < 0.05 for statistical significance and the I² statistic to assess variability across studies. Findings were presented in forest plots, and the GRADE approach was applied to assess the overall quality of the evidence [22].

Results

Study selection and literature review

The number of articles identified after electronic and manual searches was 1,061. This number was reduced to 589 after duplicate removal. Eleven articles remained after reviewing the titles and abstracts, and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The full text of the 11 studies was then reviewed, and two studies (Kilinc et al.; Yavuz et al.) were excluded from the review as they enrolled participants younger than 17 years of age [23, 24]. Nine research papers were included in the qualitative synthesis of the data. Figure 1 illustrates the PRISMA diagram of the review process.

All studies included both males and females, with a total of 246 patients, showing a clear predominance of females, 164 (66.7%), compared to males, 82 (33.3%), yielding a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1. Participants ranged in age from 18.72 to 34 years. The average age of the samples was concentrated in the early and late twenties.

All clinical trials were based on two groups, except for one study, which had three arms [4]. The treatment plan involved non-extraction in five of these studies [25–29]; extraction was used in the other four studies to achieve the necessary space for alignment [4, 30–32]. The mandibular arch was studied in three studies [25, 27, 30]; the maxillary arch was studied in three studies [4, 31, 32]. Both arches were examined in another three studies [26, 28, 29].

Self-ligating brackets were applied in six studies [4, 25–29]. Among these, one study used CAD/CAM customized brackets [29], and one study included three groups: one group received self-ligating brackets with flapless piezocision, the second group received self-ligating brackets alone, and the third group received conventional brackets as a control [4]. The remaining three studies applied conventional brackets [30–32].

Piezocision was performed in 114 patients, and traditional treatment was used in 132 patients. Although flapless corticotomy was performed in all studies, sutures were performed in only one study [29]. Supplementary Table 2 provides detailed information on the surgical protocol and the devices used in the included studies.

The follow-up period ended upon completion of the alignment and leveling process in six studies [4, 25, 27, 30–32], while in one study, both the time to complete alignment and leveling on the arches and the total treatment time were evaluated [28], and upon completion of orthodontic treatment in the other two studies [26, 29].

The primary outcome was measured in days in all studies, and pain level was studied in three studies as a secondary outcome [25, 26, 31]. However, all studies focused on evaluating the effect of flapless piezocision on accelerating the alignment and leveling phase.

Leveling and alignment duration

Seven studies investigated the effectiveness of piezocision in accelerating the Leveling and Alignment of anterior teeth [4, 25, 27, 28, 30–32]. The pooled estimate, using a random-effects model, showed that piezocision reduced the time required in days by 56.31 days, with high heterogeneity observed (MD = -56.31; 95% CI: -74, -38.63; P < 0.00001; X2 = 43.39; P < 0.00001; I2 = 86%). As high heterogeneity was observed among the seven studies (I² = 86%), subgroup analysis was performed to reduce this heterogeneity. The studies were divided into studies including non-extraction cases and studies including extraction cases.

In the non-extraction-based treatments, the end of follow-up was defined as LII between 0 and 1 mm in one study [25], less than 2 mm in another study [27], and at the insertion of the first stainless steel wire in the third study [28]. A pooled estimate of this subgroup showed that Piezocision modestly reduced the number of days required for anterior tooth alignment by 24.06 days, with low heterogeneity observed (MD = -24.06; 95% CI: -51.54, 3.42; P = 0.09; X2 = 2.67; P = 0.26; I2 = 25%; Tau²= 154.96; Fig. 5). The certainty of the evidence for this outcome, according to the GRADE approach, was assessed as very low (Table 4).

Overall treatment time

Three trials evaluated overall treatment time [26, 28, 29], and the pooled estimate indicated that piezocision reduced treatment duration by 262.68 days, with high heterogeneity (MD = -262.68; 95% CI: -429.96, -95.39; P = 0.002; X2 = 28.18; P < 0.00001; I2 = 93%; Tau²= 19936.27; Fig. 7A). Sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Strippoli et al. [28], which had the greatest impact on heterogeneity, did not substantially reduce it (MD= -170.23, 95% CI: -282.47, -57.99; P = 0.003; X2= 6.36; P = 0.01; I2 = 84%; Tau²= 5535.28; Fig. 7B). The confidence in the estimated effect for this outcome was considered low according to the GRADE approach (Table 4).

Pain or discomfort

Three studies investigated pain associated with piezocision [25, 26, 31]. Assessment methods varied across studies: Mehr et al. [25] used a 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS) with measurements at 0, 1, and 12 h, and 7 days; Charavet et al. [26] used a 0–10 VAS for postoperative pain and analgesic consumption; Sultana et al. [31]reported qualitative pain perception. Because of these differing instruments and time points, pooling the data in a meta-analysis was not possible.

In Mehr et al. [25], pain peaked at 12 h in the piezocision group (mean = 30.28 ± 23.26 mm) and decreased to 15.38 ± 19.02 mm by day 7. Charavet et al. [26] reported a mean pain score of 6.0 ± 1.9 after piezocision, similar between groups. In Sultana et al. [31], six patients (75%) experienced mild pain, and two (25%) reported no pain; satisfaction was high, with 62.5% satisfied and 37.5% highly satisfied. Overall, the certainty of the evidence regarding pain outcomes was rated very low (Table 4).

Discussion

Importantly, this systematic review was designed to address a key source of inconsistency in the existing literature by explicitly distinguishing between extraction and non-extraction orthodontic treatment protocols. Previous systematic reviews have commonly pooled these protocols together, despite their substantial differences in biomechanics, space availability, and biological response, which may partially explain the high heterogeneity and inconsistent conclusions reported. By performing protocol-specific subgroup and sensitivity analyses, the present review provides a more clinically meaningful interpretation of the effectiveness of piezocision and allows a clearer understanding of its true impact on anterior leveling and alignment. The included studies were systematically evaluated using strict methodological criteria to identify methodological differences and variations in the reported effects. Among the included randomized controlled trials, five were judged to have a low risk of bias [4, 26, 29, 30, 32], two had some concerns [25, 31], and one was assessed as having a high risk of bias [27]. The main factor contributing to the higher risk of bias in certain studies was data loss due to small sample sizes and the relatively high dropout rate among participants. The only non-randomized study [28] was rated as having a critical risk of bias due to confounding, as it compared a historically selected retrospective control group with a prospectively recruited intervention group.

Regarding study outcomes, two studies evaluated the overall treatment time [26, 29], five focused solely on the leveling and alignment phase [4, 25, 30–32], and one study assessed both phases [28]. For studies investigating the leveling and alignment stage, subgroup analyses were performed by separating extraction and non-extraction cases. This distinction was made because extractions may influence the rate of tooth movement and enhance the regional acceleratory phenomenon (RAP), and because the biomechanics of tooth movement differ between extraction and non-extraction cases. This subgrouping aimed to reduce heterogeneity and allow for a more consistent interpretation of the findings. To ensure the accuracy of the findings and minimize the impact of confounding variables, this review employed strict selection criteria, including only studies with adult populations (17 years and older). Furthermore, all included studies confirmed that a healthy periodontal status was a clear eligibility criterion to standardize the biological response to the piezocision technique. Variations in baseline crowding and orthodontic force systems were also addressed through subgroup analyses and analytical discussion, thereby enhancing the reliability of the comparisons between the experimental and control groups regarding the acceleration of the leveling and alignment process.

Non-extraction-based treatments

The results of studies investigating the effect of piezocision on accelerating the leveling and alignment phase of anterior teeth in non-extraction cases indicate a slight, statistically nonsignificant reduction in treatment duration. The pooled analysis demonstrated a mean decrease of approximately 24 days compared with conventional treatment; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09). The included studies exhibited low heterogeneity (I² = 25%), suggesting a relatively consistent methodological approach among them. Nevertheless, the certainty of the evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE approach, which limits the applicability of these findings in clinical practice. The modest reduction in treatment duration may be attributed to several factors, such as the incision depth reported in the studies by Mehr et al. [25] and Uribe et al. [27], where the corticotomy cuts were only 1 mm deep and 4 mm long. Such limited depth might have been insufficient to reach the cancellous bone and adequately stimulate the bone remodeling necessary for enhanced tooth movement. The extent of surgical invasiveness is directly related to the intensity of the (RAP), which typically peaks within 1–2 months and gradually declines over approximately four months [10, 33]. In Uribe et al. [27], patient follow-ups were scheduled every 4–5 weeks, which, combined with the minimally invasive nature of the procedure and relatively small sample size, may explain the limited acceleration observed. Conversely, when the incision depth was approximately 2–3 mm and the length 5 mm, as reported in the study by Strippoli et al. [28], a more pronounced acceleration in leveling and alignment time was observed in the Piezocision group. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, as the study carried a critical risk of bias and employed a non-randomized design. In summary, the current evidence suggests that the effect of piezocision on accelerating the leveling and alignment of anterior teeth in non-extraction cases appears limited and inconclusive. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and standardized surgical protocols are warranted to confirm or refute these findings. These findings further emphasize that the clinical benefit of piezocision is not uniform across treatment protocols, highlighting the importance of protocol-specific evaluation when assessing acceleration techniques.

Extraction-based treatments

The results of studies investigating the effect of piezocision in extraction cases revealed a clear and statistically significant acceleration of the leveling and alignment phase of the anterior teeth compared with conventional orthodontic treatment. The initial analysis revealed high heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 85%), necessitating a sensitivity analysis to identify the source of this variability. It was found that the study by Sultana et al. [31] contributed substantially to the observed heterogeneity and also presented a higher risk of bias within this group of studies. The marked discrepancy between Sultana et al. [31], and the other included studies could be attributed to differences in the archwire sequences used: Sultana et al. [31] employed a sequence of 0.012-, 0.016-, 0.017 × 0.025-, and 0.019 × 0.025-in. NiTi wires, whereas the remaining studies followed a slightly longer sequence of 0.012-, 0.014-, 0.016-, 0.016 × 0.022-, 0.017 × 0.025-in. NiTi wires, followed by 0.019 × 0.025-in. stainless steel, except for Gibreal et al. [30], which did not use 0.012-in. NiTi wires. These differences in sequencing may explain the observed variability between Sultana et al. [31] and the other studies.

After excluding Sultana et al. [31] from the meta-analysis, the results showed an even greater reduction in leveling and alignment time, approximately 80 days, representing a 48–61% reduction in alignment duration compared to control groups [4, 30, 32], with no residual heterogeneity (I² = 0%), thereby strengthening the reliability of the pooled findings.

The certainty of evidence was rated as high according to the GRADE approach, supporting the conclusion that piezocision can effectively accelerate the leveling and alignment phase in extraction cases The extraction itself may further potentiate this phenomenon. Nonetheless, the magnitude of the effect may depend on several factors, including the depth and length of the incisions, the type of extracted teeth, and individual biological characteristics. Therefore, further long-term, well-controlled comparative studies are warranted to confirm the stability of this effect and to identify the optimal parameters for achieving clinical success with piezocision-assisted orthodontic treatment. The consistent and substantial reduction in alignment time observed in extraction-based treatments supports the rationale for separating treatment protocols in evidence synthesis, as pooling extraction and non-extraction cases may obscure clinically relevant effects and lead to misleading conclusions.

Overall treatment time

The findings of studies evaluating overall treatment time indicate that the use of piezocision resulted in a notable reduction in treatment time compared with conventional therapy, with a mean decrease of approximately 263 days, representing a statistically significant difference (P = 0.002). However, this outcome was accompanied by very high heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 93%), which limits the confidence in the pooled estimate and precludes definitive conclusions. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to explore the source of this heterogeneity by excluding the study by Strippoli et al. [28], which exerted the greatest influence on the overall variance. The heterogeneity remained high (I² = 84%) even after excluding the study and re-running the test. This suggests that the variability among studies cannot be attributed to a single factor but likely reflects fundamental differences in study design and methodology. For instance, the study by Strippoli et al. [28], was non-randomized, while the degree of surgical invasiveness varied across studies. The incision depth was approximately 3 mm in Charavet et al. [26, 29] compared with 2 mm in Strippoli et al. [28], which could influence the degree of bone stimulation and, consequently, the rate of tooth movement. Additionally, the type of orthodontic brackets may represent another explanatory factor: Charavet et al. [26] employed conventional self-ligating brackets, whereas Charavet et al. [29] utilized customized CAD/CAM-designed brackets, which could have contributed to the overall reduction in treatment duration observed in the latter study. Moreover, the certainty of evidence was rated as low according to the GRADE approach, which underscores the need for caution when interpreting these results. Although the overall trend suggests that Piezocision may reduce the total orthodontic treatment duration, the substantial inconsistency among studies and the unclear influence of confounding factors render this effect uncertain.

Therefore, it can be concluded that Piezocision may contribute to shortening the overall duration of orthodontic treatment; however, the magnitude and stability of this effect remain inconclusive. Future long-term randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols and well-defined methodological parameters are recommended to confirm or refute these findings and to clarify the factors contributing to their variability.

Pain and discomfort

The findings related to the assessment of pain associated with Piezocision indicate that the level of postoperative discomfort was generally mild to moderate and well tolerated in most studies, with no clear evidence of increased pain compared with conventional orthodontic procedures. However, quantitative comparisons across studies were not feasible due to variability in measurement tools and evaluation time points. Different pain assessment scales were employed, such as Visual Analog Scales (VAS) with varying ranges (0–100 mm or 0–10), in addition to subjective qualitative assessments, which limited the ability to perform a unified meta-analysis. Mehr et al. [25] reported that pain peaked 12 h after the procedure, with a mean VAS score of approximately 30 mm, and gradually decreased to about 15 mm by day seven, reflecting the transient and self-limiting nature of discomfort following this minimally invasive surgical intervention. Similarly, Charavet et al. [26], found a mean postoperative pain score of 6 ± 1.9 on the VAS, with no significant difference between the Piezocision and control groups, suggesting that the technique does not substantially increase pain intensity. In contrast, Sultana et al. [31] reported that 75% of patients experienced only mild pain, while 25% reported no pain at all. Moreover, patient satisfaction levels were very high, with over 60% of participants expressing satisfaction with the procedure. Despite this general trend suggesting that Piezocision is well tolerated, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE approach, due to small sample sizes, heterogeneity in measurement tools, and the limited number of available studies. Consequently, the true impact of Piezocision on postoperative pain remains inconclusive. Future research employing standardized pain assessment instruments, multiple time-point evaluations, and larger sample sizes is recommended to confirm or refute these findings and to clarify the relationship between the extent of surgical invasiveness and the intensity of pain experienced following the procedure.

Limitations of the current work

Despite the promising results, there are several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results of this review. Firstly, despite the overall positive trend in results and the ability to reduce heterogeneity in studies that included both extraction and non-extraction treatment groups, this study was unable to address the high heterogeneity across the overall treatment time due to the high variability between existing studies. This underscores the need to standardize treatment and surgical protocols in future studies. Secondly, the small number of participants was observed in a number of studies (n < 40), highlighting the need for studies with a larger number of participants. Third, evidence regarding secondary outcomes, such as Pain and discomfort, remains scarce and of very low certainty due to the lack of standardized measurement tools and the inability to conduct meta-analysis. Fourth, the small number of studies in each subgroup makes it impossible to conduct statistical tests such as publication bias or prediction intervals, because they require a larger number of studies to be statistically accurate. Finally, the quality of evidence according to the GRADE system is very low to low for most outcomes except for the group of extraction treatment, which calls for caution in generalizing the results to routine clinical practice and calls for future studies examining non-extraction treatment and the overall treatment time.

Conclusions

The findings from this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that piezocision can accelerate the leveling and alignment phase of anterior teeth in extraction-based treatment, as high-quality evidence has shown a clear and definite reduction in treatment duration during this phase. In contrast, the effect of piezocision in non-extraction-based treatments was limited and statistically non-significant, with very low certainty of evidence, which weakens the possibility of relying on these results in clinical practice. Regarding the overall treatment duration, although a general trend suggests that piezocision may contribute to reducing total treatment time, the significant heterogeneity across studies and the low level of certainty make this conclusion inconclusive. Overall, piezocision appears to be a clinically well-tolerated procedure, generally associated with mild to moderate postoperative discomfort; however, the current evidence on pain outcomes remains insufficient and of very low certainty. Therefore, this review recommends future studies of higher methodological quality, with controlled randomized designs, standardized surgical protocols, and larger sample sizes to clarify the true benefits of the technique in non-extraction-based treatments, as well as its impact on the overall treatment duration and pain experience. Further research is also recommended to confirm the stability of the results obtained in extraction-based treatment and to determine the optimal criteria for the clinical use of the technique.
