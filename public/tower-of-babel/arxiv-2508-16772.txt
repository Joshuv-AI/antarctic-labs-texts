Good involutions of twisted conjugation subquandles and Alexander quandles
Lực Ta

Abstract
. We completely describe good involutions of free quandles and subquandles of twisted conjugation quandles of groups, including all Alexander quandles. As an application, we enumerate good involutions of linear quandles, and we provide explicit mappings for those up to order 23 23 via a computer search. Along the way, we completely characterize connected, involutory Alexander quandles, which may be of independent interest.

1.1.
Main result

Our most general result states that, given a subquandle
X
of a twisted conjugation quandle
\Conj(G,\varphi)
, every good involution
\rho
of
X
is completely determined by a unique function
\zeta^{*}
from the set
\mathcal{O}(X,s)
of connected components of
(X,s)
to a certain set
S
(cf. Remark
3.5
) satisfying certain conditions.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
3.4
)
.

Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of a group
G
, and let
(X,s)
be a subquandle of the twisted conjugation quandle
\Conj(G,\varphi)
. Let
\langle X\rangle
denote the subgroup of
G
generated by
X
, and define the set

S:=\{x\in\langle X\rangle\mid\varphi^{2}(y)=\varphi(x)yx^{-1}\text{ for all }y\in X\}.

(See (
3.2
) for another description of
S
in the case that
X
contains the identity element of
G
.)

Then for all functions
\rho:X\to X
,
\rho
is a good involution of
(X,s)
if and only if there exists a function
\psi:X\to S
such that

\rho(x)=\psi(x)\varphi(x^{-1}),\qquad\psi s_{x}=\psi=\psi\rho

for all
x\in X
. (Cf. Remark
3.5
and Theorem
4.2
.)

Theorem
1.3
strengthens several similar results in the literature on symmetric quandles.

Example 1.4
.

Let
\varphi:=\id_{G}
be the identity map. Then

, and
\Conj(G,\id_{G})=(\Conj G)^{\mathrm{op}}
is isomorphic to
\Conj G
via the inversion map

. Therefore, Theorem
1.3
specializes to the statement of
[
37
]
*Thm. 1.3 with

replaced by
X^{-1}
. Since we use the latter theorem to prove Theorem

, the two results are actually equivalent.

Example 1.5
.

If

equals the Alexander quandle

.
Thus, Theorem

allows us to strengthen certain classification results of Taniguchi

]

In particular, unlike some of the results in

Definition 2.21

In the setting of Theorem

(\Conj(G,\varphi))^{\mathrm{op}}
of a twisted conjugation quandle
\Conj(G,\varphi)
has the quandle structure defined by

s_{g}^{-1}(h)=g\varphi^{-1}(hg^{-1})

for all
g,h\in G
. In particular, by taking
\varphi
to be the identity map
\id_{G}
, we obtain

(\Conj(G,\id_{G}))^{\mathrm{op}}=\Conj G,

which is also evident from Example

.

Example 2.31

(
[
37
]
*Sec. 4)
.

Given a quandle

, it is straightforward to verify that good involutions

of

are a special class of quandle
antiautomorphisms
, that is, quandle isomorphisms

3.1.2.
Calculation of the center

To apply Theorem

to

, we first compute

Proposition 3.1
.

The center of

is

Z(H)=\{(x,n)\in H\mid\varphi^{n}(y)=\varphi(x)yx^{-1}\text{ for all }y\in X\}.

In particular, if
X
contains the identity element of

, then the center of

is

Z(H)=\{(x,n)\in(\Fix\varphi\cap\langle X\rangle)\times\mathbb{Z}\mid\varphi^{n}(y)=xyx^{-1}\text{ for all }y\in X\}.

Proof.

Since
Y^{-1}=\{(x,1)\mid x\in X\}
generates

, verifying that elements of the right-hand side lie in

is straightforward. Conversely, let
(x,n)\in Z(H)
, so that

(\varphi(x)y,n+1)=(x,n)(y,1)=(y,1)(x,n)=(\varphi^{n}(y)x,n+1)

for all generators
(y,1)\in Y^{-1}
. Then
\varphi^{n}(y)=\varphi(x)yx^{-1}
for all

, as desired. In particular, if

, then

contains the set

, so we can take

above to obtain

.
∎

3.1.3.
Preliminary versions of Theorem
1.3

First, we record a special case of Theorem
2.26
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all functions
\rho^{\prime}:Y\to Y
,
\rho^{\prime}
is a good involution of
\Conj Y
if and only if there exists a function
\zeta:Y\to Z(H)
such that

\rho^{\prime}((x,1)^{-1})=\zeta((x,1)^{-1})^{-1}(x,1),\qquad\zeta t_{(x,1)^{-1}}=\zeta=\zeta\rho^{\prime}

for all
(x,1)^{-1}\in Y
.

We refine Lemma
3.2
as follows. As in Theorem
1.3
, define the set

S:=\{x\in\langle X\rangle\mid\varphi^{2}(y)=\varphi(x)yx^{-1}\text{ for all }y\in X\}.

In particular, if
X
contains the identity element
1\in G
, then taking
y:=1
as in the end of proof of Proposition
3.1
shows that

(3.2)

S=\{x\in\Fix\varphi\cap\langle X\rangle\mid\varphi^{2}(y)=xyx^{-1}\text{ for all }y\in X\}.

Also, define the set

S^{\prime}:=\{(x,2)\mid x\in S\}\cap H.

Proposition 3.3
.

Lemma
3.2
holds when
Z(H)
is replaced with
S^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,
S^{\prime}
is a subset of

, so any function
\zeta:Y\to S^{\prime}
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
3.2
induces a good involution
\rho^{\prime}
of
(Y,t)

Conversely, let
\rho^{\prime}
be a good involution of
(Y,t)
, so that
\rho^{\prime}
is induced by a function
\zeta:Y\to Z(H)
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
3.2
. We have to show that
\zeta(Y)\subseteq S^{\prime}
. For all elements
(x,1)^{-1}\in Y
, write

(\zeta_{x},n_{x}):=\zeta((x,1)^{-1})\in Z(H),

By Proposition
3.1
and the inclusion
\zeta(Y)\subseteq Z(H)
, showing that

will also show that

must contain the element

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}((x,1)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\zeta((x,1)^{-1})^{-1}(x,1)

\displaystyle=(\zeta_{x},n_{x})^{-1}(x,1)

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{-n_{x}}(\zeta_{x}^{-1}),-n_{x})(x,1)

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{-n_{x}+1}(\zeta_{x}^{-1})x,-n_{x}+1),

so

. Hence,
\zeta((x,1)^{-1})\in S^{\prime}

∎

3.2.
Main results

We now prove the main theorem. As before, let
s
denote the quandle structure of
(X,s)
as a subquandle of
\Conj(G,\varphi)
.

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem
1.3
)
.

For all functions
\rho:X\to X
,
\rho
is a good involution of
(X,s)
if and only if there exists a function
\psi:X\to S
such that

\rho(x)=\psi(x)\varphi(x^{-1}),\qquad\psi s_{x}=\psi=\psi\rho

for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

We will show that the claim is equivalent to Proposition
3.3
.
Given any function
\psi:X\to S
, define a function
\mathcal{F}(\psi):Y\to S^{\prime}
by

\mathcal{F}(\psi)((x,1)^{-1}):=(\psi(x),2).

Conversely, given any function
\zeta:Y\to S^{\prime}
, define a function
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta):X\to S
by sending
g\in G
to the first coordinate of
\zeta((x,1)^{-1})\in S^{\prime}
.
Clearly, the assignments
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are mutually inverse.

Now, recall that the function
\theta=\iota\eta
is a quandle isomorphism from
(X,s)
to
(Y,t)
, where
\eta:X\hookrightarrow(\Conj K)^{\mathrm{op}}
is the embedding in (
3.1
) and
\iota:K\to K
is the inversion map
k\mapsto k^{-1}
. Therefore, the equivalence of the claim with Proposition
3.3
follows from Observation
2.22
and the following direct calculations, which are straightforward (if somewhat tedious) to verify: For all elements
x\in X
and pairs
(\rho,\psi)
and
(\rho^{\prime},\zeta)
respectively satisfying the conditions of the claim and Proposition
3.3
, we have

\theta\rho\theta^{-1}((x,1)^{-1})=(\mathcal{F}(\psi)((x,1)^{-1}))^{-1}(x,1),\qquad\theta^{-1}\rho^{\prime}\theta(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)(x)\varphi(x^{-1}),

so that

\mathcal{F}(\psi s_{x})=\mathcal{F}(\psi)t_{(x,1)^{-1}},\qquad\mathcal{F}(\psi\rho)=\mathcal{F}(\psi)\theta\rho\theta^{-1}

and, similarly,

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta t_{(x,1)^{-1}})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)s_{x},\qquad\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta\rho^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)\theta^{-1}\rho^{\prime}\theta.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

Let
\pi:X\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}(X,s)
denote the natural projection. Then Theorem

can be reformulated as follows: Every good involution

of

is induced by a unique function
{\psi^{*}:\mathcal{O}(X,s)\to S}
via the formula

\rho
. Moreover, the composition
\psi:=\psi^{*}\pi
is constant on orbits of the action of

on

.

4.2.
Connected Alexander quandles

In this subsection, we discuss good involutions of connected Alexander quandles
Q
.
First, define the function
\alpha:A\to A
by

\alpha(a):=a-\varphi(a)

for all

. Recall from Example
2.20
that

is connected if and only if

is surjective. Also, recall that

is called

-divisible
if the function

is a surjection from

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\varphi
is an involution and

is surjective, then
\varphi
is the inversion map

Proof.

Let

. Since
\alpha
is surjective, there exists an element

such that
\alpha(b)=a
, so that

\varphi(a)=\varphi\alpha(b)=\varphi(b)-\varphi^{2}(b)=\varphi(b)-b=-\alpha(b)=-a.

Since

was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

It is well-known that if
A
is finite, then

is connected if and only if
\varphi
is fixed-point-free (that is,
\Fix\varphi=\{0\}
)

]

However, neither implication holds in general if

To see one direction, let

be the direct sum

Then

is an automorphism of

, and

is surjective, but

is not fixed-point-free:

Conversely, let

be any

-torsionless abelian group that is not

-divisible, and let

be the inversion map

. Then

is a fixed-point-free automorphism of

is the nonsurjective mapping

-divisible.

Lemma 4.7
.

The Takasaki kei

-divisible.

Proof.

This is a special case of Example

General results

In the following, we consider several particular families of linear quandles counted in Proposition
5.6
. First, we give an alternative proof of a well-known result of Kamada and Oshiro
[
23
]
.

Proposition 5.9

(
[
23
]
*Thm. 3.2)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a positive integer. Then the number of good involutions of the dihedral quandle
R_{n}
is

|{\Good R_{n}}|=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n\text{ is odd,}\\
2&\text{if }n=2m\text{ with }m\text{ odd,}\\
4&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
A:=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, and let
\varphi_{-1}:A\to A
be the inversion map
x\mapsto-x
. If
n
is odd, then
\Fix\varphi_{-1}=\{0\}
, so the claim follows directly from Theorem
4.2
.

Otherwise, write
n=2m
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Evidently, the connected components of
R_{n}
are
\mathcal{O}(R_{n})=\{2A,1+2A\}
. On the other hand,
\Fix\varphi_{-1}=\{0,m\}
.

By Corollary
3.6
,
R_{n}
has at most four good involutions
\rho_{i}
. By Theorem
4.2
(cf. Remark
3.5
), each
\rho_{i}
is induced by exactly one of the following functions
\psi_{i}:A\to\Fix\varphi_{-1}
via the formula
\rho_{i}(a):=\psi(a)+a
:

\psi_{1}(a):=0,\quad\psi_{2}(a):=m,\quad\psi_{3}(a):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a\text{ is even,}\\
m&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\psi_{4}(a):=\begin{cases}m&\text{if }a\text{ is even,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
i=1,2
, it is clear that
\psi s_{a}=\psi_{i}=\psi_{i}\rho_{i}
for all
a\in A
. A straightforward check shows that the same holds for
i=3
if and only if
m
is even, and similarly for
i=4
. Hence, Theorem
4.2
completes the proof.
∎

We begin with a well-known combinatorial lemma.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a positive integer, let
X
be a set of size
n
, and let
0\leq i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
be an integer. Then the number of involutions in
S_{X}
having
i
transpositions equals

\binom{n}{2i}(2i-1)!!=\frac{n!}{(n-2i)!i!2^{i}}.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a positive integer, and let
k:=2n+1
. Then the number of good involutions of the linear quandle
\Lambda_{4n,k}
is

|{\Good\Lambda_{4n,k}}|=\left(\sum^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}_{i=0}\frac{n!}{(n-2i)!i!}2^{n-2i}\right)^{2}.

In other words, the integer sequence
(|{\Good\Lambda_{4n,k}}|)_{n\geq 1}
is precisely the OEIS sequence A
202828

[
15
]
.

Proof.

Let
A:=\mathbb{Z}/4n\mathbb{Z}
, and recall from Theorem
4.2
that every good involution
\rho\in\Good\Lambda_{n,k}
is induced by a unique function
\psi^{*}:\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{4n,k})\to\Fix\varphi_{k}
.
By
[
19
]
*Thm. 1,
\Lambda_{4n,k}
has exactly
2n
connected components, namely the orbits

(5.1)

\mathcal{O}_{m}:=\{m,m+2n\},\qquad 0\leq m<2n.

On the other hand,
\Fix\varphi_{k}=2A
.

Let
\pi:A\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{4n,k})
be the natural projection. By Theorem
4.2
(cf. Remark
3.5
), it suffices to count all functions
\psi^{*}:\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{4n,k})\to\Fix\varphi_{k}
such that
\psi=\psi\rho
, where
\psi:A\to\Fix\varphi_{k}
and
\rho:A\to A
are defined by

(5.2)

\psi:=\psi^{*}\pi,\qquad\rho(m):=\psi(m)-km

for all
m\in A
. In particular,
\psi
must be constant on each orbit

\mathcal{R}_{m}:=\{m,\rho(m)\}

of the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-action of
\rho
on
A
. Moreover, (
5.2
) implies that
\psi(m)\equiv\rho(m)+m\pmod{2n}
or, equivalently,

\psi(m)\in\{\rho(m)+m,\rho(m)+m+2n\}.

Conversely, both of these values of
\psi(m)
achieve the equality
\psi(m)=\psi\rho(m)
, with
\rho
as defined in (
5.2
). Hence, for each connected component
\mathcal{O}_{m}\in\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{4n,k})
and for each orbit
\mathcal{R}_{m}
, there exist exactly two assignments
\mathcal{O}_{m}\mapsto\psi^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{m})
that achieve the desired equality
\psi(m)=\psi\rho(m)
.

Given
\psi^{*}
satisfying the above conditions, (
5.2
) shows that
\rho
preserves parity; that is,
\rho
restricts to an involution of
2A
and similarly for the coset
1+2A
. Let
i
be the number of orbits
\mathcal{R}_{m}
such that
m
is even and
|\mathcal{R}_{m}|=2
, and let
\psi^{*}_{2A}
denote the restriction of
\psi^{*}
to connected components of the form
\mathcal{O}_{m}
with
m
even. Then
2A
has exactly
n-i
orbits
\mathcal{R}_{m}
under the action of
\rho
.

By the above discussion, each orbit
\mathcal{R}_{m}
contributes exactly two values for
\psi^{*}(m)
that achieve the desired equalities. This yields exactly
2^{n-i}
functions
\psi^{*}_{2A}
that induce an involution
\rho|_{2A}
of
2A
satisfying the above constraints and having a given orbit decomposition with
i
orbits of size
2
.

The number of all possible orbit decompositions of
2A
under the action of
\rho|_{2A}
with
i
orbits of size
2
is given by Lemma
5.10
. Multiplying by
2^{n-i}
as above and summing over all possible values of
i
, we deduce that there are exactly

\sum^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}_{i=0}\frac{n!}{(n-2i)!i!2^{i}}2^{n-i}=\sum^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}_{i=0}\frac{n!}{(n-2i)!i!}2^{n-2i}

functions
\psi^{*}_{2A}
that induce an involution
\rho|_{2A}
of
2A
satisfying the above constraints. The same goes for functions
\psi^{*}_{1+2A}
that induce an involution
\rho|_{1+2A}
of
1+2A
satisfying the above constraints. Since
\rho
must preserve parity, any two choices of
\psi^{*}_{2A}
and
\psi^{*}_{1+2A}
both satisfying the above conditions yield a function
\psi^{*}
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, so the claim follows.
∎
