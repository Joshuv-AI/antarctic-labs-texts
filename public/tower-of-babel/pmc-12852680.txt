Impacts of cold stress and temperature fluctuations on bud abortion and malformed flower development on Hayward kiwifruit
Authors: Maryam Mirarzgar, Mahmood Ghasemnezhad, Reza Fotouhi Ghazvini, Jalal Jalali Sendi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of cold stress and fluctuating temperatures on bud abortion and flower malformation in one-year-old dormant canes of ‘Hayward’ kiwifruit (Actinidia deliciosa). Cuttings were collected from a commercial orchard in Rasht, Iran, and standardized prior to exposure. Following 950 chilling hours at 4 °C, cuttings were subjected to either cold stress (− 2 °C for 40 h) or fluctuating temperature treatments (17 °C → 30 °C → −1 °C) at three phenological stages: chilled dormancy (CD), woolly bud (WB), and bud break (BB). Bud development was assessed morphologically and histologically across six developmental stages. Floral buds were classified as normal, aborted, or misshapen, and percentages were calculated per treatment. Cold stress during WB and BB stages caused severe bud damage, including incomplete floral organ differentiation, vacuolization, protoplast shrinkage, and phloem necrosis. Bud abortion rates increased with phenological advancement, reaching 51% in BB-stage buds, while abnormal flower formation peaked at 29% in WB-treated buds. Fluctuating temperatures also induced developmental instability, with abortion rates of 39%, 31%, and 58% at CD, WB, and BB stages, respectively, and histological analyses revealed widespread necrosis and organ malformation. Control buds under constant growth conditions (24/17°C day/night) showed normal organogenesis. These findings demonstrate that advanced developmental stages are particularly sensitive to low or fluctuating temperatures, which disrupt floral organ differentiation and increase the incidence of abortion and malformation. Cold stress at critical stages (WB and BB) caused the highest cellular damage, while temperature fluctuations accelerated developmental instability. These results emphasize the importance of understanding the impact of sudden climatic fluctuations on kiwifruit bud development, highlighting the need for growers to implement appropriate strategies to mitigate the negative effects of such environmental stressors.

Introduction

Kiwifruit (Actinidia spp.) is among Iran’s most significant horticultural export commodities, with an annual production of 48788.29 tons, ranking the country sixth globally after China, New Zealand, Italy, Greece, Chile and Belgium1. Commercial kiwifruit cultivation primarily involves two species: Actinidia chinensis and Actinidia deliciosa. The genus Actinidia Lindl. (Order: Theales, Family: Actinidiaceae) comprises more than 50 species, most of which are native to Southwest China2,3. Currently, the ‘Hayward’ cultivar (Actinidia deliciosa) dominates global cultivation due to its superior taste, consumer preference, and extended storage life, making it the cornerstone of the kiwifruit industry4.

Kiwifruit exhibits a biennial growth and flowering cycle, wherein axillary meristems from the previous season develop into the subsequent season’s shoots5–7. Floral morphogenesis initiates during bud swell and culminates at anthesis the following spring3,8.

All dormant winter buds in kiwifruit (Actinidia deliciosa ‘Hayward’) possess the potential to break dormancy and develop into flowers. Under optimal conditions, ‘Hayward’ kiwifruit can produce up to nine inflorescences per shoot6,9. However, during spring, many buds lose this capacity, with floral primordia either failing to develop or aborting at various stages of morphogenesis8,10,11. Environmental factors—such as seasonal temperature fluctuations, light exposure, humidity, and other growth conditions—significantly influence bud break rates12,13.

Another critical issue in ‘Hayward’ kiwifruit is its tendency to produce misshapen fruits, including flat and fan-shaped deformities, which reduce export quality and grower profitability14,15. Export-grade fruits originate from normal, radially symmetric flowers, yielding uniformly shaped ovaries16,17. In contrast, flat fruits arise from elliptic terminal flowers, which produce asymmetrical whorls of floral organs, resulting in flattened ovaries10,15,16. The exact cause of this abnormality is still unknown, although it has been proposed that uneven distribution of meristem cells may lead to irregular ovary diameter16. Alternatively, differential ovarian expansion could correlate with ovule arrangement during development.

Fan-shaped (fasciated) flowers form when the terminal flower fuses with one or more lateral flowers before or after sepal differentiation10,15. These fused structures -termed “double” (one terminal + one lateral) or “triple” (one terminal + two laterals) fruits- undergo ovary fusion during differentiation15.

Ovary primordia development is a protracted process initiated by apical flattening, marking the transition to reproductive growth. This stage is highly sensitive to environmental conditions, particularly temperature13,18. Pre-bud break low temperatures may induce floral abnormalities due to resource competition (e.g., carbohydrates, hormones)14, while excessive heat can trigger early-stage morphogenetic abortion8.

Plants generally require sufficient chilling accumulation to overcome winter endodormancy19 and sufficient heat accumulation to trigger bud break and subsequent growth20. In kiwifruit, rising spring temperatures—which coincide with the post-dormancy flowering phase—accelerate the progression of phenological stages21. However, earlier budbreak increases the likelihood of exposure to late frosts during flowering, elevating the risk of cold damage22. As such, temperature is the dominant environmental driver of plant phenology23. Previous studies have shown that key phenological events, including budburst, leaf unfolding, and flowering, exhibit distinct responses to temperature variability24.

Recent studies have highlighted that climate change not only alters mean temperatures but also increases the frequency and intensity of temperature fluctuations and extreme events, including sudden cold spells. Such environmental variability can have profound effects on perennial crops, particularly during sensitive phenological stages19,20,24. In kiwifruit (Actinidia deliciosa), buds at advanced developmental stages are highly vulnerable to cold stress and temperature fluctuations, which can lead to developmental abnormalities, flower malformation, and increased bud abortion.

In the studied region, climatic variations in certain years have led to reductions in kiwifruit yield. The aim of this study is to examine the different stages of floral bud differentiation in kiwifruit and, subsequently, to evaluate the effects of temperature stress at various phenological stages on the incidence of bud malformation and abortion. To develop the temperature-stress regimes for this research, multi-year temperature data from the study area were utilized.

Consequently, understanding the influence of temperature on flowering potential and the incidence of malformations is critical for developing effective corrective management strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant

One-year-old dormant kiwifruit canes (Actinidia deliciosa) were collected from a commercial orchard in Rasht, Guilan Province, Iran (37.2682° N, 49.5891° E) on 20 November 2019, coinciding with leaf abscission as an indicator of dormancy onset25,26. Cuttings were prepared from nodes 6 to 20, measured from the basal end of the canes27, with standardized dimensions of ~ 10 mm in diameter and ~ 20 cm in length. Prior to cold storage (4 °C), the cuttings were surface-sterilized with benomyl fungicide and rinsed twice with sterile distilled water.

Control temperature

Following exposure to 950 chilling hours at 4 °C in cold storage, the cuttings were transferred to a growth chamber under controlled conditions: a diurnal temperature regime of 24/17°C (16/8 h day/night), a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 68 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and 70% relative humidity28. Subsequently, the cuttings were moved to a programmable test chamber (KATO, Japan) for phenological evaluation at three distinct stages: chilled dormant (CD) bud, woolly bud (WB), and bud break (BB).

Temperature treatments

Low-temperature stress

Control cuttings were maintained under constant ambient conditions throughout the experimental period, while treatment cuttings were subjected to cold stress (− 2 °C for 40 h). For both control and treatment groups, 50 cuttings served as biological replicates in a completely randomized experimental design.

The temperature regimes were simulated based on meteorological data from Guilan Province, using at least ten years of historical records. To apply cold treatment, excised cuttings were subjected to − 2 °C for 40 h at three distinct phenological stages (CD, WB, BB). The temperature was gradually decreased from 17 °C to − 2 °C at a rate of 2 °C per hour. After reaching − 2 °C, the cuttings were held at this temperature for 40 h before being gradually rewarmed to 17 °C at the same rate. Finally, the treated cuttings were transferred back to a growth chamber (24 °C day/17°C night) to resume normal growth. a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 68 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and 70% relative humidity28.

Fluctuating temperature treatments

The fluctuating temperature regime was implemented using the following protocol: First, chamber temperature was gradually increased from 17 °C to 30 °C at a constant rate of 2 °C per hour. The cuttings were maintained at 30 °C for 30 h before initiating a symmetrical temperature decrease. The temperature was then reduced at the same rate (2 °C/h) from 30 °C to − 1 °C, followed by a 14-hour stabilization period at − 1 °C. For recovery, the temperature was increased progressively at 2 °C/h until reaching baseline conditions. Finally, all cuttings were transferred back to standard growth chamber conditions (24 °C day/17°C night) to resume normal growth. 50 cuttings served as biological replicates in a completely randomized experimental design. a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 68 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and 70% relative humidity28.

Bud’s developmental cycle

Bud samples were collected at six developmental stages: chilled dormancy (CD), bud swell (BS), woolly bud (WB), bud break (BB), leaf opening, and shoot elongation. All samples were immediately examined using a stereo microscope (Model 2030; Leica, Germany) for morphological characterization. Following initial examination, flower buds from each stage were carefully dissected to assess floral organ differentiation. We quantified and classified buds into three categories: (1) normal, (2) aborted, and (3) misshapen, with results expressed as percentages of total buds examined per stage3.

Histology evaluation

Bud samples and flower primordia were fixed in FAA solution (formalin: glacial acetic acid:70% ethanol, 5:5:90 v/v) and stored at 4 °C until processing29. In this evaluation, 50 buds were used for each treatment.

Following fixation, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), then embedded in paraffin wax (melting point 58–60 °C). Using a rotary microtome (Model 2030; Leica, Germany), we obtained 5-µm transverse sections which were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (Merck, Germany).

Microscopic examination was performed using an Olympus M1000 light microscope (Leica, Germany) equipped with a Canon EOS 600D digital camera (Japan). We quantified floral developmental abnormalities by classifying buds into three categories: (1) normal, (2) aborted, and (3) misshapen, with results expressed as percentages of total observed buds. Additionally, we documented treatment-induced histological alterations in floral organ development30.

Data analysis

Data for normal, abnormal, and aborted flower buds (n = 50 replicates per phenological stage) from both cold and fluctuating temperature treatments were analyzed as means ± standard error (SE). We performed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SAS software (version 9.1, SAS Institute Inc.). Post-hoc comparisons between treatment groups were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at α = 0.05 significance level. Data transformation was unnecessary as the percentage values (converted from count data) showed normal distribution without arcsine transformation, as confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk normality tests (p > 0.05).

Results

Bud development cycle

Our observations revealed that the first-order axillary buds (FOABs) were fully embedded in tissue derived from the leaf petiole after complete chilling at 4 °C for 950 h. Prior to chilling, most of the scales and leaf primordia (LPs) of the FOABs were visible (Fig. 1a).

Simultaneously, the axils of the bud scales and leaf primordia (LPs) in the FOABs produced secondary meristems. The most basal secondary meristems, located at nodes 1–4, differentiated into second-order buds (SBs), which typically ceased further growth (Fig. 1a). In contrast, the remaining secondary meristems persisted in an undifferentiated state (Fig. 1b, c).

Following bud swelling (BS) during heat accumulation, the residual meristems at the basal nodes (nodes 5–12) differentiated into inflorescences, while those at the distal nodes (up to node 13) developed into new axillary buds.

Histological sections revealed the presence of secondary meristems, though these were not yet differentiated at this phase (Fig. 2e). Following dormancy release, the principal bud growth stages—BS (Bud Swell), WB (Woolly bud), and BB (Bud Burst)—progressed sequentially (Fig. 2).

At the BS stage, the scales were covered with white trichomes and slightly protruded from the pore (Fig. 2b). Histological sections at this stage displayed one terminal flower bud (TB), two lateral flower buds (LT), as well as bract and sepal primordia, but no peduncle formation (Fig. 2f).

By the late advanced bud swell stage and the onset of WB, the scales had developed brown trichomes and protruded through the stem’s corky tissue (Fig. 2c). Histological analysis at this stage showed a terminal flower and two lateral flowers supported by a single pedicel (Fig. 2g). At the beginning of the BB stage, leaf and flower primordia were enclosed by trichome-covered scales (Fig. 2d). Petal initiation was observed histologically during this phase (Fig. 2h). Following petal initiation, the initiation of petals and stamens occurs, while the leaf-out stage takes place simultaneously (Fig. 3a). After ovary differentiation, shoot elongation is observed (Fig. 3b).

Normal and abnormal flower development

The kiwifruit inflorescence is a compound dichasium consisting of a terminal flower and two lateral flowers on a single pedicel (Fig. 3d). Floral meristems develop within the axils of leaf primordia. Those located at nodes 5 to 12 differentiated into the terminal flowers of the inflorescence (Fig. 2). At the WB stage, two bract primordia emerged on opposite sides of the floral primordium (Fig. 2g). In normal flowers, sepal, petal, stamen, and carpel primordia were initiated in a concentric sequence (Fig. 4a). In contrast, flat flowers retained an elliptic terminal floral meristem during development, with floral organs arising in an elliptical arrangement, ultimately forming a flattened ovary (Fig. 4b).

When the terminal flower fused with one or two lateral flowers, fan-shaped or fasciated flowers formed (Figs. 4c, d). The timing of fusion varied widely, occurring anywhere from pre-sepal to post-stamen initiation. Early fusion of flower buds resulted in fan-shaped flowers with fully connected ovarian tissues (Fig. 4c). Late fusion led to incomplete tissue integration within the ovaries, producing partially fused fan-shaped flowers (Fig. 4d).

Treatments

Low-temperature stress on flower bud abortion

Cold stress during later bud stages (WB and BB) caused severe floral bud damage (Fig. 5e, f), whereas no significant differences in abortion rates were observed between the control (Fig. 5a) and CD-cold treated buds (Fig. 5d, g). In WB-treated buds, floral organ differentiation was incomplete, resulting in stunted bud growth and underdeveloped petals (Fig. 5e). Additionally, protoplast shrinkage, nuclear condensation, and vacuolization were observed at this stage (Fig. 5h).

The results showed that when excised canes were exposed to cold stress at the end of the CD (Chilling Dormancy), WB (Woolly Bud), and BB (Bud Burst) stages, floral bud abortion rates increased to 28%, 37%, and 51%, respectively, compared to the control (24%). Notably, BB-stage buds exposed to cold stress for just three days exhibited the highest abortion rate (Fig. 6a), indicating that advanced phenological stages (WB and BB) are more sensitive to cold stress than the control and CD-stage buds.

Histological analysis revealed that cold stress during the BB stage had the most destructive effects (Fig. 5f), including phloem necrosis (Fig. 5i). This tissue necrosis likely resulted from enzymatic or oxidative browning of the cell wall or entire cells. In contrast, no necrosis or cellular injury was detected in control or CD-cold-treated buds (Fig. 5a, g).

Effect of fluctuated temperature in flower bud abortion

The results indicated that the response to temperature fluctuations was highly dependent on the bud phenological stage. Floral bud abortion rates were 39%, 31%, and 58% when exposed to temperature fluctuations at the CD, WB, and BB stages, respectively (Fig. 6b), with the most severe damage occurring during the BB stage.

Under temperature stress conditions (31 °C and − 1 °C), high temperatures (~ 31 °C) accelerated bud growth, whereas subsequent exposure to low temperatures (~ − 1 °C) at advanced phenological stages caused severe developmental damage.

Longitudinal sections revealed that control floral buds (grown at 24/17°C day/night) exhibited fully formed sepals, petals, and stamens, along with visible locule formation (Fig. 5a). In contrast, temperature fluctuations at different phenological stages severely disrupted floral organ development. CD-treated buds displayed incomplete organ formation in aborted floral primordia, resulting in stunted growth (Fig. 5b). BB-treated buds showed partial development, with stamens forming but many petals remaining undeveloped. Additionally, floral primordia necrosis was observed (Fig. 5c).

Low-temperature stress on abnormal flower formation

The incidence of abnormal flowers was strongly dependent on bud phenological stages under cold stress. Compared to the control (20%), abnormal flower rates were 23%, 29%, and 15% when cold stress was applied during the CD, WB, and BB stages, respectively (Fig. 6c). Cold stress at the BB stage resulted in the abortion of 51% of the buds. It should be noted that half of these aborted buds were also malformed, and due to their lack of growth, they were included in the abortion statistics. Consequently, the total percentage of bud abortion and malformation was 66%. Low-temperature stress particularly increased abnormal flower formation in WB-treated buds.

Flat flower formation was observed in WB-treated buds under low-temperature stress (Fig. 7a), accompanied by vacuolization in affected flowers. Additionally, a fan-shaped flower developed in a BB-treated bud (Fig. 7b). Cold stress also induced tissue necrosis in BB-treated floral buds (Fig. 7c).

Fluctuated temperature on abnormal flower formation

During thermal change treatment, the percentage of abnormal flowers observed in CD-, WB-, and BB-treated buds was 30%, 38%, and 26%, respectively, compared to only 20% in control buds (Fig. 6d). Histological analysis of abnormal flowers from temperature-fluctuated buds is shown in Fig. 7d–i.

Floral primordia development involved protoxylem growth extending from the bud axis base to the flower primordia, followed by progression into the primary sepals, petals, stamen filaments, and ovaries (Fig. 7e–f). However, sudden thermal shifts during distinct phenological stages led to widespread floral deformities. The highest abnormality rate, accompanied by cell necrosis, occurred in WB-stage buds exposed to fluctuating temperatures (Fig. 7e). Notably, the absence of lytic vacuoles prevented cytoplasmic clearance during necrosis, leaving necrotic cell remnants unprocessed (Fig. 7f). Necrosis also affected primary phloem cells in WB- and BB-treated buds under thermal stress (Fig. 7f, h–i).

Discussion

Floral organ morphogenesis in kiwifruit initiates during the earliest stages of bud swell (BS) in early spring and continues over an extended period until anthesis. This process involves the formation of floral meristems and the subsequent development of the four principal floral whorls: sepals, petals, stamens, and the ovary. During the forcing period, rising temperatures trigger bud swelling, marking the progression of floral development. Subsequently, the stages of woolly bud, bud burst, leaf unfolding, and shoot elongation occur in sequence.

Low-temperature stress on flower bud abortion

Cold stress damages plant cell membranes by altering their fluidity, inducing low water potential, disrupting lipid composition, reducing ATP supply, promoting the accumulation of toxic compounds, causing imbalanced ion homeostasis, and triggering solute leakage31.

In kiwifruit (Actinidia spp.), dormant buds exhibit damage upon exposure to temperatures as low as − 10 °C. However, at advanced phenological stages, even brief exposure (30 min) to − 1.5 °C to − 2.0 °C can damage ~ 10% of floral buds32. During acclimation, dormant buds are highly resistant to low temperatures, but they lose this resistance in late winter or early spring once their chilling requirement is met33.

Exposure of advanced-stage buds to − 2 °C for 40 h resulted in severe structural damage (Fig. 6a), including protophloem necrosis in cuttings at the BB stage (Fig. 7i). Since sugar accumulation is critical for cold tolerance, the decline in sugar levels due to spring respiration increases susceptibility to cold stress34. Early-spring cultivars, which resume growth sooner, are particularly vulnerable to frost damage when temperatures fall below freezing22. Mechanistically, cold stress triggers reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, leading to oxidative damage, programmed cell death, and tissue necrosis35.

Temperature changes in flower bud abortion

In this study, excised kiwifruit cuttings at different phenological stages were subjected to fluctuating temperature treatments (ranging from 30 °C to − 1 °C) and compared with a control group. High temperatures accelerated phenological progression. Toward the end of dormancy, elevated temperatures were linked to tissue and cellular rehydration. However, this substantial water influx may induce mechanical damage through extracellular freezing if advanced flower buds are exposed to subzero temperatures (− 1° C for 14 h)32,36.

During early eco-dormancy, when buds begin to swell, abrupt temperature fluctuations triggered bud abortion. Notably, these fluctuations caused a 58% flower bud abortion rate at the BB stage (Fig. 6b). This phenomenon may be attributed to an initial acceleration of floral organ growth -particularly stamens- due to high temperatures, followed by cold stress-induced bud abortion (Fig. 7c).

Flower buds at advanced developmental stages exhibit increased sensitivity to cold stress. During deep dormancy, cells enhance their freezing tolerance by adjusting lipid composition and modifying membrane properties32,36. However, as temperatures rise during the transition from endodormancy to ecodormancy, this protective capacity declines, making buds more susceptible to cold-induced injury. Temperature fluctuations—particularly warming events in late winter or early spring—can further exacerbate this vulnerability, often leading to necrosis of floral primordia. Such injuries may ultimately result in floral abortion37.

Low-temperature stress on abnormal flower formation

The low temperatures preceding budburst (BB) resulted in a high incidence of floral bud deformation (Fig. 6c). In WB-treated buds, flattened flowers exhibited protoplast shrinkage, nuclear condensation, and vacuolization (Fig. 7a). Additionally, cold stress during BB induced tissue necrosis35 (Fig. 7c). Severe stress conditions can lead to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, triggering programmed cell death or, in extreme cases, widespread tissue necrosis35. Low-temperature stress at the early stages of flower formation can cause irregular cell division, resulting in malformed floral buds or flower fusion (Fig. 7c).

Pre-budburst floral abnormalities may arise from low-temperature stress or resource competition, particularly for carbohydrates and phytohormones14. In kiwifruit, disruptions in carbohydrate allocation to inflorescences during flower morphogenesis are known to cause aberrant floral development8.

Effect of temperature fluctuations on abnormal flower development

Once the chilling requirement is fully met (4 °C for 950 h), bud phenology is primarily governed by forcing conditions, with warmer temperatures accelerating flowering. As previously noted, the temperature change treatment included warm phases—both optimal (24/17°C day/night) and high (24–30 °C for 30 h)—followed by a low-temperature phase (− 1 °C for 14 h), which may disrupt cellular function.

After transitioning from endo- to eco-dormancy, bud growth accelerates in response to rising temperatures, reactivating cellular processes. This resumption of growth depends on the transport of water, nutrients, and developmental signaling molecules between source and sink tissues. While bud development initially relies on stem-stored reserves, demand can eventually exceed the supply capacity of non-vascular (apoplastic and symplastic) pathways. Consequently, optimal vascular system function -particularly in the xylem and phloem- becomes critical at the onset of growth. However, in early spring, the vascular systems of angiosperms remain vulnerable38.

Following endo-dormancy, rising temperatures trigger the differentiation of new xylem and phloem in flower buds (Fig. 7f, i). Disrupted carbohydrate supply during kiwi inflorescence morphogenesis can lead to abnormal flower development39. Sudden temperature drops impair cell function and hinder the transport of essential growth substrates. Notably, temperature fluctuations early in eco-dormancy appear to damage multiple tissues, including primary phloem.

Pre-budbreak (BB) temperature fluctuations resulted in a high incidence of abnormalities in kiwifruit (Fig. 6d). Additionally, many floral primordia exposed to these changes exhibited deformities and tissue damage (Fig. 7g–i).

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of unseasonal temperature variations during flower development on the productivity of ‘Hayward’ kiwifruit. The findings demonstrate that climatic fluctuations can significantly limit kiwifruit production during the forcing phase, depending on the phenological stage. The results of this study showed that the sensitivity to low and fluctuating temperatures increased progressively as flower development advanced. Cold stress (− 2 °C) induced severe cellular damage, including protoplast collapse and phloem necrosis, while fluctuating temperatures (30 °C to − 1 °C) further accelerated developmental instability, leading to incomplete organogenesis and widespread tissue injury. The highest rates of bud abortion occurred at the BB stage, reaching 58% under fluctuating temperatures and 51% under low-temperature conditions. Additionally, the WB stage exhibited the highest incidence of misshapen flowers when exposed to temperature fluctuations. This study highlights the particular vulnerability of the ‘Hayward’ cultivar when flowering coincides with temperature instability.
