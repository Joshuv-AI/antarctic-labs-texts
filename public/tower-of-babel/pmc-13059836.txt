Ethnological study of birth timing decisions: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Yagana Samim, Nicholas Spence
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionBirth timing decisions, a type of reproductive decision-making (RDM), have yet to be explored in the RDM literature through an ethnological lens. Existing literature has generally observed reproductive decisions across broad racial categories, which has not allowed for a nuanced understanding of the cultural factors influencing birth timing decisions. This scoping review aims to answer the following questions: (1) How do different ethnic groups approach birth timing decisions? (1a) What social and structural factors influence birth timing/spacing decisions for different ethnic groups? (1b) What cultural logics emerge during these decision-making processes? Specifically, these questions are explored within the geographical context of the USA, as its diverse demographics, size and availability of data make it an ideal case. This scoping review thus makes two key contributions to the health literature: (1) it explores trends in birth timing decisions, which is a type of RDM that has typically been overlooked in favour of topics such as birth control use and timing, abortions and miscarriages, and wrongful births, and (2) it explores birth timing decisions from an ethnological perspective in a highly relevant context, expanding beyond homogenising race-based categorisations, toward a nuanced understanding of cultural considerations in family planning decisions across the USA.Methods and analysisThis scoping review follows the protocol laid out in the extension of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis, for scoping reviews and will use thematic analysis[1]. Using four databases, Medline, Web of Science, the International Bibliography of the Social Sciences and Sociological Abstracts, original research articles have been captured using the attached search strategy, which contains key terms related to both the independent and dependent variables of interest. Results have been filtered to include only studies published in English within the last 20 years (2005 to present) and conducted in the USA. These criteria were implemented using a verified search string. Additional filters for human-only results were applied to the Medline search. All relevant publications have been imported to Covidence, where the authors will independently conduct title/abstract screening and full-text screening, as well as a thematic analysis of the extracted data from the remaining articles that meet the inclusion criteria. The authors aim to organise and synthesise all findings using the attached data extraction table (see supplemental documents).Ethics and disseminationThis study uses existing publications and therefore does not require ethics submission or review. Introduction

Reproductive behaviours have long captured the interests of researchers across multiple disciplines, whether they have been demographers or economists looking to explain the causes and consequences of a global decline in fertility rates,1,3 feminist scholars seeking equitable access to contraception4 and encouraging reproductive agency for marginalised groups,5,7 or even health researchers discussing the complex reproductive decision-making (RDM) processes of those with heritable genetic disorders.8 9 Reproductive behaviours constitute a critical area of study, as contemporary transformations in family structure and birth planning practices reflect a new and emerging diversity in decision-making processes. The expanded range of choices for many in terms of how, when, why and even with whom to have children is important in understanding this changing landscape. Recognising how different ethnic groups navigate these decisions can potentially inform culturally competent healthcare and deepen our comprehension of how cultural variability influences reproductive practices. Any specific policy recommendations will depend on the patterns identified in the scoping review, which will determine whether proposed changes are most appropriately directed at the micro, meso or macro level, and will also determine which specific settings they should be implemented in.

Operationalisations

Definitions for RDM, and its related concepts, family planning and birth timing, have been ambiguous and inconsistently used both within and across the literature. Existing definitions have also been critiqued as reductionist, focusing primarily on the individual and routinely situated outside of the purview of necessary historical and cultural contexts.10,12 For the purpose of this scoping review, it is therefore necessary to first identify the operational boundaries of these concepts, as adapted from existing works (see table 1). From a sociological perspective, reproductive behaviours have been broadly conceptualised as a wide range of actions and decisions related to an individual’s reproductive health.6 These behaviours include the timing and method of contraceptive use,13 14 participation in rituals and practices during labour and delivery,15 16 and decisions regarding pregnancy—such as when to conceive, with whom and for what reasons.17 18 RDM refers specifically to decisions about these reproductive behaviours and about reproductive health more generally, as well as the factors that inform them. Reproductive decisions typically consider the individual’s personal beliefs, the beliefs of relevant actors around them, as well as the legal, historical and cultural context that sets the parameters of these choices.

This scoping review specifically focuses on birth timing decisions, which are a subset of family planning decisions. This latter term further falls under the umbrella of RDM more generally. Family planning, as operationalised by this review, encompasses decisions about whether to have children, with whom to have them, when to start and stop, how many children to have, the intervals between births, etc. Birth timing, as a type of family planning decision, relates to choices that individuals make regarding when to begin having children, how to space subsequent pregnancies and when to stop having children. These decisions are influenced by various factors, including personal, financial, health and cultural considerations.17 An exploration of birth timing decisions is a well-documented gap in a literature that mostly focuses on (1) unwanted, mistimed or wrongful births,19 (2) how births can be prevented through various contraceptive uses13 14 and (3) the availability, development and improvement of family planning programmes and services.20

Birth timing and spacing

The timing of first births is a key area of research, with particular attention given to maternal age at first birth (MAFB) across different racial groups. Research shows that MAFB has risen across racial categories as well as across county types from 2007 to 2017.21 White, black and Hispanic women all experienced an increase in mean MAFB, the largest difference of which was observed for black women, whose mean age rose an average of 2 years across all county types.21 Rural MAFB rose by 1.3 years, while metropolitan MAFB rose by 1.8 years, suggesting that other indicators, such as education and income, which are frequently higher in metropolitan areas, may also play a role in MAFB.21 Recent research also suggests that age at first birth varies not only across racial/ethnic groups, but also by immigration status, with immigrant women having higher MAFB than their US-born and child-migrant counterparts, but being less likely to remain childless by the age of 45.22 This suggests the interplay of multiple factors in the decision to have a child, and culture might be a particularly important, yet unexplored, factor.

Additionally, research suggests that MAFB may be intergenerationally transmitted, with daughters often mirroring the reproductive timing of their mothers.23 This intergenerational pattern has contributed to what scholars refer to as the reproductive polarisation hypothesis, which posits that the divide between younger and older mothers is likely to widen over time. Given the strong association between MAFB and educational attainment, this widening gap is believed to produce increasingly divergent life trajectories for children born to younger versus older mothers.24

Research has also shown that birth spacing (also referred to as interpregnancy interval) varies across racial groups, with disparities often linked to socioeconomic factors, healthcare access and structural inequalities.25,27 However, there has been inadequate exploration into how interpregnancy intervals differ across ethnic groups, which may obscure important cultural, social and systemic influences on birth spacing patterns. This gap in research is particularly concerning given that short interpregnancy intervals have been associated with adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight and increased maternal health risks.26 Finally, exploring not only these disparities, but the decisions behind them, is crucial to developing targeted interventions that promote maternal and infant health equity.

Decision-making processes

The decision-making processes surrounding birth timing and spacing have been largely overlooked in the existing literature—an oversight this scoping review seeks to address. Decision-making processes have been explored about reproductive decisions more generally, with a focus on decisions about pregnancy care and labour,28 29 pregnancy termination, as opposed to continuation,30 and if focused on pregnancy/family desires, have neglected a much-needed ethnological lens.29 31 For example, Temmesen and colleagues (Temmesen et al, p1)17 found that women in their reproductive years viewed having children as a ‘life-changing decision’ but were mindful of finding ‘the right time’, avoiding regret and establishing a potential ‘Plan B’ in the event that they cannot have kids. Further, research conducted by Public Health Agency of Canada (Public Health Agency of Canada, p12)31 found that just under half of women felt the timing of their pregnancy was ‘just right’, with one-fifth of respondents wishing they had conceived later, while almost a quarter wished for an earlier pregnancy. Existing work on decision-making processes about pregnancy and childbirth shows similarly nuanced dynamics. Egenberg et al identify communication, trust with providers and the organisational culture of maternity units as key factors shaping women’s experiences during childbirth decisions,28 while Yuill et al demonstrate that choices around pregnancy and birth care are shaped by complex considerations involving the search for trustworthy information, the desire to maintain bodily autonomy and the influence of moralised expectations about maternal responsibility.29 While these findings highlight key considerations in RDM, they could greatly benefit from a broader analytical scope that incorporates cultural and ethnological dimensions influencing birth timing and spacing. Without adequate understanding of the exact mechanics behind these decisions, existing policies and interventions risk being misaligned with the lived realities, cultural contexts and nuanced considerations that shape these important reproductive choices.

Rationale

There are three aspects of this scoping review protocol that require justification: (1) a focus on birth timing decisions, (2) the use of an ethnological lens and (3) limiting the scope of this review to the USA. While an exploration of all family planning decisions would be useful for future research, this scope is particularly wide and special attention should be paid to each type of family planning decision. This review’s focus on birth timing decisions is directed by existing research which has revealed group differences in MAFB and interpregnancy spacing, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding of birth timing decisions is required.23,27 Further, understanding the ways in which birth timing decisions are culturally informed will allow healthcare providers to provide culturally-sensitive and relevant health and safety information to women about their reproductive health; this is especially pertinent given that women come into most frequent contact with the healthcare system when experiencing pregnancy.6 The relationship between provider and patient should be culturally informed to ensure maximum communication and comprehension for agentic decision-making. Further, an ethnological lens allows researchers to emphasise cultural considerations unique to certain ethnic groups; given an increase in diversity, multiculturalism and interethnic marriages in the USA, exploring differences across race no longer captures the most relevant information about family planning decisions.32 While race often refers to socially-constructed categories based on perceived physical characteristics, ethnicity captures cultural identity, language, heritage and shared traditions, making it more appropriate and relevant when examining decision-making processes.

Methods and analysis

Protocol and registration

This protocol is informed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist and is registered in Open Science Framework (OSF) at the following link: https://osf.io/6f4bq

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies include those published in the last 20 years (1 January 2005 to 6 January 2025) to capture a range of data while keeping the scope feasible for review. A final search was conducted on 6 January 2025, prior to submission. After publication of this protocol, we anticipate an estimated start date of January 2026 for our screening procedure, with final data charting to be completed by April 2026. Finally, we anticipate that the synthesis of the literature and a final draft will be ready for submission to a journal by June 2026. Only original research articles will be accepted to keep the scope of the literature focused on what the academic field has produced. Studies must mention at least one specific ethnic group, in relation to the topic of birth timing, as operationalised by this review. Additionally, we will not conduct a formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias, as such evaluations are not required in scoping review methodology.33

Information sources

To capture the medical and sociological aspects of this study, this scoping review uses four databases: Medline, Web of Science, the International Bibliography of the Social Sciences and Sociological Abstracts. Our selection of databases reflects the interdisciplinary scope of this review, allowing us to draw from both medical and sociological scholarship, while remaining methodologically appropriate for a focused and manageable search.

Search strategy

The search strategy used for this scoping review can be found in the online supplemental documents. It includes search strings related to ethnicity, birth timing, as well as a validated search string that narrows down results to US-based literature.34 All search strings were developed and modified in consultation with a university librarian. The search yielded a total of 2720 relevant results across all databases. After importation into Covidence, 568 total duplicates were removed, leaving a total of 2152 documents to screen (see online supplemental documents for PRISMA-ScR flowchart).

Screening procedure

All literature has been imported into Covidence and will undergo four stages of screening: (1) a title/abstract screening, which will be independently conducted by both authors, (2) a joint title/abstract screening by both authors, for documents that did not receive consensus, (3) a full-text screening, which will be independently conducted by both authors, followed by (4) a joint full-text screening by both authors, for documents that did not receive consensus. Stages two through four will further establish the boundaries of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as scoping reviews use an iterative process.

Data charting

On final review of the included documents, both authors will chart data onto the data extraction table, which is attached as an online supplemental table 1. The data extraction table should capture information about categories including but not limited to: the type of methods used in the study (eg, quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods), which variables are controlled for in any quantitative or mixed methods studies, how groups are discussed (eg, single groups, multiple groups), the group identification category (eg, race, ethnicity, nationality), sample sizes of the groups studied, the specific birth timing decision being explored (eg, timing of the first birth, subsequent pregnancy spacing) and more.

Synthesis of results

Both authors will analyse and synthesise the data presented in the extraction chart, engaging in iterative discussions to ensure consistency, accuracy and depth of interpretation. The synthesis will be guided by the research questions and objectives outlined in this protocol, with the goal of mapping the breadth and nature of evidence related to decision-making processes around birth timing and spacing.

Descriptive statistics (eg, frequencies, proportions) will be used to summarise key study characteristics such as publication year, study design, population demographics, geographical location and methodological approaches. These statistics may be presented in tabular and visual formats (eg, summary tables, bar charts, pie charts) to provide an accessible overview of the included literature, as necessary.

Qualitative findings will be synthesised thematically. Extracted data related to birth timing and spacing decision-making processes, theoretical frameworks, cultural and contextual influences, and reported outcomes will be coded and categorised using an inductive approach. Thematic patterns will be developed collaboratively between authors to ensure comprehensive representation of the literature. Where applicable, conceptual frameworks or models that emerge from the data will be illustrated graphically to support clarity and interpretability.

The synthesis will also attend to gaps in the literature, particularly in terms of under-represented populations, methodological limitations and the potential absence of culturally grounded perspectives. Attention will be paid to how decisions are conceptualised and framed across studies (eg, as individual, relational, structural, temporal), and whether ethnological or intersectional lenses are employed.

Findings will be presented narratively, integrating both quantitative and qualitative data in a way that supports a holistic understanding of the state of the literature. This integrated synthesis will ultimately inform a set of recommendations for future research, policy and practice in the area of RDM, with specific emphasis on the cultural factors influencing birth timing and spacing.

Ethics and dissemination

This study does not involve primary data collection and therefore does not require formal ethics approval. All data have been obtained from existing literature and this study will not involve any identifiable data about participants.

Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and sharing of key review materials (eg, search strategy, data-charting) as supplementary files to enable transparency and reproducibility.

Patient and public involvement

Patients/the public were not involved in developing this protocol, but their perspectives are an integral part of the focus of this research. Our analysis will examine how individuals make decisions about birth timing and spacing with the aim of making policy recommendations that will be beneficial to the public.
