Saccular Limited Dorsal Myeloschisis with a Short Stalk at the Base of the Sac Causing Spinal Cord Deviation out of the Spinal Canal
Authors: Takato MORIOKA, Akiko KANATA, Toshiyuki AMANO, Daisuke INOUE, Satoshi O. SUZUKI, Takafumi SHIMOGAWA, Nobutaka MUKAE, Koji YOSHIMOTO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Saccular limited dorsal myeloschisis is a condition in which the spinal cord is tethered by a fibroneural stalk that links the cord to a saccular skin lesion, probably due to a small segmental failure of the disjunction between the neural and cutaneous ectoderm during primary neurulation. We previously reported that the dorsally bent cord deviated from the canal into the sac when the tethering effect of the limited dorsal myeloschisis stalk was strong. These cases of saccular limited dorsal myeloschisis should be prenatally differentiated from myelomeningoceles; however, this is complicated by the limited resolution of prenatal magnetic resonance imaging. Nevertheless, postnatal magnetic resonance imaging, including three-dimensional heavily T2-weighted imaging, to demonstrate the stalk linking the top of the bent cord to the sac dome is essential. We recently treated a patient who presented with a cord deviation to the sac due to a short stalk linking the rostral part of the bent cord to the base of the sac. While the sagittal and axial views of three-dimensional heavily T2-weighted imaging alone were insufficient to visualize the short stalk, the coronal view clearly demonstrated that the stalk ran almost parallel to it. During surgery, untethering the cord and completely returning it to the canal is important.

Introduction

Limited dorsal myeloschisis (LDM) is a distinct clinicopathological entity, firstly proposed by Pang et al.,1,2) which is thought to originate from a segmental failure of the disjunction of the neural and cutaneous ectoderm during primary neurulation. This results in a retained fibroneural stalk linking the dorsal spinal cord and skin lesion, leading to cord tethering. The recommended treatment involves prophylactic untethering of the stalk from the cord. Based on skin manifestations, LDMs were originally classified as saccular or flat (non-saccular).1,2)

Pang et al.1,2) previously classified the morphology of saccular LDM into three types: saccular-myelocystocele, in which the central canal is focally enlarged, giving the appearance of a segmental or non-terminal myelocystocele (Fig. 1a); saccular-basal nodule, in which a basal neural nodule is present at the base of the sac (Fig. 1b); and saccular-dome-stalk, in which a thin stalk is continuous with the sac (Fig. 1c).

(a-c) Schematic drawings showing sagittal views of the three types of saccular limited dorsal myeloschisis (LDM) based on Pang’s original classification: saccular-myelocystocele (a), saccular-basal nodule (b), and saccular-dome-stalk (c). (d, e) Schematic drawings showing that in saccular LDM cases with spinal cord deviation from the spinal canal to the sac reported by us,3,4) a single stalk (d) or double stalks (e) originating from the top of the bent cord terminate at the dome of the sac, tightly tethering the cord.

(f) This report presents a case of cord deviation to the sac due to a short stalk linking the short distance between the rostral part of the bent cord and the base of the huge sac.

It is well known that the tethering effect of the LDM stalk causes dorsal bending of the cord at the stalk-cord union.1,2) We have previously reported three cases of saccular LDM, in which the tethering effect was so strong that the bent cord deviated from the spinal canal to the sac.3,4) In these cases, one or two stalks originating from the top of the bent cord terminated at the dome of the sac and strongly tethered the cord (Fig. 1d, e). On preoperative examination, visualization of the LDM stalk is crucial, and is typically achieved through sagittal and axial views of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) including three-dimensional heavily T2-weighted images (3D-hT2WI), which are imaged nearly parallel to the course of the relatively long LDM stalk.3,4) We recently treated a patient who presented with cord deviation to the sac with a short stalk linking the rostral part of the bent cord and the base of the huge sac (Fig. 1f). Here, we report that three sections, including the coronal view, of 3D-hT2WI are useful for the preoperative diagnosis of the short stalk.

Case Report

A 28-year-old primigravida was diagnosed with having a cystic mass in the fetal lumbar region on ultrasonography during a prenatal checkup at 35+1 weeks of gestation. Prenatal MRI at 36+1 weeks, including a half-Fourier acquisition single-shot turbo spin-echo sequence (HASTE) image with a slice thickness of 5 mm, demonstrated a presumed huge meningocele, but failed to reveal the exact pathological anatomy (Fig. 2a). She was referred at 36+2 weeks of gestation.

(d-g) Postnatal magnetic resonance images performed in the right lateral position. (d) Bone images of the spine show findings similar to those seen in 3D-CT. (e) Serial sagittal reformat of three-dimensional heavily T2-weighted image (3D-hT2WI) shows a severe dorsal bending of the cord and deviation of the bent cord from the canal to the sac at the L1-L2 vertebral level. (f) In serial axial reformat, an inner membrane originating from the deviated cord that links the cord to the right inner wall of the sac appears to be involved in tethering. (g) Serial coronal reformat reveals a short stalk (red arrows), originating from the rostral part of the deviated cord (yellow arrows), running rostrally and slightly to the right, and terminating at the base of the sac. At 37+0 weeks, a baby girl weighing 3,354 g was delivered via scheduled cesarean section, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9. Physical examination revealed a dorsal midline sac measuring 85 × 63 × 50 mm in the upper lumbar region (Fig. 2b). The sac surface was mostly covered with healthy skin; however, some areas had thin surfaces. The patient exhibited no neurological deficits.

3D-computed tomography (3D-CT) of the spine revealed spina bifida below the T12 (Fig. 2c), and the distance between the left and right vertebral arches of L1 and L2 was particularly large (Fig. 2c, yellow arrows). Postnatal MRI was performed with the patient in the right lateral position to avoid sac compression. The findings in the bone images of the spine were similar to those seen on 3D-CT; however, the visualization of each vertebral arch tended to be inferior to that of 3D-CT (Fig. 2d). A sagittal reformat of 3D-hT2WI with fat suppression and a slice thickness of 1 mm revealed severe dorsal bending of the cord and deviation of the bent cord from the canal to the sac at the L1-L2 vertebral level (Fig. 2e-1). Two-layer thin membranes were observed on the dorsal side of the deviated cord, consisting of an outer membrane (Fig. 2e-1, 2, 3, green arrows) that contained vascular flow voids and an inner membrane (Fig. 2e-3, blue arrows); however, no clear tethering stalk was noted linking the top of the bent cord to the dome of the sac. However, a stalk originating from the rostral part of the deviated cord running rostrally and slightly to the right was suspected (Fig. 2e-2, 3, red arrows). In the axial reformat (Fig. 2f), this stalk was not apparent. However, the inner membrane originating from the deviated cord (Fig. 2f-1, 2, yellow arrows) linked the cord to the right inner wall of the dome (Fig. 2f-1-2, blue arrows), which appeared to be involved in cord tethering. However, in the coronal reformat (Fig. 2g), a short stalk (Fig. 2g-2-3, red arrows) was clearly visible, originating from the rostral part of the deviated cord (Fig. 2g-1, 2, yellow arrows), running rostrally and slightly to the right, and terminating at the base of the sac. The position of the conus medullaris was at the L4-5 level. No hydrocephalus or Chiari malformation was observed.

On the next day after birth, untethering and repair surgery for the sac were performed. The sac was incised at the midline, and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was drained. The inside of the sac was observed to have two layers of arachnoid-like membrane at the bottom, which were continuous with the inner wall of the sac and were considered to correspond to the membranes seen on MRI. The inner membrane (Fig. 3a, blue arrows) was observed through a defect in the outer membrane (Fig. 3a, green arrows) containing large veins. When the inner membrane was incised and reflected (Fig. 3b, blue arrows), a part of the cord (Fig. 3b, yellow arrows) that deviated from the canal was exposed. Although the inner membrane was in contact with the cord, it did not appear to be involved in tethering. When the inner membrane was cut away further, a short LDM stalk (Fig. 3c, white arrows) was observed, originating from the rostral part of the cord (Fig. 3c, yellow arrows), and running rostrally and slightly to the right (Fig. 3d). Bipolar electrical stimulation of the cord at 2 mA evoked compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) in the external anal sphincter muscle, hamstring, and gastrocnemius (Fig. 3d, e1-3). However, stimulation of the stalk did not evoke CMAPs (Fig. 3d, e4-5). When the stalk was cut, the tethering was released, and the cord moved slightly into the canal. After completely returning the cord to the canal, pedicled flaps were created from the tissue of the inner wall of the sac, and duraplasty was performed. The skin was sutured in two layers: the subcutaneous tissue and skin.

All operative fields are shown in the same orientation as in Fig. 2b. (a) An inner membrane (blue arrows) is observed through a defect in the outer membrane (green arrows). (b) When this inner membrane is incised (blue arrows), a part of the cord (yellow arrows) that deviated from the canal is exposed. (h, i) Histopathological examination of the resected sac shows that the inner wall of the sac is lined with arachnoid tissue. (j) The stalk has S-100 protein-positive peripheral nerves in the fibrocollagenous tissue.

No de novo neurological findings were observed postoperatively. MRI performed 2 weeks after surgery confirmed the return of the cord to the canal and correction of cord bending (Fig. 3f and g). No significant change was observed in the position of the conus medullaris. Histopathological examination of the resected sac revealed that the inner wall was lined with arachnoid tissue (Fig. 3h and i). The stalk was composed of fibrocollagenous tissue. Although no glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-immunopositive neuroglial tissue was observed in the stalk, S-100 protein-positive peripheral nerves were noted (Fig. 3j).

Discussion

The presence of GFAP-immunopositive neuroglial tissue in the fibrocollagenous tissue is a typical histopathological finding of LDM stalks, indicating that the stalk is derived from the neuroectoderm.1,2,5) All three previously reported cases with cord deviation had the macroscopic form of a saccular-dome-stalk and histopathologically contained GFAP-immunopositive neuroglial tissue.3,4) However, in this case, the presence of GFAP-immunopositive neuroglial tissue could not be confirmed. This is thought to be because the stalk was short and could only be resected in pieces rather than as a column; therefore, the sparsely distributed GFAP-immunopositive neuroglial tissue could not be confirmed, as we have previously reported.6-8) In recent years, even if GFAP-immunopositive tissue is negative, the demonstration of S-100 protein-positive peripheral nerve tissue, which is widely distributed and derived from the neural crest, is considered useful for the histopathological diagnosis of LDM.6-9) Therefore, the histopathological findings of the stalk in the present case are consistent with LDM with a saccular-dome-stalk.

The primary differential diagnosis from saccular LDM during the fetal period is myelomeningocele (MMC).4,10) In recent years, fetal surgery for MMC has been performed mainly in Europe and the United States.11) Accordingly, it is important to differentiate LDM, which has a good functional prognosis, from MMC, which is associated with a poor functional prognosis, using prenatal MRI, particularly the HASTE method.4,10) However, since the HASTE method shortens the imaging time to reduce artifacts caused by fetal movement, the slice width is thick at 3-5 mm.4,10,12,13) Consequently, it is nearly impossible to visualize the thinner LDM stalk on prenatal HASTE imaging.4,10) The short stalk in this case were especially unlikely to be visualized. In addition, even dorsal cord bending could not developed. We previously reported a case in which the cord deformity worsened due to tethering within 2 months of birth.3) However, in this case, the interval between prenatal and postnatal MRI was only 6 days, making it unlikely that the cord deformity worsened during that time. This was considered the limit of resolution of prenatal MRI; thus, prenatal differentiation between saccular LDM and MMC is difficult.4,10,12)

Therefore, visualization of the LDM stalk using postnatal MRI, primarily 3D-hT2WI, which allows for thin-slice reformation is necessary, as in the present case.6,7) We usually diagnose the presence of LDM stalks using a combination of sagittal and axial reformats,3,4,7) but in this case, the coronal reformat was the best for visualization of the short stalk. This issue most likely stemmed from the fact that the course of the short stalk was nearly parallel to the coronal plane, highlighting the importance of using three imaging planes for diagnosis.6) In contrast, in the axial reformat, the inner membrane appeared to be involved in tethering; however, in the intraoperative findings, this membrane was thin and not involved in tethering. This membrane was not confirmed histologically. However, histological examination of the inner wall of the sac revealed arachnoid tissue, and intraoperative findings showed that the membrane was continuous with the inner wall of the sac. Based on these findings, we concluded that these membranes were arachnoid membranes. Preoperative MRI was conducted with the patient in the right lateral position to avoid sac compression. Therefore, hydrodynamic pressure inside the sac was applied to the right inner wall, where the inner membrane ended, stretching the arachnoid membrane and creating the appearance of cord tethering. However, because the intraoperative findings were obtained after the CSF has been drained and with the patient in the prone position, the anatomy of the thin membranes may differ from that observed on preoperative MRI.

At surgery for saccular LDM, in which the cord has deviated from the canal, in addition to complete untethering of the cord, returning the deviated cord to the canal is essential.3,4) It is also important to perform subsequent duraplasty using vascularized tissue from the inner wall of the sac as pedicle flaps to prevent CSF leakage.3,4) In this case, correction of cord bending was confirmed on MRI 2 weeks later. However, in this case, the low-lying conus did not improve on the postoperative MRI. Under these conditions, adhesions may form at the surgical site, potentially resulting in retethering syndrome.3,4) There have been no prior reports investigating the effect of spinal cord displacement on the development of retethering syndrome, and none of the three cases that we have reported3,4) showed any neurological deterioration in the postoperative follow-up periods of 4.5-5.5 years. We have further reported that LDM may be accompanied by filar anomalies due to secondary neuration failure, such as lipoma of filar type and retained medullary cord as an additional tethering element.14) Taking these facts into consideration, strict follow-up is necessary in the future.

In conclusion, the preoperative imaging diagnosis of saccular LDM, which presents with cord deviation to the sac due to the short stalk, as in this case, requires a detailed examination of three cross-sections, including a coronal view.
