Abstract
Bilateral trade models the task of intermediating between two strategic agents, a seller and a buyer, willing to trade a good for which they hold private valuations. We study this problem from the perspective of a broker, in a regret minimization framework. At each time step, a new seller and buyer arrive, and the broker has to propose a mechanism that is incentive-compatible and individually rational, with the goal of maximizing profit. We propose a learning algorithm that guarantees a nearly tight O ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}) regret in the stochastic setting when seller and buyer valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a fixed and possibly correlated unknown distribution. We further show that it is impossible to achieve sublinear regret in the non-stationary scenario where valuations are generated upfront by an adversary. Our ambitious benchmark for these results is the best incentive-compatible and individually rational mechanism. This separates us from previous works on efficiency maximization in bilateral trade, where the benchmark is a single number: the best fixed price in hindsight. A particular challenge we face is that uniform convergence for all mechanisms’ profits is impossible. We overcome this difficulty via a careful chaining analysis that proves convergence for a provably near-optimal mechanism at (essentially) optimal rate. We further showcase the broader applicability of our techniques by providing nearly optimal results for the joint ads problem.

Introduction

Bilateral trade is a fundamental economic model where two agents—a seller and a buyer—are interested in trading a good
(
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
, e.g.,)
. Both agents are characterized by a private valuation for the item, and their goal is to maximize their own utility.
Previous work in computer science
(
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2016
;
Brustle et al. 2017
;

;
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2021
;

while ensuring that (i) agents behave according to their true preferences (
dominant-strategy incentive compatibility
) and (ii) the utility for participating in the mechanism of each agent is non-negative (

).

Profit maximization is a canonical objective in economics (already studied in the seminal paper by
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
), and it is a well-motivated objective in the applications (e.g., all e-commerce platforms that connect sellers to buyers).
It is also gaining attention from a theoretical computer science perspective (most notably in
Hajiaghayi et al. 2025
). The predominant approach considered in prior work is
Bayesian
, as it assumes that both agents’ valuations are drawn from known and typically independent distributions. In many practical applications, however, the availability of such data is a non-trivial assumption, and valuations may not be independent.

In this work, we study profit maximization in repeated bilateral trade through the lens of online learning, with the goal of designing an algorithm that minimizes the regret with respect to the best mechanism in hindsight. Note that unlike prior research on repeated bilateral trade, which has focused on gains-from-trade
(
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2024a
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2024b
;
Azar et al. 2024
;
Bernasconi et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
and has thus considered

, here we have the ambitious goal of converging to the profit of a provably complex optimal mechanism. In fact, while fixed-price mechanisms to some extent
characterize
efficiency-maximizing mechanisms, they are far from optimal in our problem (we refer to
Appendix

B
for further details). The nature of our benchmark also differentiates us from the vast majority of the online learning literature on economic problems, where the learning objective is intrinsically parametric, e.g., the best fixed price(s),
(
Blum et al. 2003
;
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
;
Gatmiry et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, the best reserve price(s)
(

, the best linear contract
(

;

or the best fixed bid
(
Feng et al. 2018
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2024c
, e.g.,)
.

As it is traditional in online learning, we study two data generation models: the adversarial input model, where the agent’s valuations are decided upfront by an oblivious adversary, and the stochastic i.i.d. case, when the agent’s valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a possibly correlated but fixed unknown distribution. Consider a generic mechanism
M

\mathcal{M}

Insufficiency of Fixed Discretization.

When faced with a large action space, the natural approach in online learning is to discretize it (e.g.,
Kleinberg et al. 2019
). This method splits the problem in two: finding offline a finite subset of actions that well approximates the optimal action
uniformly over all possible inputs
and then running online a
discrete
learning algorithm on the discretized action space. The most natural discretization of our mechanism space is to cast a uniform
\varepsilon
-grid on the
[0,1]^{2}
square and consider all the
\binom{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\varepsilon}}}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}}
mechanisms
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}_{\varepsilon}
with allocation regions that can be described as the union of the cells of the grid.

2

The family
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}_{\varepsilon}
is a uniform
\varepsilon

(
Durvasula et al. 2023

Unfortunately, as we show in the following example, this rich class of mechanisms does not provide a uniform approximation for our problem. This is due to the non-continuity (and thus non-Lipschitzness) of our objective function.

A second, natural, approach consists in implementing some sort of local search in the mechanism space, as in e.g., stochastic gradient ascent. Regardless of the metric or notion of concavity we could impose on the non-parametric mechanism space

Impossibility of Uniform Learning.
The third standard strategy leverages the uniform learnability of the objective functions. Suppose for the sake of the argument that we were able to prove a statement of the following type: for any precision
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to reconstruct the expected profit of
all
mechanisms at the same time (up to an
\varepsilon

n(\varepsilon)

\mathcal{D}

\sum_{t}\varepsilon_{t}

\varepsilon_{t}

The road-block to pursuing this strategy in our case is that the family of profit function is complex from the statistical point of view, as it exhibits unbounded pseudo-dimension and constant Rademacher complexity (even the range space of monotone allocation region has unbounded VC dimension, see
Appendix

C

is not possible
in our problem, as we demonstrate in the following example (See also

Our Techniques.
We overcome these barriers by controlling a stochastic process that simulates a branch-and-bound strategy among sets of increasingly powerful mechanisms. The branch-and-bound strategy initially considers fixed-price mechanisms (rectangular allocation regions), then iteratively branches off multiple refined mechanisms by modifying the boundaries of simpler ones. As we refine these mechanisms, they become richer and exhibit potentially larger profits. Thus, we implicitly explore the entire search space for mechanisms, avoiding the barrier inherent in local improvements. The issue with considering richer mechanisms is that we no longer can show that their empirical profit is concentrated around the real expected profit, due to the impossibility of uniform learning. Our important insight is that the impossibility of uniform learning in some sense mainly affects the mechanisms with poor empirical profit, thus we can abstain from search paths that explore mechanisms with small profit and focus on refining profitable mechanisms.

We adapt our characterization of profitable mechanisms as we explore the search space. This addresses the lack of good fixed discretization strategies, as we are learning a (data and distribution dependent) discretization on the fly. For the profitable mechanisms, this branch-and-bound strategy mimics a chaining analysis, a powerful tool for bounding suprema of stochastic processes (see
Talagrand 2014
).
Chaining has been used in a wide variety of applications including generalization bounds
(
Audibert and Bousquet 2007
;
Cohen-Addad et al. 2025b
;
Li et al. 2000
)
, dimensionality reduction
(
Bourgain et al. 2015
;
Narayanan and Nelson 2019
)
, error correcting codes
(
Rudra and Wootters 2014
)
, graph sparsification
(
Jambulapati et al. 2023
;
Lee 2023
)
, data structures
(
Braverman et al. 2017
)
, and compression
(
Cohen-Addad et al. 2022
;
Cohen-Addad et al. 2025a
)
; see
Nelson 2016
for more applications and references.
The chaining analysis gives us very tight control over the profit of good mechanisms, which allows us to learn a sequence of mechanisms with nearly optimal regret. We give a more technical overview of the key ingredients in our proof in
Section

1.3
Further Related Work

Efficiency and Profit in Bilateral Trade.
The celebrated result of
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
on bilateral trade (strengthening an earlier result of
Vickrey 1961
) states that even for independent and regular valuation distributions, it is impossible to simultaneously achieve (a) incentive compatibility—even the weaker notion of Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC)— (b) perfect efficiency,
(i.e., trade whenever the buyer has a higher valuation than the seller), (c) individual rationality, and (d) budget balance (requiring that the broker does not run a deficit). On the positive side, they also characterize the optimal BIC mechanisms (which turn out to be DSIC) for social welfare, gains from trade, and profit under some assumptions on the valuations’ distributions.

Motivated by this impossibility result, a fruitful line of work in computer science has aimed at mechanisms that are incentive compatible and budget balanced, while achieving a provable worst-case approximation guarantee — with respect to social welfare
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Cai and Wu 2023
;
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2021
;
Dütting et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2022
)
or gains-from-trade
(
McAfee 2008
;
Fei 2022
;
Cai et al. 2021
;
Deng et al. 2022
;
Brustle et al. 2017
)
.
While these results hold in the Bayesian setting, where the agents’ valuations are drawn independently from some known distribution, some of these approximation mechanisms actually make use of only a few (sometimes just a single sample) from the underlying distributions to achieve constant-factor approximations
(
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2021
;
Dütting et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2022
)
.

The recent work of
Hajiaghayi et al. 2025
studies the compatibility of profit and efficiency (both gains-from-trade and social welfare) in the Bayesian setting. While they demonstrate positive results for fixed-price mechanisms, they present strong impossibility results for general mechanisms.

Sample Complexity.
Beyond the rich line of work on online learning, a related approach considers the
offline
learning problem of economic design problems in the spirit of PAC-learning
(
Cole and Roughgarden 2014
;
Morgenstern and Roughgarden 2015
;
Devanur et al. 2016
;
Cai and Daskalakis 2017
;
Gonczarowski and Weinberg 2021
, e.g.,)
. While the standard online-to-offline reduction tells that sample-complexity results imply online regret bounds, we are not aware of any prior work yielding non-trivial results for our settings. For instance,
Cole and Roughgarden 2014
consider the setting where bidders’ valuations are independent and exhibit some regularity; we consider a bilateral trade with arbitrary and possibly correlated distributions.
Morgenstern and Roughgarden 2015
provide results for finite pseudo-dimension; in
Appendix

C
we prove that such measure is unbounded in our problem.
Guo et al. 2021
show general sample complexity bounds for product distributions. Their results imply sample complexity bounds for a range of applications, via appropriate discretizations of the value space. In contrast, a feature of our problem is the lack of a viable discretization (see
Example

1
). They also provide results for
monotone
problems, something our setting does not enjoy.

Profit in Bilateral Trade.
In the (static) bilateral trade problem, a seller and a buyer interact with each other. The seller holds a single good. The seller’s and buyer’s (private) valuations for the good are
v_{s}\in[0,1]
and
v_{b}\in[0,1]

b_{s}\in[0,1]
and
b_{b}\in[0,1]

M
. A mechanism is characterized by an allocation region
A\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
, and pricing rules
p,q:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]

The trade happens if and only if the bids
(b_{s},b_{b})
belong to the allocation region
A
, while the payments are made according to
p
and
q
.
Payments are from the broker to the seller, and from the buyer to the broker.
That is, the seller receives
p(b_{s},b_{b})
,
while the buyer pays
q(b_{s},b_{b})
.
We require that
p(b_{s},b_{b})=q(b_{s},b_{b})=0
whenever
(b_{s},b_{b})\not\in A
, i.e., there is no trade.
4

4

The buyer’s and seller’s utility with valuations
v_{b}
and

We focus on dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) and individually rational (IR) mechanisms. There, each player maximizes his utility by a truthful bid regardless of the other player’s bid, and the utility from participating in the mechanism is at least as high as that from not participating in the mechanism (i.e., it is at least

We study the repeated bilateral trade problem, defined as follows. At each time step

by
the buyer and that paid

The Learning Protocol

We aim to design a learning algorithm that minimizes the regret:

\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v^{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{t},v^{t})\right].

The typical goal is to achieve sublinear worst-case regret; this implies that on any input, the algorithm’s profit converges, on average, to that of the benchmark.

We consider both the adversarial setting, where the sequence of valuations is generated up-front by an oblivious adversary, and the stochastic i.i.d. one, where the agents’ valuations
v^{t}=(v_{s}^{t},v_{b}^{t})
are drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution. In the latter model, the
v^{t}
are independent across time, but the seller and buyer valuations can be arbitrarily correlated.

We formally introduce monotonicity and Myerson payments
(

x\preceq y
(
y
dominates
x
) if
x_{1}\geq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq y_{2}.

An allocation region
A\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
is monotone (with respect to
\preceq
) if for any
x\in A
and
y\in[0,1]^{2}
with
x\preceq y
it holds that
y\in A
.

Definition 2

(Myerson Payments)
.

Let
A
be a monotone allocation region, then the associated Myerson payments are defined as follows:

\displaystyle p(b_{s},b_{b})=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{{(b_{s},b_{b})\in A\}}}\cdot\max\{x\in[0,1]\mid(x,b_{b})\in A\}

\displaystyle\forall\;(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle q(b_{s},b_{b})=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{{(b_{s},b_{b})\in A\}}}\cdot\min\{y\in[0,1]\mid(b_{s},y)\in A\}

\displaystyle\forall\;(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

In words, for an allocation region to be monotone it should be “closed” in a north-west direction. That is, if a point
(b_{s},b_{b})
is in
A
then any point
(b_{s}^{\prime},b_{b})
with
b_{s}^{\prime}\leq b_{s}
and any point
(b_{s},b_{b}^{\prime})
with
b_{b}^{\prime}\geq b_{b}
should be in
A
as well. The payments of a point
(b_{s},b_{b})\in A
, in turn, correspond to the south-east projection to the allocation boundary. See
Figure

1(a)
for illustrations.

Proposition 1
.

A mechanism
M
for bilateral trade is dominant-strategy incentive compatible and individually rational if and only if its allocation region is monotone and the payments are Myerson payments.

The proof of
Proposition

1
follows by standard arguments
(
Myerson 1981
;
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
)
. For completeness, we provide a proof in
Appendix

. An important consequence of
Proposition

is that it reduces the learning problem to learning the monotone allocation region
A
, with the learner’s profit given by Myerson payments.
Note, though, that this is far from trivializing the problem, as we formally argue in
Appendix

C
.

Profit and Properties of Optimal Mechanism.

We conclude the preliminaries with some observations regarding the tradeoffs involved in choosing different (monotone) allocation regions. The first observation is that by shrinking the allocation region we loose potentially profitable trades, while we increase the profit of those trades that still happen.

Observation 1

(Trades vs. Profit Trade-off)
.

Consider any two mechanisms
M,M^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}
with allocation regions
A,A^{\prime}
such that
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
.
For any
v\in[0,1]^{2}
we have one of three cases:

•

v\notin A^{\prime}
, and
\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)=\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{\prime},v)=0

•

v\in A^{\prime}\setminus A
, and
\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)=0

•

v\in A
, and
\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)\geq\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{\prime},v)

This trade-off allows us to derive a useful property of the optimal mechanism: its allocation region does not contain the lower-right triangle! Denote with
U
the upper-left triangle,
U=\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}\,|\,x\leq y\}
, and with
\mathcal{M}^{+}
the subfamily of
\mathcal{M}
containing the mechanisms whose allocation region is contained in
U
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For any mechanism
M\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a mechanism
M^{+}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}
such that, for any valuation
v
, the following inequality holds:

{\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{+},v)}\geq{\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)}.

Proof.

Fix any mechanism
M\in\mathcal{M}
with allocation region
A
and payment functions
p,q
, and consider the mechanism
M^{+}
defined by the allocation region
A^{+}=A\cap U
, where
A
is the allocation region of
M
. The set
A^{+}
is monotone and thus is a valid allocation region. Fix any valuation pair
v=(v_{s},v_{b})
; we want to prove that
\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{+},v)\geq\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v).
From
1
, we know we only need to consider the case in which
v\in A\setminus A^{+}
. If
v\in A\setminus A^{+}
, then it does not belong to
U
and we have the following inequality:

\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)=(q(v)-p(v))\leq(v_{b}-v_{s})\leq 0=\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{+},v),

where the first inequality holds by individual rationality, and the second because
v\notin U
.
∎

Remark 1

(The Allocation Regions Are Closed)
.

For convenience, we always assume that the allocation regions are closed. This is without loss of generality; in fact, for any allocation region in
U
, if we consider its closure, we get a mechanism that extracts at least as much revenue. This assumption allows us to simplify and formalize some arguments; for instance, if
A
is closed, then the
\max
and
\min
operations in the definition of the payments are well defined.

3.2
Main Result:
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
Regret

The
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
provides the important insight that among the
\exp(\nicefrac{{(\log t)}}{{\varepsilon}})
many candidate mechanisms in
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
induced by the samples, there is an
M^{t}
whose expected profit is with an additive error of
t^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
of the expected profit of
M_{\textrm{opt}}
.
To ensure that the empirical profit of
M^{t}
is close to the expected profit of
M^{t}
, the previous approach consisted in simply performing a union bound over all such candidate
\exp(\nicefrac{{(\log t)}}{{\varepsilon}})
mechanisms.
This approach is very natural, as it is difficult to characterize profitable from unprofitable mechanisms a priori.
At the same time, the approach is lossy: We actually do not need strong concentration for unprofitable mechanisms in
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
.

We now describe how our chaining approach, originally designed to perform very efficient union bounds for uniform convergence, can provide some intuition on how to identify profitable mechanisms on the samples.
While we cannot guarantee that we estimate the profit of all mechanisms in
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
accurately, we show that this is possible for profitable mechanisms, while unprofitable mechanisms are either identified as such, or have their profit underestimated.
Suppose we have a set of mechanisms
\mathcal{N}_{h}
, such that for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}
with

\left|\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v^{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{\prime},v^{i})\right|\leq 2^{-h}\quad\forall v^{1},v^{2},\dots,v^{t}

(5)

A crucial ingredient in chaining is to decompose the target profit
\textnormal{{profit}}(M,\cdot)
via a telescopic argument that uses a sequence of approximating mechanisms as follows

\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v^{i})=\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{0}\left(M\right),v^{i})+\sum_{h=0}^{\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}}\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i}),

where each
\phi_{h}\left(M\right)
belongs to the corresponding
\mathcal{N}_{h}
. This sequence of approximating mechanisms is visualized in
Figure

2
. Our goal is to learn the expected profit of
M
, which translates to learning separately the terms of the above formula. The zero-th term can be addressed with standard union bound techniques by noting that
\phi_{0}\left(M\right)
is a fixed-price mechanism (see
Figure

2(a)
), so we only focus on learning the increments.
The idea inherent in chaining is that instead of performing a union bound on all target mechanisms in one shot, we perform a union bound on the set of successive increments. Specifically, by combining basic union bounds similar to those used in
Equation

3
and a reduced variance due to
Equation

5
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(M_{h\!+\!1},M_{h})\\
\in\mathcal{N}_{h\!+\!1}\times\mathcal{N}_{h}\end{subarray}}\!\left|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{t}\big(\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{h\!+\!1},v^{i})\!-\!\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{h},v^{i})\big)\!-\!\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{h\!+\!1},v)\!-\!\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{h},v)\right]\right|\!\lesssim\!\sqrt{\frac{\log|N_{h\!+\!1}||\mathcal{N}_{h}|}{t\cdot 2^{-2h}}}.

At this point, we wish to find a careful tradeoff between the magnitude of increments and the size of the families
\mathcal{N}_{h}
.
Optimistically, we would attempt to use
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
as
\mathcal{N}_{h}
, for appropriate values of
\varepsilon
, which would bound the right-hand-side of the above inequality with

\frac{1}{2^{h}}\sqrt{\frac{\log|\mathcal{N}_{h+1}||\mathcal{N}_{h}|}{t}}\lesssim\frac{1}{2^{h}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{h}\log t}{t}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log t}{t\cdot 2^{h}}}.

(6)

Summing these terms for all increments would then yield

\displaystyle\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}}\left|\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v^{i})-\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)\right]\right|\lesssim\sum_{h\geq 0}\sqrt{\frac{\log t}{t\cdot 2^{h}}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{t}},

achieving the desired regret of
\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{T}\right)
(via an analysis similar to the one in
Equation

4
).

Unfortunately, there is a major issue with this (ideal) analysis: mechanisms in
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
do not satisfy
Equation

5
, as they only provide a
one-sided
bound!
This requires several major adjustments to the chaining analysis; overcoming them constitutes the core technical contribution of our work.
Our first important ingredient is to show that for any mechanism
M
, there exists
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
such that

\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v^{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{\prime},v^{i})\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots t\}.

(7)

Notice the absence of absolute values when comparing this guarantee with
Equation

5
.
By itself,
Equation

7
does not facilitate the chaining analysis.
However, for any telescoping sequence in which the increments are strictly increasing, that is
\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})\geq\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})
for all
v^{i}
, it turns out that
Equation

7
is sufficient to prove concentration.

Let us interpret when this happens and more importantly when this does not happen. If
\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})\geq\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})
for all
v^{i}
, then
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)
extracts slightly more profit than
\phi_{h}\left(M\right)
from the distribution. In this case,
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)
is clearly preferable to
\phi_{h}\left(M\right)
on the sample. Fortunately,
Equation

7
implies
|\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})|\leq 2^{-h}
, which implies with the optimistic chaining analysis implies that the empirical profit of
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)
is concentrated around its expectation.

Next, let us consider the more challenging case where
\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})\leq\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})
. In this case, a possible configuration is
\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})\leq 0
and
\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for a few samples
6
6

6

These numbers are possible, but chosen arbitrarily for the sake of the argument. The main point is to emphasize that the absolute value of differences can be a fairly large constant.
. Based on a single sample
v^{i}
, it is typically not possible to determine whether
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)
is more profitable than
\phi_{h}\left(M\right)
as it can exclude the potential profit of
v^{i}
for more profit on other candidate samples.
Unfortunately, in this case, we also have

\left|\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right),v^{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}\left(M\right),v^{i})\right|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}.

(8)

This is fatal for a chaining analysis: If this happens too many times, then the increments (i.e. the right-hand-side of
Equation

6
) can only be bounded by
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{2^{h}}}{{t}}\cdot\log t}
, and summing up over all increments yields no concentration whatsoever.

Our main insight is that if
Equation

8
happens for too many samples
v^{i}
, then
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)

has less profit than
M_{h}
and cannot be the empirical optimum!
Moreover, the same also holds for any mechanism that refines
\phi_{h+1}\left(M\right)
via
Equation

2
, allowing us to remove all of these mechanisms for the search space of potentially optimal mechanisms (i.e., we are breaking the chain).
Thus, we can show that a (slightly weaker) form of
Equation

6
holds for mechanisms with sufficiently large profit, which still allows us to obtain the desired
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound.

In a nutshell, we have the following dichotomy working in our favor (formalized in
Proposition

5
):

•

Either all but a very tiny fraction of the increments are positive. In this case, we can continue the chaining analysis.

•

Or sufficiently many increments have a large negative value. In this case, the mechanism is suboptimal, and we no longer have to prove concentration.

While the remaining analysis still has substantial technical challenges, the on-the-fly construction and truncation of chains ultimately leading towards optimal or nearly optimal mechanisms is our main novelty. We therefore believe that our work can be a starting point for proving low regret bounds for other problems lacking strong characterizations of optima. Indeed, as a consequence of our work, we also manage to extend our analysis to the Joint-Ads problem.

Lemma 2

)

Let

Lemma 3
.

For any set

, the following equality holds true:

\sum_{v\in S}\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{\pi},v)=\sum_{e\in\pi}w_{e}\cdot|A_{e}\cap S|.

Proof.

Consider any complete path

This structural lemma allows us to argue that the optimal mechanism on a fixed and finite set

Theorem 1
.

For any finite set of points

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{v\in S}\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)=\sum_{v\in S}\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{\pi},v),

4.1.2
The Simplification Procedure

We introduce a procedure that allows us to associate to each mechanism

Definition 6

(Boundary)

For any mechanism

\partial M=\left\{v\in A_{M}|\,\forall\varepsilon>0,\left([0,1]^{2}\setminus A_{M}\right)\cap B_{\varepsilon}(v)\neq\emptyset\right\}

-

-
(1,1)

.

The boundary

of a mechanism

characterizes the allocation
A
as any point in

\partial M

We refer to points on the curve sorted according to the natural left-to-right and bottom-up parametrization:

For any

. For any mechanism

Properties of the Simplified Mechanism.

Before moving on, we present some simple but crucial properties of simplified mechanisms.

Proposition 2

(Properties of Simplified Mechanisms)
.

Consider any mechanism

and precision

, then the following properties are verified:

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

We finally address point (iii). This is equivalent to proving that

4.2
Approximating Sequence of Mechanisms

In this section, we show how to iteratively apply the simplification procedure to construct a sequence of approximating mechanisms, and analyze its properties.

Definition 8

(Approximating Sequence)

Let

Proposition 3

For any mechanism

\phi_{h}\left(M\right)=\phi_{h}\left(\phi_{h^{\prime}}\left(M\right)\right)

•

(Monotonicity)
A_{\phi_{h^{\prime}}\left(M\right)}\subseteq A_{\phi_{h}\left(M\right)}

From point (iii) of

2

. Since

, which, in turn, implies
9
9

.
that

We move to the second property. We have that

Lemma 4

Fix any precision

Proof.

By construction from

-grid is bounded by

To prove this, notice that a complete path must go from the segment
(0,0)
-
(0,1)

We derive the

Consider the probability measure

square. Independently, the learner proposes a mechanism
M^{t}
to the agents, who then declare bids
(b_{1}^{t},b_{2}^{t})
. A mechanism
M^{t}
is composed by a non-excludable allocation rule
x^{t}:[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\}
(either both agents win the auction or both lose) and two payment functions
p_{1}^{t},p_{2}^{t}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
that map bids to allocation and payment, respectively. The revenue of mechanism
M^{t}
when the bids are
b^{t}_{1}
and
b_{2}^{t}
is defined as

\textsf{rev}(M^{t},b^{t}_{1},b^{t}_{2})=x^{t}(b^{t}_{1},b^{t}_{2})\cdot[p^{t}_{1}(b^{t}_{1},b^{t}_{2})+p^{t}_{2}(b^{t}_{1},b^{t}_{2})].

Structure of Incentive Compatible Mechanisms.

As in the bilateral trade problem, the agents behave strategically, meaning that they strive to maximize their own quasi-linear utility
u^{t}_{i}(b_{1}^{t},b_{2}^{t})=v^{t}_{i}\cdot x^{t}(b_{1}^{t},b_{2}^{t})-p^{t}_{i}(b_{1}^{t},b_{2}^{t})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Note that, differently from the bilateral trade problem, here the two agents are symmetric, as they are both interested in purchasing a good. We require the mechanisms
M^{t}
to be DSIC and IR. The family of mechanisms
\mathcal{M}
that respect these two properties can be easily characterized because this model falls within the framework of single parameter auctions
(
Myerson 1981
)
, as each agent is characterized by a single value representing its value for the good for sale. This tells us that
(i)
an allocation rule is implementable if and only if it is monotone and
(ii)
for each such allocation rule there exists a unique payment rule that completes it to a DSIC and IR mechanism.
17
17

17

As in bilateral trade, payments are unique only up to constant shifts. Since our goal is to maximize revenue, we adopt the maximum payments to achieve DSIC and IR.
For this reason, we only talk about valuations in the rest of the section, as the agents are incentivized to behave truthfully.

Differences with Bilateral Trade.

There are two main differences with bilateral trade. First, the two agents are symmetric; this induces a different notion of monotonicity: valuations
(v_{1},v_{2})
dominate
(v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime})
if
v_{i}\geq v_{i}^{\prime}
for
i=1,2.
Stated differently, if at least one of the two agents raises its bid, then the likelihood of having a trade does not decrease. Second, in the joint ads problem, the objective of the learner, i.e., the revenue, lies in
[0,2]
, as opposed to the profit in bilateral trade, which lives in
[-1,1]
. This is a
crucial
distinction: while in both problems the event “no trade” induces zero gain to the learner, in bilateral trade, there is a notion of “bad trade” that induces
negative
utility for the learner. On the contrary, in the joint ads problem, every trade guarantees at least non-negative payments
to
the learner.

Appendix B
Efficiency Maximization and Budget Balance

In this section, we discuss the efficiency maximization version of the bilateral trade problem. Let
M
be a mechanism with allocation region
A
, the social welfare and gain from trade induced by
M
on agents with valuations
v_{s}
and
v_{b}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{GFT}(M,v_{s},v_{b})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{{(v_{s},v_{b})\in A\}}}(v_{b}-v_{s})

(Gain-From-Trade)

\displaystyle\textsf{SW}(M,v_{s},v_{b})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{{(v_{s},v_{b})\in A\}}}(v_{b}-v_{s})+v_{s}

(Social Welfare)

Note that social welfare is an additive factor away from gain from trade; therefore, from the regret perspective, these two objectives are exactly equivalent.

Budget Balance.

Beyond DSIC and IR, another natural property required to efficiency maximizing mechanisms for bilateral trade is budget balance: we want infact to avoid situations in which the mechanism subsidizes the market. There are two main notions of budget balance in the literature
[
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2024a
,
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2016
,
Bernasconi et al. 2024
, see e.g.,]
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v_{s},v_{b})=0\quad\forall(v_{s},v_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

(Strong Budget Balance)

\displaystyle\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v_{s},v_{b})\geq 0\quad\forall(v_{s},v_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

(Weak Budget Balance)

We have all the notions to formally recall the impossibility result by
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
: no mechanism can, at the same time, achieve full efficiency (e.g., maximize social welfare/gain from trade), individual rationality, incentive compatibility and (weak) budget balance.

Fixed-Price Mechanisms.

An important class of mechanisms is composed by
fixed-price mechanisms
, where the broker simply posts two fixed prices
p
to the seller and
q
to the buyer, and the trade happens if and only if both agents agree. In the rest of the section, we clarify what we mean in the introduction by claiming that fixed price mechanisms “characterize” efficiency maximizing mechanisms. We start by recalling a folklore result on strong budget balance mechanisms.

Proposition 8

(e.g.,
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2016
)
.

A mechanism
M
for bilateral trade is dominant-strategy incentive compatible, individually rational, and strongly budget balanced if and only if it is a fixed price mechanism for some price
p
, i.e., if its allocation region is
[0,p]\times[p,1]
.

Weak budget balance has a nice geometric interpretation following the characterization of
Proposition

1
. For any non-trivial mechanism
M
(i.e., with non-empty allocation region), let
\overline{s}
be the price paid to the seller and
\underline{b}
be the price paid by the buyer, under valuation
(0,1)
. From a geometrical point of view, these two numbers correspond to the intersections of the boundary of
M
with the left and top side of the
[0,1]^{2}
. We have the following result.

Proposition 9

(Characterization of Weak-Budget Balanced Mechansism)
.

A mechanism
M
for bilateral trade that is dominant-strategy incentive compatible and individually rational enforces weak budget balance if and only if
\overline{s}\leq\underline{b}
. Moreover, the social welfare and gain from trade of any such mechanism is dominated by the fixed price mechanism with allocation region
[0,\overline{s}]\times[\underline{b},1].

Proof.

Clearly, if a mechanism does not respect
\overline{s}\leq\underline{b}
, then it violates budget balance under valuation
(0,1)
. The other implication follows by noting that, for any valuation
(v_{s},v_{b})
that results in a trade, we have:

p(v_{s},v_{b})\leq\overline{s}\leq\underline{b}\leq q(v_{s},v_{b}).

Consider now the last part of the statement. Increasing the allocation region while retaining budget balance and monotonicity always (weakly) increases efficiency. Therefore taking the maximal monotone region compatible with
\underline{b}
and
\overline{s}
is clearly a (weak) improvement.
∎

Budget Balance and Profit.

Since the focus of this paper is on profit maximization, there is no reason to constrain the optimization procedure to DSIC and IR mechanisms that also enforce weak budget balance (clearly, the profit of any strongly budget balance mechanism is always
0
). In fact, there are simple examples in which there is a large gap between the expected profit of the best mechanism and the best budget balance one.

Example 4

(Budget Balance is Suboptimal)
.

Consider the uniform distribution over
(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}})
and
(\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}},1)
. The optimal mechanism allocates in the
(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}})
,
(\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}},1)
,
(0,1)
triangle, for an expected profit of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}.
Such mechanism is not budget balanced, as
\overline{s}=\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}
and
\underline{b}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}

(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}})
or
(\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}},1)

\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}}

Beyond Dominant-Strategy Incentive Compatibility.

The impossibility results of
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
hold in the more general setting where mechanisms only need to enforce incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and budget balance
in expectation.
These mechanisms clearly do not respect the characterization provided in

1

8

and no point in
S\setminus S^{\prime}.
Moreover, the associated mechanism

extracts exactly profit
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
from each point in

. We then have:

\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\cdot\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v_{i})\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert\frac{1}{m}\sum_{v_{i}\in S^{\prime}}\sigma_{i}\cdot\textnormal{{profit}}(M^{\prime},v_{i})\right\rvert=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{v_{i}\in S^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4},

where in the last inequality we use the assumption

.

If we now take the expectation with respect to the Rademacher random variables, we get a
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
lower bound. This means that the same bound also holds when taking an outer expectation with respect to the distribution of
S
, since it holds for almost every realization of
S
. We have then proved the desired statement:

\mathbb{E}_{S,\sigma}\left[\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\cdot\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v_{i})\right\rvert\right]\geq\frac{1}{4},

Note, our bound crucially

depend on the number

of samples.
∎
