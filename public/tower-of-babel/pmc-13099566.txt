Quantifying and combating zoonotic viruses poised for emergence: Using multiplex receptor screening and decoy engineering technologies
Authors: Alex Odoom, Eugene M. Obeng, Christian K. O. Dzuvor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viral discovery efforts have uncovered millions of zoonotic viruses across multiple continents, listed on the World Health Organization’s pandemic risk register. Over two-thirds of these zoonotic viruses pose significant risks, are poised to infect humans, and expose the limitations of current reactive countermeasures, such as vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, and small-molecule antivirals. To mediate infection, these viruses must undergo significant adaptation and exploit specific receptors on human cells, causing “spillover events.” Identifying which human receptors these zoonotic viruses utilize is crucial for assessing spillover risk, understanding viral tropism, and preparing for emergence—ultimately providing insights for pandemic preparedness, development of new antiviral therapeutics, and potentially saving many lives. However, we hypothesize that these advances will require a systemic understanding and scalable platform technologies on an unprecedented scale. Therefore, we propose and evaluate a new and unique paradigm shift toward proactive antiviral interventions centered on host-identified multivalent “decoy” receptor therapeutics that can impose high evolutionary barriers to resistance, even in convergent evolution scenarios. Specifically, we introduce a unified framework that integrates unique, unbiased high-throughput or multiplex receptor screening platforms with AI–driven design to guide the de novo engineering of pandemic-ready broad-spectrum decoys. We also discuss prominent modular engineering strategies that can translate mere molecular affinities into the avidity necessary for potent pan-viral prophylaxis and therapy. The overall integrated pipeline proposed is expected to facilitate the preemptive development of robust countermeasures that will fundamentally reshape our approach to quantifying and combating known and future zoonotic viral threats.

The COVID-19 pandemic (1) and increased frequency of other recent viral outbreaks, such as monkeypox (2) and avian influenza (3), served as a stark and painful global reminder of humanity’s profound vulnerability to novel viral pathogens. It laid bare critical weaknesses in the global health infrastructure, exposing significant gaps in disease surveillance, inherent fragility within supply chains, and deep-seated disparities in healthcare access worldwide. This crisis unequivocally underscored the devastating consequences that can arise from inadequate early detection and a delayed global response to emerging biological threats, epidemics and pandemics.

Viruses with pandemic potential (VPPs) are characterized by their high transmissibility and virulence, with the capacity for rapid, uncontrollable spread within human populations, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality. Historical and contemporary examples (4), including MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, HIV-1, Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, alphaviruses, and influenza H5N1 viruses from their respective high-risk families, Coronaviridae, Retroviridae, Filoviridae, Arenaviridae, Togaviridae, and Orthomyxoviridae, vividly demonstrate this persistent threat (Fig. 1A). Once a VPPs has surfaced or emerged, medical countermeasures must be instantly deployed at scale. Whereas, current pandemic preparedness heavily emphasizes vaccines (4), monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (5, 6) and small-molecules (7, 8) development, the intrinsic nature of these viruses, marked by their high mutation rates and rapid replication cycles, ensures their continuous evolution and circulation in populations. This relentless adaptability poses an ongoing challenge to existing medical countermeasures and perpetually increases the risk of antiviral drug resistance (9). Moreover, the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) has identified priority virus families with pandemic potential (SI Appendix, Table S1): For many, no vaccines and therapies are available (4, 10). Additionally, lessons learned from COVID-19 pandemic regarding the speed of global pathogen dissemination and consistent emergence of new variants, collectively highlight that a purely reactive approach to pandemic preparedness is insufficient to tackle the majority of these viruses.

Vaccines (4), while proven highly successful in preventing severe illness and death, and indispensable for long-term immunity, typically require months to years for their development. This inherent time lag means that by the time pathogen-specific vaccines or treatments are widely available, the virus may have already further evolved and caused extensive damage (11). Additionally, vaccine-induced antibodies(Abs) often exhibit diminished protection against rapidly emerging viral variants (12). Also, despite their initial promise as dual-purpose technology in prophylaxis and therapy, mAbs face uncontrolled challenges due to immune evasion resulting from continuous viral evolution and mutations. For example, recent Omicron subvariants such as LP.8.1.1, NB.1.8.1, and XFG have demonstrated significant resistance to previously approved anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody therapeutics, rendering many ineffective (13). Similarly, small-molecule antivirals, such as remdesivir and nirmatrelvir, have also shown susceptibility to viral resistance (14). These challenges are further compounded by their mode of transmission. While current approved antivirals primarily target cell-free virions, many viral pathogens spread predominantly through direct cell-to-cell contact (15), which can be 100 to 1,000 times more efficient than cell-free infection (15, 16). For instance, cell-to-cell transmission for HIV-1 is estimated to account for 60% of total infections (15). This high local multiplicity of infection creates a microenvironment in which high concentrations of virions can overwhelm standard antiviral drug concentrations, allowing ongoing replication even in the face of antiretroviral drugs and neutralizing Abs therapy (17, 18). Furthermore, the physical architecture of the virological synapse can shield the virus from potent neutralizing Abs and antiretrovirals, rendering them ineffective (16). Together, this rapid and continuous emergence of viral variants that evade specific countermeasures, such as vaccines, mAbs, and small-molecule antivirals, clearly illustrates an ongoing evolutionary arms race.

Therefore, this situation necessitates a critical shift toward proactive, “day-one” medical countermeasures that can be deployed immediately to mitigate the impact of an outbreak before it escalates into a full-blown pandemic. In fact, there is a compelling need for therapeutic strategies that are inherently less susceptible to viral mutations, evolution, and mode of transmission.

It is worth noting that before “spillover events (animal-to-human transmission),” majority of these VPPs once resided in zoonotic reservoirs (Fig. 1B), highlighting that viruses with zoonotic origins pose significant global health threats, and underscore the need for continued surveillance of animal species. To date, efforts have discovered millions of zoonotic viruses across multiple continents, poised for emergence, which are listed on the World Health Organization (WHO)’s pandemic risk register (19). Only a handful have been studied in the laboratory, ignoring fundamental concerns about their potential to pose dire threats to humans. Moreover, the likelihood of zoonotic spillover has increased recently due to human impact on the planet, such as climate change, urbanization, land-use changes, and global travel (20). Together, these factors raise a fundamental question: Can we identify these zoonotic viruses that pose the greatest risk to humans before their spillover event? To infect and transmit between host species, these viruses must attach and exploit specific cell-surface proteins or invasion receptors and attachment factors (Fig. 1 A-B). “Spillover events” can occur when the virus adapts and binds to specific human cell receptors via specific receptor-binding proteins. There are still known viruses and their variants with unmet clinical needs that infect the majority of the human population, causing life-threatening disease and exhibiting broad cellular tropism with unknown cellular receptor mediating entry (21). For instance, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) exhibits exceptionally broad cellular tropism, replicating in epithelial cells, connective tissue fibroblasts, hepatocytes, leukocytes, and vascular endothelial cells. However, although several attachment factors have been identified, none are currently considered bona fide functional cellular receptors (22). Moreover, influenza virus attaches to sialic acid moieties on the cell surface, but the specific cell surface receptors that trigger viral internalization remain unknown (23). Nonetheless, emerging research suggests that some influenza A viruses mediate cross-species entry via MHC-II proteins, perhaps increasing spillover risk (24, 25). Additionally, although receptors such as nectin-1 and herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM) are well characterized for general cellular entry for herpes simplex virus type 1 and 2(HSV-1, HSV-2), the specific mechanism by which HSV-1 and HSV-2 enter neuronal axons via membrane fusion is believed to involve signaling through unknown cellular receptors that interact with their gK/UL20 complexes (26).

Therefore, discovering these human receptors will be crucial for understanding viral tropism, quantifying spillover risk and preparing for the next pandemic. Ultimately, this will lead to pandemic-ready, broadly acting antiviral solutions and provide insights to inform further research on pandemic prevention, as well as potentially for applications such as gene therapy and immune interactions. These discoveries and understanding would take decades of research and would not be possible with classical biochemical and biophysical (interactomics, glycomics, and proteomics) and genetic receptor screening approaches (21, 27–29). Rather, we hypothesize that they will require advances in systematic, scalable, cost-effective, level 3/4 biocontainment-free, and high-throughput interrogation at unprecedented scale to identify these human receptors and develop universal multivalent “decoy” receptor therapeutics (UMDRTs).

This perspective proposes a unified framework to simultaneously quantify zoonotic risk and deploy preemptive countermeasures against known and zoonotic viruses with pandemic potential. This strategy is built upon two synergistic technological pillars: i) high-throughput, multiplexed receptor screening to systematically and comprehensively identify and prioritize human receptors used by zoonotic viruses, and ii) universal multivalent decoy therapeutics engineered to block viral entry and infection. Compared to current reactive approaches such as vaccine, mAbs, and small-molecule antivirals, the capability of deploying this strategy within hours to days is important for outbreak preparedness and future countermeasure development. Also, if a virus mutates to escape a host-derived therapy, it must simultaneously retain its ability to infect host cells, which often relies on conserved interactions with host receptors. This fundamental biological constraint on viral evolution makes host-mimicking approaches particularly robust, compelling future development efforts to strategically prioritize therapies that target conserved host pathways or essential viral entry mechanisms, as these present a significantly higher barrier to viral resistance than direct viral protein targets.

Host-Derived Decoy Receptors: A Conserved Pathway to Combat Evolving Viruses

As discussed above, available reactive medical countermeasures and approaches will likely be outpaced by viral evolution and escape, necessitating a paradigm shift and the immediate pursuit of more robust antiviral solutions. Decoy receptors therefore emerge as a promising alternative strategy. Decoy receptors are ingeniously designed molecules that mimic natural host receptors. Their mechanism involves binding to specific viral ligands, such as viral binding proteins, with high affinity. This competitive binding prevents the virus from engaging its actual receptor on the host cells, effectively blocking entry, infection, transmission, and replication (30).

This approach offers several distinct advantages. First, it is theoretically broad-spectrum, as it can block all viruses that use the same entry pathway. Second, and most critically, it imposes a profound fitness cost on the virus if it attempts to mutate to escape. Any mutation that disrupts binding to the decoy will almost certainly also disrupt binding to the native receptor, rendering the virus noninfectious and nonreplicative, and emergence of resistant viral strains are highly unlikely (31).

While targeting the primary receptor is often effective, some viruses use accessory factors or coreceptors that can facilitate entry. For example, in addition to the main receptor, ACE2, neuropilin-1 (NRP1), and Transferrin have been identified as a coreceptor that enhances SARS-CoV-2 infection (32, 33), and their decoys can be used in combination with ACE2 decoys to achieve synergistic effects as observed with other combination therapies against SARS-CoV-2 and other viral diseases (34–36). However, the physiological relevance and quantitative contributions of the reported coreceptors to viral entry need to be completely characterized before their combination and application in broad-spectrum decoy design. This suggests that there are multiple, evolutionarily stable points in the viral life cycle at which host factors can be targeted. The ultimate success of decoy therapies will likely depend on a nuanced understanding of the complete entry pathway for each viral family, potentially leading to combination therapies that maximize efficacy and minimize the risk of resistance. SI Appendix, Table S1 summarizes the high-risk viral families and host entry receptors or factors used by representative highly pathogenic viruses.

Despite the promise of these combination decoy therapies, there is a majority of rapidly evolved viral variants/strains of submergence and re-emerged highly virulent strains within subfamilies, against which these single decoy therapies may not be effective. The basis for phenotypic change, shift, or convergent evolution events by these viruses remains unclear. Additionally, there are hundreds of viruses within subfamilies circulating in zoonotic reservoirs and listed on WHO’s pandemic register, which are closely related to known highly pathogenic or high-risk human viruses. These viruses have been largely overlooked, with uncharacterized host receptors or factors used in mediating infections. Nonetheless, with recent complex interactions between humans, animals, and ecosystem (20), we hypothesize that these newly evolved and animal viruses may use known receptors once identified for high-risk viruses (SI Appendix, Table S1).Recent discoveries support this hypothesis and/or observation. To mention a few, neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which was once identified as an essential and universal receptor for enteroviruses (37) and astroviruses (38–40), is now recognized as a pan-receptor for arteriviruses (41). Also, DPP4, a bona fide receptor for MERS-CoV (42), has been functionally characterized as a receptor/coreceptor for classical human astroviruses (39, 40). We also note the utilization of VLDLR, ApoER2/LRP8 and PCDH10; and LRP1 by multiple arboviruses (43–46) and bunyaviruses (47–49), respectively. Additionally, recent studies have revealed that multiple close relatives of highly pathogenic MERS-CoV in bats and minks, including NeoCoV, MOW15-22, HKU25, HKU5, and MRCoV, have evolved and independently use ACE2 but not DPP4, as an entry receptor (50–57). These findings indicate independent ACE2 utilization or acquisition at least three times among merbecovirus lineages and at least twice among sarbecoviruses (58, 59) and other coronaviruses, CoVs [SARS-CoV (60), SARS-CoV-2 (61), and NL63-CoV (61)]. Moreover, we note aminopeptidase N(APN) receptor-mediated entry and infection by other CoVs from multiple convergent evolution events for 229E-CoV (62), TGEV/CCoV-HuPn-2018/FCoV-23 (63–65), PDCoV (66), and European hedgehogs merbecoviruses (67, 68). The list goes on and on, with the most recently identified human dipeptidase 1(DPEP1) and multiple LDLR family members (LRP1, LRP4, and VLDLR) (69) being functional receptors for a porcine hemagglutinating encephalomyelitis coronavirus (PHEV) (70) and yellow fever virus(YFV) cellular entry, respectively, and we anticipate numerous soon. Therefore, shall we begin to rapidly and comprehensively identify these receptors or host attachment factors before spillover events, universal multivalent decoy receptor therapeutics (UMDRTs) are near to combat multiple viral species across different lineages, and can be stockpiled and used in the event of any outbreak or pandemic.

Multiplex Screening Platform Technologies for Receptor Discovery

To develop these UMDRTs for a particular family or subfamily, it is necessary to elucidate the receptors these viruses use for tropism at an unprecedented scale. Widely used screening approaches (as recently reviewed and summarized herein (27, 29)), such as traditional ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay), surface plasmon resonance (SPR), biolayer interferometry (BLI), flow cytometry–based assay, ligand-based receptor capture, shotgun glycomics, shotgun proteomics, protein and glycan microarrays, virus overlay protein binding assay (VOPBA), pseudotype and live virus entry assays, and affinity purification followed by mass spectrometry (AP-MS), have several limitations (as summarized in SI Appendix, Table S2), such as low sensitivity, low throughput, and cell type specificity, and cannot meet the much needed expanded understanding at scale. For instance, pseudotype and live virus entry assays are time-consuming, with the latter requiring Level 3/4 biocontainment to handle live, highly pathogenic viruses. Additionally, AP-MS and VOPBA are limited to specific cell types and highly expressed receptors, making it impossible to detect virus surface protein-receptor engagements that involve protein–protein interactions (PPI) with low binding affinities.

In contrast, the advent of high-throughput gene perturbation approaches [as recently reviewed herein (27, 71)], such as RNA interference (RNAi), random insertional mutagenesis and CRISPR, CRISPR-Cas/CRISPR associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) screens (CRISPR knockouts, activation (CRISPRa), and interference (CRISPRi)) in the past 2 decades have enabled systemic identification of host entry factors or receptors for viral replication on a genome-wide scale. Despite these significant advances, these approaches have multiple off-target effects, and are limited to specific cell types and highly expressed receptors, which could influence the outcome of the screens. Consequently, these approaches are best suited for identifying the most stable and highly expressed host entry factors or receptors and are generally not suitable for detecting transient protein interactions on cell surfaces. Additionally, there is a dearth of optimal technologies to characterize virus receptor–protein interactions.

In this context, high-throughput multiplex screening platform technologies become paramount for unbiased high-sensitivity detection of multiple receptor–protein interactions. It will create a powerful discovery engine that can identify multiple entry receptors and host factors from vast libraries and revolutionize our understanding of viral tropism. Also, it will be a scalable strategic retooling of the receptor screening and discovery pipeline to keep pace with the evolution and combat emerging viruses due to increased likelihood of zoonotic spillover events from human impacts on the planet (20). Most importantly, as we advocate for UMDRTs concept, driven by our observation and/or hypothesis that many zoonotic viruses or human viruses with unknown or shifts in receptor usage due to submergence, may utilize commonly known or existing human receptors or orthologs from their cognate host species, multiplex screening is the future. It will enable simultaneous uncovering of multiple receptors and landscapes of these viruses due to their broad cellular tropism. Basically, depending on the viral family, one can easily build one-pot library-on-library interaction screening of known receptors summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1, their orthologs from cognate host species, all bioinformatically or manually curated surface-expressed transmembrane receptors based on cellular tropism against receptor-binding proteins from viruses poised for spillover and listed WHO’s pandemic register.

Motivated by recent highly multiplex bead-based (72–74) and phage display library (75) approaches for rapid, accurate and portable pathogen detection, and profiling antibody responses, we provide and evaluate five distinct, powerful, high-throughput, and highly sensitive multiplex screening platforms (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Table S2), which we believe can transform this course and collectively accelerate the development of robust, proactive antiviral solutions. Additionally, the multivalent presentation of viral proteins or receptors, respectively, on these multiplex bead nanoparticle systems enables mimicry of the densely decorated viral surface or target cells, revealing a broader receptor binding profile and facilitating the identification of weak interactions, compared to their monomeric counterparts in conventional assays. These approaches include DNA-barcoded cellular screens, Luminex xMAP bead assays, AlphaScreen, Meso Scale Discovery(MSD) technology, and DNA-barcoded phage display with next-generation sequencing, to map virus–receptor interaction landscapes at unprecedented scale, and have been thoroughly discussed in SI Appendix, section 1. Once these candidate receptors or host factors are identified, they must be validated using a combination of orthogonal biochemical approaches (SPR and BLI), cell-based in vitro assays (lentiviral overexpression, pseudotyping, and CRISPR/Cas9 knockdown), in vivo using animal models, and structural methods (cryoelectron microscopy (Cryo-EM), cryoelectron tomography (Cryo-ET), and X-ray crystallography). These final steps aim to demonstrate if these receptors are functionally active and will confer host susceptibility to viral infection.

Validation Gaps in Multiplex Screening Technologies

The discussed multiplex screening technologies (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Table S2 and section 1) have demonstrated enormous promise in diverse settings, including rapid pathogen detection, surveillance and diagnosis (74, 76), serological profiling (74, 75, 77), small-molecule screening or discovery (78) and only partially realized for multiple viral receptor screening (79). For instance, some of these platforms have been validated primarily with well-characterized viruses (such as SARS-CoV-2 and HCMV), where viral-host receptor interactions were already partially understood (79–81).The full potential of these technologies for de novo systematic multiple receptor discovery remains unrealized for other highly pathogenic viruses, which represents a significant challenge for future pandemic response and demonstrating the wholistic UMDRT pipeline in a pandemic context. Consequently, the true capability of these technologies to identify multiple receptors for completely novel human or zoonotic viruses within families with unknown entry mechanisms remains largely untested and still theoretical.

Furthermore, no systematic studies have compared the sensitivity, specificity, throughput, and cost-effectiveness of these platforms when applied to the same unknown viral threats. Without such benchmarking, it remains unclear which platforms offer the optimal balance of speed and accuracy for different viral families or outbreak scenarios. Moreover, during emerging outbreaks, obtaining enough high-quality clinical or environmental samples for comprehensive screening presents significant logistical and ethical challenges. The sensitivity thresholds of multiplex screening platforms have not been established for low-titer field samples, which often contain inhibitors and contaminants that compromise assay performance. This highlights the need for international networks with standardized protocols to simultaneously test novel viral threats across multiple platforms, generating comparative performance data under realistic time pressures. This would enable “plug-and-play” deployment of multiplex screening platforms in the event of a pandemic.

AI-Driven Analysis and Predictions for Accelerated Design

Prior prediction of invasive receptors used by zoonotic viruses for tropism and their subsequent optimization will fast-track comprehensive multiplex screening for guided decoy receptor designs and outbreak preparedness. In this context, AI, particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), can serve as a versatile decision-making engine and tool. In particular, intuitive algorithms trained on multiplex screening data can be established to predict coevolutionary trends and viral escape potentials, which are necessary to anticipate and identify PPI between viral and human proteome, and ultimately inform decoy countermeasures development (Fig. 1D). Additionally, AI could be employed to explore the vast sequence space to scale down benchtop multiplexing exercises, offering huge marginal savings on time and resources. For instance, AI can predict mutations in viral receptor-binding proteins, enabling these zoonotic viruses to adapt and utilize human receptors, and vice versa. Indeed, recent advances highlight the promise of a part of this proposed approach.

For instance, a DL model, Emvirus, based on convolutional neural networks and bidirectional long short-term memory and trained on protein–protein interaction data, has been used to predict binding affinities between viral proteins and host receptors (82). More sophisticated recent models, such as SpikePro (83), TranceptEVE (84), and EVEscape (85) have been developed, that combine sequence data with structural information to predict viral fitness and escape with improved accuracy. Moreso, some researchers have developed rapid DL models (RoseTTAFold2-Lite) that leverage residue–residue coevolution and protein structure prediction to comprehensively identify and experimentally validate PPIs in bacteria, yeast, and human at the proteome-wide scale with precision (86, 87). Together, these predictive tools will allow researchers to prioritize zoonotic viruses that require immediate laboratory studies, thereby streamlining surveillance efforts and focusing resources on the most dangerous threat. This AI-driven exercise can also drive home guided molecular-level hypotheses important for novel experimental discoveries, contrary to sole conventional trial-and-error multiplexing approaches. And perhaps the combination of these tools will even provide better insights for outbreak preparedness.

For example, AlphaFold, a DL model renowned for protein structure prediction (88), was used in a directed evolution experiment to decipher the effect of destabilizing mutations on a phage to evolve and use a new receptor (89). Also, Park and coworkers (54) leverage AlphaFold3 (88) and ProteinMPNN (90) to identify a point RBD mutation that can enable a merbecovirus, HKU5, to utilize human ACE2, providing a proof-of-concept for AI-assisted outbreak preparedness.

Finally, the decoy receptor design space stands to benefit immensely from an integrative AI framework for large and complex molecules. Unlike traditional structure-based drug design frameworks, which rely on small-molecule-focused docking algorithms, a more robust framework is required for the discovery and design of decoy receptors, which are often large and flexible biomolecules. An attempt to adopt traditional approaches could result in expensive computational bottlenecks. Interestingly, modern innovations such as Deep Generative Models have shown potential to overcome these bottlenecks, permitting a wide array of complex protein design and interactions (91, 92). Overall, these works demonstrate that AI models can accelerate multiplex receptor screening and optimization workflows, facilitating rapid design of decoy interventions.

Revolutionizing Antiviral Development with UMDRTs

Building directly on insights from multiplex receptor screening, AI-guided prediction, and optimization, researchers can now rationally develop potent, universal multivalent decoy receptor therapeutics (UMDRTs) against multiple zoonotic viruses before they spill over into human population. Following identification and validation of numerous selected unknown receptors for a particular family or subfamily, these developed (UMDRTs) can be deployed to combat multiple viral species across these families or subfamilies and can be stockpiled and used in the event of any outbreak or pandemic. This strategy is especially ingenious for proactive broad-spectrum antiviral development due to the recently noted complex convergent viral evolution observed in certain viral families, particularly multiple arboviruses (43–45, 93) and coronaviruses (54). While impossible, viral escape and resistance to our UMDRTs concept would require multiple, often functionally compromising mutations in the viral receptor-binding proteins, which can reduce viral fitness. Beyond this, UMDRTs concept is also paramount, should these viruses i) converge and utilize known but different receptors over evolutionary time or ii) diverge and utilize current receptors over evolutionary time, albeit via different binding interactions and conformations. With these scenarios and using Coronaviridae family as an example, a single UMDRT that combines ACE2 (61), DPP4 (55, 56), APN (62), transmembrane serine protease 2(TMPRSS2) (94), and DPEP1 (70) receptors can be developed to combat all human-infecting coronaviruses (95), and can be applied to other viral families (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Nonetheless, these receptors in their native form often possess low binding affinity to their receptor-binding proteins (in nanomolar or even micromolar range), other auxiliary functions, and are unstable (Fig. 2A, I), which is suboptimal to achieve pan-prophylactic and -therapeutic efficacies in UMDRTs. To bridge this gap, researchers can deploy protein engineering techniques to improve these attributes of selected or identified candidate receptors (Fig. 2A, II). For instance, directed evolution, involving iterative mutagenesis and functional selection, has been exploited to develop ACE2 and DPP4 mutants (31, 96) with enhanced binding affinity, potency, and stability, compared to their native receptors. Importantly, this protein engineering exercise can help derisk or introduce additional pharmacological attributes into selected molecules without compromising their potency. For example, the peptidase activity of single ACE2 or DPP4 decoys has been abrogated via mutagenesis to avoid unwanted side effects (31, 96). The same can be done to remove unwanted or auxiliary viral receptor functions, which are often related to cell communication, nutrient transport, and immune signaling (21, 28).

Beyond affinity and safety enhancement, researchers can also leverage multivalent and multifunctional innovations to translate mere molecular affinities of selected molecules to high-avidity interactions, which is essential to combat viral evolution and central to UMDRTs design and concept. High-avidity therapeutics often outperform their monovalent counterparts due to their ability to promote multiple target engagement. Incorporating multifunctional attributes such as immune and effector function modalities presents additional armaments to expedite recovery from disease and therapy-associated discomforts. Such multivalent and multifunctional innovations can be built on platforms such as chimeric fusions via genetic linkages, immunoglobulin, nonimmunoglobulin, and nanoparticle scaffolds (Fig. 2 B and C). In SI Appendix, section 2, we discuss these platforms in depth to develop UMDRTs after protein receptors have been identified (Fig. 2 B and I) and present recent advances that highlight a part of this proposed concept to achieve proactive, broad-spectrum antiviral solutions (Fig. 2D).

Beyond Protein Receptor Entry and Decoys

Glycan Receptors and Sialic Acid–Based UMDRTs.

Beyond protein receptors, zoonotic viruses can exploit other receptor repertoires on cell surfaces to mediate infection and tropism. One popular and central to influenza virus infection and cellular tropism is sialic acid on cell surface, attached to proteins (as glycoproteins) and lipids (as glycolipids), existing on the cell surfaces (Fig. 1A) (97). Besides, these sialic acid moieties have been reported to be indispensable for cell entry and infection by multiple viral pathogens (98–100), including respiratory syncytial virus(RSV), certain coronaviruses(HKU1-CoV, OC43-CoV, and MERS-CoV), rotaviruses, enterovirus-D, and human parainfluenza viruses. Therefore, incorporating these sialic acid molecules into decoy protein receptors will expand their breadth and potency of prophylaxis and therapy and represent the key to addressing the enigma of zoonotic virus spillover. Nonetheless, with the recent sialic acid entry receptor shift (101), related viral tropism shift (23) and selectivity (102) in different anatomic sites of infection, even makes our concept of tandem multiple receptor screening and UMDRTs a paramount and powerful approach for combating multiple viral families. One can harness a multiplex glycan microarray to screen for sialylated attachment receptors or factors to develop sialic acid–based UMDRTs, which can be used alone or in a cocktail with protein receptor-based UMDRTs to combat zoonotic spillover.

In the receptor screening, we propose presenting the viral receptor-binding proteins on a bead nanoparticle system to mimic the densely decorated viral surface, which can reveal a broader receptor binding profile from the synthesized sialic acid library. While sialic acid–based UMDRTs using multiple identified SA receptors (as illustrated in Fig. 2C, II, C) are yet to be fully realized, recent developments using molecular scaffolds, multivalently displaying sialic acids, showcase their potential. For instance, Vrettou et al. (103) engineered sialylated Fc-molecules, by presenting influenza A virus(IAV) sialic acid receptor on IgG1 Fc. While these molecules inhibited viral hemagglutination activity by 64-fold, further experimental elucidations are required before they are used as antivirals. Also, Hendricks and coworkers designed sialylneolacto-N-tetraose c (LSTc)-bearing Liposome decoy, which demonstrated potent, broad-spectrum hemagglutination inhibition and captured diverse IAV strains, including the H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes. In another study, Chung et al. (104) developed a super-multivalent sialyllactosylated filamentous phage(t-6SLPhage) as a potent inhibitor of influenza A viral infection. Interestingly, t-6SLPhage demonstrated both preventive and therapeutic efficacy in vivo against highly lethal influenza virus challenge.

Beyond influenza virus, some researchers have developed multivalent 9-O-Acetylated-sialic acid glycoclusters (105, 106), which showed potent inhibition against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants. Together, these works provide an effective avenue for developing the proposed UMDRTs to combat multiple sialic acid–dependent viral pathogens.

Intracellular Life Cycle and Postentry Decoys.

Another complementary and equally important avenue for proactive antiviral development is the disruption of processes within the host cell, which will be eased once these receptors and the cell types they target (i.e., tropism) are identified via multiplex screening. Postentry decoys target the conserved machinery required for viral replication, assembly, and release, offering the potential for broad-spectrum activity against entire viral families. Unlike entry receptors, which are often tailored for host and tissue tropism, the fundamental mechanisms of viral replication are ancient and deeply conserved. For instance, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is the central engine of replication for all RNA viruses, including coronaviruses, influenza viruses, flaviviruses, and filoviruses. Its catalytic architecture is remarkably conserved across these diverse groups, making it an ideal target. For instance, SARS-CoV-2’s NSP12 and influenza’s PB1 share a common catalytic architecture amenable to targeted disruption (107). Beyond enzyme inhibition, initiation of RNA synthesis presents a conserved vulnerability. Many positive-sense RNA viruses require a protein primer, vpg (virus protein genome-linked), covalently attached to the 5′ end of their genome. RdRp must first add a nucleotide to vpg before elongation can proceed (108). Decoys mimicking vpg or its binding site could halt replication at its very first step, offering a universal target for interventions.

A classical example of these decoys is nucleic acid–based agents, particularly small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) (109). These nucleotide duplexes guide the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to cleave complementary viral mRNA, silencing essential genes. Multiple siRNAs have been shown to be effective in clinical trials against targets such as RSV nucleocapsid gene, the HBV X gene, and multiple Ebola virus transcripts (109). Besides siRNAs, some researchers have developed locked nucleic acids (LNAs) (110), which disrupt conserved packaging stem-loop 2 (PSL2) in influenza virus isolates, provided 100% survival in mice challenged with a lethal influenza dose and evaded resistance to neuraminidase inhibitor-resistant virus. These findings highlight the potential application of these LNAs to any virus with implications for broad prophylaxis and therapy. Indeed, similar LNAs directed against highly conserved 5’ leader sequences have been developed, demonstrating in vivo potent activity against SARS-CoV-2 variants, during the COVID-19 pandemic (111). Together, these works offer another avenue for UMDRTs’ development, as they can simultaneously disrupt multiple viral life cycles and circumvent viral evolution.

Navigating Challenges Toward Clinical Translation

Given the above-discussed benefits of UMDRTs, and following the identification of multiple receptors via multiplex screening, is it possible to build a stockpile of predeveloped UMDRTs against a viral family or subfamily for clinical use in the event of an outbreak from these zoonotic viruses? The answer is yes—an arsenal of pandemic preparedness UMDRTs targeting known human and zoonotic viruses from high-priority families can be developed. However, certain challenges, including low oral bioavailability, cold-chain requirements, and complex, lengthy regulatory approval processes, among others, need to be overcome and have been outlined and discussed further in SI Appendix, section 3.

Conclusions

The era of reactive pandemic response, heavily reliant on pathogen-specific vaccines, mAbs, and small-molecule antivirals, vulnerable to viral escape, is no longer sufficient. The relentless evolution of known human viruses of pandemic potential and zoonotic viruses poised for emergence and spillover demands a paradigm shift toward proactive, “day one” countermeasures development and solutions. We note that host-derived decoy receptors, engineered to potently block viral entry, represent a cornerstone of this novel proposed UMDRTs strategy or concept. These antiviral therapeutics will offer significantly higher barriers to resistance, broad-spectrum prophylaxis, and therapy against diverse current and future viral threats. We propose that their successful realization will be critically enabled by synergistic integration of two powerful technological pillars: high-throughput, multiplex receptor screening, and AI-driven prediction or design for systematically understanding and identifying multiple human receptors at scale. Together, this proactive framework promises to reshape our approach to quantify and combat risk of known and future zoonotic viruses, enabling the world to gain precious time to mount a comprehensive, long-term response in the event of spillover and pandemic.
