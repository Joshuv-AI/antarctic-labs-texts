Abstract
. We consider m × n m\times n rectangular matrices formed from Sturmian words with slope α \alpha and we fully characterise their balance properties in terms of the Ostrowski representations of m m an n n with respect to α \alpha . This generalises recent results by Anselmo et al., as well as by Shallit and Vukusic, where only quadratic irrationals α \alpha were considered. In contrast to the two mentioned papers, our approach is based on the distribution of n ​ α mod 1 n\alpha\bmod 1 .

1.  Introduction

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be irrational and
\theta\in[0,1)
. Then the
Sturmian word
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
with slope
\alpha
and intercept
\theta
can be defined via

(1.1)

a_{n}:=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha+\theta\rfloor-\lfloor n\alpha+\theta\rfloor\in\{0,1\}.

For example, setting
\alpha=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
and
\theta=0
we get the famous infinite Fibonacci word

\mathbf{f}=101101011011010\cdots.

(Note that in the common definition of the Fibonacci word the
0
’s and
1
’s are flipped, but we stick with our definition for consistency.)

Recall that a
factor
is simply a contiguous block of symbols within a word, and the
weight
of a factor of a binary word is the number of contained
1
’s.
One of the basic properties of Sturmian words is that they are
balanced
, that is, the weight of any two factors of the same length differs by at most
1
. (For example, in the Fibonacci word above, all factors of length
5
either contain exactly three or exactly four
1
’s.)
In fact, the converse holds as well: Every non-periodic balanced word is a Sturmian word.

The notion of balance was extended to multidimensional words by Berthé and Tijdeman
[
4
]
. In particular, they proved that in dimension larger than
1
only periodic words can be balanced.
Among other results, they also considered
2
-dimensional Sturmian words and gave a quantitative measure of their non-balancedness.
Recently, Anselmo et al.
[
2
]
proved that a certain family of rectangles of the Fibonacci word is balanced, and a full characterisation was provided in
[
7
]
.
Let us discuss these results in a bit more detail because the goal of the present paper is to generalise them.

For every word
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
let us define the infinite Hankel matrix
A=(a_{k,\ell})_{k\geq 1,\ell\geq 0}
by
a_{k,\ell}:=a_{k+\ell}
.
Then we can look at
m\times n
submatrices
where the entry in the upper left corner has index sum
i
:

A(i,m,n):=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&a_{i+1}&\dots&a_{i+n-1}\\
a_{i+1}&a_{i+2}&\dots&a_{i+n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{i+m-1}&a_{i+m}&\dots&a_{i+m+n-2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The sum over all entries in the matrix
A(i,m,n)
equals

(1.2)

T(i,m,n):=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}a_{i+k+\ell}.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
be an infinite word over
\{0,1\}
. We say that the
m\times n
rectangles of
\mathbf{a}
are
balanced
if there exists an integer
c=c(\mathbf{a},m,n)
such that

T(i,m,n)\in\{c,c+1\}

for all
i\geq 1
.

For example, for the Fibonacci word it turns out that the
2\times 3
rectangles are balanced (they always have either weight
3
or
4
), whereas the
2\times 4
rectangles are not balanced (they can have weight
4
,
5
, or
6
).
Anselmo et al.
[
2
]
proved that for
m,n\geq 2
, if
\max(m,n)
is a Fibonacci number, then the
m\times n
rectangles of the Fibonacci word are balanced.
In
[
7
]
a full characterisation of balancedness was given in terms of the Zeckendorf representations of
m,n
(that is, the unique representation of
m,n
as the sum of distinct non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers).
Moreover, it was described how the software Walnut can be used to do the same for every fixed quadratic irrational
\alpha
and the corresponding representations. Note that assuming
\alpha

is a quadratic irrational is essential for using Walnut because quadratic irrationals are exactly the numbers with periodic continued fraction expansion.

In this paper, we completely solve the two dimensional balance problem for all irrational
\alpha
.
The characterisation (Theorem
2.1
in the next section) is in terms of the Ostrowski representations of
m,n
with respect to
\alpha
.
The proof is based on Diophantine approximation and ideas used by Berthé and Tijdeman
[
4
]
.
Berthé and Tijdeman also mentioned the connection between balancedness and so-called bounded remainder sets, which is a concept from dynamical systems/discrepancy theory.
We provide a little bit of background, as our main result will turn out to be equivalent to a specific statement about the distribution of
n\alpha\bmod 1
.
For some quick intuition on this, note that (
1.1
) can equivalently be phrased as

a_{n}=1:\iff\left\{n\alpha\right\}\in[1-\alpha-\theta,1-\theta).

Here
\left\{x\right\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the fractional part of
x
, and the interval is understood modulo
1
, i.e., in case
1-\alpha-\theta<0
, we mean the interval that is “wrapped around
0
”,
[\left\{1-\alpha-\theta\right\},1)\cup[0,1-\theta)
.
Thus, the exact distribution of
n\alpha\bmod 1
contains full information on our Sturmian sequence.

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Chapt. 1]
) that for irrational
\alpha
the sequence
(n\alpha)_{n\geq 0}
is
uniformly distributed modulo
1
.
In other words, if we consider the fractional parts
\left\{n\alpha\right\}=n\alpha-\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor\in[0,1)
for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, every interval
I\subseteq[0,1)
gets “its fair share of points” in the following sense:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{n\colon 0\leq n\leq N-1\text{ and }\left\{n\alpha\right\}\in I\}}{N}=|I|,

where
|I|
denotes the length of
I
.
Of course, not all intervals can get “exactly their fair share of points” if we consider finite sets of points. This is quantified by the
discrepancy

D_{N}((n\alpha)_{n\geq 0})=\sup_{I\subseteq[0,1]}\left|\frac{\#\{n\colon 0\leq n\leq N-1\text{ and }\left\{n\alpha\right\}\in I\}}{N}-|I|\right|.

In view of this, it seems appropriate to say that an interval
I
has “minimal discrepancy with respect to
\alpha
and
N
”, if

(1.3)

\big|\#\{n\colon 0\leq n\leq N-1\text{ and }\left\{n\alpha\right\}\in I\}-N|I|\big|<1.

This again is somewhat related to bounded remainder sets, where the bound
1
is relaxed to
C
but has to be satisfied for all
N
.

In this paper, we are interested in when all intervals of a fixed length have minimal discrepancy for a fixed
N
.
More specifically, the balancedness of the
m\times n
rectangles will turn out to be equivalent (see Theorem
3.1
) to the balancedness of intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
with respect to
\alpha
and
m
, in the following sense.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\alpha,d\in(0,1)
and let
N\geq 1
be an integer. We say the intervals of length
d
are
balanced with respect to
(\alpha,N)
if
there exists an integer
c=c(\alpha,d,N)
such that
for all half open
intervals
I=[x,x+d)
,
0\leq x<1
, we have

(1.4)

\#\{n\colon 0\leq n\leq N-1\text{ and }\left\{n\alpha\right\}\in I\}\in\{c,c+1\}.

Note that the intervals
I
are understood modulo
1
, i.e., if
x+d\geq 1
then the interval is
I=[x,1)\cup[0,\left\{x+d\right\})
.

It is not hard to see that (
1.4
) is indeed closely related to (
1.3
), justifying the second part of the title of this paper.

In the next section, we state the full characterisation of balanced
m\times n
rectangles.
In Section
3
we prove the equivalence between balanced rectangles and balanced intervals.
Then we make preparations for proving the characterisation of balanced intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
: In Section
4
we rephrase the balancedness in terms of the bijectiveness of a certain function; in Section
5
we recall some specific properties of Ostrowski representations.
Finally, in Section
6
, we prove the full characterisation of balanced intervals, and thus of balanced
m\times n
rectangles.

We conclude this introduction with a remark on the intercept
\theta
of Sturmian words.

Remark 1.3
.

In the rest of the paper, we will assume
\theta=0
. This can be justified by the following.
Recall that the factors of a word are all the finite contiguous blocks of symbols within the word.
It is a basic fact (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 10.5.3]
) that two Sturmian words have the same slope if and only if they have the same sets of factors.
Since each rectangle
A(i,m,n)
is fully determined by the factor
a_{i}\cdots a_{i+m+n-2}
, the infinite Hankel matrices corresponding to two Sturmian words with the same slope have exactly the same rectangles.

2.  Full characterization via Ostrowski representations

In this section we state our main result, namely the full characterisation of the
m\times n
balanced rectangles. But first, let us recall continued fractions and the Ostrowski representation; see, e.g.,
[
6
]
for a reference book.

Every irrational real number
\alpha
can be uniquely represented by its infinite
simple continued fraction expansion

\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\frac{1}{\ddots}}},

where
a_{0}
is an integer and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
are positive integers, called
partial quotients
.
We can truncate the continued fraction expansion of
\alpha
at its
k
-th partial quotient, and obtain the rational number
p_{k}/q_{k}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}]
,
called the
k
-th
convergent
to
\alpha
.
These convergents are famously particularly good approximations to
\alpha
.
In particular, the numbers
q_{k}\alpha
are very close to an integer (at least for large
k
), or, in other words, very close to
0
modulo
1
.
Since we are interested in
n\alpha\bmod 1
, the natural way to represent an integer
n
therefore is to write it as the sum of
q_{k}
’s. This is known as the Ostrowski representation. To be precise, every positive integer
n
has a unique representation

n=\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}

with
b_{N}\neq 0
,
0\leq b_{k}\leq a_{k+1}
for
k\geq 1
and
0\leq b_{0}\leq a_{1}-1
, and the additional rule that
b_{k-1}=0
whenever
b_{k}=a_{k+1}
.
For more properties of the Ostrowski representation, see Section
5
.
In the rest of the paper, if we write an expression of the shape
n=\sum_{k=L}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
, we always imply that it is a valid Ostrowski representation with respect to
\alpha
, but we do not necessarily assume
b_{L},b_{N}>0
.

Now we state our characterisation of balanced
m\times n
rectangles in terms of the Ostrowski representations of
m,n
.
Since the infinite Hankel matrix
A=(a_{i,j})_{i\geq 0,j\geq 0}
is symmetric, the balance problem for rectangles is symmetric, and from now on we assume
m\leq n
.
Moreover, we can assume
m\geq 2
because for
m=1
the rectangles are just the factors of the one-dimensional word, which are of course balanced.
It turns out that there are essentially only two situations when the rectangles with
2\leq m\leq n
are balanced:

•

The integer
m
only has small digits, and
n
only has large digits in its Ostrowski representation. In the edge case, where
m
and
n
share exactly one digit, there are some extra conditions.

•

The integer
m
is either the denominator of a convergent or of a semi-convergent.
(A semi-convergent is a number of the shape
(p_{k-1}+ap_{k})/(q_{k-1}+aq_{k})
with
1\leq a\leq a_{k+1}-1
.)
Moreover, parity restrictions on the corresponding digits in
n
apply.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be irrational and
2\leq m\leq n
. Then the
m\times n
rectangles of the Sturmian words with slope
\alpha
are balanced if and only if the Ostrowski representations of
m,n
with respect to
\alpha
are of at least one of the following four shapes:

They have “split representations” in the following sense:

(i)

m=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_{k}q_{k}
and

n=\sum_{\ell=M+1}^{N}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}
;

(ii)

m=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_{k}q_{k}
with
b_{M}\neq 0
, and

n=q_{M}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+1+2t}\neq 0
.

The smaller number
m
is the denominator of a (semi-)convergent, and we have certain parity restrictions on the large digits in
n
:

(iii)

m=q_{M}
and
n=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=M+2t}^{N}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+2t}\neq 0
;

(iv)

m=q_{M-1}+aq_{M}
with
1\leq a<a_{M+1}
and

n=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+1+2t}\neq 0
.

Example 2.2
.

For
\alpha=\pi/4
we have
\alpha=[0;1,3,1,1,1,15,2,72,\ldots]
, and
q_{0}=1,q_{1}=1,q_{2}=4,q_{3}=5,q_{4}=9,\ldots
.
Thus, if we want to know whether the
2\times 10
rectangles are balanced, we can use a greedy algorithm to get the Ostrowski representations

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=2=2q_{1},

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=10=q_{4}+q_{1}.

This falls under the case
(ii)
in Theorem
2.1
, and so we know that the
2\times 10
rectangles are balanced.
Indeed, one can check that they always have weight
15
or
16
.

On the other hand, let us consider
m=5,n=10
:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=5=q_{3},

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=10=q_{4}+q_{1}.

This does not correspond to any of the cases in Theorem
2.1
, and indeed one can check that the
5\times 10
rectangles can have weight
38
,
39
, or
40
.

3.  Correspondence between balancedness and low discrepancy intervals

In this section we prove the equivalence between balancedness of rectangles and balancedness of intervals.
We start by checking the well known fact that Sturmian words are balanced, as the corresponding formula will be useful in a moment.
We can directly compute the weight of a factor of lenght
n
starting at index
i
by using (
1.1
) with
\theta=0
and telescoping:

(3.1)

\begin{split}a_{i}+a_{i+1}+\dots+a_{i+n-1}&=\lfloor(i+n)\alpha\rfloor-\lfloor i\alpha\rfloor\\
&=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor+\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\left\{n\alpha\right\}+\left\{i\alpha\right\}\geq 1;\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Therefore, the weight of every factor of length
n
is either
\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor
or
\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor+1
, and in particular the factors of length
n
are balanced for every
n
.

Now we can use this to compute
T(i,m,n)
(defined in (
1.2
)) by summing over the rows of
A(i,m,n)
.
Note that the conditional expression in (
3.1
) can be expressed using the indicator function

\mathbbm{1}_{[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\},1)}(\left\{i\alpha\right\}).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle T(i,m,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}(a_{i+\ell}+a_{i+\ell+1}+\dots+a_{i+\ell+n-1})

\displaystyle=m\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\},1)}(\left\{(i+\ell)\alpha\right\}).

Of course, the value of
m\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor
is independent of
i
, and so the
m\times n
rectangles are balanced if and only if the sum

\displaystyle S(i,m,n):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\},1)}(\left\{(i+\ell)\alpha\right\})

takes exactly two values for all
i
.
This and the fact that
i\alpha\bmod 1
is dense in
[0,1]
lead to the following theorem. (Recall Definition
1.2
for the balancedness of intervals.)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be a Sturmian word with slope
\alpha
. Then the
m\times n
rectangles are balanced if and only if the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
are balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
.

Proof.

As explained above, the
m\times n
rectangles are balanced if and only if the sum
S(i,m,n)
is balanced as a sequence indexed by
i
.
Note that we can rewrite
S(i,m,n)
as

S(i,m,n)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\}-\left\{i\alpha\right\},1-\left\{i\alpha\right\})}(\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}).

In other words, the
m\times n
rectangles are balanced if and only if for every
i\geq 1
the interval
[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\}-\left\{i\alpha\right\},1-\left\{i\alpha\right\})
contains either
c
or
c+1
of the points
\left\{0\right\},\left\{\alpha\right\},\left\{2\alpha\right\},\ldots,\left\{(m-1)\alpha\right\}
for some fixed
c
. Here, and everywhere else, the intervals are understood modulo
1
.
Thus, the balancedness of the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
with respect to
(\alpha,m)
clearly implies the balancedness of the
m\times n
rectangles.

For the reversed implication let
[x,x+\left\{n\alpha\right\})
be an arbitrary interval of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
.
If
[x,x+\left\{n\alpha\right\})
contains none of the points
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}
with
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
, set
\varepsilon_{1}:=1
.
Otherwise, set

\varepsilon_{1}:=\min_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}\left\{x+\left\{n\alpha\right\}-\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}\right\},

i.e.,
\varepsilon_{1}
is the distance between the right endpoint of the interval and the rightmost point
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}
contained in the interval.
Similarly, set

\varepsilon_{2}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\ell\leq m-1\\
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}\neq x\end{subarray}}\left\{x-\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}\right\},

i.e.,
\varepsilon_{2}
is the distance between the right endpoints of the interval and the closest point
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}
lying strictly left of the interval.
Set
\varepsilon:=\min\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\}

and recall that
i\alpha\bmod 1
is dense in
[0,1]
.
Thus, there exists an
i\geq 1
such that
1-\left\{n\alpha\right\}-\left\{i\alpha\right\}\in(x-\varepsilon,x]
.
Then, by construction, the interval
[x,x+\left\{n\alpha\right\})
contains exactly the same points as the interval
[1-\left\{n\alpha\right\}-\left\{i\alpha\right\},1-\left\{i\alpha\right\})
.
Therefore, the balancedness of the
m\times n
rectangles implies the balancedness of the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Since the infinite Hankel matrix
A=(a_{k,\ell})_{k\geq 1,\ell\geq 0}
is symmetric, the balance problem for rectangles is symmetric.
By Theorem
3.1
we immediately get the following fact:
Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers. Then the intervals of length
\left\{m\alpha\right\}
are balanced with respect to
(\alpha,n)
if and only if the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
are balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
.

Remark 3.3
.

In view of the above, some of the cases of Theorem
2.1
become quite obvious, provided one knows the basic properties of Ostrowski representations. For example, the cases
(i)
and
(ii)
correspond to the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
being extremely short (or extremely long, but then one can think of the complements). In fact, it is not very hard to prove that in the cases
(i)
,
(ii)
each interval contains at most one point, and thus the intervals are balanced.

For other cases, one can use other known tricks as well.
For example, if
m=q_{M}
, the points
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}
, for
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
, are particularly evenly distributed and one can use the trick that
\alpha\approx p_{M}/q_{M}
, and therefore
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}\approx(\ell p_{M}\bmod q_{M})/q_{M}
, to characterise balancedness.

Overall, one can say that the intervals are balanced in two cases: Either the intervals are very short (or very long); this corresponds to the cases
(i)
and
(ii)
in Theorem
2.1
. Or the points are very evenly distributed, and the interval lengths are either slightly shorter or slightly longer than the distance
between certain two points; this corresponds to the cases
(iii)
,
(iv)
in Theorem
2.1
.

In this paper, we want to deal with all cases in a somewhat uniform way: We rephrase balancedness of intervals in terms of bijectivity of a certain map
f
. This is done in the next section. Later, we rephrase the bijectivity of
f
again in terms of another function, which is more closely related to approximation properties of Ostrowski representations.

4.  Balanced intervals and certain bijective maps

We start by defining balancedness of intervals in a slightly more general setting because this will help make the arguments clearer.
Note that, as before, all intervals are understood modulo
1
. To make this more rigorous, we speak of the torus
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, which can be thought of as the interval
[0,1)
, where we compute modulo
1
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
A=\{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}\}
be a set of
m
distinct points on the torus
\mathbb{T}
and let
\delta\in(0,1)
. We say that the intervals of length
\delta
are
balanced with respect to
A
if there exists an integer
c=c(A,\delta)
such that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
we have

\#([\xi,\xi+\delta)\cap A)\in\{c,c+1\}.

The goal of this section is to find a way to determine this balancedness property without actually counting the points in each interval (this morally corresponds to the idea in
[
7
, Lemma 1]
).
The first step is to focus on the intervals

[\xi_{\ell},\xi_{\ell}+\delta)
for
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
and to find the closest points
\xi_{j}\in A
to the left and to the right of
\xi_{\ell}+\delta
.
We define the corresponding functions in terms of the indices of the points.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
A=\{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be a set of
m
distinct points
and let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Then we define the following two maps on the set
\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
:
f_{\textnormal{left}}
maps
\ell
to the index of the closest point in
A
that lies to the left of
\xi_{\ell}+\delta
, and
f_{\textnormal{right}}
maps
\ell
to the index of the closest point in
A
that lies to the right of
\xi_{\ell}+\delta
. In other words,

\displaystyle f_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)=j\quad

\displaystyle:\iff\quad\xi_{\ell}+\delta-\xi_{j}=\min_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\xi_{\ell}+\delta-\xi_{i};

\displaystyle f_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)=j\quad

\displaystyle:\iff\quad\xi_{j}-(\xi_{\ell}+\delta)=\min_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\xi_{i}-(\xi_{\ell}+\delta),

where everything is taken modulo
1
and then ordered in the usual way in
[0,1)
.

Now in the case that the interval length
\delta
does not match the distance between any two points in
A
, we have the following main lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A=\{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be a set of
m
distinct points.
Moreover, let
\delta\in(0,1)
be such that
\delta\neq\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}
for all
i,j
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

The intervals of length
\delta
are balanced with respect to
A
.

(b)

The function
f_{\textnormal{left}}
is bijective on
\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
.

(c)

The function
f_{\textnormal{right}}
is bijective on
\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
0\leq\xi_{0}<\xi_{1}<\dots<\xi_{m-1}<1
. Moreover, in the following we use the notation
\xi_{m}:=\xi_{0}
.

Start with the interval
[0,\delta)
and observe what happens as we shift it continuously to the right, i.e., consider
[\xi,\xi+\delta)\subset\mathbb{T}
as
\xi
runs through
[0,1]
.
Every time
\xi
increases from
\xi_{\ell}
to
\xi_{\ell}+\varepsilon
for some
\ell
and small
\varepsilon>0
, we “lose” the point
\xi_{\ell}
.
Every time
\xi
increases from
\xi_{j}-\delta
to
\xi_{j}-\delta+\varepsilon
for some
j
, we “gain” the point
\xi_{j}
.
Since
\delta\neq\xi_{j}-\xi_{\ell}
for all
\ell,j
, we never gain a point and lose a point at exactly the same time.
Therefore, the intervals of length
\delta
are balanced if an only if, as we slide our interval across the torus, we always alternate between gaining and losing a point.
In other words, the intervals of length
\delta
are balanced if an only if for every
\ell
there exists a unique
j
such that
\xi_{j}
lies between
\xi_{\ell}+\delta
and
\xi_{\ell+1}+\delta
. (This corresponds to the fact that as we shift from
[\xi_{\ell},\xi_{\ell}+\delta)
to
[\xi_{\ell+1},\xi_{\ell+1}+\delta)
we lose
\xi_{\ell}
and then gain
\xi_{j}
, before losing
\xi_{\ell+1}
.)
Moreover, note that in this situation
j=f_{\textnormal{right}}(\xi_{\ell})=f_{\textnormal{left}}(\xi_{\ell+1})
, and thus balancedness implies that
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective.

For the other implication, note that
f_{\textnormal{left}}
being bijective or
f_{\textnormal{right}}
being bijective each imply that for every
\ell
there exists a unique
j
such that
\xi_{j}
lies between
\xi_{\ell}+\delta
and
\xi_{\ell+1}+\delta
. As described above, this is equivalent to the intervals of length
\delta
being balanced.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The assumption in Lemma
4.3
that
\delta\neq\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}
for all
i,j
is necessary. For example, consider the three points
\xi_{0}=0
,
\xi_{1}=1/4
, and
\xi_{2}=1/2
, and set
\delta=1/2
. Then it is easy to check that the half open intervals of length
\delta
either contain
1
or
2
points, and thus are balanced.
However,

f_{\textnormal{left}}(0)=2=f_{\textnormal{left}}(1)
and
f_{\textnormal{right}}(2)=0=f_{\textnormal{right}}(1)
.
One can also check that changing the definitions of
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{left}}
to contain the restriction “strictly to the left/right” does not resolve the issue.

Remark 4.5
.

In our application of Lemma
4.3

in Section
6
we will have

A=\{0,\alpha,\ldots,(m-1)\alpha\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}

and

\delta=\left\{n\alpha\right\}.

Then, if we assume
n\geq m
, we indeed get that
\delta\neq\left\{i\alpha\right\}-\left\{j\alpha\right\}
for all
0\leq i,j\leq m-1<n
.

Finally, we state a simple lemma which will also be useful later.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
A=\{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be a set of
m
distinct points and let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Then the intervals of length
\delta
are balanced with respect to
A
if and only if the intervals of length
1-\delta
are balanced.
Moreover, if
\delta\neq\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}
for all
i,j
, then
for all
\varepsilon
with
\lvert\varepsilon\rvert
sufficiently small, the intervals of length
\delta
are balanced if and only if the intervals of length
1-\delta+\varepsilon
are balanced.

Proof.

The first statement is clear because the half open intervals of length
1-\delta
correspond to the complements of the intervals of length
\delta
.
If
\delta\neq\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}
for all
i,j
, then it is clear that for
\lvert\varepsilon\rvert<\min_{i,j}\lvert\delta-(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})\rvert
we can modify the interval length
\delta
to
\delta+\varepsilon
without changing the occurring values of
\#([\xi,\xi+\delta)\cap A)
.
∎

5.  Properties of convergents and Ostrowski representations

The strategy for proving our main result (Theorem
2.1
) in the next section will be to apply
Lemma
4.3
to the points
\xi_{\ell}=\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}
with
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
and
\delta=\left\{n\alpha\right\}
.
Therefore, the right endpoints of the intervals
[\xi_{\ell},\xi_{\ell}+\delta)
will be of the shape
\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}+\left\{n\alpha\right\}=\left\{(\ell+n)\alpha\right\}
, where
\ell+n>m-1
.
In order to determine
f_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell),f_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)
we will need to figure out which point
\left\{j\alpha\right\}
with
0\leq j\leq m-1
lies closest to the left and which one closest to the right of
\left\{(\ell+n)\alpha\right\}
.
We will do this using the Ostrowski representation mentioned in Section
2
.

We now recall several properties of Ostrowski representations which will be useful.

All lemmas below are probably well known to experts (except perhaps for Lemma
5.17
, but this one is not very hard to see either).
Rather unfortunately though, there does not seem to exist a suitable reference book. In order to provide proofs for the lemmas, we recall some basic properties of convergents first; see, e.g.,
[
6
]
for a reference. Throughout the rest of this paper,
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a fixed irrational, and all Ostrowski representations are with respect to
\alpha
.

5.1.  Basic properties of convergents

As before, let
p_{k}/q_{k}
denote the convergents to
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Then the numerators and denominators follow the recursions

\displaystyle p_{0}=0,\quad p_{1}=1,\quad p_{k+1}=a_{k+1}p_{k}+p_{k-1}\quad\text{for }k\geq 1;

(5.1)

\displaystyle q_{0}=1,\quad q_{1}=a_{1},\quad q_{k+1}=a_{k+1}q_{k}+q_{k-1}\quad\text{for }k\geq 1.

Convergents are the
best approximations
in the following sense:
Let
\lVert\xi\rVert:=\min_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\xi-x\rvert
denote the distance to the nearest integer to
\xi
.
Then we have

(5.2)

0<q<q_{k+1}\implies\lVert q\alpha\rVert\geq\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert.

We set

\delta_{k}:=q_{k}\alpha-p_{k}.

Then we have (see, e.g.,
[
6
, p. 9]
)

(5.3)

\delta_{k}=(-1)^{k}\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert\quad\text{for }k\geq 1\quad\text{(or }k\geq 0\text{, if }\alpha<1/2\text{)},

and, moreover,

(5.4)

\lvert\delta_{k}\rvert=\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert\leq 1/q_{k+1}.

In particular, since
\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert\leq 1/q_{k+1}\leq 1/2
for
k\geq 1
, we have

(5.5)

\left\{q_{k}\alpha\right\}=\begin{cases}\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert,&\text{if $k$ is even};\\
1-\lVert q_{k}\alpha\rVert,&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

If
\alpha<1/2
, the above clearly holds for
k=0
as well.

5.2.  Basic properties of Ostrowski representations

Recall that the Ostrowski representation of
n
with respect to
\alpha
is unique and of the shape

n=\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
,
with
0\leq b_{k}\leq a_{k+1}
for
k\geq 1
, and
0\leq b_{0}\leq a_{1}-1
, and the additional rule that
b_{k-1}=0
whenever
b_{k}=a_{k+1}
.
The Ostrowski representation of
n
can be obtained by a greedy algorithm. In particular, we have

(5.6)

n=\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}\quad\text{with }b_{N}\neq 0\iff q_{N}\leq n<q_{N+1}.

Moreover, note that
if
a_{1}=1
(which is equivalent to
\left\{\alpha\right\}>1/2
, and to
q_{1}=q_{0}=1
), then
b_{0}=0
, and the lowest possible nonzero term in the representation is
b_{1}q_{1}
.
This, and the fact that

\lVert q_{0}\alpha\rVert<1/2
only holds if
\alpha<1/2
, is why we will often have to distinguish between the two cases
\alpha<1/2
and
\alpha>1/2
.

The Ostrowski representation is “the correct way to represent integers” in the following sense:
Roughly speaking, the digits of
n
with small index determine where
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
lies in
[0,1)
, and the digits with large index produce a small error term.
This is plausible in view of (
5.4
).
Let us be more precise.

First, note that from (
5.1
) and (
5.3
)
it follows that

(5.7)

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{L+2+2t}\lVert q_{L+2t+1}\alpha\rVert=\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert,

for
L\geq 1
(and for
L\geq 0
if
\alpha<1/2
).

Let
b_{k}(n)
denote the coefficient of
q_{k}
in the Ostrowski representation of
n
,
and let
k_{0}(n)
be the index of the first nonzero coefficient in the Ostrowski representation of
n
, i.e.,

k_{0}(n):=\min\{k\colon b_{k}(n)>0\}.

The next lemma describes the fact that if “
n
contains no small
q_{k}
’s”, then
n\alpha
is close to
0
modulo
1
.
In particular, this means that digits with large index only have little impact on the position of
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
.
In this paper, we are mostly interested in precise bounds for the size of
\lVert n\alpha\rVert
if
n
only has large digits.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
with
k_{0}(n)=L
. Then

(1)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert>\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert
;

(2)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
, provided that
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).

Some other, more precise estimates, are the following:

(3)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert>\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert+\lVert q_{N+2}\rVert
, if
n\leq q_{N}
;

(4)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert>\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert+\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert
if
b_{L}(n)\geq 2
and
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
);

(5)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert-\lVert q_{N+2}\rVert
, provided that
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
), and
n\leq q_{N}
;

(6)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert(q_{L-1}+q_{L})\alpha\rVert
if
b_{L}(n)\leq a_{L+1}-1
and
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
);

(7)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert n^{\prime}\alpha\rVert
if
k_{0}(n^{\prime})=k_{0}(n)=L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
) and
b_{L}(n)<b_{L}(n^{\prime})
;

(8)

\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert
if and only if
the Ostrowski representation of
n
is of the shape

n=q_{L}+\sum_{k=L+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{L+1+2t}>0
.

Proof.

All statements follow pretty straightforwardly from (
5.3
) and (
5.7
).
See also, for example,
[
6
, p. 24–25, Lemma 1 and Theorem 1]
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
and assume that
k_{0}(n)\geq 2
(or
k_{0}(n)\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Then we have

\lVert n\alpha\rVert=\begin{cases}\left\{n\alpha\right\},&\text{if $k_{0}(n)$ is even};\\
1-\left\{n\alpha\right\},&\text{if $k_{0}(n)$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows from the previous statements. See, for example,
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
for a reference.
∎

The above lemma cannot be extended to
k_{0}(n)\geq 1
(or
k_{0}(n)\geq 0
if
\alpha<1/2
), so when the smallest allowed digit in the representation of
n
shows up, then
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
might lie on “the wrong side of
1/2
”. For example, if
\alpha<1/2
and
k_{0}(n)=0
, we might have
\left\{n\alpha\right\}>1/2
, even though from the parity of
k_{0}(n)
we would expect
\left\{n\alpha\right\}<1/2
.

The next lemma will be useful when dealing with such exceptional cases.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume
\alpha<1/2
and
k_{0}(n)=0
.
Then

\left\{n\alpha\right\}>1/2\quad\implies\quad\left\{n\alpha\right\}<\left\{(n+1)\alpha\right\}.

Analogously, if
\alpha>1/2
and
k_{0}(n)=1
, then

\left\{n\alpha\right\}<1/2\quad\implies\quad\left\{(n+1)\alpha\right\}<\left\{n\alpha\right\}.

Note that in both cases the implication is that
\lVert(n+1)\alpha\rVert<\lVert n\alpha\rVert
.

Proof.

Assume that
\alpha<1/2
and that
n=\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
b_{0}>0
.
Then we get from (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) that

\displaystyle\left\{n\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{b_{0}q_{0}\alpha\right\}+\left\{(b_{1}q_{1}+\dots)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle<\left\{b_{0}q_{0}\alpha\right\}+\left\{(a_{2}q_{2}+a_{4}q_{4}+\dots)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{(q_{1}-1)\alpha\right\}+\left\{-q_{1}\alpha\right\}=1-\left\{\alpha\right\}.

Thus,
\left\{n\alpha\right\}+\left\{\alpha\right\}<1
and so
\left\{n\alpha\right\}<\left\{(n+1)\alpha\right\}
, as desired.

The proof for the case
\alpha>1/2
is analogous.
∎

5.3.  One-sided approximations

For real
\xi
, let us define the distance to the nearest integer on the left, and the distance to the nearest integer on the right:

\langle\xi\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}:=\left\{\xi\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\xi\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}:=\left\{-\xi\right\}.

The overload of notation is justified by the fact that
we will go on to focus on one of
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
at a time; see also the lemma below.
Moreover, in Section
6
we will consider the functions
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}},x_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{right}}
, and the definition above will be compatible with those functions.
Note that if
\{\langle\cdot\rangle,\langle\cdot\rangle^{\prime}\}=\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
, we have

\langle-\xi\rangle=1-\langle\xi\rangle=\langle\xi\rangle^{\prime},\quad\text{for }\xi\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}.

Moreover, we have the same “addition rules” as for
\left\{\cdot\right\}
:

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle\xi_{2}\rangle<1\quad\iff\quad\langle\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\rangle=\langle\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle\xi_{2}\rangle;

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{1}\rangle\geq\langle\xi_{2}\rangle\quad\iff\quad\langle\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rangle=\langle\xi_{1}\rangle-\langle\xi_{2}\rangle.

We will also use the fact that

\langle\xi\rangle<\langle\xi+\xi_{1}\rangle<\langle\xi+\xi_{2}\rangle\quad\implies\quad\langle\xi_{1}\rangle<\langle\xi_{2}\rangle.

We start with a simple lemma for the situation where
\langle n\alpha\rangle>1/2
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\langle\cdot\rangle\in\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
.
If
\langle n\alpha\rangle>1/2
, then either
\langle(n-1)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
or
\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
.

Proof.

Since either
\langle\alpha\rangle<1/2
or
\langle-\alpha\rangle<1/2
, we have that
\langle\pm\alpha\rangle<1/2<\langle n\alpha\rangle
for one choice of
\pm
.
Then
\langle(n\mp 1)\alpha\rangle=\langle n\alpha\rangle-\langle\pm\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
∎

Below, we will focus on one of
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
at a time. In fact, we will usually fix an integer
n
and set

(5.8)

\langle\cdot\rangle:=\begin{cases}\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},&\text{if }\lVert n\alpha\rVert=\left\{n\alpha\right\};\\
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}},&\text{if }\lVert n\alpha\rVert=\left\{-n\alpha\right\}.\end{cases}

In other words, we choose
\langle\cdot\rangle
such that
\langle n\alpha\rangle=\lVert n\alpha\rVert<1/2
.

We rephrase Lemma
5.2
in terms of
\langle\cdot\rangle
:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
n,n^{\prime}\geq 1
with
k_{0}(n),k_{0}(n^{\prime})\geq 2
(or
\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then

\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle<1/2\quad\iff\quad k_{0}(n)\equiv k_{0}(n^{\prime})\pmod{2}.

The next two lemmas describe how we can compare
\langle n\alpha\rangle
and
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle
by looking at the lowest nonzero digits in
n,n^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
and set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then for all
n^{\prime}\geq 1
we have

k_{0}(n^{\prime})<k_{0}(n)\quad\implies\quad\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle.

Proof.

Assume first that
k_{0}(n)\geq 3
(or
k_{0}(n)\geq 2
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Then if
k_{0}(n^{\prime})=k_{0}(n)-1
, we have from Lemma
5.5
that
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle>1/2>\langle n\alpha\rangle
. If
k_{0}(n^{\prime})\leq k_{0}(n)-2
, then we obtain
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle>\langle n\alpha\rangle
from Lemma
5.1
(
1
,
2
).

If
k_{0}(n)=0
(or
k_{0}(n)=1
if
\alpha>1/2
), the implication is trivial.

We are left with the cases
k_{0}(n)=1
if
\alpha<1/2
, and
k_{0}(n)=2
if
\alpha>1/2
.
We focus on
k_{0}(n)=1
and
\alpha<1/2
(the other case is analogous).
Lemma
5.2
implies that
\langle\cdot\rangle=\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
.
Assume
k_{0}(n^{\prime})=0
.
If
\left\{n^{\prime}\alpha\right\}<1/2
, then
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}>1/2>\langle n\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, as desired.
If
\left\{n^{\prime}\alpha\right\}>1/2
, then as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
we see that
\left\{n^{\prime}\alpha\right\}<1-\left\{\alpha\right\}
, and so
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}>\lVert\alpha\rVert=\lVert q_{0}\alpha\rVert>\lVert n\alpha\rVert=\langle n\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, where we used Lemma
5.1
(
2
).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
n,n^{\prime}\geq 1
with
k_{0}(n)=k_{0}(n^{\prime})=L
.
Assume
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then

b_{L}(n)<b_{L}(n^{\prime})\quad\implies\quad\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle.

Proof.

By our assumptions and by Lemma
5.5
, we have
\langle n\alpha\rangle=\lVert n\alpha\rVert
and
\langle n^{\prime}\alpha\rangle=\lVert n^{\prime}\alpha\rVert
.
Thus, the inequality is the statement (
7
) in Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Now we state the best approximation property of (semi-)convergents in the one-sided setting.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
L\geq 1
(or
L\geq 0
, if
\alpha<1/2
).
Let
\langle\cdot\rangle\in\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
be such that
\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.
Then

(5.9)

\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle>\langle(q_{L}+q_{L+1})\alpha\rangle>\dots>\langle(q_{L}+(a_{L+2}-1)q_{L+1})\alpha\rangle>\langle q_{L+2}\alpha\rangle.

Moreover, for
0\leq a\leq a_{L+2}-1
we have

(5.10)

0<q<q_{L}+(a+1)q_{L+1}\quad\implies\quad\langle q\alpha\rangle\geq\langle(q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle.

Proof.

The inequalities in (
5.9
) follow from
(
5.5
) and the fact that
a_{L+2}\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert
(which follows, for example, from (
5.7
)).

For the best approximation property (
5.10
), note that all
q
in the range
0<q<q_{L+2}
which are not of the shape
q_{L}+aq_{L+1}
with
0\leq q\leq a_{L+2}
, by (
5.6
), must either have
k_{0}(q)=L+1
, or
k_{0}(q)=L
and
b_{L}(q)>1
, or
k_{0}(q)\leq L-1
.

If
k_{0}(q)=L+1
, then Lemma
5.2
implies
\langle q\alpha\rangle>1/2>\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle
.

If
k_{0}(q)\leq L-1
, then Lemma
5.6
implies

\langle q\alpha\rangle>\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle
.

If
k_{0}(q)=L
and
b_{L}(q)\geq 2
, then in the case
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2)
, Lemma
5.7
implies
\langle q\alpha\rangle>\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle
. In case
L=0
(or
L=1
if
\alpha>1/2
), this is easy to see as well.

Overall,
\langle q\alpha\rangle>\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle
holds for all
q
that are not of the shape
q_{L}+aq_{L+1}
, and so the implication (
5.10
) follows from (
5.9
).
∎

5.4.  Minimising distances in certain ranges of integers

The lemmas in this subsection will be particularly useful for characterising balancedness.

Lemma 5.9
.

Assume that
k_{0}(n)=L
with
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then

\langle(n+q_{L+1})\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle.

More generally, the inequality holds for all
n
with
k_{0}(n)\leq L
and
\langle n\alpha\rangle<1/2
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is chosen such that
\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\langle n\alpha\rangle=\lVert n\alpha\rVert>\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert
(by Lemma
5.1
(
1
)) and
\langle q_{L+1}\alpha\rangle=1-\lVert q_{L+1}\alpha\rVert
(by Lemma
5.5
, since
L
and
L+1
have distinct parities).
∎

In the cases where
L
is too small for the above lemma, we will use the next two lemmas.

Lemma 5.10
.

Assume that
k_{0}(n)=0
and set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then we have

\langle(n-q_{1})\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle\quad\text{or}\quad\langle(n+q_{1})\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle.

If
\alpha>1/2
, then the same holds for
k_{0}(n)=1
and
\langle(n\pm q_{2})\alpha\rangle
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\langle n\alpha\rangle=\lVert n\alpha\rVert>\lVert q_{1}\alpha\rVert
, by Lemma
5.1
(
1
).
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

Assume
b_{0}(n)\geq 2
and
set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then we have

\langle(n-1)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle\quad\text{or}\quad\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle.

If
\alpha>1/2
, then the above statement holds with the assumption
b_{1}(n)\geq 2
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha<1/2
and
b_{0}(n)\geq 2
.
Then, similarly to the proof of Lemma
5.3
, one can use (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) to show that
\left\{\alpha\right\}<\left\{n\alpha\right\}<1-\left\{\alpha\right\}
.
This implies

\left\{(n-1)\alpha\right\}<\left\{n\alpha\right\}<\left\{(n+1)\alpha\right\}
, and so
\langle(n-1)\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}<\langle n\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
and
\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}<\langle n\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
. This proves the lemma in the case that
\alpha<1/2
. For
\alpha>1/2
the proof is analogous.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

Assume that either
k_{0}(n)=L
or

n=q_{L-1}+\sum_{k=L+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{L+2t}\neq 0
.
Set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then

(5.11)

\langle n\alpha\rangle=\min\{\langle x\alpha\rangle\colon n-q_{L}<x<n+q_{L}\}.

Proof.

Assume first that
k_{0}(n)=L
. If
L=0
(or
L=1
if
\alpha>1/2
), then
q_{L}=1
, so the statement is trivial.
Otherwise, by Lemma
5.1
(
2
), we have
\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
.
On the other hand, by the best approximation property of convergents (
5.2
), we have
\lVert d\alpha\rVert\geq\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
for all
1\leq d<q_{L}
.
Thus, for all
1\leq d<q_{L}
with
\langle d\alpha\rangle<1/2
, we have

\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle=\langle n\alpha\rangle+\lVert d\alpha\rVert>\langle n\alpha\rangle
and
\langle(n-d)\alpha\rangle>1/2>\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
Similarly, for all
1\leq d<q_{L}
with
\langle d\alpha\rangle>1/2
, we have

\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle>1/2>\langle n\alpha\rangle
and
\langle(n-d)\alpha\rangle=\langle n\alpha\rangle+\lVert d\alpha\rVert>\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
This implies (
5.11
).

Now assume that
n=q_{L-1}+\sum_{k=L+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{L+2t}\neq 0
. Then we know from Lemma
5.1
(
8
) that again
\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
, and so we can argue as in the previous case.
∎

Lemma 5.13
.

Assume that
k_{0}(n)\geq L
with
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Set
\langle\cdot\rangle
as in (
5.8
).
Then

\langle n\alpha\rangle=\min\{\langle x\alpha\rangle\colon n\leq x<n+q_{L-1}+q_{L}\}.

Moreover, if
b_{L}(n)\leq a_{L+1}-1
, we can extend the range to

\langle n\alpha\rangle=\min\{\langle x\alpha\rangle\colon n\leq x<n+q_{L-1}+2q_{L}\}.

Proof.

Assume
k_{0}(n)=L
with
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
, if
\alpha<1/2
) and
\langle n\alpha\rangle<1/2
.
Then Lemma
5.1
(
2
) implies
\langle n\alpha\rangle=\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
.
Let
\langle\cdot\rangle^{\prime}
denote the distance to the nearest integer in the opposite direction, i.e.,
\{\langle\cdot\rangle,\langle\cdot\rangle^{\prime}\}=\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
.
Then by Lemma
5.5
we have
\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
\langle q_{L-1}\alpha\rangle^{\prime}<1/2
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume
\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
for some
1\leq d<q_{L-1}+q_{L}
. This is equivalent to
\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle^{\prime}>\langle n\alpha\rangle^{\prime}
, and so

\langle d\alpha\rangle^{\prime}=\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle^{\prime}-\langle n\alpha\rangle^{\prime}<1-\langle n\alpha\rangle^{\prime}=\langle n\alpha\rangle<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert=\langle q_{L-1}\alpha\rangle^{\prime}

for some
1\leq d<q_{L-1}+q_{L}
, contradicting Lemma
5.8
with
a=0
.

If
k_{0}(n)>L
, then the previous case just gives us a stronger result than necessary.

Finally, assume that
k_{0}(n)=L
and
b_{L}\leq a_{L+1}-1
.
Then Lemma
5.1
(
6
) says that in fact
\langle n\alpha\rangle<\lVert(q_{L-1}+q_{L})\alpha\rVert
, and we get the better bound by the same argument as before, this time using Lemma
5.8
with
a=1
.
∎

Next, we determine integers larger than
n
which minimise
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in certain ranges strictly above
n
.

Lemma 5.14
.

Assume that
\langle n\alpha\rangle<1/2
, and let

n^{\prime}:=\min\{x>n\colon\langle x\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle\}.

Set
u:=\min\{n^{\prime},n+q_{1}\}
if
\alpha<1/2
, and
u:=\min\{n^{\prime},n+q_{2}\}
if
\alpha>1/2
.
Then

\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle=\min_{n<x<u}\langle x\alpha\rangle.

Proof.

Assume first
\alpha<1/2
and
\langle n\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
n^{\prime}>n+1
(if
n^{\prime}=n+1
, the statement is trivial).
Then
\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that there exists an integer
x
with
n<x<u
such that
\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle x\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle
.
Then we can write
x=n+d
with some integer
1\leq d<q_{1}
, and we have

\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle
.
This implies
\langle d\alpha\rangle<\langle\alpha\rangle=\lVert\alpha\rVert
, which is impossible for
1\leq d<q_{1}
, by (
5.2
).
The proof for
\alpha>1/2
is analogous.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
L\geq 1
(or
L\geq 0
if
\alpha<1/2
) and assume
\langle q_{L}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.
Let
n\geq 1
and

n^{\prime}:=\min\{x>n\colon\langle x\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle\}.

Moreover, let
0\leq a\leq a_{L+2}-1
and assume
n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1}<n^{\prime}
.
Set

u:=\min\{n^{\prime},n+q_{L}+(a+1)q_{L+1}\}.

Then we have

\langle(n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle=\min_{n<x<u}\langle x\alpha\rangle.

Proof.

First, note that the assumption
n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1}<n^{\prime}
implies
\langle(n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle>\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume there exists an integer
x
with
n<x<u
such that
\langle x\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle
.
Then, since
u\leq n^{\prime}
and
u\leq n+q_{L}+(a+1)q_{L+1}
, we have in fact

(5.12)

\langle n\alpha\rangle<\langle x\alpha\rangle=\langle(n+d)\alpha\rangle<\langle(n+q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle

with
0<d<q_{L}+(a+1)q_{L+1}
.
The inequality (
5.12
) implies
\langle d\alpha\rangle<\langle(q_{L}+aq_{L+1})\alpha\rangle
, contradicting Lemma
5.8
.
∎

5.5.  Ostrowski representations of
n
and
q_{T}-n

Finally, we want to describe how the shape of the Ostrowski representations of
n
and
q_{T}-n
are related to each other, for
T
sufficiently large.

We start with a simple lemma that follows pretty much directly from the properties of Ostrowski representations collected in Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
2\leq n\leq q_{N}
. Assume that
k_{0}(n)=L
with
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
), but that
n
is not of the shape
n=q_{L}+\sum_{k=L+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{L+1+2t}>0
.

Then for
T\geq N+2
we have that
q_{T}-n
is of the shape
q_{T}-n=q_{L-1}+\sum_{k=L+2t}^{T-1}b_{k}q_{k}
.

Proof.

If
n
is not of the shape
n=q_{L}+\sum_{k=L+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
, then Lemma
5.1
(
8
,
6
) implies that
\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert\leq\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert-\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert
.
Thus, we have
\lVert(q_{T}-n)\alpha\rVert\geq\lVert q_{L}\alpha\rVert-\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert
.
Now Lemma
5.1
(
5
) implies that
k_{0}(q_{T}-n)\leq L
. The fact that
\left\{(q_{T}-n)\alpha\right\}<1/2\iff\left\{n\alpha\right\}>1/2
implies via Lemma
5.2
that in fact
k_{0}(q_{T}-n)=L-1
. Finally, the fact that

\lVert(q_{T}-n)\alpha\rVert\leq\lVert n\alpha\rVert+\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{L-1}\alpha\rVert
implies via Lemma
5.1
(
8
) that
q_{T}-n
is of the shape
q_{L-1}+\sum_{k=L+2t}^{T-1}b_{k}q_{k}
.
∎

In preparation for our second lemma, we introduce some more notation:
For
n=\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
and
M\geq 0
let us define the “small part of
n
”, obtained by discarding terms with indices larger than
M
:

n^{[\leq M]}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{M,N\}}b_{k}q_{k}.

Note that, by (
5.6
), we have

(5.13)

n^{[\leq M]}<q_{M+1}.

Similarly, we define the “large part of
n
”, namely

n^{[\geq M]}:=\sum_{k=M}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}.

Moreover, let

k_{\geq M}(n):=\min\{k\colon k\geq M\text{ and }b_{k}>0\}.

Note that this generalises our previous definition of
k_{0}(n)=k_{\geq 0}(n)
.

Now we state and prove our second lemma regarding the shape of
n
and
q_{T}-n
.

Lemma 5.17
.

Assume that
n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
and
n^{[\geq M]}>0
.
Then if
q_{T-2}\geq n
, we have

k_{\geq M}(n)\equiv k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\pmod{2}.

Proof.

Note that the assumption
n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
implies that
M\geq 1
(or
M\geq 2
if
\alpha>1/2
). Moreover, assume
n\leq q_{N}
and
T\geq N+2
.

Case 1:

k_{\geq M}(n)\equiv M+1\pmod{2}
.
In particular, we have
b_{M}(n)=0
in this case, and we can write

n=n^{[\leq M-1]}+n^{[\geq M+1]},

and we have

k_{0}(n^{[\geq M+1]})\equiv M+1\pmod{2}.

Let us write

\displaystyle q_{T}-n=\underbrace{(q_{T}-n^{[\geq M+1]}-q_{M})}_{:=n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime}}+\underbrace{(q_{M}-n^{[\leq M-1]})}_{:=n_{\textnormal{small}}^{\prime}}.

First, note that
0<n_{\textnormal{small}}^{\prime}<q_{M}
.
(This follows from the assumption
n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
and (
5.13
).)
Now we want to show that
k_{0}(n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime})\geq M
and
k_{0}(n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime})\equiv M+1\pmod{2}
.

Choose
\langle\cdot\rangle\in\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
so that
\langle q_{M+1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.
Then by the case assumption and Lemmas
5.1
(
5
) and
5.2
, we have

\langle n^{[\geq M+1]}\alpha\rangle=\lVert n^{[\geq M+1]}\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert-\lVert q_{N+2}\alpha\rVert\leq\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert-\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert.

Since, moreover,
\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert<\langle n^{[\geq M+1]}\alpha\rangle
, we get that

\langle(-q_{T}+n^{[\geq M+1]})\alpha\rangle<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert=1-\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle.

This implies

\displaystyle 1-\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert=\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle<\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle+\langle(-q_{T}+n^{[\geq M+1]})\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(-q_{T}+n^{[\geq M+1]}+q_{M})\alpha\rangle<1,

and so

\langle n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime}\alpha\rangle=\langle(q_{T}-n^{[\geq k_{M}(n)]}-q_{M})\alpha\rangle<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert.

The fact that
\lVert n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime}\alpha\rVert=\langle n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime}\alpha\rangle<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert
implies that
k_{0}(n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime})\leq M
.
Moreover, since
\langle q_{M+1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, Lemma
5.5
implies that
k_{0}(n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime})\equiv M+1\pmod{2}
.

Overall,
q_{T}-n=n_{\textnormal{large}}^{\prime}+n_{\textnormal{small}}^{\prime}
has the correct shape, namely
k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M+1\pmod{2}
.

Case 2:

k_{\geq M}(n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
.
We want to show that
k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
as well.
Assume the contrary, i.e.,

k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M+1\pmod{2}.

If
(q_{T}-n)^{[\leq M-1]}>0
, then we can apply the result from Case 1 to
q_{T}-n
: for
n^{\prime\prime}:=q_{T+2}-(q_{T}-n)=n+(q_{T+2}-q_{T})
we have

(5.14)

k_{\geq M}(n^{\prime})\equiv M+1\pmod{2}.

From
q_{T+2}-q_{T}=a_{T+2}q_{T+1}
we see that
n^{\prime\prime}
and
n
have exactly the same digits up the digit with index
T+1
. Therefore, the congruence (
5.14
) contradicts the case assumption.

We are left with the case
(q_{T}-n)^{[\leq M-1]}=0
.
Then
k_{0}(q_{T}-n)\geq M+1
.
Lemma
5.1
(
2
) implies

\lVert(q_{T}-n)\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert
, and so
\lVert n\alpha\rVert<\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert+\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert\leq\lVert q_{M}\alpha\rVert+\lVert q_{N+2}\alpha\rVert
.
But then Lemma
5.1
(
3
) implies that
k_{0}(n)\geq M
, which contradicts the assumption
n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
.
∎

Finally, we combine the previous two lemmas in the way we will want to apply them later.

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
q_{M}<n<q_{N}
for some
M\geq 2
(or
M\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Assume that
b_{M}(n)>0
, and that
n
is not of the shape
n=q_{M}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
.
Then for
T\geq N+2
we have
k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

If
n^{[M-1]}>0
, then Lemma
5.17
implies that

k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv k_{\geq M}(n)=M\pmod{2}
.
If
n^{[M-1]}=0
, then
k_{0}(n)=M
, and so Lemma
5.16
implies that
k_{\geq M}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
.
∎

6.  Proof of the full characterisation

In this section, we prove our main result, namely the full characterisation of balanced rectangles of Sturmian sequences (Theorem
2.1
).
As usual, let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be irrational and fix integers

2\leq m\leq n
.
In view of Theorem
3.1
, we can phrase Theorem
2.1
in terms of balanced intervals.
Moreover, as mentioned in Remark
4.5
, we can use Lemma
4.3
, setting

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}\}\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{\ell}:=\left\{\ell\alpha\right\}\text{ for }0\leq\ell\leq m-1;

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\alpha\right\}.

This means that we need to characterise when
f_{\textnormal{left}}
and
f_{\textnormal{right}}
from Definition
4.2
are bijective (and we know from Lemma
4.3
that one is bijective if and only if the other is bijective, so we can focus on either of them).

Recall that to describe
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
, we need to find the closest points from the set
A
on either side of
\xi_{\ell}+\delta\bmod 1=\left\{(n+\ell)\alpha\right\}
. In other words, we need to subtract some positive integer
x
from
n+\ell
, so that
n+\ell-x
falls into
[0,m-1]
, and so that
\left\{x\alpha\right\}
causes a minimal shift to the left or to the right.

To formalise this, we define

(6.1)

\displaystyle x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)

\displaystyle:=n+\ell-f_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell),

\displaystyle x_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)

\displaystyle:=n+\ell-f_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell),

for
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
.
Note that since
f_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell),f_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)\in[0,m-1]
, we have

(6.2)

x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell),x_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)\in[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]=:X(\ell).

The next lemma captures the fact that
\left\{x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}
and
\left\{x_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}
must correspond to “minimal shifts”.

Lemma 6.1
.

With the above definitions we have

\displaystyle\left\{x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in X(\ell)}\left\{x\alpha\right\},

\displaystyle\left\{x_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in X(\ell)}\left\{x\alpha\right\}.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definitions: For
x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)
we have

\displaystyle\left\{x_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:xleft_def}}}{{=}}\left\{\ell\alpha+n\alpha-f_{\textnormal{left}}(\ell)\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{Def.\ \ref{def:flr}}}}{{=}}\min_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\left\{\ell\alpha+n\alpha-i\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{n+\ell-m+1\leq x\leq n+\ell}\left\{x\alpha\right\},

and we can check the formula for
x_{\textnormal{right}}(\ell)
analogously.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Since
\left\{x\alpha\right\}\neq\left\{y\alpha\right\}
for irrational
\alpha
and integers
x\neq y
, Lemma
6.1
gives us an alternative definition for the functions
x_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{right}}
.

Now we can phrase
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
being bijective in terms of
x_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{right}}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(f,x)=(f_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{left}})
or
(f_{\textnormal{right}},x_{\textnormal{right}})
.
Then
f
is bijective if and only if

x(\ell)-\ell\neq x(\ell^{\prime})-\ell^{\prime}

for all
0\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq m-1
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition: We have
x(\ell)=n+\ell-f(\ell)
, which gives us
f(\ell)=n+\ell-x(\ell)
, and so
f(\ell)=f(\ell^{\prime})\iff x(\ell)-\ell=x(\ell^{\prime})-\ell^{\prime}
. Since
f
is a map on the finite set
\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
, it is injective if and only if it is bijective.
Thus,
f
is bijective if and only if
f(\ell)\neq f(\ell^{\prime})
for all
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, and by the previous argument this is equivalent to
x(\ell)-\ell\neq x(\ell^{\prime})-\ell^{\prime}
for all
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
.
∎

A brief recap: We want to characterise all
2\leq m\leq n
such that the
m\times n
rectangles of the Sturmian words with slope
\alpha
are balanced (prove Theorem
2.1
).
The
m\times n
rectangles of the Sturmian words with slope
\alpha
being balanced is equivalent to the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
being balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
(Theorem
3.1
).
This again is equivalent to the function
f=f_{\textnormal{left}}
or
f=f_{\textnormal{right}}
being bijective (Lemma
4.3
).
And to decide whether
f
is bijective, we can use Lemma
6.3
and the corresponding function
x=x_{\textnormal{left}}
or
x=x_{\textnormal{right}}
, which can be defined via Lemma
6.1
.
Indeed, this is our strategy.
In order to unify our arguments, recall that in the previous section we defined

\langle\xi\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}=\left\{\xi\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}=\left\{-\xi\right\}.

In the rest of this section, we will always either set
(f,x,\langle\cdot\rangle)=(f_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}})
or
(f,x,\langle\cdot\rangle)=(f_{\textnormal{right}},x_{\textnormal{right}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}})
.
In either case, we can now phrase Lemma
6.1
as

\langle x(\ell)\alpha\rangle=\min_{x\in X(\ell)}\langle x\alpha\rangle,

where the range
X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,\leq n+\ell]
was defined in (
6.2
).
In view of this and Lemma
6.3
, we are interested in integers
x
in the range

[n-m+1,n+m-1]=\bigcup_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}X(\ell)

that minimise
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
or
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
.

Let
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
be the integers that minimise
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
or
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, respectively, in the full range
[n-m+1,n+m-1]
. Again, according to the setting, we will write just
x^{*}
for
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}
or
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
.
In other words, in our notation we have

\langle x^{*}\alpha\rangle=\min_{n-m+1\leq x\leq n+m-1}\langle x\alpha\rangle.

Note that the only integer that occurs in the range
X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
for every
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
is the integer
n
.
Therefore, the case
x^{*}=n
is particularly easy, and we start with this case.

6.1.  The case
n=x^{*}

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(f,x^{*})=(f_{\textnormal{left}},x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*})
or
(f,x^{*})=(f_{\textnormal{right}},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*})
.
If
x^{*}=n
, then
f
is bijective.

Proof.

Since
x^{*}=n\in X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
for every
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
, we have in fact
x(\ell)=x^{*}
for all
0\leq\ell\leq m-1
.
Thus
x(\ell)-\ell=x^{*}-\ell\neq x^{*}-\ell^{\prime}=x(\ell^{\prime})-\ell^{\prime}
for all
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, and by Lemma
6.3
the function
f
is bijective.
∎

We also characterise the case
x^{*}=n
in terms of the Ostrowski representations of
m,n
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n
with
q_{M-1}<m\leq q_{M}
.
We have
n\in\{x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}\}
if and only if
the Ostrowski representation of
n
with respect to
\alpha
is of one of the following two shapes:

(a)

n=\sum_{k=L}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
L\geq M
;

(b)

n=q_{M-1}+\sum_{k=M+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+2t}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let

n=\sum_{k=L}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}\quad\text{with }b_{L}\neq 0.

If
\langle n\alpha\rangle>1/2
for
\langle\cdot\rangle=\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
or
\langle\cdot\rangle=\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, then, by Lemma
5.4
, one of
\langle(n-1)\alpha\rangle,\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle
must be strictly smaller than
\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
Since
n-1,n+1
are both in the range
[n-m+1,n+m-1]
, we have
n\neq x^{*}
.
Therefore, it suffices to consider the choice of
\langle\cdot\rangle\in\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
for which
\langle n\alpha\rangle<1/2
, and check whether
x^{*}=n
.
We systematically go through all possible representations of
n
.

Case 1:

Either
L\geq M
, or
L=M-1
and
n=q_{M-1}+\sum_{k=M+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+2t}\neq 0
. In other words,
n
has the representation from
(a)
or
(b)
.
Then Lemma
5.12
says that
n
minimises
\langle n\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n-q_{M}+1,n+q_{M}-1]\supseteq[n-m+1,n+m-1]
. Thus, we have indeed
n=x^{*}
.

Case 2:

L=M-1
and
n=q_{M-1}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+1+2t}\neq 0
.
Set
n_{1}:=\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
.
By Lemma
5.2

we have
\langle n_{1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
as well, except possibly if
M-1=0
or
M-1=1
.
In the exceptional cases (i.e., if
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
is “on the wrong side of
1/2
”), we can apply Lemma
5.3
and obtain
\langle(n+1)\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
.
Otherwise, if
\langle n_{1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, then since
q_{M-1}<m
, the integer
n_{1}
is in the range
[n-m+1,n+m-1]
. By Lemma
5.6
, we have
\langle n_{1}\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
, and so
n\neq x^{*}
.

Case 3:

L=M-1
and
n=b_{M-1}q_{M-1}+\sum_{k=M}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
b_{M-1}\geq 2
.
If
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
), we set
n_{1}:=n-q_{M-1}>n-m
. Then, by Lemma
5.7
, we have
\langle n_{1}\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
, and so
n\neq x^{*}
.
If
L=1
(or
L=0
if
\alpha<1/2
), the same follows from Lemma
5.11
.

Case 4:

L\leq M-2
. If
L\geq 2
(or
L\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
), we set
n_{1}:=n+q_{L+1}<n+m
.
Then by Lemma
5.9
, we have
\langle n_{1}\alpha\rangle<\langle n\alpha\rangle
, and so
n\neq x^{*}
.
If
L=1
(or
L=0
if
\alpha<1/2
), the same follows from Lemma
5.10
.
∎

The two above lemmas give us a partial result towards the full characterisation in Theorem
2.1
; note that the cases
(a)
and
(b)
in Lemma
6.5
are almost the same as the cases
(i)
and
(ii)
in Theorem
2.1
.
Next, we provide some lemmas that will be useful in the situation where
x^{*}\neq n
.
We start with two lemmas which will, roughly speaking, allow us to assume
x^{*}<n
without loss of generality.

6.2.  Switching beween
n
and
q_{T}-n

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n
. Then, for sufficiently large
T
, the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
are balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
if and only if the intervals of length
\left\{(q_{T}-n)\alpha\right\}
are balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Lemma
4.6
and the fact that
\lVert q_{T}\alpha\rVert
gets arbitrarily small for large
T
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}=:x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}(m,n)
be defined as before, and define
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)
to be the analogous number for the pair
(m,q_{T}-n)
, i.e.,
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}
in the range
[q_{T}-n-m+1,q_{T}-n+m-1]
. Define
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,n)
and
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)
analogously.
Then we have

x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}(m,n)>n\iff x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)<q_{T}-n.

Proof.

Note that since
\langle\xi\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}=\langle-\xi\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, we have

\langle-x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}=\langle x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}=\min_{n-m+1\leq x\leq n+m-1}\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{left}}=\min_{-(n+m-1)\leq x\leq-(n-m+1)}\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}.

If
q_{T}
is sufficiently large, adding
q_{T}
to every
x
in the range
[-(n+m-1),-(n-m+1)]=[-n-m+1,-n+m-1]
does not change the ordering of the numbers
\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}
, and so the above equation implies

\langle(q_{T}-x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*})\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}=\max_{q_{T}-n-m+1\leq x\leq q_{T}-n+m-1}\langle x\alpha\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}.

This means that
q_{T}-x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}=x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)
, and so

x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}>n\iff x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)<q_{T}-n
.
∎

6.3.  Technical lemmas for the case
x^{*}<n

If
x^{*}<n
, then we do not have
x(\ell)=x^{*}
for all
\ell\in[0,m-1]
. However, it is not hard to see that
x(\ell)=x^{*}
for a certain range of
\ell
’s (which, admittedly, might be only the single integer
\ell=n-m+1
).
We can say more about the precise values of
x(\ell)
in adjacent ranges.
We start with the special case where
m
is particularly small.
Recall that we always imply that either
(x^{*},x,\langle\cdot\rangle)=(x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}})
or
(x^{*},x,\langle\cdot\rangle)=(x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}})
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n
and assume
m<q_{1}
(or
m<q_{2}
if
\alpha>1/2
).
Assume
x^{*}<n
.
Then
x(m-1+x^{*}-n)=x^{*}
and
x(m+x^{*}-n)=x^{*}+1
.
In particular,
f
is not bijective.

Proof.

Since
X(m-1+x^{*}-n)=[x^{*},x^{*}+m-1]
, we have
x(m-1+x^{*}-n)=x^{*}
by definition of
x
and
x^{*}
.
Now consider
X(m+x^{*}-n)=[x^{*}+1,x^{*}+m]
.
Let
x^{\prime}:=\min\{x>x^{*}\colon\langle x\alpha\rangle\}
. Since
x^{*}
minimises
\langle x\rangle
in the range
[n-m+1,n+m-1]
, we have
x^{\prime}>n+m-1\geq x^{*}+m
.
Since we are assuming
m<q_{1}
(or
m<q_{2}
if
\alpha>1/2
),
Lemma
5.14
implies that
x(m+x^{*}-n)=x^{*}+1
.
Thus,
x(m-x^{*}-n)-x(m-1+x^{*}-n)=1=(m-x^{*}-n)-(m-1+x^{*}-n)
, and so
f
is not bijective by Lemma
6.3
.
∎

Now we determine the value of
x(\ell)
in certain ranges of
\ell
in the case that
m
is not very small.
This is probably the most technical part of the paper; after that we will be able to prove (non-)balancedness in various cases.

Lemma 6.9
.

Assume
2\leq m\leq n
and
q_{M-1}<m\leq q_{M}
with
m\geq q_{2}
(or
m\geq q_{1}
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Moreover, assume that
x^{*}<n
.
Then we have

x(\ell)=x^{*}\quad\iff\quad\ell\in[0,m-1+x^{*}-n]=:R_{0}.

If
\langle q_{M-1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, then

\displaystyle x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-1}

\displaystyle\iff\quad\ell\in[m+x^{*}-n,\min\{m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-1},m-1\}]=:R_{1}.

If
\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M}
, then

\displaystyle x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M}\quad\iff\quad\ell\in[m+x^{*}-n,m-1]=:R_{1}^{\prime}.

If
\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and

q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\leq m<q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}

with
0\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
, and where we assume that
M-2\geq 0
(or
M-2\geq 1
if
\alpha>1/2
),
then

\displaystyle x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in[m+x^{*}-n,\min\{x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1,m-1\}]=:R_{1}^{\prime\prime};

\displaystyle x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in[x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1},

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\min\{x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+2)q_{M-1}-1,m-1\}]=:R_{2}^{\prime\prime}.

Note that the range
R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
is empty if
\min\{x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1,m-1\}=m-1
.

Proof.

By the definitions of
x^{*}
and the function
x
, we clearly have
x(\ell)=x^{*}
if and only if
x^{*}\in X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
. Under our assumption
x^{*}<n
, this happens exactly for
\ell\in[0,m-1+x^{*}-n]
. This settles the statement regarding the range
R_{0}
.
Now we go through all the cases from the statement of the lemma.

Case 1:

\langle q_{M-1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.

First, note that
x^{*}+q_{M-1}\in X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
if and only if
\ell\in[x^{*}-n+q_{M-1},m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-1}]
.
Since
x^{*}-n+q_{M-1}\leq m+x^{*}-n
, we have that indeed
x^{*}+q_{M-1}\in X(\ell)
for all
\ell\in R_{1}=[m+x^{*}-n,\min\{m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-1},m-1\}]
(and not for any larger
\ell
).
Now we only need to show that
x^{*}+q_{M-1}
indeed minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in each range
X(\ell)
for
\ell\in R_{1}
.
In other words, we need to show that

(6.3)

\langle(x^{*}+q_{M-1})\rangle=\min\{\langle x\alpha\rangle\colon x\in[x^{*}+1,\min\{m+x^{*}-1+q_{M-1},n+m-1\}]\}.

From the definition of
x^{*}
it follows that

x^{\prime}:=\min\{x>x^{*}\colon\langle x\alpha\rangle<\langle x^{*}\alpha\rangle\}>n+m-1.

Moreover, note that
m+x^{*}-1+q_{M-1}<x^{*}+q_{M-1}+q_{M}
.
Therefore, we get (
6.3
) directly from Lemma
5.15
with
a=0
. This settles the statement regarding the range
R_{1}
.

Case 2:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M}

First, note that
x^{*}+q_{M}\in X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
if and only if
\ell\in[x^{*}-n+q_{M},m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M}]=[m+x^{*}-n,2m+x^{*}-n-1]
.
Since
2m+x^{*}-n-1\geq m
, we have in fact
x^{*}+q_{M}\in X(\ell)
for all
\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime}=[m+x^{*}-n,m-1]
.
Now we only need to show that
x^{*}+q_{M}
indeed minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in each range
X(\ell)
for
\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime}
.
Since the largest element in all these ranges is
n+m-1\leq x^{*}+2m-2=x^{*}+2q_{M}-2<x^{*}+q_{M}+q_{M+1}
,
this follows again directly from Lemma
5.15
with
a=0
.

Case 3:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and

q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\leq m<q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}

with
0\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
.

First, note that
x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\in X(\ell)=[n+\ell-m+1,n+\ell]
if and only if
\ell\in[x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1},m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}]
.
On the one hand, we have
x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\leq m+x^{*}-n
. On the other hand, recall that in the lemma we assume
m>q_{M-1}
, and so

m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\geq x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1
.
Thus, we have indeed
x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}\in X(\ell)
for

\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime\prime}=[m+x^{*}-n,\min\{x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1,m-1\}]
.
We need to check that
x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[x^{*}+1,x^{*}+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1]
. Indeed, this follows directly from Lemma
5.15
.

The proof for the range
R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
is completely analogous, except for the following detail: If
a+1=a_{M}
, then
q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}=q_{M}
, and Lemma
5.15
would allow us to make
R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
even larger.
∎

In the case
x^{*}<n
we can now
use the above lemma to characterise
f
being bijective in terms of the shape of
x^{*}
and
m
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Assume
2\leq m\leq n
and
q_{M-1}<m\leq q_{M}
with
m\geq q_{2}
(or
m\geq q_{1}
if
\alpha<1/2
).
Moreover, assume that
x^{*}<n
.
Then
f
is bijective in exactly the two following cases:

•

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M}
;

•

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
,
x^{*}\geq n-q_{M-1}
, and
m=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
for some
1\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
and
M-2\geq 0
(or
M-2\geq 1
if
\alpha>1/2
).

Proof.

We distinguish between five cases, based on whether
\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
or
\langle q_{M-1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, and some extra conditions.

Case 1:

\langle q_{M-1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
.

Then, by Lemma
6.9
, we have
x(\ell)=x^{*}
for
\ell\in R_{0}
and
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-1}
for
\ell\in R_{1}
.
Since
m-1\geq q_{M-1}
and
m+x^{*}-n-1+q_{M-1}\geq q_{M-1}
, the range
R_{0}\cup R_{1}
contains at least
q_{M-1}+1
consecutive integers.
Therefore, there exist
\ell\in R_{0}
and
\ell^{\prime}\in R_{1}
such that
\ell^{\prime}-\ell=q_{M-1}
. For these
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
we have
x(\ell^{\prime})-x(\ell)=q_{M-1}=\ell^{\prime}-\ell
, and so
f
is not bijective by Lemma
6.3
.

Case 2:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M}
.

By Lemma
6.9
, we have either
x(\ell)=x^{*}
or
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M}
for all
k\in R_{0}\cup R_{1}^{\prime}=[0,m-1]=[0,q_{M}-1]
. Therefore, it is clear that we cannot have
\ell-\ell^{\prime}=x(\ell)-x(\ell^{\prime})
for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\in[0,m-1]
, and so
f
is bijective by Lemma
6.3
.

Case 3:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
with
1\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
and
x^{*}\geq n-q_{M-1}
.

Then, by Lemma
6.9
, we have
x(\ell)=x^{*}
for
\ell\in R_{0}
and
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
for
\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime\prime}
.
Moreover, since we are assuming
x^{*}\geq n-q_{M-1}
, we have

x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1\geq q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}-1=m-1.

In other words,
R_{0}\cup R_{1}^{\prime\prime}=[0,m+1]
, and so for all
\ell\in[0,m-1]=[0,q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}-1]
either
x(\ell)=x^{*}
or
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
.
As in Case 2, it is clear that
f
is bijective.

Case 4:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
m=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
with
1\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
, but now
x^{*}<n-q_{M-1}
.

Then

x^{*}-n+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}-1<m-1,

and so there is at least one
\ell\in R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
.
Now since the range
R_{1}^{\prime\prime}
contains exactly
q_{M-1}
integers (note that
m=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
), the range
R_{1}^{\prime\prime}\cup R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
contains at least
q_{M-1}+1
consecutive integers.
Moreover, Lemma
6.9
says that
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
for
\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and
x(\ell)=x^{*}+q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}
for
\ell\in R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, there must exist
\ell\in R_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and
\ell^{\prime}\in R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
with
\ell^{\prime}-\ell=q_{M-1}=x(\ell^{\prime})-x(\ell)
, and so
f
is not bijective.

Case 5:

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
and
q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}<m<q_{M-2}+(a+1)q_{M-1}
for some
0\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
.
If the range
R_{2}^{\prime\prime}
is non-empty, then
f
is not bijective by the same argument as in Case 4.
Otherwise,
R_{0}\cup R_{1}^{\prime\prime}=[0,m-1]
, and the range contains
m>q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
consecutive integers.
Thus, we can find
\ell\in R_{0}
,
\ell^{\prime}\in R_{1}^{\prime\prime}
with
\ell^{\prime}-\ell=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}=x(\ell^{\prime})-x(\ell)
, and so
f
is not bijective either.
∎

6.4.  The cases where
x^{*}\neq n

We now collect some of our results to show that “usually” we are in the not balanced situation, which is a big step towards finishing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n
.
Assume that
q_{M-1}<m<q_{M}
and that
m
is not a semi-convergent.
Moreover, assume that
n\neq x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
.
Then
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are not bijective.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
4.3
that
f_{\textnormal{left}}
being bijective is equivalent to
f_{\textnormal{right}}
being bijective, which is equivalent to the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
with respect to
(\alpha,m)
being balanced.
Moreover, recall from Lemma
6.6
that the intervals of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
are balanced if and only if the intervals of length
\left\{(q_{T}-n)\alpha\right\}
are balanced, for
T
sufficiently large.

Now, if
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}<n
, then Lemmas
6.8
and
6.10
imply that
f_{\textnormal{left}}
is not bijective.

If
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}>n
, then by Lemma
6.7
we have
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)<q_{T}-n
. Then again Lemmas
6.8
and
6.10
imply that the intervals of length
\left\{(q_{T}-n)\alpha\right\}
are not balanced with respect to
(\alpha,m)
, and so neither are those of length
\left\{n\alpha\right\}
.
∎

In the last two lemmas we deal with the cases where
m
is a (semi-)convergent.
Note that
n^{[\leq M]}
,
n^{[\geq M]}
, and
k_{\geq M}(n)
were defined in Section
5.5
.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
2\leq m=q_{M}\leq n
, and assume
n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
.
Then
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective if and only if
k_{\geq M}(n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

Choose
\langle\cdot\rangle\in\{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{left}},\langle\cdot\rangle_{\textnormal{right}}\}
so that
\langle n^{[\geq M]}\rangle<1/2
.
Note that
M\geq 2
if
\alpha>1/2
, and
M\geq 1
if
\alpha<1/2
.
We want to show that
x^{*}<n
because then we can finish with Lemma
6.10
. In fact, we want to show that
x^{*}\leq n^{[\geq M]}
.

First, note that
n^{[\geq M]}=n-n^{[\leq M-1]}\in[n-m+1,n-1]
because
0<n^{[\leq M-1]}<q_{M}=m
.
Therefore, we want to show that for all
x\in[n^{[\geq M]}+1,n+m-1]
we have
\langle x\alpha\rangle>\langle n^{[\geq M]}\alpha\rangle
.

If
k_{0}(n^{[\geq M]})\geq M+1
, then Lemma
5.13
tells us that

n^{[\geq M]}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n^{[\geq M]},n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M}+q_{M+1}-1]
.
The upper bound of this range is

\displaystyle n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M}+q_{M+1}-1\geq n-m+1+q_{M}+q_{M+1}-1=n+q_{M+1}>n+m-1.

Therefore, we indeed have
x^{*}\leq n^{[\geq M]}
.

Now assume
k_{0}(n^{[\geq M]})=M
.
By the same argument as before, Lemma
5.13
tells us that

n^{[\geq M]}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n^{[\geq M]},n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M-1}+q_{M}-1]
.
If

(6.4)

n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M-1}+q_{M}-1\geq n+m-1,

then, as before, we know that
n^{[\geq M]}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n^{[\geq M]},n+m-1]
, and thus
x^{*}\leq n^{[\geq M]}
.
Assume that the inequality (
6.4
) does not hold, i.e., after cancelling
q_{M}-1=m-1
, we get

n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M-1}<n.

Since
n^{[\leq M-1]}=n-n^{[\geq M]}
, this implies
n^{[\leq M-1]}>q_{M-1}
, and so by the property (
5.6
) of the Ostrowski representation, we have
b_{M-1}(n)>0
.
But then, by the digit rules for Ostrowski representations, we must have
b_{M}(n)\leq a_{M+1}-1
, and so we can use the stronger statement in Lemma
5.13
: In this case
n^{[\geq M]}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n^{[\geq M]},n^{[\geq M]}+q_{M-1}+2q_{M}-1]
.
This now covers the full range
[n^{[\geq M]}+1,n+m-1]
, and thus
x^{*}\leq n^{[\geq M]}
.

Overall, we have proven that
x^{*}\leq n^{[\geq M]}<n
, and so Lemma
6.10
says that
f
is bijective if and only if
\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, which is equivalent to
k_{\geq M}(n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
by Lemma
5.5
.
∎

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
m=q_{M-2}+aq_{M-1}
with
1\leq a\leq a_{M}-1
and
M-2\geq 1
(or
M-2\geq 0
if
\alpha<1/2
). Let
n\geq m
and assume that
n
is not of the shape
(a)
or
(b)
from Lemma
6.5
(or, in other words,
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}\neq n
).
Then
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective if and only if
k_{\geq M-1}(n)\equiv M\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

Let
m,n
be as in the lemma. We distinguish between two cases according to whether
q_{M-1}
shows up in the representation of
n
.

Case 1:

n=n^{[\leq M-2]}+n^{[\geq M]}
.

Since
n^{[\leq M-2]}<q_{M-1}<m
,
we have
n^{[\geq M]}\in[n-m+1,n+m-1]
.
Moreover, since
m\leq q_{M}-q_{M-1}
and
n^{[\leq M-2]}>n-q_{M-1}
,
we have
[n-m+1,n+m-1]\subseteq[n^{[\leq M-2]}-q_{M}+1,n^{[\leq M-2]}+q_{M}-1]
.
Thus, Lemma
5.12
implies that
n^{[\geq M]}=x^{*}
for the appropriate choice of
x^{*}\in\{x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}\}
.
In particular, we have
x^{*}<n
.
Now Lemma
6.10
says that
f
is bijective if and only if

\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
, which is equivalent to
k_{\geq M-1}(n)=k_{0}(n^{[\geq M]})\equiv M\pmod{2}
, by Lemma
5.5
.

Case 2:

n=n^{[\leq M-2]}+b_{M-1}q_{M-1}+n^{[\geq M]}
with
1\leq b_{M-1}\leq a_{M}
.

We want to show that
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are not bijective.

Our first goal is to show that we may assume that one of
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
is smaller than
n
.
If
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}<n
, this is of course the case.
Assume now that
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*}>n
. Then Lemma
6.7
says that if we consider
q_{T}-n
instead of
n
for sufficiently large
T
,
we get
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}(m,q_{T}-n)<q_{T}-n
.
Lemma
6.6
guarantees that the balancedness property doesn’t change if we replace
n
by
q_{T}-n
.
Lemma
5.18
implies (note that by assumption
n
is not of the shape
(b)
) that
k_{\geq M-1}(q_{T}-n)\equiv M-1\pmod{2}
.
If
q_{M-1}
does not show up in the representation of
q_{T}-n
, then we know from Case 1 that we are in the not balanced situation.
If
q_{M-1}
shows up in the representation of
q_{T}-n
, then
q_{T}-n
has the same shape (the shape of Case 2) as
n
, and we can replace
q_{T}-n
by
n
, knowing that now the new
x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
is smaller than the new
n
.

Overall, in Case 2, we may now assume that at least one of
x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
is smaller than
n
. We fix this
x^{*}<n
, and our goal is to show that
f
is not bijective.

In order to apply Lemma
6.10
we need to use the fact that
\langle q_{M}\alpha\rangle<1/2
if and only if
k_{0}(x^{*}\alpha)\equiv M\pmod{2}
. This is guaranteed by Lemma
5.5
, unless
k_{0}(x^{*})=0
(or
k_{0}(x^{*})=1
if
\alpha<1/2
).
In the exceptional cases we still have
\langle q_{0}\alpha\rangle<1/2
if
k_{0}(x^{*})=0
(and
\langle q_{1}\alpha\rangle<1/2
if
\alpha<1/2
and
k_{0}(x^{*})=1
). (To see this, assume the contrary and use Lemma
5.3
and the assumption
x^{*}<n
to obtain a contradiction.)

Now, if
k_{0}(x^{*})\equiv M+1\pmod{2}
, then
Lemma
6.10
says that
f
is not bijective, as desired.

If
k_{0}(x^{*})\equiv M\pmod{2}
and
x^{*}<n-q_{M-1}
, then Lemma
6.10
says the
f
is not bijective as well.

We are left with the case
n-q_{M-1}\leq x^{*}<n
and
k_{0}(x^{*})\equiv M\pmod{2}
.
We need to show that this is impossible for
n=n^{[\leq M-2]}+b_{M-1}q_{M-1}+n^{[\geq M]}
with
1\leq b_{M-1}\leq a_{M}
(and
n
not of the shape
(b)
).
The shape of
n
and the assumptions
n-q_{M-1}\leq x^{*}<n
and
k_{0}(x^{*})\equiv M\pmod{2}
imply that
k_{0}(x^{*})\geq M
is impossible. Thus, we must have
k_{0}(x^{*})\leq M-2
.
But then,
\langle(x^{*}+q_{M-1})\alpha\rangle<\langle x^{*}\alpha\rangle
by Lemma
5.9
. Since
x^{*}+q_{M-1}\in[n-m+1,n+m-1]
, this contradicts the fact that
x^{*}
minimises
\langle x\alpha\rangle
in the range
[n-m+1,n+m-1]
.
∎

6.5.  Finishing the proof of the full characterisation

We have now essentially proved Theorem
2.1
, and we summarise the arguments below.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Recall the four cases from Theorem
2.1
:

(i)

m=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_{k}q_{k}
and

n=\sum_{\ell=M+1}^{N}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}
;

(ii)

m=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_{k}q_{k}
with
b_{M}\neq 0
, and

n=q_{M}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+1+2t}\neq 0
.

(iii)

m=q_{M}
and
n=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=M+2t}^{N}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+2t}\neq 0
;

(iv)

m=q_{M-1}+aq_{M}
with
1\leq a\leq a_{M+1}-1
and

n=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}b_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\sum_{k=M+1+2t}^{N}b_{k}q_{k}
with
t\geq 0
and
b_{M+1+2t}\neq 0
.

Note that some of the cases overlap; for example if the small parts of
n
in
(iii)
or
(iv)
are zero, then we are also in the case
(i)
. (This was done for readability in Theorem
2.1
.)
We need to show that
m,n
are of the shape of at least one of the cases if and only if the
m\times n
rectangles of the Sturmian words with slope
\alpha
are balanced.
By Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
4.3
, this is equivalent to
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
being bijective (and we know that one function is bijective if and only if the other is bijective).

We first check that if
m,n
are of the shape of one of the cases
(i)
–
(iv)
, then
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective.
In the cases
(i)
,
(ii)
we have
m<q_{M+1}
,
and so Lemma
6.5
implies that
n\in\{x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}\}
, and Lemma
6.4
implies that the corresponding function
f
is bijective.
If we are in the case
(iii)
and
n^{[\leq M-1]}=0
, then again Lemmas
6.5
and
6.4
imply that
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective. If we are in the case
(iii)

n^{[\leq M-1]}>0
, this is provided by Lemma
6.12
.
If we are in the case
(iv)
and not in one of the previous cases, then Lemma
6.13
says that
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective.

For the implication in the other direction, assume that
2\leq m\leq n
and that
f_{\textnormal{left}},f_{\textnormal{right}}
are bijective.
If
n\in\{x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}\}
, then Lemmas
6.4
and
6.5
(and the property (
5.6
)) imply that
m,n
are of the shape
(i)
or
(ii)
.
If
n\neq x_{\textnormal{left}}^{*},x_{\textnormal{right}}^{*}
, then Lemma
6.11
implies that
m
must either be a convergent or a semi convergent. If
m
is a convergent, then Lemma
6.12
implies that
m,n
are of the shape
(iii)
(or possibly
(i)
). If
m
is a semi-convergent, then Lemma
6.13
implies that
m,n
are of the shape
(iv)
(or possibly
(i)
or
(ii)
).
∎
