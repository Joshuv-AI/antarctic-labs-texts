Transcriptome sequencing reveals Vmplc1 involved in regulating the pathogenicity of Valsa Mali under low temperature induction
Authors: Xianglong Meng, Yage Dong, Jiangtao Yin, Shufeng Gao, Antai Liu, Dongshan Li, Jiachen Guo, Bo Li, Pengbo Dai, Yanan Wang, Tongle Hu, Keqiang Cao, Shutong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApple Valsa canker (AVC), caused by the fungal pathogen Valsa mali (Vm), results in significant economic losses in Chinese apple orchards. Although cold priming has been shown to enhance the pathogenicity of Vm, the underlying molecular mechanisms of this temperature-dependent virulence remain poorly understood.ResultsRNA-seq analysis of low-temperature-primed Vm strain Vm263 (5 °C, 24 h) and infected apple tissues identified 962 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in primed mycelia (LF), escalating to 3,977 DEGs during cold-primed infection (LL). Notably, 66% of differentially expressed carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were upregulated during cold priming, with glycoside hydrolases (GHs) exhibiting predominant induction (66.15% upregulated). Through integrative analysis among LF and LL, 63 core cold-responsive virulence were prioritized, including nine secreted GHs and 12 short-chain dehydrogenases (SDRs). Venn analysis identified 229 co-expressed genes (136 upregulated, 93 downregulated) consistently altered across cold adaptation conditions (LF, LL, CKL). Among these, 44 key candidate DEGs were functionally categorized into five classes (Cold Response, Plant Cell Wall Degradation, Signal Transduction, Toxins/Secondary Metabolites, Transcription/Translation & Transport/Metabolism), collectively underpinning critical biological processes essential for cold-induced pathogenicity enhancement. In signal transduction functional class, Vmplc1 encodes canonical full-length thermosensory domains and implied as a signaling hub bridging low-temperature perception to virulence execution. Functional validation confirmed that Vmplc1 is essential for cold-primed pathogenicity. ΔVmplc1 mutants showed 58–62% reduced lesion formation despite unimpaired growth, with complementation restoring wild-type virulence. This phenotype correlated with 70–85% suppression of polygalacturonase/cellulase expression and enzymatic activity.ConclusionThis study establishes that cold priming potentiates the pathogenicity of Valsa mali by activating a coordinated transcriptional program involving five functional classes of genes, with the Vmplc1-mediated signaling pathway serving as a central hub for upregulating virulence effectors. These findings elucidate a critical thermal adaptation mechanism in which Vmplc1 functions as a pivotal regulator of temperature-dependent virulence, reprogramming the pathogen for enhanced host colonization under low-temperature stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12303-4.

Introduction

Apple Valsa canker (AVC), caused by the ascomycete fungus Valsa mali (Vm), is a devastating perennial disease that severely threatens Malus domestica cultivation across East Asia, including China, Japan, and South Korea [1]. In China, AVC is widespread and severe. A comprehensive survey across major apple-producing regions revealed that over 52.7% of trees are affected, with incidence increasing significantly with tree age [2]. The pathogen employs a stealth invasion strategy whereby conidia, which exhibit remarkable environmental adaptability including germination at temperatures as low as 0–5 °C [3], colonize fresh pruning wounds during spring or autumn rains. This establishes latent infections primarily within the xylem, where the pathogen can reside asymptomatically for a highly variable period, typically ranging from months to over a year [4, 5]. The transition from latent infection to symptomatic necrotrophy is not time-dependent but is governed by permissive conditions. Crucially, the duration of latency is influenced by environmental factors such as low temperature and high humidity, as well as host factors like wound age and overall tree vigor [3–5]. Symptomatic canker development occurs predominantly in the spring when the pathogen grows from the xylem into the phloem, often following host stress events [5, 6]. This insidious disease cycle highlights AVC as a persistent and formidable challenge in phytosanitary management.

Temperature plays a pivotal role in the epidemiology of AVC, particularly in regulating the transition from latent infection to symptomatic invasion. While conidial dispersal relies on rainwater to reach pruning wounds, experimental evidence indicates that subsequent infection does not require extended periods of free moisture, as conidia suspended in rainwater are sufficient for successful colonization [5]. The temperature window for conidial infection through wounds ranges from 5 to 35 °C, with an optimum at 20 °C [5]. This thermosensitivity underpins the pronounced seasonality of the disease. The critical period for infection aligns with spring (March to May) in China’s major apple-producing regions, when temperatures fall within this permissive range and a vast number of fresh pruning wounds are present from winter dormancy pruning [7]. Consequently, disease outbreaks peak in spring, as the pathogen, having established latent infections in the xylem, aggressively colonizes the phloem and cortical tissues [2, 8]. This pattern, consistently observed across different regions [2, 8], establishes sub-optimal spring temperatures as a key driver for the synchronous development of canker symptoms. Traditional models attributed this seasonality solely to cold-impaired host defenses, proposing that diminished tree vitality facilitates pathogen breakthrough [9]. However, this paradigm neglects potential temperature effects on Vm virulence programming—a critical knowledge gap that hinders predictive disease management strategies.

Emerging evidence challenges host-centric explanations for cold-aggravated AVC. Controlled assays demonstrate that brief low-temperature exposure intrinsically enhances Vm fitness, accelerating mycelial growth rates and amplifying virulence on detached apple tissues [10]. Notably, these conditions also upregulate key pathogenicity determinants, including pectinases, hemicellulases, cellulases, and VmFAEC1, suggesting thermal regulation of virulence pathways [10]. These findings imply that temperature acts not merely as an abiotic stressor on the host but as a bona fide virulence-inducing signal for Vm.

V. mali pathogenesis relies on a coordinated arsenal of conserved phytopathogenic effectors. During the early infection stages of V. mali, cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs) such as pectinases, cellulases, and endo-β−1,4-xylanase VmXyl1 have been experimentally demonstrated to play critical roles in decomposing cellulose and pectin in the xylem of apple branches, thereby facilitating pathogen invasion and colonization at wound sites [11]– [12]. Additionally, cytochrome P450 enzyme gene VmCYP5 has been proven to involve in synthesizing toxic secondary metabolites that compromise host membrane integrity [13]. Secreted effectors, including Hce2-domain proteins VmHEPs and early-expressed virulence factor VmPxE1, have been shown to specifically suppress apple immune responses [14]– [15]. Regarding signaling regulation, while G protein α subunits Gvm2 and Gvm3 typically belong to conserved signaling pathways [16], they have been experimentally validated in V. mali to regulate vegetative growth, asexual development, and pathogenicity [17]. It is noteworthy that although these effectors and pathways have been individually verified through experiments, their coordinated thermoregulation mechanisms under temperature stress remain poorly understood. This gap directly impacts studies on the adaptation of apple valsa canker (AVC) to climatic variability, representing a critical knowledge deficit in understanding pathogen-environment interactions.

A particularly unresolved question is how low-temperature conditions mechanistically reprogram Vm virulence at the transcriptional level. While prokaryotic studies have shown that cold shock proteins (CSPs) mediate temperature-responsive survival and virulence regulation during host colonization [18, 19], eukaryotic genomes generally lack homologous CSP-encoding genes, suggesting evolutionary divergence in thermoregulatory mechanisms. In plant-pathogenic fungi, this thermal perception is often achieved through alternative strategies, such as membrane fluidity sensors that gauge thermal cues via lipid ordering [20], and conserved signaling kinases that integrate environmental stimuli into virulence programs [21]. Intriguingly, V. mali exhibits temperature-dependent virulence patterns, with significant cold-induced upregulation of CWDEs and effector proteins under experimental condition [10]. However, the molecular components mediating this thermal response remain unidentified. This knowledge gap is critically important to address. The standard use of a static 25 °C in laboratory studies, while optimal for fungal growth, may not fully capture the pathogenic behavior of V. mali under the thermally dynamic conditions of its natural infection window, which epidemiological studies indicate primarily occurs at 5–15 °C [2, 3, 5]. Therefore, investigating pathogenicity under these ecologically relevant temperatures is essential for building accurate disease models.

This study employs an integrated transcriptomic and functional genetics approach to address three primary objectives: (1) To decipher cold-responsive regulons by comparing genome-wide expression profiles of Vm263 mycelia under low-temperature priming versus normothermic conditions during host infection. (2) To identify key thermoregulated virulence determinants through multi-omics enrichment analysis coupled with in planta validation. (3) To characterize signaling components governing temperature-dependent pathogenicity, with a focus on phospholipase C family members. These investigations aim to elucidate the transcriptional regulatory network and key genes underlying the thermal adaptation of virulence pathways in V. mali.

Results

Low-temperature induction enhances mycelial growth and virulence in Valsa mali strain Vm263

To examine the impact of low-temperature priming on V. mali fitness, strain Vm263 was pre-incubated at 5–25 °C for 24 h after reaching 3/4 coverage on PDA plates. Following pretreatment, mycelial plugs from colony margins were transferred to fresh PDA and cultured at 25 °C. Notably, low-temperature-primed samples exhibited significantly accelerated mycelial growth compared to the 25 °C controls (P < 0.05), indicating that cold induction promotes hyphal proliferation (Fig. 1A, B). More importantly, when similarly pretreated mycelia were inoculated onto detached apple leaves, low-temperature-primed Vm263 displayed markedly enhanced virulence, characterized by significantly larger lesion areas and higher disease indices than controls (Fig. 1C, D). These findings demonstrate that brief low-temperature exposure not only stimulates vegetative growth but also augments the pathogenic capacity of V. mali.

Transcriptomic profiling and sequencing quality control of Valsa mali strain Vm263

To elucidate the transcriptional response of V. mali to cold priming, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on strain Vm263 under four experimental conditions: (1) normal-temperature cultured mycelia (CKF, 25 °C control), (2) low-temperature-primed mycelia (LF, 5 °C/24 h transferred to 25 °C/24 h), (3) apple leaves inoculated with CKF mycelia (CKL), and (4) leaves inoculated with LF mycelia (LL). Twelve high-quality RNA libraries (triplicate biological replicates per condition) yielded 792 million clean reads after rigorous quality control (adapter removal, Q < 20 filtering, exclusion of > 10% N-content reads). Libraries exhibited robust sequencing depth (42–93 million reads/library) and exceptional base quality (Q20: 96.30–96.79.30.79%; Q30: 90.64–91.73%) (Table S1).

HISAT2 alignment revealed high mapping efficiency for fungal-predominant samples (CKF and LF: >95.03% genome-mapped), whereas plant-pathogen mixed samples (CKL and LL) showed variable mapping rates (1.75–93.89%) due to host RNA dilution (Table S2). Inter-sample Pearson correlation coefficients demonstrated high reproducibility across treatment groups. Specifically, R2 values for CKF and LF exceeded 0.9, while those for CKL and LL were slightly lower (R2 > 0.8), except for a few outliers (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) identified two statistical outliers: LL_1 exhibited significant separation from other LL replicates, and CKL_2 diverged from the CKL clusters (Fig. 2B). Following standard quality control (QC) protocols, both outliers were excluded from subsequent analyses to ensure data integrity.

Gene annotation and functional classification

Functional annotation revealed 5,669 genes of Function unknown, with enriched functional categories included intracellular trafficking and secretion (451 genes), protein turnover systems (418 genes), and carbohydrate metabolism (355 genes), suggesting these biological processes may play significant roles in the low temperature response of Vm (Table S3).

Gene Ontology analysis annotated 25,701 genes into three primary domains (Table S4). Within biological processes, metabolic processes (2,618 genes) and cellular processes (2,519 genes) were predominant. Cellular component analysis highlighted membrane parts (2,989 genes) and cell organelles (1,147 genes) as major constituents. Molecular functions were dominated by catalytic activity (4,482 genes) and binding functions (3,400 genes), with transporter activity (700 genes) also representing a significant category. This distribution pattern reflects active metabolic and regulatory networks under cold induction.

KEGG pathway mapping identified 3,439 genes distributed across 125 metabolic and regulatory pathways (Table S5). Five pathways were particularly prominent: (1) ribosome biogenesis and function, (2) RNA transport mechanisms, (3) spliceosome assembly, (4) cell cycle regulation, and (5) endoplasmic reticulum protein processing. These results suggest that protein synthesis machinery and RNA processing systems are key functional modules activated during cold induction.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with stringent thresholds (adjusted p-value < 0.05 and (log2 FC) ≥ 1). Comparative analysis revealed distinct transcriptional responses across treatment groups relative to the CKF control. The pure culture under low temperature (LF) showed the most conservative response, with only 962 DEGs identified (451 upregulated, 511 downregulated) (Fig. 3A, Table S6). In contrast, infected tissues exhibited more extensive transcriptional reprogramming, with the low-temperature infected group (LL) displaying 3,977 DEGs (2,225 upregulated, 1,752 downregulated), representing a 4.1-fold increase compared to LF (Fig. 3B, Table S7). The control infected group (CKL) demonstrated intermediate changes with 3,246 DEGs (2,031 upregulated, 1,215 downregulated) (Fig. 3C, Table S8), indicating that infection status alone induces substantial transcriptomic alterations independent of temperature stress. These results collectively indicate that both low-temperature exposure and host infection status significantly reshape the transcriptional landscape of V. mali, with their combination eliciting the most pronounced effects.

Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional profiles of DEGs across treatment groups (Fig. 5). In the low-temperature pure culture (LF), DEGs were predominantly associated with oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), extracellular localization (GO:0005576), and polysaccharide binding (GO:0030247), suggesting active metabolic adaptation to cold stress (Table S9). The low-temperature infected group (LL) showed significant enrichment in glycosyl hydrolase activity (GO:0016798), oxidoreductase function (GO:0016491), and carbohydrate metabolism (GO:0005975), indicating combined responses to both thermal stress and host-pathogen interactions (Table S10). Control infected samples (CKL) exhibited ribosomal (GO:0003735) and translational (GO:0006412) enrichment, consistent with active protein synthesis during infection (Table S11). Notably, oxidoreductase activity emerged as a common enriched term across all treatments, underscoring its central role in stress response. Molecular function terms represented the most abundant category (58.7% of enriched terms), followed by biological processes (31.2%) and cellular components (10.1%), demonstrating that temperature stress primarily affects enzymatic and regulatory functions in V. mali.

KEGG pathway enrichment reveals metabolic reprogramming under low-temperature priming

KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs in LF, LL and CKL cohorts revealed distinct metabolic reprogramming patterns. DEGs were enriched in 19 pathways across five major categories, with carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism being most prominently represented (Fig. 6). All three groups shared significant enrichment (p-Value < 0.05) in core metabolic pathways including starch and sucrose metabolism (map00500), fructose and mannose metabolism (map00051), pentose and glucuronate interconversions (map00040), and cysteine and methionine metabolism (map00270). LF uniquely exhibited enrichment in glutathione metabolism (map00480), aflatoxin biosynthesis (map00254), carotenoid biosynthesis (map00906, shared with fatty acid biosynthesis) (Table S12), likely reflecting specific stress responses to low temperature. In contrast, LL demonstrated exclusive enrichment in pathways related to amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (map00520), galactose metabolism (map00052), alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (map00250), cyanoamino acid metabolism (map00460), and purine metabolism (map00230) (Table S13). These pathways may facilitate cell wall biosynthesis, carbohydrate utilization, nitrogen assimilation, detoxification, and nucleotide signaling during infection under low-temperature priming. Furthermore, CKL showed unique enrichment in pathways like ribosome (map03010), glycan degradation (map00511), lysine biosynthesis (map00300), sphingolipid metabolism (map00600), and methane metabolism (map00680) (Table S14), suggesting distinct metabolic characteristics and potential regulatory mechanisms.

Cold priming induces widespread upregulation of carbohydrate-active enzymes

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) mediate hydrolysis, modification, and cleavage of glycosidic bonds essential for plant biomass deconstruction. Notably, specific families (GH, CE, PL) function as cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs) that target structural polymers including pectin, cellulose, and hemicellulose [22, 23]. Among 1291 CAZymes annotated in the V. mali genome, differential expression analysis revealed significant induction under cold priming: 123 CAZyme genes were differentially expressed during low-temperature treatment (LF) (Fig. 7A), while 593 showed altered expression during combined cold priming and host infection (LL) (Fig. 7B). Strikingly, upregulated CAZymes substantially outnumbered downregulated genes in both conditions, with glycoside hydrolases (GHs) demonstrating particularly pronounced induction (66.15% and 66.13% upregulated during cold priming and cold-primed infection, respectively). This systematic upregulation of CAZymes, especially GH family members, underscores enhanced metabolic activity supporting mycelial proliferation and represents a key mechanism whereby cold priming potentiates pathogenicity.

Cold-induced signaling network coordinates virulence in Valsa mali

Integrative analysis identified 63 up-regulated core cold-responsive virulence determinants (Fig. 8). Among them, nine secreted glycoside hydrolases (GHs) consistently upregulated in low-temperature-primed mycelia (LF) and cold-primed infection (LL) (Fig. 8A), functionally enriched in plant cell wall degradation (e.g., β-galactosidase BGALE, endoglucanase GUN4) (Table S15). Additionally, 54 PHI-base virulence genes co-upregulated in LF/LL (Fig. 8B), comprising: (i) redox regulators (32.1%), including 12 short-chain dehydrogenases (RED1, SOL3) with conserved KR/Enoyl domains modulating NAD(P)H homeostasis; (ii) detoxification systems (24.5%) such as cytochrome P450 YANC and efflux pump FUS6; and (iii) stress shields (18.9%) like glyoxalase HSP31 mitigating cold-induced carbonyl stress (Table S15).

Candidate genes involved in the cold induction response of Valsa mali

Venn analysis of conserved transcriptional responses across LF, LL and CKL identified 136 consistently upregulated and 93 downregulated genes (Fig. 9). From these co-expressed gene sets, we selected 44 candidate DEGs exhibiting significant expression changes and functional annotations implicating their roles in cold adaptation and pathogenicity regulation (Fig. 10). These candidates were categorized into five functional classes (Table 1): (1) Cold Response (12 genes: lad, nagA, ssdh, crpe, eglC, fum7, eglC, erg-4, ard1, red1, sorD, gfa), involved in stress acclimation; (2) Plant Cell Wall Degradation (8 genes: gun4, exg1, xyls, iabf, bgalE, evm.TU.contig000005.1186, dcw1, gluB), encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes; (3) Signal Transduction (9 genes: Vmplc1, atr12, plc2, plc3, sho1, evm.TU.contig000002.912, gpa3, cyaA, gap1), including key phosphatases and G-proteins; (4) Toxins and Secondary Metabolites (5 genes: sat4, patB, yanC, lip3, alnD), and (5) Transcription/Translation & Transport/Metabolism (10 genes: C1327, lot6, PfmaH, klf1, mfsB, PKNOX2, glcT, fah12, evm.TU.contig000006.417, evm.TU.contig000006.871). This classification encompasses critical biological processes underpinning cold-induced pathogenicity enhancement.

Functional characterization of Vmplc1 in cold-primed pathogenicity of Valsa mali

Genome-wide screening of Valsa mali identified five putative phospholipase C (Vmplc) genes, with contig000002.788 (designated Vmplc1) emerging as the most structurally complete candidate, exhibiting all characteristic domains of PLC-δ isoforms - including PH (pleckstrin homology), EF-hand, and catalytic X/Y-C2 regions (Fig. S1). Targeted disruption of gene Vmplc1 revealed its specialized role in pathogenicity without affecting basal mycelial growth (Fig. 11A). Quantitative pathogenesis assays demonstrated that ΔVmplc1 mutants displayed markedly attenuated virulence, with 62% shorter lesion lengths and 58% reduced disease indices compared to wild-type Vm263 across both woody (branch) and foliar (leaf) infection models (Fig. 11B-C). The most compelling evidence emerged from cold-priming experiments: while pretreatment at 5 °C for 24 h typically enhances V. mali virulence, this thermal potentiation was completely abolished in ΔVmplc1 strains. Genetic complementation (ΔVmplc1-C) fully restored both baseline pathogenicity and cold responsiveness (Table 2), establishing Vmplc1 as a non-redundant regulator of temperature-modulated virulence. At the molecular level, Vmplc1 deficiency resulted in profound impairment in plant cell wall deconstruction, manifested through 70–85% suppression of key pectinolytic (pectinase) and cellulolytic (cellulase) gene expression during host penetration (Fig. S2A), corroborated by significantly reduced enzymatic activity on both pectin and carboxymethyl cellulose substrates in vitro and in vivo (Fig. S2B, S2C). These findings position Vmplc1 as a key coordinator of temperature-dependent virulence via CWDE regulation, providing new insights into cold-primed pathogenicity in this economically significant phytopathogen.

Discussion

Apple Valsa canker (AVC), a destructive perennial disease of apple [3], demonstrates marked seasonal prevalence coinciding with low-temperature periods [2]. Traditional models attribute AVC winter epidemics primarily to cold-impaired host defenses, which reduce tree vitality and disease resistance [9]. However, our previous research demonstrated that suboptimal temperatures (5–10 °C) intrinsically enhance V. mali fitness, significantly increasing both mycelial growth and pathogenicity in weakly virulent strains [10]. Critically, 5 °C induction for 18–24 h emerged as optimal for virulence potentiation.

To elucidate the molecular basis of this phenomenon, weakly pathogenic strain Vm263 was focused under standardized conditions: 24 h at 5 °C followed by 25 °C incubation. Low-temperature priming significantly accelerated mycelial growth and enhanced virulence, evidenced by 47–63% larger lesion areas on detached leaves compared to non-primed controls. These findings confirm that cold exposure directly potentiates V. mali pathogenicity, independent of host status, challenging the traditional host-centric explanations for AVC seasonality. While our earlier work established the phenotypic consequences of cold priming [10], this study provides deeper mechanistic insights through integrated transcriptomic and functional analyses.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) are fundamental virulence determinants in V. mali, facilitating host cell wall degradation through targeted hydrolysis of structural polysaccharides [1, 6, 23, 27]. Our study revealed that cold priming triggers extensive reprogramming of carbohydrate metabolism in V. mali, with significant enrichment of upregulated genes in carbohydrate metabolic process (GO:0005975), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), and hydrolase activity (GO:0016798) functional categories. This metabolic activation was further supported by KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, which highlighted pronounced induction of starch/sucrose metabolism (map00500) and pentose/glucuronate interconversions (map00040) following low-temperature exposure.

Crucially, CAZyme families exhibited asymmetric regulation under cold stress: 66% (642/973) of differentially expressed CAZymes were upregulated during cold priming. Glycoside hydrolases (GHs) gene family that are mostly key enzymes degrading cellulose and hemicellulose showed particularly strong induction, with upregulated genes outnumbering downregulated counterparts by more than 2:1 (e.g., a GH43 family member iabf was induced 15.2-fold, p < 0.001). This pattern contrasts sharply with nutrient-limited conditions, such as biotrophic competition, where CAZyme expression is predominantly suppressed [4]. The systemic upregulation of GHs, including gun4, exg1, xyls, iabf, bgalE and other CWDEs, provides a mechanistic explanation for enhanced pathogenicity, as it likely elevates the hydrolytic capacity for plant cell wall deconstruction, equipping V. mali with accelerated tissue-invasive capabilities under cold conditions.

Prioritizing Vmplc1 for functional characterization was based on a multi-tiered rationale derived from our transcriptomic and bioinformatic analyses. Vmplc1 was identified as one of the 63 core cold-responsive virulence determinants that were consistently upregulated during both cold priming (LF) and cold-primed infection (LL), positioning it within a central cohort of thermally-regulated genes. Within the signal transduction functional class of the 44 key candidate DEGs, Vmplc1 stood out due to its pronounced upregulation and its predicted function as a phospholipase C—a canonical signaling molecule known to transduce environmental cues in other eukaryotes [28, 29]. Most critically, comparative genomic analysis revealed that among the five Vmplc paralogs in V. mali, Vmplc1 possessed the most complete and canonical PLC-δ domain architecture [30], including intact PH, EF-hand, and catalytic X/Y-C2 domains, which are essential for its role in PIP2 hydrolysis and signal amplification [31]. This structural completeness suggested that Vmplc1 was the most likely candidate to serve as a primary signaling hub. The combination of its consistent induction under cold stress, its annotation as a key signaling component, and its superior structural integrity implied that Vmplc1 acts as a critical thermosensory switch, warranting in-depth functional investigation.

Transcriptomic analysis of cold-primed V. mali revealed that low-temperature induction triggers a synergistic expression cascade involving four glycoside hydrolases (GH3, GH5, GH7, GH28 families), two NAD(P)(H)-dependent short-chain dehydrogenases/reductases (SDRs), and phospholipase C gene Vmplc1. The induced GHs—notably polygalacturonase (gun4) and β-glucosidase (bgalE)—encode enzymes that systematically degrade pectin and cellulose, directly facilitating host tissue maceration [32, 33]. Concurrently, upregulated SDRs mediate critical redox balancing during host invasion, with orthologs in Magnaporthe oryzae demonstrated to regulate virulence-factor biosynthesis [34, 35]. Notably, Vmplc1 induction precedes maximal GH/SDR expression, suggesting a hierarchical regulation wherein Vmplc-mediated signaling activates CWDE transcription. Based on the canonical PLC signaling pathway, we propose a model in which low-temperature perception triggers Vmplc1 activation, leading to the generation of second messengers IP₃ and DAG. Subsequent DAG-activated Protein Kinase C (PKC) could then phosphorylate and activate specific transcription factors that bind to the promoters of key CWDE genes, such as gun4 and bgalE, thereby driving their expression.

Phospholipase C enzymes are pivotal signal transducers in eukaryotes, hydrolyzing phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate secondary messengers’ inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP₃) and diacylglycerol (DAG) [36, 37]. IP3 mobilizes intracellular Ca2+ stores, while DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC), collectively regulating stress responses, development, and virulence in fungi [38–40]. Fungal PLCs predominantly resemble the PLC-δ isoform of mammals [41], characterized by conserved pleckstrin homology (PH), EF-hand, and catalytic X/Y-C2 domains that facilitate membrane anchoring, calcium sensitivity, and substrate specificity [42]. This structural conservation underscores fundamental role of PLCs in environmental sensing and signal transduction [43], though functional diversification across species suggests lineage-specific adaptations to ecological niches.

Genomic analysis of Valsa mali strain Vm263 identified five Vmplc paralogs (Vmplc1 to Vmplc5). Among these, Vmplc1 possesses the most complete plc-δ architecture, including intact PH, EF-hand, X/Y catalytic, and C2 domains—a configuration optimized for PIP2 hydrolysis and signal amplification (Fig. S1). In contrast, Vmplc2–Vmplc5 display truncations in conserved regions such as the absence of PH domains or EF-hand motifs, suggesting potential functional divergence or compensatory redundancy within the gene family. This structural gradation mirrors observations in plants like rice and maize, where plc isoforms partition roles in development versus stress adaptation [29, 44, 45]. The complete domain architecture of Vmplc1 positions it as the primary candidate for thermosensory signaling in V. mali.

The orthologs of plc regulate growth in a species-specific manner. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Cryphonectria parasitica, PLC1 deletion impairs hyphal extension and sporulation [46–48], while Alternaria alternata PLC1 knockdown reduces biomass accumulation [49]. The process involving PLC1-derived inositol pyrophosphates (PP-IPs) is linked to stress responses in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, exhibiting pronounced temperature-sensitive regulation on growth, stress tolerance, sexual development, and virulence [28, 50]. Notably, ΔVmplc1 mutants in this study exhibited unaltered mycelial growth under low-temperature priming, reinforcing that PLC-dependent developmental regulation is not evolutionarily conserved. By contrast, recent studies on oomycete pathogens demonstrate that other phospholipase families, such as specifically small PLD-like proteins, directly potentiate pathogenicity through catalytic activity. Expression of these enzymes in planta enhances infection by modifying host membrane structures, facilitating nutrient exchange and accelerating disease progression [51]. This divergence may reflect niche-specific adaptations that pathogens like V. mali prioritize virulence plasticity over developmental robustness under thermal stress.

While dispensable for growth, Vmplc1 is indispensable for cold-primed pathogenicity. Knockout strains ΔVmplc1 showed 58–62% reductions (p < 0.05) in lesion formation on apple leaves/branches following 5 °C induction, with complementation restoring wild-type virulence. This phenotypic manifestation is consistent with a molecular cascade characterized by the suppression of cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs). Specifically, the expression levels of pivotal pectinolytic genes, namely xghA, pglr, and rgxB, along with cellulolytic genes gh43A and xvnD, exhibited a dramatic decline of 70–85% (p < 0.05) during the process of host penetration. This decline was accompanied by a concurrent reduction in pectinase and cellulase activities measured in vitro. Thus, Vmplc1 serves as a gatekeeper for the deployment of tissue-degrading enzymes under cold stress, a role distinct from growth-related plc functions observed in other fungi [52].

The functional specialization of Vmplc1 in thermosensing represents a distinct strategy among cold-responsive fungal pathogens. For instance, the snow mold fungus Typhula ishikariensis achieves freeze tolerance through the accumulation of antifreeze proteins and glycerol [53, 54], while the psychrotolerant pathogen Sclerotinia borealis relies on robust membrane fluidity maintenance [55]. In contrast, the regulation of virulence effectors in response to mild low temperature, as seen in Vmplc1-mediated CWDE induction, is a less common but ecologically critical adaptation. This mechanism is reminiscent of the rice blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae, where an acyl-CoA binding protein senses membrane lipid ordering to restrict host colonization to a specific thermal range [20, 56]. Our discovery of Vmplc1 adds a new dimension to this paradigm, highlighting a novel signaling protein—rather than a metabolic enzyme—as a central thermosensor for virulence regulation. This suggests that phytopathogens have evolved diverse, lineage-specific molecular solutions to the challenge of temperature variability, with Vmplc1 representing a key adaptation in the V. mali armory.

While our study firmly establishes the role of Vmplc1 and the associated transcriptional reprogramming in cold-primed virulence under controlled conditions, an important question remains regarding its consistency in whole-plant and field environments across diverse apple cultivars. Our laboratory findings provide a mechanistic basis for the well-documented field correlations between cold stress and AVC outbreak severity [2, 3, 10]. It is plausible that the cold-priming effect we describe contributes significantly to the “spring peak” of the disease, as the pathogen transitions from winter dormancy. However, the net disease outcome in the field is undoubtedly a complex interplay between the pathogen’s virulence, the host’s genotype-specific defense responses, and the broader environmental microbiome. Future research should prioritize in-field pathogenicity trials on whole trees of major commercial cultivars, including highly susceptible as well as tolerant varieties, to quantify the contribution of this temperature-sensing pathway to epidemic development. Such studies will be crucial for translating this molecular understanding into predictive models and cultivar-specific management strategies for AVC.

In conclusion, we propose a model wherein Vmplc1 acts as a thermosensory switch coupling low-temperature perception to virulence effector deployment in V. mali. Unlike plcs that govern basal growth in other fungi, Vmplc1 has functionally specialized to coordinate cold-induced pathogenicity—likely through PKC-mediated activation of CWDEs and stress shields. This niche adaptation highlights an evolutionary trade-off between developmental stability and pathogenic flexibility in phytopathogens. From an applied perspective, the identification of Vmplc1 as a master regulator of cold-induced virulence opens promising avenues for novel disease management strategies. Given its dispensability for fungal growth but critical role in temperature-dependent pathogenicity, Vmplc1 represents an ideal target for developing eco-friendly control agents. Specific inhibitors of Vmplc1 could potentially disrupt the disease cycle during the critical spring infection window without exerting a strong selective pressure for fungicide resistance, as they would not affect the pathogen’s general saprophytic survival. Furthermore, understanding this pathway could improve disease forecasting models by incorporating the molecular readiness of the pathogen population, in addition to host and weather data.

Materials and methods

Cultivation of V. mali Strain Vm263

The weakly pathogenic strain Vm263 was originally isolated from Valsa canker lesions on ‘Fuji’ apple (Malus domestica) in an orchard located in Baoding, Hebei Province, China (38°49’23” N, 115°26’34” E) [57]. Stock cultures were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 28 °C. The low-temperature priming procedure was performed as previously described by Meng et al. [10] with minor modifications. Briefly, actively growing mycelia from 72-hour cultures at 28 °C were subjected to temperature priming: parallel cultures were transferred to 5 °C (low-temperature induction) or 25 °C (control) and incubated for 24 h in darkness prior to pathogenicity assays and transcriptomic analysis.

Pathogenicity and mycelium growth rate assay

The pathogenicity assay and mycelial growth rate assessment were conducted following the established method of Meng et al. [10] with minor adaptations. Briefly, for mycelial growth rates, 7-mm agar plugs collected from temperature-primed cultures (5–25 °C, 24 h) were centrally inoculated onto fresh PDA plates (n = 5 replicates per treatment) and incubated at 25 °C. Colony diameters were measured at 24-hr intervals for 72 h. For pathogenicity assessment, identically prepared plugs were placed on adaxial surfaces of detached apple leaves (cv. ‘Fuji’) within humid chambers (100% RH, 25 °C). Lesion development was quantified after 7 days using disease severity indices and lesion area of apple leaves measurements calculated based on a previous method [10]. Both assays employed three independent biological replicates.

Sample preparation for transcriptome sequencing analysis

Four experimental groups were analyzed: (1) CKF: mycelia continuously cultured at 25 °C; (2) LF: mycelia cold-primed (5 °C, 24 h) then transferred to 25 °C for 24 h; (3) LL: apple leaves inoculated with LF mycelia for 72 h; (4) CKL: leaves inoculated with CKF mycelia for 72 h. Following established protocols for cold-priming and leaf inoculation, all samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 s of collection to preserve RNA integrity, then stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction [10]. Triplicate biological replicates were processed for each group. This specific four-group experimental design was developed for this study to distinctly separate the transcriptional effects of cold priming in the fungus (LF vs. CKF) from the effects during subsequent host infection (LL vs. CKL).

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co. (Shanghai, China, website link: https://www.majorbio.com/). Total RNA was isolated from twelve samples using TRIzol Reagent (Sangon Biotech, catalog no. B610409) followed by Recombinant DNase I (TaKaRa, catalog no. 2270 A) for genomic DNA removal, as previously described [6]. RNA integrity was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN > 8.0) and quantified via NanoDrop ND-2000 [6]. mRNA libraries were constructed with the TruSeq™ RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina), including cDNA synthesis, fragmentation (200–300 bp selection), and PCR amplification [6]. Final libraries were quality-controlled before paired-end 150 bp sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw data are deposited in GenBank (PRJNA1101711).

Bioinformatics processing of sequencing data

Raw reads were processed using SeqPrep (https://github.com/jstjohn/SeqPrep) and Sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) with default parameters to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases (Q < 20), and reads with > 10% ambiguous nucleotides (‘N’), yielding high-quality clean reads [58]. These were aligned to the V. mali Vm263 reference genome (BioProject PRJNA863899) using HISAT2 v2.1.0 (Version 2.1.0, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) [59]. Transcript abundance was quantified as FPKM values with RSEM v1.3.3 [60]. Functional annotation was performed against the following databases: NR (protein homology), eggNOG v5.0 (orthologous groups), KEGG v94.0 (pathways), GO (gene ontology via BLAST2GO v3.3), and Swiss-Prot v2020_06 (curated proteins) databases [61]. Complete annotation statistics are provided in Supplementary (Table S16-S17).

Differentially expressed genes

Gene expression was quantified as FPKM values using RSEM v1.3.3 [60]. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v1.24.0 under stringent thresholds: |log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) [62]. Primary comparisons included LF vs. CKF (cold-primed vs. control mycelia), LL vs. CKF (cold-primed infection vs. control), and CKL vs. CKF (ambient infection vs. control). DEGs were functionally characterized through: (1) GO enrichment via GOATools with Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05 [63]; (2) KEGG pathway enrichment using the R Phyper package (hypergeometric test, p < 0.05 Bonferroni-corrected) [64]. Disease-relevant genes were identified by screening DEGs against PHI-base [65] and CAZy databases [22].

qRT-PCR validation

Transcriptome-derived RNA aliquots from CKF, LF, CKL, and LL groups were used for validation. cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific™, catalog no. K1622, 100 reactions) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The 20 µL reaction system was set up using the kit’s included oligo(dT)18 Primer to selectively reverse transcribe polyadenylated mRNA. The relative expression of ten DEGs (Table S18) were analyzed with G6PDH as the endogenous control [66]. Reactions were performed in triplicate using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, catalog no. Q121-02, 500 reactions) in a 20 µL reaction volume according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with relative quantification via the 2−(ΔΔCt) method [67]. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined by Student’s T-test in SAS Version 9.4 (Web link: SAS Version 9.4 http://ftp.sas.com/techsup/download/hotfix/HF2/V62.html).

Generation of Vmplc1 knockout mutants

The Vmplc1 knockout mutants were generated using a homologous recombination strategy as described in a previous study [68]. Briefly, mycelia of the strain Vm263, cultured in PDB medium, were harvested using sterile gauze, dried between sterile filter papers, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Genomic DNA was extracted from the liquid nitrogen-ground mycelia using the CTAB method. Fragments flanking the Vmplc1 target region (upstream and downstream) and the hygromycin B resistance cassette (HYG) were amplified by PCR. The upstream and downstream fragments were amplified from Vm263 genomic DNA using primer pairs 1 F-2R and 3 F-4R, respectively, while the HYG cassette was amplified from the pUC-hyg plasmid template using primers HF-HR. The pUC19 vector was linearized by double digestion, purified by gel extraction (TIANGEN Gel Extraction Kit, catalog no. DP209), and used for seamless assembly of the knockout construct with the amplified fragments using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, catalog no. CU101). Positive clones harboring the correct Vmplc1 knockout construct were identified by sequencing. The full knockout cassette was amplified from the purified plasmid (TIANGEN Plasmid Mini Kit, catalog no. DP103) using primers CF/CR, and used for protoplast-mediated transformation of Vm263 via PEG. Putative knockout mutants were validated by PCR using genomic DNA extracted from both wild-type Vm263 and transformants. Primers mF/mR confirmed Vmplc1 deletion; hF/hR verified HYG cassette insertion; and primer pairs 5 F/hR and hF/6R (Table S19) specifically checked for homologous recombination at the upstream and downstream junctions, respectively.

Complementation of Vmplc1

Complemented mutants were generated based on the method previously reported with slightly modification [68]. The Vmplc1 gene, including its start codon and 1.5 kb upstream regulatory region, was amplified from Valsa mali Vm263 genomic DNA using primers Vmplc1-F/R. The fragment was cloned into HindIII/BamHI-linearized pGTN (harboring GFP and G418 resistance markers) using a homologous recombination-based ligation kit. Construct integrity (pGTN-Vmplc1) was verified by PCR with primers Vmplc1-F/R, using empty pGTN as control. The construct was introduced into ΔVmplc1 protoplasts via PEG-mediated transformation. Putative complemented transformants (ΔVmplc1-C) were selected on G418 and validated by PCR: amplification of Vmplc1 with primers 2.788 F/R and G418 with G418F/R (Table S19). GFP expression in ΔVmplc1-C was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, contrasting with WT and ΔVmplc1. Vmplc1 expression levels in WT, ΔVmplc1, and ΔVmplc1-C under identical conditions were analyzed by qRT-PCR.

Phenotypic analysis and pathogenicity assays

Colony morphology, mycelial growth rate as well as pathogenicity of wild-type (WT) strain and different mutant were evaluated using a previous method with minor modification [69]. Colony diameters and phenotypes were recorded at 24-h intervals for 72 h. For pathogenicity assays, WT and mutant strains underwent at 5–25 °C for 24 h, and temperature-primed mycelial plugs (7-mm) were inoculated onto detached apple branches (cv. ‘Fuji’) and leaves. Inoculated tissues were maintained in humid chambers (100% RH, 25 °C) for 4 days (branches) or 3 days (leaves). Lesion areas were quantified using established methods [10] and compared by ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD (P < 0.05), with three biological replicates per treatment.

Cell wall-degrading enzyme activity and gene expression

Cell wall-degrading enzyme activity and related gene expression were analyzed using in vitro and in planta approaches using a previous method with minor modification [70]. For in vitro induction, wild-type (WT) and ΔVmplc1 strains were cultured on PDA for 48 h. Three 7-mm mycelial plugs per strain were transferred to Czapek-Dox medium containing pectin or sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) as the sole carbon source, followed by incubation at 28 °C with shaking (150 rpm) for 36 h. Mycelia were removed by sterile muslin filtration, and culture filtrates were centrifuged (10000×g, 15 min, 4 °C). For in planta analysis, mycelial plugs were inoculated onto detached 2-year-old apple branches (3 branches per strain). After 3-day incubation at 25 °C, tissue samples (3 mm from lesion margins into healthy bark) were homogenized in ice-cold extraction buffer (0.2 g tissue/mL) and centrifuged (10000×g, 10 min, 4 °C). Supernatants from both approaches were assayed for pectinase (Thermo Fisher BC2630) and cellulase (Thermo Fisher BC2540) activities per manufacturer protocols. RNA extracted from in vitro-cultured mycelia was used for qRT-PCR quantification of Pectinase, Cellulase, and Hemi-cellulase gene expression (Table S18).
