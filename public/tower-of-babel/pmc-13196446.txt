Yellow fever epizootics among non-human primates in Huila, Colombia, 2025–2026
Authors: Ivan Camilo Sanchez-Rojas, D. Katterine Bonilla-Aldana, Catherin Lorena Solarte-Jimenez, Jorge Luis Bonilla-Aldana, Edward Suárez, Jose Luis Montealegre, Mario Suárez, Alfonso J. Rodriguez-Morales
Published: 2026

Yellow fever (YF) continues to re-emerge in South America over the last two years (now with 474 confirmed human cases and 204 deaths [43%], up to May 8, 2026) (https://shiny.paho-phe.org/yellowfever/), with increasing human cases and widespread epizootics in non-human primates (NHPs), which serve as key sentinels of viral circulation [1,2]. Monitoring wildlife mortality remains essential for early detection of viral activity and for timely public health interventions, and part of the One Health surveillance efforts [[3], [4], [5]].

Here, we report an epizootic event involving multiple wildlife species in the department of Huila, Colombia (Fig. 1), between April 2025 and March 2026, with laboratory-confirmed yellow fever virus (YFV) infection by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). During the YF outbreak in Colombia, 2024-2026, only 2 human cases have been confirmed in Huila. Up to May 12, 2026, Colombia have confirmed 192 human cases with 85 deaths (∼44%) (http://www.ins.gov.co).Fig. 1Location of the YF epizootics in Huila, Colombia, 2025-2026.

Between April 5, 2025, and March 9, 2026, thirteen animal cases associated with suspected epizootics were identified through environmental and veterinary surveillance systems. These events were distributed across several municipalities in their rural locations (veredas), including Aipe, Palermo, Neiva, Iquira, and Timaná, suggesting widespread sylvatic viral circulation (Fig. 1).

The affected animals included non-human primates from the genera Alouatta (howler monkeys) and Aotus (night monkeys), as well as Potos flavus (kinkajou) (Table 1). Most cases corresponded to sudden death events in NHPs, frequently without documented preceding clinical signs, consistent with the typical presentation of yellow fever epizootics in wildlife. Field observations and necropsy findings revealed a range of gross pathological features, including hepatic icterus, pulmonary petechiae, splenic fragility, and varying degrees of decomposition. Several cases were identified in advanced stages of decomposition or as skeletal remains, reflecting challenges inherent to field-based epizootic surveillance (Table 1).

Carcasses were recovered and processed according to national protocols, and tissue samples were collected for molecular analysis. Of the 13 animals evaluated, 9 tested positive for YFV (69%), and 4 were negative (31%). All confirmed positive cases (RT-PCR for YFV) corresponded to NHPs (100%), primarily Alouatta seniculus and Aotus spp., whereas all Potos flavus cases were negative (Table 1).

The temporal distribution of cases, spanning nearly one year, suggests sustained viral circulation rather than a single-point outbreak. An initial cluster of cases occurred between April and May 2025, predominantly affecting Alouatta seniculus, followed by additional detections in mid-2025 and a later case in early 2026 involving Aotus spp. (Table 1). This pattern supports the persistence of sylvatic transmission in the region.

The detection of YFV in Alouatta seniculus is consistent with its recognized role as a highly susceptible sentinel species [2,4]. The involvement of Aotus spp., including both positive and negative cases, further highlights the broader host range of YFV and suggests variable susceptibility or exposure among different primate taxa [2,4]. In contrast, the absence of YFV detection in Potos flavus across all evaluated cases suggests that this species may not play a significant role in the transmission cycle, although further investigation is warranted.

These findings reinforce the critical role of NHP epizootics as early warning signals of YFV circulation. Mortality events in primates may precede or occur independently of human cases, providing an opportunity for early detection and implementation of preventive measures such as targeted vaccination and vector control [1,2,4].

From a One Health perspective, this epizootic highlights the complex interactions among wildlife, vectors, and human populations. Environmental changes, including deforestation, habitat fragmentation, and increased human activity in forested areas, may facilitate viral transmission and increase the risk of spillover into human populations [1,3].

The prolonged detection of YFV-positive NHPs in Huila is epidemiologically relevant, particularly given the known circulation of the virus in neighboring regions. Strengthening integrated surveillance systems, including wildlife monitoring, laboratory diagnostics, and entomological surveillance, is essential to improve early detection and mitigate the risk of human outbreaks [1].

In conclusion, we report a prolonged epizootic of YFV affecting Alouatta seniculus and Aotus spp. in Huila, Colombia, between 2025 and 2026, with no evidence of infection in Potos flavus. The temporal distribution of cases over nearly one year suggests sustained sylvatic viral circulation rather than a short-lived outbreak, highlighting the persistence of transmission in this ecological setting. These findings reinforce the critical sentinel role of non-human primates, particularly highly susceptible species such as Alouatta, while also demonstrating the involvement of additional taxa such as Aotus, which may exhibit variable susceptibility or exposure [4].

The absence of YFV detection in Potos flavus across all evaluated cases provides additional insight into species-specific dynamics. It suggests that not all sympatric mammals participate equally in the transmission cycle. This observation is relevant for refining surveillance priorities and improving the interpretation of wildlife mortality events in endemic regions. Furthermore, the identification of both positive and negative cases within the same temporal and geographic framework underscores the importance of laboratory confirmation to characterize epizootics accurately [3,5].

From an epidemiological perspective, the extended duration and spatial distribution of cases across multiple municipalities underscore the need for continuous, geographically broad surveillance systems. Passive detection of wildlife mortality, combined with timely diagnostic confirmation, remains a cornerstone for early warning of YFV circulation. Such systems are particularly valuable in areas where human cases may be absent, delayed, or underreported, allowing public health authorities to implement preventive interventions proactively [1,2].

These findings also highlight the importance of integrating wildlife health data into a broader One Health framework. Environmental and ecological factors, including land-use changes, habitat fragmentation, and increased human interaction with forest ecosystems, may contribute to the maintenance and spread of YFV. Strengthening coordination between the veterinary, environmental, and public health sectors is therefore essential to improve outbreak detection, risk assessment, and response strategies [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

Overall, this report contributes to the growing body of evidence demonstrating that YFV epizootics in South America are complex, multi-species events that may persist over extended periods. Continued investment in integrated surveillance, field investigation capacity, and laboratory diagnostics will be critical to understand transmission dynamics better and to reduce the risk of spillover into human populations [4].
