Two-Variable Logic for Hierarchically Partitioned and Ordered Data
Oskar Fiuk, Emanuel Kieroński, Vincent Michielini \affiliations Institute of Computer Science, University of Wrocław

Abstract
We study Two-Variable First-Order Logic, FO 2 \mbox{\rm FO}^{2} , under semantic constraints that model hierarchically structured data. Our first logic extends FO 2 \mbox{\rm FO}^{2} with a linear order < < and a chain of increasingly coarser equivalence relations E 1 ⊆ E 2 ⊆ … E_{1}\subseteq E_{2}\subseteq\ldots . We show that its finite satisfiability problem is NExpTime -complete. We also demonstrate that a weaker variant of this logic without the linear order enjoys the exponential model property. Our second logic extends FO 2 \mbox{\rm FO}^{2} with a chain of nested total preorders ⪯ 1 ⊆ ⪯ 2 ⊆ … \preceq_{1}\subseteq\preceq_{2}\subseteq\ldots . We prove that its finite satisfiability problem is also NExpTime -complete. However, we show that the complexity increases to ExpSpace -complete once access to the successor relations of the preorders is allowed. Our last result is the undecidability of FO 2 \mbox{\rm FO}^{2} with two independent chains of nested equivalence relations.

Introduction

Hierarchically partitioned data are pervasive in modern computer systems.
For example, Geographical Information Services often organise geospatial information using progressively more detailed fields: country, region, state, and city.
Similar hierarchies appear in numerous contexts: Data Storage (organised into folders, subfolders, and files), Network Management (addressing schemes like IPv4/IPv6 with subnet hierarchies), Dependency Maintenance (tools for tracking dependencies between modules, libraries, and services).

To model such hierarchical data, we consider domains in which elements are annotated with data values drawn from potentially infinite or very large domains. A key aspect is that these data values can be tested for equality at multiple levels of precision. This is naturally captured by a family of increasingly coarser equivalence relations: two elements are related by the
k
-th equivalence relation if the
k
-th level equality test holds between their associated data values.

In this work, we establish results on the decidability and complexity of satisfiability problems for several variants of the Two-Variable Fragment of First-Order Logic,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, extended to support such increasingly coarser equivalence relations.
Our goal is to provide a logical framework that is expressive enough to model complex multi-level relationships while retaining desirable computational properties for reasoning. As most of the investigated logics do not enjoy the finite model property, their general and finite satisfiability problems differ. Our primary focus is on finite satisfiability, while the case of general satisfiability is left for future work.

The motivation for studying
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
stems from its good algorithmic and model-theoretic properties.
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
combines an
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem and the exponential model property
[
7
]
with a reasonable expressive power. In particular, it embeds (via the so-called
standard translation
) many modal, temporal, and description logics (up to
\cal{ALCIOH^{\cap,\neq}}
).
Also, it is the maximal, in terms of the number of variables, fragment of First-Order Logic with decidable satisfiability problem, as already the Three-Variable Fragment is undecidable
[
8
]
.
In the last few decades,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
together with its variations have been extensively studied, and plenty of results have been obtained in various scenarios (cf. Subsection
1.2
on related work). All of this makes
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
often the first-choice option for various reasoning tasks.

In the following subsections of this introduction, we define our logics and present the obtained results (Subsection
1.1
), discuss related work (Subsection
1.2
), compare the expressive power of our logics (Subsection
1.3
), and outline the technical sections that follow (Subsection
1.4
).

Logics of Interest and Our Results

Our underlying formalism is the Two-Variable Fragment of First-Order Logic,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, whose formulas may use only the variables
x
and

common

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}

Order on domain elements.

Let
{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}
denote the family of special symbols

whose interpretations are constrained to
nested
equivalence relations, that is for every

:
(i) the interpretation of

is an equivalence relation, and
(ii) the interpretation of
E_{k+1}
is
coarser
than that of
E_{k}
(i.e.,
xE_{k}y\rightarrow xE_{k+1}y
).
Let
<
be a special symbol interpreted as a
strict
linear order on domain elements.

Our first considered logic is
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[<,{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
, supporting both a linear order on elements and hierarchical data values.

Potential applications of
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[<,{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
can be found in, e.g., temporal verification of multiprocess systems.
We provide a motivational example of enforcing isolation policy in an environment of processes, containers, and events.

System execution is modeled as a linear sequence of events ordered by time using the relation
<
.
Events are generated by processes, and processes are grouped into containers.
The equivalence relations
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
capture this hierarchy:
xE_{1}y
holds when events
x
and
y
come from the same process, and
xE_{2}y
when they come from possibly distinct processes yet running in the same container.
These are naturally nested:
xE_{1}y\rightarrow xE_{2}y
.

Importantly, the domain of our model consists only of events: processes are implicitly represented as equivalence classes of the relation
E_{1}
.
That is, all events belonging to the same process form a single equivalence class of
E_{1}
. (We assume that every process reports at least one event, e.g.,
spawn event
, to ensure that it has a non-empty class.) Likewise, containers are implicitly represented as equivalence classes of the relation
E_{2}
. (We assume that in every container at least one process is running, e.g.,
root process
.)

Process-level properties (e.g.,
sandboxed
,
privileged
) are expressed via class-wise unary predicates. For example:

\forall x,y.~xE_{1}y\rightarrow\big(\mathrm{sandboxed}(x)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{sandboxed}(y)\big)

This ensures that all events from the same process share the same
sandboxed
or
not sandboxed
label, even though the logic only quantifies over events.

Using the above described encoding, we can impose the following isolation policy:

“A sandboxed process must not communicate with events outside its container unless an explicit grant was made before from a privileged process.”

This policy can be formalised with a sentence:

\displaystyle\forall x_{1}.~\big(\mathrm{sandboxed}(x_{1})\land\varphi_{\mathrm{cross\text{-}container\text{-}message}}(x_{1})\big)\rightarrow

\displaystyle~~~~\big(\exists y_{1}.~y_{1}<x_{1}\land x_{1}{E_{1}}y_{1}\wedge\varphi_{\mathrm{permission\text{-}grant}}(y_{1})\big),

where
\varphi_{\mathrm{cross\text{-}container\text{-}message}}(x_{1})
stands for the formula

\exists y_{2}.~x_{1}<y_{2}\land\neg x_{1}E_{2}y_{2}\land\mathrm{message}(x_{1},y_{2}),

and
\varphi_{\mathrm{permission\text{-}grant}}(y_{1})
for

\exists x_{3}.~\mathrm{privileged}(x_{3})\land x_{3}<y_{1}\land\mathrm{grant}(x_{3},y_{1}).

In the above formulas, we annotated variables as
x_{1}
,
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
,
x_{3}
for readability to reflect their roles in different processes.
However, only two variables
x
and
y
, with reuse across quantifiers, are sufficient to express this property.

As stated above, all variables refer to events:
x_{1}
is a
dispatch message
event from Process 1 (the
sender
) that initiates the communication, and
y_{2}
is a
deliver message
event from Process 2 (the
receiver
) that receives the message from Process 1, as indicated by
\mathrm{message}(x_{1},y_{2})
. The event
y_{1}
is an earlier event from the same process as
x_{1}
, representing the
grant acknowledge
event, marking the point at which Process 1 becomes aware of and is authorised to act on the granted permission. Finally,
x_{3}
is a
grant authorise
event from Process 3 (a
privileged admin
) that issues the permission and notifies Process 1, as indicated by
\mathrm{grant}(x_{3},y_{1})
.

The intended temporal order of events
x_{3}{<}y_{1}{<}x_{1}{<}y_{2}
is enforced:
the permission is issued before it is acknowledged (
x_{3}{<}y_{1}
);
the message is sent after the permission being acknowledged (
y_{1}{<}x_{1}
) and before being delivered (
x_{1}{<}y_{2}
).

Notice also that, to detect that Process 1 and Process 2 are running in distinct containers, we refer to their representative events using
\neg xE_{2}y
. Since
E_{1}
is nested within
E_{2}
, it follows that if two events are not
E_{2}
-related, then their respective
E_{1}
-classes (i.e., processes) must also belong to distinct
E_{2}
-classes (i.e., containers).

Order on data values.

We consider now a different way to incorporate a linear order into our scenario. We trade an order on domain elements for a family of nested linear orders on data values, i.e., on equivalence classes of
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
.

Let
{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}
denote the family of special symbols
\preceq_{1},\preceq_{2},\dots
whose interpretations are constrained to
nested
total preorders, that is for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
: (i) the interpretation of
\preceq_{k}
is a total preorder
3
3

3

A
total preorder
is a transitive relation
\preceq
such that, for every

\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
, supporting hierarchical data values that can be compared at multiple levels of granularity using less-than, equal, and greater-than comparison tests.

In
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
, we naturally keep nested equivalence relations
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
Their interpretation is now given by the
\preceq_{k}
-equivalent elements:
xE_{k}y\leftrightarrow x\preceq_{k}y\wedge y\preceq_{k}x
.

\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]

A representative case study is a system supporting atomic transactions.
Here, the elements of the structure represent low-level operations.
The finest preorder
\preceq_{1}
models the underlying timeline, where
\preceq_{1}
-equivalent elements are treated as occurring in parallel.
Transactions are modeled implicitly as
E_{2}
-equivalence classes, ordered chronologically by

Coarser preorders can be used to represent higher-level structures such as
\preceq_{3}
for commit order,
\preceq_{4}

With this encoding, we can express that, e.g., “
raised exceptions must be handled in the future, yet within the same transaction
” as follows:

\displaystyle\forall x.~\mathrm{exception}(x)\rightarrow\exists y.~x\prec_{1}y\land xE_{2}y\land\mathrm{handles}(y,x)

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]

Adding successors on data values.

We consider now a logic
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}_{\rm{succ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
that enriches the syntax of
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
by adding, for each
\preceq_{k}
-symbol, its induced successor predicate
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, defined as

\mathcal{S}_{k}(x,y):=x{\prec_{k}}y\land\forall z.~\big(x{\prec_{k}}z\rightarrow y{\preceq_{k}}z\big).

Applications of
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}_{\rm{succ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
naturally extend those of
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[{\preceq}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
; we continue with the transaction-based system scenario.
For example, we can express the property:

“If a transaction fails, then in the immediate
\preceq_{1}
-successor time-slot a rollback must occur, using the last available snapshot from the immediate
\preceq_{2}
-predecessor transaction.”
Formally:

\displaystyle\forall x.~\mathrm{fail}(x)\rightarrow\big(\exists y.~\mathrm{rollback}(y)\land\mathcal{S}_{1}(x,y)\land xE_{2}y\ \land

\displaystyle\quad\exists x.~\mathrm{last\text{-}snapshot}(x)\land\mathcal{S}_{2}(x,y)\land\mathrm{restore\text{-}to}(y,x)\big)

Here,
\mathcal{S}_{1}(x,y)\land xE_{2}y
ensures that the rollback follows the failure in the very next
\preceq_{1}
-time-slot within the same transaction,
while
\mathcal{S}_{2}(x,y)
ensures that the snapshot belongs to the transaction immediately preceding the one containing the rollback.
In particular, this guarantees that the snapshot was created before the failure.
Additional axioms can be imposed to refine the scenario further: for instance, we can define
\mathrm{last\text{-}snapshot}
as the
\preceq_{1}
-maximum snapshot within each
E_{2}
-class,
or require that after a rollback the system proceeds directly to the
\preceq_{2}
-successor transaction.

\forall x.~\exists y.~x{<}y
and
\forall x.~\exists y.~x{\prec_{1}}y
).
This contrasts with pure
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
which enjoys the
finite model property
(i.e., every satisfiable sentence has a finite model).
A natural question arises: Can nested equivalence relations alone enforce infinite models, or is this phenomenon solely due to the presence of linear orders?

Let
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
denote
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
extended with the family of nested equivalence relations
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
.
We answer that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
does indeed enjoy the finite model property:

Theorem 4
.

Satisfiable sentences of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
admit models of exponential size.
The satisfiability and finite satisfiability problems for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
coincide and are
NExpTime
-complete.

Undecidability.

A natural candidate to explore next is a logic supporting two independent families of nested equivalence relations
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
and
F_{1},F_{2},\dots
.
We prove that reasoning in such a logic is undecidable—even in a very restricted setting—when each family has length
2
and the vocabulary, except the four special equivalence symbols, is composed of unary predicates only.

Theorem 5
.

The satisfiability and finite satisfiability problems are undecidable for the constant-free, equality-free, monadic fragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}

extended with four special symbols
E_{1},E_{2},F_{1},F_{2}
,
interpreted as equivalence relations such that
E_{2}
is coarser than
E_{1}
and
F_{2}
is coarser than
F_{1}
.

1.2
Related Work

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with equivalence relations.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with a single equivalence relation has the exponential model property and an
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem
[
10
]
.
With two equivalence relations (not necessarily nested), the finite model property is lost; both satisfiability and finite satisfiability are 2
-NExpTime
-complete
[
9
]
.
With three equivalence relations,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is undecidable
[
10
]
.

Since
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
can express containment between equivalence relations, the work of
[
9
]
established decidability for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
over hierarchical partitions of depth two (with no ordering).
However, it does not yield optimal complexity bounds even in this restricted setting and leave the question of the finite model property unanswered.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with linear orders.

The satisfiability and finite satisfiability problems for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with a single linear order are
NExpTime
-complete
[
12
]
.
With three linear orders, both problems become undecidable
[
11
]
.
The case of two linear orders was studied in
[
19
]
, where finite satisfiability is shown to be decidable in 2
-NExpTime
when one linear order is accessible via both the order and successor predicates, and the other only via one of them. Decidability of the general satisfiability for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with two linear orders follows from
[
18
]
(up to our knowledge, the complexity of this problem has not been studied.)

Data Words.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with a linear order and an equivalence relation has been studied extensively in the context of
data words
.
These are structures interpreting unary predicates, an equivalence relation, a linear order, and its induced successor relation.
The satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
on data words is decidable but non-elementary-hard
[
3
]
.
When the successor relation is omitted, the problem becomes
NExpTime
-complete.

Quite close to our setting are data words with nested equivalences, that where considered in
[
2
]
.
It is shown there that satisfiability is decidable when the linear order is accessible only via its successor relation,
and becomes undecidable as soon as both the order and its successor are accessible.
The variant closest to
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[<,{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
, where only the linear order (and not its successor) is available, was not explored in that work.
Notice that data words restrict the common part of the signature to unary symbols, whereas we allow full
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with arbitrary binary relations that may freely interact with the equivalences.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with total preorders.

The
ExpSpace
-completeness of the finite satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
over structures with one total preorder, its induced successor relation, a linear order, and additional unary relations was established in
[
15
]
.
As in the case of data words, no common binary relations are permitted.
When two independent total preorders are available, satisfiability becomes undecidable.
To the best of our knowledge, the case of a single total preorder combined with arbitrary binary relations, as well as settings with nested total preorders, have not been investigated so far.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
over trees.

An alternative perspective on nested equivalence relations is to interpret them as trees.
If only
k
nested equivalence symbols
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
are considered, structures can be viewed as modeling the leaves of unranked trees of fixed depth
k
, where
E_{i}(a,b)
holds if
a
and
b
share a common ancestor at depth
k{-}i
.
Importantly, only leaf nodes constitute the domain; internal tree nodes serve an auxiliary role and are not part of the universe.
This interpretation is different from standard two-variable logics over trees, cf.
[
4
,
5
,
1
]
, where all tree nodes belong to the domain and the structure is accessed via navigational predicates such as
parent
,
child
,
descendant
, etc.

Other logics.

Nested equivalence relations can be simulated in description logics such as
\mathcal{SHI}
and
\mathcal{SHOI}
, which include transitive roles (
\mathcal{S}
), inverse roles (
\mathcal{I}
), role hierarchies (
\mathcal{H}
), and, possibly, nominals (
\mathcal{O}
).
These logics have
ExpTime
-complete satisfiability problems.
However, interactions between binary relations (roles) are limited to role hierarchies, meaning one can only express containment between relations (these can be equivalences or common relations). Moreover, these logics do not include linear orderings.

Among first-order fragments, the Unary Negation Fragment,
UNFO

[
17
]
, is particularly worth to mention.
This logic restricts negation to subformulas with at most one free variable.
Its extension capturing
\mathcal{SHOI}
, denoted
UNFO
+
\mathcal{SOH}
, is decidable and 2
-ExpTime
-complete
[
6
]
.
Remarkably,
UNFO
+
\mathcal{SOH}
enables reasoning over arbitrarily many independent families of nested equivalence relations.

2.1
Notation and Conventions

We denote the set of natural numbers including
0
by
{\mathbb{N}}
. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the notation
[k]
stands for the set
\{1,\dots,k\}
, with the convention that
[0]=\varnothing
. More generally, we use interval notation
[a,b]\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
to denote the set
\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
whenever
a\leq b
, and the empty set
\varnothing
whenever
a>b
.
If
E
is an equivalence relation on a set
A
, then
E[{a}]
denotes the equivalence class of an element
a\in A
, and
B/E
denotes the quotient set via
E
of a subset
B\subseteq A
.

A
signature

\sigma
is a finite set of symbols, partitioned as
\sigma={\rm{Cons}}\cup{\rm{Rels}}
, where
{\rm{Cons}}
is the set of constant symbols and
{\rm{Rels}}
is the set of relation symbols (including special symbols such as
<
,
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
, etc.). Every relation symbol has associated arity. We do not allow function symbols of positive arity. The
signature of a formula
is the finite set of relation and constant symbols that appear in the formula.

The
size
(or
length
) of a formula
\varphi
, denoted
|\varphi|
, is defined as the total number of symbols it contains, where each occurrence of a symbol—be it a variable, relation symbol, or constant—contributes
1
to the count.

We use Fraktur letters such as
{\mathfrak{A}},{\mathfrak{B}},\dots
to denote structures, and the corresponding Roman letters
A,B,\dots
for their domains.
A structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
over a signature
\sigma
interprets the symbols in
\sigma
: a relation symbol
R
as a relation
R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}\subseteq A^{k}
with
k
denoting the arity of
R
; and a constant symbol
c
as an element
c^{{\mathfrak{A}}}\in A
.
If
B\subseteq A
, we write
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!B
for the
restriction
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
to the subdomain
B
. The
size
of a structure is the cardinality of its domain.
Elements of structures are typically denoted by
a,b,\dots
; variables by
x,y
, possibly with decorations.
We write
\varphi(\bar{x})
to indicate that all free variables of the formula
\varphi
are contained in the tuple
\bar{x}
.

An (atomic)
1
-type
over a signature
\sigma
is a maximal consistent set of literals involving only the variable
x
.
Similarly, an (atomic)
2
-type
over
\sigma
is a maximal consistent set of literals over the variables
x
and
y
; in particular, a
2
-type naturally determines the
1
-types of both
x
and
y
.
We use the symbol
\alpha
(possibly with decorations) to denote
1
-types.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a structure. For any
a\in A
, we write
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a}]
to denote the unique
1
-type
realised
by
a
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
that is the
1
-type
\alpha
such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\alpha(a)
.
Similarly, for distinct elements
a,b\in A
, the notation
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a,b}]
denotes the unique
2
-type
realised
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
by the pair
(a,b)
.

2.2
Scott Normal Form

A sentence
\varphi
is in
Scott Normal Form

[
16
]
if it is in the shape of
\forall x,y.~\psi_{0}(x,y)\wedge\bigwedge_{m=1}^{M}{\forall x.~\exists y.~\psi_{m}(x,y)}
, where
M
denotes the number of Skolem conjuncts.
For
m\in[0,M]
, the formula
\psi_{m}(x,y)
is quantifier-free and constant-free, and uses only relation symbols of arity
1
and
2
.

There is a standard polynomial-time procedure transforming any
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
sentence into Scott Normal Form, preserving satisfiability over the same domains; see e.g.,
[
14
]
.
It is readily verified that it is sound for the extensions of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
introduced in Subsection
1.1
.

We standardise the special relation symbols in
\varphi
by assuming they are the consecutive symbols
E_{1},\dots,E_{K}
for some
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We do the same in the case of
\preceq_{k}
-symbols.

For convenience, we interpret additional symbol
E_{0}
as the identity relation, and
assume that
E_{K+1}
, the first symbol not used in
\varphi
, is interpreted as the universal relation. Note that this preserves nestedness:
E_{0}\subseteq E_{1}
and
E_{K}\subseteq E_{K+1}
.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

In this section, we establish our first main result.

See
1

For this whole section, we fix a sentence
\varphi
from the logic
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}$}[<,{\mathcal{EQ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
.
Assuming that
\varphi
is finitely satisfiable, we aim to prove that it is actually satisfied by a model of exponential size.
As discussed in Subsection
2.2
, we may assume that
\varphi
is in Scott Normal Form, i.e.,
in the shape of
\forall x,y.~\psi_{0}(x,y)\wedge\bigwedge_{m=1}^{M}{\forall x.~\exists y.~\psi_{m}(x,y)}
,
where
M
denotes the number of Skolem conjuncts.
By convention,
\varphi
uses the first
K
nested equivalence symbols
E_{1},\dots,E_{K}
; in addtion, we interpret
E_{0}
and
E_{K+1}
as the identity and universal relations, respectively.
Let
\alpha
denote the set of all
1
-types over the signature of
\varphi
.

The main ingredient of our proof is the following lemma, which allows us to replace a single equivalence class with its small counterpart, without touching the rest of the structure.

Lemma 6
.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a finite model of
\varphi
.
Fix
k\in[0,K]
, and let
\mathcal{C}
be an
E_{k+1}
-class in
A/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k+1}
.
Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
such that:

1.

\mathcal{D}
is the union of at most
12\cdot M^{3}\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}|
many
E_{k}
-classes from
\mathcal{C}/E_{k}^{{\mathfrak{A}}}
.

2.

\varphi
has a model
{\mathfrak{B}}
over the domain
B=(A\setminus\mathcal{C})\cup\mathcal{D}
.

We will prove Lemma
6
in a moment. Let us first demonstrate how to derive Theorem
1
from it.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Assume that
\varphi
is finitely satisfiable, and consider a finite model
{\mathfrak{A}}
of minimal size.

Because of this minimality, for each
k\in[0,K]
, every
E_{k+1}
-class of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is partitioned by at most
12\cdot M^{3}\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}|
many
E_{k}
-classes. As otherwise, Lemma
6
would give us a model of strictly smaller size. Since each
E_{0}
-class of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is of size
1
, an immediate induction tells us that, for each
k\in[K{+}1]
, the
E_{k}
-classes of
{\mathfrak{A}}
are of size at most
12^{k}\cdot M^{3k}\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}|^{k}
.

Yet,
E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{K{+}1}
having only one equivalence class, the size of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is therefore bounded by
12^{K+1}\cdot M^{3K+3}\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}|^{K+1}
. Clearly,
K
and
M
are both
\mathcal{O}(|\varphi|)
.
As
\varphi
does not use constants, the number of
1
-types in
\alpha
is
2^{|{\rm{Rels}}|}=2^{\mathcal{O}(|\varphi|)}
.

We conclude that the size of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is indeed
2^{\mathcal{O}(|\varphi|^{2})}
.
∎

We now prove Lemma
6
.

Suppose that
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a finite model of
\varphi
,
k
is a number between
0
and
K
, and
\mathcal{C}
is any
E_{k+1}
-class of
A/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k+1}
.

We assume w.l.o.g. that the domain
A
is a finite subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
and that the order
<^{{\mathfrak{A}}}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
coincides with the usual order on
{\mathbb{N}}
, allowing us to write just
<
instead of
<^{{\mathfrak{A}}}
.

We use the following terminology:
if
m\in[M]
and
a,b
are elements of
A
, then we call
b
a
witness
of
a
whenever
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi_{m}(a,b)
.
In addition,
b
is an
internal

(a,b)\in E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
; an
external

b\in A\setminus\mathcal{C}
and
(a,b)\not\in E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
; and a
\mathcal{C}
-witness
if
b\in\mathcal{C}
and
(a,b)\not\in E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
.

We fix

\mathfrak{f}_{m}\colon A\rightarrow A
, i.e., such that for each

and every

,
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi_{m}(a,\mathfrak{f}_{m}(a))
.

Before delving into the details, let us outline our proof strategy.
We construct the set

, to replace the selected
E_{k+1}
-class

, in three steps:
First, we define a subset
W_{\textup{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
that will serve as witnesses for elements outside
\mathcal{C}
.
Next, we set
W_{\textup{II}}:=\bigcup_{m\in[M]}\mathfrak{f}_{m}[W_{\textup{I}}]
,
which will provide witnesses for elements in
W_{\textup{I}}
.
Similarly, we define

W_{\textup{III}}:=\bigcup_{m\in[M]}\mathfrak{f}_{m}[W_{\textup{II}}]

to serve as witnesses for elements in

.
It will be enough to define

-closure of
(W_{\textup{I}}\cup W_{\textup{II}}\cup W_{\textup{III}})\cap\mathcal{C}
. Finally, the structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
will be defined as

but with certain

In order to replace the original

-witnesses by other elements, we use the following notion of

Let

and

.
Assume that

\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\rangle

We say that they realise the same

(ii)

For every

if and only if

”, “

” or “

(iii)

For every

and every

if and only if
(a,c_{i})\in E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{j}
.

We define now the set

.
For this, we introduce the notion of the

subset of a finite subset

, of

For each
1
-type

of the
M

We then define

as the set of

smallest and

In the following claim, we justify that any configuration of
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses can be realised within

.

Claim 7
.

Let
\ell\in[M]
, and let

. Suppose that

are distinct elements of
\mathcal{C}\setminus E^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}_{{k}}[{a}]
.
Then there exist distinct elements

in

such that

and
\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\rangle

-configuration.

Proof.

We can partition the

’s into several subtuples by the

-types that these elements realise.
Considering these tuples independently, we obtain several tuples of the

’s to be glued together into a final tuple.
Hence, w.l.o.g. assume that all the
b_{i}
’s realise the same
1
-type
\alpha
, for some
\alpha\in\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}
.

Similarly, we can moreover assume that the
b_{i}
’s are related to

in the same way, say
b_{i}<a
holds for every
i\in[\ell]
.

Consider the set
S:=\{c\in\mathcal{C}\mid c<a\text{ and }{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{c}]=\alpha\}

Notice that
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}\}\subseteq S\setminus E^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}_{{k}}[{a}]
.
Moreover, any
\ell
-tuple of distinct element from

-configuration as the

’s.
Hence, select
\ell

from the set

Because

, we have that

.
Now, crucially among the

, in addition to the elements

, there are at most

additional “

In consequence, we have that

.
∎

Naturally, elements of

require witnesses themselves, which is why, as announced above, we define

.
Similarly, elements of

need witnesses, so we define

We do not introduce further sets

can be assigned

-witnesses again in

.
This step resembles the construction of the exponential model property for

by Grädel, Kolaitis, and Vardi

, where three sets of elements provide witnesses for each other in a circular fashion.
Our setting is slightly more intricate, but the general idea remains:

-witnesses for

,

for

, and, to close the cycle, we adjust the

-witnesses for

Moreover,

needs to provide

-witnesses for elements outside

We ensure all of this in the subsequent steps of the proof.

We define the set

(from the statement of Lemma

\mathcal{D}:=\bigcup_{a\in(W_{\textup{I}}\cup W_{\textup{II}}\cup W_{\textup{III}})\cap\mathcal{C}}E^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}_{{k}}[{a}].

This choice of

Claim 8

The set

is a union of at most
12\cdot M^{3}\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}|
many

-classes of
\mathcal{C}/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}

Proof.

We have that

|W_{\textup{II}}|\leq M\cdot|W_{\textup{I}}|

|W_{\textup{III}}|\leq M\cdot|W_{\textup{II}}|

Therefore, each of the sets

We conclude, as

∎

To conclude this proof, it remains to establish also the second item of Lemma

V_{q{+}1}
;

2.

for each
m\in[M]
and every
a,b\in W^{\mathrm{loc}}[p{-}1,p]
,
we have that
b=f_{m}(a)
iff
\rho(b)=f_{m}(\rho(a))
.

This setup of canonical isomorphisms is tailored for the subsequent reduction step:
If
W^{\mathrm{loc}}[p{-}1,p]
and
W^{\mathrm{loc}}[q,q{+}1]
are canonically isomorphic,
then, after removing all
E_{k}
-classes
\mathcal{E}_{p},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{q}
,
the classes
\mathcal{E}_{p-1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{q+1}
become consecutive.
In this case, the canonical isomorphism allows us to naturally
glue
together
the local witnessing configurations of
[p{-}1,p]
and
[q,q{+}1]

to obtain a new local witnessing block
[p{-}1,q{+}1]
.

With the above intuitions in mind,
we introduce the notion of reducible pairs pinpointing
segments of consecutive
E_{k}
-classes that may be safely removed
while still preserving the satisfaction of
\varphi
.
Formally, a pair
(p,q)
of integers with
1<p\leq q<L
is
reducible
if:

•

For every
i\in[p{-}1,\,q{+}1]
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{i}\cap W^{\mathrm{rem}}=\varnothing
.

•

The substructures of
{\mathfrak{A}}
induced by the sets
W^{\mathrm{loc}}[p{-}1,p]
and
W^{\mathrm{loc}}[q,q{+}1]
are canonically isomorphic.

Lemma 21
.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a finite model of
\varphi
. Fix
k\in[0,K]
,
and let
\mathcal{C}\in A/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k+1}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{1}\prec^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}\dots\prec^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}\mathcal{E}_{L}
are precisely the
E_{k}
-classes of
\mathcal{C}/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
.
Assume that the sets
W^{\mathrm{rem}}
and

W^{\mathrm{loc}}[1,2],\dots,W^{\mathrm{loc}}[L{-}1,L]

have been defined as above.
If
(p,q)
is a reducible pair for some
1<p\leq q<L
,
then there exists a model
{\mathfrak{B}}
of
\varphi
with domain

B\;=\;A\setminus\bigcup_{i\in[p,q]}\mathcal{E}_{i}.

Proof.

Let initially
{\mathfrak{B}}
denote the structure
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!B
.
In this structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
,
\mathcal{E}_{p-1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{q+1}
are now adjacent
E_{k}
-classes.
Since
(p,q)
is assumed to form a reducible pair, the sets
W^{\rm loc}[p{-}1,p]
and
W^{\rm loc}[q,q{+}1]
are canonically isomorphic.
Therefore, it makes sense to speak about
W^{\rm loc}[p{-}1,q{+}1]
, defined in a natural way:

\big(W^{\rm loc}[p{-}1,p]\cap\mathcal{E}_{p{-}1}\big)\cup\big(W^{\rm loc}[q,q{+}1]\cap\mathcal{E}_{q{+}1}\big).

We also adjust the structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
so that
{\mathfrak{B}}~\!\!\restriction\!\!~W^{\rm loc}[p{-}1,q{+}1]
is isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{A}}~\!\!\restriction\!\!~W^{\rm loc}[p{-}1,p]
in an obvious way.

Now, we adjust some
2
-types in the so-modified structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
so that it provides replacements for all witnesses originally provided in
\bigcup_{i\in[p,q]}\mathcal{E}_{i}
.
This is similar to the final part of the proof of Lemma
6
(i.e., the part in which we construct a sequence of structures
{\mathfrak{B}}_{0},{\mathfrak{B}}_{1},\dots,{\mathfrak{B}}_{N}
).

For each element of
a\in B\setminus(W^{\rm rem}_{\textup{I}}\cup W^{\rm rem}_{\textup{II}})
,
we search for its remote witnesses in the set
W^{\rm rem}_{\textup{I}}
using Claim
16
.
Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}
be the sequence of all remote
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses of
a
, where
\ell\leq M
.
Using Claim
16
, we find new elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\in W^{\rm rem}_{\textup{I}}
realising the same
a
-configuration.
We set
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{a,c_{i}}]:={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a,b_{i}}]
for every
i\in[\ell]
.

Having remote witnesses secured, we now search for the replacements of local witnesses for elements in
\mathcal{E}_{p-1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{q+1}
.

Fix an element
a\in\mathcal{E}_{p-1}
whose local
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses are possibly missing in
{\mathfrak{B}}
, i.e.,
a
is from the set

\mathcal{E}_{p-1}\setminus\big(W^{\rm loc}_{\textup{I}}[p{-}1,p]\cup W^{\rm loc}_{\textup{II}}[p{-}1,p]\big).

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}
be the sequence of all local
\mathcal{C}
-witness of
a
, where
\ell\leq M
, that were originally contained in the set
\mathcal{E}_{p}
.
That is,
b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}
are the distinct elements of the set

\big\{\mathfrak{f}_{m}(a)\mid m\in[M]\big\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{p}.

Using Claim
18
, we find new elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\in V_{q+1}
realising the same
a
-configuration as
b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}
.
We set
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{a,c_{i}}]:={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a,b_{i}}]
for every
i\in[\ell]
.

Notice that local
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses of
a
that were originally in
\mathcal{E}_{p-2}
remain present also in
{\mathfrak{B}}
.

In a symmetric way, we provide local
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses for elements in the set

\mathcal{E}_{q{+}1}\setminus\big(W^{\rm loc}_{\textup{I}}[q,q{+}1]\cup W^{\rm loc}_{\textup{II}}[q,q{+}1]\big).

Therefore, at this moment, every element of
{\mathfrak{B}}
has all of its local and remote
\mathcal{C}
-witnesses secured as well as internal and external witnesses.

Finally, we adjust the
2
-types between
\mathcal{E}_{p-1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{q+1}
that were not modified in any of the previous steps.
This is necessary, as these
2
-types were originally connecting elements that were not
\mathcal{S}_{k}
-successive, yet now they are.

Fix
a\in\mathcal{E}_{p-1}
and
b\in\mathcal{E}_{q+1}
such that the
2
-type of
(a,b)
is unmodified, i.e., as in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Using Claim
19
and the fact that
{\mathfrak{A}}~\!\!\restriction\!\!~V_{p}
and
{\mathfrak{A}}~\!\!\restriction\!\!~V_{q+1}
are isomorphic, we can find
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
of the same
1
-type as

.
We set
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{a,b}]:={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a,b^{\prime}}]
.

The construction of the model
{\mathfrak{B}}
for
\varphi
is finished.
∎

We now show that long enough sequences of
E_{k}
-classes
must necessarily contain a reducible pair.

Lemma 22
.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a finite model of
\varphi
, let
k\in[0,K]
, and let
\mathcal{C}\in A/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k+1}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{1}\prec^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}\dots\prec^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}\mathcal{E}_{L}
are precisely the
E_{k}
-classes of
\mathcal{C}/E^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
.
Assume that the sets
W^{\mathrm{rem}}
and
W^{\mathrm{loc}}[1,2],\dots,W^{\mathrm{loc}}[L{-}1,L]
are defined as above.
If

L\;\geq\;c\cdot|W^{\mathrm{rem}}|\cdot 2^{n^{2}\times(|{\rm{Rels}}|+M)},

where
c>0
is a constant independent from
\varphi
, and

n=\max_{i\in[L{-}1]}\big|W^{\mathrm{loc}}[i,i{+}1]\big|

and
{\rm{Rels}}\text{ is the set of relation symbols of }\varphi
,
then there exists a reducible pair
(p,q)
with
1<p\leq q<L
.

Proof.

This lemma is essentially the “pumping lemma” for regular languages.

First we notice that
2^{n^{2}\cdot|{\rm{Rels}}|}
bounds the number of distinct structures over a domain of cardinality
n
;
recall that
\varphi
is in Scott Normal Form, and thus it does not use constants and mentions only relation symbols of arity
1
and
2
.
Likewise,
2^{n^{2}\cdot M}
bounds the number of possible configurations of witnessing functions.

Therefore, if
L
exceeds the threshold
2^{n^{2}\times(|{\rm{Rels}}|+M)}
, necessary canonically isomorphic structures
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!W^{\rm loc}[i,i{+}1]
exist for at least two distinct positions
i\in[L-1]
, say,
i:=p{-}1
and
i:=q
. Assume that
1<p\leq q<L
.

Moreover, if this threshold is additionally multiplied by
c\cdot|W^{\rm rem}|
, for a large enough constant
c>0
, we can choose
p
and
q
so that
\bigcup_{i\in[p-1,q+1]}{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
is disjoint from
W^{\rm rem}
.

Consequently,
(p,q)
is a reducible pair.
∎

Appendix C
Details on the
ExpSpace
Lower Bound

In Section
5
, we sketched the proof of the
ExpSpace
lower bound for the finite satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\preceq}_{\rm{succ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
.
Here, we provide some additional details.
In fact, the result holds already for a highly restricted fragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
[{\preceq}_{\rm{succ}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\displaystyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.35284pt}{\resizebox{582267}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.29828pt}{$\textstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06897pt}{\resizebox{640094}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.66624pt}{$\scriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.06898pt}{\resizebox{735837}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.47589pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subseteq$}}}}}}]
:

Proposition 23
.

The finite satisfiability problem is
ExpSpace
-hard
for the constant-free, equality-free, monadic fragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}

extended with a single total preorder relation
\preceq_{1}
and its induced successor
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

The reduction is from the
corridor tiling problem
, which is known to be
ExpSpace
-complete.
An instance of this problem is a tuple
\mathcal{T}=\langle\mathcal{C},c_{0},c_{1},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V},n\rangle
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a non-empty finite set of
colours
,

c_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
is the
initial
colour,
c_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
is the
final
colour,
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
are the sets of
horizontal
and
vertical
constraints, and
n
is a natural number given in unary, respectively.
Then the instance
\mathcal{T}
is
solvable
if there exists
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a
tiling
function
f\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{C}
of a grid
\mathcal{G}:=[0,2^{n}{-}1]\times[0,m{-}1]
such that:

•

the initial and final conditions hold:
f(0,0)=c_{0}
and
f(2^{n}{-}1,m{-}1)=c_{1}
;

•

the horizontal constraints hold: for all
i\in[0,2^{n}{-}2],j\in[0,m{-}1]
, we have
\langle f(i,j),f(i{+}1,j)\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
;

•

the vertical constraints hold: for all
i\in[0,2^{n}{-}1],j\in[0,m{-}2]
, we have
\langle f(i,j),f(i,j{+}1)\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
.

Finally, the problem is to decide if the instance is solvable.

In the following, given a corridor tiling instance
\mathcal{T}
,
we construct a formula
\Theta
that is (finitely) satisfiable if and only if
\mathcal{T}
is solvable.

We introduce
n
unary predicates
B_{j}
for
0\leq j<n
,
together with a unary predicate
P_{c}
for each colour
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

The predicates
B_{j}
serve as bits, allowing us to associate with each element
a number between
0
and
2^{n}-1
in the natural binary encoding.
We write
\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}}(x,y)
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{succ}}(x,y)
for standard (polynomial-size in
n
) formulas asserting that
the numbers assigned to
x
and
y
are equal, respectively successive.
Similarly,
\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}off}}(x)
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}on}}(x)
are abbreviations for formulas
stating that all the
B_{j}
are false (so
x
encodes
0
), and, respectively, all the
B_{j}
are true
(so
x
encodes
2^{n}{-}1
).

The intended encoding is as follows.
Domain elements correspond to individual grid cells.
The predicates
B_{j}
determine the horizontal coordinate,
while each
E_{1}
-equivalence class represents an entire row.
Within a row, the predicate
P_{c}
marks the colour assigned to the corresponding grid cell.
The successor relation
\mathcal{S}_{1}
connects consecutive
E_{1}
-classes (i.e.,
\preceq_{1}
-equivalent elements),
thereby linking each row to its immediate vertical successor.
In this way, the structure naturally represents a grid of width
2^{n}
and height
m
.
The reduction is enforced by a collection of formulas ensuring that the structure encodes a valid tiling.

Grid formation.

Every row must contain the leftmost element (position
0
), and all elements except the last must have a successor,
ensuring that each row forms a complete horizontal sequence of width
2^{n}
:

\forall x.~\exists y.~\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}off}}(y)\wedge xE_{1}y

\forall x.~\neg\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}on}}(x)\rightarrow\exists y.~\varphi_{\mathrm{succ}}(x,y)\wedge xE_{1}y

Boundary conditions.

The leftmost cell of the first row is coloured with
c_{0}
, and
the rightmost cell of the last row is coloured with
c_{1}
:

\forall x.~\big(\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}off}}(x)\wedge\neg\exists y.~\mathcal{S}_{1}(y,x)\big)\rightarrow P_{c_{0}}(x)

\forall x.~\big(\varphi_{\mathrm{all\text{-}on}}(x)\wedge\neg\exists y.~\mathcal{S}_{1}(x,y)\big)\rightarrow P_{c_{1}}(x)

Horizontal consistency.

Within a row, horizontally aligned positions must agree on their colour,
and successive horizontal positions must satisfy the horizontal constraints
\mathcal{H}
:

\forall x,y.~\big(xE_{1}y\wedge\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}}(x,y)\big)\rightarrow\bigwedge_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\big(P_{c}(x)\leftrightarrow P_{c}(y)\big)

\forall x,y.~\big(xE_{1}y\wedge\varphi_{\mathrm{succ}}(x,y)\big)\rightarrow\bigvee_{(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}}\big(P_{c}(x)\land P_{c^{\prime}}(y)\big)

Vertical consistency.

Finally, consecutive rows are linked via
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
and corresponding positions must respect the vertical constraints
\mathcal{V}
:

\forall x,y.~\big(\mathcal{S}_{1}(x,y)\wedge\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}}(x,y)\big)\rightarrow\bigvee_{(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}}\big(P_{c}(x)\land P_{c^{\prime}}(y)\big)

Together, these formulas force any finite model of
\Theta
to encode exactly a valid tiling of a
2^{n}\times m
grid, for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
satisfying both boundary and propagation conditions.
This completes the reduction.
