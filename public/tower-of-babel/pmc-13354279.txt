Psoriasis Area and Severity Index Training via Virtual Reality Application: New Way of Effective Teaching
Authors: Sascha Gerd Decker, Falk Ochsendorf, Michael Gairing, Sebastian Kühne, Anke Koenig, Bastian Schilling, Julia-Tatjana Maul, Andreas Pinter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystemic treatment decisions for psoriasis are often initiated and guided depending on the severity of the disease, determined by the validated Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI). Therefore, accurate evaluation of disease severity is crucial. However, exact and reproducible PASI assessment requires training. A virtual reality (VR)-PASI program could improve empowerment of healthcare providers and grading accuracy. The aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness of a VR-PASI training program to standard PASI teaching.MethodsIn a randomised prospective trial, a classical lecture and a VR program were compared in 70 medical students (study participants) without previous knowledge and training in skin assessment. Effectiveness of training was measured by determining a PASI score in real patients and comparing the results to those of a trained assessor. Furthermore, the rating of quality and satisfaction of both training methods was measured in both educational groups.ResultsBoth groups were equally interested in dermatology, all study participants had little to no experience with PASI, and both groups had similarly little to no experience in dealing with VR. VR-trained PASI raters achieved better results assessing correct PASI severity compared to those trained via classical lecture (U = 397.5, p = 0.011). The effect size, calculated using Rosenthal’s method, was R = 0.302 (moderate effect). Median difference of PASI score was 1.5 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.3–2.4). Median difference scores of classical lecture group (PASI Δ: 2.7) was less accurate than in VR group (PASI Δ: 1.1), indicating a potentially clinically relevant effect for median difference (1.5 (95% CI: 0.3–2.4). VR group showed higher accuracy in the application of the PASI and reported both higher satisfaction with the learning unit and a better experience in implementing the teaching method.ConclusionIn untrained raters, PASI training via the VR program led to higher accuracy in severity assessment compared to a classical lecture.

Introduction

Psoriasis (PSO) is a chronic immune-mediated skin disease characterised by systemic immune activation and excessive cell proliferation of keratinocytes. It affects around 2–3% of the world’s population and typically manifests as erythematous, scaly plaques on the skin, which are itchy and can lead to considerable impairment of quality of life and psychological stress [1]. PSO is also a systemic disease associated with an increased risk for comorbidity affecting the cardiovascular system, joints, intestine, liver, eyes, or mental wellbeing [2]. The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score is a worldwide established and validated instrument in dermatology to assess the severity of skin symptoms in psoriasis patients and was first introduced in 1978 [3]. In addition to the PASI, further validated scores for psoriasis severity measurement include the body surface area, Physicians Global Assessment (PGA) and, in terms of quality of life, the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI). The PASI score ranges from 0 to 72 and correlates positively with disease severity. PSO is considered moderate-to-severe when the PASI score is 10 or higher, suggesting systemic treatment. Treatment eligibility as well as treatment success is mainly determined by repetitive measurement of PASI and its percentual reduction in relation to a start severity value (e.g., PASI75, PASI90) or absolute reduction (e.g., a PASI of <2). Accuracy and reliability of PASI assessment are therefore crucial for a modern management of psoriasis.

Digital teaching models became more widely available as a result of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. In recent years, digitalisation and technological advances have transformed knowledge delivery, offering an alternative to traditional teaching models. Virtual reality (VR) and augmented realty (AR) are at the forefront of this development and offer innovative opportunities as they are independent of time and location, can be standardised, and are interactive. Speed and level of education can be adapted individually to the user. VR immerses learners in simulations where they make decisions and get instant feedback, applying the learning by doing approach. Compared to passive formats such as lectures or videos, VR could promote deeper cognitive processing [4], potentially leading to improved memory retention and more realistic preparation for tasks such as interacting with patients with PSO. Additionally, features like gamification and real-time feedback, implemented in PASI training, could enhance motivation and accessibility, allowing flexible, location-independent learning experiences. Globally, digitalisation of medical education is seen as the key to improve and democratise learning and adapting to the requirements of a rapidly changing healthcare landscape. Nevertheless, the quality of a VR learning method has never been compared to a standard teaching method.

In this study, we investigated whether the use of a VR learning application as a didactic tool can help improve the learning effects when teaching the PASI compared to a traditional teaching method.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study compared the learning outcomes of participants who were trained to determine the PASI score for assessing the skin condition of plaque psoriasis patients using two different methods. The study population consisted of a total of 70 voluntary medical students, randomised into two group receiving a classical lecture training (group A; n = 35) or VR training (group B; n = 35). Inclusion criteria were male or female subjects ≥ 18 years of age, enrolment in the medical school, i.e., successfully completed preclinical training, as well as understanding of the scope of participation or full ability to give consent. Study participants with a pre-knowledge of PASI assessment were excluded. Both groups first received a 15-min introductory video on PSO, the symptoms, and the subsequent introduction to the PASI score, which served as a baseline introduction to this trial. Study participants received afterwards a 15 min PASI training depending on randomisation group. A standard PASI score method was used to evaluate psoriasis severity across head, trunk, arms, and legs, based on erythema, plaque thickness, scaling (rated 0–4), and the percentage of affected area in each region.

VR-PASI Program

Study participants first familiarised themselves for 3 min with the feeling of being in a virtual space and the handling of the VR glasses. The PASI program utilises a male and a female avatar representing moderate-to-severe forms of PSO. The VR program sequentially guides users through the predefined steps of the PASI assessment. Visual feedback has been given for correct and incorrect answers. Upon PASI completion, the program provides a detailed feedback report displaying the individual PASI scores for each body region as well as the total PASI score. Training Group and Training Procedure

All study participants had no prior knowledge in dermatology or about PSO. Questionnaires answered prior to VR and lecture evaluated baseline knowledge of affinity for technology (measured via VAS scale), PASI assessment, experience with VR, and general interest in dermatology (entirely measured via 5-point Likert scale). Participants randomised into the lecture group watched a standardised 15-min lecture explaining how to assess PASI severity in moderate-to-severe PSO assessment and demonstrating this in 2 different patients. Study participants who have been randomised to the VR-PASI assessment were trained via VR program in a 15-min immersive environment using VR goggles and joysticks to interact with a virtual PSO avatar. Assessment was trained like the lecture group on 2 different virtual patients. Examples of both virtual patients are shown in Figure 1.

After the training, individuals from both groups independently assessed the skin condition of a real PSO patient using the previously learnt PASI score. A physician’s reference assessment of the same rated patient was already available and served as a comparison with the study participants’ result for statistical analysis. Another questionnaire about the course was completed afterwards, which assessed aspects such as perceived benefit, interest, and duration of the session, and was measured via 5-point Likert scale.

Statistical Analysis

The null hypothesis was that there was no difference in the PASI scoring between the two teaching modalities measured as difference between a student and an expert assessment of a PASI score of a real patient. Comparisons were made by Mann-Whitney test to compare the variable delta between these two groups, Hodges-Lehmann estimator was used, as well as effect size according to Rosenthal. The results of the entire questionnaires from each study group were evaluated using PRISM. This study was approved by Local Ethical Committee and performed regarding good clinical practice.

Results

Comparison of the Groups

There was no difference between the two groups with respect to age, sex, or interests.

Assessment of PSO Severity

Overall, there was a higher standard in this group. In the VR group, differences between study participants and physicians were smaller (Fig. 2). PASI assessment scores were performed by each participant in both groups (lecture and VR) and compared with reference scores provided by a corresponding physician (Fig. 2a). Lecture group participants showed higher delta values (median 2.7) and a larger standard deviation between scores (max. 10.6). In contrast, the delta values in the VR group were lower (median 1.1; Fig. 2) and distribution was lower (max. 6.6). Overall difference in correction of PASI assessment in the VR group are smaller compared to the lecture group (U = 397.5, p = 0.011 (exact), R = 0.302). Median PASI Δ score of classical lecture group (2.7) was higher than in the VR group (1.1). This indicates a potentially clinically relevant effect (median difference 1.5, 95% CI: 0.3-2.4 (Fig. 2b).

Rating of Training Experience

Affinity for technology was determined via a VAS assessment (0–100). There was no significant difference (lecture group median 68, VR group median 75) (Fig. 3a). Seven questionnaire items were compared between lecture and VR group, regarding benefit, length, enthusiasm, experience, interest, prior PASI experience, as well as prior VR experience (Fig. 3b). Results showed significant differences in the evaluation of teaching methods in favour of VR. VR participants rated the overall usefulness of the learning unit higher than those in the lecture group (mean difference +2, 95% confidence interval [CI] outside 0). The results were in favour of the VR group in terms of the length of the learning unit (mean difference +1.7, 95% CI > 0), enthusiasm for the event (+2.0), and the subjective learning experience (+1.5) (Fig. 3b). There was no significant group difference in the question about “interest in dermatology”. Regarding previous experience with the PASI score and the use of VR technology, the lecture group had similar less previous experience (mean difference −2.0, 95% CI < 0), but this had no influence on the PASI evaluations.

Statistical analysis of tech-savviness comparison (lecture/VR) (a), and statistical analysis of different questionnaire item comparisons (lecture/VR) regarding benefit, length, enthusiasm, experience, interest, PASI experience, and VR experience (b).

Regarding previous experience in VR, as well as prior experience in PASI, there were no relevant differences measured between both groups. There were no major differences in PASI experience or dermatology interest. Participants rated VR learning as more positive and beneficial than traditional lectures, with higher enthusiasm and session ratings. The video lecture was also well received but showed greater variation among individuals than the VR group (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

This study presents a novel VR-based approach for PASI training, demonstrating superior learning outcomes compared to a traditional lecture among study participants. The VR group achieved PASI scores with lower variance and higher accordance with the result of a trained assessor, indicating greater consistency and precision in assessment. These results demonstrate a potential of VR as an effective tool for dermatological education, particularly for improving practical diagnostic skills for assessing skin diseases.

The evaluation of the questionnaires showed that the VR participants rated their learning experience more positively than the study participants randomised to the lecture group. This included ratings of engagement, usefulness of the learning unit, and overall satisfaction. The better evaluation of the VR learning unit in contrast to the conventional lecture is likely due, among other things, to the fact that the VR system is considered user-friendly and participants are shown interactive three-dimensional visualisations, as well as the self-explanatory navigation of the system and, at the end, direct feedback as an outcome of what they have learnt. In addition, speed of the training can be adjusted individually.

Although participants reported no significant differences in levels of prior knowledge and slightly varying degrees of technical affinity with VR, these factors apparently had no significant impact on the accuracy of the PASI assessment. This suggests that the VR platform is accessible and effective for all users, irrespective of the technological background. It is noteworthy that none of the study participants had any previous experience with PASI itself, i.e., they had not actively evaluated it before, which indicates equal starting conditions. All groups were blinded to the dermatologist PASI assessments, ensuring that the participants’ self-reported satisfaction and performance feedback were completely unbiased and reflected only their learning experiences.

VR-based simulations allow learners to practise assessing psoriasis lesions in various clinical scenarios, thereby improving their diagnostic skills and confidence in using the PASI. In addition, the use of VR enables an interactive learning experience that increases student motivation and engagement and can lead to a deeper understanding of the material.

It should be noted that VR will offer multiple training opportunities without endangering patients. Its high usability and ease of use after just a few minutes are key advantages. In medicine, VR systems are already being used successfully for patients with anxiety and behavioural disorders. People with phobias, for example, can use the VR system to experience anxiety-provoking situations in a very targeted and personalised way, thus developing conditioned behaviour more quickly. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that VR can be used as a tool in the medical operating theatre [5]. The VR application has also already shown initial therapeutic effects, with VR reducing stress, anxiety, and depression in patients and ICU staff, among other things [6], and is widely researched internationally and used clinically in a variety of ways in the fields of surgery, rehabilitation, and mental health [7]. The effect of VR on the user is demonstrated by what is known as “embodied simulation”, an important mechanism in our brain that helps us to understand and interpret the world around us by unconsciously generating physical simulations of the events we experience [8]. In the field of nursing education, a meta-analysis has shown that VR increased both knowledge gain and participant satisfaction and improved practical skills compared to traditional teaching [9], which shows parallels to our research project. Accordingly, VR has good potential to further expand and confirm these data in future studies. Possible disadvantages of using VR include the fact that technical problems with the equipment can arise at any time, and that the graphics quality used today may no longer be up to date depending on the state of the art, as technology continues to advance, meaning that the equipment may become obsolete more quickly than initially anticipated and may be phased out of teaching more quickly, particularly at the research institutions where it is used. Users of VR glasses may experience side effects such as dizziness or eye strain [10], as each person reacts individually to the VR session. In our project, it should be noted that we familiarised both glasses wearers, contact lens wearers, and non-glasses wearers with the VR session, and no one complained of any side effects either after the 3-min familiarisation period or after the VR session; this was actively asked about.

Dermatology is a visual discipline and is an ideal field for interaction with VR/AR, making it particularly suitable for immersive/interactive learning and research scenarios [11, 12]. In future, the area of ethics and safety in the use of VR will certainly also need to be considered, especially when we talk about learning sessions for children or older people, as the addictive nature of VR can lead to so-called cybersickness [12]. AR can already be used for targeted, carefully validated training sessions in dermatology, especially in the field of teaching, but evidence, specified standards, and clear framework conditions are needed for future routine use [13]. Accordingly, VR has the potential to become a realistic training tool for future doctors in dermatology teaching in standardised training sessions, whereby ultimately every patient, especially in the field of PSO, can benefit from the fact that the trained doctor can prescribe better therapy using VR because the PASI has been accurately assessed.
