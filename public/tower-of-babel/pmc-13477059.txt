Phased T2T horse and donkey assemblies from a mule reveal peculiar equid centromere evolution
Authors: Kai Li, Eleonora Cappelletti, Carey Dessaix, Julia Ciosek, Emily Robyn, Lauren Johnson, Nahla Hussien AbouEl Ela, Xiomara Arias, David L. Adelson, Terje Raudsepp, Francesca M. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present telomere-to-telomere genome assemblies of a Thoroughbred horse and a donkey derived from their mule offspring. Now adopted and annotated by NCBI as reference genomes, these assemblies resolve previously inaccessible regions, including satellite arrays, duplications, and telomeres. Equids are known to exhibit an uncoupling between satellite DNA and centromeric function. The completeness of these assemblies enabled annotation of both satellite-based and satellite-free centromeres, as well as non-centromeric satellite loci, revealing notable centromeric plasticity. They also allowed detailed characterization of the variable binding domains of CENP-A—the epigenetic determinant of centromere identity—and CENP-B, whose association with CENP-A, previously considered typical based on a few model organisms, is absent in equids. Comparative analyses of satellite repeats and centromere positions provide new insights into the accelerated karyotypic reshuffling in equid evolution. These assemblies represent foundational resources for equine genomics and support ongoing initiatives such as the Equine Pangenome Project.

Introduction

Genome resources for the horse have advanced at more or less the same rate as those of other large-animal agricultural species, such as cattle, pigs, and sheep. The first reference genome for the horse was released in 2007 and referred to as EquCab2.01 (GenBank: GCF_000002305.2). It was followed in 2018 by EquCab3.02 (GenBank: GCF_002863925.1), which was improved in both composition and contiguity by high-throughput sequencing technologies that had recently become available. Those assemblies were foundational to the hundreds of horse genetic and genomic studies that followed, including the Equine FAANG project, and made possible other high-throughput tools, including the high-density SNP chips: 50k in 2010, 70k3 in 2012, and the 670k4 in 2017. As sequencing technologies and the tools to assemble these data have dramatically improved in both length and accuracy, our efforts to build ever-better assemblies that are more contiguous, complete, and accurate have kept pace.

By virtue of both long- and ultra-long-read technologies, we are now able to uniquely resolve repeat regions and characterize centromeres. Centromeres are essential nucleoprotein structures that ensure chromosome segregation during cell division. Although centromeres are discrete genetic loci, their function is not determined by the underlying DNA sequence but epigenetically specified by the histone H3 variant CENP-A.5 While centromeric DNA sequences are neither sufficient nor required to specify centromere function, mammalian centromeres are typically associated with extended arrays of satellite DNA,6 which remained largely absent from earlier genome assemblies. This has posed a significant challenge in unraveling centromeric function at the molecular level. In this context, a turning point was the discovery that equid species are characterized by the coexistence of satellite-based and satellite-free centromeres.1,7,8,9,10 The presence of functional satellite-free centromeres made the genus Equus a powerful model system in centromere biology.11,12,13,14,15,16,17 In the horse (2n = 64), only the centromere of chromosome 11 is satellite-free, while the other centromeres are satellite based.1,8 In the donkey (2n = 62), 16 centromeres are satellite-free, demonstrating that although more than half of centromeres are devoid of satellite DNA, this configuration is compatible with genome stability and species survival.8 The positions of these satellite-free centromeres, identified as CENP-A binding domains, are not fixed but slide in an about 600 kb region, giving rise to “epialleles” that follow Mendelian inheritance.8,18 However, at several centromeres, substantial centromere movements, on the order of 50–80 kb, were observed from parent to offspring, indicating that the position of CENP-A binding domains can slide in one generation.8 In contrast, the position of the centromere is stable during mitotic propagation of cultured cells8 and during tissue differentiation,16 suggesting that centromere sliding may occur during meiosis.

The genus Equus remains unique among mammals for its abundance of satellite-free centromeres,1,7,8,9,10 which has been associated with the rapid and recent radiation of equids within the last 4.0–4.5 million years.19,20,21 In this very short evolutionary time frame, frequent centromere repositioning events and chromosomal rearrangements, particularly centric fusions, reshaped equid karyotypes.7,8,9,10,17,22,23 These mechanisms gave rise to numerous satellite-free centromeres that are considered “immature” because they did not have sufficient time to acquire the structural and sequence complexity typical of mammalian centromeres. Another consequence of this great karyotype reshuffling during equid evolution was the heterogeneous organization of satellite DNA sequences.7,17 Several satellite families were described in equids, namely 37cen, 2PI, CENPB-sat, EC137, SatA, SatB, SatC, SatD, and SatE.7,17,24,25 These loci are present either at satellite-based centromeres or at non-centromeric positions, corresponding to ancestral inactivated centromeres.7,17 Recent findings revealed that CENPB-sat, which is the satellite bound by the centromeric protein CENP-B, is not the major centromeric satellite in any equid species.17 This discovery challenges the common view, mainly based on human and mouse experimental systems, that CENP-B, the unique centromeric protein binding a specific motif (CENP-B box),26 binds the main centromeric satellite and thus increases centromere strength and segregation fidelity.27 In equids, CENP-B is expressed but does not bind the majority of satellite-based or satellite-free centromeres, while it is localized at several non-centromeric terminal positions, most likely corresponding to ancestral, now-inactive, centromeres.17 However, centromeres lacking CENP-B are functional and do not show any segregation defect in mitosis compared to CENP-B-positive chromosomes.17 According to our recent model, the equid CENPB-sat satellite was centromeric in the equid ancestor, lost centromeric function during evolution, and gave rise to a shorter CENP-A-bound repeat (37cen) not containing the CENP-B box.17 Over evolutionary timescales, this uncoupling between CENP-B and CENP-A may have played a role in the extensive evolutionary reshuffling of equid centromeres.

While equid satellite-free centromeres were well characterized at the sequence level, satellite-based centromeres and satellite loci were not included in the previous, non-telomere-to-telomere (T2T) equid genome assemblies.

Here, we present the results of our effort to create complete assemblies for horse (Equus caballus GenBank: GCF_041296265.1) and donkey (Equus asinus GenBank: GCF_041296235.1) haplotypes derived from a female mule named TB-T2T and EquAss-T2T_v2, respectively. The mule, a cross-species hybrid, offers an advantage for genome assembly, an approach that has already been applied successfully in cattle, bison, and yak.28,29,30 Because the mule’s maternal and paternal chromosomes are from species that diverged roughly 4 million years ago,20,21 the resulting species-specific alleles are abundant enough to reliably assign sequencing reads to their parent of origin—either before assembly using parental sequence or during assembly as the algorithm separates and reconstructs each parental haplotype readily based on their distinct evolutionary differences. These assemblies have been adopted as the reference genomes for the two species and were subsequently annotated by NCBI in October 2024. These new T2T assemblies are essential to the ongoing annotation efforts for variation across all popular breeds and immune response genes. A tissue repository has been created using tissues collected from the mule used in this work and may be used to continue the annotation of this resource. In this manuscript, we provide a comprehensive annotation of repetitive elements, including satellite arrays and transposons, and a characterization of centromeric domains. Comparative analyses of satellite repeats and centromere positions in horse and donkey illuminate the accelerated karyotypic reshuffling that has shaped equid evolution. Additionally, we provide an equid-focused public resource for these “community expert-created” annotations that can be downloaded or otherwise used in software or browsers capable of accessing and using web-based datasets.

Results

Karyotype

The mule was karyotypically normal (Figure S1). All twenty cells analyzed had a 63,XX female hybrid karyotype composed of a normal haploid set of horse (31,X) and normal haploid set of donkey (30,X) chromosomes. Because not all horse (2n = 64) and donkey (2n = 62) chromosomes share one-to-one homology, the horse and donkey haploid karyotypes were arranged separately.

Genome composition

Genome assembly was supported by multiple sequencing technologies, including PacBio HiFi, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), Omni-C, and Illumina whole-genome sequencing (WGS) short-read data. In total, approximately 150× HiFi coverage, 80× ONT coverage, 69× Illumina whole-genome short reads, and 386 million paired Omni-C reads were generated from the F1 mule. Additional Illumina whole-genome short-read data (∼54× coverage per haplotype) from the Thoroughbred dam and donkey sire were used to support haplotype partitioning and validation.

Both the horse (GenBank: GCF_041296265.1) and the donkey (GenBank: GCF_041296235.1) assemblies have been deposited in NCBI and have been annotated as the reference genomes for their respective species. Full details of the assembled chromosomes, including the numbers of telomeres and gaps (and descriptions thereof), are presented in Table S1. Briefly, the horse TB-T2T assembly is a 31 chromosome + chrX and mitochondrial (chrMT) haplotype. In the horse karyotype, autosomes ECA1–ECA13 and ECAX are meta- or submeta-centric, while ECA14–ECA31 are acrocentric.

Both telomeres were captured on 26 chromosomes, while the p-terminal end was missing at 6 chromosomes (1, 18, 19, 20, 22, and 31) (Table S1). The terminal regions of the five acrocentric chromosomes (18, 19, 20, 22, and 31) were identified in five unincorporated contigs containing the missing telomeres; pericentromeric and centromeric satellite arrays are described in Table 1. These were initially unplaced by the assembly pipeline but were later associated with their corresponding chromosomes. These contigs correspond to the proximal ends of acrocentric chromosomes, where assembly breakpoints overlap large blocks of centromeric satellite repeats, preventing their initial incorporation into chromosome scaffolds. To assign these contigs to their corresponding chromosomes, repetitive sequences in both chromosome scaffolds and unincorporated contigs were masked using RepeatMasker to suppress repeat-derived noise. Hi-C reads were then mapped to the masked genome, and contig-to-chromosome associations were inferred from enriched contact patterns within the remaining unique sequence backbone. These associations were manually curated using PretextView to identify consistent and chromosome-specific linkage signals. The donkey EquAss-T2T_v2 haplotype contains 30 chromosomes + chrX. In the donkey karyotype, autosomes EAS1-EAS19, EAS30, and EASX are meta- or submeta-centric, while EAS20–EAS29 are acrocentric. Both telomeres were captured on 19 chromosomes (Table S1). Seven of the missing telomeres were captured in unincorporated contigs but not anchored to chromosomes (Table 1). Following the annotation of satellite repeats in the TB-T2T assembly, we analyzed CENP-A chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets from ten horse individuals to investigate the organization of centromeric domains (Figures 3B, 3C, and S2; Methods S1). In addition, we examined the relative positioning of CENP-A and CENP-B binding domains using a CENP-B ChIP-seq dataset from one of these individuals.

We confirmed that, in the horse, only the chromosome 11 (ECA11) centromere is satellite-free (Figure S2). At this locus, CENP-A enrichment profiles across individuals were markedly improved compared to those obtained using the EquCab3.0 reference genome,16 further supporting the high quality of the TB-T2T assembly. As previously shown using different reference genomes,16,18 CENP-A enrichment peaks vary in position among individuals within a 500 kb window, confirming the presence of epialleles for CENP-A binding in the population.8,18 In agreement with our recent findings,17 no CENP-B binding is present at this satellite-free centromere Figure S2).

All other centromeres are associated with satellite DNA (Figures 3B and 3C; Methods S1). ECA3 represents a typical horse satellite-based centromere, comprising an extended 37cen array flanked by shorter CENPB-sat and 2PI arrays (Figure 3B). The position of the CENP-A ChIP-seq enrichment peaks detected in the ten individuals varies among individuals, as previously observed at the horse satellite-free centromere of chromosome 11.16,18 The peaks are consistently confined to a central 500 kb region of the 37cen array rather than extending across the entire array. This region corresponds to the most sequence-conserved portion of the array, indicating that the functional centromere lies within the conserved 37cen core, while the remaining portion of the array is pericentromeric. CENPB-sat and 2PI satellites are not bound by CENP-A and are located in the peripheral region of the pericentromere. At this centromere, CENP-B binding was detected at the short 180 kb CENPB-sat array, whereas it is absent at the centromeric domain, confirming the dissociation between CENP-A and CENP-B domains at equid centromeres.17 This centromere organization is conserved across most equid satellite-based centromeres, with the CENP-A binding domain localized within the most conserved regions of the 37cen arrays (Methods S1). In several cases, such as ECA6, ECA7, ECA10, ECA12, ECA14, ECA15, and ECA26, variation in the CENP-A binding position among individuals has been observed (Methods S1).

The metacentric chromosomes show long arrays of 37cen flanked by short arrays of CENPB-sat, 2PI, and EC137 (Methods S1). In contrast, acrocentric chromosomes are often characterized by shorter arrays of 37cen and longer arrays of CENPB-sat and 2PI compared to metacentric ones. Interestingly, these satellite arrays lie directly adjacent to the (TTAGGG)n telomeric repeats. Consistent with our recent findings,17 the ECA11 satellite-free centromere (Figure S2) and numerous horse satellite-based centromeres (ECA1, ECA4, ECA5, ECA7, ECA9, ECA12, ECA13, ECA27, ECA28, ECA29, and ECAX) (Methods S1) contain little to no CENPB-sat arrays and do not exhibit CENP-B binding. At the other satellite-based centromeres, CENPB-sat arrays are pericentromeric and often intermingled with 2PI repeats (Methods S1), confirming the dissociation between CENP-A and CENP-B domains.17

The only exception to this organization is the ECA2 centromere (Figure 3C), which lacks 37cen and was previously proposed to retain the ancestral configuration of Equus centromeres, with CENPB-sat binding both CENP-A and CENP-B.17 Consistently, this centromere is characterized by an extended 1.5 Mb CENPB-sat array bound by both CENP-A and CENP-B proteins (Figure 3C). This array is flanked by degenerated 2PI arrays intermingled with very short and degenerated CENPB-sat repetitions, while no 37cen is present.

In the primary assembly, no CENP-A enrichment peaks were detected at the ECA18, ECA19, ECA20, ECA22, and ECA31 chromosomes (Table S3). The entire p arm of these chromosomes, encompassing the telomeric end and the centromeric region, was missing. After the annotation of the assembly by NCBI, we demonstrated that these five missing ends were present in five unplaced contigs, which we were able to anchor to the corresponding chromosomes (Table 1). These sequences, which include telomeric (TTAGGG)n repeats and pericentromeric and centromeric satellite arrays, were included in the analyses shown in Methods S1. These five centromeres exhibit an organization comparable to that of the other acrocentric chromosomes, with satellite arrays extending from the telomeric boundary.

The presence of SatA and SatB at horse satellite-based centromeres is negligible (Table S3), with only a few kilobase-long stretches detected at ECA26 for SatA and at ECA25, ECA28, and ECA31 for SatB (Methods S1). In contrast, a 5 Mb array of SatA is present on the q arm of ECAX and represents the only extended non-centromeric satellite array of the horse genome (Figure S3). At this locus, neither CENP-A nor CENP-B binding was detected (Figure S3). It is important to note that SatA units contain regions of identity with LINE L1-4B elements, suggesting that these retrotransposons may be involved in the expansion of SatA arrays. Short stretches of satellite repeats, ranging from a few base pairs to a few kilobases, are otherwise present in other genomic positions, mainly within a few megabases of the centromeric and pericentromeric satellite arrays (Table S3). Interestingly, quite an extended (370 kb) array of SatB is present at the p terminus of ECA1 (Table S3), where it could represent a relic of an ancestral satellite-based centromere.

Satellite DNA and centromere organization in the donkey

To characterize the organization of satellite DNA in the donkey genome, we first localized the donkey satellite DNA families of this species (Table S2) onto the EquAss-T2T_v2 reference genome (Table S4). Satellite repeats constitute about 8.3% of the genome. Consistent with our previous data obtained from unassembled short reads,17 37cen and SatA are the most abundant satellites, while 2PI, SatB, and SatC are less prevalent and CENPB-sat, EC137, and SatD are nearly absent (Figure 3D).

Interestingly, we discovered that, in this species, the majority of 2PI loci are composed of an undescribed subfamily of about 1.2 kb monomers, consisting of about 220 bp of telomeric-like repeats followed by 2PI repeats (Figure S4). This subfamily, termed 2PI-telo, accounts for more than half of the total 2PI satellite content (Figure 3D).

Following satellite repeat annotation in the EquAss-T2T_v2 assembly, we analyzed CENP-A ChIP-seq datasets from four donkey individuals together with a CENP-B ChIP-seq dataset from one of them to assess centromere organization and the relative positioning of CENP-A and CENP-B domains (Figures 3E–3G, S5, and S6; Methods S1).

Mapping of CENP-A ChIP-seq datasets confirmed that 16 centromeres are satellite-free (Figures S5 and S6). CENP-A enrichment peaks generally display regular Gaussian-like profiles, supporting the high quality of this assembly, whereas irregular peaks at EAS13 and EAS14 suggest possible rearrangements in the analyzed individuals relative to the reference genome. Consistent with previous work,8 five satellite-free centromeres were confirmed to contain DNA duplications, most prominently EAS8, where duplicated segments span approximately 4 Mb (Figure S6). At all satellite-free centromeres, CENP-A peaks vary in position among individuals (Figures S5 and S6), confirming the presence of epialleles for CENP-A binding in the population.8,18 The analysis of these four individuals revealed that, at the EAS12 satellite-free centromere, CENP-A binding domains are distributed within a 2.8 Mb window, with donkey D exhibiting two epialleles separated by 2.5 Mb (Figure S5). This finding demonstrates that centromere sliding can occur over a broader genomic region than the previously reported 600 kb window. In agreement with our recent findings,17 no CENP-B binding is present at any satellite-free centromere (Figures S5 and S6).

The 15 donkey satellite-based centromeres display heterogeneous organization (Figures 3E and 3G; Methods S1).

In the metacentric EAS2 (Figure 3E) and EAS17 (Methods S1) chromosomes, the centromeric function is embedded within 37cen arrays, flanked by very short arrays of other repeats, including CENPB-sat regions shorter than 10 kb. At EAS2, CENP-A enrichment corresponds to the portion of the array with the highest sequence identity (Figure 3E). At EAS17, the 500-kb 37cen array is entirely covered by CENP-A domains in all four individuals. Donkey C shows additional CENP-A enrichment peaks on single-copy sequences, suggesting the presence of rearrangements of this locus in the population (Methods S1). In the metacentric EAS1 chromosome, two main CENP-A enrichment peaks, spaced 20 Mb apart, are located within a 50-Mb satellite array primarily composed of 37cen repeats, highlighting the complexity of this centromeric region.

In the metacentric EAS3 chromosome (Figure 3F), the CENP-A enrichment peaks of the four individuals cover a 120 kb array of CENPB-sat. This is the longest CENPB-sat array in the donkey genome and unique in that it is bound by CENP-B (Figure 3F; Methods S1; Table S4). Notably, EAS3 is the only donkey centromere showing CENP-B binding. Interestingly, EAS3, which represents the ancestral configuration present in the hypothetical common ancestor of equids, is orthologous to ECA2q and ECA3q,31 and ECA2 is the only horse chromosome in which CENP-A and CENP-B colocalize (Figure 3C). These findings reinforce our previously proposed model,17 suggesting that CENPB-sat represented the ancestral centromeric satellite whose centromeric function was subsequently lost at all loci except EAS3 and ECA2, which retain the ancestral organization.

In the remaining satellite-based centromeres, CENP-A binds SatC, with the highest satellite conservation in the CENP-A binding region (Figure 3G; Methods S1). While SatC is the sole satellite in the centromeric region of the metacentric chromosomes EAS6 and EAS15, the acrocentric chromosomes show a SatC centromeric region embedded within megabase-sized pericentromeric arrays primarily composed of the SatA satellite. CENPB-sat is negligible at these centromeres, with only very short stretches of a few kilobases, and CENP-B binding is absent. Overall, positional variation of CENP-A domains appears less pronounced in donkey than in horse, possibly because the high identity and homogeneity of donkey centromeric satellite arrays mask CENP-A positional shifts.

In agreement with our previous FISH results,7 we detected megabase-sized satellite loci at non-centromeric terminal positions (EAS1pter, EAS4pter, EAS6pter, EAS9pter, EAS10pter, EAS11pter, EAS13pter, EAS18qter, EAS19qter, EAS27qter, and EAS30qter) and in the q arm of EASX (Methods S1). These non-centromeric loci contain different satellite families with variable sequence identity. Short stretches of satellite repeats are otherwise present at other genomic positions, most likely representing evolutionary traces of genomic rearrangements (Table S4).

At most terminal loci, we detected arrays of the newly described 2PI-telo subfamily (Methods S1; Table S4). We cytogenetically confirmed the genome-based distribution of 2PI-telo by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with a TTAGGG repeat probe, indicating that these telomeric-probe signals largely correspond to terminal arrays of the 2PI-telo satellite subfamily rather than arrays composed exclusively of telomeric repeats (Figure S7). The additional EAS12pter signal, absent from the reference assembly, likely reflects population-level variation, as the FISH-analyzed donkey is unrelated to the mule used for genome assembly. Differences in signal intensity between homologs further suggest copy-number polymorphism of 2PI-telo repeats in the population (Figure S7).

Chromosome and centromere evolution in horse and donkey

Thanks to the T2T assemblies, a highly resolved chromosome alignment between the horse and donkey orthologs was carried out (Methods S1). We confirmed chromosome orthologies that were previously described at the cytogenetic level but detected undescribed inversions at EAS4, EAS14, EAS23, and EAS25 and complex rearrangements at EAS17 (Methods S1). These whole-chromosome alignments allowed us to investigate chromosome evolution with respect to the position of centromeres and satellite DNA. To this end, it is important to remember that the horse is considered the closest extant species to the equid ancestor and that equid ancestral chromosomes generally correspond to horse whole chromosomes or their arms.19,31,32

The majority of donkey chromosomes correspond to entire horse chromosomes or chromosome arms, indicating that they did not arise from fusion (Figure 4). Chromosome fusions were detected only at EAS1, EAS4, EAS5, EAS7, EAS8, and EAS10. In contrast, EAS3, although corresponding to ECA2q/ECA3q, was not generated by a donkey-specific fusion but represents the putative ancestral perissodactyl configuration, which was subsequently split by fission in the horse lineage and in several other species.19,31 Notably, EAS3 is the only donkey chromosome in which CENPB-sat is centromeric, further supporting its interpretation as an ancestral configuration. With the exception of EAS1, all donkey chromosomes resulting from fusion carry satellite-free centromeres (Figure 4). However, their satellite-free centromeres are located several megabases away from the fusion points (Methods S1). This distance is consistent with a centromere repositioning event that relocated the centromere away from the fusion junction. However, based on the available data, it is not possible to determine whether centromere repositioning occurred before, during, or after the fusion event. Notably, remnants of satellite repeats—mainly composed of 2PI and ranging in size from about 10 to 150 kb (Table S4)—are still detectable at the fusion regions, most likely representing relics of the ancestral satellite arrays associated with the original centromeres.

The satellite-free centromeres of EAS4 and EASX are located at inversion breakpoints, suggesting that these inversions may have played a direct role in their formation (Methods S1). All the remaining donkey satellite-free centromeres (EAS11, EAS12, EAS13, EAS14, EAS16, EAS18, EAS19, EAS27, and EAS30) clearly originated from centromere repositioning events, consistent with our previous findings.8,22

Comparison of horse and donkey orthologs revealed significant variations in satellite DNA composition, highlighting lineage-specific evolution of satellite families (Figure 4). However, in most cases, the chromosomal position of satellite loci is conserved between the two species. In particular, we can identify six different situations: (1) maintenance of satellite DNA at horse and donkey orthologous peri/centromeric positions (EAS1cen/ECA4cen, EAS2cen/ECA1cen, EAS3cen/ECA3cen-ECA2cen, EAS17cen/ECA12cen, EAS21cen/ECA16cen, EAS22cen/ECA6cen, EAS23cen/ECA23cen, EAS24cen/ECA10cen, EAS25cen/ECA5cen, and EAS29cen/ECA29cen). (2) Maintenance of satellite DNA at horse and donkey orthologous non-centromeric positions (EASXq/ECAXq), which share extended SatA arrays. (3) Maintenance of satellite DNA at donkey non-centromeric terminal positions and horse orthologous centromeric positions (EAS1pter/ECA31cen, EAS6pter/ECA15cen, EAS9pter/ECA14cen, EAS11pter/ECA17cen, and EAS30cen/ECA30cen). Discussion

T2T assemblies are the essential next step for researchers working to understand the genetic bases of health and well-being, phenotypic diversity, and genomic divergence resulting from speciation. The TB-T2T and EquAss-T2T_v2 assemblies presented here are the first step forward for the genomic scientists studying equids. Current efforts are underway to construct a pangenome for horses to enumerate structural variants across breeds, to annotate immune response genes, and to catalog short variants found across horse breeds. This will not be the last of these assemblies for the horse or other equids. It is our hope that the methods and resources presented here provide a foundation that will benefit efforts to understand centromeric and repeat structure in those new assemblies.

The generation of T2T assemblies for the horse and the donkey has made it possible to draw a complete picture of the organization of both satellite-free and satellite-based centromeres in these two species. In the horse, satellite-based centromeres are typically made by an extended array of the 37cen satellite, the major centromeric satellite, flanked by other satellite families. Similar to what has been described for human centromeres,35,36,37,38 the functional horse centromeric domain is confined to an inner region of the 37cen array, which exhibits the highest sequence conservation compared to the pericentromeric portion of the array. In the donkey, where only half of the centromeres are satellite based, the situation is much more heterogeneous, with multiple centromeric satellite families and significant variability in pericentric satellites. In donkey chromosomes, the functional centromere can indeed reside on a 37cen array, a CENPB-sat array, or a SatC array.

Equid satellite-free centromeres revealed that the position of the CENP-A binding domains can slide in an about 600 kb window, giving rise to epialleles for CENP-A binding.8,12,16,18 Here, the analysis of additional donkey individuals now extends this range to 2.8 Mb, indicating that these genomic regions can be very large. Thanks to the T2T assemblies, we demonstrated that centromere sliding also occurs at satellite-based centromeres, as previously suggested by cytogenetic data in horse spermatocytes.12 This finding is consistent with the positional variation in centromeric domains reported at human and mouse satellite-based centromeres, indicating that this phenomenon is common in mammals.39,40,41

Mapping of CENP-A and CENP-B ChIP-seq data on horse and donkey T2T references enabled sequence-level analysis of individual centromeres, confirming our recent discovery that CENP-A and CENP-B domains are dissociated in equids.17 In horse, CENPB-sat arrays are present in only a subset of pericentromeres, and among these, only a fraction is bound by CENP-B. CENPB-sat arrays are typically intermingled with 2PI arrays at the periphery of the megabase-sized 37cen arrays. This organization supports our previous hypothesis that CENPB-sat and 2PI are ancestral satellites, displaced to the pericentromeric region after 37cen expansion in the centromeric core,17 consistent with established models of satellite DNA expansion and evolution.42,43,44,45 This uncoupling is particularly pronounced in donkey, where CENP-B binding was previously undetectable by immunofluorescence despite expression of a functional protein, and CENPB-sat was found to be poorly represented in the genome, with a single FISH signal at the primary constriction of EAS3.17 The T2T donkey assembly revealed that this locus contains the longest CENPB-sat array in the genome (120 kb), only a portion of which is bound by CENP-B, supporting satellite degeneration and explaining the absence of detectable immunofluorescence signals.17 At EAS3, however, the entire CENPB-sat array corresponds to the CENP-A-bound functional centromere, as observed at horse ECA2. Therefore, EAS3 and ECA2 are the only donkey and horse centromeres that retain the ancestral configuration, in which CENPB-sat acts as the centromeric satellite bound by both CENP-A and CENP-B. In human and mouse experimental systems, CENP-B has been proposed to enhance centromere stability by promoting CENP-A/CENP-C maintenance and segregation fidelity.27 By contrast, we previously showed that in equids, CENP-A/CENP-B uncoupling is not associated with detectable segregation defects of CENP-B-negative centromeres in cultured cells, highlighting an unexpected diversity in the functional interplay between these components across mammals.17 Nevertheless, it is intriguing that equid lineages in which CENP-A/CENP-B dissociation is most pronounced (donkey and zebras) also display the highest number of satellite-free centromeres, extreme karyotype reshuffling, and marked heterogeneity in centromeric/pericentromeric satellite families, whereas in horse—where CENP-B is no longer centromeric but remains detectable at pericentromeric loci—only one satellite-free centromere has been described, and the karyotype appears comparatively more stable relative to the inferred equid ancestor. Although this comparative association does not demonstrate causality and rapid karyotype evolution can arise through multiple mechanisms, it is tempting to speculate that CENP-A/CENP-B dissociation may have contributed to the extensive reshuffling of equid centromeres by subtly modulating centromere stability over evolutionary timescales.

Comparison of horse and donkey T2T genomes provided sequence-level insight into chromosome, centromere, and satellite DNA evolution in horse and donkey lineages. The horse karyotype, considered the closest to the ancestral equid karyotype, is dominated by acrocentric chromosomes, which typically show long CENPB-sat and 2PI arrays flanking short 37cen arrays. By contrast, most horse metacentric chromosomes carry long 37cen arrays with only short peripheral 2PI and/or CENPB-sat stretches, most likely reflecting their relatively recent origin through fusion, inversion, and centromere repositioning events.17,25,31 These processes may have contributed to the reduction or loss of CENPB-sat and 2PI satellites, as well as the expansion of 37cen. Consistent with the relative karyotype stability of the horse lineage among equids, we detected only a single extended non-centromeric satellite locus in the horse assembly on chromosome X.

The donkey karyotype was restructured by numerous centromere repositioning events and chromosomal rearrangements.8,22,23,31,32 These events occurred in very short and recent evolutionary times,19,21,31 explaining the presence of such a high number of satellite-free centromeres as well as the numerous non-centromeric satellite loci that represent remnants of ancestral inactivated centromeres. Satellite blocks outside the functional centromere have also been described at the cytogenetic level in other vertebrates,46,47,48,49,50 indicating that non-centromeric satellite loci are not unique to equids. However, the uncoupling between satellite DNA and centromeric function, with the emergence of numerous satellite-free centromeres, was demonstrated only in equids.

Overall, our data are consistent with the view that most donkey satellite-free centromeres originated through centromere repositioning. In fusion-derived chromosomes, the satellite-free centromeres are located several megabases away from the fusion junctions, supporting a scenario in which a fusion between two acrocentric chromosomes carrying satellite-based centromeres was associated with centromere repositioning to an ectopic locus and loss of the ancestral satellite arrays. Nevertheless, our data do not allow us to infer whether centromere repositioning occurred before, during, or after the fusion event. Consistent with this model, residual satellite tracts persist at the fusion regions and likely represent relics of the ancestral satellite arrays associated with the original centromeres. The association between fusion and neocentromere formation was also observed at several chromosomes of Burchell’s and Grevy’s zebra.9

However, thanks to this new genome assembly, we discovered that two satellite-free centromeres (EAS4 and EASX) are located at inversion breakpoints. A similar situation was previously described for a satellite-free centromere of Burchell’s zebra.9 These observations suggest that inversions may be associated with the emergence of satellite-free centromeres in equids. Nevertheless, our data do not allow us to establish whether the inversion provided a permissive context for the centromere function to arise at a new locus or whether the inversion occurred after new centromere emergence.

Donkey chromosomes show expansion of satellite families absent or poorly represented in the horse genome, such as SatA and SatC. Long SatA arrays are present in the pericentromeric region of most donkey satellite-based centromeres and at a few non-centromeric loci in both horse and donkey. SatA is composed of 4,678 bp monomers17 containing regions homologous to LINE L1-4B elements, suggesting that LINE1-derived sequences may have contributed to the emergence and/or expansion of this satellite family, as observed for other TE-derived satellites.51,52,53,54 Interestingly, LINE L1 elements are also enriched at most equid satellite-free centromeres,8,9 raising the possibility that TE-rich sequence contexts may facilitate early steps of maturation at repositioned centromeres, potentially preceding the formation of satellite arrays.

In addition, SatA monomers contain a region with high sequence similarity to the Parascaris equorum ETSTY7-like repeat, which was previously shown to be extensively amplified on the equid X chromosome and on several autosomes.55 Taken together, these observations suggest that this sequence—proposed to have been horizontally transferred from Parascaris—expanded through the proliferation of SatA families. In addition, we observed a donkey-specific expansion of the newly discovered 2PI subfamily (2PI-telo) whose monomers are composed of 2PI and telomeric repeats. This satellite subfamily is found at terminal non-centromeric positions, which could represent remnants of ancestral inactivated centromeres or the result of satellite DNA exchange between opposite chromosomal termini. This discovery confirmed a previous hypothesis, based on cytogenetic observations, that in this species a degenerate telomere-like satellite sequence could represent an abundant satellite family associated with heterochromatin.56,57 The presence of satellite sequences containing telomeric repeats was described in other mammalian species, such as whales58 and carnivores.59,60,61 Similar to the donkey situation, these peculiar satellites were mainly localized at chromosomal termini.59,60,61 Because 2PI-telo repeats contain enough TTAGGG motifs to be detected by FISH with a telomeric probe and are located at non-telomeric terminal regions, they can be classified as heterochromatic interstitial telomeric sequences (het-ITSs). Het-ITSs were defined as extended blocks of telomeric-like repeats located at pericentromeric, terminal, or intrachromosomal regions, generally coinciding with C bands and easily detectable by cytogenetic analysis.62 These sequences were identified in several vertebrate species, some insects, and plants62,63,64,65,66,67,68,69 and seem to be intrinsically prone to breakage.70,71,72 Interestingly, most donkey 2PI-telo loci likely derived from satellite DNA exchange between opposite termini, supporting the fact that these sequences are prone to rearrangement.

Despite lineage-specific variation in satellite DNA families, we identified several satellite loci that are present at conserved positions in both species. While the composition of satellite families is conserved at some loci, in several cases, different satellite families expanded in the two lineages. In a few cases, these satellite loci underwent further expansion during donkey evolution, making them more extended than their horse counterparts. The most notable example is the very extended satellite arrays at the p terminus and at the pericentromeric region of EAS1, which are 22 and 57 Mb long, respectively. These regions were previously identified at the cytogenetic level by FISH and were found to be polymorphic in size in the donkey population,73 highlighting the variability of satellite DNA organization among individuals.

The T2T assemblies revealed substantial TE increases despite a proportional decrease of interspersed TE content in both genomes. This dilution effect reflects that the newly resolved regions are primarily composed of telomeric and satellite-rich centromeric sequences rather than TE-rich regions. LTR and LINE expansions account for the majority of the additional TE content observed in EquAss-T2T_v2 relative to TB-T2T (Figure 5A). Kimura divergence analysis localizes these expansions to specific divergence ranges (Figure 5D), including the ERV1, DIRS, and ERVK families within the divergence range spanning 11%–35% (data not shown). Such patterns would traditionally be attributed to lineage-specific transposition activity; however, with T2T assemblies resolving large and complex repeat arrays, these patterns could also reflect segmental duplications or other chromosomal rearrangements. These patterns suggest the expansions may be largely attributable to segmental duplication, especially at higher divergence, while the LINE elements at 1%–3% represent more likely candidates for recent insertional activity. While we hypothesize segmental duplication as the predominant mechanism, lineage-specific TE variation over comparable timescales has been documented in closely related taxa (e.g., Calidris sandpipers74), indicating some level of transposition-driven expansion remains plausible. Resolving the relative contributions of these mechanisms requires further study.

rDNA arrays remain among the most challenging repetitive regions to assemble because of their extreme sequence homogeneity and marked copy-number variability, even with current long-read sequencing technologies. Although a complete resolution of these loci was beyond the scope of the present study, we identified rDNA regions at chromosomal positions consistent with previous FISH localizations56,75,76 in both assemblies. The intergenic spacer within the rDNA repeat unit contains kilobase-scale tracts of the SatB satellite. SatB is otherwise sparsely represented in both genomes and is concentrated at a limited number of chromosome termini, including those flanking the rDNA arrays identified here. This organization suggests a non-random association between SatB and rDNA clusters and raises the possibility of functional or evolutionary coupling between these repeat classes. A similar satellite-rDNA association has recently been reported in camelids,77 suggesting that satellite-rDNA colocalization may be a recurring feature in mammalian genomes.

Finally, the continued improvement of both long-read sequencing technologies and genome assembly algorithms, coupled with ever-improving computational power and decreasing sequencing costs, has served to democratize the work of genome assembly. Recent advances in sequencing technologies and assembly algorithms have substantially lowered the barrier to generating high-quality genome assemblies, contributing to the democratization of genome assembly. However, expert manual curation and quality assurance remain important for resolving particularly challenging regions, especially large and highly homogeneous repeat arrays, whose lengths may still exceed those of contemporary ultra-long-read technologies. The improvements made in the two phased haploid assemblies for the horse and donkey presented here are substantial in terms of increased composition of the autosomal and sex chromosomes (11.4% for the horse and 11.5% for the donkey) over their corresponding reference assemblies they are replacing. The result is that accurate, sequence-based analyses of centromeres and repeat regions are now possible that can inform our understanding of the chromosomal evolution events that created the equid species we have today.

Limitations of the study

Extensive inter-individual variation in satellite array length and sequence composition may partially limit the precise localization of CENP-A binding domains in individuals other than the reference. Moreover, short-read ChIP-seq data resolve CENP-A binding domains only at the level of broad domains rather than at fine-scale boundaries.

Experimental model and study participant details

Sample collection

A healthy 20 year old female mule from Cornell University was euthanized under IACUC 1986-0216. Tissues were harvested immediately and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. They were subsequently moved to Texas A&M University for long term storage. A list of the tissues can be found in Table S9, and they can be requested by contacting the authors.

Method details

Karyotyping

Isolation of mesenchymal stem cells

Equine peripheral blood MSCs were isolated from heparinized whole blood samples as previously described.78 In short, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using density gradient centrifugation and seeded in a culture flask with medium to promote MSC growth. After several days in culture, immune cells died off, and MSCs developed in adherent colonies, which were expanded and cryopreserved for use in experiments.

Cell cultures and chromosome preparations

Frozen mesenchymal stem cells of the female mule, were kindly provided by collaborators at Cornell University, Ithaca, NY, USA. Cell cultures and harvest for chromosome preparations were done following standard protocols.79,80 Briefly, the cells were grown in alpha MEM with nucleosides and Glutamax (Gibco), supplemented with 20% FBS (Gemini Bioproducts) at 5% CO2. Semi-confluent (∼60–70%) and actively proliferating cultures were harvested with demecolcine solution (10 μg/mL; Sigma Aldrich), treated with Optimal Hypotonic Solution (Rainbow Scientific), and fixed in 3:1 methanol/acetic acid. The cells were dropped on clean, wet glass slides and checked under phase contrast microscope (×200) for quality.

Karyotyping and cytogenetic analysis

Chromosomes were stained by GTG-banding81 for karyotyping. Images of 20 metaphase spreads were captured and analyzed using Axioplan2 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Inc., Jena, Germany) and IKAROS (MetaSystems GmbH, Altlussheim, Germany) software. The horse and donkey chromosomes were identified and arranged into karyotypes following the International System of Cytogenetic Nomenclature of the domestic Horse82 and GTG-banded chromosome nomenclature of the donkey,56 respectively. These are shown in Figure S1.

Results provided in Data File 1.

Whole-genome sequencing

Sequencing data were generated using multiple platforms. From the F1 mule, PacBio HiFi produced 409 Gb of data (approximately 152× coverage) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies produced 114 Gb (approximately 42× coverage). Illumina whole-genome short-read sequencing generated 186 Gb (approximately 69× coverage). Additional Illumina whole-genome short-read data from the thoroughbred dam and donkey sire yielded 145 Gb and 145 Gb, respectively (approximately ∼54× coverage per haplotype), and were used to support haplotype partitioning and validation. Omni-C data generated from the F1 mule (386 million pair read/116 Gb) were used for chromosome-scale scaffolding. Detailed sequencing statistics are provided in Table S10.

Genome assembly

Genome survey

To ensure high-quality Illumina sequencing data, we performed trimming and quality assessment as follows: We used Trim Galore (version 0.6.6) to preprocess the raw mule Illumina whole genome shotgun sequencing data. Trim Galore, which is based on Cutadapt (version 3.5. Cutadapt removes adapter sequences from high-throughput sequencing reads), removes low-quality bases, sequencing adapters, and other redundant sequences. The trimming was performed with the following parameters: A quality threshold of 20 was applied to remove bases with lower quality scores. Reads shorter than 20 bp after trimming and their mates were discarded to ensure the effectiveness of downstream analyses.

Following trimming, we assessed the data quality using FastQC (version v0.12.1, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). FastQC provides detailed quality reports, including base quality distribution, GC content, and sequence length distribution. This quality control step was crucial for verifying the accuracy of the input data and minimizing potential biases in downstream analyses.

We utilized Meryl83 (version 1.3) to create a k-mer database from the cleaned Illumina sequencing data of the mule genome. K-mers of length 31 were selected, and Meryl was used to calculate the frequency of each k-mer in the dataset, resulting in a k-mer frequency histogram. The k-mer frequency histogram was then used as input for GenomeScope84 2.0, which applies a k-mer-based statistical approach to estimate genome heterozygosity(1.23%), repeat content(0.697), and genome size(haploid = 2,757,299,182 bp) from sequencing reads (Figure S8).

Contig level assembly

For the initial assembly phase, we used the Hi-C mode of Verkko85 (version 1.4.1) to construct the mule genome. Verkko’s Hi-C mode integrates multiple types of sequencing data to enhance assembly accuracy and contiguity. The input data included high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads from PacBio, Oxford Nanopore Technology ultra long (UL-ONT) reads, and Dovetail Omni-C data, all derived from the mule genome. In this version of Verkko, Omni-C data is used exclusively for phasing within contigs. Consequently, despite the Omni-C sequencing data originating from Mule, we were unable to initially obtain distinguishable haplotype contigs for either the Thoroughbred Horse or the Donkey. The sequencing data coverage is reported in Table S10.

BUSCO and Merqury analyses are presented in Data File 1.

Assembly polishing

We utilized Inspector software86 to correct small-scale assembly errors (<50 bp) by replacement and local re-assembly of regions identified with structural errors (≥50 bp). This resulted in a high-accuracy assembly of the mixed two haplotypes. Using second-generation sequencing data from both parents and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK version 4.4.0.0),87 we distinguished the horse and donkey genomes by aligning the horse dam, and donkey sire to the mule assembly and sorting horse vs. donkey based on the fewest homozygous differences. Contigs containing mitochondrial contamination were discarded based on the depth and length of the original data returned to assembly. Then two haplotype genomes were polished using Pilon with clean trimmed parental Illumina whole genome shotgun sequencing data.88 The trimming process and tools are the same with raw mule Illumina data. The polished scaffolds underwent gap filling using ONT data with RagTag89 and RFfiller.90 The nearly chromosome-level scaffolds were finally curated using Hi-C data with PretextView software.91

Mitochondrial genome assembly

Taking advantage of the long length and high accuracy of HiFi data, assembling the mitochondrial genome was straightforward, as many reads in the HiFi dataset easily span the entire mitochondrial genome. Assembly with MitoHiFi92 begins by providing the NCBI Taxonomy ID (in this case, 9796), Mitochondria Reference Finder locates the most closely related reference mitochondrial genome in the NCBI database. It extracts HiFi reads aligned to this reference genome, removes reads exceeding a certain length threshold to avoid the influence of Nuclear Mitochondrial DNA Sequences, and then assembles the reads using Hifiasm.93 The resulting contigs are circularized, redundant sequences are removed from the assembly, and the chrMT is annotated.

Mitochondrial reads were a component of the Illumina whole genome sequencing data of the Dam Thoroughbred Horse. Therefore, it is unwise to use the Pilon and the horse’s whole genome sequencing data to polish the horse genome before the assembly and isolation of the mitochondrial reads. This may introduce over-polishing of the NUMT region. Thus, we recommend that researchers using the same assembly process should perform mitochondrial assembly and eliminate reads aligned to the mitochondrial genome before the polishing steps.

IsoSeq mapping and evaluation

14 full length non-chimeric, clustered IsoSeq datasets from banked mule tissues (spleen, pituitary, lung, cerebellar vermis, PBMC, long bone marrow, loin adipose, heart left ventricle, heart left atrium, lamina front, adrenal cortex, kidney cortex, hypothalamus, and esophagus) were collected to aid in annotation and transcription of this assembly. The “Mule” genome was created from concatenating the TB-T2T, and EquAss_T2T_v2 chromosomes and contigs together to encompass both haploid assemblies. IsoSeq mapping was conducted using minimap2 version 2.22-r110194 with the following parameters: “-I 8g -ax splice:hq -uf” as indicated by the minimap guide. “-I 8g” was utilized to indicate an index batch size of equal to 8 GB which controls memory usage during indexing and was required due to the large size of the “Mule” genome. The parameters “-ax splice:hq -uf” indicate a spliced alignment preset for high quality IsoSeq reads and the input is full length nonchimeric reads. After the reads were mapped and aligned using minimap2, creating a bam file, SAMTOOLS version 1.1395 was used to sort the alignments for downstream analysis and index the newly created bam file.

For evaluation of our IsoSeq mapped dataset, we decided to indicate the number of mapped and unmapped reads for each tissue. Read counts were conducted utilizing SAMTOOLS version 1.20 to account for bitwise flags. The parameter “samtools view file.bam -F 4 -F 256 -F 2048” noted the number of reads that mapped for the IsoSeq dataset whereas “samtools view file.bam -f 4” was used to count the number of reads that do not map and align to combined “Mule” assembly for our IsoSeq dataset.

More detail is provided in Data File 1.

Centromere and satellite DNA annotation

Cell lines

Primary fibroblast cell lines from horse and donkey were previously described.7,17,18,22 The cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 2% non-essential amino acids. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Identification and analysis of satellite DNA loci

Identification of satellite DNA loci was performed with RepeatMasker (Galaxy Version 4.0.9) available at the Galaxy web platform (Community 2022) using a library made by the consensus sequences of horse and donkey satellite DNA families.17,24,25 The monomer size and the consensus sequence of each satellite are reported in Table S2. Given the high sequence degeneration of the 2PI satellite, multiple consensus sequences of varying lengths were used. Satellite regions of each family located within 3000 bp were merged using Bedtools v2.30.0. Satellite sequences were annotated only in the chromosomal sequences. For the horse TB-T2T assembly, the unplaced contigs NW_027222394.1, NW_027222395.1, NW_027222392.1, NW_027222391.1, NW_027222343.1 were also included in the analysis. Telomeric repeats were identified by searching the (TTAGGG) 4 query by BLASTN using -evalue 10 -word_size 11 -gapopen 5 -gapextend 2 -reward 2 -penalty −3 -outfmt 7 parameters. Resulting matches located within 250 bp of each other were merged into single loci using Bedtools v2.30.0. For each satellite DNA region, sequence identity heatmaps were generated using ModDotPlot96 with --bin-freq mode.

ChIP-seq with anti-CENP-A antibody and downstream bioinformatic analysis

Throughout the manuscript, individual animals are referred to using short labels (Twilight, AH1, AH2, AH3, AH4, Horse A-S and Donkey A-D). Twilight, AH1, AH2, AH3, AH4, Horse A, Horse C and Horse D are Thoroughbred horses; for the remaining horse and donkey individuals, breed information was not available.

For Horse G, Donkey C and Donkey D, chromatin from primary fibroblasts was cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, extracted and sonicated to obtain DNA fragments ranging from 200 to 800 bp. Immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described8 using an anti-CENP-A serum.12 Paired-end sequencing was performed with Illumina HiSeq2500 platform by IGA Technology Services (Udine, Italy). CENP-A ChIP-seq reads from the other individuals were previously described8,16,17 and deposited in NCBI SRA Archive: Twilight (SRX19696555, SRX19696554), AH1 (SRX19696520, SRX19696519), AH2 (SRX19696543, SRX19696529), AH3 (SRX19696552, SRX19696551), AH4 (SRX19696550, SRX19696536), Horse A (SRX2789324, SRX2789325), Horse C (SRX2789347, SRX2789336), Horse D (SRX2789370, SRX2789369), Horse S (SRX23002276, SRX23002277), Donkey A (SRX2789326, SRX2789371), Donkey B (SRX2789329, SRX2789328). CENP-B ChIP-seq reads were previously described17 and deposited in NCBI SRA Archive: Horse C (SRX22972993, SRX22972992), Donkey B (SRX22972995, SRX22972994). ChIP-seq reads were aligned with Bowtie2 (version 2.4.2) using paired-end mode and default parameters.97 Normalized enrichment peaks were obtained with the bamCompare tool available in the deepTools suite (3.5.0 version)98 using RPKM normalization in subtractive mode. Plots were obtained with pyGenomeTracks (3.6 version).99

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Metaphase spreads were obtained with the standard air-drying procedure. The telomeric probe, a mixture of 1–20 kb-long synthetic (TTAGGG)n fragments was prepared as previously described.100,101 Hybridization to donkey metaphase spreads was carried out as previously described.102 Post-hybridization washes were performed in low stringency conditions at 37°C in 4× SSC 25% formamide, 0.1% Tween 20 in 4× SSC. Chromosomes were counterstained with DAPI. Digital grey-scale images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Scope.A1) equipped with a cooled CCD camera (Photometrics) Pseudo-coloring and merging of images were performed using the IpLab software. Chromosomes were identified by computer-generated reverse DAPI banding according to the published karyotypes.

Comparative genomic analysis

Pairwise alignments between whole horse and donkey orthologous chromosomes were performed with Chromeister (version 1.5a)103 using default parameters.

Repeat annotation

Repeat annotation was performed using RepeatMasker v4.1.7-p1 in species mode (-species Equus) with the sensitive flag enabled (-s) and configured to use the Dfam 3.8 full root partition as the library (dfam38_full.0.h5, 72GB uncompressed). Kimura-corrected divergence values for transposable element classes (LINE, SINE, LTR, DNA transposons) were obtained from RepeatMasker.align output files. Divergence values were calculated using the Kimura 2-parameter model with CpG correction to account for hypermutability at CpG dinucleotide sites, and binned at 1% divergence intervals from 0 to 50% using the parseRM Perl script.104

rDNA annotation

Horse 18S (NR_046271.1), 28S (NR_046309.3) and 5.8S (XR_002802446.1) rRNA sequences were used as queries in BLASTN v2.11.0+ searches against the horse and donkey genome assemblies(Tables S6 and S7). Only hits with alignment lengths longer than 1,500 bp (18S), 650 bp (28S) and 120 bp (5.8S) were retained for downstream analyses. More detail is provided in Data File 1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

No formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed in this study. Quantitative analyses were primarily descriptive and based on genome assembly and annotation metrics. Genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content were estimated using k-mer–based approaches implemented in Meryl and GenomeScope. Assembly quality was assessed using standard metrics including contig N50, base-level accuracy (QV), and BUSCO completeness scores. Read mapping statistics, coverage distributions, and repeat annotations were calculated using SAMtools, RepeatMasker, and custom scripts as described in the method details. Structural variant counts and distributions were derived from genome alignments. All analyses were conducted using default parameters unless otherwise specified in the corresponding sections. No statistical tests were used to compare groups.
