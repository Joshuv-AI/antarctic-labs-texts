Abstract
. We prove the Kato square root estimate for second-order divergence form elliptic operators − div ( A ∇ ) -\Divv(A\nabla) on a bounded, locally uniform domain D ⊆ ℝ n D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} , for accretive coefficients A ∈ L ∞ ​ ( D , ℂ n ) A\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{C}^{n}) , under the Robin boundary condition ν ⋅ A ∇ u + b u = 0 \nu\cdot A\nabla u+bu=0 for a (possibly unbounded) boundary conductivity b b . In contrast to essentially all previous estimates of Kato square root operators, no first-order approach seems possible for the Robin boundary conditions.

1.
Introduction

This paper concerns estimates for the Kato square root
\sqrt{L}
of divergence form second-order elliptic operators
L=-\Divv(A\nabla)
. The estimate

(1)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{L}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\approx\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

conjectured by Kato
[
26
]
, was eventually proved in the seminal work
[
5
]
. Following this breakthrough, there has been many generalizations, for example to domains
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, imposing Dirichlet, Neumann or mixed such boundary conditions, to manifolds, to degenerate/unbounded coefficients
A
, to more general elliptic systems, and to parabolic operators. See for example
[
27
,
10
,
11
,
8
,
7
,
4
,
1
,
23
,
19
,
17
,
14
]
.

Although the estimate (
1
) seems to concern second-order PDEs, it can be argued that it actually concerns first-order elliptic systems. Indeed, (
1
) readily follows from the boundedness of a generalized Cauchy singular integral operator
\sgn(BD)
, where

(2)

\displaystyle B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&A\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad D=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\Divv\\
\nabla&0\end{bmatrix}.

In dimension
n=1
this observation goes back to
[
6
]
, and after the Kato square root estimate (
1
) was established in full generality in
[
5
]
, the framework from
[
6
]
was generalized in
[
9
]
to arbitrary dimension. See
[
3
]
for a simplified presentation of this first-order approach. Although the generalized first-order Cauchy–Riemann system
BD
looks more complicated than the second-order operator
L
, with essentially the same techniques one can more generally prove bounds

(3)

\displaystyle\|f(BD)u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

for all operators
f(BD)
in the holomorphic functional calculus of
BD
. This has important applications for example to the study of boundary value problems. See
[
3
,
7
,
2
]
.

In all previous works on Kato square root estimates, to the best of our knowledge (and possible with the exception of
[
23
]
),
it has been possible either to do a second-order proof (roughly following the original proof
[
5
]
), or to do a first-order proof (roughly following
[
9
]
or
[
3
]
).
Also for Kato square root estimates on domains, both choices have been available
[
10
,
15
,
21
]
.
For Dirichlet boundary conditions one uses

(4)

\displaystyle D=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\Divv\\
\nabla_{0}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
\dom(\nabla_{0})=\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{0}(D)
and
-\Divv=\nabla_{0}^{*}
. For Neumann boundary conditions, one uses

(5)

\displaystyle D=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\Divv_{0}\\
\nabla&0\end{bmatrix},

where
\dom(\nabla)=\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
and
-\Divv_{0}=\nabla^{*}
. If the domain
D
is sufficiently regular, one finds
\dom(\Divv_{0})=\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\colon\Divv(f)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D),\nu\cdot f|_{\partial D}=0\}
, where
\nu
denotes the outwards normal to
D
. Even for mixed Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions one can define a self-adjoint operator
D
by putting the Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\nabla
and the Neumann boundary conditions on
\Divv
.

In the present paper, we prove the Kato square root estimate on domains
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
under Robin boundary conditions (formally) given by

(6)

\displaystyle\nu\cdot A\nabla u+bu=0\quad\text{on }\partial D.

We recall that when Fourier’s law is used to model heat equilibrium by
\Divv(A\nabla u)=0
, then
A
is the conductivity in
D
,
while
b\geq 0
is the conductivity across the boundary
\partial D
. The extreme case
b=0
means that
D
is an insulated body and corresponds to Neumann boundary conditions, while the limit
b\to\infty
means that
u|_{\partial D}
must equal the outside temperature
0
.

Unlike for previous works, for the Robin boundary conditions we are forced to do a second-order proof of the Kato square root estimate. Indeed, it is not clear to us even how to define a first-order self-adjoint operator
D
which is relevant for the Robin boundary conditions. Also our second-order proof here presents new challenges since the Robin form

(7)

\displaystyle a(u,v)=\iint_{D}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial D}b\gamma(u)\overline{\gamma(v)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

contains a boundary term that needs to be converted to a boundary condition for the Robin operator
L
.
At the technical level, a main novelty in the proof is Lemma
5.6
, which allows us to reduce the Kato square root estimate to a square function estimate for a family of operators acting only on functions in the interior
D
. Our main result is Theorem
3.8
, where the Robin boundary conditions
\nu\cdot A\nabla u+bu=0
are imposed in a weak sense on a subset
\Gamma\subseteq\partial D
of dimension
d
,
n-2<d<n
, of the boundary, and Neumann boundary conditions on the remainder of
\partial D
.

2.
Geometry and function spaces

Fix the dimension
n\geq 2
. We work in an open, bounded and possibly non-connected set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fix a (relatively) open, non-empty subset
\Gamma\subseteq\partial D
.
Throughout, we distinguish integrals in the interior and on the boundary by writing
\iint_{D}\,\mathrm{d}x
and
\int_{\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
. To save space, we occasionally drop
\,\mathrm{d}x
and
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
from the notation.

We introduce some notions from geometric measure theory in order to ensure some crucial properties for
D
and
\Gamma
as well as for function spaces on them.

Definition 2.1

(Locally uniform)
.

Call
D
locally uniform, if there exist
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\delta\in(0,\infty]
and
c\in(0,\infty]
such that the following hold:

(i)

All points
x,y\in D
with
|x-y|<\delta
can be joined in
D
by an
\varepsilon
-cigar
, that is to say, a rectifiable curve
c\subseteq D
of length
\ell(c)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}|x-y|
such that

(8)

\displaystyle\dist(z,\partial D)\geq\frac{\varepsilon|z-x||z-y|}{|x-y|},\quad z\in c.

(ii)

All connected components
D^{\prime}
of
D
satisfy
\diam(D^{\prime})\geq c
.

Remark 2.2
.

When
D
is connected, the definition coincides with the one of Jones
[
25
]
. He has studied properties of Sobolev spaces on
D
, including extension and density results. The non-connected case is a by-product of
[
12
]
.

If a set

is locally uniform, then it is in particular interior thick in the sense of the following definition, see
[
14
, Prop. 2.9]
. Interior thickness will be important to ensure standard properties of function spaces on
D
on the one hand, and to ensure the existence of a dyadic structure on
D
on the other hand, see Section
5.4
for the latter.

Definition 2.3

(Interior thickness)
.

Call
O
interior thick if the following holds true:

(ITC)

\displaystyle\exists c\in(0,1]\colon\;\forall x\in D,r\in(0,1]\colon\quad|D\cap\mathrm{B}(x,r)|\geq c|\mathrm{B}(x,r)|.

For the boundary part
\Gamma
, we introduce the following.

Definition 2.4

(
d
-set)
.

Call
\Gamma
a
d
-set for
d\in(0,n]
if the following holds true:

(9)

\displaystyle\exists c\in(0,1]\colon\;\forall x\in\Gamma,r\in(0,1]\colon\quad cr^{d}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma\cap\mathrm{B}(x,r))\leq c^{-1}r^{d}.

Throughout the article, we impose the following geometric setting.

Assumption D
.

The pair
(D,\Gamma)
is supposed to satisfy the following:

(i)

The open set
D
is supposed to be locally uniform in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

(ii)

The set
\Gamma
is an
d
-set for some
d\in(n-2,n)
.

It ensures properties of function spaces on

as well as the existence and boundedness of trace operator.

The space
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
used in the formulation of the Kato square root estimate is the usual Sobolev space of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
functions whose distributional gradient belongs again to
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
. We introduce further spaces of

type in the subsequent definition.
To this end, recall the scale of Bessel potential spaces
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and

. First, if
t\geq 0
define the space
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)
by

\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)\coloneqq\{u|_{D}\colon u\in\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

and equip it with the quotient norm. Here, the operation
|_{D}
is the distributional restriction
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to D
. Second, if
t<0
, define the space
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)
as the topological anti-dual space
1
1

1

That is, the space of conjugate-linear bounded functionals.
of
\mathrm{H}^{-t,r^{\prime}}(D)
.

By construction,
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)
is a Banach space (Hilbertian if

),
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)\cap\mathrm{C}(\overline{D})
is dense in
\mathrm{H}^{t,r}(D)
for all

and

, and the usual Sobolev embeddings apply.
If

, then

with dense inclusion.
Moreover, as

.

Since

is locally uniform, it is a Sobolev extension domain

,
12
]
. This leads to compatibility between the
\mathrm{W}
and
\mathrm{H}
spaces.

Lemma 2.6
.

For
r\in(1,\infty)
, the spaces
\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)
and
\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(D)
coincide with equivalence of norms.

Proof.

Recall
\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
First, let
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)
and write
\mathcal{E}
for the bounded extension operator
\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)\to\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
introduced in
[

]
. Then

(10)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(D)}\leq\|\mathcal{E}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\mathcal{E}u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)},

which gives the inclusion
\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(D)
. Second, let
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(D)
and let

be any function with

. Calculate

(11)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)}\leq\|U\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|U\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Taking the infimum over all extensions

, which gives the converse inclusion
\mathrm{H}^{1,r}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(D)
.
∎

As for the boundary, for
r\in[1,\infty]
put
\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Gamma,\sigma)
, where
\sigma
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. Note that

is has positive and finite

Boundedness of the trace operator in our geometric setting was investigated in

Proposition 2.7

(Trace operator)
.

Let
r\in[2,\tfrac{2d}{n-2})
and

.
Then, the trace operator
\gamma
onto
\Gamma
, originally defined on
\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)\cap\mathrm{C}(\overline{D})

Note that, owing to Lemma

3.
Elliptic operators with Robin boundary conditions

We define elliptic operators with Robin boundary conditions and collect some important properties of them.
Also, we state our main result in Theorem
3.8
.
Let
A\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})
and
b\in\mathrm{L}^{\tilde{q}}(\Gamma)
.
It is possible to include lower-order terms, but we stick to the pure second-order case for the sake of readability.
Note that, using
d>n-2
and
n\geq 2
, the denominator
d+2-n
in the definition of
\tilde{q}
is strictly positive and
\tilde{q}>1
.

The following lemma relates
\tilde{q}
to boundedness properties of the trace operator.

Lemma 3.1
.

There are
q\in[2,\infty)
and
s_{*}\in(0,1)
satisfying

(12)

\displaystyle 1=\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}+\frac{2}{q},\qquad s_{*}=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{q},

for which the trace operator
\gamma
from Proposition
2.7
is
\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)
bounded for all
s\in(s_{*},1]
.

Proof.

Define
q
and
s_{*}
via the relations in the statement.
Since

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{q}=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}\Bigr)<\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\frac{n-2}{d}=1

on the one hand, and

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{q}=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{2\tilde{q}}\geq\frac{n-d}{2}>0

on the other hand. Hence,
s_{*}\in(0,1)
.
For the boundedness of the trace operator, we apply Proposition
2.7
with
r\coloneqq q
and
s\in(s_{*},1]
.
Indeed,
s
is admissible by definition,
q\geq 2
follows from
\tilde{q}\leq\infty
, and we have
\frac{1}{q}>\frac{n-2}{2d}
by rearranging the terms in (
13
), which concludes the proof.
∎

Throughout, fix some
s\in(s_{*},1)
. Note that the interval
(s_{*},1)
is non-empty by virtue of Lemma
3.1
.

Define the sesquilinear form
a
on
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
by

(a)

\displaystyle a(u,v)\coloneqq\iint_{D}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}b\gamma(u)\cdot\overline{\gamma(v)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\qquad(u,v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)).

Recall that
\sigma
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

Lemma 3.2
.

The form
a\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\to\mathbb{C}
is bounded.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the boundary term. In accordance with Lemma
3.1
, we use Hölder’s inequality and boundedness of the trace operator, to get

(15)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\Gamma}b\gamma(u)\cdot\overline{\gamma(v)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\Bigr|\leq\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\tilde{q}}(\Gamma)}\|\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)}\|\gamma(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)}\lesssim\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\tilde{q}}(\Gamma)}\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)}\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)}.

By virtue of the embedding
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)
, this completes the proof.
∎

To ensure ellipticity, we assume for some
\lambda>0
the inhomogeneous Gårding inequality

(E)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}a(u,u)\geq\lambda\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\qquad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Example 3.3
.

If
A\colon D\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are elliptic coefficients and
b\colon\Gamma\to(0,\infty)
, then (
E
) holds. The argument is similar to Step 1 of the proof of Proposition
4.1
. Details are left to the reader.

We define first a weak formulation of an elliptic operator with Robin boundary conditions.

Definition 3.4

(Elliptic operator with Robin BC – weak formulation)
.

Define the operator
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\to(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D))^{*}
by the relation

(16)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle=a(u,v),\qquad u,v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Note that
\mathcal{L}
is invertible by virtue of (
E
).

Rewriting the Hölder relation in Lemma
3.1
gives
\tfrac{1}{\tilde{q}}+\tfrac{1}{q}=\tfrac{1}{q^{\prime}}
. Keeping this in mind, introduce the generalized gradient operators
S
by

(17)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma),

and the coefficient matrix

(18)

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}A&\\
&b\end{bmatrix}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Gamma).

In terms of

and

(19)

\displaystyle a(u,v)=\langle BSu,Sv\rangle.

The pairing

denotes the direct sum of the

or

Consequently, we find the factorization identity

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=S^{*}BS.

To simplify notation, put

, where
\nabla\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}
is the usual distributional gradient. The operator

acts as a bounded operator
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\to(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D))^{*}
.
With this notation at hand, we can write

(21)

\displaystyle S^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}-\Div&\gamma^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

Example 3.5
.

Let
F\colon D\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
g\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
and
u\colon D\to\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
F,g,u
and

are smooth. Then

(22)

\displaystyle\langle S^{*}(F,g),u\rangle=\iint_{D}\nabla F\cdot\overline{\nabla u}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial D}(\nu\cdot F+\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}g)\overline{\gamma(u)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Hence, the range of

contains distributions in

and on
\partial D
. On

, the contributions from
\nu\cdot F
and

Now, we restrict
\mathcal{L}
to a closed, unbounded operator in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
.

Definition 3.6

(Elliptic operator with Robin BC – strong formulation)
.

Define the unbounded operator
L
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
as follows: if
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
,

, then

(23)

\displaystyle u\in\dom(L),\;Lu=f\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\colon\;\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle=\iint_{D}f\overline{v}\,\mathrm{d}x.

As is illustrated by Example

, the operator

satisfies Robin boundary conditions on

Remark 3.7
.

The choice
A=\Id
and
b=1
leads to a Laplacian

with certain Robin boundary conditions.
In its weak formulation, it is simply given by

. We will use this operator as an unperturbed smoothing operator for

later on.
Since

is self-adjoint, it satisfies the Kato square root property.

By classical form theory,

is closed, densely defined, maximal accretive and

. In particular, it possesses a unique maximal accretive square root

, which is the operator appearing in Theorem

below. The operator

is associated with the adjoint form

. Note that

is self-adjoint if and only if

(Kato square root property for Robin BC)

Let

satisfies Assumption

Let

and

, where

.
Assume that the Robin form

along with the estimate

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{L}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\approx\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}\approx\sqrt{\operatorname{Re}a(u,u)},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D),

3.1.
Off-diagonal estimates

We establish off-diagonal estimates for elliptic operators with Robin boundary conditions. Since
\gamma(u\phi)=\gamma(u)\gamma(\phi)
for suitable functions
u
and
\phi
, the proof is similar to the proof for Neumann boundary conditions.

Proposition 3.9

(Off-diagonal estimates)
.

There exist constants
C
and
c
depending on
\lambda
,
\|A\|_{\infty}
and the dimension, such that for all measurable sets
E,F\subseteq D
, all
t>0
and all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
with
\supp(f)\subseteq E
, we have for
u\coloneqq(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}f
the estimate

(25)

\displaystyle\left(\iint_{F}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\int_{\overline{F}\cap\Gamma}|t\gamma(u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}+\left(\iint_{F}|tu|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\iint_{F}|t\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq{}C\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{\dist(E,F)}{t}}\left(\iint_{E}|f|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
, let
E,F\subseteq D
measurable, and put
d\coloneqq\dist(E,F)
.
Introduce a threshold
\alpha>0
to be determined later. We divide the proof into the cases
d\leq\alpha t
and
d>\alpha t
. Note that the first case simply states boundedness, whereas the second case establishes genuine off-diagonal decay.

Step 1
: boundedness. Note that this step takes in particular care of the case
d\leq\alpha t
. Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
and set
u\coloneqq(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}f\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
. Using the Gårding inequality (
E
) and maximal accretivity, calculate

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda\|tu\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2}\leq t^{2}\operatorname{Re}a(u,u)=\operatorname{Re}\langle t^{2}Lu,u\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq\|t^{2}Lu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq 2\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}.

Taking the square root yields
\|tu\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}
.
In conjunction with boundedness of the trace operator and maximal accretivity, we deduce

(28)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}+\|t\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)}+\|tu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}+\|t\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)},

which concludes this step.

Step 2
: off-diagonal bounds.
Assume
d>\alpha t
, otherwise nothing has to be shown.
Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
with
\supp(f)\subseteq E
.
Note that
E
and
F
are disjoint owing to
d>\alpha t>0
.
Moreover, let
\rho\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(D)
satisfy
\rho=1
on
F
,
\rho=0
on
E
, and

(29)

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{\infty}+d\|\nabla\rho\|_{\infty}\leq C,

where
C
is a dimensional constant.
Put
u\coloneqq(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}f
, so that
(1+t^{2}L)u=f
.
Test this equation with
v\coloneqq u\eta^{2}
, where
\eta\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d\rho}{\alpha t}}-1
, to find

(30)

\displaystyle\iint_{D}f\overline{v}=\iint_{D}u\overline{v}+t^{2}a(u,v).

Note that indeed
v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
, which uses that
\eta
is bounded and smooth with bounded derivatives for fixed
t
. Also, taking
d>\alpha t
into account, observe the following properties of
\eta
:

(31)

\displaystyle\eta=0\;\;\text{on }E,\quad\eta=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{\alpha t}}-1\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{\alpha t}}\;\;\text{on }\overline{F},\quad|\nabla\eta|=|\frac{d}{\alpha t}\nabla\rho\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d\rho}{\alpha t}}|\leq\frac{C}{\alpha t}|\eta+1|.

Using the identities
\nabla v=\nabla(u\eta)\eta+u\eta\nabla\eta
and
\eta\nabla u=\nabla(u\eta)-u\nabla\eta
, which readily follow from the product rule, we find

(32)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pta(u,v)-a(\eta u,\eta u)=

\displaystyle-\iint_{D}uA\nabla\eta\cdot\overline{\nabla(\eta u)}+\iint_{D}\overline{u}A\nabla(\eta u)\cdot\nabla\eta-\iint_{D}|u|^{2}A\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\eta.

Note that the boundary term does not produce any error terms owing to the observation mentioned just before the proposition.
Due to the support properties of
f
and
\eta
, the left-hand side of (
30
) vanishes. Hence, by using (
32
), the identity
u\overline{v}=|\eta u|^{2}
and rearranging terms, we see

(33)

\displaystyle\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+t^{2}a(\eta u,\eta u)

(34)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle t^{2}\iint_{D}uA\nabla\eta\cdot\overline{\nabla(\eta u)}-t^{2}\iint_{D}\overline{u}A\nabla(\eta u)\cdot\nabla\eta+t^{2}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}A\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\eta.

Then, boundedness of the trace operator (with constant
C_{\Gamma}>0
), followed by the Gårding inequality (
E
), yield

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2C_{\Gamma}^{2}}t^{2}\left(\iint_{D}|\gamma(\eta u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}t^{2}\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+|\nabla(\eta u)|^{2}\\
\leq{}&\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+\lambda t^{2}\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+|\nabla(\eta u)|^{2}\\
\leq{}&\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+t^{2}\operatorname{Re}a(\eta u,\eta u)\\
\leq{}&2\Lambda t^{2}\iint_{D}|u||\nabla\eta||\nabla(\eta u)|+\Lambda t^{2}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2},\end{aligned}

where
\Lambda\coloneqq\|A\|_{\infty}
.
We estimate the terms on the right-hand side of (
35
).
To start with, use (
31
) to calculate

(36)

\displaystyle\Lambda t^{2}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Lambda C^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}|\eta+1|^{2}\leq\frac{2\Lambda C^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+\frac{2\Lambda C^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}.

By taking
\alpha
large depending on
\Lambda
and the dimension, the first-term on the right-hand side can be absorbed into the left-hand side of (
35
).
Using this bound again, Young’s inequality
2ab\leq\varepsilon a^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}b^{2}
reveals

(37)

\displaystyle 2\Lambda t^{2}\iint_{D}|u||\nabla\eta|\nabla(\eta u)|

(38)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{8}t^{2}\iint_{D}|\nabla(\eta u)|^{2}+\frac{8\Lambda^{2}}{\lambda}t^{2}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}

(39)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{8}t^{2}\iint_{D}|\nabla(\eta u)|^{2}+\frac{16\Lambda^{2}C^{2}}{\lambda\alpha^{2}}\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}+\frac{16\Lambda^{2}C^{2}}{\lambda\alpha^{2}}\iint_{D}|u|^{2}.

By taking
\alpha
large depending on
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and the dimension, the first two terms on the right-hand side can be absorbed into the left-hand side of (
35
).
In summary, we have shown so far

(40)

\displaystyle\left(\iint_{D}|\eta u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\int_{\Gamma}|t\gamma(\eta u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}+\left(\iint_{D}|t\eta u|^{2}+|t\nabla(\eta u)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\iint_{D}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that
\eta
is constant on
\overline{F}
, so that
\nabla(\eta u)=\eta\nabla u
and
\gamma(\eta u)=\eta\gamma(u)
. Then, by (
31
), we obtain the bound

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{\alpha t}}\left(\iint_{F}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{\alpha t}}\left(\int_{\overline{F}\cap\Gamma}|t\gamma(u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{\alpha t}}\left(\iint_{F}|tu|^{2}+|t\nabla u)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\iint_{D}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now, the claim follows from Step 1 upon multiplication by
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{d}{\alpha t}}
.
∎

4.
A coercivity property for the Laplacian with Robin BC

In this section, we show a coercivity property for
S^{*}S
. More precisely, we establish coercivity of fractional powers of
S^{*}S
of order slightly above
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
in the scale of Bessel potential spaces on
D
.

Proposition 4.1

(Coercivity of
S^{*}S
)
.

There exists
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that
\dom((S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}})=\mathrm{H}^{1+\alpha,2}(D)
.
In particular, there exists
c>0
such that, for every
v\in\dom((S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}})
, we have the estimate

(42)

\displaystyle\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\geq c\|v\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1+\alpha,2}(D)}.

Remark 4.2
.

Only the coercivity estimate (
42
) is needed to establish the Kato square root estimate.

Recall that
\mathrm{H}^{1,2}(D)=\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
. We start out with a technical lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
t_{0}>0
such that, if
0<t<t_{0}
, then
S^{*}S\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\to(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D))^{*}
restricts/extends to a bounded operator
\mathrm{H}^{1\pm t,2}(D)\to(\mathrm{H}^{1\mp t,2}(D))^{*}
.

Proof.

By definition, we have

(43)

\displaystyle\langle S^{*}Su,v\rangle=\iint_{D}\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(u)\overline{\gamma(v)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\quad u,v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Assume in addition
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1\pm t,2}(D)
and
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1\mp t,2}(D)
. Since the
\mathrm{H}^{\pm t,2}(D)
–
\mathrm{H}^{\mp t,2}(D)
duality extends the inner product on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
, boundedness of the gradient in the scale of Bessel potential spaces on
D
(established in our geometric setting for
t_{0}
small enough in
[
14
, Lem. 4.10]
) yields

(44)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\iint_{D}\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr|\leq\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\pm t,2}(D)}\|\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mp t,2}(D)}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1\pm t,2}(D)}\|v\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1\mp t,2}(D)}.

Arguing similarly in the boundary, this time using boundedness of the trace operator,

(45)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(u)\overline{\gamma(v)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\Bigr|\leq\|\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)}\|\gamma(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1\pm t,2}(D)}\|v\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1\mp t,2}(D)},

provided
t_{0}
is small enough so that
1-t>\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{d}{2}
. Here, we used that
d>n-2
.
Therefore, the claim follows by density.
∎

Now, we can proceed with the proof of the coercivity property.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let
t_{0}
be as in Lemma
4.3
.
The proof divides onto four steps.

Step 1
: there exists
c>0
such that
\langle S^{*}Su,u\rangle\geq c\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(D)}^{2}
.

Note that, since
\langle S^{*}Su,u\rangle=\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}^{2}+\|\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}
, it suffices to show
\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}+\|\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)}\geq c\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}
.
To this end, write
u=(u-(u)_{\Gamma})+(u)_{\Gamma}
, where
(u)_{\Gamma}\coloneqq\fint_{\Gamma}\gamma(u)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
is the mean of
\gamma(u)
over
\Gamma
.
Here, we use that
\sigma(\Gamma)>0
as
\Gamma
is a
d
-set.
First, we have

(46)

\displaystyle\|(u)_{\Gamma}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}=|(u)_{\Gamma}||D|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left(\frac{|D|}{\sigma(\Gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\gamma(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)}

by Jensen’s inequality.
Second, consider the kernel
N
of the bounded functional
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\ni v\mapsto(v)_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{C}
, which is a closed subspace of
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
containing no non-trivial constants. Since the embedding
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
is compact by boundedness of
D
, the following Poincaré inequality holds:

(47)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}},\quad v\in N.

As averaging is a projection, we find
u-(u)_{\Gamma}\in N
.
Consequently, we get

(48)

\displaystyle\|u-(u)_{\Gamma}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|\nabla(u-(u)_{\Gamma})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}=\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}.

Combining both bounds completes this step.

Step 2
: there exists
\varepsilon\in(0,t_{0})
such that
S^{*}S
is
\mathrm{H}^{1\pm t,2}(D)\to(\mathrm{H}^{1\mp t,2}(D))^{*}
invertible for all
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
.

By Step 1 and the Lax–Milgram lemma,
S^{*}S\colon\mathrm{H}^{1,2}(D)\to(\mathrm{H}^{1,2}(D))^{*}
is invertible. Moreover,
S^{*}S
is bounded between the complex interpolation scales
\{\mathrm{H}^{1+t,2}(D)\}_{-t_{0}<t<t_{0}}
and
\{(\mathrm{H}^{1-t,2}(D))^{*}\}_{-t_{0}<t<t_{0}}
. Hence, the claim of this step follows from Sneiberg’s lemma
[
28
]
.

Step 3
: there holds the identity
\dom((S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+t}{2}})=\mathrm{H}^{1+t,2}(D)
for all
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
.

We sketch the argument, further details are presented in
[
14
, Proof of Thm. 1.2]
.
The idea is to decompose

(49)

\displaystyle(S^{*}S)^{-\frac{1+t}{2}}=(S^{*}S)^{-1}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1-t}{2}}.

The left-hand side is an isomorphism onto
\dom((S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+t}{2}})
.
Hence, we need to show that the right-hand side is an isomorphism onto
\mathrm{H}^{1+t,2}(D)
.
We have just established in Lemma
4.3
that
(S^{*}S)^{-1}
is an
\mathrm{H}^{-1+t,2}(D)\to\mathrm{H}^{1+t,2}(D)
isomorphism.
As for
(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1-t}{2}}
, it follows from the self-adjoint square root property, complex interpolation and a duality argument that it is an
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1+t,2}(D)
isomorphism as desired.
Note that these are generic arguments independent of the Robin boundary conditions.
This concludes the proof.

Step 4
: conclusion.

Set
\alpha=\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}
. By Step 3, it only remains to establish the coercivity estimate (
42
). By invertibility of
S^{*}S
, the space
\dom((S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}})
can equivalently be equipped with the homogeneous graph norm. Then, (
42
) is immediate.
∎

5.
Reduction to a Carleson measure estimate

In a series of reduction steps, we first reformulate the validity of Theorem
3.8
in terms of quadratic estimates, which are successively reduced to showing that a certain measure is a Carleson measure. The Carleson measure property is shown in Section
6
.

5.1.
Reduction to a quadratic estimate

The operator family
\Theta_{t}
introduced in the following definition will be the main player in our quadratic estimate.

Definition 5.1
.

For every
t>0
, the operator
\Theta_{t}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
is defined by

(50)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}=t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}S^{*}B.

We show uniform bounds in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
for
\Theta_{t}
in the following result.

Lemma 5.2
.

The operators
\Theta_{t}
act as bounded operators

(51)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)

uniformly for
t>0
.
Moreover, there are constants
C,c\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all measurable sets
E,F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, all
t>0
and all
G\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}
,
g\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)
satisfying
\supp(G)\subseteq E
and
\supp(g)\subseteq\overline{E}
, we have for

u\coloneqq\Theta_{t}\begin{bmatrix}G\\
g\end{bmatrix}

the estimate

(52)

\displaystyle\left(\iint_{F}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{\dist(E,F)}{t}}\left[\left(\iint_{E}|G|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\iint_{\overline{E}\cap\Gamma}|g|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\right].

Proof.

Since
L
and
L^{*}
are of the same type, Proposition
3.9
yields that
tS(1+t^{2}L^{*})^{-1}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)
bounded. Hence, by duality,
t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}S^{*}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}\oplus\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Gamma)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
bounded. Consequently, the first claim of the lemma follows from the mapping property of
B
in (
18
).

Since
B
is local and Proposition
3.9
moreover yields off-diagonal estimates for
tS(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}
with respect to the same norms as above, we deduce off-diagonal estimates for
\Theta_{t}
by the same argument.
∎

It is well-known that Theorem
3.8
is equivalent to the validity of the quadratic estimates

(53)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{\infty}\|tL(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}&\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}\|tL^{*}(1+t^{2}L^{*})^{-1}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}&\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\end{aligned}

see for instance
[
20
, Prop. 12.7]
. Note that
L^{*}
is an elliptic operator with Robin boundary conditions associated with the coefficient matrix
B^{*}
. Since the ellipticity assumptions are invariant under conjugation, it suffices to verify only the first quadratic estimate in (
53
).

Let
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
. Use the decomposition (
20
) to rewrite

(54)

\displaystyle tL(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}u=t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{L}u=t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}S^{*}BSu=\Theta_{t}Su.

Therefore, the quadratic estimate for
L
in (
53
) and thus Theorem
3.8
, are equivalent to the quadratic estimate

(QE)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}Su\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\qquad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

5.2.
Smoothing of the quadratic estimate

As explained in Remark
3.7
, we are going to introduce smoothing in terms of
S^{*}S
into the quadratic estimate (
QE
).

Definition 5.3
.

For
t>0
, define the smoothing operator

(55)

\displaystyle P_{t}\coloneqq(1+t^{2}S^{*}S)^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)).

By simple algebraic manipulations with the smoothing operators, we find the following reduction.

Lemma 5.4

(Introduce smoothing)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(56)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}S(1-P_{t})u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Proof.

Observe the algebraic identity

(57)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}S(1-P_{t})u=t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}S^{*}BSt^{2}S^{*}SP_{t}u=t^{2}\mathcal{L}(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}t(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}P_{t}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u,

where we used that fractional powers of
S^{*}S
commute with
P_{t}
. Note that this argument uses the square root property of
S^{*}S
in the self-adjoint case.
Hence, using maximal accretivity, we find

(58)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}S(1-P_{t})u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\|(t^{2}S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}P_{t}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

The right-hand side is a square-function estimate for the self-adjoint operator
S^{*}S
applied to
(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u
, so the spectral theorem gives

(59)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}S(1-P_{t})u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}.

Using the square root property for the self-adjoint operator
S^{*}S
another time concludes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Reduction to small scales in the interior

So far, we have reduced (
QE
) to the quadratic estimate

(60)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Expanding the operator in the quadratic estimate yields

(61)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}SP_{t}=-t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div A\nabla P_{t}+t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}a\gamma P_{t}.

To ease notation, put

(62)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}\coloneqq-t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div A\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Note that
\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}
inherits off-diagonal estimates from
\Theta_{t}
.
By definition, we have

(63)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}SP_{t}=\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}\nabla P_{t}+t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}b\gamma P_{t}.

In this subsection, we will on the one hand treat the large scales
t\geq 1
in (
60
), and on the other hand reduce matters to the
“interior component”
, meaning the term
\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}
acting only in
D
and not on
\Gamma
.

Lemma 5.5

(Reduction to small scales)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(64)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

We remark that the proof implicitly exploits that
D
is bounded.

Proof.

Using
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
boundedness of
\Theta_{t}
, followed by boundedness of the trace operator, calculate for
t>0
:

(65)

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}+\|\gamma(P_{t}u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)}\lesssim\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}.

Specializing to
t\geq 1
and using the embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1+\alpha,2}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)
, conclude

(66)

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim t^{2\alpha}\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1+\alpha,2}(D)}^{2},

where
\alpha
was introduced in Proposition
4.1
.
Now, using Proposition
4.1
, we obtain

(67)

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim t^{2\alpha}\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}=\|(t^{2}S^{*}S)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}P_{t}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}.

Eventually, quadratic estimates and the square root property for
S^{*}S
yield

(68)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{t}SP_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\|(t^{2}S^{*}S)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}P_{t}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\approx\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2}

as desired.
∎

By virtue of the mapping properties of the trace operator, the quadratic estimate on the boundary is non-singular and can hence be controlled easily.

Lemma 5.6

(Reduction to the interior)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(70)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}a\gamma P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.9
, the resolvent family
(1+t^{2}L^{*})^{-1}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
bounded with norm controlled by
1
, and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}(D)
bounded with norm controlled by
t^{-1}
. Hence, by interpolation, it is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)
bounded with norm controlled by
t^{-s}
, where
s\in(0,1)
was introduced below Lemma
3.1
. Thus, using boundedness of the trace operator as stated in Lemma
3.1
,
\gamma(1+t^{2}L^{*})^{-1}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Gamma)
bounded with norm controlled by
t^{-s}
. Consequently, a duality argument, followed by the mapping properties of the coefficient function
a
and the trace operator, give

(71)

\displaystyle\|t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}b\gamma P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim t^{2(1-s)}\|b\gamma P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Gamma)}^{2}\lesssim t^{2(1-s)}\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)}^{2}.

Next, we use the embedding
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)
and two times the square root property for
S^{*}S
, to give

(72)

\displaystyle\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(D)}\lesssim\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}=\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}=\|P_{t}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}.

Thus,

(73)

\displaystyle\|t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}b\gamma P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim t^{2(1-s)}\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},

and therefore

(74)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\gamma^{*}b\gamma P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}t^{2(1-s)}\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\approx\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},

where we use
1-s>0
in the last step.
This concludes the proof.
∎

5.4.
Dyadic structures and dyadic averaging operator

We introduce a substitute in
D
for the dyadic cubes in Euclidean space, called
“dyadic structure”
, and present basic properties of it. Then, we define the dyadic averaging operator induced by the dyadic structure, and present the Carleson embedding result for it.

The construction of dyadic structures goes back to
[
18
]
. For our formulation here, we combine
[
22
, Thm. 7.4]
with the boundary estimate
[
14
, Lem. 5.2]
and the rescaling argument from
[
24
, Prop. 2.12]
.
Write
\mathrm{Q}(x,\ell)
for the Euclidean cube with center
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and sidelength
\ell>0
parallel to the coordinate axes.

Proposition 5.7

(Dyadic structure)
.

There exists a collection
\{Q^{k}_{i}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in I_{k}\}
of open
sets, where
I_{k}
are countable index sets, and constants
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
\eta
,
C>0
such that the following hold:

(i)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it holds
|D\setminus\cup_{i\in I_{k}}Q^{k}_{i}|=0
.

(ii)

If
\ell\geq k
, then, for each
i\in I_{k}
and each
j\in I_{\ell}
, either
Q^{\ell}_{j}\subseteq Q^{k}_{i}
or
Q^{\ell}_{j}\cap Q^{k}_{i}=\emptyset
.

(iii)

If
\ell\leq k
, then, for each
i\in I_{k}
, there is a unique
j\in I_{\ell}
such that
Q^{k}_{i}\subseteq Q^{\ell}_{j}
.

(iv)

For each
Q^{k}_{i}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i\in I_{k}
, there exists
z^{k}_{i}\in D
such that

\mathrm{Q}(z^{k}_{i},a_{0}2^{-k})\cap D\subseteq Q^{k}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{Q}(z^{k}_{i},a_{1}2^{-k})\cap D.

(v)

If
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i\in I_{k}
, and
t>0
, then

(75)

\displaystyle\bigl|\{x\in Q^{k}_{i}\colon\dist(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Q^{k}_{i})\leq t2^{-k}\}\bigr|\leq Ct^{\eta}|Q^{k}_{i}|.

Note that all properties except (v) work for
D
merely interior thick. For (v), a mild form of boundary regularity, called
porosity
, is needed. Locally uniform domains have a porous boundary.

Definition 5.8

(Dyadic cubes)
.

In the setting of Proposition
5.7
, put
\Box_{2^{-k}}\coloneqq\{Q^{k}_{i}\colon i\in I_{k}\}
. Members of this collection are called
dyadic cubes
of
generation
2^{-k}
. For
t\in(2^{-(k+1)},2^{-k}]
, define
\Box_{t}\coloneqq\Box_{2^{-k}}
. In this case, the
sidelength
of
Q\in\Box_{t}
is
\ell(Q)\coloneqq 2^{-k}
. Finally, the collection
\Box
of all (local)
dyadic cubes
is given by
\bigcup_{k}\Box_{2^{-k}}
.

Using metric arguments, a doubling operation on cubes can be defined.

Definition 5.9

(Double cube)
.

If
Q\in\Box
, then
define the
doubled cube

2Q
via
2Q\coloneqq\{x\in D\colon\dist(x,Q)<\tfrac{1}{2}\ell(Q)\}
.

Using property (iv) of dyadic cubes, interior thickness of
D
, and elementary geometric considerations, we find
|2Q|\lesssim|Q|
.

Definition 5.10
.

For
t\in(0,1]
, define the
dyadic averaging operator

E_{t}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}
by

(76)

\displaystyle E_{t}(F)(x)\coloneqq\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q_{t}}F,

where
Q_{t}
is the unique set in
\Box_{t}
containing
x
, meaningful for almost every
x\in D
.

In order to formulate a boundedness result for the dyadic averaging operator below, we need to introduce the notation of a Carleson measure first.

Definition 5.11
.

Call a Borel measure
\mu
on
D\times(0,1]
a
Carleson measure
if there exists a constant
C
such that

(77)

\displaystyle\mu(R(Q))\leq C|Q|,\quad Q\in\Box.

Here,
R(Q)
denotes the Carleson box over
Q
and is given by
R(Q)\coloneqq(0,\ell(Q)]\times Q\subseteq(0,1]\times D
. The Carleson norm
\|\mu\|_{\mathcal{C}}
of
\mu
is the infimum over all constants
C
for which (
77
) holds.

The following version of the Carleson embedding for the dyadic averaging operator in a dyadic structure can be found in
[
27
, Thm 4.3]
.

Proposition 5.12

(Carleson embedding)
.

If
\mu
is a Carleson measure on
D\times(0,1]
with Carleson norm
\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}}
, then

(78)

\displaystyle\iiint_{D\times(0,1]}|(E_{t}u)(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x,t)\lesssim\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}}\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}.

5.5.
Smoothed principal part approximation

In the previous subsection, we have reduced (
QE
) to the validity of the quadratic estimate

(79)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Next, we reduce this quadratic estimate to its
“smoothed principal part”
. The latter is defined in the subsequent definition.

Using off-diagonal estimates, the operator
\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}
can be extended to a well-defined, linear operator
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(D)^{n}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(D)
uniformly for
t>0
, see
[
5
]
. The subsequent definition uses this extension.

Definition 5.13

(Principal part)
.

Identifying the standard unit vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with their respective constant functions on
D
, define for
t\in(0,1]
the
principal part
of
\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}
by

(80)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\coloneqq(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}(e_{1}),\dots,\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}(e_{n}))\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(D)^{n}.

Remark 5.14
.

For almost every
x\in D
, the principal part
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}(x)
is a linear functional on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
acting as

(81)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}(x)w=\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}(w\mathbf{1}_{D})(x),\quad w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

The following bound is established as in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 5.15
.

For all
t\in(0,1]
and
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}
, we have

(82)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}.

As a further reduction step, for
0<t\leq 1
decompose

(83)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}=(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}.

Lemma 5.16

(Smooth principal part approximation)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(84)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Proof.

We subdivide the proof into two steps.

Step 1
: we have
\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}^{\theta,2}(D)^{n},\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))}\lesssim t^{\theta}
for
t\in(0,1]
and
\theta\in[0,1]
.

We argue by interpolation. To this end, let
t\in(0,1]
and
F\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)^{n}
. We claim the bound

(85)

\displaystyle\bigl\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})F\bigr\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim t\|F\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)^{n}}.

Indeed, this follows from a standard argument using Poincaré’s inequality, which uses in a crucial way the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
off-diagonal estimates for
\Theta_{t}
. For a version that applies to our geometric setting, see for instance
[
15
, Lem. 7.8]
.

Using Lemma
5.15
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
off-diagonal estimates for
\Theta_{t}
, we also obtain the bound

(86)

\displaystyle\bigl\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})F\bigr\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}},\quad F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}.

Then, interpolation between the bounds (
85
) and (
86
) yields the claim of this step.

Step 2
: conclusion using coercivity of
(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
.

Plug the bound from Step 1 with
\theta\coloneqq\alpha
into the quadratic estimate, where
\alpha
was introduced in Proposition
4.1
, to get

(87)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}t^{2\alpha}\|\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\alpha,2}(D)^{n}}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

Using the mapping properties of
\nabla
in the scale of Bessel potential spaces, we have
\|\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\alpha,2}(D)^{n}}\lesssim\|P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1+\alpha,2}(D)}
.
Hence, Proposition
4.1
with
v\coloneqq P_{t}u=(1+t^{2}S^{*}S)^{-1}u\in\dom(S^{*}S)
leads to

(88)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}t^{2\alpha}\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(1+t^{2}S^{*}S)^{-1}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

(89)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\|(t^{2}S^{*}S)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1+t^{2}S^{*}S)^{-1}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

The expression on the right-hand side is dominated by a quadratic estimate for the operator
S^{*}S
, and is hence controlled by
\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}
.
Finally, use the Kato square root property for the self-adjoint operator
S^{*}S
to conclude.
∎

5.6.
Removing the smoothing operator

So far, we have reduced (
QE
) to the quadratic estimate

(90)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla P_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Next, we split

(91)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla P_{t}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla(P_{t}-1)+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla

to remove the smoothing operator
P_{t}
from the quadratic estimate.

Lemma 5.17

(Removing the smoothing operator)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(92)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla(P_{t}-1)u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Proof.

By
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}=(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})E_{t}
and Lemma
5.15
, we can remove
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
from the quadratic estimate.
We start out with the reproducing identity

(93)

\displaystyle E_{t}\nabla(P_{t}-1)u=\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{t}\nabla(P_{t}-1)s^{2}S^{*}S(1+s^{2}S^{*}S)^{-2}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

The proof relies on the usual Schur-type argument: provided

(94)

\displaystyle\|E_{t}s\nabla(P_{t}-1)P_{s}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))}\lesssim\eta(t/s),\quad s\in(0,\infty),t\in(0,1),

holds for a function
\eta\in\mathrm{L}^{1}((0,\infty),\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t})
, then the claim follows from Young’s convolution inequality

(95)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|E_{t}\nabla(P_{t}-1)u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

(96)

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\eta(t)|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\|(s^{2}S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+s^{2}S^{*}S)^{-1}(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s}

(97)

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\|(S^{*}S)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}

(98)

\displaystyle\approx{}

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2}.

Here, we used again the Kato square root property of
S^{*}S
in the last step.

So, it remains to establish (
94
). Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
. First, consider
t\leq s
. Note that

(99)

\displaystyle s\nabla(P_{t}-1)P_{s}=\frac{t}{s}t\nabla P_{t}(P_{s}-1).

Hence, using that the averaging operator is a contraction, calculate

(100)

\displaystyle\|E_{t}s\nabla(P_{t}-1)P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq\|s\nabla(P_{t}-1)P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{s}\|t\nabla P_{t}(P_{s}-1)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}}

(101)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{t}{s}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}.

We also used uniform bounds for
(t\nabla P_{t})_{t>0}
here, which follow from ellipticity.
Now, consider
s\leq t
. Split

(102)

\displaystyle\|E_{t}s\nabla(P_{t}-1)P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq\|E_{t}s\nabla P_{t}P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}+\|E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}

(103)

\displaystyle\leq\|s\nabla P_{t}P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}+\|E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)},

where we used again in the last step that the averaging operator is a contraction. For the first term on the right-hand side, we readily find

(104)

\displaystyle\|s\nabla P_{t}P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq\frac{s}{t}\|t\nabla P_{t}P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\frac{s}{t}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}.

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
102
), we recall the following interpolation inequality, see
[
15
, Lem. 7.11]
for a proof in our geometric setting:

(105)

\displaystyle\left|\fint_{Q}\nabla u\right|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}},\quad t\in(0,1],Q\in\Box_{t},u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Here, the number
\eta
was introduced in Proposition
5.7
.
Also, observing that
E_{t}
is a projection, we note

(106)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}=\|\bigl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\bigr)E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}.

Hence, using the interpolation inequality and Hölder’s inequality for sequence spaces, calculate

(107)

\displaystyle\|E_{t}s\nabla P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}|Q|\left|\fint_{Q}s\nabla P_{s}f\right|^{2}

(108)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\eta}}\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}|Q|\left(\fint_{Q}|sP_{s}f|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}|s\nabla P_{s}f|^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}

(109)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\eta}\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}\left(\int_{Q}|P_{s}f|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\int_{Q}|s\nabla P_{s}f|^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}

(110)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\eta}\|P_{s}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{\eta}\|s\nabla P_{s}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2-\eta}

(111)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\eta}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}.

In summary, we have established (
102
) with
\eta(\tau)\coloneqq C\min(\tau,\tau^{-1}+\tau^{-\eta})
for a suitable absolute constant
C
. This completes the proof.
∎

5.7.
Full principal part approximation and reduction to a Carleson measure property

Combining all reduction steps so far, we have reduced (
QE
) to the quadratic estimate

(112)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

We can bound the left-hand side as follows:

(113)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\iint_{D}|E_{t}\nabla u|^{2}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

Hence, (
112
) follows from the Carleson embedding (Proposition
5.12
) provided we can show that the measure
\mu(t,x)\coloneqq|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure on
D
.

We record the full principal part approximation in the interior for later use.

Proposition 5.18

(Principle part approximation)
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

(114)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D)}^{2},\quad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D).

Proof.

Write

(115)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla=(\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t})\nabla P_{t}+\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}\nabla(1-P_{t})-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}\nabla(1-P_{t})\eqqcolon\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}.

The quadratic estimate corresponding to
\mathrm{I}
was established in Lemma
5.16
. As for
\mathrm{II}
,
\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}\nabla(1-P_{t})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))}\leq\|\Theta_{t}S(1-P_{t})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))}
, so we can invoke Lemma
5.4
.
Finally, the quadratic estimate for
\mathrm{III}
was just established in Lemma
5.17
.
∎

6.
The Carleson measure estimate

The goal of this section is to show that the measure
\mu\coloneqq\|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure on
D
. Owing to the reductions performed in the last section, this will imply our main result (Theorem
3.8
).

The key step is the construction of Tb–type test functions in the subsequent proposition. Since
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
is the principal part of
\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}
, an operator acting only in the interior, the argument follow closely the case with Neumann boundary conditions. Therefore, we will keep this section rather short and refer to the literature for arguments where the Neumann case applies verbatim.

Proposition 6.1

(Tb–type test functions)
.

There is a constant
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that, for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, all unit vectors
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and each cube
Q\in\Box
, there is a test function
b=b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)^{n}
such that the following hold:

\mathrm{(a)}

\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim|Q|
,

\mathrm{(b)}

\operatorname{Re}\left(\xi\cdot\fint_{Q}b\right)\geq 1
,

\mathrm{(c)}

\iiint_{R(Q)}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}(x)(E_{t}b)(x)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\frac{|Q|}{\varepsilon^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a unit vector,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
Q\in\Box
. Write
\ell
for the sidelength of
Q
. Fix a cutoff function
\eta\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(2Q)
satisfying
\eta\equiv 1
on
Q
and
\|\eta\|_{\infty}+\ell\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}\leq C
, where
C
is a dimensional constant, and set
\Phi(x)\coloneqq\eta(x)(x-x_{Q})\cdot\overline{\xi}
.
The important properties of
\Phi
are

(116)

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(D),\quad|\nabla\Phi(x)|\lesssim\mathbf{1}_{2Q}(x),\quad\nabla\Phi=\overline{\xi}\text{ on }Q.

Now, define the test function
b=b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon}
by

(117)

\displaystyle b\coloneqq 2\nabla(1+(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}L)^{-1}\Phi.

Observe the identity

(118)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}b-\nabla\Phi=-(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}\nabla\bigl(1+(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}\mathcal{L}\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal{L}\Phi=(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}\nabla\bigl(1+(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}\mathcal{L}\bigr)^{-1}\Div(A\nabla\Phi).

To obtain (a), use the second property in (
116
) and (
118
) to give

(119)

\displaystyle\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim\|b-2\nabla\Phi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|\nabla\Phi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim\|A\nabla\Phi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|\nabla\Phi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim|Q|,

with implicit constants independent of
\varepsilon
and
\ell
. Note that we also used uniform bounds for the family
(t^{2}\nabla(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div)_{t>0}
here, see

, Proof of Lem. 2.2]

For (b), use the third property in (

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}b-2\overline{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=\fint_{Q}b-2\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x=2(\varepsilon\ell)^{2}\fint_{Q}\nabla u,

where

. By uniform boundedness of

and

in conjunction with the second property in (

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon\ell}|Q|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\lesssim|Q|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore, (

(122)

\displaystyle\left|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}b-2\overline{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{4}\ell^{4-\eta}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\eta}.

Consequently, since
\xi
is a unit vector,

(123)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left(\xi\cdot\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}b\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=2+\operatorname{Re}\left(\xi\cdot\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q}b-2\overline{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\geq 2-\sqrt{C\varepsilon^{\eta}},

where

). Taking

concludes the proof of (b).

As for (c), use Proposition

(124)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iiint_{R(Q)}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}(x)(E_{t}b)(x)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\ell}\Bigl(\|(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}E_{t}-\widetilde{\Theta}_{t})b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\Bigr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

(125)

\displaystyle\leq C\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\ell}\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

(126)

\displaystyle\leq C|Q|+\int_{0}^{\ell}\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t},

where we used (a) in the last step. To control the last term, expand

(127)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}b

\displaystyle=-2t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div\bigl(A\nabla(1+\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{2}L)^{-1}\Phi\bigr)

(128)

\displaystyle=2t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{L}(1+\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{2}L)^{-1}\Phi

\displaystyle=-2t(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\bigl((1+\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div\bigr)A\nabla\Phi.

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{t}b\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{2}}\|A\nabla\Phi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{2}}|Q|,

as desired.
