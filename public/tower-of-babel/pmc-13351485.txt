Energy Transfer in Nanodroplet‐Based Luminescent Solar Concentrators
Authors: Lorenzo Vallan, Julia W. Mikurenda, Alex Parra Sánchez‐Camacho, Maria Lili, Sebastian J. M. Robben, Stefan C. J. Meskers, Michael G. Debije, Claudio Roscini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLuminescent solar concentrators are promising devices for building‐integrated photovoltaics, but their efficiency is limited by aggregation‐induced quenching at high fluorophore concentrations and reabsorption losses during lightguide propagation. Here, we present a nanoconfinement strategy that addresses these challenges by embedding organic dyes within oil nanodroplets dispersed in polyvinyl alcohol coatings. The use of six commercial dyes demonstrated the versatility of this approach, which allows millimolar dye loadings while preventing aggregation, resulting in LSCs with excellent optical quality, low haze, and high optical efficiency. We further demonstrated Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) systems by co‐encapsulating donor‐emitter pairs within single nanodroplets. Time‐resolved spectroscopy confirmed efficient energy transfer, while measurements on LSCs with longer optical paths revealed that intradroplet FRET reduces distance‐dependent losses. This sustainable and scalable approach, utilizing only commercial materials and simple processing techniques, offers a practical and cost‐effective preparation of efficient and aesthetically suitable LSCs for building architecture.

Introduction

Luminescent solar concentrators (LSCs) are transparent photonic devices that capture, spectrally down‐shift, guide, and concentrate light to their edges, and are of interest for various applications, with a particular focus on solar energy harvesting [1, 2, 3]. In this respect, LSCs are especially attractive for urban environments dominated by glazed facades, skylights, and curtain walls, as they can harvest diffused light and maintain good performance even under partial shading conditions [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, to achieve commercial viability LSCs must further improve their price‐per‐watt ratios through the employment of more optically efficient and cost‐effective materials, while also meeting architectural glazing standards for transparency and haze [8, 9, 10, 11]. These goals remain challenging due to an intrinsic trade‐off: the relatively high concentrations of organic fluorophores required to capture a significant fraction of sunlight also exacerbate reabsorption losses during lightguide propagation, with each reabsorption event diminishing system efficiency through non‐unity emission quantum yields and less favorable propagation angles [12]. During the last decade, inorganic luminophores, including quantum dots [13, 14, 15], rare‐earths [16, 17, 18], and perovskite nanocrystals [19, 20], have been explored to obtain highly efficient LSCs, owing to their large Stokes shift that effectively minimizes reabsorption losses. Nevertheless, the inherent toxicity of elements like cadmium and lead, costly synthesis procedures, partial loss of transparency, and environmental risks of these components are preventing their spreading as commercial products [21, 22, 23]. Recently, fluorescent carbon dots have emerged as a promising option, exhibiting high quantum yield, large Stokes shift, extended photostability, and, in some cases, suppressed aggregation‐induced quenching; however, their relatively weak and difficult‐to‐tune absorption in visible wavelengths limits their application for LSCs [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. An alternative approach relies on exploiting Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) between organic donor and emitter fluorophores, offering an elegant strategy to mitigate reabsorption losses in LSCs by decoupling the absorption and emission processes [30]. By FRET, photons absorbed by the donor are non‐radiatively transferred to the emitter, which re‐emits them at longer wavelengths, thus reducing the likelihood of reabsorption and increasing the probability of photon collection at the LSC edges [31]. The FRET mechanism exploits non‐radiative dipole‐dipole coupling to transfer energy, requiring the donor and the emitter to be in close proximity, typically 2–6 nm for efficient transfer [32]. In principle, in solid devices (e.g., polymer sheets) this could be easily achieved by increasing the overall fluorophore concentration in the material (mM scale for both donor and acceptor). However, this approach worsens reabsorption losses of the acceptor (emitter), and increases the probability of formation of fluorophore aggregates [33], which in most cases reduces the photoluminescence quantum yield (ΦPL) and enhances light scattering, and thus the haze of the material [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Besides, high fluorophore concentration leads to cost issues and strongly colored LSCs, unsuitable for window applications due to aesthetic issues, indoors color distortions, and negative impact on thermal comfort.

Current strategies to address these drawbacks involve the use of covalently linked donor and emitter molecules and complexes [39, 40], or FRET molecules grafted to polymer scaffolds or dendrimers [40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. Alternatively, FRET has been promoted by luminophore localization in small confined spaces, which enable close donor–emitter proximity upon increasing the local fluorophore concentration without requiring high overall loading: this approach involves the preparation of thin films deposited onto lightguide substrates or the integration of the fluorophores in micro/nanoenvironments (via encapsulation [45] or covalently linking in polymer particles [40], cavities of self‐assembling matrices [46], cocrystals [47], or inorganic‐organic hybrids [28]), distributed throughout the volume of the LSC slab. These approaches, however, suffer from time‐dependent dye diffusion, scattering issues, and the requirement of multistep processes for their preparation (e.g., pre‐functionalization of dyes with grafting moieties or grinding and embedding in matrices), which add significant complexity and cost to LSC fabrication, contradicting the requirement for economically viable building‐integrated photovoltaic solutions.

Herein, we overcome these issues by developing a general and straightforward one‐step approach to achieve nanoconfined fluorophores in a polymeric coating for efficient FRET‐based LSCs. Our strategy exploits dye confinement within oil nanodroplets embedded in a hydrophilic polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix, enabling high local fluorophore concentrations necessary for efficient FRET, while maintaining the low overall dye loading required for architectural transparency. The nanosize of the oil droplets guarantees the high transparency of the films, while the hydrophilic nature of the matrix prevents the diffusion and leakage of hydrophobic dyes, which prefer remaining dissolved in the oil medium. These droplet‐based coatings can be deposited onto glass from aqueous suspensions through low‐energy casting methods. Finally, such a simple and scalable approach works well with a variety of commercially‐available dyes, making it practical for cost‐effective, high‐performance LSCs suitable for building integrated photovoltaics or light diffusers and downshifters for greenhouses.

Results and Discussion

The nanocomposite preparation is based on the direct entrapment of oil droplets into a hydrophilic PVA matrix, a strategy previously developed in our group to obtain photo/thermoresponsive films for smart windows [48, 49, 50] and lens applications [51]. For the LSC preparation, Miglyol 812 (M812), a biosourced triglyceride oil, is chosen for its high boiling point, high transparency in the visible region [52], and good solubility for most organic fluorophores, which should allow for the preservation, in the solid coatings, of the high quantum yields these dyes normally display in organic solvents. PVA is selected for its nanoemulsion‐stabilizing properties [53], film‐forming capabilities, optical transparency, refractive index close to that of glass (∼ 1.5), and its hydrophilicity, which ensures phase separation from the dye/M812 solution and prevents dye migration. A M812 solution of the fluorophore is then homogenized with the PVA aqueous solution, and the resulting nanoemulsion is typically deposited onto a 5 × 5 cm2 glass substrate (0.5 cm thick) using a doctor blade. Upon water evaporation, the dye‐loaded liquid nanodroplets remain physically entrapped and homogeneously dispersed within the solid PVA matrix, forming a uniform and transparent film (Figure 1a). The detailed preparation procedure is described in the Experimental Section. Six commercial organic fluorophores were selected for the nanocomposites preparation because of their broad absorption bands which maximize their solar light absorptions, their broad emissions in the visible region, matching with most solar cells band gap, high ΦPL, and excellent solubilities in M812: perylene (PE), 9,10‐bis(phenylethynyl)anthracene (BPEA), coumarin 6 (C6), 4‐(dicyanomethylene)‐2‐methyl‐6‐(4‐dimethylaminostyryl)‐4H‐pyran (DCM), N,N′‐bis(1‐hexylheptyl)‐perylene‐3,4:9,10‐bis‐(dicarboximide) (PDI) and Perylene Red (PR) (Figure 1b). The high dye molar extinction coefficients (ε, Figure S1) and large Stokes shifts in M812 are comparable with those reported in literature in more common solvents (Table S1). Similarly, ΦPL of the M812 solutions at micromolar concentrations is generally greater than 0.9, except for DCM (ΦPL = 0.18), confirming that M812 is a good solvent for these dyes (Table S1).

For the emulsion preparation, each dye was dissolved in M812 at 0.2 wt.%. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements of the 6 resulting nanoemulsions showed average diameters of 100–150 nm and low polydispersity indices (PdI < 0.2) (Figure S2). The ΦPL of PE, BPEA, C6, and PR in the emulsions showed only small fluctuations when compared to the bulk M812 solutions (Table 1), indicating that dyes remain well‐solubilized in the nanodroplets after the homogenization process. Again, DCM constitutes an exception, as its ΦPL increased to 0.34, possibly due to the increase of polarity provided by the water‐oil interface that could promote the charge transfer state stabilization and radiative decay [54]. In the case of PDI, the lower ΦPL could be related to the higher concentration of dye used for the emulsion preparation, which increases the reabsorption and promotes the formation of the excimer, decreasing and red shifting the emission intensity (Figure S3) [55].

The differently‐ colored films obtained after depositing the emulsions via doctor blade applicator adhered strongly to the glass substrates due to the inherent affinity of PVA for glass surfaces and exhibit no visible defects (Figure 2a). The final oil concentration in PVA is 24 wt.%. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed smooth film surfaces with a uniform thickness of 28 µm (Figure 2b; Figure S4). The confinement of the nanodroplets in the PVA matrix was evidenced by spherical/ellipsoidal voids observed after CHCl3 treatment of the film cross‐section, resulting from selective removal of the oil phase while leaving the polymer matrix intact (Figure S5). To gain further insight into the structuration of the oil phase within the PVA matrix, the nanocomposite was redissolved in water, and the droplet size was measured by DLS and compared with the values obtained prior to emulsion casting (Figure S6). The results show only a slight increase in droplet size upon redispersion (from 148 to 169 nm), and a small change in the PdI (from 0.17 to 0.22), suggesting that nanodroplets coalescence within the matrix is negligible. Optical microscopy in reflectance mode revealed only rare grain impurities in the film that were no longer detected when orthogonally polarizing filters were applied, suggesting the absence of large crystalline aggregates (Figure S7). The coated glasses meet the optical quality architectural glazing standards as haze measurements revealed values below 2.0% for all samples except C6 (3.6%). For comparison, a glass substrate coated with the nanocomposite in the absence of dye exhibits a haze of 0.2%. The slight increase observed in the dye‐loaded LSCs may be attributed to surface‐emitted light from the luminophores, which can contribute to an apparent increase in haze. These low haze values confirm minimal light scattering caused by the nanodroplets and suggest the absence of significant aggregation, which is a substantial advantage of liquid‐based LSCs [56]. The LSCs show absorption in different spectral regions between 370 and 600 nm (Figure 2c). The average visible transmittance (AVT) of each LSC, calculated from the corresponding transmittance spectrum measured using air as baseline reference (Figure S8), ranged between 88% and 92% (Table 2), substantially exceeding the 55% threshold sought for window applications [10, 57]. Together, these characteristics demonstrate that our nanocomposite coatings allow high local fluorophore concentration coexisting with the optical clarity and transparency, essential for building integration.

The ΦPL of 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm2 nanocomposite‐coated glasses were slightly lower than those of the corresponding emulsions, except for DCM (Table 1). This reduction is likely due to increased reabsorption events arising from the solid‐state configuration compared to the more dilute liquid emulsion. The edge‐emission of the LSCs was measured by illuminating the device from the top with a light source simulating the AM 1.5 solar spectrum. The LSCs were placed on a black low‐reflective holder with one edge facing the entry port of the sphere (Figure S9). From the absorption spectra, edge‐emission spectra (Figure 2d), and the incident irradiation spectrum, we calculated the external optical efficiency (ηext, also referred to as ηopt in some studies) and the internal optical efficiency (ηint), as described in the Experimental Section. In contrast with ηext, the values of ηint are normalized by the light absorbed, thus giving a better idea of the material efficiency in conveying the emitted light to the edges. The maximum possible value of ηint is given by the theoretical limit of photon entrapment, which takes into account exclusively the escape cone losses and is close to 75% in glass and PVA (n ∼ 1.5) [12, 58]. Considering the non‐unity ΦPL of the PE, BPEA, C6, and PR films, comprised between 0.78 and 0.88, the maximum light entrapment achievable would lie between 59% and 66% at best. The calculated ηint values obtained from the experimental data, comprised between 46.0% and 60.9%, approach this limit, with the difference possibly ascribed to reabsorption losses. DCM and PDI exhibited smaller ηint, due to their lower ΦPL. Overall, the best performance was achieved by the C6‐based LSC, with ηext = 3.93% and ηint = 52.8%, which can be ascribed to the synergistic effect of multiple factors, including stronger absorption compared to the other dyes, arising from both its higher molar concentration and high extinction coefficient (41 000 m − 1 cm− 1), as well as its broad absorption range and favorable Stokes shift (48 nm, 0.28 eV). Furthermore, we calculated the overlap integral (OI) to quantify the reabsorption effect of the nanocomposites (see the Experimental section for the calculation) [59, 60]. The low OI values obtained (Table 2) indicate that reabsorption remains limited at the optical density investigated, suggesting the suitability of these formulations for large‐area LSC fabrication.

These results prove the suitability of the nanocomposites for light concentration applications.

Despite organic dye‐based LSCs having been abundantly reported over the past decades, a direct quantitative comparison between devices employing the same dyes is not straightforward, given the variety of methodologies used for performance evaluation and the numerous influencing factors, including surface area, thickness, dye distribution (surface‐coated vs. bulk‐doped), refractive index of the lightguide, and device architecture (e.g., tandem designs or back reflectors). In this overview, LSCs based on PR remain among the most extensively studied, owing to the excellent processability and the outstanding optical performance of this dye. Therefore, PR was selected to further investigate the optical characteristics of our nanodroplet‐based LSCs.

First, we investigated the influence of droplet size on light scattering and its impact on optical efficiency. To this end, four different emulsions containing PR (0.2 wt.% with respect to M812) were prepared by varying the homogenization pressure and/or time, as described in the Experimental Section, yielding samples S1‐S4. DLS analysis revealed average droplet sizes of 1696, 408, 250, and 109 nm for S1‐S4, respectively, decreasing with increasing homogenization pressure and time (Table 3 and Figure 3a). The resulting emulsions were deposited onto glass substrates by doctor blade coating to obtain four LSCs. The scattering of the samples clearly correlates with droplet size, as evidenced both visually (Figure 3b) and by their absorption spectra (Figure 3c) and haze measurements (Table 3). Edge‐emission measurements show moderate scattering can enhance the optical output of LSCs (Figure 3d). In particular, S3 exhibits a significant increase in ηext compared to S4 (3.1% to 4.6%), with only a moderate reduction in AVT (91.2% to 81.4%, calculated from transmittance spectra in Figure S10). In general, ηext increases from S4 to S2, while it declines for S4 as scattering losses become dominant (Figure 3e). The increase in ηext was ascribed to nanodroplet scattering allowing redirection of a broader range of visible (and near‐infrared) photons than those absorbed by the fluorescent dye directly to the emission edge. Conversely, ηint decreases with increasing droplet size, reflecting reduced lightguiding efficiency due to enhanced scattering losses. These results indicate that the performance of 5 × 5 × 0.5 cm2 LSCs can be improved by tuning the nanodroplet size; however, in larger devices, the benefits of enhanced photon harvesting through scattering may be offset by increased optical losses associated with longer optical path lengths [61].

Next, the effect of film thickness on the optical efficiency of the LSCs was investigated. Three PR‐based LSCs (0.2 wt.% with respect to M812) with increasing coating thickness were fabricated by sequential deposition of multiple layers via doctor blade coating, as described in the Experimental Section. SEM cross‐sectional analysis revealed film thicknesses of 28, 46, and 65 µm, respectively (Figure S11). As expected, the absorbance increases linearly with thickness (R2 = 0.9999, Figure S12). Notably, ηext also shows a linear dependence on thickness, reaching 2.3% for 28 µm, 3.5% for 46 µm, and 4.5% for 65 µm (Figure S12). These results demonstrate that the deposition of multiple emulsion layers is an effective strategy to tune both light‐harvesting capability and optical efficiency in LSCs.

Successively, we studied the effects of local versus overall dye concentration on LSC performance by preparing two complementary sets of PR‐based samples. In the first set, we varied the PR concentration within M812 nanodroplets (0.2, 0.5, and 2.0 wt.% with respect to M812) while maintaining a constant nanodroplet loading of 24 wt.% in the film (Figure 3f; Figure S13a). In the second set, we varied the nanodroplet loading (24, 12, 4.8, and 2.4 wt.% with respect to the film) while fixing the PR concentration at 2.0 wt.% (with respect to M812) within each droplet (Figure 3g; Figure S13b). This approach allowed independent assessment of how local (intradroplet) and overall (within the film) dye concentrations affect ΦPL, ηext, and ηint (Table S2). Increasing the PR concentration within droplets from 0.5 to 2.0 wt.% reduced ΦPL from 0.85 to 0.76, potentially due to reabsorption and/or aggregation‐induced quenching. However, when we decreased the loading of 2.0 wt.% PR‐containing droplets by tenfold (from 24 to 2.4 wt.%), ΦPL recovered from 0.76 to 0.82, comparable to films with higher droplet loadings (24 wt.%) but lower intradroplet PR concentrations (0.2 and 0.5 wt.%). This recovery suggests that ΦPL losses primarily stem from reabsorption rather than intradroplet aggregation. Supporting this interpretation, LSCs with the lowest PR concentration within the nanodroplets display both similar absorbance and ηext values to LSCs containing 10 times fewer droplets but 10 times higher intradroplet PR concentration (Figure 3h; Table S2). This convergence implies that LSC performance depends primarily on the total dye concentration in the film, regardless of whether the dye is concentrated within fewer droplets or distributed across many, and indicates that our oil nanodroplet confinement strategy successfully prevents aggregation‐induced quenching, even pushing the dye concentration in the nanocontainer to 2.0 wt.%, which is higher than other reported polymeric LSCs embedding nanoencapsulated organic fluorophores (also requiring multistep processes for the preparation) [62].

These findings were compared to previously reported PR‐based 5 × 5 × 0.5 cm3 LSCs [37], obtained through two different configurations: dye‐loaded thin films deposited onto lightguide substrates and polymer slabs doped with the dye throughout their volume. While the former configuration suffered from severely reduced ΦPL (< 0.4), and thus low ηext (∼1%) due to the aggregation‐induced quenching occurring at the high PR concentrations (9.3 mm) required to accomplish suitable light absorption, bulk‐doped LSCs (0.17 mm) achieved substantially better performance (ΦPL = 0.93, ηext = 8.1%) at much lower concentration but comparable absorbance. Despite our nanodroplets‐based LSCs relying on a thin coating deposited onto glass and high PR concentration, they maintain excellent performance (ΦPL = 0.76, ηext = 12.3%), closely resembling those of the bulk‐doped configuration. This excellent performance further validates our liquid nanoconfinement approach as an effective way for preventing the aggregation‐induced losses typically observed in concentrated thin‐film systems, opening the possibility of using this configuration, which might be preferred due to the less material usage and adaptability to existing windows.

Finally, the nanodroplets‐based LSC was exposed to ageing tests to evaluate its stability under harsh weather conditions. Thermal‐ and photostability of the LSCs were probed in a lightfastness chamber (24 h at 45°C with xenon lamp irradiation of 42 W m− 2 in the 300–400 nm range and 1.1 W m− 2 at 420 nm). While PE, PDI, and PR exhibited little to no change in their absorption spectra, BPEA, C6, and DCM were more noticeably affected, showing a decrease in the absorption bands associated with fluorescence (Figure S14). These observations suggest that, in the present system, nanostructuration and the oxygen barrier properties of PVA do not appear to significantly enhance dye photostability compared to conventional LSC lightguides and further studies will be required in the future.

On the other hand, PVA, a water‐soluble polymer, may not be suitable for application involving direct exposure to high humidity or water (e.g., rain, cleaning), as it would gradually dissolve. However, this limitation may be addressed through lamination, an approach that has already been demonstrated for comparable materials [49]. Alternatively, the application of a hydrophobic protective layer could mitigate this issue. To explore this possibility, we performed a proof‐of‐concept experiment in which a PR‐based LSC was coated with an additional layer of polyvinyl butyral (PVB), an optically transparent and hydrophobic polymer, broadly used in the glass industry. The encapsulated device was immersed in water for 2 h, and its absorption and edge emission was measured before and after immersion, with no visible signs of degradation (Figure S15). These results suggest that encapsulation represents a viable strategy to protect the nanocomposite from humidity and water exposure.

Having demonstrated the viability of nanodroplets‐based LSCs, we aimed to exploit the nanoconfinement to induce FRET and achieve larger Stokes shifts. Since FRET efficiency depends critically on dye proximity, co‐dissolving donor and emitter dyes within M812 nanodroplets ensures high local concentrations regardless of their overall concentration in the device. Among the dyes studied, C6 and PDI were chosen as suitable energy donors, while PR was selected as the FRET acceptor and emitter. Thanks to the excellent overlap between C6 and PDI emissions with PR absorption (Figure S16), these combinations of dyes are ideal for FRET [33, 63]. Thus, we investigated two FRET‐based LSCs using (C6 + PR) and (PDI + PR) pairs (Figure 4a). For each pair, we prepared four emulsions with a fixed donor concentration (0.2 wt.% in M812) and varying PR concentrations (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 wt.% in M812). Control emulsions containing only donors (C6 or PDI at 0.2 wt.%) or only acceptor (PR at each concentration) were used for comparison. Nanodroplets concentration was fixed to 24 wt.%. Spectrofluorimetric analysis of the nanoemulsions were performed, diluting them 20 times to maintain absorbance below 0.2 and minimize reabsorption artefacts. Exciting the emulsions at the donor spectral absorption (λexc = 486 nm for C6, 525 nm for PDI), PR emission (λem = 600 nm) was also observed (Figure 4b). Upon increasing the acceptor concentration, its emission was enhanced while the donor fluorescence was increasingly quenched. Residual PR emission was also observed in the control samples (with no donor), due to limited absorption of the dye at the excitation wavelength, even though its intensity was significantly lower than that obtained in the presence of the donor. Excitation spectra measured at the PR emission maximum (λem = 600 nm) confirmed that the enhanced PR emission originated mainly from donor excitation rather than direct PR absorption (Figure 4c).

Next, we deposited the emulsions prepared from oil solutions containing 0.2 wt.% of donor (either C6 or PDI) and 0.2 wt.% of PR on glass substrates of 5 × 5 × 0.5 cm3, obtaining the corresponding (C6 + PR) and (PDI + PR) LSCs (Figure 5a). As controls, we prepared tandem systems by coating opposite sides of the glass with donor‐only and PR‐only emulsions (0.2 wt.% each), obtaining (C6)/(PR) and (PDI)/(PR) (Figure 5b). While these control LSCs exhibited nearly identical absorbance to their FRET‐based counterparts (Figure S17), the physical separation of the dyes precluded any energy transfer. This was confirmed by time‐resolved fluorescence spectroscopy measurements of the four LSCs and the single‐layer C6‐ and PDI‐loaded LSCs, using a 405 nm excitation laser (Figure 5c). As expected, the monoexponential fluorescence decay at 494 nm lifetimes (τ) for the (C6)/(PR) LSC (τ = 3.11 ns) closely matched that of the C6 LSC (τ = 3.04 ns), since the radiative reabsorption by the separate PR‐containing layer is not affecting it. In contrast, the (C6 + PR) LSC exhibited a faster fluorescence decay, with a shorter lifetime of 2.31 ns. A similar trend was observed for the (PDI + PR) system as its fluorescence decay at 528 nm (τ = 2.82 ns) was significantly shorter than that of PDI only (4.33 ns) and (PDI)/(PR) (4.35 ns) LSCs. Notably, the fluorescence decay at 600 nm, corresponding to PR emission, was visibly delayed in the (C6 + PR) and (PDI + PR) LSCs compared to the other samples. Altogether, these results provide strong evidence of FRET in the nanodroplets. FRET efficiencies (E) were calculated from Equation (1): (1) E=1−τD+EτD/E where τ(D + E) represents the fluorescence lifetime of the mixed systems ((C6 + PR) or (PDI + PR)), and τ(D)/(E) corresponds to the lifetime of the tandem systems ((C6)/(PR) or (PDI)/(PR)). Using this relation, FRET efficiencies of 26% for (C6 + PR) and 35% for (PDI + PR) were obtained. The higher efficiency observed for (PDI + PR) is consistent with its lower donor‐to‐emitter molar ratio (1.4) compared to that of (C6 + PR) (3.1). While increasing the PR concentration would further enhance FRET efficiency, it also intensifies reabsorption losses, possibly counteracting the primary advantage of employing FRET in LSCs.

Subsequently, the edge‐emission of each LSC was recorded by locally illuminating the coating at various distances from the film edge (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 cm), selectively exciting the donor with monochromatic LED sources with appropriate wavelengths (455 nm for C6 and 470 nm for PDI).

The resulting edge‐emission spectra exhibited pronounced differences between FRET‐based and tandem LSCs (Figure 7b). Specifically, the spectra of the tandem (C6)/(PR) and (PDI)/(PR) systems displayed stronger donor emission components, whereas in the FRET‐based systems (C6 + PR) and (PDI + PR) these components were markedly suppressed, even at short illumination distances from the edge, while the PR emission was enhanced. This behavior indicates that in the FRET systems a fraction of the photons absorbed by the donor are efficiently downshifted to longer wavelengths through energy transfer to PR. Analysis of the integrated emission spectra, normalized to the signal recorded at a 2 cm excitation distance, revealed that the relative decrease in intensity with distance was smaller for the FRET systems compared to their tandem counterparts (Figure 7c). This suggests that in the FRET‐based LSCs, part of the donor‐absorbed light is converted into PR emission before propagating through the lightguide, thereby preventing its reabsorption by the donor.

Altogether, these findings demonstrate that FRET systems based on liquid nanodroplets provide a practical pathway to mitigate reabsorption losses in larger‐scale LSCs through the confinement of the donor and acceptor dyes.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that nanoconfinement of organic dyes within M812 nanodroplets embedded in PVA matrices offers a powerful strategy for developing LSCs. This approach allowed preventing aggregation‐induced quenching even at millimolar concentrations (∼ 2–17 mm) and provided LSCs with high optical quality (AVT > 88.0%), very low haze (1.0% < h < 3.6%), and efficient light concentration ability for the PE, BPEA, C6, and PR dyes (ηi n t = 60.9%, 50.4%, 52.8%, 46.0%, respectively). Remarkably, highly concentrated PR‐based LSCs achieved ηext of 12.3% and ηint of 47.0%, values that significantly stand out compared with those reported elsewhere for equivalent thin‐film configurations, which generally suffer from aggregation‐induced quenching at comparable dye concentrations.

In comparison to alternative encapsulation approaches where dyes are typically confined within a rigid or semi‐rigid local environment and dye loading often limited by aggregation and phase segregation, our nanocomposites provide a liquid nanoenvironment that closely resembles a macroscopic bulk solution, effectively suppressing dye aggregation. Moreover, this approach benefits from a simpler and cost‐effective one‐step preparation process, through which the dye‐loaded nanodroplets are obtained in the polymer‐containing coating formulation, which is then directly used to obtain the final LSC, making it attractive for industrial‐scale production. On the other hand, the hydrophobic nature of the oil and the hydrophilic character of the matrix should prevent dyes migration to the surface, minimizing toxicity issues due to leakage.

Furthermore, the nanodroplet strategy allowed us to successfully implement FRET‐based LSCs by co‐dissolving donor‐emitter pairs of dyes within the oil nanocontainer. Time‐resolved spectroscopy confirmed efficient energy transfer, with donor lifetime reductions of 26% and 35% for the (C6 + PR) and the (PDI + PR) system, respectively. In longer LSCs (20 cm), FRET configuration showed reduced distance‐dependent losses compared to tandem LSCs and greater power output when coupled with a photodiode, demonstrating that nanodroplet‐mediated energy transfer effectively mitigates reabsorption, a persistent challenge in LSC development.

This nanoconfinement strategy provides a scalable pathway toward efficient, aesthetically suitable LSCs for building‐integrated photovoltaics. Future work should explore optimization of donor‐acceptor combinations, improvement of dyes photostability, and implementation with solar cells for current generation.

Experimental Section

Materials

All the compounds were purchased commercially and used as received without further purification. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) 40–88 was purchased from Kuraray Co. Miglyol 812 (M812) was purchased from Oxi‐Med Express (distributors of Sasol in Spain). Perylene (PE), 9,10‐bis(phenylethynyl)anthracene (BPEA), Coumarin 6 (C6) and 4‐(dicyanomethylene)‐2‐methyl‐6‐(4‐dimethylaminostyryl)‐4H‐pyran (DCM) were purchased from Merck., N,N′‐Bis(1‐hexylheptyl)‐perylene‐3,4:9,10‐bis‐(dicarboximide) (PDI) and perylene red were purchased from BLD Pharmatech GmbH. Glass substrates were purchased from Calibre Scientific Spain S.L.U.

Methods and Instruments

The particle‐size distributions of diluted emulsions were measured in a ZetaSizer nano ZS (ZEN3600, Malvern Instruments, Ltd., Malvern, UK). SEM images of the PR film surface and cross‐section were registered with a FEI Quanta 650 ESEM microscope applying a 5 kV voltage. The film sample was metalized with a layer of about 5 nm of Pt prior to SEM imaging. Optical microscope images were taken with a Nikon Eclipse LV100DA. The absolute fluorescence quantum yields of liquid and solid samples were obtained with a Hamamatsu Quantum Yield fluorometer C9920‐02G equipped with integrating sphere. The absorbance of the films within the visible range was measured using an Agilent Cary 60 spectrophotometer. A clean glass substrate was used as baseline. The transmittance of the films between 390 and 780 nm was measured using an Agilent Cary 60 spectrophotometer, with an air baseline to account for LSCs reflection. The photostability test was carried out in a Q‐Lab Xe‐1 Xenon Test Chamber. The fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of the FRET emulsions were recorded by means of PTI Quantamaster 350 fluorimeter. Fluorescence lifetime measurements were carried out on a LifeSpec‐ps spectrophotometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK) with a 405 nm pulsed laser. The power generated by the long LSCs was measured using a Laserpoint PD‐500‐D9‐VIS photodiode, calibrated at 550 nm and positioned at the center of the short edge. A black background was placed beneath the LSC to minimize stray reflections. Each measurement was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Parameters Calculation

Average visible transmittance (AVT) of the LSCs was determined with Equation (2): (2) AVT=∫390780TλPλSλdλ∫390780PλSλdλ where T(λ) is the wavelength‐dependent transmittance normalized by 1, P(λ) is the CIE luminous efficiency function for photopic vision normalized by 1, and S(λ) is the solar irradiance spectrum ASTM G173‐03 Global Tilted 37°. LSCs edge‐emissions were recorded with a Labsphere SLMS 1050 integrating sphere connected to an International Light RPS900 diode array detector. Illumination for emission measurements is provided by a 300 W SciSun solar simulator equipped with filters approximating an AM1.5 solar spectrum, using a black (absorbing) background sample holder. To measure incident light spectrum and intensity, a 2.5 × 2.5 cm2 right‐angle quartz prism with antireflection coating was used to redirect the solar light toward the integrating sphere. Thus, the incident spectrum intensity was measured over a 2.5 × 2.5 cm2 area, and the incident intensity on the 5 × 5 cm2 surface of LSCs was determined by multiplying the measured incident power by 4.

The external optical efficiencies (ηext) and internal (ηint) optical efficiencies were calculated as described in previous studies, using Equations (3) and (4) [64, 65]: (3) ηext=Nph−outNph−in=∫400800(Poutλ−Pblankλ)λhcdλ∫4008004Pinλλhcdλ (4) ηint=Nph−outNph−abs=4∫400800(Poutλ−Pblankλ)λhcdλ∫400800Pabsλλhcdλ=4∫400800(Poutλ−Pblankλ)λhcdλ∫4008004Pinλ1−10−Aλλhcdλ where, Nph‐in is the total number of photons incident on the top surface of the LSC, Nph‐out is the total number of edge‐emitted photons summed over the four LSC edges, and Nph‐abs is the total number of photons absorbed by the LSC. Nph‐in is obtained from the input power spectrum Pin(λ) of the light source provided by the right‐angle prism measurement. This value is multiplied by 4 to consider the illuminated surface area of the LSC (units for Pin(λ): W m2/nm). Nph‐out is obtained from the averaged output power spectra Pout(λ) of the four LSC edges, after subtraction of the output power spectra of an uncoated glass Pblank(λ), considering the illuminated LSC surface as the reference area (units for Pout(λ) and Pblank(λ): W m2/nm), and multiplying by 4 to consider the emission from all edges. Nph‐abs is obtained from the absorbed input power spectrum Pabs(λ), by convolution of the absorption spectrum A(λ) of the LSC and 4∙Pin(λ). In the expressions above, λ is the wavelength of photons (in nm), h is the Planck's constant (in J∙s), c is the speed of light (in nm/s).

The overlap integral (OI) is defined as: (5) OI=∫0∞Aλ·PL∗λdλ where A(λ) represents the fraction of light absorbed by the LSC, and PL*(λ) is the normalized emission spectrum of the luminophore in the host material under sun simulated irradiation [59, 60].

The fraction of light absorbed A(λ) used for the overlap integral calculation is defined as A (λ) = 1 − R(λ) − T(λ) and was experimentally determined from absorbance spectra measured using a glass baseline.

Materials Preparation

PVA Aqueous Solution Preparation

While stirring, 20 g of PVA 40–88 were added to a round‐bottom flask containing 180 g of water at room temperature. The mixture was heated to 85°C and stirred until the polymer was completely dissolved.

Preparation of Emulsions by High‐Pressure Homogenization

A mixture of 3.2 g of M812 dye solution (2 mg/g, unless otherwise specified) and 100 g of 10 wt.% PVA aqueous solution was stirred in a beaker until a visually homogeneous oil‐in‐water dispersion was obtained. The dispersion was then converted into a nanoemulsion by processing it at 30 000 psi for 3 h using a high‐pressure homogenizer (Microfluidizer LM20) equipped with an F12Y diamond interaction chamber.

Preparation of LSCs by Coating Deposition

Glass substrates are washed with distilled water and isopropanol before use. The nanoemulsion was deposited on a glass substrate using a Zehntner ZAA 2300 doctor blade. The substrate edges were sealed with adhesive tape, and the coating was applied at 40°C, with a blade speed of 3 mm/s and a gap height of 500 µm. After drying for 40 min, a solid film was formed, and the adhesive tape was cut and removed. For LSCs with increased coating thickness, one or two additional layers were deposited using a gap height of 1000 µm, after the previous layer was dried.

Preparation of Solid Samples for ΦPL Determination

The emulsions were deposited and dried on 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm3 clean glass plates whose size allows them to be placed inside the integrating sphere, following the same method described for LSCs preparation.

Preparation of Emulsions S1‐S4

A mixture of 6.4 g of M812 PR solution (2 mg/g) and 200 g of 10 wt.% PVA aqueous solution was stirred in a beaker until a visually homogeneous oil‐in‐water dispersion was obtained. Emulsions S1‐S3 were prepared using a high‐pressure homogenizer (Microfluidizer LM20) by collecting aliquots after 10, 30, and 60 min of processing, respectively. Subsequently, the pressure was increased to 30 000 psi, and nanoemulsion S4 was obtained after 3 h of homogenization.

Protection of the PVA Coating With a PVB Layer

A 20 wt.% solution of PVB in isopropanol was deposited at 25°C on top of a PR‐based LSC using a Zehntner ZAA 2300 doctor blade. After drying for 20 min, temperature was raised to 40°C and the coating was further dried for 30 min.
