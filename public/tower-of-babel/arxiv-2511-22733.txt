1.
Introduction

We study the following Robin problem

(
P_{\lambda,\gamma}
)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda f(u)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\gamma u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(

) is a bounded domain with

boundary,
\nu(x)
denotes the outward unit normal vector to

, and
\lambda,\gamma>0
are two parameters. Here,

is a locally Lipschitz function that has at least two positive zeros,

, such that

for all
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
. We stress that no additional assumptions are made on
f
; in particular,
f
is allowed to change sign. Note that
\gamma=0
corresponds to the Neumann boundary condition, while
\gamma=\infty
formally leads to the Dirichlet boundary condition.

Our main objective is to study the set of the nonnegative solutions to (

P

λ
,
γ

) whose maximum lies between
\alpha
and
\beta
, i.e., the solutions of (

P

λ
,
γ

) that belong to

\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}=\{u\in{C(\overline{\Omega})}:u\geq 0\text{ and }\alpha<\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}<\beta\}.

In addition to existence and multiplicity, we also investigate the asymptotic behaviour of the solution set of (

P

λ
,
γ

) as
\gamma
tends to
0
and to
\infty
. Specifically, we show that it converges to the solution set of the corresponding Neumann problem as
\gamma\to 0
, and to that of the associated Dirichlet problem as
\gamma\to\infty
. For this analysis, we demonstrate the following stability result: when
\gamma\to\infty
, solutions of (

P

λ
,
γ

) converge strongly in

to solutions of the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
). As far as we know, this type of convergence, key in our study, has not been previously established in the literature.

The existence of solutions belonging to
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
has been extensively studied when the boundary conditions are Dirichlet. For the Dirichlet problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda f(u)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

the strong maximum principle precludes the existence of a solution
u\geq 0
with
\|u\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega})}}=\sigma
, where
\sigma>0
is such that
f(\sigma)\leq 0
. In particular, no nonnegative solution to (
1.1
) can have a maximum value of
\alpha
or
\beta
. In
[
22
]
, assuming
f(0)>0
, the author (inspired by
[
8
]
) showed that an area condition on
f
is sufficient to ensure the existence of two positive solutions of (
1.1
) with
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
-norm between
\alpha
and
\beta
for any
\lambda
greater than some

(1.2)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(t)\ \mathrm{d}t>0\text{ for all }s\in[0,\beta).

Years later, the condition

was dropped in
[
13
]
, where the authors showed that (
1.2
) is sufficient to guarantee the existence of a positive solution to (
1.1
) belonging to
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
for large

. Furthermore, the authors of
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
independently proved that condition (

) is also necessary for the existence of positive solutions to (

) in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
. Consequently, if

is defined as

r_{\alpha}:=\inf\left\{r\in(\alpha,\beta):\int_{s}^{r}f(t)\ \mathrm{d}t>0\text{ for all }s\in[0,r)\right\},

then any positive solution

to (
1.1
) with

must verify

[

Since these pioneering works, such results have been extended to other operators, always assuming Dirichlet boundary conditions. In general, the area condition (
1.2
) is assumed to establish the existence (and sometimes the multiplicity) of solutions in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
, whereas its necessity has been less explored. To mention a few works, this has been done for local operators, including the

-Laplacian (
[
21
,
25
]
), the

-Laplacian (
[
12
]
), the

-Laplacian (

]
), the

-Laplacian (
[
16
]
), and a Schrödinger type operator (
[
15
]
), as well as for nonlocal operators such as the fractional Laplacian (
[

]

[
4
]
).

Another extensively studied question is the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to (
1.1
) that belong to
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
when
\lambda
diverges. Whenever (

to (

More properties of (

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the article, we deal with classical solutions. For clarity, we give its definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is a
subsolution
(resp. a
supersolution
) to (

P

λ
,
γ

) if, pointwise, it satisfies

\begin{cases}-\Delta u\overset{(\geq)}{\leq}\lambda f(u)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\gamma u\overset{(\geq)}{\leq}0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

We say that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is a
solution
to (

P

λ
,
γ

) if it is both a sub- and a supersolution.

Sometimes, because of the tools used, we need to consider weak solutions. However, in our setting, classical and weak solutions coincide, as explained in Remark
2.3
. In the following, we recall its definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A function
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is a
weak subsolution
(resp. a
weak supersolution
) to (

P

λ
,
γ

) if it verifies

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\gamma\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ \overset{(\geq)}{\leq}\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\text{ with }\varphi\geq 0.

In the same way, we say that
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is a
weak solution
to (

P

λ
,
γ

) if

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\gamma\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi=\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega).

Remark 2.3
.

Any bounded weak solution of (

P

λ
,
γ

) is actually a classical solution. Indeed, let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
be a bounded weak solution of (

P

λ
,
γ

). Since the trace of
u
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
, and both
u
and
f(u)
are in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, it follows from
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
that
u\in C^{0,\eta}(\overline{\Omega})
for any
\eta\in(0,1)
. As
f
is locally Lipschitz, then Schauder estimates (see
[
18
, Theorem 6.1]
) immediately imply that
u\in C^{2,\eta}(\overline{\Omega})
.

In practice, we always deal with bounded solutions, where both concepts of solution can be used interchangeably. Specifically, the solutions we obtain are always nonnegative and less than
\beta
. Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality that
f
is a globally Lipschitz function. This implies the existence of some
M>0
such that

(2.2)

f(s)+Ms\text{ is increasing in }\mathbb{R}.

2.1.
Consequences of the strong maximum principle

Here, we prove some results that can be derived from the maximum principle. Although we believe that the following result is well-known, we include its proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\gamma\geq 0
. Assume that
f
is a Lipschitz function. Let
w\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a subsolution of (

P
γ

) and let
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a supersolution. If
w\leq v
in
\Omega
and
w\not\equiv v
, then
w<v
in
\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

Let
M>0
be the positive constant defined in (
2.2
). Taking into account that
w\leq v
in
\Omega
, we can say that

f(v)+Mv-f(w)-Mw\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega

In this way,
v-w
verifies the problem

(2.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta(v-w)+M(v-w)\geq 0&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial(v-w)}{\partial\nu}+\gamma(v-w)\geq 0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By the strong maximum principle,
v-w
reaches its minimum in
\overline{\Omega}
at some point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
. As
w\leq v
in
\overline{\Omega}
we have two possibilities: either
(v-w)(x_{0})>0
or
(v-w)(x_{0})=0
. To finish the proof, we show that the second case cannot happen. Suppose that
(v-w)(x_{0})=0
. As
w\not\equiv v
, the Hopf Lemma (
[
18
, Lemma 3.4]
) implies that
\frac{\partial(v-w)}{\partial\nu}(x_{0})<0
. But this is a contradiction since we know from the boundary condition of (
2.3
) that
\frac{\partial(v-w)}{\partial\nu}(x_{0})\geq-\gamma(v-w)(x_{0})=0
.
∎

From Proposition
2.4
, we can easily deduce the next result.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\gamma>0
. Assume that
f
is a Lipschitz function such that
f(\sigma)\leq 0
for some
\sigma>0
. Then, there is no nonnegative solution
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
to (

P
γ

) such that
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\sigma
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is some
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
nonnegative solution of (

P
γ

) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\sigma
. As
v\equiv\sigma
is a strict supersolution of (

P
γ

), and as
u\leq\sigma
in
\Omega
, using Proposition
2.4
we have that
u<\sigma
in
\overline{\Omega}
, but this is a contradiction with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\sigma
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The same result holds for the Dirichlet problem (

[

Let

This operator, which is well-defined, is compact in

,

Suppose that

,

Due to Corollary

. Therefore, the Leray-Schauder degree of these operators is well-defined in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
and we can say that
\operatorname{deg}(I-K_{\mu},\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta},0)
is independent of
\mu\in[0,1]
. As a consequence, we obtain that

\operatorname{deg}(I-K,\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta},0)=\operatorname{deg}(I,\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta},0)=0.\qed

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose that
f
is a Lipschitz function. Assume that
w\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is a strict subsolution of (

P
γ

) and that
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is a strict supersolution. If

\mathcal{O}_{1}:=\{u\in{C(\overline{\Omega})}:w(x)<u(x)<v(x)\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}\},

then
\operatorname{deg}(I-K,\mathcal{O}_{1},0)
is well-defined and its value is 1.

Proof.

First, we claim that
K
maps
\overline{\mathcal{O}_{1}}
into
\mathcal{O}_{1}
. Indeed, let us take
u_{1}\in\overline{\mathcal{O}_{1}}
and denote
u_{2}:=K(u_{1})
. We have that
w(x)\leq u_{1}(x)\leq v(x)\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}
and we want to show that
w(x)<u_{2}(x)<v(x)\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}
. We are proving only the first inequality as the other one follows in a similar way. First, notice that

f(u_{1})+Mu_{1}-f(w)-Mw\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega

as a consequence of (
2.2
). Moreover,
u_{2}\not\equiv w
. Suppose instead that
u_{2}\equiv w
. In this case,
f(u_{1})+Mu_{1}-f(w)-Mw\equiv 0
and thus
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}\equiv w
. This implies that
w
is solution (

P
γ

), but this is a contradiction since
w
is a strict subsolution.

Therefore,
u_{2}-w
verifies problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta(u_{2}-w)+M(u_{2}-w)\geq 0&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial(u_{2}-w)}{\partial\nu}+\gamma(u_{2}-w)\geq 0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

From now on, we can argue as in the proof of Proposition
2.4
to show that
w(x)<u_{2}(x)
in
\overline{\Omega}
. As the same reasoning can be applied to deduce that
u_{2}(x)<v(x)
in
\overline{\Omega}
, we have obtain that
u_{2}=K(u_{1})\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
.

Finally, as the set
\mathcal{O}_{1}
is convex, given any
\psi\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
we can assure that
sK(w)+(1-s)\psi\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
for any
w\in\overline{\mathcal{O}_{1}}
and any
s\in[0,1]
. In particular,
sK(w)+(1-s)\psi\neq w
for any
w\in\partial\mathcal{O}_{1}
and any
s\in[0,1]
. Thus, for any
s\in[0,1]
the Leray-Schauder degree
\operatorname{deg}(I-[sK+(1-s)\psi],\mathcal{O}_{1},0)
is well-defined and independent of
s
. Then, we have

\operatorname{deg}(I-K,\mathcal{O}_{1},0)=\operatorname{deg}(I-\psi,\mathcal{O}_{1},0)=1.\qed

2.2.
A stability result

Several times, we need to pass to the limit in problems like
(P_{\lambda,\gamma_{n}})
, where
\gamma_{n}
can converge or diverge. Since we are only interested in nonnegative solutions whose maximum remains below
\beta
, we can take advantage of the fact that these solutions are always bounded in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
. While proving that the limit satisfies either (

P

λ
,
γ

) (if
\gamma_{n}
converges) or (
1.1
) (if
\gamma_{n}
diverges) is straightforward, showing the strong convergence in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
is a more delicate issue.

This last point is key for our study. Given a sequence of solutions to
(P_{\lambda,\gamma_{n}})
contained in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
, the convergence in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
ensures that the limit belongs to
\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}}
. Then, by Corollary
2.5
, one can conclude that the limit is actually in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
.

When
\gamma_{n}
converges, the convergence in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
follows from the fact that these solutions can be shown to be uniformly bounded in
C^{0,\eta}(\overline{\Omega})
by applying
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
. Instead, when
\gamma_{n}
diverges, we employ a strategy introduced in
[
5
]
, which is based on the Stampacchia regularity method (
[
28
]
). To this end, we state the following lemma, whose proof can be found in
[
28
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
k_{0}\geq 0
and let
\Psi:[k_{0},\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a nonincreasing function. If there exist
c,a>0
and
b>1
such that

\Psi(h)\leq\frac{c}{(h-k)^{a}}[\Psi(k)]^{b},\quad\forall h>k>k_{0},

then

\Psi(k_{0}+d)=0,\quad\text{for }d^{a}:=c2^{\frac{ab}{b-1}}[\Psi(k_{0})]^{b-1}.

Now, we are ready to prove the stability result.

Lemma 2.10
.

Assume that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function. Let
u_{n}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of

(2.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{n}=\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial\nu}+\gamma_{n}u_{n}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\lambda_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}
are sequences of nonnegative numbers. Assume that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and that
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma\in[0,\infty]
. If
u_{n}
is bounded in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
, then, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\to u
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
, where
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is

i)

a solution of (

P

λ
,
γ

) if
\gamma<\infty
,

ii)

a solution of the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) if
\gamma=\infty
.

Proof.

First of all, we can take
u_{n}
as test function in the weak formulation of (
2.5
) (see (
2.1
)) to obtain that

(2.6)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}+\gamma_{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{n}^{2}=\lambda_{n}\int_{\Omega}f(u_{n})u_{n}.

As
f(u_{n})u_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we get that
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}<C
. Since
u_{n}
is also bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then we have that
u_{n}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore,
u_{n}
has a subsequence, still denoted by
u_{n}
, such that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
to some
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. It also holds that
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and that
u_{n}\to u

\mathrm{a.e.}
in
\Omega
.

From now on, we divide the proof into two parts.

Case
\bm{\gamma_{n}\to\gamma<\infty}
:

Here, we can easily pass to the limit in the weak formulation of (
2.5
) to show that
u
is a weak (and then a classical) solution of (

P

λ
,
γ

).

To show the convergence of a subsequence of
u_{n}
to
u
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
, we take advantage of the fact that
-\gamma_{n}u_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Then, applying
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
, we deduce that the sequence
u_{n}
is bounded in
C^{0,\eta}(\overline{\Omega})
for any
\eta\in(0,1)
. Consequently, Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem ensures the existence of a subsequence of
u_{n}
that converges to
u
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
.

Case
\bm{\gamma_{n}\to\infty}
:

Dropping a nonnegative term in (
2.6
) and using that
f(u_{n})u_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we deduce that

\gamma_{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{n}^{2}<C.

As
\gamma_{n}\to\infty
, we obtain that
\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{n}^{2}\to 0
. Therefore,
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and, as the trace embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is compact, then the trace of
u
is
0
and thus
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. From the weak formulation of (
2.5
), we have that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{n}\nabla\varphi=\lambda_{n}\int_{\Omega}f(u_{n})\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Now we can easily pass to the limit to obtain that
u
is a weak (and then a classical) solution of the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
).

It remains for us to show that, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}
converges in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
to
u
. Since we make use of the Sobolev embeddings, we present the proof only for the case
N\geq 3
; the case
N=2
can be treated similarly, while for
N=1
the result follows directly from Morrey’s Theorem. We can assume that there is some
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
\gamma_{n}\geq\gamma_{0}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that, in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, the norm defined by

\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+\gamma_{0}\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\ v\in H^{1}(\Omega),

is equivalent to the usual norm and, consequently, the classical embeddings for
H^{1}(\Omega)
remain valid with this norm. In particular, recall that the embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\partial\Omega)
are continuous for any
p\in\left[1,\frac{2N}{N-2}\right]
and
q\in\left[1,\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}\right]
(see
[
24
, Chapter 2]
).

In what follows, we adapt an idea of
[
5
]
, which is based on the Stampacchia regularity method. For any
k>0
, we define the function
G_{k}(s):=\max\{\min\{s+k,0\},s-k\}
. From this point on, we fix
k>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Taking
G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
as test function in the problem satisfied by
u_{n}
(i.e., in (
2.5
)), we obtain

(2.7)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{n}\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{n}u_{n}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)=\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})G_{k}(u_{n}-u).

Now, we multiply the equation in (
1.1
) by
G_{k}(u_{n}-u)
and, since
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
(in fact,
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
), we can apply the divergence theorem to obtain

(2.8)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)=\int_{\Omega}\lambda f(u)G_{k}(u_{n}-u).

Subtracting both (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) we arrive at

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{n}u_{n}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)=\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})-\lambda f(u))G_{k}(u_{n}-u)-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}G_{k}(u_{n}-u).

With respect to the left-hand side of (
2.9
), as
u\equiv 0
in
\partial\Omega
and
sG_{k}(s)\geq G_{k}(s)^{2}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we easily deduce that

(2.10)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{n}u_{n}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)&=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{n}(u_{n}-u)G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\\
&\geq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{0}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)^{2}\\
&=\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.\end{split}

To simplify the computations related to the right-hand side of (
2.9
), we introduce some notation. Let

A_{n}(k):=\{x\in\Omega:|u_{n}(x)-u(x)|>k\},\qquad B_{n}(k):=\{x\in\partial\Omega:|u_{n}(x)-u(x)|>k\}.

Since no ambiguity can arise, we use the same symbol
|\cdot|
to denote both the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure (for subsets of
\Omega
) and the
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure (for subsets of
\partial\Omega
). We also set
q:=\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}
.

Regarding the third integral in (
2.9
), since the term
\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})-\lambda f(u)
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, say by
C_{1}>0
, we can apply Hölder’s inequality, the continuous embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\Omega)
and Young’s inequality to deduce

(2.11)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})-\lambda f(u))G_{k}(u_{n}-u)&\leq C_{1}\int_{\Omega}|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|=C_{1}\int_{A_{n}(k)}|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|\\
&\leq C_{1}|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{3}|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}},\end{split}

for some constants
C_{2},C_{3}>0
. A similar argument applies to the last integral in (
2.9
), since
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
, although in this case we rely on the continuous embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\partial\Omega)
. Reasoning as before, we obtain for some constant
C_{4}>0
that

(2.12)

-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\leq\frac{1}{4}\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{4}|B_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}.

Returning to the equality (
2.9
), we can now combine (
2.10
), (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) to obtain that

(2.13)

\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2C_{3}|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}+2C_{4}|B_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}\leq C_{5}\left(|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}+|B_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}\right),

where
C_{5}>0
. Using the Sobolev embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\partial\Omega)
, we can find some
C_{6}>0
such that

(2.14)

\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq C_{6}\left(\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\right).

Observe that, if we take
h>k>0
, then
A_{n}(h)\subseteq A_{n}(k)
and
B_{n}(h)\subseteq B_{n}(k)
. Taking into account that
|G_{k}(s)|\geq h-k
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|s|\geq h
, we deduce that

(2.15)

\begin{split}&\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}=\left(\int_{A_{n}(k)}|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}\geq(h-k)^{2}|A_{n}(h)|^{\frac{2}{q}},\\
&\|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=\left(\int_{B_{n}(k)}|G_{k}(u_{n}-u)|^{q}\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}\geq(h-k)^{2}|B_{n}(h)|^{\frac{2}{q}}.\end{split}

Joining (
2.13
), (
2.14
) and (
2.15
), we obtain

(2.16)

C_{6}(h-k)^{2}\left(|A_{n}(h)|^{\frac{2}{q}}+|B_{n}(h)|^{\frac{2}{q}}\right)\leq C_{5}\left(|A_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}+|B_{n}(k)|^{\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}\right).

Recall that for any
s,t\geq 0
and
p>0
, the following inequality holds:
\min\{1,2^{p-1}\}(s^{p}+t^{p})\leq(s+t)^{p}\leq\max\{1,2^{p-1}\}(s^{p}+t^{p})
. Applying this algebraic inequality to (
2.16
), we deduce for some
C_{7}>0
that

(2.17)

|A_{n}(h)|+|B_{n}(h)|\leq\frac{C_{7}}{(h-k)^{q}}\left(|A_{n}(k)|+|B_{n}(k)|\right)^{\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
fixed. We consider the function
\Psi_{n}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
defined as
\Psi_{n}(k):=|A_{n}(k)|+|B_{n}(k)|
for any
k\geq 0
. As
\Psi_{n}
is nonincreasing, due to relation (
2.17
) we can apply Lemma
2.9
with
k_{0}=\varepsilon
,
a=q=\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}>0
,
c=C_{7}>0
and
b=\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}=\frac{N}{N-2}>1
to obtain, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that

(2.18)

\Psi_{n}(\varepsilon+d_{n})=|A_{n}(\varepsilon+d_{n})|+|B_{n}(\varepsilon+d_{n})|=0,\text{ with }d_{n}^{a}=c2^{\frac{ab}{b-1}}\left(|A_{n}(\varepsilon)|+|B_{n}(\varepsilon)|\right)^{b-1}.

Notice that, by the definition of
A_{n}(k)
and
B_{n}(k)
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\varepsilon|A_{n}(\varepsilon)|\leq\int_{A_{n}(\varepsilon)}|u_{n}-u|\leq\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varepsilon|B_{n}(\varepsilon)|\leq\int_{B_{n}(\varepsilon)}|u_{n}-u|\leq\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{1}(\partial\Omega)}.

Since
u_{n}
converges strongly to
u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and in
L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, we can find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
(depending on
\varepsilon
) such that
d_{n}<\varepsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. As
\Psi_{n}
is nonincreasing, from (
2.18
) we deduce that
|A_{n}(2\varepsilon)|+|B_{n}(2\varepsilon)|=0
for every
n\geq n_{0}
or, equivalently, that

\|u_{n}-u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq 2\varepsilon,\ \forall n\geq n_{0}.

Then, we can conclude that
u_{n}
converges strongly in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
to
u
, where
u
is the solution that we have found for (
1.1
).
∎

Remark 2.11
.

In Lemma
2.10
above, we have assumed that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous for simplicity, in order to ensure the equivalence between classical and bounded weak solutions. However, the same statement remains valid in the setting of bounded weak solutions under the weaker assumption that
f
is merely continuous.

Remark 2.12
.

Up to our knowledge, the result concerning the case
\gamma_{n}\to\infty
is completely new in the literature. Only in the particular case
N=3
, this result can also be deduced from
[
3
]
, where the authors establish uniform
W^{1,p}
-estimates for
u_{n}
using quite different techniques.

3.
An auxiliary problem

Throughout this section, we assume that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function with two consecutive zeros,
0<\alpha<\beta
, such that
f(s)>0
when
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
. Here, we truncate
f
to define the continuous function

\tilde{f}(s)=\begin{cases}f(s)&\text{ if }s\in(\alpha,\beta),\\
0&\text{ if }s\not\in(\alpha,\beta),\\
\end{cases}

and we study some properties of the solutions to problem

(3.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda\tilde{f}(u)&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\gamma u=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

For any fixed
\gamma>0
, our aim is to prove that the maximal solution
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to (
3.1
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
converges uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
to
\beta
as
\lambda
diverges. Therefore, for large values of
\lambda
,
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
will be also a solution of (

P

λ
,
γ

).

We start proving the existence of
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
for large
\lambda
using variational methods. We also prove that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
is an increasing sequence in
\lambda
, in the sense that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}(x)
is an increasing sequence of real numbers for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma>0
be fixed. Then, there exists some
\overline{\lambda}(\gamma)>0
such that a positive maximal solution
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to
(
3.1
)
in the interval
[0,\beta]
exists for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
. Furthermore,
\overline{u}_{\lambda}(x)
is increasing in
\lambda
for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

We point out that every nontrivial solution of (
3.1
) is positive and has its maximum in the interval
(\alpha,\beta)
due to Proposition
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
. In addition, solutions of (
3.1
) correspond to critical points of the energy functional

\tilde{I}_{\lambda,\gamma}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}u^{2}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}(u),\ \forall u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

where
\tilde{F}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{f}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t
. Since
\tilde{F}
is continuous and bounded, the functional
\tilde{I}_{\gamma}
is coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous. Therefore, we can deduce that
\tilde{I}_{\gamma}
has a global minimum. Now, we prove that this minimum is not reached at the trivial function for large
\lambda
. Indeed, taking into account that
\tilde{F}(\beta)>0
, one has

\tilde{I}_{\lambda,\gamma}(\beta)=\gamma\frac{\beta^{2}|\partial\Omega|}{2}-\lambda|\Omega|\tilde{F}(\beta):=C_{1}\gamma-C_{2}\lambda,

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, so one can take

\overline{\lambda}>\frac{C_{1}\gamma}{C_{2}}

and then for every
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
one has
\tilde{I}_{\lambda,\gamma}(\beta)<0=\tilde{I}_{\lambda,\gamma}(0)
.

Then, we have proved the existence of a positive solution to (
3.1
) with maximum in
(\alpha,\beta)
. As
\beta
is always a supersolution of (
3.1
), the existence of a maximal solution
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
of (
3.1
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
can be deduced using a standard iterative scheme starting from
\beta
.

The fact that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
is increasing in
\lambda
can be easily deduced since
\tilde{f}\geq 0
. Indeed, given
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
, one has that
\overline{u}_{\lambda_{1}}
is a strict subsolution of (
3.1
) with
\lambda=\lambda_{2}
, so the maximality of
\overline{u}_{\lambda_{2}}
and Proposition
2.4
imply that
\overline{u}_{\lambda_{1}}(x)<\overline{u}_{\lambda_{2}}(x)
for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.
∎

As mentioned earlier, our aim is to show that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}\to\beta
uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
when
\lambda\to\infty
. We begin by proving this result when
\Omega
is a ball
B_{R}(0)
of radius
R>0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
, there exists some
\overline{\lambda}(\varepsilon)
such that the Dirichlet problem

(3.2)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w=\lambda\tilde{f}(w)&\mbox{in}\;B_{R}(0),\\
w=\beta-\varepsilon&\mbox{on}\;\partial B_{R}(0),\\
w(x)\in(\beta-\varepsilon,\beta)&\mbox{for any}\;x\in B_{R}(0),\end{cases}

has a radial solution
w_{\lambda}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
. Moreover,
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Remark 3.3
.

This result immediately implies that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}\to\beta
uniformly in
\overline{B}_{R}(0)
when
\lambda\to\infty
, where
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
denotes the solution to
(
3.1
)
with
\Omega=\overline{B}_{R}(0)
given by Lemma
3.1
. Indeed, given any
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
, we can take
\lambda
large so that
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)<-\gamma(\beta-\varepsilon)
. This ensures that
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)+\gamma w_{\lambda}(R)<0
, and thus
w_{\lambda}
is a strict subsolution of (
3.1
). By
Proposition
2.4
, it then follows that
w_{\lambda}<\overline{u}_{\lambda}
in
\overline{B}_{R}(0)
for sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
. We define
g(s):=\tilde{f}(s+\beta-\varepsilon)
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that if
v_{\lambda}
is a solution of the Dirichlet problem

(3.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=\lambda g(v)&\mbox{in}\;B_{R}(0),\\
v=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial B_{R}(0),\\
v(x)\in(0,\varepsilon)&\mbox{for any}\;x\in B_{R}(0),\end{cases}

then
w_{\lambda}:=v_{\lambda}+\beta-\varepsilon
is a solution of (
3.2
) with the same derivative on the boundary. Therefore, for our purposes, it suffices to study problem (
3.3
).

The existence of a solution of (
3.3
) for large
\lambda
is standard. Indeed, as
g(s)\geq 0
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
g(\varepsilon)=0
, one can use, for instance,
[
7
, Theorem 3]
, to obtain the existence of some
\overline{\lambda}>0
for which a maximal solution
v_{\lambda}
to (
3.3
) exists for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
. Moreover,
[
7
, Theorem 3]
also guarantees that
v_{\lambda}
is an increasing sequence in
B_{R}(\Omega)
that converges uniformly in compact subsets of
B_{R}(\Omega)
to
\varepsilon
. Using the results of
[
17
]
, we can also ensure that
v_{\lambda}
is radially symmetric and radially decreasing, so we can write
v_{\lambda}(r)
, where
r=|x|
.

It remains for us to show that
v^{\prime}_{\lambda}(R)\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
. The Pohozaev identity for radial solutions gives us the relation

(3.4)

\left((v_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)\right)^{2}=2R^{-N}\lambda\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1}\left(NG(v_{\lambda}(r))-\frac{N-2}{2}v_{\lambda}(r)g(v_{\lambda}(r))\right)\ \mathrm{d}r,

where
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(t)\ \mathrm{d}t
. As
v_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)<0
thanks to Hopf’s Lemma, to our aim it suffices to show the existence of some
C>0
such that

(3.5)

\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1}\left(NG(v_{\lambda}(r))-\frac{N-2}{2}v_{\lambda}(r)g(v_{\lambda}(r))\right)\ \mathrm{d}r>C

for every
\lambda
greater than some value.

To simplify computations, we define the function
h(s):=NG(s)-\frac{N-2}{2}sg(s)
for all
s\in[0,\varepsilon]
. Observe that
h(\varepsilon)=NG(\varepsilon)>0
and, as
h
is continuous, we can find some
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that
h(s)>C_{1}>0
for any
s\in[\varepsilon-\delta,\varepsilon]
. Furthermore,
h
, that can be negative, is bounded, so there is some
C_{2}>0
such that
h(s)>-C_{2}
for all
s\in[0,\varepsilon]
.

On the other hand, since
v_{\lambda}
converges uniformly to
\varepsilon
in compact subsets of
B_{R}(0)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can find
\lambda_{n}>0
large such that
v_{\lambda}(r)\in[\varepsilon-\delta,\varepsilon]
for every
0\leq r\leq R-\frac{R}{n}
and for every
\lambda>\lambda_{n}
. Then, for any
\lambda>\lambda_{n}
we obtain that

(3.6)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1}h(v_{\lambda}(r))\ \mathrm{d}r&=\int_{0}^{R-\frac{R}{n}}r^{N-1}h(v_{\lambda}(r))\ \mathrm{d}r+\int_{R-\frac{R}{n}}^{R}r^{N-1}h(v_{\lambda}(r))\ \mathrm{d}r\\
&\geq C_{1}\int_{0}^{R-\frac{R}{n}}r^{N-1}\ \mathrm{d}r-C_{2}\int_{R-\frac{R}{n}}^{R}r^{N-1}\ \mathrm{d}r\\
&=\frac{1}{N}C_{1}\left(R-\frac{R}{n}\right)^{N}-\frac{1}{N}C_{2}\left(R^{N}-\left(R-\frac{R}{n}\right)^{N}\right).\end{split}

Since this last quantity converges to
\frac{1}{N}C_{1}R^{N}>0
when
n\to\infty
, one can take
n_{0}
large to obtain from (
3.6
) that (
3.5
) holds for any
\lambda>\lambda_{n_{0}}
. Therefore, from (
3.4
) we deduce that
v_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)\leq-\sqrt{2R^{-N}C}\sqrt{\lambda}
for any
\lambda>\lambda_{n_{0}}
and, consequently, we can conclude that
v_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R)\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
.
∎

Now, we return to consider a general bounded domain
\Omega
with
C^{2}
boundary. The next result provides the convergence in
C(\overline{\Omega})
of
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to
\beta
. The proof, which uses an original approach, is based on geometrical arguments.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\gamma>0
be fixed. Then, the sequence of solutions
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to (
3.1
) given by Lemma
3.1
converges uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
to
\beta
.

Proof.

When
N=1
, this result follows directly from Lemma
3.2
. Therefore, in the following, we assume
N\geq 2
. We also point out that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
is an increasing sequence of continuous functions in a compact domain, so due to Dini’s Theorem it suffices to prove that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}\to\beta
pointwise in
\overline{\Omega}
.

In
\Omega
, the pointwise convergence of
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to
\beta
is a consequence of the well-known behaviour of the solutions to the Dirichlet problem

(3.7)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w=\lambda\tilde{f}(w)&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
w=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

To be concrete, in
[
7
, Theorem 3]
, it is proved that a positive solution
w_{\lambda}
to (
3.7
) with maximum in
(\alpha,\beta)
exists for large
\lambda
and that
w_{\lambda}\to\beta
uniformly in compact subsets of
\Omega
. As
w_{\lambda}
is a strict subsolution of
(
3.1
)
, then
w_{\lambda}<\overline{u}_{\lambda}
thanks to Proposition
2.4
and thus
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
also converges to
\beta
uniformly in compact subsets of
\Omega
. In particular,
\overline{u}_{\lambda}(x)
converges to
\beta
for any
x\in\Omega
.

It remains for us to show that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}\to\beta
pointwise in
\partial\Omega
. This will be done by constructing, for each small
\varepsilon>0
and each point
y_{0}
of the boundary, a subsolution of (
3.1
) for sufficiently large
\lambda
that takes the value
\beta-\varepsilon
in
y_{0}
. Here, we will make use of Lemma
3.2
.

With this aim, we fix
y_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
\varepsilon>0
small. As
\partial\Omega
satisfies the interior ball condition, there is some
x_{0}\in\Omega
and some
R_{1}>0
such that
B_{R_{1}}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
and
\partial B_{R_{1}}(x_{0})\cap\partial\Omega=\{y_{0}\}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we define
r(x)=|x-x_{0}|
. Observe that, for any
y\in\partial\Omega
, we have that

\frac{\partial r}{\partial\nu}(y)=\nabla r(y)\cdot\nu(y)=\frac{y-x_{0}}{|y-x_{0}|}\cdot\nu(y),

where, we recall,
\nu(y)
denotes the outward normal unit vector to
\partial\Omega
at
y
. As
B_{R_{1}}(x_{0})
is tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
y_{0}
, the outward normal vectors to both surfaces at
y_{0}
must be equal. Then, we obtain

\frac{\partial r}{\partial\nu}(y_{0})=1.

As
\nu(y)
is a continuous function (due to the smoothness of
\partial\Omega
), we can find
R_{2}>R_{1}
such that

(3.8)

\frac{\partial r}{\partial\nu}(y)\geq\frac{1}{2},\quad\forall y\in\Gamma_{1}:=B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\partial\Omega.

Now, we construct the subsolution in the following way. Using Lemma
3.2
with
B_{R}(0)
replaced by
B_{R_{1}}(x_{0})
, we can find some
\overline{\lambda}_{1}(\varepsilon,y_{0})>0
such that a radial solution
w_{\lambda}(r)
to (
3.2
) defined in
B_{R_{1}}(x_{0})
exists for every
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{1}
(recall
r(x)=|x-x_{0}|
). Furthermore, it holds that
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})=-\infty
. When
N=2
, we extend this function to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(r)=\begin{cases}w_{\lambda}(r)&\text{ if }0\leq r<R_{1},\\
\beta-\varepsilon+\big(\log r-\log R_{1}\big)R_{1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})&\text{ if }R_{1}\leq r<R_{2},\\
\beta-\varepsilon+\big(\log R_{2}-\log R_{1}\big)R_{1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})&\text{ if }r\geq R_{2},\end{cases}

and, when
N\geq 3
, as

\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(r)=\begin{cases}w_{\lambda}(r)&\text{ if }0\leq r<R_{1},\\
\beta-\varepsilon+\left(r^{2-N}-R_{1}^{2-N}\right)R_{1}^{N-1}(2-N)^{-1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})&\text{ if }R_{1}\leq r<R_{2},\\
\beta-\varepsilon+\left(R_{2}^{2-N}-R_{1}^{2-N}\right)R_{1}^{N-1}(2-N)^{-1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})&\text{ if }r\geq R_{2}.\end{cases}

Since
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})=-\infty
, we can take
\overline{\lambda}_{2}>\overline{\lambda}_{1}
such that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(R_{2})<0
for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{2}
.

We observe that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(r)
is continuous, radially decreasing for
0\leq r\leq R_{2}
, and identically equal to a negative constant for
r>R_{2}
, provided that
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{2}
. Moreover,
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(r)
is harmonic when
R_{1}<r<R_{2}
. We claim that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}
is a weak subsolution of (
3.1
) for sufficiently large
\lambda
. Then, we have to show that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
verifies

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\nabla\varphi+\gamma\int_{\partial\Omega}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\varphi\leq\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\text{ with }\varphi\geq 0

when
\lambda
is large. From now on, we fix
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\text{ with }\varphi\geq 0
. To ease the notation, we set
\Gamma_{2}=(\Omega\setminus B_{R_{2}}(x_{0}))\cap\partial\Omega
and
\Gamma_{3}=\partial B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\Omega
(see Figure
2
).

Figure 2
.
Sketch of the different sets that appear in the proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Of course,
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. On the one hand, as
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\in H^{2}\left(B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\Omega\right)
because
\lim_{r\to R_{1}^{-}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)
is equal to
\lim_{r\to R_{1}^{+}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)
, and as
-\Delta\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\leq\lambda\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})

a.e.
in
B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\Omega
, we can integrate by parts to obtain

(3.9)

\int_{B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\Omega}\nabla\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\nabla\varphi-\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}\varphi-\int_{\Gamma_{3}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{-})\varphi\leq\lambda\int_{B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\cap\Omega}\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})\varphi,

where
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{-}):=\lim_{r\to R_{2}^{-}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)
. On the other hand, we obviously have
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\in H^{2}\left(\Omega\setminus B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})\right)
and
-\Delta\tilde{w}_{\lambda}=0\leq\lambda\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})
in
\Omega\setminus B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})
, so integration by parts yields

(3.10)

\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})}\nabla\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\nabla\varphi-\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}\varphi+\int_{\Gamma_{3}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{+})\varphi\leq\lambda\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{R_{2}}(x_{0})}\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})\varphi,

where, as before,
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{+}):=\lim_{r\to R_{2}^{+}}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)
. Taking into account that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{-})=(R_{1}/R_{2})^{N-1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})<0=\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{2}^{+})
, we can sum both (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) to obtain, after dropping the nonnegative term related to
\Gamma_{3}
, that

(3.11)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{w}_{\lambda}\nabla\varphi-\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}\varphi-\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}\varphi\leq\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(\tilde{w}_{\lambda})\varphi.

Now, we focus our attention on the boundary terms. When
y\in\Gamma_{1}
, we have that
r(y)\in[R_{1},R_{2})
. Here, we stress that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y)\leq\beta-\varepsilon
and that
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r(y))\leq(R_{1}/R_{2})^{N-1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})<0
. Using (
3.8
), we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}(y)+\gamma\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y)&\leq\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}(y)+\gamma(\beta-\varepsilon)=\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r(y))\frac{\partial r}{\partial\nu}(y)+\gamma(\beta-\varepsilon)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r(y))+\gamma(\beta-\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{2}(R_{1}/R_{2})^{N-1}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})+\gamma(\beta-\varepsilon).\end{split}

Since
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(R_{1})=-\infty
, we can find
\overline{\lambda}_{3}>\overline{\lambda}_{2}
such that, for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{3}
, it holds

(3.12)

\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}(y)+\gamma\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y)<0\text{ for all }y\in\Gamma_{1}.

Besides, when
y\in\Gamma_{2}
we have that
r(y)>R_{2}
, so
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}
is negative on this set and its normal derivative is zero. Therefore, we obtain

(3.13)

\frac{\partial\tilde{w}_{\lambda}}{\partial\nu}(y)+\gamma\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y)=\gamma\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y)<0\text{ for all }y\in\Gamma_{2}.

Joining (
3.11
), (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), we deduce that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}
is a weak subsolution of (
3.1
) provided
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{2}
. As
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
is the maximal solution of (
3.1
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
, we have that
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(x)\leq\overline{u}_{\lambda}(x)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and all
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}_{3}(\varepsilon,y_{0})
. In particular,
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}(y_{0})=\beta-\varepsilon\leq\overline{u}_{\lambda}(y_{0})
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we can affirm that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}(y_{0})\to\beta
as
\lambda\to\infty
and this concludes the proof.
∎

In the following, we aim to study problem (
3.1
) for a fixed
\lambda>0
to determine whether a solution to (
3.1
) exists when
\gamma

. Since

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\tilde{f}(u)&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+\gamma u=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proposition 3.5
.

There is some constant
\overline{\gamma}>0
such that, for any
0<\gamma<\overline{\gamma}

) has a positive maximal solution in the interval

. Moreover,
\overline{u}_{\gamma}\to\beta
uniformly in

Proof.

The existence of some
\overline{\gamma}>0
for which a nontrivial solution to (

can be proved using variational methods in the same way as in Lemma
3.1
. Due to Proposition
2.4
and Corollary

, this solution is positive and has its maximum in
(\alpha,\beta)
. As
\beta
is always a supersolution of (

the maximal solution
\overline{u}_{\gamma}
to (
3.14
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
exists.

Now, observe that
\overline{u}_{\gamma}(x)
is a decreasing function in
\gamma
for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. Indeed, let
0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\overline{\gamma}
. Then
\overline{u}_{\gamma_{2}}
is a strict subsolution to (

P
γ

) with
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
. Taking into account that
\overline{u}_{\gamma_{1}}
is the maximal solution of (

P
γ

) with
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
and using Proposition

, we easily deduce that
\overline{u}_{\gamma_{2}}<\overline{u}_{\gamma_{1}}
in
\overline{\Omega}
.

Therefore, we can define

. As
\overline{u}_{\gamma}
is bounded in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
by

implies that

Since

to ensure the existence of some

∎

Remark 4.1
.

If

Now, we can easily prove Theorem

taking into account the well-known results of existence and nonexistence of solution for the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
).

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\gamma>0
be fixed. Consider the set

A_{\gamma}:=\left\{\lambda\geq 0:\textrm{\eqref{eq:PbLambda} admits solution $u\geq 0$ with }\alpha<\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}<\beta\right\}.

Thanks to Theorem
1.1
, we have that
A_{\gamma}\neq\emptyset
. By Lemma
2.10
and Corollary
2.5
, we can also ensure that this set is closed. Then, we can define

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)=\min A_{\gamma}.

Since
0\notin A_{\gamma}
, then
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)>0
. By definition, we have that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{mult}}(\gamma)
, where
\lambda_{\mathrm{mult}}(\gamma)>0
is the one given in Theorem
1.1
. Since
\lambda_{\mathrm{mult}}(\gamma)\to 0
as
\gamma\to 0
, we immediately obtain that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)\to 0
when
\gamma\to 0
.

Finally, we study the behaviour of
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)
as
\gamma
goes to
\infty
.

On the one hand, if (
1.2
) does not hold, then
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)
cannot have any accumulation point at infinity. The reason is that, if some accumulation point
\lambda
exists, one could apply Lemma
2.10
to find a solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) belonging to
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
for this
\lambda
, but this is a contradiction with
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. Therefore, we deduce that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)\to\infty
as
\gamma\to\infty
.

On the other hand, if (
1.2
) holds, then

\lambda_{\infty}:=\min\left\{\lambda\geq 0:\textrm{\eqref{eq:PbDirich} admits solution $u\geq 0$ with }\alpha<\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}<\beta\right\}

is finite and positive by
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. Let
w_{\lambda_{\infty}}
be a nonnegative solution of (
1.1
) for
\lambda=\lambda_{\infty}
with maximum between
\alpha
and
\beta
. Thanks to the Hopf’s Lemma,
w_{\lambda_{\infty}}
is a subsolution of
(P_{\lambda_{\infty},\gamma})
for any
\gamma>0
and, as
\beta
is always a supersolution, we deduce that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)\leq\lambda_{\infty}
for all
\gamma>0
. Due to the definition of
\lambda_{\infty}
(and thanks to Lemma
2.10
), the only possible accumulation point of
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)
at infinity is
\lambda_{\infty}
. Then, we can conclude that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\gamma)\to\lambda_{\infty}
as
\gamma\to\infty
.
∎

To conclude this section, we prove Theorem

, which provides some information about the behaviour of the two solutions found in Theorem

when
\gamma
approaches zero and

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Theorem
1.1
, we know that
\lambda_{\mathrm{mult}}(\gamma)\to 0
when
\gamma\to 0
. Consequently, we can find
\tilde{\gamma}>0
such that
\lambda_{\mathrm{mult}}(\gamma)<1
for all
\gamma<\tilde{\gamma}
. Then, for
\gamma<\tilde{\gamma}
, problem (

P
γ

) has two solutions in
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\beta}
, which we denote by
\overline{u}_{\gamma}
and
\tilde{u}_{\gamma}
, where
\overline{u}_{\gamma}
is the maximal solution in
[0,\beta]
.

Arguing as in Proposition
3.5
, we can prove that
\overline{u}_{\gamma}(x)
is a nonincreasing function in
\gamma
for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and that
\overline{u}_{\gamma}\to\beta
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
when
\gamma\to 0
.

On the other hand, Lemma
2.10
implies that
\tilde{u}_{\gamma}\to\tilde{u}
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
, where
\tilde{u}
denotes a solution for the Neumann problem (
1.4
). Notice that
\|\tilde{u}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Since we are assuming that (
1.4
) has no non-constant solutions with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
, it follows that either
\tilde{u}\equiv\alpha
or
\tilde{u}\equiv\beta
. Our aim is to prove that only the first case can happen. To do that, we follow the ideas of
[
13
]
. The strategy is to show that (

P
γ

) admits at most one solution in a left neighbourhood of
\beta
when
\gamma
is small enough.

First, we prove that any solution
u
to (

P
γ

) contained in
(\beta-\delta,\beta)
is stable, where
\delta
is given by (
1.5
). Recall that a solution
u
to (

P
γ

) is stable if the first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\gamma,f^{\prime}(u))
of the problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\Delta v-f^{\prime}(u)v=\mu v&\mbox{in}\;\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}+\gamma v=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

is positive, i.e., if
\mu_{1}(\gamma,f^{\prime}(u))>0
. This eigenvalue is given by

\mu_{1}(\gamma,f^{\prime}(u))=\inf_{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega),\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)=1}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi^{2}+\gamma\int_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\varphi^{2}\right).

If
u
is such that
u(x)\in(\beta-\delta,\beta)
for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, then hypothesis (
1.5
) implies that
f^{\prime}(u)\leq 0
in
\Omega
. In this way, we deduce that
\mu_{1}(\gamma,f^{\prime}(u))\geq\mu_{1}(\gamma,0)>0
, where we have taken into account that
\mu_{1}(\gamma,0)
is just the first eigenvalue of the Robin Laplacian, which is positive. Therefore,
u
is stable.

Let
\gamma_{0}>0
be such that
\overline{u}_{\gamma_{0}}(x)\in(\beta-\delta,\beta)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. We claim that (

P
γ

) has at most one solution contained in the set

\mathcal{O}_{1}=\{u\in{C(\overline{\Omega})}:\overline{u}_{\gamma_{0}}(x)<u(x)<\beta\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}\}

when
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
. In fact, for any
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
function
\overline{u}_{\gamma_{0}}
is a strict subsolution of (

P
γ

) and
\beta
is a strict supersolution, so Lemma
2.8
implies that

\operatorname{deg}(I-K,\mathcal{O}_{1},0)=1,

where

is defined in (
2.4
) (with
\lambda=1
).

Since any solution to (

P
γ

) contained in

is stable, then any such solution has index 1. This is because any stable solution is an isolated fixed point of

, which implies that its index is 1 (see
[

]
). Together with the the additivity of the topological degree, this allows us to deduce that there is only one solution to (

P
γ

) contained in
\mathcal{O}_{1}
.

As
\overline{u}_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
for every
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
, the sequence
\tilde{u}_{\gamma}
cannot converge to
\beta
in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
. Therefore, we conclude that

in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
.
∎
