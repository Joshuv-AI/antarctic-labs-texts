Quality of life and associated factors among naturally postmenopausal women in Arar Saudi Arabia
Authors: Fathia Ahmed Mersal, Ateya Megahed Ibrahim, Fathia Gamal Elsaid Hassabelnaby, Heba Ahmed Osman Mohamed, Rasmia Abd El Sattar Ali, Safaa Ibrahim Ahmed, Anu Sam Jacob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to assess menopause-specific quality of life among naturally postmenopausal women in Arar City, Saudi Arabia, and to examine its association with selected sociodemographic, reproductive, and health-related characteristics. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 377 naturally postmenopausal women aged 40 years or older recruited from primary health care centers in Arar City. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that captured sociodemographic and health-related characteristics together with the 29-item Menopause-Specific Quality of Life (MENQOL) questionnaire. Descriptive statistics summarized the data, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests examined associations between categorized quality-of-life outcomes and participant characteristics. To complement the categorical analyses, MENQOL domain scores were also examined as continuous variables in a sensitivity analysis using independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance. Most participants were aged 46–65 years (85.1%), married (75.1%), and had secondary or university education (84.4%). Sexual symptoms were the most affected domain (81.5%), followed by physical (74.0%), psychological (72.7%), and vasomotor symptoms (71.9%). Poorer overall quality of life was significantly associated with marital status, parity, occupation, household income, body mass index, age at menarche, age at natural menopause, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and negative perceptions of life after menopause (all p < 0.05). Detailed frequencies and test statistics are presented in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Naturally postmenopausal women in this Saudi sample experienced a substantial menopausal symptom burden, particularly in the sexual, physical, and psychological domains. Several social and health-related factors were associated with poorer quality of life, supporting the need for culturally responsive nursing assessment, lifestyle counselling, and psychosocial support to enhance menopausal care and well-being.Keywords: Menopause, postmenopause, quality of life, MENQOL, Saudi Arabia, nursing practiceSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Menopause is a normal biological stage marked by the permanent cessation of menstruation after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea. The menopausal transition extends from the late reproductive stage through the early and late transition stages to postmenopause, and it is commonly accompanied by vasomotor, sleep, mood, and somatic symptoms that may interfere with daily functioning and well-being1,2.

As women live longer, a substantial proportion of life is now spent after menopause, making menopause-related quality of life an important public health issue. Quality of life during this stage is multidimensional and may be affected by vasomotor symptoms, musculoskeletal discomfort, fatigue, sleep disturbance, memory problems, emotional distress, and sexual concerns3,4.

In Saudi Arabia, menopausal experiences occur within a distinct sociocultural context that may shape symptom perception, coping strategies, help-seeking behavior, and the discussion of sensitive concerns such as sexuality and psychological distress. Recent Saudi studies suggest that women’s knowledge, attitudes, and lived experiences of menopause are influenced by social expectations, available support, and access to health information, all of which may affect quality of life and care needs5,6.

Although menopause-related quality of life has been examined in Saudi Arabia and elsewhere, important gaps remain. Existing evidence does not sufficiently explain how sociodemographic, reproductive, and health-related factors interact with menopause-specific quality of life in Saudi women, and regionally relevant evidence to guide culturally responsive nursing care remains limited. A clearer understanding of these associations is needed to support targeted education, symptom assessment, counselling, and referral for women at higher risk of poor quality of life5,7,8.

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess menopause-specific quality of life among naturally postmenopausal women in Arar City, Saudi Arabia, across the vasomotor, psychosocial, physical, and sexual domains, and to examine its association with selected sociodemographic, reproductive, and health-related characteristics. By focusing specifically on naturally postmenopausal women, the study provides contextually relevant evidence to inform nursing practice and women’s health services in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among naturally postmenopausal women in Arar City, Northern Border Region, Saudi Arabia. Participants were recruited through selected primary health care centers in Arar. For the purposes of the study, postmenopause was defined as the absence of menstruation for 12 consecutive months not attributable to pregnancy, lactation, or hormone therapy. Accordingly, women in the late reproductive stage and the early or late menopausal transition were not included.

Sample size and sampling technique

The required sample size was estimated before data collection using a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, with menopause-specific quality of life measured by the MENQOL questionnaire as the primary outcome. The calculation assumed an expected prevalence of moderate to severe menopausal symptoms of 50%, which provides the most conservative estimate, with allowance for a 10% non-response rate. A total of 377 eligible women were included in the final analysis. A convenience sampling technique was used; the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution because this approach may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability.

Eligibility criteria

Women were eligible to participate if they were aged 40 years or older, resided in Arar City, were naturally postmenopausal as defined above, were mentally competent to provide informed consent, and were able to complete the questionnaire independently. The lower age boundary was set at 40 years rather than 45 years in order to include women experiencing early natural menopause, which is clinically recognized as menopause occurring between 40 and 45 years of age and which may carry distinct symptom profiles and quality-of-life implications16. Six participants (1.6%) were aged 40–44 years, all of whom met the definition of natural postmenopause and were therefore retained in the analyses. Women were excluded if they were using hormone replacement therapy, had a history of cancer, were pregnant or lactating, or had more than two chronic conditions. The exclusion of women with more than two chronic conditions was intended to reduce substantial confounding from advanced multimorbidity when evaluating menopause-specific quality of life; this criterion may, however, limit the generalizability of the findings to women with a greater chronic disease burden.

Recruitment and data collection procedure

Eligible women attending the selected primary health care centers were informed about the study and invited to participate. Data were collected once from each participant using a structured self-administered questionnaire distributed electronically through Google Forms in order to facilitate participation, privacy, and completeness of responses. Before the main survey, the questionnaire was pilot tested on 30 women from the target population to assess clarity, readability, and cultural appropriateness; pilot responses were excluded from the final analysis.

Study instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising three sections.

Section I: Sociodemographic characteristics. This section included age, educational level, marital status, parity, occupation, monthly household income, smoking status, height, and weight. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) and classified according to the World Health Organization categories.

Section II: Reproductive and health-related characteristics. This section captured age at menarche, age at natural menopause, the presence and type of chronic diseases, and two researcher-developed descriptive items concerning women’s general perceptions of life after menopause: a single-item question on perceived change in life after menopause and a single-item question on current general feeling.

Section III: Menopause-Specific Quality of Life questionnaire (MENQOL). Menopause-specific quality of life was assessed using the 29-item MENQOL questionnaire, a validated instrument developed to measure the extent to which menopausal symptoms affect women’s daily lives9. The instrument assesses four domains: vasomotor (3 items), psychosocial (7 items), physical (16 items), and sexual (3 items). Responses were scored on a Likert-type scale, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden and poorer menopause-specific quality of life. MENQOL was the primary outcome measure.

Validity and reliability

The Arabic version of the MENQOL was used. Forward translation from English to Arabic was performed by two independent bilingual translators, followed by back translation by a third translator, with reconciliation of discrepancies by an expert committee. The questionnaire was reviewed for clarity and cultural appropriateness before use. Internal consistency in the present sample was satisfactory, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89 for the total scale, 0.82 for the vasomotor domain, 0.85 for the psychosocial domain, 0.88 for the physical domain, and 0.79 for the sexual domain.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Local Committee of Bioethics at Northern Border University (HAP-09-A-043), decision no. 62/24/H, dated 23/05/2024. Administrative approval was obtained from the relevant primary health care centers before data collection. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire completion. Participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous, and data were stored securely and analyzed in aggregate form only.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range; categorical variables were summarized using frequency and percentage. Associations between categorical variables and the categorized quality-of-life outcome were examined using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell counts were small. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The cut-points used to dichotomize MENQOL total and domain scores into “better” and “poorer” quality of life were data-driven and determined post hoc rather than pre-specified, because the MENQOL has no universally accepted clinical threshold. The cut-points were derived from the empirical distribution of scores in the present sample, with the median used as the dividing point. The observed cut-points were 0.00–2.57 (better) versus 2.61–5.00 (poorer) for the overall score; 0.00–2.33 versus 2.67–5.00 for the vasomotor domain; 0.00–2.57 versus 2.71–5.00 for the psychosocial domain; and 0.00–2.53 versus 2.60–4.67 for the physical domain. Sexual-domain analyses were restricted to the 283 women with analyzable sexual-domain responses. Because these cut-points are sample-specific and post hoc, they are reported transparently, and their interpretive limitations are addressed in the Discussion. To examine the robustness of the findings to the choice of cut-points, MENQOL total and domain scores were additionally analyzed as continuous variables in a sensitivity analysis, with comparisons across participant characteristics performed using independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance, as appropriate.

Sensitivity analysis using continuous MENQOL scores

In the sensitivity analysis using continuous MENQOL scores, mean total and domain scores were significantly higher, indicating greater symptom burden, among married women, women with children, women with overweight or obesity, smokers, women with menopause before age 45 years, and women with hypertension or diabetes mellitus, as well as among women reporting that life had become worse after menopause or that they felt less beautiful (all p < 0.05). The direction and statistical significance of these continuous-score associations were consistent with those observed for the dichotomized outcome, supporting the robustness of the findings irrespective of the categorization approach.

Discussion

This study examined menopause-specific quality of life among naturally postmenopausal women in Arar, Saudi Arabia, and identified a substantial symptom burden across all MENQOL domains. Poorer overall quality of life was observed in a large proportion of participants, with pronounced impairment in the sexual, physical, psychological, and vasomotor domains. These findings indicate that menopause in this population is not merely a biological transition but a multidimensional life stage shaped by symptom experience, chronic health conditions, socioeconomic context, and women’s perceptions of themselves and their lives after menopause. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence showing that menopause-related quality of life is influenced by the interaction of physical, emotional, and contextual factors rather than by symptom frequency alone10,11,8.

The overall pattern of results is broadly consistent with previous studies from Saudi Arabia and other regional settings, indicating that menopausal symptoms are associated with reduced quality of life, particularly when women experience high physical symptom burden, emotional distress, or social disadvantage. Prior Saudi and Gulf studies have similarly reported that fatigue, sleep disturbance, mood-related symptoms, and vasomotor complaints are common and clinically important during the menopausal transition10,12,7. The present findings, therefore, reinforce the growing regional literature indicating that menopausal quality of life should be understood within a broader health and social framework rather than as an isolated gynecological concern.

A particularly noteworthy finding is the marked burden observed in the sexual domain. This result requires careful interpretation within the Saudi sociocultural context, where sexual symptoms may be insufficiently explored in routine healthcare encounters. Symptoms such as vaginal dryness, reduced desire, and discomfort with intimacy may remain underreported because sexuality is often treated as a private and sensitive subject. Qualitative research from Saudi Arabia suggests that many women experience menopause within a framework of stigma, privacy concerns, and apprehension about ageing, femininity, and attractiveness5. The high sexual-domain burden identified in the present study may therefore reflect an important but under-recognized area of unmet need rather than simply a greater frequency of symptoms.

The findings also underscore the importance of psychosocial interpretation in menopause-related quality of life. Negative perceptions of life after menopause, including feeling that life had worsened or feeling less beautiful, were strongly associated with poorer quality of life, suggesting that women’s subjective understanding of menopause may shape symptom appraisal, coping, and well-being. The existing literature supports the view that menopause can be experienced in contrasting ways: as a natural and even relieving transition for some women, but as a socially sensitive stage associated with ageing, altered femininity, and emotional vulnerability for others5,13. Psychological distress may therefore arise not only from symptoms themselves but also from the meanings attached to menopause within family, social, and cultural environments.

The associations between poorer quality of life and lower household income, higher body mass index, smoking, chronic disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus are clinically significant. These variables likely act cumulatively, increasing physical discomfort, reducing adaptive capacity, and intensifying the psychological burden of menopause. Previous studies have similarly identified chronic disease and insufficient income as important predictors of poorer health-related quality of life among postmenopausal women14,15,11. The present findings therefore support viewing menopause within the wider context of women’s long-term health, particularly cardiometabolic risk, obesity, and lifestyle-related factors that may aggravate symptom burden and functional impairment.

The significant association between earlier menopause and poorer quality of life is consistent with prior research indicating that women who enter menopause at a younger age may report more severe symptoms and greater disruption to well-being16. Earlier reproductive ageing may shorten the period of perceived normalcy in midlife and intensify distress related to bodily change, role adjustment, and health concerns. The observed relationship between early menarche and poorer menopause-related quality of life further suggests that reproductive timing across the life course may be relevant to later symptom experience. Although causality cannot be inferred in the present cross-sectional design, these findings support closer clinical attention to women with atypical reproductive timing and highlight the need for future longitudinal studies to clarify these pathways17–19.

Compared with some regional studies, the current findings demonstrate both convergence and divergence in symptom patterns. As in previous Saudi and neighboring-country studies, physical and psychological symptoms were highly prevalent and clearly relevant to daily functioning20,10,12,6. However, the particularly high burden observed in the sexual domain may differ from reports in some Arab studies in which sexual symptoms were less frequently disclosed. One plausible explanation is that an anonymous self-administered assessment may facilitate reporting of sensitive symptoms more effectively than face-to-face interviews. Differences in educational level, health literacy, marital context, and cultural comfort with discussing sexual health may also contribute to variation across studies. These differences underscore the importance of interpreting regional evidence with caution rather than assuming uniform menopausal experiences across Middle Eastern settings.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths, including the use of a culturally adapted Arabic version of the MENQOL with satisfactory internal consistency across all four domains, the inclusion of perception-related items that broaden the interpretive context, and the focus on a relatively understudied population in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings beyond women attending the selected primary healthcare centres in Arar. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Self-reported data may be subject to recall, reporting, and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive issues such as sexual symptoms. A further methodological limitation is that the cut-points used to dichotomize MENQOL total and domain scores into “better” and “poorer” quality of life were data-driven and determined post hoc, because the MENQOL has no universally accepted clinical threshold. To address this limitation, MENQOL total and domain scores were also analyzed as continuous variables in a sensitivity analysis, which yielded results consistent with the categorical analyses. Nevertheless, sample-specific cut-points may not be directly comparable with those derived in other populations and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, analyses of the sexual domain were restricted to participants with analyzable responses, which should be considered when interpreting findings within that domain.

Conclusion

Naturally postmenopausal women in this Saudi sample experienced substantial impairment in menopause-specific quality of life across the vasomotor, psychological, physical, and sexual domains. Poorer quality of life was significantly associated with multiple sociodemographic, reproductive, and health-related factors, particularly chronic disease, excess weight, smoking, earlier menopause, and negative self-perceptions after menopause. These findings indicate that menopause should be understood as a multidimensional transition shaped by biological, psychological, social, and cultural influences rather than as a purely physiological event. The study underscores the importance of culturally responsive and clinically practical menopause care within primary healthcare settings, including education, psychosocial support, lifestyle counselling, and timely referral for persistent or severe concerns.

Implications for nursing practice

The present findings have direct implications for nursing care of postmenopausal women in Saudi primary healthcare. Nurses are well placed to incorporate routine, structured menopausal symptom assessment into midlife women’s health visits, using validated instruments such as the MENQOL to standardize inquiry across the vasomotor, psychosocial, physical, and sexual domains9,21,22. Particular attention should be given to the sexual domain, which emerged as the most affected in this sample yet remains culturally sensitive and underexplored in routine encounters. Given the observed associations with body mass index, smoking, chronic disease, earlier menopause, and negative self-perceptions, nursing care should also integrate brief lifestyle counselling, psychosocial support, and culturally appropriate referral pathways for women at higher risk of poorer quality of life8,23–25.

Recommendations

In view of the descriptive cross-sectional design and convenience-based recruitment of the present study, three concise recommendations directly supported by the findings are proposed: (i) integrate routine MENQOL-based symptom assessment into primary healthcare visits for midlife and postmenopausal women; (ii) develop culturally appropriate, Arabic-language educational resources that address the sexual, psychosocial, and lifestyle aspects of menopause; and (iii) conduct longitudinal and population-based studies to clarify temporal relationships between reproductive timing, chronic disease, symptom burden, and quality of life, complemented by qualitative research exploring how cultural beliefs and gender norms shape menopausal experiences among Saudi women.
