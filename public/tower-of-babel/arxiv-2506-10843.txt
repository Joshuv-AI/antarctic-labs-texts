A voice for minorities: diversity in approval-based committee elections under incomplete or inaccurate information
Feline Lindeboom 1, Martijn Brehm 2, Davide Grossi 1,2, Pradeep Murukannaiah 3 1 University of Groningen, 2 University of Amsterdam, 3 TU Delft

Abstract
We study diversity in approval-based committee elections with incomplete or inaccurate information. As standard in the literature on approval-based multi-winner voting, we define diversity according to the maximum coverage problem, which is known to be NP-complete, with a best attainable polynomial time approximation ratio of 1 − 1 / e 1-1/\e . In the incomplete information model, voters can vote on only a small portion of the candidates. We suggest a greedy algorithm and a local search algorithm that query voters and use the query responses to approximate the total population’s opinion. For both algorithms, we prove an upper bound on the number of queries required to get a close to ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/\e) -approximate solution with high probability. We also provide a lower bound for the query complexity of non-adaptive algorithms, that cannot adapt their querying strategy to readily obtained information. In the inaccurate information setting, voters’ responses are corrupted with a probability p ∈ ( 0 , 1 2 ) p\in(0,\frac{1}{2}) . We provide both an upper and a lower bound for the number of queries required to attain a ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/\e) -approximate solution with high probability. Finally, using real data from Polis, we see that our algorithms perform remarkably better than the theoretical results suggest, both with incomplete and inaccurate information.

Supporting such processes online in large groups necessarily involves the implementation of algorithmic solutions for information-processing problems. One such problem concerns how to feed back to users information that can effectively summarize the current state of the deliberation: how does ‘the group’ think about the issues being deliberated upon?

One approach, which is followed for instance in Polis, to aggregate the current state of the deliberation into a comprehensive summarization of it, is by selecting sets of statements proposed by users, based on how much support the statements have elicited from other users. As

For the same reason, voters can become inaccurate in representing their true beliefs, when having to answer many questions. So, such a deliberation summarization problem can be thought of as an approval-based multi-winner voting problem in which ballots are incomplete or inaccurate. From this perspective,

Related work and our contribution

Diversity in multi-winner voting and the Maximum Coverage problem

Diversity in multi-winner voting is commonly defined following the characterization in the seminal work of Chamberlin and Courant in 1983 on representative deliberations and representative decisions
CC 83
. Their most general definition uses voters’ ordinal preferences and, for any given committee, a voter’s representative in the committee is the member who ranks highest in the voter’s preference order. The committee score is obtained by summing over the individual voters’ scores, attributed to them according to the position of their representative in their preference order. In the case of approval ballots, maximizing the Chamberlin-Courant score simplifies to selecting a set of candidates that guarantees for as many voters as possible at least one candidate they approve of. In 2008,
PRZ 08
have proven, using a reduction from Exact 3-Cover, the Chamberlin-Courant decision problem is NP complete, even for the version with approval ballots. Denoting candidates by the set of their approving voters, the approval-based problem is also equivalent to selecting a set of candidates that maximizes the coverage of the set of voters. This one-to-one correspondence between Chamberlin-Courant score-based diversity in approval-based multi-winner voting, and the Maximum Coverage problem, was first studied in
SF 15
.

Preliminaries

An instance of an approval-based committee election problem consists of a set
V
of
n
voters, a set
C
of
m
candidates, and a natural number
k
. The goal is to elect a committee
W\subseteq C
of size
k\leq m
, based on the opinions of the voters in
V

k\ll m
. An instance also contains approval information: every voter
i\in V
approves of a subset of the candidates. Usually, this approval information is known upfront and expressed in the form of an
approval set

A_{i}
for each voter
i\in V
. The sequence of sets
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is called the
approval profile
, and we call this setting the
perfect information setting
. This paper studies incomplete and inaccurate information settings; the respective models are detailed in the corresponding section.

To measure diversity of an elected committee, we use the approval-based Chamberlin-Courant score, which is equivalent to the unit weight Max Cover score:

Definition 3.1

(
CC 83
)
.

On any approval-based committee election instance
(V,C,A,k)
, the
approval-based Chamberlin-Courant
(CC) score of a committee
W\subseteq C,\,|W|=k
is

\displaystyle\text{CC}(W)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{A_{i}\cap W\neq\emptyset\}}(i).

(1)

In the approval-based Chamberlin-Courant decision problem, the input is an instance
(V,C,A,k)
and an integer
x
, and the question is whether it is possible to achieve score
\frac{x}{n}
using
k
candidates. In the approval-based Chamberlin-Courant optimization problem, the input is an instance
(V,C,A,k)
and the task is to maximize the score using
k
candidates.

As mentioned in Section
2
, the approval-based Chamberlin-Courant decision problem is NP-complete, so it is not possible to, on just any instance, find an optimal solution to the optimization problem in polynomial time either (unless P=NP). To find a solution in polynomial time, we thus have to work with approximation algorithms. The performance of approximation algorithms is defined in terms of the
approximation ratio
. An algorithm
\alg
for a maximization problem
\Pi
with objective function
f
has approximation ratio
\alpha
if for any instance
I

f(\alg(I))\geq f(\opt(I))\alpha
, where
\opt(I)
denotes an optimal solution for

. The best attainable polynomial-time approximation ratio for Max Cover is
1-1/\e

Fei 98
.

Since we study only approval-based committee elections, and the approval-based Chamberlin-Courant problem is equivalent to the unit-weight Maximum Coverage problem, we use both terms interchangeably. Similarly, we refer interchangeably to voters/elements, to candidates/sets, and committees/covers. Whenever we speak about the Chamberlin-Courant problem, the approval-based version is meant, and whenever the Maximum Coverage problem is mentioned, the unit-weight version is meant.

Incomplete information

In the incomplete information setting, voters are only able to express their votes (approve/disapprove) on a small portion of the candidates, so we don’t have access to all approval information beforehand. To model this, we assume there exists an underlying true distribution
d

A:V\to\mathcal{P}(C)

nm

Firstly, the original problem, where we may elect any

4.2
Upper bound for a setting with matroid constraints

Matroids in multi-winner voting

We can imagine situations where we want to place restrictions on the set of possible committees to be elected. For instance, perhaps we want to include ‘external’ diversity requirements, such as ‘the committee must contain at least
x
candidates of category
Y
’. In practice,
x
would be a natural number and
Y
would be a category like gender, race, etc. In online deliberation settings, these attributes would extend to users. Or perhaps we want to place a restriction on how many comments can be selected that are submitted by one and the same person. For such purposes, we make a selection of which committees are “allowed” to be elected, and collect them in a set
\mathcal{M}
. This is fundamentally different from what we have so far meant with diversity, which was just defined in terms of the approval profile. It is not obvious that any algorithm adapts well to this restriction. Fortunately,
FW 13
prove that, when
\mathcal{M}
is a matroid, we can mend this problem.

Definition 4.6

(matroid)
.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}
over a set
C
is a selection of subsets (called independent sets) that satisfies

1.

\emptyset\in\mathcal{M}
,

2.

if
A\in\mathcal{M}
and
B\subset A
then
B\in\mathcal{M}
, and

3.

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{M}
with
|A|>|B|
, there exists
x\in A\setminus B
such that
B\cup\{x\}\in\mathcal{M}
.

This last property guarantees that all maximal independent sets have the same cardinality. We call such sets
bases
, and their common cardinality is called the
rank
of the matroid.

Example 4.7

(uniform matroid)
.

Consider
\mathcal{M}=\{W\subset C:|W|\leq k\}
. This is the uniform matroid of rank
k
: 1)
|\emptyset|=0\leq k
, 2) for any set
W\in\mathcal{M}
with
|W|=j\leq k
, for any subset
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
,
|W^{\prime}|\leq|W|\leq k
, and 3) for any two feasible sets

with
|W^{\prime}|<|W|
, we will have, for any

.

Thus, taking for
\mathcal{M}
the uniform matroid of rank

The standard non-oblivious local search algorithm

As done above, we start with the complete information setting. Here, a classical (or, oblivious) local search algorithm (for Max Cover) typically starts with an arbitrary (tentative) solution and, in each iteration, swaps one or multiple of the tentatively elected sets for tentatively unelected sets in order to improve the objective function value. It thus
searches
in the
local
neighborhood of the solution. It stops when no local improvement is possible anymore, which could in principle take exponentially many steps. As described in
FW 13
, this running time problem can be circumvented. We can use approximate local search, which allows us to bound the running time at a small cost in the approximation ratio, see also
OPS 04
. In practice, this means we stop the algorithm sooner, namely when we encounter, in some iteration, an improvement smaller than some parameter
\beta>0
. Then, using a partial enumeration technique, we can eliminate this small cost again, see
CCPV 11
. This is also the approach that
FW 13
take.

FW 13
prove that, over a matroid constraint, the greedy algorithm would attain only approximation ratio
\frac{1}{2}
. Also, the approximation ratio of oblivious local search (Algorithm 1 in their paper)is
\frac{k-1}{2k-\ell-1}
when
\ell
sets are exchanged in each iteration. For
k=2
and
\ell=1
, this also equals
\frac{1}{2}
, which is quite a lot smaller than
1-1/\e
. Thus, they switch to a non-oblivious local search algorithm that uses an auxiliary objective function
f
for the iterative procedure. This auxiliary objective function adds temporary weight to elements covered multiple times. This makes a path of improvements more “robust”, and thereby prevents getting stuck in bad local optima. We denote by

the temporary weight associated to an element covered
j

for the amount of times that

\displaystyle f(W)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{h_{i}(W)}.

(4)

Note that, with

for all

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \alpha_{1}=1-\frac{1}{\e},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \alpha_{j+1}=(j+1)\alpha_{j}-j\alpha_{j-1}-\frac{1}{\e}.

The sequence

Inaccurate information

In the inaccurate information setting, voters do hand in complete ballots, but the voters are inaccurate in reporting their true approvals: each time we query a voter
i
about a candidate
j
(so, posing query
(i,j)
), the response is corrupted with probability
p
. We model this by adding, to the
original
complete information approval profile
A
, a random variable
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
, with
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Posing query
(i,j)
, we also draw from
\text{Bernoulli}(p)
, and the result determines whether we see a truthful response (draw 0) or not (draw 1). Draws from
X
are independent between voters, between candidates, and between consecutive draws for the same query. If
p=0
, we would have accurate information, and with
nm
queries we would have all, accurate, information. We would then be sure to find a
(1-1/\e)
-approximate solution (e.g. using the greedy algorithm). Now
p>0
, so without adapting the algorithms, their performance will drop. We here present theoretical results on the amount of queries required to still obtain a
(1-1/\e)
-approximate solution with high probability.

When answers may be corrupted, we can simply pose every question multiple times to compensate for uncertainty. That is the strategy we use to obtain the upper bound. We can adapt Algorithm
1
(attaining Algorithm
5
, see Appendix) or Algorithm
3
; it doesn’t matter, since the new total amount of queries only depends, besides on
p
and
\delta
, on the original amount of queries, which would be
nm
for both algorithms. This also means we immediately have an upper bound for the problem over a matroid constraint.

Theorem 5.1
.

In the query model with inaccurate query responses and parameter
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, for any
\delta>0
,
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we attain a
(1-1/\e)
-approximate solution to Max Cover with probability at least
1-\delta
using
nm\cdot U
queries, where
U=\lceil-2\frac{\log((nm)/\delta)}{\log(4p(1-p))}\rceil
. This gives a query complexity in
\mathcal{O}(nm\log(nm/\delta))
.

We prove the lower bound using an information theoretic lower bound from multi-armed bandit theory. The proof disregards the possibility of matroid constraints, but since the original problem is a special case of the problem over a matroid constraint, the lower bound holds for both settings.

Theorem 5.2
.

In the query model with inaccurate query responses, for any
\delta\in(0,1),\,n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, any algorithm that outputs an (
1-1/\e
)-approximate solution to Max Cover with probability at least
1-\delta
, makes
\Omega(nm\log(1/\delta))
queries in expectation.

7
Conclusion

We studied approval-based committee elections with the goal of maximizing the Chamberlin-Courant score under incomplete or inaccurate information. The study of this type of multi-winner elections, and the focus on diversity, are motivated by applications in digital democracy for the support of online large-scale deliberation.

Analytically, the paper determines bounds on the query complexity for the above settings, in particular: for the incomplete information setting, we establish
upper bounds for adaptations to our setting of known algorithms for the Max Cover problem (Theorems Theorem
4.10
and
4.4
)
and a lower bound (Theorem
B.1
) for any non-adaptive algorithm. For the inaccurate information setting, we establish a lower bound for optimal approximations with high probability (Theorem
5.2
), as well as an upper bound using a greedy algorithm (Theorem
5.1
). Empirically, we complement the worst-case results by testing the behavior of the algorithms on augmented real-world data from Polis. Even when voters respond, possibly inaccurately, to just a handful of statements, our algorithms perform well compared to the algorithms with complete and accurate access to each voters’ ballots.

A first direction for future work is to query voters adaptively, to reduce the amount of queries required. Second, we hope to combine both incomplete and inaccurate information theoretically. Third, we wish to develop a more comprehensive theory of diversity, beyond the reliance on the Chamberlin-Courant score. Fourth, we aim to strengthen our experimental section by including synthetic data. Fifth, we intend to explore the significance of the above results also to applications in active learning
Set 09
, with the aim of optimizing the querying of a group annotators in distributed data labeling tasks (see, e.g.,
LSJM 24
).

, we first prove that, for

\displaystyle=\gr(k-1)+\gr_{k}

Appendix B
Lower bound for the query complexity of non-adaptive algorithms in the incomplete information setting

For the incomplete information setting, we have seen two algorithms. Both algorithms make use of information acquired during the run of the algorithm. Indeed, when selecting the query sets, we need these to contain the currently elected committee. We next present a lower bound for the query complexity of non-adaptive algorithms, which can not adapt their querying strategy to readily obtained information. The proof concerns the original setting, without matroid constraints, but the result extends to the problem over a matroid constraint, as it is a more general problem. The proof strategy follows
[
HKP+ 23
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Appendix D
Discussion on experiments (Section 6)

We have pre-processed the Polis data before use. First, of all 20 accessible Polis data sets, we remove the
london.youth.policing
dataset due to a low number of participants, and the
bg2025-volunteers
dataset because it contains far more statements than voters. Second, as anticipated, participating voters typically vote on only a small portion of the statements, so we obtain a sparse comment-participant-matrix. Since these discussions have been completed already, we will not be able to query voters adaptively, so, for the sake of running our querying algorithms, we need to artificially complete the data. We set all unanswered voter-candidate pairs to disapprove. Third, we removed statements with more than half of voters approving, as they made attaining a high Max Cover score trivial in some datasets.
