Sleep duration and quality among Indian anaesthesiologists: A nationwide cross-sectional survey
Authors: Kapil Singhal, Kritika Agrawal, Deepak Thapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Poor sleep can adversely affect well-being, cognitive function, and decision-making. Anaesthesiologists face unique occupational challenges (irregular shifts, on-call duties, and high stress), which exacerbate sleep disturbances. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of poor sleep among Indian anaesthesiologists and identify associated risk factors.Methods:A cross-sectional online survey was conducted among Indian anaesthesiologists. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), while data on sleep duration, demographics, work patterns, personal habits, stress, and comorbidities were collected. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with short sleep (<7 hours) and poor sleep quality (PSQI >5).Results:Out of 728 respondents, more than 60% reported short sleep (<7 hours) and more than 50% reported poor sleep quality (PSQI >5). Independent predictors of poor sleep included female gender, more than three night shifts or on-call duties per month, sedentary lifestyle, high home stress, and presence of self-reported sleep disorders. Respondents emphasised the impact of irregular hours, work–life imbalance, and the need for systematic interventions.Conclusion:Poor sleep quality is highly prevalent among Indian anaesthesiologists and is driven by both occupational and personal factors. Structured work schedules and shift patterns and emphasis on lifestyle and stress may improve sleep health and reduce patient risks.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep is a fundamental physiological requirement, with at least 7 hours of regular sleep recommended for adults to maintain physical and cognitive well-being.[1] Insufficient or poor-quality sleep can cause acute effects (irritability, impaired attention, delayed reaction time) and chronic effects (impaired immunity, cardiovascular diseases, etc.).[1] Recent surveys indicate worsening sleep health, described as a “global sleep crisis”.[2]

Anaesthesiologists are particularly vulnerable due to long and irregular working hours, frequent night shifts, and emergency workload, which contribute to stress, burnout, and insufficient sleep.[34] Sleep deprivation in this group may impair cognitive performance and reaction time, potentially affecting patient safety.

Although anaesthesiologists’ sleep quality and associated risk factors have been studied internationally,[35] there is a lack of data specific to Indian anaesthesia professionals, who number nearly 50,000.

To our knowledge, contemporary nationwide data on sleep quality and its occupational correlates among Indian anaesthesiologists remain limited. Existing literature primarily comes from Western contexts, with differing workloads, shift structures, and support systems.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of poor sleep quality among Indian anaesthesiologists using validated tools such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).[6] The secondary objectives were to identify occupational and personal risk factors associated with poor sleep quality.

METHODS

This cross-sectional, questionnaire-based observational study included anaesthesiologists working in India with ≥1 year of clinical experience. The ≥1-year threshold was chosen to ensure adequate exposure to routine anaesthesia duties, night shifts, and on-call responsibilities. Anaesthesiologists not currently in clinical practice or suffering from depression/anxiety/panic disorder were excluded from the study.

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (96/MERB/2025; 31/7/2025). The study was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2025/08/092416; 5/8/2025). Data were collected in August 2025 via an anonymous online questionnaire distributed through emails and professional anaesthesia groups, with one response permitted per email address. Approximately 3600 anaesthesiologists from all over the country were invited to participate in the survey, out of which 790 responded (response rate approximately 21%). Three reminders were issued to improve participation. Despite the modest response rate, the final sample substantially exceeded the pre-specified minimum sample size required for prevalence estimation and regression modelling. Nationwide responses were obtained to ensure diverse representation. All items were mandatory except the optional comments, ensuring complete datasets.

Participation was voluntary with implied informed consent at survey entry. No personal identifiers were collected. Exit was permitted at any stage of the survey. Data were stored on a secure restricted-access cloud drive.

The questionnaire was developed in three stages:

Item generation – Based on a validated tool, that is, PSQI, along with a focussed literature review and expert inputs.

The final questionnaire comprised 27 questions (with two additional questions for respondents reporting a diagnosed or suspected sleep disorder):

Part I: Demographics (age, gender) and professional details (experience, workplace, working hours, night shifts, Sunday duties, and on-call frequency).

Night shift, Sunday duties, and on-calls frequency were recorded as self-reported categories (0, 1–3, and >3 night shifts per month), reflecting increasing levels of occupational circadian disruption.

Part II: Lifestyle characteristics (physical activity, caffeine intake, smoking and alcohol) and stress at work and home.

Physical activity was categorised as sedentary (little or no exercise), moderately active (physical activity 2–3 times/week), and very active (physical activity >3 times/week).

Part III: Sleep quality assessed using the PSQI.[6] The PSQI has high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient 0.83) and test–retest reliability (r = 0.85).[67] The scores range from 0 to 21 with PSQI >5 indicating “poor sleep quality” (sensitivity 89.6%, specificity 86.5%) (P < 0.001).[6]

The PSQI was used due to its established reliability, reproducibility, and widespread use in assessing sleep quality, enabling meaningful comparison with prior literature. It is a validated instrument with established psychometric properties. The remaining study-specific items were developed based on literature review and expert consensus to capture relevant occupational and personal factors. These items were descriptive and exploratory and were pilot-tested for clarity and face validity but were not intended to represent a standalone validated scale. The questionnaire included additional components; however, only variables relevant to sleep duration and sleep quality were analysed in the present study.

The sample size was based on the primary objective: estimating the prevalence of poor sleep (PSQI >5) among Indian anaesthesiologists. Using a prevalence of 57.3% reported among French anaesthesiologists[3]; the following parameters were applied: Expected prevalence (p): 57.3%, confidence level: 95%, Z-value: 1.96, margin of error (d): 5%, and population size: assumed large (no finite correction applied). Using the formula for estimating a single population proportion (n = Z² × p × [1 − p]/d²), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 376. The actual sample exceeded this number significantly.

Statistical analysis was performed using Jamovi (version 2.6.44). All tests were two-sided (α = 0.05). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed through Q–Q plots and box plots.

Group comparisons for continuous variables used the independent-samples t-test when normality and homogeneity of variances (tested by Levene’s test) were satisfied. If equality of variances was violated, Welch’s t-test was applied. For non-normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. If variance homogeneity was violated, Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell post-hoc was used. For skewed data across more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, with Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner pairwise tests applied when significant differences were found.

Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s coefficients as appropriate. Categorical associations were examined using the Chi-square (χ²) test, with Yates’ correction when required.

Variables with bivariate associations at P < 0.20 were considered for multivariable modelling to avoid excluding potentially relevant predictors. Multicollinearity was excluded [variance inflation factors (VIF) <2, tolerances ~1]. Model adequacy was assessed using the likelihood-ratio χ² test, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Nagelkerke’s R².

Calibration and discrimination were evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), respectively. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. All P values are precise to three decimals or P < 0.001.

Models were based on 728 participants with adequate outcome events for short sleep duration (number of participants, n = 476) and poor sleep quality (PSQI >5; n = 371). The events-per-variable ratio exceeded recommended thresholds, ensuring statistical stability. Model adequacy was supported by significant likelihood-ratio χ² tests (P < 0.001), stable standard errors, and consistent CIs. Sensitivity analyses involving sequential removal of low-frequency and correlated predictors demonstrated negligible variation in effect estimates, indicating absence of overfitting. Effect sizes were reported to aid interpretation.

Multicollinearity was excluded using VIF and tolerance values. Although some occupational variables were conceptually related, hierarchical and domain-specific evaluations showed independent contributions. Excluding these variables reduced model performance (e.g., a decrease in Nagelkerke R² from 0.36 to 0.32 in the PSQI model), supporting their retention for maintaining construct validity.

A post-hoc sample adequacy assessment for the PSQI model based on recommended criteria for prediction modelling (Nagelkerke R² = 0.36, outcome prevalence = 50.9%, shrinkage = 0.9) indicated a minimum requirement of 649 participants with at least 331 events. The present study exceeded these thresholds (n = 728; 371 events), confirming adequate power and model reliability.

Night shifts emerged as powerful predictors, with moderate exposure (1–3 shifts/month) increasing odds by 74% (OR = 1.74, P = 0.026) and frequent exposure (>3/month) doubling the risk (OR = 2.00, P = 0.013). Sedentary lifestyle almost doubled the risk of poor sleep (OR = 1.80, 95% CI, P = 0.023).

Home stress independently predicted poor sleep (OR = 1.18, P < 0.001), while work stress showed borderline effect (P = 0.068). A self-reported sleep disorder was the single strongest determinant (OR = 5.00, P < 0.001).

The average sleep duration was 6.14 ± 0.99 hours, with 476 (65.3%) respondents sleeping for <7 hours and 158 (21.7%) sleeping for <6 hours. Significant bivariate associations with sleep duration are presented in Table 5. In multivariate analysis [Table 6], those with 6–10 years (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.22–0.67, P < 0.001) and 11–20 years (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31–0.95, P = 0.031) had lower odds of short sleep compared with participants with ≤5 years of experience.

DISCUSSION

There is growing interest worldwide in the well-being of anaesthesiologists, yet their sleep quality still remains under-explored.[89]

This nationwide survey highlights a substantial burden of short sleep duration and poor sleep quality among Indian anaesthesiologists, driven by both occupational workload and modifiable personal factors. The burden of insufficient sleep in this survey exceeded estimates from the general Indian population (~30%) and was comparable to anaesthesiologists in France and Portugal.[3101112]

In the present survey, self-reported insomnia was far lower than in the general population (18–33%), with only a small proportion seeking specialist advice, suggesting potential self-management among clinicians.[1314]

Also, as per the findings of the survey, short sleep duration was independently associated with professional experience, frequent on-call duties, home stress, and pre-existing sleep disorders. Predictors of poor sleep quality (PSQI >5) included female gender, professional experience, workplace characteristics, night shifts, physical inactivity, home stress, and sleep disorders. Female anaesthesiologists appeared particularly vulnerable, consistent with established gender differences in sleep architecture and social roles. Recent Indian data also demonstrate a disproportionate burden of domestic responsibilities and limited support systems among female anaesthesiologists, adversely affecting professional roles and career progression, which may partly explain the higher vulnerability observed in our cohort.[2515] On-call duties or night shifts (>3/month) were important contributors, likely reflecting circadian rhythm disruption.[316] Nevertheless, prior studies have demonstrated impaired neuro-behavioural performance after night duties.[317] These findings support institutional fatigue-mitigation strategies such as regulated duty rosters and protected rest periods.

The current survey revealed a protective association of sleep quality with regular physical activity, consistent with prior evidence.[18] Nonetheless, simple workplace interventions (e.g., brief exercises during breaks) may be beneficial. Home stress also contributed to poor sleep in our study, highlighting the additive impact of personal stressors. Chronic stress can disrupt sleep architecture and reduce overall sleep efficiency,[2512] suggesting that accessible mental-health support may benefit high-risk clinicians.

Pre-existing sleep disorders emerged as the strongest determinant in our study. This finding underscores the importance of targeted screening, early identification, and timely intervention as untreated sleep disorders may further exacerbate occupational fatigue and impair overall well-being in anaesthesiologists.

Several factors, including professional experience, institutional affiliation, and primary work area, were identified as significant predictors in the current study. These findings are presented in the context of existing literature.[3121920] Early career anaesthesiologists demonstrated higher vulnerability, advocating for tailored support during formative years. Anaesthesiologists in non-teaching government hospitals had significantly lower odds of poor sleep compared to freelancers, reflecting the benefit of structured work schedules.

Anaesthesiologists working in both operating theatres and ICUs had poorer sleep as per our survey findings, reflecting the strain of dual high-intensity responsibilities. Implementing structured shift rotations could help alleviate this burden.

Overall, the findings of this survey likely reflect cumulative circadian disruption from night duties and on-call work, compounded by modifiable lifestyle and stress-related factors. Targeted institutional and individual strategies may help mitigate these disturbances. Poor sleep among anaesthesiologists has implications beyond individual health, contributing to medical errors and adverse patient outcomes.[11621] Notably, India lacks national health policies addressing minimum sleep duration even for the general population.[22]

The limitations of our study include subjective assessment of stress as objective physiological measures were not feasible in this nationwide survey-based study. Secondly, the study relied on self-reported measures, and some potentially relevant variables such as bedtime screen exposure were not included to ensure conciseness and minimise respondent burden. Digital device use is increasingly recognised as a contributor to sleep disturbance and warrants further investigation in future studies. Thirdly, recall bias and night-to-night variability cannot be excluded as sleep duration could not be objectively measured. The voluntary web-based survey design may introduce selection and non-response bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the findings. Nonetheless, the large sample size and national representation strengthen the generalisability of the findings.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of poor sleep quality (PSQI >5) and short sleep duration (<7 hours) among Indian anaesthesiologists, with significant associations with frequent night duties, on-call responsibilities, sedentary behaviour, and perceived stress. These findings highlight the need for greater awareness of sleep health and consideration of occupational factors contributing to sleep disturbances in this population. Further research is needed to explore potential interventions and long-term implications.
