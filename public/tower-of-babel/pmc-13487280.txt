Continuous subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa in advanced Parkinson's disease: A clinical workflow from 50 consecutive patients at a single U.S. center
Authors: Saar Anis, Shannon Shaffer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) infusion is a new device-aided therapy for advanced Parkinson's disease (PD), but real-world implementation experience, particularly in patients with prior deep brain stimulation (DBS), remains limited.ObjectivesTo describe a clinical workflow for LDp/CDp implementation and report outcomes from the first 50 consecutive patients.MethodsQuality improvement report based on retrospective chart review at a tertiary movement disorders center between January 2025 and April 2026. Over a median follow-up of 7.2 months, 41 patients (82%) remained on therapy; skin reactions occurred in 46%, six patients (12%) discontinued, and three patients (6%) died. Introduction

Continuous subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) infusion addresses limitations of pulsatile oral levodopa delivery in advanced Parkinson's disease (PD). LDp/CDp delivers a continuously infused levodopa prodrug that provides stable dopaminergic stimulation and is indicated for the treatment of motor fluctuations in advanced PD. The pivotal phase 3 trial demonstrated a 1.75-h greater increase in ON time without troublesome dyskinesia compared with oral levodopa-carbidopa. [1] Following regulatory approval, centers have begun to report post-marketing outcomes, [2], [3], [4] yet implementation experience remains limited.

As LDp/CDp enters clinical practice, many candidates have already undergone advanced device-aided therapies, including deep brain stimulation (DBS) and levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG). [5] While clinical trial experience and implementation frameworks have been reported, [6] real-world implementation data in DBS-enriched populations remain sparse. [7], [8]

We describe the development of a clinical workflow for LDp/CDp implementation and report outcomes from the first 50 consecutive patients treated at our tertiary care center. This report focuses on practical aspects of patient selection, treatment optimization, adverse-event management, and integration of LDp/CDp within contemporary advanced PD care pathways.

Methods

This quality improvement report describes implementation of subcutaneous LDp/CDp infusion in the first 50 consecutive patients treated at a tertiary movement disorders center from January 2025 to April 2026. Data were abstracted through May 31, 2026. All patients initiated on LDp/CDp during this period were included. Patient selection for therapy was based on clinical assessment by treating neurologists. This project was conducted as an institutional quality improvement initiative reviewed and approved by the Center for Neurological Restoration Quality Committee. Consistent with institutional policy, formal Institutional Review Board review was not required because the activity did not constitute human-subject research.

Variables collected included age, sex, disease duration, baseline levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), DBS status (presence, target, laterality), prior LCIG exposure, time from referral to pump initiation, number of clinical visits required, and time to achieve stable dosing. Dosing parameters included base infusion rate, high rate, low rate, extra dose volume, loading dose, and infusion duration (16-h vs 24-h schedules). Additional variables included baseline psychiatric history, adverse events (including neuropsychiatric symptoms), and reasons for discontinuation. Among patients with DBS, total stimulation current (sum of left and right amplitudes) was recorded before LDp/CDp initiation and after dose optimization.

Clinical response was assessed retrospectively using the Clinician Global Impression of Change (CGI—C) scale for motor fluctuations and dyskinesia. The CGI-C is a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (very much improved) to 7 (very much worse), with 4 indicating no change. The CGI-C was used as a pragmatic global clinician impression rather than a measure of treatment efficacy. Two authors independently assigned CGI-C ratings based on medical chart review; discrepancies were resolved by discussion to reach consensus.

Manufacturer-recommended initial base infusion rates were calculated using the prescribing information algorithm: hourly base infusion rate (mL/h) = [(TLD × 1.3) / 240] / 16 assuming 16 waking hours. TLD is the total levodopa dose from the levodopa-containing medications being replaced, after conversion to immediate-release levodopa equivalents and adjustment for COMT-inhibitor use. Clinical management lessons identified during implementation were synthesized into a workflow addressing patient selection, pump initiation, dose optimization, adverse-event management, and patient education.

Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as count (%). Reporting follows the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) guidelines (Table S1). [9]

Results

Patient characteristics

Fifty consecutive patients initiated LDp/CDp infusion (Table 1). Median age was 68 years (IQR 61–72), disease duration 14 years (IQR 11–17), and baseline LEDD 1108 mg (IQR 862–1395). Thirty-seven patients (74%) were male. Twenty-five (50%) had prior DBS, including 23 with bilateral systems and 21 with leads targeting the subthalamic nucleus. Six patients (12%) had transitioned from LCIG.

Implementation and dosing

Median time from referral to authorization submission was 8 days (IQR 1–16), authorization submission to approval was 30 days (IQR 13–44), and approval to first pump initiation day was 27 days (IQR 17–42). Total time from referral to pump initiation was 78 days (IQR 56–98), reflecting local administrative and payer processes. A standardized clinical documentation template was developed to reduce documentation variability and support efficient payer review (Supplementary Materials). Patients required a median of 4 clinical visits (IQR 3–5), including in-person and virtual encounters, to reach stable dosing or discontinuation, over a median of 2.7 months (IQR 1.3–4.2). The implementation workflow addressing program structure, candidate selection, initiation procedures, dose optimization strategies, and management considerations is illustrated in Fig. 1. Detailed protocols for infusion site management, dose timing, cannula rotation schedules, and procedures for patients transitioning from prior intestinal gel therapy are provided in Supplementary Materials.

LDp/CDp Implementation Workflow.

Optimized median base infusion rate was 0.39 mL/h (IQR 0.25–0.53), high rate 0.42 mL/h (IQR 0.27–0.59), low rate 0.33 mL/h (IQR 0.22–0.47), extra dose 0.17 mL (IQR 0.15–0.25), and loading dose 0.80 mL (IQR 0.50–1.10). Forty-five patients (90%) used 24-h infusion schedules and five (10%) used 16-h schedules.

The median manufacturer-predicted base infusion rate was 0.38 mL/h (IQR 0.29–0.47), compared with a final optimized rate of 0.39 mL/h (IQR 0.25–0.53). Thirty-nine percent of patients required final rates more than 10% above the predicted rate, 33% required rates more than 10% below it, and 28% remained within ±10% of the predicted rate.

Clinical outcomes and adverse events

Median follow-up from initiation was 7.2 months (IQR 5.3–9.0; range 1.2–16.7). Forty-one patients (82%) continued therapy through follow-up. Six patients (12%) discontinued, all within the first five months: visual hallucinations (n = 2, both with pre-existing psychosis), skin infection requiring hospitalization with antibiotics and incision and drainage (n = 2), and worsening motor fluctuations (n = 2). The two patients who discontinued for worsening motor fluctuations did so approximately one month and six weeks after initiation. In the first, the base rate was increased (from 0.17 to 0.34 mL/h) without benefit; the patient remained predominantly OFF and returned to oral therapy. In the second, dyskinesia improved but gait and postural stability worsened, with longer OFF times, and the pump was poorly tolerated; despite rate adjustment, the patient returned to oral therapy. Three patients died between 1 and 6 months after initiation; all had advanced disease with progression-related complications, including aspiration and cachexia; none were attributed to treatment.

CGI-C ratings were available for 49 of 50 patients, as one patient died before a rating could be assigned. Ratings fell in the improved range (scores 1 to 3) in 38/49 (78%) for motor fluctuations and 31/49 (63%) for dyskinesia. Median CGI-C scores were 3 (IQR 2–3) for motor fluctuations and 3 (IQR 2–4) for dyskinesia (Table S2, Fig. S1).

Skin reactions occurred in 23 patients (46%). Minor reactions, including erythema and induration, occurred in 15 (30%) and were managed conservatively. Major reactions occurred in 8 (16%), including 2 infections requiring hospitalization and treatment discontinuation. Visual hallucinations occurred in 6 patients (12%), including 2 with pre-existing psychosis. Additional adverse events included adhesive sensitivity, transient nausea, and orthostatic symptoms (Table S3, Fig. S1).

DBS subgroup

Among 25 patients with DBS, median total stimulation current decreased from 4.4 mA (IQR 3.4–5.9) at baseline to 4.0 mA (IQR 3.2–5.3) after treatment optimization. Current reduction occurred in 7 patients (28%), with a median reduction of 1.1 mA (IQR 0.4–2.4), while 16 patients (64%) maintained stable settings and 2 patients (8%) required increases (Table S4, Fig. S1).

LCIG transitions

Six patients (12%) had transitioned from LCIG. Two transitioned due to tube- or skin-related complications and remained on LDp/CDp. Four transitioned seeking less invasive delivery; two developed hallucinations that resolved with dose adjustment while retaining the jejunal tube as contingency, while the other two reported clinical improvement and remained on therapy without adverse events.

Discussion

This report describes implementation of LDp/CDp in 50 consecutive patients with advanced PD, half of whom already had DBS. Skin reactions were the most common adverse event and a principal practical limitation. The manufacturer dosing algorithm provided a reasonable starting estimate, and LDp/CDp was implemented alongside existing DBS without conflict.

Skin reactions occurred in nearly half of patients, consistent with previous clinical trial and post-marketing experience [1], [2], and were the most frequent adverse event and a principal practical limitation of this therapy. Most were mild and did not require discontinuation, but major reactions occurred in 16% of patients, and two required hospitalization with antibiotics and incision and drainage before discontinuing. In our experience, patient counseling regarding erythema, induration, and cannula-site care was emphasized throughout treatment. Daily cannula changes during the first week also helped identify patients with higher skin reactivity early in treatment. Despite frequent skin reactions, 82% of patients remained on therapy through follow-up, similar to previous observations. [4] Skin tolerability warrants close monitoring and should be discussed with patients before initiation.

Cohort-level manufacturer-predicted and final median base infusion rates were similar, 0.38 and 0.39 mL/h, respectively. However, 72% of patients required a final rate more than 10% above or below the manufacturer-predicted rate, underscoring the need for individualized titration. [6]

Half of our patients already had DBS, which reflects how layered advanced therapy management in PD has become. [5], [10] These patients frequently continue to have motor fluctuations and other levodopa-responsive symptoms even when stimulation is well-optimized [11], and LDp/CDp was added when oral therapy could no longer control these fluctuations. LDp/CDp was implemented in patients with existing DBS, and no device-related operational conflict was documented. Stimulation settings were adjusted as clinically indicated during optimization, rather than according to a predefined protocol. We caution against interpreting the observed stimulation changes as evidence that LDp/CDp reduces DBS requirements. The specific clinical rationale for each adjustment was not systematically captured. Published experience with this combination remains limited. [7], [8]

Neuropsychiatric symptoms, mainly hallucinations, were a recurring concern. Hallucinations occurred in patients with and without prior psychiatric disease, including two patients with pre-existing psychosis. Neuropsychiatric complications remain a well-recognized limitation of dopaminergic therapies in advanced PD. [12], [13] Careful psychiatric assessment before initiation and close monitoring during titration therefore remain central components of patient selection and management. [6], [13]

Earlier real-world reports have described LDp/CDp outcomes and tolerability, including in patients with prior DBS. [3], [7] This report focuses on the implementation process itself. Our implementation relied on standardized documentation, dedicated initiation visits, structured patient education, and frequent early follow-up. Similar multidisciplinary approaches have been emphasized for other device-aided therapies in advanced PD. [14], [15] The workflow presented in Fig. 1 summarizes the processes that evolved during treatment of the first 50 patients. It describes one center's practice and may require adaptation to local resources and patient populations.

Limitations include the single-center setting, retrospective chart review, and absence of a comparator group; the use of CGI-C rather than validated outcome measures such as the MDS-UPDRS, the UDysRS, or patient diaries, which precludes quantification of OFF time or dyskinesia burden; CGI-C ratings assigned by consensus without formal inter-rater reliability testing; the absence of a design intended to evaluate treatment effectiveness; a median follow-up of 7.2 months, which limits assessment of long-term persistence and durability of DBS programming changes; and workflow development informed by local practice that may not generalize to other centers.

In conclusion, this report describes the local implementation of LDp/CDp in routine clinical care, including among patients with existing DBS.
