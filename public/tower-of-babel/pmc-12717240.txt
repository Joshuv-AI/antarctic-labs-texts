A computer vision and dynamic strain fusion approach for urban bridge weigh-in-motion
Authors: Yanjie Zhu, Yuchen Wang, Chenglin Gao, Wen Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dynamic weighing systems are crucial for bridge safety, yet current weighing systems are either too costly for widespread urban use or unable to track vehicles reliably in dense traffic. Here we present a video–strain inverse estimation system that combines computer vision with structural strain sensing to measure dynamic vehicle loads on bridges. The method employs a lightweight deep learning detector to recognize vehicle features, a multi-object tracking model to capture trajectories and lane positions, and a developed analytical algorithm to estimate vehicle weight from measured strains. We validated the system with field data from a heavily trafficked urban bridge. The system achieves vehicle recognition with near-baseline accuracy while using only 17% of the original model parameters and running 1.72 times faster, identifies lanes with complete accuracy with a missed detection rate of just 0.56%, and estimates total vehicle weights within 2% error. This low-cost and reliable approach advances intelligent bridge monitoring and supports digital twins of critical urban infrastructure.

Introduction

The construction of new bridges is rapidly advancing, while the aging and deterioration of in-service bridges are becoming increasingly prominent. Consequently, ensuring long-term service has become the core goal of bridge maintenance1. Load testing is one of the most effective technical means for quickly assessing the operational safety of bridges2,3. However, this method usually involves fully or partially closing site traffic and requires designated load vehicles to perform tests at specific positions, severely impacting the normal operation of surrounding road networks4. Complete information on vehicles is crucial to load testing5. If vehicle weight, position, and time can be obtained in real-time without disrupting traffic, and structural responses (strains) can be measured via sensors, it becomes possible to conduct real-time light load tests under regular traffic flow, allowing continuous monitoring of bridge service safety6–9.

The pavement weigh-in-motion system is a type of embedded equipment for testing vehicle loads without interrupting traffic10. As vehicle wheels pass over road-embedded sensors, axle weight information is obtained by measuring dynamic pressures11,12. However, due to high costs, destructive installation, susceptibility to damage, and difficulty in replacement, pavement weigh-in-motion systems have not been widely adopted13. Bridge Weigh-In-Motion (BWIM) systems have been a recent research focus, utilizing beam responses to directly deduce vehicle weights, axle weights, and axle spacings14,15. These systems offer uninterrupted traffic flow, high efficiency, easy installation, easy maintenance, and low costs16–18. A BWIM system comprises three parts: dynamic bridge responses, vehicle spatiotemporal information, and algorithms for the inverse estimation of vehicle weights. Among these, the Moses algorithm is particularly classic, recalculating axle and vehicle weights based on measured versus theoretical dynamic responses as vehicles traverse bridges. Many scholars have built on the Moses algorithm to enhance its accuracy and applicability. Deng et al.19,20 introduced the theory of virtual axles and beams, monitoring vehicle speed and axle count using only bending strain signals. Chatterjee et al.21 employed wavelet transformations to dynamically identify vehicle speeds, axle counts, and spacings. Ojio et al.22 proposed the strain area and support reaction methods using structural response energy ratios to inverse estimate vehicle weights. However, these methods generally apply only to simple bridge types and conditions, and complex traffic scenarios remain a major challenge for BWIM systems23.

In recent years, with the rapid development of computer vision technology, continuous traffic monitoring imagery has effectively identified characteristics and spatiotemporal distributions of vehicles moving on bridge surfaces24. Zaurin et al.25 used background subtraction techniques for dynamic extraction and tracking of vehicle features in video images. Ojio et al.26 introduced a non-contact BWIM system, utilizing sub-millimeter camera arrays to monitor bridge mid-span deflections and identify traffic flow information. Chen et al.27 implemented vehicle trajectory tracking using computer vision, integrating this with weigh-in-motion systems to analyze spatiotemporal distributions of vehicle loads. Zhang et al.28 utilized the Faster Region-based Convolutional Neural Network (Faster-RCNN) network to detect vehicle features in drone video imagery. Although computer vision technology holds immense potential for recognizing vehicle operational spatiotemporal information, the increasing complexity of learning networks and models has led to a growing demand for computational power. Especially in scenarios involving real-time dynamic tracking and inverse estimation of vehicle weights, computer vision often requires processing large volumes of high-resolution video data and complex computational tasks, which traditional hardware platforms can no longer support. Current deep neural networks used for vehicle detection and tracking, such as You Only Look Once (YOLO)29 and Faster-RCNN30, typically require high-performance computing resources to process real-time video and extract features. These models, designed to ensure accuracy and robustness, typically include numerous convolutional layers, fully connected layers, and complex computation graphs, leading to a strong dependency on computational resources. In practical applications, without sufficient computational support, these complex networks often struggle to respond in real-time, thus impacting the immediate feedback on vehicle spatiotemporal information and bridge load states.

These challenges are particularly acute within the urban landscape. The dense network of bridges, often characterized by shorter spans and subject to tight budgets, makes the widespread deployment of high-cost, computationally intensive monitoring systems economically unviable. This reality places a premium on computational efficiency. The need to process high-volume video data for real-time target detection and trajectory tracking, while simultaneously synchronizing it with dynamic strain measurements, poses a key technical dilemma. While existing deep learning models excel on static benchmarks, their computational demands often conflict with the real-time, low-cost hardware constraints of large-scale vehicle monitoring. More critically, the dynamic and often dense nature of urban traffic introduces complex, multi-vehicle loading events. In these scenarios, traditional inverse estimation algorithms become numerically unstable, struggling to accurately disentangle the superimposed structural responses. Therefore, a core difficulty lies not only in the real-time fusion of visual and strain data but in developing algorithms for both vehicle detection and weight inversion that are simultaneously fast and robust enough to handle the complexities of real-world urban traffic.

Overall, despite the potential of computer vision technology in extracting vehicle features and recognizing spatiotemporal information, existing research still faces notable challenges in computational power requirements, real-time performance, data synchronization, and accuracy, which limits the achievement of rapid synchronization and real-time dynamic tracking of vehicle features, spatiotemporal information, and vehicle weight. These issues limit the practical application of existing technologies in bridge load monitoring systems.

In response to the deficiencies of current bridge dynamic weighing system technologies, we propose a video-strain-based dynamic recognition system for vehicle load on bridges, which has been applied in practice on a steel truss bridge. We introduced the YOLOv7-MobileNetV3-SimAM target recognition algorithm and the Simple Online and Realtime Tracking with a Deep Association Metric (DeepSORT) tracking algorithm to extract vehicle spatiotemporal information, while establishing a system for the inverse estimation of vehicle weights based on the Back-Propagation Moses (BPMoses) method to calculate vehicle weights. Our results show that the detection model reduces computational demand by more than 80% while sustaining near-baseline accuracy, enabling efficient real-time deployment on standard hardware. The tracking module achieves precise spatiotemporal recognition, with counting accuracy above 99%, lane identification at 100%, and speed errors typically within 1%. The proposed inversion algorithm consistently constrains vehicle weight errors to within 2% in simple cases and remains stable under complex multi-vehicle conditions where traditional methods fail. Deployed continuously, the system produced high-fidelity load profiles confirming that operational loads remained well below design limits. These findings demonstrate a reliable and low-cost approach to intelligent bridge monitoring and the development of digital twins for critical infrastructure.

Results and discussion

Our study develops and validates an integrated system for real-time bridge traffic load monitoring, sequentially combining vehicle information recognition, dynamic traffic flow extraction, and inverse estimation of vehicle weight. We first present the performance of each core component and then evaluate the overall framework in a real-world application.

Performance analysis of the lightweight model for dynamic vehicle information recognition

Real-time structural monitoring requires a vehicle detection model that balances accuracy with computational efficiency for on-device deployment. To this end, we developed the YOLOv7-MobileNetV3-SimAM model. We benchmarked it against the standard YOLOv7 and YOLOv5 model to assess its suitability for resource-constrained hardware.

Quantitatively, the proposed model demonstrates a radical reduction in computational and storage demands compared to the YOLOv7 baseline and the widely used YOLOv5 (Fig. 1a). We achieved an 82.3% reduction in model parameters (from 37.2 M to 6.6 M) and an 82.4% smaller storage footprint (from 74.8 MB to 13.2 MB). This substantial model compression is primarily attributed to the integration of the lightweight MobileNetV3 backbone, which utilizes depth-wise separable convolutions to minimize computational cost while effectively preserving feature representation. Critically, this efficiency translates to a 41.9% faster inference time than the YOLOv7 baseline on a standard CPU (337.1 ms vs. 580.4 ms), a decisive advantage for our target application on GPU-scarce edge hardware. Our model achieves a high F1 score of 0.957 and a recall of 0.943, representing a marginal decrease of just 1.3% and 1.4% respectively, compared to the YOLOv7 baseline (F1: 0.970, Recall: 0.956). While these aggregate metrics provide a high-level summary, a micro-level analysis of the confusion matrices reveals crucial differences in the models’ behavior (Fig. 1b–d). For the high-frequency sedan car class, our model achieves a true positive rate of 0.97, outperforming both baselines and reducing misclassifications of background as sedans. More tellingly, the models’ responses to the challenging, low-frequency 5 A truck class reveal fundamental differences in their underlying architecture. As shown in Fig. 1c, d, the failure modes of the baseline models are scattered and less coherent: YOLOv7 misattributes instances across multiple classes (e.g., 0.31 to 4 A truck, 0.23 to 6 A truck), while YOLOv5 not only scatters predictions but critically misclassifies 14% of 5 A trucks as background, indicating a failure to even recognize the object’s presence. Our model displays a systematic and semantically coherent error pattern, consistently classifying all 5 A truck instances as the visually analogous 4 A truck. We attribute this structured and predictable failure mode to the architectural integration of the SimAM. As a parameter-free mechanism, SimAM enables the network to discern the importance of individual neurons and prioritize the most discriminative features without increasing model complexity. This appears to help preserve the core semantic features of truck-like vehicles. Consequently, when faced with an unfamiliar 5 A truck, the model defaults to the most semantically plausible category (4 A truck) rather than failing catastrophically. This behavior signifies a form of graceful degradation, which is highly desirable for safety-critical applications.

Overall, our model provides a validated solution that simultaneously overcomes two critical barriers in dynamic bridge assessment: it is compact and fast enough for on-device, real-time deployment, and its architecturally ingrained robustness leads to more interpretable and reliable performance, even in cases of classification uncertainty. This work thus demonstrates a practical pathway toward developing highly efficient yet dependable systems for real-time bridge load monitoring.

Performance analysis for dynamic traffic spatiotemporal information recognition

To validate the system’s performance in recognizing dynamic traffic information, we conducted a multi-stage evaluation encompassing geometric accuracy, vehicle counting, and speed estimation.

The foundation of our method rests upon establishing a precise mapping between the 3D bridge coordinates and the 2D image plane. We computed a projection transformation matrix from four calibration points (P1, P2, P5, P6) and independently verified its accuracy against two test points (P3, P4), as depicted in Fig. 2a. The analysis revealed a maximum spatial error of only 0.222 m (Fig. 2c). Relative to the ~15 m dimensions of the test area, this corresponds to a mapping error of just ~1.5%. Building upon this precise geometric foundation, we evaluated the vehicle counting and lane identification performance under contrasting conditions: a high-density morning peak (06:30-06:40) and a sparse, challenging night period (22:30-22:40). The system demonstrated perfect performance at night, achieving 100% accuracy in both counting (215/215 vehicles) and lane assignment across all eight lanes (Fig. 2b). This resilience to low-light conditions and headlight glare is attributed to the robustness of the underlying deep-learning-based detector. During the morning peak, while lane identification remained perfect, a minor undercount of 4 out of 320 vehicles occurred (98.75% accuracy). Notably, these discrepancies were isolated to lanes 3 and 4, the two most congested lanes which carried 49% of the total traffic, strongly corroborating transient vehicle occlusion as the primary cause. Overall, the system achieved a 99.25% counting accuracy across 535 vehicle events, affirming its reliability in real-world scenarios.

Finally, we evaluated the core function of vehicle speed recognition. Utilizing a dataset of 69,543 vehicle samples collected on March 20, 2023, we compared our vision-based speed measurements against a co-located roadside radar system (measurement error: 0.1 m/s), which served as the ground truth. The algorithm involves establishing detection lines (L₁ and L₂, 40 m apart) and tracking vehicle trajectories between them (Fig. 2d, e). The resulting error analysis reveals exceptional performance. The probability distribution of speed errors is characterized by a sharp, unimodal peak centered at zero, indicating a lack of systemic bias (Fig. 2f). Critically, 82.03% of all speed measurements exhibited an error of ≤1%, and 95.19% of measurements were within a ≤7% error margin (Fig. 2g). The rare instances of larger errors, with a maximum observed error of 15%, were confirmed through visual inspection to be caused by severe vehicle occlusion events. These results collectively demonstrate that the proposed system can deliver highly accurate and reliable vehicle speed data, making it a robust tool for real-time traffic monitoring.

We first established a performance baseline with single-truck crossings at speeds from 20 to 80 km/h. These tests immediately exposed a critical limitation of the conventional Moses algorithm. While it controlled GVW estimation error to within a respectable 3%, the underlying axle weight estimates were fundamentally unreliable, with errors for the first two axles fluctuating around 50% and the third axle around 15% (Fig. 3b, d). Such poor axle-level accuracy renders the method inadequate for detailed load assessment. In contrast, the BPMoses algorithm demonstrated superior accuracy and stability. It constrained GVW error to within 2% and, crucially, restricted individual axle weight errors to within a 15% margin across all speeds (Fig. 3c, e). This highlights its enhanced robustness and its capacity for reliable axle load estimation even in the simplest case.

Next, we challenged the algorithms with multi-vehicle scenarios where superimposed dynamic responses create ill-conditioned systems. In a two-vehicle following test (50 km/h), the Moses algorithm’s vulnerability became evident. In brief temporal stages with sparse data (e.g., Stage 4 with 65 frames), the underlying influence matrix became severely ill-conditioned, causing the direct inversion to catastrophically amplify noise and yield physically impossible axle weights (e.g., −26.97 t for a 2.72 t axle, Table 1). The algorithm only converged to a reasonable GVW estimation in the data-rich Stage 6 (1105 frames), and even then required over 900 frames for the GVW error to stabilize below 2% (Fig. 3g, h). BPMoses, however, was impervious to data sparsity. Its iterative gradient-descent approach, moderated by a learning rate, effectively regularizes the solution path and bypasses the matrix inversion entirely. Method

Our methodology establishes an end-to-end, non-intrusive framework for the real-time estimation of vehicular loads on bridges. The framework integrates three sequential modules: (1) a lightweight vision-based network for vehicle classification and localization; (2) a dynamic tracking algorithm to reconstruct spatiotemporal vehicle trajectories; and (3) an iterative algorithm, termed BPMoses, for accurate axle weight estimation. The entire system is validated through a field experiment on a long-span steel truss bridge.

Lightweight model for vehicle information recognition

To enable practical deployment on edge-computing devices with limited power and computational resources, we developed a lightweight yet accurate object detection model. The widely-used YOLOv7 model31, while powerful, presents a computational burden (37.2 million parameters), compared to the earlier YOLOv5 version32. Our objective was to drastically reduce this overhead while preserving detection accuracy.

To achieve this, we designed a custom model, YOLOv7-MobileNetV3-SimAM. The core modifications are twofold. First, we replaced the original feature-extraction backbone of YOLOv7 with the highly efficient MobileNetV3 network. MobileNetV3 is specifically designed for mobile CPUs and utilizes an inventive Bneck structure with depth-wise separable convolutions, which substantially reduces parameter count and computational cost33. Second, to compensate for any potential accuracy loss from the lightweight backbone, we incorporated the SimAM into the network’s head. Inspired by neuroscience principles of spatial suppression, SimAM is a parameter-free attention mechanism that computes 3D attention weights34. It enhances feature representation by assigning higher importance to neurons exhibiting strong differentiation from their neighbors, without adding computational overhead34. Having identified vehicles in individual frames, we reconstruct their continuous spatiotemporal trajectories using the DeepSORT algorithm35. It is chosen for its robustness in handling occlusions and identity switches, which are common in dense traffic scenes. It integrates motion information, predicted by a Kalman filter, with appearance features extracted by a deep neural network.

Inverse estimation method of dynamic vehicle load

A foundational challenge in BWIM is the accurate inverse estimation of individual axle weights from bridge strain responses. The conventional Moses algorithm, while computationally efficient, formulates this as a linear system of equations. This approach, however, requires a matrix inversion that becomes numerically unstable and leads to ill-conditioned problems when dealing with closely spaced axles, often yielding physically implausible results such as large estimation errors or even negative axle weights36. This limitation is a fundamental barrier to improving the resolution and reliability of BWIM systems.

To circumvent this critical issue, we reconceptualize the axle weight estimation not as a direct algebraic solution but as an optimization problem. Here, we introduce BPMoses, an iterative framework that directly minimizes the discrepancy between measured and predicted bridge responses. This approach leverages the gradient descent algorithm to robustly estimate axle weights without matrix inversion.

Experimental site, sensor, dataset, and calibration

To validate the proposed framework, we conducted a comprehensive field study on a major urban bridge. This section details the experimental bridge, the integrated monitoring system comprising vision and strain sensors, the protocol for vehicle classification, and the calibration of the bridge’s influence lines.

Bridge information

The experiment was conducted on the bridge, a three-span continuous steel box girder composite bridge in a city in China, with a total length of 215 m and a cross-section width of 46 m accommodating eight traffic lanes. A key feature of this bridge is its orthotropic steel deck, whose local stiffness characteristics provide a high-fidelity strain response to vehicular loading. This pronounced sensitivity is crucial for the inverse vehicle weight estimation algorithm. While our deployment was on this structure, the methodology is broadly applicable to any bridge type, provided its deck system has low stiffness, producing a clear, high-magnitude strain signal.

Instrumentation deployment

Our monitoring system integrates two data streams: strain measurements for bridge response and video feeds for vehicle tracking. To capture the structural response across all eight lanes, we installed 24 high-precision resistive surface strain gauges (Donghua DH1205, 200 Hz sampling frequency, 1μϵ accuracy) at the mid-span section. The specific locations on the longitudinal U-ribs of the orthotropic deck (Fig. 6a) were determined through a preliminary finite element analysis (FEA). The FEA was designed to identify locations exhibiting maximum longitudinal strain under typical vehicle loads, thereby ensuring a high signal-to-noise ratio for the measurements. Concurrently, synchronized video cameras were installed overlooking the measurement zone to capture traffic flow for vehicle identification and trajectory tracking. As shown in Fig. 6b, due to on-site limitations, the cameras are mounted only on the bridge’s overhead steel truss. This placement causes perspective distortion in the lanes farther from the cameras. To mitigate potential identification errors arising from this distortion, each camera was tasked with monitoring only the four nearest lanes, ensuring high-accuracy tracking. The cameras were configured to record at a frame rate of 25–35 Hz.

Data synchronization

To achieve precise data fusion, we designated the high-frequency strain gauge acquisition timeline as the master clock. A discrete time series was generated with a time step matching the reciprocal of the strain gauges’ sampling frequency (e.g., 0.005 s for 200 Hz). For each timestamp, the system synchronously retrieves the recorded strain value while querying a relational database for the spatial coordinates (lane and longitudinal position) of all vehicles within the monitored span. This process, enabled by high-resolution timestamping from our vehicle detection module, yields a perfectly co-registered dataset. Each entry pairs a strain measurement with a complete snapshot of the instantaneous vehicle distribution, providing a robust foundation for all subsequent load analyses.

Vehicle classification dataset

Standard traffic datasets like COCO lack the fine-grained classification required for BWIM, particularly for heavy trucks which induce the most structural effects. We, therefore, constructed a specialized dataset. This dataset was augmented with 191,275 records from a highway toll station, which provided detailed ground-truth information on truck configurations (Fig. 6c). We defined nine vehicle classes: sedan car, minibus, bus, and six distinct truck classes based on axle count, from 2 A truck to 6 A truck (Fig. 6d). Our final annotated dataset for model training consists of 61,534 labels, with a distribution reflecting typical urban traffic on the test bridge, where passenger cars are predominant (Fig. 6d).

Vision model implementation

Experiments for the vision system in this study were conducted on a Windows 10 operating system with a deep learning framework based on PyTorch 1.737. The GPU driver version was CUDA 11.7, the neural network acceleration library was cuDNN 8.2.4, and the GPU environment was an RTX 2080Ti, with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) W-2133 CPU @ 3.60 GHz as the CPU environment. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets using an 8:1:1 ratio. Experiments were set to run for 300 epochs with a batch size of 8, using default YOLOv7 network training parameters. Both YOLOv7 and YOLOv7-MobileNetV3-SimAM networks achieved satisfactory convergence for target and classification loss after 300 training rounds.

Raw strain signals contain both the desired quasi-static response from vehicle loads and noise from dynamic effects (bridge vibration). To isolate the quasi-static component, we first analyzed the signal in the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform. The power spectral density (Fig. 6f) revealed a low-frequency band (0.1–0.3 Hz) associated with the vehicle passage and a distinct peak at 2.85 Hz, corresponding to the bridge’s first natural frequency. Based on this analysis, we applied a digital low-pass filter with a 1.0 Hz cutoff frequency. This effectively removed dynamic vibrations while preserving the entire load signal, yielding a clean, quasi-static response curve (Fig. 6g) for IL extraction.

The IL for each sensor-lane combination was determined using a direct measurement method. The calibrated trucks were driven at a constant low speed (20 km/h) to minimize dynamic effects.
