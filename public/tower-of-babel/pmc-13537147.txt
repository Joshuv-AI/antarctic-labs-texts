Evaluating the Cepheid Xpert TB/LTBI Research-Use-Only Assay for Detection of Active and Latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis Infection
Authors: Brittney Sweetser, Hoang Nguyen, Hai Dang, David Katumba, Tessa Mochizuki, Qiao Wang, Mangeni Wilson, Ha Phan, Patrick Phillips, Seda Yerlikaya, Payam Nahid, Claudia M Denkinger, Adithya Cattamanchi, William Worodria, the R2D2 TB Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate nonsputum tests are essential for improving tuberculosis (TB) detection. We report the first evaluation of the Xpert TB/LTBI assay (research-use-only; Cepheid, USA), which detects 9 Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigen-stimulated mRNA targets from blood.MethodsWe enrolled individuals ≥12 years with presumptive TB from clinics in Uganda and Vietnam. All participants underwent sputum- (liquid culture; Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra) and blood-based (Xpert TB/LTBI; QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus [QFT-Plus]) testing. Participants were classified as having active TB (positive sputum results), latent TB infection (LTBI; positive QFT-Plus with negative sputum results), or no TB infection. Diagnostic accuracy for active TB (primary analysis) and of a 2-step algorithm that first predicted TB infection (active TB or LTBI) and then distinguished active TB from LTBI was assessed using logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsAmong 214 participants included, 56.5% were male, 9.3% were with HIV, 29.0% had active TB, and 31.8% had LTBI. Xpert TB/LTBI area under the curve (AUC) was 0.92 (95% CI: .88–.96) for identifying active TB, and sensitivity was 93.5% (95% CI: 84.6–97.5) and specificity 76.3% (95% CI: 69.0–82.4) at a cut-point that achieved ≥90% sensitivity. In the 2-step algorithm, Xpert TB/LTBI had high accuracy for identifying TB infection in the first step, but accuracy was lower for distinguishing active TB from LTBI in the second step.ConclusionsXpert TB/LTBI exceeded World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended minimum accuracy targets for a TB screening test. However, further refinement is needed to improve its ability to distinguish active TB from LTBI.Keywords: diagnostics, point-of-care diagnostics, screening, triage

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health challenge, with approximately 10 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths annually [1]. Although efforts to control TB have intensified, the prevalence of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) poses a significant barrier to eradication. Latent tuberculosis infection affects a considerable proportion of the population, with an estimated one-quarter of the global population harboring the infection [2]. The transition from latent infection to active TB is a critical juncture in disease control, necessitating better diagnostic tools to identify and manage both active TB and LTBI effectively. Current methods for TB screening and LTBI testing have notable limitations that impede optimal TB control strategies.

World Health Organization (WHO)-endorsed screening tests for TB, such as symptom assessment, point-of-care (POC) C-reactive protein (CRP) testing, and chest radiography (CXR), each have significant limitations [3]. Symptom-based screening and CRP testing lack adequate sensitivity and specificity across all populations, often leading to missed diagnoses or false positives [4–6]. While CXR can provide valuable diagnostic information, it requires infrastructure that may not be available in resource-limited settings, reducing its utility in many high-burden TB regions [7]. For LTBI, interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), such as QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus), are considered the gold standard [2, 3]. However, these tests are expensive and technically complex and require specialized laboratory facilities, making them impractical for widespread use in low-resource environments [8]. Crucially, none of the existing TB screening tests or IGRAs can distinguish between active TB and LTBI, a critical limitation that hampers effective TB management and control.

The Xpert TB/LTBI cartridge represents a significant innovation in TB diagnostics. This novel test integrates advanced molecular detection with immunological assessment (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The Xpert TB/LTBI test utilizes a 10-color GeneXpert instrument to analyze 9 mRNA targets from stimulated CD4+ T cells, providing results within 60 minutes. In this study, we performed the first clinical evaluation of the Xpert TB/LTBI cartridge in cohorts from Vietnam and Uganda. Our primary aim was to assess its efficacy as a screening tool for active TB. Secondarily, we evaluated its performance in distinguishing between active TB and LTBI using a 2-step approach.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

We consecutively enrolled adults and adolescents 12 years or older at outpatient clinics in Uganda and Vietnam from 31 March 2023 to 13 September 2023. Enrollment took place in outpatient settings that provide routine diagnostic and treatment services within the national TB program guidelines. Participants were enrolled if they had symptoms compatible with active TB (eg, a persistent cough lasting at least 2 weeks) or established risk factors for TB disease (eg, HIV infection, history of mining work, or close contact with a person with TB) together with an abnormal WHO-recommended TB screening test (abnormal chest X-ray or C-reactive protein >5 mg/dL for people with HIV). We excluded people who had completed active TB treatment or TB preventive therapy within the year prior, had taken antibiotics with antimycobacterial activity within the 2 weeks prior, or could not complete informed consent or study follow-up procedures.

Study Procedures

Demographic and clinical data were collected with a standardized questionnaire completed by trained study personnel blinded to participants' TB status. As routine standard of care, participants were screened for TB symptoms and other risk factors such as prior TB history, HIV status, and diabetes. All participants underwent chest radiography and had sputum collected for TB testing, including Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (Cepheid, USA) and 2 liquid mycobacterial cultures (Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube [MGIT], Becton Dickinson, USA) following standard protocols.

Index and Comparator Tests

Venous blood was collected for Xpert TB/LTBI (index) and QFT-Plus (comparator) testing. For Xpert TB/LTBI, 1 mL of blood was collected into a lithium-heparin tube and then transferred into a TB1 tube with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb)-specific antigens ESAT-6 and CFP-10 for overnight stimulation of CD4+ T cells. The sample was then transferred into an Xpert lysis reagent, loaded into the cartridge, and tested with a 10-color GeneXpert instrument. Within 60 minutes, a summary is generated of cycle threshold (Ct) values measuring 9 mRNA targets (GBP5, GBP1P1, SLPI, VEGFA, IL2, MIG, IFNg, PLAU, and CD3E). Repeat testing of Xpert TB/LTBI was performed if the initial test result was invalid, which was defined as a CD3E control Ct value falling outside the acceptable range. For QFT-Plus, approximately 1 mL of blood was directly collected into each of the 4 tubes (nil, TB1, TB2, and mitogen). After incubation for 16–24 hours, the tubes were centrifuged, and plasma supernatant was harvested to perform enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in accordance with manufacturer recommendations [9]. Results were classified as positive, negative, or indeterminate based on the interferon-gamma concentration in TB1 and/or TB2 tubes relative to the nil control. A test was considered positive if the IFN-γ response in either TB1 or TB2 minus nil was above the manufacturer's cutoff, negative if below the cutoff with an adequate mitogen response, and indeterminate if the nil was too high or the mitogen response was insufficient.

Reference Standard Definitions for Tuberculosis Classification

Tuberculosis status was classified as follows: (1) active TB, positive baseline sputum culture or Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (semiquantitative grade of very low, low, medium, or high) result; (2) LTBI, positive QFT-Plus result without evidence of active TB; (3) TB infection, active TB or LTBI; and (4) no TB infection, negative QFT-Plus result and no evidence of active TB. Individuals with indeterminate TB status or QFT-Plus results were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Our primary analysis evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of Xpert TB/LTBI for detecting active TB. We compared median differences in Ct values for each of the 9 mRNA targets by active TB status using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We then conducted logistic regression to predict active TB status using all 9 Xpert TB/LTBI mRNA targets. We generated a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with the predicted probability of active TB and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) with a 95% CI derived from 2000 bootstrap replicates [10]. We reported the sensitivity and specificity of Xpert TB/LTBI for active TB at a cut-point that maximized specificity while meeting or exceeding the WHO target product profile (TPP) minimum sensitivity target for a TB screening test (≥90%) [11]. We compared sensitivity and specificity differences between Xpert TB/LTBI and QFT-plus using McNemar's test of paired proportions. We performed subgroup analyses based on country, sex, age, prior TB history, HIV infection, diabetes status, and sputum Xpert semiquantitative grade.

Second, we explored the ability of Xpert TB/LTBI to distinguish between active TB, LTBI, and no TB infection using a 2-step approach. We first assessed the ability of Xpert TB/LTBI to predict TB infection (ie, active TB or LTBI) and then evaluated its ability to distinguish active TB from LTBI among individuals classified as having TB infection in the first step. Similar to the primary analysis, logistic regression models were built using the 9 mRNA targets for each step, followed by ROC analysis to assess diagnostic accuracy at each stage.

All statistical analyses were performed in Stata/SE 18.0 or R version 4.4.2 [12, 13].

RESULTS

Overall, 243 participants were enrolled (Figure 1). Two (0.8%) with invalid Xpert TB/LTBI results, 20 (8.2%) with indeterminate QFT-Plus results, and 7 (2.9%) with indeterminate TB status were excluded. Among the remaining 214 participants, the median age was 42 years (interquartile range [IQR], 29–56), over half (121, 56.5%) were male, and 20 (9.3%) were with HIV (median CD4 cell count, 295.5/μL) (Table 1). Almost a third (62, 29.0%) had active TB, 68 (31.8%) were classified as having LTBI, and 84 (39.3%) had no TB infection.

Median Ct values for the Xpert TB/LTBI mRNA targets were generally higher in participants without active TB, indicating lower gene expression levels compared to those with active disease. CD3E was the only exception, consistent with its function as a housekeeping control gene (Supplementary Table 1). Differences in Ct values were statistically significant (P < .05) across all targets, with the largest group differences observed for MIG and GBP1P1.

Overall, the AUC of Xpert TB/LTBI for predicting active TB was 0.92 (95% CI: .88–.96; Figure 2). At a cut-point (0.223) that achieved the WHO TPP minimum sensitivity target for a TB screening test, the sensitivity of Xpert TB/LTBI was 93.5% (95% CI: 84.6–97.5), and the specificity was 76.3% (95% CI: 69.0–82.4; Table 2). Xpert TB/LTBI had similar sensitivity to QFT-Plus (93.5% vs 95.2%; difference, −1.6%; 95% CI: −10.3 to +7.0) but higher specificity (76.3% vs 55.3%; difference, +21.1%; 95% CI: 12.7–29.4).

Xpert TB/LTBI exceeded the TPP minimum sensitivity and specificity targets in nearly all participant subgroups (Supplementary Table 2). The 1 exception was among females, where sensitivity was 80% (95% CI: 51.9–95.7). Importantly, the sensitivity of Xpert TB/LTBI was similar among participants with high/medium versus low/very low sputum Xpert semiquantitative grades (94.3% vs 95.5%).

In the 2-step analysis, Xpert TB/LTBI demonstrated excellent performance in predicting TB infection (ie, active TB or LTBI) in the first step, with an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: .97–1.00; Figure 3A). At a cutoff value of 0.629 that maximized overall accuracy, the assay correctly identified 123 of 130 participants with TB infection (sensitivity, 94.6%; 95% CI: 89.3–97.4) and 80 of 84 participants without TB infection (specificity, 95.2%; 95% CI: 88.4–98.1).

Among individuals classified as having TB infection in the first step, the AUC for distinguishing between active TB and LTBI in the second step was 0.85 (95% CI: .79–.92; Figure 3B). Using a cutoff value of 0.508 that maximized overall accuracy, the sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing active TB from LTBI were 74.6% (95% CI: 62.2–83.9) and 85.3% (95% CI: 75.0–91.8), respectively.

Applying this threshold, the 2-step approach correctly identified 71.0% (44/62) of participants with active TB, 79.4% (54/68) with LTBI, and 95.2% (80/84) with no evidence of TB infection overall (Supplementary Figure 1). Of the 7 participants misclassified as not having TB infection in the first step, 3 had active TB and 4 had LTBI (Supplementary Figure 1). None of the 4 participants misclassified as having TB infection in the first step were classified as having active TB in the second step. Lower cutoff values for the second step resulted in correctly identifying a higher proportion of people with TB but also misclassified more people with LTBI as having active TB (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

The development of effective screening tests that work reliably across diverse populations has long been a critical need in TB diagnostics [1]. The Xpert TB/LTBI assay represents a significant advancement in this area. Our study provides the first clinical evaluation of the Xpert TB/LTBI assay, examining its efficacy in screening for TB infection and its ability to differentiate between active TB and LTBI. Our findings indicate that the Xpert TB/LTBI assay exceeds the WHO TPP minimum accuracy criteria for a TB screening test [14], achieving 93.5% sensitivity and 76.3% specificity. These data support broader evaluation of Xpert TB/LTBI as a TB screening test.

Importantly, the Xpert TB/LTBI assay showed consistent diagnostic accuracy across key subgroups, including people with HIV and individuals with diabetes—populations for whom current TB screening methods often perform variably [15–17]. The wide CIs observed in these subgroup analyses reflect the limited sample sizes, and larger studies will be needed to provide more precise estimates and confirm these findings. Despite these limitations, the assay's high sensitivity was generally maintained across groups, with the exception of a modestly lower sensitivity observed among female participants (80%). This difference may be due to sampling variability rather than underlying biological or clinical factors, and future studies will be needed to determine whether this difference persists. Notably, the assay also performed well among individuals with varying sputum mycobacterial loads, indicating its potential robustness even in populations with low bacillary burdens. This suggests a potential advantage of the Xpert TB/LTBI assay in diverse clinical settings, offering reliable screening performance where current methods may falter.

Xpert TB/LTBI could serve as a more accessible alternative to current IGRA-based tests, providing a fully automated, cartridge-based platform that simplifies sample processing and minimizes the need for specialized laboratory infrastructure. The assay could be incorporated as a near POC screening tool upstream of confirmatory TB testing. In high-burden settings, it may offer a practical and rapid alternative to laboratory-based IGRAs, helping to triage individuals and prioritize diagnostic evaluation. In low-burden settings, the assay could be used in targeted screening strategies—such as among close contacts or other high-risk groups—to facilitate early detection and preventive treatment of TB infection.

However, despite its strengths as a screening tool, the Xpert TB/LTBI assay demonstrated limitations in distinguishing between active TB and LTBI. In the 2-step algorithm designed to first identify TB infection and then distinguish between active TB and LTBI, the assay showed high accuracy in the initial step but reduced performance in the second step. The AUC for distinguishing active TB from LTBI was 0.85, with a sensitivity of 74.6% and a specificity of 85.3%. This indicates that while the assay can effectively identify individuals with TB infection, it has a moderate ability to differentiate between active and latent TB, which is critical for appropriate clinical management and treatment decisions.

There are several limitations to our study that should be considered. The cohort was well defined and underwent standardized clinical evaluation, but further validation in larger and more diverse populations is necessary to confirm the assay's utility. Additionally, the lack of a gold standard for defining LTBI may have impacted our assessment of the assay's ability to differentiate between active and latent TB. The reliance on QFT-Plus to define LTBI might not capture all LTBI cases or those most likely to progress to active TB, affecting the overall evaluation. Furthermore, the precise timing of antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation among participants with HIV was not systematically recorded. Immune reconstitution following ART initiation could potentially influence immune activation and mRNA expression levels; however, given the small number of participants with HIV and with only 1 participant reported having initiated ART within the past 6 months, we do not expect this to have substantially influenced the overall study findings.

Future studies should explore the integration of Xpert TB/LTBI with other diagnostic tools, such as chest radiography, to enhance its ability to differentiate between active and latent TB. Operational aspects, including cost-effectiveness, user training, and infrastructure requirements, should be evaluated to facilitate the adoption of this novel test in real-world settings.

In conclusion, the Xpert TB/LTBI assay shows great promise as a non–sputum-based TB screening test that meets WHO TPP accuracy criteria and could offer improved case detection over existing methods. However, further refinement and validation are needed to enhance its ability to distinguish between active TB and LTBI. Continued research and evaluation will be crucial in optimizing the assay for widespread clinical use and improving TB management and control globally.
