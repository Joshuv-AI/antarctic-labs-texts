On the Stability of the Jacobian Matrix in Deep Neural Networks
Benjamin Dadoun, Soufiane Hayou, Hanan Salam, Mohamed El Amine Seddik, Pierre Youssef

Abstract
. Deep neural networks are known to suffer from exploding or vanishing gradients as depth increases, a phenomenon closely tied to the spectral behavior of the input-output Jacobian. Prior work has identified critical initialization schemes that ensure Jacobian stability, but these analyses are typically restricted to fully connected networks with i.i.d. weights. In this work, we go significantly beyond these limitations: we establish a general stability theorem for deep neural networks that accommodates sparsity (such as that introduced by pruning) and non-i.i.d., weakly correlated weights (e.g. induced by training). Our results rely on recent advances in random matrix theory, and provide rigorous guarantees for spectral stability in a much broader class of network models. This extends the theoretical foundation for initialization schemes in modern neural networks with structured and dependent randomness.

1.
Introduction

Despite their impressive performance across a wide range of applications, training deep neural networks (DNNs) remains a challenging task, requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning. A key factor that affects the trainability of DNNs is the behavior of the input-output Jacobian, which quantifies the sensitivity of the network’s output to perturbations in its input
[
SMG13
]
. Improper initialization can lead to vanishing or exploding Jacobian/gradients as network depth increases, potentially causing the training process to converge to suboptimal local optima or diverge altogether.

Prior research on the Jacobian in DNNs has primarily examined its behavior at initialization
[
PSG17
,
CH23
]
, where network weights are typically drawn from random distributions, such as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) Gaussians, or random orthogonal matrices
[
PSG17
,
HN20
,
CDK22
]
. These studies have demonstrated, both theoretically and empirically, a correlation between “stable” Jacobian behavior at initialization and improved training and generalization performance. Similar works have investigated other related quantities at initialization such as input-output covariance kernel and gradient propagation dynamics, to derive the “Edge of Chaos” initialization regime
[
PLR+16
,
SGGSD17
,
HDR19
,
HDR22
]
, which guarantees gradient stability and improved information propagation through depth.

However, these analyses are limited to the setting of random, uncorrelated initializations and do not readily extend to more structured or correlated weight distributions. Yet in many practical scenarios, such as data-dependent initialization
[
KDDD15
,
CIG22
]
, meta-learning
[
FAL17
]
, or fine-tuning from pretrained weights
[
LFLY18
]
, networks are initialized with non-i.i.d., often correlated weights
[
MM15
]
. Recent theoretical and empirical work has shown that even mild correlations in the weight matrices can significantly alter signal and gradient propagation dynamics, potentially improving convergence and generalization
[
JYZ+20
]
. Still, the behavior of the Jacobian in the presence of correlated weights remains poorly understood, particularly in the large-depth regime where signal propagation is most sensitive. Understanding how weight correlations affect Jacobian stability is therefore crucial for explaining the training dynamics of deep networks in realistic settings.

Similarly, another underexplored case is that of sparse networks. Pruning is a common technique that sparsifies neural networks to reduce their memory and computational footprint
[
LDS90
,
HSG93
,
LAT18
,
WZG20
,
FC19
]
. While score-based pruning methods such as SNIP
[
LAT18
]
, GraSP
[
WZG20
]
, and their variants
[
HTDT21
]
aim to preserve important connectivity at initialization, the effect of pruning on the input-output Jacobian remains poorly understood. Existing works suggest that unstructured sparsity can disrupt gradient flow
[
EIKD22
]
, but lack theoretical guarantees on Jacobian stability. This gap motivates a closer analysis of Jacobian behavior in pruned networks and other non-standard settings.

In this work, we focus on two scenarios:

•

Correlated weights
: Investigating the Jacobian behavior in the large-depth regime with correlated weights can shed light on training stability during the early stages, particularly near initialization.

•

Sparse networks
: Studying the Jacobian of pruned (sparse) networks can provide insights on how to adjust training algorithms to maintain stability after
pruning
.

We address these questions by developing a general theoretical framework (stability theorem,
Section

3
) based on recent breakthroughs in random matrix theory for the products of random matrices in non-i.i.d. settings
[
BvH24
]
. Our primary objective is to
gain insights
into the behavior of the Jacobian under the different scenarios stated above, in the case of Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs).

Our analysis reveals several interesting phenomena. Specifically, we show that in sparse networks, the Jacobian behaves similarly to that of a full (non-sparse) network if we scale the non-zero weights suitably, where the
scaling factor depends on the pruning method
. In
Section

5
, we provide an application of our framework in the case of networks with non-i.i.d. initialization weights, and find that there exists a width-dependent correlation threshold that guarantees similar Jacobian properties to the i.i.d. case.

In practice, neural networks are usually trained with gradient-based algorithms such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Adam, etc.

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(Y_{\text{out}}(x_{i}),z_{i}),

3.
A general stability theorem

Random Matrix Theory (RMT) has played a pivotal role in advancing both pure and applied mathematics. Initially motivated by questions in mathematical physics, RMT has been primarily concerned with random matrix models that possess a large degree of symmetry (see
[
Tao11
]
). A prototypical example is the Wigner matrix, an ensemble of symmetric matrices with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) entries above the diagonal. Over the past several decades, major breakthroughs have led to a remarkably detailed understanding of the global and local spectral behavior of such models. In recent years, the scope of RMT has expanded significantly to encompass
structured
or
inhomogeneous
random matrices, where the entries may exhibit dependencies, non-identical distributions, or reflect underlying combinatorial structures. Notable examples include sparse matrices, band matrices, adjacency matrices of random graphs, and matrix-valued functions of random inputs (we refer for example to
[
vH17
,
LvHY18
,
BGK16
,
AOSTY24
]
and references therein). These models are central to contemporary applications in statistics, machine learning, wireless communications, quantum information, and deep learning. A key challenge in this broader setting is to understand how spectral properties respond to structural constraints and dependence patterns. Addressing this challenge has led to the development of powerful new techniques, including matrix concentration inequalities
[
Tro15
]
, combinatorial methods, and tools from Free Probability Theory (see
[
MS17
]
), which models the asymptotic behavior of non-commutative random variables and proves especially effective in analyzing the spectra of products or polynomials of large random matrices. In this work, we build on recent breakthrough results of
[
BBvH23
,
BvH24
]
capable of handling in particular sparse random matrices with dependent entries. These advances crucially exploit the deep connection between RMT and the free probabilistic limit regime, a perspective particularly well-suited to the analysis of deep neural networks. In such models, the Jacobian matrix is naturally expressed as a non-commutative polynomial in the random weight matrices of each layer, cf. (
2
).
The goal of this section is to formulate and prove a general stability theorem for such Jacobian matrices. This result will serve as a foundational tool for our analysis in the subsequent sections.

Recall that the spectral norm
\|M\|
of a
n\times n
matrix is by definition the maximum of its singular values
\sigma_{1}(M),\ldots,\sigma_{n}(M)
. Since we can write

\|M\|=\lim_{p\to\infty}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sigma_{i}(M)}^{p}\right)}^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lim_{p\to\infty}{\left(\Tr{\sqrt{MM^{*}}^{p}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lim_{p\to\infty}{\left(\Tr{H^{2p}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2p}},

where
H:=({\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{cc}0&M\\
M^{*}&0\end{array}})
is a
2n\times 2n
self-adjoint matrix, it follows that the spectral norm
\|J_{k}\|
of the Jacobian matrix (
2
)
can be estimated by trace moments of products of random matrices with the same form as
H
. Under mild assumptions outlined in the theorem below,
strong asymptotic freeness
holds: these tracial moments are asymptotically the same as those of products of Gaussian matrices and thus exhibit a universal asymptotic behavior. In this regard, the stability of the Jacobian for arbitrary or sparse weight matrices is ensured by its approximability with respect to the Gaussian case.
Our general setting is as follows.

(A1)

W_{1},\ldots,W_{L}
are independent copies of a

4.
Jacobian in Sparse Networks

Network pruning aims at removing redundant weights that do not significantly affect model performance
[
LDS90
]
. Such weights are identified using some pruning criterion that determines the importance of each weight in the network.
By removing these weights, one could significantly reduce the computational requirements for both the training and deployment of DNNs
[
HSG93
]
. After pruning, the network becomes
sparse
, and training such networks has been proven challenging in practice
[
FC19
]
. In this section, we analyze the Jacobian norm of sparse networks and provide the necessary conditions for stability. Notably, we show that a simple scaling trick ensures stability. We also identify an
edge of stability
for sparsity and support our theory with empirical evidence. Our current analysis is limited to
pruning at initialization

[
LAT18
,
WQB+22
,
HTDT21
]
of MLPs, i.e. pruning performed on i.i.d weights. Extending this theory to modern architectures is not straightforward and is an interesting question for future work.

Pruning.
Consider the MLP architecture described in
Eq.

1
. Pruning involves the application of a mask
B\in[0,\beta_{p}]^{p}
(where
p
is the total number of parameters in the network)
to the weights of the network
\mathbf{W}
producing a
pruned
network with weights
\mathbf{W}^{B}
, where
\mathbf{W}^{B}=B\circ\mathbf{W}
is the Hadamard (i.e., element-wise) product.
We say weight
i\in[p]
is pruned if
b_{i}=0
. This can be performed via different procedures. A standard approach to generating masks is to compute a score
g_{k}^{ij}
for each weight
W_{k}^{ij}
according to some criterion.
The mask is then created by keeping the top
m
weights by score, where
m
is chosen to meet some desired sparsity level
s
(fraction of weights to remove). The Jacobian of the pruned network
J^{B}_{1}
is given by:

J^{B}_{1}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}W^{B}_{l}D_{l-1}.

We propose to study the Jacobian norm
\|J^{B}_{1}\|
for networks pruned at initialization with two different pruning methods:

(1)

Random Pruning
: weights are randomly pruned with probability
s
(the sparsity).

(2)

Score-Based Pruning
: weights are scored using a certain criteria (e.g., magnitude, sensitivity,).

The first requires a simple application of our main stability theorem (
Section

4.1
), while the latter requires a more delicate one (
Section

4.2
). The main takeaway is that scaling the weights is required to maintain stability in pruned networks. However, the scaling factor depends on the pruning method.

4.1.
Random Pruning

In the subsequent analysis, we use the notation
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
for two positive sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
, whenever
b_{n}=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.1}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(a_{n})
.

Recall that
J_{1}^{iid}
refers to the Jacobian of an iid randomly initialized MLP as in
Section

2
. The following result shows that using a scaling trick, the Jacobian of the pruned network is asymptotically identical to that of non-pruned iid randomly initialized network.

{theorem}
[Scaling guarantees stability]
Consider random pruning with sparsity level
s_{n}\in(0,1)
that can either depend on
n
or be constant,
that is, choose the mask
B:=(1-s_{n})^{-1/2}B_{i,j}
where the
B_{i,j}
’s are iid Bernoulli
(1-s_{n})
variables. Then, under the assumption that
1-s_{n}\gg\frac{\log(n)^{4}}{n}
, the Jacobian of the scaled sparse network given by
J^{B}_{1}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}(B_{l}\odot W_{l})D_{l-1}
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J_{1}^{B}\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J_{1}^{iid}\|.

As a result, under these conditions, the Jacobian of the pruned network is also stable.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward from
Section

3
. All conditions
(i)-(iv)
are trivially satisfied.
∎

Section

4.1
reveals that, once pruning has been performed, re-scaling is essential for stabilizing the Jacobian. Specifically, if we don’t scale the weights (or equivalently the mask) with
(1-s_{n})^{-1/2}
then the theorem can be stated as having
\|J_{1}^{B}\|
asymptotically proportional to
\tilde{\Theta}_{L}((1-s_{n})^{L/2})
.
3
3

3

Recall that
\|J_{1}^{iid}\|=\tilde{\Theta}_{L}(1)
which yields the stated result.
This exponential dependence on depth indicates that stability cannot be attained without modifying the weights. In other words, when starting with a critically initialized network, the weights must be re-scaled after pruning to account for the resulting sparsity. This scaling factor
(1-s_{n})^{-1/2}
makes the infinite-width behavior of the spectral norm similar to that of a non-pruned critically initialized neural network, thereby guaranteeing stability in the sparse network, as evidenced in
Fig.

2
. Further experiments are provided in
Section

6
.

In
Section

4.1
, the sparsity
s_{n}
can depend on
n
. We provide an upper bound on the sparsity in terms of the width, in order for the stability result to hold. This result allows us to identify an
edge of stability
, defined as a maximal sparsity (in terms of
n
) so that the stability holds.
This result highlights an interesting
phase transition
phenomenon with respect to the sparsity. When the sparsity is of order
1-s_{n}\sim n^{-1}
up to a logarithmic factor, the result of
Section

4.1
no longer holds, and the spectral norm of the Jacobian is significantly different from that of a non-pruned critically initialed network, see e.g.
[
BGBK19
,
TY21
]
. In
Fig.

2
, We observe this behavior when the sparsity hits the level
99\%
, which is of order
\log_{10}(256)/256\approx 0.009
. It is worth noting that the condition
1-s_{n}\gg n^{-1}\log^{4}n
is a sufficient condition, and it is likely that phase transition occurs at a smaller threshold.

4.2.
Score-Based Pruning

To complement the previous section, we show that a similar stability result holds with score-based pruning, however, the scaling factor is different in this case. We restrict our analysis to magnitude-based pruning, performed at initialization, where weights are scored based on their magnitude
g_{w}=|w|

[
HPTD15
]
. However, the proof can be in principle extended to other score-based methods.
The main takeaway is that the scaling factor depends on the pruning method.

In score-based pruning, we
compute some scores
g\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and prune based on the value of
g
: keep the
(1-s)\times n
weights with the highest scores, where
n
is the total number of parameters in the matrix.
4
4

4

In general, magnitude-based pruning can be performed on a matrix level or a model level. For the sake of simplification, we restrict our analysis to the matrix level.

This introduces a dependence on the coefficients, which becomes weaker when the number of parameters
p
gets larger. Asymptotically (in
n
), score-based pruning becomes to a rejection algorithm.

{theorem}

[Magnitude-based pruning]
Suppose that the coefficients of the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
are iid
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{2}{n})
variables,
and consider magnitude-based pruning in which the
r_{n}=o(n^{2})
largest weight coefficients in absolute value
are kept, where
r_{n}\gg n\log^{3}n
(e.g.,
r_{n}:=\lceil n\log^{4}n\rceil
). Specifically, choose the mask
B:=(B_{i,j})
defined by

B_{i,j}:=\frac{n}{\sqrt{r_{n}\log{\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|w_{i,j}|\geq w^{(r_{n})}\}},

where
w^{(1)}\geq w^{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq w^{(n^{2})}

is the non-increasing rearrangement of
\{|w_{i,j}|:i,j\leq n\}
.
Then, the Jacobian of the scaled sparse network given by
J^{B}_{1}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}(B_{l}\odot W_{l})D_{l-1},
satisfies,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J^{B}_{1}\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J_{1}^{iid}\|.

As a result, under these conditions, the Jacobian of the pruned network is also stable.

Proof.

We only need to check conditions (i) and (ii) of
Section

3
since
(B,\mathbf{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(B,-\mathbf{W})
.
The choice of
r_{n}\gg n\log^{3}n
ensures condition (i) since then

\beta_{n}^{2}:=\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}\log\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}}=o(n\log^{-4}n)
.
To check (ii), we need to estimate

\displaystyle\E{{\lvert b_{i,k}w_{i,k}\rvert}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}\log{\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}}}\E w_{i,k}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|w_{i,k}|\geq w^{(r_{n})}\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n}{r_{n}\log{\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}}}\E{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}X^{2}\cdot\binom{n^{2}-1}{j-1}\Phi(X^{2})^{n^{2}-j}\bigl(1-\Phi(X^{2})\bigr)^{j-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{nr_{n}\log{\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}}}\E{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\Phi^{-1}(1-U_{n}^{(j)})},

where
X
is a standard Gaussian variable,

\Phi(x):=\P(X^{2}\leq x)
(therefore
\Phi^{-1}(x)=2\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(x)^{2}
, where
\operatorname{erf}
is the standard error function), and

0\leq U_{n}^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{n}^{(n^{2})}\leq 1
is the
non-decreasing rearrangement of
n^{2}
independent, standard uniform random variables
(so the
order statistics

U_{n}^{(j)}
has a Beta
(j,n^{2}-j+1)
distribution).
But a Riemann sum approximation easily yields

\displaystyle\E{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\Phi^{-1}(1-U_{n}^{(j)})}

\displaystyle\sim n^{2}\E{\int_{0}^{U_{n}^{(r_{n})}}\Phi^{-1}(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\sim r_{n}\log{\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}}},

because
U_{n}^{(r_{n})}\sim\frac{r_{n}}{n^{2}}
and
\Phi^{-1}(1-x)
increases like
\log(\frac{1}{x})
as
x\to 0^{+}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\E{{\lvert b_{i,k}w_{i,k}\rvert}^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2}{n},

and it follows that condition (ii) of the stability theorem is also satisfied.
∎

5.
Jacobian with Dependent Weights

The reader might ask what happens to the Jacobian norm when the weights are
not
independent, or equivalently:
what level of correlation may be introduced between the weights without jeopardizing Jacobian stability?

The next theorem demonstrates that weight matrices

with correlated entries can still yield Jacobian stability, provided that the correlation does not surpass
\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.1}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(n^{-1}\log(n)^{-3})

{theorem}

[Stability with Dependent Weights]
Assume that the weights
W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L}

2/n

\textup{corr}(W^{ij}_{k},W^{ml}_{k})=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.1}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(n^{-1}\log(n)^{-3})

(i,j)\neq(m,l)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|J_{1}\|-\|J_{1}^{iid}\|\approx 0,

where
J_{1}^{iid}
denotes the Jacobian with iid weights at initialization. As a result, the stability holds in this.

Proof.

It suffices to apply the stability theorem
with
t=1

B
where all components are equal to
1

In the setting of

5

,
that is
\corr(W_{k}^{ij},W_{k}^{ml})=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.1}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(n^{-1}\log(n)^{-3})
.
∎

7.
Conclusion and Limitations

In this paper, we provided an analysis of the Jacobian norm in DNNs in different contexts. Our findings shed light on an important result: stability at initialization implies stability throughout training (under some approximations). In this regard, our work expands the existing literature on this topic and justifies the crucial role of initialization, often observed in practice. We also study the Jacobian of networks pruned at initialization and show how one can stabilize sparse networks after pruning.
However, one limitations of our theory is that it currently only applies to the MLP architecture. Extending these results to more modern architectures is an interesting question for future work.
