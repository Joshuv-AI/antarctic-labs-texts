Improve MLLM Benchmark Efficiency through Interview
Farong Wen, Yijin Guo, Junying Wang, Jiaohao Xiao, Yingjie Zhou, Chunyi Li, Zicheng Zhang, Guangtao Zhai

Abstract
. The rapid development of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLM) has led to a wide range of MLLM applications, and a number of benchmark datasets have sprung up in order to assess MLLM abilities. However, full-coverage Q&A testing on large-scale data is resource-intensive and time-consuming. To address this issue, we propose the MLLM Interview (MITV) strategy, which aims to quickly obtain MLLM performance metrics by quizzing fewer question. First, First, we constructed the interview dataset, which was built on an existing MLLM assessment dataset, by adding difficulty labels based on the performance of some typical MLLMs in this dataset. Second, we propose an MLLM Interview strategy, which obtains an initial performance situation of the large model by quizzing a small number of topics and then continuously tries to test the model’s limits. Through extensive experiments, the result shows that the MITV strategy proposed in this paper performs well on MLLM benchmark datasets, and it is able to obtain the model evaluation capability faster through a small number of questions and answers.

1.
Introduction

The rapid advancement of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has transformed numerous domains, enabling sophisticated reasoning across text, images, and other data modalities. Evaluating the performance of MLLMs is critical to understanding their capabilities and limitations. Traditionally, MLLM evaluation relies on comprehensive benchmarks comprising large sets of standardized tasks. However, as MLLMs evolve in complexity and benchmarks grow in scope, the computational and temporal costs of full benchmark evaluations have become increasingly prohibitive, prompting the need for more efficient evaluation methodologies.

Current MLLM benchmarks provide standardized metrics but often require evaluating models on thousands of questions to ensure robustness. This exhaustive approach, while thorough, is redundant and resource-intensive, particularly when benchmarking advanced MLLMs with overlapping capabilities. The redundancy in benchmarks stems from the inclusion of numerous questions that may not significantly differentiate model performance, highlighting the need for streamlined evaluation processes, as shown in the benchmark redundancy principle.

As depicted in Fig.
2
, an SRCC of 0.95 is achieved at a 40% sampling rate across MLLM benchmarks, indicating that evaluating MLLMs with just 40% of the benchmark instances yields performance rankings nearly identical to those using the full benchmark instances. However, beyond this point, further increases in the sampling rate result in minimal SRCC improvement, highlighting the presence of information redundancy in the benchmark.

To address the inefficiency of traditional benchmarks, we draw inspiration from the human interview process. Experienced interviewers can accurately assess a candidate’s abilities with only a few carefully selected questions, adapting their inquiries based on responses to probe specific strengths and weaknesses. This dynamic and adaptive approach contrasts with the static nature of benchmarks, which apply a fixed set of questions regardless of a model’s performance. Motivated by this, we propose an interview-based evaluation framework for MLLMs, designed to replicate the efficiency and adaptability of human interviews while maintaining the rigor of benchmark assessments.

Our approach employs a powerful MLLM as an interviewer to evaluate another MLLM (the interviewee). We begin by labeling benchmark question instances with their difficulty and category, creating a structured question pool. The interviewer MLLM leverages this metadata to dynamically select questions, evaluate responses, and adjust subsequent questions based on the interviewee’s performance. By iteratively refining the question selection, the interviewer efficiently probes the interviewee’s capabilities across diverse tasks and difficulty levels. The process culminates in a final score that reflects the interviewee’s overall performance. The experimental results show that the MITV strategy’s performance is better than the Random strategy’s performance for the same number of topics

Our work makes the following contributions:

•

Construction of Interview Dataset
: We construct a comprehensive interview dataset by integrating existing MLLM benchmarks and annotating questions with difficulty and category labels. This structured dataset enables targeted and efficient evaluation across diverse dimensions and difficulty levels.

•

Interview-Based Evaluation Framework
: We introduce a dynamic, adaptive evaluation methodology for MLLMs, inspired by human interviews, which significantly reduces the number of questions needed compared to traditional benchmarks. We develop a systematic approach to labeling benchmark questions by difficulty and category, paired with an intelligent question selection mechanism that optimizes evaluation efficiency.

•

Empirical Validation of Efficiency and Accuracy
: We demonstrate through experiments that our interview-based approach achieves comparable ranking accuracy to full benchmarks with a fraction of the questions, offering a scalable solution for MLLM evaluation.

This work paves the way for more efficient and practical evaluation strategies, enabling rapid assessment of MLLMs in both research and deployment contexts.

The current paradigm, largely dependent on static, predefined test suites, may not adequately reveal the dynamic, adaptive, and deeper reasoning faculties of state-of-the-art MLLMs. This suggests an exigent need for novel evaluation methodologies capable of more effectively probing these emergent, complex abilities.

4.
Proposed Method

In this section, we introduce the proposed MITV Strategy, which is framed as shown in Fig. It consists of three main modules, which are topic selection, interview, and result judgement. Firstly, the question selection module selects the difficulty and category of the next question based on the interviewee’s response. Then the interview module gives the selected question to the interviewee to answer. Finally, the result analysis module judges the interviewee’s answers and summarises the interviewee’s performance for the question selection module’s judgement. The performance of the interviewees is finally obtained through multiple rounds of interview questions and answers.

4.1.
Difficulty Determined

The difficulty determined module integrates information theory and adaptive testing theory. Its core objective is to dynamically adjust question difficulty to efficiently maximize information gain about the model’s capabilities. Prior to evaluation, the model’s ability interval is defined, assumed to be
[0,10]
. An initial estimate is made using a bisection-like strategy, starting from the midpoint of this interval.

The self-information formula from information theory serves as the cornerstone for question selection. The information obtained from a single observation is calculated as:

I=p_{0}\cdot\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)\cdot C(p)+q_{0}\cdot\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\cdot C(q)

where
p
and
q
denote the prior probabilities of the model answering correctly and incorrectly, respectively, which can be manually configured;
p_{0}
and
q_{0}
represent the observed probabilities of correct and incorrect responses;
C(p)
and
C(q)
are confidence functions. This formula indicates that higher prior correctness probability
p
yields less information, while lower
p
results in more information gain, with confidence weighting applied.

During question selection, expected information gains for questions of varying difficulty levels are computed based on the current ability estimate. Question difficulty ranges from 1 to 10, with empirical accuracy expectations: 70% for Level 3, 50% for Level 5, and 30% for Level 7, and so on. Questions maximizing expected information are prioritized. If the model consistently answers easy questions correctly, the difficulty is increased according to adaptive testing theory; conversely, failures trigger a decrease in difficulty, iteratively converging towards the interviewee’s ability level.

The feedback from the interviewer allows us to calculate the amount of information in the interviewer’s answer to the previous question, and if the amount of information is high, the difficulty of the question is adjusted according to whether or not it answered the question correctly. This process can be expressed as:

\text{L}_{\text{t+1}}=\begin{cases}\text{L}_{\text{t}}-1,&\text{if }I_{\text{t}}>I_{\text{threshold}}\text{ and }hit_{\text{t}}=0\\
\text{L}_{\text{t}}+1,&\text{if }I_{\text{t}}>I_{\text{threshold}}\text{ and }hit_{\text{t}}=1\\
\text{L}_{\text{t}},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
L_{t}
denotes the difficulty level of the t-th question,
I_{t}
denotes the amount of information in the response to the t-th question, and
hit_{t}
denotes the response to the t-th question, which is correct if it is 1, and incorrect if it is the other way round.
Although the introduction of response informativeness can lead interviewers to answer questions of comparable difficulty at their own level to a greater extent, an uneven distribution of data difficulty can keep interviewees under the same low or high difficulty level for a long time. So we introduce
R_{l}
to help adjust the level selection and avoid this problem. The process can be expressed as follows:

\text{L}_{\text{t+1}}=\begin{cases}\text{L}_{\text{t}}+1,&\text{if }R_{\text{l}}<\theta_{1}\\
\text{L}_{\text{t}}-1,&\text{if }R_{\text{l}}>\theta_{2}\end{cases}

Where
R_{l}
denotes the percentage of correct responses at difficulty level
L
,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
denote the lower and upper thresholds, and
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}
. Ceteris paribus,
R_{l}
has a higher priority than
I_{t}
.

4.2.
MLLM Interview Module

In order to make the interview questions more effective and typical, the module selects 10 different types of questions based on the target level. We chose GPT-4o as the interviewer due to its balanced performance. the interviewer MLLM selects a representative question from the selected questions, taking into account the responses of the candidates, and then passes the question to the candidate MLLM to answer. Through this systematic process of question screening and interaction, it not only ensures that the interview questions can accurately match the assessment needs, but also flexibly adjusts the direction of the investigation based on the interviewee MLLM feedback in the early stages, which helps to assess the interview MLLM competency level in a more comprehensive and in-depth manner, and improves the accuracy and relevance of the interview assessment.

4.3.
Judgment Module

The judging module is mainly responsible for data processing in the interview process, one is to check the answers of the interviewers.Due to the random nature of the MLLM answer content, we use a Large language model (LLM) to perform a comprehensive calibration of the interviewee’s competence in order to avoid the difficulties posed by varying lengths of answers and redundancy of answer information.

The judgment module is mainly responsible for the data processing in the interview process, which is to verify the answers of the interviewee. Due to the randomness of MLLM answers, we use LLM to comprehensively verify the interviewee’s ability to avoid the difficulties caused by the different lengths of answers and the redundancy of answer information. Second, we calculate the performance of the interviewee. This includes the correct rate at different difficulty levels, the amount of information in the question and answer session, and calculating the distribution of the interviewee’s ability level after the interview.
The judging module is able to statistically analyse the interviewee’s performance information in order to systematically make the next decision.

5.
Experiment

5.1.
Experiment Detail

In order to validate the generalisation of MITV, we have selected 19 typical MLLMs, as detailed in Tab.
2
. Among them, 9 large models were validated using official API calls, and for the other MLLM models, we used locally deployed models for the validation. To verify the validity of MITV, we designed a control group whose Benchmark questions were quizzed through a random sampling strategy. It is worth noting that for the random sampling strategy group experimental data we used VLMEvalKit
(
Duan et al. 2024
)
for uniform testing. It is worth noting that for both sets of experiments, we set up an absolute number of questions comparison and a relative proportional number of questions comparison.

On each Benchmark, the full coverage test performance of the model are used as the ground truth and three commonly used metrics for algorithm assessment are applied: Spearman rank order correlation coefficient (SRCC), Pearson linear correlation coefficient (PLCC), and Kendall rank order correlation coefficient (KRCC).

5.2.
Performance Discussion

The experimental results in Table
3
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed MITV strategy compared to the random sampling baseline across four MLLM benchmarks: A-Bench, Q-Bench, MMT-Bench, and SEED-Bench. With closer inspections, we can obtain several insights as follows:

5.2.1.
Superior Performance of MITV

Across all benchmarks, MITV achieves higher SRCC, PLCC, and KRCC scores than the random sampling strategy on most question number settings, both in absolute question counts (10 to 100 questions) and relative question percentages (1% to 5%). The overview SRCC performance is shown in Fig.
5
. For instance, on A-Bench with 50 questions, MITV yields an SRCC of 0.8250, compared to 0.6521 for random sampling—a 26.5% improvement. These results highlight MITV’s ability to produce rankings more aligned with full benchmark evaluations, even with a limited number of questions.

5.2.2.
Efficiency with Small Question Sets

MITV exhibits a significant advantage when the number of questions is small. For example, with just 10 questions on MMT-Bench, MITV achieves an SRCC of 0.7408, compared to 0.4830 for random sampling—a 53.4% improvement. This high starting point underscores the efficiency of MITV’s adaptive question selection, which leverages difficulty metadata and performance feedback to target the most informative questions early in the evaluation process. In contrast, random sampling struggles to capture meaningful performance differences with limited questions, as it lacks the dynamic adjustment mechanism inherent in MITV.

5.2.3.
Generalization Across Benchmarks

The performance gains of MITV are consistent across diverse benchmarks, demonstrating its generalizability. On SEED-Bench, which has a larger benchmark, MITV achieves an SRCC of 0.8088 with 50 questions, compared to 0.6102 for random sampling.On smaller benchmarks like Q-Bench and MMT-Bench, MITV maintains its superiority, with SRCC values exceeding 0.83 at 50 questions, approaching the accuracy of full benchmark evaluations

6.
Conclusion

Since the conventional Benchmark test is a full-coverage question and answer test, there is information redundancy, in order to optimise the evaluation method, this paper firstly constructs several datasets with difficulty labels through the performance of ten models on the existing Benchmark. Then the MITV strategy is proposed, which can obtain the fastest model evaluation performance through a small number of questions and answers by converting the conventional full-coverage model performance test into an interview ability evaluation. Through a large number of experiments, it is proved that the proposed method is effective, has good generalisation, and can provide suggestions and guidance for MLLM assessment work.
