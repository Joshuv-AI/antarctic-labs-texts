Cotangent functors and Herzog’s last theorem
Antonino Ficarra

Abstract
. We present the theory of cotangent functors following the approach of Palamodov, and a conjecture of Herzog relating the vanishing of certain cotangent functors to the property of being a complete intersection.



Preface

The present article is an attempt to complete Jurgen Herzog’s unfinished last paper. I thank Maja, Susanne and Ulrike (Herzog’s wife and daughters) for handing me over the manuscript of their father. This paper was being written by Professor Herzog to honor the memory of Professor Wolmer Vasconcelos. It is my intention to honor both Professors Herzog and Vasconcelos, whose works have influenced reaserchers in Commutative Algebra for over the past fifty years.

Introduction

During the 1960s and 1970s, the study of the module of differentials was a central topic in Commutative Algebra, particularly in Germany. The book of Kunz
[
13
]
reflects this trend. The module of Kähler differentials is defined as a construction satisfying a fundamental universal mapping property, making the corresponding functor representable. The fascination with the subject was partly driven by several famous, and still open conjectures, namely the Zariski-Lipman’s conjecture, the Berger’s conjecture and the Vasconcelos’s conjecture. For a nice summary about what is known about these conjectures we refer the reader to Herzog’s survey
[
10
]
. See also the papers
[
11
,
16
,
17
]
for some recent progress on Berger’s conjecture.

Another notable and related question again posed by Vasconcelos
[
22
]
somehow parallels the previously mentioned conjecture due to him.

Conjecture A
.

Let
R
be a Noetherian ring and
I\subset R
be an ideal such that the conormal module
I/I^{2}
has finite projective dimension as an
R/I
-module. Then
I
is a complete intersection.

Recently, Benjamin Briggs
[
6
]
resolved Conjecture
A
in full generality.

For any
R
-algebra homomorphism
R\rightarrow S
and any
S
-module
M
, there exist modules
\textup{T}^{i}(S/R,M)
and
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)
for
i=0,1,\dots
, the so-called
tangent
and

cotangent modules
. They are functorial in all three variables. In 1967, Lichtenbaum and Schlessinger
[
14
]
first introduced the functors
\textup{T}^{i}
for
i=0,1,2
. Later on, Quillen
[
19
]
in 1970 and André
[
1
]
in 1974 defined the higher cotangent functors and developed their theory. In characteristic 0, a different and simpler approach was given by Palamodov
[
18
]
by employing DG-algebras. We present the approach of Palamodov in Section
2
.

In 1981, Jürgen Herzog posed the following conjecture, which remains open.

Conjecture B
.

(Herzog’s Last Theorem).
Let
R
be a regular local ring,
I\subset R
a proper ideal and
S=R/I
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

I
is a complete intersection.

(b)

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for all
i>1
.

(c)

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for all
i\gg 1
.

We would like to refer to this conjecture as
Herzog’s Last Theorem
, both as a playful nod to Fermat’s Last Theorem, but also because it represents the last mathematical question he studied before his passing. We will explain the relation between Conjecture
A
and Conjecture
B
shortly thereafter.

In this note, we present the theory of cotangent functors
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)
following the approach of Palamodov
[
18
]
. Special attention is reserved for the case when
R
is a regular local ring,
S=R/I
is the quotient of
R
by an ideal
I\subset R
and
M=S
. Herzog’s last theorem is formulated and discussed in this frame.

The outlines of this note are as follows.

In Section
1
, we collect basic material on DG-algebras and the module of Kälher differentials. In Section
2
we present the construction of the cotangent functors due to Palamodov
[
18
]
. For the remaining part of the paper, we focus our attention on the following special situation. Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},K)
be a Noetherian local ring,
I\subset R
be a proper ideal,
S=R/I
and
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
be the canonical epimorphism.

In Section
3
we express the cotangent functors
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)
in terms of the homology of a certain complex
L
. This complex can be regarded as an approximation of a
S
-resolution of the conormal module
I/I^{2}
. In view of this observation and Conjecture
A
, Herzog was led to speculate that the vanishing of almost all cotangent functors
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)
is equivalent to the fact that
I
is a complete intersection. Partial progress towards Conjecture
B
was made by Herzog himself (Theorem
5.3
) under the extra assumption that all finitely generated
S
-modules have a rational Poincaré series. Additional results were contributed by Ulrich
[
21
]
.

1.
DG-algebras and the module of Kälher differentials

In this first section, we give a brief overview on DG-algebras and on the module of Kälher differentials.

1.1.
A glimpse to DG-algebras

Recall that a
differential graded algebra
, or in short a
DG-algebra
, is graded algebra
X=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}X_{k}
which is skew-symmetric together with a differential
\partial
of degree
-1
, satisfying the Leibniz rule:

\partial(ab)=\partial(a)b+(-1)^{\deg a}a\partial(b)

for all
a,b\in X
with
a
homogeneous.

An element
z\in X
such that
\partial(z)=0
is called a
cycle
. Given any DG-algebra
Y
and a cycle
z\in Y_{i}
, the DG-algebra
Y^{\prime}=Y[T:\partial(T)=z]
, which is obtained by adjoining
T
in order to kill the cycle
z
, is defined as follows.

We set
\deg T=i+1
. If
i
is even, then

Y_{j}^{\prime}=Y_{j}\oplus Y_{j-i-1}T\;\text{
for all $j$, }\;T^{2}=0\text{ and }\partial(T)=z,

and if
i
is odd, then for all
j
and
k
,

Y_{j}^{\prime}=Y_{j}\oplus Y_{j-(i+1)}T\oplus Y_{j-2(i+1)}T^{2}\oplus\cdots,\text{ $T^{j}T^{k}=T^{j+k}$ and $\partial(T^{j})=jzT^{j-1}$.}

With these definitions,
Y^{\prime}
is a well-defined DG-algebra and the inclusion
Y\hookrightarrow Y^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of DG-algebras.

For further details on DG-algebras we refer the reader to Avramov’s lectures
[
3
]
.

1.2.
A glimpse to the module of Kälher differentials

Let
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
be a ring homomorphism and let
M
be a
S
-module. Recall that a
derivation
, or more precisely an
R
-derivation into

M
is a map
D:S\rightarrow M
satisfying:

(i)

(Linearity)
D(rs)=rD(s)
for all
r\in R
and all
s\in S
.

(ii)

(Additivity)
D(s_{1}+s_{2})=D(s_{1})+D(s_{2})
for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
.

(iii)

(Leibniz rule)
D(s_{1}s_{2})=s_{1}D(s_{2})+s_{2}D(s_{1})
for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
.

Given two
R
-derivations
D,D^{\prime}
into
M
then
D+D^{\prime}:a\in S\mapsto D(a)+D^{\prime}(a)\in M
is an
R
-derivation, and for any
c\in S
the map
cD:a\in S\rightarrow cD(a)\in M
is also an
R
-derivation. Thus, the set of all
R
-derivations into
M
is a
S
-module, which we denote by
\textup{Der}_{R}(S,M)
.

Let
\textup{Mod}_{S}
be the category of
S
-modules. For any homomorphism of
S
-modules
\alpha:M\rightarrow N
and any
R
-derivation
D\in\textup{Der}_{R}(S,M)
, then
\alpha\circ D\in\textup{Der}_{R}(S,N)
is again an
R
-derivation. Hence, the map
M\in\textup{Mod}_{S}\mapsto\textup{Der}_{R}(S,M)
is a functor. This functor is
representable
. That is, there exist a
S
-module
\Omega_{S/R}
and a map
d:S\rightarrow\Omega_{S/R}
such that for any
M\in\textup{Mod}_{S}
and
D\in\textup{Der}_{R}(S,M)
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\alpha:\Omega_{S/R}\rightarrow M
making the following diagram commutative

\textstyle{S\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\scriptstyle{D}
\textstyle{\Omega_{S/R}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{M}

The
S
-module
\Omega_{S/R}
is called the
module of
(
Kähler
)
differentials
.

2.
The cotangent functors

Let
R
be a commutative ring which contains
\mathbb{Q}
as a subring. Assigned to a ring homomorphism
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
and a
S
-module
M
there is a sequence of
S
-modules
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)
, for
i\geq 0
, satisfying the following properties:

(i)

\textup{T}_{0}(S/R,M)\cong\Omega_{S/R}\otimes M
, where
\Omega_{S/R}
is the module of Kähler differentials.

(ii)

Given short exact sequence of
S
-modules
0\rightarrow M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}\rightarrow M_{3}\rightarrow 0
, there exists a long exact sequence

\cdots\to\textup{T}_{i+1}(S/R,M_{3})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{1})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{2})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{3})\rightarrow\cdots.

(iii)

(The Zariski sequence) For a sequence of ring homomorphisms
R\rightarrow S\rightarrow T
and a
T
-module
M
, there exists a long exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i+1}(T/S,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(T/R,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(T/S,M)\rightarrow\cdots.

We give a brief description of the construction of these functors, following the definition of V.P. Palamodov
[
18
]
. One first constructs a
resolvent
, sometimes also called a
Tate resolution
,
X
of
\varphi
, see
[
20
]
. The construction of
X
is obtained step by step, by adjoining variables using the procedure described in Section
1
.

Let
\Phi:P=R[\{Z_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]\rightarrow S
be a polynomial presentation with
\Phi_{|R}=\varphi
and
\Phi
an epimorphism. We construct a free acyclic DG-algebra
X
over
P
with
\textup{H}_{0}(X)\cong S
, which we call a resolvent of
\varphi
as described next.

We regard
P
as a DG-algebra and set
X^{(0)}=P
. Let
I\subset P
be the kernel of
\Phi
, and let
\{a_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}
be a system of generators of
I
. We adjoin variables

\{T_{1\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}
of degree
1
with
\partial(T_{1\lambda})=a_{\lambda}
for all

\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
to obtain the DG-algebra
X^{(1)}
.

In the next step we adjoin sufficiently many variables of degree
2
to
X^{(1)}
in order to kill a set of cycles whose homology classes generate
\textup{H}_{1}(X^{(1)})
, and we denote by
X^{(2)}
the resulting DG-algebra.

Proceeding in this way we obtain an ascending chain

X^{(0)}\subseteq X^{(1)}\subseteq X^{(2)}\subseteq\cdots

of DG-algebras. Then
X=\lim X^{(i)}
is a resolvent of
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
.

Note that the resolvent is of the form

X=P[\{T_{i\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{i},i\geq 0\}]\;\,\,\text{with\, $\deg T_{i\lambda}=i$\, for all\, $\lambda\in\Lambda_{i}$. }

(1)

It is clear that if
R
is Noetherian and
S
is a finitely generated
R
-algebra, then
P
may be chosen a polynomial ring over
R
in finitely many variables, and the resolvent may be chosen such that
|\Lambda_{i}|<\infty
for all
i
. Then
X
is a free
P
-module resolution of
S
with all
X_{i}
of finite rank.

Let
X
be a resolvent of
\varphi
. Recall that the module of
Kälher differentials

\Omega_{X/R}
of
X
over
R
is an
X
-module together with a
R
-linear derivation
d:X\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}
such that the usual universal properties hold. Since
\Omega_{X/R}
is an
X
-module, it is a complex and it is required that the differential
\partial:\Omega_{X/R}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}
satisfies the rule

\partial(av)=\partial(a)v+(-1)^{\deg(a)}a\partial(v)

for all homogeneous
a\in X
and all
v\in\Omega_{X/R}
and commutes with the derivation, that is,
\partial d=d\partial
. This is always possible. Indeed, we can define the module of differentials
\Omega_{X/R}
of
X
over
R
to be the free
X
-module

\ \Omega_{X/R}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}XdZ_{\lambda}\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}XdT_{1\lambda}\oplus\cdots,

the map
\partial:\Omega_{X/R}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}
to be defined by the formula
\partial(d(T))=d(\partial(T))
, and the map
d:X\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}
is uniquely determined by the requirement that it is
R
-linear and that
d(ab)=d(a)b+(-1)^{\deg a}ad(b)
holds for all
a,b\in X
with
a
homogeneous.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
be a ring homomorphism,
M
a
S
-module and
X
a resolvent of
\varphi
. Then the homology of the complex
\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M
is independent of the choices made in the construction of
X
.

For the proof of this theorem it is essential that
\mathbb{Q}\subset R
. A complete proof of this result was given by Bingener and Kosarev, in the Kapitel III of the book
[
5
]
.

Theorem
2.1
allows us to define

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)=\textup{H}_{i}(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M)

for
i\geq 0
, where
X
is any resolvent of
\varphi
. Here we used that
M
is an
X
-module via the augmentation map
X\rightarrow\textup{H}_{0}(X)=S
.

We sketch the proofs of the properties (i), (ii) and (iii).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
be a ring homomorphism and let
M
be a
S
-module. The following statements hold.

(i)

\textup{T}_{0}(S/R,M)\cong\Omega_{S/R}\otimes M
.

(ii)

Given short exact sequence of
S
-modules
0\rightarrow M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}\rightarrow M_{3}\rightarrow 0
, there exists a long exact sequence

\cdots\to\textup{T}_{i+1}(S/R,M_{3})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{1})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{2})\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M_{3})\rightarrow\cdots.

(iii)

Given a sequence of ring homomorphisms
R\rightarrow S\rightarrow T
and a
T
-module
M
, there exists a long exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i+1}(T/S,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(T/R,M)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(T/S,M)\rightarrow\cdots.

Proof.

We have
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)=\textup{H}_{i}((\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S)\otimes_{S}M)
for all
i
, and

(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S,\partial):\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial}\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}SdT_{1\lambda}\xrightarrow{\partial}\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SdZ_{\lambda}\rightarrow 0.

Since the derivation
d

commutes with
\partial
, it follows that for each
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
we have

\partial(dT_{1\lambda})=d(\partial T_{1\lambda})=d(a_{\lambda})=\bigoplus_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\frac{\partial a_{\lambda}}{\partial Z_{\lambda^{\prime}}}dZ_{\lambda^{\prime}}\quad\mod I.

This shows that
\textup{H}_{0}(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{R}S)=\Omega_{S/R}
and
\textup{H}_{0}(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M)=\Omega_{S/R}\otimes_{S}M
.

(ii) Taking homology of the short exact sequence of complexes

0\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M_{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}M_{3}\rightarrow 0,

we obtain the long exact sequence in (ii).

(iii) Let
X_{S/R}
,
X_{T/S}
and
X_{T/R}
be resolvents of
R\rightarrow S
,
S\rightarrow T
and the composition
R\rightarrow S\rightarrow T
, respectively. It can be seen that if one makes the appropriate choices in the construction of these resolvents, we have a short exact sequence of complexes

0\rightarrow\Omega_{X_{S/R}}\otimes T\rightarrow\Omega_{X_{T/R}}\otimes T\rightarrow\Omega_{X_{T/S}}\otimes T\rightarrow 0.

Tensoring this sequence with the module
M
, and taking the long exact sequence induced by homology yields the assertion.

∎

3.
Cotangent functors of quotient rings

Now we will focus our attention to the following situation:
(R,\mathfrak{m},K)
is a Noetherian local ring,
I\subset R
is a proper ideal,
S=R/I
and
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
is the canonical epimorphism. In this case the resolvent
X
of
\varphi
can be chosen such that
X_{0}=R
and that
X_{i}
is a free
R
-module for all
i\geq 0
. Since
X
is acyclic with
\textup{H}_{0}(X)=S
, it follows that
X
is a free
R
-resolution of
S
.

For a finitely generated
S
-module
N
, let
\mu(N)
be the minimum cardinality of a set of generators of
N
. Notice that, by construction,
X^{(i)}
is the subalgebra of
X
generated by all elements of degree
\leq i
. For each
i
we have
X^{(i)}=X^{(i-1)}[T_{i1},\ldots,T_{ie_{i}}]
, where the elements
\partial T_{ij}
are the cycles whose homology classes generate
\textup{H}_{i-1}(X^{(i-1)})
. We say that
X
is a
minimal resolvent
of
\varphi
if
e_{i}=\mu(\textup{H}_{i-1}(X^{(i-1)}))
for all
i\geq 1
.

Let
\widetilde{\varphi}:X\rightarrow S
be the augmentation homomorphism, and let
\widetilde{I}=\textup{Ker}(\widetilde{\varphi})
. Then
\widetilde{I}
is an exact subcomplex of
X
with
\widetilde{I}_{0}=I
and
\widetilde{I}_{i}=X_{i}
for
i\geq 1
. Since
\widetilde{I}
is an ideal of
X
, we may consider
\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}
, which is a complex of
S
-modules with

(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})_{i}=\begin{cases}\,I/I^{2}&\text{if}\ i=0,\\
(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})_{i}\otimes_{R}S&\text{if}\ i>0.\end{cases}

(2)

Then
\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}
is called the
cotangent complex
of
\varphi
(with respect to the resolvent
X
).

Note that for all
i>0
,
(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})_{i}=(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})_{i}\otimes_{R}S=ST_{i1}+ST_{i2}+\cdots+ST_{ie_{i}}
is free
S
-module, which is isomorphic to
\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})
.
We have

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a natural exact sequence of complexes

0\rightarrow I/I^{2}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S\rightarrow 0

of
S
-modules, where
I/I^{2}
is considered to be a complex concentrated in degree
0
.

Proof.

The derivation
d:\widetilde{I}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{R}S
is a complex homomorphism, since
d
commutes with
\partial
, and it vanishes on the subcomplex
\widetilde{I}^{2}
, because of the product rule for
d
. Therefore,
d
induces a natural map of complexes
\alpha:\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{R}S
.

Since
(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})_{0}=I/I^{2}
and
(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{R}S)_{0}=0
, it follows that
\textup{Ker}(\alpha_{0})=I/I^{2}
. On the other hand, for
i>0
, we have

\alpha_{i}:(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})_{i}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{e_{i}}ST_{ij}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{e_{i}}SdT_{ij}=(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{R}S)_{i},\quad T_{ij}\mapsto dT_{ij}.

This shows that
\alpha_{i}
is an isomorphism for
i>0
, and proves the lemma.

∎

Let
L
be the cokernel of
I/I^{2}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}
. Then
L_{0}=0
,
L_{i}=(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})_{i}
for
i\geq 1
and consequently
L\cong\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S
. Therefore, for any
S
-module
M
we have

\textup{H}_{i}(L\otimes_{S}M)\cong\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,M)\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 1.

(3)

In particular,
\textup{T}_{0}(S/R,M)=0
and
\textup{T}_{1}(S/R,M)=I/I^{2}\otimes_{S}M
.

Corollary 3.2
.

The homology of
\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}
does not depend on the chosen resolvent
X
.

Proof.

The short exact sequence given in Lemma
3.1
induces the long exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(I/I^{2})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S)\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i-1}(I/I^{2})\rightarrow\cdots.

Since
I/I^{2}
is a complex concentrated in degree zero, we then obtain that
\textup{H}_{i}(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2})\cong\textup{H}_{i}(\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S)=T_{i}(S/R,S)
. Theorem
2.1
yields the assertion.

∎

For all
i>0
, the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow X^{(i-1)}\rightarrow X^{(i)}\rightarrow X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)}\rightarrow 0

(4)

induces the exact sequence

\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i)})\xrightarrow{\eta_{i}}\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})\xrightarrow{\sigma_{i-1}}\textup{H}_{i-1}(X^{(i-1)})\rightarrow 0,

where

The map
\beta
is surjective because

\alpha

\sigma_{i}^{-1}(\textup{Ker}(\eta_{i}))/\textup{Im}(\eta_{i+1})\cong\textup{Ker}(\eta_{i}),

Corollary 3.4
.

\textup{T}_{i+1}(S/R,S)\cong\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i-1)})
for all
i\geq 3
.

Proof.

The short exact sequence (
4
) induces the long exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i+1}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i-1)})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i)})\xrightarrow{\eta_{i}}\cdots.

By Lemma
3.3
we have

\textup{T}_{i+1}(S/R,S)\cong\textup{Ker}(\eta_{i})
for all
i>0
. We claim that
\textup{H}_{i+1}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})=0
for all
i\geq 3
. Then the map
\psi:\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i-1)})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i)})
is injective and so
\textup{T}_{i+1}(S/R,S)\cong\textup{Ker}(\eta_{i})=\textup{Im}(\psi)\cong\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(i-1)})
, as desired.

It remains to show that
\textup{H}_{i+1}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})=0
for all
i\geq 3
. We even claim that
\textup{H}_{j}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})=0
for all
i<j<2i-1
. Indeed, since
X^{(i)}=X^{(i-1)}[T_{i1},\ldots,T_{ie_{i}}]
we have
(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})_{j}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{e_{i}}(X^{(i-1)})_{j-i}T_{ij}
for all integers
i<j<2i-1
. Hence
\textup{H}_{j}(X^{(i)}/X^{(i-1)})=0
for all
i<j<2i-1
, since
(X^{(i)})_{j-i}=0
in this range.

∎

4.
Koszul homology,
\textup{Tor}(S,S)
and the first Cotangent modules

The complex
\cdots\rightarrow\widetilde{I}_{3}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}_{2}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}_{1}\rightarrow 0
is a
R
-free resolution of
I
. This implies that
\textup{H}_{0}(\widetilde{I}/I\widetilde{I})=0
and
\textup{H}_{i}(\widetilde{I}/I\widetilde{I})\cong\textup{Tor}_{i-1}^{R}(S,I)\cong\textup{Tor}_{i}^{R}(S,S)
for
i>0
.

Corollary
3.4
together with the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}/I\widetilde{I}\rightarrow\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}\rightarrow 0

yields the long exact sequence

\displaystyle\cdots\rightarrow\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})\rightarrow\textup{Tor}_{2}^{R}(S,S)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{2}(S/R,S)\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\cdots\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})\rightarrow\textup{Tor}_{i}^{R}(S,S)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})\to\cdots

Here we also used that
\textup{H}_{1}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})=0
, which is the case simply because
(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{1}=0
.

We can also compute
\textup{H}_{2}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})
. Indeed, since
(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{1}=0
, then
\textup{H}_{2}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})
is the cokernel of the natural induced map

(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{3}\xrightarrow{\overline{\partial}}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{2}.

Note that

(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{2}=(IX_{2}+X_{1}^{2})/IX_{2}\cong X_{1}^{2}/IX_{2}\cap X_{1}^{2}=X_{1}^{2}/IX_{1}^{2}=\bigoplus_{i<j}ST_{1i}T_{1j}.

Similarly one shows that

(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})_{3}\cong\bigoplus_{i,j}ST_{1i}T_{2j}\oplus\bigoplus_{i<j<k}ST_{1i}T_{1j}T_{1k}.

Thus we obtain the exact sequence

V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\xrightarrow{\overline{\partial}}\bigoplus_{i<j}ST_{1i}T_{1j}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})\rightarrow 0,

where
V_{1}=\bigoplus_{i,j}ST_{1i}T_{2j}
,
V_{2}=\bigoplus_{i<j<k}ST_{1i}T_{1j}T_{1k}
, and where
\overline{\partial}(V_{2})=0
and
\overline{\partial}(T_{1i}T_{2j})=T_{1i}\partial(T_{2j})\mod I
. From this it follows that

\textup{H}_{2}(\widetilde{I}^{2}/I\widetilde{I})\cong\bigwedge^{2}I/I^{2}\cong\bigwedge^{2}\textup{Tor}_{2}(S,S).

As a consequence of this discussion we obtain

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists and exact sequence

\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\bigwedge^{2}I/I^{2}\rightarrow\textup{Tor}_{2}^{R}(S,S)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{2}(S/R,S)\rightarrow 0.

Thus, together with Corollary
4.4
we have

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
\textup{H}_{1}^{2}=\textup{H}_{2}
and that
\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)=0
. Then

\textup{Tor}_{2}^{R}(S,S)\cong\bigwedge^{2}I/I^{2}.

Let
X
be a minimal resolvent of
I
. Then
X^{(1)}
is the Koszul complex for a minimal set of generators of
I
. We will simply write
\textup{H}_{i}=\textup{H}_{i}(X^{(1)})
for the Koszul homology.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists an exact sequence

\textup{H}_{3}\rightarrow\textup{T}_{4}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}\rightarrow\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

The short exact sequence
0\rightarrow X^{(1)}\rightarrow X^{(2)}\rightarrow X^{(2)}/X^{(1)}\rightarrow 0
yields the long exact sequence

\textup{H}_{3}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)})\xrightarrow{\eta_{3}}\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)}/X^{(1)})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}(X^{(2)})\xrightarrow{\eta_{2}}\cdots

By Corollary
3.4
we have the isomorphism
\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)})\cong T_{4}(S/R,S)
, and by Lemma
3.3
we have
\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\cong\textup{Ker}(\eta_{2})=\textup{Im}(\textup{H}_{2}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}(X^{(2)}))
. Thus, we obtain the exact sequence

\textup{H}_{3}\rightarrow T_{4}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)}/X^{(1)})\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2}\rightarrow\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\rightarrow 0.

It remains to be shown that
\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)}/X^{(1)})\cong\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}
. Indeed,
\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)}/X^{(1)})
is the homology of the complex

\bigoplus_{j}(X^{(1)})_{2}T_{2j}\oplus\bigoplus_{i<j}RT_{2i}T_{2j}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{j}(X^{(1)})_{1}T_{2j}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{j}RT_{2j}

It follows that

\textup{H}_{3}(X^{(2)}/X^{(1)})\cong(\bigoplus_{j}\textup{H}_{1}T_{j})/U,

where
U
is the module generated by the elements
[\partial T_{2i}]T_{j}+T_{2i}[\partial T_{2j}]
with
i<j
. This yields the desired conclusion.

∎

Since the image of
\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}
in
\textup{H}_{2}
is just
\textup{H}_{1}^{2}
, we obtain

Corollary 4.4
.

\textup{T}_{3}(S/R,S)\cong\textup{H}_{2}/\textup{H}_{1}^{2}
.

Let
R
be a local ring and
I\subset R
be an ideal. We say that
I
is a
complete intersection
if
\textup{height}(I)=\mu(I)
. If
\mu(I)=\textup{height}(I)+1
we say that
I
is an
almost complete intersection
. If
R
is Cohen-Macaulay, then
I
is a complete intersection if and only
I
is generated by a regular sequence.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
R
be a regular local ring, and
I
be a perfect ideal such that either

(i)

\textup{height}(I)=2
and I is generically a complete intersection, or

(ii)

I
is generated by at most
\textup{height}(I)+1
many elements.

Then
\textup{T}_{4}(S/R,S)=0
if and only if
I
is a complete intersection, where
S=R/I
.

Proof.

Since all
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
if
I
is a complete intersection, we only need to show that under the given conditions
\textup{T}_{4}(S/R,S)\neq 0
, if
I
is not a complete intersection.

(i) Suppose that
I
is generated by
n+1
elements. We may assume that
n>2
, otherwise we are in the case (ii). It is shown in
[
4
, Theorem (2.1), (g)(ii)]
that
\textup{Ker}(\bigwedge^{i}\textup{H}_{1}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{i})\neq 0
for
i=2,\ldots,n-1
. Since
n>2
, then
\textup{Ker}(\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2})\neq 0
. By Proposition
4.3
, we have

\textup{Im}(\textup{T}_{4}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1})=\textup{Ker}(\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}\rightarrow\textup{H}_{2})\neq 0,

and this implies the assertion.

(ii) Since
I
is not a complete intersection, we have
\mu(I)=\textup{height}(I)+1
. In other words,
I
is an almost complete intersection. This implies that
\textup{H}_{i}=0
for
i\geq 2
, and so by Proposition
4.3
, the map
\textup{T}_{4}(S/R,S)\rightarrow\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}
is surjective. So, it suffices to show that
\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}\neq 0
. Note that
\textup{H}_{1}
is isomorphic the canonical module
\omega_{S}
. By a theorem of Kunz
[
12
, Corollary 1.2]
, an almost complete intersection is never Gorenstein. Hence,
\mu(\textup{H}_{1})>1
. Since
R
is local, this implies that
\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}_{1}\neq 0
.

∎

5.
Herzog’s Last Theorem

Let
L=\textup{Coker}(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S)
be the complex introduced in Section
3
, where
R
is a Noetherian local ring,
I\subset R
is an ideal,
S=R/I
, the map
\varphi:R\rightarrow S
is the canonical epimorphism and
X
is a resolvent of
\varphi
.

By equation (
3
) we have
\textup{H}_{i}(L)\cong\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)
for all
i\geq 1
. Since in particular
\textup{H}_{1}(L)\cong\textup{T}_{1}(S/R,S)=I/I^{2}\otimes_{S}S\cong I/I^{2}
we can regard the complex
L
as an approximation of a resolution of
I/I^{2}
. In view of Vasconcelos Conjecture
B
on the conormal module (now a Theorem of Briggs
[
6
, Theorem A]
) and several evidence, in 1981 Jürgen Herzog posed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.1
.

(Herzog’s Last Theorem).
Let
R
be a regular local ring,
I\subset R
a proper ideal and
S=R/I
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

I
is a complete intersection.

(b)

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for all
i>1
.

(c)

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for all
i\gg 1
.

We would like to refer to this conjecture as
Herzog’s Last Theorem
both as a pun to the famous Fermat’s Last Theorem and also because despite the fact that this question was posed in 1981, it is the last mathematical statement that was being studied by Professor Herzog at the end of his life.

Conjecture
5.1
is widely open. Among other nice results Bernd Ulrich confirmed in
[
21
]
the equivalence of (a) and (b) in the following cases:

(i)

(Theorem 2.20)
I
belongs to the linkage class of a complete intersection.

(ii)

(Proposition 2.38)
I
is a perfect Gorenstein ideal of grade
3
and deviation
2
.

Theorem 2.20 contains a few other interesting equivalent conditions for
I
to be a complete intersection. Note that Corollary
4.5
(i) is a special case of Theorem 2.20. Other results in
[
21
]
only require that
R
is Gorenstein. The reader is advised to consult this paper for details.

It is obvious that (a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
\Rightarrow
(c). We conclude this section, by presenting a class of ideals for which (c)
\Rightarrow
(a).

Let
(S,\mathfrak{m},K)
be a Noetherian local ring and
M
be a finitely generated
S
-module. Then
\textup{Tor}_{i}(S/\mathfrak{m},M)
is a finitely generated
K
-vector space, and one calls the number
\beta_{i}(M)=\dim_{K}\textup{Tor}_{i}(S/\mathfrak{m},M)
the
i
th Betti number
of
M
and the formal series
P_{M}(t)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\beta_{i}(M)t^{i}
the
Poincaré series
of
M
.

A formal series
P(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
is called
rational
, if it can be written as fraction
A(t)/B(t)
, where
A(t)
and
B(t)
are polynomials belonging to
\mathbb{Q}[t]
.

For a longer time before the 80ties it was expected that all Poincaré series are rational. But in 1980, Anick
[
2
]
found a counterexample. On the other hand, for a given ring in many cases
P_{M}(t)
happens to be a rational function for all modules
M
over this ring. For example this is the case for
Golod
rings.

For the proof of the last result, we need the following theorem of Mahler
[
15
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
A(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
be a rational formal series. There exists an integer
r>0
and a subset
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{g}\}
of
\{0,\dots,r-1\}
such that for all
i\gg 0
we have
\alpha_{i}=0
if and only if
i\equiv r_{i}\,(\textup{mod}\,r)
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,g\}
.

The following result due to Herzog
[
9
, Theorem (4.15)]
supports Conjecture
5.1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
R
be a regular local ring,
I\subset R
a proper ideal and
S=R/I
. Assume that all finitely generated
S
-modules have a rational Poincaré series. Then for the ideal
I
, Conjecture
5.1
is correct.

Proof.

If
I
is a complete intersection, then the Koszul complex on a minimal set of generators of
I
is the resolvent of
R\rightarrow S
. Therefore
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for all
i>1
. This proves that (a)
\Rightarrow
(b). The implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(c) is trivial.

For the proof of (c)
\Rightarrow
(a), we choose a minimal resolvent
X
of the map
R\rightarrow S
. Let
L=\textup{Coker}(\widetilde{I}/\widetilde{I}^{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{X/R}\otimes_{X}S)
. Then
\partial(L)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}L
, and this implies that

\textup{rank}_{S}L_{i}=\dim_{K}\textup{H}_{i}(L\otimes K)=\dim_{K}\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,K).

The sequence of ring homomorphisms
R\rightarrow S\rightarrow K
induces the long exact Zariski sequence

\cdots\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,K)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(K/R,K)\rightarrow\textup{T}_{i}(K/S,K)\rightarrow\cdots.

Since
R
is regular, the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
is generated by a regular sequence,
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong K
and so we have
\textup{T}_{i}(K/R,K)=0
for all
i>0
. Therefore,

\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,K)\cong\textup{T}_{i+1}(K/S,K)\quad\text{for all}\quad i\geq 1.

To compute
\textup{T}_{i+1}(K/S,K)
we choose a minimal resolvent
Y
for the residue class map
S\rightarrow K
. By a theorem of Gulliksen
[
7
, Page 55]
,
Y
is a minimal free
S
-resolution of
K
. For each
i\geq 1
, we set
\varepsilon_{i}=\dim_{K}(Y^{(i)}/Y^{(i-1)})_{i}\otimes_{S}K
. The integer
\varepsilon_{i}
is called the
i
th deviation
of
S
. Our discussion together with formula (
2
) show that

\textup{rank}_{S}L_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 1.

By assumption, there exists an integer
i_{0}>1
such that
\textup{T}_{i}(S/R,S)=0
for
i\geq i_{0}
. Let
M=\textup{Coker}(L_{i_{0}}\rightarrow L_{i_{0}-1})
, then

\cdots\rightarrow L_{i_{0}}\rightarrow L_{i_{0}-1}\rightarrow M\rightarrow 0

is a minimal free
S
-resolution of
M
. Thus,
P_{M}(t)=\sum_{i\geq i_{0}}\varepsilon_{i}t^{i}
.

We may assume that
\varepsilon_{i}>0
for all
i\geq i_{0}
. Indeed, assume that
\varepsilon_{i}=0
for some
i\geq i_{0}
. Then
\varepsilon_{j}=0
for all
j\geq i
. Indeed, in this case,

0\rightarrow L_{i-1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow L_{i_{0}}\rightarrow L_{i_{0}-1}\rightarrow M\rightarrow 0

would be a minimal free
S
-resolution of
M
. But then the vanishing of the
\varepsilon_{j}
for all
j\geq i
would imply that
I
is a complete intersection by a theorem of Gulliksen
[
8
]
.

Hence, for the rest of the proof we assume that
\varepsilon_{i}>0
for all
i\geq i_{0}
.

Let
F(t)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}t^{i}
. By assumption,
P_{M}(t)
is a rational, and hence
F(t)
is rational as well. Also by our assumption,
P_{K}(t)
is rational. Assume that
I
is not a complete intersection. We will show that in this case
F(t)
and
P_{K}(t)
cannot be rational at the same time. This will lead to the desired contradiction.

Since
Y
is a minimal resolvent of
S\rightarrow K
, it follows from
[
3
, Remark 7.1.1]
(or also
[
7
, Corollary 1]
) that we can write

P_{K}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1+t^{2i-1})^{\varepsilon_{2i-1}}}{(1-t^{2i})^{\varepsilon_{2i}}}.

We denote by
\log
the
logarithmic derivative
. That is,
\log g=g^{\prime}/g
for any
g\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
. For polynomials
f,g\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
and
a\in\mathbb{Q}
, the following three elementary rules hold:

(i)

\log(fg)=\log(f)+\log(g)
,

(ii)

\log(\frac{f}{g})=\log(f)-\log(g)
,

(iii)

\log(f^{a})=a\log(f)
.

Using the properties (i), (ii) and (iii) we see that

\displaystyle\log P_{k}(-t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}[\varepsilon_{2i-1}\log(1+(-1)^{2i-1}t^{2i-1})-\varepsilon_{2i}\log(1-(-1)^{2i}t^{2i})]

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}[-\frac{(2i-1)\varepsilon_{2i-1}t^{2i-2}}{1-t^{2i-1}}+\frac{(2i)\varepsilon_{2i}t^{2i}}{1-t^{2i}}]

\displaystyle=\ -\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{1-t}+\frac{2\varepsilon_{2}t}{1-t^{2}}-\frac{3\varepsilon_{3}t^{2}}{1-t^{3}}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i+1}(i+1)\varepsilon_{i+1}t^{i}\frac{1}{1-t^{i+1}}.

Now, we use that
1/(1-t^{i+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}t^{(i+1)j}
to obtain

\displaystyle\log P_{k}(-t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i+1}(i+1)\varepsilon_{i+1}t^{i}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}t^{(i+1)j})

\displaystyle=\ -\varepsilon_{1}+(-\varepsilon_{1}+2\varepsilon_{2})t+(-\varepsilon_{1}-3\varepsilon_{3})t^{2}+(-\varepsilon_{1}+2\varepsilon_{2}+4\varepsilon_{4})t^{3}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\sum_{j\ \textup{divides}\ i+1}(-1)^{j}j\varepsilon_{j})t^{i}.

Since
P_{k}(t)
is rational by assumption, it follows that
\log P_{k}(-t)
is also rational. Therefore, the power series
A(t)=(\log P_{k}(-t)+F^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{1}t^{i})t
is again rational, and if we put
A(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}t^{i}
, we see immediately that

\alpha_{i}\ =\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\ \textup{divides}\ i\\
j\neq 1,j\neq i\end{subarray}}(-1)^{j}j\varepsilon_{j}\quad\textup{for all odd integers}\ i.

Since
\varepsilon_{i}>0
for all
i\geq i_{0}
, it follows that for all odd integers
i\geq i_{0}
we have
\alpha_{i}=0
whenever
i
is a prime number, and
\alpha_{i}<0
if
i
is not a prime, because the integers dividing an odd number are again odd. Since
A(t)
is rational, this strange behavior of the
\alpha_{i}
contradicts the Theorem
5.2
of Mahler. The conclusion follows.

∎
