Pharmacovigilance analysis of myopathy associated with azoles and nonstatins interactions based on US FAERS database
Authors: Shen’ao Jing, Xue Zhang, Ying Zhang, Nan’nan Sun, Xiang’dong Du, Xuan Wang, Xin Huang, Xia Zhao, Yi Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAzoles are first-line antifungal agents known to increase the risk of statin-related myopathy due to drug interactions. However, myopathy events have also been observed in patients using azoles without concurrent statin use, suggesting that azoles may be linked to an increased risk of non-statin-related myopathy. Based on data from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), this study aimed to determine whether azoles are associated with a disproportionately high incidence of myopathy after excluding statin-related reports and to explore the potential contribution of interactions between azoles and nonstatins. We used Open Vigil 2.1 to conduct a disproportionality analysis based on data from the FAERS from Q1 2004 to Q2 2023. We excluded reports with at least one statin. Myopathy was defined based on a Standardized MedDRA Query (SMQ). Five azoles were screened. The reporting odds ratio (ROR) and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) information components (ICs) were used for disproportionality analysis. Subset analysis and the Ω shrinkage measure model were used to detect drug‒drug interaction (DDI) signals. Sensitivity analysis was also conducted. In the nonstatin dataset, we identified 215 cases of myopathy associated with azole use. Notably, 50.5% of the reports involved voriconazole among East Asian patients. Voriconazole, fluconazole, and itraconazole were associated with myopathy in narrow and broad SMQ searches. Posaconazole and isavuconazole were associated with myopathy in the broad SMQ search. Forty-two potential DDIs between azoles and nonstatins were identified. A literature review identified 12 related cases. Azoles, particularly voriconazole in East Asian populations, are associated with an increased reporting frequency of myopathy events in the absence of statin use. Introduction

Azoles exhibit broad-spectrum and potent antifungal activities and are thus considered first-line options for the prevention and treatment of invasive fungal infections1. However, azoles exert their antifungal effects by binding to fungal cytochrome P450; therefore, they can also interact with mammalian cytochrome P450 enzymes, potentially resulting in drug‒drug interactions (DDIs)2,15. Azoles are known to increase the risks of various adverse events, such as phototoxicity, periostitis, hallucinations, encephalopathy, peripheral neuropathy, alopecia, nail abnormalities, hepatotoxicity, hormone-related disturbances, and other adverse reactions. These adverse events may, in turn, increase the costs of treatment, promote drug resistance, and increase mortality risk3.

The severity of drug-induced myopathy ranges from asymptomatic elevations in serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels to mild-to-severe myalgias, cramps, exercise intolerance, and varying degrees of muscle weakness. In severe cases of myopathy, rhabdomyolysis may occur, which can lead to myoglobinuria and, in turn, increase the risks of renal failure and mortality4. The use of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors—commonly known as statins—has been widely associated with the development of myopathy, which can progress to rhabdomyolysis5,6. Approximately 50% of the rhabdomyolysis and myopathy cases reported in the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database list statins as the suspected primary cause7.

Azole and statin drug labels warn of increased myotoxicity risk due to a known drug interaction, likely caused by azoles inhibiting CYP enzymes involved in statin metabolism. However, azole-related rhabdomyolysis and myopathy have also been reported independently of statin using8. In some instances, supratherapeutic serum azole levels have been implicated as potential causes9–11. These cases may be due to the use of azoles in combination with certain nonstatins may result in a multitude of DDIs. Azoles inhibit the CYP450 enzyme system and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) efflux, yet are also metabolized by CYP450 enzymes. For example, fluconazole, which is primarily excreted in the urine, inhibits metabolic enzymes such as CYP3A4/5, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19; itraconazole serves as both a substrate and an inhibitor of CYP3A4/5, uridine 5’-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), and P-gp; posaconazole is primarily metabolized by UGT enzymes and is a CYP3A4 inhibitor; and voriconazole is primarily metabolized by CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4, and is a CYP3A4 inhibitor; isavuconazole is a substrate of CYP3A4/5 and CYP2B6, an inducer of CYP2B6, and an inhibitor of CYP3A4/5 and P-glycoprotein (P-gp)12. When DDIs lead to increased serum concentrations of azoles or other drugs that have myotoxic effects, the risks of rhabdomyolysis and myopathy in patients may increase. However, few studies13–16 have examined the effect of DDIs between azoles and nonstatins on the risk of myopathy.

The FAERS is a voluntary adverse event (AE) repository encompassing data from the U.S. as well as international data; this database is designed to enhance the post marketing safety surveillance of all FDA-approved drugs and therapeutic biological products17. Additionally, this database facilitates data mining via disproportionality analyses to detect DDI-induced AEs. The FAERS comprises adverse event reports submitted to the FDA by manufacturers, consumers, healthcare professionals, and other reporters. Each report classifies the role of drugs mentioned in the AE as follows: primary suspect (PS), secondary suspect (SS), interaction (I), or concomitant (C). All reported AEs are mapped to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA®) terminology. Open Vigil 2.1 (https://openvigil.sourceforge.net/) is a tool for extracting, cleaning, mining, and analyzing AE data from the FAERS database18,19. It maps various drug names (brand, generic, abbreviations, etc.) to a unique identifier via the Drugs@FDA and DrugBank. In Open Vigil 2.1, AEs are encoded via the preferred term (PT) from MedDRA.

The aim of this study was to utilize the FAERS database using Open Vigil 2.1 to determine the effects of azoles alone and DDIs between azoles and nonstatins on the risk of myopathy. A literature review was performed to identify published reports of myopathy associated with azoles in the absence of statin use.

Methods

Source and adverse event definition

This study employed disproportionality analysis on individual case safety reports from the FAERS database using. The FAERS dataset spans from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2023. Myopathy was defined with the narrow SMQ (20,000,020) because it offers higher specificity and reduces contamination from non-myopathic muscle disorders. The MedDRA® PTs in the SMQ narrow20 and broad search used to define myopathy are reported in Table S1 of the ESM. Noncases were defined as reports without the PTs related to rhabdomyolysis/myopathy. Reports containing multiple events meeting the criteria were not double counted. We targeted five azoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazole/isavuconazonium) and ten statins (acetyl-simvastatin/simvastatin, atorvastatin/atorvastatin calcium, cerivastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, mevastatin, pitavastatin, rosuvastatin/rosuvastatin calcium, pravastatin, or tenivastatin). Via OpenVigil 2.1, we extracted myopathy-related AE reports for each azole and all the statins, applying the query “(azole) AND NOT (statins)” and subtracted the corresponding statin-counts from the background denominator to generate a non-statin dataset for local analysis. OpenVigil 2.1 automatically retained only the most recent ISR per case, yielding a deduplicated dataset.

Literature review

In parallel with the search of the FAERS database, previously published case reports were reviewed to identify myopathy associated with the use of azoles without the concomitant use of statins. The PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI and Wanfang databases were searched in June 1, 2025. A causality assessment using the Drug Interaction Probability Scale was performed for the identified cases21.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analyses of the the basic information and outcomes of patients are presented as counts (N) and percentages for categorical variables (N%).

(2)Disproportionality analysis

Disproportionality analysis

The disproportionality analysis based on differences in reporting portion of AEs. We applied two standard disproportionality analyses methods in the nonstatin dataset: the reporting odds ratio (ROR)22 and the moderate Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN)23 of information components (ICs)24. We calculated the lower limits of the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the ROR (ROR025) and IC (IC025) of the azoles in the nonstatin dataset, and we employed statistical shrinkage transformation to ensure conservative result of IC02525,26. Significance was defined as ROR025 > 1 and IC025 > 0, with at least 4 cases27,28. These thresholds follow standard disproportionality guideline29,30. The calculation procedure for the ROR and IC can be referenced in Table S2 of the ESM.

(3)Drug-drug interaction analysis

Drug-drug interaction analysis

A DDI analysis were conducted between azoles (D1) and nonstatins (D2) in the nonstatin-containing dataset, by using a newly subset analysis method and Ω shrinkage measure. We created subsets of the patient group using D1 or D2, where either drug could play any role (PS, SS, I, or C). A newly subset analysis17 was conducted to calculate the ROR and the lower limit of the 95% CI for myopathy caused by D1 (denoted as and ) and D2 (denoted as and ) when taken jointly. The underlying assumption is that a true DDI signal requires the combination to yield an > 1 for both drugs, indicating an effect beyond the simple additive risk of either drug alone. Additionally, to test the consistency of the findings, we also employed the World Health Organization-endorsed Ω shrinkage measure model31 to calculate Ω025 as the conservative threshold for DDI signal analysis. This model compares the observed-to-expected reporting ratio for the D1-D2 drug pair and AE. The detection criterion is Ω025 > 0. The calculation methodology of the newly subset analysis and Ω shrinkage measure model were elucidated in Table S3 of ESM. As an earlier study suggests that the newly subset analysis may be detecting signals that the Ω shrinkage model has failed to detect and as the number of reports increased, both of the methods became more accurate17. Therefore, DDI signals were flagged when ≥ 4 reports with D1 and D2 were available and either (i) the for both drugs exceeded 1 or (ii) Ω > 0. A signal was considered strong only if, in DDI signals, both the for both drugs exceeded 2 and Ω > 0.

All analyses were performed via Microsoft EXCEL 2019. This study was reported in accordance with the requirements of the Reporting of a Disproportionality Analysis for Drug Safety Signal Detection Using Individual Case Safety Reports in Pharmacovigilance (READUS-PV) checklist32. Table S4 of the ESM shows the checklist for abstracts, and Table S5 of the EMS shows the checklist for main texts.

Sensitivity analyses

In the sensitivity analysis, a broad SMQ was initially utilized to identify myopathy, aiming to verify the non-statin-related myotoxicity of azoles.

Then, we adjusted on the basis of the role of D1 or D2 in each report and the number of drug pairs identified in each report. For an AE, assuming that the original ROR of D1 was 1 and that there were positive reports related to drug pair D1-D2, the increase in the ROR per positive report, compared with D1, was . In the i-th positive report related to the drug pair D1-D2, there were positive drug pairs detected, and each pair was potentially equally responsible for the AE. Therefore, the adjusted increase in the ROR for the drug pair D1-D2 in this report was calculated by multiplying the increased ROR of this report by . If D1 or D2 was reported as merely a concomitant drug (C) in a positive report, their weights were reduced to 0.5. The adjusted ROR of the D1-D2 drug pair relative to D1, denoted as , was the original ROR of D1 plus the sum of the adjusted increased RORs of the drug pair D1-D2 across each positive report involving that drug pair:

Additionally, for each D1–D2 pair screened out for a DDI-induced myopathy signal, the ROR (ROR025) and IC (IC025) of D2 were calculated (Table S6 of the ESM). Concurrently, we searched DrugBank to identify any known interactions between D1 and D2.

We used a positive-control design in which we repeated our main analysis using the established cytochrome P450-mediated DDI between azoles and simvastatin to assess the ability of this approach to capture a positive signal of an interaction.

SMQ analysis of myopathy

Signal detection was performed for five azoles, and the cases were categorized into two groups on the basis of the presence or absence of statins in the reports (Table S7 of the ESM). Each group was analyzed twice using both the ROR and BCPNN methods with narrow or broad SMQ searches. In the statin included dataset, posaconazole, voriconazole, fluconazole, and itraconazole yielded ROR025 and IC025 values exceeding the threshold in both narrow and broad SMQ searches. In the nonstatin dataset, voriconazole, fluconazole, and itraconazole were linked to non-statin-related myopathy events in both narrow and broad SMQ searches. However, posaconazole and isavuconazole were only associated with non-statin-related myopathy events in the broad SMQ search (Table S7 of the ESM). When azoles were classified as the PS, SS, or I, the RORs and IC values for reports mentioning voriconazole, fluconazole, and itraconazole were significantly higher than those from reports assigning azoles any role (PS, SS, I, or C) in both the statin included and nonstatin datasets (Table 3). Notably, the ROR and IC values of voriconazole with the narrow SMQ search were greater in the nonstatin dataset than in the statin included dataset.

Myopathy DDI signals of azoles and nonstatins

Among the 215 reports in absence of statin mentioning azoles and myopathy, the number of reports detecting the concomitant use of nonstatins and azoles was significantly higher than that of reports that did not involve nonstatins. Specifically, 150 reports concurrently mentioned myopathy, azoles, and nonstatins, whereas only 65 reports did not involve nonstatins. A total of 42 potential DDIs were screened for myopathy, 30 of which presented strong positive signals (Table 4). In general, RORs were much lower for reports mentioning azoles without nonstatins than reports that mentioned both azoles and nonstatins. In comparison to reports mentioning nonstatins alone, reports that mentioned both azoles and nonstatins also had higher RORs. After adjusting for potential confounding factors via sensitivity analysis, 22 drug pairs still demonstrated a strong correlation with myopathy (>2.0); 33 D2 drugs in these pairs presented myopathy signals in the nonstatin dataset, and 35 of these pairs had records of drug interactions in DrugBank (Fig. 1 and Table S8 of ESM).

literature review of case reports

The literature review identified a total of 12 case reports9,10,33–42 (Table 5), including 8 males and 4 females, with an average age of 41 years. The onset of adverse reactions ranged from 2 to 61 days post medication, with a median duration of 9.5 days. Among the 12 reports, voriconazole was suspected in 9 cases, followed by posaconazole in 2 cases and itraconazole in 1 case. A total of 19 concomitant use of nonstatins, with 9 of these having a Drug Interaction Probability Scale (DIPS) score of 6 or higher when combined with azoles, thus indicating a high likelihood of interaction.

Discussion

In this study, we employed the ROR and BCPNN statistical methods to analyze data from the FAERS and to identify potential signals of myopathy associated with azoles. Additionally, we utilized subset analysis and the Ω shrinkage measure model to investigate the potential DDIs between azoles and nonstatins.

The signals of myopathy associated with azoles in the absence of statin use

There is evidence supporting the association between muscle toxicity and the concurrent use of azoles and statins43. However, multiple analyses in this study revealed that both the ROR025 and the IC025 for myopathy were significant in non-statin-related reports involving fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole compared with the background reporting rates in the FAERS database. Additionally, the results from the broad SMQ indicated that posaconazole and isavuconazole may also be related to the incidence of myopathy. Therefore, azoles, both as a class and individually, could be associated with an increased reporting frequency of myopathy events in the absence of statin use.

Comparison of azole-related myopathy reports with and without statins

There was only one case of myopathy associated with isavuconazole; therefore, this case was excluded from further analysis. The patients with myopathy associated with both azoles and statins tended to be older, with 67.1% being over 65 years old, whereas only 25.8% of patients with myopathy associated with azoles independent of statins were older than 65 years of age (P < 0.01). Additionally, the mortality rate, hospitalization rate (both initial and prolonged), and incidence of interventions needed to prevent permanent impairment in cases of myopathy associated with both itraconazole and statins were significantly higher than those in cases of myopathy involving itraconazole independent of statins. In contrast, the prognoses for myopathy cases associated with voriconazole, fluconazole, and posaconazole were similar in both that statin included and nonstatin datasets.

Regional differences on reporting frequency of myopathy events

The regional distribution of azole-related myopathy reports was explored. When compared with other azoles, East Asian patients reported the highest number of non-statin-related myopathy events associated with voriconazole. Most reports in the FAERS database come from the United States, followed by Canada and Europe. In East Asia, most reports are from China, Japan, or South Korea. Among the cases of myopathy associated with voriconazole in the statin included dataset, 43.8% were from East Asia, whereas 10.9% were from North America, and 30.7% were from Europe. In the nonstatin dataset, over 50% of the voriconazole-related myopathy cases were from East Asia. Although the possibility of reporting bias cannot be excluded, the notably higher proportion of reports from East Asia—constituting 1.9% of all voriconazole adverse event reports in that region, versus 0.15% in North America and 0.76% in Europe—could be consistent with a potentially higher incidence of voriconazole-related myopathy among East-Asian patients.

Previous pharmacokinetic studies have shown that voriconazole is metabolized primarily in the liver by CYP2C19, with fewer contributions from CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. Intermediate or poor CYP2C19 metabolizers have significantly higher serum levels of voriconazole than normal metabolizers do44–47. A meta-analysis of 22 studies (from 11 Asian sites and 11 non-Asian sites) revealed a higher incidence of poor voriconazole metabolizers among Asian populations than among Caucasian populations (13–23% vs 2–5%), and a greater rate of hepatotoxicity was observed in Asians48. Additionally, esomeprazole and omeprazole are isomers that are both strong inhibitors of CYP2C19. Voriconazole and esomeprazole were identified in our lists of drug pairs associated with DDI-related myopathy.

In the literature review, 7 out of the 12 cases involved Asian patients who were prescribed voriconazole, and 4 cases involved patients who were prescribed a combination of voriconazole with esomeprazole or omeprazole, thus yielding DIPS scores of 7–8.

These results could suggest that the myotoxicity profile of voriconazole might vary across ethnic groups, possibly influenced by genetic variations such as CYP2C19 deficiency and the concurrent use of CYP2C19 inhibitors. Nevertheless, further investigation is required to determine whether these patterns arise from true pharmacogenetic disparities or other contributing factors.

Azole-nonstatin myopathy DDI signals

We used two popular methods to detect DDIs between azoles and nonstatins in this study: subset analysis and the Ω-shrinkage measure model. Each method has unique benefits regarding sensitivity and accuracy27. However, these methods may produce false positives, especially in reports involving multiple recorded drug combinations. Additionally, determining a drug’s role often relies on subjective judgment, potentially leading to false negatives.

To minimize the occurrence of these false positives and false negatives, we adopted a comprehensive approach by initially not specifying the roles of D1 and D2 but required at least four myopathy reports with D1 and D2. We calculated and Ω025, followed by a sensitivity analysis. This approach involved adjusting on the basis of the number of positive drug pairs identified in each report and the recorded roles of D1 and D2 and then ranking for each drug pair.

The sensitivity analysis results (Fig. 1) showed that, after adjustment, for drug pairs where the was less than 2.0, four pairs also had an Ω025 less than 0, four pairs showed no known drug interactions, and five cases of D2 revealed no non-statin-related myotoxicity, suggesting a low likelihood of myopathy interactions. For drug pairs where remained greater than 2.0, three drug pairs had no known interactions: fluconazole-caspofungin, voriconazole-cisatracurium, and fluconazole- imipenem. The first two drug pairs exhibited strong DDI signals before adjustment and all three D2 drugs (caspofungin, cisatracurium, and imipenem) themselves carry a risk of non-statin-related myopathy. Notably, the fluconazole and caspofungin pair had seven out of eight reports indicating both drugs as "P, S, I." These findings suggest a potential increased risk of severe myopathy with this drug combination.

Validation

We identified three cases of rhabdomyolysis not associated with statin use between 2022 and 2023 in our hospital, all of which were linked to voriconazole administration during the improvement phase of primary aspergillosis infection. These patients presented voriconazole plasma concentrations exceeding or reaching the pharmacological upper limit (4.8–12.0 ng/ml), with clinical improvements upon dose reduction or cessation. The Naranjo scores ranged from 8 to 9, indicating a probable association with voriconazole. Two patients underwent CYP2C19 genotyping, revealing one intermediate metabolizer and one poor metabolizer. This suggested a genetic predisposition to adverse drug reactions with voriconazole. Despite the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis, the poor prognosis was mainly due to fungal infection resurgence after the switch from voriconazole to alternative antifungal agents. All cases were reported to the Shandong Provincial Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring Center, China. And in this database, the ROR025 and IC025 of voriconazole-associated rhabdomyolysis in the absence of statin use were 16.66 and 3.61, respectively. However, limitations such as the small number of reports and lack of comprehensive concomitant medication data precluded more extensive analysis.

Interestingly, herein, we rescreened potential DDIs in all three cases, specifically with drugs related to or similar to D2, as shown in Table 4: omeprazole (the S-enantiomer of esomeprazole), metoclopramide, and amlodipine. The review of 12 published cases also highlighted 8 instances of voriconazole coadministration with drugs related to or as D2 in Fig. 1, such as omeprazole, esomeprazole, nifedipine, amlodipine, piperacillin, and acyclovir. This finding emphasizes the need for clinicians to carefully consider potential DDIs and genetic factors when patients develop rhabdomyolysis after being prescribed voriconazole. Further research into the mechanisms and management of these interactions is necessary to improve patient outcomes.

Significance and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale pharmacovigilance study to integrate disproportionality analysis with systematic DDI screening while explicitly excluding statin-induced confounding in assessing azole-associated myopathy. All signals were indentified using two independent methods and underwent multiple sensitivity analyses. Previous reports have generally been limited to individual azoles13–15 or did not comprehensively assess DDIs16,28, leaving the landscape of myopathy risks across all five azoles—and potential DDI signals involving non-statin comedications—largely unexplored. Our findings help address this important knowledge gap and may inform more safer azole use in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, voluntary reports are subjective and may introduce false negatives. Underreporting in spontaneous reporting systems can lead to an underestimation of the true incidence of myopathy events associated with azoles. Incomplete drug dosage information also hinders accurate assessment of dose–response relationships. Furthermore, inaccurate reporting of concomitant medications or the role of drugs may mask signals. For example, although CYP2C19 inhibition mechanisms and literature suggest similar myopathy risks with omeprazole and esomeprazole when co-administered with voriconazole, only the voriconazole-esomeprazole pair was detected in FAERS data. Second, disproportionality analysis, particularly ROR, can yield false positives. ROR is highly sensitive to rare events and small sample sizes. In DDI signal detection, the sample size for reporting concomitant medications is often smaller, leading to significant values even with a few reports. Additionally, reporters may be more inclined to document drugs known to interact with azoles, regardless of their relevance to myopathy.

To address these challenges, we used both ROR and BCPNN methods with SMQ narrow and broad definitions to assess myopathy risk in all azoles’ reports (any role) and separately in reports where azoles were thought to have a key role (PS, SS, or I). For DDI signal detection, we combined subset analysis and Ω-shrinkage measure model and applied an original correction method to adjust for factors that may cause excessively high values. We also integrated the myopathy signals of the D2 drugs, known DDI data and literature evidence in our analysis. Despite these efforts, residual confounding may still exist. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously. While some drug pairs in our study have been confirmed to have pharmacokinetic interactions in DrugBank, this does not directly indicate that myopathy is related to these interactions, as there was no control group for the incidence of myopathy with azoles or nonstatins used alone. Clinicians may consider maintaining awareness for the potential for statin-independent myotoxicity associated with azoles, and might adapt monitoring and management strategies as further evidence emerges. Future studies could seek to independently validate these findings in other pharmacovigilance systems such as EudraVigilance, VigiBase and Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database; where necessary, in vitro toxicology assays and physiologically based pharmacokinetic modelling should be conducted.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified signals indicative of an increased reporting frequency of myopathy events associated with azoles and their interactions with nonstatins through voluntary reports submitted to the FAERS database. Our findings suggest a potential association between azole use and myopathy, particularly among East Asian population using voriconazole. These results highlight the importance of therapeutic drug monitoring and consideration of CYP2C19 genotyping when prescribing voriconazole. Additionally, caution is advised in the use of azoles and potentially interacting agents to minimize the risk of adverse drug reactions.
