DriveNet: A comprehensive dataset for driver posture recognition to enhance ergonomics and safety
Authors: Aishwarya N, Ganesh Kumar Chellamani, Sakthi Abirami Balakrishnan, Lokesh Kumar K M, Lingeshwaramoorthy E
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoorly aligned driving posture in the occupational environment increases the risk of musculoskeletal disorders. Asymmetric pressure distribution on the neck and shoulder areas during driving can lead to severe pain and soreness. Although occupational drivers are extremely focused on safe driving, they are less aware of their seating posture. Although several automated systems have been developed for monitoring driver behavior and detecting driver drowsiness, an alert system with the capability of recognizing the driver posture can assist drivers in maintaining an optimal seating posture and minimizing ergonomic risks. To develop an efficient machine learning-based driver posture recognition system, a dataset that efficiently represents improper seating postures is required. To fulfill this requirement, this paper proposes the DriveNet dataset, a comprehensive dataset developed for the purpose of driver posture recognition. This dataset comprises 11,160 high-resolution RGB images of four posture classes, which are captured using a high-resolution RGB camera installed on the Android smartphone camera. This dataset is intended to facilitate further research on driver posture classification and recognition using advanced machine learning and deep learning techniques.Keywords: Ergonomics, Driver Posture, Machine Learning, Musculoskeletal Disorders, Occupational Health

Background

System ergonomics and poor driving posture are important factors in musculoskeletal disorders [1]. Poor driving posture is determined by the position of the driver’s neck, shoulders, and lower back. Thus, the determination of the position of these body parts can help in finding a solution to classify and recognize different driving postures. Various datasets for driver posture and behavior [[2], [3], [4], [5]] have been introduced, which emphasize the activities performed by the drivers instead of their posture. On the other hand, pressure sensors [[6], [7], [8]] have been employed to determine the information of specific body parts during activities such as gear shifting, braking, checking rearview mirrors, and so on. Although these datasets have been employed extensively for detecting driver distraction or drowsiness and analyzing driver behavior, they fail to determine the driving ergonomics.

Dataset Description

The DriveNet dataset consists of 11,160 images of four different driving positions. The images were taken from videos of three different people to make the dataset more robust and diverse. The videos were all recorded using a Samsung S24 Ultra camera in the same environment. The images are saved in .jpg format with a resolution of 1920 × 1440 pixels. The dataset consists of four classes, each with 2790 images, which correspond to the following driving positions: lean backward, move backward, sit straight, and right position. The dataset is organized into four folders corresponding to the posture classes: Lean Backward (LB), Move Backward (MB), Sit Straight (SS), and Right Position (RP), where each folder contains images belonging to the respective class. Fig. 1 shows sample images taken from different people, and Table 1 describes in detail the four driving positions included in the dataset. Each improper driving posture is related to various medical issues. The lean backward posture places additional strain on the neck and shoulder muscles and may lead to discomfort in the upper back region. The move backward posture, where the neck, shoulders, and lower back are not supported by the seat, may increase musculoskeletal stress and cause lower back discomfort. In the sit straight posture, insufficient lumbar support may lead to lower back strain during prolonged driving [9].

A metadata file in CSV format is provided along with the dataset, containing the filename, class label, participant ID, and corresponding video/session ID. The image filenames follow a structured naming convention (e.g., compressed_LB_2.jpg), where the class label is embedded in the filename along with a frame index. The participant ID and video/session information are provided in the metadata file, enabling full traceability and facilitating reproducible experimentation. The DriveNet dataset is primarily designed for controlled-condition four-class driver posture classification under consistent camera viewpoint and lighting conditions. While the current study adopts an image-wise split, the provided metadata enables subject-wise splitting, which is recommended for future evaluations to better assess model generalization.

Experimental Design, Material and Methods

Raw data collection

Fig. 2 presents an illustration of the experimental process used in the creation of the DriveNet dataset. As shown in the illustration, the data generation process began with consulting experts in the field of ergonomics to identify the driving positions that were at high risk of musculoskeletal disorders. The identified postures were categorized into lean backward, move backward, sit straight, and right position. To ensure diversity in the dataset, three volunteers were chosen. All the volunteers were males aged between 20 and 22 years. This ensured that the volunteers had different body structures. They were informed about the importance of the experiment and provided with adequate representation guidelines before the experiment. During the data collection process, the volunteers were requested to assume a certain posture while seated in the driver’s seat. After that, the videos were recorded. This process was conducted for 75 s using the rear camera of a Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra smartphone. The camera was positioned at approximately dashboard level facing the driver, capturing a frontal upper-body view under consistent indoor lighting conditions. This setup captured the upper body posture of the driver, including the neck, shoulders, and torso, which are important for posture assessment.Table 2

The camera was positioned at approximately dashboard level at a distance of about 0.8–1.2 m from the participant, capturing a frontal upper-body view. The camera angle was approximately horizontal with a slight downward tilt to ensure clear visibility of the neck, shoulders, and torso. The field of view covered the driver’s upper body within the seat region. The seat position was kept fixed during recording to maintain consistency across all participants and postures. The posture labels were assigned based on predefined ergonomic definitions of the four classes. Each recording session focused on one posture at a time, and the extracted frames inherited the corresponding label. The labeling was verified through manual inspection to ensure consistency across all samples. As the data collection was performed under controlled conditions with predefined postures, independent annotator agreement was not required.

Image data collection

A total of 12 videos were recorded, from which 11,160 images were extracted for the creation of the DriveNet dataset. The videos were recorded in MP4 format, thus eliminating the need for any format conversion. Frames were extracted from each video using OpenCV at a fixed sampling rate of 16 frames per second. Since consecutive frames contained limited posture variation, redundant frames were reduced through temporal subsampling by retaining representative frames at regular intervals throughout the video sequence. This process ensured reduction of near-duplicate consecutive frames while preserving posture diversity across the recording duration. As a result, 930 representative images were retained from each video, each with a resolution of 1920 × 1440 pixels. This corresponds to 2790 images per posture (three participants) and a total of 11,160 images across four posture classes.

Methods

The DriveNet dataset was trained and tested on six state-of-the-art image classification algorithms- ResNet50 [10], DenseNet101 [11], EfficientNetB2 [12], MobileNetV3 [13], InceptionV3 [14] and Xception [15]. All the models were trained on images with input size 224×224×3, using a batch size of 8, a learning rate of 0.001 and for 50 epochs. The dataset split used in this study is image-wise, where images from all participants are distributed across training, validation, and testing sets. The training set consisted of 8000 images (2000 images for each class), the validation set consisted of 1980 images (495 images for each class), and the testing set consisted of 1180 images (295 images for each class). The division of the set of data was made in such a way that there was an equitable distribution of images for all classes of posture. Limitations

The DriveNet dataset has some limitations. The dataset consists of recordings from only three participants. The recordings were made in a single vehicle environment with a specific camera viewpoint. Future work would involve increasing the number of participants and the vehicle environments with different camera viewpoints. The dataset currently uses an image-wise split, where images from all participants are distributed across training, validation, and testing sets. As the dataset includes only three participants, this may lead to subject overlap and optimistic performance estimates. However, the provided metadata (including participant IDs) enables subject-wise splitting for more robust evaluation. Future work will focus on increasing the number of participants and recording conditions.

Ethics Statement

The data was collected in compliance with institutional research guidelines. The study was conducted on student participants who voluntarily participated in the data collection process after being informed about the purpose and intended use of the dataset. The participants provided their informed consent prior to data collection. No personal or sensitive information was collected. To protect participant privacy, the facial regions in the shared images were blurred before releasing the dataset. The study did not require approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee since the data collection process was non-invasive and did not pose any risks to the participants.

Data Availability

The DriveNet dataset is publicly available in the Mendeley Data repository: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/v37nxdn2gh/6. The repository includes the processed image dataset, original source videos (MP4 format), and a metadata manifest file (CSV) containing filename, class label, participant ID, and video/session ID. The metadata file enables users to generate custom image-wise or subject-wise train, validation, and test splits for experimentation. These resources support reproducibility and facilitate further research. The dataset is released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license.
