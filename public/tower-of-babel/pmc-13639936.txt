The Role of Emotional Well-Being in Diabetes Prevention and Management
Authors: Weidi Qin, Julie Ober Allen, Kara M. Mannor, Alejandro Rodríguez-Putnam, Viktoryia A. Kalesnikava, Briana Mezuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article presents a nuanced analysis of current evidence regarding the role of emotions as understudied mediators of the effectiveness of type 2 diabetes (T2D) prevention and management programs. It reviews empirical literature on how indicators of poor mental health, as well as positive affective functioning, are relevant to health behaviors that contribute to T2D risk, such as compliance with recommended diets and improved nutrition. Addressing these emotional aspects of diabetes may be an effective means of improving uptake, and ultimately efficacy, of efforts to reduce the burden of T2D. This article also discusses examples of diabetes self-management efforts that aim to simultaneously address diabetes care and emotional health needs across clinical and community settings, including the collaborative care model, peer-led programs, and community health educators. Finally, this article considers what centering the emotional side of diabetes means for innovative health promotion efforts for people with T2D.

BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

More than 38 million Americans have diabetes, of which 90–95% have type 2 diabetes (T2D). Most of the remainder have type 1 diabetes (T1D), an autoimmune disorder that typically has an onset prior to age 30 and requires insulin to manage. The incidence of T2D increases with age, peaking in the mid-50s, and more than 25% of US adults aged 65+ currently have T2D (14). T2D is characterized by persistent hyperglycemia due to insufficient production and/or efficacy of insulin, which in turn increases the risk of macrovascular and microvascular complications (e.g., cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, vision loss, kidney failure, amputation). It is therefore not surprising that diabetes is among the top 10 leading causes of death in the United States (16).

While T2D has a strong genetic component, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [e.g., the US Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP), the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study] have shown that it is preventable (or at least can be delayed) both through affordable medications (e.g., metformin) or intensive, sustained dietary and physical activity changes (67). Indeed, in 2012 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) launched a nationwide effort to prevent T2D using the health behavior change programming that was developed in the DPP trial (15). Unfortunately, adoption, enrollment, and engagement in the CDC’s DPP rollout is far less than anticipated (20, 54), and there is little evidence that this program has had a significant impact on the incidence of T2D in the United States (70). Notwithstanding the clinical effectiveness of glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, these medications are typically not covered by health insurance solely for weight loss in the United States (5). As a result, health behavior change remains the primary tool for diabetes prevention.

Health behavior (e.g., dietary behavior, smoking and tobacco use, alcohol consumption, sleep, physical activity, medication adherence, glucose monitoring) also remains a critical component of daily diabetes self-management. For people with T2D or T1D, effective self-management is essential to prevent debilitating and life-threatening diabetes-related complications. Self-management behaviors include increasing physical activity, limiting tobacco and alcohol use, maintaining appropriate sleep hygiene, and managing carbohydrate and caloric intake. However, the ability to adopt and maintain these health behaviors is substantially influenced by an array of emotional factors, such as feelings of depression and anxiety (66). In addition, disease-specific emotional distress (i.e., feelings of frustration, avoidance, and burnout) stemming from the near-ubiquitous attention required to engage in self-care is common in diabetes (83). The net result is that emotional aspects play an important role in individuals’ ongoing engagement in self-management of diabetes, their glycemic control, and ultimately their risk of poor clinical outcomes (9).

Figure 1 illustrates the pathways of health behavior changes in the context of clinical care of T2D. Engagement in health behavior changes varies at different stages of diabetes. Immediately following their diagnosis, individuals may have a sudden increased perception of risk (i.e., of developing complications, often informed by witnessing family members or friends deal with this disease), which often motivates people to adopt health-promoting behaviors. That is, the diagnosis can be a cueing health event that cultivates a teachable moment. On the other hand, individuals may also feel threatened and experience low self-efficacy, leading to avoidance, denial, and/or disengagement with diabetes care (particularly if they are asymptomatic, as many individuals are when diagnosed with T2D), and only begin to engage with self-management when symptoms or complications develop (36).

Moreover, the process of adopting health behaviors needed to self-manage T2D is influenced by social context and psychosocial processes, including emotional, informational, and tangible social support. Social support from family and friends is often described as essential by individuals with T2D to maintain health-promoting behaviors and reduce health-harming ones. This is not a unidirectional relationship, but rather these psychosocial factors contribute to better diabetes management outcomes (i.e., better glycemic control), which can, in turn, improve emotional health and support engagement in health-promoting behaviors.

Many of the concepts discussed in this review are illustrated using data from the Diabetes, Distress, and Disparities (3D) study (principal investigator, B. Mezuk), a recently completed sequential mixed methods study of approximately 600 adults with diabetes (primarily T1D or T2D) recruited from a large healthcare center. Briefly, the goal of the 3D study was to investigate psychosocial aspects of diabetes and identify actionable insights for improving person-centered diabetes care. Details of the study design and data collection protocols are described elsewhere (25); findings in this paper draw from an online survey (n = 573) that assessed psychological, social, and behavioral aspects of diabetes management and semistructured qualitative interviews with a subset of 40 survey participants. The 3D study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan (HUM00223735), and all participants provided informed consent.

THE EMOTIONAL SIDE OF DIABETES

While the disease of T2D is defined by hyperglycemia and insulin resistance and/or insensitivity, it is not solely experienced as a problem of the pancreas or whose management is limited to glycemic control. Indeed, because of the interconnections between emotions and T2D, this condition serves as a prime illustration of the biopsychosocial model of health. The biopsychosocial model, first proposed by Engel in 1977 (30), provides a heuristic for considering how psychological, social, and biological factors interrelate to shape health (96). First, longitudinal studies demonstrate a bidirectional relationship between mental health and T2D onset, management, and prognosis. Emotional factors such as anxiety and depression are associated with progression of prediabetes to T2D (48, 72), poor glycemic control (1), and development of complications (80). Second, Engel’s biopsychosocial model emphasizes the importance of focusing on the experiences of the person with a health condition, not simply the physiology of disease, when seeking to understand the factors that shape the course of illness (30). Indeed, many forms of depression treatment are associated with concurrent improvements in glycemic control (109). The value of this person-centered approach is also apparent in the wealth of qualitative data that describe the emotional toll that the day-to-day management of diabetes takes on individuals and their families (89).

Finally, the emotional side of diabetes is perhaps best-represented in the distinct concept of diabetes-related distress (DRD), which captures aspects of burnout, frustration, fatigue, and anxiety that directly result from self-management. Globally, 4 in 10 people with diabetes report experiencing clinically significant DRD (78), with higher rates among younger individuals, people with lower income and/or education, and people with T1D (99) or with earlier age of T2D onset (8). Among US adults with T2D, approximately 24% report elevated DRD (2, 40). DRD, in addition to directly impacting quality of life for people with diabetes, is also associated with poor glycemic control and other clinical diabetes care outcomes (31, 82).

In sum, while, from a biomedical lens, diabetes is a disease characterized by hyperglycemia, monitored with technology, managed with medications, and associated with increased risk of potentially debilitating complications, from the perspective of the biopsychosocial model—and from people who live with it—diabetes is an unpredictable health condition requiring near-constant attention that touches most, if not all, parts of their lives. This has direct implications for efforts to support health behavior change for diabetes prevention and management.

EMOTIONAL ASPECTS OF BEHAVIOR CHANGE IN DIABETES PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

Emotional well-being and psychosocial coping resources can either reinforce or hinder the health behavior changes (98, 108), providing potential points of interventions—or aspects of care where additional support is needed—to strengthen self-efficacy around behavior change. Negative (i.e., depression, anxiety, and DRD) and positive (i.e., resilience and self-efficacy) aspects of emotional health influence health-related behaviors in various ways (90). The adoption and maintenance of health behaviors (e.g., diet, physical activity, tobacco and alcohol use) and self-management behaviors (e.g., glucose monitoring, medication adherence) are important determinants of clinical outcomes.

Moreover, a large body of empirical research has linked better mental health to the initiation and maintenance of behavior changes among patients with T2D (29, 59, 106). In addition, positive emotional support from family and friends may buffer the adverse effects of depression on health behaviors and facilitate health behavior changes among individuals with diabetes (81). Finally, interventions that focus on health behaviors can also reduce symptoms of depression in people with T2D (64), suggesting that interventions that jointly address depression and diabetes management behaviors may be more effective at improving diabetes outcomes (42).

How Depression, Anxiety, and Diabetes-Related Distress Shape Health Behaviors

Approximately 10–15% of individuals with diabetes have co-occurring major depressive disorder, which is characterized by low mood and disinterest along with “somatic” symptoms such as fatigue and disturbances in sleep, appetite, and concentration (93). A large body of research shows that depressive symptoms influence the adoption of healthy behaviors among people with T2D and other chronic diseases (106, 116). Longitudinal studies show that people with T2D who have persistent and severe depressive symptoms are significantly less likely to follow healthy dietary behaviors or engage in physical activity (59). Similarly, a study during the COVID-19 pandemic found that people with T2D who reported a higher level of depression were more likely to become less physically active, have poor sleep, and increase consumption of high-sugar/high-fat foods, alcohol, and tobacco (60).

Anxiety disorders (e.g., generalized anxiety, panic attacks, post-traumatic stress disorder, phobias) are also a prevalent condition among people with T2D (52, 100). In the context of chronic disease management, anxiety disorders can adversely affect clinical diabetes outcomes, including the ability to engage in self-management behaviors (94). Anxiety disorders have been linked to health-harming behaviors such as alcohol abuse and smoking (105), as well as poorer glycemic control and more severe complications among individuals with T2D (43).

DRD is another negative affective factor that can influence health behavior change and diabetes self-management. As the examples from the 3D study discussed above illustrate, DRD reflects the distinct emotional burden that can accompany the experience of managing diabetes (34). Elevated DRD is associated with poorer management outcomes, such as glycemic levels as well as other self-care activities (46), including adhering to a healthy diet (112). The ways in which DRD contributes to diabetes care indicators (e.g., glycemic control) are not fully understood; a recent randomized intervention study found that reductions in DRD do not directly influence glycemic control, but the adoption of healthy self-care behaviors including checking blood glucose and adjusting insulin to diets can influence glycemic control (46).

How Positive Aspects of Emotional Health Shape Health Behaviors

Positive emotional factors have also been explored as potential determinants of health behavior change for people with T2D. These positive factors include resilience, self-efficacy, and social support (90). Resilience, defined as the capacity to maintain psychological well-being despite experiencing adversity (45), is an understudied but potentially potent determinant of diabetes outcomes. For example, a recent intervention study found that programming to improve resiliency among adults with T2D was associated with greater adoption of health-promoting behaviors, such as a balanced diet and more frequent physical activity (13). Collectively, high resilience appears to be a strong predictor of behavioral changes among adults with diabetes.

Relatedly, self-efficacy, defined as the belief in one’s ability to accomplish a task, is thought to contribute to sustained engagement in complex behaviors such as those required in diabetes self-management, although empirical research is mixed (87). Generally, high self-efficacy is positively associated with health behavior change among people with T2D (50), but this is most evident for increasing physical activity (41, 61, 75, 92, 114, 115); the role of self-efficacy in reducing health-harming behaviors among people with T2D, such as heavy drinking and smoking, is less clear (85, 104).

Finally, social support—which encompasses emotional, informational, and tangible aspects of relationships—plays an important role in diabetes management behaviors (77). Social support from both family and friends is linked to changes in health behaviors such as alcohol use and physical activity among adults with T2D (77, 85). However, social support does not always correlate with engagement in health-promoting behaviors; for example, while social support from family is associated with less alcohol consumption, social support from friends is linked to greater alcohol use among adults with T2D (85). Social support represents an important component of emotional health that can influence behavioral change and provides a potential intervention point that may improve engagement with recommended diabetes care regimens. Indeed, providing meaningful social support is a core component of many innovative programs (e.g., peer-support programs) in diabetes management.

Health Behavior Change at Different Stages of Diabetes

While the onset of diabetes may be represented by a single diagnostic test in a healthcare record, this belies a more complex and dynamic process of living with this condition. Indeed, the psychological and behavioral aspects of diabetes can change over time. Specifically, a diagnosis of diabetes is often a major health event that prompts strong emotional reactions and cognitive responses, which influences the perceived risk of health-compromising behaviors and facilitates the adoption of health-promoting behavior changes. As shown in Figure 1, immediately following their diagnosis, individuals may have an increased perception of risk, which can serve as a motivator to adopt health-promoting behaviors (i.e., the teachable moment) (26, 117); for example, there is evidence that following a T2D diagnosis individuals are more likely to engage in physical activity (85, 117). On the other hand, being diagnosed with a chronic condition such as T2D is also a stressful health event that can negatively impact emotional health and lead to symptoms of depression and anxiety (62), which in turn can shape engagement in health behaviors. For example, perceiving a diabetes diagnosis as a high-risk event is linked to poor diet habits and elevated depressive symptoms (50). Additionally, some individuals feel threatened and experience low self-efficacy when diagnosed, which is linked to avoidance/denial and disengagement from diabetes care, especially if they are largely asymptomatic (36). In sum, emotional reactions to being diagnosed with diabetes can have important implications for engagement in health behavior change and diabetes self-management.

The duration of living with diabetes is also associated with health behavior changes. Diabetes is a dynamic condition that can become more complicated to manage over time, and individuals who have been living with diabetes for many years report greater levels of burnout with their care regimens (76, 119). Indeed, adults with longer durations of T2D often require more intense and reinforced encouragement in diabetes self-management (63). Taken together, these factors show that it is important to consider that health behavior promotion and self-management of diabetes are dynamic and complex processes that vary over time.

Emotional Well-Being and Dietary Behaviors

Healthy diet is an essential component in successful diabetes self-management. For example, individuals with diabetes are encouraged to consume foods that are rich in fiber, such as whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, as the ingestion of fiber can provide metabolic benefits and glycemic control (6), contributing to improved diabetes management outcomes and reducing health complications. Adherence to healthy dietary behaviors, such as the consumption of fruits and vegetables, is critical for long-term diabetes control and health-related quality of life (84, 86). However, adverse emotional health can be an important barrier that hinders compliance with recommended dietary behaviors. Prior evidence suggests that the severity of depressive symptoms is significantly associated with unhealthy and unbalanced diets among individuals with diabetes (21), which can complicate disease management efforts. Additional research also indicates that depressive symptoms and diabetes-related stress are linked to poor adherence to healthy dietary behaviors among patients with diabetes (53), highlighting the importance of emotional and affective well-being in diabetes management. Nevertheless, results from a more recent intervention study on a diabetes education program show that, while changes in depressive symptoms are not associated with changes in dietary behaviors, patients’ nutrition knowledge and self-efficacy contributed to improvements in diabetes management (74).

ADDRESSING PSYCHOSOCIAL ASPECTS OF DIABETES ACROSS DIFFERENT CONTEXTS

Unfortunately, traditional approaches to diabetes management in primary care settings do not incorporate or address the emotional aspects of this condition. While there is greater awareness of the value of screening for and addressing the psychosocial care needs of people with diabetes, it remains the case that management of diabetes and that of mental health concerns are often delivered separately, by different providers, and in different settings (91). As a result, people with diabetes have a difficult time getting care that addresses all aspects of living with diabetes in a comprehensive, integrated manner.

However, attending to the emotional side of diabetes may provide a path for engaging in more person-centered care that addresses both mental health concerns and clinical diabetes outcomes (e.g., glycemic control). As detailed below, multiple RCTs have demonstrated the efficacy of interventions that address diabetes management and mental health simultaneously. Moreover, programs outside the clinical setting (e.g., peer-support, community-based programs) are also efficacious at both improving glycemic control and addressing depressive symptoms, including for underserved populations. These innovative care models have the potential to advance the quality and efficacy of person-centered diabetes care in clinical and community settings.

The Collaborative Care Model

The collaborative care model (CCM) is a type of integrated care model that adopts a team-based approach to provide behavioral health care that aims to address health issues at the intersection of mental health and primary care systems in medical settings (7, 12). While traditional approaches to managing mental health concerns for people with diabetes typically rely on referrals to specialty care providers and limited involvement of the primary care provider in assessing and addressing emotional concerns, the CCM instead seeks to identify and manage both mental health and diabetes care concerns within a single collaborative healthcare team (22, 57, 58). In the CCM, the healthcare teams often include a medical doctor (e.g., primary care physician), a mental healthcare socialist (e.g., psychiatrist, psychologist, or social worker), and a case manager (e.g., nurse or clinical coordinator, often specializing in behavioral health) (7). The case manager coordinates care between the patient and the healthcare providers and provides support with self-management such as medication adherence, glucose monitoring, health education, and referrals to additional supports (57).

The clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness of the CCM is well-documented. For example, a meta-analysis of 37 RCTs, representing 12,355 people with depression who received primary care, suggests that the CCM is linked to sustained benefits beyond the initial intervention period (38); patients who received collaborative care demonstrate improved depression outcomes at 6 months, with improvements lasting up to 5 years (38), indicating that the CCM is more effective than traditional care, both short- and long-term. An RCT of the CCM for patients with T2D and/or coronary heart disease followed for 24 months showed that those in the collaborative care arm had an average of 114 additional depression-free days and lower outpatient healthcare costs than patients receiving traditional care (58). Taken together, these results show that the CCM represents an effective integrated care model that has the benefit of improving depression symptoms, managing diabetes clinical outcomes, and lowering healthcare costs.

In the context of diabetes and depression, multiple meta-analyses have documented the benefits of the CCM in improving both clinical diabetes outcomes and mental health symptoms. For example, a meta-analysis based on eight studies, representing 2,238 patients with T2D (49), found that the CCM was associated with improved depression treatment response and adherence to antidepressant medications, with nonsignificant improvements in glycemic control. A more recent meta-analysis focusing on behavioral interventions among patients with diabetes found that, compared to usual care, collaborative care is linked to both improved depression outcomes and improved glycemic control (22). This review identified 31 studies, representing 8,843 individuals with diabetes, and highlighted the efficacy of incorporating behavioral and emotional health into diabetes care. Importantly, this review found no differences in outcomes between primary and specialty care settings, showing that the CCM provides a promising approach to effective diabetes care with an interdisciplinary team of healthcare providers across diverse clinical care settings.

Community-Based, Peer-Support Diabetes Self-Management Programs

Community-based diabetes self-management programs with a peer-support component can produce clinically effective and cost-efficient improvements in both mental and physical health outcomes (24, 32). They have been widely used in international settings, especially in areas with limited healthcare resources. In the United States, community-based, peer-support programs have featured prominently in efforts to reduce T2D disparities. Models of peer-support programs are diverse, since these programs are adaptable and appropriate for diverse cultures and contexts. Formats reflect variable modalities (in person, telephone, online, or a combination), duration (6 weeks to 2 years), objectives (improvements in clinical, behavioral, and/or psychosocial outcomes), and characteristics (inclusion of peer counselors or leaders, training, duo versus group interactions). Common characteristics of community-based, peer-support diabetes self-management programs are (a) assistance with daily self-management and living with diabetes, (b) provision of social and emotional support, and (c) enhanced linkages to clinical care (33). The most effective models combine peer support with more structured elements such as health education and tangible resources (e.g., physical activity classes) (44). Short-term diabetes care and behavioral health outcomes of community-based, peer-support diabetes management programs are comparable to those of other models of diabetes self-management interventions (17, 24), although some studies indicate that peer-support programs may be associated with longer-term maintenance of clinical improvements (68, 102, 107). Community-based, peer-support diabetes management programs are associated with improvements in self-efficacy, DRD, quality of life, social vulnerability, and perceived social support (24, 37, 56, 73). A major strength of these programs is their capacity to engage, retain, and improve clinical outcomes among underserved groups, many of which experience diabetes disparities, such as racial and ethnic minorities, people of lower socioeconomic status, and rural populations (3, 10).

Peer-support models provide encouragement and tangible assistance in translating idealized diabetes management plans into the realities of individuals’ lives. They support the ongoing negotiation of complex behavioral changes to manage diabetes involving clinical, behavioral, social, and emotional factors, many of which are outside the expertise and schedule availability of clinicians. Community-based, peer-support programs are particularly well-suited for helping individuals with diabetes to problem-solve, helping patients to normalize inconsistent adherence with and commitment to clinical recommendations, and helping to connect participants to personally relevant knowledge and local resources (3). Some peer-support programs generate workout buddies and walking groups that provide social engagement, friendship, and accountability, while simultaneously promoting healthy behaviors. Participants emphasize the importance of peer support for goal setting and accountability (35). Others have noted that their involvement in a peer-support diabetes self-management program was the first time they had someone to talk to who really understood their condition and challenges, and thus they no longer felt so isolated (3). Finally, peer-support diabetes programs allow participants to serve as role models and help others, thereby providing opportunities for meaningful service that benefits mental health, provides a sense of purpose, and aids in diabetes management self-efficacy (35, 44).

Community Health Workers

Community health workers (CHWs) are another effective partner in diabetes self-management, especially among high-risk populations. Having CHWs as a part of diabetes care teams is associated with improvements in both mental health symptoms and diabetes clinical outcomes (97). CHWs can provide contextually tailored education, community resources, and social support to individuals with diabetes. CHW interventions have demonstrated promising outcomes in patients with T2D as they involve multilevel factors, including individuals, communities, and healthcare systems (79). CHWs play an important role in bridging communication between patients and healthcare providers to foster sustained engagement in diabetes self-management; key roles of CHWs include being a trusted source of health education and supporting and reinforcing care delivered by healthcare providers (79). CHWs can also provide emotional support, fostering trusting relationships and empowerment that promote patients’ engagement in diabetes care (28).

Additionally, CHW-led interventions are inherently collaborative, often integrating community partners and healthcare providers as part of a care team (4). This collaborative approach can enhance the communication and coordination of diabetes care and connect patients to clinical care and community resources. Focusing on a community-based and person-centered approach, CHWs ensure that interventions are responsive to patients’ specific needs and build trust within underserved communities to promote diabetes care (51). Since they are situated in the community rather than in the clinic, CHWs are well-suited to addressing barriers and challenges to diabetes care that stem from social and economic contexts (e.g., food insecurity, low health literacy). The net result is that by providing emotional support and tailored and relevant diabetes education, CHW-led programs facilitate access to care, improve health literacy, and contribute to equitable diabetes outcomes across diverse socioeconomic populations.

An array of studies suggest that involvement of CHWs in diabetes management programs can support both clinical outcomes and emotional health, especially among underserved and racial and ethnic minority populations. For example, an RCT of a CHW-led self-management program for racial/ethnic minority adults with T2D delivered in a low-resource setting found that the CHW arm had improved diabetes care and mental health outcomes compared to usual care (101). A more recent RCT compared CHW-delivered diabetes education with and without stress-management intervention among Latinos with T2D (111) and found that while overall there were no significant intervention differences, increasing attendance at the stress-management sessions was linked to both better glycemic outcomes and lower levels of DRD. Taken together, these results show that CHW-led diabetes interventions may be an effective approach in addressing self-management behaviors and emotional health needs; however, additional research is needed.

IMPLICATIONS FOR THE (RE)DESIGN OF DIABETES PREVENTION AND INTERVENTION PROGRAMS

The health behaviors that help prevent T2D and those that support living well with diabetes have long been known; what remains unclear is how to develop (and then implement) programs that not only impart health education but also fundamentally shift the default settings on managing this condition. This calls for a radical change in what we think of as the goals for these programs, the settings where they are delivered, and the levers in play for promoting diabetes prevention and management (47, 71). Incorporating the perspectives of people who live with T2D is an important component of this redesign. Future programs should look beyond short-term interventions and develop approaches that cultivate supportive contexts, encourage healthy lifestyle behaviors, and integrate mental health care. Incorporating perspectives and lived experiences of individuals with T2D is also essential to the design of such interventions. The first prompt was, “Can you tell me something that is important to you when it comes to your diabetes that isn’t likely to show up in your medical record: that is, something that matters to you but isn’t a blood test, diagnosis, or medication?” The top bar chart shows the frequencies of the five most common themes of responses: managing daily frustrations, the importance of establishing routines, technological support for self-management, finding balance in self-management, and minimizing criticism and judgment (including from healthcare providers). Collectively, these data demonstrate that while glycemic control may be the central focus of most diabetes care providers, this is just one of many priorities that people with diabetes have when it comes to their health. Moreover, self-management is both about making gains (e.g., improving glycemic control) and avoiding losses (e.g., fear of complications).

In sum, it is essential to consider both physiologic (e.g., glycemic control, blood pressure, kidney functioning) and psychological (e.g., depression, diabetes-related distress) aspects of diabetes to implement person-centered prevention and intervention programs (113). Shifting away from rigid ideas of compliance with self-management recommendations to a more dynamic process of adaptive behaviors and broader strategies that individuals employ in their efforts to stay healthy may be a more effective approach to achieve successful diabetes management (70). Human-centered design principles, paired with empirical behavioral and social science, can guide the development of programs that address all aspects of diabetes care in a comprehensive, integrative manner—consistent with how this condition is experienced by those who are living with it (18, 88). Indeed, interventions that leverage principles of stress management, such as mindfulness training, are being evaluated as part of person-centered diabetes care (23, 95).

Technologies such as smart phones, mobile applications, wearable devices, and activity trackers also open up new opportunities for diabetes self-management that integrate health behavior changes and mental health needs in a coordinated way (19). These digital tools can facilitate real-time monitoring of diabetes management behaviors such as diet and physical activity, while also offering customized feedback and prompts that encourage adherence and sustain motivation. A recent mixed-methods study finds that it is common among adults with diabetes to use mobile apps and activity trackers to manage diabetes management behaviors (11). However, it is also challenging to link behavior tracking to glycemic control and diabetes self-management goals, suggesting that additional efforts are needed to design digital tools focusing on patient-centered approaches and effective disease management.

Additionally, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI)-powered lifestyle intervention demonstrate a promising alternative to diabetes management (69). Incorporating AI into digital diabetes prevention programs may enhance the effectiveness by providing personalized support for health behavior changes (65). Emerging evidence based on RCTs among individuals with prediabetes shows that an AI-based diabetes intervention program significantly improves patient outcomes of weight loss, engagement in physical activity, and glycemic control without a traditional human health coach (69). Despite positive clinical outcomes, it is unclear whether AI-based diabetes interventions can be effective in simultaneously addressing emotional and clinical needs of diabetes patients. Future directions need to consider developing an integrated approach that combines clinical care of diabetes with emotional support, ensuring that AI-powered tools are responsive to both medical and emotional needs among patients with diabetes.

Addressing emotional health in diabetes care requires new incentive structures within healthcare payment schemes (39) and fostering new types of partnerships outside the clinical setting with advocates, peer-support programs, CHWs, and community-based organizations. The role of technology as a mitigator versus a contributor to diabetes-related distress and poor mental health for people with T2D is not yet clear (110, 118). It is also important to recognize that adoption of diabetes technology is shaped by existing racial and socioeconomic disparities in access to care (27, 55). Diabetes interventions need to consider how technology and digital tools introduce new ways to manage both the physiological and emotional aspects of diabetes, including patient–provider relationships, but also need to be wary that such advances often widen, rather than shrink, existing social disparities in care.
