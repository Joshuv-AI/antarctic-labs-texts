Thoracic electrical impedance tomography from technological promise to clinical reality: the Chinese experience
Authors: Zhanqi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic electrical impedance tomography (EIT) has emerged as a powerful, non-invasive, and radiation-free tool for real-time bedside monitoring of regional lung ventilation and perfusion. Its unique ability to visualize spatial and temporal heterogeneity has established clinical value across intensive care, perioperative management, and chronic respiratory disease settings. Despite decades of recognized potential, the global translation of EIT from laboratory research to routine clinical practice has faced significant challenges, including high device costs, lack of standardization and guidelines, workflow integration challenges, and insufficient evidence linking its use to clinical outcomes. China’s experience over the past decade presents a compelling and instructive case of rapid adoption that contrasts sharply with the slower global pace. Unlike prior descriptive reviews that catalogued milestones achieved, this article provided an analytical framework that examined how and why this accelerated translation occurred in China. It first evaluated the specific clinical value driving adoption across diverse scenarios. It then identified the distinctive ecosystem that enabled this rapid translation. Several factors worked synergistically: rapid clinical validation was achieved through China’s vast hospital network. Domestic industry innovation drove down device costs and improved portability. Chinese researchers provided proactive leadership in building national and international standards. Most importantly, strategic government support—including reimbursement policies—removed economic barriers to adoption. Transferable lessons from this model were discussed for overcoming implementation barriers and accelerating the adoption of innovative medical technologies globally.

Introduction

Thoracic electrical impedance tomography (EIT) has matured from a promising research technology into a non-invasive, bedside tool for real-time monitoring of regional lung ventilation and perfusion. Despite its compelling clinical potential, the global translation of EIT from laboratory to routine practice has been protracted, hindered by cost, standardization, and evidence-generation barriers. In contrast, the experience in China over the past decade stands as a notable exception, demonstrating a markedly accelerated pathway to clinical integration.

While previous reports have catalogued what was achieved in China and when (1), this article provides an analytical perspective focused on how and why this rapid translation occurred. The established and emerging clinical applications were synthesized, which define EIT’s value proposition. The persistent challenges that slow adoption globally were analyzed. The core of the discussion deconstructs the Chinese model—examining the synergistic interplay of clinical validation, domestic innovation, and strategic consensus-building—to extract a transferable blueprint for overcoming implementation barriers and accelerating the adoption of innovative medical technologies worldwide.

EIT is important for clinical use

EIT is a non-invasive, radiation-free imaging technique that enables real-time monitoring of regional lung ventilation and perfusion at the bedside (2). It applies a safe, high-frequency alternating current through electrodes placed around the subject’s thorax and measures the resulting surface voltages. Since air is a poor electrical conductor, and blood is good conductor, changes in lung air and blood content cause measurable changes in thoracic electrical impedance. Various reconstruction algorithms may be used to visualize these changes into dynamic images, generating functional “maps” of lung ventilation and perfusion.

The clinical importance of EIT lies in its unique ability to visualize physiological processes that are invisible to conventional monitoring. Traditional methods like blood gas analysis or global lung mechanics provide only whole-lung parameters, masking significant regional heterogeneities. For instance, in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), the lung injury is notoriously uneven, with collapsed, poorly ventilated regions coexisting with overinflated, healthy areas (3). In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the degree of airway obstruction can vary in different regions (4). Although the spatial resolution is poor compared to computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, EIT captures regional and functional defects in ventilation or perfusion with high temporal resolution (typically 20–50 frames per second).

Intensive care unit (ICU): the foundation

Chest EIT was first and mainly applied in the ICU. The research and evidence are most advanced in this field.

Guide protective ventilation: titrate positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and tidal volume to optimally balance alveolar recruitment against overdistension, thereby mitigating ventilator-induced lung injury [e.g. (5,6)].

Assess ventilation-perfusion matching: using contrast agents like hypertonic saline, EIT can assess regional pulmonary perfusion at the bedside, aiding in the diagnosis of conditions like pulmonary embolism (7,8).

Monitor treatment efficacy: objectively evaluate the effects of interventions such as recruitment maneuvers (9,10), prone positioning (11,12), and chest physiotherapy in real-time (13,14).

Guide weaning from mechanical ventilation: detect unfavorable patterns like significant pendelluft (intra-pulmonary redistribution of gas between different lung regions with heterogeneous time constants) or heterogeneous ventilation that may predict weaning failure (15,16).

Perioperative medicine: optimizing care from anesthesia to recovery

EIT’s role extends throughout the surgical journey, providing critical insights for high-risk patients.

Preoperative risk assessment: in patients with conditions like severe scoliosis or COPD, EIT can quantify the degree of ventilation heterogeneity, helping anesthesiologists assess pulmonary risk and plan strategies (17,18).

Intraoperative ventilation management: EIT-guided PEEP has shown significant advantages over fixed PEEP settings in various surgeries, including laparoscopic, thoracic, and cardiac procedures (19,20). It improves intraoperative oxygenation and lung compliance, potentially reducing postoperative atelectasis and pulmonary complications (21,22).

One-lung ventilation: during thoracoscopic surgery, EIT helps optimize ventilation in the dependent lung and assess re-expansion of the non-ventilated lung afterward (23,24).

Postoperative monitoring: EIT is used to monitor lung function recovery after extubation, especially in complex surgeries like esophagectomy or lung transplantation, guiding early intervention for complications (25,26).

Pulmonary function testing and chronic disease management: the novel regional information

EIT offers a dynamic, regional alternative to traditional global pulmonary function tests.

COPD, asthma, cystic fibrosis, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: EIT can visualize and quantify regional flow limitation, air trapping and lung function defect (27,28). This allows for a more nuanced assessment of disease severity and response to bronchodilators than spirometry alone.

Pulmonary rehabilitation: EIT provides visual biofeedback to both patients and physiotherapists. Studies show that EIT-guided breathing exercises (e.g., yoga breathing, pursed-lip breathing) can improve the efficacy of rehabilitation programs for patients with COPD or post-surgery by ensuring targeted regional lung expansion (29,30).

Long-term monitoring: with the development of more portable and wireless EIT systems, there is potential for monitoring lung function in outpatient settings or during activities of daily living (31,32).

Other emerging applications

EIT also shows promising potential in other fields.

Pediatrics: EIT is particularly valuable in children where radiation exposure is a concern. It has been used to monitor lung function in preterm, asthma, pediatric ARDS and severe pneumonia (33-35).

Extreme physiology and special populations: research has even explored EIT’s potential for aerospace medicine and monitoring specialized populations, e.g. during anti-gravity strain maneuvers or parabolic flights (36,37).

The transition from lab to clinical practice is difficult

Despite its compelling advantages, the journey of EIT from a research novelty to a standard clinical tool is slow and difficult. There are several persistent challenges:

High cost and reimbursement issues

As a monitoring tool rather than a life-support or primary diagnostic device, the cost-benefit ratio of EIT is frequently debated in the hospital management. The significant initial investment and maintenance costs are major burden for hospitals, especially without established reimbursement models from healthcare systems in most countries. The first commercial chest EIT device is made in Germany, however, nowadays, the EIT examination is still not chargeable in Germany.

Lack of standardization and guidelines

The first international consensus on measurement procedures, data analysis methods, and clinical interpretation was published in 2017 (38). An update for EIT applications in ICU was published recently (2). Despite the efforts, standardization and guidelines are still missing. Without clear, application-specific guidelines, clinicians were hesitant to rely on EIT for critical decisions. The variety of information obtained with this technology required a deeper level of understanding. A simple medical device usage training provided by manufacturers is insufficient.

Technical and human factor challenges

Achieving reliable image quality can be affected by motion artifacts and electrode placement (39). Furthermore, integrating a new monitoring technology into the high-pressure workflow of an ICU requires motivation and training. Early promotion faced resistance due to the perceived additional workload for clinical staff. The placement of the electrode belt is a major issue for EIT measurement in ICU, operating room, and lung function testing. The attach of the electrode belt often requires assistance from a second person, as the patients in the ICU are uncooperative. The time-consuming nature of individualized PEEP titration hinders integration into fast-paced clinical workflows.

Thoracic surgery creates chest incisions at the site where the electrode belt is placed, which may interfere with the electrode-skin contact. The belt may also compromise the disinfection of the area. Additional practical challenges include the inability to use EIT during electrocautery in the operating room due to electrical interference, and signal distortion caused by metallic objects such as trocars.

Simultaneous measurement of EIT and spirometry requires private room for the electrode placement, which adds extra planning burden on the hospital management.

Need for robust clinical evidence

While numerous single-center studies demonstrated feasibility and potential benefit, the widespread adoption of any new technology requires large-scale, multi-center randomized controlled trials (RCTs) proving its impact on hard outcomes like mortality. A recent multi-center study explored the EIT-guided PEEP and the patients’ mortality (40). Nevertheless, this level of evidence was initially scarce. The commercial EIT devices cannot provide absolute values, but only relative impedance changes (41). The reference values for healthy subjects have been explored but the lack of large sample size and various age makes it hard to interpret the results.

The Chinese experience and its implications for global practice

The Chinese experience with thoracic EIT, particularly from 2014 onward, presents a remarkable example for rapid clinical integration. While the timeline and scale of adoption have been documented (1), the underlying drivers are multifaceted. This section analyzes the distinctive structural, regulatory, and collaborative ecosystem that facilitated this translation, moving beyond description to examine the enabling and limiting factors.

The Chinese pathway

Exploring and early adoption [2014–2019]: after its approval in EU, the first commercial device (PulmoVista 500) for chest EIT was imported to China in 2014. Following the approval by China’s regulatory authority in 2017, EIT was initially adopted by leading tertiary hospitals. A previous review documented that within 5 years, 47 hospitals were equipped with 50 devices (42). Research focused on validating core applications like PEEP titration in ARDS and perioperative monitoring. The research groups and key opinion leaders published a series of scientific articles about the use of chest EIT.

Fast growth and domestic innovation (2020–present): the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic acted as a catalyst. Various clinical groups have shared their experience in COVID-19 management with EIT. The urgent need for effective ventilator management in heterogeneous lungs led to EIT’s inclusion in national treatment guidelines. This dramatically increased exposure and acceptance beyond elite academic centers. Concurrently, as highlighted in a recent review (1), over ten local Chinese companies began developing and manufacturing their own EIT devices, reducing costs and improving accessibility. This domestic innovation fostered a vibrant ecosystem for device improvement and application expansion. Deconstructing the Chinese ecosystem

The accelerated translation was not serendipitous but supported by a convergent model.

A model for rapid clinical validation: China’s vast, tiered hospital network allowed rapid piloting in leading tertiary centers followed by trickle-down dissemination. Huge and diverse patient population allows for the rapid accumulation of clinical evidence across a wide spectrum of diseases. The corresponding fields can benefit from this accelerated research output, which validates EIT’s utility in various clinical scenarios.

The emergence of multiple Chinese manufacturers drives competition, reduces device costs, and spurs innovation (e.g., towards more portable, user-friendly devices). This makes technology more accessible globally, particularly in resource-limited settings. Besides, it also drives the establishment of reimbursement from the national healthcare system.

Limitations and challenges of the Chinese model

While the Chinese pathway offers valuable lessons, it is not without its own limitations and ongoing challenges: the rapid generation of clinical data are often from single-center observational or RCT studies. They may introduce publication bias and require consolidation through larger, multi-center RCTs. A recent multi-center RCT suggests EIT should be used in a more dedicated manner (40). Implementation remains concentrated in major urban tertiary hospitals, with slower uptake in resource-limited regional centers, potentially exacerbating healthcare inequalities. Despite national insurance coverage for the procedure, securing consistent reimbursement at the local hospital level and fully integrating EIT into daily clinical workflows remain practical hurdles.

Future perspectives and a collaborative roadmap

To transition from successful “experience” to sustained, evidence-based “practice”, a concerted roadmap is essential. Research is the key to produce reliable evidence. The highest priority must be the design and execution of international, multi-center RCTs. These trials should investigate whether EIT-guided strategies, compared to standard care, improve definitive outcomes in specific, high-stakes populations [e.g., moderate-to-severe ARDS with recruitable lungs (43)]. Concurrently, research should focus on establishing references for various purposes. The next generation of EIT must focus on intelligence and integration to minimize the requirement of expertise in data interpretations. Development should prioritize artificial intelligence (AI)-powered, automated interpretation tools that provide clear, protocolized suggestions (e.g., “consider recruitment maneuver”, “PEEP potentially too high in dorsal regions”) to reduce expertise dependency. Engineers must work towards plug-and-play interoperability with ventilators and electronic medical records.

Nationally and internationally, efforts must strengthen standardized training and certification pathways to create a cohort of proficient clinicians. Professional societies should develop condition-specific clinical practice guidelines that incorporate EIT (e.g., for mechanical ventilation in ARDS, management of one-lung ventilation). Crucially, advocacy is needed to generate robust cost-effectiveness analyses to support applications for insurance reimbursement, which is the ultimate driver for equitable access. The accelerated clinical validation and market innovation observed in China highlight a viable model for technology translation. To build upon this momentum and transition from regional “experience” to global, evidence-based “practice”, international collaboration must be prioritized. This includes the formation of global research consortia to conduct definitive multi-center trials. Furthermore, advancing EIT towards intelligent, personalized care requires open science initiatives, such as sharing large, anonymized datasets to fuel the development of robust AI algorithms, and the establishment of universal data standards to ensure interoperability across platforms and facilitate widespread adoption. Finally, testing and adapting these technologies across diverse healthcare ecosystems will be crucial to ensure their effectiveness and accessibility worldwide, ultimately improving personalized respiratory care for a broader patient population.

Conclusions

In conclusion, thoracic EIT has matured from a promising research tool into a clinically impactful technology for personalized respiratory care. The challenges of cost, standardization, and evidence generation are real but possible to deal with. The Chinese experience over the past decade provides an invaluable roadmap, demonstrating that through concerted efforts in clinical research, domestic industrial support, and proactive leadership in standardization, the full potential of EIT can be realized for patients worldwide.
