Merge before Forget: A Single LoRA Continual Learning via Continual Merging
Fuli Qiao Mehrdad Mahdavi The Pennsylvania State University {fvq5015,mzm616}@psu.edu

Abstract
Parameter-efficient continual learning has emerged as a promising approach for large language models (LLMs) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting while enabling adaptation to new tasks. Current Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) continual learning techniques often retain and freeze previously learned LoRAs or generate data representations to overcome forgetting, typically utilizing these to support new LoRAs learn new tasks. However, these methods not only ignore growing computational memory with tasks and limited storage space but also suffer from potential task interference due to the lack of effective LoRA merging mechanisms. In this paper, we propose a novel continual learning method that orthogonally initializes and sequentially merges LoRAs updates into a single unified LoRA. Our method leverages orthogonal basis extraction from previously learned LoRA to initialize the learning of new tasks, further exploits the intrinsic asymmetry property of LoRA components by using a time-aware scaling mechanism to balance new and old knowledge during continual merging. Our approach maintains constant memory complexity with respect to the number of tasks, minimizes interference between past and new tasks via orthogonal basis initialization, and improves performance over asymmetric LoRA merging via adaptive scaling. We provide theoretical analysis to justify our design and conduct extensive experiments across diverse continual learning benchmarks using various Llama models, demonstrating the effectiveness and efficiency of our method.

Related Works

Continual Learning.

Continual learning aims to retain knowledge of previously learned tasks while adapting to new data. It faces two main challenges: (1) catastrophic forgetting
(
23

(i)
Rehearsal-based methods

(
18

stores task-specific gradients and projects new gradients to avoid interference with previous knowledge.

(ii)
Regularization-based methods
utilize constraints on the parameters of the model to prevent model updates of new tasks from interfering with knowledge acquired on previous tasks. Elastic weight consolidation,
EWC

(
13

, uses Fisher Information Matrix to identify and protect parameters critical for previous tasks. Orthogonal Gradient Descent,
OGD

(
5

, projects gradients of new tasks onto a subspace that preserves model outputs on previous tasks, while ensuring the direction remains effective for learning new tasks.

(iii)
Architecture-based methods
dynamically adjust the structure of the model to isolate task-specific weights or expand model capacity
(
20
;
38
)
.
Packnet

(
20

performs iterative pruning and network re-training.
Progressive Prompts

(
26

mitigate forgetting by maintaining a growing sequence of soft prompts, where each new task contributes an additional prompt.

3
Background and Motivation

In this section we formalize the problem setup and highlight the central trade-offs in continual learning with a single shared LoRA: preventing forgetting of past tasks while maintaining adaptability to new ones. We then outline why continual merging is both promising and nontrivial due to LoRA’s structural asymmetry and its behavior in the NTK regime, which motivates two principles developed in later subsections: (i) orthogonal initialization to reduce task interference, and (ii) asymmetry-aware merging to update LoRA components in a stable and effective manner. These insights establish the foundation for designing the proposed SLAO algorithm in Section
4
.

Problem setup.

Let
f_{\bm{W}_{0}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
denote a pre-trained model parameterized by
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, which remains frozen throughout the continual learning (CL) process. Here,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
represent the input and output spaces, respectively. We consider a sequence of
T
tasks. For each task
t\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}
, the model is continually fine-tuned using the LoRA algorithm based on its associated training dataset

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(\bm{X}_{t,i},\bm{Y}_{t,i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and evaluated on a separate test dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{t}=\{(\bm{X}^{\prime}_{t,i},\bm{Y}^{\prime}_{t,i})\}_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}
, where
N
and
N^{\prime}
denote the number of training and testing samples, respectively. The goal is to continually learn a single set of LoRA parameters, specifically, matrices
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
with
r\ll\min(m,n)
such that the resulting merged LoRA model remains competitive with models optimized for expected risk (multi-task objective):

\min_{\bm{B},\bm{A}}\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\nolimits_{(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{Y}_{t})\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(f_{\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}\bm{A}}(\bm{X}_{t}),\bm{Y}_{t}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
denotes the empirical risk (e.g., cross-entropy or mean squared error) for task
t
.

In the single shared LoRA setting for CL, we are restricted to maintaining only one pair of LoRA parameters, denoted by
\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t}
and
\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t}
, across all tasks. When task
t
arrives, we fine-tune on its training data, possibly initialized with current merged models
\{\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1},\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1}\}
, to obtain task-specific LoRA parameters
\bm{B}_{\text{ft},t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
\bm{A}_{\text{ft},t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
. The fine-tuned model for task
t
is represented as
f_{\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{\text{ft},t}\bm{A}_{\text{ft},t}}(\cdot)
. After fine-tuning, we
merge
the previously accumulated LoRA parameters
\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1}\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1}
with the new task-specific parameters
\bm{B}_{\text{ft},t}\bm{A}_{\text{ft},t}
, resulting in an updated
merge

\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t}\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t}
. Due to the inherent asymmetry in LoRA components, the merging of the LoRA components is performed separately for
\bm{B}
and
\bm{A}
as formalized below:

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t}=\text{Continual\text{Merge}\_B}(\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1};\bm{B}_{\text{ft},t}),\ \bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t}=\text{Continual\text{Merge}\_A}(\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t-1};\bm{A}_{\text{ft},t}),\ t\geq 2

where
\bm{B}_{\text{ft},0}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{A}_{\text{ft},0}

is initialized using a Gaussian distribution, following the standard LoRA initialization
(
9
)
.
\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{1}=\bm{B}_{\text{ft},1}
and
\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{1}=\bm{A}_{\text{ft},1}
are initialized as the first task fine-tuned LoRA, and
\bm{B}_{\text{merge}}^{t}\bm{A}_{\text{merge}}^{t}

3.1
Opportunities and Challenges in Continual Learning via Continual Merging

In this section, by contrasting freezing-based LoRA continual learning and model merging, we highlight how continual merging with LoRA continual learning fundamentally alters both the computational footprint and the learning objective, offering a scalable and efficient alternative for sequential task adaptation.

Storage and memory efficiency
is one of the core advantages of continual merging for CL. Unlike existing continual learning methods that benefit from freezing and retaining previously fine-tuned LoRAs, using continual merging after fine-tuning task
t
only requires storing a fixed number of LoRAs: (1) the current merged LoRA and (2) the fine-tuned LoRA to be merged. This strategy results in a constant memory complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|\bm{B}|+|\bm{A}|)=\mathcal{O}((m+n)r)
, where
|\bm{B}|+|\bm{A}|
denote the parameter sizes of a single LoRA. Critically, this memory requirement remains independent of the number of sequential tasks
T
. In contrast, as shown in Figure
2
, existing freezing-based continual learning methods require storing all LoRAs, incurring a linear memory complexity of
\mathcal{O}(T(|\bm{B}|+|\bm{A}|))
, which is
\mathcal{O}(T(m+n)r)
, growing linearly with the number of tasks.

Training efficiency
is an evident advantage of continual merging for CL. Prior works do not simply keep previous tasks’ LoRAs without any operations. Instead, when training new tasks, prior works use these multiple LoRAs during training through constraints, i.e., making new task LoRA parameters orthogonal to all previous LoRAs, heavily increasing computational cost during training. However, continual merging in CL would only use the parameters of a single previously fine-tuned LoRA to initialize new task LoRA parameters before training, avoiding extra computation during training.

Difference between continual learning and merging
is mainly in the objective. In the context of multi-task model merging, the task arithmetic property, as defined by
24
, refers to the ability to add task-specific vectors without interfering with performance on other tasks. However, in CL, the objective extends beyond retention: the model must both preserve previously acquired knowledge and generalize effectively to unseen data. Hence, while merging can support CL, its underlying objectives are not entirely equivalent to those of CL, leading to fundamental differences in both theoretical analysis and algorithmic design.

3.2
Orthogonal Initialization Motivated by LoRA NTK Analysis

To inform our algorithmic design, we evaluate the performance of CL using LoRA by two key metrics, forgetting and intransigence errors
(
15
)
, defined as below:

(1)
Forgetting error:
It measures how much knowledge of old tasks has been forgotten after learning the current task. Specifically, after learning task
t\in[2,T]
, the average forgetting over all old tasks
i\in[1,t-1]
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t-1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})\big)

(2)

In Equation
2
,
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})
denotes the performance difference between
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
(result after training task
i
) and
\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}
(result after training task
t
) on test data of task
i
.

(2)
Intransigence error:
It evaluates the ability of the algorithm to adapt to a new task after having already adapted to a sequence of old tasks.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\big)

(3)

In Equation
3
,
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*})
denotes the performance difference between
\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}
(optimal result of training task
i
) and
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
(result after training task
i
) on test data of task
i
.

To examine these errors, we draw on the empirical observation
(
19
)
that, when prompt-based fine-tuning is employed
(
29
;
6
)
, the fine-tuning of a pre-trained language model tends to remain within the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime. Specifically, under the NTK regime, assuming
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
, the empirical risk for task
t
using LoRA can be approximated as
(
12
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}\big(f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})+\langle\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\rangle,\bm{Y}_{i}\big)

(4)

As detailed in Appendix
A
(Lemma
1
), by extending the analysis in
12
;
22
, we show that, under the NTK regime, the term in forgetting error can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq G\|\langle\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i},\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})\rangle\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}^{(j)}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq GR\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}},

(5)

where
K
is output dimension and
\|\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}^{(j)}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq R
. Thus, to minimize Equation
2
, we should make
\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
as small as possible. Similarly, to minimize Equation
3
,
\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
should be minimized. Thus, the term in forgetting-intransigence decomposition can be written as:

\displaystyle\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\bm{B}_{t}(\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{A}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|(\bm{B}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i})\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|\bm{B}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|(\bm{B}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*})\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

(6)

Algorithmic motivation.
From above bound, we observe that forgetting-intransigence error in CL with LoRA depends asymmetrically on the choice of frozen and trainable components. For example, freezing
\bm{A}
and fine-tuning
\bm{B}
is at least as effective, if not better, than the reverse
(
52
)
. However, if we apply freezing
\bm{A}
in CL, then
\|\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
but
\|\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
may be unintentionally increased due to random
\bm{A}_{i}
. Instead, if we propose to fine-tune
\bm{A}_{i}
and extract orthogonal basis
\bm{Q}_{i-1}
from
\bm{A}_{i-1}
, where
\bm{Q}_{i-1}\bm{Q}_{i-1}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}
, to initialize
\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}
via
\bm{Q}_{i-1}
, then we have
\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}(\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)})^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}
where

, thereby minimizing both

and

. This motivates our design of orthogonal initialization. The complete derivation is in Appendix

Then, for the operation of merging
\bm{B}
, we build on parameter-efficient module linear arithmetic composition, including addition and negation. Linear connectivity implies that model parameters fine-tuned from the same pretrained checkpoint can be added to improve generalization
(
40
)
, a property that extends to PEFT adapters, whose small updates likewise allow linear composition
(
46
)
. Hence, we can write merging operations on
\bm{B}
using task vectors as

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{merge}=\bm{B}_{merge}+\lambda\cdot(\bm{B}_{new}-\bm{B}_{merge})

(12)

This makes the foundation of the operation of merging
\bm{B}
in continual merging.

4
Methodology

Drawing on the insights outlined in the preceding section, we now trun to introducing the SLAO algorithm that incrementally integrates all task-specific LoRA updates into a single continually updated LoRA. SLAO is designed to preserve past information while enabling efficient adaptation by combining four core principles: enforcing orthogonality across tasks to mitigate forgetting, continually merging updates to control memory footprint, respecting the intrinsic asymmetry between LoRA’s components in a time-aware manner to ensure stable accumulation of knowledge. Together, these insights allow SLAO to maintain a compact yet expressive representation of all learned tasks, providing a simple, scalable, and effective solution for LoRA-based continual learning. In what follows, we first introduce the proposed algorithm and then analyze its parameters’ dynamics to illustrate its effectiveness.

SLAO: Single LoRA Continual Learning

We propose
SLAO
to utilize continual merging into a single LoRA to minimize task interference and improve generalization for CL.
SLAO
is motivated by four key insights: (1)
Orthogonal Retention:
To minimize forgetting error and intransigence error, it is crucial to maintain orthogonality in the LoRA components across tasks; (2)
Continual Merging:
To reduce memory usage in LoRA-based CL, continually merging new task fine-tuned LoRA updates into a single
merge
d LoRA is a highly efficient strategy; (3)
Asymmetry of LoRA:
Given the distinct learning roles of LoRA components
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, they should be handled separately; and (4)
Time-aware Scaling:
To retain prior knowledge while adapting to new tasks, the merging process for new LoRA updates should be scaled in a time-aware manner that reflects its training trajectory.

The
SLAO
consists of two main operations: (1) Initialize each new task learning LoRA by extracted orthogonal basis from the previous task’s fine-tuned LoRA; (2) After fine-tuning on the new task, we utilize the asymmetry of LoRA components to employ adaptive time-varying scaling for new LoRA updates to merge into the merged LoRA. The complete procedure is outlined in Algorithm
1
and illustrated in Figure
3
. Starting with the first task’s fine-tuned LoRA
\bm{B}_{\text{ft},1}\bm{A}_{\text{ft},1}
by standard fine-tuning, our method iteratively integrates LoRA updates of subsequent tasks in a continual fashion.

Orthogonal basis extraction for initialization.
For new task
i

\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i-1}

\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)}

\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)}(\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)})^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}

\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i-1}

\bm{Q}_{i}\bm{R}_{i}=QR((\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i-1})^{\top})\ \rightarrow\ \bm{Q}_{i}=\bm{Q}_{i}\cdot\text{sign}(\text{diag}(\bm{R}_{i}))^{\top}\ \rightarrow\ \bm{A}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)}=\bm{Q}_{i}^{\top}

(13)

As a result, the initialization
\bm{A}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)}

\bm{B}

\bm{B}_{\text{ft},i}^{(0)}

After fine-tuning task

where

Dynamics of SLAO

To better understand the effectiveness of
SLAO
, inspired by the analysis of
8

Theorem 1
.

Let the parameters

\bm{A}
and

\bm{B}
be updated using SGD at each step

s
for task

i
as follows:

\bm{A}_{i}^{s+1}=\bm{A}_{i}^{s}-\eta(\bm{B}_{i}^{s})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{s}),\ \ \bm{B}_{i}^{s+1}=\bm{B}_{i}^{s}-\eta(\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{s})(\bm{A}_{i}^{s})^{\top}\\

(15)

where
\eta
is the learning rate. We assume
\bm{A}_{i}^{s}=\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}+\eta\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}f_{A}(s)
and
\bm{B}_{i}^{s}=\bm{B}_{i}^{(0)}+\eta f_{B}(s)(\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)})^{\top}
holds with such functions
f_{A}
and
f_{B}
for
1,\dots,s
, and
\|\sum_{s=1}^{S}\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq L
for every
S
during training task
i
, which implies that the model stays within a finite Euclidean ball. If we assume
\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}(\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)})^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}

\bm{A}_{i}
satisfies
\|f_{A}(s)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\eta L^{2}(1-(\eta^{2}L^{2})^{s})}{1-\eta^{2}L^{2}}

\bm{B}

f_{B}(s)=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}(\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j})(\eta f_{A}^{\top}(j)+\bm{I})

f_{B}(s)\approx-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}(\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j})
. Thus

\Delta\bm{B}_{i}=-\eta\big(\sum_{s=0}^{S}(\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{s})\big)(\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)})^{\top}
.

The proof is deferred to Appendix

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel parameter-efficient continual learning based on continual merging of LoRA, enabling no additional training or access to any data representations. Our approach leverages the orthogonal basis from previous fine-tuned LoRA to initialize for new task learning and constructs a single shared merged LoRA via time-aware scaling, thus ensuring constant memory usage regardless of task number. Through comprehensive experiments, we demonstrated the effectiveness and efficiency of our method across multiple benchmarks and model scales.

\mathcal{A}_{D}=\{\bm{X}_{i}\rightarrow f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})+\langle\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{\delta}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\|\bm{\delta}\|_{*}\leq D,\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\}
is class of affine predictors with bounded nuclear norm
D
. For
1\leq j\leq K
, suppose
\|\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}^{(j)}(\bm{X})\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq R
almost surely with respect to the random data
\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}
. For
1\leq i\leq N
, suppose
\ell_{i}=\ell(\cdot,\bm{Y}_{i})
is G-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{A}
on the first argument (with respect to the Euclidean norm) for almost surely with respect to the random data
\bm{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}
. That is

\displaystyle|\ell_{i}(a(\bm{X}_{1}))-\ell_{i}(a^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{2}))|\leq G\|a(\bm{X}_{1})-a^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{2})\|_{2}\quad\textit{for any }a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A},\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}

(16)

Proof.

First, let
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying the following property with
c>0
:

\displaystyle|g(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{i+1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N})-g(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{X}_{i+1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N})|\leq c

(17)

for all
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N},\bm{X}_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|g(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N})-\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N})]|\geq\epsilon)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{Nc^{2}}\right)

(18)

Take
g
to be
g=\sup_{\|\bm{\delta}\|\leq D}(\mathcal{\hat{L}}(\bm{\delta}_{0})-\mathcal{\hat{L}}(\bm{\delta})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\delta}_{0})+\mathcal{L}(\bm{\delta}))
, which is a function of
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N}
. Since
\|\bm{\delta}\|_{*}\leq D
implies
\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq D
and by the Lipschitz continuity of
\ell(\cdot,\bm{Y}_{i})
, we have the following for any
(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})\in\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle|\ell(f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})+\langle\bm{G}(\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{\delta}_{0}\rangle,\bm{Y}_{i})-\ell(f_{\bm{W}_{0}}\bm{X}_{i})+\langle\bm{G}(\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{\delta}\rangle,\bm{Y}_{i})|\leq G\|\langle\bm{\delta}_{0}-\bm{\delta},\bm{G}(\bm{X}_{i})\rangle\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{\delta}_{0}-\bm{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|\bm{G}^{(j)}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{\delta}_{0}-\bm{\delta}\|_{*}^{2}\|\bm{G}^{(j)}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}4D^{2}\cdot R^{2}}

\displaystyle=2GRD\sqrt{K}.

(19)

∎

Thus, from this lemma, we apply it to LoRA-based continual learning and have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq G\|\langle\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i},\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})\rangle\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{W}}f_{\bm{W}_{0}}^{(j)}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}R^{2}}

(20)

where
K
is the output dimension. From this, we can see that to minimize forgetting error, we should make
\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
as small as possible. Similarly, to minimize intransigence error,
\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
should be also small. Besides, it’s evident that
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})=0
. Thus, if we makes
\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq D
and
\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq D
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}+\mathcal{I}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t-1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})\big)+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})\big)+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})\big)+\big(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}R^{2}}+G\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}R^{2}}

\displaystyle=GR\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}+\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4DGR\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{K}

(21)

To make both
\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
minimized, the forgetting-intransigence decomposition can be written as:

\displaystyle\|\bm{B}_{t}\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\bm{B}_{t}(\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{A}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|(\bm{B}_{t}-\bm{B}_{i})\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|\bm{B}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A}_{i}^{*})\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|(\bm{B}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*})\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

(22)

Algorithmic motivation.
From the above bound, we observe that generalization error in CL with LoRA depends asymmetrically on the choice of frozen and trainable components. Interestingly, this insight contrasts with standard fine-tuning practices. For example, as concluded in
[
52
]
, freezing
\bm{A}
and fine-tuning
\bm{B}
is at least as effective, if not better, than the reverse. However, if we apply freezing
\bm{A}
in CL, it implies
\|\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
, which may unintentionally increase
\|\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
due to limited task-specific expressiveness. In
9
,
\bm{A}
is initialized to a random Gaussian matrix satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}^{(0)}(\bm{A}^{(0)})^{\top}]=\bm{I}_{r}
. Instead, if we propose to fine-tune
\bm{A}_{i}
while extracting orthogonal basis
\bm{Q}_{i-1}
from
\bm{A}_{i-1}
, where
\bm{Q}_{i-1}\bm{Q}_{i-1}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}
, to initialize
\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}
via
\bm{Q}_{i-1}
, then we will have
\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)}(\bm{A}_{i}^{(0)})^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}
where
i\in[1,\dots,t]
. This orthonormal structure not only keeps geometric consistency across tasks but also allows
\bm{A}_{j}

(t\geq j>i)
, to remain well-aligned with previous
\bm{A}_{i}
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{j}\bm{A}_{i}^{\top}]\approx\bm{I}_{r}
, thereby minimizing both
\|\bm{A}_{t}-\bm{A}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\|\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A}_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. This motivates our design of continual merging with orthogonal initialization to reduce forgetting and maintain adaptability.

Appendix B
Experiments Details

Our experiments are conducted on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs using the DeepSpeed repository. We evaluate five LLMs: Llama-2-7B-chat, Llama-2-13B-chat, Llama-3-2-3B, Qwen2.5-3B, and Qwen2.5-7B. Each individual experiment (e.g., running a single task order from the large number of tasks benchmark on Llama-2-13B-chat) can be executed on a single A100 GPU. We apply LoRA to the query and value projection matrices in the attention modules of each Llama model, with a fixed rank of 8. Each task order is evaluated over 3 random seeds.

For Standard CL benchmark and the large number of tasks benchmark on Llama-2-7B-chat and Llama-2-13B-chat, we follow the setting in
36
, and train the models with one epoch, a constant learning rate of 1e-4, the training batch size is 1, and the gradient accumulation step is 8. And for Standard CL benchmark and the large number of tasks benchmark on Llama-3-2-3B, we set the learning rate as 1e-4. Comparison of orthogonality among LoRA-based CL methods

•

Initialization:

(a)

O-LoRA: New task’s LoRA
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
is randomly initialized and is not orthogonal to previous tasks’ LoRAs
\{\bm{B}_{1}\bm{A}_{1},\dots,\bm{B}_{i-1}\bm{A}_{i-1}\}
.

(b)

InfLoRA: Use all of new task
i
data to compute its input matrix
\bm{H}_{i}
on current full model parameters, and use all previous
i-1
tasks’ gradient spaces which denote as
\bm{M}_{i}
to make new
\bm{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
lie in
\bm{N}_{i}\cap\bm{M}_{i}^{\perp}
where
\bm{N}_{i}
is the subspace spanned by the columns of
\bm{H}_{i}
.
\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
is initialized as zero.

(c)

SLAO: Extract orthogonal basis from previous fine-tuned
\bm{A}_{i-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
as new task’s
\bm{A}_{i}
, which makes
\bm{A}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{r}
.
\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
is initialized as previous fine-tuned
\bm{B}_{i-1}
.

•

Training:

(a)

O-LoRA: Compute orthogonal loss to make new task’s
\bm{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
orthogonal to all previous tasks’
\bm{A}
, and update
\bm{A}_{i}
and
\bm{B}_{i}
.

(b)

InfLoRA: Compute standard cross entropy loss and update
\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
.

(c)

SLAO: Compute standard cross entropy loss and update
\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\bm{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
.

•

Post-Training:

(a)

O-LoRA: Store all tasks’ LoRA
\{\bm{B}_{1}\bm{A}_{1},\dots,\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}\}
.

(b)

InfLoRA: Use new task
i
data to compute new task input matrix
\bm{R}_{i}
on new learned
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
, then compute new gradient orthogonal bases memory
\bm{M}_{i}
through DualGPM, where
\bm{M}_{i}
represents the gradient space of all
i
tasks. Then, integrate
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{A}_{i}
to
\bm{W}_{i-1}
and store the updated gradient space
\bm{M}_{i}
of all
i
tasks.

(c)

SLAO: Merge
\bm{B}_{i}
to previously merged
\bm{B}_{i-1}
and keep fine-tuned
\bm{B}_{i}
, merged
\bm{B}_{i}
, and fine-tuned
\bm{A}_{i}
.

Overall, InfLoRA and SLAO both focus on the orthogonality of initialization and post-training, while O-LoRA focuses on the orthogonality of the updating process during training. Moreover, for initialization, InfLoRA makes new
\bm{A}_{i}
lie at the intersection of input matrix and previous gradient spaces
\bm{M}_{i}
, while SLAO extracts orthogonal basis from previous fine-tuned
\bm{A}_{i-1}
as new
\bm{A}_{i}
; for post-training, InfLoRA computes and stores all previous tasks’ orthogonal gradient spaces, while SLAO uses the asymmetry of LoRA to obtain a merged
\bm{B}
.
