What drives people to use self-driving cars? An integrated model
Authors: Ziguang Dong, Thuy Dung Pham Thi, Nam Tien Duong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the factors associated with users’ behavioral intentions to adopt self-driving cars by integrating the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Task–Technology Fit (TTF), and self-efficacy. Data from 293 self-driving car users were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM). The results show that TTF is positively associated with both perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU), underscoring the importance of system–task alignment. However, PEOU is not significantly related to PU, diverging from traditional TAM assumptions, while PU’s association with behavioral intention is fully mediated by user attitudes. Self-efficacy and opinion leadership are positively related to TTF, highlighting the role of individual capability and social influence. System features such as interactivity and flexibility are positively related to user perceptions, whereas inadequate system guidelines show a negative association with TTF. These findings provide theoretical and practical insights for improving user acceptance of autonomous vehicles by emphasizing self-efficacy development, opinion leadership, and user-centered system design.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of technology and the increasing accessibility of autonomous systems, the demand for user-friendly and reliable transportation solutions has surged [1]. Self-driving cars offer a revolutionary approach that transcends the limitations of human driving, providing users with a seamless, efficient, and innovative transportation experience [2]. Unlike traditional vehicles, self-driving cars boast distinct advantages, such as high adaptability to various driving conditions, interactive systems for user engagement, and advanced safety features that enhance trust and convenience [3]. However, challenges remain in structuring user interactions effectively and ensuring that the technology supports smooth communication and integration with users' routines, thus avoiding concerns about trust, safety, or disengagement. Developing high-quality self-driving systems has become a priority for automotive companies and researchers, as understanding user behavior, trust, and acceptance in these autonomous environments is critical to maximizing the benefits of self-driving technology [4]. Beyond their technical promise, self-driving cars represent a profound shift in human–machine interaction, where automation assumes tasks traditionally performed by drivers, making psychological adaptation, trust formation, and perceived agency central to adoption outcomes.

Previous research on user behavior toward technology has primarily emphasized either cognitive theories [5, 6] or affective theories [7, 8]. Focusing on only one dimension can lead to an incomplete understanding, especially in the context of emerging technologies like self-driving cars. Therefore, this study integrates both cognitive and affective perspectives to examine how the unique characteristics of self-driving vehicles impact users’ cognitive trust and emotional responses to transportation. This holistic approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of how self-driving technology can improve user experiences and is a key motivation for this research. In doing so, we extend psychological insight into automation trust and perceived agency by exploring how cognitive appraisal and emotional engagement jointly shape user adoption intentions in high-autonomy systems.

In the context of technology adoption literature, both the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [9] and Task-Technology Fit (TTF) [10] have demonstrated strong explanatory power in understanding how users adopt and effectively utilize technology. However, integrating TAM and TTF models yields even greater insight into user behavior [11, 12]. Furthermore, social cognitive theory, widely supported by scholars, highlights self-efficacy as a crucial factor influencing user behavior [13]. Therefore, combining TAM, TTF, and self-efficacy to develop and validate a theoretical model that explains users' behavioral intention to adopt self-driving cars serves as a key motivation for this study. This integration moves beyond a direct application of established models by framing self-efficacy not merely as a personal belief variable, but as a psychological mechanism that influences both perceived ease of use and the cognitive fit between user capabilities and autonomous driving systems.

Vietnam presents a compelling and underexplored context for autonomous vehicle (AV) adoption due to its distinctive transportation ecosystem, rapid economic growth, and evolving policy framework. The country is characterized by high motorcycle density, heterogeneous traffic flows, and complex road conditions, which pose unique operational challenges for AV integration compared to markets with standardized road infrastructures [14]. Coupled with one of the fastest-growing middle-class populations in Southeast Asia, rising disposable incomes, and active government initiatives—such as the National Digital Transformation Program and smart city projects—Vietnam offers a dynamic environment where AV adoption is shaped by both infrastructural complexity and increasing technology readiness [15]. Studying AV adoption in this setting provides an opportunity to extend TAM and TTF insights beyond developed economies, revealing how task–technology alignment, self-efficacy, and local socio-economic factors interact in influencing behavioral intentions [16]. This contextual focus also allows us to examine psychological dimensions of AV acceptance, such as trust formation under uncertainty and adaptation to automation in culturally and infrastructurally distinct environments.

Building on the motivations mentioned, this study will conduct a survey among users who have experienced self-driving cars in Vietnam. In this study, the term “self-driving cars” refers specifically to conditionally and fully autonomous vehicles—primarily SAE Levels 3 and 4—where the automated driving system performs all or most dynamic driving tasks under defined conditions but may require driver intervention in certain scenarios. This scope reflects the types of autonomous driving technologies currently being trialed and discussed in the Vietnamese market.

This study aims to achieve the following objectives: (1) To integrate the TTF model, TAM, and self-efficacy into a unified framework that advances theoretical understanding of how cognitive, affective, and fit-based factors jointly shape user adoption intentions for self-driving cars. (2) To further analyze how key factors associated with user engagement with self-driving cars correlate with their behavioral intention to adopt this innovative technology.

Theoretical background

Self-driving cars

This study aims to integrate the TTF model, the TAM, and self-efficacy to construct and validate a theoretical model explaining users' behavioral intention to adopt self-driving cars. To accomplish the research objectives, it is essential to clarify the nature of self-driving car systems. Kuru [3] and Goodall [17] emphasize that self-driving cars leverage advanced autonomous technology to enhance driving safety, convenience, and efficiency. These systems should meet three fundamental criteria: being adaptive and user-centric, using standardized autonomous vehicle technology, and emphasizing innovative practices that extend beyond traditional driving methods [18].

Self-driving cars can fulfill three key user needs: unrestricted access to autonomous driving capabilities, seamless integration with user preferences, and balancing advanced automation with user control. Unrestricted access refers to enabling users to rely on autonomous driving features in various environments and conditions. These capabilities encompass four dimensions: connection to smart infrastructure, access to reliable navigation systems, user flexibility in customizing settings, and compatibility with diverse driving scenarios. Seamless integration ensures that the system reliably processes, analyzes, and organizes real-time data to provide accurate and efficient driving assistance. Balancing advanced automation with user control means maintaining a harmonious relationship between the system's autonomous functions and user intervention, as merely automating traditional driving tasks without leveraging the full potential of autonomous technology does not constitute a true self-driving experience [17].

According to the definition of self-driving systems and the requirements for users to engage effectively with autonomous vehicles above, this study posits that the essential characteristics of self-driving car systems include flexibility (the system must adapt to different driving contexts and user preferences), compatibility (the system's features and functions must align with users' prior experiences, values, and current transportation needs), and interactivity (emphasizing effective user-system interaction to balance automation with user control). These characteristics are likely to influence users' behavioral intentions and, in turn, affect their self-driving car usage behavior.

Theoretical foundation

Understanding self-driving car adoption requires explaining how cognitive, social, and technological factors jointly shape user evaluations and behavioral intentions. This study integrates the TRA, the TAM, the TTF model, and self-efficacy to build a unified framework that captures these dimensions.

TRA, TPB, and TAM

The TRA [19] proposes that attitudes—personal evaluations of a behavior—and subjective norms—perceived social pressure—determine behavioral intentions. The TPB [20] extends this model by adding perceived behavioral control (PBC), which reflects an individual’s sense of control over performing a behavior. Together, these theories highlight that intention results from both internal evaluations and external constraints.

The TAM [9], derived from TRA, refines these determinants into two cognitive beliefs: PU—the belief that a technology enhances performance—and PEOU—the belief that it requires minimal effort. TAM posits that PU and PEOU shape attitudes and behavioral intention, and have become the dominant predictors in technology adoption research. However, TAM assumes stable beliefs and omits contextual and task factors that may shape adoption in complex systems such as self-driving cars [21]. Thus, extending TAM with complementary theories improves explanatory power.

Task–Technology Fit (TTF)

The TTF model [10] posits that technology is adopted when its features fit the tasks users must perform. Fit arises from alignment among task requirements, technological capabilities, and user characteristics. In the self-driving car context, fit depends on how well automated driving functions (e.g., navigation, lane-keeping, obstacle avoidance) support real-world driving needs [22, 23]. High TTF enhances PU, satisfaction, and safety by ensuring that system capabilities effectively complement human goals and constraints. Empirical evidence shows that users are more willing to adopt advanced automation when the system’s design aligns with their driving styles and expectations [24].

Self-efficacy and capability beliefs

Self-efficacy, derived from social cognitive theory [13], refers to individuals’ beliefs in their ability to execute a behavior successfully. In autonomous driving, self-efficacy captures confidence in interacting with and supervising self-driving systems under varying traffic and environmental conditions. Users with higher self-efficacy perceive greater ease of use and stronger task alignment [25]. Conceptually related to PBC Ajzen, I. (2020). Building on the self-efficacy-based value adoption model (SVAM) [27], this study posits that self-efficacy enhances perceived ease of use and task fit, thereby contributing to perceived value and adoption intention. Users confident in managing automation are more likely to trust its functionality, perceive it as intuitive, and evaluate it as aligned with their driving needs [28, 29].

Integrated perspective

Integrating TAM, TTF, and self-efficacy provides a multidimensional view of self-driving car adoption. TAM explains cognitive evaluations (usefulness and ease of use), TTF contextualizes these beliefs within system–task alignment, and self-efficacy introduces a psychological enabler that strengthens both. This integration captures cognitive, affective, and capability-based pathways that jointly shape adoption intentions in high-autonomy contexts. The resulting model thus extends prior frameworks by situating perceived fit and user capability at the center of autonomous vehicle acceptance.

Hypotheses development and conceptual model

Both TAM and TTF aim to explain users' adoption and evaluation of self-driving cars. Based on attitude/behavior theories, TAM emphasizes that users' attitudes and beliefs are the primary determinants of whether they choose to adopt self-driving cars. TAM focuses on the technology itself rather than its capabilities, assuming that users do not adopt self-driving cars because they enjoy the technology, but because it enhances their driving outcomes, such as safety and convenience. However, TAM lacks a dimension that accounts for how task characteristics align with the capabilities of self-driving cars, leading to inconsistent results when applied to complex systems like autonomous vehicles [30]. TAM includes some knowledge about tasks; it does not explicitly differentiate between the nature of the tasks and the functionalities of the technology. Explicitly defining and incorporating task characteristics into the model can improve its ability to explain self-driving car adoption in practical contexts.

The TTF model supports that users adopt self-driving cars because of the benefits these cars provide, such as improved safety, reduced driving effort, and better travel efficiency, rather than solely due to their attitudes toward the technology [31]. Within TTF, “fit” emerges from the precise alignment between the measurable attributes of the driving task and the objectively verifiable capabilities of the self-driving system. The characteristics of driving tasks and the alignment of self-driving cars' functionalities are key factors influencing user behavior. The emphasis on TTF complements TAM’s focus, addressing its limitation in considering the task–technology pairing process and how well the system’s technical attributes meet specific driving demands.

Dishaw and Strong [32] were the first to attempt integrating TAM and TTF, and the results showed that the integrated TAM/TTF model could enhance the model’s explanatory power regarding the use of technology in task-oriented contexts. Al-Rahmi et al. [33] used TTF to supplement the integrated model in studying online behaviors, and their results also demonstrated that the integrated model had greater explanatory variance than the standalone TAM model.

Therefore, this study combines both TTF and TAM theories, applying them to the context of self-driving cars to verify which features of the technology effectively support users’ driving needs and align with their driving characteristics. By explicitly defining both Task Characteristics and Technology Characteristics, the model addresses conceptual ambiguity in prior work and enables clearer measurement and validation. The model aims to explain the degree to which the alignment of driving task requirements and the functionality of self-driving cars affects users' intentions to adopt these vehicles. Additionally, this approach leverages TTF to explain external variables in TAM, enhancing the model’s explanatory power regarding technology adoption behavior.

In the TTF framework, “Task Characteristics” are defined as the specific attributes of the driving activities that a user must perform when interacting with a self-driving car [10]. In this study, we extend this definition to capture three critical aspects: (1) Non-routineness – the degree to which a driving task involves uncertainty, variability, or unique problem-solving demands. In the self-driving context, high non-routineness may occur in unpredictable urban traffic, construction zones, or sudden hazard avoidance, requiring the autonomous system to adapt rapidly and assist decision-making under ambiguity. (2) Interdependence – the extent to which the task requires coordination between the self-driving car and external agents (e.g., other vehicles, pedestrians, traffic control systems). High interdependence might include cooperative lane merging, vehicle platooning, or responding to hand signals from traffic officers. (3) Complexity – the cognitive and perceptual demands placed on the user by a task, such as interpreting real-time navigation changes, monitoring multi-sensory system feedback, or managing multiple control modes (manual vs. autonomous).

We further anchor these characteristics in measurable, scenario-based items for empirical testing. For example, “navigation complexity” is operationalized as the perceived difficulty of system-assisted route adjustments in high-density traffic; “environmental conditions” as the system’s ability to handle diverse weather, lighting, and road surface conditions; and “driving scenarios” as specific operational contexts (e.g., highway cruising, city intersections, or rural roads) that differ in their non-routineness and interdependence levels.

Extending this to the context of self-driving technology, driving inherently involves high non-routineness and varying degrees of interdependence. Relevant studies indicate that factors such as accessibility, system adaptability, user characteristics (such as experience and knowledge), responsiveness, and ease of use influence whether users engage with self-driving cars [34]. Padmaja et al. [35] found that system characteristics affecting user satisfaction with autonomous systems include user-friendliness, system reliability, navigation precision, and personalization. Additionally, Li and Shang [36] found that usability, quality, and perceived value positively influenced user satisfaction and their likelihood of continued usage.

In summary, driving task requirements in this study refer to the process of achieving safe, efficient, and adaptive driving performance in diverse conditions, with task characteristics including the quality of self-driving system instructions, particularly their adaptability, responsiveness, and ability to assist in decision-making during complex driving scenarios.

Technology refers to the tools that users rely on to complete driving tasks [10]. The system's features can be analyzed to understand how they provide resources for users to complete driving tasks [37]. Supporting various user needs during the driving process is a key factor in helping users achieve trust, confidence, and satisfaction with autonomous systems. In this context, “interactivity” is defined as the degree to which users can participate in modifying the form or content of a mediated environment in real time [38]. Applied to self-driving cars, this refers to human–system exchanges that enable immediate system feedback, adaptive navigation support, and multi-modal communication channels (e.g., visual HUD displays, conversational voice commands, and adaptive alerts). Such interactivity allows self-driving systems to respond dynamically to users’ preferences and situational needs, enhancing perceived control, trust, and engagement—key determinants of sustained technology acceptance in high-stakes contexts.

Similarly, “system guidelines” are grounded in human factors and usability standards, conceptualized in line with the principles of affordances and constraints and the ISO 9241–210:2019 framework on human-centered design [39, 40]. These guidelines refer to structured, context-sensitive instructions—whether embedded in the in-vehicle interface, adaptive prompts, or user manuals—that promote safe, efficient, and intended interactions. In self-driving cars, system guidelines function as procedural scaffolds that help users align their behavior with the technology’s operational logic, thereby reducing cognitive load and minimizing misuse or disuse. This aligns with Kuru [3] and Leikas et al. [41], who highlight that advanced autonomous systems embed not just functionality but also value-laden usage objectives into their design.

Given that self-driving cars offer users the flexibility to access advanced driving capabilities without constraints of time or effort, the term “flexibility” is adopted here in line with the IS success framework [42] as the degree to which a system can be adapted to user preferences, contexts, and tasks. This includes allowing users to customize routes, manage driving modes (e.g., eco-friendly vs. high-performance), dynamically allocate control between manual and autonomous operation, and tailor feedback modalities. The flexibility construct also extends to environmental adaptability, such as system performance across diverse conditions (urban congestion, highways, adverse weather) and seamless integration with real-time traffic, route planning, and remote-control functionalities [43, 44]. Thus, this study identifies interactivity, system guidelines, and flexibility—each concretely linked to identifiable technological capabilities of self-driving systems—as the three core dimensions that define the “Technology Characteristics” construct within the TTF framework.

Personal characteristics are factors that impact how easily or with what difficulty users engage with self-driving cars. Research has consistently demonstrated that when users possess high self-efficacy, their performance and adoption likelihood improve [45]. Similarly, if users have prior experience using autonomous vehicles, these experiences shape their future driving behavior—whether positive or negative [13]. In the literature on technology innovation, users who are more open to new systems, such as self-driving cars, tend to take risks [46]. Begum et al. [47] defined innovation as the process by which individuals perceive and engage with a new idea, method, or technology. The adoption of self-driving cars is influenced by three primary factors: users’ innovativeness, peer influence, and the characteristics of the self-driving system itself.

Studies on user innovativeness show that opinion leadership significantly influences innovation-related behaviors, such as the adoption of self-driving cars [48]. Within the realm of autonomous vehicle systems, individuals exhibiting strong opinion leadership tendencies are likely to be early adopters, actively shaping broader public attitudes and acceptance of self-driving cars. Early adopters with high levels of innovativeness are more likely to perceive self-driving cars as compatible with their needs and, as a result, have higher adoption intentions (Eastlick, 1993; Hoffer and Alexander, 1992). Wang et al. [49] highlighted that users' perception of system compatibility positively influences adoption intentions. Similarly, in the context of self-driving cars, prior experience with advanced technologies and the perceived compatibility of autonomous vehicles with users’ driving habits and preferences significantly impact their intention to adopt these systems [50].

Therefore, this study incorporates the constructs of self-efficacy, opinion leadership, and compatibility to represent the personality traits of users adopting self-driving cars. Method

Measures

This study primarily adopts the TAM, self-efficacy, and the TTF model as the theoretical framework to investigate the intentions when adopting self-driving cars, specifically SAE Levels 3 and 4. Apart from the construct of users' self-efficacy, all other constructs are measured using a 7-point Likert scale. The survey design is tailored to align with the research theme, ensuring the questionnaire items are appropriately revised and phrased to facilitate data collection for this study.

Self-efficacy, adapted from Compeau and Higgins [56], refers to the belief that users have in their ability to effectively use self-driving cars for transportation tasks. In this context, self-efficacy reflects users’ confidence in operating conditionally and fully autonomous vehicles, shaped by prior driving experiences and the challenges they have encountered. Self-efficacy is measured using a 10-point Likert scale, where users rate their confidence levels from "not confident at all" (1) to "completely confident" (10) across five items. For instance, “Even if no one is available to assist me, I am confident in operating the self-driving car","If I observe others using a self-driving car, I am confident I can manage it effectively"; "With sufficient time to practice, I am confident in using the system successfully"; "If online support tools are available, I am confident in handling the system"; "If I have previously used similar driving systems, I am confident in adapting to self-driving cars."

Opinion leadership, adapted from Venkatraman [57], refers to users who are proactive in adopting self-driving cars, innovative in their driving behaviors, and active in sharing insights about autonomous driving with others. Opinion leadership is measured with two items, such as "My peers rely on me to explore and introduce self-driving cars," and "My peers ask for my recommendations on self-driving cars."

Compatibility, adapted from Rogers et al. [58], refers to the degree to which users perceive that self-driving cars align with their driving habits, past experiences, and current needs. Compatibility is measured with two items, such as "Using self-driving cars fits well with my driving preferences," and "Self-driving technology aligns with my transportation habits and values."

Flexibility, adapted from Khare et al. [59], pertains to the ability of self-driving cars to accommodate users' individual needs and preferences. Flexibility is measured with two items, such as "I can control the driving mode to suit my preferences," "I can adjust the timing and routes of my trips," and "The self-driving car offers flexible options for when and where I can use it."

Interactivity, adapted from Daft and Lengel [60], refers to the degree to which self-driving cars facilitate communication and engagement, allowing users to interact seamlessly with the system and address their queries effectively. Interactivity is measured with three items such as "The self-driving system supports interactive features that meet my driving needs," "I can receive immediate feedback from the system," and "The self-driving car enables meaningful interactions for trip planning."

System guidelines, adapted from Brooke [61], refer to the protocols and rules guiding users on how to utilize self-driving car systems effectively. The two items include: “I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use self-driving car systems”, and “I found the self-driving car systems unnecessarily complex”.

Instructional content, adapted from Zhang et al. [62], refers to the organization and presentation of information within the self-driving car system interface, designed to make it easy for users to comprehend and engage with. The three items include: “intuitive and user-friendly display formats that facilitate clear understanding”, “retention of information related to the system's features and functionality”, and “assists in exploring key aspects of the system and effectively utilizing its capabilities for driving needs”.

TTF, adapted from Goodhue and Thompson [10], refers to the extent to which self-driving cars support users in accomplishing their transportation tasks by providing relevant and compatible features and functionalities. TTF is measured with three items, such as "The self-driving car provides detailed and relevant driving support," "The features are well-suited to my driving needs," and "The system effectively supports my transportation activities."

PEOU, adapted from Davis [9], refers to the extent to which users believe that self-driving cars are easy to operate. PEOU is measured with two items, such as "The features provided by the self-driving car are intuitive and user-friendly," and "It is easy for me to accomplish driving tasks using this system."

PU, adapted from Davis [9], refers to the degree to which users believe that self-driving cars will enhance their driving experience and help them achieve their transportation objectives effectively. PU is measured with two items, such as "Using this self-driving car improves my driving efficiency," and "The self-driving car facilitates safer and more reliable travel."

Attitudes, adapted from Taylor and Todd [63], refer to users' overall feelings, both positive and negative, about using self-driving cars. Attitude is measured with three items, such as "Using self-driving cars is a good idea," "I enjoy using self-driving cars for transportation tasks," and "The system makes driving more convenient."

Behavioral Intentions, adapted from Ajzen [19], refer to users' intent to use self-driving cars for future transportation needs. Behavioral intention is measured with two items, such as "I plan to continue using self-driving cars for transportation" and "I am likely to use self-driving cars in future travel."

Pretesting and pilot testing

To ensure the accuracy and clarity of the questionnaire used in this study on self-driving cars, pretesting and pilot testing were conducted to minimize potential issues with question interpretation and response accuracy. The pretest involved three doctoral students specializing in autonomous driving systems—specifically SAE Levels 3 and 4—who provided detailed feedback on the clarity, appropriateness, and expression of the survey items. The pilot test included 20 participants who had prior experience using self-driving cars or similar autonomous driving technologies. These participants were engaged in in-depth interviews to provide insights into their understanding of the survey items and overall feedback. Based on the findings, the survey items were refined and adjusted as necessary.

In this study, experienced users are operationally defined as individuals who have interacted with SAE Level 3 or Level 4 self-driving vehicles for at least one year and who use such systems at least once every two weeks. This criterion was used during participant screening to ensure that respondents possessed sufficient familiarity and practical engagement with autonomous driving technologies to provide reliable evaluations.

To assess potential heterogeneity among respondents, a supplementary descriptive analysis was conducted comparing three subgroups based on duration of self-driving car experience (1–2 years, 3–4 years, and over 4 years). A one-way ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups in PEOU (F = 1.27, p = 0.28; M₁₋₂ = 5.84, SD = 0.72; M₃₋₄ = 5.91, SD = 0.68; M₄₊ = 6.02, SD = 0.75), PU (F = 0.96, p = 0.38; M₁₋₂ = 6.12, SD = 0.70; M₃₋₄ = 6.05, SD = 0.74; M₄₊ = 6.18, SD = 0.69), and behavioral intention (F = 1.11, p = 0.33; M₁₋₂ = 6.20, SD = 0.67; M₃₋₄ = 6.24, SD = 0.65; M₄₊ = 6.33, SD = 0.71). Mean scores across the three experience groups were highly similar, suggesting that the sample is relatively homogeneous in terms of experiential background and technology engagement with SAE Level 3–4 self-driving cars.

This rigorous process ensured the development of a reliable and validated survey instrument tailored for exploring the factors influencing the adoption and use of self-driving cars.

Participants

This study targets users who have experience using self-driving cars to explore their behaviors and attitudes. Consistent with the study’s scope, participants were required to have prior interaction with SAE Level 3 or Level 4 vehicles. A convenience sampling method was used to select participants who had previously engaged with self-driving car technologies. After discussions with a transportation technology association, a survey was distributed to users from 15 driver-assistance and autonomous-driving user groups in Vietnam to gather data on their experiences with self-driving cars.

The survey was conducted over three weeks, with 750 questionnaires distributed and 327 responses received. The structured questionnaire included multiple-choice questions to ensure response accuracy. Incomplete or inconsistent responses were removed, resulting in 293 valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 67.62%.

Among the respondents using self-driving cars, 52.6% were male, while 47.4% were female. Regarding the frequency of self-driving car usage, 45.7% reported using the vehicles once every few weeks, followed by 24.9% once every two weeks, 24.2% once a month, and only 4.1% less than once a month. The findings suggest that many users display "light user" characteristics, with irregular usage patterns. These behaviors indicate that self-driving cars are primarily used for supplementary or situational transportation rather than for intensive or regular usage.

Among respondents, 59.7% reported having more than four years of experience using self-driving cars, followed by 21.2% with three to four years, 13.7% with two to three years, 2.4% with one to two years, and the lowest, less than one year. This distribution shows that over 70% of users had more than three years of experience, indicating that the sample consists largely of individuals who are relatively familiar with and experienced in utilizing autonomous driving technology.

Results

This study primarily employed SEM for data analysis to evaluate the theoretical framework for self-driving cars. First, the study analyzed the measurement model to assess construct validity and reliability, followed by verifying the structural model to examine the relationships among constructs. LISREL 12 was utilized as the analysis tool, with input data being a covariance matrix derived from survey responses. A total of 38 observed variables and 12 latent variables were analyzed, with 129 unknown parameters estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation method.

Common-method bias and measurement model

To ensure that the observed relationships were not inflated by CMB, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. Harman’s single-factor test was performed by loading all measurement items into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis. The first factor accounted for 32.4% of the total variance, which is below the recommended 50% threshold [64], indicating that CMB is not a serious concern.

The primary objective of the measurement model analysis was to identify constructs with reliability and discriminant validity relevant to self-driving cars. The initial measurement model for this study comprised 48 observed variables and 12 constructs. After conducting pretests, revising items based on feedback, and validating the constructs through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to address measurement errors, the final measurement model included 38 observed variables and 12 constructs: self-efficacy, opinion leadership, compatibility, flexibility, interactivity, system guidelines, instructional content, TTF, PEOU, PU, attitudes, and behavioral intentions. Model Fit

Bagozzi and Yi [65] suggested using the chi-square value divided by the degrees of freedom to evaluate model fit, recommending a value below 3 as a stringent criterion [67]. The normed chi-square value in this study is 1.629, which is below 3, indicating that the model is acceptable. As for the auxiliary indices, only GFI and AGFI fall slightly below the recommended values. While GFI and AGFI values close to 1 are desirable, there is no absolute standard for determining the fit between data and the model. Baumgartner and Homburg [68] found that GFI and AGFI values below the recommended levels occurred in 24% and 48% of the cases. In summary, the constructed model in this study for explaining user behavior and attitudes toward self-driving cars is deemed acceptable for interpreting the observed data.

As shown in Fig. 2, the study posited that these indirect associations with PEOU occur through TTF. Accordingly, higher self-efficacy and opinion leadership, as well as stronger compatibility and interactivity, are associated with better perceived fit, which in turn is associated with higher PEOU. Notably, interactivity shows the largest indirect association with PEOU (0.26), followed by opinion leadership (0.25); system guidelines show the smallest (− 0.06).

Total effects

Total effects (treated here as overall statistical associations) are calculated as the sum of direct and indirect paths. As shown in Table 5, among the 12 latent variables, 45 overall associations were examined; 39 are statistically significant (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01), and six are not.

For example, the overall association of self-efficacy with PEOU is 0.33 (direct = 0.26; indirect = 0.07), indicating that self-efficacy is statistically linked to higher perceived ease of use, with the direct association being larger. Similarly, the overall association of TTF with PU (0.95) comprises a strong direct component (0.90) and a smaller, non-significant indirect component (0.05), suggesting that TTF is primarily related to usefulness through the direct path. The overall associations of PU with attitude and behavioral intention are predominantly direct, with limited indirect components.

Overall, TTF shows the strongest overall association with PU (0.95) and PEOU (0.75), while system guidelines exhibit the weakest overall association with behavioral intention (− 0.04). These results indicate that perceived alignment between tasks and technology, together with user-centric design features, is statistically linked to higher ease of use, perceived usefulness, and reported intentions. At the endpoint, behavioral intention is most strongly associated with attitude (0.69), followed by TTF (0.50), PU (0.44), and PEOU (0.14).

Discussions and implications

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of user adoption of self-driving cars by integrating the TAM and TTF frameworks, alongside constructs such as self-efficacy and system characteristics. These contributions provide a comprehensive perspective on the factors associated with behavioral intentions toward self-driving cars and address gaps identified in prior research on advanced autonomous technologies. Importantly, this integration moves beyond a direct application of existing models by positioning TTF, TAM, and self-efficacy as complementary psychological mechanisms that jointly capture cognitive appraisal (fit and usefulness), affective evaluation (attitudes), and capability beliefs (self-efficacy) in high-autonomy contexts. This framing advances psychological insight into how perceptions of agency and human–machine communication are statistically associated with user evaluations, while automation trust is treated as an emergent evaluative process rather than a tested construct.

Prior research [10, 70] emphasized the significance of TTF in evaluating how well a system supports user needs. This study reinforces these findings by demonstrating that TTF is strongly associated with PU (β = 0.90) and substantially associated with PEOU (β = 0.75). These results highlight that users perceive self-driving cars as more beneficial and accessible when their functionalities align with users’ driving tasks. Unlike traditional TAM studies [9], which primarily focus on ease of use and usefulness as predictors, this study underscores the importance of evaluating how the system aligns with the complexity and interdependence of driving tasks. This reconceptualization positions TTF not as a causal determinant but as a salient correlate of automation-related confidence, broadening its theoretical role in human–technology interaction literature and conceptually informing trust development in autonomous contexts.

A key theoretical contribution of this study lies in its finding that the direct relationship between PEOU and PU—a cornerstone of TAM—is not significant in the context of self-driving cars (β = 0.06, p > 0.05). This suggests that in emerging markets such as Vietnam, operational simplicity may not be statistically associated with perceived functional benefits due to limited user exposure to SAE Level 3–4 technologies. Similarly, PU is not associated with behavioral intention (β = 0.12, t = 0.81), diverging from traditional TAM expectations. One possible explanation is that trust deficits, skepticism, and negative media coverage about autonomous driving safety [71] may overshadow perceived usefulness when forming adoption decisions. In such contexts, behavioral intentions are more closely linked to attitudes (β = 0.69), indicating that affective evaluations mediate the link between PU and adoption behavior. This insight extends TAM by demonstrating that canonical cognitive pathways can be disrupted in high-autonomy environments, requiring an integrated cognitive–affective framework to fully explain adoption behavior.

In this study, self-efficacy focuses on its functional role as a correlate of PEOU and TTF. Drawing on the Self-Efficacy-based Value Adoption Model (SVAM) [27], which has been applied in contexts such as mobile applications and autonomous vehicles, the findings are consistent with the proposition that self-efficacy is positively associated with perceived ease of use and task alignment. In the context of self-driving cars, users with higher confidence in operating autonomous vehicles were more likely to perceive the systems as both user-friendly and well-suited to their driving tasks (total effect on PEOU = 0.33) [56, 59]. Additionally, opinion leadership is significantly associated with (total indirect effect = 0.32). By showing that self-efficacy and opinion leadership are correlated with both cognitive fit and perceived value, this study highlights the interplay between individual capability beliefs and social influence in shaping adoption-related evaluations of automation technologies.

Crucially, the analysis reveals that TTF operates as a central mediating mechanism linking individual traits and system characteristics to behavioral intention. From a psychological perspective, this means that personal capabilities (self-efficacy) and social influence (opinion leadership) do not directly determine adoption decisions; instead, they are associated with users’ perceptions of how well the self-driving car fits their driving needs, which is linked to perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, and ultimately, intention. This pattern of association reflects the way cognitive appraisal processes transform underlying dispositions into concrete behavioral intentions, emphasizing that fit perceptions are the bridge between “who the user is” and “what the user chooses to do.” By articulating this pathway, the study strengthens the theoretical rationale for indirect associations and clarifies how enhancing perceived fit may statistically magnify the influence of personal and social enablers on adoption evaluations.

System characteristics, particularly interactivity and flexibility, play a vital role in user perceptions. Interactivity shows the largest indirect association with PU (0.33) and PEOU (0.26). These results align with studies on advanced systems (Patel, 2024), underscoring the importance of interactive features, such as real-time feedback and adaptive system responses, in enhancing user engagement and perceived satisfaction. Conversely, inadequate system guidelines are negatively associated with TTF (β = −0.08), highlighting the need for clear and user-friendly system instructions to minimize barriers to adoption. Integrating system design variables into the TAM–TTF–Self-efficacy framework provides a correlational lens on how adoption is shaped by both system responsiveness and user-level attributes.

Although this study gestures toward the concept of automation trust, it is not directly operationalized or empirically tested within the present model. Instead, trust is positioned as an emergent theoretical implication arising from the interplay among TTF, PU, POEU, and self-efficacy. The results indicate that when self-driving car systems demonstrate high task alignment, ease of operation, and responsiveness to user capabilities, users are likely to develop cognitive and affective confidence in the system— conditions statistically associated with automation trust. In this sense, trust formation is embedded indirectly within the integrated TAM–TTF–Self-efficacy framework, reflecting an outcome of positive cognitive appraisal and perceived system reliability rather than a standalone construct. This positioning preserves model parsimony while laying a theoretical foundation for future empirical extensions in which automation trust could be explicitly modeled as a mediating or moderating mechanism, linking cognitive fit and affective attitude to adoption behavior. By framing trust as a downstream psychological state emergent from cognitive–affective alignment, this study advances understanding of how users internalize confidence in autonomous vehicle systems.

While studies on autonomous vehicle adoption have frequently focused on trust or perceived risk [72, 73], this study shifts the focus to task-technology alignment and user-centric design. Moreover, this study identifies TTF as a key correlate of perceptions of ease of use and usefulness. Additionally, by incorporating self-efficacy and opinion leadership, this study extends the literature on autonomous vehicle adoption by emphasizing individual and social dynamics associated with adoption intentions. This broader integration not only supports technology adoption research but also contributes to human–technology interaction theory by demonstrating how perceptions of agency, fit, and capability converge to shape behavior in high-autonomy contexts.

TTF is statistically associated with PU and PEOU and also serves as a critical mediating correlate between system characteristics and behavioral intentions. The total association of TTF with behavioral intentions (0.50) underscores its central role in fostering positive user perceptions and adoption evaluations. This aligns with findings by Wang et al. [49] on the importance of aligning system capabilities with user needs in autonomous vehicle contexts.

This study confirms that behavioral intentions are primarily associated with attitudes (total effect = 0.69), followed by TTF (0.50) and PU (0.44). These findings emphasize the importance of fostering positive attitudes toward self-driving cars to drive adoption. Unlike previous studies that overemphasized the direct effects of PU on behavioral intentions [74], this study highlights the indirect correlational pathways through attitudes and TTF. In sum, the study advances theoretical discourse by reframing self-driving car adoption as a multidimensional process that is contingent on cognitive fit, affective appraisal, and individual capability beliefs—while conceptually situating automation trust as an emergent outcome of these processes—thereby offering a richer understanding of human–machine interaction in the era of autonomous mobility.

Practical implications

The findings of this study provide valuable insights for manufacturers, marketers, and policymakers to promote the adoption of self-driving cars. By focusing on user-centric design, leveraging social influence, and addressing individual and system-related factors, stakeholders can effectively enhance user engagement and acceptance of autonomous vehicles.

The significant role of TTF, as statistically associated with user perceptions, highlights the need for manufacturers to ensure that vehicle functionalities correspond closely to user tasks and preferences. Incorporating interactive features, such as real-time feedback and voice-activated controls, can be linked to improved user experience by making the technology appear more intuitive. Flexible customization options, such as personalized driving modes and route planning, can align with diverse user needs. Moreover, providing clear, easily accessible system guidelines is critical for reducing user confusion and fostering greater confidence, particularly among new adopters. Addressing these design-related factors can help self-driving cars better meet user expectations and perceived value.

This study underscores the observed association between opinion leadership and positive user attitudes and intentions. Early adopters and tech-savvy individuals can act as ambassadors for self-driving cars, influencing others through word-of-mouth and social media. Companies can capitalize on this by engaging these users in targeted marketing campaigns, offering them exclusive access to new features, and encouraging them to share their experiences. Providing incentives, such as referral bonuses or promotional discounts, can further motivate opinion leaders to advocate for self-driving cars, creating a ripple effect that broadens the technology’s reach.

The importance of self-efficacy in PEOU suggests that empowering users is essential to fostering acceptance of self-driving cars. Automakers should prioritize initiatives that build user confidence, such as interactive tutorials, hands-on training sessions, and accessible customer support channels. These resources can reduce the learning curve for new users and alleviate concerns about operating autonomous vehicles. By addressing user uncertainties and enhancing their sense of competence, companies can increase satisfaction and adoption rates.

Given the strong statistical link between attitudes and behavioral intentions, manufacturers and marketers should emphasize creating favorable perceptions rather than assuming direct behavioral outcomes. Highlighting key benefits, such as enhanced safety features, reduced environmental impact, and improved driving convenience, can help build trust and appeal to diverse user groups. Addressing common concerns—such as system reliability, cybersecurity risks, and ethical considerations—through transparent communication and robust testing can further strengthen user confidence and willingness to adopt the technology.

Policymakers play a vital role in fostering the adoption of self-driving cars by establishing guidelines that ensure safety while encouraging innovation. Investment in infrastructure upgrades, such as smart road systems and vehicle-to-infrastructure communication networks, can enhance the performance and reliability of self-driving cars. Public awareness campaigns and pilot projects showcasing the benefits of autonomous vehicles in real-world settings can also help mitigate skepticism and promote broader acceptance.

Limitations and future research

While this study provides valuable insights into the adoption of self-driving cars, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the use of a convenience sampling method—focusing on experienced self-driving car users in Vietnam—introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. The sample reflects a population with higher technological readiness and prior exposure to autonomous driving technology, which may differ significantly from novice or less tech-savvy users in other regions. Cultural factors (e.g., collectivist norms and community influence), technological readiness (e.g., familiarity with digital mobility services), and infrastructural conditions (e.g., urban traffic density, road quality) in Vietnam may have shaped user perceptions in ways not directly transferable to other markets. Future studies could incorporate more diverse demographic and geographic samples to capture a broader range of perspectives, including non-users and those unfamiliar with self-driving cars, and conduct cross-cultural comparisons to test model robustness across varying socio-technical environments. Additionally, this study did not include constructs such as trust, perceived risk, or safety concerns, which are critical in the context of autonomous vehicles. Future research could integrate these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of user adoption.

Second, this study employed a cross-sectional design, which captures user perceptions at a single point in time. Accordingly, the structural model should be interpreted as identifying patterns of association rather than verified causal effects. While the model reveals statistically significant relationships among variables, the data do not allow for confirmation of temporal or directional causality. Additionally, the outcome variable is behavioral intention rather than actual behavior—a well-known limitation in TAM-based research—meaning that the findings capture stated intentions, which may not fully translate into real-world usage. However, attitudes and behaviors toward self-driving cars are likely to evolve with continued exposure and technological advancements. To aid interpretation, the proposed model should be viewed as a theoretically grounded explanatory framework that identifies correlational patterns between user characteristics, system perceptions, and behavioral intentions rather than as a causal model. Future research should consider incorporating behavioral trace data (e.g., driving logs, system usage records) and experimental or longitudinal designs to verify directional and causal pathways and observe actual adoption patterns over time. Longitudinal studies could offer deeper insights into how user perceptions and adoption behaviors change over time, particularly as self-driving cars become more integrated into daily life. Examining novice-user adoption trajectories and the effects of incremental exposure to autonomous technologies will be particularly valuable for understanding how perceptions shift across the user adoption lifecycle.

Third, while this study carefully adapted and contextualized measurement items for SAE Level 3–4 self-driving cars, the operationalization of Task Characteristics and Technology Characteristics could be refined in future research. Specifically, subsequent studies could incorporate more granular task-related dimensions (e.g., navigation complexity, variability of environmental conditions, specific driving scenarios such as urban congestion or highway merging) and technology-related attributes (e.g., automation level, sensor accuracy and reliability, vehicle-to-infrastructure communication capabilities, safety feature sophistication). Such refinements would enhance conceptual clarity, strengthen construct validity, and provide a more nuanced understanding of how task–technology alignment is associated with user perceptions and adoption intentions.

Finally, although this study conceptually links automation trust to the integrated TAM–TTF–Self-Efficacy framework, it does not operationalize trust as an empirical construct. Future research should explicitly measure automation trust as a mediating or moderating variable to test its functional role in shaping behavioral intention. Operationalizing trust through validated dimensions such as competence, reliability, and integrity [75] could provide deeper insights into how users translate cognitive fit and self-efficacy into sustained confidence and adoption of autonomous vehicles. Incorporating trust into future models would transform the current positional framing into a testable psychological mechanism, thereby enriching both theoretical precision and explanatory power in autonomous technology adoption research.
