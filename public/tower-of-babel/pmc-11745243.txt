Alteration in EEG Delta and Beta Power During Working Memory Performance Differentiates Patients with Parkinson’s Disease with and without Dementia
Authors: Anita Pal, Madhuri Behari, Ratna Sharma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Working memory (WM) impairment is a common and progressive abnormality in Parkinson’s disease (PD), which ultimately leads to dementia. Individuals with PD exhibit altered electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns while engaging in tasks that require WM. The study examined the EEG patterns associated with WM maintenance in PD patients.Methods:The study consisted of two groups of PD patients with a sign of dementia (PDD) who had a Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≤24, PD patients with no sign of dementia (PDND) with an MMSE score >24, and a group of healthy individuals serving as controls (CON). We obtained EEG during a word memorization task using a 128-channel system. The spectral power of the EEG was computed using the fast Fourier transformation algorithm.Results:Compared to both PDND and CON groups, PDD group exhibited higher power in the delta and beta frequency bands. Conversely, there was a notable decrease in power in the gamma band in both the PDD and PDND groups compared to the CON group. In addition, the PDD group exhibited higher power in the gamma band compared to the PDND group. Power in the alpha (LA1, LA2, and UA) and theta bands was greater in PDND and PDD compared to CON, but was similar between PDND and PDD groups.Conclusion:While performing WM tasks, PDD exhibits increased delta and beta power. These findings can be used as a quantitative biomarker to evaluate the severity of initial WM impairments, which could potentially develop into dementia in the future.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is marked by the gradual depletion of dopaminergic neurons located in the substantia nigra, which has implications for a range of neural, behavioral, and motor pathways.[1] According to reports, apart from motor deficits, people with PD exhibit inconsistent patterns of cognitive impairments in areas such as language, attention, working memory (WM), semantic memory, spatial reasoning, and executive functions.[2] The disease has two overlapping, but separate cognitive systems: one with a cholinergic origin, characterized by rapid loss in visuospatial skills, semantic fluency, and episodic memory, and the other with a frontostriatal disorder caused by dopamine, characterized by early impairment of executive function (EF) and WM.[3]

WM is deficient in both early- and late-stage PD patients.[4] It is a cognitive system that encodes and temporarily stores perceptual representations for access without external input.[5] Recent findings suggest that both difficulties of recalling and problems storing information contribute to memory impairments in PD.[6] PD patients exhibit diminished cognitive abilities, particularly in terms of memory capacity, as assessed through tasks involving storage and processing.[78] Several studies have investigated whether electroencephalogram (EEG) slowing is associated with total cognitive performance or certain domains of cognition in non-demented PD patients. Research has shown a link between a decrease in EEG activity and cognitive decline in PD patients, and a direct relationship between the median frequency of EEG signals and performance in attention, EFs, fluency, and episodic memory tasks in PD.[910]

A greater increase in theta power could indicate a greater likelihood of a significant cognitive decline in PD.[11] A previous study observed increased delta and gamma frequencies during rest in PD dementia (PDD), which can serve as markers of early stages of dementia in individuals with PD.[12] No study has examined the neural correlates of WM maintenance in PD with noticeable signs of dementia (PDD) or PD with no sign of dementia (PDND) using high-density, 128-channel EEG. Expanding upon these findings, our objective was to investigate the EEG patterns during the period of WM retention. Our hypothesis is that there will be differences in EEG power between patients and controls (CON), which can be attributed to variations in cognitive status and pathologic changes. Distinguishing these differences could aid in the early detection of cognitive decline in WM and its subsequent development into dementia.

Methods

Participants

A total of 80 PD patients were enrolled in the study. PD patients were diagnosed as per the diagnostic criteria established by the UK’s Parkinson’s Disease Brain Bank criteria. In addition, PD patients were evaluated by the neurology outpatient department to collect demographic and clinical information. The inclusion criteria consisted of the following: individuals diagnosed with PD, regardless of their gender, who were aged between 45 and 75 years, had experienced disease onset after the age of 40, had a Hoehn and Yahr stage on a scale of 1–3, and exhibited noticeable tremors. In this study, we specifically excluded patients with comorbidities like diabetes and stroke, following the advice and clinical confirmation of a neurologist. Additional exclusion criteria included a previous history of stroke, prior head injury with loss of consciousness, drug abuse, alcoholism, and the concurrent use of sedatives, hypnotics, and neuroleptic drugs. Preapproval for the study was obtained from the ethical committee of human studies (Ref. No. IESC/T-158/30.3.2012). All participants (PD patients and healthy controls [HC]) were provided with all pertinent information, and written consent was obtained. All the participants were assessed for their cognitive status based on their Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores,[13] and to ascertain their status of dementia symptoms, a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale was used. PD patients were classified as either PDND (PD patients with no sign of dementia) or PDD (PD patients with noticeable signs of dementia) according to their MMSE and CDR scores.[14] The control group consisted of 26 healthy adults who were similarly aged and educated and who had no history of neurologic or mental illness. The demographic and clinical details of the participants can be found in our previous article.[12]

Word memory task

The word memory task used in the study[15] was self-deigned in E-Prime 2.0 software. For this task, a computer screen was used to randomly present 10 neutral words to subjects for 2 sec each. This was followed by a question asking them to read aloud and remember them. After that, once they had finished, they were asked to open their eyes and name the words they could recall.

EEG data acquisition and preprocessing

This article adopts the methodology outlined in a previous study (resting state) to collect, preprocess, and analyze EEG data across all seven frequency bands.[12]

EEG acquisition

The 128-channel EEG data was collected at 500 Hz using a Philips Neuro Net Station 300 system. To ensure satisfactory data collection, the electrode resistance was maintained under 50 kΩ. EEGs were recorded from both groups of PD patients and HC. The EEG recordings lasted for a duration of 3 min for each participant, specifically during the period when they were retaining words in their memory. To avoid being influenced by circadian rhythm, all EEG recordings were made between 9 a.m. and 12 p.m.

Preprocessing of data

Using Electroencephalography Laboratory (EEGLAB) and Net Station, offline processing of EEG signals was conducted using a band-pass filter set to allow frequencies between 0.5 and 100 Hz and a notch filter set specifically for 50 Hz. WM task data with a 15-sec retention time that was visually reviewed and devoid of artifacts was chosen, and it was further divided into 1-sec epochs. Epochs were marked up for eye blinks, movements, and a channel as bad if it was bad for 20% of the recording epochs using a semi-automated artifact identification system. An epoch with a 200-V average of 10 ms was considered bad if the channel drifted 200 V throughout. A channel was flagged as bad if it was bad for 20% of the recording epochs. We used spherical spline interpolation to interpolate bad channels. The EEG segments that had more than 20% of channels with poor quality were removed. Furthermore, in EEGLAB, the default RUNICA algorithm was utilized to conduct independent component analysis[16] to filter out artifacts such as sweat artifacts, power line noise, and electrocardiograms, among others. The seven frequency bands were classified according to their individual alpha frequencies (IAFs).[17] An analysis of the spectral power of 30 PDND, 28 PDD, and 26 CON was then performed.

Estimation of spectral power

The preprocessed data was referenced to the average reference using MATLAB (R2018a, The MathWorks, Inc.). A customized MATLAB script utilizing fast Fourier transformation (FFT) was applied to convert the time-domain EEG signal into frequency domain. This transformation allowed for the estimation of absolute power, measured in V2. Applying a Hamming window of 500 samples in length, FFT was performed on EEG data from a 1-sec epoch with a resolution of 0.488 Hz. The mean power for each subject’s designated band was determined by the predicted absolute power. For each trial over 15 epochs, the average of the absolute spectral power for each electrode was calculated from seven predetermined frequency bands.

Statistical analysis

To compare the age at which a disease began and the duration of it across groups, STATA software was used. The age at which the disease first appeared (disease onset) and the length of time it had been present (disease duration) were compared using a t-test. In addition, a one-way analysis of variance was used to assess group differences in age, education, and IAF. For spectral power comparison among groups, we first examined the data distribution with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Due to the non-uniformity of the data, square roots were used. To minimize the occurrence of incorrect positive results, a rank-sum test along with a post hoc Bonferroni correction was used. Significant electrodes were further sorted region of interest wise to interpret regional power difference between groups. The topoplot tool was employed to graphically represent significant data in EEGLAB. A significance level of P < 0.05 was chosen. In addition, we conducted a correlation analysis between the MMSE scores, age, and duration of the disease, and the EEG power changes across various frequency bands in all three groups. However, we did not find any significant relationships.

Results

According to our findings, cognitive impairment and dementia were indicated by significantly lower MMSE and higher CDR scores in the PDD and PDND groups compared to the HC group. PDD had much more cognitive deficits than PDND or CON, as evidenced by both MMSE and CDR scores. Age and educational levels among the three groups were comparable. There were no discernible variations in IAF. In our previous article, we published statistics on demographics and clinical characteristics of the same subjects.[12] In addition, we conducted a correlation analysis between the MMSE scores, age, and duration of the disease, and the EEG power changes across various frequency bands in all three groups. However, we did not find any significant relationships.

The study compared EEG power during the word memory retention period (WM) among the three groups: PDND, PDD, and CON. Findings are discussed subsequently.

Beta and delta power

The PDD group consistently showed higher beta and delta power compared to both the PDND and CON groups throughout the WM period. Specifically, beta power was more pronounced in the mid-frontal, right and left frontal, center parietal, left parietal, right occipital, and right parietal regions in the PDD group compared to the CON group. In addition, beta power was higher in the mid-frontal and left parietal regions in the PDD group compared to the PDND group.

Gamma band power

High-frequency gamma band power was significantly lower in both PDND and PDD groups compared to the CON group at several electrode locations, including the mid and right frontal, centroparietal, and right occipital regions. However, in the centroparietal region, gamma band power was higher in the PDD group compared to the PDND group at three electrode locations.

Alpha and theta bands

Power in all alpha and theta sub-bands was higher in both PDND and PDD groups compared to the CON group. Despite these increases, alpha and theta power were comparable between the PDND and PDD groups. These findings indicate distinct patterns of EEG power across different frequency bands and brain regions among the three groups, highlighting significant differences particularly in the PDD group [Figure 1].

Discussion

In the present study, high-density EEG was used to decode neural correlates of word memory in PD patients with or without dementia. Findings revealed that the maintenance of words in WM showed no significant difference in delta and beta band power in PDND compared to HC. The presence of high power in the delta, theta, alpha, and beta frequencies, along with low power in gamma band in PDD, indicates that deficits may be associated with extensive changes in power, which is a common feature of neurodegenerative conditions.

The increased delta power observed in PDD during WM tasks can be attributed to multiple factors, such as neuronal compensation, dysfunction in the cortex and subcortex, inhibitory processes, neurodegenerative changes, cognitive decline, and compromised WM networks. The brain may enhance delta power to sustain cognitive function in the face of ongoing neurodegeneration, facilitate impaired neural processes, and inhibit irrelevant or non-relevant neural activity. Neurodegenerative alterations impact the brain’s electrical activity, and the decline in cognitive function among patients with PD can result in modifications in the power spectra of EEG. Therefore, the significant increase in delta power observed in PDD is likely a complex and multifaceted phenomenon.

Lower power in the gamma band was seen topographically in the mid and right frontal regions. Research indicates that gamma oscillations are highly significant in various cognitive processes, including visual perception, learning, memory, and visuospatial focused attention.[18] It has been hypothesized that gamma oscillations are responsible for the active maintenance of WM representations when there are no external stimuli present.[192021] Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that gamma activity synchronization can accurately predict an individual’s WM ability.[2223] Low beta and gamma power are related to lower attention and feature binding, respectively. In PDD patients, both beta and gamma power were lower than those in CON and higher compared to PDND patients, suggesting alterations in brain activity because of pathologic changes.

The beta rhythm (~20 Hz) has been found to be associated with working memory functions such as motor behavior, response inhibition, and top-down signaling processes.[2425] PD patients have been reported to have increased oscillations and interregional coherence in the beta band, which ranges from 13 to 30 Hz, caused by the loss of dopamine.[26] In their study, Engel and Fries[27] investigated the relationship between beta band activity and its potential role in regulating motor and cognitive performance. They found evidence suggesting that the origin and control of beta oscillations may be the same at the cortical level, which could explain their influence on both motor and cognitive functions. The theta frequency range is particularly significant for episodic memory and WM.[28] The hippocampus has strong reciprocal links with almost every cortical region, which is why Klimesch[17] hypothesizes that the hippocampus may be the source of synchronous theta band activity during WM tasks.

PD patients may have a broadly distributed power distribution due to localized impairment or degeneration of specific brain regions, similar to diseases associated with neurodegeneration. The effect of local degeneration is not only confined to the same region, but also has an effect of altered anatomic and functional changes in other regions, leading to alterations in the large brain network.[29] Therefore, it would be worth identifying disruptions in whole-brain functional networks from noninvasive EEG during cognitive activation. In the present cross-sectional study, we found the changes in various frequency bands, and these changes are specific to groups, but we are not reflecting any linear trend projecting from CON to PD groups. The lack of a linear progression in EEG frequency bands may be attributed to the intricate nature of disease advancement, the variability in EEG signals, or the impact of many confounding factors. In future, a more intricate methodology may be required to comprehend the correlation between alterations in EEG patterns and the advancement of PD.

Conclusion

The current study aims to find the neural correlates of WM retention by applying the approach of spectral power analysis. Our findings indicate that the PDD group exhibits low power in the gamma band and high power in the delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands. This suggests that during WM tasks, the brain dynamics of PD patients undergo global disturbances rather than local ones. In PDND, higher power in theta and alpha band power indicates increased brain activity. However, there was no difference in power of delta frequency, suggesting that the deterioration in PDND patients was still in its early stage. This deterioration worsened in the PDD stage, as evidenced by the higher power observed. From the findings, we can conclude that working memory maintenance involves a large variability in the temporal patterning of neural activity both in PDD and PDND.

However, our study introduces a novel method to investigate alterations in EEG activity pertaining to the retention period of WM in PD patients. Thus far, the majority of studies have focused on establishing a relationship between WM scores and EEG signatures. Moreover, based on our current understanding, these results represent the initial evidence of alterations in the strength of various frequency ranges while performing a WM task. This implies that distinct neurophysiologic processes are responsible for the impairments in retaining WM. We explored the neural correlates of the word retention period only because we were interested in the role of brain oscillations in WM retention. Future studies should investigate neural correlates of all components of WM in PD patients using other validated robust WM tasks. Moreover, we also investigated whether EEG activity in different frequency bands correlates with the WM task score.[30] No significant correlation was found between the two.

Limitations of the study

There are few constraints of this exploratory study that need to be addressed. First, the task we used to assess WM is not as strong and robust. We used this simple test as a preliminary test to assess the neural correlates during the maintenance of WM in PD. If we had taken any other validated task, it would have been difficult to acquire EEG in PD patients. Thus, future studies should also be performed using more robust WM tests.
