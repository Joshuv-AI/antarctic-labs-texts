Effect of intrasocket application of hyaluronic acid on pain, swelling, and trismus after surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molar – A randomized control trial
Authors: Deepshikha Bhave, Mouneshkumar Chapi Devendrappa, Kumar N. Mishra, Vinay K. Gupta, Vidit Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Mandibular third molar removal is one of the most widely performed treatments in the oral cavity. Nevertheless, surgeons often encounter undesirable postoperative sequelae. These inflammatory responses after wisdom teeth surgery may badly affect a patients’ quality of life. Though traditional measures such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids proposed for control of postoperative complications work well, their potential side effects have led to a search for an easily applicable way to reduce postoperative pain and swelling. Hyaluron or Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a biomaterial that has been introduced as an alternative approach to enhance wound healing. Hyaluronic acid is a major carbohydrate with multifaceted roles. It plays a critical role in wound healing by inducing early granulation tissue formation, inhibiting destructive inflammation, and promoting angiogenesis. It is available in gel or liquid form to be applied topically in oral cavity. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of hyaluronic acid in reducing pain, swelling, and trismus after surgical extraction of the lower third molar.Materials and Methods:Forty patients underwent surgical extraction of impacted lower third molar for this study A.0.7 mL 2%hyaluronic acid gel (Hyalgun®) along with abgel as scaffold was placed in the extraction socket in study group and abgel alone was placed in control group. Data collection was performed to assess the pain using the visual analog scale on 1st, 3rd, and 7th postoperative days, swelling using the tape measure method, and trismus by measuring interincisal distance preoperatively, on 1st, 3rd, and 7th postoperative days.Results:The hyaluronic acid gel group demonstrated statistically significantly less swelling and trismus. Pain assessed on the visual analog scale (VAS) was significantly less (P < 0.05) on 1st, 3rd, and 7th day. Swelling showed a significant decrease (P < 0.05) and trismus scores were highly significant (P < 0.001).Conclusion:The application of HA gel following third molar extraction effectively alleviates postoperative pain, swelling, and trismus, enhancing patient comfort and recovery. HA gel, with its anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, offers promise as an adjunct in postoperative management.

INTRODUCTION

An impacted tooth is one that does not emerge in the oral cavity by its expected age or isn’t anticipated to do so after a certain age. The mandibular third molar is the most commonly impacted tooth. The timing, morphology, and position of these molars, particularly in the mandible, can vary significantly.[1] Extracting the mandibular third molar is a routine outpatient dental procedure, frequently leading to postoperative issues such as pain, swelling, and reduced mouth opening. Currently, there are numerous uncertainties and questions regarding the post-surgery quality of life after the surgical removal of the mandibular third molar.[2] Inadequate pain management in the early postoperative period is associated with delayed recovery and an increased likelihood of developing chronic pain.[3]

Consequently, these complications remain a significant problem for patients and surgeons; reducing these complications can significantly improve patient outcomes.[4] To decrease the intensity and duration of clinical symptoms, numerous surgical techniques and materials have been designed to address inflammation.

Many studies have examined postoperative discomfort after third-molar surgery, assessing the effectiveness of various medications, including corticosteroids, NSAIDs, and enzymes like chymotrypsin and serratiopeptidase.[5] However, routinely prescribing these drugs may lead to undue side effects and may also be contraindicated in some patients. Though, in small doses, they may not cause side effects, some practitioners do not prefer their use as they have been shown to interfere with wound healing.

Additionally, local interventions such as immediate cold compression, oral surgical drain placement, and low-level laser therapy have been noted to reduce the impact of third-molar surgery.[6]

Hyaluronic acid (HA), or hyaluronan, is a biomaterial introduced as an alternative approach to improving wound healing. Hyaluronan is a high-molecular-weight glycosaminoglycan compound found throughout the body, including the skin, synovial joints, and periodontal tissue. The unique viscoelastic properties of exogenous HA and its biocompatibility and non-immunogenicity have enabled its use in many clinical applications, such as filling synovial fluid, aiding in wound regeneration, and making dermal fillers. It plays an imperative role in wound healing during the inflammatory, granulation, and re-epithelialization stages.[7]

Hyaluronan induces beneficial effects at the early granulation tissue formation stage of wound healing. It inhibits destructive inflammation during the healing phase and promotes re-epithelialization and angiogenesis. As it is non-immunogenic and non-toxic in nature, it has been used in ophthalmology, dermatology, and rheumatology. The synthetic hyaluronan is available in gel and liquid form for oral use.[8]

Previous studies have used hyaluronic acid in the form of topical spray or gel, which has shown a beneficial effect in reducing the postoperative effects of surgical extraction of the third molar.[9101112] However, the results of the previous studies failed to demonstrate significant results in all three parameters, i.e. pain, swelling, and trismus, owing to its topical application. The study aimed to assess the effectiveness of the use of intrasocket hyaluronic acid gel in alleviating postoperative symptoms of pain, trismus, and swelling following the surgical extraction of an impacted mandibular third molar.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present study is randomized double-blind clinical trial involving 40 patients recruited in the department of oral and maxillofacial surgery, requiring surgical extraction of a mesioangular impacted mandibular third molar from August 2022 to September 2023, after due approval of Institutional ethical committee approval (Protocol No. 008/2022-2023). The study was registered in clinical trial registry of India (CTRI/2024/08/073116). The degrees of surgical difficulty calculated for the lower third molar were based on the Pederson scale.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated using the following formula:

Substituting these values from the previous study, the sample size determined was n = 20 per group.

A sample size of 40 patients was obtained with 20 patients in each group.

Inclusion criteria

The study included patients between 18 and 45 years old with mesioangular impacted mandibular third molar, no systemic disease, allergy history, or bleeding difficulties, and had moderate surgical difficulty on the Pederson’s difficulty scale.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded patients with pericoronitis or pain before surgery as it may alter the perception of pain, those who were pregnant or nursing, those with third molar extraction lasted more than 40 minutes, those who had taken antibiotics or analgesics in the previous 2 weeks, those with active carious lesions or periodontal diseases, and those with contraindications to the drugs or anesthetics.

Methodology

The study was designed as a double-blind trial, with both participants and the examiner blinded to group assignments. The patients were randomly divided into study and control group by lottery method. A written and informed consent was taken from all participants. All extractions and preoperative measurements were performed by same operator. Preoperative measurements were taken for facial swelling by tape measure method, trismus by maximum interincisal mouth opening for both study and control group [Figures 2–4].

Local anesthesia was given through inferior alveolar nerve block, lingual nerve block, long buccal infiltration using 2% lignocaine with 1:2,00,00 epinephrine. Ward’s incision was placed and full thickness mucoperiosteal flap was raised to completely expose the tooth. Bone guttering and tooth sectioning was performed for easy extraction of molar [Figure 5]. Copious irrigation of extraction socket was performed with 0.9% normal saline.

0.7 mL of 20 mg/2 mL of hyaluronic acid was applied to gelfoam and gently placed intrasocket, and primary wound closure was performed using 3–0 silk suture material [Figures 6 and 7]. In the control group, only gelfoam was placed in the socket, and wound closure was performed with silk 3–0 suture material [Figure 8].

Suture removal was performed on the 7th day. All patients were recalled on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days. Postoperative measurements were taken for swelling by the tape measure method, pain by the visual analog scale, and trismus by the maximum inter-incisal mouth opening by different examiners. All parameters were evaluated and recorded on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days with the same device and settings. All patients were given the same postoperative instructions and drugs: Tab Amoxicillin 500 mg + Tab Clavulanic Acid 125 mg (twice a day for 5 days after meal), Tab Diclofenac sodium 50 mg (twice a day for 5 days after meal), Tab Omeprazole 40 mg (once a day, half an hour before meal). All the data was collected and tabulated to evaluate the results of the study. The statistical analysis was performed. Descriptive statistics of the demographic details and measurements for different parameters like pain, swelling, and mouth opening for both groups were performed at the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days of follow-up by different examiner. To avoid bias, the examiner was blinded to the study and control group.

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 26. The level of significance was kept at 5%. P values < 0.05 were considered significant. The data was subjected to normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the results of normality testing, an intragroup comparison of the change in each variable within each group between different time intervals was performed using either the repeated measure ANOVA test or the Friedman test, followed by a post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Intergroup comparison of each variable between two groups was performed using an independent t-test or Mann-Whitney test.

RESULTS

Demographic details

The study included a total of 40 patients requiring surgical extraction of mesioangular impacted third molar. Participants were recruited from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery between August 2022 and September 2023. The participants consisted of 19 males (47.5%) and 21 females (52.5%). The age distribution was similar between the two groups, with the experimental group (HA gel) having a mean age of 32.2 years (SD = 5.32) and the control group having a mean age of 30.15 years (SD = 7.49). Statistical analysis showed no significant difference in gender distribution between the groups (P = 0.752), nor were there significant differences in age or tooth location (either tooth number 38 or 48), indicating that both groups were demographically comparable.

Additionally, the average duration of the extraction procedure was comparable between the groups, with a mean time of 35.5 minutes in the experimental group and 35.0 minutes in the control group (P = 0.714). This similarity in procedure time suggests that surgical complexity was consistent across the groups.

These demographic details confirm the homogeneity between the experimental and control groups, minimizing the risk of confounding factors that might otherwise impact study outcomes related to age, gender, or procedural complexity. Assessment of pain

The pain was assessed by the VAS scale in two groups. Throughout the postoperative period, pain levels were significantly lower in the experimental group compared to the control group. On postoperative day 1, the experimental group reported a mean pain score of 3.15, while the control group had a higher mean of 5.0. By postoperative day 3, the mean pain scores decreased to 1.9 in the experimental group and 3.1 in the control group. On postoperative day 7, the mean pain scores further decreased to 1 in the experimental group and 1.5 in the control group. These differences were statistically significant with P values < 0.001.

Overall, the experiment group patients experienced less pain than the control group [Table 5]. Also, no complications were seen during study period.

DISCUSSION

Surgical extraction of third molars frequently leads to notable pain, swelling, and trismus shortly after the procedure, significantly impacting the patient’s quality of life. These postoperative symptoms arise from a complex array of factors, primarily rooted in the inflammatory response triggered by the surgical trauma. The postoperative impact of surgical removal of lower third molar surgery on quality of life is reportedly three times greater in patients experiencing pain, swelling, and trismus, either individually or in combination, compared to asymptomatic patients.[13] Consequently, many clinicians emphasize the necessity for improved control over pain, swelling, and trismus in patients undergoing third-molar surgery.[6]

The sequelae can be minimized, if not completely eliminated, by gentle handling of tissues and the use of modified surgical techniques or materials that aim to alleviate the postoperative symptoms. Adequate anti-inflammatory therapy can prevent or reduce postoperative inflammation and associated symptoms.

Among these, corticosteroids are widely used to decrease postoperative edema and trismus, but they have potential side effects like adrenal suppression, delayed wound healing, and increased chances of infection.[14] Pain emerges as the foremost anticipated complication subsequent to third molar surgery, primarily arising from inflammation triggered by injury to the tissue. The sensation of pain is attributed to the compression of nerve endings due to exudation, subsequent to the release of various inflammatory mediators such as arachidonic acid metabolites, 5-HT, and bradykinin. These mediators enhance the responsiveness of nearby nociceptors, heightening the experience of pain. The pain message travels through neurons in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, which then passes it on to higher brain centers for analysis and coordination.[15]

As inflammation advances, the buildup of interstitial fluid, caused by leakage from damaged blood vessels, along with the blockage of lymphatic drainage due to fibrin and fibrinogen clots from plasma and nearby injured vessels, contributes to postoperative swelling.

Quantifying facial swelling accurately poses a challenge due to its three-dimensional shape, uneven convex surface, and potential presence both internally and externally.[5] Trismus, or jaw stiffness, commonly ensues after third molar surgery and is a significant postoperative complication often caused by the swelling commonly associated with surgical trauma. Additionally, postoperative pain can contribute to restricted mouth opening following third molar surgery.[16]

Hyaluronic acid (HA), also known as hyaluronan, is a significant component of tissue that plays diverse roles, especially in the early phases of inflammation. Its use in treating cutaneous and subcutaneous wounds began in the 1990s, with research highlighting its potential benefits in pain relief, functional improvement, and intra-articular lubrication of the knee and ankle. As a prominent carbohydrate in the extracellular matrix, HA is prevalent across various tissues. Its unique physicochemical and biological properties make it an excellent candidate for wound healing. It has been shown to promote early granulation tissue formation, reduce harmful inflammation during the healing process, and support re-epithelialization and angiogenesis. Additionally, due to its non-toxic and non-immunogenic nature, HA is utilized in various medical fields, including dermatology, ophthalmology, and rheumatology.[17]

Hyaluronic acid (HA) plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory processes through its interactions with specific binding proteins. It modulates the expression of inflammatory genes, regulates the recruitment of inflammatory cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, and influences the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These interactions contribute to the attenuation of inflammatory responses by suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, reducing oxidative stress, and promoting tissue repair. Notably, high-molecular-weight HA exhibits significant anti-inflammatory properties, protecting tissues from excessive inflammation and mitigating the risk of damage. These characteristics underscore HA’s potential as a therapeutic agent for managing inflammation-related conditions, including postoperative recovery, such as following lower third molar impaction extractions.[18]

Amr Bayoumi et al.[9] conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial on the effect of cross-linked hyaluronic acid in the surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars.

In this study, scores for facial swelling, pain, and trismus were highest on the 2nd postoperative day and gradually decreased on the 4th and 7th days in both groups. The hyaluronic acid group exhibited a statistically significant reduction in swelling, pain, and trismus on the 7th postoperative day compared to the control group.

In 2008, Lee et al.[19] evaluated the effectiveness of applying 0.2% hyaluronic acid gel topically to recurrent oral ulcers and observed that the healing period for ulcers was reduced with hyaluronic acid gel. The inflammatory symptoms, such as swelling and local heat, showed significant improvement. Laboratory-based research and extensive randomized controlled trials imply that hyaluronic acid may aid in promoting tissue regeneration in both mineralized and non-mineralized periodontal tissue. Preliminary evidence suggests that hyaluronan may be a potential candidate as a mediator of non-mineralized periodontal tissue healing and as an aid to periodontal disease treatment.[13]

In 2003, Gauri Gontiya and Sushama R. Galgali[10] conducted a study examining the impact of hyaluronan on periodontitis. This research explored the clinical and histological effects of applying 0.2% hyaluronic acid gel (Gengigel®) sub-gingivally in conjunction with scaling and root planning for patients with chronic periodontitis. Histologically, the sites demonstrated decreased inflammatory infiltrate, although statistically significant results were not achieved.

In 2007, Lucia Lago-Mendez et al.[20] examined the correlation between surgical difficulty and postoperative pain in lower third molar extraction. Their study unveiled a statistically significant association between surgical difficulty and postoperative pain. In our study, the surgical difficulty was assessed by Pederson’s difficulty index, including only cases with a score of 5 or 6 in the study.

In 2011, Thiago de Santana-Santos et al.[21] evaluated the relationship between facial swelling, pain, and trismus following mandibular third molar extraction and preoperative variables. The conclusion was that the short-term outcome of mandibular third molar surgery differs depending on the patient’s characteristics, i.e., age, gender, and body mass index. In our study, the gender distribution, age of patients, and difficulty level of the procedure were statistically insignificant (P ≥ 0.05) between the two groups. This minimizes the potential bias stemming from variations in these factors. In this study, we utilized the visual analog scale (VAS) to gauge the intensity of pain following the surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars. This choice was made because the VAS is known for its reproducibility, simplicity, and ease of comprehension for patients. Our observations revealed a reduction in pain scores on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7 in the group treated with hyaluronic acid gel (the experimental group) compared to control group patients. This finding aligns with a study conducted by Yilmas et al.[11] in 2016, where they concluded that local application of hyaluronic acid possesses analgesic properties.

In 2014, Koray et al.[2] conducted a study focusing on the efficacy of hyaluronic acid spray in managing swelling, pain, and trismus subsequent to the surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars.

Their findings indicated that, while there was no statistically significant difference in visual analog scale (VAS) scores between the two groups, the administration of hyaluronic acid spray yielded better outcomes in reducing swelling and trismus compared to benzydamine hydrochloride spray. Although no substantial evidence of pain reduction was observed, hyaluronic acid appeared to be more effective in managing swelling and trismus immediately following impacted lower third molar surgery. This could be due to superficial application of “spray” form of application. However, in the current study, the hyaluronic acid was injected intra-socket, which led to significant decrease in pain as well.

In 2016, Raj Merchant et al.[12] conducted a prospective randomized controlled split-mouth study to explore the clinical effectiveness of hyaluronic acid spray compared to normal saline spray in reducing swelling, pain, and trismus after surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars. Although the study did not find evidence of a reduction in pain levels, hyaluronic acid appeared to provide valuable benefits in managing swelling and trismus during the early postoperative period following surgical removal of impacted lower third molars. Similarly, in our study, hyaluronic acid gel also demonstrated beneficial effects on pain, swelling, and trismus. In contrast, our study evaluated pain levels on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th postoperative days using the VAS scale and statistically analyzed the difference between the study group (those treated with hyaluronic acid gel) and the control group. Patients in the experimental group reported lower VAS scores on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days compared to those in the control group. The reduction in VAS scores in the study group compared to the control group was statistically significant. Additionally, the experimental group exhibited significantly reduced swelling on the 3rd postoperative day and improved mouth opening compared to the control group.

In 2017, Ibrahim Murat Afat et al.[22] conducted a study to explore the effects of leukocyte- and platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF), both alone and in combination with hyaluronic acid, on edema, pain, and trismus after the surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars.

The findings of this research suggested that L-PRF, especially when combined with hyaluronic acid, could be employed to reduce postoperative edema following surgery to remove mandibular third molars. In our study, we assessed the swelling score both preoperatively to establish a baseline value and, on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days postoperatively.

Patients in the study group, who received treatment with hyaluronic acid gel, showed less swelling on the 3rd postoperative day compared to those in the control group. This discrepancy was analyzed and found to be statistically significant.

In our study, we assessed trismus by measuring mouth opening, which was evaluated preoperatively and, on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days postoperatively. Preoperative measurements were taken to establish a baseline, and then mouth opening at each time point was recorded. Patients in the study group who received hyaluronic acid gel demonstrated improved mouth opening compared to the control group on the 1st, 3rd, and 7th days postoperatively. This difference was found to be statistically significant. Similarly, in the literature, authors such as Koray et al., Amr Bayoumi et al., and Merchant et al.[2912] have also reported positive effects of hyaluronic acid in reducing postoperative trismus in their respective studies.

This study demonstrated that HA solution could reduce pain, swelling, and trismus after the extraction of lower third molars, though it is important to acknowledge several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings, suggesting that larger studies are necessary to confirm these results across a broader population.

Additionally, pain assessment relied on the visual analog scale (VAS), a subjective measure based on patients’ self-reported pain levels. This reliance on subjective evaluation introduces potential variability, as individual differences in pain tolerance, emotional state, and past experiences may influence reported scores.

CONCLUSION

Our findings indicate that the use of intrasocket hyaluronic acid (HA) solution may effectively reduce postoperative pain and trismus associated with edema following lower third molar impaction extraction. This approach offers potential as an adjunctive measure for oral surgeons to enhance patient comfort and potentially reduce the need for postoperative analgesics. Although our study presents promising results, further investigations with larger sample sizes are essential to substantiate these findings and to establish the efficacy of intra-socket HA solutions in routine clinical practice.
