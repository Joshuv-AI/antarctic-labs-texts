Lifestyle and Metabolic Health, Low-Grade to Clinically Elevated Albuminuria, and Cardiovascular-Renal Diseases
Authors: Yi Ding, Qiuyu Cao, Xianglin Wu, Youjin Jiang, Xiaoran Li, Yu Xu, Min Xu, Tiange Wang, Yuhong Chen, Zhiyun Zhao, Jie Zheng, Shuangyuan Wang, Hong Lin, Xuejiang Gu, Li Yan, Miao Zhang, Zhengnan Gao, Lulu Chen, Tianshu Zeng, Ruying Hu, Zhen Ye, Xuefeng Yu, Gang Chen, Qing Su, Yiming Mu, Xulei Tang, Qin Wan, Guixia Wang, Guijun Qin, Zuojie Luo, Yingfen Qin, Li Chen, Xinguo Hou, Yanan Huo, Qiang Li, Hong Qiao, Yinfei Zhang, Chao Liu, Youmin Wang, Shengli Wu

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlbuminuria from low-grade to clinically elevated range confers cardiorenal risks.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to investigate the differential roles of lifestyle/metabolic factors in the association of fine-categorized and continuously measured urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) levels with cardiorenal outcomes.MethodsA total of 82,509 participants from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study were included, with a median follow-up of 3.0 years. Outcomes included incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), incident CKD, their composite, along with surrogate markers. Lifestyle factors included tobacco use, alcohol use, diet, physical activity and sleep. Metabolic factors included diabetes, hypertension, abdominal obesity, and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Cox proportional hazard models were used to calculate hazard ratios.ResultsCompared to the lowest UACR, participants with mildly elevated UACR (≥10 to <30 mg/g) had a 39% higher risk of composite cardiorenal outcomes (multivariable-adjusted HR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.29-1.50), whereas those with clinically elevated UACR (≥30 mg/g) had over double the risk (HR: 2.29, 95% CI: 2.12-2.47). A nonlinear J-shaped association was observed between continuous UACR and cardiorenal outcomes (Pnonlinearity = 0.0009), with UACR ≥10 mg/g already conferring risks, a pattern also reflected by the deterioration of PREVENT score and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Optimal lifestyle and metabolic status generally attenuated CVD risk related to low-grade albuminuria, while improvement of the latter additionally decreased CVD and cardiorenal risk, even in clinically elevated albuminuria (additive Pinteraction <0.0001). Hypertension showed the largest relative contribution to cardiorenal risk, particularly in UACR <30 mg/g.ConclusionsOur findings highlighted low-grade albuminuria as a key preventive window for cardiorenal outcomes requiring both aspects of modifiable risk factors, and the cardiovascular benefits of metabolic improvement in established albuminuria.Key words: albuminuria, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, lifestyle, metabolic risk factor, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)

Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), a sensitive and readily obtainable biomarker of endothelial dysfunction and glomerular damage, is widely recognized as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD).1 Current guidelines2, 3, 4 do not recommend the initiation of UACR screening until metabolic abnormalities or impaired kidney function reflected by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) are present, but still rely on a conventional dichotomous UACR cutoff value (30 mg/g) to define albuminuria, since most available evidence from randomized controlled trials only demonstrated definite benefits of treating albuminuria at this threshold.1

However, accumulating evidence argues that even a UACR level within “normal” range (<30 mg/g) may also convey cardiovascular, renal, and mortality risks in populations with5, 6, 7 and even without8, 9, 10 major metabolic risk factors. Retrospective meta-analyses have underscored that compared to the lowest UACR, mildly elevated UACR (10-30 mg/g) denoting low-grade albuminuria is already associated with adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes and mortality.11,12 Despite previous evidence, significant knowledge gaps remain. First, none of the prior studies have extended the outcome profile to a broad range of endpoints incorporating cardiovascular, renal, and composite events and the surrogate continuous markers, limiting the comprehensiveness and consistency. Second, the findings have not been validated in non-European populations, especially in Chinese populations with a substantial cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) disease burden, where nearly 80% of adults met the criteria for CKM syndrome.13 Third, although metabolic risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity have been reported to influence the association between UACR and adverse outcomes, whether healthy status of and improvement in modifiable risk factors covering both lifestyle and metabolic components can attenuate the detrimental association related to low-grade or clinically elevated albuminuria remains unknown. Addressing these uncertainties may aid in timely recognition and intervention regarding high-risk individuals.

Therefore, based on a large-scale community-based Chinese cohort, we aimed to: 1) investigate the association between fine-categorized UACR (<10, ≥10 to <30, and ≥30 mg/g) and incident CVD, CKD, and composite cardiorenal outcome; 2) detect the dose-response relationship of UACR level with cardiorenal outcomes and the surrogate markers of cardiorenal risk; and 3) examine how lifestyle and metabolic factors shape this association.

Methods

Study design

The prospective China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study is a multicenter, population-based, prospective cohort study that investigated the risk factors of CVD, metabolic diseases, cancer, and mortality.14 The protocol of the 4C Study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Briefly, 193,846 participants aged ≥40 years were recruited from 20 communities covering 16 provinces in mainland China in 2011 to 2012, and the follow-up visit was conducted in 2014 to 2016; 23,606 participants lost to follow-up. Participants with incomplete information on UACR or to determine baseline CVD or CKD status (n = 15,957), those with CVD or CKD already at baseline (n = 13,873), or those with missing information to determine CVD or CKD events during follow-up (n = 57,901) were further excluded, leaving a final sample of 82,509 participants (Supplemental Figure 1).

Measurements

Data collection was conducted face-to-face at community clinics. Sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, and medications were assessed using a standard questionnaire at baseline. Measurements were conducted by trained nurses. Height, weight, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured according to protocols. Fasting blood samples and first-morning spot urine samples were collected and shipped in dry ice to the central laboratory in Shanghai, where serum creatinine was measured for the calculation of eGFR based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD-EPI 2009) for Asian individuals,15 and urinary albumin and creatinine concentrations were measured for UACR calculation. Lifestyle information and UACR were collected and measured only at baseline, while metabolic factors including blood glucose, blood pressure, blood lipids, anthropometric measurements as well as eGFR were evaluated at both baseline and follow-up visits. The definitions of lifestyle factors (tobacco use, alcohol use, diet, physical activity, and sleep) and metabolic factors (diabetes, hypertension, abdominal obesity, and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) were described previously16 (Supplemental Table 2). Overall lifestyle status was defined by the number of healthy lifestyle factors (poor: 0-2 healthy factors; intermediate: 3 healthy factors; optimal: 4-5 healthy factors), and overall metabolic status was defined by the number of metabolic risk factors (poor: 3-4 risk factors; intermediate: 1-2 risk factors; optimal: no risk factor).

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Outcomes were collected from local death and disease registries of the National Disease Surveillance Point System and National Health Insurance System. Clinical events were verified independently by 2 experts on the outcome adjudication committee who were unaware of the baseline information; discrepancies were discussed and adjudicated by other committee members. Incident CVD events were defined as a composite of the first occurrence of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, hospitalized or treated heart failure, and cardiovascular death. Incident CKD was defined as the composite of CKD development (eGFR: <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and CKD progression (a ≥50% decrease in eGFR from baseline, an eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, development of end-stage renal disease requiring chronic dialysis or renal transplantation, or death due to CKD). Because follow-up serum creatinine was assessed once, CKD outcomes were defined based on the available follow-up eGFR measurement without confirmatory repeat testing. Composite cardiorenal outcomes were defined as the occurrence of CVD or CKD outcome, whichever came first. Finally, baseline value and annual change (slope) in PREVENT (Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs)14 score or eGFR were calculated as surrogate markers to quantify CVD or CKD risk, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Baseline key characteristics between the participants included and excluded were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student's t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. In addition, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess between-group differences, with values <0.10 considered indicative of negligible imbalance (Supplemental Table 1). Baseline characteristics of the included participants were summarized as mean ± SD, as counts and percentage, or as median with interquartile range (Supplemental Table 3). Differences across UACR categories were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. Incidence rates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Poisson distribution–based methods. We tested and confirmed the proportional-hazards assumption by calculating the Schoenfeld residuals (Supplemental Figure 2). The Cox proportional hazards model was used to calculate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals of UACR categories (<10 mg/g as reference) associated with incident events, with Wald tests applied for coefficient inference. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and education level. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for each lifestyle and metabolic factor. Model 3 was further adjusted for baseline eGFR, lipid-lowering treatment, antihypertensive treatment, hypoglycemic treatment, and depression status as assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted to explore the association between log-transformed UACR and incident events and surrogate markers for CVD or CKD risk (ie, the annual change [slope] of the PREVENT score or eGFR) to check the dose-response relationship. The optimal number of knots was determined by comparing model fit using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). A 4-knot configuration was selected, as it yielded the lowest AIC value compared to 3-knot and 5-knot models, providing the best balance between model parsimony and flexibility. Following the standard recommendations by Harrell, these 4 knots were positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the log-transformed UACR distribution.17,18

We analyzed the effect of overall lifestyle or metabolic status across UACR categories and evaluated their interactions by adding the corresponding interaction terms to the Cox proportional hazards models, with significance assessed using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) for interaction terms. In addition to multiplicative interaction, we further evaluated this interaction on the additive scale using an Aalen additive hazards model with constant coefficients, which allows direct assessment of the absolute risk difference.19 To further investigate the role of UACR in the association between lifestyle, metabolic status, and cardiorenal outcomes, we conducted a regression-based causal mediation analysis with the regdemint package to decompose the total, direct, and indirect effects (log-hazard scale) through UACR (log-transformed),20,21 guided by a directed acyclic graph mapping the causal pathways (Supplemental Figure 3, Supplemental Table 10). The combination association of UACR categories and different lifestyle or metabolic status (low, intermediate, optimal) with outcomes were explored by creating a 9-category variable, using the lowest UACR (<10 mg/g), with optimal status as reference. The contribution of separate lifestyle or metabolic factor was measured by both explained relative risk (ranging from 0 to 1)22 as well as hazard ratios (Supplemental Table 12). We also compared nested Cox models with and without each factor using LRT and changes in AIC to assess model fit (Supplemental Table 11).23

The main analysis was conducted in the original dataset with complete information on key exposure (UACR) and outcomes. As for missing covariates, a total of 390 distinct nonmonotone intersections were identified, and missingness ranged from 0.00% to 16.26% (Supplemental Figure 4A, Supplemental Table 4). The missing completely at random (MCAR) assumption was rejected based on Little's MCAR test (P < 0.001). For each incomplete variable, missingness indicators were regressed on observed covariates; missingness in all variables was significantly associated with at least 1 observed covariate already included in the imputation model, supporting the missing at random (MAR) assumption. Therefore, missing covariates were imputed by multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) (mice package in R), which accommodates arbitrary missing data patterns by estimating each variable's conditional distribution in turn, using observed values for the variable being imputed and the most recently imputed values for all other variables at each iteration, cycling through all incomplete variables until convergence. Methods are detailed in Supplemental Table 4. Ten imputed datasets were generated, with 10 iterations performed per dataset. Trace plots of the mean and variance of imputed values across 10 iterations showed stable mixing with no systematic trends for all incomplete variables, confirming convergence of the imputation algorithm (Supplemental Figure 4B). When comparing the distributions of variables before and after imputation, no significant differences were observed, indicating that the imputation process maintained the original data structure and distributions (Supplemental Figure 4C). Estimates were combined based on Rubin's rule. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age (<60 and ≥60 years), sex, and education level (high school or above and middle school or below) (Supplemental Table 5). Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect heterogeneity and were considered exploratory; no formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied. Sensitivity analysis was performed among participants without missing data on either lifestyle or metabolic factors (n = 52,903) (Supplemental Table 6). We additionally applied the propensity score–weighting method to control for confounding factors by balancing differences in the baseline characteristics among the 3 UACR groups by using the WeightIt and cobalt packages. The propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model including a comprehensive set of covariates: age, sex, educational attainment, lifestyle and metabolic factors, baseline eGFR, lipid-lowering treatment, antihypertensive treatment, hypoglycemic treatment, and depression status. Weights were then calculated based on the inverse probability of treatment weighting to estimate the average treatment effect. The balance of covariates was assessed using the WeightIt and cobalt packages, with a standardized mean difference of <0.1 considered as achieved balance (Supplemental Figure 5). Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were then fitted using survey-weighted regression methods to account for the applied weights using the survey package (Supplemental Table 7). Robust variance estimation was used to obtain valid standard errors under the weighted pseudo-population framework. In addition, considering the substantial intraindividual fluctuation of UACR particularly within the lower range,24 we reran the analysis after excluding those with boundary UACR values (10 mg/g ≤ UACR < 20 mg/g) (Supplemental Table 8). Finally, we analyzed the association between UACR and CKD outcome by treating cardiovascular death as a competing risk (Supplemental Table 9). The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was employed, with subdistribution hazard ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals being estimated using the riskRegression and FGR packages.

SAS software (version 9.4) and R software (version 4.3.3) were used for statistical analyses. Statistical significance was a 2-tailed P value of <0.05.

Results

Study population

Comparison of baseline characteristics between the included and excluded participants (Supplemental Table 1) showed that while study participants were slightly younger and had marginally better metabolic status, the SMDs for most variables were <0.1, indicating that the included cohort was largely representative of the broader population. Among 82,509 included participants (mean ± SD age, 56.29 ± 8.60 years; 66.2% [54,610] women), the participants in the 3 UACR categories were 56,333 (68.27%) for UACR <10 mg/g, 17,747 (21.51%) for UACR ≥10 to <30 mg/g, and 8,429 (10.22%) for UACR≥30 mg/g (Supplemental Table 3). Compared to the lowest UACR, participants in the higher UACR categories were older, less educated, and had higher body mass index, blood pressure levels, blood glucose, blood lipids, and lower eGFR (P < 0.001). With the elevation of UACR, the proportion of participants with metabolic risk factors rose in turn, whereas the metabolic risk factors of participants who met optimal lifestyle status generally dropped.

Association between urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio and cardiorenal outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 3.0 years (IQR: 2.9-3.2 years), 4,280 (5.19%) participants experienced a composite of cardiorenal outcomes, with an incidence rate of 16.32 (15.83-16.82) per 1000 person-years. Compared with UACR <10 mg/g, those with a UACR ≥10 to <30 mg/g had a 39% higher risk of composite outcomes (multivariable-adjusted HR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.29-1.50), and those with a UACR ≥30 mg/g had over double the risk (HR: 2.29, 95% CI: 2.12-2.47) (Table 1). As for separate CVD and CKD outcomes, 2,308 (2.80%) participants experienced incident CVD, among whom 302 (0.37%) experienced nonfatal myocardial infarction, 1,274 (1.56%) experienced nonfatal stroke, 184 (0.22%) were hospitalized or treated for heart failure, and 599 (0.73%) died from CVD. The upper 2 UACR categories were associated with significantly higher risks of incident CVD (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.27-1.56 for UACR ≥10 to <30 mg/g; HR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.81-2.25 for UACR ≥30 mg/g). Similar and progressively more pronounced associations were observed for nonfatal stroke, heart failure, and CVD death, while only the highest but not the mildly elevated UACR category was associated with an elevated risk of nonfatal myocardial infarction. As for renal outcomes, 2,088 (2.55%) participants experienced incident CKD, among whom 2,043 (2.49%) individuals had CKD development and 224 (0.27%) had CKD progression. Compared with the lowest UACR, the risk of incident CKD significantly increased among participants with UACR ≥10 to <30 mg/g (HR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.19-1.48) and UACR ≥30 mg/g (HR: 2.72, 95% CI: 2.44-3.03). Similar associations were found for CKD development and progression. No significant modification was found regarding sex and education level, while more evident cardiorenal and CKD risks were observed in participants aged <60 years (Supplemental Table 5). Results were not materially changed when excluding those with missing covariates at baseline (Supplemental Table 6), or when controlling for potential confounding using propensity score weighting (Supplemental Table 7). Associations remained robust for most outcomes except nonfatal stroke after excluding those with boundary UACR values (UACR 10 to <20 mg/g; Supplemental Table 8). When accounting for the competing risk of cardiovascular mortality using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models, the positive associations between higher UACR categories and the risk of all CKD outcomes were slightly attenuated but remained significant (Supplemental Table 9).

The dose-response relationships between baseline UACR and risk of cardiorenal outcomes are demonstrated in Figure 1A using RCS analyses, suggesting a U-shaped association between UACR and heart failure and a J-shaped association regarding the wcomposite cardiorenal outcome and incident CKD, with a nonlinear increasing trend. The adjusted HRs of CVD and key components, including nonfatal myocardial infarction and nonfatal stroke, increased linearly with baseline levels of UACR (P for nonlinearity >0.05). In particular, a UACR level of <5 to 10 mg/g was consistently associated with lower risks of composite cardiorenal outcomes, incident CVD, nonfatal stroke, and CVD mortality, whereas UACR >10 mg/g had already conferred risks for almost all cardiorenal outcomes. In addition to discrete outcomes, Figure 1B depicts the continuous relationship of baseline UACR with baseline and annual change (slope) in PREVENT score and eGFR. Baseline PREVENT score showed slight variation at a UACR level of less than 5 mg/g, but showed a continuous upward trend once UACR exceeded this threshold, whereas baseline eGFR exhibited a progressive decline when UACR exceeded 10 mg/g. Likewise, the annual change (slope) of both eGFR and PREVENT score also showed more rapid worsening when UACR exceeded 5 mg/g and 10 mg/g, respectively.

Lifestyle and metabolic status shape the association between UACR and cardiorenal outcomes

The association of UACR and cardiorenal outcomes was further analyzed according to baseline lifestyle or metabolic status (Figure 2). The positive association between UACR level and incidence of all cardiorenal outcomes persisted across different strata of lifestyle status, with slight variations. When stratified by metabolic status, compared with poor metabolic status, participants who had optimal metabolic status were less susceptible to a pronounced increase in incident cardiorenal or CKD risks when baseline UACR went beyond 30 mg/g. We also modelled the annual change of PREVENT score and eGFR according to lifestyle or metabolic status, and the most evident deterioration in CVD risk score and renal function with the increase of UACR was observed in subgroups with the worst lifestyle and metabolic status at high UACR levels (Figure 2B).

We further examined the associations of baseline lifestyle and metabolic status with cardiorenal outcomes across each UACR category (Table 2). Compared with poor lifestyle status, optimal lifestyle status was related to lower risks of composite cardiorenal outcome in those with UACR <10 mg/g and lower risks of incident CVD in those with UACR <30 mg/g, while no significant improvement was observed for CKD risk. Compared with poor metabolic status, the most prominent cardiorenal risk reduction by optimal metabolic status was observed in the lowest UACR category, with a significant additive interaction effect (P for interaction [multiplicative: 0.1824; additive <0.0001]). On the other hand, optimal baseline metabolic status induced CVD risk reduction in all UACR categories, especially the highest, but only mitigated CKD risk in those with a UACR <10 mg/g (P for interaction [multiplicative: 0.4470; additive <0.0001]).

When taking follow-up metabolic status transition into consideration, patients with persistent or worsening metabolic status exhibited significantly higher annual increases in PREVENT score, especially in those with the highest UACR, indicating accelerated cardiovascular risk accrual; the slope for those who sustained intermediate-to-optimal metabolic status was flatter. As for the decline in eGFR, the most rapid deterioration observed in those whose metabolic health status deteriorated or remained poor when UACR exceeded 10 mg/g (Supplemental Figure 6).

In mediation analyses, UACR partially mediated the associations of both metabolic status and lifestyle with cardiorenal outcomes (Supplemental Table 10). The associations between metabolic status (optimal vs poor) and cardiorenal outcomes were partially explained by significant indirect effects through log-transformed UACR, with mediation proportions of 22.75% (95% CI: 15.12%-30.39%) for the composite outcome, 9.87% (95% CI: 4.07%-15.66%) for CVD, and 50.39% (95% CI: 25.97%-74.80%) for CKD. Discussion

In this Chinese cohort, we found that elevated UACR even within the traditionally defined “normal” range (<30 mg/g) was associated with elevated risks of a broad spectrum of cardiovascular and renal outcomes (Central Illustration). Notably, optimal lifestyle status would attenuate CVD risk related to low-grade albuminuria, but the benefits mostly diminished in clinically elevated albuminuria; however, metabolic improvements reduced cardiovascular risk across the full spectrum of UACR especially those with highest UACR, but renal benefits appeared to be confined largely to those with UACR <10 mg/g. Furthermore, hypertension served as a critical modifiable risk factor for cardiorenal outcomes, especially in those with UACR <30 mg/g. These findings reinforce the clinical relevance of low-grade albuminuria, challenge the conventional notion that UACR ≥30 mg/g constitutes a meaningful risk threshold, and further provide evidence for the importance of timely prevention targeting both lifestyle and metabolic profiles among populations across all albuminuria strata.

Modifiable Risk Factor, Low-Grade Albuminuria, and Cardiorenal Risk

Lifestyle and metabolic health in relation to the associations of low-grade to clinically elevated albuminuria with cardiovascular-renal diseases in a Chinese population. 4C = China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort; CKD = chronic kidney disease; CVD = cardiovascular disease; eGFR = estimated glomerular filtration rate; LDL-C = low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; PREVENT = Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs; UACR = urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio.

This study provided the first large-scale prospective evidence in the Chinese population that UACR levels of 10 to 30 mg/g exhibit consistently elevated risks of nearly all individual and composite cardiorenal events, a trend independent of baseline eGFR levels (Table 1). Our findings add to current knowledge by comprehensive evaluation of subtypes, composite, and surrogate markers of cardiovascular and renal outcomes associated with albuminuria (especially within the normal range) in a general Chinese population, compared to previous literature focused on mortality8,25 or single outcomes10,26,27 in restricted subpopulations.28 Notably, we revealed that the unique association between UACR and CVD outcomes differed by subtypes; specifically, the greatest increase in risk with the upgrading UACR categories was observed for CVD mortality, followed by heart failure and nonfatal stroke, a pattern also mirrored by the corresponding steepness of slopes from the RCS. The only exception was the lack of a significant association between a UACR level ≥10 to <30 mg/g and nonfatal myocardial infarction. Findings coincide with a meta-analysis identifying UACR as a strong predictor for CVD death, heart failure, and stroke,29 and are partly consistent with prior evidence reporting nonsignificant association for nonfatal myocardial infarction6,30 in a mildly elevated UACR group. The lower event rates for myocardial infarction may also contribute to this weaker association. We also extended the renal harm of low-grade albuminuria not only to CKD progression but also to CKD development and faster eGFR decline,31 emphasizing its prognostic relevance in identifying early-stage kidney injury and future renal risk.

Our findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on a dichotomous UACR cutoff. When evaluated as a continuous variable, a generally linear relationship was observed between UACR and CVD components, including nonfatal myocardial infarction or stroke, in a dose-response pattern (Figure 1A). A U-shaped association was observed for heart failure. The monotonic increasing trend starting from 5 to 10 mg/g was similarly observed in patients with diabetes or atrial fibrillation,32,33 which may be attributed to damage to the kidney filtration barrier and subsequent volume overload, while the apparent upturn at the very low end of UACR likely reflects sparse data and instability at the boundary of the distribution rather than a true risk increase. In contrast, the relationship between UACR and incident CKD as well as composite cardiorenal outcome followed a more skewed J-shaped pattern, characterized by a relatively flat risk at very low UACR values followed by a marked acceleration beyond a low-grade albuminuria threshold. This may be attributed to the differential pathophysiological processes: Although endothelial dysfunction and vascular inflammation may linearly influence CVD risk, kidney damage and integrated cardiorenal injury may accumulate more gradually until a critical point is reached given the vicious cycle created by the crosstalk of endothelial cell damage with podocyte and tubular injury.34,35 Accordingly, we observed a particularly pronounced risk elevation beyond a certain threshold—as low as 5 to 10 mg/g—well below the current guideline threshold of 30 mg/g. It was also corroborated by the sharp worsening of CVD risk score or kidney function when UACR exceeded 5 to 10 mg/g, measured by PREVENT score and eGFR slope (Figure 1B). In accordance with previous studies reporting a risk-increase threshold for CVD and/or all-cause death of approximately 5 to 10 mg/g,8,36,37 and that for CKD incidence or progression ranging from 7 to 10 mg/g,10,38 particularly extending evidence from East Asian populations,39,40 our study also indicated the presence of a “subclinical” risk zone around 5 to 10 mg/g. Given the high prevalence of CKM in China,13 such finding may have unique implications for guidelines, as this easy, scalable marker may yield substantial public health and health-economic benefits by shifting prevention efforts upstream and averting costly cardiorenal events.

Although the preventive roles of modifiable factors on cardiovascular and renal outcomes have been widely documented, evidence remains scarce regarding how these factors, especially lifestyle, affects the association between UACR and outcomes. Using the enriched data from the 4C Study, we conducted a stratified analysis based on UACR categories as well as their combination with modifiable risk profiles defined by a broad range of lifestyle and metabolic factors. We found that the deteriorative impact of mildly elevated UACR on adverse CVD outcome could be counteracted by achieving more healthy lifestyle factors, while the effect was barely detectable for high UACR levels (Table 2). Likewise, as for joint association, improvement to an optimal lifestyle status would partly attenuate the risk of incident CVD events related to mildly elevated UACR, but this was not observed for UACR ≥30 mg/g (Figure 3). Although lifestyle was only collected at baseline, evidence from community-based cohorts suggests that it tends to show considerable stability over time and thus reasonably captures long-term behavioral patterns.41, 42, 43 Our results therefore indicate that optimal lifestyle may confer greater cardiovascular protective effects at lower UACR levels. Further research investigating the effects of lifestyle maintenance or improvement is still warranted. In contrast, better baseline metabolic status exerted favorable effects on CVD in all 3 UACR categories, especially UACR ≥30 mg/g, a trend that coincided with the most evident cardiovascular risk reduction after dynamic metabolic status improvement during follow-up, suggesting that baseline metabolic health continues to play a critical role in determining long-term prognosis. The benefit of better metabolic status on CKD was constrained to the lowest category, with a significant additive interaction effect. However, participants with intermediate metabolic status manifested the most pronounced risk gradients for CKD, whereas their metabolically unhealthy counterparts displayed more flattened risk curves. This paradoxical attenuation may reflect competing risk displacement, whereby individuals with poor metabolic health face an elevated probability of fatal cardiovascular events before CKD could be formally ascertained.44 Another possible explanation is a form of risk saturation among metabolically unhealthy individuals, for whom elevated UACR conferred limited additional risk. More intuitively, both optimal lifestyle and metabolic status ameliorated the worsening of cardiorenal risk, manifesting as a deceleration in both PREVENT and eGFR slope as UACR surpassed 10 mg/g (Figure 2B). Taken together, these results further reinforce the necessity of synergistic management of both lifestyle and metabolic factors for cardiorenal health, especially the significance of low-grade albuminuria (10-30 mg/g) as a pivotal preventive window for mitigating cardiorenal risk by lifestyle and metabolic management, and the extra benefits from metabolic improvement in clinical albuminuria.

Furthermore, the pattern of how overall lifestyle and metabolic status shape the UACR-risk association varied by metrics. The analysis of diverse modifiable risk factors is thus essential for delineating their heterogeneous roles across UACR categories. Of note, the relative importance and hazard ratio particularly strengthened the role of blood pressure control for cardiorenal health in those with UACR <30 mg/g (Figure 4). In this stage featured by reversible subclinical injury, systemic hypertension primarily translates into intraglomerular hypertension and glomerular hyperfiltration, leading to endothelial and glycocalyx damage, increased albumin permeability, and activation of inflammatory and fibrotic pathways that initiate cardiorenal injury.45 However, following the disease progression, the cumulative burden of hypertension-induced damage overlaps with other complex pathological process and comorbidities, diluting the specific contribution. Nevertheless, among individuals with mildly elevated UACR, blood pressure remains a dominant and actionable determinant of cardiorenal risk. Although the mechanisms are not fully understood, accumulated evidence from clinical trials indicates that antihypertensive treatments involving intensive blood pressure lowering targets46 or specific drug classes such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin 2 receptor blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists with antiproteinuric effects improve cardio-kidney outcomes.47,48 Intriguingly, abdominal obesity demonstrated a significant interaction with UACR category for cardiorenal outcomes. While abdominal obesity was associated with increased risk at low UACR (<10 mg/g), this association attenuated and even reversed at higher UACR levels, potentially reflecting the obesity paradox previously described in CKD populations.49 In summary, these insights collectively support an early, multifaceted, and targeted intervention strategy for cardiorenal health during a seemingly clinically safe, low-grade albuminuria phase, with a priority of hypertension management as a linchpin intervention.

Strengths of the present study include the large and well-characterized Chinese sample, the prospective design, and the extension of outcome profile to a broad range of endpoints incorporating cardiovascular, renal, and composite events, and their surrogate continuous markers.

Study limitations

First, a large proportion of participants were excluded because of missing data. However, most imbalances between excluded and included participants assessed by SMD were considered negligible (Supplemental Table 1). Excluded participants also had a generally higher cardiometabolic risk profile, which would be expected to bias associations toward the null. Within the analytic cohort, consistency across multiple imputation, propensity score weighting, and complete-case analyses supports the robustness of our findings. Second, the follow-up duration of the current analysis was relatively short; however, the large sample size yielded a substantial number of adjudicated cardiorenal events, providing adequate statistical power to detect meaningful associations. Caution is still needed in interpreting causal relationships, and studies with longer follow-up are warranted. Third, lifestyle information was only collected at baseline. However, since habitual behaviors show moderate stability over time in this community-based cohort, baseline lifestyle status still provides meaningful insight into long-term risk patterns, even though dynamic changes during follow-up could not be captured. Fourth, repeated measurements of serum creatinine or urinary albumin were unavailable, so CKD cases were defined without confirmation, and the longitudinal trajectory of UACR could not be captured; the day-to-day variability may cause bias.50,51 However, to mitigate potential misclassification from short-term biological variability, sensitivity analyses excluding participants with UACR values close to the cutoff yielded consistent results. Fifth, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups or populations with different cardiorenal risk profiles may be limited. In addition, residual confounding from genetic susceptibility, inflammatory burden, neurohormonal activation, and health care access cannot be fully excluded. Finally, findings from subgroup analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and are not be used for causal inference.

Conclusions

Based on a large-scale, nationwide, community-based Chinese cohort, we observed that even modest elevations in UACR, well below current clinical thresholds (<30 mg/g), were significantly associated with increased risks of cardiorenal outcomes. The risk gradients appeared to increase at UACR levels as low as 10 mg/g, underscoring the prognostic relevance of low-grade albuminuria. Furthermore, the risk attenuation effect by favorable metabolic profiles or healthier lifestyle in individuals with mildly elevated UACR supported the potential of early intervention, while metabolic improvement still conferred additional cardiovascular benefits, even in those with clinically elevated UACR. Beyond challenging the adequacy of current dichotomous UACR thresholds, our finding stressed the importance of lifestyle and metabolic status by elucidating how they differently attenuate the detrimental association related to low-grade to clinically elevated albuminuria, and they provide actionable clinical insights. Future trials are warranted to rigorously assess the benefits of screening and intervening upon the albuminuria spectrum.
