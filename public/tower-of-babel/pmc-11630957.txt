Evaluating the efficacy of full-scale and tabletop exercises in enhancing paramedic preparedness for external disasters: A quasi-experimental study
Authors: Ghadeer A. Alakrawi, Ahmed M. Al-Wathinani, Juan Gómez-Salgado, Abdullah M. Alobaid, Mohammed Abahussian, Riyadh Alhazmi, Abdulmajeed Mobrad, Albaraa Jebreel, Saqar Althunayyan, Krzysztof Goniewicz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The increasing frequency and severity of disasters worldwide needs effective disaster management training to enhance the preparedness and response capabilities of emergency responders. This study compares the outcomes of tabletop exercises (TTEs) and full-scale exercises (FSEs) in training senior paramedic students at Prince Sultan bin Abdul Aziz College for Emergency Medical Services, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.Methods:A nonequivalent two-group quasi-experimental design was used. Forty-five senior paramedic students who had completed a disaster management course were randomly assigned to either TTE or FSE groups. Both groups participated in a simulated multi-vehicle accident scenario. Data were collected using an adapted CLUT Scale survey, which measured learning, trust, and usefulness immediately after the exercises. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare outcomes between the 2 groups.Results:Both TTE and FSE resulted in high levels of perceived learning, trust, and usefulness among participants, indicating a positive impact on disaster preparedness. While the TTE group scored slightly higher on average across all variables, these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that both exercise types are similarly effective. The findings underscore the cost-effectiveness of TTEs and their potential to foster collaborative learning environmentsConclusions:Both TTE and FSE are effective in enhancing disaster preparedness among senior paramedic students. Given their adaptability and cost-effectiveness, TTEs may be particularly valuable for resource-constrained training settings. Further research with larger, more diverse samples and pre-/post-intervention assessments is recommended to validate these findings and improve disaster management training programs.

1. Introduction

The global impact of disasters continues to rise, resulting in increased human suffering and devastating losses of life. Consequently, risk reduction and resilience are being prioritized worldwide to mitigate these adverse effects.[1,2] As the demand for effective disaster management exercises and enhanced personnel competencies grows, it becomes imperative to develop and implement training programs that prepare responders for real-world emergencies.[3]

Shalhoub et al (2017) conducted an observational study in private hospitals across Central of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia to assess their preparedness for disasters. The study revealed that only a limited number of hospitals conducted mass casualty incident exercises, highlighting the urgent need for better education, training, and monitoring of hospital staff to effectively respond to disaster emergencies.[4] Despite the focus of the Ministry of Health and private hospitals on addressing the immediate needs of patients, there remains a critical need for financial agreements to allocate funds for education, readiness planning, backup supplies, and training expenses.[5]

Another study by Al-thunayyan et al (2021) collected cross-sectional data from Riyadh’s Saudi Red Crescent Authority stations to assess the knowledge of emergency medical service workers about the disaster triage system. Surprisingly, the study found no correlation between years of experience and triage performance, but it did find a positive association with previous disaster training experience.[6] Furthermore, 2 additional studies underscore the crucial role of disaster management exercises (DMEs) in acquiring the necessary knowledge and competencies for effective disaster response.[7,8]

To decrease the negative effects of hazards and improve knowledge and training to avoid worst-case scenarios, disaster education is essential.[9,10] Effective disaster response training requires practical exercises that enhance both skills and knowledge, emphasizing active teamwork, task allocation, and communication skills, which are vital in disaster management.[11,12] DMEs such as full-scale exercises (FSEs) and Tabletop Exercises (TTEs) provide tailored approaches to training, offering knowledge and skills according to the requirements of the learning process.[13]

The FSE is a fully simulated learning approach used to practice and test nearly all functions of a disaster response plan. In this exercise, an actual event is simulated under realistic conditions, and participants act and use medical instruments to simulate responses using volunteer actors or manikins. A discussion follows the exercise to review performance and identify areas for improvement.[14–19] On the other hand, the TTE involves team members discussing their roles in managing a disaster in a low-stress environment. Participants simulate action responses using pens, graphs, papers, and mini-dolls, with a facilitator guiding the discussion and reviewing achievements and areas for improvement. While TTEs are more time, cost, and resource-efficient, they compromise on realism and practicality, particularly in demonstrating medical procedures. In contrast, FSEs offer a comprehensive and realistic disaster response experience that can simulate highly stressful situations encountered in real life.[20]

Several past studies have investigated TTE and FSE as separate entities, confirming their effectiveness in improving disaster management competencies through direct education.[21–24] Roud et al (2020) conducted a survey comparing the 2 exercises, examining their impact on usefulness, learning, collaboration, and trust among emergency management personnel in Norway and Canada. The study found that both exercises were beneficial, enhancing collaboration, learning, and trust. However, FSEs were perceived as more practical and showed greater positive effects on learning. The author suggested that further research could provide more valuable insights by examining both exercises under similar situations.[13]

Conducting FSE and TTE is crucial for training and improving the capabilities of disaster responders. In Saudi Arabia, FSE exercises are more common, while TTE exercises are less prevalent, possibly due to a lack of awareness or understanding of TTE among those responsible for disaster preparedness. Although FSEs require significant resources, it is essential to evaluate the impact of both FSE and TTE on disaster preparedness. This study examines the effectiveness of FSE and TTE in enhancing individual preparedness for external disasters, specifically their impact on trust, learning, and perceived usefulness.

The study seeks to address the following key questions: How do FSE and TTE compare in terms of enhancing learning outcomes among senior paramedic students? This question aims to evaluate which exercise type better facilitates the acquisition of knowledge and skills necessary for effective disaster response. Additionally, the research explores the impact of FSE and TTE on the perceived trust and confidence of participants. Understanding this aspect is crucial, as trust and confidence are vital for effective teamwork and decision-making during actual disasters.

Another important aspect of this study is assessing how participants perceive the usefulness of FSE and TTE in preparing them for real-world disaster response. By gathering insights into participants’ perceptions, the study aims to identify which exercise type is viewed as more practical and beneficial for real-world application. Answering these questions will provide valuable insights that can inform the development and implementation of more effective disaster management training programs globally.

The aim of this study is to compare the effectiveness of FSE and TTE in enhancing individual preparedness for external disasters. Specifically, this research focuses on their impact on learning outcomes, perceived trust, and the confidence of participants in disaster management scenarios, as well as the overall perceived usefulness of these exercises in preparing participants for real-world disaster response.

2. Materials and methods

A nonequivalent two-group quasi-experimental design was used in this study. The same external disaster simulation is used as the intervention, where FSEs are used for the second group and TTEs for the first group.

2.1. Study environment

The study was conducted on January 18, 2024, at Prince Sultan bin Abdul Aziz College for Emergency Medical Services (PSCEMS), which is part of King Saud University located in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The setting provided a controlled environment where senior paramedic students could participate in structured disaster management exercises. These exercises included both TTEs and FSEs, designed to simulate external disaster scenarios.

The PSCEMS facilities are equipped with advanced medical training equipment and simulation tools, which enabled the realistic enactment of disaster scenarios. The exercises took place in designated simulation rooms and outdoor spaces that were configured to mimic real-life emergency situations. This setup allowed for comprehensive assessment of participants’ responses to simulated disaster events, ensuring the collection of robust and relevant data for the study.

To facilitate the exercises, experienced instructors and facilitators from the college oversaw the activities, providing guidance and ensuring adherence to the simulation protocols. This structured and supportive environment was essential for maintaining the integrity of the study and ensuring that the data collected accurately reflected the participants’ performance and learning outcomes.

2.2. Population and sample

The study involved a total of 45 senior paramedic students who had completed a disaster management academic course at PSCEMS. Initially, 87 students were invited to participate in the study. Out of these, 51 students responded to the invitation. However, 6 students withdrew from the study at a later stage, resulting in a final sample size of 45 participants.

To ensure an unbiased distribution, the students were assigned to the 2 different types of exercises –TTEs and SFEs – using a simple random sampling method. This approach ensured that each student had an equal chance of being placed in either exercise group, thereby minimizing selection bias and enhancing the reliability of the study’s findings. The random assignment was performed using a computer-generated randomization list to ensure objectivity in the group allocation process.

2.2.1. Sample size calculation

A power analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate sample size for this study using an estimated effect size derived from similar quasi-experimental studies in disaster preparedness training. Using a significance level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the analysis indicated a minimum sample size of approximately 64 participants per group to detect small-to-moderate effect differences between TTE and FSE groups. Given the actual sample size of 45 participants, we recognize the limitation in power and recommend caution in interpreting the findings. Future research with a larger sample size may provide more robust conclusions.

2.2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants in this study were required to meet specific inclusion criteria. Eligible participants were senior paramedic students at PSCEMS who had completed a disaster management academic course. Additionally, only those with no prior experience in similar tabletop or full-scale disaster preparedness exercises were included to ensure a uniform baseline in disaster management training. Availability on the scheduled exercise day was also required to maintain consistency in the intervention experience.

Exclusion criteria included students who had previously participated in structured disaster management or simulation exercises, as previous exposure could influence their learning outcomes and responses. Students were also excluded if they reported medical conditions or physical limitations that could prevent full participation in the exercises.

2.3. Survey instrument

This research utilized an English version of the CLUT Scale, initially developed by Swedish experts in the field of accidents and disasters. The survey aimed to evaluate participants’ perceived learning, trust, and usefulness of the disaster preparedness exercises. Each item was rated on a Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).[25]

To align the survey with the study’s focus on individual preparedness for external disasters, the collaboration dimension was excluded. The final survey comprised 3 variables – learning, trust, and usefulness – with a total of 18 items. Cronbach alpha was used to assess the reliability of these scales, yielding an overall coefficient of 0.85, indicating a high level of internal consistency. Specifically, the learning subscale had a Cronbach alpha of 0.82, the trust subscale 0.87, and the usefulness subscale 0.84, all exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.70.

To ensure clarity and comprehensibility of the English-language items, a pilot test was conducted with a subset of senior paramedic students proficient in English. Feedback from this pilot confirmed that the survey items were understandable and contextually relevant for the participants. The detailed items of the survey are provided in Appendix 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O92.

2.4. Data collection and procedure

Data collection was carried out immediately after the completion of each exercise to capture the participants’ immediate responses and minimize recall bias. To prevent any influence on responses, the students completed the surveys individually in a controlled environment. Each student was given a survey immediately following their exercise session, ensuring that their reflections and evaluations were fresh.

To ensure the accuracy and quality of the collected data, a thorough cross-check was conducted. This involved the researcher meeting each participant in a private and quiet area where distractions were minimized. During this session, the researcher reviewed the completed questionnaire with the participant, addressing any ambiguities or misunderstandings in their responses. This process not only helped in clarifying the participants’ answers but also in maintaining the consistency and reliability of the data.

Additionally, all survey responses were anonymized to protect participants’ privacy and encourage honest and unbiased feedback. The data collection procedure was meticulously planned and executed to ensure that the results were as accurate and reflective of the participants’ true perceptions and experiences as possible.

Although the sample size was small, meticulous efforts were made to ensure the integrity and reliability of the data. These efforts included a controlled data collection environment, individual survey completion to avoid influence among participants, and thorough cross-checking of responses. Future studies should consider larger sample sizes to build upon these findings and provide more generalizable results.

2.5. Emergency management exercises

As part of the best disaster response team competition, senior paramedic students at PSCEMS, classified as “level 8” within their academic program, participated in 2 types of comprehensive emergency management exercises designed to simulate a realistic multi-vehicle accident scenario involving 15 patients: TTE and FSE.

In the TTE group, 24 students (divided into 8 teams of 3) engaged in structured, discussion-based sessions where they simulated responses to a hypothetical disaster scenario. These exercises focused on strategic planning, team coordination, and communication in a low-stress environment. Guided by a facilitator, participants discussed their roles and actions without physical enactment, using visual aids like charts and maps to simulate response actions. TTEs prioritize collaborative problem-solving and theoretical understanding, making them a time- and cost-efficient option for disaster preparedness training.

In contrast, the FSE group, comprising 21 students (in 7 teams of 3), participated in a fully immersive, hands-on experience. FSEs simulate real-world disaster response conditions, requiring participants to interact with physical elements such as medical equipment and mock patients. This format emphasizes practical, immediate decision-making and task allocation under stress, replicating the pressures of actual disaster scenarios. Though resource-intensive, FSEs enhance practical skills that are critical in real-life emergencies.

Each team in both groups responded independently to the same scenario, with 17 repetitions of the exercise ensuring consistency. Following each session, a structured debriefing allowed participants to reflect on their performance, discuss challenges, and identify improvement areas. This iterative process, completed within a single day, provided a comprehensive assessment of each team’s response capabilities and learning outcomes.

2.5.1. Scenario development and validation

The disaster response scenarios used in both TTE and FSE exercises were developed by a team of emergency management and disaster response experts from PSCEMS. These scenarios were designed to reflect realistic multi-vehicle accident conditions based on established protocols and past disaster response cases, ensuring alignment with actual emergency response requirements. To validate the exercises, the scenarios were reviewed by faculty members specializing in disaster management and emergency medical services, as well as external consultants with field experience in large-scale disaster response. Feedback from these experts helped refine the scenarios to ensure they accurately tested key competencies, such as teamwork, decision-making, and adaptability under pressure. This validation process ensures that the exercises provide realistic and meaningful training experiences that effectively prepare participants for real-world disaster scenarios.

2.6. Statistical analysis

The research data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0 to ensure robust statistical evaluation. Before choosing the appropriate statistical test, the normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Both tests showed P-values <.05, indicating that the data were not normally distributed. Consequently, nonparametric analysis methods were utilized for further data assessment.[24]

To ensure comparability between the TTE and FSE groups, baseline tests were conducted on key sociodemographic and preparedness variables prior to the exercises. Using the Mann–Whitney U test, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups on these baseline measures (P > .05), indicating that the groups were comparable and suitable for the quasi-experimental intervention.

2.7. Ethical considerations

Informed consent was provided by all participants in the research, who were assured that their involvement was entirely voluntary, with the freedom to withdraw without any adverse consequences. After collecting the data, it was stored at the college’s research center and was only used for research purposes, with strict confidentiality maintained. The researchers were not allowed to disclose any participant’s identity, ensuring the anonymity of participants. The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at King Saud University which issued the certificate of permission for the study (IRB Log Number: E-23-7991; dated September 13, 2023).

3. Results

Descriptive statistics of participants’ responses to questionnaire items were calculated using mean and standard deviation. All items were rated on a 1 to 5-point Likert scale. According to Pimentel,[13] with an equal interval length of (4/5 = 0.80), the mean score was classified as follows: (1–<1.80) very low; (1.80–<2.60) low; (2.60–<3.40) medium; (3.40–<4.20) high; and (4.20–5) very high levels.

The study sample included 45 senior paramedic students at PSCEMS, aged between 20 and 25 years, with a balanced gender distribution among male and female students. All participants had completed a disaster management course prior to the study, ensuring a uniform level of preparedness in disaster response. This sociodemographic information provides context without compromising the anonymity of participants. This approach adheres to confidentiality agreements and ethical guidelines as mandated by the informed consent process and approved by the Institutional Review Board.

4. Discussion

This study bridges a knowledge gap by comparing the effectiveness of TTE and FSE exercises in preparing paramedic students for disaster response scenarios. Both exercise types were administered using the same realistic multi-vehicle accident scenario to ensure consistency, and data were collected immediately after this to capture participants’ immediate perceptions. High mean scores across learning, trust, and usefulness were found in both groups, indicating that TTE and FSE are effective in disaster management training.

However, the Mann–Whitney U test results revealed no statistically significant differences between TTE and FSE in terms of learning outcomes, trust, and perceived usefulness, despite slightly higher mean scores in the TTE group. This non-significance implies that both exercises contribute similarly to preparedness, and neither can be conclusively preferred based on these findings alone. The small sample size may have limited the statistical power, suggesting that future studies with larger groups could reveal more definitive trends.

These findings align with the importance of DMEs in Saudi Arabia, as highlighted in studies by Al Thobaity[26] and Sultan et al,[8,27] which emphasized the role of exercises in improving skills such as collaboration and perceived utility. These results differ from those observed by Roud et al, who found FSEs to be more effective in learning and usefulness. This discrepancy may be due to methodological differences; Roud et al used an observational design, while our quasi-experimental approach controlled for background variables such as education and experience.[13]

TTEs have unique advantages in disaster training due to their discussion-based, collaborative structure, which enhances understanding of response protocols, communication skills, and team trust. The less stressful environment of TTEs may facilitate deeper understanding of roles and responsibilities, fostering a cooperative team dynamics that is essential in real-world scenarios where effective communication can have an impact on the outcomes. Additionally, TTEs are adaptable, allowing integration of various disaster scenarios – from natural hazards to man-made crises – and enabling participants to build a broad skill set. The ability to tailor TTEs to emerging threats and recent disaster experiences makes them a versatile tool in dynamic training environments.[28,29]

Reflective learning in TTEs, supported by structured debriefing sessions, allows participants to assess their actions, identify alternative strategies, and learn from successes and errors, thus promoting critical thinking and ongoing skill development. Introducing digital tools like simulation software and virtual reality can further enhance TTE effectiveness, offering immersive experiences that simulate complex scenarios.[30] This integration not only engages participants but also allows for detailed performance tracking, providing a clear path for skill refinement over time.[31–34]

Although the hands-on, immersive nature of FSEs is highly valuable for testing immediate, practical skills under realistic pressures, the lack of stress in TTEs may aid cognitive processing, contributing to improved learning outcomes. TTEs generally require fewer resources, making them a cost-effective alternative for disaster preparedness training. By balancing TTE and FSE approaches based on resource availability, institutional goals, and training objectives, organizations can optimize disaster preparedness programs to effectively equip responders without overwhelming them.

Moreover, both TTE and FSE exercises may contribute to building resilience and adaptability among responders, which are essential qualities in real-world disaster response scenarios. While FSEs provide practical experience and a realistic environment for applying immediate response skills, TTEs promote critical thinking and collaborative problem-solving that can support long-term knowledge retention. These complementary qualities may enhance participants’ confidence in handling various disaster scenarios by developing both technical skills and decision-making abilities in stressful situations. Future studies could assess the impact of each exercise type on resilience and confidence to determine their effectiveness in fostering a workforce that can respond dynamically to evolving emergencies.

Furthermore, cultural factors specific to Saudi Arabia, such as hierarchical decision-making norms, may have influenced participants’ interactions, particularly in TTE exercises where open discussion and equal contribution are encouraged. Cultural differences in communication styles, authority dynamics, and teamwork may affect engagement levels and the perceived effectiveness of each exercise type. To provide more universally applicable conclusions, future studies could examine TTE and FSE training outcomes across varied cultural and institutional contexts. Understanding how cultural factors impact disaster preparedness training could lead to tailored, more effective training methods in different regions.

Ultimately, this study suggests that both TTEs and FSEs are valuable for disaster preparedness. Future research with larger, more diverse samples, as well as longitudinal studies exploring long-term impacts on skills and knowledge retention, could provide a clearer understanding of how each exercise type supports sustained disaster response capabilities. This research could also investigate how combining TTE and FSE elements within training curricula might maximize the strengths of each, building a comprehensive foundation in both theoretical and practical disaster response skills.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations affecting the generalizability and robustness of its findings. First, the small sample size of 45 participants limits statistical power, making it challenging to detect significant differences and reducing the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the study was conducted within a single institution, restricting its applicability to other settings with different resources and student demographics.

The quasi-experimental design lacked randomization and did not include a pretest to measure baseline levels of learning, trust, and usefulness prior to the intervention. This design choice limits the ability to directly assess changes motivated by each exercise type, introducing a potential weakness in the findings. Future studies would benefit from incorporating a pretest to offer clearer insights into the effects of each exercise.

Potential response bias is also a concern, as participants knew their responses were part of a study, which could possibly affect data reliability. Moreover, the cultural context of Saudi Arabia may have influenced perceptions and interactions during the exercises. Cultural factors, such as societal norms around hierarchy, teamwork, and communication, may have affected how participants engaged in each exercise type, especially in TTEs, which rely on open discussion. In cultures that emphasize hierarchical structures, participants may be more likely to defer to authority figures, potentially influencing engagement and response patterns. These cultural nuances might limit the applicability of the findings to other regions with different cultural attitudes toward teamwork and authority.

Lastly, while TTEs offer a low-stress environment, they lack hands-on practice in medical procedures, affecting their realism compared to FSEs. Addressing these limitations in future research, including larger samples, pretest and posttest designs, and randomized experimental methods could strengthen the evidence on the effectiveness of disaster management exercises.

6. Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of both TTE and FSE in enhancing disaster preparedness among senior paramedic students. Both exercise types yielded high levels of perceived learning, trust, and usefulness, highlighting their value in disaster management training. Although the TTE group exhibited slightly higher average scores across all variables, these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that both TTEs and FSEs contribute comparably to preparedness.

The findings suggest that TTEs, with their cost-effectiveness and capacity to foster a collaborative learning environment, are particularly suitable for training programs where resources or time may be limited. This adaptability supports their broader integration into disaster preparedness frameworks. However, the immersive, hands-on nature of FSEs provides practical experience that may be critical in real-world scenarios, especially for skill application under stress.

The study also underscores the need for larger sample sizes and more diverse participant groups in future research to validate these findings. The inclusion of pre- and post-intervention assessments could further clarify the specific impacts of each exercise type. Additionally, future studies should explore long-term effects on knowledge retention and skill application to build a comprehensive evidence base for optimizing disaster preparedness training. This research contributes valuable insights to the field, informing the development of more effective and resource-efficient disaster management training programs.
