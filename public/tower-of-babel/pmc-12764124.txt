The combined association of the neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) and the uric acid-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) with adverse cardiac events in patients with chronic heart failure: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yibei Fu, Guo Song, Shaojun Wu, Lingyu Ma, Xue Bao, Xiaoli Liu, Rong Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR) and uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) are associated with adverse cardiovascular (CV) outcomes. However, their combined prognostic value in chronic heart failure (CHF) patients remains unexplored. This retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate their combined prognostic value in CHF patients.MethodsThis study included 1,331 patients diagnosed with CHF at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital from 2019 to 2023. The primary outcomes encompassed CV death and major adverse cardiac and cerebral events (MACCEs). Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazard model were performed to explore the association between these indices and clinical outcomes. The discriminatory performance of the models was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis at 1-, 2-, and 3-year follow-up intervals.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 1.9 years in 1,331 patients, 357 MACCEs (26.8%) and 148 CV deaths (11.1%) were recorded. After multivariable adjustment (Model 3), the group with high UHR and high NPAR exhibited a significantly higher risk of CV death (hazard ratio [HR] = 3.53, 95% CI: 2.09–5.97; ) and MACCEs (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.03–2.06; ), compared to the group with low UHR and low NPAR. ROC analysis demonstrated superior discriminatory ability of the UHR–NPAR combined model for 1-year CV events (AUC = 0.787) and 2-year MACCEs (AUC = 0.609), significantly outperforming UHR alone. The model showed variable performance across time points, with NPAR alone demonstrating better discrimination for 3-year outcomes in both CV events (AUC = 0.676) and MACCEs (AUC = 0.597).ConclusionThe UHR and NPAR were independently and jointly associated with the risk of CV death and MACCEs in patients with CHF. By integrating these complementary biomarkers, our composite approach represents a potential novel tool for enhancing risk stratification and could inform patient management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02807-z.Keywords: Neutrophil percentage to albumin, Uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, Chronic heart failure, Adverse cardiac events

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents the end-stage of cardiovascular diseases, characterized by systemic congestion and multiorgan dysfunction. Its global prevalence is estimated to range from 0.37% to 6%. HF mortality rates have been increasing since 2012, with a pronounced acceleration during 2020–2021 [1]. The interplay of inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and ventricular remodeling drives HF progression and underlies subsequent clinical decline. Despite significant advances in biomarker discovery, current indices focusing on single pathways remain limited [2–5]. For instance, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels are influenced by extra-cardiac factors such as renal impairment, obesity, and atrial fibrillation [6], while inflammatory markers like high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) lack cardiovascular specificity [7]. These inherent limitations across biomarker categories underscore the need for integrated models that combine complementary pathophysiological pathways.

The neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) is a novel composite biomarker that reflects both inflammatory response and nutritional status. Previous studies have demonstrated an association with clinical outcomes across various chronic diseases [8]. In patients with chronic heart failure (CHF), NPAR has been identified as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality, suggesting a potential role in risk stratification [9]. Nevertheless, as a standalone indicator, NPAR has limitations, including inadequate sensitivity for long-term mortality prediction and insufficient accuracy for the demands of precision medicine. Consequently, it has been proposed that NPAR be used in combination with other biomarkers to develop more robust predictive models [9].

The uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) is an emerging composite metabolic indicator that captures both risk and protective factors associated with cardiovascular health. Previous research have shown that UHR is strongly linked to the development, progression, and prognosis of cardiovascular diseases, and it has been extensively validated as a reliable predictor of cardiovascular events [10]. Moreover, elevated UHR has been established as an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including cardiovascular mortality [11]. Nevertheless, the prognostic value of UHR in patients with CHF has not been fully elucidated, partly due to the pronounced overlap of comorbidities such as renal impairment, malnutrition, and systemic inflammation, which disrupt metabolism and may confound its interpretation [12]. Multivariable adjustment is therefore necessary to isolate the true predictive contribution of UHR.

While both NPAR and UHR have shown individual associations with adverse outcomes in cardiovascular diseases, their combined prognostic potential in CHF has not been thoroughly investigated. This area merits attention given that NPAR primarily reflects inflammatory and nutritional dysregulation, whereas UHR is more indicative of metabolic disturbances—two pathways that may operate through distinct yet potentially interrelated mechanisms in CHF progression. We hypothesized that integrating NPAR and UHR would enhance prognostic accuracy for long-term clinical outcomes beyond either biomarker alone or conventional risk factors. To test this, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of 1,331 patients with CHF, aiming to evaluate the combined predictive capacity of UHR and NPAR for cardiovascular (CV) death and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) over a median follow-up of 1.9 years.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study that initially involved 4329 patients diagnosed with CHF at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023. The index date was defined as the date of the first CHF diagnosis that met the study criteria during the enrollment period. The inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting clinical diagnostic standards for CHF; (2) aged between 18 and 80 years; (3) availability of complete baseline data, including basic information, clinical history, laboratory test results, echocardiography, and information on in-hospital drug treatment. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of admission data; (2) age below 18 or above 80 years; (3) concurrent acute coronary syndrome; (4) concurrent malignant tumor; (5) loss to follow-up. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1,331 participants were included in the final analysis. The cohort comprised predominantly males (68.44%) with a median age of 67 years (interquartile range: 57–72). The detailed patient selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the enrolled patients

The study population was stratified into three groups according to baseline UHR and NPAR levels, using the 33rd and 66th percentiles of the cohort distribution as tertile cut-points. This data-driven method enabled the objective division of participants into three similarly sized groups for comparative analysis. Based on tertiles of NPAR (T1: <149.30; T2: 149.30–171.22; T3: >171.22) and UHR (T1: <0.30; T2: 0.30–0.48; T3: >0.48), high levels of NPAR and UHR were defined as the highest tertile (T3), with the lower two tertiles (T1–T2) combined as the low-level group. According to the combined NPAR and UHR categories, participants were further classified into four groups: low NPAR and low UHR; low NPAR and high UHR; high NPAR and low UHR; high NPAR and high UHR.

Data collection

Upon patient addmission, medical histories were systematically collected. Venous blood samples were collected after an 8–12 hour overnight fast within the first 24 hours of admission to ensure that all laboratory parameters, including NPAR and UHR components, reflected the patient’s baseline status upon presentation. Laboratory analyses encompassed routine panels, including blood biochemistry, complete blood count, and coagulation function. Blood collection was performed by uniformly trained nursing staff adhering to standardized operating procedures, while all laboratory analyses were conducted by experienced technicians to ensure protocol compliance and result reliability. Clinical outcomes during follow-up were obtained through structured telephone interviews. To uphold data integrity, all data collection and endpoint adjudication were conducted by physicians who had received specialized training. All potential outliers were verified by cross-referencing extreme laboratory values with original medical records to exclude data entry inaccuracies. All confirmed values were retained for analysis as they represented biologically plausible measurements. No values below the detection limit were encountered for any analyzed biomarkers in this study.

Definition of variables and diagnoses

All biomarker assessments were performed on blood samples obtained within 24 hours of admission. The exposures of interest were UHR and NPAR. The UHR was calculated as serum uric acid (SUA, mg/dL) divided by high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mg/dL) [13]. The NPAR was calculated as neutrophil percentage (%) multiplied by 100 and divided by albumin level (g/L) [14]. The diagnosis of CHF was based on established clinical guidelines, which involve the assessment of high-risk factors—including coronary heart disease and hypertension—along with characteristic symptoms such as dyspnea. Additionally, physical examination was performed to identify signs indicative of HF, including pulmonary rales and lower extremity edema. Auxiliary tests included chest X-rays to screen for pulmonary congestion and cardiomegaly. Patients with abnormal electrocardiogram findings underwent evaluation of natriuretic peptide levels, specifically NT-proBNP ng/L or brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) ng/L, and those meeting these preliminary criteria underwent echocardiography to assess cardiac function and measure left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) for final diagnosis. Finally, based on the LVEF, the diagnosis was further clarified [15]. Hypertension was diagnosed based on systolic blood pressure 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication [16]. Inflammatory diseases referred to active systemic autoimmune or chronic inflammatory conditions. Common, minor, or localized infections were not considered exclusionary. Kidney disease was defined as a documented physician diagnosis of chronic kidney disease (any stage) or other significant renal pathology (e.g., polycystic kidney disease, glomerulonephritis) present at study enrollment, as recorded in the patient’s medical history. It was not defined by a specific eGFR cutoff at baseline [17].

Follow-up and endpoint events

Systematically trained clinical researchers implemented a comprehensive follow-up strategy to obtain patient information through the following methods: (1) regular telephone follow-ups; (2) retrieval of data from the electronic health record system; (3) review of outpatient clinic records. Follow-up commenced at discharge and continued until the first occurrence of any of the following events: the study deadline (November 2023), the patient’s voluntary withdrawal from the study, or the occurrence of a primary endpoint event. The median follow-up period for the cohort was 1.9 years.

The ascertainment of the primary composite endpoint (CV death and MACCEs) was rigorously defined. Deaths were classified as cardiovascular in origin unless a noncardiovascular cause was definitively established. MACCEs encompassed non-fatal stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and rehospitalization for worsening HF requiring intravenous diuretic therapy. Myocardial infarction was defined as positive myocardial troponin with typical chest pain, typical continuous changes in electrocardiogram, angiography or autopsy revealing coronary artery thrombus, or imaging evidence showing new necrosis of viable myocardium or new local wall motion abnormalities [18]. Stroke was diagnosed based on new focal neurological deficits persisting for over 24 hours, with supporting evidence from neuroimaging [19]. To ensure objectivity and accuracy, all reported endpoint events were strictly reviewed and underwent final adjudication by an independent clinical endpoint committee, composed of cardiologists and neurologists blinded to this study.

Statistical analysis

The time scale for all time-to-event analyses began at the index date. Participants were censored at the earliest of the following: occurrence of the target endpoint, loss to follow-up, death from non-cardiovascular causes, or the study closure date (December 31, 2023). Follow-up duration was calculated from the index date until the censoring date.

The variable with the highest proportion of missing data was BNP at 29.6%, followed by LVEF at 5.1%. We employed multiple imputation with predictive mean matching, generating five imputed datasets over 20 iterations. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of our findings, showing perfect concordance between results from multiply imputed and complete-case datasets (all AUC differences = 0.000).

We assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residual tests. To address any identified violations, we employed stratified Cox models and conducted sensitivity analyses using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models. Multicollinearity among all covariates was evaluated to ensure model stability.Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while those with non-normal distributions are summarized as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages (n, ). Associations involving ordered categorical variables were evaluated using complementary statistical approaches. Binary outcomes were analyzed with the Cochran–Armitage trend test and chi-square test, while continuous outcomes were assessed using linear trend tests, ANOVA trend analysis, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Spearman rank correlation (two-sided ). Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To complement traditional significance testing, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMD) for all variables, with SMD considered potentially meaningful regardless of statistical significance.

Covariates were selected a priori according to their established clinical relevance to heart failure prognosis. Univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable analyses were performed using three progressively adjusted models: Model 1: adjusted for gender, age, and BMI; Model 2: adjusted for variables in Model 1 plus history of hypertension, coronary heart disease, history of inflammatory diseases, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, smoking history, drinking history, diabetes history, diuretics, Sodium-Glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor(SGLT2i); Model 3: adjusted for all variables in Model 2 plus BNP, D-dimer, LVEF, C-Reactive protein(CRP) and hemoglobin. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to assess the cumulative probability of clinical events, with inter-group comparisons conducted using the log-rank test. All Kaplan-Meier curves represent unadjusted observational data.

The potential nonlinear associations of UHR and NPAR with clinical outcomes were examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of each biomarker’s distribution. HRs were calculated using the median value of each biomarker as the reference.

In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic performance of UHR, NPAR, and their combination in patients with congestive heart failure. The areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated and compared.

To assess the multiplicative interaction between UHR and NPAR, an interaction term (UHR NPAR) was incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards models. The significance of this term was evaluated using the Wald test in both minimally and fully adjusted models.

We constructed a baseline clinical model incorporating established cardiovascular risk factors, then developed nested models by sequentially adding UHR and NPAR to assess their incremental prognostic value. For comparison with conventional inflammatory markers, we performed head-to-head analyses against hs-CRP using nested logistic regression. Model performance was evaluated through discrimination (AUC improvement assessed by likelihood ratio tests), reclassification (net reclassification improvement [NRI] and integrated discrimination improvement [IDI]), and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test). NRI and IDI were calculated to quantify the improvement in risk classification when new biomarkers were added to established predictors. We further employed decision curve analysis (DCA) to estimate the net benefit of the models across a range of clinically relevant risk thresholds. Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap replicates, with statistical significance defined as two-sided .

All statistical analyses were performed primarily using R software (version 4.5.1). Cox regression, restricted cubic spline analyses, and bootstrap internal validation (1000 replicates) were conducted using the rms package; time-dependent ROC analysis was performed with the timeROC package; and model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test from the ResourceSelection package, among other analyses.

Results

Trend analyses revealed no statistically significant associations between ordered categorical variables and clinical outcomes. For disease severity, no significant trend was observed for binary outcomes (Cochran–Armitage test, ; chi-square test, ) or continuous outcomes (linear trend test, ; Spearman correlation, ). Similarly, for disease stage, no significant linear trend (ANOVA trend test, ) or between-group differences (Kruskal–Wallis test, ) were observed.

Baseline characteristics stratified by outcome categories

The study included 1,331 participants with a mean age of 63.06 ± 12.94 years; 68.4% were male. According to the combined NPAR and UHR levels, patients were stratified into four groups: Group 1 (, 47.3%), Group 2 (, 19.4%), Group 3 (, 19.3%), and Group 4 (, 14.0%). As shown in Table S1, significant differences were observed across groups for most baseline characteristics (). BNP levels showed a significant gradient increase. Compared with Group 1 (reference), Group 4 exhibited the highest burden of comorbidities, including hypertension (65.8% vs. 55.3%; ) and diabetes (46.5% vs. 27.0%; ), along with the most frequent use of diuretics (87.7% vs. 57.2%; ). This group also showed the most adverse profiles in renal function (serum creatinine, 106.0 vs. 68.0 mol/L; ) and systemic inflammation (CRP, 8.1 vs. 3.3 mg/L; ), as well as the lowest LVEF (35.0% vs. 42.9%) and highest uric acid levels. In contrast, Group 3, despite having the oldest median age (70.0 years), displayed significantly poorer nutritional status, as evidenced by lower albumin (37.2 vs. 40.4 g/L; ), BMI (23.9 vs. 24.8 kg/m; ), and hemoglobin levels (129 vs. 136 g/L; ). This group also demonstrated relatively preserved cardiac function (LVEF, 42.0%) but markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, 14.7 mg/L). Meanwhile, Group 2 was distinguished by more pronounced metabolic dysregulation, particularly elevated triglyceride levels (median 1.37 mmol/L vs. 1.15 mmol/L in Group 1; ), along with the highest utilization of MRAs (79.1%). Pharmacological profiles demonstrated selective variation across groups. While anticoagulant (38.7%, ) and ARB usage remained comparable, significant disparities were observed in antiplatelet therapy (41.0%, )—notably reduced in Group 2—and ACEI administration, which was lowest in Group 4 (5.4%).

Association of UHR and NPAR with the long-term prognosis in CHF patients

During a median follow-up period of 1.9 years, 357 (26.8%) events of MACCEs occurred, including 148 (11.1%) CV deaths. Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed that the group with a high UHR and NPAR levels (Tertile 3) had the lowest cumulative survival free from MACCEs and CV death (Fig. 2; all log-rank ). Visually, the curves began to separate markedly around day 500 of follow-up. This group maintained a sustained prognostic disadvantage, showing substantial and consistent divergence from the other groups for the entire duration. The cumulative incidence of both primary endpoints increased substantially over time: CV death rose from 3.91% at 1 year to 16.64% at 2 years, while MACCEs increased from 3.95% to 15.62% over the same period. The associations of UHR and NPAR with clinical outcomes were further quantified using Cox proportional hazards regression models (Table 1). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the highest UHR tertile (T3 vs. T1) was not significantly associated with the risk of CV death (HR 1.42, 95% CI 0.91–2.20) or MACCEs (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.83–1.43). In contrast, the highest NPAR tertile (T3 vs. T1) remained significantly associated with an increased risk of CV death (HR 3.21, 95% CI 1.90–5.41) and MACCEs (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.17–2.14).

Competitive risk model and time-dependent effect analysis

The proportional hazards assumption was violated (Schoenfeld test ), prompting the use of a time-dependent Cox model, which revealed declining hazard ratios over time for all biomarkers. For instance, The hazard ratio for UHR regarding cardiovascular events decreased from 20.36 to 1.38 by day 500. In Fine-Gray competing risks analyses, NPAR demonstrated the strongest predictive power with subdistribution hazard ratios of 2.36 for CV death and 1.49 for MACCEs (both ), while the combined indicator T also showed consistent associations. Negligible multicollinearity (variance inflation factor [VIF] = 1.03) supported the inclusion of both biomarkers in the same model.

Incremental predictive value analysis

The baseline clinical model demonstrated an AUC of 0.706 (95% CI: 0.665–0.747) for predicting cardiovascular events. Incorporating UHR significantly improved model performance (AUC 0.747; AUC +0.041, ), while NPAR provided greater enhancement (AUC 0.771; AUC +0.065, ). The combined model achieved optimal predictive ability (AUC 0.787; AUC +0.081, ). All improvements in model fit were statistically significant based on likelihood ratio (). The combined model showed good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow , ) and robust performance upon internal validation (optimism-corrected AUC = 0.751). Furthermore, the integration of both biomarkers significantly improved risk reclassification, with a categorical NRI of 0.280 (95% CI: 0.184–0.376), a continuous NRI of 0.635 (95% CI: 0.469–0.800), and an IDI of 0.082 (95% CI: 0.056–0.108), all .

The addition of NPAR to established risk factors resulted in a significant improvement in risk stratification, with a continuous NRI of 0.238 () and an IDI of 0.057 (). DCA suggested a modest incremental net benefit (average 0.011 across threshold probabilities), which, after external validation, could correspond to approximately 1 additional net-benefited case per 100 patients. Furthermore, the combined UHR–NPAR model showed an appreciable improvement in reclassification (continuous NRI 0.442, ; IDI 0.026, ). These findings support the potential complementary value of integrating inflammatory-nutritional and metabolic pathways for risk prediction in this cohort.

ROC curve analysis of CV death and MACCEs prediction ability for UHR and NPAR

As shown in Fig.4, ROC analysis indicated varying discriminative capacity among the evaluated models. The UHR model alone demonstrated limited predictive value for both CV death and MACCEs, with AUC values ranging from 0.469 to 0.660 across different time points. In contrast, the NPAR model showed substantially better performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.597 to 0.773. The integration of both biomarkers contributed to improved predictive accuracy, with the combined UHR–NPAR model achieving the highest AUC values for 1-year CV death (AUC = 0.787), 2-year CV death (AUC = 0.734), and 2-year MACCEs (AUC = 0.609). However, for 3-year outcomes, the NPAR model alone yielded higher AUC values than the combined model for both CV death (0.676 vs. 0.668) and MACCEs (0.597 vs. 0.571), suggesting that the additive value of UHR may be less pronounced in longer-term risk assessment. The corresponding optimal cut-off values for UHR and NPAR across follow-up periods were identified, ranging from 0.46 to 0.50 for UHR and from 168.32 to 177.35 for NPAR, depending on the endpoint and time frame.

Subgroup analyses and interaction effects

Subgroup analyses evaluated the consistency of UHR and NPAR in predicting outcomes across clinically relevant strata (Table S2). For CV death, both biomarkers showed significant predictive value in all predefined subgroups. NPAR exhibited notably consistent performance, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.008 to 1.021 per unit increase (all ). Significant interaction effects were observed for smoking () and alcohol use (), with enhanced prediction among non-smokers (HR 1.021, 95% CI 1.016–1.025) and non-drinkers (HR 1.021, 95% CI 1.017–1.025).

UHR demonstrated higher effect sizes for CV death (HRs 1.846–6.231) but greater heterogeneity across subgroups. Enhanced prediction was observed in females (HR 6.091, 95% CI 2.507–14.796), normotensive patients (HR 6.231, 95% CI 3.058–12.697), and non-diabetic individuals (HR 5.660, 95% CI 2.868–11.169), though its predictive capacity was attenuated in those with hypertension (p for interaction = 0.148) or diabetes (p for interaction = 0.100).

For MACCEs, both biomarkers remained predictive in most subgroups, albeit with lower effect sizes. NPAR again showed consistent performance, while UHR demonstrated selective utility in non-diabetic patients (HR 2.380, 95% CI 1.447–3.914) and those with reduced left ventricular function (HR 2.214, 95% CI 1.370–3.578). A significant interaction was also noted between NPAR and alcohol consumption ().

Analysis of multiplicative effects between UHR and NPAR

To assess potential synergistic (multiplicative) effects between UHR and NPAR, we incorporated an interaction term (UHR NPAR) into Cox proportional hazards models. The interaction term was not statistically significant in either the minimally adjusted model (HR 1.002, 95% CI 0.989–1.016; ) or the fully adjusted model (HR 1.001, 95% CI 0.987–1.015; ).

Comparative performance of NPAR versus CRP

In direct comparison, NPAR demonstrated comparatively stronger predictive performance over CRP for both cardiovascular endpoints. For CV death, NPAR provided a substantially greater improvement in discrimination (AUC +0.066 vs. +0.012, ) and model fit (AIC vs. ). NPAR also showed significant risk reclassification improvement (categorical NRI 0.238, ; IDI 0.057, ), while CRP demonstrated minimal reclassification benefit. For MACCEs, NPAR maintained a stronger association (AUC +0.023 vs. +0.001, ) with significant reclassification improvement. In both analyses, the predictive information of CRP appeared to be largely captured by NPAR, as CRP provided no significant incremental value beyond NPAR (CV: ; MACCEs: ).

Discussion

In this cohort of 1,331 patients with CHF, the combination of elevated UHR and NPAR levels was associated with adverse outcomes, suggesting a potential role in risk stratification. First, compared to the reference group, patients in the highest joint-tertile had significantly increased risks of CV death (adjusted HR 3.53) and MACCEs (adjusted HR 1.46). Second, the combined UHR–NPAR model demonstrated higher predictive accuracy for 1-year cardiovascular events (AUC 0.787) and 2-year MACCEs (AUC 0.609) compared to UHR alone. Furthermore, RCS analyses suggested nonlinear dose–response relationships for both biomarkers. Taken together, these findings support the prognostic value of NPAR and suggest that combining it with UHR could enhance short- to mid-term risk stratification in CHF, especially when applied to certain patient subgroups.

Underlying mechanisms and clinical implications

The UHR is recognized as a prognostic biomarker for metabolic and cardiovascular dysregulation, a notion supported by our findings in CHF and by prior studies linking elevated UHR to adverse outcomes in diverse conditions including peritoneal dialysis, chronic total occlusion, and acute myocardial infarction [20–23]. Its physiological rationale stems from the integration of opposing metabolic pathways: the pro-atherogenic effects of uric acid (UA) [24, 25] and the atheroprotective functions of HDL-C [26]. This composite measure may provide a more holistic risk profile than either component alone. An additional layer of complexity is introduced by sex-related differences. Yan et al. reported a U-shaped association between UHR and mortality in males, potentially attributable to androgen’s influence, contrasting with a linear relationship in females, likely mediated by estrogen [27]. These findings highlight the potential importance of considering sex-specific differences in clinical interpretation. Future research could usefully focus on prospectively validating these associations and establishing evidence-based, sex-stratified UHR cutoffs to help improve risk prediction in heart failure.

The neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) represents a cost-effective and readily accessible inflammatory biomarker, with its potential prognostic value in cardiovascular disease being increasingly explored. Several clinical investigations have reported associations between elevated NPAR levels and adverse outcomes, including increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the general population, as well as in patients with conditions such as atrial fibrillation and acute type A aortic dissection [28–30]. The prognostic value of NPAR may be explained by its reflection of the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways. Neutrophils, as primary mediators of acute inflammation, have been implicated in myocardial inflammation and fibrosis [31–33]. Beyond classical inflammatory pathways, they promote tissue injury through the release of proteolytic enzymes and reactive oxygen species, which can compromise cellular function and structural integrity [34]. In contrast, albumin exhibits significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capabilities, which may modulate neutrophil-endothelial interactions and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine release [35]. Beyond its nutritional role, albumin functions as the primary circulating antioxidant, mitigating oxidative stress—a key pathogenic process in chronic diseases [36]. It also helps maintain colloid osmotic pressure and endothelial stability, which contributes to vascular homeostasis [34]. An elevated NPAR may therefore reflect not only inflammation but also a broader state of physiological dysregulation, potentially characterized by concurrent immune activation, heightened oxidative stress, and compromised vascular protection. Thus, NPAR may be conceptualized as a composite marker reflecting both active tissue injury and the body’s capacity to maintain multisystem homeostasis, thereby providing an integrated measure of immune-metabolic dysregulation. This proposed pathophysiological basis suggests a potential distinction between NPAR and other biomarkers like UHR. While UHR appears to be more indicative of cardiorenal and metabolic homeostasis, NPAR could potentially offer complementary information by concurrently reflecting aspects of systemic inflammatory activity and nutritional status [37, 38].

This study investigated the integration of UHR and NPAR—two biomarkers capturing distinct yet potentially overlapping pathophysiological pathways—for prognostic assessment in patients with CHF. The results suggest that their combined use may be associated with improved prediction of adverse clinical outcomes in this population. Specifically, patients concurrently exhibiting high UHR and high NPAR levels appeared to show a substantially elevated risk of CV death and MACCEs compared to the low-risk group. The observed improvement in predictive performance may be partly explained by the superimposed contributions of the pathophysiological pathways captured by UHR and NPAR. Although their interaction was found to be additive rather than synergistic, the combination could still hold clinical relevance. UHR primarily reflects aspects of metabolic and cardiorenal dysregulation, whereas NPAR is considered an indicator of systemic inflammation and nutritional status. When both biomarkers are elevated, the co-occurrence of these distinct processes might contribute to more severe disease manifestations and higher risks of adverse outcomes. Therefore, integrating both biomarkers could offer a more comprehensive pathophysiological perspective, potentially enhancing risk stratification compared to the use of either biomarker alone. Inflammatory and metabolic reprogramming are increasingly recognized as core mechanisms within the complex pathophysiological network of heart failure (HF) [39]. Hyperuricemia (HUA), as a metabolic condition, can induce oxidative stress (e.g., promoting ROS generation), suppress Glutathione Peroxidase 4, promote ferroptosis, and contribute to cardiomyocyte death and myocardial fibrosis [40]. Furthermore, the combination of high uric acid and low HDL-C could not only trigger insulin resistance (IR) but might also be exacerbated by IR, potentially forming a self-reinforcing cycle of metabolic dysregulation [41–43]. This metabolic state appears to promote endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to systemic inflammation [41–43].

NPAR may serve as an indicator of systemic inflammatory burden, where elevated neutrophil percentage reflects activation of the innate immune response. Activated neutrophils are implicated in tissue injury, hypercoagulability, and reperfusion damage through mechanisms including tissue infiltration, release of pro-inflammatory mediators, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and respiratory burst [44–47]. In HFpEF, NETs might impair mitochondrial function via the NE-TLR4 signaling pathway, potentially inhibiting Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha(PGC-1) and disrupting cardiomyocyte energy metabolism [39, 48]. Cumulative evidence suggests that NETs may drive CHF progression through a "NETosis-thrombosis-fibrosis" axis, promoting microvascular obstruction, cardiac fibrosis, and ventricular dysfunction [49].

Concurrently observed low albumin levels may compromise antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticoagulant, and antiplatelet functions while potentially promoting LDL oxidation and foam cell formation, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses and exacerbating metabolic disturbances [44–46]. This condition appears to be both a consequence and a driver of pathological processes: chronic inflammation, which can be driven by cytokines such as TNF- and IL-6, may contribute to hypoalbuminemia [50], which in turn might reduce antioxidant capacity, impair oxidative stress response, and promote a pro-fibrotic myocardial microenvironment [50]. Clinically, this mechanistic pathway is supported by the observed correlation between low serum albumin and increased myocardial extracellular volume in HFpEF patients, which is indicative of myocardial fibrosis [50]. In this fibrotic process, the macrophage-fibroblast axis is thought to play a role, with cardiac C-C chemokine receptor type 2 macrophages producing IL-10 and IL-1 to stimulate fibroblast activation and promote myocardial fibrosis [39, 47].

In summary, while UHR primarily reflects metabolic risk and oxidative stress, NPAR appears to represent inflammatory status. These biomarkers may interact through oxidative stress and hypoalbuminemia, potentially promoting myocardial fibrosis via the macrophage-fibroblast axis. The combined use of UHR and NPAR may help elucidate superimposed effects within the metabolic-inflammatory network in heart failure. This multi-faceted approach therefore allows for a more comprehensive pathophysiological assessment. Given that both UHR and NPAR are derived from inexpensive and routinely available blood tests, they represent promising candidates for integration into early CHF risk screening protocols or electronic health record systems, where they could help identify patients who may benefit from intensified management.

This study explored potential high-risk thresholds using two approaches: empirical tertile-based cutpoints (UHR >0.48; NPAR >171.22) and ROC-optimized values (e.g., for 1-year CV death: UHR >0.467; NPAR >172.446). These thresholds require validation but may indicate a transition toward more pronounced inflammatory-metabolic dysregulation and appear to be associated with elevated event rates. From a clinical perspective, NPAR’s observed sensitivity (77.4%) for 1-year cardiovascular mortality could support its potential role as an auxiliary screening tool to help identify patients who might benefit from closer monitoring, while its balanced specificity (70.1%) may help mitigate excessive alerts. The combined UHR–NPAR model showed consistent predictive performance for cardiovascular mortality over time, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.787, 0.734, and 0.666 at one, two, and three years, respectively. This pattern supports the notion that integrating both biomarkers may provide a more comprehensive assessment of inflammatory and metabolic risk than either measure alone.

Although the model’s performance—while statistically significant—may not reach the level typically associated with strong diagnostic tests, it appears consistent with several validated prognostic models in heart failure and falls within a range often considered clinically useful (AUC 0.65–0.75) [51, 52]. The observed improvement in predictive accuracy beyond conventional risk factors seems to indicate potential value for risk stratification.

The thresholds identified are specific to this study’s population and require external validation before broader application. If confirmed in future studies, these biomarkers might be considered for incorporation into clinical decision support tools. Additionally, serial measurement of UHR and NPAR might offer insights into treatment response, although their role as dynamic monitoring tools requires further investigation.

Identification of an extreme-risk phenotype

We acknowledge that adding UHR to NPAR provided a modest improvement in overall model discrimination. However, the combined assessment of UHR and NPAR helped identify a patient subgroup with notably elevated risk profiles. Patients with concurrent elevation of both biomarkers—representing just 14% of the cohort—experienced a markedly high cardiovascular event rate of 31.2% and accounted for nearly 40% of all events. This elevated risk likely reflects the convergence of distinct pathophysiological processes: metabolic dysregulation (captured by UHR) and systemic inflammation with nutritional compromise (reflected by NPAR). The combined UHR–NPAR assessment thus provides a clinically applicable approach to identify a subgroup of patients who could potentially derive substantial absolute benefit from intensified preventive strategies.

Principal findings on biomarker superiority

In predicting composite endpoints, NPAR demonstrated modest but consistently stronger predictive performance than CRP (AUC: 0.023 vs. 0.001). Notably, after accounting for NPAR, CRP provided no significant incremental predictive value (). These findings suggest that NPAR may be best viewed as a complementary marker to CRP, rather than a replacement for it. While CRP remains a well-validated inflammatory indicator, NPAR captures distinct pathophysiological aspects by integrating inflammatory activity with nutritional status, thereby potentially contributing to a broader assessment of patient pathophysiology.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, as an observational analysis, it can identify associations between UHR, NPAR, and clinical outcomes but cannot establish causality. Although we adjusted for numerous known confounders, residual confounding remains possible. Second, the single-center design (Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital) may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Third, outcome data were primarily obtained through telephone follow-up, which, while practical, may be subject to recall and information bias. Finally, only baseline UHR and NPAR values were analyzed; serial measurements were not available, preventing assessment of their dynamic relationship with outcomes.

Regarding data constraints, systematic etiological classification (e.g., ischemic vs. non-ischemic cardiomyopathy) was unavailable. In addition, several factors known to influence albumin and uric acid levels—such as allopurinol treatment—were not fully accounted for. Important cardiac biomarkers such as troponin were also frequently missing due to inconsistent clinical testing protocols.

While the combined UHR/NPAR model showed clinical value in identifying a high-risk phenotype, its incremental predictive performance over NPAR alone remained modest. This suggests potential overlap in the pathophysiological dimensions captured by both biomarkers for general risk prediction. Future studies should focus on externally validating NPAR as a robust prognostic indicator and evaluating the clinical utility of the UHR/NPAR stratification in prospective trials to assess whether treatment guided by this combined profile leads to improved patient outcomes.

These considerations highlight the need for future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating standardized laboratory protocols, systematic endpoint adjudication, and serial biomarker measurements to validate and extend our findings.

Future directions and clinical translation

Our findings indicate that NPAR, derived from routine laboratory parameters, shows promise as a practical biomarker for cardiovascular risk assessment. While combining UHR with NPAR could help identify patients at particularly elevated risk, NPAR alone demonstrated predictive performance that may be adequate for general risk stratification in this study. Before clinical implementation, several key steps are needed: external validation across diverse populations, prospective studies confirming NPAR’s utility in guiding clinical decisions, and randomized trials testing whether targeted interventions benefit identified high-risk patients. Cost-effectiveness analyses would also be needed to establish the strategy’s value in real-world settings. These findings are best considered hypothesis-generating; they point to potential directions for refining risk assessment but require validation before they can influence clinical practice.

To facilitate clinical translation, future studies could evaluate the incremental value of the UHR–NPAR model when integrated with established risk scores (e.g., MAGGIC Heart Failure Risk Score or Seattle Heart Failure Model). To enhance the validity of future research, a robust methodological framework incorporating objective electronic medical record data, standardized laboratory protocols, and blinded endpoint adjudication following international criteria, for instance, those from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) or the World Health Organization (WHO), would be valuable. Key next steps would include: (1) external validation in multi-center prospective cohorts; (2) longitudinal assessment of biomarker dynamics through serial measurements; and (3) formal clinical utility evaluation using decision-curve analysis. These steps represent a logical progression toward evaluating the potential for future clinical implementation.

Conclusion

Our findings support UHR and NPAR as independent predictors of adverse outcomes in patients with CHF. The combination of these two biomarkers was associated with improved risk stratification in this cohort. These biomarkers could therefore provide valuable information to help identify high-risk individuals and inform clinical management decisions. Given that both biomarkers are derived from inexpensive and routinely measured laboratory parameters, their integration into clinical workflows appears feasible and has the potential to contribute to optimized patient management.
