Artificial intelligence for predicting transient hypocalcemia after total thyroidectomy
Authors: Genival Barbosa de Carvalho, Luan Martins, Renan Valieris, Alexandre Defelicibus, Pedro Cesar Souza, Daniela Tamega Joaquim, Jose Guilherme Vartanian, Luiz Paulo Kowalski, Israel Tojal da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transient hypocalcemia is a common complication of total thyroidectomy. This study aimed to evaluate whether machine learning (ML)-based models could enhance early risk prediction and support a robust clinical decision support system for hypocalcemia. We proposed a comprehensive ML-based framework using real-world health record-derived data from the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods of 7,066 patients treated and followed longitudinally at the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center, São Paulo, Brazil, between January 2014 and January 2024. The model was prospectively validated in 149 newly operated patients, of which 31 were confirmed cases of transient hypocalcemia. In this evaluation, the postoperative model achieved an AUC of 0.94 and a precision of 0.88. Known risk factors such as low postoperative parathyroid hormone (PTH) and bilateral neck dissection were reaffirmed, while novel insights such as prolonged surgery duration, earlier start times, and surgeon workload were identified as additional contributors to risk. Our study demonstrates that machine learning models, particularly when integrated with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, offer a reliable and interpretable tool for predicting transient hypocalcemia, enhancing personalized postoperative care and enabling timely, risk-based interventions.

Introduction

Acute postoperative hypocalcemia remains one of the most frequent and clinically relevant complications following total thyroidectomy, affecting up to 30% of patients1. This condition results from surgical manipulation or inadvertent devascularization of the parathyroid glands2, leading to a decrease in serum calcium levels. Symptoms may range from mild manifestations, such as paresthesia and muscle cramps, to more severe complications including seizures and cardiac arrhythmias3. Inadequate management of hypocalcemia can worsen the patient’s experience, prolong the length of hospital stay, or increase the rate of return to the emergency room4,5.

Traditionally, diagnosis and management rely on a combination of clinical symptoms and postoperative laboratory tests. However, clinical signs are often nonspecific, and laboratory values can be influenced by preanalytical factors such as hypoalbuminemia3, making early identification of patients at risk for hypocalcemia difficult. Such diagnostic limitations underscore the need for predictive strategies capable of anticipating this complication with greater accuracy and reliability.

In this context, machine learning (ML), a subset of artificial intelligence (AI), leverages large clinical datasets to predict outcomes by modeling complex patterns and feature interactions6–8. Owing to their ability to integrate multiple variables and capture intricate, non-linear relationships9, ML-based models offer new opportunities to identify patterns associated with specific complications, such as acute hypocalcemia. Prior studies have attempted to apply ML or statistical models to identify predictors of hypocalcemia; yet most are limited by small sample sizes, lack of prospective validation, or reliance on static models10–14.

We developed and validated a novel ML-based clinical decision support framework trained on longitudinal data from 7,066 patients. The framework models the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative stages, enabling dynamic risk assessment as new data become available. Compared to static models, this temporal framework improves real-world applicability by capturing shifts in risk factors over time. This enables the prediction of hypocalcemia in a prospective clinical setting, mirroring decision-making timelines. We additionally integrated explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, specifically SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), to interpret our model’s predictions and enhance clinical transparency. The model highlights variables such as parathyroid hormone (PTH) level, surgical duration, and surgeon workload as key features. By providing clinicians with interpretable outputs, our system may help guide preventive measures and improve patient safety in thyroid surgery.

Materials and methods

Cohort description

We conducted a retrospective, single-institution, longitudinal cohort study using data from 7,066 patients who underwent total thyroidectomy at A.C. Camargo Cancer Center between 2014 and 2024. Demographic and clinical data were systematically extracted from electronic health records and the institutional oncology registry. All methods were performed according to the relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria included patients undergoing total thyroidectomy, with or without associated neck dissection, for benign or malignant thyroid disease, and for whom preoperative and postoperative measurements of total serum calcium and PTH were available. Patients with musculoskeletal disorders or who were undergoing preoperative calcium replacement were excluded, as A.C. Camargo Cancer Center is a tertiary oncology hospital, and the database contained very few patients with these characteristics. In addition, the symptomatology of these diseases or previous calcium replacement could influence the clinical diagnosis of hypocalcemia or the results of postoperative calcium or PTH - central variables in our definition of outcome. Therefore, their exclusion was deemed necessary to preserve the internal validity of the model.

Stage-specific variable selection for predicting transient hypocalcemia

To capture the temporal dynamics of patient management, clinical data were categorized into three distinct phases of the treatment journey: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative. The preoperative feature set comprised variables available prior to surgical intervention. The intraoperative set integrated these preoperative variables with those measured during surgery. The postoperative feature set encompassed all preoperative and intraoperative variables, augmented by those assessed in the immediate postoperative period. For each stage, a machine learning model was trained using only the features available at that point, enabling a time-aware prediction framework and an analysis of stage-specific risk factors associated with transient hypocalcemia. Model construction

We developed a ML framework to predict transient hypocalcemia following total thyroidectomy, leveraging structured data derived from the patient journey. The model construction process encompassed four key steps: (1) definition and labeling of the clinical outcome; (2) preprocessing and time-based cohort splitting; (3) supervised training and hyperparameter optimization; and (4) model interpretability analysis. Each stage is detailed in the subsections below.

Outcome definition

The transient hypocalcemia outcome was strictly defined as the occurrence of clinical symptoms consistent with hypocalcemia, including perioral paresthesia and muscle cramps. Biochemical parameters were used to support clinical assessment, including a serum total calcium concentration <8.0 mg/dL measured within 8 hours postoperatively and/or a serum PTH level <12 pg/mL measured within 3 hours postoperatively, in accordance with international guidelines and prior studies15,16. However, biochemical values alone were not sufficient to define the outcome in the absence of clinical symptoms.

Cohort splitting and data preprocessing

The cohort of 7,066 patients was divided into training set and an independent holdout test set based on the date of surgery. Patients treated from January 2014 to December 2022 comprised the training set, while those treated between January 2023 and January 2024 were reserved for testing. This approach enabled the evaluation of model generalization on unseen clinical data, better simulating real-world deployment.

To further evaluate temporal robustness, the training set was divided into seven overlapping temporal folds using a sliding window approach. Each training fold spanned 480 days and was followed by a 149-day validation window (Table 1). Evaluation and interpretation

We use the ROC AUC and Cohen’s Kappa to evaluate the model. The ROC AUC measures the overall performance of the model, while the Cohen’s Kappa measure is employed for tuning the model’s parameters. Cohen’s Kappa was chosen as the primary metric for model tuning due to its ability to correct for chance agreement, a crucial consideration given the imbalanced nature of our cohort (15% positive cases). In imbalanced datasets, accuracy and precision can be misleadingly high by favoring the majority class; Kappa provides a more realistic assessment of classification performance by discounting predictions that could occur by random chance alone. This is particularly important for clinical applications, where false positives and false negatives have differing costs. This, in turn, improves the model’s performance when testing on previously unseen data. We additionally conducted baseline experiments using alternative machine learning algorithms. Comparative results are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

To interpret the decision-making process of our XGBoost models, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework18. SHAP values quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to a specific prediction by measuring the change in the model’s output relative to the expected value when that feature is present. This method leverages game theory and explainability metrics to create an explainer model that estimates each feature’s impact on the final prediction, thereby quantifying its importance. SHAP has gained popularity in various fields, including medical research14,17, with applications demonstrating its ability to uncover meaningful insights19. However, SHAP may yield unstable or misleading interpretations in high-dimensional or highly correlated feature spaces20. These limitations were considered in the interpretation of our results, and findings were cross-checked with head and neck surgeon to avoid overinterpretation.

Decision curve analysis

Net benefit was calculated for each model and compared with default clinical strategies, including “treat all” and “treat none”. The analysis was conducted on the complete test set and on a clinically relevant subset of patients defined by serum calcium < 8 mg/dL and PTH < 12 pg/mL. For each threshold probability, we also estimated the reduction in avoidable interventions per 100 patients relative to a treat-all strategy.

A machine-learning architecture to predict postoperative hypocalcemia

The pipeline developed in this study comprises three structured components designed to reflect the temporal progression of patient care (Figure 2). First, clinical and surgical features were organized into three temporal categories: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative (Figure 2A). Each phase served as the basis for an independent model trained using only the information available up to that point. This design enables the evaluation of predictive performance across the continuum of care, and simulates real-world deployment conditions where information becomes available incrementally. Second, a stage-specific model was constructed using the XGBoost algorithm for each phase (Figure 2B). This strategy allows each stage-specific model to learn patterns that are unique to its respective clinical phase, while accommodating the progressive incorporation of information and the evolving risk profile throughout the patient journey. Training is conducted using only complete stages of data, excluding instances with uncompleted data at the time of training. Supplementary Table 5 describes missing values for each feature. Additionally, the XGBoost model handles missing data internally by treating it as a distinct category. The training procedure incorporated temporal sliding-window cross-validation (Section "Cohort splitting and data preprocessing"), and hyperparameters were optimized using Bayesian search, as detailed in Table 2. Finally, to enhance transparency and support clinical interpretability, we used SHAP to quantify the contribution of individual features to model predictions (Figure 2C). Predictive performance varies throughout the oncology journey

To assess the model’s predictive ability at different stages of the oncological journey, three classifiers were independently trained using stage-specific variables: preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative. These models were then evaluated on a holdout test set using ROC AUC as the primary metric.

The preoperative model achieved a ROC AUC of 0.645 (95% CI: [0.563–0.727]), indicating limited discriminatory ability in the absence of surgical data. The intraoperative model showed improved performance with a ROC AUC of 0.70 (95% CI: [0.618–0.783]), suggesting that intraoperative variables provide additional predictive information. Finally, the postoperative model demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance, achieving a ROC AUC of 0.882 (95% CI: [0.828–0.936]), highlighting the added predictive value of immediate postoperative data. Brier scores for the three models were 0.152, 0.140, and 0.073 respectively. The incremental improvements in performance between models were statistically evaluated using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves. The difference between the preoperative model and intraoperative model was statistically significant (p = 0.041), whereas the differences between the preoperative and postoperative models (p < 0.001) and between the intraoperative and postoperative models (p = 0.001) were more pronounced. These results confirm that the inclusion of intraoperative and, particularly, postoperative features significantly enhances the model’s discriminatory performance.

Although the increase from 0.64 to 0.70 may appear modest, it represents a statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in early-stage prediction. The rise to 0.88 in the postoperative model reflects a substantial enhancement in discriminatory performance, consistent with the availability of more comprehensive clinical data at this later stage. Overall, model performance in predicting transient hypocalcemia improved progressively as additional stage-specific data were incorporated along the patient’s trajectory. In particular, the inclusion of intraoperative variables substantially enhanced discriminatory performance, underscoring the added predictive value of surgical information. These findings indicate that surgery-related factors are strongly associated with hypocalcemia risk, as reflected by their consistent prominence in the model interpretability analysis (see Section "Model Interpretability reveals actionable data for hypocalcemia prediction"). However, these associations should not be taken as evidence of causality. Further studies employing causal inference approaches or appropriately adjusted multivariate analyzes are necessary to clarify their independent contributions.

Assessment of model benchmarking, calibration, and clinical implementation

To support the selection of XGBoost, we benchmarked its performance against other widely used machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Logistic Regression, across all three operative stages. As summarized in Supplementary Table 2, XGBoost consistently demonstrated superior overall performance, particularly in the postoperative phase, where it achieved an AUC of 0.88, an F1 score of 0.59, and Cohen’s Kappa of 0.55. Although Random Forest and Logistic Regression reached comparable AUCs in this phase, they yielded lower sensitivity and Kappa coefficients, indicating less balanced performance. In addition to discrimination performance, we assessed model calibration at each operative stage (Supplementary Figure 1). The calibration plots illustrate the relationship between predicted probabilities and observed event rates for both the original and calibrated models, with the postoperative models showing closer agreement with the ideal calibration line compared with earlier stages. These results support the choice of XGBoost as the primary modeling approach, based on its combined discrimination performance and acceptable calibration across stages of care.

We also assessed the clinical utility using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), which quantifies net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. In the complete test set (Supplementary Figure 4A), the postoperative model demonstrated consistently higher net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies across clinically relevant threshold ranges. In the clinically relevant subset of patients with calcium < 8 mg/dL and PTH < 12 pg/mL (Supplementary Figure 4B), the postoperative model maintained superior net benefit across intermediate threshold probabilities, whereas calcium-only strategies provided negligible benefit. Although PTH-only showed reasonable performance at lower thresholds, its net benefit declined more sharply at higher thresholds. These findings indicate that the integrated postoperative model provides more robust and clinically actionable risk stratification than single-marker strategies, particularly in patients not clearly identified as high risk by conventional criteria.

Finally, to translate the probabilistic output of the postoperative hypocalcemia model into actionable clinical pathways, we defined three decision thresholds that jointly optimize sensitivity, specificity and the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity - 1), as reported for the test set (Supplementary Figure 2).

Longitudinal-based approach highlights predictability in transient hypocalcemia

We further quantified temporal robustness by evaluating model performance across each validation window defined by the sliding temporal splits. This longitudinal validation enables the assessment of both performance stability over time and sensitivity to distributional shifts across clinical periods. For this analysis, we re-trained the final model using only the training data from each respective window split and calculated the ROC AUC on the corresponding validation set. Overall, model performance remained relatively stable across temporally restricted training windows in both the preoperative and intraoperative phases, suggesting that predictive capacity during these stages is largely constrained by the information intrinsically available at those time points. In contrast, the postoperative phase exhibited progressively improved predictability in later years, even when trained on windows of comparable duration, indicating a potential enhancement in signal quality or consistency of postoperative variables over time..

Importantly, our study leverages a decade-long longitudinal dataset, enabling explicit modeling of temporal dynamics in clinical practice. By accounting for time-dependent structure in the data, we systematically evaluated model performance across temporally segmented subsets, rather than relying solely on aggregate training over the full cohort. This design provides a more rigorous assessment of temporal generalization and better reflects real-world deployment conditions, where patient characteristics, surgical workflows, and outcome prevalence may evolve over time.

To ensure clinical applicability and robustness, the model was prospectively validated in real-world clinical settings over a 10-month period, from January 2024 to October 2024, following its development using retrospective data. The postoperative model demonstrated strong performance during prospective validation, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.94, with a precision of 0.88 and a recall of 0.70. These findings indicate that the model maintains high predictive accuracy when applied in routine clinical practice, beyond the retrospective dataset used for its initial development and internal validation. Discussion

Transient hypocalcemia is a common complication following total thyroidectomy, with reported incidence rates of up to 53%23. Clinically, it may present with symptoms ranging from mild manifestations, such as perioral tingling and muscle cramps, to severe complications, including seizures and cardiac arrhythmias, thereby adversely affecting postoperative recovery and patient quality of life. Moreover, hypocalcemia has been associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and reduced long-term quality of life3. These clinical and economic burdens underscore the need for accurate, data-driven predictive models to improve risk stratification and optimize postoperative management.

While some previous studies have developed nomograms to predict hypocalcemia, these tools are often not user-friendly, rely on a limited number of variables, and are limited in their ability to capture complex clinical interactions, which may compromise their performance. Similarly, traditional predictive models, such as logistic regression, have been used to assess post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia23,24, however, they are frequently constrained by small sample sizes and limited capacity to model nonlinear relationships between variables. In contrast, ML models have demonstrated enhanced predictive performance by effectively leveraging large datasets and identifying complex patterns associated with postoperative complications25,26.

Our study presents a machine learning-based model for predicting transient hypocalcemia following total thyroidectomy, leveraging longitudinal data from a single institution comprising more than 7,000 patients. By incorporating XAI, the model not only demonstrates robust predictive performance but also provides clinically actionable insights to inform preventive strategies. During prospective validation, the model achieved an AUC of 0.94 and an accuracy of 0.88, outperforming a previously published nomogram from the same institution16.

In light of the prospective validation results, the potential clinical application of this framework should be interpreted within its institutional context. In a tertiary cancer referral center, where surgical complexity and baseline hypocalcemia risk are inherently higher, prioritizing sensitivity and negative predictive value, even at the expense of a modest reduction in specificity, represents a clinically appropriate trade-off. Minimizing false-negative predictions is particularly critical to avoid delayed calcium replacement and prevent more severe hypocalcemic manifestations. Compared with traditional approaches, the proposed model demonstrated balanced performance across discrimination and calibration metrics. Importantly, the improved performance observed in the postoperative phase reflects the contribution of postoperative biochemical markers, particularly PTH. Rather than replacing isolated postoperative PTH or serum calcium measurements, the model integrates these markers with additional perioperative variables to provide a more structured and contextualized risk assessment. Importantly, the associations observed between hypocalcemia risk and variables such as surgical duration, timing, and surgeon workload should be interpreted as non-causal. Rather, these factors likely serve as proxies for case complexity, workflow constraints, or institutional practice patterns, rather than independent determinants of outcome. Their inclusion enhances predictive performance by capturing contextual information that is typically absent from patient- or disease-only models, and may help identify organizational patterns associated with higher-risk scenarios. As such, these findings should be viewed as targeted improvements in perioperative management rather than implying direct causal relationships.

To address the evolving nature of clinical information, we modeled the patient journey as three distinct stages (preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative), training separate models for each phase using temporally partitioned datasets. This strategy enabled us to evaluate how predictive performance changes as new clinical data become available, rather than assuming a static risk profile throughout treatment. Such stage-wise modeling more closely reflects real-world clinical practice and supports more context-aware, time-sensitive decision-making. In contrast to static models, this approach captures temporal shifts in risk determinants, thereby enhancing clinical applicability. Consistent with previous findings15,27, our results reinforce the relevance of patient-related factors (e.g., age, BMI) and surgical factors (e.g., bilateral neck dissection) in hypocalcemia risk. However, our model extends prior approaches by incorporating granular operative metrics, including surgery duration as a proxy for procedural complexity and the number of surgeries performed per day as a potential indicator of surgeon workload, both of which may influence perioperative risk. Of note, Decision Curve Analysis further indicated that these stage-specific performance gains translated into potential clinical benefit, with the postoperative model demonstrating incremental net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities compared with default management strategies.

The integration of AI into clinical settings can optimize patient management, particularly by reducing unnecessary calcium and vitamin D supplementation and thereby improving discharge planning. Moreover, XAI enables clinicians to understand which variables most influence model predictions. This transparency reinforces the model’s role as a clinically relevant and reliable decision-support tool. Explainability is particularly critical in medical AI, where interpretability directly influences trust and adoption in clinical practice18. Building on the results of this study, we are currently developing a decision support system (DSS) designed to integrate transient hypocalcemia risk prediction into routine care. This ongoing effort aims to translate the current modeling framework into a deployable clinical tool with institutional workflow and existing data infrastructure. Future work will include user interface optimization, clinician-centered usability testing, and pilot implementation studies to evaluate feasibility and adoption in routine surgical practice. Ultimately, this approach seeks to provide clinicians with seamless access to individualized risk estimates at each stage of care, thereby supporting informed decision-making throughout the perioperative journey.

Our study has several limitations. The analysis was conducted using data from a single tertiary cancer center. Although we implemented robust internal validation strategies, including a temporally separated hold-out test set, the absence of an external test cohort limits the generalizability of these findings to other institutions or clinical environments. In addition, the retrospective design and the potential institutional biases in surgical decision-making may have influenced variable distributions and model performance. Class imbalance may also affect the stability and reliability of certain performance metrics. While measures were taken to reduce overfitting, the possibility that the model captures institution-specific practice patterns cannot be excluded. Future studies should prioritize external validations across diverse hospital systems and geographic regions. Expanding the dataset to include heterogeneous patient populations will be essential to enhance the model’s generalizability, fairness, and clinical robustness.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that AI-based models may assist in the early identification of patients at risk for transient hypocalcemia following total thyroidectomy, thereby supporting more personalized and timely clinical interventions. Notably, the identification of surgeon workload–both daily and yearly–as a relevant predictor represents a novel and potentially actionable contribution to the surgical literature. While the model exhibited strong internal performance, including a high AUC in prospective validation, its applicability should be interpreted with caution due to limitations such as single-center data, class imbalance, absence of external or multicenter validation, and potential biases inherent to retrospective electronic health record data. Future work should focus on validating these findings across diverse settings and assessing integration with real-world clinical system. These steps are essential to fully assess the model’s utility, generalizability, and impact on clinical care.
