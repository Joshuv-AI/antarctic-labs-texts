Multiscale molecular dynamics investigation of high-efficiency hydrogen storage in titanium-doped carbon nanotubes
Authors: Yuanjie Lu, Zhentao Qin, Ru Yang, Qi Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the hydrogen storage performance of titanium-doped carbon nanotubes (Ti-CNTs) through a multiscale simulation framework that integrates electronic structure calculations, atomic-scale dynamics, and macroscopic property analysis. A pressure-modified Lennard–Jones (LJ) potential function was developed to simulate hydrogen adsorption behavior in (12,12) armchair-type and (24,0) zigzag-type single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) under a pressure of 3 MPa. Simulation results reveal that 5% Ti doping in (12,12) CNTs yields a hydrogen storage density of 8.04 wt.% at 77 K (i.e., approximately four times greater than the undoped counterpart). Furthermore, the system maintains over 90% capacity retention after 100 adsorption-desorption cycles. The proposed multiscale strategy, combined with the pressure-doping synergistic optimization mechanism, offers a systematic approach for the rational design of stable and efficient hydrogen storage materials, demonstrating potential for practical engineering applications.Keywords: Titanium-doped carbon nanotubes, Hydrogen storage density, Molecular dynamics simulations, Pressure-modified potential functionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the development of carbon-based materials for hydrogen storage applications. Laranjeira et al.1 investigated the high-capacity hydrogen storage performance of -decorated Irida-graphene, while Zhang et al.2 examined the hydrogen adsorption behavior of a topological semimetallic microporous carbon material. Bi et al.3 employed density functional theory (DFT) to study lithium decoration strategies for boron-doped fullerenes and nanotubes. Collectively, these studies provide comprehensive theoretical insights into hydrogen storage in carbon-based materials. Hydrogen storage technology is critical for enhancing carbon neutrality by enabling the safe and efficient storage and transportation of hydrogen near ambient temperatures. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has established a 2025 target for onboard hydrogen storage systems to achieve a gravimetric density exceeding 5.5 wt.%4, considering hydrogen storage density alone. This target imposes two primary constraints on the design of nanoscale hydrogen storage materials: the necessity for a high specific surface area to facilitate physical adsorption of hydrogen molecules, and the requirement for active sites exhibiting moderate binding strength and effective interaction with hydrogen molecules. Compared to two-dimensional materials (e.g., graphene and borophene) and three-dimensional porous materials (e.g., metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)), carbon nanotubes (CNTs) possess distinctive one-dimensional hollow channels and tunable chiral structures that promote the formation of three-dimensional adsorption networks. CNTs demonstrate superior hydrogen molecule accommodation capacity and diffusion kinetics, particularly under high-pressure conditions5,6. Transition metal-doped CNTs have shown considerable promise in hydrogen storage applications due to their tunable electronic structures and high specific surface areas7,8. Titanium (Ti) atoms, in particular, are widely regarded as ideal dopants due to their unique electronic configuration and moderate electronegativity8,9. However, existing studies exhibit several limitations: most investigations rely on idealized single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) models, often neglecting the enhancement effects of practical multi-walled structures or defect sites7. Systematic analyses of hydrogen molecule diffusion kinetics under coupled temperature-pressure conditions remain scarce4. Additionally, the challenge of cycling instability caused by dopant atom migration has yet to be effectively addressed9. Beyond doping strategies, approaches such as strain engineering10 and metal surface decoration1,11 also demonstrated potential for enhancing hydrogen storage performance. Although magnesium-based hydrides (e.g., ) have been extensively studied due to their high theoretical hydrogen storage capacity (7.6 wt.%) and low cost, their high desorption temperature (>300 C), slow kinetics, and poor cycling stability significantly limit their commercial viability12. Efforts to improve their performance through nanostructure design and catalyst doping have been undertaken13,14. Nevertheless, their reversible hydrogen storage capacity near room temperature remains substantially below the DOE target. In contrast, CNT-based materials exhibit greater potential for hydrogen storage near ambient temperatures due to their high specific surface area, tunable electronic structure, and favorable diffusion kinetics. However, a systematic investigation of Ti-CNTs under coupled pressure-temperature conditions is still lacking.

This study focuses on the synergistic interaction between Ti doping and the pressure field, distinguishing itself from prior research that predominantly focuses on single-scale analyses or idealized conditions. The principal innovation of this work lies in the development of a comprehensive research framework that integrates electronic, atomic, and macroscopic scales to elucidate the fundamental physical mechanisms underlying the pressure-doping synergy. While the pressure-modified Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential function represents an effective engineering approach, its incorporation into this multiscale framework facilitates a quantitative characterization of Ti-H interactions under high-pressure conditions (i.e., an endeavor that presents significant challenges for isolated DFT calculations or classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations). Beyond merely reporting adsorption energies, this study preliminarily investigates the effect of external pressure on the electronic structure of doping sites, examining the resulting effects on adsorption configurations, diffusion kinetics, and the cycling stability of hydrogen molecules. Ultimately, this approach establishes a quantitative connection from microscopic electron cloud overlap to macroscopic hydrogen storage performance. The proposed strategy not only yields profound insights into the Ti-CNT system but also serves as a valuable reference for the design and optimization of other nanoporous hydrogen storage materials, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for their practical application in addition to complementary methodologies.

By using a pressure-modified potential function, the adsorption configuration of hydrogen molecules was effectively controlled. Application of a 3 MPa pressure resulted in an increased potential well depth for Ti-H interactions. Additionally, a comparative analysis of CNTs with various chiral indices was conducted, demonstrating that the (12,12) armchair-type CNT, with a diameter of 16.3 Å, is most favorable for the formation of a three-dimensional hydrogen molecule adsorption network (Figure 1). Finally, kinetic cycling tests revealed that the capacity retention rate remains at 90% after 100 adsorption-desorption cycles, which is attributed to the elevated migration energy barrier of Ti atoms (4.40 eV).

Schematic of hydrogen adsorption configurations with different chiralities.

In summary, while Ti-CNTs exhibit potential for hydrogen storage, their practical application remains hindered by challenges including limited stability due to dopant atom migration and an insufficiently understood mechanism governing the combined effects of high pressure and low temperature. This study aims to elucidate the hydrogen storage mechanism of Ti-CNTs under coupled temperature and pressure conditions through multiscale MD simulations, thereby offering a theoretical foundation for the development of high-performance hydrogen storage materials.

Methods

To address the challenges previously outlined, this study proposes a synergistic optimization strategy combining pressure and doping and establishes a multiscale simulation framework (Fig. 2).

Flowchart of the proposed multiscale simulation framework.

The rationale of this framework is based on the organic integration of the following three scales:

Electronic Scale: The parameters for the LJ potential function characterizing Ti-H interactions were obtained from DFT calculations conducted by Yang et al.9. They optimized the Ti-H interaction parameters by comparing results from DFT and MD simulations to ensure quantum mechanical accuracy in modeling interatomic interactions.

Atomic Scale: MD simulations were utilized to investigate the adsorption and diffusion behaviors of hydrogen molecules on Ti-CNTs, focusing on the kinetic processes under combined temperature and pressure conditions. These simulations were conducted at temporal scales on the order of picoseconds and spatial scales at the nanometer level.

Macroscopic Scale: Performance metrics relevant to engineering applications were reported, including hydrogen storage density (wt.%), adsorption energy (eV), and cycling stability (capacity retention rate), thereby offering direct guidance for material design.

This multiscale framework effectively integrates physical mechanisms spanning from electronic structures to macroscopic performance, thereby addressing the limitations inherent in single-scale methodologies. Notably, the pressure-modified LJ potential function introduced in this study encapsulates the coupling between the electronic scale (i.e., the electron cloud overlap effect) and the macroscopic scale, represented by the external pressure field. This function constitutes a central innovation of the proposed multiscale approach.

Results and discussion

Nonlinear relationship between hydrogen storage density and doping concentration

This study conducted a comparative analysis of CNTs with varying chiral indices ((5,5), (12,12), (9,0), (24,0)) and determined that the (12,12) variant, characterized by a larger diameter (16.3 Å) and an armchair-type configuration, is more favorable for the formation of a three-dimensional adsorption network, thereby exhibiting the highest adsorption density. These results suggest that both diameter and chirality significantly impact the hydrogen molecule capture capability. MD simulations further demonstrated that the incorporation of Ti at specific concentrations effectively enhances the hydrogen storage capacity of CNTs (Fig. 3). Under conditions of 3 MPa pressure and 77 K temperature, the (12,12) armchair-type CNT doped with 5% Ti achieved a hydrogen storage density of 8.04 wt.%, which is approximately four times greater than the undoped SWCNT system (1.5–2.5 wt.%). This improvement is attributed to the d-orbital polarization of Ti atoms and the -orbital hybridization of hydrogen molecules, with each Ti atom adsorbing approximately five hydrogen molecules24, resulting in stable, large-cluster adsorption configurations. However, as the doping ratio increases beyond this point, the hydrogen storage density does not exhibit a linear increase but rather approaches a saturation plateau.

The LJ potential exhibits limitations in accurately describing strong chemisorption processes that may involve charge transfer. However, in the Ti-CNT hydrogen storage system investigated in this study, numerous reports have demonstrated that Ti atoms predominantly serve as strong adsorption sites, with the interaction between Ti and hydrogen molecules governed by physisorption24,25. Yang et al.9 employed DFT calculations to show that the adsorption energy of hydrogen molecules on Ti sites typically ranges from -0.2 eV to -0.6 eV. Furthermore, Bader charge analysis revealed minimal charge transfer (<0.1 eV), consistent with enhanced physisorption rather than chemical bond formation. Therefore, the application of a validated LJ potential to model Ti-H interactions is justified within the specific framework of this study. For chemisorption phenomena potentially occurring under higher pressures or lower temperatures, reactive force fields or quantum mechanical approaches would be necessary to provide a more accurate description. However, such considerations lie beyond the scope of this research, which focuses on the physisorption mechanism under medium pressure and low temperature conditions.

Structural stability and cycling performance

In Fig. 5, the lattice migration energy barrier for Ti atoms is 4.40 eV, which exceeds the typical thermal disturbance energy barrier of 0.68 eV at 524 K. This elevated barrier inhibits atomic agglomeration at elevated temperatures. After 100 adsorption-desorption cycles (Fig. 6), the hydrogen storage capacity retention rate of the 5% Ti-doped sample remains at 90%. After the purification process, the experimental temperature was adjusted to 273 K. These results suggest that the observed changes in hydrogen storage capacity are attributable exclusively to the hydrogen storage mechanism associated with the Ti atoms and the potential well depth, rather than to the presence of impurities.

By integrating the adsorption energy data obtained in this study (ranging from -0.22 eV to -0.57 eV) with the substantial migration energy barrier (4.40 eV), the dehydrogenation process in the 5% Ti@ (12,12) CNT system is predominantly governed by thermodynamic factors. Specifically, the energy barrier for hydrogen molecule desorption from Ti sites is significantly lower than the migration energy barrier of Ti atoms, which aligns with the Langmuir desorption model9,26. Furthermore, Yang et al.9 revealed that the desorption energy barrier for Ti-H systems generally lies between 0.3 eV and 0.5 eV, thereby reinforcing the reversible and thermodynamically controlled nature of the desorption process in this system.

The primary contribution of this study is the proposal of a “pressure-doping synergistic optimization” strategy, whose efficacy is validated through multi-scale simulations. This approach focuses on generating highly active sites through metal doping, while employing an external pressure field to further modulate the electronic properties of these sites, thereby maximizing performance. This concept is extendable to other transition metal-doped carbon materials or MOFs (e.g., scandium (Sc) and vanadium (V)), offering a novel theoretical framework and design paradigm for the high-throughput screening and development of advanced hydrogen storage materials. To assess the long-term cycling stability of hydrogen storage in Ti-CNTs, the capacity retention rate was predicted by integrating MD simulation outcomes with a thermodynamic theoretical model. In Section 3.4, the high binding energy (-4.28 eV) and substantial migration energy barrier (4.40 eV) between Ti atoms and the carbon framework underpin the observed cycling stability. Consequently, capacity degradation during cycling is attributed to the irreversible slight deactivation of a minimal number of adsorption sites, rather than to Ti atom agglomeration or detachment. Utilizing the initial simulated hydrogen storage capacity (8.04 wt.%) and applying the Langmuir desorption model alongside the aforementioned stability parameters, a physical model of capacity decay was developed to predict performance retention after 100 consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles. This model ensures that the predicted results align with the system’s thermodynamic and kinetic characteristics.

In Table 3, the proposed 5% Ti-doped (12,12) CNT investigated in this study achieves a hydrogen storage capacity of 8.04 wt.% under conditions of 77 K and 3 MPa. This performance notably exceeds most reported carbon-based materials (e.g., 1.5-2.5 wt.% for SWCNTs) as well as certain metal-decorated materials (e.g., 6.22 wt.% for Li-decorated borophosphene). Importantly, the system retains over 90% of its storage capacity after 100 adsorption-desorption cycles, indicating excellent stability. This durability is attributed to the high migration energy barrier of 4.40 eV between Ti atoms and the carbon framework. Furthermore, the high crustal abundance and low cost of Ti further enhance its suitability for engineering applications.

In comparison to materials such as Y-functionalized [1,1] paracyclophane, the proposed system facilitates efficient hydrogen storage under moderate pressure (3 MPa) and low temperature (77 K). These conditions closely correspond to those of liquid hydrogen storage, thereby demonstrating the system’s potential for practical application as an auxiliary hydrogen storage module. The incorporation of a pressure-doping synergistic mechanism enhances the adsorption energy (ranging from -0.22 eV to -0.57 eV per hydrogen molecule), effectively balancing physisorption and weak chemisorption. This balance mitigates the challenges associated with desorption that are typically encountered in systems dominated by strong chemisorption. Furthermore, Ti doping incurs lower costs relative to other materials or alternative metal doping strategies31. The selection of 77 K for this study was intended to align with liquid hydrogen storage conditions and to optimize adsorption performance at this temperature. Although hydrogen storage density is expected to decrease at ambient temperature, the proposed pressure-doping synergistic mechanism remains broadly applicable. Future research will focus on extending performance evaluations to room temperature conditions.

Compared to other transition metals such as palladium (Pd), manganese (Mn), and niobium (Nb), Ti-CNTs exhibit different advantages in hydrogen storage performance. Although Pd demonstrates excellent hydrogen adsorption capabilities, its high cost and susceptibility to poisoning limit its feasibility for large-scale applications. While Mn and Nb are more cost-effective, their binding energies with the carbon framework are generally weaker (typically below 3.0 eV), rendering them susceptible to agglomeration or desorption during cycling, which compromises stability. In contrast, the Ti atoms investigated in this study possess moderate electronegativity (1.54) and a d-orbital electronic configuration that facilitates interactions with hydrogen molecules in a regime between physisorption and weak chemisorption (binding energies ranging from -0.2 eV to -0.6 eV). Furthermore, Ti exhibits a notably strong binding energy with the carbon skeleton (-4.28 eV) and a high migration energy barrier (4.40 eV), both substantially exceed those of other common transition metals (approximately 3.2 eV for Nb and 2.8 eV for Mn). Ti’s high crustal abundance and low cost enhance its suitability for engineering applications. Additionally, the high crustal abundance and relatively low cost of Ti enhance its suitability for large-scale engineering applications. The research was conducted in a region commonly referred to as the “Vanadium-Titanium Capital”, known for its rich Ti reserves, which supports the material’s accessibility and industrial relevance. Therefore, Ti-CNTs exhibit an optimal balance of high hydrogen storage capacity, excellent structural stability, and economic feasibility, making them promising candidates for advanced hydrogen storage systems.

The pressure-doping synergistic optimization mechanism proposed in this study, although demonstrated using a Ti-CNT system, is fundamentally characterized by the enhancement of adsorption energy through the hybridization of the metal atom’s d-orbitals with the -orbitals of the hydrogen molecule, coupled with the modulation of electron cloud overlap by external pressure. This underlying principle exhibits a degree of generality and can be extended to other carbon-based materials (e.g., graphene, carbon nanofibers, porous carbon) as well as MOFs doped with transition metals possessing similar electronic configurations (e.g., Sc, V, and zirconium (Zr)). However, in materials exhibiting strong chemisorption properties (such as certain rare-earth metal hydrides) or effective charge transfer mechanisms, more sophisticated potential fields or quantum mechanical approaches are necessary for accurate description. Therefore, this mechanism is most appropriately applied to nanoporous materials where physisorption predominates but weak chemisorption effects are also present.

Conclusion

Through comprehensive multiscale MD simulations, this study elucidates the mechanism underlying the high-efficiency hydrogen storage performance of Ti-CNTs. The key conclusions are summarized as follows as Pressure-doping synergistic optimization: A synergistic optimization strategy combining Ti doping and external pressure was proposed and validated using a multiscale simulation framework. This approach offers a rational pathway for the design of next-generation, high-performance hydrogen storage materials. Pressure-temperature coupling effect: A pressure-modified LJ potential function, defined as (P) = 0 (1 - 0.005P) and (P) = 0 (1 + 0.25P), was introduced to quantitatively capture the enhancement of adsorption energy under elevated pressure. This formulation effectively describes the interplay between external pressure and atomic-scale interactions. Balance between kinetic efficiency and structural stability: Ti atoms exhibit a strong binding energy with the CNT framework (-4.28 eV), resulting in a high migration energy barrier (4.40 eV), which exceeds typical thermal fluctuation energies (0.68 eV at 524 K). This contributes to excellent cycling stability, as confirmed by dynamic simulations showing a capacity retention rate exceeding 90% after 100 adsorption-desorption cycles. The observed stability is attributed to both pressure feedback through the virial stress tensor and the topologically protected nature of the Ti adsorption sites.

while simulations were performed at 77 K, the proposed pressure-doping synergistic mechanism exhibits a degree of universality and may be extended to room-temperature hydrogen storage systems. Under the tested conditions of 77 K and 3 MPa, the 5% Ti-doped (12,12) CNT achieves a hydrogen storage density of 8.04 wt.%, outperforming most conventional carbon-based and metal hydride materials. Notably, its compatibility with liquid hydrogen storage environments positions it as a promising auxiliary hydrogen storage module in onboard or stationary applications. Further enhancements (e.g., bimetallic doping or hybrid composite design) could broaden its applicability to room-temperature fuel cell systems and distributed energy storage networks, providing a valuable theoretical reference for future development within the hydrogen economy.
