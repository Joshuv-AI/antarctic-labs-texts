Extension of voxel-based lesion mapping to multidimensional neurophysiological data
Authors: Richard Hardstone, Lauren M. Ostrowski, A. Nicole Dusang, Eduardo López-Larraz, Jessica Jesser, Sydney S. Cash, Steven C. Cramer, Leigh R. Hochberg, Ander Ramos-Murguialday, David J. Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Focal brain lesions cause neurophysiological changes in local and distributed neural systems. While electroencephalography (EEG) has a long history in post-stroke neurophysiological assessment, the observed changes have rarely been linked to specific lesion locations, leaving neuroanatomical-neurophysiological relationships after stroke unclear. Current data-driven methods, such as voxel-based lesion symptom mapping (VLSM), relate lesion locations to single-feature “symptoms” but currently cannot associate anatomical injury with multidimensional data such as EEG, with its rich spatiotemporal information. To overcome this limitation, we introduce MD-VLM, an extension of VLSM to multidimensional “symptoms” that identifies relationships between lesion locations and neurophysiology. MD-VLM is data-agnostic, compatible with various lesion (e.g., lesion maps, lesion network maps) and neurophysiological (e.g., channel-level or source-localized EEG) inputs, and uses robust statistics to test for the existence of significant neuroanatomical-neurophysiological relationships. We demonstrate MD-VLM’s feasibility by applying it to EEG from chronic stroke patients performing a cued-movement task. MD-VLM revealed significant associations between frontal white-matter lesions and reduced ipsilesional parietal cue-evoked responses, consistent with damage to known fronto-parietal networks. MD-VLM is a novel data-driven extension to VLSM for multidimensional “symptoms”. Applying MD-VLM to link lesions to neurophysiological data can improve mechanistic understanding of post-stroke neurological impairments and guide future biomarker development.

Introduction

Injury to the structure of the brain (i.e., focal neuroanatomical lesions as in stroke) causes complex changes to the functioning of local1 and distributed neural systems2. Since the introduction of EEG, recordings have been performed to characterize the cortical neurophysiological changes that occur after stroke3–8. Before the availability of non-invasive imaging, this research was primarily focused on relating EEG to lesions (via post-mortem assessment), to determine whether EEG could be used to localize lesions3,9–12. However, since the ability to perform non-invasive imaging (CT and MRI) of lesions has become commonly available, the primary focus of post-stroke EEG research has shifted to how metrics derived from neural activity relate to the severity of specific impairments6–8 or predict the extent to which patients recover5,13,14 or respond to a restorative therapy15 (i.e. neurophysiological biomarkers). The question of how different observed EEG patterns after stroke are related to the precise neuroanatomy of the underlying lesion (Fig. 1A) is now rarely addressed. The few post-stroke EEG studies4,6,16,17 that have addressed lesion characteristics use relatively coarse characterizations of the lesion (e.g., lesion size, cortical lobe, or region of interest), or reduce the multidimensional EEG activity to a single feature per patient, which prevents more data-driven investigations of which aspects of neural activity are affected by particular lesions (Fig. S1).Fig. 1Motivation for linking post-stroke neurophysiology with lesion neuroanatomy. Since the nineteenth century, lesion mapping has been a fundamental tool in neuroscience for localizing behavioral functions to specific brain regions. Early studies analyzed the functional impairments caused by lesions using post-mortem analysis18,19, ablations in animal models20, or intra-operative electrical stimulation21. However, it was not until the latter half of the 20th Century that a series of methods (pneumoencephalography22, isotope mapping23, Computed Tomography (CT)24 and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)25) were introduced that could non-invasively image lesions which allowed more patients to be studied with progressively finer resolution imaging of lesions. To take advantage of the higher imaging resolution of these techniques, new data-driven statistical methods (voxel-based lesion symptom mapping, VLSM26 were developed that could map the association between lesions and symptoms at the voxel-level. VLSM allows brain-wide associations to be mapped, moving away from previous a priori defined Region-Of-Interest (ROI) hypothesis-based analysis. While data-driven methods do have potential drawbacks (including the increased computation required for brain-wide measures, and the greater potential for spurious results), the rigid nature of confirmatory-hypothesis methods such as ROI-testing also have several potential drawbacks including the possibility that the ROI contains multiple subregions that correlate differently with the “symptom”, that the ROI is only a small part of the relevant functional region, or that the relevant functional region is outside the pre-specified ROI. Current VLSM techniques enable brain wide mapping of behavioral metrics. However, current techniques require that the behavioral metric be a single feature per patient. While methods exist that take advantage of the multivariate nature of lesion maps to predict a single behavioral metric27–30, there currently exists no method that relates lesion maps to multidimensional behaviors, “symptoms”, or, more broadly, data derived from the same patient.

As a neurophysiological assessment tool, EEG contains rich multidimensional information spanning channels and frequencies (Fig. 1A). Applying traditional VLSM techniques to this data typically requires reducing all of this information to a single feature31,32, based on a priori selection of specific channels and frequencies. While this a priori approach allows testing a strong hypothesis, it also carries a significant risk of leading to erroneous conclusions. Consider, for instance, selecting beta activity (the classical 13–30 Hz band) from electrodes overlying the motor cortex (Fig. S1, Left) to investigate how neuroanatomical lesions affect this rhythm33–35. Applying VLSM in this manner risks drawing incorrect conclusions (Fig. S1, Right) for several reasons: 1) it assumes homogeneity within the selected EEG channels and frequencies potentially overlooking subsets exhibiting opposing effects. 2) It presumes effects are confined to the selected parameters, whereas broader patterns across different channels and/or frequencies might exist, suggesting the effect is not specific to, for example, motor-related beta activity. 3) It inherently cannot detect relevant effects occurring outside the pre-selected parameters. Therefore, a lesion mapping method that avoids a priori selection of EEG features potentially offers a valuable complementary, data-driven approach. Such a method can effectively capture neural activity changes resulting from specific brain lesions. Further validation and confirmatory hypothesis testing can then be done in an independent sample of patients. Furthermore, this methodology is broadly applicable to analyzing other multidimensional datasets and complex symptoms, including data collected from diverse neuroimaging, neurophysiological, as well as behavioral assessments.

In this paper, we present a new method that discovers significant relationships (Fig. 1B) between multidimensional “symptoms” (Fig. 1A, Left) and the 3D spatial information found in stroke lesion maps (Fig. 1A, Right) whilst accounting for infarct volume. Building up from lesion mapping of zero-dimensional (scalar) data, using EEG as our example datatype (conceptually equivalent to VLSM, (Fig. 2)) we provide a detailed explanation of the additional steps required to discover relationships that are interpretable for one-dimensional (Fig. 3) and N-dimensional data (Fig. 4). We apply the method to whole-brain lesion maps, and broadband EEG data across the whole cortical surface using a previously recorded dataset of EEG and lesions from chronic stroke patients during a cued-movement task (Fig. 5). As EEG data during movement might be more contaminated by movement, eye and muscle artifacts36,37, we analyzed the 0–500 ms post-cue time window to determine the effects of lesions on neural activity. We found a significant relationship between the presence of lesions and EEG activity, with lesion clusters found in frontal white matter related to an EEG feature cluster showing reduced post-cue evoked beta-band responses in an ipsilesional parietal electrode (Fig. 6), consistent with the importance of attentional processes in cued movement tasks. The novel method presented can be used to discover associations between neurophysiology and neuroanatomy, or more generally between multidimensional behaviors or “symptoms” and neuroimaging, in a hypothesis-free or hypothesis-driven fashion (e.g., by reducing the search space of neuroanatomical regions or EEG features). It is critical to distinguish this group-level statistical approach from single-subject source localization techniques (e.g., sLORETA38, MUSIC39, SilenceMap40) that are used to estimate the neural origin of sensor-level data. Rather than replacing such methods, MD-VLM is designed to work synergistically with them. Methods

Our method builds upon voxel-lesion symptom mapping (VLSM), using cluster-based statistics41,42 to link brain lesions to a single patient outcome (a zero-dimensional, or 0D, feature, Fig. 2). We extended this approach to handle multi-dimensional data, such as correlating lesions with data from multiple EEG channels (1D, Fig. 3) or even more complex datasets (ND, Fig. 4). In our framework, a feature is any single measurement, like the power at a specific frequency on a single EEG channel. The goal is to identify statistically significant clusters that connect lesioned brain voxels to specific outcome features. The “size” of a given cluster is defined by the sum of the statistical test scores (e.g., t-scores) from every significant voxel-feature pair within it. This single metric naturally accounts for the cluster’s spatial extent (number of voxels), its feature-space extent (number of outcome features), and the strength of the correlation (Fig. 1B). A significant computational challenge arises because exhaustively testing all possible combinations of voxels and features is intractable. For example, with just 100 features, the number of potential clusters reaches astronomical figures (~ 1030). Therefore, we developed an efficient algorithm that can identify the largest and most significant clusters in the data without needing to perform this impossible, exhaustive search.

Zero-dimensional (0D, Scalar) Voxel-based Lesion Symptom Mapping (VLSM)

0D: input data

For all forms of VLSM, lesion maps are required for each patient. These consist of a set of voxels (in a common coordinate space (e.g., Talaraich, MNI, etc.43) which covers the entire brain and indicates whether or not there is a lesion present at that voxel. For statistics to be performed at a voxel, there needs to be a large enough set of subjects that do and do not have a lesion at that voxel. For this VLSM has typically used a heuristic of N > = 5 do have a lesion and N > = 5 do not have a lesion44. The other input required is EEG data. For the 0D case, EEG data need to be reduced to a single feature for each subject, for example by averaging beta power over motor electrodes.

0D: statistics

The relationship between lesion presence and the EEG feature is assessed at every voxel, to give a statistic at every voxel. Standard practice is to use linear regression (see Supplementary Methods, Eq. 1), with t-statistics extracted (Supplementary Methods, Eq. 2). However, the MD-VLM method is agnostic to the exact statistical method used, and it is possible to substitute linear regression for non-parametric equivalents. If required, confounders (such as overall lesion size, age, sex) can also be added (Supplementary Methods, Eq. 3).

0D: clustering

Due to the large number of statistical comparisons (equal to the number of voxels), statistics need to be corrected for multiple comparisons. This can either be done at the voxel level (e.g., using Bonferroni correction or False Discovery Rate (FDR)), or clustering can be applied, and statistics done for each cluster. Here we apply a clustering procedure42, based on adjacency of voxels (Fig. 2B). In the first step, the t-map is thresholded at a set significance level (the cluster defining threshold, alpha (e.g., p < 0.05)). The choice of cluster-defining threshold is relatively arbitrary45, with more lenient thresholds leading to the detection of more spatially widespread weaker effects, and a more stringent threshold leading to more focal clusters with stronger effects. Significant voxels (with same sign t-statistic) that are adjacent to each other are then formed into clusters. Each cluster is assigned a cluster size, which is a summary statistic equal to the sum of the t-statistics from all voxels within the cluster. By assigning a size to each cluster, we can then compare the relative size of different voxel clusters.

0D: permutation

To calculate the significance of each cluster, we compare the cluster size to a null distribution, which consists of the maximum cluster sizes that you expect to see by chance. This distribution is calculated by permuting the data multiple times (e.g., 1,000 times). For each permutation, the link between EEG and lesion maps is broken by shuffling the subject labels of the EEG data (Fig. 2C). After shuffling, statistics (Fig. 2A) and clustering (Fig. 2B) are applied in the same manner as was done for the unshuffled data. The maximum cluster size is then taken for each permutation to form the null distribution (Fig. 2D). Taking the maximum cluster size across all permutations forms the null distribution, and the original cluster sizes are compared to this. Clusters in original data with summary statistics exceeding the 95th percentile of null distribution are significant (corresponding to p < 0.05, cluster-corrected, one-tailed test. This cluster-level significance threshold controls the familywise error rate, and is a separate threshold to the cluster-defining threshold used in 0D:Clustering (Fig. 2B). E.g., whether the cluster-defining threshold is set to 0.05 or 0.005, the family-wise error rate is still determined by the cluster-level significance threshold.

One-dimensional (1D) voxel lesion symptom mapping (VLSM)

For one-dimensional EEG data, there are additional steps that are required (Fig. 3A) compared to the 0D EEG data. These steps (Steps 2 and 3) are necessary to find the EEG clusters (which are defined sets of connected EEG features) that are present in the data (meaning that for at least one voxel, all EEG features in the cluster have a t-statistic > threshold) and maximal (meaning that for all voxels significant for this EEG feature cluster it is not possible to add another additional EEG feature to the cluster that would be significant for all of the voxels).

1D: input data

For 1D data (Fig. 3B), the input data consist of the same lesion map data as required for 0D (scalar) analysis, but the EEG data now have X features per patient. Depending on the analysis, these X features could represent different EEG frequencies, timepoints or channels (as shown in Fig. 3B). EEG data naturally have spatio-temporal correlations (e.g., autocorrelations in time, volume conduction between nearby channels, correlations between adjacent frequencies). Therefore, it is usually not possible to consider each of the X features as independent. Cluster based permutation analysis45 addresses this, by forming clusters of significance in EEG feature data. Using adjacency matrices, which assign the features that are reasonably close to be considered as a single item. For example, in the spatial dimension, nearby channels would be considered adjacent (Fig. 3C). Therefore, for 1D-VLSM, an adjacency matrix, is an additional component required for the EEG dimension, as compared to 0D-VLSM.

1D: statistics

In Step 1 (Fig. 3B), the relationship between lesion presence and EEG feature is assessed for every [voxel, EEG feature] pair resulting in a t-map with dimensions (#voxels x #EEG features) using the same statistics as were used for the 0D case. (Supplementary Methods, Eq. 1,2,3).

1D: clustering

Compared to the 0D case, we now have a t-map for every EEG feature. Due to spatial and temporal dependencies inherent in the data, these features (t-maps) cannot be treated as independent. One method to address this is to combine significant results across EEG features that are adjacent to form clusters and then find the voxels where all the EEG features in the cluster are significant. This will result in EEG clusters (set of adjacent EEG features, e.g., two adjacent occipital EEG channels) that are significant for a specific lesion cluster (set of adjacent voxels). Therefore, to make inferences about the resulting t-map, it is easier to split clustering into a three-step process: first define the adjacency between EEG features (Fig. 3C), then find the maximal EEG clusters (Fig. 3D), and finally cluster the voxels for which every feature in the EEG cluster is significant (Fig. 3E).

Finding maximal EEG clusters

With n EEG features there are 2n – 1 possible combinations of these features (Fig. 3C). However, some of these combinations will not form a connected cluster (meaning that all features in the cluster can be connected through the adjacency matrix, e.g., channels 1, 2 and 4 would form a connected cluster as 1 is connected to 2 and 2 is connected to 4). With large numbers of EEG features it quickly becomes computationally intractable to assess each combination (e.g., with a 128 channel EEG net, there would be 2128–1 possible combinations of channels). The number of possible connected clusters depends on the EEG feature adjacency matrix, and the set of possible connected clusters will be smaller than the set of all combinations (if all features are not adjacent) but often will nonetheless be computationally intractable with large numbers of features. The set of EEG clusters that we are interested in (those that are present in the thresholded t-map and maximal) will be much smaller than the set of connected clusters, therefore identifying this set in an efficient manner can dramatically reduce the computational load and make the problem tractable.

Therefore, in Step 2ii, the aim is to find all maximal EEG clusters that are present in the thresholded t-map. By maximal we mean that this EEG cluster is not a subset of another EEG feature cluster, which is significant for all the same voxels. While there are usually thousands of voxels present in the t-map, there will be many voxels that have the same EEG features that are significant. As such, we can reduce the size of the problem by considering unique combinations of EEG features (Fig. 3Dii). While each of the feature combinations may be present, it is possible that the features are not adjacent (e.g. although there are voxels significant for channels 1, 4 and 5 (Fig. 3Di,ii); these do not form an EEG cluster as channel 1 is not adjacent to channel 4 or 5). Therefore, in the next step (Fig. 3Diii), each of the unique feature combinations is clustered using the adjacency matrix (Fig. 3C). As some of these clusters will be common across the combinations, the set of unique maximal EEG clusters is formed (Fig. 3Div). Note that while step 3Dii is not strictly necessary, it does reduce the computational load of the problem, as each combination of EEG features only needs to be clustered once.

Clustering voxels for each EEG feature cluster

We can now find which voxels are associated with each EEG feature cluster (Fig. 3E) by identifying which voxels are significant for every feature in the EEG feature cluster. We can then cluster these voxels in 3D space (as in Fig. 2B) and calculate a cluster size. The cluster size is summed across all pairs of EEG features in the EEG feature cluster and voxels in the lesion cluster (Supplementary Methods, Eq. 4).

Interpreting cluster-based permutation results

Interpretation of p-values obtained from cluster-based permutation analysis requires careful consideration45,46. The p-value pertains specifically to the entire cluster, not to any individual EEG feature or lesion voxel within that cluster. Therefore, finding a significant cluster indicates, with 95% confidence, that a relationship between the presence of lesions and EEG activity exists somewhere within that cluster boundary, but it does not imply the statistical significance of any particular point or sub-region within the cluster itself.

N-dimensional (ND) voxel lesion symptom mapping (VLSM)

ND: input data

For N-dimensional data (Fig. 4A), the input data consist of the same lesion map data as required for 0D and 1D analysis, but the EEG data now have X x Y x… features per patient, where each X, Y,… represents a different dimension of EEG data. For example, X could represent different channels, and Y could represent different frequencies (as shown in Fig. 4A). Each dimension will have its own associated adjacency matrix. All EEG features can be collapsed into a single dimension and adjacency matrices combined.

ND: statistics

As in the 1D case, the relationship between lesion presence and EEG is assessed for every [voxel, EEG feature] pair resulting in a t-map with dimensions (#voxels x #EEG features).

ND: clustering

As in the 1D case, this is a two-step process with the second step (clustering voxels) being identical to the 1D case. For the first step, it is required to define an adjacency matrix for each EEG dimension (in this case, for frequency and channel). As these ND data can be flattened to a single dimension for each patient (as seen in Fig, 4A), it is possible to combine the adjacency matrices for each dimension (Fig. 4B). This puts the data into a form where the same method as 1D data can be applied.

However, when finding maximal EEG feature clusters, it may be preferable to add an additional step that refines the EEG feature clusters. When there is more than 1D, it is possible after finding the unique maximal EEG feature clusters that the EEG feature cluster contains a scattered mix of feature combinations across the different dimensions. Here we introduce a property of an EEG feature cluster, which is whether it is “solid”. In a non-solid EEG feature cluster, although there is adjacency between all EEG features, it is not the case that all combinations across the N (X, Y, …) dimensions are present. For a solid EEG feature cluster, all combinations will be present. For example, in Fig. 4B, frequencies F1, F2 and F3 are significant in the non-solid cluster for C5, but only F3 is significant for channel C3. This limits the interpretability of the EEG feature cluster, as rather than just knowing the features along each EEG dimension, you will need to know all pairs that are present, changing the amount of info to remember from X + Y + … to X*Y*… Therefore, we introduce a method that decomposes non-solid clusters into maximal solid clusters, which can be defined by a set of features for each dimension, where all features in that dimension are adjacent and all tuples across the dimensions are significant. Figure S2 describes the additional steps necessary to decompose a non-solid cluster into its constituent maximal solid clusters. The restriction of analysis to solid clusters, as opposed to including non-solid clusters, is an optional methodological decision. It is important to note that focusing solely on solid clusters will likely increase the resultant number of EEG clusters, given that each non-solid cluster is decomposed into multiple solid constituents. Consequently, future research is necessary to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages associated with analyzing solid versus non-solid clusters in MD-VLM, assessing their replicability in an independent test dataset.

Task

The EEG task and dataset was previously described in36, with relevant details reproduced here.

Patients

Thirty-one chronic stroke patients (19 male, mean age 54.0 ± 11.7 years, range 29–73, time since stroke 60.3 ± 58.2 months, range 10–232) were recruited for this study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) hand paralysis with no finger extension; (2) minimum time since stroke 10 months; (3) age between 18 and 80 years; (4) no psychiatric or neurological condition other than stroke; (5) no cerebellar lesion or bilateral motor deficit; (6) no pregnancy; (7) no epilepsy or medication for epilepsy during the last 6 months; (8) eligibility to undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); and (9) ability to understand and follow instructions. The experiments were conducted at the University of Tübingen, Germany. The experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Tübingen and have been performed in accordance with the revised Declaration of Helsinki and all the patients provided written informed consent.

Experimental design and procedure

The patients were asked to perform an assessment task in which they had to move their healthy hand, or to attempt to move their paralyzed hand, while their EEG and electromyographic (EMG) activity was recorded. Each patient performed between 4 and 6 blocks, each of which contained, in a random order, 17 trials of movement execution (healthy hand) and 17 trials of movement attempt (completely paralyzed hand). The target number of blocks for patients was 6, though the actual number of blocks recorded depended on patient fatigue levels, as in some cases they requested to stop the measurements before finishing the 6 expected blocks. Audiovisual cues guided the patients regarding the phases of a trial: rest (random duration between 4 and 5 s), movement execution/ attempt (4 s), and an inter-trial interval that was included every three trials (random duration between 8 and 9 s). The patients were asked to perform (or try to perform) openings and closings with their healthy (or paretic) hand at a comfortable personal pace during the 4 s of the movement execution/attempt interval (generally, it took them around 1.5 s to perform a complete open-close cycle). Before starting the first block, they were instructed to minimize compensatory movements and any other source of contamination for the EEG activity during the rest and movement intervals. In the control (rest) condition, the timing of each trial was exactly the same, but the subjects were instructed not to move in response to the cue.

Data recording and preprocessing

EEG activity was recorded using a commercial Acticap system (BrainProducts GmbH, Germany), with 16 electrodes placed on Fp1, Fp2, F3, Fz, F4, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, CP3, CP4, P3, Pz, P4, and Oz (according to the international 10/20 system). The ground and reference electrodes were placed on AFz and FCz, respectively. Vertical and horizontal electrooculography (EOG) was also recorded to capture eye movements. Electromyographic activity was recorded using bipolar Ag/ AgCl electrodes (Myotronics-Noromed, USA) from four muscle groups of each arm: extensor carpi ulnaris, extensor digitorum, external head of the biceps and external head of the triceps. All signals were synchronously recorded at 500 Hz. Ocular artifacts were removed, (as previously described in36. The EEG data were filtered between 0.1 and 48 Hz with a 4-th order causal Butterworth filter. The signals were trimmed down to 7-s trials, from − 3 to + 4 s with respect to the movement cue. Although the shortest duration of the rest intervals was 4 s, we decided to consider only the last 3 s to ensure that the patients were in resting state. Data was then re-referenced using a Laplacian montage using the nearest neighbors for each electrode to reduce volume conduction recorded by neighboring electrodes. Trials were statistically rejected based on EMG and EEG as previously described in36. Two subjects were rejected due to excessive artifacts in the EEG data, leaving 29 subjects to be analyzed.

Time–frequency analysis

Time–frequency analysis was applied using the fieldtrip toolbox47, to extract trial-averaged power in frequencies from 3 to 45 Hz in 1 Hz bins, at a time resolution of 0.01 s using Morlet wavelets (3 cycles). Trial averaged power was then baseline corrected using the decibel method and the baseline period -2.5 to -1 s from the cue onset. To look at cue-evoked neural responses, the time-period from 0–0.5 s from the cue was averaged, giving EEG data for each subject with dimensions 16 channel × 42 frequencies.

EEG feature adjacency matrices

EEG Channel adjacency matrix (Fig. 5B) was defined based using Euclidean distance between electrodes. The EEG frequency adjacency matrix was defined by consecutive frequencies being adjacent.

Lesion maps

The method for extraction of the lesion masks used in this study was previously described in48 and is summarized here. MRI recordings were performed with a 3-T Magnetom (Tim Trio, Siemens, Erlangen) whole-body scanner. A T1-weighted anatomical MR image was acquired using a 1-mm isotropic MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 3.03 ms, TI = 1100 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 256 × 256, matrix size = 256 × 256, number of slices = 176, slice thickness = 1 mm, bandwidth = 130 Hz/Px. Individual lesion masks were created manually (by a trained neurological expert) on axial slices of a T1-weighted images using the MRIcron software package (Version 09/2009, https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricron). Normalization to standard space was accomplished using the SPM8 normalization function. SPM8 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) was used as this was the version of SPM available when the MRI data was processed49. After normalization the brain and mask were checked for distortions and the mask borders were adjusted if necessary. This ensured that the original borders of the mask were in the same localization according to the patient’s individual anatomical specifics concerning sulci and gyri.

MD-VLM settings for analysis

Data was analyzed with the MD-VLM toolbox (https://github.com/rhardstone/MDVLM) using MATLAB version R2022B. MD-VLM was applied using lesion maps, cue-evoked EEG activity, and adjacency matrices. Statistics for each pair [voxel, EEG Feature] included a lesion size confound. Each t-map was thresholded using a stringent cluster-defining threshold equivalent to p < 0.005 to increase our confidence that the resulting clusters represent regions of a strong, reliable effect rather than a more diffuse, weaker one. A total of 1,000 permutations were run to form the null distribution of cluster sizes. We set the cluster-level significance threshold (which controls the family-wise error rate) at p < 0.05 so that positive or negative cluster sizes were defined as significant if they were larger than the 95th percentile of the corresponding null distribution.

The MD-VLM method was applied to this dataset, using the lesion maps, 2D EEG data and adjacency matrices. For the statistics, lesion size was included as a confounder. This resulted in 3,775 MD-VLM clusters showing a positive relationship between lesion presence and EEG (i.e., subjects with a lesion overlapping with the lesion cluster tended to have higher values for those features than subjects who did not), and 3,425 MD-VLM clusters showed a negative relationship.

Testing significance of the cluster sizes showed that eleven of the clusters showing a positive relationship were significant but none of the MD-VLM clusters showing a negative relationship were, indicating that there was a significant relationship between EEG and lesion presence in the dataset. Of the 11 positive clusters, all were related to a single channel (P3), and the frequencies were largely overlapping, with the largest range being 20–32 Hz and all the clusters including 23–28 Hz. Voxels for these 11 clusters were also highly overlapping, sharing at least 65% of the same voxels. The MD-VLM with the largest cluster size (Fig. 6A), consisted of frontal white matter lesion (1,291 voxels), with lesions in this cluster positively related to 20–32 Hz EEG evoked activity (i.e. 12 EEG Frequency Features) in electrode P3, which overlies the ipsilesional parietal cortex. Investigating the subjects overlap with this lesion (Fig. 6B), there was a clear bimodal distribution with most subjects (22) overlapping < 50% of the lesion, and 7 subjects showing almost 100% overlap with the lesion. Comparing the EEG data between the two groups showed that subjects without a lesion exhibited a broad beta desynchronization in this post-cue period, whereas subjects with the lesion showed little change from baseline during cued movement, suggesting an impaired cue-evoked response in subjects with this frontal-white matter area lesioned. Finally, the lesion overlap maps showed that the lesioned group tended to have infarcts that spread more anterior than those in the non-lesioned group. To evaluate whether this effect was related to the cue or to the movement, the same cluster was tested in the control (rest) condition (where the trial structure was the same, but subjects were instructed not to move after the cue). Here, the cluster showed a significant effect in the same direction (p = 0.003), suggesting that this effect was in response to the cue rather than a movement-related finding. Taken together, these results suggest impaired cue-evoked responses in frontal-parietal networks when this frontal white matter region is damaged.

Discussion

Here we introduce a method (MD-VLM) for extending voxel-lesion mapping to multidimensional data, which clusters all relationships between lesion presence (in voxels) and multidimensional features and defines the significance of those clusters. As proof of principle, we applied this method to lesion-map data from a group of chronic stroke patients who had EEG recorded while attempting hand movements with their paretic hand (Fig. 5). We found significant relationships between lesion presence and cue evoked EEG activity (Fig. 6). Anatomical Lesion clusters were located in frontal white matter regions and were related to reduced beta desynchronization (20–32 Hz) in parietal EEG in the lesioned hemisphere. Testing the significance of this relationship in a control condition (where subjects were instructed not to move in response to the cue) indicated that this relationship was not specific to movement.

Despite decades of EEG research in patients with stroke, there has not been a concerted effort to understand how stroke lesion location affects neural activity. In animal models, the effects of induced lesions on different areas on EEG activity has long been studied50, and have been used to determine the genesis of cortical activity such as specific neural oscillations. However, it remains unclear how these findings translate to stroke-related infarcts in humans, among whom lesion patterns differ substantially from those induced experimentally in preclinical models51. One classic study4 investigated the potential of EEG to dissociate cortical from lacunar infarction in humans during the acute phase post-stroke (< 2 weeks from infarction), and found that cortical strokes in the middle cerebral artery territory were more likely to produce lateralized abnormalities (as assessed qualitatively) in the EEG as compared to lacunar strokes, which rarely did. However, despite these early findings showing that lesion location differentially affects neural activity, there has been little follow-up. Other recent studies have also looked at the relationship between EEG and relatively coarse measurements of lesion location6,52 or infarct volume53, that do not include the voxel-level information in lesion maps. We propose that the ability to define accurate statistical relationships between lesion location and EEG abnormalities will enable an improved mechanistic understanding of the effect that stroke-related injury to different cortical and subcortical regions has on the genesis of post-stroke EEG signal changes. This knowledge could help understand EEG findings related to neurologic impairment and recovery seen in studies where structural imaging was not available (i.e., improved biomarkers54).

A key finding of our study is the observation of ipsilesional diaschisis, whereby focal frontal lesions produced functional disruptions in the remote, but structurally intact, parietal cortex. This highlights the network-level consequences of focal damage. We did not, however, observe significant contralesional effects. This is unlikely to be a limitation of the MD-VLM method itself, but rather a consequence of our specific dataset and design. Detecting more subtle contralesional effects may require greater statistical power than our sample size afforded, or may be more evident using tasks that explicitly demand interhemispheric processing. Future work with larger, more heterogeneous patient cohorts is needed to map the full extent of both ipsilesional and contralesional network disruptions following focal brain injury.

A key methodological choice in our approach is the cluster-defining threshold. By using a stringent threshold (p < 0.005), our analysis was optimized to detect focal clusters with strong effects. Future users should consider that a more lenient threshold would instead emphasize larger clusters with weaker effects, depending on their specific research question. While future extensions of our method could incorporate techniques like Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE)55 to remove this choice, there would be a large increase in computational cost that may decrease its utility.

The presented MD-VLM algorithm, linking multidimensional features to lesions, can be readily extended, notably by incorporating “disconnectome” maps56–58. Standard lesion mapping (e.g., VLSM) can be limited as distinct lesions causing similar disconnections (like along the corticospinal tract) are treated differently. Disconnectome maps, derived from lesion locations using normative connectivity data, instead estimate disconnected brain regions and tracts. MD-VLM can directly utilize these disconnectome maps in place of raw lesion data without algorithmic changes. This allows for relating specific brain dysconnectivity patterns to multidimensional features.

It is well-established that scalp-recorded EEG signals are influenced by volume conduction, meaning that activity at a single channel reflects neural activity from a broader region, not just the area directly beneath the electrode59. This is particularly important in the context of brain lesions, as individual differences in lesion location and extent can affect signal propagation due to altered electrical conductivity60. MD-VLM toolbox is agnostic to the nature of the EEG used and can use source reconstructed EEG data in the same way as channel-level data. While interpreting results from sensor-level data, it’s crucial to consider the potential impact of altered conductivity due to lesions. When using source-reconstructed EEG, we recommend employing methods that incorporate lesion information during the construction of the volume conductor model to ensure accurate source localization. A key distinction between MD-VLM and other novel techniques like SilenceMap40 or sLORETA38 is one of purpose and scope. The latter are source localization methods designed to identify the origins of neurophysiological signals within a single subject. In contrast, MD-VLM is a group-level statistical technique for mapping the relationship between lesion anatomy and complex, multidimensional outcomes. These two classes of methods are not mutually exclusive; they are highly complementary. For instance, to investigate network-level phenomena like diaschisis, one could first apply SilenceMap61 to each patient’s EEG data to characterize remote functional abnormalities. These individual abnormality profiles could then serve as the input for an MD-VLM analysis, which would identify the specific lesion locations across the group that reliably predict these distant effects. While our current study did not employ this two-step process, it represents a promising future application for mapping lesion-network interactions. A limitation of MD-VLM is that, like all lesion-mapping techniques, its statistical power is dependent on the sample size and the distribution of lesion locations across the cohort.

Effective application of MD-VLM necessitates careful consideration of sample size to ensure robust and reproducible findings62,63. Research in Voxel-Based Lesion-Symptom Mapping (VLSM)64, often using resampling techniques, indicates that smaller samples (N ≤ 40) are generally underpowered. This leads to an increased risk of false negatives (missing true relationships) and an overestimation of effect sizes for any significant results detected. Even larger samples (N > 100) do not inherently guarantee sufficient statistical power at the voxel-level65, as the distribution of lesions across patients critically impacts the power for statistical inference in any given region. For multidimensional EEG data, power analyses for cluster-based effects are further influenced by statistical properties like inter-feature correlations. Currently, EEG studies in stroke populations, especially those investigating acute periods, rarely achieve sample sizes beyond 40 subjects (e.g., median sample of size of 34 in studies assessed in66). Overcoming this challenge hinges on promoting data sharing and multi-site collaborations, guided by standardized experimental protocols and data formats like BIDS67, to aggregate an adequate number of subjects. This approach has enabled the acquisition of large structural MRI datasets in stroke, exemplified by the ENIGMA consortium’s dataset of over 2,100 patients from 39 sites68. While these sample size considerations apply to the dataset employed in this study, the internal validity of our findings is strengthened by the replication of results in a separate control task. Furthermore, rigorous experimental design can enhance the ability to draw meaningful inferences from smaller sample sizes, though the pursuit of larger, shared datasets remains critical for advancing the field.

We found post-cue changes in neural activity (from parietal cortex on lesioned hemisphere) in chronic stroke patients with white matter PFC lesions. Studies have implicated lesions in this region with damage to the fronto-parietal attention network, leading to deficits in attention in these patients69. A recently published study70 reported that frontal lesions caused cue-related reductions in P100 amplitude over ipsilesional parietal channels (also seen in71–74, which could be related75 to the evoked changes in beta-band oscillations found in the current study. While this dataset demonstrated the feasibility and potential of the MD-VLM algorithm, its inherent limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Firstly, the small subject pool necessitated collapsing lesion locations across hemispheres, precluding the analysis of lateralized relationships, such as those observed in post-stroke motor ability76. Secondly, patient data were collected during the chronic stroke stage, making it difficult to disentangle whether observed EEG patterns stem from the initial lesion or subsequent recovery and rehabilitation processes77. Lastly, patients had largely overlapping lesion maps, thereby restricting the generalizability of conclusions about lesion effects on EEG activity to these specific regions. Applying our proposed methods to datasets collected in earlier stages after stroke and with larger sample sizes is of high future interest.

The introduction of any new analytical method, such as the one presented, necessitates subsequent simulation studies to thoroughly investigate the influence of different algorithmic parameters and ensure its robustness. This follows the precedent set by other neuroimaging techniques, like VLSM, where the foundational paper26 spurred a series of follow-up studies examining methodological choices and their consequences65,78–80. To effectively conduct these simulations, an optimal dataset should encompass the following attributes: (1) A large cohort is crucial for partitioning data into training and independent test sets, thereby enabling rigorous evaluation of model generalizability. (2) High-density EEG recordings are needed to systematically explore the impact of varying electrode densities (through subsampling) and to compare the efficacy of different approaches to constructing connectivity matrices. (3) The dataset should support the testing and comparison of various statistical techniques, including non-parametric methods like the Brunner-Munzel81 test as alternatives or complements to standard linear regression. (4) It must allow for detailed investigation into the effects of methodological choices on identifying and consistently reproducing EEG phenomena, particularly concerning the differentiation between ‘solid’ and 'non-solid’ cluster types.

MD-VLM is a novel and unbiased method for relating neurophysiologic changes after stroke with neuroanatomic lesions. We propose that this method will enable an improved mechanistic understanding of stroke-induced changes in EEG signals and improve hypothesis generation for future biomarker development. Further, this method can be applied to other multidimensional datasets such as different neurophysiological recording methods (MEG, fNIRS) or kinematic recordings, to allow this data to be mapped to the presence of lesions without a priori selection of features of interest.
