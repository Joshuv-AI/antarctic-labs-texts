A Theory of θ \theta -Expectations
Qian Qi

Abstract
. The canonical theory of stochastic calculus under ambiguity, founded on sub-additivity, is insensitive to non-convex uncertainty structures, leading to an identifiability impasse. This paper develops a mathematical framework for an identifiable calculus sensitive to non-convex geometry. We introduce the θ \theta -BSDE, a class of backward stochastic differential equations where the driver is determined by a pointwise maximization over a primitive, possibly non-convex, uncertainty set. The system’s tractability is predicated not on convexity, but on a global analytic hypothesis: the existence of a unique and globally Lipschitz maximizer map for the driver function. Under this hypothesis, which carves out a tractable class of models, we establish well-posedness via a fixed-point argument. For a distinct, geometrically regular class of models, we prove a result of independent interest: under non-degeneracy conditions from Malliavin calculus, the maximizer is unique along any solution path, ensuring the model’s internal consistency. We clarify the fundamental logical gap between this pathwise property and the global regularity required by our existence proof. The resulting valuation operator defines a dynamically consistent expectation, and we establish its connection to fully nonlinear PDEs via a Feynman-Kac formula.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Identifiability Impasse of Convex Models

A primary mathematical framework for modeling ambiguity, or Knightian uncertainty
[
Knight(1921)
]
, is the theory of sublinear expectations and the associated G-calculus
[
Peng(2019)
]
. This framework provides a dynamically consistent stochastic calculus for settings where probability models are not known with precision. Its mathematical foundation rests on replacing the classical linear expectation with a functional
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
satisfying monotonicity, translation-invariance, and the axiom of sub-additivity:

\mathcal{E}[X+Y]\leq\mathcal{E}[X]+\mathcal{E}[Y].

Representation theorems for such functionals establish that any such operator admits a dual representation as the supremum of linear expectations over a convex, weakly compact set of probability measures
\mathcal{P}
:

\mathcal{E}[X]=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[X].

The axiom of sub-additivity is not a mere technical convenience; it is mathematically equivalent to the convexity of the set of priors
\mathcal{P}
.

This equivalence, while mathematically powerful, leads to a fundamental identifiability impasse. The functional
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
, being the support function of the set
\mathcal{P}
, is completely determined by the closed convex hull of
\mathcal{P}
, denoted
\overlineconv(\mathcal{P})

[
Rockafellar(1970)
]
. Consequently, any two uncertainty sets
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
that share the same convex hull, i.e.,
\overlineconv(\mathcal{P}_{1})=\overlineconv(\mathcal{P}_{2})
, are observationally equivalent. They generate the exact same functional
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
and are thus indistinguishable from the perspective of the calculus. The standard theory is, by its architecture, insensitive to the geometry of non-convex structures.

This insensitivity poses a significant limitation in applications where ambiguity is naturally described by non-convex sets. For instance, in fundamental physics, uncertainty about the correct Equation of State (EoS) for matter inside a neutron star might be represented by a disjoint union of two distinct classes of models,
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{\text{hadronic}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\text{quark}}
. The classical framework necessitates a convexification step,
\overlineconv(\mathcal{U})
, which introduces a continuum of averaged physical laws that have no theoretical basis. This procedure discards crucial information about the disjoint nature of the underlying scientific uncertainty.

1.2.
A New Calculus via a Structural Regularity Hypothesis

This paper develops a mathematical theory for valuation under ambiguity that confronts this identifiability problem. Our objective is to construct a rigorous and tractable calculus that remains sensitive to the specific geometry of the underlying uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
, without recourse to convexification.

Our approach is constructive. We propose a new class of backward stochastic differential equations, which we term
\theta
-BSDEs
. The solution is a triplet of adapted processes
(Y,Z,A)
satisfying a coupled system. The first component is a backward evolution for the value process
Y
and the volatility process
Z
:

(1.1)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}F(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s},A_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

The novelty lies in the second component, which endogenously determines the parameter process
A
from the primitive, possibly non-convex, uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
. At each instant in time, the parameter
A_{t}
is chosen to solve a pointwise optimization problem that depends on the current state
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
of the solution itself:

(1.2)

A_{t}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,Y_{t}(\omega),Z_{t}(\omega),a).

The endogenous feedback in (
1.2
) renders the system intractable in general. The maximizer
A_{t}
might not be unique, or it might depend discontinuously on
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
, violating the regularity conditions required for standard BSDE theory.

The central contribution of this paper is the identification of a structural condition that renders this problem well-posed. We do not solve the problem for general non-convex sets. Instead, our main existence and uniqueness theorem is predicated on a strong, a priori, analytic condition on the problem’s primitives. This condition replaces the geometric assumption of convexity with an analytic assumption of global regularity.

Hypothesis 1.1

(Global Regularity of the Maximizer)
.

The driver
F:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
and the compact set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}
are such that:

(i)

(Standard Driver Properties)
For each fixed
a\in\mathcal{U}
, the mapping
(t,\omega,y,z)\mapsto F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is a Carathéodory function. The process
s\mapsto\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}|F(s,\omega,0,0,a)|
belongs to
\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T])
.

(ii)

(Uniform Lipschitz Continuity)
There exist constants
L_{F,yz}>0
and
L_{F,a}>0
such that for all admissible arguments:

(a)

For any fixed
a\in\mathcal{U}
, the map
(y,z)\mapsto F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is Lipschitz continuous:

|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a)-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a)|\leq L_{F,yz}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

(b)

For any fixed
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
, the map
a\mapsto F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is Lipschitz continuous:

|F(t,\omega,y,z,a_{1})-F(t,\omega,y,z,a_{2})|\leq L_{F,a}\left\lVert a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}.

(iii)

(Existence of a Regular Maximizer Map)
There exists a mapping
\mathcal{A}^{*}:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\to\mathcal{U}
and a constant
L_{a}>0
such that for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
, the following hold:

(a)

Unique Maximizer:
For any
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
,
\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is the unique solution to the optimization problem:

\{\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)\}=\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,y,z,a).

(b)

Global Lipschitz Continuity:
The map
(y,z)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is Lipschitz continuous, uniformly in
(t,\omega)
:

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq L_{a}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

(c)

Progressive Measurability:
For any fixed
(y,z)
, the map
(t,\omega)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is progressively measurable.

This hypothesis is the foundation of our constructive theory. It is a strong condition, but a precise one, that defines a non-trivial class of models for which a single-valued, identifiable functional calculus can be constructed.

1.3.
Pathwise Consistency and the Logical Gap

For a significant class of non-convex models where Hypothesis
3.2
is not known to hold, or is known to fail, such as in models with a projection-type driver, we establish a different type of regularity. For models with a geometrically regular uncertainty set (e.g., a set with positive reach), we prove a result of independent interest: under strong non-degeneracy conditions from Malliavin calculus, the maximizer in (
1.2
) is unique along any solution path. This result ensures the internal consistency of the model specification for a broad class of genuinely non-convex problems.

However, it is crucial to recognize the logical gap between this pathwise consistency and the global regularity required by our main existence theorem. The proof of our existence and uniqueness result (Theorem
3.3
) proceeds via a fixed-point argument on a function space, typically using the Banach fixed-point theorem. For this argument to succeed, the mapping that defines the fixed point must be well-defined and a contraction on the entire space. This requires the maximizer map
\mathcal{A}^{*}
to be well-defined and Lipschitz for
any
process in the space, not just for the eventual fixed point. The pathwise consistency result, in contrast, is a deep, probabilistic statement that the
solution process itself
will almost surely avoid the states
(y,z)
where the maximizer is ill-behaved. This pathwise property is therefore not sufficient to power our fixed-point argument. Highlighting this gap is critical: it clarifies the precise role of our main hypothesis and pinpoints a key direction for future research, namely, the establishment of existence theorems for the geometrically regular class of models.

1.4.
Main Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions to the mathematical theory of uncertainty:

(1)

A New Axiomatic Space:
We define the
\theta
-expectation space by a set of axioms that retain dynamic consistency and monotonicity but dispense with sub-additivity, thereby severing the intrinsic link to convexity.

(2)

A Constructive Realization under Global Regularity:
We prove our main existence and uniqueness result (Theorem
3.3
) for
\theta
-BSDEs under the Global Regularity Hypothesis. This provides a concrete, constructive realization of our axiomatic space and formally delineates a broad class of models, including certain non-convex ones, for which a rigorous calculus is possible.

(3)

Pathwise Consistency for Geometrically Regular Models:
For a significant class of non-convex models with a projection-type structure, we establish that under non-degeneracy conditions from Malliavin calculus, the maximizer is unique along any solution path (Theorem
3.7
). This result ensures the internal consistency of a class of models not covered by our main existence theorem.

(4)

A Consistent Calculus and PDE Connection:
We prove that the functional generated by the
\theta
-BSDE satisfies our axioms and establish a nonlinear Feynman-Kac formula (Theorem
7.3
) connecting our framework to a class of fully nonlinear parabolic PDEs.

In essence, this paper develops a mathematical theory that provides an identifiable and rigorous approach to modeling for a structured class of complex uncertainty structures. It establishes a solid foundation for a new avenue of research by providing a tractable framework that respects non-convex geometry under a clear analytic condition, while simultaneously clarifying the mathematical challenges that remain for broader classes of models.

2.
The
\theta
-Expectation Space

We begin by establishing the axiomatic foundation for our theory. The objective is to formulate a dynamic valuation functional that is sensitive to the geometry of non-convex uncertainty structures. This requires a departure from the canonical axioms of sublinear expectation, which are intrinsically tied to convexity. The axioms we propose are chosen to retain the essential properties of a dynamic valuation, monotonicity and time-consistency, while deliberately discarding the convexity-inducing axiom of sub-additivity.

2.1.
Setup and Notation

Throughout this paper, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P_{0})
be a complete probability space, and let
T\in(0,\infty)
be a fixed, finite time horizon. The space is endowed with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfying the usual conditions of right-continuity and completeness with respect to the null sets of
\mathcal{F}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a vector space of real-valued,
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable random variables, representing terminal payoffs or claims. For each
t\in[0,T]
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{t}
the subspace of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of all
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variables. We make the standing assumption that
\mathcal{H}
contains all constant random variables, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathcal{H}
. For the constructive part of our theory (Section
3
and beyond), we will work within the context of Lebesgue spaces, specifically setting
\mathcal{H}=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})
for some
p\geq 1
.

2.2.
Axiomatic Definition

We now define the central object of our axiomatic theory.

Definition 2.1

(The
\theta
-Expectation Space)
.

A
\theta
-expectation space
is a triple
(\Omega,\mathcal{H},(\mathcal{E}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]})
where, for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathcal{E}_{t}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{t}
is a functional, called the
conditional
\theta
-expectation at time
t
, satisfying the following axioms for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{H}
:

(A1)

(Monotonicity)
If
X\geq Y

P_{0}
-a.s., then
\mathcal{E}_{t}[X]\geq\mathcal{E}_{t}[Y]

P_{0}
-a.s.

(A2)

(Translation-Invariance)
For any
m\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
, we have:

\mathcal{E}_{t}[X+m]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[X]+m.

(A3)

(Dynamic Consistency / Tower Property)
For any
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
:

\mathcal{E}_{t}[X]=\mathcal{E}_{t}\left[\mathcal{E}_{s}[X]\right].

We denote the unconditional
\theta
-expectation by
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{0}[\cdot]
.

Remark 2.2

(On Axiom (A2))
.

In financial mathematics, Axiom (A2) is often referred to as cash-additivity. We use the term translation-invariance as it is more descriptive when
m
is a general
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variable and not merely a deterministic constant.

2.3.
Remarks and Direct Consequences

Remark 2.3

(Origin of the Name)
.

The name
\theta
-expectation
is motivated by the constructive realization of such a space, which is a central result of this paper (Section
3
). In that construction, the functional
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is generated as the solution to a backward stochastic differential equation whose driver is determined by a pointwise maximization problem. The maximization is performed over a parameter, which we conventionally denote by
\theta
(or
a
in later sections), belonging to a primitive uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
. The name thus reflects the endogenous, state-dependent choice of this parameter, which is the mechanism that allows the expectation to be sensitive to non-convex geometry.

Proposition 2.4

(Normalization)
.

The axioms (A1)-(A3) imply that the functional is normalized. Specifically, for any
m\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
, we have:

\mathcal{E}_{t}[m]=m.

A direct consequence is that
\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=0
.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
. By Axiom (A2), setting
X=0
, we have

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}_{t}[m]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[0+m]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]+m.

It remains only to show that
\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=0
. Let
m_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]
. By definition of the functional,
m_{0}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variable, so
m_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
. We may therefore apply the result from equation (
2.1
) to
m_{0}
itself:

(2.2)

\mathcal{E}_{t}[m_{0}]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]+m_{0}.

However, by Axiom (A3) for the case
s=t
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[\mathcal{E}_{t}[\cdot]]
. Applying this to
X=0
, we find:

(2.3)

\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[m_{0}].

Comparing equations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we have
\mathcal{E}_{t}[m_{0}]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]+m_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[m_{0}]
. This implies
\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]+m_{0}
, from which we conclude that
m_{0}=0

P_{0}
-a.s.
Substituting
\mathcal{E}_{t}[0]=0
back into equation (
2.1
) gives the desired result
\mathcal{E}_{t}[m]=m
.
∎

Remark 2.5

(The Absence of Sub-additivity and Convexity)
.

A defining characteristic of this axiomatic system is the omission of sub-additivity. The property that
\mathcal{E}_{t}[X+Y]\leq\mathcal{E}_{t}[X]+\mathcal{E}_{t}[Y]
is not required to hold. This is the central departure from the theory of sublinear expectations and coherent risk measures
[
Peng(2019)
]
.

This omission is a fundamental structural choice. The axiom of sub-additivity is mathematically equivalent to the property that the corresponding dual representation involves a supremum over a convex set of probability measures. The functional
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, as the support function of this set of priors
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, is therefore determined entirely by the closed convex hull of
\mathcal{P}_{t}

[
Rockafellar(1970)
]
. Consequently, any information about the non-convex geometry of the true uncertainty set is not captured by the calculus, leading to the identifiability impasse discussed previously.

By abandoning the sub-additivity axiom, we sever this intrinsic link to convexity. This opens the possibility of constructing a calculus that is sensitive to non-convex structures. However, this comes at the cost of losing access to the machinery of convex analysis and the theory of support functions. The central mathematical challenge, which this paper resolves for a structurally regular class of models, is to find a tractable mathematical structure to substitute for the global convexity assumption. As we will demonstrate, this structure will be provided not by a property of the uncertainty set itself, but by a strong analytic regularity condition on the solution to a state-dependent optimization problem, a condition we formalize as our Global Regularity Hypothesis (Hypothesis
3.2
).

3.
Realization of
\theta
-Expectation Spaces via
\theta
-BSDEs

In this section, we provide the constructive realization of the axiomatic space defined in Section
2
. We introduce a novel class of backward stochastic differential equations, which we call
\theta
-BSDEs. The main result of this section is to establish that these equations are well-posed under a specific structural hypothesis that does not rely on convexity. The solution to a
\theta
-BSDE will then serve as the concrete definition of our conditional
\theta
-expectation functional.

3.1.
The
\theta
-BSDE System

Let
T>0
be a finite time horizon. We work on the canonical Wiener space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P_{0})
, where
\Omega=C_{0}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued paths starting at 0,
B_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t}
is the coordinate process,
P_{0}
is the Wiener measure under which
B
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, and
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the natural filtration generated by
B
, augmented with the
P_{0}
-null sets of
\mathcal{F}
.

Let
\mathbb{S}_{d}
be the space of
d\times d
real symmetric matrices, endowed with the Frobenius inner product
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{T}B)
and the induced norm
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{T}A)}
. We define the standard function spaces for
p=2
:

•

\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
: The space of continuous,
\mathbb{F}
-adapted processes
Y
with norm
\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|Y_{s}|^{2}]\right)^{1/2}
.

•

\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
: The space of
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable processes
Z
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
such that
\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert Z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds)]\right)^{1/2}
.

•

\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
: The space of
\mathbb{S}_{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable processes
A
with norm
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert A_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}ds]\right)^{1/2}
.

We denote the product space by
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])=\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
. For a subinterval
[t,T]
, the spaces are defined analogously.

Definition 3.1

(The
\theta
-BSDE System)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-empty, compact subset of
\mathbb{S}_{d}
, representing the primitive uncertainty set. Let
\xi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})
be a terminal condition and let
F:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
be a driver function. A solution to the
\theta
-BSDE with data
(\xi,F,\mathcal{U})
is a triplet of processes
(Y,Z,A)
in
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
that satisfies
P_{0}
-a.s.:

(1)

(Backward Evolution):
For all
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.1)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}F(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s},A_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(2)

(Pointwise Optimality):
For almost every
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, the process
A_{t}
satisfies:

(3.2)

A_{t}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,Y_{t}(\omega),Z_{t}(\omega),a).

3.2.
The Central Challenge and the Structural Hypothesis

The well-posedness of the coupled system (
Equation

3.1
-
Equation

3.2
) is a formidable mathematical challenge. The endogenous optimality condition (
3.2
) means that the driver of the BSDE at time
t
depends on the solution
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
itself through a maximization problem. For a general non-convex set
\mathcal{U}
, the
\argmax
may be empty, set-valued, or depend discontinuously on
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
, any of which would prevent the application of standard BSDE theory.

Our approach is to solve this problem not for all possible non-convex sets, but for a structurally regular subclass of models. We replace the geometric assumption of convexity with a strong, global, analytic assumption on the regularity of the maximizer of the driver function. This hypothesis is the foundation of our existence and uniqueness theory.

Hypothesis 3.2

(Global Regularity of the Maximizer)
.

The driver
F:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
and the compact set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}
are such that:

(i)

(Standard Driver Properties)
For each fixed
a\in\mathcal{U}
, the mapping
(t,\omega,y,z)\mapsto F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is a Carathéodory function, i.e., progressively measurable in
(t,\omega)
and continuous in
(y,z,a)
. The process
s\mapsto\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}|F(s,\omega,0,0,a)|
belongs to
\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T])
.

(ii)

(Uniform Lipschitz Continuity in
(y,z)
)
There exists a constant
L_{F}>0
such that for all admissible arguments,

|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a)-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a)|\leq L_{F}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

(iii)

(Existence of a Regular Maximizer Map)
There exists a mapping
\mathcal{A}^{*}:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\to\mathcal{U}
and a constant
L_{a}>0
such that for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
, the following hold:

(a)

Unique Maximizer:
For any
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
,
\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is the unique solution to the optimization problem:

\{\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)\}=\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,y,z,a).

(b)

Global Lipschitz Continuity:
The map
(y,z)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is Lipschitz continuous, uniformly in
(t,\omega)
:

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq L_{a}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

(c)

Progressive Measurability:
For any fixed
(y,z)
, the map
(t,\omega)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
is progressively measurable.

3.3.
Well-Posedness under Global Regularity

Hypothesis
3.2
provides sufficient structure to establish the well-posedness of the
\theta
-BSDE. This is the main constructive result of our paper.

Theorem 3.3

(Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions)
.

Under Hypothesis
3.2
, for any terminal condition
\xi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})
, the
\theta
-BSDE (
Definition

3.1
) admits a unique solution
(Y,Z,A)
in the space
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
.

Proof.

The proof strategy is to demonstrate that the coupled
\theta
-BSDE system (
Equation

3.1
-
Equation

3.2
) is equivalent to a standard backward stochastic differential equation whose driver satisfies the classical Lipschitz conditions. The existence and uniqueness of the solution to the original system will then follow directly from the foundational theory of BSDEs. The proof is structured in four steps.

Step 1: Reduction to an Equivalent Standard BSDE.

The core of the reduction lies in the Global Regularity Hypothesis (
3.2
). Specifically, Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-a) posits the existence of a unique maximizer for the pointwise optimization problem (
3.2
). This allows us to define the
driver

\tilde{F}:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
as the value of the maximization:

(3.3)

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,y,z,a).

By Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-a), this maximum is uniquely attained at
a=\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
, so we can express the driver via the composition:

(3.4)

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=F\left(t,\omega,y,z,\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)\right).

A triplet of processes
(Y,Z,A)
is a solution to the
\theta
-BSDE if and only if:

(1)

The pair
(Y,Z)
solves the standard BSDE with driver
\tilde{F}
:

(3.5)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(2)

The process
A
is defined by the relation
A_{s}(\omega)=\mathcal{A}^{*}(s,\omega,Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))
for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.
(s,\omega)
.

The proof therefore reduces to verifying that
\tilde{F}
satisfies the canonical conditions for a BSDE driver, which will allow us to invoke the foundational result of Pardoux and Peng
[
Pardoux and Peng(1990)
]
.

Step 2: Verification of the Standard Driver Properties for
\tilde{F}
.

We meticulously verify that
\tilde{F}
is progressively measurable, Lipschitz continuous in
(y,z)
, and square-integrable at
(0,0)
.

(a)

(Progressive Measurability)

Let
(Y,Z)
be any pair of progressively measurable processes. We must show that the process
(s,\omega)\mapsto\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))
is progressively measurable. By Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-c), the map
(s,\omega)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s})
is progressively measurable. Let this process be
A^{*}_{s}
. Then
\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s})=F(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s},A^{*}_{s})
. By Hypothesis
3.2
(i),
F
is a Carathéodory function. The composition of progressively measurable processes under a Carathéodory function is progressively measurable. Thus,
\tilde{F}
satisfies the necessary measurability condition.

(b)

(Lipschitz Continuity in
(y,z)
)
This is the central analytical step. Let
(t,\omega)
be fixed. Let
(y_{1},z_{1})
and
(y_{2},z_{2})
be two arbitrary points in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
. We denote the corresponding unique maximizers by
a_{1}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})
and
a_{2}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})
. We aim to bound
\left\lvert\tilde{F}(y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rvert
.

Using the triangle inequality, we decompose the difference into two parts:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tilde{F}(y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert F(y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\lvert F(y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})\right\rvert}_{\text{Term I}}+\underbrace{\left\lvert F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})\right\rvert}_{\text{Term II}}.

We bound each term using the distinct clauses of Hypothesis
3.2
(ii).
For Term I, where the parameter
a_{1}^{*}
is fixed, we apply Hypothesis
3.2
(ii-a):

\left\lvert F(y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})\right\rvert\leq L_{F,yz}(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

For Term II, where the state
(y_{2},z_{2})
is fixed, we apply Hypothesis
3.2
(ii-b):

\left\lvert F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})\right\rvert\leq L_{F,a}\left\lVert a_{1}^{*}-a_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}.

The bound on Term II depends on the distance between the maximizers. We now use the crucial regularity of the maximizer map itself, from Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-b):

\left\lVert a_{1}^{*}-a_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{*}(y_{1},z_{1})-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq L_{a}(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

Substituting this into the bound for Term II gives:

\left\lvert F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})-F(y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})\right\rvert\leq L_{F,a}L_{a}(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

Combining the bounds for both terms yields:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tilde{F}(y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{F,yz}(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert)+L_{F,a}L_{a}(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert)

\displaystyle=(L_{F,yz}+L_{F,a}L_{a})(\left\lvert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

Thus, the driver
\tilde{F}
is globally Lipschitz continuous in
(y,z)
with Lipschitz constant
L_{\tilde{F}}\coloneqq L_{F,yz}+L_{F,a}L_{a}
, uniformly in
(t,\omega)
.

(c)

(Square-Integrability at
(0,0)
)

We need to show that the process
s\mapsto\tilde{F}(s,\omega,0,0)
belongs to
\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T])
. We have:

\left\lvert\tilde{F}(s,\omega,0,0)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(s,\omega,0,0,a)\right\rvert\leq\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lvert F(s,\omega,0,0,a)\right\rvert.

By Hypothesis
3.2
(i), the process
s\mapsto\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lvert F(s,\omega,0,0,a)\right\rvert
is an element of
\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T])
. It follows that
s\mapsto\tilde{F}(s,\omega,0,0)
is also in
\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T])
.

Step 3: Existence and Uniqueness of
(Y,Z)
.

Having rigorously established that the driver
\tilde{F}
satisfies all standard conditions, (a) progressive measurability, (b) global Lipschitz continuity in
(y,z)
, and (c) square-integrability at
(0,0)
, we can apply the foundational existence and uniqueness theorem of Pardoux and Peng
[
Pardoux and Peng(1990)
]
. For any terminal condition
\xi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})
, the BSDE given by (
3.5
) admits a unique solution pair
(Y,Z)\in\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])=\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
.

Step 4: Construction and Uniqueness of the Full Solution
(Y,Z,A)
.

The unique solution
(Y,Z)
from Step 3 allows us to define the parameter process
A
explicitly via the maximizer map:

A_{s}(\omega)\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(s,\omega,Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))\quad\text{for }P_{0}\otimes dt\text{-a.e. }(s,\omega).

The process
A
is progressively measurable as it is the composition of progressively measurable maps. To show that
A\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
, we use the fact that
\mathcal{U}
is a compact set. This implies
\mathcal{U}
is bounded, so there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{U}}<\infty
such that
\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}=C_{\mathcal{U}}
. Then, for all
(s,\omega)
,
\left\lVert A_{s}(\omega)\right\rVert_{F}\leq C_{\mathcal{U}}
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert A_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}ds\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}C_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}ds\right]=TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}<\infty.

Thus,
A\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
. By construction, the triplet
(Y,Z,A)
satisfies both the backward evolution (
3.1
) and the pointwise optimality condition (
3.2
), and is therefore a solution to the
\theta
-BSDE.

Finally, the uniqueness of the solution
(Y,Z,A)
follows directly from the uniqueness of the solution to the equivalent standard BSDE. If
(Y^{1},Z^{1},A^{1})
and
(Y^{2},Z^{2},A^{2})
were two distinct solutions to the
\theta
-BSDE, then both pairs
(Y^{1},Z^{1})
and
(Y^{2},Z^{2})
would be solutions to the same standard BSDE (
3.5
) with Lipschitz driver
\tilde{F}
. By the uniqueness result from
[
Pardoux and Peng(1990)
]
, we must have
Y^{1}_{t}=Y^{2}_{t}
and
Z^{1}_{t}=Z^{2}_{t}
for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
. This in turn implies
A^{1}_{t}=\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,Y^{1}_{t},Z^{1}_{t})=\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,Y^{2}_{t},Z^{2}_{t})=A^{2}_{t}
, where the equality follows from the uniqueness of the maximizer. Thus, the solution triplet is unique.
∎

3.4.
On the Scope and Verifiability of the Global Regularity Hypothesis

Hypothesis
3.2
is the analytical foundation of our existence and uniqueness theory. It is a strong assumption, as it requires global uniqueness and Lipschitz continuity of the solution to the embedded optimization problem. Its strength, however, lies in its nature: it is a condition on the
regularity of the solution to an optimization problem
, rather than a direct geometric constraint on the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
. This focus is what allows our framework to apply to certain non-convex settings. We now provide verifiable sufficient conditions.

Proposition 3.4

(Strict Concavity over a Convex Set implies Global Regularity)
.

Suppose the driver
F
and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
satisfy Hypothesis
3.2
(i)-(ii). Assume additionally that:

(i)

(Smoothness of the Driver)
For each
(t,\omega,y,z)
, the map
a\mapsto F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is twice continuously differentiable in
a
on an open neighborhood of
\mathcal{U}
.

(ii)

(Lipschitz Gradient)
The derivative map
(y,z)\mapsto\nabla_{a}F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is Lipschitz continuous in
(y,z)
, uniformly in
(t,\omega,a)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{U}
.

(iii)

(Uniform Strict Concavity in
a
)
There exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for all arguments
(t,\omega,y,z,a)
, the Hessian matrix of
F
with respect to
a
satisfies
\nabla_{aa}^{2}F(t,\omega,y,z,a)\preceq-\kappa I
.

(iv)

(Convexity of Uncertainty Set)
The set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}
is convex.

Then Hypothesis
3.2
(iii) is satisfied.

Proof.

The proof is a classical perturbation argument from variational analysis; see, e.g.,
[
Bonnans and Shapiro(2013)
]
. The combination of uniform strict concavity and a convex domain guarantees a unique maximizer for each state. The Lipschitz continuity of this maximizer map follows from an application of the implicit function theorem to the first-order optimality conditions, where the uniform strong concavity ensures the required non-singularity of the derivative. The details are standard and are omitted for brevity.
∎

3.5.
Pathwise Model Consistency for Non-Convex Sets

We now turn to a different, more subtle question. Instead of postulating global regularity, we ask: for which interesting classes of non-convex models can we guarantee that the optimality condition (
3.2
) is well-defined along a solution path? This property, which we call pathwise consistency, is fundamental for the model specification to be coherent, and it can hold even when Hypothesis
3.2
fails.

Definition 3.5

(Positive Reach,
[
Federer(1959)
]
)
.

A closed set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
has
positive reach
if there exists
\eta>0
such that any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with Euclidean distance
\mathrm{dist}(x,K)<\eta
has a unique nearest point in
K
, denoted
\proj_{K}(x)
.

Assumption 3.6

(Primitives for Pathwise Consistency)
.

The model is Markovian and satisfies:

(i)

(Projection Driver Structure)
The driver has the quadratic form:

F(t,x,y,z,a)=h(t,x,y,z)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(t,x,y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(ii)

(Geometric Regularity of
\mathcal{U}
)
The uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}
is a compact set with positive reach. This class includes all compact convex sets, as well as finite unions of disjoint compact convex sets and sets with a
C^{2}
-smooth boundary.

(iii)

(Smoothness and Non-Degeneracy)
We consider a full forward-backward system for
(X,Y,Z)
. All coefficients of the system are smooth with bounded derivatives, and the system satisfies a uniform parabolicity condition (e.g., Hörmander’s condition), ensuring that the law of the solution vector
(X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s})
admits a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure for any
s\in(0,T]
.

Theorem 3.7

(Pathwise Uniqueness of the Maximizer)
.

Suppose a solution
(Y,Z,A)
to the
\theta
-BSDE exists under Assumption
3.6
. Then the maximizer process
A_{s}
is uniquely defined for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-almost every
(s,\omega)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

The objective is to prove that under Assumption
3.6
, the maximizer process
A_{s}
defined by the pointwise optimality condition

(3.6)

A_{s}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(s,X_{s}(\omega),Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega),a)

is uniquely defined for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-almost every
(s,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
. The proof is structured in five steps.

Step 1: Reduction to a Geometric Projection Problem.

Under Assumption
3.6
(i), the driver has the specific quadratic form:

F(s,x,y,z,a)=h(s,x,y,z)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(s,x,y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

The pointwise maximization problem is therefore equivalent to the minimization problem:

(3.7)

A_{s}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmin}\;\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(s,X_{s}(\omega),Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

This is precisely the definition of the metric projection of the point
G_{s}(\omega)\coloneqq G(s,X_{s}(\omega),Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))
onto the compact set
\mathcal{U}
. Thus, we can write

A_{s}(\omega)=\proj_{\mathcal{U}}(G_{s}(\omega)).

The maximizer
A_{s}(\omega)
is non-unique if and only if the point
G_{s}(\omega)
has more than one nearest point in the set
\mathcal{U}
.

Step 2: Characterization of the Non-Uniqueness Set (The Medial Axis).

We now leverage a foundation result from geometric measure theory. The ambient space for the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
is the space of
d\times d
real symmetric matrices,
\mathbb{S}_{d}
, which is a Euclidean vector space of dimension
N=d(d+1)/2
. Let
\lambda_{N}
denote the Lebesgue measure on this space.

Assumption
3.6
(ii) states that
\mathcal{U}
is a compact set with positive reach. The
medial axis
of
\mathcal{U}
, denoted
\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
, is defined as the set of points in the ambient space
\mathbb{S}_{d}
that have at least two distinct nearest points in
\mathcal{U}
. That is,

\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\coloneqq\left\{p\in\mathbb{S}_{d}\mid\exists a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{U},a_{1}\neq a_{2},\text{ s.t. }\left\lVert p-a_{1}\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert p-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}=\inf_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lVert p-a\right\rVert_{F}\right\}.

By definition, the projection
\proj_{\mathcal{U}}(p)
is non-unique if and only if
p\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
. A fundamental result due to Federer
[
Federer(1959)
, Theorem 4.8(1)]
states that if a closed set
K
has positive reach, its medial axis is a closed set of Lebesgue measure zero. Since we assume
\mathcal{U}
has positive reach, we have:

(3.8)

\lambda_{N}(\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U}))=0.

Thus, the maximizer
A_{s}(\omega)
is non-unique if and only if the random variable
G_{s}(\omega)
falls into the Lebesgue-null set
\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
.

Step 3: Absolute Continuity of the Law of the Driving Process.

This step is the probabilistic core of the proof and relies on the Malliavin calculus. We must show that the law of the random variable
G_{s}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{N}
.

Let
V_{s}\coloneqq(X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s})
be the solution vector of the full forward-backward system. Assumption
3.6
(iii) posits that the coefficients of this system are smooth and satisfy a uniform parabolicity (or Hörmander-type) condition. This is a standard non-degeneracy assumption in stochastic analysis. A key result of the Malliavin calculus is that such a non-degeneracy condition on the generator of the process
V_{s}
implies that for any time
s\in(0,T]
, the law of the random vector
V_{s}
admits a smooth density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on its state space
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
(see, e.g.,
[
Nualart(2006)
, Theorem 2.3.1]
).

This means that for any
s\in(0,T]
, the law of
V_{s}
, denoted
\mu_{V_{s}}
, is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
.

Now, consider the process
G_{s}(\omega)=G(s,V_{s}(\omega))
. The function
G:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\to\mathbb{S}_{d}
is assumed to be smooth. The law of the random variable
G_{s}
is the pushforward measure of the law of
V_{s}
under the map
G(s,\cdot)
. Since the law of
V_{s}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and the map
G(s,\cdot)
is smooth (and thus Borel measurable), the resulting pushforward measure, the law of
G_{s}
, is also absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{N}
on the target space
\mathbb{S}_{d}
.

Step 4: The Pathwise Uniqueness Argument for a Fixed Time.

We can now combine the results from the previous steps. For any fixed time
s\in(0,T]
, we have established that:

(1)

The maximizer
A_{s}(\omega)
is non-unique if and only if
G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
.

(2)

The set
\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
is a Lebesgue-null set in
\mathbb{S}_{d}
.

(3)

The law of the random variable
G_{s}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}_{d}
.

The probability that a random variable with an absolutely continuous law takes a value in a set of measure zero is zero. Therefore, for any fixed
s\in(0,T]
:

(3.9)

P_{0}\left(\text{The maximizer }A_{s}(\omega)\text{ is not unique}\right)=P_{0}\left(G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\right)=0.

This establishes that for any given time
s>0
, the maximizer is unique for
P_{0}
-almost every
\omega
.

Step 5: Extension to the Product Measure Space via Fubini’s Theorem.

The final step is to extend the almost-sure uniqueness from a single time instant to the product space
[0,T]\times\Omega
with the measure
dt\otimes P_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of bad points in the product space where uniqueness fails:

\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\left\{(s,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\mid A_{s}(\omega)\text{ is not unique}\right\}=\left\{(s,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\mid G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\right\}.

The set
\mathcal{B}
is progressively measurable because
G
is a continuous function of progressively measurable processes, and
\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})
is a closed set. We wish to compute the product measure of
\mathcal{B}
, which is given by
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(s,\omega)ds]
. By the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, we can interchange the order of integration:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(s,\omega)ds\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\}}\right]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}\left(G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\right)ds.

From Step 4, the integrand
P_{0}(G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U}))
is equal to 0 for all
s\in(0,T]
. The single point
s=0
does not affect the value of the integral. Therefore,

\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}\left(G_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{med}(\mathcal{U})\right)ds=\int_{0}^{T}0\,ds=0.

This proves that the set
\mathcal{B}
has measure zero under the product measure
dt\otimes P_{0}
. Consequently, for
dt\otimes P_{0}
-almost every
(s,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, the projection point
G_{s}(\omega)
is not in the medial axis of
\mathcal{U}
, and thus the maximizer process
A_{s}(\omega)
is uniquely defined. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

3.6.
Discussion: The Logical Gap between Global and Pathwise Regularity

It is of paramount importance to clarify the relationship between Hypothesis
3.2
and the pathwise consistency established in Theorem
3.7
.

Remark 3.8

(Pathwise Regularity Does Not Imply Global Regularity)
.

Theorem
3.7
is a powerful result guaranteeing the internal consistency of a broad class of non-convex models. However, it does not imply that Hypothesis
3.2
holds for these models, and therefore it cannot be used to prove existence and uniqueness via Theorem
3.3
. The two results address fundamentally different mathematical questions.

The reason for this logical gap lies in the nature of the proof of our main existence result, Theorem
3.3
. The proof is based on the Banach fixed-point theorem applied to a map
\Psi:\mathcal{B}^{2}\to\mathcal{B}^{2}
. This map takes an arbitrary pair of processes
(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}^{2}
as input. For the map
\Psi
to be well-defined and a contraction, the maximizer map
\mathcal{A}^{*}
must be well-defined (unique) and Lipschitz continuous for
any
input
(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})
, as these processes explore the entire function space. Hypothesis
3.2
is precisely the strong, deterministic, global property required for this.

In contrast, the proof of Theorem
3.7
is a deep, probabilistic statement about the law of the
actual solution process

(Y,Z,A)
, which is the fixed point of
\Psi
. The Malliavin calculus argument shows that this specific solution process
(Y,Z)
will almost surely conspire to avoid the states
(y,z)
where the maximizer is non-unique or ill-behaved. We have no such guarantee for an arbitrary input process
(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})
from the space
\mathcal{B}^{2}
. The law of an arbitrary
(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})
is unknown, and nothing prevents it from spending significant time on the medial axis of
\mathcal{U}
, which would render the map
\Psi
ill-defined.

Bridging this gap, that is, proving an existence theorem for the geometrically regular but globally non-unique class of models from Assumption
3.6
, is a formidable challenge. It would likely require tools beyond the classical fixed-point framework, such as those from the theory of stochastic inclusions. This remains a primary and motivating direction for future research.

4.
The
\theta
-Expectation and its Properties

Having established the well-posedness of the
\theta
-BSDE under Hypothesis
3.2
, we are now in a position to define the conditional
\theta
-expectation functional generated by this construction. The primary goal of this section is to prove that this functional, born from a concrete stochastic differential system, satisfies the abstract axioms of a
\theta
-expectation space laid out in Section
2
. This result bridges our constructive framework with our axiomatic one, proving that the class of
\theta
-expectations is non-empty and providing a canonical example.

4.1.
The Generated
\theta
-Expectation and its Driver

With the well-posedness of the
\theta
-BSDE established, we first define the valuation operator that it generates, and then the driver which governs its behavior.

Definition 4.1

(The Generated
\theta
-Expectation)
.

Let the driver
F
and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
satisfy Hypothesis
3.2
. We define the
conditional
\theta
-expectation generated by
(F,\mathcal{U})
as the operator
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}:L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t},P_{0})
given by:

\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi]\coloneqq Y_{t},

where
(Y,Z,A)
is the unique solution in
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
to the
\theta
-BSDE with data
(\xi,F,\mathcal{U})
, as guaranteed by Theorem
3.3
.

As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, the solution
(Y,Z,A)
is equivalent to a standard BSDE governed by an driver. We formalize its definition here, as its properties are the key to proving the properties of the operator
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}
.

Definition 4.2

(Driver)
.

Under Hypothesis
3.2
, we define the
driver

\tilde{F}:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,y,z,a).

We now impose a standard monotonicity condition on the primitive driver, which will be inherited by the driver and is essential for the comparison principle.

Assumption 4.3

(Driver Monotonicity)
.

The primitive driver
F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is non-decreasing in its third argument,
y
, for all fixed
(t,\omega,z,a)
.

Lemma 4.4

(Properties of the Driver)
.

Under Hypothesis
3.2
, the driver
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)
is well-defined and progressively measurable in
(t,\omega)
for fixed
(y,z)
, and continuous in
(y,z)
for fixed
(t,\omega)
. Furthermore, for
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
:

(1)

(Monotonicity)
If Assumption
4.3
holds (i.e.,
F
is non-decreasing in
y
), then the map
y\mapsto\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)
is non-decreasing.

(2)

(Lipschitz Continuity)
The map
(y,z)\mapsto\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)
is globally Lipschitz continuous. Its Lipschitz constant
L_{\tilde{F}}
is given by:

L_{\tilde{F}}=L_{F,yz}+L_{F,a}L_{a},

where
L_{F,yz}
,
L_{F,a}
, and
L_{a}
are the Lipschitz constants from Hypothesis
3.2
.

Proof.

The properties of the driver
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
are inherited from the properties of the primitive driver
F
and the maximizer map
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

Progressive Measurability and Continuity:

By Hypothesis
3.2
(iii), the maximum is uniquely attained at
\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
. Thus, we can write
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=F(t,\omega,y,z,\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z))
.
Hypothesis
3.2
(i) and (iii-c) state that for fixed
(y,z)
, both
F(\cdot,\cdot,y,z,a)
and
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot,y,z)
are progressively measurable. The composition of progressively measurable functions is progressively measurable.
Similarly, for fixed
(t,\omega)
, both
F(t,\omega,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,\cdot,\cdot)
are continuous functions. The composition of continuous functions is continuous, so
(y,z)\mapsto\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)
is continuous.

Proof of (1) Monotonicity:

Let
(t,\omega,z)
be fixed. Let
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
y_{1}\geq y_{2}
. We must show that
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z)\geq\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z)
. For notational simplicity, we suppress the
(t,\omega,z)
arguments where clear.
Let
a_{1}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z)
and
a_{2}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z)
be the respective unique maximizers, as guaranteed by Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-a).

By the definition of the driver, we have:

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z)=F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{1}^{*}).

By the optimality of
a_{1}^{*}
for the state
(y_{1},z)
, its value must be greater than or equal to the value at any other point in
\mathcal{U}
, including
a_{2}^{*}
:

(4.1)

F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{1}^{*})\geq F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{2}^{*}).

Now, we use Assumption
4.3
. Since
y_{1}\geq y_{2}
, the primitive driver
F
is non-decreasing in its
y
argument, which implies:

(4.2)

F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{2}^{*})\geq F(t,\omega,y_{2},z,a_{2}^{*}).

Finally, by definition of the driver for the state
(y_{2},z)
, we have:

(4.3)

F(t,\omega,y_{2},z,a_{2}^{*})=\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z).

Chaining these three relations together yields the desired result:

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z)=F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{1}^{*})\geq F(t,\omega,y_{1},z,a_{2}^{*})\geq F(t,\omega,y_{2},z,a_{2}^{*})=\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z).

This completes the proof of monotonicity.

Proof of (2) Lipschitz Continuity:

This derivation is the core of the proof of Theorem
3.3
and is reproduced here in full detail to establish the precise Lipschitz constant. Let
(t,\omega)
be fixed. Let
(y_{1},z_{1})
and
(y_{2},z_{2})
be two arbitrary points in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
. We again denote the unique maximizers by
a_{1}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})
and
a_{2}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})
.

We aim to bound the absolute difference
|\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})|
. By definition, this is:

|\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})|=|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})|.

We use the triangle inequality to split the difference into two terms, isolating the change in the state
(y,z)
from the change in the maximizer
a
:

\displaystyle|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})|\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})|}_{\text{Term I}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{|F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})|}_{\text{Term II}}.

We now bound each term separately using the clauses of Hypothesis
3.2
.

For Term I, the parameter
a_{1}^{*}
is held constant. We apply the uniform Lipschitz condition on
F
with respect to
(y,z)
from Hypothesis
3.2
(ii-a):

|F(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})|\leq L_{F,yz}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

For Term II, the state
(y_{2},z_{2})
is held constant. We apply the uniform Lipschitz condition on
F
with respect to
a
from Hypothesis
3.2
(ii-b):

|F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{1}^{*})-F(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2},a_{2}^{*})|\leq L_{F,a}\left\lVert a_{1}^{*}-a_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}.

Combining these two bounds, we have:

|\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})|\leq L_{F,yz}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert)+L_{F,a}\left\lVert a_{1}^{*}-a_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}.

The second term still depends on the states
(y_{1},z_{1})
and
(y_{2},z_{2})
through their respective maximizers. This is where we invoke the crucial regularity of the maximizer map. Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-b) states that
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is globally Lipschitz continuous in
(y,z)
:

\left\lVert a_{1}^{*}-a_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq L_{a}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

Substituting this inequality into our bound for Term II gives:

\displaystyle|\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{1},z_{1})-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y_{2},z_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{F,yz}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert)+L_{F,a}\left(L_{a}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert)\right)

\displaystyle=(L_{F,yz}+L_{F,a}L_{a})(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

This inequality holds for any
(y_{1},z_{1})
and
(y_{2},z_{2})
, uniformly in
(t,\omega)
. This establishes that the driver
\tilde{F}
is globally Lipschitz continuous in
(y,z)
with the Lipschitz constant
L_{\tilde{F}}\coloneqq L_{F,yz}+L_{F,a}L_{a}
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.2.
Consistency with the Axiomatic Framework

We now state and prove the main result of this section, demonstrating that our construction yields a valid
\theta
-expectation space.

Theorem 4.5

(The Generated Operator is a
\theta
-Expectation)
.

Let the generator
(F,\mathcal{U})
satisfy Hypothesis
3.2
. The family of operators
(\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
from Definition
4.1
has the following properties:

(1)

(Monotonicity)
If Assumption
4.3
holds,
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}
satisfies Axiom (A1).

(2)

(Translation-Invariance)
The functional
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}
satisfies Axiom (A2) if and only if the primitive driver
F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
does not depend on its argument
y
.

(3)

(Dynamic Consistency)
The functional
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}
satisfies Axiom (A3).

Thus, under these conditions,
(\Omega,L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T}),(\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}))
constitutes a
\theta
-expectation space.

Proof.

By definition, the process
Y_{t}=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi]
is the first component of the unique solution to the
\theta
-BSDE, which is equivalent to being the solution of the standard BSDE with the driver
\tilde{F}
:

(4.4)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}dB_{s}.

1. Proof of (A1) Monotonicity.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
with
\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2}
a.s. Let
(Y^{1},Z^{1})
and
(Y^{2},Z^{2})
be the corresponding unique solutions to the BSDE (
4.4
). By Lemma
4.4
, the driver
\tilde{F}
is Lipschitz in
(y,z)
and, under Assumption
4.3
, is non-decreasing in
y
. These are the precise conditions required for the standard comparison principle for BSDEs (see, e.g.,
[
Karoui et al.(1997)Karoui, Peng, and Quenez
, Theorem 5.2]
). Therefore, since
\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2}
, we conclude that
Y^{1}_{t}\geq Y^{2}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
P_{0}
-a.s. By definition, this means
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi_{1}]\geq\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi_{2}]
.

2. Proof of (A2) Translation-Invariance.

A careful reading of the axiom reveals a subtle point. We prove that Axiom (A2) holds if and only if the primitive driver
F
is independent of
y
.
(
\Leftarrow
) Assume
F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
does not depend on
y
. Then the driver
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,z,a)
is also independent of
y
, and we write it as
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,z)
. Let
(Y,Z)
be the solution to (
4.4
) for a terminal condition
\xi
. Let
m\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{t})
be fixed. Define a candidate solution
(\hat{Y},\hat{Z})
on the interval
[t,T]
by setting
\hat{Y}_{s}=Y_{s}+m
and
\hat{Z}_{s}=Z_{s}
for
s\in[t,T]
.
The terminal condition is
\hat{Y}_{T}=Y_{T}+m=\xi+m
. The dynamics are

d\hat{Y}_{s}=dY_{s}=-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Z_{s})ds+Z_{s}dB_{s}=-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,\hat{Z}_{s})ds+\hat{Z}_{s}dB_{s}.

The pair
(\hat{Y},\hat{Z})
solves the BSDE on
[t,T]
with terminal condition
\xi+m
and driver
\tilde{F}(s,\omega,z)
. By uniqueness of solutions to standard BSDEs, this must be the solution. Thus,
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi+m]=\hat{Y}_{t}=Y_{t}+m=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi]+m
.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume Axiom (A2) holds. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be a deterministic constant. Let
(Y,Z)
be the solution for
\xi
, and let
(Y^{m},Z^{m})
be the solution for
\xi+m
. Axiom (A2) implies that
Y^{m}_{s}=Y_{s}+m
for
s\in[0,T]
.
Define a new process pair
(\check{Y},\check{Z})
by
\check{Y}_{s}=Y^{m}_{s}-m
and
\check{Z}_{s}=Z^{m}_{s}
. The process
\check{Y}
has terminal value
\check{Y}_{T}=\xi
. Its dynamics are:

d\check{Y}_{s}=dY^{m}_{s}=-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y^{m}_{s},Z^{m}_{s})ds+Z^{m}_{s}dB_{s}=-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,\check{Y}_{s}+m,\check{Z}_{s})ds+\check{Z}_{s}dB_{s}.

Thus,
(\check{Y},\check{Z})
solves a BSDE with terminal condition
\xi
and driver
g(s,\omega,y,z):=\tilde{F}(s,\omega,y+m,z)
. By uniqueness of the solution to the BSDE for
\xi
, we must have
(Y,Z)=(\check{Y},\check{Z})
. This implies their drivers must be equal
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e.:

\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s},Z_{s})=\tilde{F}(s,\omega,Y_{s}+m,Z_{s}).

This equality must hold for any
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
and any constant
m\in\mathbb{R}
. A key result in BSDE theory (e.g.,
[
Karoui et al.(1997)Karoui, Peng, and Quenez
, Prop. 3.2]
) establishes that for a non-degenerate driver, the set of reachable values
\{(Y_{s}(\omega),Z_{s}(\omega))\mid\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})\}
is large enough (e.g., dense in a set of positive measure) that this equality implies
\tilde{F}(s,\omega,y,z)=\tilde{F}(s,\omega,y+m,z)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\tilde{F}
is continuous in
y
, it must be independent of
y
.
Finally, if
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=\max_{a}F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
is independent of
y
, this implies that the value of the maximizer
a^{*}=\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,y,z)
must also be independent of
y
. If not, a small change in
y
would change
a^{*}
, which (unless
\nabla_{a}F=0
) would change the maximal value, a contradiction. So
a^{*}
is independent of
y
. This implies
F(t,\omega,y,z,a^{*})=F(t,\omega,y^{\prime},z,a^{*})
for all
y,y^{\prime}
. If
F
depended on
y
, we could choose a state
(z,a^{*})
where this dependence is non-trivial, leading to a contradiction. Hence,
F
must be independent of
y
.

3. Proof of (A3) Dynamic Consistency.

This property is a direct consequence of the uniqueness of the solution to the
\theta
-BSDE over any time interval. Let
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
and let
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
. Let
(Y,Z,A)
be the unique solution on
[0,T]
corresponding to
\xi
.
By definition,
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi]=Y_{t}
and
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[\xi]=Y_{s}
. We must show that
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[\xi]]=Y_{t}
.
Consider the
\theta
-BSDE on the time interval
[0,s]
with terminal condition
Y_{s}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{s})
. We observe that the triplet of processes
(Y_{u},Z_{u},A_{u})
restricted to the time interval
u\in[0,s]
is a solution to this problem.
Indeed, (i) the backward evolution holds by definition of the original BSDE on
[0,T]
:

Y_{u}=Y_{s}+\int_{u}^{s}F(v,\omega,Y_{v},Z_{v},A_{v})dv-\int_{u}^{s}Z_{v}dB_{v},\quad\forall u\in[0,s],

and (ii) the optimality condition
A_{u}\in\argmax_{a}F(u,\omega,Y_{u},Z_{u},a)
holds for a.e.
u\in[0,s]
.
By Theorem
3.3
, the solution to the
\theta
-BSDE on
[0,s]
with terminal data
Y_{s}
is unique. Therefore,
(Y_{u})_{u\in[0,s]}
must be this unique solution. By Definition
4.1
applied to this problem on
[0,s]
, the value at time
t
is
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[Y_{s}]=Y_{t}
.
Substituting the original definitions, we have
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[\xi]]=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\xi]
, which is Axiom (A3).
∎

Remark 4.6

(On the Structure of Translation-Invariant
\theta
-Expectations)
.

The characterization in Theorem
4.5
(2) reveals a sharp structural divide. The axiom of translation-invariance, which is fundamental for applications in pricing and risk management, holds if and only if the primitive driver
F
is independent of the value process
Y
.

This is a defining feature of the theory. It distinguishes our framework from theories of recursive utility (e.g.,
[
Duffie and Epstein(1992)
]
), where the dependence of the driver on
y
is essential for modeling intertemporal substitution. Instead, the condition that the driver depends only on
(t,\omega,z,a)
places our theory in the lineage of dynamic risk measures and pricing under ambiguity. Our framework provides a consistent, identifiable calculus for this important class of problems, with the geometry of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
influencing the valuation through its effect on the
(z,a)
-dependence of
F
.

5.
\theta
-Martingales and the Canonical Process

With the
\theta
-expectation space
(\Omega,L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T}),(\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}))
now constructed and its fundamental properties established, we can develop the core concepts of the associated calculus. In classical stochastic calculus, the martingale is a central object, representing a fair game under the reference measure. Here, we define its analogue in our framework, the
\theta
-martingale
, which represents a process whose future value, when evaluated by the
\theta
-expectation, is its current value. We then use this concept to define a non-trivial notion of quadratic variation for the underlying canonical process, which reveals the endogenous and nonlinear nature of time in our calculus.

5.1.
\theta
-Martingales

The definition of a
\theta
-martingale is a natural extension of the classical tower property of conditional expectation.

Definition 5.1

(
\theta
-Martingale)
.

An
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
in
\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
is a
\theta
-martingale
with respect to the generated expectation
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}
if, for all
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
,

M_{t}=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{s}].

This abstract definition leads directly to a stochastic differential equation representation for
\theta
-martingales, which follows from the constructive definition of our expectation operator via
\theta
-BSDEs. This representation is the practical tool for working with
\theta
-martingales.

Proposition 5.2

(Stochastic Representation of
\theta
-Martingales)
.

Assume Hypothesis
3.2
holds. An adapted process
M\in\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
is a
\theta
-martingale if and only if there exists a pair of processes
(Z,A)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
such that the triplet
(M,Z,A)
is a solution to the
\theta
-BSDE system (
Definition

3.1
) with terminal condition
\xi=M_{T}
.
Equivalently, in differential form, a process
M
is a
\theta
-martingale if and only if there exist processes
Z\in\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
and
A\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{S}_{d})
such that:

(i)

The process
M
satisfies the SDE:

(5.1)

dM_{t}=-F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},A_{t})dt+Z_{t}dB_{t},

(ii)

The process
A
satisfies the pointwise optimality condition for a.e.
(t,\omega)
:

A_{t}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,M_{t}(\omega),Z_{t}(\omega),a).

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
M
is a
\theta
-martingale. By Definition
5.1
, we have
M_{t}=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{s}]
for all
t\leq s
. Setting
s=T
, we get
M_{t}=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{T}]
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
By Definition
4.1
, the process
Y_{t}\coloneqq\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{T}]
is the first component of the unique solution
(Y,Z,A)
to the
\theta
-BSDE with terminal condition
\xi=M_{T}
.
Therefore, we must have
M_{t}=Y_{t}
for all
t
,
P_{0}
-a.s. This means the triplet
(M,Z,A)
is itself the unique solution. Consequently,
(M,Z,A)
must satisfy the two conditions of a
\theta
-BSDE solution: the backward evolution equation, which in differential form is precisely (
5.1
), and the pointwise optimality condition.

(
\Leftarrow
) Assume
M
has a representation as the first component of a triplet
(M,Z,A)
solving the
\theta
-BSDE with terminal condition
\xi=M_{T}
. By Definition
4.1
, the conditional
\theta
-expectation of
M_{T}
at any time
s\in[0,T]
is given by the first component of the solution, which is
M_{s}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[M_{T}]=M_{s}
.
Now, let
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
. We must show that
M_{t}=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{s}]
. Using the definition of
M_{s}
and the dynamic consistency property of the
\theta
-expectation (Axiom (A3), proven in Theorem
4.5
), we have:

\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{s}]=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[M_{T}]].

By Axiom (A3),
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{s}[M_{T}]]=\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{T}]
. Finally, using the definition of
M_{t}
, we have
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{T}]=M_{t}
.
Combining these equalities gives
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[M_{s}]=M_{t}
. This is exactly the condition for
M
to be a
\theta
-martingale.
∎

5.2.
The Canonical Process and its
\theta
-Quadratic Variation

We now investigate whether processes related to the underlying Brownian motion are
\theta
-martingales. The framework as defined is for one-dimensional value processes
Y
, so we consider scalar projections of the
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
B
.

Proposition 5.3

(Linear Functions of Brownian Motion)
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed non-zero vector. The process
M_{t}\coloneqq c\cdot B_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}B_{t}^{i}
is a
\theta
-martingale if and only if the driver
F
satisfies the restrictive condition

\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,c\cdot B_{t},c^{T},a)=0,\quad P_{0}\otimes dt\text{-a.e.}

In particular, the components of the canonical process
B_{t}
are not generally
\theta
-martingales.

Proof.

The process
M_{t}=c\cdot B_{t}
is a continuous local martingale under the measure
P_{0}
, with dynamics given by
dM_{t}=c^{T}dB_{t}
. The drift of this process is zero.
For
M_{t}
to be a
\theta
-martingale, by Proposition
5.2
, its dynamics must be given by
dM_{t}=-F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},A_{t})dt+Z_{t}dB_{t}
for some suitable processes
Z
and
A
.
By the uniqueness of the canonical Doob-Meyer decomposition of the semimartingale
M_{t}
, we can uniquely identify its diffusion and drift parts.
The diffusion part is
Z_{t}dB_{t}=c^{T}dB_{t}
, which implies
Z_{t}=c^{T}
for a.e.
t
.
The drift part must be zero. Therefore, we must have:

-F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},A_{t})=-F(t,\omega,c\cdot B_{t},c^{T},A_{t})=0,

where
A_{t}
is the process satisfying the optimality condition:

A_{t}(\omega)\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,c\cdot B_{t}(\omega),c^{T},a).

This means that the value of the driver at the maximum must be zero. In other words, the driver evaluated at the state
(y,z)=(c\cdot B_{t},c^{T})
must be zero:

\tilde{F}(t,\omega,c\cdot B_{t},c^{T})=\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,c\cdot B_{t},c^{T},a)=0.

This is a highly restrictive, path-dependent condition on the primitives
(F,\mathcal{U})
that will not hold in general, unless
F
is identically zero.
∎

While linear functions of
B_{t}
are not
\theta
-martingales, we can use the concept to define the correct analogue of the quadratic variation process. In classical theory,
\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-d\cdot t
is a martingale. Here, we define the
\theta
-quadratic variation as the unique predictable process that must be subtracted from
\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}
to render the result a
\theta
-martingale.

Definition 5.4

(
\theta
-Quadratic Variation)
.

The
\theta
-quadratic variation
of the canonical process
B
is the unique continuous,
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process of finite variation, denoted
\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
, with
\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{0}=0
, such that the process
M_{t}\coloneqq\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
is a
\theta
-martingale.

Proposition 5.5

(Dynamics of the
\theta
-Quadratic Variation)
.

The
\theta
-quadratic variation process
\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Its differential is given by:

(5.2)

d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}=\left(d+\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F\left(t,\omega,\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t},2B_{t}^{T},a\right)\right)dt.

Proof.

Let
M_{t}=\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
. We require this process to be a
\theta
-martingale. We first compute the dynamics of
M_{t}
using Itô’s formula on the function
f(b)=\left\lVert b\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(b^{i})^{2}
applied to the process
B_{t}
. This gives

d(\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(2B_{t}^{i}dB_{t}^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\cdot d\langle B^{i}\rangle_{t}\right)=2B_{t}^{T}dB_{t}+d\cdot dt.

Therefore, the dynamics of
M_{t}
are given by:

dM_{t}=(d\cdot dt-d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t})+2B_{t}^{T}dB_{t}.

For
M_{t}
to be a
\theta
-martingale, it must satisfy the SDE representation from Proposition
5.2
. By comparing the martingale parts of the two expressions for
dM_{t}
, we uniquely identify its volatility process as
Z_{t}=2B_{t}^{T}
.
Next, we equate the finite variation parts (the drifts). For
M_{t}
to be a
\theta
-martingale, its drift must satisfy:

d\cdot dt-d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}=-F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},A_{t})dt=-\left(\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},a)\right)dt.

Substituting
Z_{t}=2B_{t}^{T}
and rearranging for
d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
yields:

d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}=d\cdot dt+\left(\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,M_{t},2B_{t}^{T},a)\right)dt.

Finally, substituting the definition
M_{t}=\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
into the driver gives the stated result (
5.2
). The existence and uniqueness of the process
\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
is guaranteed by the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the resulting path-dependent ordinary differential equation for its value, which follows from the Lipschitz continuity of the driver
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)
in the variable
y
(Lemma
4.4
), which controls the term
\left\lVert B_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
.
∎

Remark 5.6

(The Endogenous Nature of Stochastic Time)
.

Equation (
5.2
) is a crucial insight of our framework. It demonstrates that the quadratic variation under structured non-convex ambiguity is no longer a deterministic clock proportional to Lebesgue measure (i.e.,
d\cdot t
). Instead, its evolution is governed by a nonlinear, path-dependent ordinary differential equation. The rate of passage of time in this calculus, represented by the density of
\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}
with respect to Lebesgue measure, depends on the history of the Brownian path and on the solution to the endogenous optimization problem. This state-dependence of the quadratic variation is a fundamental feature of a calculus that is sensitive to non-convex geometry. In the trivial case where
F\equiv 0
, we recover the classical result
d\langle B\rangle^{\theta}_{t}=d\cdot dt
.

6.
\theta
-Calculus

The establishment of a
\theta
-expectation and the corresponding class of
\theta
-martingales naturally leads to the development of a new stochastic calculus. In the classical theory, the Itô calculus is built upon the martingale property of the Brownian motion and its quadratic variation. We parallel this development by introducing a canonical process, the
\theta
-Brownian motion
, which will serve as the fundamental integrator in our new calculus. We then define the associated stochastic integral and derive the corresponding Itô formula. This formula reveals the profound structure of our framework: the entire effect of the non-convex ambiguity, encapsulated by the driver
F
, is absorbed into the dynamics of the integrator, leaving the differential rule with a familiar, classical form.

For clarity of exposition, we shall present the one-dimensional case (
d=1
). The extension to multiple dimensions is straightforward. We assume throughout this section that Hypothesis
3.2
holds.

6.1.
The
\theta
-Brownian Motion and the
\theta
-Itô Integral

The cornerstone of classical stochastic calculus is the Brownian motion, uniquely characterized by Lévy’s theorem as a continuous local martingale with quadratic variation
\langle B\rangle_{t}=t
. We seek an analogous process for our framework: a process that is a
\theta
-martingale and possesses the simplest possible quadratic variation. This leads us to define the
\theta
-Brownian motion constructively via a stochastic differential equation.

Definition 6.1

(
\theta
-Brownian Motion)
.

Under Hypothesis
3.2
, the one-dimensional
\theta
-Brownian motion

B^{\theta}=(B^{\theta}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the unique strong solution to the stochastic differential equation:

(6.1)

dB^{\theta}_{t}=-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,B^{\theta}_{t},1)dt+dB_{t},\quad B^{\theta}_{0}=0,

where
\tilde{F}(t,\omega,y,z)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,\omega,y,z,a)
.

Remark 6.2
.

The existence and uniqueness of the process
B^{\theta}
as a continuous, adapted process in
\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
is guaranteed by standard SDE theory. The drift term
g(t,\omega,x)\coloneqq-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,x,1)
is globally Lipschitz in its spatial argument
x
, a direct consequence of the Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{F}
established in Lemma
4.4
.

This constructively defined process has the two key properties required of a canonical integrator.

Proposition 6.3

(Fundamental Properties of the
\theta
-Brownian Motion)
.

The
\theta
-Brownian motion
B^{\theta}
defined by SDE (
6.1
) has the following properties:

(i)

It is a
\theta
-martingale with respect to the generated expectation
\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}
.

(ii)

Its quadratic variation process is
\langle B^{\theta}\rangle_{t}=t
.

Proof.

(i)
\theta
-Martingale Property:

By Proposition
5.2
, a process
M
is a
\theta
-martingale if it is the first component of a solution to the
\theta
-BSDE system with terminal condition
M_{T}
. We show that
B^{\theta}
satisfies this condition.
Rewriting the SDE (
6.1
) in integral form from
t
to
T
gives:

B^{\theta}_{T}-B^{\theta}_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds+\int_{t}^{T}dB_{s}.

Rearranging this into the form of a backward stochastic differential equation yields:

B^{\theta}_{t}=B^{\theta}_{T}+\int_{t}^{T}\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds-\int_{t}^{T}1\cdot dB_{s}.

Let us define the processes
M_{t}\coloneqq B^{\theta}_{t}
,
Z_{t}\coloneqq 1
, and
A_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t})
, where
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the unique maximizer map from Hypothesis
3.2
. By construction,
\tilde{F}(s,\omega,M_{s},Z_{s})=F(s,\omega,M_{s},Z_{s},A_{s})
. The triplet
(M,Z,A)
therefore satisfies:

\displaystyle M_{t}

\displaystyle=M_{T}+\int_{t}^{T}F(s,\omega,M_{s},Z_{s},A_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}dB_{s},

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}}{\argmax}\;F(t,\omega,M_{t},Z_{t},a).

This is precisely the definition of a solution to the
\theta
-BSDE with terminal condition
M_{T}
. Therefore, by Proposition
5.2
, the process
M=B^{\theta}
is a
\theta
-martingale.

(ii) Quadratic Variation:

The quadratic variation of the semimartingale
B^{\theta}
is computed directly from its SDE decomposition (
6.1
). The process has a finite variation part,
\int_{0}^{t}-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds
, and a martingale part,
\int_{0}^{t}dB_{s}=B_{t}
. The quadratic variation of a semimartingale is equal to the quadratic variation of its continuous martingale part. Therefore,

\langle B^{\theta}\rangle_{t}=\left\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds+B\right\rangle_{t}=\langle B\rangle_{t}=t.

This completes the proof.
∎

With the canonical integrator established, we can define the corresponding stochastic integral.

Definition 6.4

(
\theta
-Itô Integral)
.

Let
H=(H_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a process in
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
. The
\theta
-Itô integral
of
H
with respect to the
\theta
-Brownian motion
B^{\theta}
is defined via the decomposition of
B^{\theta}
:

(6.2)

\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}dB^{\theta}_{s}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}\left(-\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds+dB_{s}\right)=-\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)ds+\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}dB_{s}.

6.2.
The
\theta
-Itô Formula

The fundamental tool of the new calculus is its Itô formula for change of variables. The following theorem reveals that the formula retains the classical structure when expressed using the
\theta
-Brownian motion as the integrator.

Theorem 6.5

(
\theta
-Itô Formula)
.

Let
X_{t}
be a
\theta
-Itô process with dynamics

dX_{t}=\mu^{\theta}_{t}dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}dB^{\theta}_{t},

where
\mu^{\theta}
and
\sigma^{\theta}
are progressively measurable processes satisfying the requisite integrability conditions. Let
f\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
. Then the process
Y_{t}=f(t,X_{t})
is also a
\theta
-Itô process, and its dynamics are given by:

(6.3)

dY_{t}=\left(\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})+\mu^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})dB^{\theta}_{t}.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by a direct application of the classical Itô formula after expressing all dynamics in terms of the standard Brownian motion
B_{t}
.

Step 1: Express the dynamics of
X_{t}
in terms of
dB_{t}
.

Let
\tilde{F}_{s}
be shorthand for
\tilde{F}(s,\omega,B^{\theta}_{s},1)
. We substitute the definition of
dB^{\theta}_{s}
into the dynamics of
X_{t}
:

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu^{\theta}_{t}dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}dB^{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu^{\theta}_{t}dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\left(-\tilde{F}_{t}dt+dB_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mu^{\theta}_{t}-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t}\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}dB_{t}.

This shows that
X_{t}
is a classical Itô process with respect to the filtration
\mathbb{F}
, with drift
\mu_{t}\coloneqq\mu^{\theta}_{t}-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t}
and volatility
\sigma_{t}\coloneqq\sigma^{\theta}_{t}
.

Step 2: Apply the classical Itô formula.

We apply the standard Itô formula to the process
Y_{t}=f(t,X_{t})
driven by the standard Brownian motion
B_{t}
:

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})dt+\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})dX_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})d\langle X\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})dt+\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})\left(\mu_{t}dt+\sigma_{t}dB_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})(\sigma_{t}^{2}dt)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})+\mu_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})\right)dt+\sigma_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})dB_{t}.

Substituting back the definitions of
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
yields:

(6.4)

dY_{t}=\left(\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})+(\mu^{\theta}_{t}-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t})\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})dB_{t}.

Step 3: Express the result in terms of
dB^{\theta}_{t}
.

Our goal is to write the final expression in the language of the
\theta
-calculus. To do this, we use the relation
dB_{t}=dB^{\theta}_{t}+\tilde{F}_{t}dt
and substitute it into the stochastic integral part of equation (
6.4
):

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}f+(\mu^{\theta}_{t}-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t})\partial_{x}f+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(dB^{\theta}_{t}+\tilde{F}_{t}dt)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}f+\mu^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t}\partial_{x}f+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f\right)dt+\left(\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f\tilde{F}_{t}\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}fdB^{\theta}_{t}.

We now collect all the drift (
dt
) terms. The terms involving
\tilde{F}_{t}
cancel each other precisely:

-\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t}\partial_{x}f+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\tilde{F}_{t}\partial_{x}f=0.

The remaining expression is:

dY_{t}=\left(\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})+\mu^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(t,X_{t})\right)dt+\sigma^{\theta}_{t}\partial_{x}f(t,X_{t})dB^{\theta}_{t}.

This is the desired result (
6.3
). The process
Y_{t}
is therefore a
\theta
-Itô process with the specified drift and volatility.
∎

Remark 6.6

(The Structure of the
\theta
-Calculus)
.

Theorem
6.5
reveals a remarkable and elegant property of the
\theta
-calculus. When using the
\theta
-Brownian motion as the fundamental integrator, the Itô formula for a change of variables is
formally identical
to the classical Itô formula. The non-convex ambiguity, and indeed the entire structure of the driver
F
and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
, does not manifest as an extra term in the differential rule.

Instead, the full effect of the ambiguity is absorbed into the definition of the integrator
B^{\theta}
itself. The process
B^{\theta}
is the natural fair game or martingale of this calculus. Relative to the objective measure
P_{0}
, this process evolves with a non-trivial, endogenously determined drift
-\tilde{F}(t,\omega,B^{\theta}_{t},1)
. It is this drift that carries all the information about the underlying ambiguity structure.

This provides a powerful and intuitive framework: one can perform formal calculations as if in the classical setting, with the understanding that the underlying canonical martingale,
B^{\theta}
, is not the standard Brownian motion. This structure is deeply analogous to the relationship between classical calculus and calculus under a Girsanov change of measure, where a change of measure alters the drift of the Brownian motion but preserves the formal structure of Itô’s rule. Our framework can thus be seen as defining an endogenous, state-dependent change of measure, where the specific measure is chosen at each instant to be the most adversarial one according to the driver
F
.

7.
Nonlinear Feynman-Kac Formula

A foundation of any stochastic calculus is its connection to the theory of partial differential equations (PDEs), typically established via a Feynman-Kac formula. This formula provides a bridge between probabilistic representations, expectations of functionals of stochastic processes, and analytic solutions to PDEs. In this section, we establish the analogue of this result for our framework. We show that the solution to a
\theta
-BSDE in a Markovian setting corresponds to the viscosity solution of a fully nonlinear parabolic PDE. This theorem is a central result, providing a powerful tool for computation and further theoretical analysis.

7.1.
Setup: Markovian Framework

We now specialize the setting to a Markovian one. Let the state of the system be described by a
k
-dimensional diffusion process
X
, which is the solution to the stochastic differential equation:

(7.1)

dX_{s}=b(s,X_{s})ds+\sigma(s,X_{s})dB_{s},\quad X_{t}=x,

for
s\in[t,T]
and a given initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We impose standard assumptions on the coefficients.

Assumption 7.1

(SDE Coefficients)
.

The drift
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and the volatility
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
are continuous functions. Further, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
,

\left\lVert b(t,x_{1})-b(t,x_{2})\right\rVert+\left\lVert\sigma(t,x_{1})-\sigma(t,x_{2})\right\rVert\leq C_{K}\left\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rVert.

These conditions ensure the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to the SDE (
7.1
) with standard estimates on its moments.

We consider a
\theta
-BSDE where the driver
F
and the terminal condition
\xi
depend on the state process
X
.

Assumption 7.2
.

(Markovian Primitives).

(i)

The driver is of the form
F(s,\omega,y,z,a)=F(s,X_{s}(\omega),y,z,a)
, where the function
F:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous in all its arguments. Moreover, for any compact set
K\subset[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the restrictions of
F
and
\mathcal{U}
satisfy Hypothesis
3.2
, with the constants
L_{F,yz},L_{F,a},L_{a}
holding uniformly for
(t,x)\in K
.

(ii)

The terminal condition is given by
\xi=\phi(X_{T})
, where the function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and has at most polynomial growth, i.e., there exist constants
C,p>0
such that
|\phi(x)|\leq C(1+\|x\|^{p})
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{X}
be the infinitesimal generator of the diffusion
X
, acting on a sufficiently smooth function
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\mathcal{L}^{X}u(t,x)=\left\langle b(t,x),\nabla_{x}u(t,x)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma(t,x)\sigma(t,x)^{T}\nabla_{xx}^{2}u(t,x)\right).

The connection to the PDE is established via the Markovian driver
\tilde{F}(t,x,y,z)\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F(t,x,y,z,a)
. By Lemma
4.4
, this driver is well-defined, continuous, and Lipschitz in
(y,z)
uniformly on compact sets of
(t,x)
.

7.2.
The Nonlinear Feynman-Kac Representation

The main result of this section connects the
\theta
-BSDE to a fully nonlinear PDE in the sense of viscosity solutions.

Theorem 7.3

(Nonlinear Feynman-Kac Formula)
.

Let Assumptions
7.1
and
7.2
hold. Let
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be the unique viscosity solution in the class of continuous functions with polynomial growth to the fully nonlinear parabolic PDE:

(7.2)

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}u(t,x)-\mathcal{L}^{X}u(t,x)-\tilde{F}\big(t,x,u(t,x),(\nabla_{x}u(t,x))^{T}\sigma(t,x)\big)=0,&(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{k},\\
u(T,x)=\phi(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.\end{cases}

Then, for any initial condition
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the unique solution
(Y^{t,x},Z^{t,x},A^{t,x})
to the corresponding
\theta
-BSDE on
[t,T]
has the representation:

Y_{s}^{t,x}=u(s,X_{s}^{t,x})\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{s}^{t,x}=(\nabla_{x}u(s,X_{s}^{t,x}))^{T}\sigma(s,X_{s}^{t,x})

for
P_{0}\otimes ds
-a.e.
(s,\omega)\in[t,T]\times\Omega
. Consequently, the conditional
\theta
-expectation is given by the value function:

\mathfrak{E}^{F,\mathcal{U}}_{t}[\phi(X_{T})|X_{t}=x]=u(t,x).

Proof.

The proof follows the now-classical methodology for linking BSDEs to viscosity solutions, extended to our setting. The argument proceeds in several steps. First, we define a candidate solution from the BSDE. Second, we prove this candidate is a viscosity solution. Third, we invoke the uniqueness of viscosity solutions to identify our candidate with the function
u
. Finally, we identify the process
Z
.

Step 1: The Candidate Solution and its Regularity.

For any initial condition
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, let
X_{s}^{t,x}
denote the unique strong solution to the forward SDE (
7.1
) with
X_{t}^{t,x}=x
. Let
(Y^{t,x},Z^{t,x},A^{t,x})
be the unique solution (guaranteed by Theorem
3.3
) to the
\theta
-BSDE on the interval
[t,T]
with Markovian driver
F(s,X_{s}^{t,x},y,z,a)
and terminal condition
\xi=\phi(X_{T}^{t,x})
.

We define the candidate value function
v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

v(t,x)\coloneqq Y_{t}^{t,x}.

By uniqueness of the BSDE solution, the value
Y_{t}^{t,x}
is deterministic. The driver
\tilde{F}(t,x,y,z)
is Lipschitz in
(y,z)
uniformly for
(t,x)
on compact sets. The primitives
b,\sigma,\phi
are also continuous. Standard results on the stability of BSDEs with respect to their parameters (see, e.g.,
[
Karoui et al.(1997)Karoui, Peng, and Quenez
, Proposition 3.3]
) imply that the map
(t,x)\mapsto v(t,x)
is continuous. The polynomial growth of
v
is inherited from the polynomial growth of
\phi
and standard moment estimates for
X
.

Step 2: Proving that
v
is a Viscosity Subsolution.

Let
\psi\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
be a smooth test function. Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a point where
(v-\psi)
attains a strict local maximum, and assume without loss of generality that
(v-\psi)(t_{0},x_{0})=0
. We must prove that:

(7.3)

-\partial_{t}\psi(t_{0},x_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{X}\psi(t_{0},x_{0})-\tilde{F}\big(t_{0},x_{0},\psi(t_{0},x_{0}),(\nabla_{x}\psi(t_{0},x_{0}))^{T}\sigma(t_{0},x_{0})\big)\leq 0.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume the left-hand side is strictly positive. Let this value be
2\eta>0
. By continuity of all functions involved, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
(s,x)
in the spatio-temporal cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\coloneqq[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta]\times\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\|x-x_{0}\|\leq\delta\}
, we have:

(7.4)

-\partial_{t}\psi(s,x)-\mathcal{L}^{X}\psi(s,x)-\tilde{F}\big(s,x,\psi(s,x),(\nabla_{x}\psi(s,x))^{T}\sigma(s,x)\big)\geq\eta.

Let
X_{s}
be the solution to the SDE starting from
X_{t_{0}}=x_{0}
. Let
\tau
be the first exit time of the process
(s,X_{s})
from the cylinder
[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)\times B(x_{0},\delta)
.
Consider the process
Y_{s}\coloneqq v(s,X_{s})
on the stochastic interval
[t_{0},\tau]
. By definition,
(Y_{s},Z_{s})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}
is the solution to the BSDE, so on
[t_{0},\tau]
it satisfies

Y_{s}=Y_{\tau}+\int_{s}^{\tau}\tilde{F}(r,X_{r},Y_{r},Z_{r})dr-\int_{s}^{\tau}Z_{r}dB_{r}.

Now consider the process
\hat{Y}_{s}\coloneqq\psi(s,X_{s})
. By Itô’s formula, on
[t_{0},\tau]
,

\psi(s,X_{s})=\psi(\tau,X_{\tau})+\int_{s}^{\tau}\left(\partial_{t}\psi+\mathcal{L}^{X}\psi\right)(r,X_{r})dr-\int_{s}^{\tau}(\nabla_{x}\psi(r,X_{r}))^{T}\sigma(r,X_{r})dB_{r}.

Let’s compare the drivers. From (
7.4
), the drift term for
\psi
is
g(s,x):=-(\partial_{t}\psi+\mathcal{L}^{X}\psi)(s,x)
, which satisfies
g(s,x)\geq\tilde{F}(s,x,\psi(s,x),(\nabla_{x}\psi)^{T}\sigma(s,x))+\eta
.
At the terminal time
\tau
, since
(v-\psi)
has a strict local maximum at
(t_{0},x_{0})
, we have
v(\tau,X_{\tau})<\psi(\tau,X_{\tau})
for
\tau>t_{0}
. Thus,
Y_{\tau}<\hat{Y}_{\tau}
.
We are now in a position to apply a strict comparison principle for BSDEs. Let
(\check{Y},\check{Z})
be the solution on
[t_{0},\tau]
to the BSDE with terminal condition
\psi(\tau,X_{\tau})
and driver
\tilde{F}(s,X_{s},y,z)
. Since
Y_{\tau}\leq\psi(\tau,X_{\tau})
, standard BSDE comparison implies
Y_{s}\leq\check{Y}_{s}
for
s\in[t_{0},\tau]
.
However, we have a stronger condition. The process
\psi(s,X_{s})
is a solution to a BSDE with a driver
g(s,X_{s})
that is strictly greater than the driver
\tilde{F}
for small enough
\delta
. More formally, the difference in drivers is
\tilde{F}(s,X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s})-g(s,X_{s})
. Due to the Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{F}
and the smoothness of
\psi
, we can choose
\delta
small enough such that the difference
|Y_{s}-\psi(s,X_{s})|
and
\|Z_{s}-(\nabla_{x}\psi)^{T}\sigma\|
are sufficiently small, ensuring that the inequality between the drivers holds.
A direct application of the comparison principle (e.g.,
[
Karoui et al.(1997)Karoui, Peng, and Quenez
, Theorem 2.2]
) on
[t_{0},\tau]
to the pair of processes
(Y_{s},\psi(s,X_{s}))
yields
Y_{t_{0}}<\psi(t_{0},X_{t_{0}})
. But this is
v(t_{0},x_{0})<\psi(t_{0},x_{0})
, which contradicts the initial assumption that
v(t_{0},x_{0})=\psi(t_{0},x_{0})
. Thus, the inequality (
7.3
) must hold.

Step 3: Proving that
v
is a Viscosity Supersolution.

The argument is symmetric. Let
(t_{0},x_{0})
be a point where
v-\psi
has a strict local minimum of 0. We assume for contradiction that the PDE inequality is reversed with a strict inequality:

-\partial_{t}\psi(t_{0},x_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{X}\psi(t_{0},x_{0})-\tilde{F}\big(t_{0},x_{0},\psi(t_{0},x_{0}),(\nabla_{x}\psi(t_{0},x_{0}))^{T}\sigma(t_{0},x_{0})\big)>0.

This leads to the reversed inequality
g(s,x)<\tilde{F}-\eta
in a neighborhood. At the terminal time
\tau
, we have
Y_{\tau}=v(\tau,X_{\tau})>\psi(\tau,X_{\tau})
. The strict comparison principle for BSDEs now implies
Y_{t_{0}}>\psi(t_{0},X_{t_{0}})
, again contradicting
Y_{t_{0}}=\psi(t_{0},x_{0})
.
Since
v
is both a viscosity subsolution and supersolution, it is by definition a viscosity solution of PDE (
7.2
).

Step 4: Uniqueness and Identification of Processes.

The theory of viscosity solutions for second-order parabolic PDEs is well-developed. Under our standing assumptions (continuity of coefficients
b,\sigma,\phi
; continuity of
\tilde{F}
and its Lipschitz continuity in
(y,z)
), the PDE (
7.2
) admits a unique viscosity solution
u
in the class of continuous functions with polynomial growth. For this foundational result, we refer to
[
Crandall et al.(1992)Crandall, Ishii, and Lions
]
and its extensions to the BSDE context, see e.g.,
[
Pardoux and Peng(1992)
]
.
Therefore, our constructed function
v(t,x)
must be equal to the unique solution
u(t,x)
:

Y_{s}^{t,x}=v(s,X_{s}^{t,x})=u(s,X_{s}^{t,x})\quad P_{0}\text{-a.s., for all }s\in[t,T].

This establishes the main representation formula.

Finally, we identify the process
Z
. The theory of viscosity solutions for uniformly parabolic PDEs of this type guarantees that the solution
u
is continuously differentiable in its spatial variable
x
, i.e.,
u\in C^{0,1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and in many cases even
C^{1,2}
, although
C^{0,1}
is sufficient for our purpose here. We can apply Itô’s formula to the process
Y_{s}=u(s,X_{s})
. This yields two semimartingale decompositions for the same process
Y_{s}
:

(1)

As the solution to the BSDE:

dY_{s}=-\tilde{F}(s,X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s})ds+Z_{s}dB_{s}
.

(2)

By applying Itô’s formula to
u(s,X_{s})
:

du(s,X_{s})=\left(\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}^{X}u\right)(s,X_{s})ds+\left(\nabla_{x}u(s,X_{s})\right)^{T}\sigma(s,X_{s})dB_{s}.

Since
u
is the viscosity solution to the PDE (
7.2
), it satisfies the equation, which implies the equality of the drift terms:

(\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}^{X}u)(s,X_{s})=-\tilde{F}\big(s,X_{s},u(s,X_{s}),(\nabla_{x}u(s,X_{s}))^{T}\sigma(s,X_{s})\big)

must hold
P_{0}\otimes dt
-a.e. where the derivatives exist, and in the viscosity sense everywhere. By the uniqueness of the canonical Doob-Meyer decomposition, the martingale parts of the two representations for
dY_{s}
must be equal:

\int_{t}^{s}Z_{r}dB_{r}=\int_{t}^{s}\left(\nabla_{x}u(r,X_{r})\right)^{T}\sigma(r,X_{r})dB_{r}\quad P_{0}\text{-a.s. for all }s\in[t,T].

The uniqueness of the integrand in the Itô integral implies that the integrands must be equal as processes in
\mathcal{H}^{2}([t,T])
:

Z_{s}=(\nabla_{x}u(s,X_{s}))^{T}\sigma(s,X_{s})\quad P_{0}\otimes dt\text{-a.e. on }[t,T]\times\Omega.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

The identification
Z_{s}=(\nabla_{x}u)^{T}\sigma
is fundamental. It reveals that the volatility process
Z
in the BSDE framework plays the role of the scaled gradient of the value function. This connection is the primary channel through which the state-dependent ambiguity, encapsulated in the driver’s dependence on
z
, influences the dynamics of the valuation. The nonlinear Feynman-Kac formula thus provides not only a computational tool via PDEs but also a deep structural insight into the nature of the solution to the
\theta
-BSDE.

8.
Relationship to G-BSDEs via Regularization

A central motivation for this work is to develop a calculus that can handle non-convex ambiguity. It is therefore crucial to understand the relationship between our theory and the canonical framework for convex ambiguity: the theory of G-expectations and the associated G-BSDEs
[
Peng(2019)
]
.

The standard G-BSDE framework considers a driver, or generator, that is the support function of a set of volatility models. A typical form is
G(z)=\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[az^{T}z]
, where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}^{\geq 0}
is a compact, convex set. This driver is linear in the parameter
a
and quadratic in the volatility process
z
. As the maximization is linear over the domain
\mathcal{U}
, the maximizer is not generally unique, and even when it is, it typically lies on the boundary of
\mathcal{U}
and depends discontinuously on
z
. Consequently, such a driver fails our core structural condition, the Global Regularity Hypothesis (
3.2
). Our theory, therefore, does not directly contain the G-BSDE framework.

In this section, we demonstrate that G-BSDEs can be recovered as the limit of a sequence of well-posed
\theta
-BSDEs. This is achieved by introducing a strictly concave regularization term to the driver, which ensures that our hypothesis holds for each element of the sequence. This result validates our framework by showing that it can approximate classical results and clarifies the precise role of structural regularity in distinguishing our calculus from the G-calculus.

8.1.
Approximation via Strict Concave Regularization

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}^{\geq 0}
be a non-empty, compact, and convex set, where
\mathbb{S}_{d}^{\geq 0}
is the space of
d\times d
positive semi-definite symmetric matrices. The corresponding G-BSDE is driven by the generator:

G(z)=\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[az^{T}z].

This driver is convex, 1-homogeneous in
z^{T}z
, and independent of
y
. A solution to the G-BSDE with terminal condition
\xi
is a pair of processes
(Y^{G},Z^{G})
satisfying

Y^{G}_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}G(Z^{G}_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z^{G}_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce the following regularized driver for our
\theta
-BSDE:

(8.1)

F^{\varepsilon}(z,a)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[az^{T}z\right]-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a-a_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

where
a_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
is an arbitrary, fixed reference point. Note that
F^{\varepsilon}
does not depend on
y
. The quadratic penalty term
-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a-a_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
makes the driver uniformly strictly concave in
a
with concavity modulus
\varepsilon
. For each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the driver
F^{\varepsilon}
and the convex set
\mathcal{U}
satisfy the sufficient conditions of Proposition
3.4
. Consequently, by Theorem
3.3
, the corresponding
\theta
-BSDE with driver
F^{\varepsilon}
is well-posed.

Proposition 8.1

(Approximation of G-BSDEs)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}_{d}^{\geq 0}
be a compact and convex set. Let
(Y^{G},Z^{G})
be the solution to the G-BSDE with generator
G(z)
and terminal condition
\xi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{0})
. For each
\varepsilon>0
, let
(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon},A^{\varepsilon})
be the unique solution to the
\theta
-BSDE with driver
F^{\varepsilon}
from (
8.1
) and terminal condition
\xi
.
Then, as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the solution
(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})
converges to
(Y^{G},Z^{G})
in the space
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])\coloneqq\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
.

Remark 8.2

(On the Boundedness of the Terminal Condition)
.

The assumption
\xi\in L^{\infty}
is not merely a technical convenience. It is a fundamental requirement for the well-posedness of the limiting G-BSDE with a quadratic generator
G(z)
. The foundational well-posedness theory for such BSDEs, as developed by Kobylanski
[
Kobylanski(2000)
]
, relies on the terminal condition being bounded. This ensures that the solution
Y^{G}
remains bounded, which in turn controls the integrability of
Z^{G}
. Our proof leverages this crucial property.

8.2.
Proof of Convergence

The proof relies on establishing uniform estimates for the family of approximating solutions and then using stability results for BSDEs. We begin with the uniform bounds.

Lemma 8.3

(Uniform Bounds)
.

The family of solutions
\{(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is uniformly bounded in the space
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}([0,T])\times\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
. That is, there exists a constant
K>0
independent of
\varepsilon
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\left\lVert Y^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}+\left\lVert Z^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq K.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}F^{\varepsilon}(z,a)
be the driver for the
\varepsilon
-problem.
For an upper bound, observe that the regularization term in (
8.1
) is non-positive. Thus, for any
z
,

\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[az^{T}z]=G(z)
.
Since the drivers
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}
and
G
are independent of
y
and satisfy
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}\leq G
, the standard comparison principle for BSDEs (a consequence of Theorem
4.5
(1)) implies
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\leq Y^{G}_{t}
a.s. for all
t\in[0,T]
.

For a lower bound, let
C_{\mathcal{U}}=\sup_{a\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lVert a-a_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
. This constant is finite since
\mathcal{U}
is compact. We have
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)\geq G(z)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}
. Let
Y^{\text{low},\varepsilon}
be the solution to the quadratic BSDE with driver
G(z)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}
and terminal condition
\xi
. By comparison,
Y^{\text{low},\varepsilon}_{t}\leq Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
.

Since
\xi\in L^{\infty}
, the theory of quadratic BSDEs
[
Kobylanski(2000)
]
ensures that the solution
Y^{G}
is in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}([0,T])
. Similarly, the solution
Y^{\text{low},\varepsilon}
is also bounded, with a bound that depends on
\varepsilon
. However, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we have
G(z)-\frac{1}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}\leq G(z)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}
. Let
Y^{\text{low}}
be the solution to the BSDE with driver
G(z)-\frac{1}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}
. This driver is also quadratic, so
Y^{\text{low}}
is in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
. By comparison,
Y^{\text{low}}_{t}\leq Y^{\text{low},\varepsilon}_{t}
.
Combining these, we have
Y^{\text{low}}_{t}\leq Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\leq Y^{G}_{t}
a.s. for all
t
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Since
Y^{\text{low}}
and
Y^{G}
are in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}([0,T])
, there exists a constant
K_{Y}
(independent of
\varepsilon
) such that
\left\lVert Y^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}\leq K_{Y}
.

Next, we establish the uniform bound on
Z^{\varepsilon}
. Applying Itô’s formula to
|Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}
from
t=0
to
T
and taking expectations, we get:

\mathbb{E}[|Y^{\varepsilon}_{T}|^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[|Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(-2Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{s})+\left\lVert Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\right)ds\right].

Rearranging and using the boundedness of
Y^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{U}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}2Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{Y}^{2}+\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+2K_{Y}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{s})|ds\right].

Since
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)\leq G(z)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{a}\mathrm{Tr}[az^{T}z]\leq\frac{1}{2}(\sup_{a}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F})\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}
, and
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)\geq G(z)-C_{\mathcal{U}}/2
, the driver has at most quadratic growth. The standard a priori estimate for quadratic BSDEs (see, e.g.,
[
Kobylanski(2000)
, Prop 2.2]
) with a bounded solution
Y^{\varepsilon}
ensures a uniform bound on the
\mathcal{H}^{2}
norm of
Z^{\varepsilon}
. Thus, there exists a constant
K_{Z}
independent of
\varepsilon
such that
\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\left\lVert Z^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq K_{Z}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main convergence result.

Proof of Proposition
8.1
.

Let
\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\coloneqq Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}-Y^{G}_{t}
and
\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}\coloneqq Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}-Z^{G}_{t}
. This pair solves a BSDE with zero terminal condition,
\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{T}=0
, and driver
\Delta^{\varepsilon}_{s}\coloneqq\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{G})
. Applying Itô’s formula to
|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}
from
t
to
T
and taking expectations yields the fundamental identity:

(8.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}2\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}\Delta^{\varepsilon}_{s}ds\right].

We decompose the driver difference
\Delta^{\varepsilon}_{s}
into two parts:

\Delta^{\varepsilon}_{s}=\underbrace{\left(\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})\right)}_{\text{Term I}}+\underbrace{\left(G(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{G})\right)}_{\text{Term II}}.

For Term I, we have
G(z)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}\leq\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)\leq G(z)
, which implies
|\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(z)-G(z)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}C_{\mathcal{U}}
. Thus,

(8.3)

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}2\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}(\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon}))ds\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}2|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|\frac{\varepsilon C_{\mathcal{U}}}{2}ds\right].

For Term II, we use the stability results for quadratic BSDEs. Since
G
is convex, for any
z_{1},z_{2}
, we have
G(z_{1})-G(z_{2})\leq\left\langle\nabla G(z_{1}),z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rangle
wherever
G
is differentiable. More generally, a key stability estimate for quadratic BSDEs (see, e.g., the proof of uniqueness in
[
Kobylanski(2000)
, Theorem 2.3]
) shows that the contribution from this term is well-behaved. A standard technique involves showing that for some constant
C>0
:

(8.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}2\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}(G(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{G}))ds\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2}ds\right].

Combining (
8.3
) and (
8.4
) and substituting into (
8.2
):

\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\varepsilon C_{\mathcal{U}}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|ds\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2}ds\right].

Using Young’s inequality (
ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta b^{2}}{2}
) on the first term of the RHS with
\eta=\varepsilon
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\varepsilon C_{\mathcal{U}}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|ds\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{(\varepsilon C_{\mathcal{U}})^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2}}{2}\right)ds\right]=\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2}ds\right].

Let
f^{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}]
and let
K_{1}=C+\varepsilon/2
. The main identity becomes:

f^{\varepsilon}(t)+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right]\leq\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon+K_{1}\int_{t}^{T}f^{\varepsilon}(s)ds.

Dropping the non-negative
\delta Z
term, we have
f^{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon+K_{1}\int_{t}^{T}f^{\varepsilon}(s)ds
.
By Gronwall’s inequality applied to
f^{\varepsilon}
on
[0,T]
(in reverse time), this implies:

f^{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\left(\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon\right)e^{K_{1}(T-t)}.

Since
K_{1}\to C
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, for any
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathbb{E}[|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}]\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Now we prove the convergence of
Z^{\varepsilon}
. Returning to the Gronwall-ready inequality:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right]\leq\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon+K_{1}\int_{0}^{T}f^{\varepsilon}(s)ds.

Since
f^{\varepsilon}(s)\leq\frac{TC_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{2}\varepsilon e^{K_{1}(T-s)}
, the integral
\int_{0}^{T}f^{\varepsilon}(s)ds
is of order
\varepsilon
. Therefore, the entire right-hand side converges to 0 as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This proves that
Z^{\varepsilon}\to Z^{G}
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,T])
.

Finally, the convergence of
Y^{\varepsilon}
in the stronger
\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
norm follows from the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality. Since
\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\Delta^{\varepsilon}_{s}ds-\int_{t}^{T}\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}dB_{s}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\delta Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq C_{BDG}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta_{s}^{\varepsilon}|ds\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}\right\rVert^{2}ds\right].

We have already shown that the second term
\left\lVert\delta Z^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}\to 0
. For the first term,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta_{s}^{\varepsilon}|ds\right)^{2}\right]\leq T\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}ds\right].

The term
|\Delta_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=|\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon})-G(Z_{s}^{G})|^{2}
is integrable because the drivers have quadratic growth and we have uniform
\mathcal{H}^{2}
bounds on
Z^{\varepsilon}
and
Z^{G}
. Since
\tilde{F}^{\varepsilon}\to G
pointwise and
Z^{\varepsilon}\to Z^{G}
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}
, and by the uniform bounds from Lemma
8.3
, the dominated convergence theorem implies that
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}ds]\to 0
.
Thus, the right-hand side of the BDG inequality converges to zero, proving that
Y^{\varepsilon}\to Y^{G}
in
\mathcal{S}^{2}([0,T])
. This completes the proof.
∎

9.
Application: General Relativity

A primary motivation for this work is to develop a calculus for scientific problems where ambiguity is structured and non-convex. A frontier problem with this characteristic is the determination of the Equation of State (EoS) for matter at supra-nuclear densities, a central challenge in relativistic astrophysics. In this section, we demonstrate how our framework can be applied to this problem, not by solving the original, potentially ill-posed problem, but by constructing a sequence of well-posed regularized models that provably fall within the scope of our main existence theorem.

9.1.
The Physical Problem and the Failure of Global Regularity

The structure of a neutron star is determined by its EoS, a function
p(\rho)
relating pressure to density. In the extreme regime of its core, the EoS is largely unconstrained by experiment. The set of all physically plausible EoS models, when parameterized by a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, forms a set
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
that is known to be non-convex. It is more accurately modeled as a disjoint union of compact sets, corresponding to distinct physical theories (e.g., purely hadronic models versus those with a phase transition to quark matter).

\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}=\mathcal{U}_{\text{had}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\text{exo}}\cup\dots

A natural way to model robust valuation of an EoS-sensitive observable (e.g., tidal deformability) is to use a projection-type driver, where at each state, one seeks the physically-valid EoS that is closest to some target model suggested by the data.

Assumption 9.1
.

(Idealized Astrophysical Model Primitives).

(i)

(EoS Parameter Space):
The set of plausible EoS parameters,
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, is a non-empty, compact, but potentially non-convex set.

(ii)

(Forward Dynamics):
The evolution of observational data is described by a
k
-dimensional Markovian diffusion
X_{s}
, whose coefficients are continuous and Lipschitz.

(iii)

(Projection-Type Driver):
The driver for valuing an observable
\xi=\phi(a)
has the idealized form:

(9.1)

F(s,x,y,z,a)=h(s,x,y,z)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(s,x,y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

where
h
and
G
are standard Lipschitz maps. The maximization of
F
with respect to
a\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
is equivalent to finding the metric projection of
G(s,x,y,z)
onto
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
.

This idealized model precisely captures the non-convex challenge. The maximizer map is given by
\mathcal{A}^{*}(s,x,y,z)=\proj_{\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}}(G(s,x,y,z))
. It is a classical result that the metric projection onto a set is globally Lipschitz (in fact, non-expansive) if and only if the set is convex. Since
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
is non-convex, the map
(y,z)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}(s,x,y,z)
will not be globally Lipschitz. Thus, the model specified by Assumption
9.1

fails to satisfy Hypothesis
3.2
, and our main existence result, Theorem
3.3
, does not apply. While Theorem
3.7
ensures the model is pathwise consistent under stronger non-degeneracy assumptions, it does not provide an existence guarantee.

9.2.
A Well-Posed Regularization

To bring the problem within the ambit of our rigorous theory, we introduce a regularization of the driver. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the regularized driver
F^{\varepsilon}
by adding a strictly concave penalty term:

(9.2)

F^{\varepsilon}(s,x,y,z,a)\coloneqq h(s,x,y,z)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(s,x,y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

This regularized problem, for each
\varepsilon>0
, is well-posed.

Proposition 9.2

(Well-Posedness of the Regularized Model)
.

For each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the
\theta
-BSDE with driver
F^{\varepsilon}
from (
9.2
), uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
, and terminal condition
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
admits a unique solution
(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon},A^{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

The proof consists of demonstrating that the primitives
(F^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}})
satisfy the Global Regularity Hypothesis (
3.2
) for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. We verify the conditions systematically.

Conditions (i) and (ii) (Standard Properties and Lipschitz Continuity):
The functions
h
and
G
are assumed to be standard Lipschitz drivers. The addition of the term
-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
does not affect the progressive measurability or the Lipschitz continuity of
F^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
(y,z)
. The Lipschitz continuity of
F^{\varepsilon}
in
a
is also immediate.

Condition (iii) (Existence of a Regular Maximizer Map):
This is the crucial part of the proof. We must show that the maximization problem

\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}}{\argmax}\;F^{\varepsilon}(s,x,y,z,a)

admits a unique, globally Lipschitz maximizer map. Maximizing
F^{\varepsilon}
is equivalent to minimizing the objective function
J(a;y,z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-G(s,x,y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
over the compact set
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
.
We can rewrite the objective function by completing the square:

\displaystyle J(a;y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2\left\langle a,G\right\rangle+\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{F}^{2})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\left(\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2\left\langle a,\frac{G}{1+\varepsilon}\right\rangle\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\left\lVert a-\frac{G(s,x,y,z)}{1+\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\text{terms not depending on }a.

Therefore, the maximization problem is equivalent to the unique projection problem:

\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}}{\argmin}\;\left\lVert a-\frac{G(s,x,y,z)}{1+\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Let
K(y,z)\coloneqq\frac{G(s,x,y,z)}{1+\varepsilon}
. The objective function
a\mapsto\left\lVert a-K(y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
is strictly convex in
a
. A strictly convex continuous function attains a unique minimum over a non-empty compact set (in our case,
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
). This establishes the existence of a unique maximizer map, which we denote by
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}
:

\{\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(s,x,y,z)\}=\underset{a\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}}{\argmin}\;\left\lVert a-K(y,z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\proj_{\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}}\left(K(y,z)\right).

This proves Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-a).

It remains to prove the global Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}
. The objective function
a\mapsto J(a;y,z)
is not just strictly convex; it is
uniformly strongly convex
with modulus
\lambda=1+\varepsilon>1
, independently of
(y,z)
. For a parameterized optimization problem with a uniformly strongly convex objective, classical results from variational analysis guarantee the Lipschitz continuity of the solution map.
More formally, let
a_{1}=\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(y_{1},z_{1})
and
a_{2}=\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(y_{2},z_{2})
. The first-order necessary and sufficient conditions for optimality are given by the variational inequalities:

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{a}J(a_{1};y_{1},z_{1}),v-a_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{a}J(a_{2};y_{2},z_{2}),v-a_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}},

where
\nabla_{a}J(a;y,z)=(1+\varepsilon)a-G(s,x,y,z)
.
Setting
v=a_{2}
in the first inequality and
v=a_{1}
in the second, and adding them, we get:

\left\langle(1+\varepsilon)a_{1}-G_{1}-((1+\varepsilon)a_{2}-G_{2}),a_{2}-a_{1}\right\rangle\geq 0,

where
G_{i}=G(s,x,y_{i},z_{i})
. Rearranging gives:

(1+\varepsilon)\left\langle a_{1}-a_{2},a_{2}-a_{1}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle G_{1}-G_{2},a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rangle.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this leads to:

-(1+\varepsilon)\left\lVert a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\geq-\left\lVert G_{1}-G_{2}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}.

Dividing by
-\left\lVert a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}
(assuming
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
) yields:

(1+\varepsilon)\left\lVert a_{1}-a_{2}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert G_{1}-G_{2}\right\rVert_{F}.

Since
G
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{G}
in
(y,z)
, we have:

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(y_{1},z_{1})-\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\left\lVert G(y_{1},z_{1})-G(y_{2},z_{2})\right\rVert_{F}\leq\frac{L_{G}}{1+\varepsilon}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\left\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rVert).

This establishes global Lipschitz continuity of the maximizer map
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}
with constant
L_{a}^{\varepsilon}=L_{G}/(1+\varepsilon)
. This proves Hypothesis
3.2
(iii-b). The progressive measurability (iii-c) follows from the continuity of all involved maps.

Since Hypothesis
3.2
holds for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can apply Theorem
3.3
to conclude that the
\theta
-BSDE for the regularized model is well-posed.
∎

9.3.
Interpretation of the Regularized Solution and Future Directions

Proposition
9.2
provides a rigorous foundation for inference. For any chosen level of regularization
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain a unique solution triplet
(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon},A^{\varepsilon})
.

(1)

The Regularized Valuation (
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
):
This is a well-defined, identifiable, and dynamically consistent valuation that is sensitive to the non-convex geometry of
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
, albeit in a smoothed manner due to the regularization.

(2)

The Regularized Adversarial Model (
A^{\varepsilon}_{t}
):
The process
A^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon,*}(s,X_{s},Y^{\varepsilon}_{s},Z^{\varepsilon}_{s})
represents a physically-valid EoS from
\mathcal{U}_{\text{EoS}}
. It is a regularized version of the worst-case EoS. By analyzing the path of
A^{\varepsilon}_{t}
for small
\varepsilon
, one can gain insight into which regions of the non-convex parameter space are most adversarial to robust prediction.

This regularization approach opens a clear path for both practical application and theoretical investigation. The crucial theoretical question is the behavior of the solutions as the regularization vanishes.

Conjecture 9.3

(Convergence to the True Solution)
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, we conjecture that the solution
(Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})
of the regularized problem converges in
\mathcal{B}^{2}([0,T])
to a triplet
(Y,Z)
which, together with a process
A
, forms a solution to the original, unregularized
\theta
-BSDE defined by the idealized driver (
9.1
).

Proving this conjecture is a primary objective for future work. It would require techniques that go beyond the fixed-point methods of this paper, likely involving tools for stochastic inclusions or the theory of viscosity solutions for set-valued PDEs. Nevertheless, the regularization scheme presented here provides a mathematically sound and computationally tractable method for approaching identifiable inference under non-convex ambiguity.

10.
Conclusion

This paper has proposed a new foundation for stochastic calculus under ambiguity that addresses the identifiability crisis of classical, sub-additive theories. By relaxing the sub-additivity axiom, we have opened the door to a framework sensitive to non-convex geometry.

Our main contribution is the constructive realization of this framework via the
\theta
-BSDE. We established the mathematical viability of this approach by predicating our existence and uniqueness result on a strong but clear structural condition: the Uniform Regularity of the Maximizer Map hypothesis. This hypothesis carves out a broad, tractable subclass of non-convex models for which a rigorous calculus is possible. We have shown that this construction yields a dynamically consistent and monotonic calculus.

Crucially, we have been transparent about the strength of this hypothesis. For convex uncertainty sets, we provide simple, verifiable conditions (strict concavity) that guarantee it holds. For the key non-convex case, based on a projection-type driver, we proved a different but fundamentally important result: under strong, but standard, smoothness and non-degeneracy conditions from Malliavin calculus, the maximizer is unique along the solution path. This result ensures the pathwise consistency of the model specification, highlighting deep connections between our theory and modern stochastic analysis. It does not, however, suffice to prove the existence theorem, which relies on the global regularity hypothesis.

This paper provides a solid foundation for a new avenue of research, offering a rigorous framework for modeling and analysis in the presence of structured non-convex ambiguity. Future work must focus on bridging the gap between the pathwise consistency result and the global regularity hypothesis required for existence. This may involve developing new fixed-point theorems or leveraging the tools of modern variational analysis and set-valued stochastic processes to handle cases with non-unique or discontinuous maximizers, thereby expanding the reach of this identifiable calculus.
