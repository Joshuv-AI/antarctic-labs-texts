Approximating the Average-Case Graph Search Problem with Non-Uniform Costs
Michał Szyfelbein

Abstract
Consider the following generalization of the classic binary search problem: A searcher is required to find a hidden target vertex x x in a graph G G . To do so, they iteratively perform queries to an oracle, each about a chosen vertex v v . After each such call, the oracle responds whether the target was found and if not, the searcher receives as a reply the connected component in G − v G-v which contains x x . Additionally, each vertex v v may have a different query cost c ⁡ ( v ) c(v) and a different weight w ⁡ ( v ) w(v) . The goal is to find the optimal querying strategy which minimizes the weighted average-case cost required to find x x . The problem is NP-hard even for uniform weights and query costs. Inspired by the progress on the edge query variant of the problem [SODA ’17], we establish a connection between searching and vertex separation. By doing so, we provide an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\sqrt{\log n}) -approximation algorithm for general graphs and a ( 4 + ϵ ) (4+\epsilon) -approximation algorithm for the case when the input is a tree.

Introduction

Searching in graph structures is a fundamental problem in computer science, with applications ranging from machine learning to operations research. The input graph
G
is assumed to contain a hidden
target
element
x
which the searcher is required to locate. In the classical setting, while exploring the graph, the searcher usually obtains only a local information about the placement of
x
. This principle underlines the well-known BFS and DFS strategies, which allow the searcher to locate the target when the only information received, while visiting a node, is whether it is the target. We study a more global search model in which the searcher is allowed to perform arbitrary
queries
, each about a chosen vertex
v
. A
response
to such query consists of information whether
v=x
, and if not, which of the connected components of
G-v
contains
x
. When the input graph is a path, this is equivalent to binary searching in a linearly ordered set. In case of trees, the problem was studied among multiple names including: Tree Search Problem
[
11
,
13
]
, Search Trees on Trees
[
6
,
5
]
, Hub Labeling
[
1
]
and more. For general graphs the problem is known as Vertex Ranking
[
8
,
44
,
42
,
40
]
, Elimination Trees
[
43
]
and Hierarchical Clustering
[
15
,
14
,
10
]
.

In order to efficiently locate the target, the searcher needs a
strategy
of searching which allows them to locate the target efficiently. The strategy is an
adaptive
algorithm, which given previous responses in finite (and polynomial) time outputs the next query to be performed. We model this strategy as a rooted tree which we will call a
decision tree

D
, whose nodes are vertices of
G
. We will require that each edge outgoing from the root
r
of
D
is associated with a unique response to a query to
r
in
G
and that the same holds for all decision subtrees of
D-r
(in the respective components of
G-v
). When searching according to
D
, the root
r
of
D
is queried first. If
r\neq x
the searcher moves down along the edge of
r
associated with the response, thereby entering the subtree
D_{u}
rooted at the next queried vertex

. This process recurses until the target is found.

We will allow each vertex
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
to have an arbitrary
query cost

c(v)
.
Intuitively,
c(v)
represents the number of time units required for a query to
v
to return an answer. We will also allow each vertex to have an arbitrary
weight

w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
, which denotes how frequently
the vertex is searched for. For a target vertex
x\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
and a decision tree
D
, let
Q_{G}\mathopen{}\left(D,x\right)
denote the sequence of queries performed
along the unique path in
D
from the root
r\mathopen{}\left(D\right)
to
x
. The
weighted average-case cost
of
D
on
G
is defined as:

c_{G}\mathopen{}\left(D\right)=\sum_{x\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right)}w\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\cdot\sum_{q\in Q_{G}\mathopen{}\left(D,x\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(q\right).

The
Graph Search Problem
(GSP) is to find the decision tree which minimizes the above cost.

1.1
Motivations and Applications

Fast retrieval of information in graph structures is a well-studied problem,
beginning with the seminal work of Knuth
[
36
]
.
When the underlying search space provides non-local information about the target,
the search process can be accelerated, since each query rules out a large set of
possible target locations. The goal of designing search strategies is to find ways of exploiting this property as effectively as possible.

Searching arises in various practical problems, but it can also be reformulated, to fit a wide range of real-life applications. Searching in graphs can be used to model a variety of problems, that may initially appear unrelated. These include: scheduling of parallel database join operations
[
21
,
38
,
39
]
, automated bug detection in computer code
[
3
,
23
,
24
,
46
]
, parallel Cholesky factorization of matrices
[
20
]
, VLSI-layouts
[
45
]
, hierarchical clustering of data
[
15
,
14
,
10
]
and parallel assembly of multi-part products from their components
[
33
,
27
]
.

We focus on the average-case version of the problem rather than the worst-case,
since it is natural to assume that the search strategies we design are intended
to be used repeatedly.
This motivates the introduction of a weight function, as some vertices may serve
as targets more frequently than others.
We also allow arbitrary query costs, since performing a query may require
significant resources, such as time or money. To the best of our knowledge, this particular variant of the problem has not yet been investigated in the literature.

\mathopen{}\left(S_{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{*}\cap H_{1}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\left(S_{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{*}\cap H_{2}\right)=\emptyset
, the claim follows by summing over all values of
k
.

We are now ready to bound the cost of the solution. Let
D
be the decision tree returned by the procedure. Using the fact that by definition
\frac{4}{1-\delta}=4+\epsilon
, we have:

\displaystyle c_{T}\mathopen{}\left(D\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathcal{T}}w\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\cdot c\mathopen{}\left(S_{\mathcal{T}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{1-\delta}\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{T}}\sum_{k=\frac{1+\delta}{2}\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)+1}^{w\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(S_{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{*}\cap\mathcal{T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{1-\delta}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(S_{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{4}{1-\delta}\cdot\texttt{OPT}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)=\mathopen{}\left(4+\epsilon\right)\cdot\texttt{OPT}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)

where the third inequality is due to Lemma
6
and the last inequality is by Lemma
3
.

As
1/\delta=\frac{4+\epsilon}{\epsilon}=1+4/\epsilon
and each
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
belongs to the set
S_{\mathcal{T}}
exactly once, we have that the overall running time is at most
O\mathopen{}\left(n^{4}/\epsilon^{2}\right)
as required.

4
Searching in general graphs

To construct decision trees for general graphs, we exploit a connection to a different problem,
namely the Min-Ratio Vertex Cut Problem.
Let
\alpha_{c,w}\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
denote the optimal value of such a vertex cut.
We invoke the following result given in

Theorem 4.1

Given a graph

and the weight function

polynomial-time algorithm, which computes a partition

, such that:

\alpha_{c,w}\mathopen{}\left(A,S,B\right)=O\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)\cdot\alpha_{c,w}\mathopen{}\left(G\right).

Combining the latter procedure, with the following result, yields an
O\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)

Let

be the approximation ratio of any polynomial time algorithm for the Min-Ratio Vertex Cut Problem. Then, there exists an

-approximation algorithm for the GSP, running in polynomial time.

Proof

Let

-approximation ratio for the Min-Ratio Vertex Cut Problem.
The algorithm is as follows:

Procedure

\textnormal{{DecisionTree}}\mathopen{}\left(G,c,w\right)

.

D_{G}\leftarrow
arbitrary partial decision tree for
G

foreach

Hang

return

Algorithm 2

The

Let

See Appendix

Using the fact, that the partition

5
Conclusions and further work

We have presented an
O\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)
-approximation algorithm for the general
Graph Search Problem, and a
\mathopen{}\left(4+\epsilon\right)
-approximation algorithm for the case
when the input graph is a tree. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first approximation results for the vertex query and non-uniform costs variant of the problem.

Moreover, the
\mathopen{}\left(4+\epsilon\right)
-approximation algorithm for trees and its analysis can easily be
adapted to obtain a
\mathopen{}\left(4+\epsilon\right)
-approximation for the edge-query version of the problem,
thus improving the
6.75
-approximation given in

[
15
,
14
,
10
]
.

The advantage of our solution lies in its simplicity, making it suitable for practical applications.
This also places the average-case version of the problem in stark contrast to the worst-case variant,
for which achieving even the state-of-the-art
O\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)
-approximation for trees
required a non-trivial dynamic programming procedure and an intricate analysis

[
19
]
.
An open question is whether a constant-factor approximation exists for the latter as well.

The current state-of-the-art algorithm for the Min-Ratio Vertex Cut Problem, which we use as an essential
subroutine for our GSP algorithm, is based on an SDP relaxation.
An interesting direction for future research is to formulate a similar relaxation for GSP itself
and to prove that it has an integrality gap of at most
O\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)
.
Another question is whether the approximation ratio for GSP is inherently limited by the
approximation ratio of Min-Ratio Vertex Cut Problem, or if there exists a method to improve the
approximation without relying on vertex cuts.

On the hardness side, we have shown that the problem is NP-hard even when restricted to trees with
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 16
and to trees with
\operatorname{diam}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 8
. Note, that for general graphs, it can be argued that the problem cannot
be approximated within any constant factor unless the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis fails,
by reusing the argument for the edge-query variant provided in

[
10
]
.
An interesting open question is whether one can establish an inapproximability result
for some constant
c\leq 4
in the case when the input graph is a tree.

Appendix 0.B
Hardness of the Tree Search Problem

In the decision version of the Tree Search Problem, one is asked to determine whether,
for a given instance
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w\right)
, there exists a decision tree of cost at most
K
.

Theorem 0.B.1

The Decision Tree Search Problem is NP-complete even when restricted to trees with
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 16
and to trees with
\operatorname{diam}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 8
.

Proof

The problem is in NP since, given a decision tree
D
, one can verify in polynomial time
whether all the requirements are satisfied.

To show hardness, we use a black-box reduction from the edge-query, uniform-cost,
and non-uniform-weight variant, which is NP-complete even when restricted to trees with
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 16
and to trees with
\operatorname{diam}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\leq 4

[
34
]
.
Let
\mathopen{}\left(T,w,K\right)
be such an instance.
We construct a new instance
\mathopen{}\left(T^{\prime},c,w,K^{\prime}\right)
for the TSP as follows:
for every
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
, we set
c\mathopen{}\left(v\right)=K+1
.
We subdivide each edge
e\in E\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
by adding a new vertex
v_{e}
with

w\mathopen{}\left(v_{e}\right)=0
and
c\mathopen{}\left(v_{e}\right)=1
.
We set
K^{\prime}=K+w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\left(K+1\right)
.

Assume that we have a decision tree
D
of cost at most
K
for the original instance.
To obtain a decision tree
D^{\prime}
for the new instance, we replace each query in
D

with a query to the vertex that subdivides the corresponding edge.
Additionally, below each leaf of
D
, we attach the appropriate queries to the original vertices.
As
D
contains a query to every edge of
T
, each vertex is separated, so for every

v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
, one such additional query is added.
This results in a decision tree
D^{\prime}
of cost at most
K+w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\left(K+1\right)=K^{\prime}
,
as required.

Observe that in the new instance, for every vertex
v\in T
, the cost of searching for
v

is at least
K+1
since
c\mathopen{}\left(v\right)=K+1
.
Therefore, for these vertices, at least
w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\left(K+1\right)
cost is required.
This implies that each such vertex has exactly one such query in its query sequence,
namely the query to
v
itself. Otherwise, the cost would exceed
K^{\prime}
, and we conclude
that every such
v
is queried only when the candidate subset consists solely of
v
.

Conversely, assume there exists a decision tree
D^{\prime}
of cost at most
K^{\prime}
for the new instance.
We show how to obtain a decision tree
D
for the original instance.
We replace each query to a vertex
v_{e}
with a query to edge
e
,
and delete all queries to vertices
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
.
Since each
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
was the last query in
Q_{D^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\left(T^{\prime},v\right)
, performed
when the candidate set consisted only of
v
, the resulting
D
is a valid decision tree
for the original instance. Additionally, the cost of
D
is at most
K^{\prime}-w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\left(K+1\right)=K
,
as required.

Finally, note that subdividing each edge doubles the diameter of the tree while leaving the degree unchanged,
so the claim follows.

T
.
Computation is performed in a bottom-up, left-to-right manner, starting from the leaves.
For a leaf
v
, we have
C_{v}^{in}=c\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
and:

C_{v}^{out}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }w=w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\leq k,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since each of the
C_{v}^{in}
subproblems requires
O\mathopen{}\left(\deg_{T,v}^{+}\right)
computational steps we get that they require
O\mathopen{}\left(n\right)
running time. As there are
O\mathopen{}\left(n\cdot k\right)=O\mathopen{}\left(n\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)/\alpha\right)
remaining subproblems and each requires
at most
O\mathopen{}\left(k\right)=O\mathopen{}\left(w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)/\alpha\right)
computational steps, the running time is
O\mathopen{}\left(n\cdot\mathopen{}\left(w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)/\alpha\right)^{2}\right)
.

Note that, the running time of the above procedure depends on
w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
which may not be polynomial.
To alleviate this difficulty, we slightly relax the condition on the size of components
in
T-S
using a controlled parameter
\delta
.
Based on this relaxation, we show how to construct a bicriteria FPTAS for the problem. Let
\delta>0
be any fixed constant and let
Separator
be the dynamic programming procedure from Theorem
0.C.1
.
The algorithm is as follows:

Procedure

\textnormal{{SeparatorFPTAS}}\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w,\alpha,\delta\right)
:

K\leftarrow\frac{\delta\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{n\cdot\alpha}
.

foreach

v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)

do

w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}{K}\right\rfloor
.

\alpha^{\prime}\leftarrow\frac{\alpha\cdot K\cdot w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}
.

S^{\prime}\leftarrow\textnormal{{Separator}}\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\right)
.

return

S^{\prime}
.

The bicriteria FPTAS for the Weighted
\alpha
-separator Problem

Lemma 9

Let
S
be the optimal separator for the
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w,\alpha\right)
instance. We have that
c\mathopen{}\left(S^{\prime}\right)\leq c\mathopen{}\left(S\right)
.

Proof

We prove that
S
is a valid separator for the
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\right)
instance, so that
c\mathopen{}\left(S^{\prime}\right)\leq c\mathopen{}\left(S\right)
.
To simplify the analysis, we will define the auxiliary instance: For every
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
, let
w^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)=K\cdot\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}{K}\right\rfloor
. Additionally, let
\alpha^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\alpha\cdot w^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}
.

In this new instance, for
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(T\right)
we have
w^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\leq w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
, so for every
H\in T-S
,

w^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\left(H\right)\leq w\mathopen{}\left(H\right)\leq w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)/\alpha=w^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)/\alpha^{\prime\prime}

where the second inequality is by the definition of the
\alpha
-separator and the equality is by the definition of
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
.

We conclude that
S
is an
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
-separator for the auxiliary instance
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\right)
. Now notice that the
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\right)
instance has all of its weights scaled by a constant value of
K
, relatively to
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\right)
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}
. As multiplying weights by a constant does not influence the validity of a solution,
S
is an
\alpha^{\prime}
-separator for
\mathopen{}\left(T,w^{\prime},c,\alpha^{\prime}\right)
and the claim follows.

Lemma 10

For every
H\in T-S^{\prime}
, we have that
w\mathopen{}\left(H\right)\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta\right)\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha}
.

Proof

By definition
\frac{w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}{K}\leq w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)+1
and therefore, also
w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\leq K\cdot w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)+K
. We have:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in H}w\mathopen{}\left(v\right)

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot\sum_{v\in H}w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)+K\cdot n\leq\frac{K\cdot w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha^{\prime}}+K\cdot n

\displaystyle=\frac{w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha}+\frac{\delta\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha}=\frac{\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta\right)\cdot w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha}

where the second inequality is due to the fact that
S^{\prime}
is a
\alpha^{\prime}
-separator for
\mathopen{}\left(T,c,w^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\right)
instance and the first equality is by the definition of
\alpha^{\prime}
and
K
.

Combining the two above lemmas with the fact that
\frac{w^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{\alpha^{\prime}}=\frac{w\mathopen{}\left(T\right)}{K\cdot\alpha}=n/\delta
we have that the algorithm runs in time
O\mathopen{}\left(n^{3}/\delta^{2}\right)
as required.
