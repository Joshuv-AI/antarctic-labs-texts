On the Complexity of Quantum States and Circuits from the Orthogonal and Symplectic Groups
Oxana Shaya, Zoë Holmes, Christoph Hirche, Armando Angrisani

Abstract
Understanding the complexity of quantum states and circuits is a central challenge in quantum information science, with broad implications in many-body physics, high-energy physics and quantum learning theory. A common way to model the behaviour of typical states and circuits involves sampling unitary transformations from the Haar measure on the unitary group. In this work, we depart from this standard approach and instead study structured unitaries drawn from other compact connected groups, namely the symplectic and special orthogonal groups. By leveraging the concentration of measure phenomenon, we establish two main results. We show that random quantum states generated using symplectic or orthogonal unitaries typically exhibit an exponentially large strong state complexity, and are nearly orthogonal to one another. Similar behavior is observed for designs over these groups. Additionally, we demonstrate the average-case hardness of learning circuits composed of gates drawn from such classical groups of unitaries. Taken together, our results demonstrate that structured subgroups can exhibit a complexity comparable to that of the full unitary group.

Fields and groups.

The

over the field

\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}
.
We denote by
\mathbb{R}
,

Bra-ket notation.
Let

be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}

\mathcal{H}_{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

Schatten Norms.

For a vector
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}

\displaystyle\|\ket{\psi}\|_{p}\;=\;\biggl(\sum_{i}|\psi_{i}|^{p}\biggr)^{1/p}.

(1)

Let

denote the set of linear operators
A:\mathcal{H}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{n}

For an operator

For

, while for

For

-norm is called

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\infty}\;=\;\sup_{\|\ket{\psi}\|_{2}=1}\|A\ket{\psi}\|_{2}.

(2)

For convenience, we usually drop the subscript for the operator norm, i.e.

\displaystyle\|A\|\;\coloneqq\;\|A\|_{\infty}.

(3)

Vector

-norms and Schatten

-norms are monotone with respect to the index

\displaystyle\norm{A}_{p}\geqslant\norm{A}_{q}\qquad\text{if $1\leqslant p\leqslant q\leqslant\infty$}.

(4)

Moreover, a reverse inequality with an additional dimensional factor also holds:

\displaystyle\norm{A}_{p}\leqslant D^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\norm{A}_{q}\qquad\text{if $1\leqslant p\leqslant q\leqslant\infty$},

In particular, the operator norm and the Hilbert-Schmidt norm satisfy the following:

\displaystyle\norm{A}\leqslant\norm{A}_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{D}\norm{A}.

(6)

Distances induced by Schatten norms play a central role in quantum information theory.
In particular, the distance induced by the Schatten

-norm, commonly called the

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\bigr\|_{1}=\sqrt{1-\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi}{\phi}}^{2}}.

(7)

Furthermore, for the an operator
A
acting on a
D
-dimensional Hilbert space, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\absolutevalue{ A_{ij}}\leqslant D\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}\absolutevalue{ A_{ij}}^{2}}\eqqcolon D\norm{A}_{2}\leqslant D\sqrt{D}\norm{A},

(8)

where we used the inequality in Equation (
5
) twice, first for the vector of the entries of
A
with dimension

and second for
A
itself, and we noted that the
2
-norm of that vector coincides with the Schatten 2-norm of

.

Theorem 3

(Lévy’s Lemma, Theorem 5.16 in Ref.

)

Let

\displaystyle\Pr_{U\sim\mu_{G}}(|f(U)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mu_{G}}[f(U)]|\geqslant\tau)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2L^{2}C_{G}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant L\norm{U_1 - U_2}

\displaystyle\leqslant L\norm{U_1 - U_2}_{2}.

Computing moments of the Haar measure.

In order to compute the moments of the Haar measure, we consider the notion of

Denote the space of linear operators acting on

\displaystyle\text{comm }(G^{\otimes k})\coloneq\{A\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes t}\,|\,\forall U\in G:[A,U^{\otimes t}=0]\}

(20)

The

-th moments can be calculated via the projector into the commutant of the

-th fold tensor representation of the group

[

]
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{G}

\displaystyle\colon\mathcal{B}^{\otimes k}\to\text{comm }(G^{\otimes k})

(21)

\displaystyle A\mapsto\mathbb{E}U^{\otimes k}AU^{{\dagger}\,\otimes k}=\int_{G}dUU^{\otimes k}AU^{{\dagger}\,\otimes k}=\sum_{\eta=1}^{\dim(\text{comm}(G^{\otimes k}))}\Tr(B_{\eta}^{(k)}A)B_{\eta}^{(k)}

(22)

where
\{B_{\eta}^{(k)}\}_{\eta=1}^{\dim\text{comm}(G)}
is a Hermitian, orthonormal basis of the commutant of the

-th fold tensor representation of

-fold tensor representation is characterized by Schur-Weyl duality

-fold tensor representation is spanned by a representation of the symmetric group

. In contrast, for the orthogonal and symplectic groups, the commutant is spanned by representations of the Brauer algebra

\displaystyle\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}_{\frac{D}{2}}\\
-\mathbb{I}_{\frac{D}{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

For the orthogonal group, the commutant consists of

In what follows, we consider an

, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{G}=\left\{U\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}U^{\dagger}\,\middle|\,U\in G\right\}.

Measuring a circuit

\displaystyle P_{U}(x)=\left|\bra{x}U\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\right|^{2}.

We denote the resulting ensemble of probability distributions by

Lemma 2

Let the average Born distribution be
\mathbb{E}_{U}P_{U}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left|\bra{x}U\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\right|^{2}.
Then for all classical compact and connected groups, the average Born distribution is the uniform distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}P_{U}(x)=\frac{1}{D}.

Proof.

We will use the fact that all classical compact groups are unitary 1-designs.
Given that

\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{D}(\mathbb{I}+Z)^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}U\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}U^{\dagger}=\mathcal{E}^{(1)}(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n})=\sum_{\eta=1}^{\dim\text{comm}(G)}\Tr(B_{\eta} \ketbra{0}^{\otimes n})B_{\eta}

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\eta}\left(\Tr({B}_{\eta})+\sum_{i}\Tr({B}_{\eta} Z_i)+\sum_{i<j}\Tr( {B}_{\eta} Z_i Z_j)+\cdots\Tr({B}_{ \eta} Z^{\otimes n})\right){B}_{\eta}

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D}\left(\mathbb{I}+\sum_{\eta}\left(\sum_{i}\delta_{{B}_{\eta}Z_{i}}Z_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\delta_{{B}_{\eta}Z_{ij}}Z_{ij}+\cdots+\delta_{{B}_{\eta}Z^{\otimes n}}Z^{\otimes n}\right)\right).

(30)

where in the last equality, we used that all Pauli operators are traceless except the identity.
Therefore, the expectation is determined by the intersection of basis elements of the commutant with
\left\{\frac{\mathbb{I}}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}^{\otimes n}
. Given that the commutant consists solely of the identity, the average state is the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{E}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{D}}{D}
.
Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,P_{U}(x)=\langle\mathbb{E}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\outerproduct{x}{x}\rangle=\frac{1}{D}\eqcolon\mathcal{U}(x).

(31)

Hence, the average Born distribution is the uniform distribution.

∎

In this work, we let

\frac{1}{2}

3
Strong state complexity

The concentration of measure phenomena has ramifications in entanglement theory
[
41
]
, statistical mechanics
[
42
]
, measurement-based quantum computation
[
43
]
, quantum complexity
[
14
]
and quantum machine learning
[
44
]
. In this section, we illuminate the ramifications for ensembles of orthogonal and symplectic states as well as designs thereof. The results have significance in the context of pseudorandomness and chaos
[
45
]
.
In particular, we show that the strong state complexities of states from the ensembles
\mathcal{S}_{Sp}
and
\mathcal{S}_{SO}
are exponentially large. This is consistent with the fact that the corresponding random matrix distributions serve as effective models of quantum chaos
[
46
]
.

For fixed
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathsf{M}_{r}
denotes the class of POVM measurements on
n
qubits that can be implemented by combining at most
r
2-local gates from a fixed universal gate set
\mathsf{G}\subset U(4)
.
We denote the number of elementary gates required to implement a unitary
V
by
size(V)
and by
\mathsf{G}_{r}
the set of circuits of size
r
built from the gate set
\mathsf{G}
.
As noted in Ref.
[
14
, Appendix B2]
,

\absolutevalue{\mathsf{M_r}}\leqslant 2D(n+1)^{r}\absolutevalue{\mathsf{G}}^{r}
where we recall that
D=2^{n}
.
Traditionally, the notion of state complexity is related to the minimum size of a quantum circuit which approximates the state when evolved from an initial product state.

Definition 4

(Weak
\delta
-state complexity, Definition 5 in Ref.
[
14
]
)
.

For
\delta\in[0,1]
, a state
\ket{\psi}
has
\delta
-state complexity of at most
r
if there exists a unitary circuit
V\in\mathsf{G}_{r}
such that

\frac{1}{2}\big\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-V\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}V^{\dagger}\big\|_{1}\leqslant\delta\,,\quad\textrm{denoted as}\quad\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\delta}(\ket{\psi})\leqslant r\,.

In contrast, also an operational definition related to distinguishing the state from the maximally mixed state has been proposed in Ref.
[
14
]
.

Definition 5

(Strong state complexity, Definition 1 in Ref.
[
14
]
)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. A pure state
|\psi\rangle
has strong
\delta
-state complexity at most
r
if

\beta_{\mathrm{qs}}^{\sharp}(r,|\psi\rangle)\coloneq\max_{M\in\mathsf{M}_{r}(D)}\quad\left|\Tr\left(M\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|-\frac{\mathbb{I}_{D}}{D}\right)\right)\right|\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{D}-\delta\,,\quad\textrm{denoted as}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(|\psi\rangle\right)\leqslant r\,.

(34)

This strong definition of state complexity implies other (weaker) definitions, such as the minimal circuit size required to approximate the state.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 6 in Ref.
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose that
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
obeys
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}(|\psi\rangle)\geqslant r+1
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then,

\min_{\textrm{size}(V)\leqslant r}\frac{1}{2}\left\||\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|-V|0\rangle\!\langle 0|^{\otimes n}V^{\dagger}\right\|_{1}>\sqrt{\delta}.

(35)

3.1
Exponential strong complexity of classical compact groups

Now we are ready to derive our results.
Following the steps in Ref.

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\mathsf{M}_{r}\right|\max_{M\in\mathsf{M}_{r}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\left|\Tr\left(M\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|-\frac{\mathbb{I}_{D}}{D}\right)\right)\right|\geqslant 1-D^{-1}-\delta\right]

e^{\frac{3}{32}}\exp\left(-\frac{D(1-\delta)^{2}}{16}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{SU}.\end{cases}

For
\varepsilon

\displaystyle f(U)=\Tr(M \ketbra{\psi})=\Tr(M U \ketbra{0}^{\otimes n} U^\dag)=\sum_{j,k}M_{jk}U_{k0}U^{\dagger}_{0j},

Again, by Theorem

(43)

On the other hand, for

Therefore,

.

3.2
Geometric separation of high-complexity states

Concentration of measure is also the key to show that the high-complexity states do not occur clustered, but are also maximally geometrically separated.
In fact, there are doubly exponentially many states that are almost orthogonal
[
45
, Appendix B]
. We show that the same phenomenon manifests also within ensembles of states
\mathcal{S}_{SO}
and
\mathcal{S}_{Sp}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\Delta\in(0,1)
and
k>3
. Then, for each of the ensembles below, there exist
N
pure states
\ket{\psi_1},\ldots,\ket{\psi_N}
in the corresponding ensemble such that their pairwise trace distance satisfies

\displaystyle\forall i\neq j,\quad\frac{1}{2}\|\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}-\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\|_{1}\geqslant 1-\Delta.

(44)

The value of
N
depends on the ensemble as follows:

1.

Exact ensembles
(
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{G}
):
Letting
\mathcal{S}_{G}
denote the ensemble associated with symmetry group
G
, we have

\displaystyle N=\frac{1}{4}\times\begin{cases}e^{-29/64}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{32}\right)&\text{for }\mathcal{S}_{SO},\\
e^{-1}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{8}\right)&\text{for }\mathcal{S}_{Sp},\\
e^{-1/4}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{16}\right)&\text{for }\mathcal{S}_{SU}.\end{cases}

(45)

2.

Approximate designs
(
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{G}
):
For
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-designs, we have

\displaystyle N=\frac{1}{2}\left((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D}\right)^{k}\times\begin{cases}\left(2\left(\frac{16k}{D-2}\right)^{k/2}+2^{k}\varepsilon\right)^{-1}&\text{for }\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SO},\\
\left(2\left(\frac{8k}{D+2}\right)^{k/2}+2^{k}\varepsilon\right)^{-1}&\text{for }\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{Sp},\\
\left(2\left(\frac{8k}{D}\right)^{k/2}+2^{k}\varepsilon\right)^{-1}&\text{for }\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SU}.\end{cases}

(46)

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the one provided in Ref.
[
14
, Appendix A 1.b]
. We can construct an exponentially large set with high complexity states that have almost maximal trace distance via a probabilistic method.
In a first step, we show that on average, a state sampled from these ensembles is far away from any fixed pure state
|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|
.

Using that for pure states the trace distance is the square root of one minus the fidelity, see Eq. (
7
), we have

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\|_{1}\leqslant 1-\Delta\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}\geqslant(2-\Delta)\Delta\right].

Next, we apply Lévy’s Lemma to
f_{\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}}\colon\ket{\psi}\mapsto|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}
. This leads to
2\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{8C_{G}}\right)\geqslant\mathrm{Pr}[\absolutevalue{\abs{\braket{\psi}{\phi}}^2- \frac{1}{D}}\geqslant\tau]\geqslant\mathrm{Pr}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi}{\phi}}^{2}\geqslant\tau+\frac{1}{D}]
for
\tau\geqslant 0
.

Now for any
\Delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}\geqslant(2-\Delta)\Delta\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D})^{2}(D-2)}{32}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{SO}\\
\exp\left(-\frac{((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D})^{2}(D/2+1)}{8}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{Sp}\\
\exp\left(-\frac{((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D})^{2}D}{16}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{SU}\end{cases}

(47)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\begin{cases}e^{\frac{29}{64}}\exp\left(-\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{32}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{SO}\\
e\exp\left(-\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{8}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{Sp}\\
e^{\frac{1}{4}}\exp\left(-\frac{D\Delta^{4}}{16}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}_{SU},\end{cases}

(48)

where basic algebraic manipulations lead to the last inequality.
Using Theorem
4
, with
\alpha(f_{\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}})\leqslant D
and
\mathbb{E}_{U}f_{\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}}(U)=\frac{1}{D}
, we obtain for
k
-designs

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\|_{1}\leqslant 1-\Delta\right]\leqslant\begin{cases}\left((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-2m}\left(2\left(\frac{32m}{D-2}\right)^{m}+\frac{\epsilon}{D^{k}}(D+\frac{1}{D})^{2m}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SO}\\
\left((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-2m}\left(2\left(\frac{8m}{D/2+1}\right)^{m}+\frac{\epsilon}{D^{k}}(D+\frac{1}{D})^{2m}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{Sp}\\
\left((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-2m}\left(2\left(\frac{16m}{D}\right)^{m}+\frac{\epsilon}{D^{k}}(D+\frac{1}{D})^{2m}\right)&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SU}.\end{cases}

(49)

Setting
m=\frac{k}{2}
to its maximum value leads to the following bounds,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\|_{1}\leqslant 1-\Delta\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left((2-\Delta)\Delta-\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-k}\begin{cases}2\left(\frac{16k}{D-2}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}+2^{k}\varepsilon&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SO}\\
2\left(\frac{8k}{D+2}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}+2^{k}\varepsilon&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{Sp}\\
2\left(\frac{8k}{D}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}+2^{k}\varepsilon&\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{S}^{(k,\varepsilon)}_{SU}.\end{cases}

(50)

By Equation (
39
) (resp. (
42

(resp.

) such that the probability for a state to have low complexity

is strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{2}
.
Equivalently, there is an

4
Average-case complexity of learning Born distributions

In this section, we shift from state complexity to the task of learning the output distributions of quantum circuits. Specifically, we study the query complexity required to approximate the Born distributions
P_{U}
arising from circuits with gates drawn from the classical compact groups. Our focus is on the statistical query (SQ) model, which restricts the learner to coarse statistical information rather than direct sample access. This makes the model more realistic for noisy near-term devices, while also revealing fundamental average-case hardness phenomena.

Within this framework, we establish the hardness of learning
\mathcal{P}_{SO}
and
\mathcal{P}_{Sp}
.
A central technical challenge is to evaluate average properties of Born distributions under the Haar measure on
SO(D)
and
Sp(D)
. Many of these quantities of interest, such as distances to the uniform distributions, can be expressed as homogeneous functions of random states.

To address this, we employ a Gaussian integration method that reformulates Haar averages as expectations over standard Gaussian variables. This approach extends the unitary-group analysis of Ref.
[
22
]
. First, we derive Gaussian integration theorems for
SO
and
Sp
, which will be applied in Section
4.2
to bound the average total variation distance. Finally, combining this bound with Lévy’s lemma for the classical compact groups yields query complexity lower bounds in Section
4.3
, from which we conclude the average-case hardness of learning Born distributions arising from unitaries in these groups.

4.1
Haar-random averages via Gaussian integration

To analyze moments of functions beyond simple polynomials in the entries of unitaries, we extend the result of
[
22
, Theorem 17]
, originally formulated for the unitary group, to cover all classical compact groups. Specifically, we consider general homogeneous functions of even degree and show that their expectation values over these groups can be reformulated as expectation values with respect to standard independent Gaussian random variables. This reformulation provides a powerful tool that we will later use in Section
4.2
to bound the average total variation distance, and in Section
4.3
to establish query complexity lower bounds.

The connection between the Haar measure and the Gaussian measure relies on the fact that normalized Gaussian vectors are uniformly distributed on the sphere
[
47
]
. Consequently, Haar-random pure states from
SO(D),SU(D)
, and
Sp(D)
can be generated by normalizing standard Gaussian vectors over the reals, complex numbers, and quaternions, respectively.
First we recall the previous result for the unitary group.

Theorem 6

(Theorem 17 in Ref.
[
22
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be a homogeneous function with even degree
2k\in 2\mathbb{N}
, i.e.
f(ax)=|a|^{2k}f(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, we can rewrite the Haar expectation value as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu_{U}}\big[f(|\psi\rangle)\big]=\frac{1}{k!\,2^{k}}\binom{D+k-1}{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{g_{i},h_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[f(g_{1}+ih_{1},\ldots,g_{D}+ih_{D})\right],

(54)

where
g_{i},h_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
denote independent standard Gaussian random variables (
\mu=0
,
\sigma^{2}=1
).

In words:
the left-hand side features the Haar average over all pure states (complex unit vectors), while the right-hand side features an expectation value over
2D
statistically independent standard Gaussian random variables

5
Discussion and future work

In this work, we discussed the complexity of states and unitaries sampled from structured subgroups of the unitary group.
By applying Lévy’s Lemma, we showed that almost all states evolved under unitaries drawn from the classical compact groups exhibit high circuit complexity. For unitary designs of order
k>3
over these groups, we derived lower bounds on the circuit depth required to generate them. This opens several directions for future research, such as analyzing the circuit depth needed to achieve general
t
-designs over these subgroups, in the spirit of Ref.
[
48
]
, or studying the growth of complexity under restricted, non-universal gate sets, as in Ref.
[
49
]
. Furthermore, again leveraging Lévy’s Lemma, one could show that state ensembles generated by the classical compact groups exhibit thermalization behavior, following the framework of Ref.
[
41
,
42
]
. In addition, it would be interesting to explore the hardness of random circuits that are far from approximate
k
-designs.
We focused on subgroups of the unitary group with exponentially large Lie algebras. A recent classification
[
50
]
rules out the existence of other sub-exponential Pauli Lie algebras beyond the free-fermionic one, which is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(2n)
. For all such exponentially large subgroups we established the average-case hardness of learning their output distributions, except for matchgate circuits, which remain an open case.
We have contributed to clarifying the similarities among the classical compact groups. While Ref.
[
51
]
emphasized their differences in the construction of designs, our results highlight structural features they share. A natural direction for future work is to provide a unified explanation of both their commonalities and divergences, thereby deepening the understanding of why these groups behave alike in some aspects yet differ in others.

An important avenue for future exploration lies in understanding the implications of average-case properties of unitaries for proposals of quantum generative modeling implemented on noisy near-term devices. In this context, quantum circuits have been proposed as a model class for generative tasks, known as quantum circuit Born machines (QCBMs)
[
52
,
53
]
. In near-term applications, these models are trained via noisy gradient-based optimization, making the SQ framework a natural setting for analyzing their limitations. The average-case hardness of learning the output distributions of quantum circuits thus would directly translates into fundamental limitations for QCBMs as generative models, placing constraints on the advantages quantum devices might offer for probabilistic modeling.
