Associations of long-chain and very-long-chain saturated fatty acids with neutrophil related inflammatory markers and insulin resistance markers
Authors: Xuan Li, Qin Yang, Long Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSaturated fatty acids (SFAs) have been recognized as an important risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). SFAs with varying carbon chain lengths may differentially influence CVD progression. Both inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) are primary drivers of CVDs. Therefore, this study aims to compare the associations of long-chain SFAs (LCSFAs) and very-long-chain SFAs (VLCSFAs) with neutrophil-related inflammatory markers and IR markers using data from the NHANES 2011–2014 cohort.MethodsWe measured five LCSFAs (C14:0, C15:0, C16:0, C17:0, C18:0) and four VLCSFAs (C20:0, C22:0, C23:0, C24:0) in blood samples. Subsequently, weighted linear regression and restricted cubic spline models were used to assess associations between different SFAs and neutrophil-related and IR-related markers.ResultsA total of 3424 individuals were included in the analysis. Relative to participants in the lowest quartile of C16:0, those in the highest quartile exhibited 0.048 to 4.023 units higher levels of neutrophil counts, neutrophil-to-erythrocyte ratio (NER), neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio (NHR), Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), and metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR). Conversely, participants in the highest quartile of C24:0 showed 0.016 to 1.709 units lower levels of these markers compared with the lowest quartile.ConclusionsCirculating LCSFAs were positively associated with neutrophil-mediated inflammation and IR markers. In contrast, VLCSFAs, specifically C22:0, C23:0, and C24:0, exhibited inverse associations with these inflammatory and IR markers.Subject terms: Risk factors, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Saturated fatty acids (SFAs) have been globally recognized as an important risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) for decades. The American Heart Association (AHA) strongly recommends reducing dietary SFAs intake and replacing it with unsaturated fatty acids to improve cardiovascular health [1]. Furthermore, SFAs are classified into long-chain SFAs (LCSFAs, ≥ 14 carbons) and very-long-chain SFAs (VLCSFAs, ≥20 carbons) based on carbon chain length [2].

Recent observational studies have reported inverse associations between VLCSFAs, a distinct subgroup of SFAs, and cardiovascular risk. These studies also highlight protective effects on cardiovascular-related health factors. Specifically, plasma VLCSFAs were independently linked to favorable lipid profiles, including elevated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), as well as a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and coronary heart disease (CHD), as demonstrated in the Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-Up Study [3, 4]. Moreover, a nested case-control study within the PREDIMED trial found that a one standard deviation (SD) increase in baseline C22:0, C24:0, and total VLCSFAs was associated with 56%, 59%, and 55% lower odds of CHD, respectively [5]. Additionally, the Guangzhou Nutrition and Health Study (GNHS) examined erythrocyte membrane VLCSFAs and reported an inverse association with CHD risk in the Chinese population [6]. A cohort study based on the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) further found that higher circulating C22:0 and C24:0 were associated with reduced all-cause, CVD, and CHD mortality [7]. These findings collectively suggest that the mechanisms underlying VLCSFAs’ cardiovascular effects may differ from those of other SFAs.

Inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) are widely recognized as both primary drivers of CVDs [8, 9]. Past studies have highlighted the critical roles of neutrophils in cardiovascular inflammation and tissue repair. Specifically, neutrophils promote all stages of atherosclerosis—including atherogenesis, plaque destabilization, and erosion—through monocyte recruitment and macrophage activation [10]. Observational and genetic evidence support a causal link between elevated neutrophil counts and atherosclerotic CVD risk [11]. Furthermore, neutrophil-derived ratios, such as the neutrophil-to-erythrocyte ratio (NER) and neutrophil-to-HDL ratio (NHR), are associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) risk [12]. In contrast, IR is quantified using distinct indicators. The Homeostatic Model Assessment for IR (HOMA-IR) directly calculates IR from fasting insulin and glucose levels. Alternatively, the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) and metabolic score for IR (METS-IR) provide simple, low-cost assessments without requiring fasting insulin measurements [13, 14].

Based on the preceding evidence, we hypothesize that LCSFAs and VLCSFAs may exert differential associations with neutrophil-derived inflammatory markers (neutrophil counts, NER, NHR) and IR indices (HOMA-IR, METS-IR, eGDR). To test this hypothesis, we analyzed cross-sectional associations between five LCSFAs (myristic acid [C14:0], pentadecanoic acid [C15:0], palmitic acid [C16:0], margaric acid [C17:0], stearic acid [C18:0]) and four VLCSFAs (arachidic acid [C20:0], docosanoic acid [C22:0], tricosanoic acid [C23:0], lignoceric acid [C24:0]) with the aforementioned markers using data from the NHANES 2011-2014.

Methods

Study population

NHANES is an ongoing program aimed to evaluate the nutritional and health status of the U.S. citizens for every two years. NHANES is a nationally representative survey of the U.S. population that combines interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory data. In the NHANES cycles of 2011–2012 and 2013–2014, individual serum fatty acids were measured. Therefore, the study population of this analysis was derived from NHANES 2011–2014. A detailed description of study population in the NHANES 2011–2014 has been explained elsewhere [15]. Briefly, a total of 19931 participants were enrolled in the NHANES 2011–2014 and this analysis focused on 11329 individuals aged 20 years or older. Participants were excluded sequentially based on data availability: 6984 were excluded due to missing circulating fatty acid measurements (required for primary exposure assessment), followed by 202 without core biomedical data (serum lipids, fasting glucose, or insulin levels). An additional 11 participants were excluded for incomplete blood count results, and 708 were removed due to missing covariate data, including blood pressure measurements, anthropometric parameters (BMI, waist circumference), and dietary nutrient intake. The final analytic cohort consisted of 3424 participants with complete data for all exposure variables (LCSFAs/VLCSFAs), outcome measures (inflammatory markers and IR indices), and prespecified covariates, ensuring robustness for subsequent analyses. A flowchart of the study population selection is shown in Fig. 1. NHANES 2011–2014 was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) institutional review board (Protocol #2011-17). All procedures performed in studies were in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Demographic variables (age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status, and family income) were collected by trained staff during in-home structured interviews using a Computer-Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) system. Ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic, White, Black, and other racial/ethnic groups, following the National Institutes of Health (NIH) classification criteria. Education was categorized into three levels: less than high school, high school graduate, and some college or higher. Marital status was categorized into three groups: married or living with partner, separated/widowed/divorced, and never married. The family monthly poverty level index, derived from federal poverty guidelines, was used as a proxy for socioeconomic status. The index was categorized into three levels (≤ 1.30, 1.31–1.85, > 1.85), specifically reflecting thresholds commonly used in public health research to identify low-income households.

Lifestyle variables, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, sedentary behavior, and dietary habits, were assessed using a self-reported questionnaire. Based on questionnaire responses, smoking status was categorized into three groups: current smokers, former smokers, and never smokers. Alcohol consumption was defined as participants reporting ≥ 12 drinking episodes in the past 12 months. Sedentary time served as a primary physical activity indicator in this study. It was defined as the total time spent sitting during a typical day, excluding sleep.

Dietary data were collected via two nonconsecutive 24-hour dietary recalls. The Healthy Eating Index (HEI)-2015, a scoring algorithm developed by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) to assess diet quality against the Dietary Guidelines for Americans [16], was calculated based on food groups, energy, and nutrients from the recalls. The HEI-2015 comprises 13 components: 9 adequacy components (total vegetables, greens and beans, total fruits, whole fruits, whole grains, dairy, total protein foods, seafood and plant proteins, and fatty acids) and 4 moderation components (sodium, refined grains, saturated fats, and added sugars). Each component was scored separately based on intake relative to recommended levels. Scores were summed to obtain the total HEI-2015 score (range: 0–100).

Body measurement

Standing height and body weight were measured by trained health technicians using a stadiometer (with a fixed vertical backboard and an adjustable head piece) and a digital weighing scale, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared (kg/m²). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the level of the iliac crest to 0.1 cm precision at the end of normal expiration using a flexible measuring tape. Blood pressure (BP) was measured in duplicate after the participant rested quietly in a seated position for 5 minutes, with the average of the two readings recorded. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg and/or current use of antihypertensive medications.

Laboratory test

Serum specimens were processed, stored, and delivered to the Division of Laboratory Sciences, National Center for Environmental Health, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, for analysis. Total fatty acids were extracted from 100 µL serum using hexane, along with an internal standard solution containing eighteen stable isotope-labeled fatty acids to correct for recovery. The extract was derivatized with pentafluorobenzyl bromide in the presence of triethylamine to form pentafluorobenzyl esters. The derivatized sample was injected into a capillary gas chromatograph column to separate target fatty acids from matrix components. Individual fatty acids were detected within 34 min via electron capture negative-ion mass spectrometry using selected ion monitoring for 30 fatty acids (11 saturated, 6 monounsaturated, and 13 polyunsaturated). Quantification was performed by comparing the peak area of each analyte in the sample to that of a known concentration in a calibrator solution, with corrections applied based on the internal standard peak area ratio between the sample and calibrator. Complete blood count (CBC) parameters were determined using the Beckman Coulter principle for cell counting and sizing, integrated with an automated sample dilution and mixing system. Hemoglobin measurement was performed using a single-beam photometer. All CBC analyses and blood cell distribution assessments for participants were conducted on the Beckman Coulter DxH 800 analyzer. Triglyceride (TG) levels were measured using an enzymatic colorimetric assay on a Roche/Hitachi Modular P Chemistry Analyzer. HDL-C levels were measured using the dextran sulfate-Mg²⁺ precipitation method. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were measured by ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Fasting glucose levels were measured using a hexokinase-based enzymatic method (Roche Diagnostics), while serum insulin levels were measured using a two-site immunoenzymometric assay.

Definitions of outcomes

Neutrophil-related inflammatory markers and IR-related markers are calculated using the following formulas according to previous publications [12–14].

NER= neutrophil counts/erythrocyte counts.

NHR= neutrophil counts/HDL.

HOMA-IR= glucose×insulin/22.5.

METS-IR= (Ln((2×glucose)+TG) × BMI)/(Ln(HDL)).

eGDR=21.158-(0.09×WC)-(3.407×hypertension)-(0.551×HbA1c).

Statistical analysis

In accordance with the NHANES Analytic Guidelines, continuous variables were presented as weighted mean ± standard error (SE), and categorical variables were presented as n (weighted percentage). LCSFAs and VLCSFAs were categorized into quartiles (four groups each). A weighted linear regression model was fitted to examine the associations of different SFAs with neutrophil counts, NER, NHR, HOMA-IR, METS-IR, and eGDR, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status, family monthly poverty level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, sedentary time, HEI-2015, and BMI. The multivariable-adjusted regression coefficient (β) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported.

Restricted cubic spline regression with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) was used to explore dose-response relationships between different SFAs and neutrophil-related inflammatory markers as well as IR-related markers. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the ‘rms’ package for restricted cubic splines.

The weighted mean age of the total population was 47.30 ± 0.28 years, and the weighted mean BMI was 28.97 ± 0.12 kg/m2. Weighted means of neutrophil-related and IR-related markers according to quartiles of different circulating SFAs are shown in Table 2. In general, as the levels of circulating LCSFAs increase, there is an upward trend in neutrophil related markers and IR related markers, except for eGDR. It should be noted that there is an inverse relationship between eGDR and IR, meaning that lower eGDR indicates higher IR levels [13]. Taking palmitic acid (C16:0) as an example, the neutrophil counts in the 1st to 4th quartile groups of C16:0 were 3.75 ± 0.06, 3.81 ± 0.05, 4.02 ± 0.05, and 4.25 ± 0.06 103/µL, respectively (p for trend < 0.0001). Similar upward trends were also observed in NER, NHR, HOMA-IR, and METS-IR. In contrast, there is a downward trend in neutrophil-related markers and IR-related markers with the increase of circulating VLCSFAs. For example, the neutrophil counts in the 1st to 4th quartile groups of lignoceric acid (C24:0) were 4.16 ± 0.06, 4.01 ± 0.05, 3.85 ± 0.05, and 3.86 ± 0.05 103/µL, respectively (p for trend < 0.0001). Similar downward trends were also observed in NER, NHR, HOMA-IR, and METS-IR. The eGDR levels in the 1st to 4th quartile groups of C24:0 were 7.62 ± 0.11, 7.99 ± 0.10, 8.36 ± 0.09, and 8.05 ± 0.08, respectively, showing an upward trend (p for trend = 0.0013).

The associations of circulating LCSFAs and VLCSFAs with neutrophil-related and insulin resistance-related markers are shown in Table 3. Compared with participants in the lowest quartile category of palmitic acid (C16:0), those in the highest quartile had 0.272 (95% CI: 0.120–0.424) 103/µL higher levels of neutrophil counts, 0.048 (95% CI: 0.014–0.081) higher levels of NER, 0.011 (95% CI: 0.007–0.015) higher levels of NHR, 1.342 (95% CI: 0.885–1.800) higher levels of HOMA-IR, and 4.023 (95% CI: 3.674–4.371) higher levels of METS-IR. Participants in the highest quartile category of C16:0 had a significant lower levels of eGDR (adjusted β: −0.386, 95% CI: −0.542 to −0.230) compared with those in the lowest quartile. On the contrary, participants in the highest quartile category of lignoceric acid (C24:0) had lower levels of neutrophil counts (adjusted β: −0.136, 95% CI: −0.286 to 0.014), NER (adjusted β: −0.054, 95% CI: −0.087 to −0.020), NHR (adjusted β: −0.016, 95% CI: −0.020 to −0.012), HOMA-IR (adjusted β: −0.540, 95% CI: −0.994 to −0.087), and METS-IR (adjusted β: −1.709, 95% CI: −2.080 to −1.337), compared with those in the lowest quartile. Participants in the highest quartile category of C24:0 had 0.264 (95% CI: 0.109 to 0.419) higher levels of eGDR compared with those in the lowest quartile.

Discussion

In this community-based cross-sectional study, circulating LCSFAs including myristic acid (C14:0), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), and margaric acid (C17:0) were positively associated with neutrophil-related inflammation and IR levels, whereas VLCSFAs including docosanoic acid (C22:0), tricosanoic acid (C23:0), and lignoceric acid (C24:0) were inversely associated with inflammation and IR levels.

Although SFAs have been considered harmful to cardiovascular health for many years, in fact, they are a general term for a large class of fatty acids that do not contain unsaturated double bonds in the carbon chain. Different types of SFAs have significant heterogeneity in sources, chemical structures, metabolic pathways, and biological functions [17]. Results from the Insulin Resistance Atherosclerosis Study (IRAS) have shown that C14:0 and C16:0 were positively associated with principal component (PC) 1 (a cluster of fibrinogen, white cell count, and c-reactive protein) and PC 2 (a cluster of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, TNF-α, and IL-18). C20:0 and C22:0 had an inverse association with PC 2, but no significant association with PC 1 [18]. The European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC)-InterAct study found that C16:0 was positively and C17:0 was inversely associated with c-reactive protein. Meanwhile, C14:0, C15:0, C18:0, C20:0, C22:0, C23:0, and C24:0 had no significant association with c-reactive protein in this study [19]. Plasma C20:0, C22:0, and C24:0 was all negatively correlated with high sensitivity c-reactive protein in the Nurses’ Health Study, while only C24:0 exhibited a statistically inverse association in the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study [3]. In the Cardiovascular Health Study conducted in the U.S. older adults, serum C20:0 was found to be negatively correlated with fasting insulin but not significantly associated with c-reactive protein or HOMA-IR. There was no significant association between serum C22:0 and c-reactive protein, as well as IR indicators. However, serum C24:0 had an inverse association with c-reactive protein, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR [20]. The overall trends of our findings generally align with those of the aforementioned studies, though there are some subtle yet noteworthy differences in specific details. In our research, all five LCSFAs displayed a significant positive association with NHR. Furthermore, with the exception of C18:0, the remaining four LCSFAs (C14:0, C15:0, C16:0, C17:0) exhibited a pronounced positive association with neutrophil counts. Notably, our study made a novel discovery that these five LCSFAs were positively associated with IR indices, specifically HOMA-IR and METS-IR. This finding has not been previously documented in those studies. We also found that among the four VLCSFAs, C20:0, in addition to showing a significant negative association with NHR, did not demonstrate any significant associations with other inflammatory markers or IR indices. In contrast, C24:0 showed negative associations with all inflammatory markers as well as IR indices.

Our findings revealed a consistent pattern indicating that LCSFAs are associated with higher levels of inflammatory and IR markers, whereas VLCSFAs show associations with lower levels of these markers, though some variability exists in the relationships between specific SFAs and individual inflammatory or IR indices. However, the mechanisms underlying these findings are not well understood. There exist certain differences in the sources between even-chain and odd-chain LCSFAs. Even-chain LCSFAs, such as C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0, can be obtained through both dietary intake and endogenous synthesis. For example, C14:0 and C16:0 are synthesized endogenously, with stearoyl- coenzyme A desaturase (SCD) acting as the rate-limiting enzyme for fatty acid synthesis [21]. Inhibition of stearoyl-coenzyme A desaturase 1 (SCD1) prevented diet-induced obesity and IR in a previous study[22]. C16:0 can stimulate the inflammatory signaling cascade by binding to and activating toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in adipocytes and macrophages, resulting in nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and c-Jun-NH2-terminal kinase (JNK) activation and cytokine production [23, 24]. Given that the endogenous synthesis of odd-chain LCSFAs, such as C15:0 and C17:0, is extremely restricted in the human body and they are predominantly sourced from dietary intake, these fatty acids can serve as valid biomarkers for dietary consumption, thereby holding more well-defined public health significance. Full-fat dairy products represent the primary dietary sources of C15:0 and C17:0 [25]. Although previous studies have suggested that odd-chain LCSFAs might be associated with a reduced risk of coronary heart disease and type 2 diabetes [26, 27], our current study reveals a positive association between these fatty acids and both inflammation levels and IR. This finding underscores the ongoing uncertainty regarding the health impacts of odd-chain LCSFAs, highlighting an area that warrants further in-depth investigation in future research.

Unlike the β-oxidation of LCSFAs, which is regulated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α, the β-oxidation of VLCSFAs is mainly regulated by PPARδ [3]. PPARδ has several inhibitory effects on inflammation including inhibition of NF-κB activation, induction of anti-inflammatory mediators such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and releasing anti-inflammatory corepressor B-cell lymphoma 6 (BCL-6) protein [28]. Work from animal models suggests that activation of PPARδ can increase HDL levels, reduce TG levels, and improve insulin sensitivity [29]. Studies have revealed the presence of VLSFAs in certain nuts, seeds, and their oils. Among them, peanut oil, macadamia nut oil, and rapeseed oil contain the highest total amounts of VLSFAs. Moreover, the consumption of peanuts and macadamia nuts shows a positive correlation with the levels of C22:0 and C24:0 [7]. Our findings regarding the potential benefits of VLCSFAs are generally in line with those of previous studies, suggesting that an appropriate increase in the intake of nuts and certain specific plant oils may be conducive to alleviating inflammation and improving IR. Future research is warranted to further elucidate the specific pathways or mechanisms through which VLCSFAs ameliorate inflammation and IR, thereby gaining a deeper understanding of the role of VLCSFAs in cardiovascular-metabolic health.

Strengths and limitations

As far as we know, no previous study has evaluated the associations of LCSFAs and VLCSFAs with neutrophil counts as well as newly proposed neutrophil-related ratios, which have important role in the progression of atherosclerosis [12, 30]. For the first time, we conducted a comprehensive analysis and comparison of the associations of five types of serum LCSFAs and four types of VLCSFAs with three neutrophil-related inflammatory indicators and three IR markers in a representative population with a relatively large sample size. Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design cannot establish a causal relationship between SFAs and inflammatory and IR markers. Second, although we adjusted for potential confounding factors such as demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and dietary factors using NHANES’ comprehensive data collection, we cannot totally rule out the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as genetic predispositions or chronic stress may still influence our findings. Future studies incorporating polygenic risk scores are warranted to disentangle these effects. Third, we excluded a substantial number of participants with missing serum fatty acid measurements, which may introduce selection bias. To minimize the potential impact of such bias on our findings, we applied sample weights specific to the fatty acid dataset in the analyses. These specialized weights accounted for both the differential probabilities of selection into the subsample (due to stratified sampling or oversampling strategies) and the additional nonresponse associated with laboratory test participation. By adjusting for these factors, the weighted analyses aimed to enhance the representativeness of the study population and improve the validity of the inferred associations. Fourth, erythrocyte fatty acids are not available in this study, and serum fatty acids may not reflect long-term exposure.

Conclusions

Our study revealed distinct associations between serum SFAs of varying chain lengths and metabolic-inflammatory markers. Specifically, LCSFAs (including C14:0, C15:0, C16:0, and C17:0) exhibited positive associations with neutrophil-related inflammatory indicators (e.g., neutrophil count, NHR) and IR parameters (e.g., HOMA-IR, METS-IR). In contrast, among VLCSFAs, C22:0, C23:0, and C24:0 exhibited particularly strong inverse associations with both inflammatory indices and IR metrics. These findings suggest that chain length may modulate the metabolic and inflammatory effects of SFAs and support refining dietary recommendations to distinguish between SFA subtypes, rather than lumping them together as uniformly harmful.
