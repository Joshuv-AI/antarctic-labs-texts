Voice and Speech in Deep Brain Stimulation in Dystonia: A Retrospective Study, Systematic Review, and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Ian O. Bledsoe, Nutan Sharma, Marta San Luciano, Jill L. Ostrem, Nicole E. Buie, Gaia Arno, Mikayla Day, Kristina Simonyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRelatively little is known about voice and speech abnormalities and their changes after deep brain stimulation (DBS) in patients with dystonia.ObjectiveThe aim was to determine the incidence of speech abnormalities, including laryngeal dystonia, among patients with dystonia receiving DBS and to characterize their response to this neurosurgical neuromodulation.MethodsA three‐part study, including a retrospective analysis, systematic literature review, and meta‐analysis, was completed. Patients with dystonia receiving DBS at Massachusetts General Hospital and the University of California San Francisco between 2004 and 2024 were included in the retrospective study. Those with preoperative voice and speech abnormalities were grouped by type of alterations and response to DBS and evaluated using the Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) speech and swallowing subscores. A systematic literature review was conducted for studies of voice and speech outcomes in DBS for dystonia. A meta‐analysis of studies reporting BFM speech and swallowing subscores pre‐ and post‐DBS was performed.ResultsOne hundred eighty‐three participants received DBS for dystonia, and 34 (18.6%) had baseline voice and speech abnormalities. BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores improved with DBS in isolated (but not combined) dystonia, driven primarily by two subgroups. Twenty studies were included in the systematic literature review. A majority of included speech measures were unchanged with DBS; a smaller number showed improvement or worsening. Nine studies reporting pre‐ and post‐DBS BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores were included in the meta‐analysis, which showed improvement with DBS.ConclusionsData are mixed on response of speech to DBS in dystonia, though worsening of speech appears rare. © 2026 The Author(s). Dystonia is the third most common movement disorder after Parkinson's disease and essential tremor. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of both the globus pallidus interna (GPi) and subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an effective treatment for medically refractory cervical, segmental, and generalized dystonia. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Relatively little is published on the effects of voice and speech in the setting of DBS for dystonia, including the likelihood of improvement in speech alterations present before surgery or worsening of unaffected speech after surgery.

Voice and speech impairments in the context of dystonia may have multiple causes. One is from direct involvement of laryngeal muscles in dystonia (ie, laryngeal dystonia [LD]). Others include speech disruptions from dystonia in the tongue, mouth, jaw, or pharynx (ie, orofacial dystonia). Speech abnormalities may also occur after DBS surgery as an adverse effect of stimulation, 6 a common indication for reprogramming. However, little is known about the incidence of voice and speech abnormalities in patients undergoing DBS for dystonia, including relative proportions of those with laryngeal versus orofacial involvement.

We conducted a three‐part study to better understand the incidence and types of voice and speech impairments in dystonia patients undergoing DBS. First, we performed a retrospective analysis of patients undergoing DBS for dystonia at our two medical centers, University of California San Francisco (UCSF) and Massachusetts General Brigham (MGB), over the past two decades to determine the preoperative incidence of voice and speech alterations in this cohort. Among these, we evaluated the change in Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) speech and swallowing and speech disability subscores after DBS. Second, we performed a systematic review of published studies evaluating voice and speech changes after DBS for dystonia. Third, we completed a meta‐analysis of those studies in the systematic review that specifically reported pre‐ and post‐DBS BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores.

Patients and Methods

UCSF and MGB Combined Cohort Retrospective Review

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who received DBS of any brain target for either isolated or combined dystonia at UCSF and MGB between 2004 and 2024. Patients with a genetic form of dystonia were classified as (1) isolated dystonia if the genotype consisted of genes associated with a phenotype of dystonia as the only presenting neurologic abnormality (including TOR1A and THAP1) or (2) combined dystonia if the phenotype featured clinical symptoms in addition to dystonia (including KMT2B, ATP1A3, TAF1, PANK2, and ADCY5). 7 Patients lacking a genetic diagnosis with a phenotype of dystonia without other neurologic abnormalities were classified as isolated dystonia. Patients with secondary/acquired (eg, cerebral palsy or poststroke) dystonia were excluded.

To calculate the incidence of voice and speech abnormalities in patients with dystonia undergoing DBS, we surveyed all patients who had available preoperative BFMDRS evaluations and extracted data of patients who had available speech and swallowing movement and speech disability subscores.

All patients with preoperative speech abnormalities, except one, had preoperative videotaped examinations, which were reviewed for this study (I.O.B., N.S., K.S.) to characterize voice and speech impairments based on auditory characteristics and the visual appearance of movements. A chart review was conducted to classify speech alterations for one participant who lacked a preoperative video. Speech impairments were assigned to the following categories: (1) LD, (2) jaw or orobuccolingual dystonia‐related speech changes, (3) combined LD + orobuccolingual dystonia‐related speech changes, and (4) other dysarthria, encompassing complex speech disturbances, such as slurring, that were not fully explained by dystonia of speech effector muscles.

Preoperative BFMDRS speech and swallowing movement and disability subscores were compared to the 12‐month postoperative subscores using a repeated‐measures linear model. Speech impairment type and sex were included as between‐subjects factors, and age as a covariate at an overall significance level of 0.025. The same model was used separately for the isolated and combined dystonia cohorts, respectively. Post hoc tests compared differences among speech impairment types, adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Šidák method. If a patient had postoperative data at multiple time points, that closest to 12 months postoperatively was selected.

Systematic Literature Review

A keyword search to identify DBS studies in dystonia patients reporting voice and speech outcomes was conducted using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). 28 Sources queried through the Covidence software were EMBASE, Medline, PubMed, and PsychInfo databases. The following query was used: “((((((spasmodic dysphonia) OR (laryngeal dystonia) OR (spastic dysphonia) OR (dystonia) OR (dysphonia)) AND ((exp speech) OR (exp voice) OR (exp oral communication) OR (speech) OR (spoken) OR (speak) OR (vocal* phonation) OR (voice) OR (oral)) AND ((DBS) OR (Deep brain stimulation) OR (brain depth stimulation) OR (brain excit*) OR (brain stimulat*))) NOT (Review[Publication Type])) NOT (Case Reports[Publication Type])) AND (Humans[Filter])) AND (Adult[Filter]).”

The resulting article titles and abstracts were screened to determine their relevance according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) dystonia patients receiving DBS; (2) an original peer‐reviewed article in English; (3) no review, meta‐analysis, conference paper, or case study; (4) studies with cohorts of more than 3 patients; and (5) inclusion of a voice/speech measure. The full‐text review of selected articles was conducted to extract the following data: (1) study design (longitudinal/cross‐sectional, prospective/retrospective, presence of control group); (2) cohort size; (3) subject demographics (age, disease duration, native language); (4) DBS stimulation parameters at the time of data collection (stimulation ON or OFF, laterality, stimulation patterns); and (5) collected speech data (perceptual/acoustic/self‐report or rating scales). Study quality and risk of bias were evaluated using the NIH/NHLBI Quality Assessment Tool for Before‐After (Pre‐Post) Studies With No Control Group, selected because the majority of studies used single‐arm before‐after designs. 8

Two statistical analyses were conducted. First, an exploratory one‐sample χ2 goodness‐of‐fit test against equal proportions was performed to evaluate the distribution of outcomes of study measures (improvement, no change, or worsening) without a priori assumptions. Second, a targeted binomial test was conducted to compare frequency of improvement and worsening as the most clinically relevant distinction.

Meta‐Analysis

The subset of articles identified in the systematic literature review that specifically reported pre‐ and post‐DBS BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores was included in a meta‐analysis. This measure was chosen as it was the only quantitative speech measure from the systematic review represented in more than two studies and, therefore, amenable to meta‐analysis. A random‐effects model was used with a pre‐/postintervention design, in which post‐op BFMDRS subscores were considered the “treatment group” and pre‐op BFMDRS subscores were considered the “control group” at an overall significance level of 0.05. Cohen's d was used as the measure of effect size. In studies in which multiple time points were included for the post‐DBS BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores, the data collected at the time point closest to 12 months postsurgery were used. A second analysis was performed using studies that included only patients with GPi DBS.

Results

UCSF and MGB Cohort Demographics

One hundred eighty‐three patients received DBS for dystonia at UCSF and MGB, of whom 34 (18.6%) were identified as having baseline voice and speech alterations. Of these, 25 had sufficient data for pre‐ and post‐DBS comparisons of voice and speech measures. One patient developed severe progressive ataxia and cerebellar degeneration in the years after DBS surgery and, therefore, was excluded from the final analysis due to substantial phenotypic deviation from the rest of the cohort. The final cohort included 10 women (41.6%) and 14 men (58.3%), with a mean age at surgery of 28.9 years. Seventeen patients (70.8%) had generalized dystonia, 5 (20.8%) craniocervical, 1 (4.2%) craniocervical and truncal, and 1 (4.2%) hemidystonia. The hemidystonia patient had no structural or acquired cause for dystonia identified on brain magnetic resonance imaging. The most common type of preoperative voice and speech disturbance was related to jaw or orobuccolingual dystonia (45.8%), followed by other dysarthria (29.2%), LD (16.7%), and combined LD and jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia (8.3%). Fifteen patients (62.5%) were classified as isolated dystonia and 9 (37.5%) combined dystonia.

The majority had unknown/no known gene mutation. Five had KMT2B‐related dystonia, and 1 each had dystonia related to TOR1A, TAF1, THAP1, ATP1A3, ADCY5, and PANK2 genes. Twenty patients (83.3%) received bilateral GPi DBS, with the exception of 2 (8.3%) with bilateral STN DBS, 1 (4.2%) with bilateral thalamic DBS, and 1 (4.2%) with unilateral left GPi DBS.

Isolated Dystonia Cohort Outcomes

The overall test of within‐subject effects in the repeated‐measures linear model for BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores showed a significant reduction (improvement) in mean scores post‐DBS compared to pre‐DBS (Fig. 1; F 1,9 = 9.049, P = 0.015) and a significant interaction between surgery response and type of speech abnormality (F 3,9 = 5.748, P = 0.018). However, post hoc tests showed no significant differences between any speech abnormality groups (LD vs. jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia, P = 0.940; LD vs. other dysarthria, P = 0.968; LD vs. combined LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia, P = 0.906; jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia related vs. other dysarthria, P = 1.00; jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia related vs. combined LD + jaw/orobuccolingual, P = 0.999; other dysarthria vs. combined LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia, P = 0.998). There was no significant interaction between response to surgery and sex (P = 0.813) or age at surgery (P = 0.516). Whereas post hoc testing failed to show definite differences in surgery response among the groups, paired t tests showed a significant reduction in BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscore post‐DBS in LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia group (P = 0.021) but not in the other groups (LD, P = 0.321; jaw/orolingual dystonia, P = 0.101; other dysarthria, P = 0.086).

To further evaluate whether this subgroup was driving the group‐level responses, the repeated‐measures linear model was recomputed while excluding the LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia speech abnormality group, showing no significant improvement in scores post‐DBS (F 1,8 = 3.009, P = 0.121). As an additional subgroup analysis, the 2 participants with STN DBS were excluded, and the repeated‐measures linear model was conducted for isolated dystonia participants with GPi DBS, showing no significant change in post‐DBS scores (F 1,7 = 3.656, P = 0.097). One participant (participant ID: 2; Table 1) was represented in both subgroups (STN target and LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia).

The within‐subject effects in the repeated‐measures linear model for mean BFMDRS speech disability subscores showed a significant reduction in post‐DBS (F 1,9 = 7.483, P = 0.023) and a significant interaction between DBS response and type of speech abnormality (F 3,9 = 11.577, P = 0.002). Post hoc tests showed no significant differences between any two speech abnormality groups. There was no significant interaction between the response of speech disability subscores to DBS and sex (P = 0.07) or age at surgery (P = 0.515). The repeated‐measures linear model for BFMDRS speech disability subscores was recomputed after excluding the LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia group, showing no significant improvement post‐DBS (F 1,8 = 0.894, P = 0.121). When only patients with GPi DBS were analyzed, no significant change was observed in post‐DBS scores (F 1,7 = 2.608, P = 0.150).

DBS significantly improved the total BFMDRS motor scores (Fig. 1; F 1,9 = 8.125, P = 0.019) without significant interaction by type of speech abnormality (P = 0.253), age at surgery (P = 0.119), or sex (P = 0.590). DBS significantly improved total BFMDRS disability scores (F 1,9 = 10.553, P = 0.010), without significant interaction by type of speech abnormality (P = 0.068), age at surgery (P = 0.113), or sex (P = 0.817).

Combined Dystonia Cohort Outcomes

DBS did not result in a significant change in BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores (Fig. 1; F 1,4 = 0.54, P = 0.828), speech disability subscores (F 1,4 = 0.144, P = 0.724), total BFMDRS motor scores (Fig. 1; F 1,4 = 1.096, P = 0.354), or total BFMDRS disability scores (F 1,4 = 0.364, P = 0.579) in patients with combined dystonia. For the subgroup with GPi DBS only, there was no significant change with DBS in BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores (F 1,3 = 0.042, P = 0.850) or speech disability subscores (F 1,3 = 0.070, P = 0.808).

Systematic Literature Review: Patient Demographics and Outcomes

The keyword searches of EMBASE, Medline, PubMed, and PsychInfo yielded 454 articles (Fig. 2A). Of these, two were duplicates. Of the remaining 452, 2 reviewers read through titles and abstracts, selecting articles with mention of voice and speech outcomes, and determined that 390 were irrelevant according to inclusion/exclusion criteria. A full‐text review was performed on the remaining 62. Of these, 42 were excluded (18 conference papers, 10 with no voice/speech data, 7 case studies, 3 meta‐analyses/reviews, 2 letters/comments, 1 wrong patient population, 1 wrong intervention). The final analysis and data extraction were performed on 20 papers that met the inclusion criteria. Meta‐Analysis

Of the 10 studies in the systematic literature review that utilized BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores, 9 included sufficient data for inclusion in the meta‐analysis of this measure (Table 3). 2 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 23 One study was excluded from the final analysis as it reported percentage improvement but not specific values for BFMDRS subscores. 21 The calculated Cohen's d statistics ranged from −0.15 to −1.9, with an overall effect size of −0.85 (P < 0.001, 95% confidence interval −0.51 to −1.18) (Fig. 2C), demonstrating a significant reduction in the BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscore across studies in the post‐DBS compared to pre‐DBS time points. A second analysis was performed on seven studies that enrolled only patients with GPi DBS, also showing a significant reduction with calculated Cohen's d statistics ranging from −0.15 to −1.90 and an overall effect size of −0.87 (P < 0.001, 95% confidence interval −0.44 to −1.32).

Discussion

Our retrospective study found that 18.6% of patients undergoing DBS for dystonia at our centers had a preoperative voice or speech abnormality. Of these, only a minority had LD, whereas a greater proportion had speech impairments related to dystonic movements affecting the jaw or orofacial muscles or other forms of dysarthria. BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores and speech disability subscores significantly improved with DBS at the group level in the isolated, but not combined, dystonia cohort. However, improvement in the isolated dystonia cohort appeared to be driven especially by one subgroup of 2 patients with combined LD + jaw/orobuccolingual dystonia (1 of whom had received STN DBS) and particularly robust benefit to speech with DBS. The systematic review identified 20 relevant papers, which were heterogeneous in dystonia type, DBS target, and speech measure employed. The greatest number of speech measures showed no change with DBS, though there were 11 instances of a speech measure showing improvement and only 2 of worsening with DBS. The meta‐analysis of studies reporting pre‐ and post‐DBS BFM speech and swallowing subscores showed significant improvement post‐DBS.

A problematic ambiguity in the BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscore is the combination of both speech and swallowing in the same measure. Due to the lack of granularity of the BFMDRS assessment, its improvement after DBS in both our retrospective analysis and meta‐analysis could indicate improvement in only speech, only swallowing, or both. Our retrospective study also examined the BFMDRS speech disability subscore, which is more specific to speech. Its improvement in the isolated dystonia cohort corroborated speech improvement in this group. As noted, however, 2 participants with LD + jaw/orolingual dystonia and particularly robust response of both these measures to DBS appeared to drive positive results in the groupwise analysis, which lost significance with their exclusion. The groupwise analysis also lost significance when the 2 patients with STN DBS were excluded, though one of these patients was represented in both groups (STN target and LD + jaw/orolingual dystonia speech disturbance), creating ambiguity as to which was the more relevant factor.

It is important to emphasize that only a minority of patients in our retrospective analysis had LD as the cause of their speech difficulty, and none had focal LD. All patients were receiving DBS for segmental or generalized dystonia, and those with LD had it as part of a broader phenotype. We did not find a significant reduction in BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscores in the LD group; therefore, our groupwise results should not be interpreted as an indication for DBS to target isolated focal LD.

No benefit was found for any of the speech measures in the combined dystonia cohort. This mirrored overall response to DBS in the BFMDRS motor and disability subscores, with no improvement in the combined dystonia cohort but significant improvement in the isolated dystonia cohort.

The meta‐analysis of BFMDRS speech and swallow subscores showed a pattern similar to the isolated dystonia cohort in the retrospective analysis, with significant reduction after DBS but variability among included studies. As only two of the included studies reported speech disability subscores, this additional measure could not be evaluated statistically, limiting the ability to determine whether improvement in speech or swallow subscores led to improvements in these subscores. Like our retrospective cohort, meta‐analysis studies included a variety of dystonia phenotypes and DBS targets.

A majority of speech measures in the systematic review showed no change with DBS and confirmed divergence of outcomes from equal proportions. “Improvement” was more frequent than “worsening” in statistical comparison. An important caveat is that many contributing studies included multiple measures, and the observations can thus not be interpreted as independent, which may overstate significance. Nonetheless, a descriptive evaluation of the results shows a majority of speech measures with no change, fewer with benefit, and only two with worsening. No change in speech with DBS could have different interpretations depending on the population. For participants with no speech abnormality pre‐DBS, a lack of change in speech with DBS is a desirable outcome due to the possibility of stimulation‐related speech changes as an adverse effect. Conversely, for participants with speech abnormalities as part of their dystonia pre‐DBS, no change in speech with DBS could be interpreted as a failure of stimulation to improve speech problems associated with dystonia.

Only two speech measures showed worsening in one study each, including speech fluency 6 and the number of speech pauses. 10 Negative effects of GPi DBS on speech have commonly been associated with inadvertent spread of stimulation to corticobulbar fibers in the internal capsule, 26 though others have attributed isolated cases of speech changes to other mechanisms, including changes to pallido‐thalamo‐cortical interactions. 9 One takeaway from these results is some reassurance that worsening of speech with DBS appears uncommon in the context of dystonia, which may be an important consideration when counseling patients about expected clinical outcomes with DBS.

Strengths of our retrospective study include the direct review of speech abnormalities in all patients but one in preoperative clinical videos, allowing more accurate characterization of speech abnormalities than by chart review. We were limited, nonetheless, by the fact that speech was not systematically evaluated using a standard protocol in these videos that were recorded as part of pre‐DBS clinical evaluation at our centers and not within a prospective study. Postoperative videos were not available for the majority of patients, and we were unable to characterize features of speech changes in detail in response to DBS. Although the results of this study do not directly inform recommended voice and speech protocols for DBS, the previous update of a multidisciplinary panel regarding LD can serve as a starting template. 27 Specific optimal protocols for speech and voice evaluations in the context of DBS require additional systematic and prospective research.

Due to the rarity of dystonia and the limited number of patients undergoing DBS, our retrospective cohort represents a relatively sizable number. The use of a heterogeneous cohort, including patients with variable genotype, phenotype, and DBS target, may limit interpretation in specific patient populations. Additionally, the BFMDRS speech and swallowing subscore offers limited detail regarding the characteristics of speech difficulties and does not distinguish between speech and swallow effects.

Additional research is needed on speech abnormalities in dystonia, in general, and their responses to DBS, in particular. Prospective, systematic, and formal evaluations of speech in dystonia using more precise voice and speech measures would allow for a better understanding of how different speech abnormalities, genotypes, and phenotypic features respond to DBS in this clinical population.
