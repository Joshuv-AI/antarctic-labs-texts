Prevalence and clinical significance of distomolar and paramolar teeth

Introduction

The presence of teeth exceeding the normal number in the dental arch is defined as supernumerary teeth [1]. Supernumerary teeth represent a developmental dental anomaly with varying prevalence reported in the literature. Among these, paramolar and distomolar teeth are relatively rare forms that occur in the molar region but outside the normal dental sequence [2]. Paramolars are generally found on the buccal or lingual side of molars, while distomolars are positioned behind the third molars. Paramolar teeth arise as small, rudimentary tooth-like structures with a distinct crown and root morphology, on the buccal or palatal side of the molars, and can contribute to an increase in the total number of teeth [3]. Although these teeth may not cause symptoms, they can interfere with normal dental eruption, alignment, and may even lead to complications such as cysts or impaction [4, 5]. The exact origin of these teeth remains unclear, but theories such as excessive dental lamina activity or evolutionary remnants have been proposed [6]. Paramolar and distomolar teeth, which are usually detected incidentally in routine radiographs, can cause difficulties during orthodontic, surgical, or prosthetic treatment planning and should be carefully evaluated due to potential complications [7–9].

Knowing the frequency of these anomalies and their potential effects is important for dentists to provide a smooth treatment plan [1, 10]. Studies conducted on different populations and ethnic groups in the literature have observed varying rates of prevalence, possibly due to genetic or environmental factors [2, 11, 12]. There are still conflicting ideas about the etiology of paramolars and distomolars. Some studies suggest a genetic origin, while others point to environmental stimuli or localized abnormalities in the dental lamina during development. Supernumerary teeth can occur in isolation or in association with certain syndromic conditions [13, 14].

This study aims to investigate the prevalence, clinical significance, and potential implications of paramolar and distomolar teeth in a group of Turkish patients.

Materials and methods

Population and study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study examined digital panoramic radiographs made between May 2024 and May 2025 at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Faculty of Dentistry, Kahramanmaras Sütcü Imam University. A total of 10,000 panoramic radiographs (5097 male- 4903 female) of patients aged 18–65 were included in the study. Radiographs were evaluated between July and October 2025 by two oral and maxillofacial radiologists with 4 and 8 years of experience, respectively. To minimize observer fatigue and maintain diagnostic accuracy, evaluations were performed in multiple sessions, with approximately 100–200 radiographs reviewed per session. To reduce bias due to visual fatigue, readings were spread across multiple sessions with controlled daily workloads and scheduled breaks. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which showed a high level of agreement (κ = 0.87).

Radiographic evaluation

All radiographs were evaluated independently by two experienced oral radiologists. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached by joint discussion. Supernumerary teeth located distal to the third molars were classified as distomolars, while those situated buccal or lingual/palatal to the molars were defined as paramolars.

Imaging protocol

Panoramic radiographs were retrospectively retrieved from the digital archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology. All images were acquired using two digital panoramic imaging systems: KaVo OP 3D and Sirona Orthophos E. Radiographic acquisitions were performed according to the manufacturers’ recommended exposure parameters and standard patient positioning protocols routinely used in clinical practice.

All panoramic images were archived digitally and evaluated using dedicated imaging software (TurcaSoft) under standardized viewing conditions. Radiographic assessments were performed on high-resolution monitors in a dimly lit room to ensure optimal image interpretation.

Crowding and malocclusion status were evaluated based on the panoramic radiographs and patient records. Radiographic assessment was performed by examining dental alignment and the presence of overlapping or irregularly positioned teeth visible on the panoramic images. In addition, patient charts were reviewed to determine whether the individuals had a history of orthodontic examination or orthodontic treatment. Considering the inherent limitations of panoramic radiography in detailed occlusal analysis, the assessment was limited to radiographically detectable signs of crowding and available clinical record information.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed descriptively and expressed as frequencies and percentages. Inferential statistical analysis was not performed due to the descriptive nature of the study.

Results

Of the 10,000 patients examined (5097 male − 4903 female), distomolar teeth were identified in 37 individuals, a prevalence of 0.37%, as summarized in Table 1. Among these patients, 17 were female (45.95%) and 20 were male (54.05%). A total of 53 distomolar teeth were observed. In terms of laterality, bilateral distomolars were present in 16 patients (43.24%), while unilateral distomolars were present in 21 patients (56.76%). In terms of anatomical location, distomolar teeth were present in the maxilla only in 23 patients (62.16%), in the mandible only in 12 patients (32.44%), and in both jaws in 2 patients (5.4%). Additionally, 42 (79.24%) of the 53 distomolar teeth examined were impacted. In 20 cases (37.74%), the presence of distomolar teeth contributed to malocclusion and crowding of the third molars, while in 33 cases (62.26%), the distomolar teeth were asymptomatic and not associated with any pathological condition.

Paramolar teeth were detected in 12 of 10,000 patients, corresponding to a prevalence of 0.12%, as summarized in Table 2. Among the 12 patients, 9 were male (75%) and 3 were female (25%). A total of 15 paramolar teeth were observed, with 3 patients having bilateral (25%) and 9 patients having unilateral (75%) paramolar teeth. Discussion

The present study investigated the prevalence and clinical significance of distomolar and paramolar teeth in a large sample of 10,000 patients. The overall prevalence of distomolars was found to be 0.37%, while paramolars were observed in 0.12% of individuals. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that supernumerary molars are rare entities, with prevalence rates ranging between 0.1% and 0.5% in different populations [15–17].

In our study, distomolars were observed slightly more frequently in males (54.05%) than in females (45.95%). Similarly, several studies in the literature have reported a higher prevalence of supernumerary teeth in men than in women [18, 19]. Gender percentages in different populations may be related to ethnicity. Anatomically, distomolars were more common in the maxilla (62.16%) in the study. Numerous previous studies have also noted that distomolars are more common in the maxilla than in the mandible [20–22]. In addition, distomolar teeth are often observed unilaterally and this has been supported by previous studies [22–24]. Similarly, the prevalence of unilateral distomolars (56.76%) was higher in this study. A significant proportion (79.24%) of distomolars were impacted, in agreement with literature stating that most distomolars fail to erupt due to lack of space or an aberrant eruption path [17, 18]. 37.74% of cases were associated with malocclusion or tooth crowding, while 62.26% were asymptomatic. This suggests that although these teeth are usually detected incidentally radiographically, they can sometimes contribute to tooth crowding or complicate third molar extraction.

In this study, the prevalence of paramolars (0.12%) was lower than the prevalence of distomolars, which is consistent with previous findings reporting a lower prevalence of paramolars. Paramolars were significantly more common in males (75%). Most paramolars were located in the maxilla (58.3%), again confirming the maxillary arch as the most common site of occurrence. The majority were unilateral (75%), and 46.6% were impacted. 40% of the paramolars were associated with malocclusion or third molar crowding, emphasizing that although they are usually asymptomatic, their presence can occasionally affect occlusal harmony and create plaque-retentive areas predisposing to periodontal issues.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. Its retrospective design and reliance solely on panoramic radiographs may have limited the detection of certain clinical findings. Additionally, the absence of clinical examination and three-dimensional imaging methods, such as cone-beam computed tomography, may have restricted a more detailed evaluation of supernumerary teeth. Future prospective studies incorporating clinical and advanced imaging techniques are recommended.

Conclusion

Although the majority of distomolars and paramolars in the present study were asymptomatic, their clinical significance should not be underestimated. Supernumerary molars may contribute to malocclusion, dental crowding, periodontal complications, caries due to plaque retention, or may complicate surgical procedures involving adjacent third molars. Therefore, careful clinical and radiographic evaluation is essential. Conservative follow-up may be sufficient for asymptomatic cases, whereas surgical removal should be considered in the presence of associated pathology, functional impairment, or orthodontic complications.

Prospective studies incorporating clinical examination findings, genetic analysis, and three-dimensional imaging techniques are recommended to further elucidate the etiopathogenesis and clinical effects of distomolar and paramolar teeth. Long-term follow-up studies may also help determine the most appropriate management strategies for asymptomatic supernumerary molars.
