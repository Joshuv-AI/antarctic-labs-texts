Exploring procrastination and anhedonia among medical science students at Jahrom University: a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Leili Mosalanejad, Mohammad Saleh Zerafat Jahromi, Samaneh Abiri, Mohammad Hossein Zareian, Atefeh Karamzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical education at the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences is associated with strong academic pressures and psychosocial stressors because of procrastination and anhedonia among medical students. It is crucial to comprehend these issues in order to implement effective strategies to enhance performance and improve medical students’ welfare.MethodsA total of 32 medical sciences students (16 preclinical and 16 clinical: 18 female and 14 male) enrolled in medicine-related programs such as medicine, nursing science, lab sciences, or allied health disciplines participated in 32 semi-structured interviews designed to generate maximum variation sampling. The results were analyzed stepwise employing Braun and Clarke’s technique for thematic analysis to facilitate trustworthiness of the findings.ResultsFive themes and 11 subthemes appeared: demotivation to academics (job insecurity), content dissatisfaction (too theoretical curriculum and lack of application to reality), ineffective learning processes (inability to interact and inappropriate conduct by professors and staff members or administrators), structural difficulties (planning deficiency and poor training skills or capacities), and difficulties for students (time management and emotional pressures).ConclusionsBoth procrastination and anhedonia result from interrelated personal, educational, and institutional issues. Intervention strategies designed for each context, such as interactive learning techniques, stepwise curriculum development, cost-effective peer support programs, and easy access to counseling assistance, are required for the improvement of independent student motivation and psychological well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08426-7.Keywords: Procrastination, Anhedonia, Medical education, Academic performance, Motivation

Background

Medical education at the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences is highly demanding in terms of requiring learners to cope with high information and substantial workloads [1]. This high-stress setting often introduces medical learners to numerous psychosocial and academic stresses that have negative impacts on their mental health status and overall performance, thereby limiting their ability to render high-quality care to patients [1, 2]. Poor mental health status among medical learners predicts or affects healthcare outcomes [3].

Conceptual framework

Both procrastination and anhedonia have their conceptual foundations grounded in self-regulation failure and reward dysfunction at the neurobiological level. While procrastination indicates poor emotional regulation and self-regulation deficits [4], anhedonia is associated with dysfunction in reward-related dopamine pathways, leading to demotivation and loss of pleasure [5]. Both represent components of a cycle of disengagement where learners put off academic pursuits while concurrently suffering a loss of vigor to derive interest from academics or unfocused activities themselves [6].

Academic procrastination—the irrational putting off of tasks even though one is aware of possible negative outcomes—and anhedonia—the decreased response to pleasant stimuli—are also widespread among medical students around the world [7, 8]. Systematic reviews have shown that 45%–60% of medical students suffer from chronic procrastination, while also experiencing anhedonia at rates as high as 35% during clinical rotations [8, 9]. with regard to Jahrom University specifically, note that regional socioeconomic pressures for employment and very high academic competition also exist.

The unsuccessful self-regulation theory assumes that procrastination is impacted by poor regulation of behavior and emotions and is further influenced by high levels of tasks and high exam pressures [10]. Anhedonia, one of the key symptoms of depression, is associated with chronic stress altering dopamine pathways to reduce academic involvement [5]. Contributing factors include personal (perfectionism and impulsiveness), institutional (pasasive learning and unreasonable assessment demands), and environmental (inadequate psychological facilities and highly competitive settings) dimensions [1, 11, 12].

Despite the existence of quantitative studies on procrastination and anhedonia among Iranian medical students [13, 14], no study to date has adopted a qualitative approach to gain insights into the experience of medical sciences students at Jahrom University regarding these two concerns. Henceforth, this paper will attempt to use thematic analysis to provide region-specific information on how GCEs amplify these concerns to further expand the already-global database on this subject matter. This particular study examines both concepts simultaneously to create a conceptual framework to deal with these concerns at the policy and institutional level.

Methods

Study design

This is because this study adopted Braun and Clarke’s theme analysis technique to entail six stages to properly discern patterns among intricate learning and psychology events [15]. This is due to its flexibility to aptly determine the complexities associated with procrastination and anhedonia themes, aligned with approved conventions among medical studies [16, 17]. Data for this project were gathered between January and March 2024 at the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences located in Iran.

Setting and participants

Participants were medical sciences students at Jahrom University, namely medicine, nursing science, laboratory sciences, and allied health sciences. Purposive maximum variation sampling was conducted to benefit from variation in the field of study, level of study (preclinical vs. clinical), gender, and performance recommended for wide perspectives [16, 17].

Criteria for exclusion

Lack of accessibility for participation during the conduct of this study or unwillingness to give consent to participate is excluded. Purposive sampling was conducted to meet data saturation or where no new codes or themes were generated, giving 32 participants [18]. The participants consisted of 16 preclinical and 16 clinical students (18 female participants and 14 male participants). The disciplines included 12 medicine, 10 nursing, 6 laboratory sciences, and 4 allied health disciplines.

A medical education researcher and expert in qualitative methods designed this study and conducted interviews to analyze its findings. The study adopted reflexive journaling to reduce bias among its findings by assuming nothing while maintaining emotional preparedness to influence or alter its processes of investigation [19]. Another independent coder and skilled methodologist conducted peer debriefing for analysis rigor among its findings to avoid any bias associated with familiarity with the study setting among researchers.

Data collection

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted in Persian to maintain confidentiality and encourage honest answering. Interviews lasted 45–60 min and consisted of a guiding interview developed from published work [5, 10, 20]. Questions probed procrastination events (e.g., “Tell me of a time you put off studies and why”), feelings of anhedonia (e.g., “How have your feelings of pleasure or interest in your studies or activities changed?“), cause, consequences, and strategies for management. Pilot-testing with three undergraduate students helped to further adapt these questions for clarity and differences between cultures.

All interviews were recorded audibly after seeking informed consent from participants and transcribed verbatim before being translated for analysis to avoid any misinterpretation during the translation or back-translation processes. Field notes also provided information on non-verbal communications such as the emotional expressions of participants or environmental elements. Trustworthiness.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jahrom University of Medical Sciences (Approval code: 404000106). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki ethical standards (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/). Participants provided written informed consent after a briefing on the study purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures. Participation was voluntary, and students were assured of their right to withdraw without consequences. Confidentiality was maintained through anonymized transcripts and secure data storage on password-protected servers. All participants consented to the publication of anonymized findings.

Discussion

Integration of the findings with the existing literature

This study confirms that procrastination and anhedonia among students at the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences arise from interconnected individual, academic, and structural factors, a finding which is in line with other studies previously conducted on this subject matter. However, our findings showed a higher degree of demotivation and psychological distress compared to global findings, perhaps due to the particular socioeconomic context in Jahrom.

Academic demotivation and career uncertainty

The main theme of demotivation related to job insecurity is supported by the findings of Wang et al., who report that procrastination is heightened through career uncertainty [22]. Jahromi’s regional context—economic constraints and limited job opportunity—increase such concerns beyond levels reported in more economically stable contexts [23]. This supports the idea that, in resource-poor contexts, procrastination is not simply a failure of self-regulation but a rational response to systemic barriers; therefore, interventions should address both student behavior and institutional structures.

Disengagement with overly theoretical content

This is supported by the evidence of Pereira et al. that passive pedagogy encourages procrastination. The lectures at Jahrom University reflect the status quo in medical education globally, with conventional approaches failing to engage digital-native students. The extent of disaffection, as portrayed here—students claiming that the content has “no real-world relevance”-surpasses even that reported in studies at institutions where problem-based learning has been integrated, and this therefore constitutes an urgent call for pedagogic reform.

Ineffective teaching and faculty behavior

Poor teaching styles, including non-interactive lectures, coupled with inappropriate faculty conduct, agree with previous research by Naushad et al. and Sönmez et al. that a negative educational environment in any respect can enhance the procrastination and demotivation of students [11, 12]. Of note, the extent of the reported faculty disrespect at Jahrom—including humiliation and absent feedback—seems more serious compared to similar studies [21], which points to an important issue in institutional culture requiring immediate action. This extends prior work by showing how the cultural and hierarchical aspects of Iranian medical education may intensify the power differential between faculty and students.

Structural obstacles and delay in practical training

Poor curricular planning and delayed clinical exposure, which were identified in this study as sources of excessive workload and insufficient practical training, have also been reported by other studies. Nonetheless, most of the specific challenges encountered in Jahrom, such as inadequate access to cadavers and compressed examination schedules, represent the resource constraints characteristic of universities in developing regions. This situates procrastination partly as an environmental rather than purely an individual problem, challenging deficit models that locate the problem solely within students.

Psychological distress and competitive environment

Student challenges, including procrastination and psychological pressure, align with neurobiological models linking chronic stress to disrupted reward systems and anhedonia [5]. These findings resonate with Romash’s observations of heightened anxiety and depression during stressful academic periods [24]. Jahrom’s intensely competitive environment, characterized by constant peer comparison, aligns with Khurshid et al.’s findings that social comparison intensifies stress and procrastination [25]. However, the reported severity of psychological distress at Jahrom—described as “severe anxiety”-may reflect limited mental health service availability compared to well-resourced institutions [21].

This study found that, within Iranian medical education, economic hardship, limited practical training opportunities, and inadequate mental health resources intensify procrastination and anhedonia. Contributing to the global literature, we highlight how local socioeconomic and institutional barriers intensify individual regulatory failures. Theoretically, we contend that procrastination within resource-constrained settings expresses personal self-regulation difficulties and even rational responses to systemic constraints, and thus contests Western models of individual responsibility alone.

Limitations

This single-institution study at Jahrom University may limit the generalizability to universities with different academic and cultural environments, although the detailed contextual description facilitates the transferability assessment. While qualitative data provide rich insights, future studies should incorporate quantitative measures to enable triangulation and assess intervention effectiveness. The dual role of the primary researcher as interviewer and analyst, though rigorously managed through reflexivity and peer debriefing, may introduce subtle biases. Longitudinal research tracking students across training stages would strengthen the understanding of procrastination and anhedonia trajectories.

Conclusions

This study has explained that interconnected personal, pedagogical, and institutional factors, intensified by regional socioeconomic constraints, cause procrastination and anhedonia among Jahrom University students of medical sciences. Evidence-based and contextually suitable interventions are required for.

Emphasize respectful, interactive teaching with case-based learning and small-group discussions. Offer structured academic and emotional support, not just assessment.

Operate curriculum designs that show a balance between theoretical and practical content through phased reforms. Ensure that workloads are manageable through systematic auditing. Provide for earlier clinical exposure using innovative solutions for resource limitations.

For this, institutional leaders should integrate accessible psychological support services, establish peer mentoring programs, and commit resources to faculty development focused on student-centered pedagogy and respectful communication.

Realize that student procrastination in resource-constrained contexts is symptomatic of systemic barriers that require structural solutions to supplement individual interventions. Advocate for increased mental health resources and career pathway development in the medical sciences.

These educator-focused strategies can directly enhance students’ motivation, reduce anhedonia, improve academic performance, and ultimately better prepare compassionate, competent healthcare professionals. Intervention effectiveness should be studied in future research, and such phenomena should be explored across diverse Iranian medical education contexts.
