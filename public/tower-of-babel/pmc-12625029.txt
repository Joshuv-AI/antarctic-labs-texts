Decision aids for the care of patients with multimorbidity: a systematic review
Authors: Yuanxin Chen, Rui He, Zhiyi Chen, Yang Bai, Chen Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with multimorbidity face complex decision-making needs, including decreasing polypharmacy burden, maintaining physical functioning and prolonging life. Patient decision aids (DAs) have been developed and implemented as clinical tools to support complex decision-making in the management of multimorbidity in primary care.AimThis study aimed to identify DAs developed for patients with multimorbidity, describe their characteristics, and assess their quality, feasibility, and effectiveness.MethodsCitations from searches of nine databases were screened by title and abstract, and then full text articles were subsequently identified and screened. Any article utilising a DA for patients with multimorbidity was eligible. The International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) checklist was used to assess the quality of DAs. The characteristics, quality, feasibility and effectiveness of DAs were summarized using descriptive methods.ResultsIn total, 10 articles including six DAs were included. The DAs were categorised as targeting either general multimorbidity or specific disease-combined multimorbidity. All included DAs need to be discussed and used together by general practitioners (GPs) and patients with multimorbidity. Four DAs focused on goal setting or preparing for physician-patient conversation in primary care settings. According to IPDAS evaluations, only one DA was rated as high-quality; the others were considered substandard, largely due to a lack of detail on decision trade-offs. Two DAs demonstrated good feasibility, but one showed feasibility only among clinicians and the other only among patients. For the effectiveness of DAs, two DAs was evaluated. One based on the quality of communication and collaboration in encounter and adherence to prescription medication, and the other the other based on the overall level of SDM or in patient-reported outcomes (such as decisional conflict). The evidence of effectiveness was very limited in the current studies.ConclusionsThis study identified six DAs for patients with multimorbidity, five of which lacked descriptions of the consequences of the options and their positive and negative features. The evidence for feasibility and effectiveness of current DAs is limited. High-quality experimental research should be conducted in the future to verify effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03082-8.

Background

More than one-third of adults and two-thirds of older adults globally live with multimorbidity (defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in the same individual), presenting a significant public health challenge [1–3]. Patients with multimorbidity, particularly older adults, often have complex decision-making needs and have to navigate numerous health-related decisions. Previous research indicates that these needs typically include alleviating discomfort, reducing medication burden, optimising complex prescriptions, maintaining physical functioning and social engagement, and extending life [4–6]. These decisions can encompass many aspects such as managing health behaviors and attitudes, selecting appropriate services and providers, and addressing social issues [7], all of which contribute to the complexity and difficulty of decision-making for this population [8]. Patients with multimorbidity utilize healthcare services in primary care more frequently than individuals with single conditions [9–11]. For healthcare providers in primary care, engaging in decision-making with these patients is often time-consuming and stressful [8, 12], potentially resulting in inappropriate treatment or care decisions.

Inappropriate treatment approaches or flawed decision-making processes can lead to adverse outcomes, including decision conflict, decision regret, decreased quality of life, and increased healthcare utilization such as repeated consultations or hospitalisations [13–15]. Therefore, healthcare providers should adopt a patient-centred approach that prioritises the individual’s overall health and well-being rather than focusing exclusively on single disease management [16, 17]. Previous research has shown that older patients with multimorbidity are often reluctant to express their preferences during consultations [18, 19]. To address this challenge, tools such as patients decision aids (DAs) can be implemented to facilitate communication and shared decision-making between patients with multimorbidity and their healthcare providers [8]. DAs can assist patients in understanding information, clarifying priorities, and collaboratively developing disease management plans with healthcare providers [20–22]. Given the challenges of managing multiple conditions within limited consultation time, patients with multimorbidity face difficulties in acquiring disease-specific expertise and self-efficacy for participatory decision-making, potentially leading to adverse outcomes [18]. These complexities highlight the mutual benefits of implementing DAs during clinical encounters in primary care.

Several systematic reviews have assessed the effects of DAs on patient outcomes across various healthcare domains. Most existing studies have focused on DAs designed for the treatment or screening of a certain disease or health condition, such as osteoporosis [23], early-stage breast cancer [24], or colorectal cancer [25]. However, multimorbidity represents a complex disease state rather than a single, well-defined disorder. No universal or standard treatment exists for patients with multimorbidity, as each individual may present with a unique combination of medical conditions. Therefore, single-disease DAs, which primarily address disease-specific clinical parameters, have limited applicability in multimorbidity care; they often overlook patient-centred outcomes such as preferences for treatment, patient knowledge, patient and carer priorities for care. This gap highlights an urgent need to develop DAs specifically for patients with multimorbidity, incorporating holistic outcomes and addressing complex decision-making needs.

In recent years, several DAs have been developed for patients with multimorbidity. However, these DAs have not been systematically classified and summarised. The quality and effectiveness of these tools remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to identify and review existing DAs for patients with multimorbidity and assess their current use in clinical practice and their effectiveness. Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the principles of the PRISMA statement (Supplementary Material 1) and Cochrane Handbook [26, 27]. Citations from searches before January 2024 of nine databases including Cochrane CENTRAL, EBSCO CINAHL, EMBASE, OVID MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Database and Chinese Biomedical Database were screened by title and abstract, and then full text articles were subsequently identified and screened. The complete search strategy was presented in the Supplementary Material 2. References of the included studies were also searched manually. In addition, grey literature was searched, including OpenGrey, ClinicalTrials.gov and WorldWideScience.org. Relevant repositories and DA collections, such as the Ottawa Decision Aid Library and the International Database for Support in Medical Choices, the Harding Centre for Risk Literacy, the University of Laval decision boxes and Option Grid, were also searched. No language restrictions were implemented in the database searches. The review protocol had been registered to PROSPERO (CRD42024519374).

Eligibility criteria

Any article that utilises a DA for the care of patients with multimorbidity was eligible. DAs that focused exclusively on the management of only two pre-specified conditions (e.g., hypertension and coronary heart disease) were excluded, as they represent dyad disease management rather than multimorbidity management. Instead, the review included DAs for ‘specific disease-combined multimorbidity’. This category referred to interventions designed for a broader set of conditions, typically three or more, such as those within a particular disease category (e.g., cardiovascular-metabolic diseases) or a cluster of commonly co-occurring comorbidities (e.g., diabetes combined with its various complications). Due to the fact that these DAs were typically developed to address health-related concerns associated with specific diseases, they may not be suitable for broader populations with multimorbidity. The types of study included quantitative studies, qualitative studies, mixed-method studies, and case reports. Reviews and conference abstracts were excluded. All types of DAs provided to patients with multimorbidity to support decision-making at any stage of the treatment process (including screening decisions and self-management decisions) was eligible, regardless of format (e.g., video, audio, paper-based, or web-based). The DA can be applied within any intervention, whether utilized independently or as an integral component of a complex intervention. Outcomes for assessing the effectiveness of DAs included decision-related outcomes (such as level of knowledge, decision conflict, decision regret, shared decision-making involvement, decision satisfaction, autonomy support, decision self-efficacy), health and behavioural outcomes (such as activation and adherence to treatment) and patient satisfaction, and outcomes for assessing the feasibility including ease in reading and time in minutes. There were no language restrictions for DAs. Studies involving patients under the age of 18 years were excluded. DAs used for end-of-life decision-making were not included.

Data extraction

Search management was performed using Covidence for deduplication and triphasic screening (title/abstract/full-text) conducted by three investigators following standardized protocols. Data extraction was carried out independently by two researchers. Extracted data included the title, author(s), year, country, characteristics of the DA, study design, outcome measures, comparators, and study outcomes. Any disagreements regarding article inclusion or data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus between the two researchers (YC and RH), with final decisions made in consultation with a third researcher (CY).

Assessment instrument

The risk of bias for the inclusion of randomised controlled trials was assessed using the version 2 of the Cochrane tool for assessing Risk of Bias in randomised trials [28]. Nonrandomised controlled trials were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomised Studies of Intervention tool [29]. Similar to previous systematic reviews, other types of studies were not evaluated in the present paper [30]. Two researchers (YC and RH) independently conducted the risk of bias assessment. Any disagreements regarding the risk of bias assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus between the two researchers (YC and RH), with final decisions made in consultation with a third researcher (CY). Each DA was reviewed using the criteria of the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS, version 4.0) checklist to assess its quality, which is recommended by ​National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline as an effective tool for assessing the quality of DAs [31, 32]. IPDAS included 44 items in 10 dimensions. These items consisted of 6 qualifying criteria, 10 certification criteria and 28 quality criteria. Qualifying criteria employed binary (yes/no) assessments, while certification and quality criteria utilised 4-point Likert scales (1–4, from strongly disagree to strongly agree). The scores of quality criteria quantified comprehensiveness rather than superiority of DAs. Dual independent researcher assessed the quality of DAs. After independent evaluation, two researchers (YC and RH) jointly reviewed and synthesized outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third independent researchers (CY) to reach consensus.

Strategy for data synthesis

Descriptive analysis was used to comprehensively analyse the characteristics of included studies and the characteristics of DAs such as type (such as video, audio, paper-based and web based), language, target population, main decision content and development methodology. The results of the DA quality assessment were also analysed using descriptive methods, offering a thorough and systematic evaluation. We conducted a descriptive analysis to summarize the feasibility and effectiveness of the included DAs. Feasibility was primarily evaluated through outcomes such as readability, acceptability, and comprehensibility. The assessment of effectiveness focused on key metrics including decision-related outcomes, health outcomes, and patient satisfaction. A quantitative meta-analysis was not performed due to the limited number of studies (n = 2) and the significant heterogeneity in the intervention approaches, such as the distinct types of DAs used.

Results

Overview of studies

In total, 8233 articles were screened, and 23 full texts were reviewed. Finally, five articles were included [16, 33–36]. Another five articles were supplemented through manual retrieval [37–41]. Ultimately, 10 publications presenting six DAs were included in the study. Some included articles described the same DA, such as three articles describing the ICAN discussion aid [37, 38, 41]. Study characteristics

The including studies were two nonrandomised trials [34, 37] and one cross-sectional study [41]. The other seven studies were DA development studies [16, 33, 35, 36, 38–40], with five of them having qualitative components [16, 33, 35, 38, 40]. The studies included in this review were conducted in the UK (n = 3), USA (n = 3), Netherlands (n = 2), Australia (n = 1) and Canada (n = 1).

Study quality

Nonrandomised controlled trials were categorised as having a high risk of bias [34, 37]. The main risks of bias were due to confounding, loss to follow-up and measurement of outcomes. The evaluation results of these two articles were presented in Supplementary Material 3.

Target user

The included DAs can be categorised into two types: DAs targeting individuals with general multimorbidity [16, 35, 38, 39] or complex care needs [40] and DAs tailored for patients with specific disease-related multimorbidity (such as stroke survivors with multimorbidity) [33]. In all the included DAs, individuals who met the criteria were middle-aged and older. And all included DAs need to be discussed and used together by general practitioners and patients with multimorbidity.

Development methodology

The theoretical framework and guidance employed in the development of the included DAs were not uniform, but they all adhered to a patient-centred design, which involves patients in the development of the DA. The theoretical framework and guidance included the IPDAS checklist [42], the EBCD Australia Toolkit [43], the Ottawa Decision Support Framework [44], the interprofessional shared decision-making conceptual model [45], the user-centred design [46], the dynamic model of SDM with frail older patients [35] and the Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis [47].

Content

Four DAs (My well-being journal [16], patient brochure [35], ICAN discussion aid [38] and Grest [39]) did not focus on specific treatment choices. The targets of these DAs included setting goals about healthcare, preparing for conversation with doctors and taking ownership for the decisions about whether to participate in case management. Two paper-based DAs (My well-being journal [16] and PtDA for case management [40]) are typically divided into several sections consisting of an introduction, a main text, a conclusion with contact and author information and some other patient experience about actual decision-making (such as the health problems that the patient is facing). Other paper-based DAs (patient brochure [35] and ICAN discussion aid [38]) are brief and composed of some open-ended questions (such as what issue would you like to discuss with the doctor?). The contents of two computerised DAs (DOTT [33]and Grest [39]) could not be evaluated due to lack of access to full versions.

Assessment of DA quality

The IPDAS checklist revealed that only one DA (PtDA for case management [40]) met al.l the qualifying criteria and provided comprehensive choice. Five DAs (My well-being journal [16], patient brochure [35], ICAN discussion aid [38], DOTT [33] and Grest [39]) were deemed to have poor quality due to their failure to provide the pros and cons of decisions. The detailed evaluation results of assessment of DA quality were presented in Supplementary Material 4.

Effectiveness and feasibility of DAs

Two nonrandomised controlled trials evaluated the effectiveness of two DAs (patient brochure and ICAN discussion aid) [34, 37]. For the feasibility of ICAN discussion aid, clinicians (n = 11) rated feasibility via 5-point Likert scale (ease of use), with 62% reporting positive responses. Patient feasibility was not evaluated. For the effectiveness of the ICAN discussion aid, the consultation care measure (CCM, a valid and discriminating tool to measure communication and partnership within a single encounter) was used to evaluate the quality of communication and collaboration between clinicians and patients, and adherence to prescription medications was also evaluated. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in CCM score. Furthermore, no significant differences were found in adherence to prescription medications. No other related outcomes (decision-related outcomes, health outcomes and patient satisfaction) were reported.

For the feasibility of patient brochure, 67% of patients (n = 56) and 63% of caregivers (n = 20) rated it as clear and informative; feasibility was not assessed among healthcare providers. For assessing the effectiveness of the patient brochure, after the intervention that comprised shared decision-making (SDM) training for geriatricians and a tool tailored for older patients and their caregivers, no significant differences were found in either the overall level of SDM or patient-reported outcomes (including match between preferred and perceived role in decision making of patients and informal caregivers, match between preferred and perceived involvement of patients and caregivers in their care, the level of perceived SDM and decisional conflict). No other related outcomes (decision-related outcomes, health outcomes or patient satisfaction) were reported.

Discussion

This systematic review is the first known review of DA for patients with multimorbidity. Previous reviews mostly targeted at patients with a single disease. Although a considerable number of DAs have been published and used, DAs for patients with multimorbidity are still relatively rare, and most of them (5 of 6) have poor quality.

The included DAs can be divided into two categories: for patients with multimorbidity [16, 35, 36, 38, 40] and for those with specific disease-combined multimorbidity (such as stroke survivors with multimorbidity) [33]. This review was not targeted a specific population, which is different with most previous studies, such as for a certain type of patient with cancer, patients in end-of-life and patients with a specific chronic disease (heart failure, diabetes or hypertension).

All the six DAs included in this study were developed and utilised in high-income countries (Australia, Canada, Netherlands, UK and USA). This result is consistent with the findings of a previous review [48]. High-income countries have more well-developed primary medical care service systems than low- and middle-income countries, and the health literacy level of their residents is higher [49, 50]. Meanwhile, the primary care services in numerous low- and middle-income countries are not well developed, and the implementation of shared decision-making is rather late. Thus, the promotion and use of DAs in these countries and regions may not be appropriate.

All included DAs were constructed on the basis of theoretical frameworks and established an evidence-based process. The IPDAS checklist created by IPDAS Collaboration, which was established in 2003, is frequently used in developing DAs and extremely influential and authoritative [31, 51, 52]. It is worth noting that most identified DAs fail to meet the criteria of IPDAS checklist, prompting us to critically consider the applicability and practicality of the criteria within the context of multimorbidity. The NICE guideline for shared decision-making recommends the use of IPDAS standards to ensure access to quality-assured DAs [32], but it does not mention multimorbidity. And we found two included DAs that aim to provide options (PtDA for case management [40]and DOTT [33]) were developed in accordance with the IPDAS standards. However, four included DAs for multimorbidity currently usually do not meet some of basic IPDAS standards, because they used open-ended questions instead of specific options for multimorbidity management or treatment. Thus, when designing a DA for open-ended questions or setting goals, it may be more appropriate to prioritize other frameworks over IPDAS standards. Based on the above considerations, the application of IPDAS standards may indeed encounter certain limitations in the context of multimorbidity. When developing DAs for multimorbidity, it is essential to carefully select an appropriate reference framework or set of criteria that aligns with the specific clinical problem or decision under consideration.

Many existing DAs (My well-being journal, patient brochure, ICAN discussion aid and Grest) are not specifically designed for specific decisions in the context of multimorbidity. While DOTT demonstrates efficacy in stroke-related multimorbidity care through recurrence risk modelling, its disease-specific focus limits broader applicability. The lack of specific treatment and care decisions constitutes a significant challenge in the design of DAs for patients with multimorbidity. The PtDA for case management seems to be a good example of DA for the general population of patients with multimorbidity with a specific decision. This DA aims at promoting to support these patients in their decision-making process to engage in case management [40]. Case management programs involve an interprofessional team (nurses, physician and social workers) and the patient to work together to evaluate needs, priorities and available resources [40]. This innovative DA introduces collaborative care frameworks, particularly multidisciplinary models, as decision-making foundations for developing DAs for patients with multimorbidity, especially for older patients.

While disease-specific decisions remain important for developing a DA for patients with multimorbidity, tools facilitating personalized care goals and clinician-patient communication demonstrate equivalent clinical relevance. Goal setting is a valuable strategy for promoting communication and shared decision-making, particularly amongst older patient with multimorbidity, aligning with the core principles of evidence-based medicine and person‐centred care [16]. Goal setting also requires multidisciplinary teamwork, including physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists, social workers and case managers [53]. Moreover, the DAs that promote conversations and discussions can clearly prioritise tasks and discussion topics, thereby alleviating the time burden on doctors and patients [34, 37, 38]. The DAs for goal-setting or promoting conversations and decision-specific DAs that have clinical significance and value are worth promoting and using.

The criteria of the IPDAS (version 4.0) checklist was used to assess quality [31]. Only one DA (PtDA for case management) met al.l the qualification criteria. For the 10 certification criterias, the average score of the six DAs was 18.7. Given that the included DAs did not target at disease screening, the scores of these DAs in the test dimension (for screening tests) were relatively low, which has an effect on the total score of the certification criteria. When developing a high-quality DA, developers should pay attention to the qualification criteria and certification criteria listed in the IPDAS checklist and provide specific decisions and their pros and cons. However, as mentioned previously, proposing specific treatments and care approaches is challenging due to the distinctiveness of multimorbidity. It considerably enhances the difficulty in designing a high-quality DA. For quality criteria, the disclosure dimension exhibited a high score ratio. After the test dimension was excluded, the dimensions with a low score ratio included the probability dimension, the information dimension and the evaluation dimension. Similar to other studies, the scores for Plain language dimensions were not high [54, 55]. When developing a high-quality DA, developers should consider as much relevant information from the quality standards as possible and use an intelligible and simple language.

A feasibility test was conducted in two DAs (ICAN discussion aid and patient brochure). The current feasibility test was conducted on one side only: patients or physicians. A comprehensive assessment of the feasibility of DA requires the joint participation of health providers and users, and it should cover multiple aspects such as readability, usage time and availability [56, 57]. The feasibility assessment of the DAs included in this study was incomplete. Both two DAs (ICAN discussion aid and patient brochure) underwent assessment of effectiveness. For the DA ICAN discussion aid, it was evaluated the quality of communication and collaboration in encounter and adherence to prescription medications, and the DA patient brochure was evaluated on the overall level of SDM or in patient-reported outcomes (such as decisional conflict). However, it showed no significance. More experimental studies must be conducted to independently verify the effectiveness of DAs. The findings of our study should be interpreted within the broader context of evidence on patient DAs. The 2024 update of the Cochrane review by Stacey et al. provides robust, high-quality evidence of the effectiveness of DAs in supporting patient-centred decision-making, particularly in single-disease settings [22]. Our study complements these findings by highlighting the critical importance and unique complexities of applying DAs to patients with multimorbidity. Specifically, our work underscores that while DAs are a well-established intervention for individual diseases, their adaptation and implementation for patients with multiple conditions necessitate a distinct evidence base and likely require different design considerations. Subsequent research could assess the feasibility and effectiveness of existing DAs to further explore the practical role and value of real clinical environment. Evaluation of the feasibility of DAs for patients with multimorbidity by health providers, users and informal caregivers and from multiple aspects, including readability, usage time and availability, is strongly suggested. Similarly, when assessing the effectiveness of DAs, comprehensively assessing outcomes from patient satisfaction, decision-related outcomes (such as level of knowledge, decision conflict, decision regret, shared decision-making involvement, decision satisfaction, autonomy support, decision self-efficacy) and health outcomes (quality of life, activation and adherence to treatment) is recommended.

Limitations

This review has some limitations. Firstly, the complete versions of two DAs (DOTT and Grest) were unavailable, which adversely affected their quality assessment and may have led to reduced scores in certain dimensions. Secondly, only two experimental studies were included. And the two types of DAs investigated in these trials differed substantially in terms of target user and development methodology, and the reported outcomes were heterogeneous. Consequently, as well as the issue of there being only to included trials, a formal quantitative or qualitative synthesis was not feasible. Finally, all the DAs included in this review were derived from high-income countries. These DAs may not be applicable in low- and middle-income countries due to the developing primary care services, the lower popularity of shared decision-making and the lower health literacy of resident [48].

Implications

This research found six existing DAs for patients with multimorbidity, and most of them had poor quality. When developing a high-quality DA for providing options, developers should pay attention to the qualification criteria and certification criteria listed in the IPDAS checklist. The PtDA for case management is considered to have good quality. This DA has the potential to help high-user patients with complex care needs make better choices concerning case management [40]. Although the four remaining DAs (My well-being journal, patient brochure, ICAN discussion aid and Grest) are regarded to have low quality, they can help clarify what patients with multimorbidity want to discuss with healthcare providers or GPs, thus playing an auxiliary role in their communication.

Future development of high-quality DAs for patients multimorbidity should (1) integrate IPDAS standards as a major development methodology for reference when developing a DA for providing options; (2) actively engage patients and caregivers in co-design processes; and (3) prioritize quantitative metrics like health-related quality-of-life and intrinsic capacity to address therapeutic decision gaps. These strategies would optimize shared decision-making implementation in complex primary care contexts.

The DA PtDA for case management utilising multidisciplinary collaboration show promise for the development of DAs for care of multimorbidity. Non-decision-specific tools like My Well-being Journal [16] and ICAN Discussion Aid [38] demonstrate clinical value through facilitating personalized goal-setting and provider-patient communication, having the potential for further development. In future primary care settings, integration and utilization of these evidence-based tools will facilitate patient-provider communication and shared decision-making processes and enhance patients’ ability to articulate health priorities and may achieve clinically significant outcomes.

High-quality experimental studies are important for validating the utilization of DAs. Future research should (1) Evaluate DA feasibility through multidimensional assessments (readability, time efficiency, accessibility) involving health providers, patients, and caregivers; (2) Measure effectiveness via patient-reported satisfaction, decision metrics (level of knowledge/conflict/regret/SDM engagement/self-efficacy/autonomy support), and clinical outcomes (treatment adherence, quality-of-life). Real-world implementation studies are critical to ascertain practical value.

Conclusion

This study identified six DAs for patients with multimorbidity, five of which lacked descriptions of the consequences of the options and their positive and negative features. All DAs need to be discussed and used together by GPs and patients with multimorbidity. Four DAs did not focus on specific treatment choices but rather aimed at setting goals for personalised care and preparing for conversation with GPs. Developing DAs for this patient population presents challenges given the complex nature of multimorbidity and its lack of specific treatment options. The evidence for the feasibility and effectiveness of current DAs is limited. High-quality experimental studies are recommended to explore the effectiveness and feasibility of current DAs for patients with multimorbidity in real-world primary care settings, which is crucial for validating their utilization.
