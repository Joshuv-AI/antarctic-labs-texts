Waiting List Survival Trends in Pediatric Lung Transplantation: A National Cohort Study
Authors: Elijah Roberts, Maya Sela, Chase Robinson, Zhi Mei Sonia He, Garrett Wortham, Kiran Thirukonda, Rodrigo Zea‐Vera, Abbas Rana, David S. Moreno McNeill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvancements in pediatric lung transplantation, including support devices, the Lung Allocation Score (LAS), and the subsequent Composite Allocation Score (CAS), have led to significant improvements in survival on the waiting list. Policy changes, like the removal of the Donation Service Area in 2017, aimed to further improve outcomes, but their impact in more recent years remains unclear. Our study examines recent waiting list survival trends and key factors affecting outcomes for pediatric lung transplant candidates.MethodsPediatric patients (n = 535) listed for lung transplantation in the OPTN Database were categorized into four eras based on year of listing: 2014–2017 (n = 182), 2017–2020 (n = 174), 2020–March 2023 (n = 134), and March 2023 onward (n = 45). Our primary outcome was death on the waiting list. Predictors of waitlist mortality were identified with multivariable logistic regression. Kaplan‐Meier curves with a log‐rank test were used to evaluate outcomes across eras. The Fine and Gray method was used to account for competing outcomes, including transplantation and removal from the waitlist due to clinical improvement.ResultsPoor functional status, ventilation at time of listing, African American ethnicity, and listing in region 1 were associated with increased waitlist mortality. One‐year survival increased from 2014–2017 to 2020–2023 (p < 0.05). When accounting for competing risks, Fine and Gray showed a declining incidence of waitlist mortality with each successive era.ConclusionWaiting list survival for pediatric lung transplant candidates has improved since 2014, with notable progress in the 2020–2023 era. The reasons for these improvements are likely multifactorial, and further studies are needed to evaluate their impact.

Introduction

Since the first reported pediatric lung transplant in 1987, lung transplantation has transformed into an increasingly viable therapy for patients with incurable lung disease [1]. While initially more popular, the incidence of combined pediatric heart‐lung transplants has decreased markedly over the past few decades, opening the door to study single organ transplants [2]. In recent years, advancements in pediatric lung transplantation, including support devices to bridge to transplant and the usage of the lung allocation score for determining lung status, have led to significant improvements in survival on the waiting list [3, 4]. In 2017, the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) replaced the Donation Service Area (DSA) with a 250 nautical mile radius around the donor institution as the primary geographical method of organ allocation [5].

While the waiting list survival improvements have been documented prior to the DSA change, the impact of this change in more recent years remains much less clear [2, 6]. According to the most recent Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network/Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (OPTN/SRTR) Data Report, which examines data within a 1‐year period, some factors, such as race and geographic location, were difficult to properly assess due to the extremely small number of pediatric transplant recipients every year [7]. Previous studies have dealt with specific populations of patients or specific variables such as pre‐transplant functional status, and none have broadly examined pre‐transplant outcomes following the allocation changes [8, 9]. For the past several years, a focus of lung transplant research has been to examine the changing indications for transplant in pediatric populations. Specifically, studies have examined the decreasing prominence of cystic fibrosis (CF) in lung transplant compared to other common indications such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and interstitial lung disease (ILD) [2].

This study aims to comprehensively examine pediatric lung transplant waiting list survival trends in light of recent changes to organ allocation to determine whether waiting list survival has continued to improve in recent years. We sought to compare waitlist outcomes of recent pediatric lung transplant recipients to those in previous eras of a comparable time frame, specifically looking for variables associated with death on the waitlist across eras and specific to certain populations, such as patients on life support. A more nuanced understanding of these outcomes will aid in transplant candidacy selection, optimizing future allocation strategies and improving patient care.

Methods

All patients under the age of 18 from 2014 to 2024 listed for lung transplantation in the OPTN Database were retrospectively identified for inclusion in the study and categorized into four eras based on year of initial listing, with candidates from March 8th, 2023 onward being grouped into the fourth era (CAS Era) and examined separately. The first three eras were the focus of this study's subsequent analyses. Exclusion criteria included multi‐organ transplant, previous lung transplant, previous transplant of another organ, or incomplete data entry for necessary variables such as transplant date. The characteristics of waitlisted patients were recorded at the time of listing, including demographic information, primary listing diagnosis, presence of diabetes as a secondary diagnosis, and others.

Eras

The three primary eras described for this study (Jan 1, 2014–Nov, 1 2017; Nov 2, 2017–Dec 31, 2019; and Jan, 1 2020–Mar 8, 2023) were intended to be roughly comparable, both in terms of time encompassed and number of patients transplanted, in order to provide adequate power for statistical analyses. The first era was selected as the reference era for multivariate regression to allow for trends to be best observed. The different eras were chosen to better compare policy changes, such as the aforementioned 2017 DSA change. The 2020 cutoff, while not associated with a specific policy change, was chosen to roughly correlate with the onset of the COVID pandemic, a significant event in the lung health of the general population. The specificity of the March 8th date cutoff is due to the introduction of the CAS to replace the LAS, with the two not being easily compared.

CAS Era

Because the LAS to CAS change occurred in March 2023, there were not enough patients in the March 2023–2024 time frame to study as its own full era (n = 45). For study purposes, these patients were only compared to the preceding era and were not included in comparative analyses.

Variables

The variables selected for analysis in this study were consistent with recent research and based on clinical experience. While all variables had over two‐thirds completion rate, two variables with less than full completion (functional status and LAS) were imputed using multiple imputation with predictive mean matching before analysis. Discrete variables were coded and studied in the same format as they were entered in the OPTN database. Continuous variables, such as BMI, were grouped into discrete ranges based on clinical experience in order to facilitate logistic regression analysis. Functional status, while normally measured from 0% to 100% in increments of 10%, was divided into 0%–50% and 60%–100% categories to achieve sufficient power to show significance.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX). Continuous variables were reported using mean (SD) values and were compared using the t‐test. Chi‐squared testing was used for categorical analysis.

Our primary outcome of interest was death on the waiting list at any time, as recorded in the OPTN database. Predictors of waitlist mortality were identified with multivariable logistic regression using factors that were significant (p < 0.05) on univariable analysis. Kaplan‐Meier curves with a log‐rank test were used to evaluate waiting list survival across eras. Because transplantation is more common than death while on the waiting list, traditional survival analyses can overestimate mortality risk. Therefore, the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to account for this competing risk, as well as the competing event of improvement warranting removal, in order to provide a more accurate estimate of the cumulative incidence of waitlist mortality.

Results

Study Population

The study population consisted of 535 pediatric patients listed for transplant who met inclusion criteria (307 female and 228 male, mean [SD] age, 10.2 [5.9] years), with 327 (189 female and 138 male, mean [SD] age, 11.3 [5.8] years) receiving a transplant within the study time period. The 45 patients listed after March 8th, 2023 in our dataset were too few to constitute a full era and were only examined in supplemental analysis, leaving the 490 in our 3 eras of interest. Similarly, the 27 patients transplanted after March 2023 were only included in the supplemental analysis, leaving 300 transplanted patients. Compared to the reference era (2014–2017), BMI significantly increased in 2020–2023 among listed and transplanted patients (p < 0.05). There were significantly fewer listed and transplanted patients with cystic fibrosis as an indication for lung transplantation in 2020–2023 (p < 0.05), while significantly more patients had “other” as the primary diagnosis (p < 0.05). Among patients who received a transplant in 2020–2023, significantly fewer were white (p < 0.05) and significantly more were on ECMO and ventilator at time of listing (p < 0.05). Notably, time spent on the waitlist has trended downwards (Table 1a, b).

Discussion

Our findings show a consistent improvement in pediatric waitlist outcomes over time, with roughly the same number of pediatric lung transplants being performed per year. The study did not definitively establish a significant change in waitlist mortality between 2014–2017 and 2017–2020, but this does not necessarily mean that the 2017 change lacked impact. The continuation of the trend into 2020–2023 suggests that the improvement may have been present earlier but was not statistically significant due to limited sample size. Additionally, fewer patients were listed in the 2020–2023 era, possibly contributing to the improving trend due to decreased competition for donor organs. Patient demographics, while largely similar from year to year, show a few key trends. Notably, the percentage of patients receiving a transplant because of cystic fibrosis has decreased markedly in the past decade. Previous studies have described a trend of improvement in patients indicated for transplant due to cystic fibrosis lung disease [10, 11]. They largely attribute this to the success of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulator therapy over the past decade, which likely constituted the majority of the patients who were taken off the waiting list due to improvement. Some concerns were raised that the trend might change following the COVID‐19 pandemic, but subsequent studies indicated that the trend seemed to hold fast following 2020 [7, 11]. Our data further confirm this, as transplantation rates for cystic fibrosis in recent years are significantly less than they were a decade ago. Additionally, there has been a commensurate increase in the number of candidates with “other” diagnosis. The concomitant increase of patient BMI and decrease of diabetes prevalence may be directly related to this. There has also been an increased proportion of ECMO usage among patients receiving transplants in recent years, indicating that organs are being allocated to sicker patients who need them the most. Our analyses suggest that overall survival on the waiting list is improving for ECMO patients in a similar manner to non‐ECMO patients. These improvements are supported by our Fine‐Gray analyses, which demonstrated significantly reduced subhazard for mortality in the 2020–2023 era, likely due to their increased priority on the waiting list. Our study also indicated a possible population and regional inequality in outcomes on the waitlist. Specifically, African American recipients were substantially more likely to experience death on the waitlist. There was some regional variation in outcomes, namely with worse outcomes in region 1, but due to the small number of pediatric transplant centers nationwide, this data has limited applicability when comparing regions. The most recent Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network/Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (OPTN/SRTR) Data provides no information about outcomes based on geographic location, so our study may provide some insight [8].

The size and comprehensive scope of our study offer greater clarity on many of these points. While a general upward trend in positive outcomes has been noted before, no studies have compared the most recent data to comparable eras in a comprehensive manner. One study noted that a potential modifiable risk factor for patients on the waitlist was functional status at listing [9]. Our study seems to confirm this, as functional status is predictive of waitlist mortality. We also noted that functional status among transplant patients has not changed significantly over time. Even with the 2017 DSA change and the 2020 COVID pandemic, our eras did not contain significantly different LAS scores during the time that the LAS was in use.

Our study represents the most comprehensive and up‐to‐date analysis of waitlist outcomes for pediatric lung transplant patients in recent years. Our study corroborates and strengthens several previously established trends, such as those affecting cystic fibrosis and pre‐transplant LAS. It establishes with greater certainty the progressively better waiting list outcomes we can expect for patients using ECMO while also confirming its previously studied effectiveness as a bridge to transplant [12, 13]. As previous studies have shown, the improvements leading to better access to transplant, and subsequent better waitlist outcomes, translate into better survival outcomes overall [14]. Our study highlights the dynamic nature of the field of transplantation, as patient presentations, organ allocation, and time spent on the waitlist are all very different from what they were 10 years ago. The exact forces driving these trends are multifactorial, especially given varying populations. In terms of the overall increase in positive outcomes, much of the improvement is likely due to advancements in mechanical support devices and ICU care, although further studies are needed to evaluate their exact impact.

It is important that clinicians be up to date and familiar with the ever changing populations and trends present in pediatric lung transplantation. While the yearly reports offer a tremendous amount of useful information about the current state of transplantation, such as patient populations and recent outcomes, it is important to examine the interplay of all of these factors over time to understand how they contribute to one another and can be improved [7].

Conclusion

Waiting list survival for pediatric lung transplant candidates has improved since 2014, with notable progress in the 2020–2023 era. Waiting list survival has also seen marked improvement among candidates on ECMO, which is becoming an ever more viable method for sustaining patients until transplant. The risk factors influencing these survival rates are varied, with race, region, and functional status all showing association with waitlist outcomes. Despite the improvements in waiting list survival, previously noted regional disparities may persist, even following allocation changes. These evolving trends are key to understanding the modern landscape of pediatric lung transplantation and emphasize the need for further study into the specific factors driving them and how best to address and improve them.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include reliance on the accuracy of the recorded data, lack of generalizability to populations outside the United States, and a small sample size. Even when looking at all available data points, pediatric lung transplantation is a rare event, such that there is not always enough data to power a study capable of answering the desired question with sufficient granularity. Furthermore, the stratification of patients into eras may not fully capture the advancements taking place in clinical practices and treatment protocols nationwide. The 2020 onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic also poses a challenge. Despite only being coded as a primary diagnosis variable in the OPTN database, COVID‐19 almost certainly affected our study population beyond that, meaning a potentially major factor influencing pediatric lung health could not be fully accounted for. Due to the low number of post‐CAS patients in the OPTN 2024 Thoracic dataset, our study could not identify significant relationships between the post‐CAS era and preceding eras. The authors of this study intend to conduct a subsequent study using more recent data to compare pre and post CAS trends.
