Allocation of Heterogeneous Resources in General Lotto Games
Keith Paarporn, Adel Aghajan, Jason R. Marden

Abstract
The allocation of resources plays an important role in the completion of system objectives and tasks, especially in the presence of strategic adversaries. Optimal allocation strategies are becoming increasingly more complex, given that multiple heterogeneous types of resources are at a system planner’s disposal. In this paper, we focus on deriving optimal strategies for the allocation of heterogeneous resources in a well-known competitive resource allocation model known as the General Lotto game. In standard formulations, outcomes are determined solely by the players’ allocation strategies of a common, single type of resource across multiple contests. In particular, a player wins a contest if it sends more resources than the opponent. Here, we propose a multi-resource extension where the winner of a contest is now determined not only by the amount of resources allocated, but also by the composition of resource types that are allocated. We completely characterize the equilibrium payoffs and strategies for two distinct formulations. The first consists of a weakest-link/best-shot winning rule, and the second considers a winning rule based on a weighted linear combination of the allocated resources. We then consider a scenario where the resource types are costly to purchase, and derive the players’ equilibrium investments in each of the resource types.

In the standard formulations, the players have access only to a single common resource type, and the winner of each contest is determined purely by the amount of resources allocated to that contest – this is known as the winner-take-all winning rule

I-A

Contributions

We propose a framework to study classes of multi-resource General Lotto games, where players compete by allocating multiple types of resources. Our goal is to provide equilibrium characterizations for this new class of models. Specifically, we seek to analytically derive the equilibrium payoffs and strategies for both players. Our main contributions are as follows.

•

The formulation of a novel multi-resource General Lotto game in Section
II
. We consider a finite number of resource types, and both players have limited budgets pertaining to each type. The winning rule on each contest is a function that depends on the allocation of all resource types to that contest.

•

Our main contribution provides a complete equilibrium characterization in the case of a weakest-link/best-shot winning rule (Theorem
3.1
). That is, one of the players must send more resources of
every
type in order to win a contest (weakest-link), while the other player only needs to send more of any one resource type (best-shot).

•

In our second main contribution, we consider a scenario where utilizing resources incurs a cost. We derive a unique equilibrium that describes the optimal investments into each type of resource for both players (Theorem
4.1
).

•

Our third contribution provides a complete equilibrium characterization in the case that the winning rule is given by a weighted linear combination of the allocated resource types (Proposition
5.1
). That is, each resource type has a relative effectiveness against all other types.

Numerical experiments are also included throughout to illustrate the main results. Figure

II
Problem formulation

In this section, we first review the classic General Lotto game, which considers the allocation of a single resource type. We then formulate a novel setting where the players’ depends on the allocation of multiple resource types.

II-A

Classic, Single-Resource General Lotto Game

Two players
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
compete over a collection of simultaneous valuable contests,
\mathcal{C}=\{1,2,\ldots,C\}
. The contests have values
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{C})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{C}
. Without loss of generality, the values are normalized such that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}=1
. The players have access to a common single-dimensional resource, e.g. only money, security forces, or human resources, that they use to compete. A resource allocation for player
\mathcal{X}
is a vector
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{C})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and similarly
\textbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{C})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for player
\mathcal{Y}
. The amount
x_{c}
is interpreted as the quantity of resources allocated to contest
c\in\mathcal{C}
. A player wins a contest by allocating more resources than the opponent. Thus, the payoff to player
\mathcal{X}
is defined as

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\triangleq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot\mathds{1}\{x_{c}\geq y_{c}\},

(1)

and the payoff to player
\mathcal{Y}
is defined as

\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\triangleq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot\mathds{1}\{x_{c}<y_{c}\}=1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}).

(2)

In this formulation, each player is able to randomize its allocation, but cannot allocate an amount of resources that exceeds a fixed budget
in expectation
. Player
\mathcal{X}
has a fixed budget
X\geq 0
and player
\mathcal{Y}
has a fixed budget
Y\geq 0
. An admissible strategy for player
\mathcal{X}
is a cumulative distribution function
F_{\mathcal{X}}
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{C}
whose expected value does not exceed
X
. That is, the strategy space for player
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathbb{F}(X)\triangleq\left\{F_{\mathcal{X}}\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{C}):\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim F_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c}\right]\leq X\right\}

(3)

and the strategy space for player
\mathcal{Y}
is
\mathbb{F}(Y)
. With slight abuse of notation, we denote the players’ expected payoffs with respect to a strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})
as

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\sim F_{\mathcal{X}}\\
\textbf{y}\sim F_{\mathcal{Y}}\end{subarray}}\left[\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\right].

(4)

and
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})
is similarly defined. These payoff functions define a two-player simultaneous-move game, termed the
General Lotto game
, which we will refer to as
\text{GL}(X,Y;\boldsymbol{v})
. A diagram of this setup is provided in Figure
1
(Left).

Definition 1
.

An
equilibrium
is a strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})
that satisfies

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})\leq\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})\leq\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}})

(5)

for any
F_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}(X)
and for any
F_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}(Y)
.

In an equilibrium, player
\mathcal{X}
cannot increase its payoff by unilaterally changing its strategy, and player
\mathcal{Y}
cannot decrease player
\mathcal{X}
’s payoff by unilaterally changing its strategy. Provided below is a well-known result from the literature detailing the unique payoffs that the players obtain in any equilibrium of the General Lotto game.

Theorem 2.1

(Adapted from
[
10
]
)
.

Consider a General Lotto game
\text{GL}(X,Y;\boldsymbol{v})
. The unique equilibrium payoff for player
\mathcal{X}
is

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(X,Y)\triangleq L\left(\frac{Y}{X}\right),

(6)

where
L:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow(0,1]
is defined as

L(\alpha)\triangleq\begin{cases}1-\frac{\alpha}{2},&\text{if }\alpha\leq 1\\
\frac{1}{2\alpha},&\text{if }\alpha>1\\
\end{cases}.

(7)

The equilibrium payoff to player
\mathcal{Y}
is
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}(X,Y)\triangleq 1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(X,Y)
.

It is important to note that in
\text{GL}(X,Y;\boldsymbol{v})
, the equilibrium payoff depends only on the ratio of the players’ budgets, and the total value of all contests
\sum_{c}v_{c}
. Here, since
\boldsymbol{v}
is normalized, the total value is just 1, but in cases where
\boldsymbol{v}
is not normalized, the equilibrium payoff would simply be
(\sum_{c}v_{c})L(Y/X)
. A plot of the equilibrium payoff
\pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is shown in Figure
2
(Left).

II-B

Multi-resource General Lotto game

We now introduce an extension of the classic General Lotto game to a scenario where the players have multiple types of resources at their disposal (e.g. money, human resources, …), and their success on any contest depends on the combination of allocations of all resource types. Suppose there are
T\geq 1
distinct resource types, which we enumerate as
\mathcal{T}=\{1,\ldots,T\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}\triangleq(X_{1},\ldots,X_{T})
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\triangleq(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{T})
be player
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
’s resource budgets for each type, respectively. Here, an allocation for player
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
is

\displaystyle\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{C})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{CT}

(8)

\displaystyle\textbf{y}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{C})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{CT}

where for each contest
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}=(x_{c,1},\ldots,x_{c,T})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
. We note that
T=1
recovers the classic setup.

The payoff to player
\mathcal{X}
is

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\triangleq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot W(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c})

(9)

where
W:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is the
winning rule
for player
\mathcal{X}
. The payoff to player
\mathcal{Y}
is then

\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\triangleq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot(1-W(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}))=1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}).

(10)

The winning rule
W
determines the winner of contest
c\in\mathcal{C}
based on the composition of the allocations of all resource types from both players,
\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}
. This significantly differs from the classic setup that utilizes the “winner-take-all” winning rule: whoever allocated more resources (of homogeneous type) wins the contest. An illustration that contrasts these setups is depicted in Figure
1
. We will focus on the following winning rules defined below.

•

Weakest-link:
Player
\mathcal{X}
is required to allocate more than
\mathcal{Y}
for
all resource types
,

W_{\text{WL}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\triangleq\mathds{1}\left\{x_{t}\geq y_{t},\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

(11)

•

Best-shot:
Player
\mathcal{X}
is required to allocate more than
\mathcal{Y}
for
at least one resource type
,

W_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\triangleq\mathds{1}\left\{x_{t}\geq y_{t},\text{ for some }t\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

(12)

Note that a weakest-link winning rule for
\mathcal{X}
,
\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{c}v_{c}W_{\text{WL}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, necessarily implies
\mathcal{Y}
has a best-shot winning rule,
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{c}v_{c}W_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})
, and vice versa. The weakest-link/best-shot winning rules reflect interactions between a defender and attacker. For example, a defender is required to have superiority on all fronts in order to ensure security of a networked system or critical infrastructure – e.g. ensuring security against malware, denial-of-service, and social engineering attacks.

To complete the setup, we consider an admissible strategy for player
\mathcal{X}
to be any distribution
F_{\mathcal{X}}
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{CT}
such that the expected total allocation of resource type
t
across all contests does not exceed
X_{t}
. That is, the strategy space for player
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\triangleq\left\{F_{\mathcal{X}}\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{CT}):\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim F_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{c,t}\right]\leq X_{t},\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}\right\}

(13)

and the strategy space for player
\mathcal{Y}
is
\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
. In the same way that expected payoffs were defined in (
4
), the expected payoffs with respect to a strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\times\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
is given by

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\sim F_{\mathcal{X}}\\
\textbf{y}\sim F_{\mathcal{Y}}\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot W(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c})\right],

(14)

with
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})
. This defines a two-player simultaneous-move game, which we will refer to as a
Multi-resource General Lotto game
, and denote it as
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W,\boldsymbol{v})
. An
equilibrium

(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})
in this game is analogously defined as in Definition
1
.

III
Results: Equilibrium characterization of ML game

In this section, we present our first main contribution of this paper, which provides the full equilibrium characterization for the multi-resource General Lotto game under the weakest-link/best-shot winning rule. The results detail the unique equilibrium payoffs to both players as well as the equilibrium strategy profiles.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider a Multi-resource General Lotto game under the weakest-link winning rule,
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})
. The unique equilibrium payoff to player
\mathcal{X}
is

\pi_{\text{WL}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))

(15)

where
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}}{X_{t}}
. The equilibrium payoff to
\mathcal{Y}
is
\pi_{\text{BS}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq 1-\pi_{\text{WL}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
. An equilibrium strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\times\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
is given as follows. If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\leq 1
, then for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
and any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
,

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

(16)

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=1-\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle+\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}},1\right\}.

If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})>1
, then for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
and any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
,

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}

(17)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})},1\right\}.

details the full proof of Theorem

by developing a series of intermediate Lemmas.

We first give some remarks about the form of the equilibrium payoff (
15
). We note that it takes the same form
L(\alpha)
of the equilibrium payoff from the classic General Lotto game (Theorem
2.1
), but instead the budget ratio
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
is evaluated to be the summation of the budget ratios over all resource types. Thus, we observe that under the weakest-link winning rule for
\mathcal{X}
, it is necessary that it has a positive resource budget
X_{t}>0

\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
in order to attain a non-zero payoff, since player
\mathcal{X}
needs to win on all resource types for any given contest. We note this is not the case for player
\mathcal{Y}
, since it has the best-shot winning rule on any given contest.

An illustration of the equilibrium payoff is shown in Figure
2
(Center). A notable feature here is that there is a
contour
of resource budgets
(X_{1},X_{2})
satisfying
\frac{Y_{1}}{X_{1}}+\frac{Y_{2}}{X_{2}}=\alpha
that achieve the same, fixed equilibrium payoff

. The multi-dimensional dependence of the players’ performance metrics suggests one can evaluate the most cost-effective investments in combinations of resources to achieve a given performance level. We will address this problem in Section

.

We also give a brief discussion of the equilibrium strategies here, but we direct the reader to Section
VI
for their full interpretations and derivations. With strategy
F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
(
16
), player
\mathcal{X}
chooses its allocation to contest

according to
x_{c,t}=2v_{c}X_{t}\cdot U
for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
, where
U\in[0,1]
is an independent uniform random sample. Given
\mathcal{X}
has the weakest-link rule, this strategy ensures that a positive amount of every resource type is allocated to each contest. With strategy
F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}
, player
\mathcal{Y}
chooses its allocation
\boldsymbol{y}_{c}
to contest
c
as follows. With probability
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
, it randomly selects a resource type
t\in\mathcal{T}
with probability
\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}
. Then, it allocates an amount

of type

is an independent uniform random sample, and does not allocate any other resource types, i.e.

for all

. While the best-shot rule specifies that
\mathcal{Y}
can win by allocating more on

resource type, the equilbrium strategy

attempts to win the contest by winning on only one resource type.

IV
Strategic investment of multiple resources

In this section, we examine how valuable each resource type is by considering a scenario where the players must decide how much of each type to invest in, given there are costs of investment. We formulate a two-stage interaction where the players make investment decisions in the first stage to acquire resources, and then engage in a multi-resource Lotto game in the second stage. For resources of type
t\in\mathcal{T}
, player
\mathcal{X}
pays a per-unit cost
\kappa_{t}>0
and player
\mathcal{Y}
pays a per-unit cost
\sigma_{t}
. The interaction unfolds as follows.

Stage 1:
Both players simultaneously decide their resource investments,
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
.

Stage 2:
The players engage in a multi-resource General Lotto game
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W,\boldsymbol{v})
.

In the case that the winning rule is weakest-link, the final payoffs obtained are

U_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq\pi^{*}_{\text{WL}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa_{t}\cdot X_{t}

(18)

for player
\mathcal{X}
, and

U_{\mathcal{Y}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq\pi^{*}_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sigma_{t}\cdot Y_{t}

(19)

for player
\mathcal{Y}
. Here, each player uses its resources invested from Stage 1 to compete in the simultaneous-move game
ML
in stage 2, and the resulting payoffs from stage 2 are the unique equilibrium payoffs of
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W,\boldsymbol{v})
characterized in Theorems
3.1
and
5.1
. Consequently, the only strategic decisions that need to be investigated are how the players should invest in Stage 1. This two-stage interaction can thus be viewed as a two-player strategic-form game with strategy spaces
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
for
\mathcal{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
for
\mathcal{Y}
. We denote this game as the
Multi-resource General Lotto game with costs
,
\text{ML-C}(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W)
.

Our goal is to identify the equilibria of
\text{ML-C}(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W)
. Here, an equilibrium is an investment profile
(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
that satisfies

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq U_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}),\ \forall\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}

(20)

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{Y}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq U_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}),\ \forall\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}

Note that unlike the General Lotto games GL and ML, the strategic interaction with costs is not a constant-sum game because the associated costs of investment are included in the players’ payoff functions. The result below provides the equilibrium investments and payoffs for both players under the weakest-link winning rule.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the Multi-resource General Lotto game with costs under the weakest-link winning rule
\text{ML-C}(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
. It admits a unique equilibrium investment profile
(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
, wherein both players spend an identical amount of money, i.e.

\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa_{t}X_{t}^{*}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sigma_{t}Y_{t}^{*}.

(21)

The unique equilibrium investment profile is given as follows. If
r\triangleq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}>1
, then

X_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}r^{2}},\quad Y_{t}^{*}=\frac{\kappa_{t}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}r^{2}}\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

(22)

yielding equilibrium payoffs of
U_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\triangleq U_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})=0
and
U_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\triangleq U_{\mathcal{Y}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})=1-\frac{1}{2r}
.

If
r\triangleq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\leq 1
, then

X_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}},\quad Y_{t}^{*}=\frac{\kappa_{t}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

(23)

yielding equilibrium payoffs of
U_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}=1-\frac{r}{2}
and
U_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=0
.

Interestingly, regardless of the cost parameters, both players spend an equivalent amount of money in equilibrium. However, the total amount of resources purchased is not necessarily identical. Player
\mathcal{X}
purchases
X_{tot}^{*}=\sum_{t}X_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\sum_{t}\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}
total resources, and player
\mathcal{Y}
purchases
Y_{tot}^{*}=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\sum_{t}\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}
total resources. We note that both of these quantities depend on the cost parameters
\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

The players’ equilibrium payoffs have a discontinuity when the parameter
r=1
. This is unlike the General Lotto games GL and ML, where although there are two separate cases, the equilibrium payoffs are still continuous in the
\alpha
parameter. Figure
3
(Top Right) provides a plot of the players’ equilibrium payoffs.

The fraction of each resource type relative to the total resources purchased is also different among the players. For player
\mathcal{X}
, the fraction invested in type
t
is
\frac{X_{t}^{*}}{X_{tot}^{*}}=\frac{1/\sigma_{t}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}1/\sigma_{t^{\prime}}}
. Interestingly, these fractions only depend on the cost parameters of
\mathcal{Y}
, and do not depend on its own cost parameters
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
. For player
\mathcal{Y}
, the fraction invested in type
t
is
\frac{Y_{t}^{*}}{Y_{tot}^{*}}=\frac{\kappa_{t}/\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\kappa_{t^{\prime}}/\sigma_{t^{\prime}}^{2}}
. These fractions depend on both cost parameters
\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. A numerical case study that illustrates the equilibrium resource investments is given in the plots of Figure
3
.

To establish the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we first approach the problem of finding the equilibrium resource investments when the players are given fixed use-it-or-lose-it monetary budgets
M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0
to spend on resources. In this scenario, the monetary budgets are viewed as sunk costs, so that player
\mathcal{X}
can choose any
\boldsymbol{X}
for which
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa_{t}X_{t}=M_{\mathcal{X}}
with the objective of maximizing only
\pi_{\text{WL}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
. Similarly, player
\mathcal{Y}
can choose any
\boldsymbol{Y}
for which
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sigma_{t}Y_{t}=M_{\mathcal{Y}}
with the objective of maximizing only
\pi_{\text{BS}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
. We denote this constrained game as
\text{ML-M}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

With sunk costs
M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the equilibrium investments
(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
in the game
\text{ML-M}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
are given by

X_{t}^{*}=\frac{M_{\mathcal{X}}}{\kappa_{t}}\frac{\kappa_{t}/\sigma_{t}}{r},\quad Y_{t}^{*}=\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{\sigma_{t}}\frac{\kappa_{t}/\sigma_{t}}{r},\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

(24)

The equilibrium payoffs are

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{WL}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})

\displaystyle=L\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{M_{\mathcal{X}}}r\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{BS}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})

\displaystyle=1-L\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{M_{\mathcal{X}}}r\right).

The proof of this intermediate result is deferred to the Appendix. Lemma
4.1
asserts that if any positive amount of monetary investment is spent, then in an equilibrium, the proportions of that investment that go towards each resource type is given precisely by (
24
). The resulting payoffs are then given in (
25
). This result allows us to view the strategic decision-making in Stage 1 of
\text{ML-C}(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
simply as a monetary investment
M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0
.

Thus, the problem of finding equilibria of
\text{ML-C}(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
can be re-cast as finding the equilibria of the game associated with payoff functions

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{X}}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=L\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{M_{\mathcal{X}}}r\right)-M_{\mathcal{X}}

(26)

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=1-L\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{M_{\mathcal{X}}}r\right)-M_{\mathcal{Y}}

and strategies
M_{\mathcal{X}}\geq 0
,
M_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0
. We are now able to proceed with the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Our approach is to derive the best-response curves for both players. For a fixed
M_{\mathcal{X}}\geq 0
,

\text{BR}_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}}):=\max_{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}U_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}})

(27)

Omitting the algebraic steps, we can precisely characterize this as

\text{BR}_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\mathcal{X}}}{2r}},&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{X}}<r/2\\
[0,\frac{1}{2}],&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{X}}=r/2\\
0,&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{X}}>r/2\\
\end{cases}

(28)

In a similar manner, we derive the best-response for player
\mathcal{X}
as

\text{BR}_{\mathcal{X}}(M_{\mathcal{Y}})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}r}{2}},&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{X}}<\frac{1}{2r}\\
[0,\frac{1}{2}],&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{Y}}=\frac{1}{2r}\\
0,&\text{if }M_{\mathcal{Y}}>\frac{1}{2r}\\
\end{cases}

(29)

An equilibrium is any point
(M_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},M_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})
at which
M_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=\text{BR}_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}}^{*})
and
M_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}=\text{BR}_{\mathcal{X}}(M_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})
, i.e. where the two graphs of
\text{BR}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\text{BR}_{\mathcal{X}}
intersect. If
r>1
, then
\text{BR}_{\mathcal{X}}(M_{\mathcal{Y}})
intersects
\text{BR}_{\mathcal{Y}}(M_{\mathcal{X}})
uniquely at the point
(\frac{1}{2r},\frac{1}{2r})
. If
r\geq 1
, then they intersect uniquely at the point
(\frac{r}{2},\frac{r}{2})
.

The full equilibrium investment profile
(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
can then be deduced using the result from Lemma
4.1
.

∎

V
The Weighted Contribution Winning Rule

In this section, we consider an alternate winning rule that we refer to as a
weighted contribution
rule. Here, each resource type is associated with an effectiveness weight
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
for player
\mathcal{X}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{T})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
for player
\mathcal{Y}
. The winning rule for player
\mathcal{X}
is

W_{\text{WC}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\triangleq\mathds{1}\left\{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}a_{t}x_{t}\geq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}b_{t}y_{t}\right\}.

(30)

The weighted contribution winning rule
W_{\text{WC}}
reflects the heterogeneity of resources with respect to their relative effectiveness for winning a contest.

We state the full equilibrium characterization of the ML game under the weighted contribution rule in the Proposition below.

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider a Multi-resource General Lotto game under the weighted contribution winning rule,
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WC}},\boldsymbol{v})
, with parameters
\{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\}
. The unique equilibrium payoff to player
\mathcal{X}
is

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}=L(\beta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})),

(31)

where
\beta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\triangleq\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}Y_{t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}X_{t}}
. The equilibrium payoff to player
\mathcal{Y}
is
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=1-\pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
. An equilibrium strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\times\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
is given as follows. If
\beta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

(32)

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta}\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}Y_{t}\beta},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

If
\beta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})>1
, then

\begin{aligned} F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})&=1-\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta}\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}\beta},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\\
F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})&=\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}Y_{t}},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\\
\end{aligned}.

(33)

Unlike the weakest-link formulation, player
\mathcal{X}
can attain a non-zero payoff by only possessing a single resource type. The above result is comparable to the classic, single-resource General Lotto game where the “effective total budgets” are given by the linear combinations
X=\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}X_{t}
and
Y=\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}Y_{t}
. Indeed, observe that the equilibrium payoffs coincide with that of the classic single-resource game
L(Y/X)
(Theorem
2.1
) using the effective total budgets
X,Y
.

We omit a full proof of this result since it largely follows the same techniques that we develop to establish the proof of Theorem
3.1
in Section
VI
. In particular, one can establish the proof using the same sequence of Lemmas stated in Section
VI
. The main difference is that instead of applying the CDF
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}[y_{c,t}\leq x_{c,t},\forall t]
in the calculations of expected utility (i.e. starting in Lemma
6.2
, (
40
)), we apply the CDF
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}b_{t}y_{c,t}\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}a_{t}x_{c,t}]
that corresponds to the winning rule
W_{\text{WC}}
.

VI
Equilibrium Analysis and Derivations

In this section, we establish the proofs for the main result (Theorem
3.1
) regarding equilibrium characterizations of the Multi-resource game,
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})
under the weakest-link/best-shot winning rules. Below, we describe a rough outline of the technical approach we take to prove Theorem
3.1
.

•

We propose and define two classes of randomized allocation strategies,
\mathbb{F}_{\text{WL}}
for the player with weakest-link rule (
\mathcal{X}
), and
\mathbb{F}_{\text{BS}}
for the player with the best-shot rule (
\mathcal{Y}
). They are given in Definitions
2
and
3
, and are strict subsets of the strategy set
\mathbb{F}
(
3
).

•

In Lemmas
6.2
and
6.3
, we establish the smallest upper-bound that player
\mathcal{Y}
can impose on the payoff of player
\mathcal{X}
using strategies from
\mathbb{F}_{\text{BS}}
.

•

In Lemmas
6.4
and
6.5
, we establish the largest lower-bound that player
\mathcal{X}
can ensure on its own payoff using strategies from
\mathbb{F}_{\text{WL}}
.

•

We find that both the upper and lower bounds coincide in value. Because
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})
is a two-player constant-sum game, this establishes an equlibrium strategy profile.

We begin by observing that one can write the expected payoff from (
14
) in terms of marginal distributions of the players’ strategies.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider a Multi-resource Lotto game
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W,\boldsymbol{v})
. Then for any strategy profile
(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\times\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
,

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}_{c}\sim F_{\mathcal{X},c}\\
\boldsymbol{y}_{c}\sim F_{\mathcal{Y},c}\end{subarray}}\left[W(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c})\right].

(34)

where for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
, the
c
-
marginal distribution

F_{\mathcal{X},c}
is given by

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}(\boldsymbol{u}_{c})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{P}[x_{c,t}\leq u_{c,t},\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}]

(35)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\boldsymbol{u}_{d}\rightarrow\infty^{T},\forall d\neq c}F_{\mathcal{X}}((\boldsymbol{u}_{d})_{d\in\mathcal{C}})

Similar definitions apply for
F_{\mathcal{Y},c}
.

Each expectation term in (
34
) is equivalent to an expectation with respect to the
T
-variate
marginal distributions
for each contest
c\in\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, any full strategy
F_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})
can be specified in terms of its
c
-marginals,
\{F_{\mathcal{X},c}\}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposed strategies for weakest-link formulation

\mathcal{X}

Definition 2
.

We define the class of strategies

\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}_{\text{WL}}(\boldsymbol{X})
has
c

(36)

for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
. Here, the number
\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\in[0,1]

For player
\mathcal{Y}

.

\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{Y})

for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}

\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\in[0,1]

The strategy (

\boldsymbol{y}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}

\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We provide a series of Lemmas in order to establish Theorem
3.1
. The following Lemma establishes an upper bound on player
\mathcal{X}
when player
\mathcal{Y}
uses any
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{Y})

Lemma 6.2
.

Consider
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})

\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{\text{BS}}(\boldsymbol{Y})

Proof.

For any
F_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}(X)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{X},c}(F_{\mathcal{X},c},\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}):=\mathbb{E}_{F_{\mathcal{X},c},\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{x_{c,t}\geq y_{c,t},\forall t\in\mathcal{T}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{F_{\mathcal{X},c}}\left[\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}(x)\right]

Lemma 6.3

Consider

. Then,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\in[0,1]\\
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})\end{subarray}}\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{p})=L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})).

(42)

The strategy
F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
that imposes the bound upper bound
L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))
is given as follows.
If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=1-\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})

(43)

\displaystyle+\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}},1\right\}.

for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
. If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})>1
, then

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Y},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})},1\right\}.

(44)

for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
.

Proof.

The optimization problem on the left-hand side of (
42
) can be sequenced as

\text{UB}^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\min_{\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\in[0,1]}\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{p}).

(45)

Observing that
\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{p})
is a convex quadratic function in
\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\in[0,1]
, the minimizing
\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}
for the inner minimization must satisfy

\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{p})},1\right\}

(46)

for any fixed
\boldsymbol{p}
, where
g(\boldsymbol{p}):=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}p_{t}^{2}\frac{X_{t}}{Y_{t}}
. Then, the optimization problem can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{p})=

(47)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{p})},1\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{p})},1\right\}^{2}g(\boldsymbol{p})\right)

We now observe that
g(p)
is a convex function on
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})
, and attains its minimum at the point
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
whose entries are

p^{*}_{t}=\frac{Y_{t}/X_{t}}{\alpha},\ t\in\mathcal{T},

(48)

where
\alpha=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t^{\prime}}}{X_{t^{\prime}}}
as defined in Theorem
3.1
. We then have
g(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})=1/\alpha
.

Case 1:

\alpha\leq 1
. It holds that
g(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})\geq 1
, and thus
g(\boldsymbol{p})\geq 1
for all
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})
. The optimization problem (
47
) is

\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{p})=\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\left(1-\frac{1}{2g(\boldsymbol{p})}\right)

(49)

The minimizer is identical to the minimizer of
g(\boldsymbol{p})
, which is just
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
. Therefore, we have
U_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
, with
\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=\alpha
.

Case 2:

\alpha>1
. It holds that
g(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})<1
. We can write (
47
) as

\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})}\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{p})=\begin{cases}\frac{g(\boldsymbol{p})}{2},&\text{if }g(\boldsymbol{p})<1\\
1-\frac{1}{2g(\boldsymbol{p})},&\text{if }g(\boldsymbol{p})\geq 1\\
\end{cases}

(50)

Observe that
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
is the minimizer in the region
g(\boldsymbol{p})<1
. We can further deduce that
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
also minimizes
\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{p})
over the entire simplex
\Delta(\mathcal{T})
, since
\text{UB}(\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{p})\geq\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{g(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})}{2}
for any
p\in\Delta(\mathcal{T})
such that
g(p)\geq 1
. Thus, we have
\text{UB}^{*}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}
, with
\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}=1
.

We then obtain
\text{UB}^{*}=L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))
, and the form of the strategy
F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}
follows from (
37
).

∎

We note that the smallest upper bound from Lemma
6.2
coincides with the reported equilibrium payoff in Theorem
3.1
. Our next Lemma establishes lower bounds.

Lemma 6.4
.

Consider
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})
. Suppose
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})
is a strategy of the form (
36
). Then it holds that for any
F_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{Y})
,

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})\geq\text{LB}(\delta_{\mathcal{X}})

(51)

where
\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\in[0,1]
is the parameter of
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
, and

\text{LB}(\delta_{\mathcal{X}})\triangleq\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\left(1-\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\right)

(52)

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
utilizes a strategy
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
of the form (
36
). For any
F_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}(Y)
, the payoff to
\mathcal{Y}
in contest
c
is

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{Y},c}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c},F_{\mathcal{Y},c}):=1-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c},F_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{x_{c,t}\geq y_{c,t},\text{ for all }t\in\mathcal{T}\}}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c}}\left(x_{c,t}\geq y_{c,t},\text{ for all }t\in\mathcal{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[1-\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T},|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|+1}\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c,\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c})\right]

The equality above is due to the Inclusion-Exclusion principle, and
F_{\mathcal{X},c,\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c})=\lim_{y_{c,d}\rightarrow\infty,\forall d\notin\mathcal{S}}

\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c,\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c}):=\lim_{y_{c,d}\rightarrow\infty,\forall d\notin\mathcal{S}}\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c})

(54)

is the marginal distribution of
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c}
with respect to variables
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Denoting
m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t}):=\min\left\{\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}}{2v_{c}X_{t}}\cdot y_{t},1\right\}
, we can write from (
36
):

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c,\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c})

\displaystyle=(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\min\left\{m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{S}}

(55)

Continuing, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{Y},c}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c},F_{\mathcal{Y},c})

(56)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[1-(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T},|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|+1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad-\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T},|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|+1}\min\left\{m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T},|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|+1}\min\left\{m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\max\left\{m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\right]

The second equality is due to the Binomial Theorem:
\sum_{k=1}^{T}\binom{T}{k}(-1)^{k+1}=1
. The third equality follows from the technical result Lemma
-A.1
found in the Appendix. The inequality in the last line above follows from the fact that
\max_{t}\{n_{t}\}\leq\sum_{t}n_{t}
for any collection of numbers
n_{t}
. Moreover, since
m_{\mathcal{X},c,t}(y_{t})\leq\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}}{2v_{c}X_{t}}y_{t}
, we can write

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{Y},c}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c},F_{\mathcal{Y},c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}\left[(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{y_{t}}{v_{c}X_{t}}\right]

(57)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{\mathcal{Y},c}}[y_{c,t}]}{v_{c}X_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}}{X_{t}}

The final inequality follows from
F_{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}(Y)
(
13
). Summing over all contests to get the total payoff
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_{c}\cdot\pi_{\mathcal{Y},c}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X},c},F_{\mathcal{Y},c})
, we obtain the bound

\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})\leq(1-\delta_{\mathcal{X}})+\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}}{X_{t}},

(58)

as the contest values are normalized. We can equivalently express this as a lower bound on player
\mathcal{X}
’s payoff,

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}})\geq\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\left(1-\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{Y_{t}}{X_{t}}\right).

(59)

∎

The next Lemma provides the greatest lower bound that player
\mathcal{X}
can ensure by using strategies
\hat{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
of the form (
36
).

Lemma 6.5
.

Consider
\text{ML}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y};W_{\text{WL}},\boldsymbol{v})
. Then,

\max_{\delta_{\mathcal{X}}\in[0,1]}\text{LB}(\delta_{\mathcal{X}})=L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})).

(60)

The strategy
F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{X})
that ensures the payoff
L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))
is given as follows.
If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

(61)

for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
. If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})>1
, then

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{X},c}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}

(62)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}\cdot\min\left\{\min\left\{\frac{u_{t}}{2v_{c}X_{t}\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})},1\right\}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

The maximum lower bound attainable through the choice of

From Lemmas

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}},F_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*})\leq L(\alpha)\leq\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{\mathcal{X}}^{*},F_{\mathcal{Y}})

VII
Conclusion

The allocation of heterogeneous resources is increasingly becoming a significant aspect for system planners to consider, given the complexity and diversity of objectives that need to be accomplished. In this manuscript, we considered the allocation of multiple and heterogeneous resource types for a strategic adversarial setting known as General Lotto games. We extended the classic and pre-dominantly single-resource General Lotto games considered in the literature to scenarios where players allocate multiple types of resources. Here, the specification of winning rules for each individual contest is the novel modeling consideration in our framework. We derived equilibrium characterizations in the cases where the winning rules are based on weakest-link objectives, and when they are based on a weighted linear combination of the allocated resource types. We then analyzed how investment decisions should be made when it is costly to utilize each resource type.

Technical Result for Theorem
3.1

The following result is utilized in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, specifically in Lemma
6.4
.

Lemma
-A
.1
.

Suppose
(z_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}
is a vector of non-negative numbers, where we denote
T=|\mathcal{T}|
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{T},|S|>0}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}=\max_{t\in T}z_{t}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
T
, the size of the index set
\mathcal{T}
. The case
T=1
trivially holds. Therefore, suppose that the statement is correct for arbitrary
T
. We want to prove that it is correct for
T+1
. Let
m=\min\{z_{T},z_{T+1}\}
and
M=\max\{z_{T},z_{T+1}\}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq[T+1]}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\in S,M\not\in S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\not\in S,M\in S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\in S,M\in S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}.

Note that for every
S\subseteq[T+1]
such that
m\in S,M\not\in S
, we have the set
S\cup\{M\}\subseteq[T+1]
. Also, for every
S\subseteq[T+1]
such that
m\in S,M\in S
, we have the set
S\setminus\{M\}\subseteq[T+1]
. Therefore, since

\displaystyle(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}+(-1)^{|S|+2}\min_{t\in S\cup\{M\}}z_{t}=0

where
m\in S,M\not\in S
,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\in S,M\not\in S\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\in S,M\in S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}=0.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq[T+1]}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\\
m\not\in S,M\in S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[T+1]\setminus\{m\}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|+1}\min_{t\in S}z_{t}

\displaystyle=\max\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{T-1},M\}

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in[T+1]}z_{t},

where the third equality follows from the induction assumption on sets of size
T
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1

Consider the game
\text{ML-M}(M_{\mathcal{X}},M_{\mathcal{Y}},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\sigma},W_{\text{WL}})
. The best-response problem for player
\mathcal{X}
is

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{X}}L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))\text{ s.t. }\begin{cases}&\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa_{t}X_{t}\leq M_{\mathcal{X}}\\
&X_{t}\geq 0,\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}\\
\end{cases}

(66)

with
\boldsymbol{Y}
fixed. The necessary condition is

L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))\cdot\left(\frac{Y_{t}}{X_{t}^{2}}\right)+\lambda\kappa_{t}-\lambda_{t}=0,\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

(67)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the multiplier associated with the monetary budget constraint, and
\lambda_{t}
is the multiplier associated with the constraint
-X_{t}\leq 0
. We first observe that any optimal response must have
X_{t}>0
, for otherwise the payoff is zero. Moreover, the payoff is strictly increasing in
X_{t}
for any
t\in\mathcal{T}
, so an optimal response must utilize the entire monetary budget
M_{\mathcal{X}}
. Thus,
\lambda_{t}=0
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
\lambda>0
. We then get

X_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{-L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))Y_{t}}{\lambda\cdot\kappa_{t}}}

(68)

We can then write the budget constraint as
M_{\mathcal{X}}=\sqrt{\frac{-L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))}{\lambda}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sqrt{\frac{Y_{t}}{\kappa_{t}}}
. The multiplier is evaluated to be

\lambda=\frac{-L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}))}{M_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sqrt{\frac{Y_{t}}{\kappa_{t}}}.

(69)

Using this expression, the optimal allocation can then be calculated to be

X_{t}^{*}=\frac{M_{\mathcal{X}}}{\kappa_{t}}\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{t}Y_{t}}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{T}}\sqrt{\kappa_{s}Y_{s}}}.

(70)

To find the equilibrium, we can now consider the best-response problem for player
\mathcal{Y}
as

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{Y}}L(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}))\text{ s.t. }\begin{cases}&\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sigma_{t}Y_{t}\leq M_{\mathcal{Y}}\\
&Y_{t}\geq 0,\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}\\
\end{cases}.

(71)

where
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
is given in (
70
). The necessary condition is

L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}))\cdot\left(\frac{1}{X_{t}^{*}}\right)+\lambda\sigma_{t}-\lambda_{t}=0,\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

(72)

Using a change of variable
Z_{t}:=\sqrt{\kappa_{t}Y_{t}}
, we obtain

Z_{t}=\frac{-L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}))}{M_{\mathcal{X}}\lambda}\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{T}}Z_{s}

(73)

The budget constraint requires
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sigma_{t}\frac{Z_{t}^{2}}{\kappa_{t}}=M_{\mathcal{Y}}
. Using the expression (
73
), we can evaluate the multiplier to be

\lambda=\frac{-L^{\prime}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{X}^{*},\boldsymbol{Y}))}{M_{\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{T}}Z_{s}\right)\sqrt{r}

(74)

where

. We then obtain

Z_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{r}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}.

(75)

The optimal allocation
Y_{t}^{*}
is recovered via

Y_{t}^{*}=\frac{Z_{t}^{2}}{\kappa_{t}}=\frac{M_{\mathcal{Y}}}{\sigma_{t}}\frac{\kappa_{t}/\sigma_{t}}{r}.

(76)

The optimal allocation of player
\mathcal{X}
can then be written as

X_{t}^{*}=\frac{M_{\mathcal{X}}}{\kappa_{t}}\frac{\kappa_{t}/\sigma_{t}}{r}.
