Slower but Not Safer: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Return to Play and Graft Re-Rupture After ACL Reconstruction in Professional Soccer Players
Authors: Riccardo D’Ambrosi, Alessandro Carrozzo, Edoardo Monaco, Amit Meena, Luca Maria Sconfienza, Elmar Herbst, Elisabeth Abermann, Christian Fink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is one of the most severe injuries in professional soccer players, often leading to long absences from competition and career-threatening consequences. Understanding how return to play (RTP) outcomes have evolved over time is essential for optimizing recovery and minimizing re-injury risk. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare RTP rate, level of RTP, time to RTP, and ACL graft re-rupture rate in professional soccer players across three distinct time periods after ACL reconstruction (ACLR).MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Elsevier), and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched on March 8, 2025, and updated on March 22, 2025. Studies reporting RTP outcomes after ACLR in professional soccer players were included. Methodological quality was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS). Four primary outcomes—RTP rate, level of RTP, time to RTP, and ACL graft re-rupture rate—were extracted and meta-analyzed across three publication periods (2011–2016, 2017–2021, 2022–2025).ResultsTwenty studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 1,988 professional soccer players (5 published before 2017, 5 between 2017 and 2021, and 10 after 2021). The mean player age at surgery did not differ across time periods (p > 0.05). Overall, 92.3% of players returned to play, 80.1% regained their pre-injury performance level. The mean time to RTP was 263.2 days, with a significantly slower return observed in 2022–2025 (295.1 days) compared with 2011–2016 (203.5 days; p = 0.006), and the pooled graft re-rupture rate was 8.2%. No significant differences were found among periods for overall RTP, level of RTP, or re-rupture rate (p > 0.05).ConclusionMore than 92% of professional soccer players successfully RTP after ACLR, and approximately 80% recover their pre-injury level. However, in recent years, RTP has become significantly slower—by over 40%—without a corresponding reduction in graft re-rupture rates. These findings suggest a shift toward more conservative, criteria-based RTP decision-making in professional soccer, reflecting greater emphasis on graft protection and long-term knee health.Level of Evidence Systematic review and meta-analysis of level IV.Trial Registration PROSPERO - CRD420251012143Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-025-00962-2.Keywords: ACL, Soccer, Return to sport, Time to return, Re-rupture

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury represents one of the most serious and career-threatening traumas in professional soccer players, often leading to prolonged absence from competition and major implications for both players and clubs. From a team perspective, the loss of key athletes can disrupt tactical stability, affect performance outcomes, and challenge financial investments, whereas from the player’s standpoint, an ACL rupture threatens not only physical capability but also psychological resilience and career continuity [1, 2].

ACL rupture can have profound and lasting effects on athletic performance and career longevity [3, 4]. Beyond the immediate loss of competition time, athletes frequently experience decreased match performance, reduced playing time, and shorter careers following ACL injury [5–7]. Furthermore, up to one-third of athletes never regain their pre-injury level of play [6, 7]. Long-term sequelae include an increased risk of early post-traumatic osteoarthritis, which may develop within the first decade after injury [8–10]. Psychological factors—such as fear of re-injury, decreased confidence, and lower self-efficacy—also play a key role in limiting return to play (RTP) and sustaining elite performance [6, 7].

In most athletes, ACL reconstruction (ACLR) is performed to facilitate a return to pre-injury performance levels. Despite improvements in ACLR surgical techniques, rehabilitation protocols, and RTP standards, more than 80% of patients who undergo ACLR participate in some form of sports activity; however, only about 65% return to their pre-injury level, and approximately 55% return to competitive sports [2, 5, 6, 11–13, 15]. In particular, sports involving jumping, pivoting, and cutting are associated with lower rates of return to sport after ACL surgery [2, 5, 6, 11–13, 15].

ACL injury accounts for up to 2% of all time-loss injuries in professional soccer, leading to an average absence of 6–9 months [13–15]. Despite modern rehabilitation and surgical advancements, approximately 80% of elite and professional athletes return to their pre-injury level after ACLR, but the risk of graft failure and contralateral injury remains substantial—around 7.5% among professional players [15].

Among elite athletes, RTP rates after ACLR are generally high, but timelines remain variable. Later RTP is associated with a lower re-injury risk, yet whether RTP rate, level, time, and graft re-rupture in professional soccer have changed over time remains uncertain—hence the rationale for our time-period comparison [2, 16–18]. Returning to level I sports (i.e., sports involving frequent pivoting, cutting, and jumping movements such as soccer, basketball, and handball) within nine months after ACLR increases the risk of a new ACL injury more than fourfold over two years, whereas delaying RTP beyond nine months significantly reduces this risk [16].

Despite numerous studies on RTP after ACLR, existing evidence remains heterogeneous, with limited data focusing exclusively on professional soccer players. Previous systematic reviews have often included mixed cohorts of recreational and elite athletes, limiting generalizability [2, 6, 15, 19]. Moreover, while surgical techniques, graft choices, and rehabilitation protocols have evolved substantially, it remains unclear whether these improvements have translated into faster or safer RTP among professional players [11, 18, 20].

Addressing this gap, the present systematic review and meta-analysis provides a temporal perspective by comparing outcomes across three distinct publication periods. This approach allows for the evaluation of clinical evolution over time and offers insights into whether modern evidence-based practices have improved recovery and reduced re-injury risk. The findings may assist clinicians, team physicians, and performance staff in refining post-ACLR management strategies for professional soccer athletes.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare RTP rate, level of RTP, time to RTP, and ACL graft re-rupture in professional soccer players across three different time periods in order to assess whether modern evidence-based practices have improved recovery and reduced re-injury risk over time.

Methods

Study Design and Search Strategy

A systematic search strategy was developed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and is registered in the PROSPERO Registry (CRD420251012143) [21, 22].

An electronic database search was performed to identify potentially relevant research articles that analyzed RTP, time taken to RTP, level of return RTP, and re-rupture rates in professional soccer players after ACLR. The MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Elsevier), and Cochrane Library databases were searched on March 8, 2025, and repeated on March 22, 2025, using the following Boolean strategy: (“ACL reconstruction” OR “anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction” OR “ACL”) AND (“professional” OR “elite” OR “competitive”) AND (“soccer” OR “football”).

Eligibility Criteria

The literature selected for this study was based on the criteria outlined in the following sections.

Study Design

Studies were selected using predefined eligibility criteria. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), controlled clinical trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, and case series reporting on RTP outcomes after ACLR were included. Case reports and case series were excluded if they lacked data on RTP, time to return, level of return, or re-injury rates. To avoid duplication, when multiple publications analyzed the same cohort with identical outcomes, only the most comprehensive or most recent study was included.

Definition of Professional Soccer Player

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported outcomes of ACLR in professional soccer players, defined as athletes competing under a professional contract in top- or second-tier national leagues or equivalent professional competitions at the time of injury. This definition is consistent with previous studies on ACL injuries and RTP outcomes among professional athletes [5, 13–15]. For the purpose of this review, a professional soccer player was defined as an athlete who meets the following criteria: they play soccer as a paid occupation, under a formal contract with a professional club competing in a league recognized by national or international governing bodies (e.g., FIFA, UEFA, CONMEBOL); they engage in structured training and competition schedules, often including travel, recovery, and media duties; they operate within an organized multi-tier league system, typically within the top or secondary divisions; and they adhere to professional standards of conduct, physical conditioning, and contractual obligations.

This operational definition ensures that the study population accurately reflects full-time professional athletes whose livelihood depends on sport participation, aligning with established criteria used in previous ACL literature [5, 13–15].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies including mixed cohorts of amateur and professional players were eligible only if data for professionals were clearly reported or separately analyzable.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies including only amateur or youth players; (2) case reports, technical notes, or reviews; and (3) studies with insufficient data for extraction of primary outcomes.

Type of Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures of interest were:

Overall RTP rate, defined as the proportion of players who participated in at least one official match after ACLR;

Overall RTP rate, defined as the proportion of players who participated in at least one official match after ACLR;

RTP at the pre-injury level, defined as the percentage of players returning to the same professional tier or level of competition as before the injury;

RTP at the pre-injury level, defined as the percentage of players returning to the same professional tier or level of competition as before the injury;

Time to RTP, expressed in days or months between surgery and the first official match played;

Time to RTP, expressed in days or months between surgery and the first official match played;

ACL graft re-rupture rate, defined as the proportion of players sustaining a subsequent graft failure in the reconstructed knee.

ACL graft re-rupture rate, defined as the proportion of players sustaining a subsequent graft failure in the reconstructed knee.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported at least one of these predefined outcomes. Studies that did not include data on any of these parameters were excluded. When multiple outcomes were reported, all available data were extracted and analyzed.

Time-Based Subgroup Classification

Studies were divided as follows based on publication period:

Studies published before 2017.

Studies published before 2017.

Studies published between 2017 and 2021.

Studies published between 2017 and 2021.

Studies published after 2021.

Studies published after 2021.

Rationale for Time-Period Subgrouping

To explore temporal trends while maintaining adequate subgroup sample sizes, we pre-specified three publication epochs: 2011–2016, 2017–2021, and 2022–2025.

These timeframes were chosen a priori to represent clinically meaningful eras in the evolution of ACLR and RTP practice in professional soccer.

The period 2011–2016 represents the baseline era, characterized by foundational epidemiological reporting and the early description of RTP rates in European professional football before widespread adoption of standardized RTP criteria [13, 14]. The 2017–2021 window reflects a transitional era in which key evidence emerged linking delayed RTP (≥ 9 months) to a significantly reduced risk of re-injury [16], alongside increasing use of structured RTP test batteries, greater attention to psychological readiness, and evolving surgical strategies such as broader use of the quadriceps tendon graft and selective lateral extra-articular tenodesis [5, 18, 23–26]. Finally, 2022–2025 corresponds to the modern era, marked by codified football-specific RTP recommendations and the publication of the ISAKOS 2022 Consensus on soccer RTP after ACLR, emphasizing test-based, criteria-driven decision-making [27]. From a methodological standpoint, these epochs were also selected to achieve reasonably balanced subgroups, thereby minimizing sparse-data bias and improving the precision and interpretability of between-period comparisons.

Predefining these cut-points aligns with Cochrane recommendations to use clinically and statistically meaningful subgrouping to explore time-related heterogeneity in meta-analysis [28].

Study Selection and Data Extraction

The retrieved articles were first screened by title and, if found relevant, further screened by reading the abstract. After excluding studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria, the entire content of the remaining articles was assessed for eligibility. To minimize the risk of bias, the authors reviewed and discussed all the selected articles, references, and articles excluded from the study. In case of any disagreement between the reviewers, the senior investigator made the final decision. At the end of the process, further studies that might have been missed were searched for manually by checking the reference lists of the included studies and relevant systematic reviews.

Data Collection Process

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (RD and AC) using a standardized Excel sheet. For each included study, the following information was collected: authors, year of publication, country, study design, and corresponding level of evidence according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine criteria; number of participants and sex distribution; mean age and follow-up duration; type of graft used and any concomitant procedures; as well as RTP outcomes, including overall RTP rate, RTP at pre-injury level, time to RTP, and graft re-rupture rate. Study Characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies were collected according to the PICOS framework, including study design, level of evidence (Oxford Levels of Evidence) [29], sample size, population characteristics, surgical technique, follow-up duration, and reported outcomes.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of each included study was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS). Each item was scored as 0 (not reported), 1 (reported but inadequate), or 2 (reported and adequate), with a maximum score of 16 for non-comparative studies and 24 for comparative studies. In accordance with published thresholds, MINORS scores were categorized as high quality (≥ 12), moderate quality (9–11), or low quality (≤ 8) [30, 31].

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the weighted average age, i.e., the average age weighted by the number of patients included in each study, and tested differences by sport with a weighted linear regression model.

Four meta-analyses were conducted overall and by publication period on the frequency of (i) overall RTP, (ii) RTP at pre-injury level, (iii) time to RTP, and (iv) graft re-rupture. For each of the first three outcomes, a random-effects model was performed using the Der Simonian-Laird estimator for the variance. The raw proportions were stabilized using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. The pooled estimates were presented as pooled proportions with corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI). For the average time to RTP a random-effect model on raw means using the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator for variance estimation was performed. In studies where the range was available instead of standard deviation (SD), we estimated the SD based on sample size and range [32]. In subgroup analyses, for each outcome, we tested within and between group heterogeneity with a Cochran’s Q χ2 test. Time period “2022–2025” was selected as the reference category due to the highest frequency of studies and patients being in this group. Between-study variations were assessed for each model with the Cochran’s Q χ2 test of heterogeneity and the Higgins I2 statistic. Statistical heterogeneity was defined as substantial if I2 >50% [32]. Two-tailed tests were performed. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The analysis was carried out using R (version 4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL https://www.R-project.org/) specifically with meta (version 8.0.1) and metafor packages (version 4.2.0).

Results

Study Selection

A total of 2154 records were identified across the three databases. After removal of duplicates, 820 titles and abstracts were screened, with 758 articles excluded. Sixty-two full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 20 studies met the inclusion criteria [5, 13, 14, 33–49]. Five studies were published before 2017, five between 2017 and 2021, and ten after 2021. The PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow chart indicating research article inclusion for final analysis

Study Characteristics and Quality Assessment

Twenty studies were included, all classified as level III or IV evidence. Most investigations involved professional male soccer players competing in top-tier national or international leagues, with sample sizes ranging from 21 to 235 athletes. Follow-up durations varied between 1 and 16 seasons.

To standardize heterogeneous reporting, extracted outcomes were harmonized according to the predefined metrics of this review. Table 1 summarizes the study designs, athlete demographics, follow-up periods, and key outcomes (overall RTP, RTP at pre-injury level, time to RTP, and re-rupture rates).

Methodological quality assessed using the MINORS criteria is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Among non-comparative studies, MINORS scores ranged from 8 to 14. Comparative studies ranged from 17 to 23, with all scoring within the high-quality category.

Age Comparison Across Study Periods

No significant differences were found in mean age at the time of ACLR across the three time periods (p > 0.05).

Quantitative Synthesis (Meta-Analysis)

Return to Play

The pooled RTP rate was 92.3% [95% CI 88.8–95.2], with no significant differences across time periods (p > 0.05). Figure 2 presents the corresponding forest plot.

Forest plot of return to play for different time periods after ACL reconstruction

Level of Return to Play

Pooled data revealed that 80.1% [95% CI 65.5–91.5] of professional soccer players returned to their pre-injury level. Meta-analysis showed no difference (p > 0.05) in level of RTP rates between different time periods, ranging from 75.6% (2022–2025) to 85.3% (2011–2016). Figure 3 presents the detailed forest plot.

Forest plot of level of return to play for different time periods after ACL reconstruction

Time to Return to Play

The estimated mean time to RTP was 263.2 days [95% CI 238.79 − 287.61]. A significant delay was observed in the 2022–2025 cohort (295.06 days [95% CI 261.81–328.30]) compared with 2011–2016 (203.51 days [95% CI 147.73 − 259.20 ]) (p = 0.006). Figure 4 shows the corresponding forest plot.

Re-rupture Rates

The pooled graft re-rupture rate was 8.2% [95% CI 6.7–9.8], with no significant differences across time periods (p > 0.05), ranging from 10.4% (2017–2021) to 4.5% (2011–2016). Figure 5 displays the detailed comparison.

Discussion

This meta-analysis evaluated four key outcomes in professional soccer players after ACLR—overall RTP, return to pre-injury level, time to RTP, and ACL graft re-rupture—to determine how these parameters have evolved over the past decade. The main finding of this systematic review and meta-analysis is that the RTP in professional soccer players after ACLR has become significantly slower over time—by more than 40%—while the overall RTP rate, return to pre-injury level, and graft re-rupture rates have remained stable. These findings indicate that the management of ACL injuries in professional soccer has evolved toward a more conservative, criteria-driven approach, prioritizing graft protection and functional readiness over rapid return to competition. However, despite this temporal delay, re-rupture rates have not significantly decreased, suggesting that the biological, mechanical, and sport-specific demands of professional soccer still expose players to considerable re-injury risk.

The pooled RTP rate of 92.3% in the present meta-analysis aligns with several previous reports on professional footballers, who all reported rates between 88% and 95% [34, 39, 40]. This high percentage confirms that, despite the physical and psychological challenges, the majority of professional players return to competitive sport following ACLR. However, only about 80.1% of athletes in our synthesis regained their pre-injury level of play. This finding parallels those of Arundale et al., and Jones et al. [5, 43], who similarly found that 15–25% of professional players never recover their prior level of performance or participation [5, 43]. Performance-related limitations after ACLR have been attributed to persistent deficits in quadriceps strength, altered neuromuscular coordination, and sport-specific technical inefficiencies [50, 51]. In soccer, these residual deficits are particularly relevant because of the high rotational demands, cutting movements, and repetitive high-speed accelerations that stress the reconstructed graft [52].

An important and novel finding of the current study is that the time to RTP has increased substantially over the last decade. Between 2011 and 2016, players typically returned after about 6–7 months, whereas after 2022, the mean time to RTP exceeded 9 months [53]. This temporal shift likely reflects the growing emphasis on criteria-based RTP decision-making and improved understanding of graft maturation. Webster and Feller [23] and Nagelli and Hewett [18] both emphasized that biological graft integration and restoration of neuromuscular control require longer rehabilitation, and early RTP (< 9 months) is associated with a 4- to 7-fold higher re-injury risk [18, 23]. The widespread adoption of structured RTP batteries—incorporating objective strength testing, hop performance, limb symmetry indices, and movement quality—has improved safety standards in professional sport [16]. These criteria are also reflected in the ISAKOS 2022 Football Consensus, which recommends delaying RTP until objective readiness and movement competency are achieved [27]. The increasing use of imaging to evaluate graft ligamentization and incorporation may also contribute to clinicians’ preference for extended recovery periods before competition [54].

The pooled graft re-rupture rate of 8.2% is comparable to the rates reported by several authors, suggesting that re-rupture remains a persistent challenge despite surgical and rehabilitative advancements [38, 39, 43]. The persistently high re-rupture rates in professional soccer likely reflect the intrinsic biomechanical demands of the sport, characterized by repetitive pivoting, rapid deceleration, and multiplanar cutting maneuvers that impose substantial rotational and shear stresses on the reconstructed graft—often exceeding those encountered in non-pivoting sports [13, 45].

Beyond soccer-specific evidence, our findings align closely with recent data from other elite pivoting sports, further contextualizing the performance trajectory of professional athletes after ACLR. In a large multicenter analysis, D’Ambrosi et al. examined RTP outcomes across 11 elite and professional sports, showing remarkably consistent patterns with our results: high overall RTP rates, a substantial proportion of athletes returning at their pre-injury level, graft failure rates around 7%, and a mean RTP timeframe approaching 9–10 months. These similarities suggest that the recovery pattern observed in professional soccer is not sport-specific but reflects a broader trend shared by high-demand athletic populations exposed to similar biomechanical loads [15].

This interpretation is further strengthened by a recent study from the same group evaluating RTP outcomes among NBA players—a cohort characterized by exceptional physicality, intense pivoting, and high exposure to deceleration and cutting movements. Despite high RTP rates, NBA athletes demonstrated a delayed return, mirroring the progressive prolongation of RTP timelines we observed in elite soccer. These parallels reinforce the notion that contemporary rehabilitation protocols increasingly emphasize not only structural graft healing but also neuromuscular control, load tolerance, and psychological readiness, ultimately extending the time required to return safely to competition [55].

Taken together, the convergence of findings from elite soccer, basketball, and multi-sport professional cohorts indicates that the post-ACLR recovery pathway of modern high-performance athletes is evolving toward a more cautious and comprehensive model. Integrating evidence from these closely related sporting contexts strengthens the external validity of our results and supports the broader interpretation that prolonged RTP timelines may reflect improved standards of care rather than diminished athletic capacity.

The introduction of the lateral extra-articular procedure (LEAP) and anterolateral ligament (ALL) augmentation has been one of the most significant surgical evolutions in recent years. Randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses [25, 26] have shown that adding LEAP to ACLR reduces anterolateral rotatory instability and the risk of graft failure, particularly in high-risk subgroups such as young athletes, hyperlax individuals, and pivoting sport professionals [56, 57]. Despite these promising results, few studies included in the present review reported whether a LEAP was performed, highlighting a gap in the literature. Future high-level studies are warranted to determine whether the increased use of LEAP in professional soccer contributes to the stabilization of re-rupture rates despite greater game intensity and competition frequency.

It should be noted that there was considerable variability in surgical techniques among the included studies, with incomplete or inconsistent reporting in several cases. This heterogeneity limits the ability to draw direct comparisons between time periods. However, the observed variability likely reflects broader shifts in clinical practice over the past decade, as described in contemporary literature. Recent reviews have shown a gradual diversification in graft selection, with ongoing debate regarding the optimal choice among hamstring, bone–patellar tendon–bone, and quadriceps tendon autografts, each offering distinct biomechanical and clinical profiles [58, 59]. Similarly, contemporary surveys have documented an increasing, though not universal, interest in adjunctive anterolateral procedures and individualized surgical strategies tailored to patient-specific risk factors [60, 61]. These evolving trends underscore the complexity of comparing outcomes across studies performed in different eras and reinforce the need for standardized reporting of surgical details in future ACL research involving professional athletes.

While physical recovery has been extensively studied, psychological readiness is increasingly recognized as a decisive factor for successful RTP after ACLR. Fear of re-injury, reduced confidence, and diminished self-efficacy are frequently reported among athletes and have been shown to predict delayed or incomplete RTP [62, 63]. Even in professional athletes with optimal physical recovery, psychological barriers can result in hesitancy during pivoting, tackling, or landing movements, ultimately increasing the risk of compensatory movement patterns and secondary injuries [62]. Recent data indicate that 30–40% of professional soccer players exhibit signs of anxiety or reduced self-confidence during late-stage rehabilitation [7], underscoring the relevance of psychological monitoring throughout the RTP continuum.

Recent literature has expanded the conceptualization of psychological readiness beyond traditional constructs. In young athletes, low psychological readiness is strongly linked to elevated kinesiophobia, with nearly half demonstrating insufficient readiness despite meeting physical RTP criteria. This mismatch highlights that physical and psychological recovery often progress at different rates [64].

Similarly, in adult and professional athletes, demographic and clinical factors—including age, sex, symptom burden, and subjective knee function—have been shown to influence ACL-RSI scores and subsequent RTP trajectories [65]. In elite football, confidence appears to be a particularly influential dimension of psychological readiness. The recent validation of the Injury-Psychological Readiness to Return to Sport (I-PRRS) scale in professional male players provides a sport-specific tool to evaluate confidence during the transition to training and match play. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency and structural validity, emphasizing confidence as a central psychological requirement for RTP in high-performance contexts [65]. Emerging work also suggests that psychological readiness interacts with neurocognitive and neurophysiological changes following ACL injury. Altered cortical activation patterns, reduced motor planning efficiency, and compensatory neurocognitive reliance may create discrepancies between demonstrated physical capacity and perceived readiness, particularly in complex game-like scenarios [66–68]. Such mechanisms may explain why some athletes report persistent hesitation or reduced confidence despite completing standard functional testing. Although our meta-analysis did not specifically include psychological variables, the progressive delay in RTP observed over time may partly reflect the increasing integration of psychological and neurocognitive assessments into evidence-based RTP frameworks. This evolution aligns with modern approaches emphasizing holistic recovery rather than purely physical criteria.

Despite improvements in rehabilitation and surgical strategies, the unchanged re-rupture rates observed over time suggest that delayed RTP alone is insufficient to prevent graft failure. One potential explanation relates to the cumulative physical and match load experienced by professional players. Although public perception suggests an increasingly congested football calendar, recent epidemiological data from the International Centre for Sports Studies (CIES) Football Observatory show otherwise. Between 2012 and 2024, the average number of matches per club and season has remained stable at just over 40, with only about 5% of clubs playing 60 or more matches per season (excluding friendlies). Even when examining the major European leagues and the UEFA Champions League, the total number of official matches has actually decreased slightly over the past three seasons (CIES, 2024) [69]. Therefore, the observed delay in RTP cannot be attributed to an increased number of competitive matches. Instead, it more likely reflects the evolution of clinical decision-making—particularly the adoption of more stringent RTP testing and longer rehabilitation timelines aimed at minimizing re-injury risk.

Concurrently, intrinsic sport-specific demands—rapid deceleration, pivoting, and unpredictable contacts—continue to challenge graft integrity even under optimized rehabilitation. Furthermore, variations in graft selection (e.g., hamstring vs. bone–patellar tendon–bone vs. quadriceps tendon) and the selective addition of LEAP influence mechanical stability and may contribute to the observed heterogeneity in outcomes [3, 25, 26, 70].

From a practical standpoint, these findings emphasize that a longer rehabilitation period should not be interpreted as a guarantee of safety. Instead, clinicians should integrate multidimensional RTP criteria—including strength symmetry, movement analysis, and psychological assessment—into the clearance process. For club physicians, these results underscore the importance of individualized rehabilitation planning, objective monitoring of performance metrics, and progressive workload reintroduction, ideally within a multidisciplinary model involving surgeons, physiotherapists, sport scientists, and psychologists. In addition, surgical decision-making should account for player-specific risk factors: younger age, high pivot exposure, and revision cases may benefit from adjunctive procedures such as LEAP to mitigate rotational instability.

Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to evaluate RTP outcomes with standardized definitions and validated RTP test batteries. The establishment of international ACL registries in professional soccer, akin to the UEFA Elite Club Injury Study, would allow longitudinal monitoring of graft survival, career longevity, and functional recovery. Furthermore, integrating psychological readiness assessments—such as the ACL-Return to Sports Injury (RSI) scale—into future research may yield a more holistic understanding of RTP determinants. Finally, advanced imaging, wearable sensor data, and neuromuscular assessments may offer more objective indicators of functional readiness and re-injury risk.

Overall, our findings suggest that while modern rehabilitation and surgical strategies have shifted toward safety and evidence-based progression, a meaningful reduction in graft re-rupture has not yet been achieved. This highlights the multifactorial nature of successful RTP in professional soccer, in which biological recovery, mechanical stability, and psychological readiness must all be addressed concurrently.

This is the first meta-analysis to assess RTP outcomes and graft re-rupture across time in professional soccer players. The main strengths include strict adherence to PRISMA 2020 standards, a large cumulative sample size, and time-based stratification of studies reflecting evolving clinical practice. Nevertheless, limitations exist. Most included studies were retrospective and lacked uniformity in RTP definition, graft selection, and follow-up duration. Potential publication bias and unreported variables (such as surgical technique, psychological status, and concomitant injuries) may have influenced pooled estimates. Despite these constraints, the trends identified provide valuable insight into the evolving landscape of ACL management in professional soccer.

Conclusions

Following ACLR, more than 92% of professional soccer players successfully RTP, with approximately 80% regaining their pre-injury level of performance. The mean time to RTP was 263 days, and the overall graft re-rupture rate was 8.2%.

Over the past decade, the time to return has increased by more than 40%, reflecting a shift toward a more cautious, criteria-based approach. However, this delay has not been accompanied by a corresponding reduction in re-rupture rates, suggesting that extended rehabilitation alone is insufficient to enhance graft durability.

These findings underscore the need for a multidimensional strategy integrating surgical refinement, objective functional testing, and psychological readiness to optimize both safety and performance outcomes in professional soccer players.
