Neonatal torsion of the vermiform appendix presenting as generalized peritonitis: a case report of a 10-day-old infant
Authors: Lai Van Chu, Quynh Thi Vu Huynh, Trang Lu Quy, Ho Tran Ban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Torsion of the vermiform appendix is an extremely rare surgical emergency in children and has not previously been reported during the neonatal period. Because clinical manifestations are nonspecific, the diagnosis is usually established intraoperatively. We report a case of appendiceal torsion occurring in a 10-day-old male neonate who was diagnosed and treated at our institution.Case presentation:A 10-day-old male neonate was admitted with poor feeding, non-bilious vomiting, and fever. Emergency surgery was indicated based on clinical findings and abdominal radiographic features suggestive of generalized peritonitis secondary to perforated appendicitis. The preoperative diagnosis was peritonitis due to perforated appendicitis, and appendiceal torsion was identified only during surgery. The appendix was hemorrhagic, necrotic, and twisted 360° counterclockwise, with a narrow mesoappendix at its base. Appendectomy was performed, and the patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery.Discussion:This case represents the first reported appendiceal torsion in Vietnam and, to our knowledge, the youngest patient and the first neonatal case described worldwide. A narrow mesoappendix and inflammatory changes of the appendix were considered possible contributing factors. Because the clinical presentation is difficult to distinguish from acute appendicitis, most cases are diagnosed intraoperatively.Conclusion:Appendiceal torsion can occur in neonates. Although rare and unlikely to alter surgical decision-making, it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a neonatal surgical abdomen.

Introduction

Torsion of the vermiform appendix is defined as the rotation of the appendix around its mesentery, resulting in vascular compromise and subsequent ischemia and necrosis of the appendiceal wall[1]. This condition is extremely rare but has been reported in both adult and pediatric populations[2,3]. Up to 2020, only 22 pediatric cases had been documented, and the youngest previously reported patient was 50 days old[4]. Because the clinical manifestations are similar to those of acute appendicitis, the diagnosis is established intraoperatively in the majority of cases[3,5,6].

Neonatal appendiceal pathology itself is uncommon and often presents with nonspecific signs that may rapidly progress to peritonitis. Consequently, rare appendiceal conditions in the neonatal period may be difficult to recognize before surgical exploration. The purpose of this report is to describe a case of appendiceal torsion presenting as generalized peritonitis in a 10-day-old male neonate, thereby contributing additional clinical evidence to the limited literature on this rare surgical entity.

This case report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[7].

Case presentation

A 10-day-old male neonate was brought to our hospital with poor feeding, non-bilious vomiting, and fever. The mother also reported progressive abdominal distension and an absence of bowel movements during the preceding 24 hours. The infant was born vaginally at 38 weeks’ gestation to a 19-year-old mother, with a birth weight of 3.2 kg. Meconium passage occurred within the first 4 hours after birth, and feeding was initially adequate. No antenatal abnormalities were reported.

On admission, the neonate appeared lethargic and febrile (38.8°C), with tachycardia (180 beats/min) and a respiratory rate of 40 breaths/min; oxygen saturation was 97% on room air. Laboratory investigations showed a white blood cell count of 4900/mm3 with 65% neutrophils, hemoglobin level of 17.2 g/dL, and platelet count of 353 000/mm3. C-reactive protein was elevated at 72.6 mg/L, and serum lactate was 18.4 mg%. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed pH 7.53, pCO2 31.1 mmHg, pO2 113 mmHg, and HCO3− 25.7 mmol/L on FiO2 21%. The coagulation profile as well as hepatic and renal function tests were within normal limits.

The patient was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit with an initial diagnosis of gastrointestinal-source sepsis. Empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated with meropenem (40 mg/kg every 8 hours) and vancomycin (15 mg/kg every 8 hours), along with intramuscular vitamin K1 (5 mg). A nasogastric tube was inserted for gastric decompression. Six hours after admission, pediatric surgeons were consulted when greenish aspirate was noted from the nasogastric tube, raising suspicion of a surgical abdomen.

On surgical examination, the patient had a temperature of 38.2°C, a heart rate of 155 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 50 breaths/min, and a body weight of 3.3 kg. Abdominal examination revealed moderate distension without erythema of the abdominal wall, guarding in the right hemiabdomen. The mass in the right lower abdomen measured approximately 3 cm in diameter, had relatively well-defined borders, was non-mobile, and was associated with localized tenderness on palpation. Rectal examination using a catheter yielded a small amount of loose yellow stool. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated distended small bowel loops with gas but did not reveal any specific abnormal findings. Plain abdominal radiography showed a “sick belly” bowel pattern, characterized by diffusely dilated bowel loops scattered throughout the abdomen, suggestive of peritonitis (Fig. 1). Emergency laparotomy was performed through a supra-umbilical transverse incision. Approximately 50 mL of turbid yellow fluid was encountered in the peritoneal cavity, and no intra-abdominal abscess was identified. The appendix was located retrocecally in the right iliac fossa and was twisted 360° counterclockwise. After detorsion, the appendix was found to have a narrow mesoappendix, measuring 5 cm in length and 0.5 cm in diameter. It appeared soft and non-distended but showed hemorrhagic necrosis with a 2-mm perforation on the free border of the appendiceal body. A clear demarcation was observed between the necrotic segment and the appendiceal base (Fig. 2). No fecalith, lipoma, or mucocele was identified. Appendectomy was performed, the peritoneal cavity was irrigated with warm normal saline, and a Penrose drain was placed in the Douglas pouch for postoperative drainage. Histopathological examination demonstrated congestion, hemorrhage, edema, mucosal necrosis, transmural neutrophilic infiltration, and reactive lymphoid hyperplasia.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Bowel peristalsis returned on postoperative day 3, and oral feeding was resumed on postoperative day 4. A culture of peritoneal fluid grew Escherichia coli, which was sensitive to the initial antibiotic regimen. The Penrose drain was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 14.

Discussion and conclusions

Discussion

Appendiceal torsion is a rare cause of surgical abdomen in both adults and children and is defined as the twisting of the appendix around its mesentery, leading to ischemia and necrosis[1,8]. In children, it accounts for approximately 0.15% of all appendiceal surgical conditions[5], and only 22 pediatric cases had been reported up to 2020[3]. To our knowledge, this case represents the first reported appendiceal torsion in Vietnam, as well as the youngest patient and the first case described during the neonatal period worldwide.

Although the exact pathogenesis remains unclear, several anatomical and acquired factors have been associated with appendiceal torsion in children, including a narrow mesoappendix at the appendiceal base, lack of appendiceal fixation, fecalith, inflammatory changes, excessive appendiceal length, and adhesions to the abdominal wall[1,3,8,9]. In this patient, intraoperative findings demonstrated a narrow mesoappendix, while histopathological examination confirmed inflammatory involvement of the appendix. These findings suggest that both anatomical predisposition and inflammatory processes may have contributed to the development of torsion.

The clinical presentation of appendiceal disease in neonates is often nonspecific and frequently progresses to generalized peritonitis[10,11]. In this case, it remains uncertain whether the initial symptoms of abdominal distension and delayed bowel movements represented early manifestations of appendiceal inflammation that predisposed to torsion or whether they were secondary consequences of established peritonitis.

The indication for emergency surgery in this patient was based on clinical examination and abdominal radiographic findings suggestive of perforated appendicitis with peritonitis, and appendiceal torsion was identified only intraoperatively. Because the clinical features closely resemble those of acute appendicitis, most reported cases are diagnosed during surgery[3,5,6]. Recent reports suggest that ultrasonography may assist in preoperative diagnosis[12,13], whereas computed tomography is considered the most reliable imaging modality, particularly in the context of increasing interest in non-operative management of uncomplicated appendicitis[1,3,5].

Therefore, despite its rarity, appendiceal torsion should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a neonatal surgical abdomen. Early surgical intervention remains essential to prevent perforation and reduce the risk of morbidity and mortality.

Conclusion

This case confirms that torsion of the vermiform appendix can occur in neonates. Although rare and unlikely to influence surgical decision-making, it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a neonatal surgical abdomen.
