OpenDance: Multimodal Controllable 3D Dance Generation Using Large-scale Internet Data
Jinlu Zhang, Zixi Kang, Yizhou Wang

Abstract
Music-driven dance generation offers significant creative potential yet faces considerable challenges. The absence of fine-grained multimodal data and the difficulty of flexible multi-conditional generation limit previous works on generation controllability and diversity in practice. In this paper, we build OpenDance5D , an extensive human dance dataset comprising over 101 hours across 14 distinct genres. Each sample has five modalities to facilitate robust cross-modal learning: RGB video, audio, 2D keypoints, 3D motion, and fine-grained textual descriptions from human arts. Furthermore, we propose OpenDanceNet , a unified masked modeling framework for controllable dance generation conditioned on music and arbitrary combinations of text prompts, keypoints, or character positioning. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that OpenDanceNet achieves high-fidelity and flexible controllability. Project page: https://open-dance.github.io .

Music-driven dance generation, also termed AI Choreography, shows immense potential for various applications. The professional, realistic, and diverse dance can be used for video games, AR/VR, and digital human.
Music2Dance aims to automatically generate natural and professional dance movements

However, the automatic creation of controllable and diverse dance movements remains challenging. This is because a well generated dance requires both professional and smooth, and it must be aligned with musical rhythms and beats. Traditionally, achieving this has required either labor-intensive manual animations or the use of advanced motion capture (MoCap) systems, both of which come with considerable costs and challenges in the field of 3D vision.

. In practice, controllable choreography from multiple conditions determines the reusability of generation results and whether it can be aligned with user preferences. We argue that dance generation that is only driven by music can not precisely control results to satisfy user demand, which will lead to uncontrollable and low-utilized results. Recent works introduce editing ability by masking spatial body parts and temporal in-betweening motion

Specifically, we construct OpenDance5D by collecting large-scale in-the-wild videos. Table

OpenDance5D aggregates professional dance movements spanning 14 detailed dance genres and provides five synchronized modalities: RGB video, audio waveform, 2D skeletal keypoints, 3D motion, and fine-grained textual annotations. We first retrieve raw videos by querying a predefined list of dance genres. Subsequently, a semi-automatic annotation pipeline—comprising professional artists, human annotators, and a vision-language model (VLM)—is employed to extract and align each modality.
Among these modalities, the 2D keypoint sequence is obtained from the pretrained 2D pose estimator, represented as the COCO

To better support the dance generation task, we also provide text descriptions, including the gender, genre, environment, and action flow annotations for each dance sequence from professional artists.

To enable controllable and expressive dance generation under arbitrary multimodal guidance, we propose OpenDanceNet, a unified masked modeling framework. OpenDanceNet integrates multiple user-defined conditions—music, 2D pose sketches, and global trajectory constraints—to represent choreography preferences. During training, we employ a masking-based scheme to simulate sparse condition inputs (e.g., keyframe sketches and discrete trajectory points), thereby enhancing robustness under incomplete guidance.

In summary, our contributions can be summarized as follows:
(1). We build a multimodal large-scale 3D dance dataset OpenDance5D by designing a semi-automatic annotation pipeline to improve the 3D dance generation controllability and diversity;
(2). OpenDanceNet is presented to encourage multimodal conditional dance generation to better align with user demands and input music for various applications;
(3). Comprehensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our model and dataset in both evaluation metrics and user study.

3.1
Overview

Although lab-controlled motion capture systems deliver clear and consistent results, they are often prohibitively expensive and typically fail to provide aligned RGB videos.
As shown in Table
1
, we collect the multimodal dataset OpenDance5D, which includes five data sources: RGB videos, audio, 2D keypoints, 3D SMPL motions, and fine-grained text annotations. It comprises 101.68 hours, extracted from over 600 hours of wild videos, featuring over 75 different dancers, and 41K long-duration (over 60 seconds) dance sequences. All annotations have been verified by professional artists to ensure quality and all videos have been resampled to 25 FPS.
With rich annotations, OpenDance5D can support other generative tasks,
e.g.
music-driven dance generation, text-driven dance generation, pose-driven video generation.

3.2
Data Collection and Pre-process

Using a predefined list of dance genres and music style prompts provided by artists, we first employ the vision language model ChatGPT
[
31

to generate queries and check videos based on given prompts for searching appropriate videos.
Upon acquiring approximately 600 hours of internet videos, in the pre-process multimodal data filter, we assess video-prompt alignment by human verification to filter out non-compliant videos. And we utilize the YOLOX
[
9
]
to extract human bounding boxes, eliminating videos with incomplete bodies to isolate videos featuring solo dancers.

3.3
Data Annotation Pipeline

In our method, as shown in Figure
2
, to efficiently capture an extensive range of dance motions from a vast array of videos, which is hard for human annotator, we introduce a semi-automatic annotation pipeline. This pipeline synergizes the strengths of a pre-trained estimator
[
9
,
51
,
38
]
, LLM
[
13

2D Keypoints.

Directly employing the 2D pose estimator to extract COCO
[
25
]
keypoints from filtered videos may cause jittery problems due to the large variance of data. To overcome this issue, we apply a 2D keypoints estimator
[
51
]
to obtain the 2D keypoints sequence and conduct further frame-level filtering to remove jitter poses, retaining clean videos that are suitable for capturing human motion:

J(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(k_{i_{1},i_{2}}-k_{i_{1}+s,i_{2}+s})^{2},

(1)

where
J(i)
measures the jitter between two keypoint sequences: from
i_{1}
to
i_{2}
frames and from
i_{1}+s
to
i_{2}+s
frames, where
s
is the stride length of the sampling window, and
k
represents the mean value of each keypoint within the sequence. If
J(i)
exceeds the threshold, we remove the corresponding frames and split videos.

3D Motion Annotation.

Based on the extracted keypoints, we estimate 3D dance movements using the SMPL
[
27
]
model, a differentiable function that maps pose parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, shape parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and root translation
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 3}
to a set of 3D human body mesh vertices
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{6890\times 3}
and 3D joint positions
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times 3}
, where
J
denotes the number of body joints.
Unlike the prior approach
[
18
]
that utilized optimization-based approach
[
32
,
33
]
for capturing robust motion parameters from single image, we do not follow these methods due to their slow frame-aware optimization process in massive videos. Furthermore, it is hard to provide temporally smooth results and global trajectories.
We observed that the method relying on 2D keypoints for motion capture introduces significant depth ambiguities and global trajectory jitter, particularly in fast-moving dance sequences.
To mitigate these issues, we adopt the more efficient and accurate world-grounded 3D motion estimator
[
38
]
, a learning-based 3D pose estimator, to obtain more pixel-aligned and robust motion parameters (local joint parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and global translation
\{\boldsymbol{\phi_{t}}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and beta parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
for each sequence).

Text Descriptions.

To obtain detailed text descriptions for each motion sequence, we allocate the annotation tasks based on the annotators levels of expertise and the difficulty of the task.
Specifically, we employ: (1). Professional artists are responsible for annotating the three main genres and 14 detailed genres from a predefined list with identified genre_id and d_genre_id.
(2). Human annotators without professional dance knowledge are required to describe the start and end times of the dance video, gender, and the movement style for each video.
(3). LLM is utilized to generate detailed descriptions of the body movements, including arms, legs, head and whole-body movements, as well as the dance style. The LLM annotation is for 10 second video slice, using COCO keypoints
as input.

Audio Features.

We employ Jukebox
[
7
]
and Librosa
[
29
]
to extract audio features from WAV files, where Jukebox can produce 4800 high-dimensional features to support better parsing of music. Librosa can extract beats and accompanying 35 low-dimensional features, which can support fast inference.

3.4
Post-process and Dataset Analysis

We enhance the temporal jitter by applying a temporal optimization term in the post-processing phase following
[
18
]
, aiming to improve the temporal smoothness of the captured motions:

E_{s}=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|X_{j,t+1}-X_{j,t}\|^{2}.

(2)

Besides, during human verification, our artists observed that estimated motions tend to predominantly move in one direction (usually forward), even though the feet in dance sequences are expected to remain stationary. This issue leads to misalignment with the source video and results in unnatural foot movements. To address this problem, we employ the Physical Foot Contact score (PFC)
[
44
]
as a penalty term during the optimization process.
Finally, we apply a post-process data filter to remove processed data in terms of jitter and PFC scores, and we also remove the first 5 seconds of each video to eliminate the initial jitter caused by the dancer entry into the frame.

During training, we slice the data to facilitate efficient training processes. To ensure coherence between music and motion, we require the shortest motion sequence must exceed 10 seconds. Furthermore, we employ data augmentation by using larger stride steps to sample genres with abundant dance data, such as
Street
, and dense steps for genres with scarce data, to ensure a balanced representation across different genres.

To mitigate these challenges, we propose
OpenDanceNet
, a novel model that integrates information from multiple modalities via a mask prediction strategy, achieving state-of-the-art performance in dance generation. We first present the training procedure of the Motion-Keypoints Residual VQ-VAE (MK-RVQ) in

4.1
Training of Motion-Keypoints Residual VQ-VAE (MK-RVQ)

Residual VQ-VAE (RVQ) has proven to be more effective than conventional VQ-VAE as a motion tokenizer
[
11
,
37
]
. Our proposed MK-RVQ extends this capability by not only reconstructing motion sequences but also enabling transformation from rotation-angle-based representations to 2D keypoint representations. To facilitate this, we incorporate keypoint supervision during RVQ training, as both global trajectories and 2D keypoints are strongly correlated with motion, unlike modalities such as music or text.

Given a motion sequence of length
T
, denoted as
X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
, the RVQ encoder compresses it into latent features
z\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{\prime}\times d}
. These features are then quantized into
N
layers of discrete tokens
\hat{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T^{\prime}\times d}
using a learned multi-layer codebook
C^{l}=\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
for each layer
l=1,2,\ldots,N
. Starting with the first quantization layer using codebook
C^{1}
, the residual between the input latent and the quantized output is computed as
R^{2}=z-\hat{z}^{1}
, where
\hat{z}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{\prime}\times d}
. This residual is then passed to the second codebook
C^{2}
for further quantization, and the process continues iteratively. The final quantized representation is formed by concatenating the outputs from all layers:
\hat{z}=[\hat{z}^{1},\hat{z}^{2},\ldots,\hat{z}^{N}]
.

The motion decoder reconstructs the motion sequence
X_{\text{pred}}
from the sum of all quantized layers in
\hat{z}
. Notably, RVQ not only reduces reconstruction loss but also encodes critical information in the shallowest layers, particularly in
C^{1}
. Leveraging this property, we use only the shallowest quantized layer
\hat{z}^{1}
as input to a keypoints decoder to predict the corresponding 2D keypoints
K_{\text{pred}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}}
.

The MK-RVQ model is trained using the following loss function. In this formulation,
R^{i}
denotes the residual input to the
i
-th quantization layer, with
R^{1}=z
, and
\text{sg}(\cdot)
represents the stop-gradient operation. The coefficients
\lambda_{\text{vel}}
,
\lambda_{\text{kpts}}
, and
\lambda_{\text{commit}}
are used to balance the corresponding loss components.

L_{\text{MK-RVQ}}=\|X-X_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{vel}}\|\dot{X}-\dot{X}_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{kpts}}\|K-K_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{commit}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|R^{i}-\text{sg}(\hat{z}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}

(3)

4.2
Architecture of the Multimodal-Condition Transformer (MCT)

As illustrated in
fig.

4
, our proposed Multimodal-Condition Transformer (MCT) is designed to handle a diverse set of input modalities, including a music sequence
M\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{m}}
, 2D keypoints
K\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}}
, a textual description
text\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}
, and a global trajectory
G\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 3}
. Each modality is independently encoded using a dedicated, modality-specific encoder.

During training, a special
[\text{MASK}]
token is randomly inserted into selected frames of the first-layer motion tokens
\hat{z}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{\prime}\times d}
. The MCT is trained to reconstruct the original motion tokens by leveraging all available conditions through multiple self-attention transformer layers. The model predicts the likelihood of the masked token IDs, guided by a cross-entropy loss, where
\hat{z}^{1}_{\text{mask}}
denotes the masked sequence of first-layer tokens and
\hat{z}^{1}_{i}
represents the
i
-th target token.

L_{\text{CE}}^{\text{mask}}=-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}^{1}}\sum\limits_{i\in\text{mask positions}}\log p_{\theta}(\hat{z}^{1}_{i}\mid\hat{z}^{1}_{\mathit{mask}},\mathit{cond})

(4)

For deeper motion token layers
\hat{z}^{2},\ldots,\hat{z}^{N}
, the MCT is trained to predict the
n
-th layer tokens
\hat{z}^{n}
using both the multimodal conditions and the discrete tokens from the previous layer
\hat{z}^{n-1}
. The corresponding cross-entropy loss is defined as:

L_{\text{CE}}^{\text{layer}}=-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}^{n}}\sum\limits_{i\in T^{\prime}}\log p_{\theta}(\hat{z}^{n}_{i}\mid\hat{z}^{n-1},\mathit{cond})

(5)

4.3
Multimodal Conditioning in Training

When training different models on our proposed dataset, we observe that different input modalities exhibit varying degrees of correlation with dance motion. In particular, global trajectory and 2D keypoints show strong alignment with motion patterns, whereas modalities such as text and music are less directly correlated. Consequently, the model tends to over-rely on the strongly correlated modalities, potentially limiting its ability to learn meaningful representations from weaker ones.

To address this imbalance, we introduce a targeted masking strategy during the mask prediction phase for the first-layer RVQ tokens. Specifically, with fixed probabilities
P_{k}
and
P_{g}
, we randomly mask the 2D keypoints and global trajectory tokens, respectively, encouraging the model to rely more on weaker modalities such as text and music during training. For deeper RVQ layers, we supervise the model using unmasked inputs, as these layers exhibit reduced dependence on global trajectory and 2D keypoints. Additionally, to improve generalization, we train MCT using a diverse set of condition combinations, enabling it to generate high-quality dance motion during inference even when only a subset of input modalities is provided. The condition mask sampling process is defined as:

\mathit{cond}=\mathit{sample}(M,\mathit{mask}(K),\mathit{mask}(G),t)

(6)

4.4
Cross-Modal Supervision for Enhanced Representation Learning

During MCT training, we incorporate multiple control signals to strengthen inter-modal correlations and improve the alignment between each modality and the target dance motion, thereby enhancing overall model performance. Specifically, a Motion Decoder and a Keypoints Decoder are used to process the predicted first-layer tokens
\hat{z}^{1}_{\text{pred}}
, reconstructing the global trajectory and 2D keypoints, respectively. These predictions are supervised by comparing them with corresponding ground truth. The output of the Motion Decoder,
X_{\text{pred}}
, is additionally used to compute both a reconstruction loss and a forward kinematics loss, which together promote faster convergence. To further improve the physical plausibility of the generated motion, we incorporate contact label supervision into the training process.

The overall training objective for MCT is defined as follows:

\displaystyle L_{\text{MCT}}=

\displaystyle L_{\text{CE}}^{\text{mask}}+L_{\text{CE}}^{\text{layer}}+\lambda_{\text{rec}}\|X-X_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{g}\|G-G_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}

(7)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{kpts}}\|K-K_{\text{pred}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{fk}}\|FK(X)-FK(X_{\text{pred}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{contact}}\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\|\left(FK(X)-FK(X_{\text{pred}})\right)\cdot b^{i}\|_{2}^{2}

Here,
FK(\cdot)
denotes the forward kinematics operation, and
b^{i}
represents the binary contact label for the
i
-th joint. The coefficients
\lambda_{\text{rec}},\lambda_{g},\lambda_{\text{kpts}},\lambda_{\text{fk}},
and
\lambda_{\text{contact}}
control the relative contributions of each loss component.

Implementation Details

We evaluate our proposed method on both the AIST++ dataset

Following the original dataset splits, we preprocess all training sequences into 5-second clips at 30 frames per second (FPS) for AIST++, while maintaining 10 FPS for OpenDance5D. We represent poses using the 24-joint SMPL model, with 6 degrees of freedom (DOF) rotation for each joint
[
55
]

44

We use Jukebox
[
7
]
to encode the music wav, and apply CLIP
[

to extract text features.
Our model is trained on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU with 16GB of memory.

6
Limitation and Future Work

Despite the advances demonstrated, our work opens multiple avenues for future research.
The OpenDance5D does not capture detailed hand–finger articulations or facial expressions, which are critical for conveying emotion in dance, particularly for applications in emotional choreography and video generation. Future work should incorporate high-resolution hand and facial keypoint annotations or adopt multi-camera capture to enrich the expressiveness of generated movements.

Besides, although we collect raw RGB video as one modality, we have not benchmarked or adapted state-of-the-art video generation models on our OpenDance5D due to differing research focus and safe-policy constraints.

We currently encode all textual annotations into a single token by CLIP
[
36
]
encoder, disregarding distinctions between genre, scene, and body-movement descriptions. In the future, we plan to adopt a vision–language modeling architecture to learn more text tokens for different descriptions, thereby enhancing controllability and semantic understanding of text prompts.

7
Conclusion

We present OpenDance, a multimodal-condition dance generation framework, which introduces a novel multimodal dataset (OpenDance5D)—comprising 101.68 hours of in-the-wild recordings across 14 genres with 5 synchronized modalities—and a unified masked modeling architecture (OpenDanceNet) to achieve multiple conditional dance generation. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that our method generates highly diverse and realistic dance motions finely attuned to user-defined conditions (music, text, keypoints, and spatial trajectory), outperforming existing methods in both fidelity and controllability.

A.1.3
Multimodal Dance Generation

Recently, Music2Dance has evolved from single-modal (music-only) to multimodal approaches that integrate various inputs such as text descriptions, lyrics, for more expressive, controllable, and contextually coherent dance generation.
Recent works have explored the integration of music and text to guide dance generation with richer semantic context. TM2D
[
10

pioneered bimodality-driven 3D dance generation by utilizing a cross-attention mechanism to fuse music and text features.
LM2D
[
52

Large Motion Model (LMM)
[
53

UDE
[
57

56

[
54

extends this concept by introducing a generalized framework that can convert any input modality (images, videos, text, music) into human motions through a shared latent space, significantly improving the versatility of motion generation systems.

Although these approaches employ hybrid training or latent space fusion to achieve multimodal conditional generation, the scarcity of high-quality multimodal dance data restricts control to coarse action labels or dance genres, preventing its application to more complex scenarios.

A.2
Evaluation Metrics

Frechet Inception Distance (FID) Score

The Frechet Inception Distance (FID) score is extensively employed to gauge the proximity between the distributions of synthesized dances and the actual data. Analogous to the approach in
[
20
]
, we train a style classifier on our dataset to derive motion features, which we subsequently use to compute the FID.

\mathrm{FID}_{k}
and
\mathrm{FID}_{g}
represent the Frechet Inception Distance (FID) based on kinematic and geometric features, respectively. These evaluation metrics are used to measure the quality, diversity, and consistency between generated motion sequences and real motion sequences.
\mathrm{FID}_{k}
primarily assesses the physical attributes of motion sequences, such as velocity and acceleration, while
\mathrm{FID}_{g}
focuses on the geometric relationships between specific points on the body within the motion sequences.

Diversity

Diversity assesses the variance among dance sequences generated in response to musical stimuli. It serves as an indicator of the model’s capacity for generalization and its dependency on the input music. We measure diversity by calculating the mean feature distance, utilizing the same classifier as in the FID evaluation. This approach is consistent with methodologies in preceding research
[
40
]
. We denote motion diversity in kinetic and geometric feature spaces as
Div_{k}
and
Div_{g}
, respectively.

Consistency

Consistency quantifies the congruence between music and the resulting motions. It is measured by the Beat Align Score (BAS), which computes the average temporal distance between each musical beat and the nearest corresponding dance beat.

Physical Foot Contact (PFC) Score

The Physical Foot Contact (PFC) score, introduced by EDGE
[
44
]
, evaluates the physical plausibility of dance motions. It does so by examining the consistency of character acceleration with foot-ground contacts, rather than relying on explicit physical modeling. The foundational premise is that credible character acceleration is contingent upon stable foot-ground contact.

A.3
Broader impacts

Our work on controllable, multimodal dance generation has the several positive impacts:
1. By lowering the barrier to create professional-quality dance motions, our model can empower artists, educators, and game/VR developers without access to expensive motion-capture studios.
2. The ability to specify high-level textual, kinematic, or positional conditions enables novel forms of artistic expression, interactive storytelling, and educational tools for dance training.
3. A large, genre-diverse dataset encourages exploration of global dance styles, fostering cultural appreciation and promoting diversity in digital media.
However, there are also potential negative impacts to consider. High-fidelity motion synthesis could be employed to generate non-consensual or deceptive videos, raising concerns around misinformation.
