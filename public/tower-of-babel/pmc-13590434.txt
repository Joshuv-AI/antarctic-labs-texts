From Prediction to Decision Making: PBPK and QSP as Regulatory‐Grade NAMs
Authors: Karen Rowland Yeo, Piet H van der Graaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew approach methodologies (NAMs) encompass a diverse and rapidly evolving set of experimental and computational tools designed to generate human‐relevant mechanistic data for use in drug development and regulatory decision making. Experimental NAMs provide insights into drug disposition, pharmacological activity, and disease biology that are difficult or impossible to obtain from traditional animal‐based models. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) models represent a complementary class of computational NAMs. Together, experimental and computational NAMs form an integrated translational framework that converts mechanistic biological data into quantitative predictions of human exposure, efficacy, and safety across the drug development continuum. In this state‐of‐the‐art review, we present our perspective on the current and emerging role of PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs, supported by case studies spanning a range of regulatory and clinical applications. Across all case studies, the integration of human‐relevant experimental data into mechanistic models is shown to be the critical determinant of translational success. We also discuss the evolving regulatory landscape for NAMs, including the recent FDA draft guidance on QSP‐based MABEL determination, and the ICH M15 framework for model‐informed drug development. Collectively, these developments signal a fundamental shift in how mechanistic models are positioned within drug development and regulatory decision making: not as alternatives to animal testing alone, but as quantitative decision‐support frameworks that generate the human‐relevant evidence needed to support safer, more effective, and more equitable medicines.

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently issued guidance on New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), providing a framework for the validation and regulatory application of innovative approaches with the aim of improving prediction of toxicity in humans while reducing reliance on animal testing. 1 As part of an investigational new drug (IND) application, sponsors are required to submit nonclinical pharmacology and toxicology data to support assessment of whether a candidate drug can safely proceed to clinical evaluation. 2 Historically, animal studies have formed the cornerstone of this assessment, but significant advances in experimental and computational approaches offer new opportunities to generate evidence that can improve the translation of nonclinical findings to humans. The guidance reflects the FDA's commitment to facilitating broader integration of NAMs into drug development and regulatory submissions, with the goal of advancing more predictive, efficient, and human‐centric safety assessment strategies.

Historically, the NAMs movement emerged largely from the fields of toxicology and chemical safety assessment, where the primary goal was to reduce or replace animal testing. 3 , 4 Although the FDA guidance is primarily focused on predictive toxicology, the growing regulatory acceptance of in silico mechanistic modeling raises important questions about the role of physiologically based pharmacokinetics (PBPK) 5 , 6 , 7 and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) 8 , 9 within the NAM framework, questions this review seeks to address. Data generated from NAMs can be integrated into PBPK and QSP models, translating experimental findings into predictions of human exposure, biological response, and safety outcomes, thereby informing decisions throughout the drug development lifecycle. This is timely because PBPK and QSP are increasingly being used not just for safety assessment, but for regulatory decision making, dose optimization, specific population predictions, and evidence generation under frameworks including the very recent ICH M15. 10 Most significantly, the FDA published draft guidance in June 2026 specifically endorsing QSP‐based approaches for determining the minimum anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) in first‐in‐human (FIH) trials, a direct regulatory signal that computational mechanistic models are now considered fit‐for‐purpose NAMs in the context of early clinical dose selection, particularly for complex biologics where traditional animal‐based methods are considered insufficient. 11

Although the term is commonly associated with in vitro experimental approaches, the underlying principles that define a NAM can be applied to any system that is designed to reflect human biology or drug behavior mechanistically, with the explicit aim of generating human‐relevant predictions and reducing dependence on traditional animal‐based methods. On this basis, PBPK and QSP models represent a distinct but complementary class of computational NAMs, a position that reflects an emerging rather than a universally settled consensus, and one we return to below.

There are many publications on the application of PBPK modeling in drug development, especially for predictions of drug–drug interactions (DDIs), and case studies focusing on biopharmaceutics and specific populations. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Similarly, the growing body of QSP publications over the past decade reflects increasing recognition of mechanistic systems models as valuable decision‐support tools throughout drug development. Applications have expanded from early target evaluation and candidate selection to dose optimization, trial design, biomarker qualification, and regulatory interactions. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 PBPK and QSP occupy a unique position within the NAM ecosystem: they serve both as integrating frameworks for experimental NAM data and, increasingly, as quantitative decision‐support tools in their own right. In this review, we present case studies demonstrating how the integration of experimental data with mechanistic modeling is redefining PBPK and QSP not only as alternatives to animal testing, but as quantitative decision‐support frameworks that translate human‐relevant data into predictions that drive decisions across the translational continuum.

PBPK AND QSP WITHIN THE NAM ECOSYSTEM

Experimental NAMs as sources of mechanistic data

NAMs encompass a diverse range of experimental systems designed to reflect drug behavior and biological responses in humans, spanning a spectrum of complexity. At the simpler end, well‐established in vitro assays such as Caco‐2 monolayers, human liver microsomes, and hepatocyte suspensions or co‐cultures provide high‐throughput measures of individual absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME processes), including permeability, intrinsic clearance, and transporter‐mediated efflux. At the more complex end, advanced systems such as organoids, microphysiological systems (organ‐on‐chip platforms), and molecular profiling technologies including proteomics and metabolomics aim to more closely mimic human tissue architecture, functionality, and inter‐organ connectivity, offering mechanistic insight into drug disposition, pharmacological activity, and disease biology that simpler assays cannot capture. 24 , 25 , 26 For example, microphysiological systems are increasingly used to generate physiological data for specific populations, including pregnant women, who are typically under‐represented in clinical studies; these platforms offer unique opportunities to characterize physiological changes, placental drug transfer, and maternal–fetal exposure, addressing data gaps in these populations. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Both categories of NAM are sources of quantitative data for mechanistic models that translate experimentally observed biology into human‐relevant predictions. The ways in which these data are integrated into mechanistic frameworks, and the translational value this integration confers, are considered in the following section.

PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs

Both PBPK and QSP modeling frameworks align closely with the core principles that characterize the NAM paradigm. PBPK models are inherently human‐centric, built around physiological parameters: tissue volumes, blood flows, enzyme abundances that can be specified for defined human populations, including patients with organ impairment, pediatric subjects, or pregnant women. QSP models are similarly grounded in human biology, encoding known mechanisms of disease progression, receptor pharmacology, and systems‐level drug response. Critically, both approaches are mechanistic rather than empirical: they seek to represent the underlying biological and physiological processes that govern drug behavior, rather than simply fitting observed data. This mechanistic foundation is what enables prospective prediction, the ability to forecast drug exposure, pharmacodynamic response, or disease trajectory in populations and scenarios that have not yet been studied experimentally. In this respect, PBPK and QSP share one of the most consequential goals of the NAM agenda: generating reliable human predictions before, or in place of, studies that would otherwise require animal or human subjects.

PBPK models provide a natural framework for integrating data from NAMs because both are built on compartmentalized, mechanistic representations of drug disposition. Simple in vitro ADME parameters have long informed PBPK models via conventional in vitro–in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) approaches, but organ‐on‐a‐chip (OoC)‐derived measures of permeability, hepatic clearance, and transporter kinetics are increasingly being used to refine or replace these single‐endpoint inputs with data that better reflects tissue‐level architecture and multiorgan interplay. In a recent study, it was demonstrated that data generated from multiorgan chips could deliver accurate predictions of the pharmacokinetics of drugs administered intravenously when combined with PBPK models. 31 Furthermore, in coupled gut‐liver platforms, flow rates and chamber dimensions can be scaled to physiological blood flows and organ volumes, enabling prediction of first‐pass metabolism and systemic exposure following oral administration. 32 , 33 In these more complex systems, translatability becomes a central challenge: allometric or mechanistic scaling functions are required to bridge OoC and human organ systems. 34 These functions remain a source of predictive uncertainty, and validated scaling strategies are so far compound‐class and platform‐dependent rather than generalizable. Although the more routine simpler in vitro ADME assays have their own issues in terms of prediction accuracy, the scaling relationships to human physiology are comparatively well characterized.

QSP models draw on this same spectrum of NAM data but extend its use toward mechanistic prediction of pharmacodynamic response, efficacy, and toxicity. Simple in vitro assays can supply discrete parameters such as receptor binding affinity or enzyme kinetics for individual QSP model components, while organoids and OoC platforms, by preserving cellular and, in some cases, multicellular architecture, generate richer data on receptor engagement, signaling pathway activation, immune cell behavior, and tissue‐level functional readouts that simpler assays cannot provide. 35 These more complex data can be used to parameterize or validate the mechanistic sub‐models within a QSP framework, linking local drug concentrations, often generated by a PBPK model, to cellular and physiological responses such as biomarker changes, cytotoxicity, or disease‐relevant phenotypic shifts. This is particularly valuable for immuno‐oncology, fibrosis, and inflammatory disease applications, where such systems can recapitulate patient‐ or disease‐specific cellular contexts that static in vitro assays cannot. However, translatability constraints again become most apparent with the complex NAMs: the abbreviated timescales, absence of systemic feedback loops (endocrine, neural, whole‐organism immune surveillance), and limited longevity of most chip cultures mean that QSP models calibrated on OoC data can capture acute or short‐term mechanistic responses well but require substantial extrapolation and corresponding uncertainty when projected to chronic dosing or whole‐body disease dynamics. 36 A recent example of integrating a multiorgan microphysiological system with QSP is the work by Rigal et al., 37 who showed that diabetic phenotypes seen in patients can be mimicked in a microfluidic chip connecting cell models of pancreas and liver.

Recent advances in quantitative proteomics further illustrate the synergistic relationship between experimental and computational NAMs across this spectrum. 38 By providing quantitative measurements of enzyme, transporter, and receptor abundance, as well as signaling pathway components, proteomics can refine parameterization within both PBPK and QSP frameworks, whether applied to simple in vitro systems or complex tissue models. An example of such an approach is the step‐by‐step tutorial by Lazarou et al. published in this journal on integration of omics data into a QSP model of immune‐oncology. 39 Together, these examples illustrate the extent to which experimental NAM data now depends on mechanistic modeling to realize its translational value.

Although PBPK and QSP are not universally classified as NAMs across all stakeholder communities, the definitions adopted by the major regulatory and interagency bodies have largely converged. The term originated in chemical safety assessment, where it was defined broadly from the outset: the Interagency Coordinating Committee on the Validation of Alternative Methods (ICCVAM) describes NAMs as any non‐animal technology, methodology, or approach providing information for hazard and risk assessment 40 , whereas the EPA's definition under the Toxic Substance Control Act (TSCA) explicitly encompasses computational and in silico models. 41 The medicines regulators have also adopted inclusive definitions: the FDA states that NAMs encompass in vitro human‐based systems, in silico modeling, and other innovative platforms, 1 and the EMA lists computer modeling among its examples of NAMs. 42

In much of the toxicology‐related literature, the term is used mainly to denote human‐relevant experimental systems, with mechanistic models regarded as decision‐support tools rather than as NAMs in their own right. This distinction is not merely semantic: it reflects genuine differences in how evidence is generated, validated, and interpreted. Experimental NAMs directly generate human‐relevant biological information; computational NAMs translate that information into quantitative predictions of exposure, efficacy, and safety, at the whole‐body or population‐level, which is more directly relevant to regulatory decision making. Neither is sufficient alone: an experimental system cannot reproduce systemic exposure, and a mechanistic model cannot generate the underlying biology. In regulatory practice, it is interesting to note that computational approaches are already well established: a recent CDER analysis of 15 years of submissions found that in silico methods accounted for 49% of NAMs used in drug applications. 43 We therefore treat PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs, which are consistent with the definitions adopted by major regulatory bodies, while recognizing that usage of the term still varies between communities.

A conceptual framework for integration

We propose that the four key features for validation of NAMs (context of use, human biological relevance, technical characterization, and fit‐for‐purpose) described in the FDA draft guidance “General Considerations for the Use of New Approach Methodologies in Drug Development” 1 map directly to the framework for assessment of model‐informed drug development (MIDD) evidence outlined in ICH M15. 10 Therefore, combining these two guidelines can serve as a framework for integration of experimental and computational NAMs.

The preceding sections have described experimental and computational NAMs as components of a single, integrated translational infrastructure rather than parallel or competing approaches. Figure 1 presents a conceptual framework illustrating this relationship. Experimental NAMs occupy the upstream position in this framework, generating mechanistic biological data with direct relevance to human physiology that capture aspects of drug behavior and disease biology that traditional animal models cannot reliably reproduce. These data flow into PBPK and QSP models, which function as computational NAMs: integrating and contextualizing experimental inputs within mechanistic representations of human physiology, disease biology, and drug action to generate quantitative predictions of exposure, response, and safety.

The relationship is not simply a one‐way transfer of data. Computational models actively shape the experimental NAM design, identifying parameter gaps, defining the precision and biological context required of experimental measurements, and providing the quantitative framework that determines whether experimental data are fit for translational purpose. A proteomics experiment designed to quantify hepatic CYP and transporter abundances for PBPK scaling, or a microphysiological system study configured to generate placental transfer parameters for a pregnancy model, illustrates this bidirectionality: the experimental NAM is designed around the needs of the computational model, and the computational model is only as human‐relevant as the experimental NAM data that inform it. This interdependence means that the limitations in either domain, the scaling uncertainty inherent to complex experimental NAMs or the structural assumptions built into a PBPK or QSP model, propagate directly into the reliability of the resulting prediction. Critically, the consequences of this uncertainty are related directly to regulatory impact and patient safety. This is the principle underlying the recently published ICH M15 guideline, which ties the required rigor of model verification and validation to the model's influence and the consequence of the decision it supports, rather than applying a single fixed standard across all applications. The case studies presented in this review illustrate the range of contexts in which this integration delivers quantitative predictions that support regulatory and clinical decision making.

PBPK CASE STUDIES

Approved drugs have decades of safety and efficacy data, but repurposing or reformulating them for new contexts, a new route, population, or indication, often runs into a translational gap. How can we determine whether a drug reaches the intended tissue at the appropriate concentration when direct tissue sampling is not feasible, or when the only safety signal is observed in a different species?

Application of PBPK modeling for prediction of DDIs is well established, with dedicated regulatory agency guidance and a substantial body of accepted submissions behind it. 7 The three case studies below were chosen because each relates to an emerging PBPK application argued in a live regulatory setting, and each addresses a different translational gap: demonstrating bioequivalence (BE) for a topical generic, recontextualizing an animal toxicology finding for breastfeeding infants, and resolving a contested choice of in vitro‐to‐in vivo comparator during the COVID‐19 era.

Diclofenac sodium topical gel — Virtual bioequivalence for a generic

Topical drugs pose a complex BE issue: the site of action for diclofenac gel is the synovial fluid of the joint, which obviously has limited access for conventional PK sampling. The traditional approach is a comparative clinical endpoint study, which is expensive, time‐consuming, and often not sensitive enough to capture formulation differences. The generic diclofenac sodium topical gel 1% approval was a gamechanger: it was the first ANDA (Abbreviated New Drug Application) in which a dermal PBPK model‐based virtual BE assessment, informed by in vitro permeation data, supported the evidentiary package for approval. 44

In the first instance, a minimal PBPK model for diclofenac was developed and validated for IV and oral administration. Thereafter, the route of administration was changed to dermal and the Multi‐Phase Multi‐Layer Mechanistic Dermal Absorption (MPML MechDermA) model spanning stratum corneum, viable epidermis, dermis, subcutis, and a deeper tissue compartment was used to predict percutaneous absorption and local distribution. 45 The deep‐tissue compartment was resized to match reported synovial fluid volume, allowing direct simulation of concentration at the actual site of action. Sensitivity analysis identified formulation pH, viscosity, and droplet size as the parameters that most influenced predicted exposure via the dermal route. These parameters were measured for both the reference product (Voltaren topical gel 1%) and the generic, alongside an in vitro release test confirming equivalent diclofenac release rates. These data served as the mechanistic link translating formulation composition into predicted in vivo exposure. Validation of the model occurred at two levels: diclofenac‐specific model performance against plasma and skin tissue data from multiple dermatological formulations, and broader platform performance across drugs spanning a wide physicochemical range (logP from −1.6 to 6.4). Performance was judged fit for purpose across most compounds tested. FDA approval was based on a totality‐of‐evidence package, with the dermal PBPK virtual BE modeling replacing the comparative clinical endpoint study normally required.

The diclofenac case study illustrates how experimental and computational NAMs complement one another. A sensitivity analysis performed using the dermal PBPK model identified the key formulation parameters affecting exposure, determining which in vitro permeation measurements were worth performing. The generated data were used to parameterize the model, and once integrated, became regulatory‐enabling.

Moxidectin and lactation–Recontextualising an animal safety signal

Moxidectin is an FDA‐approved macrocyclic lactone for onchocerciasis (river blindness), given as a single oral 8 mg dose. 46 Mass drug administration (MDA) programs treat entire at‐risk communities in sub‐Saharan Africa, where roughly 8% of the eligible population are pregnant or breastfeeding at any given time. Excluding this group denies them treatment and blanket recommendations against breastfeeding in settings where formula feeding carries its own significant risks. Moxidectin's US label restricts breastfeeding during treatment and for 7 days afterward. 47 Although the figure looks arbitrary, it is the result of regulatory review of a data package including clinical lactation data, rat pre‐ and postnatal (PPN) toxicology and PBPK modeling.

The clinical lactation study came first. In a regulatory‐standard study, moxidectin was measured in plasma and breast milk from 12 lactating women following a single 8 mg oral dose, with milk collected for about 28 days and plasma for up to 90. 48 The relative infant daily dose, a “gold‐standard” metric used to assess medication safety during breastfeeding, was reported to be <10%, which is generally considered to be safe. This dataset became the basis for the original label language on lactation. Critically, no PK samples were taken from breastfeeding infants.

Then a rat PPN toxicology study raised a concern. Dams dosed daily through gestation and lactation led to increased pup mortality, decreased body weight, and developmental delays at a dose less than twice the recommended human dose based on BSA. The fact that the rat observed no adverse effect level (NOAEL) corresponded to an estimated pup plasma concentration close to the human maternal plasma C max raised concerns. 49

A PBPK model for moxidectin validated with the maternal plasma data from the clinical lactation study was used to predict exposures in breastfed infants. 49 The daily amount of moxidectin measured in breast milk from the clinical study was used as the infant dose input and plasma concentrations in the infant from birth through 6 months were simulated applying a conservative slow‐ontogeny assumption given the uncertainty over cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) vs. biliary clearance in neonates. The model addressed an issue related to the PPN data; rat pups were exposed trans‐placentally and via milk from dams dosed daily through gestation and lactation, unlike a human infant exposed only through milk after a single maternal dose. Also, rats lack a functional blood–brain barrier until after postnatal Day 14, with P‐glycoprotein absent until after Day 10, so early rat toxicity plausibly reflects an immature barrier as much as systemic drug levels. Predicted Day 1 infant exposure of about 3.29 ng/mL was about 20‐fold less than maternal C max and roughly 65‐fold below the rat NOAEL‐equivalent concentration, widening to a 3,500‐fold margin by 4 weeks.

In summary, cross‐species inference proved unreliable here, and no human infant PK data were available. A PBPK model was used to extend the existing maternal plasma and breast milk dataset, generating the infant exposure predictions needed to inform a regulatory decision. Rather than the conservative default of a 6month breastfeeding restriction, based on the drug's long half‐life and sustained presence in maternal plasma, the model, as part of a quantitative clinical pharmacology approach, 49 supported a substantially narrower restriction of avoiding breastfeeding during treatment and for 7 days afterward.

Tafenoquine and COVID‐19 – From regulatory uncertainty to clinical proceed

Tafenoquine, a long‐half‐life 8‐aminoquinoline antimalarial, distributes extensively into tissue relative to plasma, making it an appealing candidate against pulmonary SARS‐CoV‐2. It was found to inhibit the virus in VERO E6 cells with EC50/EC90 values near 2.6/5.1 μM. 50 A permeability‐limited PBPK lung model was used to demonstrate that the approved prophylactic dosing regimen was sufficient to keep unbound intracellular lung concentrations above the EC90 for at least 3 weeks, the rationale behind a proposed phase II trial in ambulatory COVID‐19 patients. 51

The FDA questioned whether lung concentration was a valid comparator to the in vitro EC50 values. 52 For drugs like hydroxychloroquine, high tissue levels mostly reflect organelle trapping, so free plasma concentration, not tissue, is the meaningful comparator; whether tafenoquine followed the same pattern required direct mechanistic testing. 52 A virtual in vitro distribution (VIVD) model was applied for the mechanistic prediction of intracellular concentrations of tafenoquine. By accounting for lipid and protein binding in the culture medium, binding to cell culture plastic, air‐partitioning, and lipid binding in the cell, the VIVD model can predict chemical concentrations (free and total) in medium and cells. 53 After confirming that the unbound intracellular concentration closely matched unbound nominal‐media concentration at a ratio of 1.18, it was confirmed that, after correction for binding, the in vitro EC50 could validly be compared against PBPK‐predicted lung concentrations. The scientific rationale facilitated granting of a “clearance to proceed” letter from the FDA for this study. 50

This case underscores that binding corrections are essential, not optional, for lipophilic, protein‐bound compounds nominal in vitro concentrations can misrepresent actual cellular exposure by an order of magnitude or more. Here, resolving a specific question, that is, whether tissue concentration was mechanistically comparable to in vitro potency, proved more consequential than any general supportive modeling could have been.

Summary

Across all three case studies, the in vitro data became regulatory‐enabling once embedded in a mechanistic model that translated it into the specific question a regulator was asking. Whether the challenge was an inaccessible tissue, a translational gap between species, or a contested choice of comparator, PBPK modeling supplied the missing analytical bridge, and in each case, that was what ultimately helped to move a regulatory decision forward (Table 1 ).

QSP CASE STUDIES

QSP can be applied across all drug modalities, although the emphasis has been on complex biologicals for which there are no conventional PKPD approaches available. The potential impact of QSP in different stages of drug development and regulatory decision making (FIH dose selection to late‐stage trial design to inform drug label) is nicely illustrated by its application in the class of CD3 multi‐specific T‐cell engagers (TCEs) in oncology. Blinatumomab was the first FDA‐approved CD3 TCE targeting CD19 on B cells for the treatment of relapsed/refractory leukemia, followed by tebentafusp (gp100xCD3), mosunetuzumab, epcoritamab, glofitamab (CD20xCD3), talquetamab (GPRC5DxCD3), and teclistamab and elranatamab (BCMAxCD3). More recently, trispecific CD3 TCEs have entered clinical development, such as ISB 2001 (BCMAxCD38xCD3). 54 One of the main challenges specific to TCEs is dose and dose‐regimen finding, due to complex PKPD relationships, narrow therapeutic index, and a high degree of between‐patient variability. 55 The 5 case studies described below illustrate how QSP can tackle this challenge arguably more effectively than more conventional MIDD approaches.

ISB 2001: QSP‐informed MABEL for FIH

ISB 2001 is a trispecific T‐cell engager targeting BCMA, CD3, and CD38, representing a novel and structurally more complex class of therapeutic than conventional bispecifics. The additional binding arm introduces further nonlinearity into the dose–response relationship, making empirical approaches to FIH dose selection particularly unreliable.

A QSP model was developed and used to support the IND application for ISB 2001, 54 , 55 , 56 focusing on the mechanistic pharmacology of trispecific complex (trimer) formation, incorporating the binding kinetics of all three targets, cell population dynamics for tumor cells and T cells, and downstream pharmacodynamic consequences including cytokine release and cytotoxic killing. Virtual patient simulations were used to characterize variability in predicted pharmacological response (trimers and tetramers cross‐linking T cells (CD4+ or CD8+) to tumor cells) across a relevant patient population, enabling the derivation of a starting dose of 5 μg/kg SC, substantially higher than the starting doses used in earlier bispecific programs that relied on RO‐ or PK‐based MABEL, alongside a predicted minimum efficacious dose of ~ 50 μg/kg. This compressed the anticipated therapeutic dose range to ~ 10‐fold, compared with the >1,000‐fold ranges seen historically with conventional MABEL directly using the potency read‐out from the most sensitive in vitro assay. 57 The first results in patients, recently reported in this journal 54 were in line with the QSP model predictions and therefore provided clinical validation of the approach.

The regulatory acceptance of this QSP‐informed IND represented a milestone for the field. It demonstrates that a mechanistic computational model, developed prior to any clinical data being available, can provide sufficient scientific justification to satisfy regulatory requirements for FIH dose selection for a complex, first‐in‐class molecule. The ISB 2001 case study also illustrates the alignment between QSP‐based MABEL and the principles of FDA's Project Optimus, which calls for early optimization of dose and schedule rather than pursuit of the maximum tolerated dose. As a computational NAM, the QSP model replaced the need for additional animal toxicology studies to justify the starting dose, and its prospective predictions have been borne out in early clinical experience.

In June 2026, the FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research issued draft guidance formalizing the use of QSP to anchor the MABEL for FIH dose selection. 11 The draft guidance is an example of how innovation in drug development can be consolidated into a regulatory, risk‐based framework and situates QSP squarely within the agency's MIDD agenda. The guidance responds to the well‐recognized inadequacy of allometric scaling in modern drug development (in particular in relation to novel modalities and innovative mechanisms of action) and it advances QSP as the preferred method to integrate in vitro human cell pharmacology, nonclinical, and in silico data into a mechanistic prediction of the dose just at the onset of biological activity. It is the first example of a draft guideline directly derived from ICH M15, 10 with the framework of context of use and model risk at its core, built on foundations of expected rigor of model construction, parameterization, verification, calibration, validation, and documentation. The draft guideline marks a transition of QSP‐based MABEL determination from an ad hoc, sponsor‐specific practice to a codified regulatory expectation. In summary, this case study shows how QSP can be used as an in silico NAM to guide efficient translation of a complex biotherapeutic into the clinic, reducing the number of animal and patient studies.

Mosunetuzumab: QSP‐informed step‐up dosing

The phase I program of the CD20xCD3 bispecific TCE, mosunetuzumab, provided one of the earliest case studies illustrating the impact of QSP on decision making in early clinical development. Hosseini et al. 58 constructed a mechanistic model of the immune and antitumor response, capturing B‐ and T‐lymphocyte kinetics across blood, lymphoid tissue, and tumor, using mosunetuzumab preclinical in vitro and in vivo data alongside clinical observations from blinatumomab. The model predicted that step‐fractionated dosing would suppress the systemic T‐cell activation and interleukin‐6 surge underlying cytokine release syndrome (CRS) without impacting efficacy. The authors concluded that “This result provided a strong rationale and quantitative guidance for the dosing schedule in the phase I clinical trial of mosunetuzumab (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT02500407). Emerging results from the trial appear to confirm the predictions and have enabled higher dosing with reduced safety events.” Ultimately, the QSP model provided the basis for the step‐up dosing in the Boxed Warning of the mosunetuzumab (LUNSUMIO) label (“Cytokine release syndrome (CRS), including serious or life‐threatening reactions, can occur in patients receiving LUNSUMIO. Initiate treatment with the LUNSUMIO step‐up dosing schedule to reduce the risk of CRS.”) and the recommended step‐up dosing schedule. 59 In summary, QSP supplied the quantitative rationale for the phase 1 schedule and ultimately underpinned the labeled dosing regimen.

Epcoritamab: QSP‐informed recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D)

Epcoritamab is a CD3xCD20 bispecific antibody for B‐cell lymphomas. The dose escalation study in early clinical development met its primary endpoint. However, the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was not reached and traditional PKPD modeling approaches were deemed to be inadequate to determine the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) because they do not take into account the unique pharmacological mechanism of action of TCEs (trimer formation) that can result in bell‐shaped dose–response curves. Therefore, a mechanistic model incorporating preclinical in vitro and in vivo, clinical, and patient biomarker data was developed to determine RP2D that would achieve optimal trimer formation and clinically relevant response rates while minimizing the risk of CRS events. 60 In summary, this example demonstrates how QSP can complement empirical PKPD modeling to select a recommended phase 2 dose.

Elranatamab and Teclistamab: QSP‐informed change in dose regimen and label

Although to the best of our knowledge, mechanistic modeling was not used to inform the FIH MABEL dose selection of the BCMAxCD3 TCEs, elranatamab, and teclistamab, recent publications exemplify how QSP helped to justify reduced dosing frequency for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) and impacted regulatory decisions and drug labeling.

Guo et al. 61 integrated population–PK, exposure–response, and a multiscale QSP model of teclistamab's mechanism of action with MajesTEC‐1 data to interrogate the switch from 1.5 mg/kg weekly (QW) to every‐other‐week (Q2W) dosing in responders. Although the estimated serum trough concentration fell after switching, it remained above the EC90, and (critically) the QSP model predicted comparable trimer formation, tumor volume reduction, and duration of response with Q2W vs. continued QW. The model further estimated that 3 mg/kg Q4W would match 1.5 mg/kg Q2W. The authors concluded that “Our findings support the label‐approved switch to teclistamab 1.5 mg/kg Q2W in patients who have maintained ≥ CR for ≥ 6 months, without diminishing the clinical benefits achieved with QW dosing”.

Similarly, Poels et al. 62 built a QSP model of elranatamab capturing trimer dynamics, disease biomarkers (M protein, FLC), and the soluble BCMA “drug‐sink” effect, calibrated to MagnetisMM‐1/−3 via virtual populations (VPops) stratified by baseline sBCMA. Simulations supported 76 mg QW as the lowest dose giving broad efficacy coverage—including in high‐sBCMA patients—thereby reinforcing the approved dose, while a left‐shift in the dose–response curve among early responders supported de‐escalating frequency from weekly to Q2W and then monthly without loss of efficacy. In fact, the authors further concluded that persistent responders would benefit from gradual reduction of the dosing frequency from QW to Q2W because on achieving biochemical response, the levels of free BCMA and CD3 receptors decrease, requiring less drug in the system to form trimers. If a reduction in the dosing frequency is not applied, elranatamab will saturate the free receptors, creating an excess of dimers in the system and inhibiting the formation of functional trimers that trigger tumor death. With the lower trimer formation, the risk of the tumor escaping therapy increases, resulting in a higher probability of disease progression. 62 In summary, these case studies illustrate how QSP can be used in the post‐approval setting, providing the mechanistic justification for reductions in dosing frequency.

Summary

Across all five case studies, the common thread is that the pharmacology of T‐cell engagers is not adequately captured by exposure alone. In each case, QSP supplied the mechanistic link between drug concentration, target engagement, and the biological outcome on which the decision rested—prospectively for ISB 2001, mosunetuzumab, and epcoritamab, retrospectively for teclistamab and elranatamab. Taken together, these examples span the full breadth of drug development from FIH dose selection to dosing recommendations in labels and demonstrate that a QSP model can carry regulatory weight at every stage (Table 2 ).

ESTABLISHING MODEL CREDIBILITY: UNCERTAINTY, EVALUATION, AND MODEL RISK

Although the case studies described the development and regulatory outcome of the mechanistic models, it is equally important to consider how confidence in those predictions was established. ICH M15 provides the framework to address this, using six elements: the question of interest, the context of use, model influence, the consequence of a wrong decision, model risk, and model impact. 10 Model influence is the weight given to model outcomes relative to other sources of evidence contributing to the same question; the consequence of a wrong decision reflects the severity and likelihood of the resulting harm; and model risk combines the two, setting the standard of evaluation expected rather than applying a fixed bar to all models. It should be noted that influence is rated per question of interest rather than per model. For example, in the epcoritamab case, empirical PKPD retained its role in characterizing exposure while the mechanistic model carried the choice of RP2D. Where influence and consequence diverge, the rating is driven by the higher of the two. For the teclistamab and elranatamab analyses, risk remained high despite only medium influence, because although mature trial data reduced the model's relative weight, the consequence of a wrong dosing recommendation in the label did not diminish. All the case studies discussed here predate its adoption, so the assessments that follow are illustrative applications of the framework rather than ratings agreed with regulatory authorities.

A major source of uncertainty for computational NAMs is that arising from the experimental data used to parameterize the models. Whatever the experimental system, biological variability between donors, differences between platforms, assay reproducibility, and the scaling assumptions required to relate an in vitro measurement to whole‐organ function, all propagate through the model into the prediction. As noted previously, this is most acute for complex experimental NAMs: the scaling functions required to bridge OoC systems to human organ physiology remain compound‐class and platform‐dependent, whereas the corresponding relationships for routine in vitro ADME assays are better characterized. Eliminating this uncertainty entirely is not practical. What computational NAMs allow is identification of the components that most affect exposure or response, through sensitivity analysis directed particularly at parameters associated with substantial uncertainty. The diclofenac case illustrates this, with sensitivity analysis determining which in vitro permeation measurements were worth performing. Where a parameter remains poorly characterized, its plausible range can be propagated through the model so that the prediction is expressed with explicit bounds: if the decision is unchanged across that range the uncertainty is not decision‐relevant, and if it is not, the analysis has identified where further experimental work is needed. The moxidectin case is a worked example: faced with uncertainty over the relative contributions of CYP3A4 and biliary clearance in neonates, a conservative slow‐ontogeny assumption was applied, and the resulting exposure margin, ~ 65‐fold below the rat NOAEL‐equivalent concentration on Day 1, was wide enough that the conclusion held irrespective of that uncertainty.

Mechanistic models carry their own credibility challenges. Structural uncertainty, the possibility that an alternative model structure would fit the same data, can rarely be resolved from the data alone, and plausible alternatives often need to be tested. Parameter identifiability is a persistent problem in PBPK and QSP approaches; some parameters need to be fixed from independent sources and the consequences of doing so examined. Virtual populations require qualification in their own right: the elranatamab analysis stratified virtual patients by baseline soluble BCMA precisely because that variable governs the drug‐sink effect that determines the dose–response relationship, and the ISB 2001 model used virtual patient simulations to characterize variability in predicted pharmacological response across the target population before the starting dose was derived. Extrapolation beyond the calibration range is the most consequential of these since it is where models are most useful and least verifiable, as exemplified by the teclistamab prediction that 3 mg/kg Q4W would be equivalent to 1.5 mg/kg Q2W. The tafenoquine case is a reminder that an assumption about which concentration constituted the valid in vivo comparator can be assessed using mechanistic justification not additional data.

According to the ICH M15 guidance, evaluation of mechanistic models should be proportionate to model risk. Evaluation comprises verification of the code or platform itself, together with validation and applicability assessment of the model. External validation with independent data is encouraged and, depending on the context of use and model risk, may be essential. 10 Several of the case studies were externally validated in this way: the ISB 2001 predictions were subsequently borne out in patients, the mosunetuzumab schedule predictions were confirmed in the phase 1 trial, and the teclistamab conclusions were corroborated by independent population–PK and exposure–response analyses. The diclofenac case illustrates applicability assessment at two levels, with model performance evaluated both for diclofenac against plasma and skin tissue data and for the underlying platform across compounds spanning a wide physicochemical range. Platform‐level qualification offers a complementary route: in 2025, the CHMP adopted a qualification opinion for the Simcyp Simulator, the first PBPK platform to be qualified by the EMA, covering the prediction of CYP‐mediated DDIs within a defined context of use. 63 The qualification rested on an explicit characterization of predictive performance across a large validation dataset, quantifying bias and imprecision, allowing sponsors to rely on the platform within that context of use without re‐establishing its credibility case by case.

Taken together, these activities are what distinguish a prediction from evidence. It is the transparent characterization of uncertainty, the rigor of evaluation proportionate to risk, and the explicit statement of context of use that allow a mechanistic model to function as a decision‐support framework rather than simply a predictive tool.

EMERGING APPLICATIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

In the above case studies, the computational NAMs were mainly informed by simpler experimental NAMs. Despite their obvious mechanistic advantages, industrial adoption of OoC platforms has remained low. The International Consortium for Innovation and Quality (IQ) founded a group of thought leaders and end users of microphysiological systems (MPS) to drive the area forward including engagement with regulators to qualify translationally relevant MPS models. 64 The IQ‐MPS survey data found that most companies use these platforms for internal decision making rather than for regulatory submissions, probably reflecting both a cautious industry stance and the absence of standardized regulatory guidelines for MPS qualification. This gap is consistent with the risk‐based logic later formalized in ICH M15: without an agreed framework for the validation rigor required at different levels of model influence, sponsors have defaulted to using the OoC data for low‐consequence, internal purposes only, rather than as submission‐ready evidence. Having said that, with the increasing number of applications and publications on case studies, regulatory confidence will build and submissions involving these more complex systems will increase.

Other converging trends are likely to reshape the practical application of PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs over the coming years, extending their role well beyond the case studies discussed above. 65

Artificial intelligence and machine learning integration

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are increasingly being embedded within mechanistic PBPK and QSP frameworks. Rather than replacing the physiological structure that gives these models their interpretability and regulatory acceptability, AI/ML methods are being applied to specific bottlenecks within the modeling workflow: automated parameter estimation from sparse or heterogeneous experimental datasets, generation of virtual patient populations that better capture the covariance structure of real‐world variability, and pattern recognition across large compound libraries to flag candidates likely to require nonstandard model structures. This hybrid approach is likely to prove more acceptable to regulators, since it preserves the physiological interpretability that has underpinned regulatory acceptance of PBPK and QSP models to date. The FDA's own recent regulatory activity reflects this direction: in June 2026, the agency accepted the first Letter of Intent for an in silico drug development tool into the ISTAND program, an artificial intelligence‐driven digital liver model designed to predict drug‐induced liver injury and reduce reliance on animal testing, 66 a clear signal that AI‐augmented mechanistic tools are now being evaluated as legitimate computational NAMs in their own right, rather than as exploratory adjuncts to conventional modeling.

Virtual twins and individualized prediction

A related and increasingly discussed application is the “virtual/digital twin” concept: a patient‐specific PBPK or QSP model, continuously updated with an individual's physiological, genetic, and real‐world monitoring data. The aim of these models is to predict an individual's drug exposure or response prospectively rather than relying solely on population‐level covariates. In principle, this extends the specific population PBPK models that are currently available for pregnant women, pediatric patients, or patients with organ impairment toward a single‐patient resolution, continuously refined as new data (wearable‐derived physiological signals, therapeutic drug monitoring results, genotype) become available. 67 , 68 In a recent review, 22 studies were identified as demonstrating the application of VT‐PBPK across a range of populations, disease states, and drug classes. 69 The clinical translation of this concept remains at an early stage relative to population‐level PBPK and QSP applications. 70 , 71 Significant questions remain regarding validation standards for individualized predictions, and the regulatory framework required for continuously updating these models using live data post‐approval. Nonetheless, the direction is consistent with the broader trajectory described throughout this review: mechanistic models moving from a population‐level, prespecified evidentiary tool toward a more dynamic, individualized decision‐support instrument. For example, Kaddi et al. 72 built a QSP model of Pompe disease linking lysosomal acid α‐glucosidase (GAA) deficiency to tissue glycogen and urine Hex4 across the late‐ and infantile‐onset (IOPD) spectrum. Calibrating residual enzyme activity to each trial patient's baseline biomarker, they generated a “digital twin” of every IOPD patient in the avalglucosidase alfa program. Because each twin could be simulated under any regimen, they ran in silico head‐to‐head and switch comparisons vs. standard‐of‐care alglucosidase alfa, which would have been impossible to do in an actual clinical trial given the very low patient number. Thus, the virtual trial supplemented sparse trial data, mechanistically attributed greater urine Hex4 reduction to enhanced glycogen clearance, supported the switch between standard‐of‐care and a novel treatment, and de‐risked efficacy interpretation for rare disease decision making.

The most recent development is to combine QSP approaches with real‐world data. For example, in this journal, Kostiou et al. 73 very recently presented the first multiple myeloma‐specific QSP model of CAR T‐cell therapy that was calibrated against published trial data and validated against real‐world patient data—a methodological step that distinguishes it from prior CAR T models. The mechanistic model couples normal and malignant plasma‐cell dynamics, CAR T expansion/contraction and killing, and clinically measurable serum biomarkers (M protein, serum‐free light chains), while explicitly representing the two leading MM CAR targets, BCMA and GPRC5D. This provides a new approach to capture and predict patient heterogeneity. Rather than fitting average behavior, the authors use a VPop inference workflow to generate large ensembles of virtual patients whose parameter combinations reproduce inter‐patient variability. The model was calibrated to public cellular‐kinetic and response data (ide‐cel, cilta‐cel, MCARH109) and then validated against 29 real‐world RRMM patients (14 cilta‐cel, 15 ide‐cel) treated at MSKCC, effectively matching each patient with its virtual twin, enabling in silico interrogation of why responses diverge. For example, high tumor burden, low antigen expression, and low CAR T killing rate were identified as main determinants of poor outcome, whereas (perhaps counterintuitively) low baseline levels of normal plasma‐cell fractions were linked to better treatment response. In addition, the QSP model was used to optimize trial design for potential CAR T combination therapy and predicted that sequential administration yields greater durability. Overall, this work offers an expandable, real‐world‐validated platform for patient stratification, rational multi‐antigen sequencing, and model‐informed CAR T trial design in myeloma.

A next step in NAM approaches would be to combine virtual twins with OoC technology to create “virtual triplets” to support individualized precision medicine and dosing (Figure 2 ). 67 A virtual triplet connects three elements into a single framework: the patient, an individual with unique biology; the virtual twin, comprising computational models such as PBPK and QSP; and the biological twin, an OoC derived from that patient's cells or engineered to replicate human physiology. Data from the OoC are used to parameterize the virtual twin, and the model refines the predicted dose and regimen for the patient, with each cycle narrowing the uncertainty in the prediction until an optimized strategy emerges.

The next horizon for NAMs: the combination of PBPK/QSP in silico models with innovative experimental technology gives rise to the virtual triplet. QSP, quantitative systems pharmacology; PBPK, physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling.

Extension to complex modalities

PBPK and QSP frameworks, historically developed and validated predominantly for small molecules and, more recently, for bispecific and trispecific biologics, are now being extended to cell and gene therapies, antibody–drug conjugates, and RNA‐based therapeutics, each of which introduces disposition and PD characteristics that existing model structures were not originally designed to capture—persistent cellular engraftment and proliferation, payload‐linker dissociation kinetics, or tissue‐specific nucleic acid delivery and durability. Compared with QSP, PBPK's expansion into these modalities is an active and emerging area rather than an established one. For ADCs, PBPK models are more established as they are based on prior model‐building experience with both therapeutic proteins and small molecules. In a recent CBER landscape analysis of 26 regulatory submissions involving PBPK modeling, gene and cell therapy products represented a comparatively small but growing category relative to established submission types such as therapeutic proteins. 74 Most of the models were used to justify drug dosing in development, guide dosing strategies for pediatrics, and for predicting DDIs. For QSP, adapting mechanistic modeling frameworks to these modalities will likely follow a similar pattern to that seen with T‐cell engagers in this review's case studies: an initial period in which conventional, empirically derived dosing approaches are applied by default, followed by increasing regulatory and scientific pressure to develop modality‐specific mechanistic structures once the limitations of empirical approaches become apparent in clinical practice. The teclistamab and elranatamab case studies show how QSP can be used as an informative prior to design more efficient clinical studies and also illustrate how QSP could become a pillar for the recently launched FDA “One Pivotal Trial” paradigm, 75 where convergence of an actual clinical trial with a virtual one would be part of the required “confirmatory evidence.”

Encouraging innovation in regulatory submissions

A further barrier to realizing the full potential of PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs is not scientific but cultural. In our experience, some sponsors remain risk‐averse to proposing mechanistic modeling in novel areas as primary evidence in regulatory submissions, often defaulting to more conventional approaches. This is not unreasonable: in the case of diclofenac and ISB 2001, the sponsors were willing to rely on PBPK or QSP models where a more conventional but more expensive path existed, and each carried genuine program risk if the regulatory argument had not been accepted. Encouraging greater innovation in this space requires deliberate action on both sides of the regulatory interface. The introduction of ICH M15 guidance is itself a step in the right direction. By linking the required standard of model validation to model influence and decision consequence, rather than applying a fixed bar to all computational models, it encourages exploratory modeling, which is where real innovation can emerge. It also gives sponsors, for the first time, a harmonized evidentiary framework across ICH regions, providing more confidence in the regulatory predictability needed to justify investment in novel model development. 76 This harmonization is already visible in practice: a recent HMA/EMA multi‐stakeholder workshop dedicated specifically to the reporting and qualification of mechanistic models covered the qualification of PBPK and QSP models through the EMA framework, evaluation of predictive performance and uncertainty quantification, and international regulatory guidance gaps, with participants recommending greater international convergence on mechanistic modeling standards. ICH M15 also provides the shared framework needed to characterize, quantify, and communicate uncertainty across all contexts, giving sponsors and regulators a common, risk‐tiered basis for judging when a given model is fit for its intended purpose.

Critically, the ICH M15 framework also encourages early engagement with sponsors to discuss the proposed context of use and model‐influence rating with regulators a priori, allowing expectations to be agreed before substantial resource is committed to a given modeling approach. This should encourage sponsors to engage regulators earlier and more directly with model‐based arguments as the primary proposed evidence, as exemplified by some of the case studies in this review. The programs that benefited most from PBPK and QSP were those in which the model was integral to the regulatory strategy from the outset. Cultivating this kind of innovation will depend as much on institutional confidence and regulatory precedent as on further scientific advancement of the models themselves.

CONCLUSION

The case studies presented herein demonstrate that mechanistic models can deliver regulatory‐grade evidence when they are parameterized with human‐relevant experimental data and directed at an explicitly stated regulatory question. This is the clearest argument for regarding PBPK and QSP as computational NAMs rather than merely supporting NAM‐derived data. Experimental NAMs generate human‐relevant biological information; computational NAMs convert that information into quantitative predictions of exposure, efficacy, and safety in the population of interest, and it is that conversion which makes the underlying experimental data actionable in a regulatory submission.

The regulatory infrastructure is now catching up with the science. The FDA draft guidance on QSP‐anchored MABEL determination establishes a mechanistic alternative to allometric scaling for exactly the modalities where scaling fails, while ICH M15 provides the globally harmonized, risk‐tiered basis on which the credibility of any model is to be judged. Together they mark the shift we have aimed to highlight throughout this review: mechanistic models are now positioned as quantitative decision‐support frameworks in their own right. These approaches can also generate evidence for populations in which direct clinical study is constrained or unethical, including pregnancy, pediatrics, organ impairment, and rare disease among them, offering a route not only to safer and more effective medicines but to more equitable ones.

The modeling science, the regulatory framework, and the clinical precedents have converged. The moment for computational NAMs is not approaching — it is here.
