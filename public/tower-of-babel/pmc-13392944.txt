Unraveling the Genetic Basis of Self-Harm Phenotypes: New Perspectives on Its Links to Depression and Chronic Pain
Authors: Weixin Liu, Yanyue Ye, Zhongting Huang, Jijun Zhu, Shenghan Wang, Pan Li, Haowen Zhuang, Shunjie Zhang, Fuqiang Cai, Zhijian Song, Yuxin Liu, Fei Ling, Hui Chen, Sihong Li, Xianliang Chen, Huajia Tang, Junfang Chen, Jiansong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-harm is a complex behavior with substantial mental health implications across the lifespan, including among middle-aged and older adults, an often underrecognized population in suicide prevention efforts. While chronic pain and depression are known correlates of self-harm, the underlying genetic and mechanistic relationships among these conditions remain poorly understood.MethodsIn this study, we focused on self-harm as the primary outcome and investigated whether depression genetically mediates the link between chronic pain and self-harm risk. Using a large-scale dataset from the UK Biobank (>500,000 individuals), we applied an integrative analytic framework combining polygenic risk scoring, genetic correlation, causal inference, and genomic structural equation modeling (SEM).ResultsBoth depression and chronic pain were significantly associated with self-harm phenotypes in epidemiological analyses, and these associations remained robust after accounting for psychiatric comorbidities, including anxiety, as well as analgesic and psychotropic medication use. Genetic analyses further supported depression as a key mediator linking chronic pain to self-harm phenotypes, with evidence from genetic correlation, Mendelian randomization, and genomic SEM analyses. Genomic SEM identified candidate genes and enriched pathways involved in synaptic signaling and neurodevelopment, pointing to a shared biological basis.ConclusionsThese findings suggest a new depression-mediated genetic model of self-harm, emphasizing the importance of integrated psychiatric and somatic screening to inform early intervention strategies in aging populations.Keywords: Chronic pain, Depression, Genetics, GenomicSEM, Mediation, Self-harm

Self-harm remains a major public health concern, particularly among middle-aged and older adults, a population underrepresented in psychiatric research (1,2). Chronic pain, a prevalent condition in this population, has emerged as a key psychosomatic factor linked to increased risk for both depression and self-harm (3,4). Although the clinical comorbidity among chronic pain, depression, and self-harm is well documented (5), the shared genetic architecture and potential causal pathways remain poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear whether chronic pain exerts a direct genetic influence on self-harm or whether this association is mediated by depression. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for elucidating transdiagnostic mechanisms and guiding precision intervention for individuals with complex mental-physical comorbidities.

Recent advances in psychiatric genetics have revealed that chronic pain, major depressive disorder (MDD), and self-injurious behaviors are each influenced by genetic factors to varying degrees (6, 7, 8). Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) have been widely used to quantify individual-level genetic liability for depression (9) and chronic pain (10) and, more recently, for suicidal ideation and self-harm (9, 10, 11). These approaches have uncovered overlapping genetic architectures, suggesting shared biological vulnerabilities across conditions once considered distinct (12). Furthermore, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) analyses have demonstrated moderate-to-high genetic correlations among these traits (13). Complementing these findings, bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have provided evidence for causal influences in both directions between chronic pain and depression (14) and from depression to suicidal behavior (15). However, most of these studies have relied on pairwise comparisons, leaving the interdependencies among all 3 traits insufficiently characterized. In particular, whether depression serves as a mediating mechanism linking chronic pain to self-harm has not yet been tested using genetically informed multivariate approaches (12,16).

To address these gaps, we aimed to investigate the genetically mediated relationship between chronic pain and self-harm phenotypes, focusing on the role of depression as a potential mediator. We first sought to characterize the phenotypic relationships among chronic pain, depression, and self-harm and to assess whether these associations remained robust after accounting for common psychiatric comorbidities, including anxiety, as well as analgesic and psychotropic medication use. Then, we used a comprehensive genomic framework integrating PRSs, LDSC, MR, genomic structural equation modeling (SEM), and multivariate genome-wide association studies (GWASs). This multimethod approach enables quantification of both direct and mediated genetic effects and facilitates the identification of shared loci and enriched biological pathways across chronic pain, depression, and self-harm. Notably, genomic SEM allows for latent modeling of shared psychiatric dimensions, while multivariate GWAS improves power to detect pleiotropic signals. Our study contributes to a more integrated understanding of somatic-psychiatric interactions. Our findings may inform biologically grounded models of self-harm risk in later life and guide the development of precision medicine strategies for individuals with elevated polygenic liability.

Methods and Materials

Sample

This study used data from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large-scale population-based cohort of >500,000 individuals ages 40 to 70 years at recruitment (17). The UKB provides extensive phenotypic and genotypic data, including questionnaire-based assessments, physical health measures, and genome-wide genotyping (18,19). All participants gave informed consent, and the study was approved by the National Health Service National Research Ethics Service.

Primary Phenotypes

The primary phenotypes used in the study are self-harm phenotypes (lifetime self-harm ideation and self-harm), depression, and chronic pain.

Self-Harm Ideation and Behavior

Lifetime self-harm ideation and self-harm were assessed using 2 pairs of UKB questionnaire items administered at different time points (ideation: Data-Field 20485/29109; self-harm: Data-Field 20480/29111). Responses of “Prefer not to answer” were recoded as missing values for both questionnaire pairs, while “No,” “Yes, once,” and “Yes, more than once” were coded as 0, 1, and 2, respectively. Participants with a total score range from 1 to 4 were defined as the case group for the corresponding phenotype, while those with a score of 0 were defined as the control group.

Depression

Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the 9-item depression module of the PHQ, in the UKB (20). Participants with PHQ-9 scores ≥5 were classified as having depressive symptoms, while those scoring 0 to 4 were considered nondepressed, consistent with standard clinical thresholds (21).

Chronic Pain

Chronic pain was measured using Data-Field 120019, which assesses pain or discomfort lasting for more than 3 months. Participants who answered “yes” to this question were defined as having chronic pain, reflecting widely accepted definitions in epidemiological studies.

Statistical Analysis

Associations between self-harm phenotypes (ideation and behavior) and exposures (depression, chronic pain) were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for a comprehensive set of covariates (22). Detailed definitions and data sources for all covariates are provided in Table S1. Additive interaction analysis between chronic pain and depression was conducted using Rothman’s approach via the epiR package (version 2.0.80), focusing specifically on quantifying the relative excess risk due to interaction as the principal metric for additive interaction effects. Multiplicative interaction effects were assessed by incorporating interaction terms into the multivariable logistic regression models. Stratified analyses were conducted by sex and age group (<60 vs. ≥60 years) to examine potential effect heterogeneity.

Exploratory analyses were performed to examine the temporal ordering of exposures (depression and chronic pain) and self-harm phenotypes. Sensitivity analyses included models with stepwise adjustment for covariate groups and bootstrap validation procedures. Detailed descriptions of these analyses are provided in Supplement, Section 1. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) with statistical significance defined as 2-tailed p values < .05.

Genotyping and Quality Control

Genome-wide genotyping in the UKB was performed on approximately 488,000 participants using 2 complementary arrays: the UK BiLEVE Axiom array and the UKB Axiom array. These platforms shared 95% probe content overlap and collectively identified approximately 800,000 genetic markers (19). Standard quality control procedures were applied using PLINK (version 2.0), excluding variants and individuals with missing rates >2%.

For the primary British ancestry cohort, participants were excluded based on 1) non-British ancestry (non-White British/Irish/White), 2) sex chromosome aneuploidy (heterozygosity >0.2), 3) discordance between genetic and reported sex, and 4) relatedness (kinship coefficient >0.0442). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium p values <1 × 10−10 or minor allele frequency <0.001 were removed (23).

Quality control and ancestry classification for non-British participants were performed using procedures consistent with those applied to the British White cohort. Detailed genotype filtering criteria and ancestry assignment procedures are provided in Supplement, Section 2.

Genome-Wide Association and Polygenic Risk Analysis

Genome-wide association analyses and the integration of external summary statistics were performed as described in Supplement, Section 3.

We implemented a 10-fold cross-validation framework (24). Depression and chronic pain GWASs were conducted within training subsets, and PRSs were computed in held-out validation sets. To assess robustness, training data were also split into 2 equally sized subsets using stratified sampling, and analyses were repeated. PRSs were calculated using PRS-CSx with default parameters (25). Logistic regression models were used to test the association between each PRS and self-harm phenotypes, while controlling for the covariates described (Table S1), as well as ancestry principal components. As PRSs for 7 traits were tested for association with 2 self-harm phenotypes, a total of 14 hypothesis tests were conducted. Multiple testing was controlled using Bonferroni correction. Accordingly, a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < 3.57 × 10−3 (.05/14) was applied. To assess independent and joint effects of PRSs, both single- and multiple-PRS logistic regression models were fitted. In addition, we leveraged the multiancestry genotype data from the UKB to examine the associations between disease-related genetic risks and self-harm phenotypes across different ancestral groups.

Genetic Correlation Analysis via LDSC

For the computation of LDSC, we utilized LDSC software (version 1.0.1) (26), with LD scores computed using PLINK (version 1.9). Specifically, the --l2 and --ld-wind-snps 500 flags were applied to compute LD scores based on a window of approximately 500,000 SNPs. For the summary statistics related to psychiatric disorders and chronic pain, the analyses followed the recommended procedures by using the --w-ld-chr and --ref-ld-chr flags, with identical LD scores applied across the analyses. The default European reference LD scores from the 1000 Genomes Project were used. Genetic correlation coefficients (rgs) were estimated for each phenotype pair, with p values adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction.

Bidirectional MR Analysis

To further investigate the causal relationships between self-harm phenotypes, depression, and chronic pain, we performed a 2-sample bidirectional MR analysis using 6 different methods, including the generalized summary-data-based MR (GSMR) method implemented in the gsmr2 package (version 1.1.1) (27) and 5 other approaches implemented in the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8), namely inverse variance weighted (IVW) (28), MR-Egger regression (29), weighted median (30), simple mode, and weighted mode estimates (31). Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test (29). Instrumental variables were selected as SNPs associated with a p value < 1 × 10−4 and LD r2 < 0.1 within a 1000-kb window. Causal effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs), with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple testing in both forward and reverse MR analyses.

Genomic SEM

To investigate the role of chronic pain in the genetic association between self-harm phenotypes and depression, we conducted an analysis using the GenomicSEM package (version 0.0.5) (32). This approach allows for the synthesis of genetic data to help us understand how genes may affect disease risk or other complex phenotypes through mediating pathways, such as environmental factors or phenotypic changes. In this study, we used 3 commonly used fit indices in SEM to assess model fit: χ2 tests, comparative fit index (CFI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The model factors, denoted as Ps, were measured using 2 observed variables: self-harm ideation and self-harm. The analysis was aimed at testing whether chronic pain directly influences the latent variable (independent of depression) or whether it exerts an indirect effect on latent self-harm through depression.

Multivariate GWAS and Gene Annotation

To examine how chronic pain genetically influences self-harm phenotypes through depression, we extended the genomic SEM model to incorporate individual SNP effects. Approximately 500,000 SNPs were sequentially modeled to estimate their genetic contributions to chronic pain and their mediated effects on self-harm via depression. For the multivariate GWAS results, we performed clumping with the following parameters: SNP p value < 1 × 10−3, 500-kb window size, and LD r2 < 0.1. The resulting loci were annotated using the dbSNP annotation file to assign them to the corresponding genes. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) categories, including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). Enrichment significance was assessed using false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p values, with significantly enriched pathways provided in the Supplement (Table S9). Given the clear synaptic signals, we further examined the fine synaptic ontology defined by the SynGO alliance (33) and found consistent enrichment in postsynaptic components and processes (Figure S5).

Results

Epidemiological Association Study for Self-Harm Phenotypes

We first assessed the associations of depression and chronic pain phenotypes with self-harm ideation (34,160 cases vs. 180,856 controls) and self-harm behavior (11,147 cases vs. 204,027 controls) (Figure S1 and Table S1) in the UKB using multivariate regression analysis, adjusting for potential confounding factors. After adjusting for relevant covariates, depression was significantly associated with increased odds of self-harm ideation (OR [95% CI] = 2.71 [2.61 to 2.81], p < 1.00 × 10−300) and self-harm behavior (OR [95% CI] = 2.31 [2.18 to 2.45], p = 1.73 × 10−177). Chronic pain was also significantly associated with both self-harm ideation (OR [95% CI] = 1.27 [1.22 to 1.31], p = 6.61 × 10−40) and self-harm behavior (OR [95% CI] = 1.26 [1.19 to 1.34], p = 4.13 × 10−15). These associations were consistent across age- and sex-stratified analyses (Table 1). Interaction analyses revealed a scale-dependent pattern. For self-harm ideation, there was no evidence of additive interaction, whereas a significant interaction was observed on the multiplicative scale. In contrast, for self-harm behavior, a significant additive interaction was detected, while no multiplicative interaction was observed. Evidence of heterogeneity in interaction estimates was observed across strata, although the overall pattern remained consistent (Table 1).

In exploratory analyses accounting for temporal ordering, the association with depression remained robust, whereas the effect of chronic pain was attenuated. Sensitivity analyses using stepwise covariate adjustment and bootstrap validation indicated that the association of chronic pain with self-harm outcomes was of modest magnitude but remained stable (Tables S3–S5).

Associations of PRSs With Self-Harm Phenotypes

To assess the genetic contributions of psychiatric and pain-related traits to self-harm, we computed PRSs for depression, chronic pain, and other psychiatric disorders (Table S6). After adjusting for covariates, the PRS for depression derived from PRS-CSx explained 3.6% of the variance (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.036) (Table S7). All PRSs except for anxiety showed significant positive associations with both self-harm ideation and self-harm behaviors in single-PRS logistic regression models (Figure 1), after applying a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of p < 3.57 × 10−3 (.05/14). Notably, the PRS for depression was strongly associated with self-harm ideation (OR [95% CI] = 1.86 [1.62 to 2.13], p = 4.80 × 10−19) and self-harm behavior (OR [95% CI] = 1.76 [1.41 to 2.2], p = 5.04 × 10−7), with the PRS for MDD showing the strongest effect (self-harm ideation: OR [95% CI] = 4.28 [3.68 to 4.99], p = 1.19 × 10−77; self-harm behavior: OR [95% CI] = 5.49 [4.28 to 7.04], p = 6.50 × 10−41). The PRS for chronic pain was also significantly associated with self-harm ideation (OR [95% CI] = 1.51 [1.32 to 1.73], p = 3.31 × 10−9) and self-harm behavior (OR [95% CI] = 1.47 [1.17 to 1.83], p = 7.51 × 10−4), reinforcing the shared genetic liability among these phenotypes. In the joint model including psychiatric disorder PRSs, the association of the chronic pain PRS with self-harm phenotypes became nonsignificant. This suggests that chronic pain shares substantial genetic liability with psychiatric disorders, and its genetic influence on self-harm may be partially mediated through psychiatric risk (Figure S2 and Table S7). In the independent validation set, both the direction and statistical significance of the associations were consistent with those observed in the cross-validation, demonstrating the robustness and stability of the results (Figure S3 and Table S7). In addition, we performed multiple-PRS regression analyses across other ancestry groups. However, in this analysis, only the PRS for MDD showed a significant positive association in the non-British White population (Figure S4 and Table S7).

Genetic Correlation Between Self-Harm and Psychiatric or Pain-Related Traits

Given that most discovery GWASs were conducted in European ancestry (EUR) populations, all subsequent analyses and interpretations were based on EUR samples. To validate the findings from the PRS analysis, we conducted LDSC to estimate genetic correlation between self-harm phenotypes and various psychiatric disorders (including depression), as well as chronic pain. All GWAS summary statistics used for these analyses were derived from EUR populations. Notably, the GWAS summary data for chronic pain were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (EBI Accession No. GCST90309352) rather than generated within the current study. After adjusting p values for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction, we observed a strong and significant genetic correlation between self-harm phenotypes and depression (self-harm ideation: rg [95% CI] = 0.64 [0.51 to 0.78], p = 2.06 × 10−19; self-harm behavior: rg [95% CI] = 0.49 [0.30 to 0.67], p = 1.95 × 10−6) (Figure 2), with an exceptionally high genetic correlation between self-harm phenotypes and MDD (self-harm ideation: rg [95% CI] = 0.67 [0.60 to 0.74], p = 1.42 × 10−74; self-harm behavior: rg [95% CI] = 0.75 [0.61 to 0.89], p = 1.08 × 10−23). In contrast, the genetic correlation between self-harm phenotypes and chronic pain were relatively weak and either marginally significant or nonsignificant (self-harm ideation: rg [95% CI] = 0.14 [0.05 to 0.22], p = .02; self-harm behavior: rg [95% CI] = 0.13 [0.01 to 0.26], p = .47). Other psychiatric traits also showed different but generally weaker genetic correlations with self-harm phenotypes.

Causal Links Between Self-Harm, Depression, and Pain

To further delineate the causal architecture linking self-harm phenotypes with depression and chronic pain, we performed bidirectional 2-sample MR analyses across all pairwise combinations of these traits. Six MR methods were applied, with the primary methods being GSMR and IVW, while other methods were used as supplementary analyses (Table S8). Bonferroni-adjusted analyses were applied to account for multiple comparisons in bidirectional MR analyses. We found robust and consistent significant positive effects (self-harm ideation: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.23 [1.17 to 1.30], p = 2.61 × 10−12; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.25 [1.18 to 1.33], p = 4.51 × 10−12; self-harm behavior: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.26 [1.16 to 1.38], p = 1.14 × 10−5; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.29 [1.16 to 1.42], p = 3.52 × 10−5) (Figure 3) and negative effects (self-harm ideation: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.34 [1.28 to 1.40], p = 2.64 × 10−34; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.29 [1.17 to 1.42], p = 1.62 × 10−5; self-harm behavior: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.15 [1.11 to 1.19], p = 1.17 × 10−11; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.19 [1.08 to 1.32], p = 5.00 × 10−2) between depression and self-harm phenotypes, indicating a complex genetic correlation between depression and self-harm. No significant positive causal relationship was observed between chronic pain and self-harm phenotypes (self-harm ideation: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.13 [1.04 to 1.23], p = .20; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.16 [1.05 to 1.27], p = .14; self-harm behavior: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.14 [1.00 to 1.30], p > .99; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.00 [0.99 to 1.01], p > .99), although the results for reverse causality were inconsistent across the 2 methods, leaving the exact reverse causal effect of chronic pain on self-harm inconclusive (self-harm ideation: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.03 [1.01 to 1.04], p = 7.39 × 10−3; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.04 [1.01 to 1.08], p > .99; self-harm behavior: GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.00 [0.99 to 1.01], p > .99; IVW OR [95% CI] = 0.99 [0.95 to 1.02], p > .99). Furthermore, we confirmed a significant positive causal pathway from chronic pain to depression (GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.27 [1.16 to 1.39], p = 6.19 × 10−6; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.34 [1.21 to 1.47], p = 5.57 × 10−7); however, the reverse direction again showed conflicting results between methods (GSMR OR [95% CI] = 1.04 [1.03 to 1.06], p = 1.00 × 10−4; IVW OR [95% CI] = 1.05 [0.97 to 1.12], p > .99), precluding definitive causal inference.

Identifying Depression-Mediated Pathway With Genomic SEM

Building on the observed genetic associations, where self-harm phenotypes exhibit stronger correlations with depression than with chronic pain, we hypothesized a potential mediating pathway through which chronic pain influences self-harm indirectly via depression. To evaluate this, we used genomic SEM, modeling self-harm ideation and self-harm behavior as a latent factor (Ps) representing a unified self-harm construct. The model demonstrated excellent fit to the data (χ21 = 0.045, p = .83, CFI = 1.00, and SRMR = 2.94 × 10−3) (Figure 4). Consistent with our MR findings, no significant direct path was detected between chronic pain and Ps (standardized loading [95% CI] = −0.13 [−0.35 to 0.09], p = .25). However, chronic pain exhibited a significant effect on depression (standardized loading [95% CI] = 0.40 [0.27 to 0.53], p = 5.86 × 10−10), which in turn significantly predicted Ps (standardized loading [95% CI] = 0.82 [0.34 to 1.30], p = 7.45 × 10−4). These results support a depression-mediated indirect pathway linking chronic pain to self-harm risk.

Pathway Enrichment Highlights Synaptic Mechanisms

To further explore the mechanisms through which chronic pain, mediated by depression, influences self-harm phenotypes, we incorporated SNP effects into the genomic SEM and conducted a multivariate GWAS. This analysis identified 595 independent genes (Table S9), which were subsequently subjected to GO enrichment. A total of 78 pathways across BP, MF, and CC categories met the FDR threshold of q < .05. These pathways were predominantly involved in nervous system development and function, including the positive regulation of cell projection organization, regulation of neuron projection development, and axon genesis. Given the prominent neurodevelopmental signals, we further interrogated synaptic-specific annotations using SynGO and observed consistent enrichment in postsynaptic components and synaptic processes (Figure S5), reinforcing a synaptic basis underlying the observed genetic effects.

Discussion

In this study, we delineated a genetically mediated pathway linking chronic pain, depression, and self-harm in middle-aged and older adults. Notably, our findings indicate that the genetic influence of chronic pain on self-harm is predominantly indirect and is mediated through depression. This distinction offers critical insights for improving clinical risk stratification and informing more effective prevention strategies.

Consistent with previous studies, our findings demonstrate a significant association between self-harm phenotypes and both chronic pain and depressive symptoms (5). Although the effect size for chronic pain was modest, its high prevalence in middle-aged and older populations suggests that it may still have substantial public health relevance. Importantly, interaction analyses revealed a scale-dependent pattern, with evidence of interaction varying across additive and multiplicative scales depending on the outcome. Notably, in sex-stratified analyses, additive interactions were observed in both males and females, with a tendency toward stronger effects among males. This suggests that the combined impact of chronic pain and depressive symptoms on self-harm risk may be amplified at the population level across both sexes, with potential sex-specific differences in magnitude, which has not been reported previously. In contrast, multiplicative interactions were not consistently observed across strata. These epidemiological findings should be interpreted as reflecting population-level associations rather than strict temporal or causal relationships.

Our findings are consistent with accumulating evidence indicating that the genetic liability for depression and chronic pain may contribute to the risk of self-harm (9,10). In addition, our findings are consistent with previous research indicating genetic correlations between chronic pain and depression. Previous studies have reported shared genetic architecture and causal links between depression and multiple pain phenotypes (34,35). Our study extends these insights by demonstrating that the genetic association between chronic pain and self-harm phenotypes is relatively weak and by elucidating the mediating role of depression in the relationship between chronic pain and self-harm, a connection that has been explored less in the literature.

Based on these observations, we applied genomic SEM to construct a genetically informed mediation framework linking chronic pain, depression, and self-harm phenotypes. Our findings provided empirical support for this model, offering a refined understanding of the potential causal architecture underlying these commonly comorbid conditions. Importantly, multivariate GWAS identified a set of candidate genes implicated in this mediating pathway. Subsequent enrichment analyses highlighted significant overrepresentation of biological processes related to the postsynaptic membrane, a finding that was independently validated using SynGO-based functional annotation. The observed mediation effect suggests that chronic pain may induce neurobiological changes, contributing to depressive symptoms, which in turn increase vulnerability to self-harm behaviors. This is consistent with existing theories on the neurobiological underpinnings of depression and pain, where shared pathways may influence both conditions (36, 37, 38, 39). More directly, the multivariate GWAS analysis revealed enrichment in pathways related to cell adhesion and cadherin binding, suggesting a potential involvement of neuroinflammation in this mediating process (40,41). The identification of synaptic and neurodevelopmental pathways in our multivariate GWAS further supports the involvement of central nervous system processes in this mediating relationship (42, 43, 44).

Methodologically, our study leverages a comprehensive analytic framework that combines multiple genetic approaches to elucidate the architecture of complex traits. By incorporating PRSs, LDSC, bidirectional MR, genomic SEM, and multivariate GWAS, we strengthened the validity and reproducibility of our findings. The application of genomic SEM to formally test mediation effects within a genetic framework remains uncommon in psychiatric genetics and represents a methodological advancement. Furthermore, pathway-level annotation using the SynGO ontology adds biological precision, particularly in implicating synaptic mechanisms.

At the field level, our findings underscore the importance of considering the interconnectedness of physical and mental health conditions in understanding self-harm risk. Recognizing depression as a mediator between chronic pain and self-harm emphasizes the need for integrated treatment approaches that address both somatic and psychiatric symptoms, particularly in aging populations where these conditions are prevalent (45,46). For middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, routine screening for depression should be integrated into standard care protocols. Therefore, incorporating mental health assessment into pain management strategies may enhance clinical outcomes and support a more holistic approach to care in this vulnerable population. This integrative perspective may inform the development of more effective prevention and intervention strategies for self-harm.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analyses are based on data from individuals of EUR, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in diverse cohorts to enhance their applicability. Second, while our genetically informed methods strengthen causal inference, they cannot fully account for all potential confounders or establish definitive causality. Longitudinal studies and experimental designs are needed to further elucidate the causal pathways linking chronic pain, depression, and self-harm.

Conclusions

Our study provides compelling evidence for a genetically mediated pathway from chronic pain to self-harm through depression. These insights have significant implications for the prevention and treatment of self-harm, highlighting the need for comprehensive approaches that address both physical and mental health in middle-aged and older adults. Future research should continue to explore the complex interplay between somatic and psychiatric conditions to inform the development of integrated care models.
