Synchronous spectrofluorimetric determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in human plasma using hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin complexation for investigating their role in major depressive disorder
Authors: Adnan Alharbi, Maram H Abduljabbar, Reem M Alnemari, Ahmed H Abdelazim, Atiah H Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is recently associated with immunological activation and deregulation of the kynurenine pathway, which redirects tryptophan metabolism away from serotonin production and towards neuroactive metabolites. Precise determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in biological samples is important to investigate their association with MDD pathophysiology. This study presents a highly sensitive spectrofluorimetric method for determining tryptophan and kynurenine in human plasma utilizing hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) inclusion complexation and synchronous fluorescence scanning. Furthermore, the work represents the first report describing the simultaneous determination of these analytes using HP-β-CD-assisted synchronous spectrofluorimetry in human plasma, offering a cost-effective alternative to conventional chromatographic techniques. HP-β-CD enhanced the fluorescence intensity of tryptophan and kynurenine and provided distinct emission peaks, minimizing spectral overlap. Synchronous fluorescence spectra were integrated by scanning both excitation and emission monochromators simultaneously at a constant wavelength offset (Δλ) of 50 nm and the fluorescence peak values were measured at 400 nm for selective tryptophan determination and 455 nm for selective kynurenine determination. The method exhibited linear relationship over a concentration range of 1–100 µM, with limits of detection (LOD) of 0.20 µM for tryptophan and 0.22 µM for kynurenine, and limits of quantification (LOQ) of 0.60 µM and 0.66 µM, respectively. Quantum yield evaluations, performed in triplicate (n = 3), confirmed significant fluorescence enhancement by HP-β-CD, increasing tryptophan quantum yield from 0.170 ± 0.006 to 0.520 ± 0.011 and kynurenine quantum yield from 0.090 ± 0.004 to 0.320 ± 0.009 (p < 0.001). The method demonstrated high accuracy, with mean recovery rates of 99.25% for tryptophan and 98.87% for kynurenine, and low RSD values across intra- and inter-day measurements. The method was applied to human plasma samples and the findings exhibited significantly decreased tryptophan levels in MDD patients (41.20 ± 3.22 µM) compared to healthy controls (62.50 ± 6.20 µM, p = 0.003), and elevated kynurenine levels (3.50 ± 1.20 µM vs. 1.20 ± 0.20 µM, p = 0.004).Keywords: Tryptophan, Kynurenine, Fluorescence spectroscopy, Major depressive disorder, BiomarkerSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biomarkers, Chemistry, Medical research, Neuroscience

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is increasingly recognized as an inflammatory disorder, with accumulating evidence linking immune activation to its pathogenesis1. One of the principal mechanisms connecting inflammation to neuropsychiatric dysfunction is the kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism, which regulates both neuroinflammatory responses and neurotransmitter homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, tryptophan serves as the precursor of serotonin, whereas inflammatory mediators, particularly interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), activate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), diverting tryptophan metabolism toward kynurenine formation and consequently reducing serotonin biosynthesis2. This metabolic shift has been implicated in the development and progression of depressive symptoms. Chemically, tryptophan is an aromatic amino acid possessing an indole chromophore that exhibits strong native fluorescence, whereas kynurenine, the primary product of the kynurenine pathway, displays considerably weaker fluorescence and partially overlapping emission spectra. These distinct photophysical properties make their simultaneous spectrofluorimetric determination particularly challenging, especially in complex biological matrices where endogenous constituents further compromise selectivity and sensitivity.

Following its formation, kynurenine readily crosses the blood-brain barrier and undergoes further metabolism through two competing pathways. Kynurenine monooxygenase (KMO) generates quinolinic acid, a neurotoxic N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor agonist associated with excitotoxicity and oxidative stress, whereas kynurenine aminotransferases (KATs) produce kynurenic acid, an endogenous NMDA receptor antagonist with neuroprotective properties3–5. Consequently, alterations in circulating tryptophan and kynurenine concentrations provide valuable biochemical indicators of kynurenine pathway dysregulation and have attracted considerable interest as potential biomarkers of MDD.

Peripheral immune activation further influences central nervous system function through increased circulating kynurenine, which is transported into the brain via large neutral amino acid transporter-1 (LAT1), thereby regulating downstream neuroactive metabolite production6,7. In addition, genetic and epigenetic alterations affecting enzymes of the kynurenine pathway, together with neuroimaging and postmortem studies, further support the involvement of this metabolic pathway in MDD pathophysiology8–10. Accordingly, reliable simultaneous determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in biological samples is essential for investigating alterations in the kynurenine pathway and their relationship to neuroinflammation and depressive disorders.

Several analytical techniques have been reported for the determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in biological matrices. HPLC coupled with UV or fluorescence detection remains one of the most frequently employed approaches because of its simplicity11–13, whereas electrochemical detection using multi-wall carbon nanotube-modified electrodes improves sensitivity but is susceptible to electrode fouling and matrix interference14. LC-MS/MS offers excellent sensitivity, specificity, and simultaneous quantification of multiple kynurenine pathway metabolites, although its widespread clinical application is restricted by expensive instrumentation and the need for highly trained personnel15–17.

Indeed, modern pharmaceutical analysis increasingly couples rigorous validation with environmental sustainability. For instance, stability-indicating RP-HPLC remains vital for unstable compounds like S-adenosylmethionine18. Concurrently, Quality by design optimization and eco-friendly UV spectrophotometry enable robust, simultaneous estimations of multi-drug formulations with demonstrating excellent ecological profiles19,20.

Beyond chromatographic techniques, synchronous spectrofluorimetry has emerged as a rapid, sensitive, and selective approach for the determination of analytes in complex biological matrices. By simultaneously scanning the excitation and emission monochromators at a constant wavelength interval, this technique enhances spectral resolution, minimizes background interference, and enables the analysis of biological samples with minimal sample preparation. Recent applications have further demonstrated its potential as an environmentally friendly and cost-effective alternative for bioanalytical determinations21,22.

Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) provides an attractive supramolecular strategy to overcome these limitations. Owing to its hydrophobic cavity and hydrophilic exterior, HP-β-CD forms stable inclusion complexes that restrict molecular motion, reduce solvent-induced quenching, and enhance fluorescence quantum yields23. Complementarily, synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy significantly improves spectral resolution by simultaneously scanning the excitation and emission monochromators at a constant wavelength interval, thereby minimizing spectral overlap and improving analytical selectivity24. The combination of HP-β-CD inclusion complexation with synchronous fluorescence therefore provides a derivatization-free, rapid, highly selective, and environmentally friendly analytical platform for the simultaneous determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in biological matrices.

The present study had two complementary objectives. The analytical objective was to develop, optimize, and validate a novel HP-β-CD-assisted synchronous spectrofluorimetric method for the simultaneous determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in human plasma according to ICH guidelines. The clinical objective was to apply the validated method to quantify plasma tryptophan and kynurenine concentrations in patients with MDD and healthy controls, thereby evaluating its suitability for investigating kynurenine pathway dysregulation associated with MDD.

Experimental

Materials, reagents and instruments

Standard samples of tryptophan and kynurenine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Methanol, acetonitrile, Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and chloroform (HPLC grade), and trichloroacetic acid (≥ 99.0%, analytical grade) were also sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. HP-β-CD was purchased from TCI Europe, Belgium. A 5 mM HP-β-CD working solution was freshly prepared by dissolving 69.0 mg of HP-β-CD in distilled water and diluting to 10 mL in a volumetric flask under gentle magnetic stirring until complete dissolution. The solution was stored in an amber glass bottle at 4 °C and allowed to equilibrate to room temperature before use. Fresh working solution was prepared weekly. The fluorescence measurements were performed using an Agilent Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer equipped with a xenon flash lamp. Fluorescence spectra were recorded using excitation and emission slit widths of 5 nm, a photomultiplier tube (PMT) voltage of 600 V, a scan speed of 600 nm min⁻¹, an integration time of 0.1 s, and a spectral data interval (bandwidth) of 1 nm. All measurements were carried out at ambient laboratory temperature under identical instrumental conditions.

Standard solutions

Individual standard stock solutions of tryptophan and kynurenine (each 10 mM) were prepared by accurately weighing 20.4 mg of tryptophan and 20.8 mg of kynurenine, respectively, and dissolving each in 0.5 mL of DMSO. The mixtures were then diluted to volume in separate 10 mL volumetric flasks with distilled water to achieve a final solvent composition of 5% DMSO. These stock solutions were stored in amber glass bottles at 4 °C and remained stable for up to two weeks. Working standard solutions of each analyte were prepared freshly daily by serial dilution of the respective stock solution with distilled water to obtain concentrations ranging from 1 µM to 100 µM.

Method development

Serial aliquots of tryptophan and kynurenine stock solutions were transferred into a series of 10 mL volumetric flasks to prepare solutions with final concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 µM. Calibration standards were prepared using pooled human plasma collected from healthy volunteers. Since analyte-free (stripped) plasma was unavailable, the same pooled plasma batch was used throughout the study to ensure a constant endogenous background. Plasma proteins were precipitated by mixing an initial volume of 1.0 mL of pooled plasma with 2.0 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to achieve complete protein precipitation. Subsequently, a precise aliquot of 1.0 mL of the resulting supernatant was collected and transferred into each of the 10 mL volumetric flasks containing the standard analytes. To this mixture, 1.0 mL of the HP-β-CD working solution (5 mM) was added (yielding a final concentration of 0.5 mM, which provides at least a 5-fold molar excess over the maximum analyte concentration to ensure quantitative host–guest complexation), and the mixtures were diluted to volume with distilled water. The prepared solutions were allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min to ensure complete host–guest complexation. Synchronous fluorescence spectra were then recorded by scanning both excitation and emission monochromators simultaneously at a constant wavelength offset (Δλ) of 50 nm. The synchronous fluorescence signals were measured at 400 nm for tryptophan and 455 nm for kynurenine. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting the measured fluorescence signals against the corresponding analyte concentrations in µM.

Method validation

The analytical performance of the proposed method was validated according to the recommendations of ICH Q2(R2), together with the principles outlined in ICH Q14 for analytical procedure development25. Considering that the proposed method was intended for the determination of endogenous analytes in human plasma, additional bioanalytical validation experiments were performed to evaluate matrix effect, extraction recovery, carry-over, dilution integrity, and robustness26,27. Thus validation protocol therefore included assessment of linearity, limits of detection and quantification, accuracy, precision, selectivity, matrix effect, extraction recovery, carry-over, dilution integrity, and robustness.

Accuracy was evaluated by analyzing three different concentrations of tryptophan and kynurenine: 20 µM, 40 µM, and 60 µM. Recovery percentages were calculated for each concentration to assess the method’s accuracy in both intra-day and inter-day experiments. Precision was determined by measuring each concentration in triplicate within a single day (intra-day) and on three consecutive days (inter-day). The relative standard deviation (RSD) was calculated in each case to evaluate the repeatability and reproducibility of the method.

Selectivity was evaluated by analyzing potential interfering compounds structurally related to tryptophan and kynurenine. Tyrosine and phenylalanine were selected because of their structural similarity as aromatic amino acids, whereas 3-hydroxykynurenine, quinolinic acid, and anthranilic acid were included as biologically relevant metabolites within the kynurenine pathway with the greatest potential to interfere with the proposed assay. Each compound was prepared at a final concentration of 50 µM. These solutions were processed under the same experimental conditions used for tryptophan and kynurenine, including the addition of 1.0 mL of 5 mM HP-β-CD and synchronous fluorescence measurement at a fixed wavelength offset (Δλ) of 50 nm. Fluorescence signals were measured at 400 nm and 455 nm to evaluate possible spectral interference.

Matrix effect was assessed using plasma obtained from six individual healthy volunteers to evaluate potential variability arising from different biological matrices. Blank plasma samples from each donor were processed according to the proposed protein precipitation procedure using trichloroacetic acid. The resulting extracts were separately spiked with tryptophan and kynurenine at three concentration levels (20, 40, and 60 µM), and each concentration was evaluated using all six plasma lots. The fluorescence responses were compared with those obtained from neat standard solutions prepared at the corresponding concentrations. Matrix factors were calculated as the ratio of the fluorescence response obtained from post-extraction spiked plasma samples to that of the corresponding neat standard solutions. The coefficient of variation (CV%) of the matrix factors among the six plasma lots was used to assess the consistency of the matrix effect.

Furthermore, extraction recovery was evaluated at three concentration levels (20, 40, and 60 µM). Plasma samples were spiked with the analytes before protein precipitation and processed according to the proposed extraction procedure. The fluorescence responses obtained from these samples were compared with those obtained from blank plasma extracts spiked with the analytes after extraction at identical concentrations. Extraction recovery was calculated as the percentage ratio of the fluorescence response obtained from pre-extraction spiked samples to that obtained from post-extraction spiked samples. Each concentration level was analyzed in triplicate. Carry-over was assessed by analyzing a blank processed plasma sample immediately after the highest calibration standard (100 µM). The fluorescence responses recorded at the analytical wavelengths (400 nm for tryptophan and 455 nm for kynurenine) were compared with those obtained at the 1µM. Carry-over was considered negligible when the blank response did not exceed 20% of the response measured at the lowest calibration standard concentration. Dilution integrity was demonstrated by preparing plasma samples containing tryptophan and kynurenine concentrations above the upper calibration limit. The samples were diluted two-fold and five-fold using blank pooled plasma before extraction and subsequent analysis. The diluted samples were analyzed in triplicate, and the measured concentrations were compared with the theoretical concentrations after applying the corresponding dilution factors.

Robustness of the proposed method was evaluated by introducing small deliberate variations into the optimized experimental conditions. The investigated parameters included wavelength offset (Δλ = 50 ± 2 nm), HP-β-CD concentration (5.0 ± 0.2 mM), standing time before fluorescence measurement (15 ± 2 min), and excitation and emission slit widths (5 ± 0.5 nm. Each condition was evaluated using samples containing 40 µM of tryptophan and kynurenine, and each experiment was performed in triplicate. The obtained recoveries and relative standard deviations were compared with those obtained under the optimized analytical conditions to evaluate the robustness of the method.

Method application

Study population and clinical assessment

The study population was recruited from the hospitals of the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Azhar University, Damietta, Egypt, and included 24 patients diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD) and 24 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Patients with MDD were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and psychiatric evaluations were performed by licensed psychiatrists. Depression severity was assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). Healthy controls were screened to confirm the absence of psychiatric or neurological disorders. Body mass index (BMI), medication status, antidepressant therapy, smoking habits, dietary restrictions, and relevant medical history were recorded for all participants. Individuals with acute or chronic inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders, active infections, hepatic or renal dysfunction, malignancies, or other systemic conditions known to influence tryptophan metabolism were excluded from the study.

Sample collection and processing

Blood samples were collected from all participants after an overnight fast to decrease metabolic variability. Venous blood (3 mL) was drawn under sterile conditions into EDTA-coated tubes to prevent clotting. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected and plasma proteins were denatured and precipitated by adding 0.2 mL of trichloroacetic acid (10% w/v), followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The protein-free supernatant, containing tryptophan and kynurenine, was then determined using the described method.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Azhar University, Damietta., Egypt. The institutional review board (IRB) approval number is DFM-IRB 00012367-25-02-090.

Results and discussion

Rationale for the method development

Immune activation via the kynurenine pathway diverts tryptophan metabolism away from serotonin synthesis toward neuroactive kynurenine metabolites, contributing to neuroinflammation and mood dysregulation in MDD28,29. Reliable determination of both analytes, whose chemical structures are shown in Fig. 1, is therefore critical for mechanistic demonstration and biomarker-based stratification of MDD patients. Nevertheless, kynurenine’s inherently weak intrinsic fluorescence and significant spectral overlap with the tryptophan signal present fundamental analytical challenges precluding their direct simultaneous determination. HP-β-CD inclusion complexation, in conjunction with synchronous fluorescence measurement, was therefore strategically utilized to amplify fluorescence signal, shift emission maxima to resolvable wavelengths, and develop interference-free, selective determination of kynurenine and tryptophan in a plasma matrix.

Chemical structure of tryptophan and kynurenine.

Photophysical behavior of tryptophan and kynurenine

Tryptophan is an aromatic amino acid with well-characterized fluorescence features resulting from π–π* transitions within its indole chromophore. Upon excitation at 280 nm, tryptophan typically strongly emits in the 350–360 nm range, as shown in Fig. 2a, with its fluorescence highly dependent on the surrounding environment and the solvent polarity. In contrast, kynurenine, a key metabolite in the tryptophan degradation pathway, displayed significantly weaker intrinsic fluorescence. Its structural transformation resulting in a conjugated, planar system reduces its quantum yield due to efficient non-radiative relaxation. Upon excitation at 280 nm, kynurenine shows weak fluorescence in the 350–360 nm range, as shown in Fig. 2b.

Overlay of conventional fluorescence emission spectra for tryptophan (50 µM) and kynurenine (50 µM), recorded in the absence and presence of HP-β-CD (5 mM), showing fluorescence enhancement upon complexation.

Inclusion complexation with HP-β-CD

HP-β-CD is a cyclic oligosaccharide with a hydrophilic outer surface and a hydrophobic internal cavity. It forms host-guest inclusion complexes with hydrophobic sites of small molecules. HP-β-CD enhances fluorescence by creating a protected milieu conducive to radiative transitions. Adding HP-β-CD to tryptophan and kynurenine caused a significant rise in fluorescence signal under experimental conditions. This improvement results in effective fluorophore encapsulation within the cyclodextrin cavity, limiting non-radiative decay and stabilizing the excited state30,31. Tryptophan exhibited a red-shifted emission maximum measured at 400 nm after interaction with HP-β-CD, as shown in Fig. 2c. This shift reflects a change in the local microenvironment due to inclusion complex formation. Nevertheless, upon excitation at 280 nm and in the presence of HP-β-CD, kynurenine exhibited an enhanced emission centered at 455 nm, as shown in Fig. 2d. This fluorescence enhancement is attributed to reduced solvent quenching and restricted molecular motion within the cyclodextrin cavity.

Resolving spectral overlap using synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy

Despite enhanced fluorescence signals following complexation, conventional emission spectra of tryptophan and kynurenine exhibited significant spectral overlap, limiting their simultaneous determination. To solve this issue, synchronous fluorescence scanning was implemented. This technique provides simultaneous scanning of excitation and emission monochromators with a constant wavelength offset (Δλ), effectively reducing spectral bandwidths and improving resolution. Using a Δλ of 50 nm, distinct synchronous fluorescence peaks were observed at 400 nm for tryptophan and 455 nm for kynurenine allowing effective spectral separation between the two analytes as shown in Fig. 3. This spectral discrimination eliminates overlap between their emission profiles, providing selective determination of each compound without interference from the other. As a result, the presence of tryptophan does not affect the signal of kynurenine, and vice versa.

Synchronous fluorescence emission spectra of tryptophan (50 µM) and kynurenine (50 µM) in the presence of HP-β-CD (5 mM), recorded at a fixed wavelength offset (Δλ = 50 nm), illustrating spectral separation between the two analytes.

Optimization of the experimental conditions

Selection of optimum Δλ

The wavelength interval (Δλ) between excitation and emission has a substantial effect on synchronous fluorescence scanning performance, including spectrum resolution and signal quality. A constant Δλ reduces spectrum widening, allowing for more exact separation of analytes. To assign Δλ for simultaneous determination of tryptophan and kynurenine, values between 20 and 80 nm were explored. Lower Δλ values (20–30 nm) resulted in poor resolution due to spectrum overlap. In contrast, higher values (70–80 nm) decreased sensitivity by reducing fluorescence intensity. An ideal Δλ of 50 nm achieved a balance between resolution and sensitivity, resulting in well-defined, narrow peaks with minimum spectral overlap.

Effect of diluting solvent

The physicochemical properties of the diluent solvent exert a profound effect on the fluorescence behavior of the analytes through modulating the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding the fluorophore, the efficiency of solvent-induced quenching, and the stability of the HP-β-CD inclusion complex. To assign the optimal diluent, five candidate solvents spanning a broad polarity range including distilled water, methanol, acetonitrile, ethanol and chloroform were systematically tested for their impact on synchronous fluorescence signal and spectral resolution of tryptophan and kynurenine. As presented in Fig. 4, distilled water exhibited the highest synchronous fluorescence signals and the best-defined spectral peaks for both compounds. The efficiency of distilled water is associated with its high dielectric constant and extensive hydrogen-bonding network, which stabilize the ground and excited electronic states of both compounds while simultaneously favoring the formation and thermodynamic stability of HP-β-CD inclusion complexes through the hydrophobic effect. Organic solvents reduced fluorescence intensities due to competitive disruption of host-guest complexation and alterations in the polarity microenvironment of the encapsulated fluorophores. Ethanol exhibited lower signals, consistent with destabilization of the HP-β-CD complex and enhanced quenching via enhanced intersystem crossing in low-polarity environments. Moreover, distilled water is compatible with biological plasma matrices, enables direct sample dilution without the matrix modification artifacts introduced by organic co-solvents. Thus, distilled water was selected as the optimal solvent for the proposed measurements.

Method validation

The developed method was validated to guarantee compliance with essential parameters such as linearity, accuracy and precision, Table 1. The approach showed a linear relationship between tryptophan and kynurenine at concentrations ranging from 1 µM to 100 µM, with an acceptable correlation coefficient. The LOD values for tryptophan and kynurenine were 0.20 µM and 0.22 µM, respectively, indicating the lowest amounts for reliable detection. The LOQ values for tryptophan and kynurenine were 0.60 µM and 0.66 µM, respectively, indicating the lowest quantities for accurate and precise quantification.

The accuracy of the method was determined by monitoring three different concentrations of tryptophan and kynurenine: 20 µM, 40 µM, and 60 µM, over the course across intra-day and inter-day experiments. The mean recovery percentages for tryptophan and kynurenine were 99.25 and 98.87, respectively, showing that the method accurately and consistently measured the analytes within the tested limits. The method’s precision was assessed using three different concentrations of tryptophan and kynurenine: 20 µM, 40 µM, and 60 µM, each examined three times inside a single day (intra-day precision) and across multiple days (inter-day precision). The relative standard deviation (RSD) values were continuously low, demonstrating the measurements’ repeatability in both situations.

To assess the selectivity of the proposed method, several structurally related compounds including tyrosine, phenylalanine, 3-hydroxykynurenine, quinolinic acid, and anthranilic acid were assessed for potential spectral interference. Each compound was tested at a concentration of 50 µM under identical experimental conditions, including complexation with HP-β-CD and synchronous fluorescence scanning at a fixed wavelength offset (Δλ) of 50 nm. Fluorescence intensities were measured at 400 nm and 455 nm, corresponding to the analytical wavelengths for tryptophan and kynurenine, respectively. The results demonstrated negligible fluorescence signals from the tested compounds at the selected wavelengths. None of the interferents produced significant emission intensities at either 400–455 nm, with responses remaining below 5% of those recorded for tryptophan and kynurenine at equivalent concentrations (Table 2). The selected compounds represent structurally related aromatic amino acids and biologically relevant metabolites of the kynurenine pathway that are most likely to interfere with the proposed assay. Although these findings demonstrate excellent selectivity under the investigated conditions, evaluation of additional endogenous plasma fluorophores may further strengthen the selectivity profile and will be considered in future studies.

To further demonstrate the method reliability, additional bioanalytical validation studies were performed and presented in Table 3. Matrix effect was evaluated at three concentration levels (20, 40, and 60 µM) using plasma obtained from six independent healthy volunteers. The calculated matrix factors were close to unity for both analytes, averaging 0.99 ± 0.02 for tryptophan and 0.98 ± 0.03 for kynurenine, while the variability among the six plasma lots did not exceed 3.4% and 3.8%, respectively. These results indicate that endogenous plasma constituents exerted negligible influence on the fluorescence response, confirming the accepted selectivity of the proposed method. Extraction recovery was evaluated by comparing plasma samples spiked before extraction with blank plasma extracts spiked after extraction at identical concentration levels. Mean recoveries of 99.1 ± 1.6% for tryptophan and 98.4 ± 1.8% for kynurenine were obtained, with RSD values below 2.4%, demonstrating efficient and reproducible extraction using the proposed trichloroacetic acid precipitation procedure. Carry-over was evaluated through analyzing processed blank plasma immediately after the highest calibration standard. The residual fluorescence signals corresponded to less than 2.8% of the response at the lower calibration standard concentration for both analytes, confirming the absence of significant carry-over. Dilution integrity was demonstrated using plasma samples containing analyte concentrations above the upper calibration limit followed by two-fold and five-fold dilution with blank plasma. Mean recoveries ranged from 99.5% to 100.2%, with precision values not exceeding 3.2%, indicating that samples exceeding the validated concentration range can be accurately quantified after appropriate dilution.

Robustness studies demonstrated that deliberate variations in wavelength offset (Δλ = 50 ± 2 nm), HP-β-CD concentration (5.0 ± 0.2 mM), standing time (15 ± 2 min), excitation and emission slit widths (5 ± 0.5 nm) did not significantly influence the analytical performance. Alterations in tryptophan and kynurenine levels in major depressive disorder: insights from spectrofluorimetric analysis

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 4. No statistically significant differences were observed between the MDD and healthy control groups regarding age, sex distribution, body BMI, smoking status, or dietary restrictions (p > 0.05), indicating that the two groups were well matched. As expected, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) scores were higher in the MDD group than in healthy controls (p < 0.001). The developed spectrofluorimetric method was used to test the alterations in tryptophan and kynurenine levels between MDD patients and healthy controls, Table 5. MDD patients exhibited markedly lower plasma tryptophan levels (41.20 ± 3.22 µM) compared to healthy controls (62.50 ± 6.20 µM, p = 0.003). Conversely, kynurenine levels were significantly elevated in MDD patients (3.50 ± 1.20 µM) compared to healthy controls (1.20 ± 0.2 µM, p = 0.004). The observed decrease in tryptophan and increase in kynurenine are consistent with heightened kynurenine pathway activity, likely driven by inflammation-induced indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) activation. This metabolic shift suggests reduced serotonin availability, reinforcing the role of tryptophan metabolism in MDD pathophysiology. The observed results align with previous studies linking kynurenine pathway dysregulation to neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter imbalances in MDD.

Comparison of the proposed method with previously reported analytical methods

To contextualize the analytical performance of the proposed method, Table 6 presents a comparison with previously reported analytical methods for the determination of tryptophan and kynurenine. The HPLC-UV/FLD method12 enabled simultaneous detection in serum but required derivatization of kynurenine to achieve sufficient fluorescence intensity, increasing sample preparation time and potential variability. Although this method achieved a low LOD of 2 nmol L⁻¹ for kynurenine, the derivatization step remains a limitation. The HPLC-electrochemical detection method using multi-wall carbon nanotube-modified glassy carbon electrodes14 eliminated the need for derivatization and achieved LODs of 0.4 and 0.5 µmol L⁻¹ for tryptophan and kynurenine, respectively, but is susceptible to electrode fouling in complex matrices. LC-MS/MS15 provided the highest sensitivity, with LOQs of 0.55 and 0.47 ng mL⁻¹ for tryptophan and kynurenine, respectively, over linear ranges of 1.25–4000 and 0.5–1600 ng mL⁻¹. However, this approach requires an internal standard and relies on expensive instrumentation that may not be readily available in routine analytical laboratories. In contrast, the developed synchronous spectrofluorimetric method with HP-β-CD complexation provides LODs of 0.20 µM for tryptophan and 0.22 µM for kynurenine over a 1–100 µM linear range without derivatization. The method enables direct, simultaneous plasma analysis with high sensitivity while using accessible instrumentation and simplified sample preparation, making it a practical alternative for routine analysis.

Study limitations

Although the established approach was successfully applied to identify between MDD patients and healthy controls, the clinical portion of the study relied on a small sample size, which limits the generalizability of the findings. While statistically significant differences in tryptophan and kynurenine levels were found, bigger and more diverse populations would allow for more generalization and a better assessment of inter-individual variation. Additionally, diagnostic performance measures including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and predictive accuracy were not evaluated in this work, as the primary focus was on establishing analytical validity rather than clinical diagnostic thresholds.

Furthermore, the current study aimed to validate the method’s analytical capabilities rather than investigate longitudinal or treatment-related variations in metabolite levels. Additionally, a key limitation of this work is the absence of parallel validation and cross-method comparison against an independent, gold-standard analytical reference method, such as liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), to fully verify the absolute accuracy of the spectrofluorimetric clinical measurements. These factors underline the necessity for additional clinical investigations to corroborate the observed trends while also strengthening the established method’s dependability as a sensitive and selective instrument for simultaneously quantifying tryptophan and kynurenine.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel synchronous spectrofluorimetric method incorporating HP-β-CD inclusion complexation was successfully developed for the simultaneous determination of tryptophan and kynurenine in plasma. The method demonstrated high sensitivity, selectivity, and precision across a wide concentration range. Quantum yield measurements and clinical pilot data supported the method’s analytical reliability. While these findings are encouraging, the proposed approach represents a promising analytical tool that requires further validation in larger and more diverse patient populations before it can be considered for routine clinical implementation. Nonetheless, it offers a straightforward, non-derivatizing, and cost-effective alternative for monitoring tryptophan metabolism in future clinical research and broader neuroinflammatory studies.
