Lung ultrasound morphology patterns predict treatment response and weaning outcomes in high-risk mechanically ventilated patients
Authors: Hongyu He, Sheng Xu, Qinnan Yang, Xin Zheng, Yimei Liu, Lizhen Xuan, Zhunyong Gu, Ming Zhong, Minjie Ju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between lung ultrasound morphology patterns—localized tissue-like patterns (TLP) and diffuse B-lines (DBP)—and patients’ response to routine clinician-directed management in high-risk mechanically ventilated patients undergoing weaning.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 97 high-risk mechanically ventilated patients with a Lung Ultrasound Score (LUS) > 13 were stratified into TLP and DBP groups based on their lung ultrasound patterns. Lung reaeration was assessed using the Lung Recruitment Score (LRS) before and after the application of routine therapeutic measures. Primary outcomes included changes in LRS. Secondary outcomes included weaning failure rates, duration of mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) mortality, and length of ICU stay.ResultsThe study included 97 high-risk mechanically ventilated patients, of whom 49 had TLP and 48 had DBP on lung ultrasound. Upon ICU admission, the TLP group exhibited significantly higher global LUS compared to the DBP group (17.20 ± 2.01 vs. 14.21 ± 2.04, P < 0.001), with more pronounced differences observed in the posterior (9.65 ± 0.90 vs. 7.5 ± 0.58, P < 0.001) and lateral regions (4.94 ± 1.05 vs. 4.35 ± 1.24, P = 0.014). Following routine clinician-directed, the TLP group demonstrated more substantial improvement in global LRS compared to the DBP group (5.43 ± 3.42 vs. 4.04 ± 2.99, P = 0.036), indicating enhanced lung reaeration. Multivariate analysis identified Apache II score (OR = 1.105, P = 0.021) and global LRS (OR = 0.476, P = 0.003) as independent predictors of weaning failure. Among successfully weaned patients, those in the TLP group exhibited significantly higher LRS than those in the DBP group (6.71 ± 2.31 vs. 5.39 ± 2.26, P = 0.021), suggesting a more favorable response to interventions in the TLP group.ConclusionsPatients with TLP demonstrated superior response to routine clinician-directed management and achieved comparable weaning outcomes to those with DBP, despite higher initial LUS. Lung morphology patterns and regional LRS assessments may assist in tailoring management strategies and predicting weaning outcomes in high-risk mechanically ventilated patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03842-3.

Introduction

Lung ultrasound (LU) has become a pivotal non-invasive tool in critical care, particularly beneficial for managing mechanically ventilated patients [1]. It provides real-time insights into lung pathology, facilitating prompt diagnosis and continuous monitoring of complications associated with mechanical ventilation, such as pneumothorax [2], ventilator-associated pneumonia [3], atelectasis [4], and pleural effusion [5, 6]. LU also plays a crucial role in optimizing mechanical ventilation settings to mitigate the risk of ventilator-induced lung injury and improve gas exchange. Moreover, LU enables effective evaluation of various therapeutic interventions, including effusion drainage [7], prone positioning [8], and adjustments in positive end-expiratory pressure [9]. By integrating these functionalities, LU serves as a comprehensive tool for both preventing and managing complications in mechanically ventilated patients.

Despite its widespread use, there are gaps in LU’s predictive capabilities, especially concerning weaning outcomes from mechanical ventilation. The Global Lung Ultrasound Score (LUS), a semi-quantitative measure categorizing lung aeration loss into four stages, illustrates this. High LUS scores (> 17) often indicate post-extubation distress, while low scores (< 13) suggest successful weaning; however, scores between 13 and 17 yield uncertain outcomes [10–12]. Previous research indicates that the composition of lung morphology contributing to a high LUS can vary among patients, necessitating detailed analysis of regional scores [13]. For instance, a high score may result from diffuse B-lines (DBPs) across multiple regions or a mix of localized tissue-like patterns (TLPs) with higher scores in fewer regions alongside normally aerated regions scoring 0 [5, 14]. This variability underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of LU patterns to optimize weaning management, particularly in high-risk patients.

This study aims to address this gap by investigating the relationship between specific lung ultrasound morphologies—TLPs versus DBPs—and their response to standard clinical management strategies We hypothesize that localized TLPs are associated with more favorable responses to treatment interventions, ultimately leading to more successful weaning outcomes compared to those with DBPs.

To test this hypothesis, we utilize both the established LUS and the Lung Recruitment Score (LRS) to assess regional pulmonary changes pre- and post-application of routine therapeutic measures in mechanically ventilated patients, thereby evaluating lung recruitment efficiency. By quantifying the effectiveness of interventions and enhancing the predictive accuracy of ultrasound scoring in the context of mechanical ventilation weaning, this study seeks to provide a more robust framework for tailoring patient care based on specific diagnostic insights.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This study involves a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data and lung ultrasound (LUS) information. Data were gathered from patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, during the period of September 2021 to April 2022. The Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (No. B2019-188R) approved the protocol for the prospective collection of LUS data and this subsequent retrospective analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or their legal guardians for the collection and research use of their data. For the current analysis, patients meeting the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were identified retrospectively from this cohort. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed for this retrospective observational study; all eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the specified study period were included in the analysis. This retrospective study has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with the registration number NCT06521515.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Medical records of ICU-admitted patients during the study period were continuously reviewed. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) mechanical ventilation for over 24 h before the first spontaneous breathing trial, (2) high risk for reintubation due to age (> 65 years) or underlying conditions (chronic heart disease: left ventricular dysfunction, history of cardiogenic pulmonary edema, documented ischemic heart disease, or chronic atrial fibrillation; chronic lung disease: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or restrictive lung disease), and (3) complete ultrasound data and initial LUS score > 13.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age under 18 years, pregnancy, mechanical ventilation duration less than 24 h, or diaphragmatic dysfunction(defined as a diaphragm thickening fraction < 20% or tidal-breathing excursion < 1 cm on M-mode ultrasonography) [15]; (2) low risk of extubation failure; (3) peripheral neuromuscular disorders; or (4) do-not-reintubate order at the first spontaneous breathing trial.

Ultrasonography evaluation and grouping

Daily lung ultrasound examinations were systematically performed by two trained investigators (ICU physicians or research personnel with expertise in LUS) as part of routine patient monitoring and an ongoing observational data collection protocol within the ICU. These investigators, at the time of performing and initially interpreting the LUS examinations, were blinded to the specific hypotheses and comparative analyses of this particular retrospective study. They were not involved in the subsequent patient grouping for this study or the specific data extraction related to the study’s primary and secondary outcomes. Each lung was scanned in six regions, categorizing ultrasound findings into four aeration patterns: normal (N-score 0), moderate (B1-lines score 1), severe (B2-lines score 2), and consolidation (C-score 3). The global LUS score ranged from 0 to 36. Patients were grouped into TLP if they exhibited a tissue-like pattern in any region or DBP if they displayed diffuse B-lines and shred signs in most regions. Localized TLPs indicated concentrated areas of non-aeration. To ensure consistency in ultrasound evaluations, following standardized training and inter-operator reliability assessments for all operators who acquired the initial LUS images, the archived clips were retrospectively scored for this study by two independent investigators (not involved in clinical management and blinded to all clinical outcomes), with disagreements adjudicated by a third senior sonologist.

Clinical management and weaning

Patients received standard medical care from clinicians. Lung recruitment maneuvers and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) ventilation were utilized for patients with aeration loss; When RMs were performed, they generally followed a unit-wide standardized incremental PEEP protocol: PEEP was increased from a baseline (typically 10 cmH₂O) to 20 cmH₂O, then to 25 cmH₂O, with each step maintained for approximately 2 min, while monitoring hemodynamics and oxygenation. This was followed by a sustained inflation to 30 cmH₂O for 30 s [16]. After the recruitment, a decremental PEEP titration was performed to identify the PEEP level associated with the best respiratory system compliance or oxygenation, guided by clinician assessment. The decision for pronation was based on clinical judgment of severe, refractory hypoxemia. Prone positioning was typically maintained for at least 16 h per cycle, and the number of cycles was determined by the clinical response and resolution of hypoxemia [17].Pulmonary ultrasound guided pleural effusion drainage when fluid collections were detected. Antibiotics were administered for pneumonia, and fiberoptic bronchoscopy was performed if atelectasis or consolidation was observed on lung ultrasonography. Strategies for achieving negative fluid balance were implemented for patients with DBPs. Weaning criteria included: (1) improved respiratory failure; (2) PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≥ 150; (3) pH > 7.25; (4) hemodynamic stability (defined as MAP ≥ 65 mmHg with norepinephrine ≤ 0.1 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ for ≥ 2 h and no escalation in vasoactive support); and (5) ability to initiate an inspiratory effort [15].

The Spontaneous Breathing Test (SBT) was conducted using a T-tube or mechanical ventilation with PEEP ≤ 5 cmH2O and pressure support ≤ 7 cmH2O, as determined by the clinician. Patients were closely monitored during the SBT for signs of failure, including respiratory rate > 35 breaths/min for more than 5 min, SpO₂ <90%, heart rate > 140 beats/min or a sustained change of > 20%, systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg or < 90 mmHg, or development of agitation, diaphoresis, or anxiety [15]. Lung ultrasound was performed daily from ICU admission until weaning or extubation, with decisions to discontinue SBT or extubate made by the attending physician based on overall clinical assessment and SBT tolerance.

Clinical endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was the change in Lung Recruitment Score (LRS) between the TLP and DBP groups. LRS assesses lung recruitment efficiency, assigning positive scores for reaeration and negative scores for loss of aeration (see Supplementary Appendix 1). Secondary endpoints included the rate of weaning failure, defined as inability to successfully complete a spontaneous breathing trial, need for mechanical ventilation reintroduction within 48 h post-weaning, or mortality related to weaning. Additional secondary endpoints comprised duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU mortality, total ICU stay duration, and PaO2/FiO2 ratio before and after the spontaneous breathing trial (SBT). The PaO2/FiO2 ratio, an oxygenation status indicator, was assessed to evaluate readiness for weaning and to compare oxygenation function between TLP and DBP groups. Assessments after the application of standard therapeutic measures focused on pre-weaning oxygenation status, crucial for evaluating readiness and potential success of the weaning process.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), mean [95% confidence interval (CI)], or median [interquartile range], depending on the data distribution. Comparisons between the DBP and TLP groups were conducted using the two-tailed Student’s t-test, χ2 test, or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Paired comparisons of data before and after SBT were assessed using the paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed rank sum test, depending on data distribution.

Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were utilized to identify risk factors associated with weaning outcomes. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate regression model. The final model was determined using a backward stepwise elimination method, with a significance level of 0.05 for variable retention. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this retrospective study, a total of 145 mechanically ventilated patients were initially enrolled. Figure 1 illustrates that 48 patients were subsequently excluded: 15 were ventilated for less than 24 h, 16 were not deemed high-risk, 5 lacked complete ultrasound data, and 12 had an initial LUS score less than 13. The remaining 97 patients were divided into two groups: 49 in the TLP group and 48 in the DBP group.

Study flowchart

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 97 included patients. The mean age was 74.3 ± 18.4 years, with 69.1% being male. Patients had been on mechanical ventilation for a median of approximately 7 days prior to weaning attempts. Significant differences were observed between the TLP and DBP groups. Compared to the DBP group, the TLP group had a higher proportion of males (70.6% vs. 65.5%, P = 0.024), a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (36.7% vs. 16.7%, P = 0.026), higher APACHE II scores (24.5 ± 9.2 vs. 21.0 ± 8.0, P = 0.049), and lower left ventricular ejection fraction (56.6 ± 7.2% vs. 59.9 ± 6.3%, P = 0.018). Additionally, upon admission, the TLP group exhibited significantly lower PaO₂ (113.9 ± 59.3 mmHg vs. 152.9 ± 71.5 mmHg, P = 0.004), required a higher FiO₂ (0.60 ± 0.19 vs. 0.52 ± 0.14, P = 0.023), and showed a lower PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio (206.6 ± 104.5 mmHg vs. 312.6 ± 150.5 mmHg, P < 0.001) compared to the DBP group. No significant differences were found in other baseline characteristics between the two groups.

Clinical management received by patient groups

As depicted in Supplementary Appendix 2, significant differences were observed in interventional measures between the TLP and DBP groups during mechanical ventilation. Specifically, the TLP group required more frequent pleural fluid drainage compared to the DBP group (79.2% vs. 37.5%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the volume of pleural fluid drained was significantly higher in the TLP group (418.3 ± 432.5 ml) than in the DBP group (167.0 ± 291.1 ml, P = 0.001). However, no significant differences were found in the utilization of renal replacement therapy (24.5% vs. 8.3%, P = 0.454) or in the volume balance before the SBT (−236.2 ± 948.7 ml per 24 h vs. −161.2 ± 905.0 ml per 24 h, P = 0.692) between the two groups. Furthermore, urine output (1817.8 ± 696.4 ml per 24 h vs. 1962.4 ± 781.1 ml per 24 h, P = 0.338) and central venous pressure (10.22 ± 3.41 mmHg vs. 9.58 ± 3.14 mmHg, P = 0.339) before the SBT were similar between the TLP and DBP groups.

Primary and secondary outcomes

As indicated in Table 2, a significant difference was observed in the primary outcome, the LRS, between the TLP and DBP groups (5.43 ± 3.42 vs. 4.04 ± 2.99, P = 0.036), with the TLP group showing a higher score, indicating greater lung reaeration following the application of routine clinical management However, secondary outcomes related to weaning did not differ significantly between the two groups, including weaning failure rates (28.6% vs. 31.3%, P = 0.773), duration of mechanical ventilation (7.8 ± 12.5 vs. 7.9 ± 11.8 days, P = 0.983), ICU length of stay (17.0 ± 21.7 vs. 12.2 ± 23.0 days, P = 0.297), and ICU mortality (30.6% vs. 20.8%, P = 0.271).

Significant differences were also noted in the PaO2/FiO2 ratio between the TLP and DBP groups at different time points. Before the SBT, the TLP group exhibited a lower PaO2/FiO2 ratio compared to the DBP group (300.2 ± 92.8 mmHg vs. 330.9 ± 103.5 mmHg), though this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.127). At the conclusion of the SBT, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio was significantly lower in the TLP group than in the DBP group (293.6 ± 121.9 mmHg vs. 375.2 ± 175.1 mmHg, P = 0.009). These differences in the PaO2/FiO2 ratio may be attributed to the higher FiO2 requirements (0.60 ± 0.19 vs. 0.52 ± 0.14, P = 0.004) and lower PaO2 levels (113.9 ± 59.3 mmHg vs. 152.9 ± 71.5 mmHg, P = 0.023) observed in the TLP group at enrollment. Despite these statistical differences, both groups achieved relatively high levels of oxygenation before and after the SBT, indicating significant improvement in oxygenation function in both cohorts.

Association of lung recruitment scores with weaning outcomes

The initial LU assessments, detailed in Table 3, indicated notable differences between the TLP and DBP groups in LUS. At admission, the TLP group showed significantly higher LUS in the posterior (9.65 ± 0.90 vs. 7.5 ± 0.58, P < 0.001), lateral (4.94 ± 1.05 vs. 4.35 ± 1.24, P = 0.014), and global regions (17.20 ± 2.01 vs. 14.21 ± 2.04, P < 0.001) compared to the DBP group. However, there was no significant difference in anterior LUS between the two groups (2.61 ± 1.18 for TLP vs. 2.35 ± 0.86 for DBP, P = 0.223). Subsequent assessments before the SBT showed a decrease in LUS across all regions, with the TLP group maintaining higher scores, albeit reduced, in global LUS (12.06 ± 3.19 vs. 10.35 ± 2.98, P = 0.008).

Independent predictors of weaning failure

Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to identify factors independently associated with weaning failure, as detailed in Table 4. Univariate and multivariate analysis of weaning risk factors

In the multivariate analysis, however, only the APACHE II score and global lung reaeration score remained as independent predictors of weaning failure. The OR for APACHE II score was 1.105 (95%CI = 1.015–1.202, P = 0.021), indicating that higher APACHE II scores were associated with a slightly increased likelihood of weaning failure. Conversely, the global lung reaeration score had an OR of 0.476 (95%CI = 0.294–0.772, P = 0.003), suggesting that lower scores significantly increased the risk of weaning failure.

Discussion

Our study reveals that patients with TLPs and DBPs on LU exhibited varied responses to treatment, despite both groups initially presenting with high LUS at ICU admission. The TLP group demonstrated significantly higher initial LUS in the posterior, lateral, and global regions compared to the DBP group. However, following routine management strategies, the TLP group showed more notable improvements in LRS, particularly in the posterior and global regions, leading to weaning success rates comparable to those of the DBP group. This observation underscores the significant role of lung morphology patterns in influencing patient responses to standard clinical management and subsequent weaning outcomes, with TLP patients benefiting more from routine therapeutic measures aimed at enhancing lung aeration.

Our findings offer new insights into the prognostic value of LU in predicting weaning outcomes among high-risk mechanically ventilated patients. Previous studies have indicated that high LUS scores (> 17) often predict post-extubation distress, while lower scores (< 13) suggest successful weaning. Scores between 13 and 17 have been associated with uncertain outcomes [10, 11]. In contrast, our study demonstrates that despite higher LUS scores, patients with TLP do not necessarily exhibit higher rates of weaning failure. This discrepancy underscores the importance of considering lung morphology patterns alongside LUS when evaluating readiness for weaning and forecasting patient outcomes.

Our study addresses a critical gap in the existing literature by highlighting distinct responses to standard clinical management based on lung ultrasonography patterns. While previous research has predominantly focused on the prognostic role of LUS [10, 12], our findings suggest that the localized nature of pathologies in TLP may show greater improvement following clinician-directed therapeutic measures such as PEEP ventilation and pleural drainage [7], compared to the diffuse pathologies indicated by B-lines in DBP. This understanding of the relationship between lung morphology and treatment response can assist clinicians in tailoring management strategies for high-risk patients undergoing weaning from mechanical ventilation.

The differential treatment responses observed between TLP and DBP patients can be attributed to underlying pathophysiological differences. TLP involves localized pathologies (pleural effusions, atelectasis, and ventilator-associated pneumonia) where targeted interventions can achieve complete resolution in discrete anatomical areas while preserving surrounding normal lung tissue. These conditions respond well to the interventions like pleural drainage [7], recruitment maneuvers [18], and antimicrobial therapy [3]. In our study, the TLP group had significantly higher rates of pleural effusion requiring drainage, and successful drainage likely contributed to the significant improvement in LRS observed in this group. In contrast, DBP reflects diffuse alveolar-capillary membrane dysfunction requiring systemic rather than localized approaches. The diffuse B-line pattern typically indicates cardiogenic pulmonary edema or widespread lung inflammation. These conditions are inherently more resistant to standard interventions because the pathology is distributed throughout the lung parenchyma. Strategies involving glucocorticoids [19]and negative fluid management [20] have shown limited efficacy in improving outcomes for these patients. This mechanistic difference explains why TLP patients demonstrated superior lung recruitment responses compared to DBP patients despite similar baseline management approaches.

Another significant finding of our study is the regional distribution of LRS improvements observed in the TLP group. The posterior lung regions showed the most notable enhancements in reaeration, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of lung recruitment in these patients. This observation underscores the importance of regional lung ultrasound assessment in addition to global LUS when evaluating high-risk patients for weaning readiness [21].

In the multivariate analysis, LRS emerged as an independent predictor of weaning outcomes, alongside the APACHE II score. This finding suggests that among high-risk patients with LUS scores greater than 13, stratifying based on LRS improvements may aid in identifying individuals with better treatment responses and a higher likelihood of successful weaning. Although initially developed for use in ARDS patients [13], our study demonstrates the utility of LRS in assessing lung reaeration in high-risk patients undergoing weaning. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate whether LRS can reliably predict weaning failure rates in this specific patient population.

Our study has several limitations. First, inherent to its single-center, retrospective design, our findings may have limited generalizability to other patient populations or clinical settings. Consequently, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients during the study period, which may affect the statistical power to detect smaller differences. Prospective, multi-center studies are essential to validate our results. Second, limitations related to data collection and interpretation include potential inter-operator variability in LUS image acquisition and interpretation, despite adherence to a standardized protocol and examinations being conducted by experienced, regularly trained operators. While LUS is a valuable bedside tool, its findings were not systematically compared with less operator-dependent imaging modalities like CT scans or EIT in this cohort for serial aeration assessment, an avenue for future research. Third, our study focused on a specific high-risk cohort of mechanically ventilated patients (LUS > 13), which, while allowing exploration in a challenging group, means our findings may not extend to patients with lower LUS scores or those at lower risk of weaning failure. Fourth, important confounding variables were not systematically controlled due to the retrospective nature of routine clinical care documentation. Specifically, vasopressor dosages and duration, exact frequency and timing of recruitment maneuvers, and prone positioning rates were not systematically recorded, limiting our ability to assess their individual impact on treatment responses between TLP and DBP groups. While our weaning criteria included hemodynamic stability requirements, this only controlled for vasopressor dependency at weaning assessment rather than throughout the ICU course. These uncontrolled variables may have influenced the observed differences in lung recruitment responses between groups. Therefore, while our study suggests potential benefits for TLP patients from measures aimed at improving lung aeration, the precise impact of each specific therapeutic component remains to be definitively established. Future prospective studies should incorporate systematic documentation of these variables to better control for confounders and validate our findings. Finally, to overcome these limitations and determine optimal treatment approaches for patients with diverse lung morphology patterns, randomized controlled trials are necessary to assess the impact of specific, protocolized management strategies on weaning outcomes and other clinically relevant endpoints.

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the significance of evaluating lung morphology patterns and regional lung recruitment when assessing high-risk patients for weaning readiness. The distinct responses to routine clinical management observed between TLP and DBP patients underscore the necessity for personalized management strategies tailored to lung ultrasound findings. The inclusion of LRS in the assessment of high-risk patients holds promise for enhancing the prediction of weaning outcomes and informing clinical decision-making. Future research efforts should prioritize prospective validation of LRS predictive value in larger cohorts of high-risk patients undergoing mechanical ventilation weaning.
