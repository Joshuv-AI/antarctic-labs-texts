Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Mediates Spontaneous Resolution of Acute Gouty Inflammation Via Inhibiting Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent Macrophage Pyroptosis
Authors: Xin Li, Lei Liu, Shuting Tong, Fangying Wang, Lingjiang Zhu, Qi Cheng, Peiyu Zhang, Mo Chen, Yifan Xie, Li Wang, Hang Yu, Huaxiang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Research on spontaneous resolution of acute gout remains limited. Macrophages pyroptosis is crucial for the inflammation of acute gout, while current research mainly focus on Caspase 1/Gasdermin D axis. We aimed to investigate the involvement of other Gasdermin proteins in MSU crystal-induced macrophages, and to explore the role of Caspase 3-interacting protein alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) in regulating macrophage pyroptosis. Here, clinical evidence demonstrated elevated Gasdermin E (GSDME) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and CD68+ synovial macrophages from patients with acute gout. In THP-1-derived macrophages, activated Caspase 3/GSDME axis was found after MSU crystals stimulation, and knockdown of Caspase 3 and GSDME significantly suppressed pyroptosis. In vivo, the Caspase 3 inhibitor effectively alleviated MSU crystal-induced acute gouty arthritis in mice. Cytologically, Caspase 3 interacting protein AAT was identified using immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry technology. Meanwhile, AAT was elevated in serum, PBMCs, synovial fluids, and CD68+ synovial macrophages from patients with acute gout. Furthermore, AAT inhibited Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis axis by binding to Caspase 3 in MSU crystal-induced macrophages. Additionally, AAT was internalized into macrophages via low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1. Collectively, elevated AAT in synovial fluids from patients with acute gout attenuates macrophage pyroptosis by inhibiting Caspase 3/GSDME axis, providing a novel explanation for the spontaneous resolution of acute gout.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02424-z.

Introduction

Gout is a prevalent type of inflammatory arthritis caused by the deposition of monosodium urate monohydrate (MSU) crystals, characterized by redness, swelling, and pain in the joints. Symptoms associated with gout attacks typically resolve spontaneously within 7 to 10 days, a phenomenon referred to as the spontaneous resolution of gouty arthritis [1, 2]. However, current research on the underlying mechanisms remains limited.

Macrophages are thought to play important roles in initiating, driving, and alleviating MSU crystal-induced inflammation of synovitis by secreting large amounts of cytokine [3]. Increasing evidence suggests that pyroptosis is involved in the inflammatory process within MSU crystal-induced macrophages [4, 5]. Pyroptosis is a programmed cell death executed by Gasdermin proteins forming membrane pores that induce permeabilization, cell lysis, and release of pro-inflammatory molecules, such as lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-18 [6], often as a consequence of inflammatory Caspase activation. Among the family members, Gasdermin D (GSDMD) and Gasdermin E (GSDME) are important executors in the pathogenesis of pyroptosis. GSDMD can be cleaved by Caspases 1 into N-terminal fragments to become executor of pyroptosis [7]. In a similar manner, GSDME is cleaved by activated Caspase 3 to execute pyroptosis [8, 9]. The study by Rashidi et. al [10] demonstrated that GSDMD is activated by MSU crystals but is not necessary for macrophages to release IL-1β. Gsdmd-/-mice did not exhibit a reduction in IL-1β induction in MSU crystal-induced peritonitis, which is consistent with the in vitro results. Therefore, we wondered that whether any other Gasdermin protein is involved in the pyroptosis of MSU crystal-induced macrophages.

Caspase 3-interacting protein alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) is synthesized by SERPINA1 gene mainly expressed by hepatocytes, monocytes, and macrophages, and then was secreted into the blood [11]. As an acute phase protein, the concentration of AAT ranges from 0.9 to 2 mg/mL in healthy controls, while in the acute phase, the level can suddenly increase by more than fourfold [11]. AAT plays a role in regulating inflammatory response by inhibiting neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and proteinase-3 [12]. Specifically, AAT ameliorates gouty arthritis in mice by inhibiting proteinase-3-mediated cleavage of the precursor into active IL-1β and inducing endogenous IL-1 receptor antagonist [13]. Of note, AAT could be internalized into the cytoplasm and function intracellularly [14, 15], acting as an inhibitor of Caspases. For example, AAT binds to Caspase 3 in pulmonary epithelial cells, significantly reducing damage to the alveolar wall [16]. Besides, treatment with AAT prevents streptozotocin-induced islet β-cells from apoptosis by inhibiting Caspase 3 [17]. However, the expression and mechanism of AAT in acute gout are not yet clear.

The purpose of this study is to investigate whether Caspase 3/GSDME is involved in MSU crystal induced macrophage pyroptosis, as well as to explore the expression characteristics of AAT in acute gout and its role in Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent macrophage pyroptosis.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine granted approval for the current study (2021-0027). Male patients aged 18 to 80 years and diagnosed with primary gout from November 2023 to January 2025 in the Rheumatology Department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine were admitted, with healthy males of the same age range, exhibiting serum urate levels < 420 µmol/L, as the control group. Patients were identified as primary gout based on the 2015 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism Gout Classification Criteria [18]. Specifically, patients who experienced gout flares within 3 days were classified as acute gout, while those who did not experience gout flares for at least 14 days were identified as patients in intermittent phase. Blood samples were taken from patients during medical visits and from healthy controls during physical examinations. PBMCs from gouty patients and healthy controls were isolated by Ficoll-gradient centrifugation, subsequently RNA was extracted from the isolated PBMCs. Serum samples were used to test the concentration of AAT. Synovial fluids were collected from 4 male patients with gouty knee arthritis and 4 male patients with osteoarthritis receiving an articular aspiration. Cells from the synovial fluids were prepared for subsequent fluorescent staining, while the supernatants of centrifuged synovial fluids were used for ELISA assays.

Cell Culture and Treatments

THP-1 cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium added with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37℃ under 5% CO2. To induce differentiation into macrophages, THP-1 cells from passages 10 to 15 were treated with 100 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h to induce plastic-adherent properties under serum-free conditions.

Preparation of MSU Crystals

1 g of uric acids (Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 200 ml of double-distilled water and heated to 80 °C until completely dissolved, then 0.5 N NaOH was titrated to adjust the pH to 8.9. After standing overnight at 4 °C, crystal precipitation was observed. The crystals were washed with double-distilled water, dried at 40 °C, then milled, suspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 20 mg/ml, and sterilized at 180 °C for 2 h for subsequent experiments. Endotoxin levels in the prepared crystals were verified by Limulus amebocyte lysate assay, demonstrating undetectable contamination (< 0.015 EU/ml). Needle-shaped or rod-shaped crystals were observed under a polarizing microscope.

Small Interfering RNA (siRNA) Transfection

SiRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma, and the sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4. About 8 × 10⁵ THP-1-derived macrophages plated in 6-well plates at a confluency of 70% to 90% were transfected with siRNA at a final concentration of 50 nmol/L using GP-transfect-Mate (GenePharma) in antibiotic-free medium for 48 h, and about 90% viable cells remained after 48 h.

Cell Cytotoxicity and Viability Assays

About 5 × 104 cells were plated in 96-well plates and treated with related reagents. Subsequently, after treatment with MSU crystals for 12 h, cell cytotoxicity was evaluated by the release of LDH (C0017, Beyotime), and cell viability was assessed by a Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0037, Beyotime), following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The optical density values were measured using a Microplate Reader (ELX-808, BioTek).

Flow Cytometry

4 × 105 cells were seeded per well in the 12-well plates and treated with related reagents. Cells were resuspended and tagged with Annexin V-FITC and PI (C1062S, Beyotime). Subsequently, cells were detected on a flow cytometer (BD Fortessa). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software, version 10 (Treestar), and PI+ cells were considered as pyroptotic cells.

ELISA

The cytokine levels in cell supernatants, and synovial fluids were measured with a human IL-1β ELISA kit (EK0392, Boster) and human SERPINA1 ELISA kit (KE00178, Proteintech) following the instructions by the manufacturer.

Elab Fluor® 488 Conjugation of AAT

Elab Fluor® 488 was conjugated with AAT according to the instructions of Elab Fluor® 488 Labeling Kit (ELK E003B, Elabscience). Briefly, Elab Fluor® 488 was incubated with AAT for 30 min, next, labeled AAT was purified by ultrafiltration.

Next Generation RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 3 control samples and 3 MSU crystal-induced samples from THP-1-derived macrophages using Trizol reagent, and 1 µg of total RNA was utilized for library preparation. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and an Illumina HiSeq/Novaseq instrument was used to complete the cDNA sequencing during the process. For RNA-Seq analysis, differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with a |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1, and FDR < 0.05. DAVID6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp) and KOBAS3.0 (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/kobas3) were used for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, respectively, with an adjusted P value < 0.05 considered significant. Bubble diagrams were generated using the ggplot2 package in R.

Real-time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Thermo Scientific) and reverse transcription is performed to convert RNA into complementary DNA (RR047A, TaKaRa). Next, the PCR reaction mixture was prepared using a SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ Kit (RR820A, TaKaRa), then quantitative PCR was conducted on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Scientific), which underwent 40 cycles for denaturation, annealling and extension. The sequences of primers (Biolegend) for the genes are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. Following denaturation, the prepared cell protein loading samples were loaded on gels, and electrophoresis was performed to separate different proteins based on their molecular weight, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were then blocked in 5% nonfat milk in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 at room temperature for 1 h. Next, primary antibodies were incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the blots were visualized by an increased chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore) and imaged with a chemiluminescent imaging system (Amersham ImageQuant 800).

Co-immunoprecipitation

To generate an antigen-antibody complex, 500 µg of cell lysate was incubated with 10 µg primary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Next, protein A/G agarose beads (IK-1004, Biolinkedin) were added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature under constant shaking. After washing, the proteins bound to the beads were then eluted into sample loading buffer, and supernatants were then separated and detected by immunoblotting as described above.

Immunoprecipitation and Mass Spectrometry

Co-immunoprecipitation was conducted by the guidelines described as above, and the antibody against Caspase 3 was used for immunoprecipitation. Next, the protein sample prepared was used for electrophoresis, and gel staining was performed with Coomassie brilliant blue staining to preliminarily determine the proteins. After washing, the gel strip stained with Coomassie brilliant blue was cut, and sent to Hangzhou Lanhe Biotechnology for detection, and LC-MS was used to detect the type of protein in the sample.

Immunofluorescence

Cells plated in confocal dishes underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Next, the cells were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min, and subsequently they were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with the corresponding primary and fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody successively, followed by re-stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma-Aldrich). Fluorescence images were captured by a laser scanning confocal microscopy (Leica STELLARIS5).

Antibodies and Reagents

Antibody against GSDME (ab215191) was purchased from Abcam. Antibodies against AAT (16382-1-AP), Caspase 3 (19677-1-AP and 66470-2-Ig), CD117 (18696-1-AP), CD68 (28058-1-AP and 66231-2-Ig), Vimentin (10366-1-AP and 60330-1-Ig), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig) were from Proteintech. Antibody against CD66b (55428) was from Signalway antibody. Antibody against LRP1 (BM4098) was from Boster. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (7074 S) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (7076 S) were from Cell Signaling Technology. Dylight 594-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (A23410) and Dylight 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (A23220) secondary antibodies were from Abbkine. Z-DEVD-FMK (HY-12466) was from MedChemExpress. AAT was from Sigma-Aldrich (A6150).

MSU Crystal-induced Acute Gouty Arthritis Model Establishment

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were utilized for experiments. 40 µl of 25 mg/ml MSU crystals suspension was injected into the right footpad, while the left footpad received a control injection of 40 µl of PBS, under intraperitoneal anesthesia with 100 µL of 1% pentobarbital sodium. The swelling index was calculated as the ratio of the thickness of the footpad injected with MSU crystals to that injected with PBS. The thickness of the footpads was measured using a vernier caliper at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection of MSU crystals, and the mice were sacrificed 24 h after the injection by cervical dislocation. For Immunofluorescence examination, footpad tissue samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently incubated with the corresponding primary and fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody successively, followed by re-stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Additionally, footpad tissue samples were homogenized with PBS, and the supernatants were collected to measure the concentration of IL-1β using a mouse IL-1β ELISA kit (EK0394, Boster), meanwhile BCA assay (P0012S, Beyotime) was employed for protein quantification.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. All experiments were conducted independently at least three times. For normally distributed variables, data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with differences assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons for comparisons involving more than two groups. For variables not normally distributed, data were reported as median (range), with differences evaluated using the Mann-Whitney test for two groups and the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparisons involving more than two groups. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was utilized to assess the correlation between two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant when the adjusted P < 0.05.

Results

GSDME Is Elevated in PBMCs and Synovial Macrophages from Patients with Acute Gout

To evaluate the expression of GSDME and Caspase 3 in patients with gout, we collected peripheral venous blood samples from 81 patients with gout (41 patients with acute gout and 40 gout patients in intermittent phase) and 42 healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). GSDME and IL1B mRNA expression in PBMCs from patients with acute gout were much higher than those from healthy controls and gout patients in intermittent phase, while Caspase 3 mRNA expression from patients with acute gout were higher than those from healthy controls (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we identified a significant positive correlation between mRNA expression of GSDME and IL1B, which may reflect the disease activity of the patients (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we focused on the two main types of synovial resident cells, synovial macrophages (SMs) and Vimentin+ fibroblast-like synoviocytes (SFs) [19]. GSDME expression was found to be higher in gout SMs (CD68+) compared to osteoarthritis (OA) SMs, while no difference was found between gout SFs (Vimentin+) and OA SFs, according to cell immunofluorescence staining of synovial fluids (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that SMs primarily accounted for the differential expression of GSDME.

MSU Crystals Induce Macrophage Pyroptosis Via GSDME Cleavage by Activated Caspase 3

We conducted transcriptome sequencing analysis using THP-1-derived macrophages, with 3 biological replicates for both the MSU crystal-stimulated and unstimulated conditions. And 1244 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, of which 727 exhibited upregulation and 517 showed downregulation (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1C. Additionally, 247 pyroptosis-related genes were extracted from GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), and the intersection with the up-regulated DEGs identified 19 pyroptosis-related DEGs associated with gout (Supplementary Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1E), with GSDME being one of the identified genes.

To investigate the changes in the expression of Caspase 3 and GSDME, THP-1-derived macrophages were treated with 200 µg/ml MSU crystals for 12 h. The results of RT-qPCR demonstrated that MSU crystals stimulation resulted in elevated mRNA levels of Caspase 3 and GSDME (Fig. 1D). Additionally, western blot analysis revealed an increased protein expression in cleaved Caspase 3 and GSDME-N (Fig. 1E). Prior studies reported that cleaved Caspase 3 induces pyroptosis by cleaving GSDME. Thus, to evaluate the role of Caspase 3, THP-1-derived macrophages were incubated with Z-DEVD-FMK for 1 h prior to stimulation with MSU crystals. And treatment with Z-DEVD-FMK significantly decreased the levels of IL-1β and LDH, and improved cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Morphologically, THP-1-derived macrophages displayed cell swelling under after treatment with MSU crystals, while the number of pyroptotic cells was decreased after treatment with Z-DEVD-FMK (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Similarly, siRNA was successfully used to knockdown the expression of Caspase 3 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). As expected, knockdown of Caspase 3 led to a significant decrease in the protein expression of cleaved Caspase 3 and GSDME-N induced by MSU crystals (Fig. 2A), and attenuated MSU crystal-induced pyroptosis (Fig. 2B). Consistently, knockdown of GSDME with siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3B) diminished the generation of GSDME-N (Fig. 2C), as well as pyroptotic cells induced by MSU crystals (Fig. 2D and E). And co-immunoprecipitation revealed that GSDME was precipitated by Caspase 3 (Fig. 2 F). These results demonstrate the activation of Caspase 3/GSDME axis in MSU crystal-induced macrophage pyroptosis.

Z-DEVD-FMK Alleviates Acute Gouty Arthritis in Mice

We further investigated the effects of Caspase 3 inhibitor Z-DEVD-FMK in the model of gouty arthritis induced by MSU crystals in mice. Z-DEVD-FMK (1.8 mg/kg) or vehicle was intraperitoneally injected 1 h before and 5 h after MSU crystals injection [20]. And the paw swelling index in mice treated with Z-DEVD-FMK was significantly lower than that in the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 3A and B). Consistent with the reduction in paw swelling, the ELISA assay indicated less IL-1β release in the Z-DEVD-FMK-treated group (Fig. 3C). Tissue immunofluorescence staining revealed that GSDME expression was higher in SMs (CD68+) of MSU group than in SMs of CTRL group, but treatment with Z-DEVD-FMK effectively decreased the expression of GSDME (Fig. 3D). Taken together, these findings suggest AAT alleviates the severity of MSU crystal-induced acute gouty arthritis.

Identification of Caspase 3 Interacting Protein AAT and its Expression Characteristics in Acute Gout

Cytologically, IP-MS identified the interaction between and Caspase 3 and AAT in MSU crystal induced macrophages (Supplementary Table 2). Given AAT is an acute phase glycoprotein closely involved in acute inflammatory diseases, we aimed to assess the expression of AAT in acute gout. We collected serum samples from 78 patients with gout (41 patients with acute gout and 37 gout patients in intermittent phase), and 47 healthy controls (Supplementary Table 3), as well as peripheral venous blood samples from 81 patients with gout (41 patients with acute gout and 40 gout patients in intermittent phase) and 42 healthy controls as mentioned before. The level of serum AAT in patients with acute gout was significantly higher than that in healthy controls and gout patients in intermittent phase (Fig. 4A), and the mRNA expression of SERPINA1 in PBMCs from patients with acute gout was significantly higher than those from healthy controls (Fig. 4B). Besides, we collected synovial fluids from 4 patients with acute gout and 4 patients with osteoarthritis, and ELISA assay indicated increased level of AAT in acute gout compared with osteoarthritis (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining of synovial fluids was employed to identify the specific type of synovial cells that serve as the primary source of AAT. The results demonstrated that AAT expression was elevated in gout SMs (CD68+) compared to OA SMs, while no difference was found between neutrophils (CD66b+), mast cells (CD117+), and SFs (Vimentin+; Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that elevated AAT in gout synovial fluids is primarily from CD68+ SMs. In vitro, similarly, the mRNA expression of SERPINA1(Fig. 4E), protein expression of AAT (Fig. 4F), and concentration of AAT in the supernatants (Fig. 4G) were found to be elevated in MSU crystal-induced THP-1-derived macrophages.

Internalized AAT Attenuates Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent Pyroptosis by Binding To Caspase 3

To detect whether AAT could be internalized, THP-1-derived macrophages were incubated with or without 1 mg/mL Elab Fluor® 488-conjugated AAT for 1 h, followed by culture with or without 200 µg/ml MSU for 12 h. As a result, Elab Fluor® 488-labeled AAT internalization was observed under fluorescence microscopy, and the internalization was obviously increased in macrophages stimulated with MSU crystals than those without (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, it was showed that treatment with AAT ameliorated the release of IL-1β and LDH, improved cell viability, and reduced the number of pyroptotic cells induced by MSU crystals (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 4). Mechanistically, AAT suppressed the protein expression of cleaved Caspase 3 and GSDME-N (Fig. 5C). To further investigate whether internalized AAT directly interacts with Caspase 3, THP-1-derived macrophages were pretreated with AAT for 1 h before stimulation with MSU crystals. Co-immunoprecipitation indicated an interaction between AAT and Caspase 3 (Fig. 5D and E). Moreover, co-immunoprecipitation showed that pretreatment with AAT resulted in reduced binding of GSDME with Caspase 3 in MSU crystal-induced macrophages, providing further evidence for the interaction between AAT and Caspase 3 (Fig. 5F). The above results confirm that internalized AAT attenuates Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis by binding to Caspase 3.

To investigate the function of AAT in vivo, mice were injected with 20 µl of 10 mg/ml AAT or 10 mg/ml BSA into the right footpad 1 h prior to MSU crystals injection into the right footpad [21–25]. As a result, we observed a lower paw swelling index (Fig. 5G and H). Consistently, ELISA assay indicated a decrease in local IL-1β production from footpad tissue extracts (Fig. 5I). Tissue immunofluorescence staining indicated treatment with AAT effectively decreased the expression of GSDME (Fig. 5J). Taken together, these findings suggest AAT alleviates the severity of MSU crystal-induced acute gouty arthritis.

LRP1 Binds To AAT, and Is Responsible for its Internalization into MSU crystal-induced Macrophages

LRP1, identified as the SERPIN-enzyme complex receptor, has the capacity to recognize AAT for subsequent endocytosis and degradation [14, 26]. However, the involvement of LRP1 in the internalization of AAT into MSU-induced macrophages has not been reported. Clinical evidence showed elevated mRNA expression of LRP1 in patients with acute gout compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 5 A) prompting our investigation into the potential mechanism. To elucidate the role of LRP1 in the internalization of AAT, we effectively employed siRNA to knockdown LRP1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 3 C). After knocking down of LRP1, a significant decrease in fluorescence intensity of Elab Fluor® 488-conjugated AAT was detected in MSU crystal-induced THP-1-derived macrophages (Fig. 6 A and B). Next, THP-1-derived macrophages were pretreated with AAT, followed by stimulation with MSU crystals, and the co-immunoprecipitation assay using an anti-AAT antibody revealed that AAT was pulled down in association with LRP1 (Fig. 6 C). Furthermore, after LRP1 knockdown, the protective effect of AAT against MSU crystal-induced pyroptosis (release of IL-1β, LDH and cell viability) was significantly diminished (Fig. 6D). However, no significant difference was observed in mRNA expression of LRP1 after MSU stimulation in THP-1-derived macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 5 B). These findings indicate that LRP1 is responsible for the internalization of AAT into MSU crystal-induced macrophages. Discussion

Interestingly, acute gout flares often resolve spontaneously within 7 to 10 days [1]. And the known mechanism behind involves the polarization of macrophages into M2-type macrophages, an increased release of anti-inflammatory cytokines [27], and the formation of aggregated neutrophil extracellular traps that sequester pro-inflammatory [28]. Whereas, further research on spontaneous resolution in gouty arthritis remains limited. Pyroptosis of macrophages plays a role in the in initiation and progression of inflammation in acute gout [10]. Current research on gout-related pyroptosis mainly focuses on NLRP3/Caspase 1/GSDMD [29, 30], while pyroptosis mediated by other Gasdermins has not been reported. In our study, we investigated whether elevated AAT could inhibit Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis stimulated by MSU crystals.

Recent studies have shown that activated Caspase 3 cleaves GSDME to execute pyroptosis, and GSDME-dependent pyroptosis has been proved to be involved in atherosclerosis [9], rheumatoid arthritis [31], chemotherapy drugs induced pyroptosis [32, 33] and so on. In GSDME-negative cells, apoptosis is triggered by Caspase 3, whereas pyroptosis is initiated in GSDME-high cells. Consequently, the conversion between apoptosis and pyroptosis is regulated by the abundance of GSDME [32]. Since the study by Rashidi et.al [10] demonstrated that GSDMD is dispensable for IL-1β release in macrophages stimulated by MSU crystals. This promoted us to investigate whether any other Gasdermin protein is involved in MSU crystal-induced pyroptosis. First, clinical evidence showed increased expression of GSDME in PBMCs and CD68+ synovial macrophages from patients with acute gout. Next, transcriptome sequencing results revealed significantly elevated expression of GSDME in MSU crystal-induced macrophages. Subsequently, in vitro experiments verified the elevated protein expression of cleaved Caspase 3 and GSDME-N in MSU crystal-induced macrophages, and knockdown of Caspase 3 reversed the protein expression of GSDME-N and MSU crystal-induced pyroptosis. Furthermore, it was proved that the cleavage of GSDME was induced through its binding to Caspase 3. Meanwhile, Zhao et al. [34] proposed a novel two-way positive feedback regulation between the Caspase 1/GSDMD and Caspase 3/GSDME axes, discovering that cleaved Caspase 1 could influence the activation of Caspase 3 via GSDMD, while cleaved Caspase 3 enhance the activation of Caspase 1 in turn. Given that studies have demonstrated the activation of Caspase 1/GSDMD axis in MSU crystal-induced macrophages [30, 35], the association between Caspase 1/GSDMD and Caspase 3/GSDME axes warrants further investigation in future studies.

Meanwhile, mRNA expression of SERPINA1 gene in PBMCs, and levels of AAT in serum and synovial fluids were significantly elevated in patients with acute gout. And AAT was primarily found to originate from CD68+ macrophages in synovial fluids from patients with acute gout. Consistent with clinical evidence, increased expression of AAT was observed in MSU crystal-induced macrophages in vitro. Notably, AAT is known to suppress macrophage activation [36, 37]. However, it is still unclear whether AAT functions intracellularly in MSU crystal-induced macrophages. As expected, we found that AAT reduced the release of IL-1β and LDH, and promoted macrophage survival. Thus, the spontaneous increase in AAT expression and its protective effect on macrophage survival prompted us to investigate the role of AAT in the spontaneous resolution of acute gout. It was reported that AAT protects lung endothelial cells [16], islet β-cells [17, 38], cardiomyocytes [39] and astrocytes [40] from apoptosis by inhibiting Caspase 1, Caspase 3 or Caspase 9, and co-location was observed between AAT and Caspase 3 [16]. Subsequently, we investigated whether a similar association exists between AAT and Caspase 3 in MSU crystal-induced macrophages, as a result, the interaction between AAT and Caspase 3 was proved, thereby inhibiting Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis. However, the mechanism by which AAT enters MSU crystal-induced macrophages remains to be elucidated. Previous studies identified LRP1 as the endocytic receptor for AAT [41]. In this study, the interaction between AAT and LRP1 was found on macrophages. Furthermore, knockdown of LRP1 blocked the internalization of AAT, and also abolished its inhibitory effect on pyroptosis.

Considering the differing mechanisms of gout in males and females, this study was intentionally limited to male participants to ensure mechanistic homogeneity. Second, since obtaining primary synovial macrophages in clinical practice presents several challenges, we conducted in vitro experiments entirely on THP-1-derived macrophages. Third, our study reflects only a limited aspect of the intracellular function of AAT, but whether AAT could function extracellularly in acute gouty arthritis requires further investigation. For example, AAT-deficient macrophages exhibit impaired efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils [42]. Whereas AAT supplementation enhances phagocytosis and efferocytosis [43], as well as inhibits the activity and production of the neutrophil chemotactic factor leukotriene B4 [44]. Besides, effects of AAT on phagocytosis and efferocytosis in alveolar macrophages are time-dependent [43], and the mechanisms of its time-dependent effects requiring further clarification. On the other hand, elevated AAT has been implicated in gout flares that occur during urate-lowering therapy by inhibiting neutrophil elastase, which leads to release of inflammatory cytokines [45]. Thus, a comprehensive and systematic exploration of AAT is warranted to determine its potential application in acute gout. At last, it is important to note that there are various macrophage subpopulations in synovial tissue, exhibiting functional heterogeneity [46, 47]. Therefore, further exploration of specific macrophage subpopulations in patients with acute gout is necessary. However, our study demonstrates elevated LRP1 expression of acute gout patients and demonstrates its role in AAT internalization, however, the specific mechanism by which the inflammatory microenvironment modulates LRP1 expression and AAT internalization in synovial tissue remains unclear. Potential regulatory factors including changes in pH, oxidative stress, or cytokine profiles require further exploration to clarify the specific role in this process.

Conclusion

In summary, as illustrated in Fig. 7, our study demonstrated for the first time the activation of Caspase 3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in macrophages from patients with acute gout. Besides, the results suggest that the anti-pyroptotic effects of AAT may be direct, intracellular, and mediated by its binding to Caspase 3, which may partially explain the underlying mechanisms involved in the spontaneous resolution of acute gout. Furthermore, AAT binds to LRP1 on macrophages to induce internalization. Therefore, targeting AAT and Caspase 3/GSDME axis may be a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with acute gout.
