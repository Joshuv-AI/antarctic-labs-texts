A low-protein diet supplemented with an α-ketoacid-diet modulates intestinal flora and urinary metabolism in diabetic nephropathy mice
Authors: Zhonghan Chen, Xiaoqian Jin, Xiaoyan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESDiabetic nephropathy (DN) is one of the most frequent and serious complications of diabetes mellitus. A low-protein diet supplemented with an α-ketoacid diet (LPD-KA) is a crucial intervention for individuals with chronic kidney disease. Nevertheless, the effects and mechanism of LPD-KA in DN remain unclear.MATERIALS/METHODSDb/db mice were randomly assigned into three groups: normal protein diet (NPD), low-protein diet (LPD), and LPD-KA groups, with db/m mice fed with NPD as the Control (Cont) group. PAS and Masson staining were performed after detecting the weight, blood glucose, and renal biochemical indicators. 16S rRNA sequencing and Urine metabolomics were conducted.RESULTSLPD-KA for DN mice improved the renal tissue damage and fibrosis, with lower body and kidney weights, blood glucose, urinary glucose, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, urinary albumin/creatinine ratio, 24-h urine microalbumin, and glycosylated hemoglobin A1c levels. Compared to the NPD group, LPD-KA increased relative abundance of Firmicutes/Bacteroides, f_Lachnospiraceae, g_Akkermansia, g_Clostridium, g_Staphylococcus, g_Enterococcus, and f_Enterococcaceae. Moreover, LPD-KA altered urinary metabolomics, including up-regulation of nephroprotective differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) L-histidinol and gluconolactone, and down-regulation of nephrotoxic DEMs 2-ketobutyric acid, dihydrocortisol, citramalic acid, and L-erythrulose, which were enriched in the protein digestion and absorption and tyrosine metabolism pathway. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed strong correlation between the crucial genus flora (Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Adlercreutzia, Rikenella, and Clostridium) and the above metabolites.CONCLUSIONLPD-KA modulated the intestinal flora and urinary metabolism to attenuate DN progression, with Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, L-histidinol, gluconolactone, 2-ketobutyric acid, and dihydrocortisol as potential biomarkers, providing new insights into the clinical application of LPD-KA for DN treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is one of the most common and serious complications of diabetes mellitus, affecting approximately 40% of individuals diagnosed with the condition [12]. Currently, the global prevalence of diabetes is 564 million, with 783 million expected by the year 2045 [3]. The overall prevalence of DN in Chinese diabetic patients ranges from 29.6% to 49.6% [4]. Despite the ongoing advances in the treatment modalities for DN, pharmacological options remain limited and focus on symptomatic management, including the regulation of hyperglycaemia, hypertension, and proteinuria [5]. Therefore, the development of more effective therapeutic strategies is imperative.

The primary clinical characteristics of DN include persistent proteinuria, elevated creatinine levels, and an abnormal glomerular filtration rate [6]. In DN, thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, accumulation of matrix in the tunica and tubular interstitium, and podocyte dysfunction or depletion contribute to impaired renal function [7]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of DN remain unclear. A recent study has highlighted the significant role of intestinal flora in the progression of DN [8]. In DN mice with severe proteinuria, Allobaculum and Anaerosporobacter led to a deterioration of the renal function, and Blautia was negatively correlated with the 24-h urinary protein content, with a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio [9]. Inulin-type fructans led to the enrichment of Akkermansia and Candidatus Saccharimonas, attenuating glomerular injury and renal fibrosis to improve DN [10]. Thus, the intestinal flora plays a role in the pathogenesis of DN. Moreover, as an evolving histological technique, metabolomics is a crucial tool for the early diagnosis and determining the prognosis of DN [11]. Tang et al. [12] clarified the potential mechanism of Huang-Lian-Jie-Du Decoction for DN based on metabolomics and identified 22 potential biomarkers in the urine of DN mice that were involved in phenylalanine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, glucose metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism. In particular, uracil, sphingosine 1-phosphate, and 4-acetamidobutyric acid have been identified as promising biomarkers for the early diagnosis of DN [13]. Therefore, metabolomics provides valuable insights for probing biomarkers targeting DN therapeutic interventions.

Dietary interventions play a pivotal role in the progression of DN, with low-sodium and low-protein diets (LPDs) being the prevailing strategies for managing DN development [1415]. A meta-analysis showed that a LPD for DN patients caused a significant decrease in urinary urea and glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels [16]. In addition, ketoanalogue supplements were found to prolong survival in patients with pre-dialysis advanced DN [17]. Compared to the LPD group, diabetic rats intervened with LPD and keto amino acids exhibited a suppressed inflammatory response, decelerating the progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [18]. Thus, dietary interventions with LPD and ketoacids may show a renoprotective effect to improve DN. A significant reduction in the abundance of the butyrate-producing bacteria family Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidaceae was observed in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) receiving LPD [19]. In CKD rats, the α-ketoacid treatment resulted in an increase in the abundance of Akkermansia, Blautia, Methanobrevibacter, and Anaerositipes, with lower serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and 24-h urinary protein levels, accompanied by improvements in tubular atrophy, glomerulosclerosis, and interstitial fibrosis [20]. At the same time, untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed a decrease in cholesterol, choline, indole sulfate, and betaine levels [20]. Therefore, dietary intervention with LPD and ketoacids may decelerate the progression of DN by modulating the gut flora and metabolic profiling.

In the present study, different feeding methods were applied to db/db mice to determine the role and potential mechanisms of a low-protein diet supplemented with an α-ketoacid diet (LPD-KA) on the gut flora and urinary metabolism, providing a theoretical basis for the clinical application of LPD and ketoacid dietary intervention in the treatment of DN, and exploring the potential biomarkers for DN therapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Eighteen specific pathogen-free grade male db/db mice and six db/m mice (seven weeks old, GemPharmatech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) were bred by Zhejiang Eyong Pharmaceutical Research and Development Center (No. SYXK (Zhe) 2023-0027) in the environment with a constant temperature of 22 ± 2°C, 50–60% relative humidity, and a light-dark cycle every 12 h.

Experimental design

After achieving the blood glucose measurement result that conformed to established standards, the db/db mice were randomly grouped into three groups: normal protein diet (NPD, 22% protein, Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) group, LPD (6% protein, Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd.) group, and LPD + ketoacids (LPD-KA, 5% protein and 1% α-ketoacid, Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd.) group (n = 6). The diet protocol was designed with reference to previous studies [2122]. The six db/m mice in the control group (Cont) were also given the NPD.

After a 12-week feeding period during which the blood glucose levels and body weight were measured every 4 weeks, the mice were then housed in metabolic cages. After an overnight fasting period, blood and urine samples were collected the following day for biochemical assays and metabolomics. Fecal samples were collected for 16S rRNA sequencing. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized with CO2 inhalation, followed by the collection of kidney tissues. After photographing and weighing, the kidney weight/body weight ratio was calculated as the renal index. One part of the kidney tissues was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin section preparation, and the other part was stored at −80°C.

Measurement of blood glucose

After collecting blood from the tail vein, the fasting blood glucose levels of the mice were measured using a blood glucose meter (6870279001, Roche Life Science, Basel, Switzerland) at weeks 4, 8, and 12.

Biochemical detection

The collected blood samples were left to stand for 1 h and centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 3,500 rpm. The SCr and BUN levels in the serum, as well as urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR), 24 h urinary protein, urine glucose, and HbA1c levels were measured using an automatic biochemistry analyzer (HITACHI 3110, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

PAS staining

PAS staining was intended to detect the glomerular, tubular, and interstitial changes. The paraffin sections were dewaxed by immersion in xylene, followed by immersion in a graded series of ethanol. Staining was conducted using a PAS staining kit (C0142S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The sections were oxidized with 0.5% aqueous periodic acid for 5 min, followed by a 5-min rinse. Subsequently, Schiff reagent was added for 30 min, and the nuclei were stained lightly using a Harris hematoxylin staining solution for 1 min. After dehydration by immersion in a graded series of ethanol, the xylene transparency, neutral gum sealing, and microscopic observation were performed. The PAS-positive material, thylakoid matrix, basement membrane, and glycogen were reddish-purple, while the nuclei were stained blue.

Masson staining

The degree of renal tissue fibrosis was assessed by Masson staining. The paraffin sections underwent a dewaxing process through immersion in xylene, followed by gradient ethanol immersion. Hematoxylin (G1142, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was stained for 10 min, followed by differentiation in a 1% hydrochloric acid–alcohol solution. Subsequently, the sections were stained for 6 min with an acidic complex red-richmond red (71019360, 71033761, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), followed by a 3-min differentiation in phosphomolybdic acid (XW514297441, Wokai, Beijing, China), and staining with aniline blue (71003644, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) for 5 min. During the process, 0.2% acetic acid water (10000208, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) was added for washing. The sections were subjected to dehydration through a graded series of ethanol, followed by transparency, sealing, and observation under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci-L, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The nuclei were blue–black, and the collagen fibers and mucus were blue. The myofibers, fibrils, and erythrocytes were orange–red.

16S rRNA sequencing

The genomic DNA was extracted from the collected mouse fecal samples according to the instructions of the DNA extraction kit (116540600, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The DNA concentration and purity were determined using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 regions of bacterial 16S rRNA were amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). After purifying and recovering the PCR products using VAHTSTM DNA clean beads (N411-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), quantification was performed using a Quant-iTTM PicoGreenTM dsDNA Assay Kit (P7589, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Sequences with more than 97% similarity were assigned to the same operational taxonomy unit (OTU). The alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, Observed species, Simpson and Shannon indexes, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith_PD), Pielou’s Evenness, and Good’s coverage. The beta diversity was assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed to explore the differential flora among groups (LDA > 4.0, P < 0.05).

Metabolomics

A 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 ppm) solution was prepared in 80% methanol–water, added to the collected urine samples at a 1:1 ratio and vortexed for 1 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) detection.

Chromatography conditions: The LC detection was conducted on a Vanquish UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters) at a 0.3 mL/min flow rate, 2 μL injection volume, and 40°C column temperature. In positive ion mode, the mobile phases consisted of (B2) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) and (A2) 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v). The gradient elution procedure was shown as follows: 0–1 min, 8% B2; 1–8 min, 8–98% B2; 8–10 min, 98% B2; 10-10.1 min, 98–8% B2; 10.1–12 min, 8% B2. In negative ion mode, the mobile phases consisted of (B3) acetonitrile and (A3) ammonium formate (5 mM). The separation procedure was shown as follows: 0–1 min, 8% B3; 1–8 min, 8–98% B3; 8–10 min, 98% B3; 10–10.1 min, 98–8% B3; 10.1–12 min, 8% B3.

Mass spectrometry conditions: MS detection was conducted on Q Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an ESI ion source. The parameters were shown as follows: 40 arb of sheath gas pressure, 10 arb of aux gas flow, 3.50 kV and −2.50 kV of spray voltage in the positive and negative ion modes, 325°C of capillary temperature, MS1 range of m/z 100–1,000, and 30 eV normalized collision energy.

The metabolites were identified by the accurate mass and MS/MS data, which were matched with the HMDB, massbank, KEGG, LipidMaps, mzcloud, and the metabolite database built by Panomix Biomedical Tech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The molecular weights of the metabolites were determined based on the m/z (mass-to-charge ratio) of the parent ions in MS data. The molecular formula was predicted by ppm (parts per million) and adduct ion, and then matched with the database to realize the MS identification of metabolites. The MS/MS data from the MS/MS quantification table were matched with the fragment ions and other information of each metabolite in the database to facilitate the MS/MS identification of metabolites. Metabolites with variable importance in projection > 1, log2(FC) > 2, P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs). Dynamic exclusion was used to remove the unnecessary MS/MS information.

Relevance analysis

Spearman’s correlation analyses were performed to evaluate the relevant association with the differential gut microbiota and DEMs.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) were used for data analysis. All statistics were reported as the mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed variance chi-squared data analysis, with a Tukey test for further two-by-two comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference.

Ethical approval

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Eyong Pharmaceutical Research and Development Center (Certificate No. SYXK (Zhe) 2023-0027).

RESULTS

LPD-KA attenuated hyperglycemia and renal function in db/db mice

The body weights (Fig. 1A) and fasting blood glucose levels (Fig. 1B) measured at every 4 weeks showed that the body weight and blood glucose levels of mice in the NPD group at weeks 4, 8, and 12 were higher than those of the Cont group (P < 0.01). Compared to the NPD group, the LPD group had a lower body weight at week 12 (P < 0.05) and lower blood glucose levels at weeks 8 and 12 (P < 0.01). Similarly, the LPD-KA group showed a lower body weight and blood glucose levels at weeks 8 and 12 compared to the NPD group (P < 0.01).

The biochemical indicators related to renal function were measured, as shown in Fig. 1C-H. The serum SCr and BUN contents, as well as the levels of UACR, 24-h urine microalbumin, urine glucose, and HbA1c in the urine of mice of the NPD group were higher than those of the Cont group (P < 0.01). Moreover, the LPD and LPD-KA treatment led to a decrease in the above indicators (P < 0.01).

LPD-KA alleviated renal hypertrophy and histopathological damage in db/db mice

As shown in Fig. 2A-C, the kidney weight was higher in the NPD group than that of the Cont group (P < 0.01), which was reduced by further LPD and LPD-KA treatment (P < 0.01 or P < 0.05). Despite the observed changes in kidney weight, the renal index showed no significant variation. Subsequently, PAS staining was carried out (Fig. 2D). The renal tissue structure was basically normal in the Cont group, whereas the NPD group had hyperplasia of the membranous stroma of renal tissue and thickening of the glomerular basement membrane. Compared to the NPD group, the LPD and LPD-KA groups exhibited alleviated renal tissue membrane matrix hyperplasia and glomerular basement membrane thickening. Furthermore, renal tissue fibrosis was detected by Masson staining (Fig. 2E). In contrast to the predominantly normal renal tissues observed in the Cont group, the glomeruli of the NPD group exhibited a large number of blue deposits and severe renal tissue fibrosis. In contrast, the LPD and LPD-KA groups exhibited a decrease in the blue deposits within the glomeruli and a reduction in the extent of renal tissue fibrosis.

LPD-KA restored the intestinal flora homeostasis in db/db mice

The collected fecal samples were analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing to assess the changes in the flora following the various interventions. The Venn diagram (Fig. 3A) showed that 217 OTUs were shared among the four groups. In Fig. 3B, principal component analysis (PCA) based on the OTU level also revealed distinct differences in the gut microbiota composition across the four groups. The α-diversity analysis results (Fig. 3C) suggested that although the abundance (as measured by Chao1 and Observed species) and diversity (assessed through Shannon and Simpson indices) of the gut flora in the LPD-KA group were similar to those in the LPD group, both of the two groups showed a decreasing trend. The changes in the α-diversity within the LPD group compared to the NPD group were consistent with these findings. The β-diversity was detected using PCoA (Fig. 3D). An altered diet resulted in significant segregation of β-diversity in the gut microbiota.

Fig. 4A presented histograms of the flora composition at the phylum level in the four groups. Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Verrucomicrobia, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria were the dominant phyla in the four groups. Correlation analysis of differential flora and DEMs in db/db mice

The correlations with the differential flora and DEMs were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. The correlation analysis of differential flora and the top 50 DEMs between the LPD-KA and NPD groups (Fig. 7A) showed that Akkermansia and Lactobacillus were negatively correlated with 6-beta-hydroxytestosterone and positively correlated with 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, 5-hydroxyindoleacetylglycine, and 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine. In particular, Rikenella was negatively correlated with 4-acetamido-2-aminobutanoic acid, 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine, and 2-hydroxycinnamic acid. Supplementary Fig. 1 presented the analysis results of differential flora and DEMs among the Cont, NPD, and LPD-KA groups. The nephroprotective metabolites epsilon-caprolactone, L-histidinol, and gluconolactone were positively correlated with Lactobacillus and Adlercreutzia, and epsilon-caprolactone was negatively correlated with Clostridium. In addition, Lactobacillus was negatively correlated with the nephrotoxic metabolites styrene oxide, octadecanamide, and dihydrocortisol. A comparison of the LPD-KA group with the LPD group (Fig. 7B) revealed Akkermansia and Desulfovibrio to be positively correlated with (R)-4-hydroxymandelate, o-cresol, gluconolactone, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, and nicotinuric acid, as well as negatively correlated with Carbimazole, Uric acid, and gallate. In addition, the correlation of Parabacteroides was almost the exact opposite of the results of Akkermansia.

DISCUSSION

DN is the leading cause of CKD and ESRD. Most DN patients die of cardiovascular disease before reaching ESRD, placing a global heavy economic pressure and social burden [23]. With the ongoing advances in research methodologies and the proposed “gut-kidney axis” theory, an increasing number of studies have focused on modulating the intestinal flora and metabolism in DN [24]. In this study, dietary intervention with different protein contents was provided for db/m and db/db mice to reveal the inhibitory effects of LPD-KA on the body weight, kidney weight, renal tissue damage, and the levels of SCr, BUN, 24-h urine microalbumin, UACR, urinary glucose, and HbA1c. Meanwhile, our study elucidated the role of LPD-KA in modulating the intestinal flora and urinary metabolomics of DN.

More than 90% of the human intestinal flora consists of Firmicutes and Bacteroides [25]. The Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio is closely related to diabetes and its complications, as well as obesity, and serves as a marker of disturbed intestinal flora [26]. In early kidney injury, the db/db mice showed a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio, decreased relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136, Lachnoclostridium, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae_UCG-004, and Roseburia, and elevated relative abundance of Bacteroidales_S24-7 [25]. In addition, San-Huang-Yi-Shen capsule treatment caused an improvement of the renal function in DN rats by modulating the intestinal flora, as evidenced by a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio and higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus [27]. Consistent with the above findings, in the present study, LPD-KA reduced the Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio compared to the NPD and LPD groups, and increased the family Lachnospiraceae, genus Christensenella, Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, and Clostridium, which belonged to Firmicutes. Moreover, a combination of Astragalus membranaceus and Salvia miltiorrhiza improved DN via the “gut-kidney axis,” with an increase in the relative abundance of Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, which were closely associated with sphingolipid metabolism, galactose metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism [28]. Correlation analysis showed that Akkermansia and Lactobacillus were positively correlated with phytosphingosine, galactaric acid, and dicumarol. Lai et al. [29] reported that LPD led to enhanced relative abundance of Akkermansiaceae in CKD patients. Furthermore, the Jin-Gui-Ren-Qi pill up-regulated carbohydrate metabolism in the intestinal flora [30]. Li et al. [31] used a rich-polyphenols extract of Dendrobium loddigesii to improve diabetes and complication symptoms of db/db mice and their KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differential flora were mainly enriched in carbohydrate metabolism (10%), amino acid metabolism (9%), metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (4%), and lipid metabolism (3%), which concurred with our results. Therefore, LPD-KA may mitigate the development of DN by inducing Akkermansia and Lactobacillus and affecting urinary metabolism, including carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism.

Urine biomarkers are used widely to investigate the progression of DN and explore more effective treatment strategies [32]. L-histidinol, a natural amino acid alcohol, is an intermediate in the biosynthesis of L-histidine [33]. KEGG analysis showed that L-histidinol was enriched in the histidine metabolism pathway. The CKD patients showed reduced circulating histidine concentrations [34]. In the streptozotocin/nicotinamide-induced type 2 diabetic rats, the histidine treatment exhibited the ameliorative effects of oxidative stress, inflammation, and renal histological alterations [35]. Gluconolactone is involved in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [36], and KEGG analysis also showed that gluconolactone was enriched in the PPP. Reduced gluconolactone levels caused by free Nε-(carboxymethyl) lysine have been considered to be a possible risk factor for the development of diabetes [36]. In the present study, LPD-KA enhanced the decrease in the levels of L-histidinol and gluconolactone due to NPD, which were considered nephroprotective metabolites. Specifically, gluconolactone alleviated skin lesions in systemic lupus erythematosus by restoring the Th17/Treg immune imbalance, primarily by suppressing Th17 differentiation and enhancing Treg differentiation [37]. More importantly, the imbalance between the Th17 and Treg cell subsets contributed to the pathogenesis of DN [38]. Therefore, it was hypothesised that gluconolactone may decelerate the progression of DN by modulating the immune response, providing new ideas for follow-up studies.

In patients with stage 5 CKD who were not dependent on dialysis, the 2-ketobutyric acid levels increased significantly in the presence of inflammation [39], consistent with the results of elevated 2-ketobutyric acid levels due to NPD in the present study. The 2-ketobutyric acid is involved in the metabolism of various amino acids, as well as in the glucose oxidation process [40]. In a cohort of obese patients who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, the 2-ketobutyric acid concentrations decreased at 1, 3, and 6 mon postoperatively[40]. This reduction was strongly associated with weight loss, improvements in insulin resistance, and a decrease in liver fat content [40]. In the present study, 2-ketobutyric acid was enriched in the KEGG pathways, including glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism; valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis; and cysteine and methionine metabolism. Amino acid metabolism plays an important role in the repair of renal function in DN [41]. Furthermore, 2-ketobutyric acid is an α-keto acid, which can be converted to succinyl coenzyme A and enter the TCA cycle [42]. Up-regulation of 2-ketobutyric acid promotes glucose oxidation and mitochondrial respiration [43]. Hasegawa and Inagi [44] pointed out that reducing the accumulation of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and glucose metabolites may ameliorate the dysregulation in renal energy metabolism in DN. These results also showed that LPD-KA caused lower 2-ketobutyric acid compared to the NPD group. According to the above findings, 2-ketobutyric acid, reduced by LPD-KA, may alleviate the progression of DN by regulating metabolic reprogramming. In addition, dihydrocortisol is a steroid hormone and belongs to the group of urinary steroid metabolites [45]. The body of DN patients produces excessive amounts of steroid hormones, such as aldosterone and cortisol, to activate the mineralocorticoid receptor in the kidney, leading to inflammation and renal impairment [46]. Therefore, steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) are considered a promising therapeutic option for DN because they have the potential to reduce the albuminuria, fibrosis, and inflammatory responses [47]. Furthermore, nonsteroidal MRAs are also novel agents for DN treatment, albeit at various stages of clinical development [48]. In agreement with the above results, the LPD-KA treatment reduced the increase in dihydrocortisol caused by the NPD group in the present study. Nevertheless, further study will be needed to elucidate the detailed mechanisms underlying these nephroprotective and nephrotoxic metabolites.

This study had several limitations. The present study focused primarily on the in vivo experiments to modulate the intestinal flora and urinary metabolism by LPD-KA in mice with DN and verify the ability of using LPD-KA to ameliorate DN. Nevertheless, this study did not apply the fecal microbiota transplantation or probiotic intervention experiments, which could have provided more definitive evidence regarding whether these microbial alterations constitute the precise mechanism through which LPD-KA exerts its beneficial effects against DN progression. Hence, further validation of these findings using clinical and cellular experiments is warranted.

In conclusion, LPD-KA exhibited significant renoprotective effects, characterized by decreased renal tissue damage and a decrease in the SCr, BUN, 24-h urine microalbumin, UACR, urinary glucose, and HbA1c levels. Moreover, LPD-KA effectively modulated the dysbiosis of the intestinal flora and alterations in urinary metabolites. Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Adlercreutzia, Rikenella, Clostridium, L-histidinol, gluconolactone, 2-ketobutyric acid, and dihydrocortisol may be the candidate biomarkers. Despite the study limitations, these findings suggest that LPD-KA has therapeutic potential for DN, offering a theoretical basis and new insights into the clinical application of LPD-KA for DN treatment, underscoring the need for further clinical investigation.
