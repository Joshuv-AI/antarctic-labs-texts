How molecular, supramolecular structure and gelatinization properties of starch influence fermentation behavior of sorghum cultivars
Authors: Yuanlong Chi, Zongchen Li, Ying Han, Pan Zhen, Qian Gao, Yunhao Lu, Yan Zeng, Junlan Mei, Zhenyu Liu, Zhenghong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fermentation behavior of sorghum influences the distilled spirits quality, yet the differences among cultivars cannot be explained by amylopectin content alone. Six cultivars were studied to investigate how starch structure relates to fermentation. Fermentation stability was associated with supramolecular structure, correlating positively with single helix content (r = 0.81, p < 0.001), crystal heterogeneity (r = 0.62, p < 0.01), and retrograded crystal stability (r = 0.64, p < 0.01), which support sustained substrate release. The average fermentation rate was associated with gelatinization properties, correlating with amylose (r = 0.58, p < 0.05), short amylopectin chains (r = 0.74, p < 0.001), and long amylopectin chains (r = −0.74, p < 0.001). Principal component analysis revealed that gelatinization properties dominated PC1 (60.2%) and supramolecular structure dominated PC2 (23.2%), separating rapid, slow, and medium fermentation types. This study links starch structure to fermentation behavior, providing a basis for selecting sorghum cultivars for liquor production.Keywords: Sorghum, Starch supramolecular structure, Gelatinization properties, Fermentation performance

Introduction

Sorghum [Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench], the world's fifth-largest cereal crop, is renowned for its exceptional drought tolerance, adaptability to poor soils, and high nutritional value (Hadebe et al., 2017; Silva et al., 2021). Beyond its role in food and feed, it is pivotal in the brewing industry, serving as the primary grain for traditional African beers (Sawadogo-Lingani et al., 2021) and as an indispensable raw material for producing distilled spirits such as Chinese baijiu (Weiss et al., 2022). In these processes, starch is the principal component of sorghum and the core carbon source for the microbial fermentation.

Based on glycosidic bond patterns, sorghum starch can be classified into amylose (linear α-1,4-linked glucan chains) and amylopectin (branched α-1,4- and α-1,6-linked glucan polymers). Fermentation performance is often assumed to correlate with the relative content of these two components (Li et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025). However, Zhang et al. (2017) documented fermentation completion times ranging from 36 to 60 h across sorghum cultivars with nearly identical amylose contents (20.01%–23.76%), and Ma et al. (2025) observed higher starch utilization in high-amylopectin cultivars while Li et al. (2024) reported contradictory findings. These observations indicate that the amylose or amylopectin content of sorghum grain alone cannot adequately explain the differences in fermentation behavior. Starch is characterized by a complex multi-scale structure spanning from molecular structure, through helical arrangements and crystalline domains, to lamellar organization and overall granular morphology (Bertoft, 2017). Zong et al. (2022) revealed that during multi-cycle sorghum fermentation, starch granules progressively transformed into uniform flake-like structures with crystalline polymorphism, transitioning from A-type to more thermally stable B-type, while exhibiting significant reductions in both starch content and bioavailability in fermentation residues. Li et al. (2024) has reported that fermentation degraded high-molecular-weight starch components and increased low-molecular-weight fractions, with amylose showing higher utilization efficiency and faster ethanol conversion rates. During fermentation, multi-scale structural transformations in starch, including changes in molecular structure, crystallinity, and granule morphology, critically influence fermentation behavior.

In liquor production, sorghum is cooked prior to fermentation, inducing starch gelatinization and partial solubilization of low molecular weight fractions (Yang et al., 2025). Prior to Duqu fermentation inoculation, sorghum undergoes cooling, during which starch retrogradation occurs in the grains. Subsequent microbial and enzymatic hydrolysis by Duqu convert starch into ethanol and flavor compounds (Zhu et al., 2025). The cooking process fundamentally shapes the fermentation behavior by modulating the physicochemical state of starch. Although starch multi-scale structure-gelatinization-digestibility relationships are well-established for cereals like rice and wheat (Bertoft et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2019), these relationships may not be directly applicable in explaining the behavior of sorghum in actual brewing systems. Because sorghum starch exists in a relatively complex form within sorghum grains, and its brewing involves the combined action of various enzymes and microorganisms. In this situation, the in vitro starch digestibility may not be considered equivalent to sorghum fermentation performance. E. Li et al. (2021) has reported that starch leaching during sorghum steaming is primarily governed by crystalline rather than molecular structure. Yang et al. (2025) further revealed that high protein content, large psize, and high amylose content can inhibit starch swelling and leaching by forming a cross-linked three-dimensional structure, while higher crystallinity and gelatinization enthalpy enhance sorghum starch swelling capacity and promote starch leaching. However, these studies have primarily focused on the impact of starch supramolecular structures and gelatinization properties on cooking properties of sorghum. Studies on how starch structure influences fermentation behavior of sorghum cultivars remain limited, especially in comparisons between japonica (non-waxy) and glutinous (waxy) sorghum.

Based on the differences in fermentation behaviors of sorghum cultivars and structure-function relationship of starch, we hypothesized that the supramolecular structure and gelatinization properties of starch influence the fermentation behavior of sorghum. To test this hypothesis, we systematically investigated the multi-scale structure of starch from four glutinous and two japonica sorghum cultivars specifically used for liquor production. The starch gelatinization, retrogradation properties, and fermentation behavior of the sorghum cultivars were also evaluated. Subsequent correlation analyses elucidated how the supramolecular structure and gelatinization properties of sorghum starch influence the fermentation behavior of sorghum cultivars. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to classify the cultivars based on these multidimensional features. These findings provide insights into the relationship between starch supramolecular structure and fermentation behavior, providing a theoretical basis for the selection of sorghum cultivars tailored for liquor production.

Materials and methods

Materials

Four glutinous (HYZ, LNH, GJH, and QKY) and japonica (JZ and FZ) sorghum cultivars were used. All six cultivars were harvested in each and every one of the three growing seasons, providing three independent batches per cultivar. There are natural differences in temperature, rainfall and sunshine hours in these three years. The interannual climate fluctuations in the crop growing season will affect the field hydrothermal, light conditions and crop growth (Iizumi & Ramankutty, 2016). The interannual variation of field conditions formed in different years can reflect the variation of sorghum starch caused by the difference of environmental changes in the harvest area to a certain extent. Therefore, the three batches were considered as independent biological replicates rather than technical replicates. All subsequent analyses were performed separately on each batch, and each batch was measured in triplicate technical replicates. The mean value of the three technical replicates was used as the representative result for that batch. The sorghum seeds were stored at 4 °C before analysis. Unless stated otherwise, all chemicals were analytical grade and used without further purification.

Total starch and apparent amylopectin content

According to the China Association for Standardization Group standard T/QAS 076–2022. Briefly, the sorghum grains were crushed and sieved through a 100-mesh screen. The powder (500.00 mg) was weighed into a 150 mL conical flask. The sample was first wetted with 2.5 mL of absolute ethanol for dispersion, followed by addition of 10 mL NaOH solution (1 M). The mixture was vortexed for 1 min, ultrasonicated for 30 s, and boiled for 40 min to completely dissolve the starch. After cooling, the solution was diluted to 100 mL and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 3 min.

For analysis, 1 mL of supernatant was taken (0.75 mL for japonica sorghum), mixed with 15 mL water, and adjusted to pH 3–4 using 1 M HCl. Then 0.75 mL iodine reagent (0.02 g/mL KI + 0.002 g/mL I2) was added, and the volume was adjusted to 25 mL. After storage in the dark for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 596, 476, 534, and 750 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-6000PC, Yuanxi Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The apparent amylose and amylopectin content were calculated based on standard curves, with the sum representing the total starch content. The measurement and reference wavelengths for amylopectin and amylose determination were redefined according to the Chinese Provincial Standard DB32/T 2265–2012. Due to the limitation of the iodine binding method itself, the amylose and amylopectin content measured by this method will be affected by its own chain length, so it is necessary to explain that the content data obtained is apparent (Takeda et al. Gidley & Bulpin, 1987; Zhu et al., 2008). And results should be higher than the detection limit (apparent amylose content >0.25 g / 100 g, apparent amylopectin content >0.16 g / 100 g). The apparent amylose and amylopectin contents were determined in sorghum grains from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Sorghum starch extraction

Sorghum grains (300 g) were washed, crushed, and sieved through a 200-mesh screen. After settling, the precipitate was collected. The precipitate was mixed with 300 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, 100 mM) and 1 g of neutral protease (200,000 U/g, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and incubated overnight at 40 °C. After hydrolysis, the upper liquid was discarded and the lower precipitate was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min. The precipitate was washed three times with deionized water, followed by two ethanol washes with centrifugation. Dark-colored impurities on the surface were removed after each centrifugation. Starch was independently extracted from 18 batches. After freeze-drying, the samples were stored in a desiccator at room temperature.

Field-emission scanning Electron microscopy (FE-SEM)

Starch granules were observed using a cold-field emission high-resolution scanning electron microscope (JSM-7500F, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The starch samples were mounted on copper stubs with conductive adhesive tape, sputter-coated with gold, and examined at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. Imaging was performed at magnifications of 500×and 1000× to examine granule morphology. The granular morphology was examined using FE-SEM on starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate imaging per batch.

Laser psize analysis (LPS)

The psize distribution was determined using a laser diffraction psize analyzer (HORIBA LA-960, HORIBA Scientific, Japan) with a measurement range of 0–5000 μm. The refractive indices of water and starch were set to 1.33 and 1.52. The psize distribution was determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystalline properties of starch were analyzed following the methodology described by Lopez-Rubio et al. (2008) using an Empyrean X-ray diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical, The Netherlands). Measurements were conducted using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 30 mA, scanning from 4° to 40° (2θ) with a step size of 0.03°. The diffraction patterns were processed using Jade 9.0 software (MDI, USA) to determine and calculate according to Equ.(1), (2), respectively. The crystalline properties were determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

FTIR spectral analysis was performed on samples homogenized with KBr and pressed into pellets. The spectra were processed using OMNIC 8 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with smoothing, baseline correction, and Fourier self-deconvolution. Peak absorbance values at 1047, 1022, and 995 cm−1 were recorded. The degree of order (DO) was calculated as the ratio of 1047/1022 cm−1, while the degree of double helix (DD) was determined as 995/1022 cm−1 (Warren et al., 2016). The DO and DD were determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

13C CP/MAS NMR spectroscopy

13C CP/MAS NMR analysis was performed on moisture-equilibrated samples packed in 7 mm rotors using a Bruker Avance Neo 400WB spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, USA) with the following parameters: resonance frequency of 100.625 MHz for 13C, magic angle spinning at 6 kHz, contact time 1.2 ms, relaxation delay 2.0 s, and 1000–1600 scans. Raw data were processed using MestReNova software (v14.0, Mestrelab Research S.L., Santiago de Compostela, Spain). The amorphous spectrum intensity was adjusted by subtraction to match the native starch spectrum at 84 ppm, and the ordered spectrum was obtained by subtracting the adjusted amorphous spectrum from the native spectrum. Peak fitting of the C1 region (99–105 ppm) for native, amorphous, and ordered spectra was performed using PeakFit v4 software (v4, Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, all fits achieved r2 > 0.99). The ratios of amorphous spectrum C1 peak area, ordered spectrum C1 peak area at 103 ppm, and ordered spectrum C1 peak area (excluding 103 ppm) to native spectrum C1 peak area were calculated as the amorphous, single helix, and double helix contents, respectively (Tan et al., 2007). The parameters were determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)

The chain length distribution of branched starch was analyzed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) according to the method described by Lin et al. (2016) with minor modifications. Briefly, the starch sample (5 mg) was dissolved in 0.9 mL water in a boiling water bath for 15 min. Sodium azide solution (5 mL, 40 mg/mL), acetate buffer (0.1 mL, 0.1 M, pH 3.5, prepared using 0.1 M acetic acid solution and 0.1 M sodium acetate solution), and isoamylase (10 uL, 1400 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the starch dispersion, and the mixture was incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 3 h. The debranched starch was precipitated using 5 mL absolute ethanol, centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min, and, finally, redissolved in 1 mL ultrapure DMSO/LiBr solution for 2 h at 80 °C in a thermomixer with shaking at 350 rpm.

The homogeneity and molecular weights of various fractions were measured using SEC-RI. The molecular weight distribution of debranched starch was analyzed using a differential refractive index detector (Optilab T-rEX, Wyatt Technology Co., Santa Barbara, CA, USA) equipped with two tandem columns (300 × 7.5 mm, Plael 10 um MIXED-B and Plgel 5 um MIXED-D; Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA) held at 80 °C using a model column heater. Technical support was provided by Sanshu Biotech. Co., LTD. The flow rate was 0.8 mL/min. Standard dextrans of known molecular weights (342; 3650; 21,000; 131,400; 610,500; 821,700; 3,755,000 Da; American Polymer Standards Corporation, APSC) were used for column calibration. The data were acquired and processed using ASTRA6.1 (Wyatt Technology). The chain length distribution was determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

A differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 204 F1, NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Germany) was used to measure the thermal properties of sorghum starch. Each sorghum starch sample (8.0 mg) was mixed with 13 μL of ultrapure water in an aluminum pan and left at 20 °C for 30 min. The samples were then uniformly heated from 20 °C to 100 °C at 10 °C/min to measure gelatinization thermal parameters. Following this, the gelatinized samples were stored at 4 °C for 24 h and reanalyzed using the same method to determine retrograded starch thermal parameters. The thermal properties were determined in starch from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Fermentation behavior

The fermentation behavior was determined by inoculating Alcohol Active Dry Yeast (Angel Yeast Co., Ltd., China) in sorghum liquid medium with unidirectional sealing and monitoring CO2 production (Kong et al., 2014). Crushed and sieved sorghum (50.00 g) was dispersed in 250 mL water, followed by addition of 0.180 g thermostable α-amylase (40,000 U/g, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and liquefaction in boiling water bath for 2 h. After cooling to 60 °C, 0.050 g glucoamylase (1:100,000, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added and saccharification was performed at 60 °C with shaking for 4 h. Then 100 mL of medium was transferred to a 500 mL conical flask and inoculated with appropriate amount of yeast. The flasks were sealed with a one-way valve containing 5 mL of sterile water. Static cultivation was conducted at 35 °C, and the total weight of the fermentation system was recorded every 24 h. A blank control containing 100 mL water was set up to eliminate the effect of water evaporation. Fermentation was terminated when weight loss was less than 0.05 g. The fermentation behavior was determined in sorghum grains from all 18 independent batches, with triplicate measurements per batch.

Statistical analysis

Starch extraction, structural characterization and fermentation were performed independently on each of the three seasonal batches per cultivar, with three parallel technical replicates per batch. The mean of these triplicates was used for each batch. Results of each cultivar are presented as mean ± standard deviation, calculated from the mean values of the three seasonal batches. Spearman correlation and PCA analysis were based on 18 mean values (6 cultivars × 3 batches of years). Data were statistically processed with SPSS 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Variations among groups were assessed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) supplemented by Tukey's post hoc test, considering differences statistically significant when the p-value was below 0.05. Origin 2025 (OriginLab Corporation, USA) was used to conduct principal component analysis (PCA), Spearman correlation analyses and plot each figure.

The apparent amylopectin content ranged from 73.85% (JZ) to 78.53% (FZ) in japonica cultivars and from 90.74% (HYZ) to 98.02% (QKY) in glutinous cultivars. However, as iodine binding to starch molecules is influenced by chain length (Takeda et al., 1987; Zhu et al., 2008), the measured amylopectin content should be considered apparent, and the total starch content may be affected by the chain length difference between amylose and amylopectin. Granule morphology and psize distribution

The granule morphology of sorghum starch is shown in Fig. 1, as captured by scanning electron microscopy. The starch granules exhibited polyhedral shapes, with smaller particles adhering to the surface of the larger ones. This aligns with the starch granule shapes reported by Kang et al. (2022). No significant morphological differences were observed among starch granules from different sorghum cultivars. The psize distribution data obtained by laser diffraction analysis are presented in Table 1. Among glutinous sorghum cultivars, GJH and QKY showed the largest granule sizes, while HYZ and LNH displayed smaller granules. For japonica cultivars, FZ granules were larger than those of JZ. Glutinous sorghum starch exhibited larger granule sizes than japonica sorghum. Smaller starch granules have a greater specific surface area, facilitating complete gelatinization and efficient enzymatic hydrolysis (Cheng Liang et al., 2025).

Crystalline structure

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured to compare the crystalline structures of starch from different sorghum cultivars (Fig. 2A), and the relative crystallinities of the different polymorphs were investigated (Table 2). All samples showed diffraction peaks at 2θ = 15°, 17°, 18°, and 23°, which are characteristic of A-type crystals. Additionally, a weak signal at 2θ = 20° was detected. This peak is primarily attributable to the ordered arrangement of single helices, indicating minor V-type crystallinity. Although it may also partially originate from the structure of A-type crystallites themselves, its relative contribution is minor. Therefore, the peak at 20° can serve as an effective indicator of the V-type crystalline structure present in the sample (Lopez-Rubio et al., 2008). As shown in Table 2, HYZ and QKY exhibited the highest RC values, LNH and GJH showed intermediate values, and the japonica cultivars JZ and FZ displayed the lowest RC values. Regarding V-type crystallinity, JZ and FZ had the highest RCV and RCV/RC values, HYZ showed moderate values, and GJH, QKY, and LNH showed the lowest values. A negative correlation (Fig. S3) was observed between AM and RC (r = −0.64, p < 0.01), attributable to the difficulty of amylose in forming ordered structures (Cheetham & Tao, 1998). Furthermore, AM showed positive correlations with RCV (r = 0.80, p < 0.001) and RCV/RC (r = 0.91, p < 0.001), as V-type crystals are primarily formed by amylose-lipid complexes (Wang et al., 2023).

Helical structure and short-range order

The degree of double helix (DD) and degree of order (DO) of starch were measured using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Table 2, Fig. S1). The DO values ranged from 0.840 (JZ) to 1.110 (LNH), while DD values ranged from 1.105 (JZ) to 1.360 (GJH). In general, glutinous sorghum cultivars exhibited higher DO and DD values. As double helices generally serve as the foundation for the formation of long-range ordered crystals, the discordance between the high RC and relatively low DD in HYZ starch is worthy of further investigation. Given the significant V-type crystallinity (RCv, Table 2) and the higher amylose content of HYZ compared with other glutinous sorghum cultivars (Table 1), this discordance is likely attributed to more single helices in HYZ starch, which can be formed by amylose and further contributes to V-type crystallinity. While single helices are not detected by FTIR as the degree of double helix (Warren et al., 2016), V-type crystallinity formed by single helices does contribute to the overall relative crystallinity. To further validate this hypothesis, we employed 13C CP/MAS NMR to characterize the amorphous, single helix, and double helix contents of the starch (Fig. 2B for full spectra, Fig. 2C for C1-region magnification, Table 2 for amorphous, single helix, and double helix contents, and Fig. S2 for decomposition spectra). HYZ exhibited the highest single helix content (6.79%), providing additional support for the earlier hypothesis. The exterior of single helices is hydrophilic, while the interior contains a hydrophobic cavity. This cavity can include hydrophobic guest molecules such as fatty acids and lysophospholipids to form starch-lipid complexes (Putseys et al., 2010) or it may remain empty without any guest molecules (Shi et al., 2019). When starch-lipid complexes are formed, the involvement of lipids reduces the hydrophilicity compared with that of pure starch. For instance, He et al. (2025) reported that amylose-lipid complexes create a physical barrier on the starch surface, hindering water penetration. Where the cavity does not contain guest molecules, the increased number of empty single-helical cavities is more readily available to bind with guest molecules to form complexes, thereby further decreasing hydrophilicity. Overall, an increase in single helix content is unfavorable for water absorption by starch. Correlation analysis (Fig. S3) revealed that the single helix content showed negative correlation with APC (r = −0.57, p < 0.05) and positive correlation with XAM (r = 0.70, p < 0.01). This implies that single helices are primarily formed by amylose, and the formation may require amylose having a sufficient chain length.

Molecular structure

To compare the starch molecular structures across sorghum cultivars, we analyzed their debranched chain length distributions (CLD, normalized to maximum signal intensity, Fig. 2D). The profiles were segmented into three fractions: short branched chains of amylopectin (first peak in CLD, approximately 2 < DP < 50, AP1), long branched chains of amylopectin (second peak in CLD, approximately 50 < DP < 2000 for glutinous cultivars and 50 < DP < 300 for japonica cultivars, AP2), and amylose chains (third peak in CLD, approximately DP > 2000 for glutinous cultivars and DP > 300 for japonica cultivars, AM). Compared to other similar studies, the chain length distribution of sorghum starch in this research exhibits distinct characteristics. While some studies classified DP >100 as amylose chains (Li et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024), our data showed GJH, QKY and LNH have minimal apparent amylose content despite their prominent DP >100 peaks. This suggests that these high-DP components represent the internal chains (B2/B3) of amylopectin that interconnect double helices. Due to the steric hindrance imposed by the external double helices, these internal chains are difficult to complex with iodine (Bertoft et al., 2016).

The parameters of the molecular structure of sorghum starch are presented in Table 3. Glutinous cultivars exhibited negligible amylose content (AM), with only trace amounts detected in HYZ (3.21%). Both the length and content of AP2 were higher in glutinous cultivars. Among glutinous cultivars, HYZ showed the highest AP2 content (55.10%), while LNH contained the lowest (50.16%). The AP1/AP2 ratio, which reflects the fine structure of amylopectin, displayed the same ranking across cultivars as the AP2 content. The chain length distribution exhibited unique characteristics in the native starch of the studied cultivars.

Gelatinization and retrogradation properties

Gelatinization and retrogradation thermal parameters of sorghum starch from different cultivars are presented in Table 4, with DSC thermograms shown in Fig. 3A, B. For native starch, the parameters recorded were the onset (To), peak (Tp), conclusion (Tc) temperature, temperature range (Tc - To), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH). For retrograded starch, the corresponding parameters were denoted as the onset (Tor), peak (Tpr), conclusion (Tcr) temperature of retrograded starch, temperature range of retrograded starch (Tcr - Tor), and retrogradation enthalpy (ΔHr). ΔH reflects the energy required to disrupt hydrogen bonds in starch helical structures. Tc - To indicates the melting temperature range of starch crystals, reflecting the homogeneity of crystalline stability distribution (i.e., crystalline heterogeneity). Retrograded strach exhibited decreased To, Tp, Tc, and ΔH but increased Tc - To, suggesting reduced thermal stability and enhanced heterogeneity in the recrystallized structures. Cheng Liang et al. (2025) also observed the similar phenomenon in starch of glutinous rice. GJH and QKY showed the highest ΔH and low Tc - To, indicating difficult gelatinization and low crystalline heterogeneity; HYZ and LNH indicated relatively difficult gelatinization and the highest heterogeneity; FZ and JZ exhibited the easiest gelatinization.

Fermentation behavior

To characterize fermentation behavior differences among sorghum cultivars, simulated fermentation was conducted. CO2 weight loss during fermentation is shown in Fig. 4A. During the first day of fermentation, JZ and FZ produced the highest amounts of CO₂, generating 1.94 g and 1.84 g, respectively. GJH and QKY produced intermediate levels, with 1.57 g and 1.48 g of CO₂, respectively, whereas HYZ and LNH produced the least, at 1.34 g and 1.31 g, respectively. On the second day of fermentation, CO₂ production declined across all cultivars. HYZ and LNH exhibited only slight decreases of 0.09 g and 0.02 g, respectively, whereas the other cultivars showed reductions of at least 0.47 g. HYZ and LNH demonstrated more stable fermentation profiles. The fermentation process was divided into phases: 0–1 day as the initial phase (average CO2 production rate denoted as Vi); 2–4 days as the deceleration phase (Vd), and the overall average rate as Vave. Calculated Vi, Vd, and Vave values are presented in Table S1. Based on their fermentation rates, the cultivars can be classified into three types: (1) rapid-fermentation type (JZ, FZ) showing quick initial rate increase followed by rapid decline; (2) slow-fermentation type (HYZ, LNH) exhibiting gradual initial increase and slower decline, demonstrating more stable fermentation; (3) medium-fermentation type (GJH, QKY) with intermediate characteristics. This typology is descriptive and based on observed fermentation patterns. This classification was used to help discuss the relationships between starch structural features and fermentation behavior, rather than representing a statistically validated grouping. As indicated in the introduction, amylopectin content and total starch content cannot adequately explain the differences in sorghum fermentation behavior. LNH (slow-fermentation type) exhibited similar apparent amylopectin content (APC) to cultivars belong to the medium-fermentation type. The slow-fermentation type HYZ showed lower APC, while JZ and FZ with the lowest APC were rapid-fermentation types. Meanwhile, LNH with the lowest total starch content exhibited a high average fermentation rate. Considering these starch structural variations, we propose that the starch supramolecular structure affects fermentation behavior, which will be specifically discussed in the following section. It should be acknowledged that the liquid monoculture system used here simplifies industrial solid-state fermentation. Industrial solid-state baijiu fermentation involves more complex microbial communities and additional grain components that may modulate fermentation behavior. The fermentation rates may thus not directly translate to industrial practice. Despite this simplification, the liquid monoculture system offers a controlled platform to isolate the effects of starch structure on fermentation.

Correlation and principal component analysis

Since sorghum cultivars exhibited significant differences in starch content, and total starch content showed a significant positive correlation with Vi (r = 0.98, p < 0.001), partial spearman correlation analysis with total starch content included as a control variable (Fig. 4B, C) was employed to analyze the relationship between starch supramolecular structure and fermentation behavior. Correlation analysis (Fig. 4B) revealed that Vi demonstrates a negative correlation with Tc - To (r = −0.64, p < 0.01), while Vd shows a positive correlation with Tc - To (r = 0.62, p < 0.01). This indicates that more heterogeneous crystals promote a steadier and more sustained fermentation process. This is because the ordered regions in highly heterogeneous crystals undergo sequential disordering, rather than collapsing simultaneously within a narrow temperature range, resulting in more sustained sugar release. Both HYZ and LNH exhibited high Tc - To values, which partially explained their stable fermentation characteristics. While existing research has extensively investigated how treatments like retrogradation and storage enhance crystalline heterogeneity in starch (Gu et al., 2019; C. Liang et al., 2025), limited attention has been paid to its role in enzymatic digestibility or fermentation performance. Our finding offers new perspectives for advancing this area.

Higher Vd indicates a more stable fermentation profile, which generally facilitates the formation and accumulation of flavor compounds (Hu et al., 2021). Correlation analysis (Fig. 4C) revealed that Vd showed a positive correlation with single helix content (r = 0.81, p < 0.001). This is because single helix complex can inhibit starch swelling, thereby reducing starch dissolution and decreasing enzyme accessibility. He et al. (2025) has found that single helix complex can form a physical barrier on the starch surface and inhibit water penetration. Building upon this foundation, our study further demonstrates that single helix complexes influence fermentation behavior. Vd is positively correlated with Tor (r = 0.64, p < 0.01), as shown in Fig. 4B. This occurs because retrograded starch with higher crystalline stability exhibits greater resistance to degradation This result aligns with the findings of Lv et al. (2021) using in vitro digestion models. These supramolecular structures ensure the sustained supply of substrates required for late-stage fermentation. Both HYZ and LNH exhibited high single helix content and Tor, which explained their high Vd values.

Vave is primarily determined by gelatinization properties, with sorghum cultivars exhibiting more readily gelatinized starch showing higher Vave values. This is consistent with previous findings on starch structure and in vitro digestibility (Chung et al., 2006). This also explains the positive correlations between Vave and AM (r = 0.58, p < 0.05), AP1 (r = 0.74, p < 0.001) and negative correlations between Vave and AP2 (r = −0.74, p < 0.001), D50 (r = −0.80, p < 0.001), which is shown in Fig. 4C. Amylopectin with longer internal chains typically demonstrates higher crystallinity and gelatinization enthalpy (Vamadevan et al., 2013; Witt & Gilbert, 2014), while excessive short branches and amylose reduce both crystallinity and gelatinization enthalpy (Bertoft et al., 2016; C. Li et al., 2021). Additionally, large granule size results in a low specific surface area, making it more difficult for water to penetrate the growth rings during heating and thereby inhibiting complete gelatinization. These phenomena were observed in japonica sorghum JZ and FZ, which exhibited higher average fermentation rates than glutinous cultivars. Both JZ and FZ were characterized by high AM, high AP1, low AP2, and small granule size. Among glutinous cultivars, LNH showed the lowest AP2/AP1 ratio and relatively low ΔH, explaining why it had a high average fermentation rate despite having low total starch content.

To visually demonstrate the differences in starch supramolecular structures among the discussed sorghum cultivars and their relationship with fermentation behavior, principal component analysis (PCA) was employed (Fig. 4D). The PC1 axis accounted for 60.2% of the total variance, primarily reflecting starch gelatinization properties and their influence on Vave. The PC2 axis explained 23.2% of the variance, mainly representing single helix content, crystalline heterogeneity, retrograded crystal thermal stability, and their effects on Vd. The three fermentation-type cultivars were clearly clustered into separate groups. PCA further supported the relationship between fermentation behavior and supramolecular structure: the rapid-fermentation type was characterized by easy gelatinization, while slow-fermentation type exhibited high retrogradation crystal stability, high single helix content, and high crystal heterogeneity. Medium-fermentation type was characterized by low retrogradation crystal stability, single helix content, and crystal heterogeneity but high resistance to gelatinization.

Proposed mechanisms for starch hierarchical structure governing fermentation behavior

Through a correlation analysis between the structural parameters and fermentation rates, we identified the key structural indicators that influence the fermentation behavior. To elucidate the hierarchical mechanism by which starch structure affects fermentation properties, this study further discusses these key indicators in the order of molecular structure, supramolecular structure, gelatinization properties, and fermentation behavior. We emphasize that the hierarchical model presented here is an interpretive proposal based on physicochemical plausibility and established structure-function relationships from the literature, rather than a statistically demonstrated mediation effect. The proposed causal ordering reflects a reasoned synthesis of the observed correlations and prior mechanistic knowledge.

Tₒᵣ, single helix content, and Tc-Tₒ are important indicators of fermentation stability. AP1 is positively correlated with Tₒᵣ (r = 0.49, p < 0.05), and Tₒᵣ is positively correlated with Vd (r = 0.64, p < 0.01), which is shown in Fig. S3. This indicates that external chains of amylopectin tend to form double helices (GIDLEY & BULPIN, 1987) and that high AP1 favors enhanced thermal stability of retrograded crystallites. These retrograded starches exhibit greater resistance to hydrolysis, thereby promoting stable fermentation. XAM is positively correlated with single helix content (r = 0.70, p < 0.01), whereas APC is negatively correlated with single helix content, and single helix content is positively correlated with Vd (r = 0.81, p < 0.001). This suggests that amylose with a higher content and sufficient chain length promotes single helix formation. These single helices form physical barriers that hinder starch swelling and reduce enzyme accessibility, thereby facilitating stable fermentation. XAP2 is positively correlated with Tc-To (r = 0.65, p < 0.01), and Tc-To is positively correlated with Vd (r = 0.62, p < 0.01). This indicates that longer amylopectin inner chains may promote crystalline heterogeneity (Vamadevan & Bertoft, 2018). Starch with high crystalline heterogeneity avoids the concentrated disintegration of ordered regions within a narrow temperature range, thus promoting the stable release of fermentation substrates.

The average fermentation rate is primarily governed by the gelatinization properties (gelatinization temperature and enthalpy). AM (r = −0.64, p < 0.01) and AP1 (r = −0.73, p < 0.001) are negatively correlated with RC, whereas AP2 is positively correlated with RC (r = 0.73, p < 0.001). RC is positively correlated with ΔH (r = 0.56, p < 0.05) and Tₒ (r = 0.53, p < 0.05), and both ΔH (r = −0.77, p < 0.001) and Tₒ (r = −0.75, p < 0.001) are negatively correlated with Vₐᵥₑ. These findings suggest that long branch chains enhance the flexibility of amylopectin, thereby facilitating arrangement of double helices and crystallite formation, whereas amylose and excessive short branch chains are unfavorable for crystallite formation. Higher crystallinity reinforces the gelatinization resistance. Starch with a lower degree of gelatinization is less susceptible to hydrolysis, which reduces the average fermentation rate.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the fermentation behavior of sorghum is governed by the molecular and supramolecular structure of starch rather than by amylose or amylopectin content alone. Molecular structure indirectly regulates fermentation behavior by shaping supramolecular structure. Specifically, external chains of amylopectin enhance the thermal stability of retrograded crystallites (Tₒᵣ), long amylose chains promote the formation of single helices, and internal chains of amylopectin increase crystalline heterogeneity (Tc-To). These three supramolecular structural features modulate the rate of starch release and substrate availability during the deceleration phase of fermentation, thereby influencing fermentation stability. Both AM and AP1 inhibit crystallization, whereas AP2 promotes crystalline ordering. The resulting differences in relative crystallinity further regulate gelatinization resistance (ΔH, Tₒ). Gelatinization properties affect enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency and consequently influence the average fermentation rate.

Based on these structural determinants, sorghum cultivars can be classified into three distinct fermentation types, rapid-fermentation (easy gelatinization), slow-fermentation (high retrogradation crystal stability, high single helix content, and high crystalline heterogeneity), and medium-fermentation (low values of these structural features but high gelatinization resistance).

These findings established a mechanistic framework linking starch multi-scale structure to fermentation performance, offering a theoretical basis for the selection of sorghum cultivars tailored to specific brewing applications. However, the number of cultivars is limited, and the use of three batches per cultivar may carry a potential risk of pseudoreplication in correlation analyses. Additionally, fermentation was evaluated in a monoculture liquid system, which differs from industrial solid-state baijiu fermentation. Future studies should validate these structure–fermentation relationships using a larger and more diverse set of cultivars, and investigate starch interactions with other grain components under realistic fermentation conditions.
