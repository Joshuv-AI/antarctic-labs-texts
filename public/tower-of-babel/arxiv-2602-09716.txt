Abstract
. Computing node importance in networks is a long-standing fundamental problem that has driven extensive study of various centrality measures. A particularly well - known centrality measure is betweenness centrality, which becomes computationally prohibitive on large - scale networks. Graph Neural Network (GNN) models have thus been proposed to predict node rankings according to their relative betweenness centrality. However, state - of - the - art methods fail to generalize to high - diameter graphs such as road networks. We propose BRAVA - GNN, a lightweight GNN architecture that leverages the empirically observed correlation linking betweenness centrality to degree - based quantities, in particular multi-hop degree mass. This correlation motivates the use of degree masses as size-invariant node features and synthetic training graphs that closely match the degree distributions of real networks. Furthermore, while previous work relies on scale-free synthetic graphs, we leverage the hyperbolic random graph model, which reproduces power - law exponents outside the scale - free regime, better capturing the structure of real - world graphs like road networks. This design enables BRAVA-GNN to generalize across diverse graph families while using 54 × \times fewer parameters than the most lightweight existing GNN baseline. Extensive experiments on 19 real-world networks, spanning social, web, email, and road graphs, show that BRAVA-GNN achieves up to 214% improvement in Kendall–Tau correlation and up to 70 × \times speedup in inference time over state-of-the-art GNN-based approaches, particularly on challenging road networks.

2.
Related Work

The exact computation of betweenness centrality relies on Brandes’ algorithm
(
6
)
, which becomes prohibitive for large-scale graphs due to its time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|V||E|)
for unweighted networks and
\mathcal{O}(|V||E|+|V|^{2}\log(|V|))
for weighted networks. Under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(
19
)
, no truly subquadratic algorithm exists for computing betweenness centrality, as this would imply a subquadratic solver for
k-SAT*

(
3
)
.
Consequently, there is considerable interest in distributed computing approaches and approximation algorithms.

2.1.
Unsupervised Approximation Algorithms

13

5
by introducing linear and bisection scaling—simple multipliers that rescale centrality estimates, together with correction terms to reduce overestimation for nodes that happen to be close to the randomly chosen pivots.

34

4
combine a balanced bidirectional BFS with adaptive sampling, achieving speed-ups of over
100\times
in the approximation of betweenness centrality.

25
address the centrality maximization problem via a randomized algorithm based on shortest-path sampling that provides theoretical approximation guarantees.

42
use hypergraph sketches to compute adaptive betweenness centrality, updating scores as nodes are selected in almost linear time.
Leveraging network topology,
41

top-k
influential nodes in mobile social networks.
Regarding centrality variants,
43
combine deterministic graph traversals with random walk sampling to approximate spanning centrality, while
7

2.2.
Parallel and Distributed Algorithms

1

2

39

This approach optimizes graph traversal by unifying exploration frontiers during Breadth-First Search (BFS) visits to avoid redundant computations, thereby improving performance on heterogeneous architectures. In parallel, within the context of large-scale distributed systems,
37

18

\mathcal{O}(\text{log}n)

17

-

\mathcal{O}(n)

2.3.
Graph Neural Networks

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have enabled the development of several deep learning–based methods for approximating centrality measures. Among the first contemporary GNNs presented at the same conference were those of
26
and
9
.

26
introduced a GNN-based method to approximate betweenness centrality, notably by pruning nodes from the adjacency matrix that lie on no shortest paths and by using graph convolutional layers while excluding the node’s own feature vector during aggregation. This approach was later extended to closeness centrality in the journal version of the paper
(
27
)
.

9

29

top-k
nodes. Notably, this latter work did not compare with
26

3.
Preliminaries

This section introduces the mathematical definition of betweenness centrality and degree mass, defines the core prediction task, and describes the hyperbolic random graph model.

3.1.
Betweenness Centrality

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of nodes and
E
is the set of edges, the betweenness centrality
C_{B}(v)
of a node
v\in V
is defined as:

(1)

C_{B}(v)=\sum_{s\neq v\neq t}\frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}

where
\sigma_{st}
is the total number of shortest paths from node
s
to node
t
, and
\sigma_{st}(v)
is the number of those paths that pass through node
v
.

3.2.
Problem Definition

Given an unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
(which can be either directed or undirected), our goal is to learn a function
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that assigns each node
v\in V
a real-valued score
f(v)
such that the ranking induced by these scores closely approximates the ranking based on the true betweenness centrality values
C_{B}(v)
.
Following standard practice in machine learning, this task is formulated as a ranking problem rather than a regression problem. The objective is not to accurately predict the absolute betweenness centrality values, but to preserve the relative ordering of nodes induced by
C_{B}(v)
. This formulation better reflects the use of betweenness centrality in many downstream applications, where the relative importance of nodes is more relevant than their exact centrality scores.

3.3.
Multi-hop Degree Mass

The
m
-th order degree mass of a node in a graph
G=(V,E)
captures the cumulative connectivity of nodes within
m
hops from that node. Originally introduced by
24
, the
m
-th order degree mass is formally defined as:

(2)

\mathbf{d}^{(m)}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}A^{k}\right)\mathbf{d},

where
A
is the adjacency matrix of the graph and
\mathbf{d}
is the vector of node degrees.
24
empirically demonstrate a strong correlation between higher-order degree masses and betweenness centrality in real-world networks.

3.4.
Hyperbolic Random Graph Model

The hyperbolic random graph model was first introduced by
20
. The graph is constructed by placing
N
nodes quasi-uniformly within a disk of radius
R
in a hyperbolic plane of negative curvature
K=-\zeta^{2}
. Writing node positions in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
, where
r\in[0,R]
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, the angular density is
\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}
and the radial coordinate density is:

(3)

\rho(r)=\alpha\frac{\sinh(\alpha r)}{\cosh(\alpha R)-1}.

The parameter
\alpha
controls the nodes’ positions: if
\alpha=\zeta
, the points follow a uniform distribution; if
\alpha>\zeta
, the points are more likely to be near the disk’s border, otherwise, they tend to cluster near the center. Two nodes
u
and
v
are connected if they are at a hyperbolic distance
d_{H}
less than
R
, where, considering the polar coordinates
u=(r_{u},\theta_{u})
and
v=(r_{v},\theta_{v})
,
d_{H}
is defined as:

(4)

d_{H}\bigl((r_{u},\theta_{u}),(r_{v},\theta_{v})\bigr)=\frac{1}{\zeta}\operatorname{acosh}\Bigl(\cosh(\zeta r_{u})\cosh(\zeta r_{v})-\\
-\sinh(\zeta r_{u})\sinh(\zeta r_{v})\cos(\pi-|\pi-|\theta_{u}-\theta_{v}||)\Bigr).

20
proved that the resulting degree distribution follows a power law
P(k)\sim k^{-\gamma}
, where the exponent
\gamma
is a function of the curvature and the node distribution parameters:

(5)

\gamma=\frac{2\alpha}{\zeta}+1>2.

We use the generator of hyperbolic random graphs proposed by
40
which is parameterized according to four parameters: the number of nodes
N
, the temperature
T
controlling clustering, the average degree
\bar{k}
, and the exponent
\gamma
of the power-law degree distribution. Equation
5
relates
\gamma
to the parameters used by
20
. For the full analytical derivations and the parameter relationships, we refer the reader to the original work
(
20
)
.

Recent literature has demonstrated that hyperbolic geometry provides an optimal embedding space for real-world datasets due to its inherent ability to encode hierarchical structures. For example,
Bogu~ná_Papadopoulos_Krioukov_2010
show that the Internet’s topology can be mapped to a hyperbolic disk to effectively discover and encode community structure. In the context of machine learning,
31
and
12
showed that hyperbolic embeddings significantly outperform Euclidean approaches in representing taxonomic and hierarchical data.

4.
Technical Contributions

We propose BRAVA-GNN, a lightweight framework designed to approximate the ranking induced by betweenness centrality in large-scale directed and undirected graphs.
Our approach is based on a preprocessing step designed to remove nodes that do not contribute to any shortest paths and a message passing GNN that leverages higher-order degree mass features.

4.1.
Preprocessing

We adopt the preprocessing heuristic introduced in
27
, which removes nodes that are guaranteed not to lie on any shortest path. In particular, nodes satisfying either of the following two conditions are removed:

(1)

Isolated or Leaf Nodes:
Any node with fewer than two neighbors has zero centrality, as betweenness is strictly defined for intermediate points.

(2)

Clique Neighborhoods:
If a node’s neighbors form a clique, any shortest path between them will favor the direct edge (length 1) over a path through the node (length 2).

Observe that, while such nodes have zero betweenness centrality, removing them impacts the betweenness of the remaining node.
Such impact appears however negligible in practice, compared to the obtained simplification of the graph topology.

4.2.
Architecture

The core of BRAVA-GNN is a message-passing architecture. Since betweenness centrality in directed graphs depends on the number of directed shortest paths, our model processes flow from both incoming and outgoing directions independently before fusing them. For complete details on hyperparameters, we refer to Section
5.2
. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm
2
and illustrated in Figure
2
.

4.2.1.
Input Features

We employ structural features specifically, the higher-order degree masses introduced in Section
3.3
. This is motivated by findings that node degree
(
28
)
and higher-order degree masses
(
24
)
are highly correlated with betweenness centrality in real-world networks. Consequently, we employ as initial feature vector for a node a concatenation of degree masses up to the 6-th order degree mass, which are processed through a dense layer with ReLU activation as detailed in Algorithm
1
.

4.2.2.
Dual Message Passing

To capture information from both incoming and outgoing edges, we apply two parallel GNN encoders sharing the same weights but operating on the original adjacency matrix
A
of the input graph and its transpose
A^{T}
. The encoder consists of two message-passing layers, where each node aggregates features from its
k
-hop neighbors without incorporating its own features. At each layer, the aggregated features are processed through a shared multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to produce intermediate node scores.

4.2.3.
Score Fusion

After 2 layers, the hidden states from all layers are passed through a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to project them to scalars
y_{in}(v)
and
y_{out}(v)
. The final node scores are obtained by multiplying the resulting embeddings
y_{in}
and
y_{out}(v)
of both directions.

Experimental Setup

In this section, we describe the experimental environment, the architectural configurations, and the evaluation framework used to validate our model’s ability to approximate betweenness centrality rankings.

5.1.
Software

Our proposed model is implemented in PyTorch

(

is used for graph generation and data processing, and NetworKit
(

5.2.
Implementation Details

We train the model for 10 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-3}
. The entire training process is highly efficient, requiring approximately 10 minutes on a single Nvidia GPU.

Hyperparameters were selected via ablation studies balancing accuracy and parameter efficiency (Appendix 8.1). The hidden dimension
d=12
was chosen as it achieves near-peak Kendall-tau correlation (
\tau_{b}=0.7899
) while keeping the model lightweight vs. larger dimensions like 128 (Table

[12\rightarrow 24\rightarrow 24\rightarrow 12\rightarrow 1]
with ReLU activations and dropout
p=0.3
, which yields optimal performance across tested rates 0.1–0.7 (Table
10

We apply
L_{2}
normalization post-activation per layer for stability. Ablations confirm 2 GNN layers strike the best expressiveness-efficiency tradeoff (Tables
6

7
), while 6-hop degree mass outperforms lower orders, especially on road networks (Tables
4

5
). Hyperbolic training substantially boosts generalization to high-diameter graphs (Table

To evaluate statistical robustness, we repeat the training process with 5 different random seeds and report the mean performance and standard deviation across these independent runs.

5.3.
Baselines

To establish a rigorous benchmark, we compare BRAVA-GNN against two state-of-the-art GNN-based methods:
GNN-Bet

(
27
)
and
ABCDE

(
29
)
.

We select GNN-Bet as our primary baseline, as it represents a foundational GNN architecture for betweenness centrality approximation. We additionally include ABCDE to capture recent advancements in the field. Notably, ABCDE has been extensively validated against a wide spectrum of prior techniques, including sampling-based algorithms (e.g., ABRA
(
35
)
, RK
(
34
)
, KADABRA
(
4

(

(

Since ABCDE was not originally evaluated against GNN-Bet, including both models ensures a comprehensive assessment of the current landscape of GNN-based approximation algorithms.

5.3.1.
GNN-Bet

While our framework incorporates the effective preprocessing heuristics and the bi-directional message passing scheme introduced by
27
, our core learning paradigm diverges.
The architecture of GNN-Bet relies on learnable node-specific embeddings, which inherently tie model complexity to input graph size. This design necessitates padding graphs to the size of the largest graph in the dataset, resulting in a parameter-heavy model (requiring over 58M parameters in our experiments).
In contrast, BRAVA-GNN replaces these high-dimensional embeddings with size-invariant degree-mass features. This shift allows our model to process graphs of arbitrary size natively without padding, reducing the parameter count by orders of magnitude (to
\approx 1.3
k ) and enabling true inductive generalization across disparate topologies.

5.3.2.
ABCDE

ABCDE
(
29
)
represents a significant step towards parameter-efficient centrality approximation. The model employs a deep Graph Convolutional Network architecture combined with a specialized regularization technique,
DropEdge
, which randomly removes a fraction of edges during each training iteration to mitigate overfitting and over-smoothing.
Regarding input features, ABCDE utilizes the aggregated degrees of first-order neighbors. While this design drastically reduces the parameter count compared to GNN-Bet (down to
\approx
72k), it remains significantly heavier than our proposed approach (
\approx
1.3k) and relies on strictly local (1-hop) structural information, whereas BRAVA-GNN integrates higher-order degree masses to capture longer-range dependencies.

5.4.
Loss Function

Since the absolute values of betweenness centrality can span several orders of magnitude and vary significantly across different graph topologies, optimizing directly for exact values is often unstable. Instead, we use the formulation which set the learning objective as a pairwise ranking problem to preserve the relative importance of nodes. Specifically, we employ a
Margin Ranking Loss
to enforce the correct ordering of nodes based on their centrality scores. Given two nodes
u
and
v
, the loss is defined as:

(6)

\mathcal{L}(s_{u},s_{v},y)=\max(0,1-y\cdot(s_{u}-s_{v})),

where
s_{u}
and
s_{v}
are the predicted scores generated by the model for nodes
u
and
v
, respectively. The label
y\in\{1,-1\}
serves as the ranking indicator: we set
y=1
if
u
is expected to be ranked higher than
v
, and
y=-1
otherwise. This formulation aligns with the pairwise hinge loss used in Learning to Rank frameworks
(
8
)
.

5.5.
Evaluation Metric

To evaluate the performance of our model in the node ranking task, we employ the
Kendall Tau
(
\tau
) correlation coefficient. This metric measures the ordinal association between the predicted and the ground-truth rankings. In particular, we adopt the

\tau_{b}
variant of the Kendall Tau coefficient, which explicitly accounts for ties in the rankings. This is particularly relevant for betweenness centrality, where large subsets of nodes (e.g., peripheral nodes or symmetric structures) often share identical scores.
Formally, the Kendall
\tau_{b}
coefficient is defined as:

(7)

\tau_{b}=\frac{C-D}{\sqrt{(C+D+T)(C+D+U)}},

where
C
denotes the number of
concordant pairs
(pairs of nodes
(i,j)
whose ranks agree in both the predicted and true rankings),
D
denotes the number of
discordant pairs
(pairs whose ranks are reversed),
T
is the number of ties only in the predicted ranking, and
U
is the number of ties only in the true ranking.
The coefficient of
\tau_{b}
ranges from
-1
(perfect disagreement) to
1
(perfect agreement), where a higher score indicates that the model has successfully learned to preserve the topological hierarchy of the network.

6.2.
Accuracy and Generalization.

BRAVA-GNN establishes a new state-of-the-art on the majority of the datasets, demonstrating superior generalization capabilities. The performance gap is most evident in road networks, which are characterized by high diameters and long-range dependencies—structures where traditional GNNs typically struggle.
For instance, on the
road-luxembourg-osm
graph, BRAVA-GNN achieves a correlation of
\tau_{b}=0.6518
, representing a
+214.2%
improvement over GNN-Bet (
\tau_{b}=0.2075
) and completely outclassing ABCDE, which fails to capture the ranking structure (
\tau_{b}=-0.1772
). Similar trends are observed in
road-belgium-osm
(+162.6%) and
road-netherlands-osm
(+86.1%).
While our model occasionally trades marginal accuracy for speed on specific topologies (e.g.,
cit-Patents
or
p2p-Gnutella31
), it remains highly competitive, consistently ranking as the best or strong second-best method while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters.

6.3.3.
Jeju Island

To further stress-test the model’s inductive generalization, we evaluate BRAVA-GNN on the road network of the KDD’26 conference location, Jeju Island in South Korea. Unlike continental road networks often used in training, this dataset represents a closed system with distinct boundary conditions and finite topology. We extract the largest strongly connected component from OpenStreetMap (OSM) data, adhering method used to generate the other OSM datasets in our study. Despite the structural shift from the training distribution, BRAVA-GNN achieves a strong Kendall Tau correlation of
\tau_{b}=0.7708
. As visualized in Figure
1
, the predicted ranking (a) closely mirrors the ground-truth structure (b). This result suggests that the model has successfully learned the fundamental physical properties of betweenness centrality, specifically the flow dynamics constrained by geometry, rather than merely memorizing specific graph features or overfitting to continental patterns.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we presented BRAVA-GNN, a lightweight and scalable framework for approximating betweenness centrality. By shifting the learning paradigm from node-specific embeddings to size-invariant degree-mass features, our architecture achieves a drastic reduction in parameter count, requiring
54\times
fewer parameters than the most efficient baseline, while maintaining superior expressiveness.

A key innovation of our approach is the integration of hyperbolic random graphs into the training set. We demonstrated that this strategy equips the model with the necessary inductive bias to generalize to high-diameter topologies, solving a persistent limitation of prior GNN-based methods on road networks.
Extensive experiments on 19 real-world datasets confirm that BRAVA-GNN establishes a new state-of-the-art, delivering up to
214\%
improvement in ranking accuracy and up to
70\times
inference speedup.

Future work will extend this topological learning framework to other path-based centrality measures, such as closeness centrality, and adapt the architecture for centrality ranking in temporal graphs.

8.1.
Ablation Study

To assess the impact of different components and hyperparameters of our proposed model, we conduct an ablation study focusing on the choice of initial node features, the number of layers, the training dataset composition, the hidden dimension, and dropout rate. All results reported in this section display the average Kendall Tau
\tau_{b}
correlation across five random seeds.
Regarding the selection criteria, for hyperparameters that directly influence the number of learnable parameters (e.g., hidden dimension, number of layers), we chose the configuration that strikes the best balance between performance and parameter efficiency. Conversely, for hyperparameters that do not affect model size (e.g., dropout rate), we selected the values that achieved the highest correlation. The selected hyperparameter for the final model is indicated with a
\dagger
symbol.

8.1.1.
Initial Node Features

We evaluate the effect of using different initial node features on the model’s performance. We evaluate the model’s performance using
m
-th order degree mass features, where
m
ranges from 1 (simple degree) to 12.
The results, summarized in Table
4
and
5
, show that simple degree features (D1) provide a strong baseline (
\tau=0.7485
). Next, incorporating higher-order degree features enhances the model’s ability to approximate betweenness centrality rankings. This suggests that capturing more extensive local connectivity information is beneficial for the task. Interestingly, performance does not increase monotonically with neighborhood
hop count: adding 2-hop features slightly degrades performance in Kendall’s
\tau_{b}
(0.7485
\rightarrow
0.7477), while
3-hop features provide a substantial improvement (0.7477
\rightarrow
0.7737). These large gains are mainly observed in high-diameter road networks, for instance,
road-luxembourg-osm
sees Kendall’s
\tau_{b}
increase from 0.42 (D2) to 0.55 (D3). This finding suggests that 3-hop neighborhoods capture structural patterns relevant to betweenness that 2-hop neighborhoods cannot. However, this trend does not persist uniformly as we scale to 12-hop neighborhoods.

8.1.2.
Number of Layers

We investigate the impact of the number of GNN layers on model performance. We experiment with configurations ranging from 0 to 10 layers. The 0-layer configuration represents a baseline where the initial features are passed directly through the MLP without neighborhood aggregation. The results in Tables
6
and
7
demonstrate that message passing is important as the 0-layer model underperforms. While deeper networks show marginal improvement, the 2-layer configuration strikes the best balance between model parameters and performance, able to sufficiently capture neighborhood information.

8.1.3.
Training Dataset Composition

To evaluate the effect of training dataset composition, we compare models trained solely on scale-free graphs against those trained on hyperbolic graphs and the combination of both. The results, shown in Table
8
, demonstrate that including hyperbolic graphs in the training set substantially improves generalization to real-world datasets, particularly road networks.
