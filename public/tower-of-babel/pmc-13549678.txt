Rapidly Enlarging Dark Purple Flank Vascular Tumour in a 12-Month-Old Infant with Thrombocytopenia
Authors: Eui Deuk Hwang, Jinhee Kim, Jung Eun Kwon, Dae-Lyong Ha
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 4-month-old preterm female infant (gestational age 29 weeks, birth weight 750 g) presented with a 3 × 3 cm hemangioma on the left flank [Figure 1a]. She had bronchopulmonary dysplasia and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. However, no syndromes associated with infantile hemangioma were identified. Minimum dose of propranolol (0.5 mg/kg) was given but immediately stopped due to bradycardia.

Clinical appearance of infantile hemangioma. (a) Progressively enlarging infantile hemangioma on the left flank of a 4-month-old infant, measuring 3 × 3 cm at the time of referral. (b) Sudden colour change from bright red to dark purple, with the lesion measuring 4.3 × 3.7 cm at 12 months of age

At 12 months, the hemangioma enlarged to 4.3 × 3.7 cm and changed to dark purple, accompanied by septic shock [Figure 1b]. The platelet count decreased to 14,000/µL, and inflammatory markers were elevated. Chest X-ray showed right upper lobe infiltration and atelectasis. Human rhinovirus/enterovirus and respiratory syncytial virus were detected.

Despite 5-day methylprednisolone treatment (30 mg/kg/day) and platelet transfusion, thrombocytopenia worsened. An excisional biopsy was performed. Histopathology confirmed infantile hemangioma with necrosis and clot formation, showing GLUT-1 and CD31 positivity [Figure 2]. Post-operatively, desaturation events decreased, and inflammatory markers and platelet count normalised.

Histopathology. (a) Low-power view showing lobules of capillary-sized vascular channels, along with thrombosis and necrotic areas in the deep layer (hematoxylin-eosin staining, ×5). (b and c) High-power view revealing capillary-sized vascular channels with a single layer of flattened endothelial cells (hematoxylin-eosin staining, b: ×50, c: ×200). (d) Necrotic lesion with blood clots and ghost capillaries (hematoxylin-eosin staining, ×200). (e) Positive staining for GLUT-1, a specific marker for infantile hemangioma (×10). (f) Positive staining for CD31, an endothelial cell marker (×10)

Diagnosis

Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon in infantile hemangioma

Review

Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon (KMP) is a rare but serious complication of vascular tumours. It is most commonly associated with Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma and tufted angioma.[1] Its occurrence in infantile hemangioma (IH) is extremely rare, with only a few cases documented in the literature. This case highlights that, although rare, KMP can be associated with IH.

The hallmark feature of KMP is profound thrombocytopenia, with platelet levels typically ranging from 3000 to 60,000/µL.[2] In most cases, platelet transfusions provide only transient benefits as platelets are sequestered within the tumour with a shortened half-life.[3] In this case, the patient exhibited a marked decrease in platelet count with IH confirmed by histopathological examination. Despite continued treatment for other infection and systemic conditions, there were no significant changes in general condition or blood test results. Notably, following platelet transfusion and subsequent surgical excision of the IH, the platelet count increased rapidly, with a significant improvement in the patient’s overall condition. KMP can also lead to significant anemia resulting from intralesional hemorrhage, coagulopathy, blood sequestration within the tumor, or hemolytic anemia due to mechanical destruction of red blood cells in the abnormal tumor vasculature.[2] In the present case, the patient’s red blood cell (RBC) count and hemoglobin levels declined from 44,40,000/µL and 12.3 g/dL, respectively, 3 days before the hemangioma exhibited colour changes, to 37,80,000/µL and 10.2 g/dL immediately after the colour change. Following leukocyte-filtered RBC transfusion and hemangioma excision, both RBC count and hemoglobin levels increased to 51,30,000/µL and 14.6 g/dL, respectively, and remained stable without needing additional transfusions. Furthermore, histological examination revealed coagulation and clot formation within the lesion, suggesting KMP as a cause of anemia and highlighting the therapeutic role of excision.

Complications of KMP may include severe hemorrhage, high-output cardiac failure, shock, and even death.[4] Given its high morbidity and mortality rates, aggressive therapeutic intervention is required.[5] Systemic corticosteroids remain the first-line treatment for KMP, with adjunctive options such as vincristine or sirolimus for refractory cases. However, surgical intervention is often overlooked due to infiltrative characteristics of the related tumor and the presence of coagulopathy. While systemic therapy remains the standard approach, surgical removal of the lesion might be a viable option for cases with a steroid-refractory KMP.

In this case, the patient did not respond to systemic steroids. She was unable to receive beta-blocker therapy due to bradycardia. Furthermore, necrotic changes in the large hemangioma might have contributed to a septic condition, further complicating the clinical course. Given the histological evidence of necrotic areas and coagulation within the lesion, surgical excision was pursued as a last resort, ultimately leading to platelet recovery and clinical improvement. Six months after the surgery, the patient has been under follow-up with no signs of recurrence or other abnormalities.

We report a rare case of KMP occurring in IH successfully managed with surgical excision after failure of systemic corticosteroid therapy. This case underscores the need for early recognition and consideration of surgical intervention in similar cases.
