Navigating inhabited institutionalism: A framework for electrocardiogram workshops in teaching hospitals
Authors: Vidya Lalloo, Jan Heystek, André du Plessis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis narrative review examines the challenge of poor electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation by Emergency Medicine (EM) clinicians from the perspective of EM consultants in a teaching hospital.MethodsThis study is underpinned by the Inhabited Institutionalism Theoretical framework. Pressures faced by EM consultants are analysed within a system that navigates both macro-level institutional and regulatory mandates and micro-level pressure from the clinicians and EM trainees they manage. Analysis of these literature reviews highlights the challenge faced by ‘middle managers’ in balancing the often-opposing forces.SynthesisThe study advocates the use of Continuous Professional Development (CPD)-accredited ECG in-service training workshops to support quality improvement and meet both macro and micro needs. It recognises the complexity in the implementation of such workshops and offers a “Framework for a Sustainable ECG Workshop” that meets the CPD requirements of the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA), the University’s needs to train registrars and to decolonise the curriculum, the hospital’s needs for quality improvement, addresses the unique challenges faced by clinicians working in emergency departments, and addresses clinicians’ inherent negative attitudes towards ECG interpretation.ConclusionThis narrative review addresses ECG misinterpretation in South African EM. Through the analytical lens of Inhabited Institutionalism, it interrogates macro-, meso‑, and micro-level influences on ECG training in teaching hospitals and proposes a meso‑level framework to guide consultants in designing sustainable CPD-accredited ECG workshops responsive to institutional mandates, local clinical realities, and learning needs. Further empirical work should evaluate its effect on ECG competence and patient outcomes.

African Relevance

This narrative review is conducted in the context of high rates of Electrocardiogram (ECG) misinterpretation in emergency medicine, which is a phenomenon extrapolatable to the broader African community.

This review highlights the challenge of balancing institutional (top-down) mandates with the micro-level (bottom-up) needs of the clinicians in institutions.

The paper presents strategies which can be readily adopted and adapted by other African healthcare institutions for similar in-service CPD training initiatives.

Introduction

In South Africa (SA), cardiovascular disease has become the leading cause of death from non-communicable diseases, due to increases in hypertension and obesity in the population [1]. Electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation is valuable for diagnosing important time-sensitive cardiac pathologies such as myocardial infarction or heart blocks, which require immediate intervention. Its value extends beyond the heart to diagnose pericardial and pulmonary pathology, provide clues to electrolyte abnormalities, assess the severity of certain toxic ingestions, and screen for life-threatening conditions [2,3].

Despite its value, ECG interpretation remains challenging. Up to 33% of ECGs are misinterpreted, and 11% of these misdiagnoses may lead to inappropriate management [4]. Among specialist trainees in the USA, the misinterpretation rate rises to 40% [5]. Globally, newly qualified doctors often lack sufficient ECG proficiency [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Furthermore, medical malpractice related to myocardial infarction misdiagnosis is high [10]. Limited understanding is compounded by negative attitudes and low confidence in ECG interpretation among varied medical personnel [[11], [12], [13]].

Textbook titles such as "ECGs Made Easy," suggest that ECGs are a complex, cognitively-loaded topic [14]. Undergraduate students memorise ECG patterns rather than grasping underlying vectors and concepts [13]. In addition, the art of ECG interpretation continues to evolve, necessitating ongoing professional development [2].

Continuous Professional Development (CPD) refers to structured educational activities through which healthcare professionals maintain and improve their knowledge, skills, and professional competence. In South Africa, CPD is regulated by the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA), which requires medical practitioners to obtain a minimum of 25 clinical CPD points annually [15,16]. Teaching hospitals, also referred to in some contexts as academic, university-affiliated, or training hospitals, provide patient care while fulfilling education and training functions for undergraduate students, interns, registrars, and other health professionals [17,18].

Within South African Emergency Medicine (EM), consultants are specialist emergency physicians responsible for senior clinical decision-making, supervision, training, governance, quality improvement, and patient safety [17]. They occupy a meso‑level position within teaching hospitals, mediating between macro-level mandates and micro-level clinical realities [19,20]. These mandates include Department of Health service delivery and complaints management expectations; HPCSA CPD requirements; university and South African Committee of Health Sciences Deans (SACoHSD) training mandates; and College of Emergency Medicine (CEM) trainee assessment requirements [[15], [16], [17], [18],[21], [22]]. Price proposes balancing these demands through CPD-accredited activities for institutional quality improvement projects, thereby fulfilling two mandates with one activity [20]. Locally pertinent ECG workshops may also support curriculum decolonisation in a medical education system shaped by unequal access to training and resources [[23], [24]].

Problem statement and aim

ECG interpretation is a critical skill in EM, yet it is often misinterpreted, with negative consequences for patient care. EM consultants are faced with the challenge to educate their staff about ECG interpretation while balancing service delivery needs, meeting macro-level institutional mandates; and addressing micro-level prevailing negative attitudes, cognitive overload, and logistical challenges to in-service training in a department that never closes its doors. Using the Inhabited Institutionalism analytical lens, this narrative review examines the factors that influence the adoption of in-service ECG training in a South African teaching hospital context, and proposes an evidence-based framework for a successful ECG workshop, with strategies for successful, sustainable implementation.

Theoretical framework

This paper uses Inhabited Institutionalism as a framework to analyse the challenges associated with improving ECG interpretation in a teaching hospital, following the macro‑meso-micro analysis of Gulasaryan [19].

Inhabited institutionalism draws on Institutional and Neo-Institutional theories. Institutional Theory explains how regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive factors shape organisational legitimacy and performance [25]. It has been criticised for focusing too much on creating stable and uniform institutions lacking innovation [26]. In contrast, neoinstitutional theory recognises that institutions evolve across contexts when faced with various challenges [26]. Agency (the capacity of individuals in an organisation to interpret, negotiate, and act) and legitimacy (the socially constructed perception that organisational practices align with accepted institutional norms) are central to how individuals and organisations respond to performance pressures [26,27]. EM consultants may act as institutional entrepreneurs seeking to improve ECG interpretation by making new practices more legitimate than established ones [26]. Macfarlane, Barton-Sweeney and Woodard [28]. describe three legitimacy-shaping pillars in healthcare:1.Coercive or regulative pillar: This is a top-down approach where laws and contracts dictate actions. Change behaviour involves altering the regulatory framework. In practical terms, this could mean mandating that all clinicians attend an ECG workshop.2.Normative or mimetic pillar: Expectations are shared regarding appropriate behaviours. Behaviour changes when organisations imitate best practices to enhance legitimacy. This suggests encouraging staff to enhance their ECG interpretation skills, noting that proficiency in this area is already expected among their peers.3.Cultural-cognitive pillar: Deeply held beliefs and mental frameworks shape perceptions. Change is driven by the conviction that the institution is acting morally. This emphasises the clinician’s ethical responsibility to enhance their proficiency in ECG interpretation, fostering better patient outcomes.

Inhabited institutionalism explains how institutions change through the ongoing interactions of organisational members [27]. It offers a combined top-down and bottom-up perspective for understanding teaching hospitals, viewing its form and function as neither solely determined by institutional structures nor solely constructed by individuals [19]. Managers in teaching hospitals find themselves inhabiting this meso (‘middle’) level, navigating a complex landscape shaped by stakeholders at macro and micro levels [19]. Inhabited institutionalism is marked by three key aspects: [19]1.Firstly, "double embeddedness" involves paying close attention to the local contexts of people's activities while recognising the constraints imposed by the broader organisational environment. For example, the HPCSA requires CPD activities, yet the shift-work structure in EDs prevents some staff from attending, leaving ‘inhabitants’ to grapple with how to meet both operational and regulatory requirements.2.Secondly, there's an emphasis on meanings, incorporating both local meanings derived from interactions within defined contexts and the more abstract norms, beliefs, and values pertaining to institutions. For example, ‘quality emergency care’ to a registrar might mean spending extra time discussing a complex case for educational purposes (their meaning of quality being learning), which an administrator might see as inefficient use of time (their meaning of quality being efficiency).3.Thirdly, a sceptical and inquisitive attitude challenges assumptions about organisational operations, using empirical data to reveal hidden complexities. For instance, a sceptical attitude might question the assumption that automated ECG interpretation is reliable, prompting clinicians to audit discrepant cases, compare algorithm results with expert readings, investigate training gaps, and revise protocols to require mandatory clinician confirmation of ECG interpretation.

In this review, Inhabited Institutionalism served as the organising analytical lens for the literature search, review structure, and interpretation of findings. The macro‑meso-micro structure used in the literature review is derived from this theoretical framing. Macro-level literature addressed institutional, regulatory, professional, and educational “top-down” pressures, while micro-level literature addressed clinical, cognitive, emotional, and environmental “bottom-up” realities in the ED. The meso‑level analysis considers how EM consultants, as institutional inhabitants and middle managers, mediate the tensions between these levels. Thus, the theory shaped what literature was included, how it was organised, and how tensions between institutional expectations and local practice were interpreted.

Literature review: analysing inhabited institutionalism from the EM consultant context

Macro-level analysis

From an Inhabited Institutionalism perspective, the macro-level analysis identifies the broader institutional, regulatory, professional, and educational structures that shape the conditions under which EM consultants attempt to implement ECG training [19]. Teaching hospitals are governed by both the healthcare and higher education sectors, and are influenced by complex inherent power dynamics. On a global scale, entities such as the World Health Organisation, shape health initiatives through their institutional roles, resources, and collaborative networks [29]. Global medical education is implicated in asymmetrical power relations perpetuating historical inequalities [23]. The Global North (GN) and Global South (GS) construct reinforces hierarchies by positioning nations such as SA, as ‘catching up’ to the GN. GN-centric curricula, research paradigms, and accreditation standards prevail in the GS often at the expense of local knowledge and expertise [23].

EM consultants are further influenced by national bodies such as the Department of Health, whose mandates are enacted through hospital managers [22]. The HPCSA regulates healthcare provider standards, registration, education and training, including annual CPD requirements [15,16]. The Office of Health Standards Compliance (OHSC) manages complaints and expects managers to address diagnostic errors through interventions such as in-service training [30].

Teaching hospitals are also regulated by university governance structures, SACoHSD and the College of Emergency Medicine (CEM), which provide training, transformation and assessment mandates [17,18,21]. The University Division of EM and CEM expect emergency clinicians to be medical experts interpreting ECGs with competence, and can accredit training activities for CPD points [17,21].

Micro-level analysis

From an Inhabited Institutionalism perspective, the micro-level analysis foregrounds the clinical realities, meanings, attitudes, and interactions that influence how clinicians engage with ECG interpretation and educational interventions in everyday ED practice [24]. This section is informed by insights from middle management professionals in this context and is supported by relevant literature.

The scope of the challenge in SA: ECG interpretation ability varies among healthcare providers in SA. In 2010, EM registrars demonstrated inferior ECG interpretation with average scores of 46.4%, scoring lower than their international peers [31]. In 2020, general practitioners scored 14,7%, and in 2025, interns achieved 60% accuracy in myocardial ischaemia recognition [32,33]. More recently, Mogoale, Lalloo and Engelbrecht reported a mean score of 51.5% among junior doctors in Gauteng, with only 34,2% achieving the pass standard [34]. These findings highlight the inconsistency in ECG interpretation by clinicians who often perform overtime duties in ED and support the need for structured ECG training in SA.

Negative attitudes: There is considerable evidence indicating that ECG interpretation causes anxiety and cognitive overload among ICU nurses, medical students and prehospital staff, with paediatricians expressing low confidence in their ECG interpretation abilities [[11], [12], [13],35]. A multicentre French survey found that confidence levels were particularly low in medical students during emergency situations [36]. Canadian emergency medicine trainees frequently report negative attitudes and anxiety toward ECG interpretation, with many indicating low confidence and discomfort interpreting ECGs in clinical settings [37]. A 2024 study of intern doctors at a public hospital complex in SA found that most participants reported moderate insecurity when interpreting ECGs prior to training [33].

A qualitative study conducted in Malaysia in 2020 examined the factors contributing to medical students' negative attitudes towards ECGs. The findings indicated that students faced challenges related to retention of knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, and interpretation of ECGs. The authors advocate for educational reforms and innovative approaches to enhance the learning of ECGs within undergraduate medical training [12].

To shift attitudes from negative to positive, Hart proposes a multidimensional framework for attitude, which should be dissected and targeted [38]. It comprises of three components: Affective response (e.g., aversion to or affinity for ECG interpretation), cognitive beliefs (e.g., whether ECGs are perceived as simple or complex, and acknowledging their usefulness in practice), and conative (behavioural) responses (e.g., whether one actively engages with or avoids ECG interpretation).

Social-psychological interventions, such as utility-value interventions which elucidate the relevance and applicability of the content, and growth mindset interventions which foster the understanding that intelligence and achievement are malleable and develop through learning from failures, have been linked to improved attitudes and enhanced performance [39].

The ED environment: Emergency medicine in SA operates in resource-constrained settings with high patient volumes, trauma burden, academic responsibilities, and competing priorities [40]. Prolonged patient boarding, access block and non-urgent ED presentations worsen overcrowding and workload [41,42]. Burnout among Gauteng emergency clinicians is characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced personal accomplishment which may impair engagement, concentration, diagnostic confidence [43], and contribute to negative attitudes. In addition, the demanding workload and shift-based nature of emergency medicine limit opportunities to attend educational CPD activities such as ECG workshops [20,44].

Meso-level analysis

Within an inhabited institutionalism perspective, consultants at the meso level play a key role in mediating between institutional expectations and everyday clinical practice. In the ED context, they are responsible for ensuring patient safety and high-quality emergency care while navigating multiple macro-level institutional pressures. These include requirements from the HPCSA for CPD, university expectations related to training and curriculum reform, and oversight from the OHSC and the Department of Health, particularly regarding quality improvement initiatives following complaints or complications arising from ECG misinterpretation. These institutional expectations must be balanced with the micro-level clinical realities of daily ED practice. Price [20]. advocates leveraging the mandated CPD framework to implement quality improvement strategies, arguing that integrating quality improvement methodologies with targeted interventions to facilitate stakeholder buy-in may be transformational within the complex meso‑level institutional dynamics [20].

Evidence from international studies further supports structured educational interventions as a quality improvement strategy. Studies conducted in Turkey and Canada, together with a systematic review of data from the Health Care Provider Performance Review (HCPPR), a collaborative programme involving the Centre for Disease Control, Harvard Medical School, World Health Organisation, Management Sciences for Health and Johns Hopkins University, recommend in-service training as more effective than peer-to-peer training or self-directed learning [[45], [46], [47]].

Synthesis: framework for a sustainable ECG workshop within an inhabited institutional context

This narrative review positions EM consultants as meso‑level inhabitants of teaching hospitals navigating macro- and micro-levels pressures. Within this context, a CPD-accredited ECG workshop may serve as a meso‑level strategy aligning regulatory expectations with clinical practice through targeted education. Its sustainability depends on both institutional buy-in and management of micro-level challenges. The following section synthesises the literature and applies it to the design and implementation of an ECG workshop.

Inhabited Institutionalism identifies isomorphism and coupling as mechanisms of institutional change [19]. Isomorphism encourages institutions to adopt peer practices due to regulatory requirements, best-practice, or competitive pressures. Coupling examines the relationship between policy statements and actual practice. These mechanisms may be strategically utilised by change agents seeking institutional support for an ECG workshop. Benchmarking ECG training against other hospitals may create pressure for adoption, while coupling quality care missions to ECG misinterpretation may justify implementation. Multi-level leadership support has been shown to further enhance clinician engagement in CPD activities [48].

Successful implementation of an ECG workshop requires recognition that normative and mimetic influences often shape clinician attitudes, intentions, and uptake, more than coercive top-down pressures [49]. Educational strategies should therefore address existing negative attitudes, low confidence, and cognitive overload by fostering a growth mindset among EM clinicians [50]. Incorporating elements of medutainment may further enhance engagement, motivation, and knowledge retention during the workshop [51,52].

Practical implementation should acknowledge the diversity of ED clinician experience and the service delivery demands of full-time emergency care. These factors may necessitate multiple training sessions and the use of varied teaching approaches to accommodate differing levels of experience and availability among clinicians.

Previous research on institutional change suggests that the successful adoption of new initiatives is influenced by several factors, including meaning-making, social interaction, negotiation of practice, relative advantage, compatibility, simplicity, trialability, observability of impact, and the evidence-based nature of the intervention [19,20].

The proposed framework is situated at the meso level because it is intended for EM consultants as middle managers within teaching hospitals. The macro and micro levels are therefore not separate components of the framework, but contextual forces that shape its design and implementation. Macro-level pressures include CPD requirements, training mandates, quality improvement expectations, and service delivery obligations. Micro-level pressures include clinicians’ attitudes toward ECG interpretation, cognitive overload, variable experience, shift work, time constraints, and the high-pressure ED environment. Conclusion

This narrative review addresses ECG misinterpretation in South African EM. Through the analytical lens of Inhabited Institutionalism, it interrogates macro-, meso‑, and micro-level influences on ECG training in teaching hospitals and proposes a meso‑level framework to guide consultants in designing sustainable CPD-accredited ECG workshops responsive to institutional mandates, local clinical realities, and learning needs. Further empirical work should evaluate its effect on ECG competence and patient outcomes.
