Protocol for preparing and using a task to study “delayed safety-seeking behavior” in rats
Authors: Junghwa Lee, Sukwoo Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe study of defensive behaviors in predicting action outcomes in novel situations without trial and error is limited by the lack of appropriate animal models. Here, we present a protocol for preparing and using a delayed safety-seeking task where rats temporarily hold information for future safety seeking. We describe steps for constructing a two-compartment chamber and provide testing procedures during which either in vivo recordings or optogenetics can be performed. This protocol is applicable for further research on defensive behavior.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Han et al.1Subject areas: model organisms, neuroscience, behavior

Before you begin

Research on defensive behavior in novel situations, where the subject has no prior experience or opportunity for trial-and-error learning, has been limited due to the absence of a suitable behavior model. Therefore, we established a new behavior model called 'delayed safety-seeking behavior'.

We have developed a new behavioral test in which a rat can move between two value-neutral spaces connected by an openable outlet. When a conditioned stimulus associated with a threat is presented in one space, the rat moves to the other space more quickly after a delay compared to when a neutral stimulus is presented. The test for 'delayed safety-seeking behavior' is conducted only once to rule out the possibility that this behavior is based on learning during the test. The possibility that 'delayed safety-seeking behavior' is driven by instinctual tendencies toward a safe shelter was thoroughly excluded through various experiments in our recent paper.1 Therefore, 'delayed safety-seeking behavior' indicates that the rat is utilizing higher cognitive functions to predict that the opposite space may be safer.

Therefore, this model requires the subject to extrapolate the outcomes of actions in novel situations based on previous experiences, including the awareness of the other compartment’s existence and the contingency between conditioned stimuli (CS) and painful unconditioned stimuli (US). During this behavior, the subject must hold information concerning the threat stimulus during the delay period, necessitating higher cognitive functions. The protocol below describes ‘delayed safety-seeking behavior’ conducted on rats surgically targeted for in vivo single unit recording or optogenetics. We focus here not only on each behavioral step but also on the methods of handling the rats and the environmental conditions to which they are exposed.

Step-by-step method details

Pre-behavioral task: Handling

Timing: 1–5 days

This step describes the handling procedure for rats used in in vivo single unit recording or optogenetics experiments. The final goal of handling is to have the rat freely move back and forth between the inside of the cage and the cage lid placed next to the cage.

1.Handle all rats one day before the behavioral experiments for in vivo single unit recording.a.Sit on a chair and place the rat onto a towel on experimenter’s knee, allowing the rat to roam around.Note: Gently stroke the rat’s back occasionally to ensure that the rat is not frightened by the experimenter’s touch.Note: This step is carried out in the bright anteroom of the rat housing room (see Figure 1A).b.When the rat adapts well and actively, replace the cover of the electrode holder on its head with a new one.Note: If the rat has not fully recovered from surgery or is extremely aggressive, handling is stopped. Handling will be attempted again the following day.c.Put the rat back into the cage from the experimenter’s knee.Note: At this time, place the rat on the cage lid next to the cage, allowing it to move freely on the lid.Note: From this step onward, proceed in an area with dim light. Specifically, the procedure is conducted in the rack storage area of the rat housing room with the door opened. The anteroom is bright (approximately 190 lux), while the rat housing room is completely dark, so the door is left open to create dim light conditions (approximately 5 lux).Note: If the rat tries to move towards the ground instead of moving inside the cage, please use the experimenter’s hand to prevent it.d.When the rat extends its front paws towards the cage wall, gently support its hindquarters to guide it smoothly out of or into the cage.Note: This process is usually repeated approximately twice.e.If the rat freely moves back and forth between the inside of the cage and the cage lid placed next to the cage, stop handling.

Expected outcomes

For in vivo single unit recording experiments, during the fear conditioning process, freezing in response to the light cue increase by over 80% for CS group (Figure 6B). In the two-compartment exposure session, the success rate of crossing is 64% (n = 32/50) with crossing at 82.7 s on average by around the 3rd trial for CS group and success rate of crossing is 86.5% (n = 45/52), with crossing at 71.4 s on average by around the 3rd trial for Neutral group (Figures 6C and 6D). In the test session of day 3, the success rate for crossing are 62.5% (n = 20/32) for CS group and 71.1% (n = 32/45) for Neutral group. These rats that passed histology are finally included in the data. As a result, CS group shows a significant difference in crossing latency 48.7 s, compared to Neutral group, which is 111.2 s (Figure 6E). Freezing in response to the cue is observed to be 54.8% in CS group (Figure 6F).

In the case of optogenetics experiments, cue freezing in the fear conditioning increase by 65% for eNpHR and EYFP groups (Figure 6G). In the two-compartment exposure session, the success rate of crossing is 53.2% (n = 99/186) with an average crossing latency at 151.4 s by around the 2nd trial for eNpHR group and success rate of crossing is 55.8% (n = 101/181) with and average crossing latency 106.6 s on average by around the 2nd trial for EYFP group (Figures 6H and 6I). In the test session of day 3, the success rate for crossing are 77.7% (n = 77/99) for eNpHR group and 88.1% (n = 89/101) for EYFP group. These rats that passed histology are finally included in the data. As a result, eNpHR group shows a significant difference in crossing latency, 73.3 s, compared to EYFP group, which is 29.6 s (Figure 6J). Freezing in response to the cue is observed to be around 40% in both eNpHR and EYFP groups (Figure 6K).

Based on the results of this protocol, it can be concluded that the threat stimulus in compartment A prompts the rats in the CS group to seek safety in compartment B. This safety-seeking behavior cannot be considered reflexive or innate, as the rats retain information about the threat-triggering stimulus for tens of seconds even after the threat stimuli disappear. Additionally, it has been observed that the conditioned stimulus used as the threat stimulus itself does not trigger flight or escape behavior.1 Furthermore, since the rats undergo the test only once in an unfamiliar situation, this behavior cannot be attributed to learning. Therefore, this behavioral model offers new insights into understanding defensive behaviors beyond those previously studied in animal research. In a previous study, we demonstrated that this defensive behavior is associated with the persistent activity of the claustrum.1 Building upon this finding, we anticipate that future in-depth research utilizing this behavioral model will elucidate the brain mechanisms underlying defensive behaviors with higher cognitive processes in rats.

Limitations

If the mechanism of extrapolation is to be studied using delayed safety-seeking behavior, the major limitation is conducting the test only once per animal. While this is an unavoidable choice to prevent further learning during the test, it results in a significantly reduced data acquisition rate. Consequently, to obtain statistically significant results, a larger number of rats need to be used, which is labor-intensive. Another issue is the considerable proportion of rats showing omission during the exposure or test phases. This is an inevitable result as both compartments hold neutral value, posing a challenge to data acquisition. It also serves as another drawback hindering data acquisition.

Problem 2

As soon as a rat crosses over to compartment B, it immediately returns to compartment A in exposure to the two-compartment chamber (step 16a).

Potential solution

•When the rat extends its head towards compartment B while placing its feet on the outlet as if attempting to cross, the experimenter prepares to close the outlet door. As the rat crosses into compartment B, the experimenter gradually closes the outlet door to ensure that the rat does not get caught in it. If only the tail remains across the outlet while the body has crossed, the experimenter leaves enough space for the tail and closes the door to prevent the rat from returning to compartment A. Once the tail has completely crossed into compartment B, the door is then fully closed.
