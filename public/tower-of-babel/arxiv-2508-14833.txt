An Investigation Into Secondary School Students’ Debugging Behaviour in Python CCS: Social and professional topics K-12 education
Laurie Gale, Sue Sentance

Abstract
. Background and context: Debugging is a common and often frustrating challenge for beginner programmers. Understanding students’ debugging processes can help us identify the difficulties and misunderstandings they possess. However, we currently have limited knowledge of how secondary students debug in a text-based language, a medium through which millions of students will learn to program in the future. Objectives: In this paper, we investigate the debugging behaviour of K-12 students learning a text-based programming language, as part of an effort to shape how to effectively teach debugging to these students. Method: We collected log data from 73 students attempting a set of debugging exercises using an online code editor. We inductively analysed these logs using qualitative content analysis, generating a categorisation of the debugging behaviours observed. Findings: A range of behaviours were exhibited by students, skewed towards being ineffective. Most students were able to partially locate errors but often struggled to resolve them, sometimes introducing additional errors in the process. We argue that students struggling to debug possess fragile knowledge, a lens through which we view the results. Implications: This paper highlights some of the difficulties K-12 learners have when debugging in a text-based programming language. We argue, like much related work, that effective debugging strategies should be explicitly taught, while ineffective strategies should be discouraged.



1.
Introduction

Debugging, the process of locating and resolving errors, is a vital skill within programming. Encountering errors cannot be avoided when programming; even professional software engineers reportedly spend a significant portion of their time debugging
(
Britton et al. 2020
;
Perscheid et al. 2017
)
. Proficiency in debugging reduces difficulties with learning to program for novices, with some studies suggesting that ‘good debuggers’ are usually good programmers, but not always the other way around
(
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
;
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
.

Learning a text-based programming language (TBPL) at a secondary level is mandated in several national and statewide curricula (e.g.,
(
Department for Education 2013
;
Sysło 2020
;
British Columbia Ministry of Education 2016
;
Ripani and Vazquez-Brust 2023
)
), with this number likely to increase over time. While this inclusion exposes students to traditional programming languages, debugging is a common source of struggle and emotional discomfort among students. Some students have trouble locating errors
(
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
;
Liu et al. 2017
)
and often use ineffective debugging strategies which cause errors to persist in their programs
(
Murphy et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2021
)
. Struggling to solve errors can quickly become emotionally aggravating
(
Gorson et al. 2022
)
, which may be more true for younger learners. If these struggles come at the beginning of a student’s journey of learning to program, they will likely tarnish one’s attitudes towards programming
(
Bandura 1978
;
Lishinski and Yadav 2019
)
, which may negatively influence their enjoyment of computing.

Although some studies investigate the debugging patterns used by novices, these tend to focus on undergraduate students
(
Murphy et al. 2008
;
Alqadi and Maletic 2017
;
Katz and Anderson 1987
;
Whalley et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, studies at a secondary level tend not to use TBPLs
(
Liu et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2021
;
Parkinson et al. 2024
)
, which provide a larger scope for errors. It is therefore important to investigate the behaviours exhibited by secondary learners debugging text-based programs, who may differ in the complexity of the programs they write and the methods they use to debug. Awareness of such strategies will help in improving them, in turn contributing to improving students’ programming ability and self-efficacy.

This paper reports on the debugging behaviours observed among lower secondary students (aged 12-14 years old) learning to program in Python. Students were given erroneous Python programs that they attempted to debug in an online code editor. Logs of students’ code were collected every time they ran their programs. Qualitative content analysis was used to identify students’ common behaviours when debugging. The research question for this study is:

•

What behaviours are exhibited by secondary school students when debugging erroneous text-based programs?

The behaviours that emerge from this study can be used to inform educators of common approaches that secondary students may take to try and solve errors in their programs, which is important given the rise of text-based programming in secondary schools. Researchers and educators may also use our results as a basis for developing methods that encourage positive debugging behaviours.

2.
Novice Debugging

Debugging can be conceptualised as a four-step process, comprising of evaluating the program, identifying the error in question, locating it, and finally resolving it
(
Carver and Klahr 1986
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Carver and Risinger 1987
)
. For experienced programmers debugging other’s code, whose programming comprehension ability is superior to novices
(
Gugerty and Olson 1986
;
Fix et al. 1993
)
, program evaluation is a vital first step
(
McCauley et al. 2008
)
. Software engineering studies have reported an interesting range of debugging behaviours among professional developers. The use of the debugger, a program designed to closely examine program state and facilitate successful debugging, is popular. Specialist tools such as line breakpoints, step-by-step execution, and variable inspection are most frequently used
(
Perscheid et al. 2017
;
Hirsch and Hofer 2021
;
Beller et al. 2018
)
. On the other hand, ‘printf debugging’ is almost as widespread a debugging technique
(
Perscheid et al. 2017
;
Hirsch and Hofer 2021
;
Beller et al. 2018
)
.

Beginner programmers tend to debug rather differently from their more experienced counterparts, partly due to the nature of the errors they are debugging. Many studies document the common errors encountered by novice programmers
(
Altadmri and Brown 2015
;
Pea 1986
;
Spohrer and Soloway 1986
;
Denny et al. 2012
)
. These indicate that misunderstandings about program execution tend to account for most repeatedly occurring errors in students’ programs. While ‘slips of the hand’ are common, they are more easily fixed
(
Altadmri and Brown 2015
)
. More recent K-12 studies echo the idea that a few common mistakes account for most errors students make
(
Qian et al. 2019
;
Qian and Lehman 2020
;
Staub and Chothia 2022
)
.

In addition to understanding the common errors students encounter, it is important to understand how they go about resolving them. Several studies have investigated the debugging behaviour of K-12 students, or students using a TBPL, using a range of methods.

2.1.
Undergraduate Debugging Behaviour

Success with debugging has been documented in several studies, reporting high rates of finding and fixing errors in students’ own programs
(
Katz and Anderson 1987
)
and debugging exercises
(
Alqadi and Maletic 2017
;
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
. From researchers’ observations and logs, some students were found to employ effective debugging strategies, including hand-tracing code
(
Katz and Anderson 1987
;
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
and using external tools such as the debugger
(
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
. Ineffective debugging strategies were also observed, notably tinkering
(
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
, the small and insignificant changes made to a program between compiles
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
, and rewriting large sections of programs
(
Alqadi and Maletic 2017
)
.
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
posed that the difference between less and more competent students was not the number of debugging strategies they used, rather the effectiveness with which they used them. However, we must remember that these studies investigated motivated and partially experienced students who were either in CS2
(
Alqadi and Maletic 2017
;
Katz and Anderson 1987
)
or had completed a substantial period of CS1
(
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
)
. Their debugging behaviour would likely have differed when first learning to program.

In general, debugging ability among undergraduates is mixed.
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
categorised 193 students on an introductory Java course into good and weak debuggers using programming log data. Successful debuggers could isolate errors but varied in their understanding of their programs. Weak debuggers, on the other hand, had difficulties localising errors and were unable to apply effective debugging strategies, similar to
Alqadi and Maletic 2017
. When examining three students’ debugging processes through screen recordings and a think-aloud protocol,
Whalley et al. 2023
also found a range of debugging strategies employed. The one student who completed all of the debugging exercises first spent time reading the program specification and input data. The two students who skipped this stage frequently context-switched between different parts of the program. All students resorted to a trial and error strategy at points, making speculative changes over deliberate ones.

Of course, students may not reach a stage where they can debug logical errors.
Jadud 2006
and
Denny et al. 2011
document the constant struggle that many students face with debugging syntax errors. Attempts to resolve them often involved repeatedly running and tinkering with their programs. Learners who lack sufficient knowledge of the programming language can become stuck when their code is not even compiling.

2.2.
K-12 Debugging Behaviour

One study involving K-12 students debugging in a TBPL is
Chen et al. 2013
. 72 grade 12 students self-reported their debugging strategies in an online questionnaire, which were generally ineffective. Trial and error, commenting out potentially erroneous code, and copying and pasting code were all mentioned. Most students also believed debugging skills were based on one’s ability and could not be learned, implying a fixed mindset
(
Dweck 2017
)
.

Other studies at a K-12 level tend not to focus on TBPLs.
Liu et al. 2017
collected logs from a programming game administered to 22 secondary students, observing ineffective debugging patterns such as mass deletion of code, undoing corrective changes, and again, tinkering. The authors postulate that these behaviours were due to a lack of initial program comprehension, hindering their ability to properly localise errors. In another game-based environment, trial and error and asking for help were identified as two of the four main debugging strategies employed by four primary students
(
Yan et al. 2021
)
, though the sample size is notably small here.

Tinkering is a particularly popular strategy among novice programmers, originally observed by
Perkins et al. 1986
in their study of introductory K-12 programmers. While tinkering has the potential to be a positive strategy with appropriate self-regulation, unsystematic tinkering often makes the state of the program worse
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
. In a related study of secondary students programming in BASIC, the authors coin the term ‘fragile knowledge’ to express “knowledge that is partial, hard to access, and often misused”
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
, p. 4)
. This affects the quality of the programs they write and the debugging strategies they employ.

2.3.
Summary

The studies cited repeatedly document the use of
a common set of debugging strategies by novices. Although some effective strategies are reported, these may be due to prior programming instruction, or the motivation of the students in question. When first learning a TBPL, tinkering, rewriting, and scrolling through code typically ensue. These strategies are unhelpful and unlikely to help students reach the goal state of a correct program. Such failure is not without consequence. Students are likely to emotionally react to unsuccessful debugging efforts
(
Gorson et al. 2022
;
Kinnunen and Simon 2010
)
. Educators must bear in mind the affective consequences of these cognitive difficulties.

3.
Method

3.1.
Study Design

The overall design of the study is as follows. Several classes of lower secondary students individually completed five debugging exercises and an online survey concerning their attitudes and emotions towards debugging. The debugging exercises were attempted within an online code editor that logged each time a student ran a program. This log data was then visualised and qualitatively analysed to understand the between-run changes students’ made when debugging the programs. The analysis of the survey responses are reported in a separate publication
(
Gale and Sentance 2023
)
. In this section, we report the main components of the study in more detail.

3.2.
Participants

Students were purposively sampled due to the study’s focus on lower secondary students learning a TBPL. We focused on lower secondary education (grades 6-8, ages 11-14) as this is when students begin learning a TBPL in England
(
Department for Education 2013
)
. Sampling began by inviting local secondary teachers to partake in the study and verifying that they taught a TBPL to their lower secondary students. Three teachers from two schools accepted, both of which were located in a suburban area with relatively high socioeconomic indicators and above-average general achievement. They were briefed on the format of the study and given the opportunity to provide feedback on it.

75 students participated in the study, though the final sample consisted of 73 students due to the attrition of two students. These students had been learning Python in their computing lessons for up to a year and had been taught a block-based programming language in their secondary schools prior to this. The consent of the student, their teacher, and a parent or guardian was provided before participation, in accordance with the study procedure approved by our department’s ethics committee.

The gender, school, and year group of the students was self-reported. 36 identified as male, 24 as female, three identified as other and 10 preferred not to disclose. All students were in grades 7-8 and therefore almost certainly aged 12-14. However, since their age was not collected, some may have been slightly outside of this age range.

3.3.
Procedure and Data Collection

The study was run by the three participating computing teachers, without the authors present, in an hour-long computing lesson.

3.3.1.
Debugging Exercises

Students’ first task was to complete a set of five debugging exercises in an online code editor. We used a custom code editor that students had not used before to allow us control over what data we collected. To ensure ease of use, the editor was minimal in its design, containing only an editor, a run button, and an output terminal. Although the study was conducted in a regular computing lesson, students were instructed not to collaborate nor ask for the teacher’s help, as we were interested in the individual debugging behaviours of students. We advised teachers to spend half an hour on the debugging exercises, but the actual time spent on them was ultimately up to them.

Each exercise comprised of a short erroneous Python program, a description of the program’s intended function, and the number of errors present in the program. Python was the chosen programming language due to the popularity of the language in English secondary schools
(
Sentance et al. 2022
)
and the fact that all participating teachers taught it.

The Python programs included a range of syntax, runtime, and logical errors common among beginner programmers
(
Pea 1986
;
Smith and Rixner 2019
;
Kohn 2019
;
Sorva 2021
)
. The exercises were developed by the authors, with the second author being an experienced computing educator. Figure

A key distinction we make here and in the remainder of the paper is that of the runtime and type error. We define runtime errors as errors that are raised during the execution of a program. In Python, this does include
TypeError
’s, as Python is dynamically typed. However, we categorise type errors separately from runtime errors as we perceive them as semantically distinct. This is also the case for some of the studies used to design the debugging exercises. Students who struggle to debug type errors may display different misunderstandings from those struggling with other runtime errors.

Students were not given feedback on the number of errors they had resolved and were consequently not informed if they had successfully completed a debugging exercise. Instead, students were encouraged to test the code frequently and move on to the next task when they wished.

3.3.2.
Programming Log Data

Logging, the recording of a series of events to analyse some process, is a practice utilised in many computing education studies (e.g.,
(
Jadud 2006
;
Norris et al. 2008
;
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
;
Thomas et al. 2003
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Brown et al. 2014
)
). This provides a dependable picture of students’ actual debugging behaviour, which is what we wanted to investigate. This is perhaps particularly useful for younger secondary students, who may be less able to articulate their strategies effectively.

Like some of these studies
(
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
;
Jadud 2006
)
, we logged every time a student ran their program, enabling fine-grained analysis of students’ changes on a run-by-run basis. We believe the granularity of this data is sufficient to understand students’ debugging behaviour. Students must run a program, which is in turn logged, for their changes to have any effect on the program. This would allow a suitably sized dataset of students’ runs to be generated.

Keystroke data was also considered as this would have tracked every single change that each student made
(
Thomas et al. 2003
)
. However, as we discuss in Section
3.4
, we primarily wanted to qualitatively analyse students’ log data. The number of keystrokes made by all of the students would have been far greater than the number of snapshots, making any qualitative analysis hard to conduct reliably. This comes at the cost of missing changes students make and revert between runs, but we see this as a suitable compromise. As mentioned, if students want to test the changes they make, they must run their program.

Each time a student ran their program, the following was logged:

•

The exercise the student was attempting.

•

The students’ program at the time of running (snapshot).

•

Whether the code successfully executed. If unsuccessful, the error message thrown by the program was also stored.

•

A timestamp of when the student ran their code.

This data was saved to a database when a student completed an exercise attempt.

3.3.3.
Post-Survey

Immediately after attempting the debugging exercises, students completed a short survey related to their attitudes and emotions towards debugging. We reference responses to the survey items listed below, as they help to contextualise some of the students’ debugging behaviours.

•

“How well do you feel you performed when solving the errors in the programming exercises?”
(answered on a Likert scale of 1-5).

•

“What techniques did you use to find and fix the errors in the programming exercises?”
(free-text response).

3.4.
Analysis

Programming log data has not been extensively analysed compared to other data in computing education research. Usually, studies report results on a large quantitative scale (e.g.,
(
Jadud 2006
;
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
;
Altadmri and Brown 2015
)
). We, however, wanted to manually examine students’ changes between runs, as we believed this would give us an in-depth insight that was likely to be missed with quantitative analysis, a point also noted by
Jadud 2006
.

As a result, our analysis is mostly qualitative. While individual snapshots provide little scope for interpretation, a sequence of snapshots can be considered a body of text. These snapshots express a set of debugging behaviours, that is, a collection of changes made by students in an attempt to fix one or more errors. We believe these behaviours indicate students’ thought processes when debugging.

We define strategies as groups of behaviours intentionally employed to accomplish a particular task
(
Alexander and Judy 1988
)
. Although the survey responses provide some insight into students’ intentions, we can only infer the meaning behind their actions with their log data. Furthermore, strategies may include physical components, such as asking for help
(
Yan et al. 2021
)
, that we did not collect. We therefore only consider debugging strategies in the discussion section.

Based on the above considerations, we analysed students’ debugging behaviour using qualitative content analysis
(
Mayring 2000
)
. An inductive approach was decided due to the lack of research exploring secondary students’ debugging, or even programming, behaviour in a TBPL, which may differ from typical undergraduate behaviour.

The content analysis began with an extended period of reviewing the log data, first to consider what claims our analysis could soundly and confidently make, then to inductively generate descriptive open codes for each exercise attempt. After initial examination of the log data, we acknowledged the range of planes that students’ changes could be interpreted on. We decided to interpret students’ changes based on the particular programming constructs edited, and the effect, if any, these changes had on the program state. This approach was taken to improve the repeatability of the coding process. Other approaches would have required interpretation across sets of runs, which soon becomes overly complex and unreliable.

The open codes were later abstracted into more generalisable axial codes after further examination of the data. This led to low-level codes within the categorisation that appear quite objective. Students’ changes were still interpreted when deciding on the level of detail to maintain in the low-level codes, however. Further interpretation was also necessary when formulating categories of debugging behaviour, which came next. After initial formulation, these categories were progressively refined through discussions between the authors and re-examination of the log data. This importantly allowed comparison between the prevalence of different behaviours, which was one of the main reasons for applying content analysis. Where appropriate, we also conducted quantitative analysis of the dataset to support the findings yielded from the content analysis.

To facilitate the content analysis, we developed a custom tool to visualise students’ exercise attempts. This can be viewed online
2
2

2

https://github.com/LaurieGale10/program-logs-viewer
, though we did not have ethical approval to publish students’ data with it.

3.5.
Validity and Reliability

To ensure content validity of the debugging exercises, two of the participating teachers provided feedback on the exercises’ appropriateness for their students, which was acted upon accordingly. The teachers were satisfied with the difficulty of the final exercises.

To ensure the categorisation was interpretable and reliable, the second author performed coding at several stages, using the categorisation developed by the first author. The first of these was at the end of the open coding stage, where the second author coded five exercise attempts to gauge the scope of the coding system. They then coded 10 students’ attempts after the initial categorisation was formed. The categorisation was refined after both of these discussions.

Once the categorisation was complete, the inter-rater reliability of the final categorisation was verified on 10 other students’ exercise attempts using Kappa’s coefficient. This was important to calculate as we wanted to compare the prevalence of different behaviours, which is derived from their frequencies
(
Mayring 2000
;
McDonald et al. 2019
)
. The authors reached a consensus of
\kappa=0.64
, indicating moderate agreement
(
McHugh 2012
)
. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved to refine the clarity of the final categorisation.

We categorised students’ debugging behaviours into six main themes defined below.

(1)

First move:
Whether a student ran their program before making changes to it, or vice versa.

(2)

Positive debugging behaviours:
Behaviours that facilitate successful debugging.

(3)

Introduced additional errors:
Added errors that did not originally exist in a program.

(4)

Resolved errors:
Removed one of the errors originally present in a program.

(5)

Inconsequential changes:
Changes that have no impact on the state of a program.

(6)

Miscellaneous changes:
Changes that do not fit into the above categories.

4.1.
First Move

Students demonstrate two broad behaviours with respect to the first time they run the program. They may run the program before making any changes (run-first), or edit the program before initially running it (change-first).

Most exercise attempts (66%) began by running-first, which often happened soon after beginning the exercise. The median run-first time was 17 seconds (
skew=4.26
), likely not enough time to read the exercise description nor comprehend the program. While these students may have used the error message to help localise the error, the median time between their runs was 0.922 seconds (
skew=5.90
), suggesting a general lack of time spent comprehending the programs.

The picture for change-first students is different. At the point of making initial edits to the program, students did not have the error message for guidance. Changes hence varied in their correctness, though 84% of the change-first attempts still failed to initially run. Naturally, students who changed-first on an exercise usually first ran their program later on, taking a median of 63 seconds (
skew=1.47
) to do so.

4.2.
Positive Debugging Behaviours - First Change on Line Referred to in Error Message

First change on line referred to in error message
sub-category, applied whenever a new error message appears and a student’s next run contains changes to the line it referred to. Almost all students (71, 97%) exhibited this behaviour.

count
variable being declared as a string on line 2. However, the error message, as shown below, refers to line 4. As a result, 31 students (52% of attempts on the exercise) made initial non-corrective changes on this line after running-first.

4.3.1.
Syntax Errors

Out of the error types in the categorisation, students most often introduced additional syntax errors. Assignments, variable references, and program whitespace were all common places for incorrect syntactical changes. Consistent with the theme of small edits, students often removed or added a few characters that violated Python syntax. Some of these could be considered ‘slips of the hand’, which were fixed in the following few runs. Some students, however, did not resolve the syntax errors they introduced; in 75 (23%) of the exercise attempts, students added syntax errors without resolving any. As Leandro’s example above demonstrates, if a student’s changes were predominantly on the same line in the program, the error message would not typically change, giving them no feedback that their changes were worsening the program’s syntactic validity. Students were in a syntax error state for a median of 88 seconds per attempted exercise (
skew=1.59
).

4.3.2.
Runtime and Type Errors

We report type errors together with runtime errors due to their similarity in the categorisation. Changes in this category tended to involve variables in some respect. A particularly common edit exhibited by 23 students (32%) was converting an assignment operator (
=
) to an equality operator (
==
) in assignment statements, usually leading to a
NameError
. Type errors added often involved variable assignments or references, typically erroneously removing or adding casting functions.

4.4.
Resolved Errors

As well as introducing additional errors to their programs, 66 students (90%) resolved at least one error in their exercise attempts. We only focus on errors originally present in the debugging exercises. This is because it is difficult to discuss every error a student solved due to the added complexity of students resolving errors they introduced themselves.

4.4.1.
General Behaviours

We observed some common styles of resolving errors for behaviours related to syntax, runtime, and type errors. The first of these is a ‘correct resolution’. These changes entirely remove the error in question, such that the part of the program previously containing an error is completely correct after the change.

Leah’s attempt to exercise 2 exemplifies the correct resolution of all syntax and logical errors originally present in the program. Leah resolves all of these errors before running the code, taking 100 seconds to do so. The sole consequence of these changes is the complete removal of the errors.

4.4.2.
Resolved Logical Errors

Resolving logical errors can be a more arduous process than the aforementioned error types, often requiring more intentional testing and debugging. As the inputs entered by students were not logged, it was difficult to assert whether a student was testing their program. Nevertheless, the point at which a logical error was correctly resolved, relative to the program state, can be derived through qualitative inspection.

Interestingly, 53 students (73%) resolved a logical error while their program was not successfully executing. This meant that they received no feedback on the success of their change. In some cases, students seemingly believed that resolving a logical error would remove, or at least affect, the error message their program was throwing. An unfortunate consequence of this was the reversion of logical corrections in their following runs.

Some logical resolutions took place after one or more successful executions, usually executed in quick succession. These may have been testing the program or verifying its output, though we cannot conclude students’ intentions. This was particularly popular for exercises containing no
input()
statements, namely exercises 1, 4, and 5. We discuss the nature of these repeated runs more in Section
4.6.1
.

4.5.
Inconsequential Changes

Some changes that students made had no effect on the state of their programs. They did not introduce additional errors nor resolve existing ones. We name these inconsequential changes. These behaviours were almost universal; 71 of the 73 students (97%) made an inconsequential change at some point in the debugging exercises. Popular examples include altering the whitespace between or within lines (64 students, 88%), editing the output to add or remove some spacing (48 students, 66%), and adjusting the Python symbols used in a program (30 students, 41%).

4.6.
Miscellaneous Behaviours

Not all behaviours fitted into the above categories. They nonetheless provide insight into students’ thought processes.

4.6.1.
Repeated Runs

A particularly widespread behaviour observed in 69 students (95%) was the repeated running of code. This is easily measured automatically; if two or more consecutive snapshots are identical, the student has repeatedly run their program without making changes or reverted any changes they did make between runs. There were 4,809 snapshots that were identical to their previous snapshot across the exercise attempts, accounting for 67% of all snapshots in the sample. 15 students (21%) even repeatedly ran a program at the beginning of an exercise attempt before making any changes. Despite its prevalence, only 10 students mentioned repeatedly running code in the post-survey.

The slightly more common version of this behaviour was when students’ programs were successfully executing, accounting for 2,526 runs (35%). This may imply testing for logical errors, as students may have been re-running the same program but entering different inputs. Since we did not log students’ inputs into the programs, this is not something that we can conclusively assert. However, this sort of testing does not seem to generally be the case. 86% of the repeated successful runs were made in exercise 5, a program that does not contain any input statements, by 40 of the students who attempted the exercise (78%). While it does have some random variables that encourage multiple repeated runs, the median time between these successful executions was 0.317 seconds
skew=16.40
. This does not give the student time to comprehend and verify the output’s correctness, so this behaviour seems confusing. Of course, some repeated runs may have been legitimate testing with different inputs that were masked by the large number of runs generated in short durations.

Students also repeatedly ran their programs when they contained errors, accounting for 2,283 (32%) runs. The time between these unchanged runs was again largely skewed (median = 0.367 seconds,
skew=7.86
). While students may be repeatedly entering incorrect inputs, or deliberately trying to break their programs, the times between the runs again suggests otherwise. This means students were usually met with the same error message when exhibiting this behaviour. This version of repeated runs serves no function in getting the students closer to a correct program.

4.6.2.
Reverted Previous Changes

61 students (84%) restored part or all of a program to an earlier state. In context, this behaviour can be viewed as part of an iterative process of making small experimental changes, running the program repeatedly several times, and then reverting changes if they were not corrective. However, as noted in Section
4.4.2
, students sometimes reverted corrective changes.

4.7.
End State of Programs

In addition to the categorisation, we manually examined the final snapshot of each exercise attempt. If an exercise contained no runs, or each run in the exercise attempt was identical to the original state of the program, we classified the program as unattempted. We defined a correct program as one that included no errors and outputted what was specified in the exercise descriptions. This can be treated as a proxy of performance on the debugging exercises as it indicates the correctness of the program and the point at which the student decided to move on.

Upon closer examination, eight students (11%) ended every attempted exercise in a correct state, in contrast to 26 students (36%) who ended every attempted exercise with at least one error. The remaining 39 students (53%) both successfully and unsuccessfully completed some of the exercises.

The Levenshtein distance between the original program and Declan’s final snapshot is 53, 40 above the median across all attempted exercises.

6.
DISCUSSION

The categorisation and case studies evidence the wide range of behaviours exhibited by the students in our study. While most students were able to debug some errors, many of the behaviours exhibited were ineffective and moved them
further away
from a correct solution. We now provide some detailed commentary on these behaviours and the categorisation, looking to previous work and a theoretical lens to do so.

6.1.
Fragile Knowledge

To help understand the behaviours observed and the strategies they imply, we view the results through the theoretical lens of fragile knowledge
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
)
, a concept we introduce in Section
2.2
. Fragile knowledge is comprised of four components, all of which are relevant to our findings:

•

Partial knowledge:
Concepts that students do not fully understand, possibly because they have not yet been taught them. This is considered the simplest form of fragile knowledge and can be reduced with further instruction. For most of the students in this study, their knowledge of Python lists is likely to be partial as they had not been taught this data structure at the point of participation.

•

Inert knowledge:
Knowledge that a student possesses but is unable to recall when required. For example, a student may know to use the equality operator (
==
) when asked by their teacher, but not able to successfully apply this knowledge when an if statement erroneously reads ‘
if x = 4:
’.

•

Misplaced knowledge:
Knowledge that a student recalls at the incorrect time. Related to the previous example, a student may incorrectly replace a
=
with
==
in an assignment with the vague assurance that this operator is sometimes necessary.

•

Conglomerated knowledge:
The combination of several programming elements in a semantically or syntactically incorrect manner ‘in an attempt to provide the computer with the information it needs’
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
, p. 7)
. Some students demonstrated this with if statement conditions, merging several syntactically invalid expressions or symbols in a potential attempt to convey some meaning.

Close tracking
is another idea linked to fragile knowledge
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
;
Perkins et al. 1986
)
, defined as reading a portion of or the whole program to understand the function of its execution.

Fragile knowledge has been referenced and expanded upon in several studies
(
Jadud 2006
;
Lister et al. 2004
)
. We believe several aspects of fragile knowledge are prevalent among the students we observed.

6.2.
Ineffective Strategies

The majority of students experienced periods of difficulty with debugging. During such periods, they tended to use groups of behaviours that resemble ineffective debugging strategies. Indeed, these may not even be considered strategies due to the apparent lack of intent behind them
(
Alexander and Judy 1988
)
. Although the log data does not permit us to conclude students’ intentions, we do discuss some common patterns of behaviours here to further address our research question.

6.2.1.
Lack of Initial Program Comprehension

Given that students were debugging foreign code, it is vital to spend time reading the program and any relevant information, such as the exercise description
(
Katz and Anderson 1987
)
. This marks part of the first step of debugging
(
Carver and Klahr 1986
)
, providing the contextual knowledge necessary to identify discrepancies.

In actuality, many students ran their programs within seconds, with little indication of program comprehension in the following runs, corroborating with other studies
(
Perkins et al. 1986
;
Whalley et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2017
)
. Although students may have used the error message for initial guidance, running the program so quickly does not allow them to view the program as a functional whole, instead viewing erroneous parts in isolation. To reference the block model
(
Schulte 2008
)
, students here seem to be stuck at the atom or block level. This is particularly problematic for logical errors, which may involve interactions between several parts of the program.

This strategy suggests that students are not
close tracking
the programs to begin with, which is an important component of debugging
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
.

Of course, the relative importance of initial program comprehension is lesser for students debugging their own programs. We argue that it is still important for several reasons. First, students writing larger-scale programs will likely be unable to retain information on what every part of their program does, especially if their work is spaced over several sessions. In this case, they may have to ‘re-comprehend’ parts of programs when encountering errors. Even if this is not the case, previous work has highlighted the importance of developing some tracing and reading ability before writing code
(
Lopez et al. 2008
;
Lister et al. 2009
)
. Program comprehension is therefore still critical for students debugging their own programs. Additionally, students will also encounter errors in programs written by peers or large language models. In this context, the lack of program comprehension found in our results is still worrying.

6.2.2.
Partial Localisation

A widespread observation among struggling students was the ability to locate the line of an error coupled with the inability to resolve it. These students’ changes were often not directed beyond the line specified in the error message, making it difficult to discern whether students had actually located the errors referred to in the error messages displayed. This finding aligns with
Ahmadzadeh et al. 2005
but contradicts
Katz and Anderson 1987
, who claimed that the bug location strategy used has little effect on the rest of the debugging process.

This may be in part due to the ambiguity of the error messages thrown by the code editor: ‘
bad input on line 2
’ provides little context into the cause of the error. However, error messages have been reported as a source of struggle for other students in other studies
(
Becker et al. 2019
)
.

We believe that this strategy indicates
partial knowledge
of the language used in error messages, leading to a lack of
close tracking
. If students had more knowledge of such language and keywords, their changes may have appeared more fully directed. Alternatively, the error messages themselves could be made clearer, which has been experimented with in many studies (e.g.,
(
Becker 2016
;
Phung et al. 2023
)
) and implemented in some beginner programming environments
(
Kohn and Manaris 2020
)
.

6.2.3.
Tinkering

Students displaying either of the aforementioned strategies often followed a process of making small changes, running the program repeatedly, sometimes reverting changes, and repeating. This is what
Perkins et al. 1986
define as
tinkering
and what some studies describe as trial and error
(
Chen et al. 2013
;
Yan et al. 2021
;
Whalley et al. 2023
)
.

In the context of debugging, tinkering has significant problems as a strategy. First, additional errors can quickly accumulate, as students are unlikely to revert every incorrect change they make
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
. Second, students may make corrective changes and revert them soon after. This has been observed among secondary
(
Liu et al. 2017
)
and undergraduate students
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
, arguably demonstrating a level of
partial knowledge
in that students do not possess the understanding to assess the impact of their change.

The changes made when tinkering vary widely. Students often added Python symbols to locations that were syntactically or logically invalid, or incorrectly combined several constructs. These behaviours embody the idea of
misplaced
and
conglomerated knowledge
respectively, as such changes are correct in the right context, but add errors when incorrectly applied.
Perkins and Martin 1986
hypothesise that misplaced knowledge may be activated in situations of desperation. When students’ initial changes do not resolve any errors, they may feel little choice but to apply those changes in slightly different configurations, in the hope that one of them is corrective. The link between cognitive and affective struggles with debugging starts to be established here.

6.3.
Barriers to Successful Debugging

Not everyone struggled with the debugging exercises. Some students resolved errors with apparent ease, demonstrating the successful application of the debugging process. Although these students do demonstrate effective behaviours, there do not seem to be as many of them as ineffective behaviours. Rather, these students tended not to resort to the ineffective behaviours that prevented successful debugging so often. The way we interpreted the log data may have caused us to yield a higher prevalence of ineffective behaviours. There are a very limited number of ways to correct the syntax of ‘
while x = 2:
’; there are a large number of ways to incorrectly alter it.

Nonetheless, there were some trends seen among students who debugged successfully, which we use to consider barriers preventing more students from adopting effective debugging behaviours and strategies.

6.3.1.
Fragile Knowledge

Through the theoretical lens we have used, we see fragile knowledge as a fundamental barrier to successful debugging. The relationship between strategy and knowledge is undoubtedly complex, but
Perkins and Martin 1986
state that ‘fragile knowledge will not sustain any sophisticated problem-solving’ (p. 28), which seems an apt summary of much of our observations. Where students demonstrate fragile knowledge of programming concepts, flow, or syntax, unsuccessful debugging strategies often follow.

This is not to say that students who successfully debugged possess no fragile knowledge. Students will possess differing magnitudes of fragile knowledge and certain aspects of Python are more costly to have fragile knowledge about. We discuss one of these below.

6.3.2.
Knowledge of Program Syntax

Much variation in debugging behaviour took place when students were trying to fix syntax errors. However, students who generally debugged more effectively often corrected them without much difficulty. These students seemed well aware of Python syntax and resolved subtle inconsistencies where less confident students would tinker. Because the majority of the debugging exercises contained syntax errors, students less confident with Python syntax would often struggle to progress beyond them. Even when attempting to debug other errors, some students still made syntactically invalid changes.

One way of mitigating this struggle is to use tooling that makes invalid syntax impossible, or at least less possible. Block-based languages of course do this, but frame-based editing
(
Kölling et al. 2015
;
Weill-Tessier et al. 2024
)
is another approach for developing text-based programs while reducing the possibility of syntax errors. Adoption of such environments would in turn eradicate some of the ineffective syntax-related behaviours we have observed.

6.3.3.
Time Taken to Achieve Program Execution

Related to this point is the time taken for students to get their programs successfully executing. As we saw in Section
5.2
, a benefit of Declan’s debugging approach was that he was able to get the program running relatively quickly. This provided a foundation for further experimentation; when he introduced additional errors, it was obvious as the program threw an error message that it did not previously. Reverting these changes becomes the next natural step.

For students who do not get their program running, however, the tinkering that often follows usually increases the distance away from a correct program. Students frequently received no feedback when they made erroneous changes and hence had less pressure to revert them, allowing errors to accumulate and debugging to become more difficult.

6.3.4.
Affective Factors

Students’ attitudes and emotions towards debugging in the post-survey varied widely
(
Gale and Sentance 2023
)
. Although we did not compare these responses with behaviours, the overall strategies students used were likely affected by their attitudes to resolving errors. Some students may have been more emotionally composed and motivated to continue debugging when struggling to resolve errors, while some students’ tinkering may express an emotional disconnect with the exercises
(
Perkins et al. 1986
)
. Alternatively, some students may have spent little time comprehending the programs due to a lack of self-regulation or negative initial experiences with debugging. Our point here is not to speculate ways that students’ attitudes and emotions may have affected their debugging behaviour, but rather to acknowledge that some influence from the affective domain is highly likely.

6.4.
Limitations

The main limitations of this study regard the categorisation. First, it is binary, which skews the true prevalence of certain behaviours. A student may have made the same incorrect change to one or five assignments in a program; their behaviour would still constitute the same sub-category. Second, the categorisation does not include resolutions to errors that students introduced themselves. These changes were deemed to add too much complexity and volume to the analysis which may have affected its reliability. However, this does hide certain behaviours students used when rectifying their own errors. Third, the majority of the behaviours in the categorisation focus on changes between pairs of runs. This was also decided upon as it becomes difficult to reliably code for behaviours over several runs, though we accept the depth of analysis is consequently reduced. Nonetheless, even large changes between runs can be difficult to interpret, helping to explain the moderate agreement achieved in Section
3.4
.

The debugging exercises are also inevitably limited in their ability to capture the debugging behaviours that students would typically use in the classroom. Some of the ineffective debugging behaviours observed may have been because the exercises were too difficult for some of the students. However, the participating teachers agreed in advance that the exercises were of appropriate difficulty for their students. Post-survey responses also indicated that most students felt they performed adequately on the exercises
(
Gale and Sentance 2023
)
, suggesting that the exercises were not overly difficult for most students. Regardless, a pilot study would have been useful to gain feedback from students.

Despite the utility of the log data, it did not track students’ inputs into successfully executing programs. Such information, appropriately sanitised, would have provided clarity into students’ testing behaviours, which we could not properly analyse. Furthermore, some data expressing students’ intentions would have been very useful in understanding the strategies they were intentionally utilising when debugging. This could have been done through further survey questions or retrospective interviews
(
Gorson et al. 2022
;
Atiq and Batra 2024
)
, for example. We recommend such data be collected in future studies.

Finally, we acknowledge that the huge variation in students’ changes makes it hard to achieve true theoretical saturation. While the categorisation is intended to be both exhaustive and generalisable, more students’ log data may have displayed new behaviours that could not be presently categorised. To help achieve saturation, we included all-encompassing sub-categories relating to ‘other statements’.

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

This study has investigated the debugging behaviour of lower secondary students learning to program in a text-based programming language. Qualitative content analysis was applied to the logs of 73 students’ attempts at a set of online Python debugging exercises, generating a categorisation of common behaviours.

Many students struggled to successfully resolve the errors in the exercises, demonstrating an array of ineffective debugging behaviours in the process. Although students were generally able to partially locate errors, their subsequent changes were often illogical, providing no corrective benefit. In some cases, these changes added previously non-existent errors to the program.

Not all students struggled to successfully debug. Rather than displaying definitively effective behaviours, more successful students tended to resort to ineffective behaviours less often. We used the idea of fragile knowledge
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
)
to discuss the strategies inferred from the behaviours observed, as well as barriers that may be preventing more students from successfully debugging.

Our findings contribute to our understanding of the debugging behaviour of secondary students learning a TBPL, which is currently limited due to the lack of studies in this area. Given the increasing number of curricula mandating the teaching of TBPLs at a secondary level (e.g.,
(
Department for Education 2013
;
Sysło 2020
;
British Columbia Ministry of Education 2016
;
Ripani and Vazquez-Brust 2023
)
), we believe this is an important area of work. Millions of students will learn to program in a TBPL, meaning many students may resort to the ineffective debugging behaviours we have observed when struggling to resolve errors. These commonly have negative emotional and attitudinal consequences
(
Gorson et al. 2022
;
Kinnunen and Simon 2010
)
which can diminish one’s experiences of learning to program.

Educators must therefore be aware of both the effective and ineffective debugging behaviours that students are likely to employ, several of which we have identified. To strengthen these findings, more small- and large-scale research into the debugging behaviour of secondary students should be conducted. Ideally, studies should combine both students’
actual
behaviours, through methods such as logging or screen recordings, and
intended
behaviours, through methods such as retrospective interviews or think-aloud protocols. This would give a clearer idea of students’ intent and strategy with regard to debugging, which we were not able to conclude. Based on this, teaching approaches and tools that promote effective debugging strategies must be developed, investigated, and refined. Some work has been done in this area
(
Carver and Risinger 1987
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
)
, yet it does not seem to be widespread in teaching practice. Such research will help students to improve their debugging ability, positively affecting their experiences of learning to program.
