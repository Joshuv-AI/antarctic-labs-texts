The intraoperative localization in recurrent tracheoesophageal fistula after esophageal atresia repair: a comparative study
Authors: Kaiyun Hua, Junmin Liao, Dayan Sun, Dingding Wang, Yong Zhao, Yichao Gu, Shuangshuang Li, Peize Wang, Yanan Zhang, Jinshi Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent tracheoesophageal fistula (rTEF) is a rare complication following initial esophageal atresia (EA) surgical repair, posing challenges in localization the fistula during surgery due to severe thoracic adhesions and structural ambiguity from previous operations.ObjectiveWe introduced two new localization methods for rTEF patients during surgery and aimed to compare the impact of using these localization techniques versus not using them on the surgical outcomes for rTEF patients.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of rTEF cases that underwent thoracoscopic repair at our hospital from September 2017 to December 2024. Patients were divided into localization group and non-localization group based on whether using intraoperative localization techniques, and comparative analysis of clinical variables was conducted between groups.ResultsA total of 106 patients were included in this study, undergoing a total of 113 thoracoscopic rTEF repair surgeries at our center. Their fistula type included 89 cases of tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF), 19 cases of esophageal-pulmonary fistula (EPF), 3 cases of esophageal bronchial fistula (EBF), and 2 cases of combined EPF and TEF. All cases were categorized based on whether using localization techniques, resulting in the localization group (n = 52) and the non-localization group (n = 61). The median operation time in the localization group (2.5 h) was significantly lower than in the latter (3.0 h) (P = 0.001), and regardless of the fistula type being TEF or EPF. Additionally, the average postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the localization group (17.7 ± 7.5 days) than in the non-localization group (23.6 ± 20.0 days) regarding the fistula type of TEF (P = 0.03).ConclusionsThe use of localization techniques in thoracoscopic surgery for rTEF leads to better outcomes, evidenced by reduced operation time and hospital stay, suggesting enhanced surgical accuracy and improved patient postoperative recovery.Level of evidenceLEVEL III.Keywords: Thoracoscopic surgery, Recurrent tracheoesophageal fistula, Pediatrics, Intraoperative localization, Indocyanine green

Background

Approximately 10% of children who have undergone surgery for EA will experience a recurrence of tracheoesophageal fistula [1, 2]. Although endoscopic treatments are increasingly reported [3], surgical intervention remains the main approach [4]. This complication presents a formidable obstacle in surgical repair due to the anatomical distortions and dense adhesions that often result from the initial EA anastomosis.

In response to these challenges, our team introduced the use of a guidewire for intraoperative localization of our rTEF patients, a technique first introduced by Coran [5]. However, due to the risk of injury to the fragile trachea and esophagus with a rigid guidewire, our team used a thread instead of a rigid guidewire. Although, there were still potential difficulties in placing the guidewire [6, 7]. As a result, our team also introduced indocyanine green (ICG), a contrast agent used for localization in other pediatric surgeries [6], to assist in the localization of the fistula under fluorescence thoracoscopy. In order to compare the impact of using these localization techniques versus not using them on the surgical outcomes for rTEF patients, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of patients with rTEF in our center over the past six years.

Materials and methods

Participants inclusion

Children who experienced rTEF after EA surgery between September 2017 and December 2023 were included to research. The surgical procedure for these patients is thoracoscopic fistula repair. Cases of rTEF repaired through a cervical approach were excluded from the study.

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the medical records, focusing on the clinical data of their initial esophageal atresia surgeries and the rTEF repair surgeries, including gender, gestational age, birth weight, EA classification according to Gross, initial EA surgical approach (thoracoscopic or open), and postoperative complications arising from the initial EA surgery. We also documented the recur times of rTEF, weight and age at the time of rTEF surgery, the type of rTEF (TEF, EPF or EBF), whether using the location technique in thoracoscopic repair surgery for rTEF, the operation time, the duration of postoperative mechanical ventilation, the length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU) following surgery, the overall postoperative hospitalization duration, and the incidence of postoperative complications such as pneumothorax, pneumonia, pyothorax, esophageal leak, esophageal stricture, and recurrence of rTEF. Follow-up data was obtained through telephone calls and outpatient visits. This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University, National Center for Children’s Health (2019-k-333) in full compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. The requirement for patient informed consent for this retrospective study was waived.

Selection of localization technique

In November of the year 2021, the implementation of an enhanced guidewire for the precise localization of fistula was used. Subsequently, in November of the year 2022, the utilization of ICG to aid in localization was introduced. The choice of localization method was guided by the algorithm depicted in Fig. 1: Patients first underwent a bronchoscopy while under anesthesia in the operation day. In cases where the orifice of the fistula was discernible upon bronchoscopy, an effort was made to insert a guidewire, which was then followed by the application of ICG spray directly into the fistula. In instances where the guidewire could not be passed through, ICG was applied directly into the fistula during the bronchoscopy. In situations where the fistula orifice can’t be detected under bronchoscopy, such as EPF, an esophagoscopy was conducted to further seeking of the fistula orifice. If feasible, ICG was then applied under esophagoscopy through the orifice. Should the orifice remain elusive even under esophagoscopy, ICG was sprayed in the vicinity of the initial esophageal anastomosis. The specific process for guidewire placement and ICG application was introduced by another article of our team.

Algorithm of intraoperative localization techniques: On the day of surgery, patients underwent bronchoscopy under anesthesia. If the fistula orifice was visible, a guidewire was inserted followed by direct ICG spray. If the guidewire failed, ICG was applied directly. For undetectable orifices, like in EPF, esophagoscopy was performed to locate and apply ICG to the orifice, or else ICG was sprayed near the initial anastomosis site

Guidewire localization of fistula

Under general anesthesia, the surgical team inserted a guidewire from the tracheal aspect of the fistula into the esophageal under the guidance of bronchoscopy. The guidewire was then extracted from the esophagus or stomach under esophagoscopy shaping a ‘U’ loop, and then a silk thread was tied to the end of the guidewire. The guidewire was subsequently replaced with the silk thread and the ends of the thread were fixed at the corner of the mouth. This method allows for precise intraoperative localization of the fistula by pulling the thread to provide guidance (Fig. 2).

Guidewire localization of fistula: Translation: (A) Model of the intraoperative guide wire (Boston Scientific); (B) The surgeon operates the bronchoscope to insert the guide wire into the fistula from the tracheal side; (C) The surgeon switches to a gastroscope to retrieve the guide wire from the esophageal side, creating a U-shaped loop; (D) Due to the stiffness of the guide wire and to prevent tissue injury, the surgeon replaces it with a silk suture, which is secured to the child’s head and face. During surgery, the assistant uses tension on the suture to locate the position of the fistula

ICG fluorescence imaging technique

During bronchoscopy, the surgeon administered 0.5 ml of ICG (at a concentration of 0.125 mg/ml) directly into the fistula, if the fistula orifice can be found. If not, the ICG will be sprayed by esophagoscopy. The patient then underwent thoracoscopic repair using a fluorescence imaging thoracoscopy system (DPMENDOCAM-01). The surgeon initially separated the pleural adhesions in natural light mode. After exposing the esophagus, the fluorescence imaging mode was activated, revealing the location of the fistula with clarity.

Patient grouping

The thoracoscopic surgeries involving this cohort can be divided into two groups based on the use of localization techniques during surgery: the localization group and the non-localization group. In order to analyze the impact of intraoperative localization techniques on surgeries for different types of fistulae, the TEF cases can be further divided into the localization group and the non-localization group. Regarding the patients with EPF, they can be divided into the ICG group and the non-ICG group based on whether using of ICG in surgery. In order to investigate the influence of localization techniques on the repair surgeries for patients with repeated recurrences of TEF, because of their severer thoracic adhesions and structural ambiguity may make bigger challenges for surgeons, we divided these patients into the localization group and the non-localization group for comparison.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by SPSS 28.0. Continuous variables were presented as the mean with standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on the results of the normality hypothesis test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Pearson’s χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, two independent samples t-tests and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test were used to compare characteristics between groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Patient characteristics

A total of 106 patients were included in this study. According to the Gross classification, there were no cases of type I and II EA. The cohort included 103 cases of type III, two cases of type IV, and one case of type V. And 113 thoracoscopic rTEF repair surgeries were performed for these patients. There were 89 cases of TEF, 19 cases of EPF, 3 cases of EBF, and 2 cases of EPF combined with TEF. Ninety-two cases were the first-time repair of rTEF, 18 were the second-time repair, and 3 were the third-time repair. The median operation time for the surgeries was 2.5 (2.0, 3.5) hours, the median postoperative duration of mechanical ventilation was 115 (89.4, 144.0) hours, the median postoperative ICU stay was 6 (5.0, 8.5) days, and the median duration of total hospitalization after surgery was 16 (13, 23) days. Postoperatively, 20 patients experienced pneumothorax, 29 had esophageal leakage, and 34 developed severe pneumonia and pyothorax. After a mean follow-up of 38.2 ± 22.4 months, 64 children required dilation for esophageal stricture, and 16 tracheoesophageal fistulas recurred. There were 6 deaths in this group: four patients succumbed to severe pneumonia and respiratory failure following rTEF recurrence, one patient died from esophageal perforation due to esophageal dilation, and one patient died from severe pneumonia in ICU.

Among the 113 cases of rTEF repair surgery, 61 children (54.0%) did not use localization techniques, while 52 children (46.0%) used intraoperative localization techniques. Of the 52 cases using intraoperative localization techniques, there were 39 cases of TEF, 11 cases of EPF, 1 case of EBF, and 1 case of TEF combined with EPF. In 30 cases of TEF, 1 case of EBF, and 1 case of TEF combined with EPF, a guidewire was placed under a bronchoscope, and surgery was performed after successful guidewire placement in 10 cases. The remaining 22 cases combined ICG-assisted localization. Guidewire placement failed in 9 cases of TEF, and 11 children with EPF did not use a guidewire, so a total of 20 cases could only rely on ICG-assisted localization (Fig. 3).

Of 113 rTEF surgeries, 52 (46.0%) used intraoperative localization: 39 TEF, 11 EPF, 1 EBF, and 1 TEF with EPF. Guide wires were placed in 30 TEF, 1 EBF, and 1 TEF with EPF cases, with 10 proceeding directly to surgery and 22 aided by ICG. Guide wire insertion failed in 9 TEF cases, and 11 EPF cases eschewed wires, relying solely on ICG for localization

Comparative analysis of localization group and non-localization group

Regarding of all the surgeries for 113 rTEF, there were no significant differences in preoperative parameters between the two groups. The comparative analysis of operation and postoperative data revealed that the median operation time in the localization group was 2.5 (1.8, 3.0) hours, which was significantly lower than the 3.0 (2.4, 4.0) hours recorded in the non-localization group (P = 0.001). No other significant differences were identified in postoperative parameters (Table 1).

Discussion

The surgical management of rTEF is exceedingly challenging due to the presence of severe adhesions and unclear structural boundaries, which particularly complicate the localization and dissection of the fistula. To address this problem, Coran initially introduced the Fogarty catheter for localization of the fistula [5], which was later replaced by a thinner guidewire. The process of placing the guidewire requires the use of a bronchoscope and gastroscope, and close collaboration between the anesthesiologist and the endoscopist. However, as previously noted, there are several scenarios where guidewire placement may be impractical or impossible as mentioned by Wang [6]: (1) the fistula orifice cannot be found under bronchoscopy in patients with EPF or EBF; (2) some fistulas with a smaller diameter and those combined with anastomotic stricture may lead to a tightly closed fistula orifice; (3) the mucosa around the orifice forms folds, ultimately making it difficult to place the guidewire. Although they invented a technique of placing the guidewire from esophagus that may improve this predicament, it does not guarantee successful placement in all patients, and the injuries caused by the placement of the guidewire cannot be ignored. Since rTEF and the surrounding tracheoesophageal tissue are in a state of chronic inflammation, the process of placing the guidewire may cause unexpected sharp injuries. In addition, because the rigid guidewire has poor tissue compliance and cannot closely conform to the fistula and surrounding structures, it may cause compressive injuries. Our team’s technique of using thread instead of a rigid guidewire may avoid such compressive injuries.

In addition to the use of guidewire, Bruch creatively utilized the endoscopic light [8], which can penetrate tissue, to assist in intraoperative localization of TEF. However, because the bronchoscope cannot usually enter the fistula and the light has a large emission range, leading to inaccurate localization, it has not been widely adopted. But this did inspire us to put ‘light’ into the fistula to help us locate the fistulas. Indocyanine green, a medical dye, generates fluorescence under near-infrared light excitation and releases it in the short-wave infrared window, which also can penetrate tissue [9]. Therefore, through a specific fluorescence reception device, the imaging of the fistula can be achieved. The fluorescence thoracoscope system is such a device, which can help surgeons by switching back and forth between conventional light sources and fluorescence modes. Our previous reports on the use of this technique for rTEF localization in eight children confirmed its usefulness [10]. Additionally, the low cost of ICG and the rarity of reported adverse effects are key reasons for our recommendation. However, this localization technique is not without its drawbacks. Due to the distance decay effect of the ICG fluorescence, it can only penetrate tissue less than 1–1.5 cm in thickness, and thicker tissue may not be imaged. Moreover, some rTEF are not tubular but window-like [7], may allow ICG liquid to diffuse between the trachea and esophagus, leading to blurred boundaries and inaccurate localization. Therefore, we use a combination of thread guidewire and ICG fluorescence to assist in the localization of rTEF.

To the best our knowledge, this study is the first to support the impact of intraoperative localization techniques on the repair and prognosis of rTEF surgery with data. We categorized 113 rTEF into two groups, one with localization techniques and the other without, and found that intraoperative localization techniques significantly reduced surgery time, although there was no difference in postoperative recovery time or complications rates. Regardless of the type of fistula, this fast-surgery effect can appear in the TEF or EPF surgical repair. Additionally, the shorter postoperative hospital stay in the localization group of TEF patients may suggest that the localization technique could potentially reduce the length of hospitalization.

There are few reports on EPF following EA surgery, and the etiology of this complication remains unclear [11]. Our comparative data show that the use of ICG assisted localization during surgery can reduce the operative time for EPF. Since it cannot be localized by placing a guidewire, ICG localization is more valuable. As previous literature [12], approximately 10% of children still experience a second recurrence of fistula after rTEF surgery. In our group, there were 21 cases of repeated recurrences of TEF, and the comparative analysis didn’t show the fast-surgery effect. The possible reason is that even though localization techniques can help surgeons find the fistula, due to the much more severe thoracic adhesions in this group of patients, it still takes a considerable amount of time to separate the fistula. Toczewski suggested that performing thoracoscopic rTEF repair through a clean left-sided chest can facilitate the surgical process [13]. This approach may be more beneficial for children with repeated recurrences of TEF. Additionally, the localization technique requires coordination between gastroscopy and bronchoscopy, which involves more personnel and longer anesthesia time. Both guidewire localization and ICG localization have their own limitations, and even when used together, they may not always provide precise identification of the fistula location.

Our study acknowledges certain limitations, primarily stemming from its single-center design, which inherently raises questions about the generalizability of the findings. While the results are significant within our institution, they may not be easily replicated in other centers or under different conditions. Factors such as unique patient demographics, healthcare practices, and local protocols at our center could influence the outcomes, limiting the wider applicability of our conclusions. To fully validate these findings, further confirmation through multicenter randomized trials is necessary, which would provide more robust evidence across diverse patient populations and healthcare environments.

Conclusion

In summary, the use of guidewires and/or ICG during surgery assists surgeons in rapidly identifying and separating the fistula, thereby reducing operation time, and may fasten postoperative recovery. ICG-assisted localization of EPF in the surgery is efficacious.
