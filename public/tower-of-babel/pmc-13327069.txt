Losartan Enhances Radiosensitivity by Reversing Immunosuppressive Tumor Microenvironment Induced by Radiotherapy in TNBC
Authors: Xu Wang, Cuiwei Liu, Zihan Xia, Ting Hu, Yuting Li, Dan Han, Yunjie Song, Yuxi Ma, Yanxia Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTriple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer, with limited therapeutic options. Radiotherapy (RT) is a cornerstone of TNBC treatment; however, preclinical studies suggest that RT may both stimulate and suppress immune responses within the tumor microenvironment (TME). While RT can enhance immune responses, it may also induce an immunosuppressive TME, contributing to radioresistance. The renin‐angiotensin system (RAS) is also involved in the immunosuppressive TME and tumor radioresistance. Previous research showed blocker of angiotensin II receptor (AGTR1), losartan, as a RAS inhibitor, could alleviate hypoxia, thereby enhancing RT efficacy. Our study shows that high levels of AGTR1 may confer a poor prognosis and radioresistance in TNBC, and innovatively demonstrates losartan could enhance the radiosensitivity of TNBC in an immune dependent way. Furthermore, we found losartan could inhibit tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) polarizing towards M2 phenotype, impedes the immunosuppressive function of myeloid‐derived suppressive cells (MDSCs) induced by RT, and ultimately up‐regulates the number and function of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T lymphocytes. Besides, RNA‐seq data reveals losartan impedes JAK2/STAT1 signaling activation upon irradiation, suppresses the interferon‐related DNA damage resistant signature (IRDS) expression and diminishes the immune suppressive factors PD‐L1 and IDO induced by irradiation in TNBC cells. Collectively, in our study, we investigate the role of losartan in radiosensitization and demonstrate losartan could reverse the immunosuppressive TME induced by RT in TNBC, which suggests that losartan combined with RT may represent a promising strategy for the treatment of TNBC.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy in women worldwide [1]. Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents about 12%–17% of all breast cancers [2], and is defined by the lack of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression, leading to fewer treatment choices and a worse prognosis [3]. Although advances have been made in understanding TNBC biology, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy (RT) remain cornerstone treatments [4]. RT improves locoregional control after breast‐conserving surgery or mastectomy and increases survival in roughly 30% of patients with residual disease [5]. Moreover, growing evidence indicates that RT significantly shapes the tumor microenvironment (TME) in multiple cancers, including BC [6]. However, RT exerts a “double‐edged” sword’ effect on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), provoking both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive changes [7]. It can induce immunogenic cell death, alter immune cell infiltration, upregulate immune checkpoint molecules, and trigger local and systemic immune responses. Nonetheless, RT may also drive immunosuppression and immune tolerance, potentially leading to RT resistance [8], an area that has drawn substantial research interest.

A subset of patients with TNBC show limited response to RT due to de novo or acquired radioresistance. Studies indicate that the TME plays a pivotal role in the development of radioresistance [9, 10]. RT‐induced alterations in the TME may promote tumor invasion and metastasis in certain contexts [6], and can foster an immunosuppressive TME that further drives radioresistance [11]. These changes involve dynamic shifts in immune cell composition and cytokine or protein expression within the TIME. Additionally, genomic alterations in cancer cells caused by RT significantly contribute to radioresistance [6]. Altered gene signatures can modulate immune‐related cytokines and proteins, thereby facilitating RT resistance. Such RT‐driven adaptations in the TIME are implicated in radioresistance across multiple cancers [12, 13, 14, 15], including BC. These insights support the potential of combined strategies—such as RT with immunomodulation or targeted gene regulation—to improve radiosensitivity. However, how to effectively reverse the immunosuppressive TME or target RT‐related genomic changes to overcome radioresistance in TNBC remains inadequately studied.

The renin‐angiotensin system (RAS) plays a vital role in cardiovascular homeostasis and has been implicated in the development and progression of BC [16]. Preclinical evidence suggests that RAS influences the TIME [17]. In experimental models, RAS activation promotes tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) infiltration, whereas RAS blockade downregulates monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1, reduces TAMs recruitment, and inhibits tumor growth [17, 18]. RAS also regulates myeloid differentiation and maturation [19]; for instance, angiotensin‐converting enzyme knockout models exhibit extramedullary myelopoiesis and increased MDSCs [20]. RAS components are expressed in both cancer cells and immune cells—including monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and T cells—and generally promote tumor progression [21]. Signaling through RAS enhances cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation [16], suggesting that RAS inhibition may exert antitumor effects partly via modulation of the TIME. Recent studies indicate that the angiotensin II receptor 1 (AGTR1) antagonist losartan—an FDA‐approved antihypertensive agent—can enhance chemotherapy efficacy in ovarian cancer [22] and improve RT outcomes in neurofibromatosis type 2 [23]. In HER2‐positive breast cancer, losartan combined with RT improved tumor control and suppressed lung metastasis [24]. It is thus plausible to hypothesize that losartan may increase radiosensitivity in TNBC by inhibiting RAS and reversing RT‐induced immunosuppression in the TME. However, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

In this study, we combined in vitro and in vivo models to evaluate whether losartan enhances radiosensitivity in TNBC. We further examined its effects on immune and cancer cells within the TIME after RT to explore how losartan counteracts RT‐induced immunosuppression. Our results reveal a synergistic antitumor and immunomodulatory role of losartan following RT, elucidating its mechanism of radiosensitization and supporting its potential as a combinatory treatment with RT in TNBC.

Material and Methods

Mice and Tumor Model

All animal procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and conducted in accordance with national animal welfare guidelines. Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) and BALB/c nu/nu mice (3–4 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions. Subcutaneous tumor model details are in Supporting Information Methods Doc S1.

Cell Culture, Transfection and Infection

Human breast cancer cell lines (MDA‐MB‐231, BT‐549, MCF‐7, T47D), murine 4 T1, MC38 cells and human HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured as recommended. All human cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed mycoplasma‐free. MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7, 4 T1, MC38 and HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco), and BT549 and T47D cells in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, both supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. Lentiviral particles carrying Agtr1‐targeting shRNAs (shAgtr1#1: 5′‐GCGTCAGTTTCAACCTCTACG‐3′; shAgtr1#2: 5′‐GCCCATAACCATCTGCATAGC‐3′) were packaged in HEK293T cells and used to infect 4 T1 cells, followed by puromycin selection.

Reagents and Antibodies

Losartan potassium was dissolved in saline (5 mg/mL) for in vivo use and in PBS (100 mM) for in vitro experiments based on previous studies [23, 25]. Angiotensin II (HY‐13948) was purchased from MCE. The information for the antibodies used in our study is provided in Supporting Information Methods Doc S1.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Single‐cell suspensions from 4 T1 tumors harvested 2 weeks post‐irradiation were prepared by mincing and digesting with collagenase IV, hyaluronidase, and DNase I. Cells were filtered, washed, and red blood cells lysed. The strategies for cells staining were described in Supporting Information Methods Doc S1.

Clonogenic Cell Survival Assay

4 T1 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells were plated at varying densities and irradiated with X‐rays (0–8 Gy). After 14 days, colonies were fixed with formalin and stained with crystal violet. Colonies with ≥ 50 cells were counted.

Protein Extraction and Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Samples (50 μg) were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using Oligo(dT) primers and Superscript III reverse transcriptase. Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Premix on a StepOne Plus Real‐Time PCR System. Gene expression was normalized and calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All primers (listed in Table S1) were designed using PrimerBank and validated by NCBI Primer‐BLAST.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

4 T1 xenograft tumors were harvested 2 weeks post‐irradiation, fixed in 10% formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and peroxidase blocking, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells grown on chamber slides or 4 T1 tumor tissues harvested 2 weeks post‐irradiation were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.01% Triton X‐100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using confocal or fluorescence microscopy, and relative fluorescence intensity was quantified from three random fields using ImageJ software.

ELISA For Arginase and NO

MDSCs from 4 T1 tumors post‐RT were sorted. The concentrations of arginase and NO in cell homogenates of MDSCs were measured with Arginase‐1 ELISA kit (CSB‐EL002005MO, CUSABIO, China) and NO ELISA kit (S0023, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturers' instructions.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 4 T1 cells using TRIzol reagent, and RNA quality was verified with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using STAR, and differential gene expression analysis was conducted with the DEGSeq R package. Unsupervised clustering and heatmap visualization were performed using R. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out with MSigDB C2 gene sets, and normalized enrichment scores were calculated. The RNA‐seq data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1441458 (see Supporting Information Doc S3 for details).

Bioinformatic Analysis

TCGA Breast Cancer data were obtained via UCSC Xena [26]. RSEM‐normalized RNA‐seq from 123 TNBC samples was analyzed. Radio‐resistance‐associated genes were identified using the signature from Speers et al. [27]. Correlation between AGTR1 expression and radioresistance genes was assessed using Pearson correlation. Survival analysis divided patients by AGTR1 expression. TILs scores from FUSCC TNBC cohort [28] were associated with AGTR1 using Wilcoxon test.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 and are presented as mean ± SEM. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test (two groups) or one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (multiple groups). Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests. Sample sizes for animal studies were determined through preliminary data and power analysis with G*Power software (version 3.1), targeting 80% statistical power at a two‐sided alpha of 0.05. These sample sizes align with established practices in the field and provide adequate power to detect meaningful intergroup differences [29]. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were independently replicated at least three times with consistent results. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Losartan Enhanced the Radiosensitivity of TNBC Depending on the Immune System

Based on our previous finding that AGTR1 is expressed in various subtypes of BC tissues and cell lines [25], we analyzed clinical data of BC from TCGA database and found that high AGTR1 expression was associated with significantly worse disease‐free interval (DFI) and progression‐free interval (PFI) specifically in the TNBC cohort (Figure 1A), but not in other types of BC, such as ER+PR+HER2−, ER−PR−HER2+, and ER+PR+HER2+ (Figure S1). RT is an important local treatment for TNBC patients. To further explore the clinical relevance of AGTR1 in RT response, we examined its correlation with previously established radiosensitivity gene signatures. Speers et al. previously identified gene sets positively and negatively correlated with intrinsic radiosensitivity in BC [27]. Specifically, they reported 67 genes whose expression positively correlated with radioresistance and 80 genes negatively correlated with radioresistance. We performed correlation analysis between AGTR1 expression and these gene sets, and found that AGTR1 expression was significantly positively correlated with a radioresistance gene signature in TNBC (Figure S1), suggesting that high AGTR1 expression predicts poorer response to RT. As losartan competitively inhibits the AngII/AGTR1 axis, we hypothesized it might reverse radioresistance.

To test this, we orthotopically implanted 4 T1 cells into immunocompetent Balb/c mice. Two weeks post‐inoculation, mice received local irradiation (12Gy) combined with saline or losartan (Figure 1B; Figure S1). Finally, in the following animal experiments, we chose a concentration of 20 mg/kg/day of losartan, which did not affect tumor growth. Our results revealed that the growth of the 4 T1 xenograft tumors was inhibited by RT, and the combination of RT and losartan led to significantly greater inhibition of tumor growth and reduction in tumor volume/weight compared to RT alone (Figure 1C–E). To better mimic clinical RT regimens, a fractionated irradiation protocol (2 Gy × 5 fractions) was also tested [30]. This fractionated irradiation also significantly suppressed tumor growth, and again, the combination with losartan resulted in superior inhibition and greater reductions in tumor volume and weight compared to fractionated RT alone (Figure S1H–J). These data suggest that losartan could enhance the radiosensitivity in TNBC in vivo. Additionally, it was known that RT could directly induce DNA damage and different types of cell death including apoptosis of tumor cells [31]. Then we found that losartan did not potently affect the numbers of proliferating (Ki67+) tumor cells but increased the apoptotic (cleaved‐caspase3+) tumor cells in RT plus losartan group (Figure 1F,G).

To further explore whether the radio‐sensitization effect of losartan observed in immunocompetent Balb/c mice might also be apparent in vitro or not. Mouse TNBC cell line 4 T1 and human TNBC cell line MDA‐MB‐231 were pre‐treated with losartan (Figure S1K) or PBS prior to exposure to irradiation, we then chose a concentration of 200 nM losartan which had no effect on tumor cell survival for the subsequent cell experiments. However, studies in vitro showed that losartan did not affect the viability (Figure S1L,M), cell cycle, apoptosis (Figure S2A–D), or DNA damage repair (Figure S2E,F) of irradiated 4 T1 or MDA‐MB‐231 cells. This indicated losartan does not directly affect the intrinsic radiosensitivity of TNBC cells. Crucially, when the experiment was repeated in immunodeficient (Balb/c‐nu) mice (Figure 1B), the radiosensitizing effect of losartan was abrogated (Figure 1H–J). To further validate the causal role of AGTR1 in mediating radioresistance, we established stable Agtr1‐knockdown models in 4 T1 cells, with efficient knockdown confirmed by western blotting and qRT‐PCR (Figure S2G,H). Our results demonstrated that shAgtr1#1 achieved a more effective knockdown of Agtr1, and it was therefore selected for all subsequent experiments. Furthermore, studies in vitro also showed that Agtr1 knockdown did not affect the viability and colony formation (Figure S2I–K) of irradiated 4 T1 cells. In immunocompetent Balb/c mice, tumors derived from Agtr1‐knockdown cells exhibited significantly enhanced sensitivity to RT compared with control tumors. Interestingly, the addition of losartan to Agtr1‐deficient tumors undergoing RT resulted in further tumor suppression, suggesting that losartan may exert additional effects through modulation of the host immune microenvironment (Figure 1K–M). These results collectively demonstrate that losartan enhances the radiosensitivity of TNBC in an immune‐dependent manner.

Losartan Inhibits TAMs Polarized to M2 Phenotype and Impairs the Immunosuppressive Function of MDSCs Induced by RT in TNBC

RT leads to the activation of an interconnected series of processes in the TIME, and it cannot be dichotomized as solely immunosuppressive or solely immunostimulatory due to the diversity and complexity alterations of immune cells induced by RT [6]. It is reported that the recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells induced by RT such as TAMs and MDSCs [11, 32, 33], could lead to the establishment of immunosuppressive TME and decrease the radiosensitivity. We investigated how losartan modulates these populations in 4 T1 tumors.

TAMs polarized to the M2 phenotype promote immunosuppression [34]. To determine the effects of irradiation and losartan on TAMs, 4 T1 xenograft tumors were harvested and analyzed. Flow cytometry analysis (Figure S3A) revealed that neither RT nor losartan affected total TAMs infiltration (Figure 2A,B). However, RT decreased M1 TAMs (CD86+CD206−) while increasing M2 TAMs (CD206+CD86−) polarization. Losartan combined with RT significantly increased M1 TAMs and showed a decreasing trend in M2 TAMs (Figure 2C,D), indicating that losartan inhibits RT‐induced M2 polarization.

MDSCs are another kind of immunosuppressive cells in TIME, identified by their surface expression of CD11b and Gr1 in mice (CD11b+ and CD33+ in humans) [35]. MDSCs inhibited T cell proliferation and function, suppressing anti‐tumor immunity through Arg‐1 mediated arginine depletion, iNOS production and ROS generation [36]. For MDSCs (Figure S3B), neither RT nor losartan altered the overall infiltration of CD11b+Gr1+ cells (Figure 2E,F). It has been reported that either the infiltration of M‐MDSC or G‐MDSC correlated positively with tumor burden [37]. Then, we further analyzed and found that there was still no significant difference in the infiltration of either M‐MDSC or G‐MDSC group (Figure 2G,H). However, RT significantly increased protein levels of immunosuppressive factors Arg‐1 and iNOS in tumor tissues, which was reversed by losartan combination (Figure 2I,J). Further analysis of MDSCs isolated from tumors (Figure S3C) confirmed that RT enhanced both expression and enzymatic activity of Arg‐1 and iNOS, while losartan treatment inhibited these effects (Figure 2K–N).

Losartan Alleviates Loss of T Cells Activities Induced by RT in TNBC

Recently, multiple researches had shown that M2 TAMs and MDSCs facilitate tumor progression and radioresistance via suppressing cytotoxic T cells, mainly the CD8+ T lymphocytes [38, 39]. Therefore, we sought to determine whether the infiltration and the activities of T cells were altered by RT or in combination with losartan. Flow cytometry (Figure S3D) revealed that losartan combined with RT increased the infiltration of CD45+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 3A–D), while decreasing CD4+ T cells, indicating a shift in T cell subsets. Furthermore, it is widely known that CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes exert antitumor effects by secreting granzyme B (GB) and IFNγ. Functionally, although IFNγ levels were unaffected (Figure 3E,F), the combination treatment significantly enhanced GB release from CD8+ T cells compared to RT alone (Figure 3G,H), indicating improved CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. We further evaluated T‐cell exhaustion markers. Compared with RT alone, the combination of RT and losartan significantly reduced the proportion of CD8+ T cells co‐expressing the exhaustion markers PD‐1 and TIM‐3 (Figure S3D,E), indicating an improved functional status of T cells. These results indicate that losartan not only enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration and function but also improves their functional state and delays the progression of exhaustion.

Tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), including stromal TILs (sTILs) and intratumoral TILs (iTILs), play an important role in breast tumor immune response. Moreover, our findings revealed a correlation between elevated AGTR1 expression and diminished TILs infiltration, encompassing both sTILs and iTILs, as observed in the TCGA‐TNBC cohort (Figure 3I). Besides, the infiltration of Tregs (CD4+Foxp3+) was not affected by RT or losartan treatment (Figure S3F). These data suggested that losartan might not only enrich infiltrating T cells but also alter the subtypes of T cells and alleviate the loss of T cells activities in TIME induced by RT, which may enhance the radiosensitivity of TNBC.

CD8+ T cells have typically been thought to be the key effector T cells in the anti‐tumor immune response [40]. To confirm the role of CD8+ T cells, we depleted them using an anti‐CD8α antibody (Figure 3J; Figure S3G). Consistent with the foresaid results, the growth of isotype IgG treated tumors was suppressed by RT, and the tumor growth was significantly further inhibited in the RT plus losartan group. CD8+ T cell depletion partially reversed the tumor growth suppression achieved by RT plus losartan (Figure 3K), demonstrating that CD8+ T cells are essential for losartan‐mediated radiosensitization.

Losartan Leads to a Reduction of IRDS Induced by RT Via Disruption of STAT1‐Mediated Pathways in TNBC Cells

Recently, more and more studies have shown that TIME is widely involved in the development of radioresistance, but we still could not ignore the critical role of the cancer cells themselves. To investigate genomic changes in TNBC cells, we performed RNA‐seq on irradiated 4 T1 cells with/without losartan (Figure S4A). Losartan treatment resulted in 57 differentially expressed genes (30 down, 27 up; Figure 4A,B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of down‐regulated genes in “Interferon Alpha Response” and “Interferon Gamma Response” pathways (Figure 4C,D). Which might be correlated with the immune system. Consistently, high AGTR1 expression correlated with these interferon responses in TCGA‐TNBC data (Figure S4B,C).

Interferon responses mediate expression of interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs), and 7 specific ISGs (STAT1, IFIT1, IFIT3, IFI44, ISG15, OAS1, MX1) constitute an interferon‐related DNA damage resistance signature (IRDS) that promotes therapy resistance in BC [41, 42]. We identified a core losartan response signature comprising these 7 IRDS genes plus CD274 (PD‐L1). Losartan significantly inhibited radiation‐induced expression of all 8 genes in 4 T1 cells (Figure 4E).

Validation in multiple TNBC cell lines (4 T1, MDA‐MB‐231, BT‐549) confirmed that losartan abrogated radiation‐induced IRDS upregulation (Figure 4F–H), without affecting basal IRDS expression. Furthermore, we also found that AGTR1 expression is not significantly associated with most IRDS genes (Figure S4D) in the TCGA‐TNBC cohort, which means that losartan may not affect the expression of IRDS without RT, which is consistent with our above results. According to our results, we considered that losartan might only inhibit the expression of IRDS induced by RT in BC. Additionally, it was reported that different dose/fractionation of RT may cause different immune responses in TME [43]. As shown in Figure 4I–K, this inhibition was consistent across different radiation schemes (8Gy single dose vs. 2Gy × 4 fractions), and specific to TNBC, as losartan did not affect IRDS in MCF7 (luminal A) or MC38 (colon cancer) cells (Figure S4E,F).

STAT1 is the key transducer of IFN signaling that drives IRDS expression [41]. Analysis of TCGA data confirmed positive correlations between STAT1 and other IRDS genes/CD274 in BC (Figure 4L). These results indicate that losartan specifically inhibits radiation‐induced IRDS expression in TNBC via disruption of STAT1‐mediated pathways, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity.

Losartan Inhibits IRDS, PD‐L1 and IDO Expression Induced by RT by Disrupting JAK2/STAT1 in TNBC Cells

Since STAT1 activation is primarily regulated by JAK2 [44] and angiotensin II can activate JAK2 [45], we hypothesized that losartan inhibits IRDS by disrupting JAK2/STAT1 signaling. To elucidate the potential mechanisms through which losartan regulates the expression of the STAT1 gene and other IRDS, type I and II IFNs, the upstream of JAK2/STAT1 were firstly detected. While irradiation (8Gy) increased type I IFN but not type II IFN, losartan did not affect IFN expression or secretion Figure S4G,H. As the primary transcription factor involved in IFN‐related intracellular signaling [41], the levels of both total STAT1 (t‐STAT1) and phosphorylated STAT1 (Y‐701) (p‐STAT1) were assessed. However, losartan significantly reduced both total and phosphorylated STAT1 (Y‐701) protein levels induced by irradiation in TNBC cells (Figure 5A,B). Similarly, irradiation‐induced JAK2 phosphorylation (Y1007) was inhibited by losartan without affecting total JAK2 levels (Figure 5C–F; Figure S4I,J). These effects were consistent across different irradiation regimens (Figure 5G–J). Our results indicated that losartan suppresses the JAK2/STAT1 pathway without affecting the expression of IFNs in TNBC cells. In contrast, losartan had minimal effects on JAK2/STAT1 signaling in non‐TNBC cell lines such as MCF7 and T47D (Figure S4K), indicating subtype specificity.

Recent research has brought to light that the JAK2/STAT1 pathway is not only involved in the modulation of IRDS, but may also contribute to the gradual deterioration of the immune system and tumor progression following irradiation [46], particularly through the activation of PD‐L1 and IDO [47, 48, 49, 50]. In TNBC cells, irradiation significantly increased PD‐L1 and IDO expression, which was reversed by losartan treatment at both RNA (Figure 6A–C) and protein levels (Figure 6D). To further confirm that these effects were mediated through AGTR1 signaling, reverse validation experiments were performed using angiotensin II (Ang II), the endogenous AGTR1 agonist. As shown in Figure 6E,F, treatment with Ang II (10 nM) alone significantly activated the JAK2/STAT1 pathway and increased the expression of IRDS genes and PD‐L1. Importantly, losartan pretreatment effectively blocked Ang II‐induced signaling, as demonstrated by the significant reduction of JAK2/STAT1 activation and downstream gene expression in the Ang II + LOS group compared to Ang II alone. These results indicate that losartan suppresses the JAK2/STAT1 pathway independently of IFN expression in TNBC cells, supporting that its effect is mediated by specific antagonism of AGTR1 signaling. In vivo validation using 4 T1 xenografts confirmed that losartan inhibited radiation‐induced upregulation of STAT1, p‐STAT1, PD‐L1 and IDO (Figure 6G,H). The correlation analysis further revealed a positive association between PD‐L1 and IDO expression with STAT1 and its phosphorylated form (Figure 6I), which were also confirmed by the data from GEPIA analysis (Figure 6J). Taken together, our findings indicated that losartan inhibits JAK2 phosphorylation and subsequent STAT1 activation, thereby suppressing radiation‐induced expression of IRDS, PD‐L1 and IDO in TNBC cells. This mechanism alleviates the immunosuppressive microenvironment and contributes to enhanced radiosensitivity.

Losartan Enhances the Efficacy of Radio‐Immunotherapy in TNBC

PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune‐checkpoint signaling has been proved as a promising target in multiple human cancers including TNBC [51]. It has been reported that PD‐L1 is present on tumor cells and has the ability to bind with PD‐1 on T cells, resulting in tumor immune escape [52]. Given the observed enhancement of T lymphocyte responses and reduction in PD‐L1 expression following RT with losartan, we sought to investigate whether the combination of losartan could augment the efficacy of immunotherapy and RT (Figure 7A). Our results demonstrated that RT suppressed the tumor growth in the 4 T1 xenograft tumor model, and the tumor growth was further inhibited by the combination of PD‐1 blockade (Figure 7B). Moreover, the growth curve of the 4 T1 xenograft tumor model exhibits a synergistic impact on tumor inhibition when losartan is combined with RT and PD‐1 inhibitor, as depicted in Figure 7B. Additionally, RT was observed to increase the survival duration of the 4 T1 xenograft tumor model and displayed a tendency towards prolonged survival following the combination with anti‐PD‐1 therapy, as illustrated in Figure 7C. And the survival curve further illustrates a significant extended survival period resulting from the combination with losartan which was consistent with the tumor growth curve in the 4 T1 xenograft tumor model (Figure 7C). Taken together, these results indicated that losartan might not only enhance the radiosensitivity of TNBC by remodeling the immunosuppressive TME after RT but also sensitize TNBC to radio‐immunotherapy and prolong the survival time of TNBC in the 4 T1 xenograft tumor model.

Discussion

TNBC patients do not benefit from established endocrine or HER‐2 targeted therapies due to the absence of relevant receptor, leaving treatment primarily limited to surgery, chemotherapy, and RT. Although RT reduces locoregional recurrence and improves survival [5], radioresistance remains a major cause of relapse and metastasis. While factors such as cancer stem cells, hypoxia, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, and DNA damage repair have been implicated in radioresistance, targeted strategies have shown limited success. Recently, the immunosuppressive TIME induced by RT has gained attention, yet the underlying mechanisms and potential radiosensitization strategies require further exploration.

The RAS inhibitor has exhibited significant antineoplastic activity in preclinical studies in diverse tumor types, including BC [53, 54, 55, 56, 57]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. As a RAS inhibitor, losartan has been reported to enhance chemo‐radiotherapy efficacy by reducing extracellular matrix composition, alleviating solid stress, normalizing tumor vasculature, and improving oxygen supply. Our previous study also showed that losartan improved paclitaxel efficacy and reduced ascites by enhancing drug delivery and normalizing the TME in ovarian cancer [22]. Additionally, several studies indicate that losartan can enhance radiosensitivity in various cancers, though the mechanisms remain unclear [24]. Given its radiosensitization potential and emerging role in TIME modulation, we hypothesized that losartan could enhance TNBC radiosensitivity by modifying the TIME.

In our study, we innovatively found that losartan can significantly augment the radiosensitivity in an immunocompetent mice model of TNBC. However, the radiosensitivity in immunodeficient TNBC mice model and the intrinsic radiosensitivity of TNBC cells in vitro were not altered by losartan. These findings strongly suggest that losartan may enhance radiosensitivity in TNBC via a mechanism that is reliant on the immune system. While recent studies suggest RAS modulates immunotherapy efficacy by regulating tumor stroma [58, 59], our investigation revealed that losartan primarily acts on tumor cells and immune cells rather than stroma, reversing the suppressive TIME induced by RT and ultimately improving radiosensitivity and tumor regression in TNBC.

The TIME contains diverse immune populations with both anti‐tumor and pro‐tumor functions. The immune suppression caused by MDSCs and M2 TAMs plays a significant role in establishing a suppressive TIME and enabling tumors to evade the immune system [60]. Our findings indicated that RT can induce TAMs with an M2 phenotype and promote the expression of Arg‐1 and iNOS in MDSCs, leading to the formation of an immunosuppressive TME. Notably, our results revealed that losartan effectively impaired the immune‐suppressive functions of MDSCs and inhibits TAMs polarization to the M2‐like phenotype following RT. This facilitated anti‐tumor immunity within the TIME and enhanced radiosensitivity in TNBC.

Inhibition of immunosuppressive myeloid cells can lead to CD8+ T cells activation and enrichment within the TIME [61]. We found that RT‐induced immunosuppression—characterized by T cell dysfunction, M2 TAM polarization, and enhanced MDSC activity—contributes to radioresistance in TNBC. Losartan combination therapy significantly increased CD8+ T cells infiltration, altered T cell subset composition, inhibited T cell exhaustion and restored T cell activity, likely through its inhibition of M2 polarization and MDSCs function. These findings indicate the reversal of the immunosuppressive microenvironment by losartan after RT, thereby providing novel insights into the therapeutic efficacy of losartan in the treatment of TNBC.

While immune cells in TIME play crucial roles in radioresistance, tumor cells also shape the microenvironment through immune factor expression. RNA‐seq analysis of 4 T1 cells revealed that irradiation markedly upregulated IRDS genes and CD274 (encoding PD‐L1), which are associated with radiation resistance and immune checkpoint blockade. Losartan effectively suppressed this radiation‐induced upregulation. Interestingly, inhibition of IRDS and the JAK2/STAT1 pathway was preferentially observed in TNBC models but not in luminal BC or colon cancer cells, suggesting subtype‐specific molecular regulation. This observation may explain why AGTR1 expression correlates with survival exclusively in TNBC patients. Our results suggest IRDS could serve as a potential biomarker for predicting RT efficacy or losartan‐mediated radiosensitization in TNBC, consistent with previous findings [42], though further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Previous studies have documented that the IRDS were predominantly regulated by IFNs/JAK2/STAT1 pathway, which coordinates interactions between tumor and TIME [62, 63]. Our data demonstrate that losartan does not affect type I or type II interferon expression or secretion, nor does it influence DNA damage repair, indicating its action is independent of direct IFN modulation or DDR. Instead, losartan appears to target the downstream JAK2/STAT1 pathway. The JAK2/STAT1 pathway has been implicated in radioresistance and tumor progression through immune regulation and survival signaling [64]. We found irradiation significantly activates JAK2 and STAT1 in TNBC, consistent with reports in other malignancies. Losartan effectively suppressed this activation across different RT regimens. Furthermore, irradiation induced expression of immunosuppressive factors PD‐L1 and IDO—known JAK2/STAT1 targets that promote tumor growth [41]‐ and losartan treatment substantially inhibited their upregulation. This suggests losartan reverses RT‐induced immunosuppression at least partially through JAK2/STAT1 inhibition. Genetic experiments further confirmed the causal role of AGTR1 in mediating radioresistance. Knockdown of AGTR1 significantly enhanced tumor sensitivity to RT in immunocompetent mice, demonstrating that AGTR1 signaling contributes to radioresistance in TNBC. Of note, AGTR1 knockdown did not affect radiosensitivity in vitro, indicating that its radiosensitizing effect in vivo requires an intact immune system. In contrast, losartan failed to radiosensitize tumors in immunodeficient mice, confirming that losartan's effect is also immune‐dependent. Mechanistically, AGTR1 knockdown specifically reduces AGTR1 in tumor cells, whereas systemically administered losartan acts on both tumor cells and host immune cells. Consistently, losartan treatment further improved the response of AGTR1‐deficient tumors to RT, indicating that losartan exerts additional radiosensitizing effects through modulation of the host immune microenvironment. Together with pharmacological validation using Ang II stimulation, these findings support a dual mechanism whereby losartan enhances radiosensitivity by both antagonizing tumor‐cell AGTR1 signaling and remodeling the TIME.

Furthermore, it has been reported that TNBC exhibits a high level of PD‐L1 expression [65], indicating that this tumor may potentially benefit from anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune‐checkpoint therapy. However, the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune checkpoint therapy for TNBC observed in clinical were not as effective as anticipated, which implied that certain mechanisms may have constrained the response to immunotherapy in TNBC patients. In this study, we found that losartan could significantly inhibit the activation of JAK2/STAT1 pathway induced by RT. Consequently, through integrating losartan treatment with RT and anti‐PD‐1, we found a significantly increased efficacy for this combined therapy in TNBC mouse model. These findings suggest that losartan could potentially convert a low immunotherapy responsive tumor into an active one. Therefore, our results suggest novel immunotherapeutic approaches that combining losartan with PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibodies, which might be promising strategies in human TNBC treatments. However, in our TNBC mouse model, losartan was administrated each day and anti‐PD‐1 was administrated 4 times. Though the progression of the tumors is significantly delayed, further work with multiple rounds of treatment is still needed to assess long‐term efficacy and the generation of resistance for losartan.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, our experiments primarily employed the 4 T1 syngeneic mouse model, which features a highly immunosuppressive TIME. Although this model is widely used for investigating tumor–immune interactions, it may not fully recapitulate the complexity and heterogeneity of the human TNBC microenvironment. Future studies using humanized mouse models or patient‐derived xenografts with functional human immune systems will be important to further validate the translational potential of losartan in combination with RT. Second, our immune profiling focused principally on MDSCs, TAMs, and CD8+ T lymphocytes. Although we assessed the expression of the exhaustion markers PD‐1 and TIM‐3 on CD8+ T cells, other important exhaustion markers such as LAG‐3, CTLA‐4 were not evaluated. Given that co‐expression of multiple exhaustion markers is often associated with severe T‐cell dysfunction and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [66], a more comprehensive assessment of T‐cell exhaustion status would provide deeper insights into how losartan modulates T‐cell functionality following irradiation. Finally, the TIME is a complex ecosystem that also includes innate immune populations such as natural killer (NK) cells and dendritic cells (DCs), both of which play important roles in RT induced immune remodeling [8]. Given that the JAK/STAT pathway also regulates NK‐cell activity and DCs maturation [67, 68], it is possible that losartan may influence these populations as well. Future studies using more comprehensive immune profiling approaches, such as single‐cell RNA sequencing or spatial transcriptomics, will be necessary to comprehensively map the effects of losartan on the full spectrum of immune cells within the irradiated TME.

In conclusion, our study indicated that RT might promote the establishment of a suppressive TIME which correlated with the radioresistance of TNBC through multiple mechanisms: polarizing TAMs towards M2 phenotype, enhancing MDSC immunosuppressive function, altering T‐cell subsets, and upregulating PD‐L1, IDO, and IRDS via JAK2/STAT1 signaling. These processes eventually contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME, which facilitates tumor immune escape and partly contributes to the development of radioresistance in TNBC. Losartan counteracts these effects by reducing M2 TAMs, impairing MDSCs function, increasing effector T cells, and suppressing PD‐L1, IDO and IRDS expression through JAK2/STAT1 inhibition, ultimately enhancing radiosensitivity and radio‐immunotherapy efficacy (Figure 7D). The TNBC‐specific inhibition of IRDS suggests its potential as a predictive biomarker for RT or losartan response. Additionally, losartan, as one of the most commonly prescribed drugs for hypertension, the safety and affordability have been extensively confirmed. In the future, losartan combined with RT or radio‐immunotherapy might be a promising and novel strategy for TNBC treatment. However, further research and evidence are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to substantiate the widespread utilization of losartan in the clinical treatment of TNBC.
