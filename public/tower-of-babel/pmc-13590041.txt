Chronic Contractile Activity Remediates Lysosomal Dysfunction in a Muscle Cell Lysosomal Disease Model
Authors: Debasmita Bhattacharya, Neushaw Moradi, David A Hood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLysosomes are important organelles for the degradation of unwanted biomolecules via autophagy. Lysosomal dysfunction is apparent in ageing tissues, and can cause various neurodegenerative diseases. It is imperative to understand the mechanisms and implications of lysosomal malfunction and to find strategies to ameliorate diseases. To investigate this, we induced lysosomal dysfunction with Bafilomycin A1 (BAF), a drug that hinders lysosomal acidification by blocking vATPase‐mediated proton pumping, in myoblasts and myotubes. Myotubes were subjected to chronic contractile activity (CCA) to mimic “exercise” to evaluate any therapeutic potential and reversal of pathophysiology. Induced lysosomal dysfunction was evident from impaired processing of the protease cathepsin B, enhanced lysosomal accumulation and increased autophagic markers Lamp1, p62, and the LC3II/I ratio. BAF attenuated lysosomal protease degradation measured using the substrate DQ‐BSA in both myoblasts and myotubes. Many of the adverse effects generated by BAF in myotubes were reversed by CCA, manifested by a decrease in immature cathepsin B, a down‐regulation of Lamp1, p62, LC3II/I and partial restoration of lysosomal protein degradation enzymatic capacity. To investigate further improvements in lysosomal function in a healthy cellular model, we treated myotubes with C1, a curcumin analogue. C1 decreased Lamp1, p62 and the LC3II/I ratio, all of which indicate improved lysosomal function and autophagosome clearance with a greater lysosomal ability to degrade substrates. Additionally, C1 was able to mimic the improved mitochondrial content induced by CCA. Thus, contractile activity and curcumin analogues may provide useful therapeutic potential to resuscitate lysosomal function, improve muscle health and ameliorate lysosome‐mediated diseases.New and NoteworthyThis study explores the potential of chronic contractile activity (CCA) in reversing the compromised lysosomes caused by the disruption of lysosomal acidification in myotubes. Remarkably, CCA mitigated the effects of Bafilomycin A1‐induced lysosomal dysfunction, enhancing protease activity and increased mitochondrial content. Treatment of myotubes with the curcumin analogue C1 reduced lysosomal accumulation and enhanced mitochondrial content suggesting that contractile activity‐based interventions and small‐molecule modulators of lysosomal pathways may represent promising complementary strategies for treating lysosome‐related diseases and enhancing muscle health.Keywords: autophagy, bafilomycin A, exercise, lysosomes, mitochondria

Introduction

Lysosomes are crucial organelles for the degradation of protein aggregates, long‐lived proteins, and whole cargoes by a mechanism called autophagy (Hood et al. 2019; Aman et al. 2021). The autophagy‐mediated breakdown of biomolecules and organelles is essential to maintain cellular health as this catabolic system removes dysfunctional organelles that would otherwise perturb cellular functions. Apart from this canonical function, lysosomes are also important for cellular energy metabolism, cell signaling, nutrient sensing, and membrane repair (Trivedi et al. 2020).

Through autophagy, targeted dysfunctional organelles are tagged and sequestered in phagophores maturing into double membranous autophagosomes that eventually fuse with lysosomes for proteolytic degradation (Yim and Mizushima 2020). Disrupted autophagy due to the accumulation of detrimental cytosolic components has been shown to affect cellular and physiological health leading to chronic diseases. Compromised lysosomal functions have been implicated in the pathophysiology of neurodegenerative diseases, autoimmune disorders, malignant tumors, cancer, cardiomyopathies and lysosomal storage diseases (Chi et al. 2020; Gros and Muller 2023; Tang et al. 2020; Udayar et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2021). Furthermore, lysosomal activity is affected during cellular stress and hypoxia (Nakashima et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023). Notably, increasing evidence demonstrates lysosomal dysfunctions as one of the pivotal characteristics of musculoskeletal disorders that elicit muscle atrophy, neuromuscular junction decay, and weakness (Carnio et al. 2014; Hood et al. 2019; Masiero et al. 2009; Raben et al. 2008). Disrupted autophagy is also associated with inactivity, ageing, and obesity (Drummond et al. 2014; C. He et al. 2012). Thus, studies focusing on enhancing the autophagy pathway as well as lysosomal function during such conditions would be helpful to ameliorate various diseases.

Autophagy is upregulated as an adaptive response to various intracellular and extracellular stress and signals. The process is triggered by nutritional and energy stresses to recycle damaged organelles or nutrients for cell survival (He et al. 2018). Additionally, autophagy is also triggered by exercise. While evidence has suggested that muscle with compromised autophagic function and aberrant proteostasis led to muscle myopathy (Masiero et al. 2009), physical exercise stimulated autophagy and dampened the age‐related deterioration of muscle mass (He et al. 2012; Masiero et al. 2009). There have been a considerable number of studies related to the upregulation of autophagy with exercise in murine and rat skeletal muscles (Drake et al. 2021; Jiang and Mizushima 2014; Ju et al. 2016; Nair and Klionsky 2011; Santiago‐Fernández et al. 2025) whereby the cytosolic components are engulfed by the autophagosome and directed to lysosomes for degradation (Vainshtein and Hood 2016). In response to exercise, autophagy helped in providing substrates for energy production (Dokladny et al. 2015; Fry et al. 2013; Vainshtein and Hood 2016), keeping physiological homeostasis, and inducing exercise‐mediated beneficial adaptations (Halling and Pilegaard 2017; Vainshtein et al. 2014) manifested by lysosomal biogenesis and enhanced mitochondrial content in many instances (Ju et al. 2016; Kim and Hood 2017a). Our previous work has shown that chronic contractile activity caused autophagosome formation in aged skeletal muscle (Carter et al. 2018). Paradoxically, aging muscle also exhibited morphological characteristics of lysosomal insufficiency encompassing lipofuscin accumulation and enlarged dysfunctional vesicular structures. This might suggest that a rate‐limiting step related to autophagic dysfunction in aging and diseased muscle might be at the level of the lysosome rather than at autophagosome formation or transport. Despite this insight, experimental models that systematically investigate lysosomal dysfunction in muscle cells and strategically rescue the functions remain limited. In view of the above, we sought to investigate whether chronic contractile activity mimicking exercise in cellular models would resuscitate lysosomal functions in conditions where their activities are hindered. We also wished to consider the use of pharmacological interventions that would render the same benefits imparted by exercise during lysosomal dysfunction.

To address the gap, we established a model of lysosomal dysfunction in skeletal muscle cells using Bafilomycin A1 (BAF), an agent that hinders vATPases that are crucial for maintaining low luminal pH thereby inhibiting the lysosomal enzymes (Mauvezin and Neufeld 2015). Our results indicate that inhibition of lysosomal function increases lysosomal accumulation and dampens lysosomal activity, as expected, in myoblasts and myotubes which are muscle cells at different stages of differentiation. However, subjecting the differentiated myotubes to CCA prevented the detrimental consequences imposed by BAF. In parallel, we explored a complementary pharmacological strategy aimed at improving lysosomal quality using the transcription factor EB (TFEB) activator Curcumin (C1). We found that C1 treatment enhanced the mitochondrial content and reduced lysosomal markers independent of BAF treatment, while improving lysosome function and enhancing mitophagy flux in myoblasts and myotubes. This treatment largely replicated the effects triggered by contractile activity. Overall, the novel aspect of the study focusses on the establishment and characterization of a muscle–specific cellular model of lysosomal dysfunction induced at the level of v‐ATPase inhibition, and the potential ameliorative role of a physiological intervention such as chronic contractile activity. We supplemented this with the use of C1 to examine its additivity or similarity to the effects of muscle contraction with the aim of treating muscle lysosomal‐related diseases in the absence of an exercise intervention.

Methods

Cell Culture

Proliferating C2C12 myoblast cells were grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplied with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin streptomycin. For differentiation experiments, myoblasts were grown in 6‐well plates. 2% horse serum and 1% penicillin streptomycin were added to the myoblasts at 85% to 90% confluency. Differentiation continued for 5 days before myotubes were subjected to chronic contractile activity (CCA). All cells and their conditions were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. An average of 5–6 wells of each plate were considered for each experiment. For 5 n's, five different biological replicates from different cellular passages were used. Each biological replicates were grown in 5 to 6 wells of 6‐well tissue culture plates.

Chronic Contractile Activity (CCA)

Lids from plastic 6‐well plates were fitted with 2 mm platinum wire electrodes such that lengths ran parallel to each other at opposite ends. When placed on their respective dish, the lids became submerged in media. Fully differentiated myotubes after 5 days of differentiation were subjected to electrical stimulation‐induced contractile activity in a parallel circuit attached to a stimulator unit at a frequency of 5 Hz and an intensity of 10 V. The stimulator unit was fabricated in‐house (York University Electronics Shop) and has been used for many years (Connor et al. 2001). The stimulation was administered for 3 h, followed by a 21‐h recovery for 4 consecutive days. Media was replaced following each stimulation session. This protocol has been previously described in detail (Connor et al. 2001; Parousis et al. 2018).

Reagents and Treatments

Myoblasts were treated with 6 nM BAF (BAF002.1, BIOSHOP, 95% purity; commercial use) or vehicle (DMSO) as done previously (Parousis et al. 2018) for 24 h, or with 3 μM Curcumin (C1) (Millipore Sigma, C1386) or vehicle for 48 h before the cells were collected for different assays. Before using the final concentration, different concentrations from 3 nM to 12 nM of BAF were tested to evaluate the toxicity and cell viability. We chose 6 nM because there were no toxic effects of BAF at that concentration. Similarly, for C1, concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 6 μM were tested 3 μM was chosen for BAF experiments on myotubes, 6 nM BAF or DMSO only was added after the third day of CCA and before and after the fourth day of CCA. For C1 treated cells, 3 μM C1 or DMSO only was added on day 3 after CCA, on day 4 before and after CCA. For the CCA experiments, cells were harvested on day 5, 21 h post CCA. Autophagy flux was calculated by subtracting the LC3‐II levels between cells treated with BAF in the absence and presence of CCA and those treated with a DMSO only in the absence and presence of CCA, reflecting the rate of autophagic activity.

Nuclear Isolation

C2C12 cells untreated or treated with C1 were pelleted, washed in phosphate buffered saline

(PBS), dissolved in 500 µL of Buffer 1 (10 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP‐40 with 1 mg/mL of each pepstatin, leupeptin and aprotinin protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 5 min followed by rocking on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation at 2500 g for 5 min at 4°C, the cell pellet that represents the “nuclear fraction” was washed 8–10 times with the Buffer 1 and lysed with Buffer 2 (nuclear lysis buffer) containing 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 40% (w/v) glycerol and 0.15 unit/µL benzonase (Novagen).

Western Blotting

Whole cell, cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions were isolated from the myoblasts and myotubes. Equal amounts of protein (~20–30 μg) were loaded and separated via SDS‐PAGE (Parousis et al. 2018). Proteins were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes and blocked in 5%–10% milk in Tris‐buffered saline‐Tween 20 (TBS‐T). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with the appropriate concentration of primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and then with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary anti‐mouse (Cell Signaling 7076S) or anti‐rabbit antibodies (Cell Signaling 7074S) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (iBright 1500, Invitrogen). Band quantification was performed using FIJI software.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used were Lamp1 (Abcam: ab24170) (Dilution 1:1000), LC3 A/B (Cell Signaling: 4108S) (Dilution 1:1000), p62 (Cell Signaling: 51145) (Dilution 1:1000), Cathepsin B (Cell Signaling: D1C7Y) (Dilution 1:1000), Cox4 (Abcam: ab14744) (Dilution 1:5000), VDAC (Abcam: ab14734) (Dilution 1:1000), α‐tubulin (Millipore‐Sigma: CP06‐100UG [DM1A]) (Dilution 1:1000). The loading control was GAPDH (Abcam: ab8254) (Dilution 1:10000) (Triolo et al. 2022). The secondary antibodies used horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary anti‐mouse (Cell Signaling 7076S) or anti‐rabbit antibodies (Cell Signaling 7074S) in the dilution of 1:10000.

Immunostaining

Both C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes treated with BAF or C1 or vehicle (DMSO) following their respective conditions with or without CCA were stained with MitoTracker™ Red or LysoTracker™ Green for 30 min, and Hoechst for 10 min, before they were imaged. C2C12 myoblasts treated with BAF or DMSO were stained with DQ‐BSA for 4 h before they were imaged. Images were taken by Nikon eclipse TE‐2000 confocal microscope equipped with a ×60/1.5 oil objective lens and a custom‐designed chamber for live cell imaging that maintained a constant temperature of 37°C with 5% CO2.

Flow Cytometry

The flow cytometry experiments were done on Beckman Coulter's CytoFLEX using CytExpert software. C2C12 myoblasts or differentiated myotubes treated with BAF or C1 or DMSO in the presence or absence of CCA were stained with DQ‐BSA Green, LysoView™ and MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos for 30 min. The cells were then washed with PBS and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were then resuspended in 500 μL PBS for flow cytometry analysis. In the myoblasts and myotubes, the lysosomal content was assessed by gating the cells stained positive for LysoView™ that stain only the lysosomes. We have quantified the fluorescence intensity of LysoView™ by measuring the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (geo‐mean) in the PB‐450 channel. For lysosomal function assessment, the cells that stained positive for DQ‐BSA Green were taken and quantification was done by measuring the geo‐mean intensity in the FITC channel. For mitochondrial content, the cells stained positive for MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos indicating mitochondrial content were gated and quantification was done by measuring the geo‐mean intensity in the APC channel. For assessing the mitochondrial or lysosomal content, percentages of the LysoView™ or MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos positive cells were taken for analysis, whereas to measure the fluorescence intensity, geo‐mean fluorescence intensity was considered.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Unpaired t‐test or Two‐Way ANOVA with post‐hoc Tukey's test were used. Data are presented as +/− standard deviation. Asterisks denote significance *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Inhibition of Autophagy Increases Lysosomal Accumulation in the Myoblasts

Autophagy and lysosomal functions are intimately interconnected and crucial for maintaining cellular functions. Therefore, hindrance of this interaction would have negative impacts on cellular health (Aman et al. 2021). To assess the effect of autophagy inhibition on lysosomal content, we used BAF, which is known to inhibit lysosome acidification and dampen autophagosome‐lysosome fusion. To accomplish this, we began by studying proliferating myoblasts and then investigated differentiated myotubes that can undergo contractile activity when electrically stimulated.

We treated C2C12 myoblasts without (Ctl) or with 6 nM BAF for 24 h, before the myoblasts were lysed for whole cell extraction (Figure 1A). Western blot and subsequent quantification revealed that lysosomal associated membrane protein Lamp1 increased by twofold (p = 0.0042) with BAF treatment (Figure 1A,B). The ubiquitin associated protein p62/SQSTM1, as well as LC3‐II which reflect the autophagosome accumulation, also increased by threefold (p < 0.0001) and eightfold (p < 0.0001), respectively (Figure 1A,C,D) with no significant change in LC3‐I level (Figure 1A,E) when treated with BAF. Furthermore, the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio also increased by fivefold with BAF treatment (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1A,F).

To confirm the effect of BAF on lysosomal content we used immunostaining assays, where we marked the lysosomes with LysoTracker™ Green, mitochondria with MitoTracker™ Red, and nuclei with Hoechst (Figure 1G). We found that BAF treatment resulted in a significant accumulation of the lysosomes as evidenced by increased fluorescent intensity of LysoTracker™ Green in the BAF‐treated myoblasts (Figure 1G). Further, we conducted flow cytometry analysis, where myoblasts were treated with BAF or without (Ctl) for 24 h and stained with LysoView™ for 30 min before the cells were collected. Consistent with our immunoblot and immunostaining data, we found that the BAF treatment increased lysosomal content (Figure 1H). Geo‐mean fluorescent intensity of LysoView™ showed a > 20‐fold (p < 0.0001) increase in lysosomal fluorescence with BAF treatment (Figure 1I), indicating that the inhibition of autophagy with BAF increased lysosomal accumulation. The twofold increase in lysosomal proteins such as Lamp1 is discrepant from the larger 20‐fold change evident in the LysoView™ staining (Figure 1I) likely because Lamp1 is only one marker of lysosome content, and flow cytometry is a more sensitive tool to detect organelle changes than immunoblotting. Furthermore, quantification of the integrated density of the immunofluorescence images of the BAF‐treated myoblasts showed fivefold (p < 0.0001) increase of LysoTracker™ Green compared to the control cells (Suppl. Figure 1). Consistently, these data indicate that BAF treatment induces large increases in lysosome content and its representative marker proteins.

BAF Treatment Decreases Lysosomal Activity in the Myoblasts

As BAF increased lysosomal accumulation in the myoblasts, we also investigated its effect on lysosomal activity. We treated the myoblasts without (Ctl) or with 6 nM BAF for 24 h and conducted western blot analysis. We found that BAF treatment decreased the proteolytically cleaved mature active cathepsin B by 13‐fold (p = 0.0010), indicative of impaired lysosomal proteolytic activity (Figure 2A,C). This was accompanied by a 13‐fold increase in immature, precursor cathepsin B (p = 0.0082) (Figure 2A,B) and a 33‐fold (p = 0.0010) increase in pre/total cathepsin B further illustrating significant lysosomal dysfunction with BAF treatment. We further confirmed the changes in lysosomal activity with BAF treatment via immunostaining and flow cytometry analyses. Ctl and BAF‐treated myoblasts were stained with DQ‐BSA green for 30 min before the cells were collected for analysis. Under homeostatic conditions, DQ‐BSA green moves to lysosomes where it undergoes cleavage, thus producing a bright green fluorescent stain. We found that compared to vehicle‐treated cells, BAF‐treated myoblasts showed a significant reduction in DQ‐BSA green fluorescence intensity, as observed by immunostaining (Figure 2E). Histogram analysis (Figure 2F) and subsequent quantification (Figure 2G) of DQ‐BSA green‐treated cells also showed that BAF treatment significantly reduced the fluorescent intensity of DQ‐BSA green by 2.2‐fold (p = 0.0008), indicating that treatment with BAF impaired proteolysis, an indicator of diminished lysosomal function. Taken together, our results showed that BAF treatment increases the lysosomal accumulation and attenuates lysosomal activity in the proliferating myoblasts.

CCA Mitigates the Effect of BAF and Attenuates the BAF‐Induced Increase in Lysosomes in Myotubes

Since we found that BAF increased lysosomal accumulation in myoblasts, we were intrigued to investigate the effect in differentiated myotubes. To elucidate a strategy to improve lysosomal content in situations where autophagy is inhibited, we used CCA and assessed the effect of BAF in the presence and absence of CCA. Myoblasts were grown until they were 85%–90% confluent and then differentiated into myotubes for 5 days prior to being subjected to CCA for 4 days and compared to non‐stimulated control myotubes (Figure 3A). On day 3 of stimulation, 6 nM BAF was added following the bout of CCA, as well as on day 4 before and after CCA. Cells were collected 21 h after the last bout of stimulation. CCA increased Cox4 by threefold (p = 0.0010) in the untreated (Unt CCA) myotubes, indicative of mitochondrial biogenesis (Figure 3B,C). CCA also produced a similar threefold enhancement in the mitochondrial content of BAF‐treated myotubes (BAF CCA) (p = 0.0004) (Figure 3B,C). This suggests that CCA activates mitochondrial biogenesis irrespective of lysosomal activity. We also assessed VDAC as another marker of mitochondrial content. Although VDAC did not increase in the untreated (Unt CCA) cells in presence of CCA, BAF‐treated myotubes (BAF CCA) showed a considerable increase in VDAC protein content (1.4‐fold) (p = 0.0014), when subjected to CCA (Figure 3B,D). On analyzing the effectiveness of CCA by immunostaining we found that, similar to the western blot results, CCA enhanced the fluorescent intensity of MitoTracker™ Red (Figure 4A). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that the geo‐mean intensity of MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos was increased by 1.7‐folds in both the control (Unt CCA) (p = 0.0001) and and BAF‐treated myotubes (BAF CCA) (p = 0.0041) (Figure 4D), thereby further validating that the contractile activity worked well both in the absence or presence of any pharmacological interventions.

After confirming that the CCA treatment was effective in increasing mitochondrial content, we investigated the effect of BAF on autophagy and lysosomal proteins. Interestingly, BAF treatment alone significantly increased both p62 (7‐fold) (p < 0.0001) and Lamp1 (4‐fold) (p < 0.0001), similar to our observations in myoblasts (Figure 3B,E,F). BAF also increased LC3‐II by threefold (p = 0.0006) compared to the Unt cells, (Figure 3B,H). This manifested in an increased LC3‐II/I ratio (p = 0.0173), illustrating the high level of autophagic accumulation which takes place under control conditions (Figure 3B,I,J). These results are similar to those found with myoblasts (Figure 1F). CCA itself did not have any appreciable effect on the expression of Lamp1, p62 or LC3‐II in differentiated myotubes. However, in BAF‐treated myotubes CCA caused a reduction in protein accumulation by 2.4‐fold (p = 0.0004) for p62, twofold for Lamp1 (p = 0.0033), and 1.4‐fold for LC3‐II levels (Figure 3B,3E–3H). While CCA had no effect on LC3‐I level, it reduced LC3‐II//‐I ratio by 1.8‐fold (Figure 3B,G,I) as well as the accumulation of LC3II in the presence of BAF by 2.2‐fold (p = 0.0253) (Figure 3J). Immunostaining and flow cytometry assays were performed to confirm these changes. BAF treatment increased the fluorescence intensity of LysoTracker™ Green, indicative of increased lysosomal accumulation, whereas CCA was able to reduce this fluorescence (Figure 4A). Quantification of the integrated density of LysoTracker™ Green in the immunostaining images showed that with BAF treatment the integrated density was threefold (p < 0.0001) higher compared to the control cells. Similar to the Western blot data, it showed that although CCA alone did not cause any appreciable changes, in BAF‐treated myotubes CCA caused a reduction in the integrated density by 1.5‐fold (p < 0.0001) (Suppl Figure 2). We also treated myotubes with LysoView™, MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos and DQ‐BSA green for 30 min and examined them by flow cytometry. Quantitative analysis of the LysoView™ positive cells showed that BAF treatment increased lysosomal content by fivefold (p = 0.0004). CCA effectively reduced the lysosomal accumulation in the BAF treated myotubes by 1.3‐fold (p = 0.0458) (Figure 4B,C). These data reveal that while lysosomal content accumulates in situations of inhibited autophagosome‐lysosome formation, it can be mitigated by contractile activity in myotubes.

CCA Revitalizes the Decreased Lysosomal Activity Caused by BAF Treatment

As CCA was shown to reduce the exaggerated lysosomal content produced under BAF‐treated conditions, we then sought to investigate its effect on lysosomal activity. As observed in the myoblasts, we found that BAF treatment decreased the level of mature cathepsin B in differentiated myotubes by 2.4‐fold (p < 0.0001) with modestly increasing the precursor cathepsin B. This significantly increased the pre/total cathepsin B ratio by 3.6‐fold (p < 0.0001) (Figure 5A–5D) indicative of dysfunction in the maturation of cathepsin B. CCA did not ameliorate this maturation process in the untreated or BAF‐treated cells (Figure 5A–5D). However, when a more global assessment of lysosomal degradation capacity was measured using DQ‐BSA Green staining, the reduction in proteolytic capacity observed with BAF (p < 0.0001) was improved by 1.8‐fold (p = 0.0025) in CCA‐treated myotubes (Figure 5E,F). Our data illustrate that contractile activity has the potential to prevent the accumulation of lysosomes and rejuvenate the function of the compromised lysosomes in autophagy pathway‐deficient conditions.

Pharmacological Improvement of Lysosomal and Mitochondrial Content by Curcumin (C1) in Myoblasts

As opposed to increasing lysosomal accumulation and dysfunction using BAF inhibition, we used a contrasting strategy to improve lysosome function using Curcumin (C1) which is a natural polyphenolic compound derived from the herbal medicine, turmeric (Curcuma longa Linn.). C1 is known to activate the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and function, TFEB by direct binding and activation (Figure 6A). First, we wanted to confirm that C1 activates TFEB in the myoblasts as shown in other cell types (Song et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2016). Therefore, we treated the myoblasts with 3 uM C1 for 48 h and isolated the nuclear fractions. Western blot analysis of the nuclear fractions of myoblasts treated with or without C1 revealed enhanced TFEB subcellular localization in the nuclear compartment compared to the control (Ctl) cells by 1.4‐fold (p = 0.00275) (Figure 6B,C).

To decipher the effect of C1 on autophagy and lysosomal content, we treated myoblasts with C1 (3 uM) or vehicle (Ctl) for 48 h. Western blot analysis revealed that C1 treatment increased LC3‐I by 1.5‐fold (p = 0.0131) but had no impact on LC3‐II content, such that the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio decreased significantly by 1.5‐fold (p = 0.0077) (Figure 6D–6G). This suggests that C1 acts to increase the synthesis of LC3‐I without impacting its maturation to LC3‐II. Western blot analysis revealed that C1 reduced Lamp1 by 1.7‐fold (p = 0.0056) (Figure 6D,6H) but had no significant impact on the premature or mature forms of cathepsin B (Figure 6D,I–K). Interestingly, we found that C1 markedly increased Cox4 by 2.3‐fold (p = 0.0557), plausibly due to the transcriptional effect of TFEB translocation into the nucleus (Figure 6D,L). This is in line with a previous study that showed that curcumin was able to increase Cox4 protein expression and citrate synthase activity in rat skeletal muscle (37).

We wanted to further confirm the data using immunostaining and flow cytometry analysis. Therefore, we stained untreated (Ctl) or C1‐treated myoblasts with LysoTracker™ Green, MitoTracker™ Red and Hoechst. Immunostaining showed that the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker™ Red also increased in C1‐treated myoblasts (Figure 7A) corresponding to the Cox4 results. This was fortified by flow cytometry analysis which showed that the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos, increased by 1.5‐fold (p = 0.0075) in C1 treated myoblasts compared to the control cells (Figure 7F,G). Similar to the Lamp1 results, C1 treatment decreased the fluorescence intensity of LysoTracker™ Green (Figure 7A). Quantifications of the integrated density of the LysoTracker™ Green also showed 1.4‐fold (p < 0.0001) attenuated intensity in the C1 treated myoblasts compared to the control cells (Suppl Figure 3). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis on the Ctl and C1‐treated cells that were stained with LysoView™ also showed that lysosomal content was reduced by 1.4‐fold (p < 0.0001) (Figure 7B,C). However, histogram and subsequent quantitative analysis showed that there was 1.5‐fold (p = 0.0209) enhancement of the fluorescent intensity of DQ‐BSA (Figure 7D,E). This suggests that C1 was able decrease lysosomal content, enhance lysosomal function and improve the mitochondrial content of myoblasts.

C1 Decreased Lysosomal Content and Increased Mitochondrial Content in Differentiated Myotubes

As we observed promising results in myoblasts, we further investigated whether C1 could modify the phenotype of differentiated myotubes. We also employed CCA to enhance the mitochondrial content as the positive control. Myoblasts were differentiated into myotubes for 5 days, after which they were either kept as controls or were subjected to CCA for 4 days (Figure 8A). C1 (3 uM) was added on day 3 after CCA and on day 4 before and after CCA, prior to collection 21 h after the last bout of CCA. C1 increased Cox4 significantly by 2.3‐fold (p < 0.0001). CCA alone increased Cox4 by ninefold (p < 0.0001) (Unt CCA), and by fourfold (p < 0.0001) in the presence of C1 (C1 CCA) (Figure 8B,C) to identical levels. This suggests that these interventions may act via similar or discrete mechanisms. We also found that VDAC was increased with C1 treatment by twofold (p = 0.0453) (Figure 8B,D). This indicates that C1 may be a useful pharmacological intervention that can enhance mitochondrial content.

As C1 was effective in reducing the lysosomal accumulation in myoblasts, we evaluated this in fully differentiated myotubes, in the presence or absence of contractile activity. Similar to our findings with myoblasts, we found that C1 tended to increase LC3‐I by 1.5‐fold (p = 0.0285) and decreased LC3‐II by 1.9‐fold (p = 0.0603), resulting in a significant reduction in the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio by 2.4‐fold (Figure 8B,E–G). These results triggered by C1 were also very similar to those obtained when the myotubes were subjected to CCA alone. This was manifested by a 1.5‐fold increase in LC3‐I, a 2.4‐fold (p = 0.0002) decrease of LC3‐II and a 3.3‐fold decrease of LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio (Figure 8B,E–G). The decrease in the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio in the presence of C1 and CCA suggests a treatment‐induced improvement in lysosomal function. Furthermore, C1 significantly decreased Lamp1 by 2.2‐fold (p = 0.0209), and the effect was comparable to the 1.9‐fold (p = 0.0498) decline induced by CCA (Figure 8B,H). We found that C1 did not have any influence on the precursor or mature cathepsin B which was similar to the effect observed due to CCA (Figure 8B,I,J).

To further validate our findings, we also performed immunostaining and flow cytometry analysis. Immunostaining by confocal microscopy showed that, similar to the western blots, C1 diminished the fluorescence intensity of LysoTracker™ Green, representing lysosomal content and the same effect was shown by CCA alone or with C1 (Figure 9A). Quantification of the integrated density of the LysoTracker™ Green also showed 1.7‐fold (p < 0.0001) attenuated intensity in the C1 treated myotubes compared to the control cells (Suppl. Figure 4). The cells treated or untreated with C1 in the presence or absence of CCA were also stained with LysoView™ and analyzed by flow cytometry. Analysis of the total lysosomal fluorescent intensity and subsequent quantification showed that C1 reduced the intensity by1.5‐fold (p < 0.0001), which was again very similar to that induced by CCA (p < 0.0001) alone or with C1 (1.4‐fold) (p < 0.0001) (Figure 9A–C). To verify the effects of C1 on mitochondrial content, we stained myotubes with MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos and analyzed them by flow cytometry. Mitochondrial content was observed to be prominently enhanced in C1‐treated cells and this was also similar to that induced by CCA (Figure 9A). C1 and CCA increased MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos staining in myotubes by twofold (p = 0.0211) and 2.2‐fold (p = 0.0031), respectively (Figure 9D,E). Thus, together these data suggest that C1 can enhance mitochondrial content, reduce lysosomal content and modestly improve lysosomal function. These effects are similar to those that were promoted by CCA.

Discussion

Autophagy is a highly conserved and quality control mechanism that maintains skeletal muscle health and cellular homeostasis (Franco‐Romero and Sandri 2021; Romanello and Sandri 2016; Vainshtein and Hood 2016). Central to this are the acidic organelles, lysosomes that contain over sixty luminal hydrolases and other important cellular regulators. The initiation of autophagy starts with the expansion of a membrane segment called a phagophore around cytoplasmic material, which is selectively targeted for degradation. Phagophores expand and engulf more cytoplasmic materials forming autophagosomes, the outer membrane of which fuses with lysosomes forming autolysosomes. Thereafter, the lysosomes provide the acidic hydrolases necessary for degradation of the inner autophagosomal membrane and the sequestered materials (Nanayakkara et al. 2023; Yim and Mizushima 2020). Inhibition of the full autophagy pathway has been observed during ageing and in several diseases, where autophagy‐regulated genes are inhibited (Lei and Klionsky 2021; Yim and Mizushima 2020). For example, the failure of autophagy has been observed in congenital muscular dystrophies and other myopathies, and has been identified as one of the major causes of several muscle‐related disorders (Xia et al. 2021). Inhibition of autophagy causes muscle atrophy, neuromuscular junction decay, sarcomere disarrangement, and muscle weakness (Carnio et al. 2014; Nemazanyy et al. 2013; Raben et al. 2008). Moreover, muscles deficient in autophagy failed to elicit exercise‐mediated benefits (Grumati et al. 2011; C. He et al. 2012; Lira et al. 2013). Collectively, this information strengthens the fact that autophagy plays a crucial role in maintaining muscle health.

Although, there has been a considerable advancement in understanding the underlying mechanism of autophagic inhibition, the strategies to improve degradative pathway functions still have not been fully elucidated. Identifying therapeutic strategies would help combat the progression of several muscle‐related diseases with potential improvements in the lysosomal functions. Importantly, lysosomal storage disorders arising from deficiencies in lysosomal enzymes or membrane proteins, represent genetic models of lysosomal dysfunction associated with impaired autophagy in skeletal muscle. Pharmacological activation of lysosomal biogenesis transcription factor EB (TFEB), is being actively explored as a therapeutic strategy in these disorders, where enhanced lysosomal clearance improved cellular homeostasis in experimental models (Feeney et al. 2013; Sato et al. 2016). These genetic anomalies highlight the translational relevance of interventions for resuscitating the lysosomal quality and autophagic function in skeletal muscle. Our previous research has shown that autophagy flux, up to the point of the lysosome (i.e. autophagosome transport along microtubules) is accelerated in ageing muscle (Carter et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2018). However, ageing muscle also displays morphologic features that suggest that lysosome function is impaired (O'Leary et al. 2013; Triolo et al. 2022a). Lipofuscin accumulation and vesicular bodies are abundantly observed in electron micrographs of old muscle. This suggests to us that although autophagosome flux is accelerated, the limiting step in the process of complete autophagic degradation of cargo in ageing muscle may lie at the point of the lysosome. We have also directly shown that lysosome function is impaired in isolated organelles derived from aged skeletal muscle (Mahendran et al. 2025).

Thus, to study the mechanisms involved in lysosome dysfunction and its effects on muscle health, along with the potential therapeutic effects of contractile activity, we created a chemically‐induced muscle cell model of lysosome dysfunction using a specific inhibitor of the lysosomal vacuolar‐type H+‐translocating ATPase, Bafilomycin (BAF). BAF is known to inhibit acidification as well as autophagosome‐lysosome formation, thereby preventing targeted materials to be sequestered (Klionsky et al. 2008). We also investigated this in cells at different stages of the skeletal muscle differentiation process. This enabled us to study the lysosomal functions at the myogenic proliferating precursor cell level, as well at the stage of differentiated myotubes that have the ability to undergo electrical stimulation‐induced contractile activity. We have used CCA for many years (Connor et al. 2001; Oliveira et al. 2023; Parousis et al. 2018) to investigate the potential of “exercise” as a therapeutic modality in a highly controlled myotube cell culture system.

Our results, obtained using a variety of techniques, including immunoblotting, flow cytometry and confocal microscopy in both myoblasts and myotubes indicate that BAF led to an accumulation of lysosomes with markedly diminished abilities to function as an organelle in degrading delivered cargos. We then sought to reverse the detrimental effect of autophagy inhibition in presence of BAF by subjecting the differentiated myotubes to CCA in order to mimic exercise at the cellular level. Thus, we treated the myotubes with BAF in the last 48 h of the 4‐day CCA protocol to monitor the effects of CCA on lysosomal quality in conditions of sustained impaired autophagy. As shown previously (Parousis et al. 2018; Uguccioni and Hood 2011), CCA was able to increase mitochondrial quantity as shown by the inner membrane Cox4 protein level both in the presence and absence of BAF. CCA did not enhance outer membrane VDAC protein levels, which suggests that CCA causes selective protein remodeling rather than global mitochondrial expansion. This selective adaptation might be advantageous, facilitating skeletal muscle to enhance energy production while preserving mitochondrial membrane integrity and cellular homeostasis.

To investigate the effects of CCA on autophagy, we measured Lamp1, LC3‐II and p62 accumulation, as well as the LC3‐II:LC3‐I ratio and autophagic accumulation in myotubes treated with either BAF or vehicle to assess flux. Intriguingly, we found that CCA was able to reverse the effects of BAF in the differentiated myotubes, manifested by significant drops in Lamp1, LC3II, p62, LC3II/LC3I ratio protein levels, and also in the autophagic accumulation of autophagosomes as assessed by immunoblotting. These results were further validated by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. In addition to the increased clearance of lysosomes, CCA was able to enhance lysosomal activity as shown by improved BSA‐DQ fluorescence in the presence of BAF.

We were also intrigued to investigate if pharmacological intervention could improve lysosomal quality in both myoblasts and differentiated myotubes. We used Curcumin (C1) which is a hydrophobic polyphenol derived from turmeric. C1 was shown to have beneficial effects in cancer cells by reducing cancer cell proliferation and invasion via induction of autophagy (Zhao et al. 2016; Zhu and Bu 2017). C1 also interacts with the TFEB (Song et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2016) and we, and others (Zhang et al. 2016) have shown that C1 promotes TFEB nuclear translocation. Despite this, C1 effectively reduced lysosome content but accelerated autophagosome clearance, manifested by the decreased levels of Lamp1 and p62 proteins and decreases in the LC3II/LC3I ratio. In addition, we observed an increased lysosomal proteolytic activity in myoblasts as measured by DQ‐BSA treated with C1, although it did not affect the maturation of cathepsin B. Thus, C1 appears to exert broad effects on the lysosome, and the specific pathways involved remain to be identified.

The effects of C1 and CCA exhibit some similarities and some differences. As observed in previous studies in cardiomyocytes (Hou et al. 2024), hepatocytes (Kuo et al. 2012), skeletal muscle in vivo (Ray Hamidie et al. 2015), C1 also enhanced mitochondrial content in the myoblasts and myotubes, as assessed using immunoblotting, flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. Both C1 and CCA consistently increased LC3II clearance as well as mitochondrial content. C1 increased DQ‐BSA fluorescence, as did CCA in the presence of BAF. In addition, we have shown previously that CCA can also augment DQ‐BSA fluorescence under normal conditions (Oliveira et al. 2023), similar to that observed with C1, and that it promotes the translocation of TFEB to the nucleus both in vivo (Triolo et al. 2022b) and in cultured myotubes (Erlich et al. 2018). However, C1 treatment led to a reduction in lysosome content, and the prevailing view from previous studies is that contractile activity enhances lysosome content both in vivo (Kim and Hood 2017a) and in myotubes (Memme et al. 2023; Oliveira et al. 2023). Thus, it will be interesting in future studies to investigate the common and divergent signaling pathways triggered by both C1 and CCA that may be used additively to produce high quality lysosomes in muscle, particularly under conditions of a buildup of dysfunctional organelles, as observed following BAF treatment. The effect of C1 to reduce the number of dysfunctional lysosomes under those conditions would certainly be a welcome adaptation. However, an important consideration arising from the above observation is whether reducing lysosomal content is beneficial in unstressed muscle cells. The effects of C1 seem to suggest a trade‐off effect in which potentially excessive lysosomes are exchanged for a smaller content of higher functioning organelles, perhaps mediated by TFEB.

In conclusion, our results suggest that dysfunctional autophagy, induced at the level of lysosomal acidification and autophagosome‐lysosome interactions in myoblasts and myotubes, leads to an accumulation of lysosomes. The imposition of CCA was able to attenuate the disrupted autophagy by reducing excessive lysosomal numbers, normalizing flux and enhancing the lysosomal hydrolytic activity, as well as exerting its known effects to increase mitochondrial content. The mechanisms remain to be defined, but may include CCA‐induced changes in lysophagy, or the induction of the autophagy‐lysosome recycling (ALR) pathway, that upon elucidation would have potential therapeutic benefits. Pharmacological intervention by the curcumin analogue C1 was itself able to promote some similar benefits as contractile activity, improving lysosomal clearance of LC3II and enriching the mitochondrial pool. To further investigate the mechanistic aspects of autophagy–lysosome regulation and contractile activity–induced adaptations, future work in primary myoblasts derived from murine skeletal muscle could be performed, particularly if derived from an animal model deficient in lysosome fusion or function (Eg. Lamp or mucolipin deficiency). Instead of a chemically induced defect, these models would serve to more fully recapitulate the phenotypic heterogeneity, metabolic complexity, stress responsiveness and differences in lysosomal capacity and mitochondrial content associated with age, sex and diseased microenvironment of the skeletal muscle cells. At a translational level, our data suggest that exercise is likely capable of mitigating disrupted autophagy function at different stages of the muscle differentiation process, and that this can be mimicked by pharmacological intervention to enhance mitochondrial and lysosomal quality, thereby promoting muscle cell health. This provides us with an optimistic view of the important role of exercise in mitigating the disruption of mitophagy at the level of the lysosome in ageing muscle, as discussed above. Our results also suggest that skeletal muscle lysosomes are a labile pool of organelles, capable of rapid biogenesis as well as removal, and this offers an exciting future avenue of research in investigating the cellular signaling mechanisms of lysosomal turnover.
