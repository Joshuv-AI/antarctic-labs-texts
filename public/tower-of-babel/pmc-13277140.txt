SARS-CoV-2 intra-host viral diversity associated with host innate and vaccine-induced immunity in an obese mouse model
Authors: Wenchen Song, Yanxia Chen, Yihan Tong, Feifei Liu, Can Li, Zhanhong Ye, Peidi Ren, Jiaxuan Wang, Xinrui Cui, Alvin Hiu-Chung Lam, Hin Chu, Junhua Li, Jasper Fuk-Woo Chan, Kwok-Yung Yuen, Zhihua Ou, Anna Jin-Xia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity associated immune dysfunction is attributed to severe outcome of infectious diseases including Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) and suboptimal vaccine responses.MethodsUtilizing diet-induced obese (DIO) mice vaccinated with COVID-19 mRNA vaccine, we investigated SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 variant intra-host mutation under the influence of different host immune states with or without vaccination. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 infected mouse lung transcriptome and virus genome were acquired by RNA-sequencing.ResultsConsistent with previous report that COVID-19 mRNA vaccine failed to induced serum neutralizing antibody but boosted host innate immunity upon virus challenge. At 2 days after virus challenging, the interferon and antiviral related genes were upregulated in the lungs of lean mice (regular weight controls) and vaccinated DIO mice, but not in unvaccinated DIO mice suggesting host innate immune responses to infection was not elicited in DIO mice, which was then restored by vaccination. Analysis of viral genomes in the different lung samples showed that SARS-CoV-2 was predominately under neutral or slightly negative selection in mouse lungs irrespective of immune status. However, increased intra-host viral genomic diversity was found at later infection time and vaccination-boosted host innate antiviral status in DIO mice. In total, 59 iSNVs (intra-host single nucleotide variant) were detected from 22 viral-positive-samples, 1 to 8 iSNV sites in each sample. Among which, most of the iSNVs were detected from the lungs of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine immunized DIO mice. Correlation analysis of viral mutation and host transcriptome signatures showed that the appearance of iSNV in ORF1ab, Nucleocapsid (N) and Membrane (M) genes was associated with host innate immunity. Remarkably, the Leu176His mutation in M gene was identified in all the vaccinated obese mouse lungs whereas only in one lung at 4dpi in unvaccinated DIO, which may imply the interplay between viral mutation and host antiviral immunity in the context of obesity.ConclusionsThis study advances our comprehension of SARS-CoV-2 mutation and evolution in obese related insufficient vaccine response, highlighting the impact of immune compromised host on viral evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11418-z.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Obesity, Vaccine, Immune response, iSNV, RNA-seq

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic, SARS-CoV-2 evolved rapidly and generated diverse variants in different geographical regions. The variant strains with increased pathogenicity and/or transmissibility posed heavy burden to disease control. During the replication cycle of SARS-CoV-2, abundant intra-host single-nucleotide variants (iSNVs) will emerge due to the intrinsic error-prone characteristic of the viral RNA polymerases. The accumulation of high frequency iSNV may give rise to single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), driving the evolution of the viral population [1, 2]. The iSNV profiles revealed by deep sequencing technology have enabled the estimation of transmission chains and human-to-human transmission bottleneck size of SARS-CoV-2 [2, 3]. Exploring viral iSNV dynamics during the stress of vaccines may reveal insightful information for virus control and disease treatment.

Multiple cohort studies showed that obese individuals, especially those with BMI > 40 kg/m2, are at higher risk of developing severe disease of COVID-19 and fatal outcome [4–10]. Additionally, clinical reports indicated a delayed clearance of SARS-CoV-2 in overweight or obese COVID-19 patients which could be due to the well-known status of immune dysfunction associated with obesity [11, 12]. What’s worse, obesity hinders the protection effectiveness of vaccines. Vaccine induced humoral responses were found to wane more quickly in severely obese individuals, increasing the possibility of breakthrough infections and hospitalization caused by SARS-CoV-2 variants in the obese population [13–15]. Animal experimental data showed that viral clearance was delayed in vaccinated obese mice [16]. Given the increasingly high prevalence of obese population worldwide and their relatively higher susceptibility to pathogen infection, exploring the vaccine efficacy and viral characteristics at the context of obesity would provide guidance to the disease control strategy in this fragile population.

Since the emergence of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sublineage, there accumulated over 30 mutations in the spike protein and 13 of the mutations in the viral genes that were not present in the previously known sublineages [17]. Among the proposed origin of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sublineage, the more plausible one is the infection of immunocompromised individuals which provide microenvironments for a longer duration of virus replication and adaptative mutation [18]. For example, antigenic mutations were identified during the chronical infection of SARS-CoV-2 in an HIV-positive individual [19]. The vaccination (whether vaccinated, type of vaccine administered, or dosage) may influence the mutation rate of SARS-CoV-2 by modulating the host immune environment, including antibody levels and cellular immune responses [20, 21]. Obesity may affect immune-driven selection pressure, prolonging the viral replication cycle and thereby elevating the likelihood of viral mutations [22]. Given the larger number of populations with obesity worldwide and the associated immune-dysfunction, viral replication and mutation dynamic infection in obese individuals warrants investigation.

While the earlier strains of SARS-CoV-2 were unable to infect wild-type mice [23, 24], the Omicron variants with N501Y mutation in the spike protein can successfully infect C57/BL6N mice and cause lung inflammatory damage [25, 26]. Herein we used SARS-CoV Omicron strain BA.1 to conduct infection experiments in normal and diet-induced obese (DIO) mice with or without receiving COVID-19 mRNA vaccine to advance our understanding on SARS-CoV-2 intra-host mutation under circumstances of obesity associated immune dysfunction and insufficient vaccine responses.

Methods and materials

Viral stocks and animal model

The virus strain used for the infection experiment was derived from hCoV-19/Hong Kong/HKU-691/2021 (HKU691) (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_7138045) [27] and contained one amino acid mutation from the original human strain (NSP6: L260F). C57BL/6 N mice at 3–4 weeks of age were obtained from the Center of Comparative Medicine Research of HKU and kept in BSL-2 animal laboratory with a 12-hour-inteval day/night cycle. Obesity induction was performed as described previously [28]. Briefly, the mice were randomly divided into groups that fed with high-fat diet induce diet-induced obesity (DIO), the rest groups of mice from the same batch were fed with normal chew diet in parallel housing to raise control lean mice with regular body weight. At the time of experiment, the mice were at age of 22 weeks, average weight of 50–55 g for DIO and 30–32 g for lean mice, respectively. All experiments involving live SARS-CoV-2 were performed according to the standard operating procedures of Biosafety Level-3 facility at Department of Microbiology, the University of Hong Kong (HKU).

Vaccination procedure and virus infection experiment

The mice received a two-dose regimen of the COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2, lot number 1B004A, BioNTech, Germany) with a 14-day interval between doses. They were then randomly divided into groups. Diet-induced obese (DIO) and lean mice were intramuscularly injected with 50 µl (5 µg) of the COVID-19 mRNA vaccine or normal saline as a control [29]. Blood samples were collected at day 14 after the first dose of vaccination, and the second dose of mRNA vaccine was administered. Subsequently, blood samples were collected at 14 days after the second boost dose to assess antibody responses. For virus challenge, DIO and lean mice anesthetized by Ketamine (100 mg/kg) and Xylazine (10 mg/kg) were intranasally inoculated with 103 PFU of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 diluted in 20 µl phosphate buffered saline (PBS), mice of the control group were inoculated with 20 µl PBS. Mice were sacrificed at 2 and 4 dpi. Blood was harvested from per 5 mice in each group, and the lung tissue sample size were per 7 ~ 9 mice in each group for analysis.

Virus detection in lung tissues

Total RNA from the lung tissues of mice were extracted by the MiniBEST Universal RNA extraction kit (9766, Takara Bio Inc. Shiga, Japan). SARS-CoV-2 RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) gene copies were quantified by qRT-PCR with the QuantiNova Probe RT-PCR Kit (208354, Qiagen) and specific primers (5‘-CGCATACAGTCTTRCAGGCT-3’, 5‘-GTGTGATGTTGAWATGACATGGTC-3’) and probe (5‘-FAM-TTAAGATGTGGTGCTTGCATACGTAGAC-lABkFQ-3’) [30]. House-keeping gene β-actin which was amplified in parallel for sample RNA normalization and RdRp were performed on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche Applied Sciences, Indianapolis, USA). Multiplex PCR amplicon based sequencing was also conducted to confirm SARS-CoV-2 infections using ATOPlex SARS-CoV-2 Full Length Genome Panel following the manufacture’s protocol (MGI, Shenzhen, China) [31].

Library preparation and RNA-seq

Total RNA from lung tissues of DIO and lean mice was extracted using NucleoSpin ® RNA Kit (740955.250, MACHEREY-NAGEL, Duren, Germany). Total RNA was quantified using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the kit’s instructions. About 200 ng of total RNA was taken for rRNA removal using MGIEasy rRNA Depletion Kit (MGI TECH, Shenzhen, China). rRNA-depleted RNA was used to construct the dual barcode library with the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Set (MGI TECH, Shenzhen, China) and the MGIEasy UDB Primers Adapter Kit (MGI TECH, Shenzhen, China). RNA was fragmented before reverse transcription and double-strand synthesis. Then the double-stranded cDNA was subjected to end repair, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification. After the circularization reaction using the MGIEasy Dual Barcode Circularization Kit (MGI TECH, Shenzhen, China), the library was sequenced using a paired end 100 bp strategy in a DNBSEQ-T1 sequencer (MGI TECH, Shenzhen, China).

Host transcriptomics analysis

To reduce batch effect, 40 M reads were extracted from the RNA-seq data of each sample. The raw sequencing data were filtered with fastp (v0.20.1) [32] to remove adapters and low-quality reads. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was removed with URMAP (v1.0.1480) [33]. The clean reads were aligned against the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/ENSEMBL 84) with HISAT2 (v2.2.0) and the gene expression levels were normalized as TPM (transcripts per million) and quantified by StringTie (v2.1.538) [34]. Principal components analysis (PCA) was conducted with R v4.0. The differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| >1 and FDR value < 0.05) between groups were determined using DESeq2 (v3.1539) [35]. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with clusterProfiler (v4.0) [36] and Metascape [37].

iSNV calling of SARS-CoV-2 and phylogenetic analysis

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed with fastp and deduplicated by BBmap clumpify.sh. The reads were then aligned to the Omicron variant HKU691 with BWA-MEM (v0.7.17-r1188). The virus reads were then extracted with samtools (v0.20.1). Variants were called by freebayes (v1.3.6, --min-coverage 20 -F 0.05). The sequencing depth and coverage of SARS-CoV-2 for each sample were calculated with the samtools depth function. The raw vcf files were then processed with bcftools (v1.8). snpEFF was used to annotate the mutation position according to the HKU691.gff, and the file format was converted with the snippy-vcf_to_tab function [38]. To remove false positives caused by potential contamination and batch effect, we performed normalization to obtain virus reads per million sequencing reads (RPM) from ultra-deep RNA-Seq using the following formula: Total SARS-CoV-2 reads * 106/Total sequencing reads of the sample. Only samples with SARS-CoV-2 RPM > 3 and over 5% viral genome covered by > 10x sequencing depth were used for downstream analysis. The final SNP result was further filtered by the following criteria: (1) more than 80% of the gene length was covered by > 50x sequencing depth; (2) the sequencing depth of the variation site was over 50x. We assessed mutations through two distinct methodologies: (1) Mutation Ratio: This metric quantified the mutation ratio within gene coding regions as indicated in the annotated GFF file. (2) Actual Mutation Ratio: This parameter measured the mutation ratio within the actual 50x sequenced length of the gene or genome. For further phylogenetic analysis, the mouse iSNV-derived SARS-CoV-2 genomes and representative iSNV-associated genomes of human strains were aligned utilizing MAFFT with default configurations. A maximum likelihood (ML) tree was constructed using IQ-TREE (v.1.6.12) [39]. Visualization of the resulting ML tree was carried out using the R package ggtree (v.3.10) [40].

Genetic diversity estimation

The estimation of genetic diversity in each sample was measured by Shannon entropy, which was calculated based on the AAF of each iSNV site. The assumption made was that all iSNVs are independent from each other. In this calculation, P(i) represents the AAF at the specific variable site i. To evaluate and compare the genetic diversities within different groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed [41].

Nucleotide diversity (π)

Nucleotide diversity (π) serves as a summary measure quantifying the level of polymorphism exhibited by iSNVs within a given sample which accommodated potential biases arising from variations in sequencing depth [42]. Nucleotide diversity can be divided into non-synonymous nucleotide diversity (πN) and synonymous nucleotide diversity (πS). For every sample, the πN and πS was calculated using SNPGenie [43] with self-curated input vcf files.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IFN-α and IFN-β concentrations of lung homogenate were determined using the mouse IFN-α (Invitrogen, USA) and IFN-β (R&D systems, USA) ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microneutralization (MNT) assay

Serum samples were 2-fold serially diluted starting from 1:10 in PBS and mixed with 100 TCID50 of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 for 1 h at 37 °C. The mixture was added into pre-seeded VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells in 96-well plate and cultured at 37 °C for 72 h. CPE were observed, and neutralizing antibody titres were determined as the highest dilution of serum that completely inhibited the cytopathic effect.

Statistical analysis and plotting

For ELISA, qRT-PCR, and MNT data represented means and standard deviations. Statistical differences between two groups were evaluated with Student’s t-test and statistical differences between three or more groups were evaluated with one-way or two-way ANOVA by using GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical analyses of π and genetic diversity, including Kruskal-Wallis test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test, were performed in R (v4.0). Asterisks indicate the significance levels of p-values: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001.

Results

The infection of SARS-CoV-2 in mice with different immune statuses

Previously we demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 infection caused more severe inflammatory damages in the lung of DIO mice which were attributed partly to the impaired innate immune responses [28]. In this study, to investigate SARS-CoV-2 intra-host mutation under host innate immune pressure and vaccine-induced immunity, we inoculated SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 to four groups of C57/BL6N mice, including naïve DIO and lean mice, DIO and lean mice receiving two-doses of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines (prototype antigen) prior to virus inoculation (Fig. 1a). We investigated the intra-host mutations of SARS-CoV-2 using lung samples collected at 2 days post infection(dpi) or 4 dpi from lean or DIO mice with or without vaccination (Fig. 1a). Consistent with previous reports [28, 44], interferon-α (IFN-α) and interferon-β (IFN-β) production in the lung of unvaccinated DIO mice were significantly lower than those in unvaccinated lean mice after virus challenge (Fig. 1b), which confirms the impairment of antiviral interferon responses in the lung of DIO mice. However, vaccination boosted the production of IFN-α and IFN-β in the DIO mice against virus challenge, especially IFN-β (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, two-doses of mRNA vaccines induced serum neutralizing antibodies in the lean mice the titer of which were further increased upon virus challenge at 2 dpi (VLN2), but no neutralizing antibody was detected in vaccinated DIO mice, even after virus challenging at 2dpi (Fig. 1c). These data indicated vaccination did not elicit sufficient adaptive immune responses in DIO mice as in lean mice. In line with this results, viral load could not be detected from the lungs of vaccinated lean mice after virus challenge, while virus RNA was readily retrieved from the lung of vaccinated DIO mice (Fig. 1d).

To better understand the antiviral responses in the lung, transcriptomic profiles of lung tissues collected at 2 or 4 dpi were analyzed (Fig. 1e). The results showed a distinct enrichment of innate immune-regulating genes at 2 dpi, which encompassed interferon-stimulated genes (ISG15, OAS3), genes governing defense response to viruses (Ddx58, Irf7, Ifih1), genes response to Type I (Oas1a, Oas1g) and Type II interferons (Ccl7, Ccl8) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). But in unvaccinated mouse lungs, pathway enrichment analysis showed most of the anti-viral innate immune genes significantly upregulated in lean mice at 2 dpi (CLN2) comparing to DIO mice (COB2) (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1a). The expression of innate immune pathways, such as the defense response to viruses and the response to IFN-β, was only upregulated at 4dpi in DIO mice (COB4) compared to lean mice (CLN4), which suggests a delayed responses in DIO mice. Interestingly, comparing to unvaccinated DIO mice, all listed immune genes were significantly upregulated in vaccinated DIO mouse lungs at 2 dpi (VOB2) (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1a), suggesting vaccination restored the innate antiviral responses in the lung of DIO mice, despite no adaptive antibody were induced. Moreover, B cell activation and other innate immune responses in the CLN2 group were up-regulated (Supplementary Fig. 1b). These results suggest that COVID-19 mRNA vaccination somehow restored the weakened innate antiviral responses in DIO mice.

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 iSNVs in mouse lungs

To investigate the intra-host mutations of SARS-CoV-2, the lung samples collected at 2 or 4 dpi were used for iSNV analysis. Given the abundance of host mRNAs, virus reads constituted only a small proportion of the sequencing data, and the number of detected iSNVs relied on sequencing depth [2]. To address this, we conducted ultra-deep RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) (average 6.33 × 106 read pairs per sample). However, due to factors such as low viral load or undesirable RNA quality, some samples yielded insufficient data for virus analysis. At the same time, we additionally employed multiplex PCR amplicon-based sequencing to confirm their positivity [31] (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, no detectable viral genomes were observed in lung samples from vaccinated lean mice (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2), which is consistent with qPCR results (Fig. 1d). Since vaccinated lean mice had no breakthrough infection in the lungs, they were not included in the viral mutation analysis. To reduce false positives, only samples with virus reads per million sequencing reads (vRPM) exceeding 3 and over 5% of the viral genome covered by > 10x sequencing depth were retained for iSNV analysis. Subsequently, 27 positive samples were identified for iSNV detection. Moreover, considering potential variations in sequencing coverage among genes, iSNV calling of viral genes were conducted for genes with over 80% of protein coding sequences with a sequencing depth over 50x. The iSNVs were further filtered based on alternative allele frequency (AAF), using a cut-off of 5%, to remove potential contaminative reads. At last, we detected 59 iSNV variants across 22 samples, with each positive sample containing 1 to 8 iSNV sites. The AAF of these iSNVs ranged from 0.5% (our filtering threshold) to 34% (Supplementary Table 1), indicating the predominant presence of the parental viral population in the mouse lung samples. There were few iSNVs shared between the challenge stock and the mouse samples (Supplementary Tables 1&2), indicating that the iSNVs identified in the mouse samples may emerge during the infection course.

To estimate the evolutionary relationship of these iSNVs, we performed phylogenetic analysis using the iSNV-derived genomes and SARS-CoV2 human strains containing the corresponding mutations. The iSNV-derived genomes were generated by introducing corresponding nucleotide changes into the parental genome based on the iSNVs in each sample. We only identified a very limited number of human strains containing iSNV-related mutations from public databases, and 46 strains including 23 Omicron variants (BA.1 ~ BA.5) were selected for the phylogenetic analysis. An early strain, Wuhan-Hu-1 (MN908947.3), was included as the outgroup. The maximum likelihood phylogeny showed that all iSNV-derived genomes from mice clustered with the parental virus strain, distinct from iSNV-containing human isolates (Fig. 2a). This suggests that the mutations observed in mice may represent host-specific variations. While these iSNV-derived genomes closely clustered with the BA.1 lineage, genomic variants in the vaccinated DIO mice (VOB2 group) exhibited greater divergence from the parental genome, implying higher intra-host diversity.

Occurrence and distribution of the non-synonymous and synonymous iSNVs in the virus genome

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the mutational dynamics within the virus genome, we delved into the mutation types of each iSNV and assessed their prevalence across the genome and distinct proteins. Of the 59 iSNV types identified, a majority were distributed randomly throughout the viral genome and exhibited minimal overlap across different groups (Fig. 2b). We calculated the actual mutation ratio of non-synonymous and synonymous iSNVs across the genome by dividing the number of iSNVs of the same type by the 50x sequencing length of the viral genome in the same sample. We found a higher prevalence of non-synonymous mutations across the entire genome (Fig. 2c). However, a greater number of iSNVs were detected in the ORF1ab, Spike, and M genes (Fig. 2b). For ORF1ab, this observation was partly attributed to its larger size, resulting in a relatively higher number of iSNVs in comparison to the other coding regions across various groups, irrespective of the immune status or infection duration. Several iSNVs were shared among multiple samples from different groups (Fig. 2b), such as Lys456Arg in ORF1ab (shared by 4 mice), Leu7083* in RdRp (shared by 7 mice), synonymous mutant Val6Val in ORF10 (shared by 11 mice), and Leu176His in M gene (shared by 6 mice).

Stratifying the iSNVs into synonymous and non-synonymous mutation types for each sample revealed that the average non-synonymous mutation count per group for COB2 was 3.5, surpassing the 2.4 observed for CLN2 (Fig. 2b). Nonetheless, no significant intergroup differences were identified regarding the AAFs between the non-synonymous nor synonymous sites (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Mutation distribution within the spike gene showed a random pattern across the receptor-binding domain (RBD), N-terminal domain (NTD), and S2 regions. Intriguingly, none of the 22 iSNVs found in the VOB2 group were in the spike protein (Figs. 2b and 3a). Moreover, we noted a post infection trend were subject to predominantly neutral to negative selection (πN < πS, mean πN − πS = − 2.4 × 10⁻4) across the entire genome, even with statistically significant differences between πN and πS observed in CLN2, CLN4, and VOB2 groups by genome-wide selection analysis (Fig. 3b).Notably, within the spike gene region, we observed uneven synonymous and non-synonymous nucleotide diversity (πS and πN), with a relatively heightened level of πS suggesting a negative selection pressure (Fig. 3c). These observations suggest that viruses from mRNA vaccinated mice, similar to those with weaker innate immunity, were largely subjected to neutral or weak negative selection pressure at the within-host individual level.

We further employed Shannon entropy calculation on the iSNVs to estimate the viral diversity within each group. Results indicated that at 4 dpi, iSNVs across the full viral genome exhibited higher diversity in the unvaccinated lean group compared to 2 dpi. Additionally, at 2 dpi, the vaccinated obese group showcased greater iSNV diversity than the unvaccinated obese group (Fig. 3d). While a stronger immune response appeared to correlate with higher iSNV diversity, such differences between groups did not attain statistical significance (p < 0.05), underscoring the requirement for further investigation to substantiate these observations.

Correlation analysis between virus variation and boosted host immune response after vaccination

To further estimate the viral protein interactions amid enhanced immunity against Omicron, we explored the co-occurring iSNVs in the M protein and Orf1ab. Three co-shared iSNVs situated in ORF1ab (Leu7083*), M (Leu176His), and ORF10 (Val6Val) were identified in 4 out of 5 samples within the vaccinated obese group (VOB2) (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, the M gene from the VOB mice exhibited a higher mutation rate compared to other viral genes, with iSNVs detected in 5 out of 6 samples (mean AAF of M protein in VOB2 was 0.06), while the other groups at 4 dpi showed either one mutation or none. Notably, we noticed more evident purifying selection pressure in the M gene compared to ORF1ab, ORF3a, and spike (Fig. 4a). The enhanced negative selection pressure in the M coding region of viruses detected from the 2-dose mRNA-vaccination-specific (VOB2) and impaired immunity (COB4) samples suggests diversifying mutations in the M protein (Fig. 4a).

To delve deeper into the connection between iSNVs and the host transcriptome signatures, we performed correlation analysis between DEGs which were indicative of an enhanced host antiviral immune response and iSNVs from vaccinated re-infected Omicron mice. There are reports that adaptation to innate interferon signaling can result in immune suppression mutation [45]. Leu7083* in the ORF1ab region exhibited a positive correlation with 20 DEGs (r2 > 0.8), including Lag3, Ccl8, Trem1, and Nod2, which are in associated with cytokine production (GO: 0001816) or innate immune response (GO:0045087) pathways (Fig. 4b), reflecting the positive impact on innate immunity. Conversely, Leu176His in the M protein showed a significant negative correlation with the regulation of T cell activation (GO:0050863), based on genes including Fas, Il12rb1, and Trex1 (Fig. 4b), indicative of a potential down-regulation of adaptive immune responses. Therefore, we observed a remarkable correlation between SARS-CoV-2 intra-host mutations and host immune signatures, the co-occurring iSNVs in the M protein (Leu176His) and Orf1ab (Leu7083*) particularly indicated the influence of mRNA vaccine induced immunity in obese mice on virus evolution.

Intriguingly, all viruses in the VOB2 group possessed a co-occurring iSNV in M protein, Leu176His. The amino acid at position 176 in the M protein lies within the β7 structure. The presence of a charged amino acid, when Leu is replaced by His, introduces steric hindrance, leading to alterations in the beta-sheet structure (Fig. 4c). The M protein has positive charges on its cytosolic region while the N protein has negative charges on its C-terminus. The co-localization of M and N proteins have been demonstrated in cells [46]. This co-occurring iSNV, Leu176His in M protein, may increase the positive charges to form electrostatic interactions to improve the viral protein assembly, thus affecting progeny production and fitness. Existing studies have indicated that M might disrupt the host’s innate immune response and potentially contribute to the severe lung damage seen in critical patients [47, 48]. It is noteworthy that while the mRNA vaccine primarily targets the spike protein as an immunogen, the concurrent antibody-independent host immunity can also impact the other viral proteins.

Discussion

To understand the intro-host mutation dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 under the influence of host innate antiviral immunity, we firstly demonstrated the delayed and weakened antiviral responses upon Omicron BA.1 infection DIO. Our results showed significantly lower production of type I IFNs in lung tissues of DIO mice than those of lean mice. Furthermore, lung tissue transcriptomic profiles demonstrated that the expression of innate immune related genes were quickly upregulated in lean mice at 2 dpi while delayed until 4 dpi in DIO mice. These are consistent with previous reports and confirm impairment of antiviral immune function in an obese state [28, 44, 49, 50]. Not surprisingly, COVID-19 mRNA vaccination induced serum neutralizing antibody in lean mice and protected the animals from subsequent virus challenge from which no virus was extracted from the lung tissues. To the contrary, same immunization scheme with COVID-19 mRNA vaccine induced no detectable serum neutralizing antibody in DIO mice, which is in line with poor vaccination responses in obese state and resulted in high proportion of vaccine-breakthrough infection in DIO mice. Clinical evidence supports the association of obesity with reduced vaccine efficacy in humans, which is attributed to a delayed humoral immune response [13, 51]. Our study showed a complete lack of neutralizing antibody production in obese mice, which contrasts with the robust immune response in lean mice. We showed significant enhancement of innate immune gene expression in the lung of vaccinated DIO mice upon virus challenge, particularly in IFN-β regulation and infection responses. Collectively, in terms of host lung innate immune states at early time after SARS-CoV-2 infection, we observed a limited hierarchy as vaccinated lean mice (VLN) > vaccinated DIO mice (VOB) and none vaccinated lean mice (CLN) > none vaccinated DIO mice (COB). Particularly, one of the explanations for COVID-19 vaccine enhanced innate immune responses in obese mice is that mRNA vaccination sensitizes the innate immune system by upregulation the expression of pathogen recognition receptors (RIG-I, MAD5, TLRs) alveolar macrophages. We previously showed that stimulation of alveolar macrophage in vitro upregulated RIG-1, MAD5 and TLR3 expression; alveolar macrophage collected from vaccinated DIO mice showed increased responsiveness to Poly I: C and SARS-CoV-2 S protein stimulation in vitro [28].

Viral genetic variation often diminishes due to narrow transmission bottlenecks, especially for SARS-CoV-2 [52]. While examining evolution on a between-host scale, the diversity of the virus within individual host is commonly disregarded, and attention is predominantly directed towards the consensus sequence which essentially represents the sequence derived by selecting the most prevalent nucleotide at each genomic site. However, certain mutations are occasionally transmitted by chance, without conferring any selective advantage [52]. The present study found that the extent of intra-host genetic diversity in SARS-CoV-2 was highly variable among individual animal, regardless of vaccination status [53]. The constrained genetic diversity of the virus in individuals with acute infections, coupled with a tight transmission bottleneck, implies that the majority of the genetic variability observed at the consensus level between hosts arises from neutral or marginally detrimental mutations that fortuitously surmounted the narrow transmission bottleneck. This randomness facilitates the dissemination of mutations lacking significant selective advantages within the population.

Vaccination induced adaptive immunity is aimed to protect the hosts from infection by the targeted pathogen. However, when vaccine-induced immunity is not sterile or not effective enough to completely block infection, such as in the case of COVID-19 vaccination, SARS-CoV-2 variants have a big chance of break-through infection to replicate to evade the host immune response [52], and this will contribute greatly to viral divergence. In our DIO model, COVID-19 vaccine induced suboptimal immune responses without neutralizing antibody production while boosted innate immune responses to virus challenge, this suboptimal immune state consequently contributes greatly to viral divergence. 5/7 DIO mice experienced breakthrough infections and generated 11 iSNVs in the lung samples, which exhibited higher within-host genetic diversity compared to their unvaccinated counterparts. This suggests that stronger innate immunity could potentially increase intra-host viral genetic diversity, hence influencing adaptive evolution. The Spike protein, an inhibitor of the type I interferon (IFN-I) response, can effectively counteract the signaling of the viral RNA pattern recognition receptor RIG-I. The RBD in spike is the primary target of host neutralizing antibody. All these put spike under the pressure of host immunity [54]. The iSNVs in the Spike gene identified from mouse lungs were randomly distributed across the RBD, NTD, and S2 regions (Fig. 3a), which from a different angle suggested the immune pressure was not directed by neutralizing antibody. In support of this, none of the 22 iSNVs in the vaccinated DIO mice resided within the Spike protein (Fig. 2b). That once again suggests immune pressure for SARS-CoV-2 in DIO mice and the observed viral mutations were attributed to enhanced host innate immunity. From this we can speculate the complicated immune pressure exerted by global massive vaccination on SARS-CoV-2 evolution.

Our results suggested that the viral mutations occurred in the infected vaccinated DIO mice were driven by neutral or purifying selection force. Despite the limited accumulation of non-synonymous iSNVs under enhanced host immunity, a distinct genetic differentiation was evident when comparing the genetic diversity of the iSNVs across intra-host populations from different immune statuses. When strong immune responses were present (vaccination and 4 dpi), regardless of the lean or DIO condition, the diversity of intra-host variants seemed higher. Meanwhile, the presence of co-occurring non-synonymous iSNVs specific to the vaccinated DIO group suggests the possibility of selection by innate immunity contributing to intra-host viraldynamic changes. The precise biological mechanisms underlying these intra-host viral population dynamics remain to be investigated.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, SARS-CoV-2 infection of normal mice (without transgenic expressing the human ACE2 receptor) is not a very good infection model, which only gives us narrow time window for study, virus mutation rate was also confined by this. So we are not able to explore in host virus mutation in longer infection and virus replication time course. Secondly, only Omicron strain was studied, whether different virus variant would evolve the same way under the condition of obesity and vaccination remains unknown. Thirdly, the use of a mouse model may not fully reflect the physiological conditions observed in human. Therefore, conducting long-term human cohort studies are warrant.

Collectively, our findings shed light on the evolutionary processes within the SARS-CoV-2 population under different immune statuses at the intra-host level. This research may provide novel insights into the virus-host interactions of coronaviruses and other RNA viruses against host immunity.

Conclusions

Using a diet-induced obese mouse model, we identified a series of iSNVs from the lungs of vaccinated obese mice, indicating a link between viral mutations and insufficient host immune response in the context of obesity. Collectively, our findings shed light on the evolutionary processes within the SARS-CoV-2 population under different immune statuses at the intra-host level to highlight the impact of immune compromised host on viral evolution. This research may provide novel insights into the virus-host interactions of coronaviruses and other RNA viruses against host immunity.
