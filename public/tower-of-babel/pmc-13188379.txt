An end-to-end system for explainable clinical coding across languages and diverse medical data sources
Authors: Alicia Ramirez-Arrabe, Juan Martinez-Romo, Andres Duque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveThe International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system standardizes clinical texts to improve the information extraction process in healthcare and research. However, most existing automated coding systems operate as “black boxes”, highlighting the need for explainable approaches that offer transparency and interpretable solutions. In this work, we present an end-to-end system that provides explainable clinical coding predictions.MethodsWe develop a three-phase system for explainable clinical coding. In Phase 1, text spans potentially describing ICD codes are detected using different Named Entity Recognition (NER) models. Phase 2 applies a supervised text classification model with a confidence threshold, while low-confidence cases are classified in Phase 3 using a semantic similarity model built from ICD code descriptions and related keyphrases.ResultsThe system is evaluated on four corpora in Spanish and English, annotated with ICD codes from three variants (ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-PCS, and ICD-O-3) and their corresponding textual mentions. Overall, the system proves to be robust and competitive with state-of-the-art approaches, outperforming most of them and achieving an average F1-score improvement of 3.42%.ConclusionsThis study presents one of the most comprehensive evaluations of an explainable clinical coding system across languages and ICD variants. The proposed approach demonstrates strong robustness and generalization, effectively handling unseen codes as well as discontinuous and overlapping entities.Keywords: Automated Clinical Coding, Explainable Artificial Intelligence, ICD-10 codes, Interpretability, Clinical Natural Language Processing

Introduction

Healthcare systems generate vast amounts of clinical texts every day worldwide. This information is an important resource but often contains unstructured data in the form of free text, and its transformation into structured data remains a challenge [1]. The application of Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to clinical documents, such as discharge summaries, radiology reports or Electronic Health Records (EHRs), has proven to be highly valuable across various areas, like symptom identification [2] or extraction of medical concepts [3], among others. In this work, we focus on a strongly important NLP task of the biomedical domain: automated clinical coding. The main objective of clinical coding is to label the text written by doctors in clinical reports with standardized codes representing the patient’s medical information, which may be very useful to improve the efficiency of information management for healthcare professionals, researchers and managers of clinical institutions [4]. Nonetheless, manual coding of clinical texts is a complex, time-consuming, and error-prone task [5], making the implementation of automated solutions essential. Specifically, automated clinical coding systems leverage NLP techniques to identify medical terminology in clinical reports and assign the corresponding standardized codes. Several coding systems exist for this purpose, such as the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine-Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) [6], Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) [7], and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [8], among others. This work focuses on the latter, specifically on its tenth revision (ICD-10), or CIE-10 in Spanish, which is the most widely used in hospitals to describe diagnoses (ICD-10-CM) and medical procedures (ICD-10-PCS). The first versions of ICD-10 were published in the 1990s; however, its implementation in most countries, including Spain, did not begin until the 2010s. The ICD-10-CM code set allows for up to 155,000 diagnosis codes but currently includes around 69,000. Similarly, ICD-10-PCS contains approximately 72,000 procedure codes. In addition to ICD-10 codes, this research also addresses the clinical coding of a domain-specific extension of the ICD system for oncological terminology: the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O), or CIE-O in Spanish, which describes the morphological characteristics of malignant neoplasms, that is, cancers. The third revision of ICD-O (ICD-O-3), which is the most commonly used version in hospitals nowadays and is the one considered in this work, includes approximately 14,000 possible codes.

Regarding the currently existing clinical coding systems, a major drawback of most of them is that they operate as “black boxes”, that is, they generate code predictions without providing any explanation for their decisions. This lack of transparency raises concerns regarding their reliability and trustworthiness [9], creating the need to apply eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques. In this context, the concepts of explainability and interpretability are frequently employed. Although some authors distinguish between them [10] and also introduce the related notion of comprehensibility [11], many researchers treat explainability and interpretability as synonymous [12]. In this work, we follow this latter view, using explainability and interpretability interchangeably. Therefore, the task of explainable clinical coding represents a significant step forward by introducing interpretability into clinical coding systems. From this perspective, such systems should not only assign ICD codes to each clinical report, but also identify the specific text spans that justify each prediction. That is, within the context of automatic clinical coding, we consider a system to be explainable in terms of correct code+entity predictions. We have selected this methodology and evaluation to ensure comparability with state-of-the-art results, following approaches proposed in previous studies [13–17].

With this goal in mind, we have designed an explainable automated clinical coding system that focuses on two main tasks: (1) assigning the correct ICD codes to clinical reports, and (2) allowing for the interpretability of the resulting ICD code predictions by identifying the text spans that justify each coding decision. Due to the nature of the problem and the corpora employed in this study, both tasks present several challenges. The main issues of the first task are the following.

Each of the ICD code variants considered includes thousands of possible labels; that is, the set of possible codes is very large. This leads to challenges like data sparsity and scalability, since models must handle a huge number of labels efficiently [18].

Due to these factors, the task is highly complex and can be considered an eXtreme Multilabel Text Classification (XMTC) problem.

As for the second task, it is essential for developing an explainable system, but it also presents several challenges. While most textual ICD mentions are flat entities, some may be nested or overlap with others. Additionally, some entities can be discontinuous, and their components may appear in separate parts of the text, sometimes close together, and other times spread across different or unrelated sections of the clinical report. Most existing clinical coding systems do not consider non-flat entities, leaving many relevant mentions outside their scope.

Taking these challenges into consideration, we designed a system organized into three phases. In the first phase, the system employs a NER-based system for identifying parts of the text that could potentially describe an ICD code. The second and third phases rely on classification models, based on supervised and unsupervised learning, respectively, that assign each textual evidence its most probable code.

Background and previous work

Over the years, several corpora and studies focused on the task of clinical coding have been published. However, most of them address only the code assignment task, without incorporating an explainable component to justify predictions and help healthcare professionals to better interpret the results. The main corpora and systems proposed for explainable clinical coding are discussed below.

Annotated corpora in explainable clinical coding

For the task of explainable clinical coding, some corpora have been publicly released that explicitly include explainability annotations. The CodiEsp shared task [13] at CLEF eHealth 2020 introduced one of the first corpora focused on explainable ICD coding of medical reports in Spanish, specifically requiring the classification of diagnoses and medical procedures. It was later followed by Cantemist [14], developed within the SEPLN IberLEF 2020 shared task, which focused on the explainable coding of neoplasms in a collection of clinical reports written in Spanish. Similarly, the FALP corpus [15] also centers on oncology reports in Spanish. Regarding other languages, MDACE [16] is an English-language corpus annotated with text spans corresponding to diagnoses and procedure codes, derived from MIMIC-III clinical texts [20]. Additional contributions in other languages include the BRONCO corpus [21], a collection of German discharge summaries annotated with diagnosis codes, and ElCardioCC [17], released for the CLEF BioASQ 2025 challenge, which focuses on explainable automated ICD-10 coding of Greek cardiology discharge letters.

Apart from clinical coding corpora that explicitly include textual annotations for each code, other valuable resources exist for ICD-10 text classification. Since this work focuses on the explainability component and, as will be explained later, the designed system architecture requires data with annotated textual mentions, these resources unfortunately cannot be used directly in this study. For Spanish, the CARES (Corpus of Anonymised Radiological Evidences in Spanish) corpus [22] contains radiology reports manually labeled with ICD-10 codes. In English, the MIMIC-III corpus [20], and its updated version MIMIC-IV [23], provide discharge summaries and radiology reports from over 350,000 patients, annotated with ICD-10 diagnosis and procedure codes. From 2016 to 2018, the CLEF eHealth evaluation lab conducted shared tasks on coding death certificates with ICD-10 codes in French, English, Hungarian, and Italian [24–26]. The tasks involved mapping sentences with one or more diagnoses to ICD-10 codes. Additionally, CLEF eHealth 2019 published a corpus of over 8,000 German non-technical summaries of planned animal experiments, manually annotated with ICD-10 codes [27].

Methodologies and systems for explainable clinical coding

While it is true that explainable ICD-10 coding corpora are scarce, several studies have attempted to address this issue using different approaches. In [28] the authors evaluated six different biomedical Transformer models for classifying the ICD codes of neoplasms in a private corpus containing over 20,000 oncology health records. To incorporate the explainability component, they applied Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [29] to highlight the most relevant words associated with the predicted codes. There exists another work [30] that introduces an interpretable ICD-10 coding system using keyphrase extraction to represent codes with both literal and statistically inferred keyphrases from a corpus of 12,966 Spanish EHRs. In [19] the authors developed a Longformer-based system to classify Spanish EHRs and applied three explainability methods: Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and Integrated Gradients (IG), to identify parts of the text influencing each prediction. They also proposed a new metric, called Leberage, to evaluate the contribution of the explainability techniques, concluding that LIME provides the most effective explanations. Another study [31] used a hierarchical approach with two components: Medical Entity Recognition (MER) to identify clinical entities, and Medical Entity Normalization (MEN) to classify these entities according to ICD-10 codes. They utilized the experimental framework of the CodiEsp and Cantemist corpora. Since such corpora are annotated with ICD code mentions, this system is one of the few whose explainability results can be evaluated with quantifiable metrics, enabling an objective comparison.

Apart from these diverse proposals for explainable systems, one of the most widespread approaches to interpretability in clinical coding has been the use of attention mechanisms, which are used to assign specific weights to the tokens of the text for each code prediction. Using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in combination with attention mechanisms [32], assigns ICD-10 codes to Spanish EHRs while highlighting the specific text segments that contribute to each code’s prediction. Attention-based approaches for interpretable predictions have also been employed in several studies using the older ICD-9 coding system. Examples include CAML [33] and DLAC [34], which use label-wise attention with code descriptions; hierarchical models such as HA-GRU [9], HiLAT [35], and HLAN [36], which incorporate word- and sentence-level attention; and a recent approach [37], that uses a Longformer-based model to encode long clinical documents and applies multi-synonym and attention to capture code associations.

Materials and methods

This section describes the technical and methodological aspects of the study. It presents the main characteristics of the collected corpora and details the system developed to assign ICD codes to clinical reports, ensuring that each prediction is provided with an interpretable explanation.

The corpora collected for this work include annotations for a total of three different ICD variants.

ICD-10-CM codes (CIE-10-ES Diagnósticos in Spanish) are used to identify medical diagnoses. These codes follow a hierarchical structure, ranging from a minimum of three to a maximum of seven characters (see Fig. 1-A).

Corpora

In this work, four clinical corpora were collected and studied. Each corpus consists of clinical documents annotated with ICD codes and their corresponding textual mentions, using a specific variant of the ICD coding system. The corpora used in this study vary in language (Spanish or English), and may contain overlapping or discontinuous mentions. Textual mentions including non-adjacent spans were considered discontinuous entities, while mentions that shared at least one token were considered overlapping entities. The four corpora used in this study are described below. Other valuable clinical coding corpora were mentioned in Subsection 2.1, such as CARES [22] in Spanish, and MIMIC-III [20] and MIMIC-IV [23] in English. However, because the focus of this work is on the explainability component, the system proposed and evaluated in this study requires data with annotated textual mentions; that is, code–mention alignments, which unfortunately prevents their direct use in this work.

System description

As previously mentioned, the goal of this study is not only to assign the appropriate ICD codes to each document, but also to integrate an explainability feature within the system that justifies each predicted code. This explainability component works by providing the specific textual mentions or evidence corresponding to each code, and helps improve and ensure the robustness and reliability of the system. Figure 3 presents a schematic overview of the proposed system, which is organized into three phases. Phase 1 employs a NER-based model to identify textual mentions or evidence that could potentially lead to the assignment of an ICD code. Three distinct NER models, each with unique characteristics and arquitectures, and capable of detecting different entity types (flat, discontinuous, overlapping), were studied and evaluated. Subsequently, the extracted mentions from Phase 1 are then passed to Phase 2, which uses a supervised classification model to assign the most appropriate ICD code to each mention. This step allows us to simplify the XMTC problem by transforming it into a single-label approach, that is, assigning one code per entity instead of multiple codes per text. Moreover, because the corpora include unseen codes during training and validation, mentions with low-confidence code predictions are filtered and sent to Phase 3. In this final phase, a semantic similarity model employs a knowledge base built from code descriptions and enhanced with a keyphrase-based approach to assign the most similar ICD code. The proposed research constitutes an extension of the approach described in [41].

Flow diagram of the proposed system, showing its three phases

Phases 1 and 2 of the system were addressed using Transformer-based models, which capture bidirectional context and generate richer semantic and syntactic representations. In recent years, several studies have employed this type of architecture to identify medical concepts [42–44] and assign ICD codes [31, 35, 45], showing notable improvements in most cases. All the Transformer-based models used were pretrained in the biomedical or clinical domain and publicly available on the HuggingFace platform9. For training and inference on the Spanish corpora, four pretrained models were utilized, while two were employed for the English corpora (see Table 5).

Phase 1: entity extraction

The first stage of the system employs a NER-based approach to identify text spans that may potentially correspond to an ICD code. In total, three different NER systems were evaluated. They are described below:

SimpleNER is a token classification model developed from scratch. Texts were annotated at the token level using the BIO (Beginning, Inside, Outside) [54] annotation scheme. As the annotation process allows only one label per token, in cases of overlapping mentions, the longest span was selected, as recommended in [55].The models were trained for a maximum of 50 epochs, using the F1-micro score for validation and applying early stopping based on the performance on the development set.

SimpleNER is a token classification model developed from scratch. Texts were annotated at the token level using the BIO (Beginning, Inside, Outside) [54] annotation scheme. As the annotation process allows only one label per token, in cases of overlapping mentions, the longest span was selected, as recommended in [55].

The models were trained for a maximum of 50 epochs, using the F1-micro score for validation and applying early stopping based on the performance on the development set.

GliNER [56] is a model for entity extraction that uses a Bidirectional Language Model (BiLM) like BERT, along with entity type prompts marked by a special ENT token. GLiNER has been shown to outperform LLMs like ChatGPT in zero-shot tasks while requiring fewer resources, and it also performs well across multiple languages.For GliNER, the pretrained multilingual gliner_multi model was fine-tuned on the corpora for 25 epochs. It was selected after evaluating the performance of three publicly available GliNER multilingual models on HuggingFace. During decoding, GliNER uses a greedy span selection strategy, considering only entity spans with scores above a predefined threshold. In this study, the threshold was set to 0.5, following the recommendation in [56].

GLiNER has been shown to outperform LLMs like ChatGPT in zero-shot tasks while requiring fewer resources, and it also performs well across multiple languages.

For GliNER, the pretrained multilingual gliner_multi model was fine-tuned on the corpora for 25 epochs. It was selected after evaluating the performance of three publicly available GliNER multilingual models on HuggingFace. During decoding, GliNER uses a greedy span selection strategy, considering only entity spans with scores above a predefined threshold. In this study, the threshold was set to 0.5, following the recommendation in [56].

WNER [57] is a model that reformulates the NER task as a word-word relation classification problem. It builds a 2D grid of word pairs and uses relations like Next-Neighboring-Word (NNW) and Tail-Head-Word (THW). The architecture combines a BERT and BiLSTM encoder, and 2D convolutions to classify word-word relations. The original paper reports that WNER achieves state-of-the-art performance on 14 benchmark datasets across flat, overlapped, and discontinuous NER tasks in both English and Chinese.Due to its higher complexity, the training process in WNER typically takes longer than in simpler NER-based systems. Therefore, training was run for up to 150 epochs, selecting the model from the epoch that achieved the highest F1-micro score on the development set. Additionally, the pretrained model longformer-base-4096-biomedical-clinical-es could not be used, as the WNER architecture only supports BERT-based models.

The textual ICD mentions in each of the corpora used in this study present different characteristics. Some corpora contain both discontinuous and overlapping mentions, while others include only different amounts of overlapping entities. As summarized in Table 6, the SimpleNER model supports only flat mentions. Hence, we theorize that SimpleNER could obtain good results for Cantemist, FALP Topography, and MDACE, which contain mostly flat entities with minimal overlapping cases. Moreover, GliNER handles flat and overlapping mentions, so it may be well suited for FALP Morphology, where overlapping entities are more frequent. Comparing the three NER-based models allows us to balance performance and resource usage, providing insight into which model is more suitable depending on the level of complexity of the corpora.

Phase 2: supervised classification

Once the potential ICD code mentions are extracted from the clinical reports, a supervised multi-class classification model assigns a code to each mention. In the case of ICD-10-CM codes, some state-of-the-art studies have designed classification systems that leverage their hierarchical structure [31, 39]. However, one of the main goals of this study is to build a unified and robust system for the three ICD code variants considered. Since the structures of ICD-10-PCS and ICD-O-3 are not hierarchical but multiaxial, a hierarchical-based approach was not considered. Therefore, for each corpus, a classification model was finetuned to assign a complete ICD code to each extracted mention.

The Optuna framework [58] was employed for hyperparameter optimization. Each model was trained for up to 50 epochs, with early stopping based on the F1-micro score on the development set.

The set of possible labels for the classification models included only those ICD codes present in the training and development sets. However, the test sets across the eight corpora contain codes that were not seen during training or validation. In such cases, the model cannot possibly identify the correct code. To address this issue, a probability threshold-based strategy was applied. For each mention, if the predicted code’s softmax probability was greater than or equal to a predefined threshold (), the prediction was accepted and included in the final output. On the other hand, if the probability was below the threshold (), the instance was rejected and passed on to Phase 3. This approach ensures that predictions where the classification model lacks sufficient confidence (most of which corresponding to unseen codes that cannot be correctly assigned) are filtered out and handled in the next phase. Two probability thresholds () were optimized for each corpora, corresponding to the two main metrics used to evaluate the results: F1-micro and Mean Average Precision (MAP).

Phase 3: semantic similarity classification

In this final stage, the model receives only the textual mentions that were discarded during Phase 2. Most of these likely correspond to codes not present within the training or development sets, as the classification model was not confident enough to assign them a label. Since no predefined set of possible labels exists in this phase, an unsupervised approach must be implemented.

To develop this unsupervised system, it was first necessary to gather as much information as possible about each ICD code. The organizers of the CodiEsp and Cantemist tasks provided a list of valid codes for each ICD variant: 98,288 ICD-10-CM codes, 87,170 ICD-10-PCS codes, and 58,062 ICD-O-3 codes. However, not all of the codes were given a description and, in addition, the code descriptions alone may not provide enough information to assign the correct code to each textual mention, as many descriptions are phrased differently than how they appear in the reports. To enrich the information available for each code, the system proposed in [30] was implemented.

This methodology combines two keyphrase extraction approaches. The first makes use of a taylored regular expression for identifying Spanish medical noun and prepositional keyphrases, extracted after some pre-processing steps such as lemmatization and POS-tagging and finally filtered and selected using TF-IDF techniques. The second employs IXAMedTagger [59] to annotate terms from SNOMED-CT across categories. Both approaches are complementary, since the regular expression captures more general keyphrases, whereas IXAMedTagger ensures the inclusion of specific medical terms. Then, the knowledge base is created by assessing the co-occurrence of the extracted keyphrases and ICD-10 codes across the reports. In particular, the statistical significance of each pair (keyphrase, ICD-code) is calculated. The result is a list of keyphrases associated to each ICD-10 code, ordered by the significance of that association.

The described system was applied to all four corpora using the training and development sets, enabling the creation of a knowledge base. Following the results of some exploratory experiments, the two most representative keyphrases per code, according to their significance, were selected for populating the knowledge base. The result was a list of the three ICD code variants considered in this study, in which each code may include one of the following types of information:

A description and its two most significant keyphrases, if we have a description of the code and keyphrases were found in the corpus.

A description and its two most significant keyphrases, if we have a description of the code and keyphrases were found in the corpus.

Only a description, if the organizers of the corpora provided a description but no keyphrases were found, likely because the code was not present in the training and development sets of the four corpora.

Only a description, if the organizers of the corpora provided a description but no keyphrases were found, likely because the code was not present in the training and development sets of the four corpora.

Only the two most significant keyphrases, if keyphrases were computed for the code but no description was available.

Only the two most significant keyphrases, if keyphrases were computed for the code but no description was available.

To further enrich the information attached to each code, the keyphrases computed from the dataset used in [30] were also incorporated. This corpus consists of 12,966 Spanish medical discharge reports from 2016 and includes 7,592 ICD-10-CM and ICD-10-PCS codes. Moreover, because the knowledge base was built in Spanish, when the system is applied to the English MDACE corpus, the text spans processed in Phase 3 are translated into Spanish to compute their embeddings and cosine similarity with the knowledge base information. It is worth noting that this introduces a limitation and may result in a translation bias, potentially causing changes in semantics and structure and affecting the results. For example, even words written in a different order may yield a different cosine similarity. However, since our knowledge base is in Spanish, one of the components must be translated, and we consider that translating only the entities that reach Phase 3 likely has less impact than translating the entire knowledge base.

Having collected the list of possible ICD codes along with their corresponding descriptions and keyphrases for each corpus, the embeddings of the entities, descriptions, and keyphrases were computed using the Arctic-Embed 2.0 multilingual embedding model [60]. For each entity, its embedding was compared to the embeddings of all ICD code descriptions and keyphrases using the Sentence Transformers framework [61]. For each comparison pair, the cosine similarity was computed. Figure 4 shows a few examples of how Phase 3 works.

Example diagram illustrating the process in phase 3

Although the code with the highest similarity was selected, it is possible that the similarity score was low or that some gold standard codes from the test set were not present in the list of possible codes. To address this issue, another threshold-based strategy was applied, using cosine similarity as the criterion. If the cosine similarity between an entity and its assigned code was greater than or equal to a predefined threshold (), the entity and its assigned code were included in the final predictions. Conversely, if the similarity was below the threshold (), such prediction was permanently discarded and excluded from the output, as none of the classification models from Phases 2 and 3 were able to confidently assign a code to it. For each corpora, two thresholds () were optimized, corresponding to the two metrics used for evaluation.

Experiments and results

This section presents the experiments conducted to evaluate the proposed approach and the results obtained. Additionally, an error analysis of the system is performed to highlight its main areas for improvement.

Results

The results are organized into two main sections. First, we present the clinical coding results, where we evaluate only the classification of ICD codes for each text report. Then, we provide the results of the explainability task, in which, in addition to the ICD code classification, the predicted text spans associated with each code are also evaluated.

When evaluating the task of clinical coding, only the correct assignment of ICD codes to each text was evaluated. For each corpus, different configurations were considered: the three NER-based models described in Section “Phase 1: Entity Extraction”, along with their corresponding ensemble versions. Since threshold optimization introduces a trade-off between precision-oriented performance (F1) and ranking quality (MAP), each system was evaluated under both criteria by optimizing the thresholds separately for F1 and MAP. In this context, “accepted” means that they have surpassed the threshold of the corresponding phase, regardless of whether they are correct or not. Phase 3’s threshold accepts 79 instances, of which 57 are correctly predicted, including 31 unseen codes and 26 codes seen during training. In contrast, the FALP Topography corpus did not recover any unseen codes in Phase 3. This is because its test set contains only four unseen codes, while the rest were seen during training. Consequently, the classification task in Phase 2 is much easier, and the model is likely confident in most predictions; in fact, only five instances are passed on to Phase 3.

Error analysis

To identify the primary types of errors made by the system and detect potential areas for improvement, an in-depth evaluation was conducted on the misclassified medical reports from the test set. The following analysis highlights the main issues observed and suggests possible ways to address them.

Low-frequency codes: As stated in previous sections, clinical coding is an extreme multilabel classification problem, not only because it involves an extremely large set of possible codes, but also because these codes tend to occur at very low frequencies. For instance, out of the 2,557 codes that comprise CodiEsp-D, 1,162 (45.44%) appear only once across the entire corpus, while MDACE-D Inpatient contains 468 codes (51.77%) that occur only once among its 904 total codes. Examining the model’s performance, we can observe a tendency to make more mistakes when classifying entities whose codes are infrequent. The main reason is that the supervised model in Phase 2 has encountered very few training examples for these rare codes and thus cannot generalize well. Similarly, when building the knowledge base in Phase 3, there was likely insufficient information to assign accurate and significant keyphrases to such infrequent codes, as also highlighted in [30], and therefore, the unsupervised similarity model is more prone to misclassify those entities.This type of error could potentially be addressed by gathering additional annotated data to train the supervised model and enrich the knowledge base with more robust keyphrases. Moreover, data augmentation techniques, such as generating synthetic data, or applying transfer learning from other corpora, could help improve the model’s robustness toward rare codes.

Discontinuous mentions: As previously mentioned, the NER system GliNER does not support discontinuous mentions, and SimpleNER only does it under specific circumstances. Hence, only the WNER system allows for the complete inclusion of any discontinuity. Among the four corpora, only CodiEsp contains discontinuous entities. Specifically, the test sets of CodiEsp-D and CodiEsp-P include 524 and 430 such entities, respectively, of which WNER correctly detects 30.92% and 25.81%. It is worth noting that WNER is the only system among the three evaluated that supports discontinuous entities. Therefore, although these percentages may appear low, they represent a significant improvement over the other systems. Furthermore, an analysis of the discontinuities identified by the model reveals that it tends to detect discontinuous entities more accurately when their spans are closer to each other. In particular, the predicted entities have an average span separation of 27.20 characters in CodiEsp-D and 111.95 characters in CodiEsp-P, whereas the average separation in the gold standard is 140.76 and 253.02 characters, respectively. This suggests that the greater the separation between entities, the more difficult they are to detect. Additionally, some discontinuous entities in the corpus are divided into three segments throughout the clinical reports, and WNER was unable to detect any of these. However, this limitation affects only a small number of cases, specifically, 14 and 37 entities in CodiEsp-D and CodiEsp-P, respectively.All of these undetected discontinuous entities directly affect the Recall metric, decreasing its value while increasing the number of false negatives.

Inconsistency in procedure annotation: CodiEsp-P is the only corpus in this study that considers medical procedures (ICD-10-PCS). An examination of its annotations revealed a primary inconsistency that makes it a particularly challenging task. Although the corpus contains a total of 870 unique codes, 229 (26.32%) of them consist of only four characters, as annotators considered the available contextual information insufficient to assign the full seven-character code. As shown in Figure 1, the structure of ICD-10-PCS codes is multiaxial, and, according to the manual of the version used in CodiEsp12, the character Z indicates None or Not Applicable, meaning that it is used when the procedure does not require further specification. However, there exist 20 codes in CodiEsp-P that sometimes appear in their 4-character form and other times as full 7-character codes ending in ZZZ, without any reasonable explanation. Hence, this inconsistency represents a major issue for detecting those codes. For instance, the entity “ecografía urinaria” (“urinary ultrasound”) is sometimes labeled with the code BT4J and other times assigned the code BT4JZZZ, even though both indicate the same procedure. These 20 codes account for 10.07% of the total instances in the test set and are particularly confusing for both the supervised model in Phase 2 and the unsupervised similarity model in Phase 3, as it is unclear which version of the code should be selected in each case because both represent the same medical procedure.This issue directly affects the Precision and MAP metrics, as the models may predict the code with four characters instead of the other one, or vice versa. It also decreases the Recall by introducing false negatives when the correct code is not predicted. No straightforward solution exists for this problem, other than unifying the annotations by using only one of the possible codes in each case either in training or evaluation.

Unseen codes during training: All test sets of the four corpora contain a certain number of codes that do not appear in either the training or the development sets. For example, CodiEsp-P includes 143 unseen codes (16.44%) among its 870 total codes, while MDACE-D Profee contains 97 unseen codes (14.41%) out of 673. In contrast, the proportion is the lowest in FALP Morphology and FALP Topography, with 11 (4.87%) and 4 (2.61%) unseen codes, respectively. Although the main purpose of Phase 3 is to address these specific cases of unseen code occurrences, there remains considerable room for improvement, as a common type of prediction error involves missing some of these codes. One main reason for this is that, since these codes do not appear during training, their keyphrases are unavailable, unless they are found in the other corpora. In some cases, even their descriptions are missing, so the unsupervised model lacks sufficient information to classify them confidently and accurately.One possible solution for this type of error is to improve the unsupervised classification by using more sophisticated techniques. Mismatch between predicted entities and gold mentions: Some of the errors in the explainability task evaluation arise from mismatches of one or a few words in the identified entities. For instance, in the Cantemist corpus, the model predicts “sarcoma de Ewing extraesquelético” (“extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma”), whereas the gold standard mention is “sarcoma de Ewing” (“Ewing sarcoma”). The predicted code for the entity (9260/3) is right, which means that in the clinical coding task it is considered correct, while in the explainability task it counts as a mistake since the text span does not match.This type of error negatively affects both Precision and Recall, as it is counted as a false positive (the predicted span is considered incorrect) and a false negative (the gold standard span is considered missed). In the evaluation of the CodiEsp corpus, a tolerance of 10 characters is allowed regarding the entities’ spans. However, in the case of the Cantemist corpus, such tolerance was not applied, in order to ensure full comparability with the state of the art. Hence, one possible way to mitigate this issue would be to introduce flexible matching criteria that allow minor variations in entities.

Conclusions and future work

This paper presents a three-stage explainable system designed to address the clinical coding task on medical reports annotated with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system. The proposed approach stands out for its interpretability, as it provides the text spans that justify each coding decision.

To evaluate the robustness of the system, four corpora, organized into seven subcorpora, were collected. They comprise medical reports written in Spanish and English and include three different ICD variants: diagnoses (ICD-10-CM), medical procedures (ICD-10-PCS), and oncological (ICD-O-3) codes. These datasets are, as far as we know, the only publicly available English and Spanish corpora that provide annotations for both ICD codes and their corresponding textual mentions, that is, they are annotated for the task of explainable clinical coding. In addition, three NER-based approaches were evaluated, each built with a different architecture and characterized by distinct features and levels of complexity, allowing us to evaluate their suitability depending on the characteristics of each corpus. As far as we know, this study constitutes the most extensive analysis so far of a system addressing the task of explainable clinical coding by being evaluated across four different tasks, represented by four distinct corpora in two languages, and covering three ICD variants. Overall, the system performs well in many scenarios, demonstrating robustness and generalization, while highlighting challenges in more difficult settings.

Furthermore, the system explicitly incorporates explainability, ensuring that each coding decision is interpretable by clinicians and researchers. Additionally, as previously mentioned in Section “Introduction”, two main limitations are commonly found in explainable clinical coding systems: (1) unseen codes are typically not considered, and (2) discontinuous and/or overlapping entities are often ignored. The system proposed in this work addresses both issues, allowing models to detect and handle such cases effectively.

The experiments show that the system performs well overall, with balanced and generally satisfactory F1 values across corpora. As noted earlier, threshold optimization ( and ) introduces a trade-off between optimizing for F1 and optimizing for MAP. The best overall Precision and F1 scores were achieved by the WNER model using more restrictive thresholds (MF1), while ensemble methods, particularly WNER + GLiNER with less restrictive thresholds (MMAP), obtained the highest Recall and MAP. The experiments have been conducted within the experimental framework and using the same evaluation metrics as those of the reported state-of-the-art results. Among the systems that have outperformed the reported results, the average percentage improvement is 3.42%, with the highest being obtained in the explainability task of CodiEsp (CodiEsp-X, see Table 21), with a percentage improvement of 8.06%. Among the corpora, CodiEsp and MDACE proved to be the most challenging, with MDACE in particular showing lower explainability performance and notable differences between Precision and Recall. Performance remains limited in scenarios involving low-frequency or unseen codes and in cases with long or discontinuous entities.

Several potential directions for future work have been discussed throughout the paper. One of the main proposals is to expand the experiments to additional corpora, including the use of synthetic data, since the availability of publicly annotated clinical data, particularly with explainability annotations, is very limited due to privacy and sensitivity concerns. Moreover, a promising research direction is the application of transfer learning to other valuable ICD-10 corpora not used in this study due to the lack of textual annotations, such as the Spanish CARES corpus [22], the CARMEN-I corpus [72] or an extended version of the MIMIC-III corpus [20]. Furthermore, to align the system with current research trends, integrating LLMs poses a promising direction for future work, especially in Phase 3 to handle unseen codes more effectively. Finally, for comparability, this study follows the evaluation setup of state-of-the-art systems, which is the most widely used evaluation for automatic clinical coding. This means that explainability was evaluated strictly in terms of correct code+entity predictions. Future work could benefit from clinician validation to better assess the quality and clinical relevance of all predicted entities, especially those not marked as correct by this study’s evaluation criteria.
