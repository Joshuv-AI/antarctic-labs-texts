Photostimulation of skull bone marrow modulates neuroimmunity in sepsis-associated encephalopathy via the skull bone marrow-dura mater-brain axis
Authors: Yicheng Li, Yutao Du, Fanfan Ye, Hanxiao Cai, Wenmian Huang, Tao Liu, Yaoju Li, Xiaochang Xue, Guodong Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is a brain dysfunction caused by systemic inflammation, involving mitochondrial dysfunction, glial damage, and dysregulated inflammatory responses. Recent studies emphasize the role of the skull bone marrow (SBM) and dura mater (DM) in regulating neuroimmune responses through the Skull Bone Marrow-Dura Mater-Brain Axis (SBM-DM-B Axis), which could serve as a potential therapeutic target for neuroinflammatory disorders.MethodsIn this study, infrared (IR) at different power levels (10, 30, 50, 100, 300, 500 mW) was tested to assess the photonic, thermal, and biological effects, aiming to determine the optimal power for confining IR effects to the SBM. The identified optimal power was subsequently applied in all experiments. To establish an SAE mouse model, LPS was administered intraperitoneally, and daily 10-minute IR exposures were applied for 4 consecutive days. The effects of SBM-confined IR on neuroimmune regulation and neuronal protection were assessed by quantitatively analyzing microglial morphology, area, density, connectivity, and neuronal morphology and density, as well as by motor behavioral tests (four-limb grip strength, 45° pole, and rotarod tests) and cognitive behavioral tests (Y-maze spontaneous alternation and novel object recognition). All analyses were conducted separately for the cortex and hippocampus to investigate spatial effects. Additionally, the role of the SBM-DM-B Axis was explored by evaluating the diameter of meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs), evaluating dural neutrophils, and quantifying dural macrophage number and morphology to assess the impact of IR on DM immune regulation. To further explore SBM-restricted IR effects and underlying mechanisms in the skull and cortex, reactive oxygen species (ROS), cytochrome c oxidase (CCO), ATP, and nitric oxide (NO) levels were measured, and paired transcriptomic profiling was conducted on skull and cortex tissues from the same mice.ResultsEx vivo and in vivo penetration assays confirmed that 50 mW IR photostimulation was confined to the SBM with minimal penetration into deeper brain regions, while temperature monitoring and HSP70/HSP90 expression demonstrated that scalp temperature remained within safe limits and no thermal damage or stress responses occurred, in contrast to higher power levels (500 mW), which induced significant inflammation and neuronal loss. In SAE mice, daily 10-min SBM-targeted IR for four days promoted macrophage polarization in the SBM and facilitated body weight recovery, remodeled cortical microglia, mitigating neuronal loss and structural disruption in the cortex and hippocampus, and improved motor function. At the DM level, 50 mW IR dilated mLVs, reduced scattered and aggregated neutrophils, and restored macrophage density, area, and morphology, reversing the LPS-induced enlargement and sparse distribution of macrophages. Paired skull-cortex transcriptomic analyses revealed opposite baseline responses: IR enhanced metabolic and proliferative programs in the skull while suppressing biosynthetic and mitochondrial pathways in the cortex. In SAE mice, both tissues showed concordant downregulation of scavenger receptor uptake and IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, indicating reduced phagocytosis and inflammatory signaling.ConclusionsWe demonstrate that 50 mW IR can be safely confined to the SBM, effectively modulating skull, meningeal, and brain immunity to protect neurons in SAE mice. This study establishes SBM-restricted IR photobiomodulation as a safe and effective strategy to reprogram neuroimmune responses, highlighting the SBM-DM-B Axis as a novel pathway for neuroimmune regulation and a promising target for non-invasive interventions in SAE and other acute brain injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03617-x.Keywords: Sepsis-associated encephalopathy, Skull bone marrow, Near-infrared, Photobiomodulation, SBM-DM-B axis

Introduction

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is a diffuse brain dysfunction induced by systemic inflammatory responses, with a prevalence of up to 70% in septic patients and mortality rates approaching 50%1. SAE is characterized by unexplained alterations in consciousness, manifesting as delirium, coma, or seizures. Notably, neurological examinations and neuroimaging findings are often unremarkable [1], with no evidence of central nervous system (CNS) infection, structural abnormalities, or other types of encephalopathy. The pathogenesis of SAE is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of mechanisms. The major pathogenic factors include mitochondrial dysfunction, glial cell impairment, dysregulation of inflammatory mediators, and inflammation at the brain borders [2–5].

The CNS has traditionally been considered an immune-privileged organ. However, accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of its border structures, particularly the skull bone marrow (SBM) and dura mater (DM), in neuroimmune regulation [6–10]. Unlike long bone marrow, SBM maintains a unique microenvironment shaped by its proximity to the CNS. Recent studies [6–9] suggest that during neuroinflammatory conditions, SBM can rapidly mobilize immune cells, facilitating their infiltration into the DM and even the brain parenchyma. The DM, as the outermost meningeal layer anatomically anchored to the inner surface of the skull, facilitates the trafficking of SBM-derived immune cells, metabolic byproducts, and inflammatory mediators to the brain parenchyma through channels between the SBM and the brain surface [6, 10]. The interactions among the SBM, the DM, and the brain establish the Skull Bone Marrow-Dura Mater-Brain Axis (SBM-DM-B Axis) [6–9, 11], a critical yet underexplored pathway that regulates immune cell trafficking and inflammatory signaling between the brain and its borders. Therefore, the SBM-DM-B Axis may serve as a promising therapeutic target for neuroinflammatory disorders.

By acting on cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria, infrared (IR) enhances the production of ATP, reactive oxygen species, and nitric oxide [12–14], and promotes the polarization of macrophages from the glycolysis-dependent pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the oxidative phosphorylation-dependent anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [12, 13, 15–17]. IR also stimulates the expansion of lymphatic vessels [17–20]. Many studies have explored the use of transcranial IR to exert direct photobiomodulation effects on brain parenchyma, revealing the therapeutic potential of IR for neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, traumatic brain injury, and depression [21–25]. However, delivering sufficient IR to the brain parenchyma requires higher light intensities to penetrate the scalp and skull, which may pose potential safety risks, particularly in terms of thermal damage.

Here, We aimed to confine IR photostimulation strictly to the SBM, ensuring no direct impact on brain tissues while leveraging the SBM-DM-B Axis to indirectly regulate neuroinflammation.

Materials and methods

Animals

The experiments were conducted using male C57BL/6 J wild-type mice (6–8 weeks), which were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, at room temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and humidity of 50–60%, with no food or water restriction. Only male mice were used to minimize potential confounding effects of hormonal variations. A minimum of one week was allowed for the mice to acclimate to the environment before the experiment commenced. All experiments were carried out in accordance with the policies of Animal Ethics Committee of the Central Laboratory of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, China.

LPS treatment

Mice were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control groups and received intraperitoneal injections of 0.5 mg/kg phosphate buffer saline (PBS) or 0.5 mg/kg lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from E. coli O55:B5 (Sigma Aldrich L2280) for four consecutive days, as previously described [3, 26].

IR radiation scheme

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and their scalps were shaved. The following irradiation parameters were kept constant throughout the experiments: wavelength (808 nm), irradiation distance (10 cm), exposure duration (10 min of continuous irradiation), and frequency (once per day for 4 consecutive days). A gradient of output power (0, 10, 30, 50, 100, 300, 500 mW) (Shenzhilei Optical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) was applied. For safety assessment experiments, the irradiation site was positioned at the right parieto-occipital region, enabling bilateral and inter-group comparisons. For SBM modulation experiments, the irradiation site was positioned at the occipital-parietal junction (posterior fontanel), where the SBM is most abundant and concentrated in mice, to maximize SBM modulation. Mice in the LPSIR group and PBSIR group were irradiated within 2 h after intraperitoneal injection.

IR penetration measurement

Irradiation power (mW/cm²) was measured using an infrared irradiometer (LH-131, Lianhuichen, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) equipped with an 808 nm infrared probe. To preserve intact scalp and calvarial bone, the brain tissue was removed by cutting along both temporal regions from the foramen magnum with tissue scissors. The laser beam was first directed onto the irradiometer probe positioned 10 cm from the source, corresponding to the distance between the laser source and the scalp in vivo; this measurement represented the irradiation power received at the scalp surface. The scalp and calvarial bone were then placed on the probe for subsequent measurements under identical irradiation settings. This setup allowed measurement of the transmitted irradiation intensity through both scalp and skull, corresponding to the irradiation power reaching the brain surface in vivo. Subsequently, the skull was removed and the scalp alone was placed on the probe to measure the transmitted light intensity through scalp only, corresponding to the irradiation power at the skull surface in vivo. Measurements were repeated in four independent mice to control for individual variation, with paired scalp and skull samples from each mouse. For each sample, laser intensities of 10, 30, 50, 100, 300, and 500 mW were tested sequentially.

CT-based optical forward model at 808 nm and ratio-based validation

We built a CT-driven forward model to estimate intracranial light transmission at 808 nm. From the sagittal CT volume, seven planes were extracted at 10-slice intervals to sample cranial variability. Each plane was zero-padded to 1024 × 1024 with − 1000 HU (air) to standardize the field of view. The in-plane voxel size was 0.045 cm/pixel. HU values were thresholded into tissue classes (air, fat, water/CSF, white matter, gray matter, muscle, blood, parenchyma, cancellous bone, cortical bone) to generate an anatomical label map. For each class, optical properties at 808 nm were taken from literature values: absorption coefficients (µa, cm− 1) and scattering coefficients (µs′) 27–32. This yielded spatial maps of µa and µs′.

Within the posterior fontanel region of each plane, near-normal (vertical) line segments were placed using two collinear points: pskin just beneath the scalp (outer skull surface) and pctx at the cortical surface; placement was repeated 4 times per plane (4 lines; 8 points/plane). Along each line, µa and µs′ were sampled with bilinear interpolation. We computed µeff(x) and the straight-path internal transmission I/I0. Transmission evaluated at pskin and pctx yielded scalp transmittance Tskin (to the skull surface) and total transmittance Ttotal (to the cortex). To avoid recalibrating model parameters with ex-vivo data, agreement between simulated (in silico) and ex-vivo results was assessed using the calibration-free ratio (R = Tskin/Ttotal).

Tissue collection

Mice were euthanized 2 h after the final treatment to capture the acute phase of neuroinflammatory responses, as commonly adopted in systemic LPS models [26]. Mice were transcardially perfused with saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Skull caps and brains were then immersed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 48 h. Then, DMs were carefully stripped from skull caps, and skulls were immersed in decalcifying solution for 1–2 weeks, while the brains were immersed in sucrose solution for 48 h prior to sectioning. Tissue sections were cut at 5 μm thickness for paraffin embedding and at 20 μm thickness for frozen sections.

Histological and immunofluorescence staining

Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) and Nissl staining were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For immunofluorescence, tissue sections were first incubated with blocking buffer (P0260, Beyotime) at room temperature for 1.5 h. The primary antibody (listed in Supplementary Table 1) were diluted in the specified proportions using a diluent (P0262, Beyotime) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then washed 3–5 times with PBS (5 min per wash), followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted in P0265, Beyotime) at room temperature for 1.5 h. After PBS washes, the nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg/mL) for 5 min. Following final PBS washes, tissues were carefully transferred to glass slides using fine brushes and forceps, air-dried in the dark, and mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching agent under coverslips for imaging. Fluorescent images were captured using the Olympus BX43 microscope.

Morphology analysis of microglia and DM macrophage

Microglia and DM macrophage morphology were analyzed using the MicrogliaMorphologyR pipeline [26, 27]. Fluorescent images were preprocessed in Fiji ImageJ (v1.8.0, NIH, USA) [28] to isolate single cells and remove non-cellular particles. Skeletonized single-cell images were quantitatively analyzed for cell body area, branch length, branch points, and dendritic complexity. Post-processing and statistical analysis were performed in R (v4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and the ColorByCluster feature used for microglia clusters visualization.

Quantification of the mLVs diameter

The diameter of mLVs was measured based on established methods [19] with improvements. Lymphatic vessel diameters were quantified from binarized vessel masks obtained after edge detection and morphological closing. Vessel centerlines were extracted by skeletonization, and local radii were determined by Euclidean distance transform as the radius of the maximal inscribed circle, i.e., the shortest distance from each centerline pixel to the vessel boundary. Radius values were sampled along the centerlines, and local diameters were computed as twice the corresponding radii. These values were subsequently summarized for statistical comparisons between groups.

Quantitative analysis and spatial mapping of DM neutrophils

To characterize neutrophil responses in the DM, we established an image-based quantification pipeline. Four anatomical regions were analyzed, including the whole dura (all), as well as the frontal, parietal, and occipital compartments. Neutrophils were segmented using particle analysis and represented as ellipses to capture their size and shape. Based on area thresholds, neutrophils were categorized into three morphological types: scattered, aggregate, and swarm. This approach enabled both spatial mapping and quantitative comparison of neutrophil densities across experimental groups.

Behavioral assessments

Four-limb grip strength test

Whole-body grip strength was measured using a wire grid connected to a digital grip strength meter. Mice were allowed to grasp the grid with all four limbs and were gently pulled backward until they released. The peak force (gf) generated during each trial was automatically recorded. Each mouse performed three consecutive trials with brief intervals, and the maximum value was taken as the representative grip strength to minimize variability from incomplete grasps. Body weight was measured on the same day, and normalized grip strength (gf/g) was calculated to account for inter-animal differences in body size.

45° pole test

A rough-surfaced pole (length 50 cm, diameter 1 cm) was fixed at a 45° angle relative to the cage floor. Mice were placed head-upward at the top of the pole, and the latency to turn completely downward (T-turn) as well as the time required to descend to the base (T-down) were recorded with a stopwatch. The total descent time (T-total = T-turn + T-down) was also calculated. Each mouse completed three consecutive trials with 3–5 min inter-trial intervals. The mean value across trials was used for analysis.

Rotarod test

Mice were placed on a rotating rod that accelerated linearly from 5 rpm to 40 rpm over a maximum duration of 300 s. Each animal performed three consecutive trials separated by 30 min intervals. The latency to fall was automatically recorded for each trial. The mean latency across trials was used as the representative performance for statistical analysis.

Y-maze spontaneous alternation

Each mouse was initially placed at the central junction of the three arms and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. Arm entries were manually scored, and total distance moved was recorded automatically. The spontaneous alternation percentage (SAP) was calculated as alternations/(total entries − 2)×100 using sliding triplets, where an alternation was defined as consecutive entries into all three arms without repetition.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

Mice first underwent a 10-min habituation in an empty arena, followed by a 10-min training session with two identical cylindrical objects placed at diagonally opposite locations. After a 1 h retention interval, a 10 min test session was conducted in which one cylinder (familiar object) was replaced by a novel square-based pyramid at the same location. All phases (habituation, training, and test) were performed in the same arena.

Locomotion (total distance, cm; mean velocity, cm/s) was recorded during training and test phases. Exploration was defined as the snout oriented toward the object within ≤ 2 cm (sniffing or whisker contact), excluding climbing or sitting; manual scoring used a minimum bout of 1 s. Derived behavioral indices were computed as follows: training side bias=|T1 − T2|/(T1 + T2); discrimination index (DI)=(Tnew-Told)/(Tnew + Told); preference for the novel object (PrefNew) =(Tnew)/(Tnew + Told), where T represents cumulative exploration time (s). A priori exclusion criteria were applied: (i) training total exploration < 20 s; (ii) training side bias > 0.60; (iii) test total exploration < 10 s;

Measurement of ROS, CCO, NO, and ATP

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), cytochrome c oxidase (CCO), nitric oxide (NO), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels in skull or cortex homogenates were quantified using commercial assay kits following the manufacturers’ instructions. ROS was measured with the ROS Assay Kit (ET0008M, Wellbio, Shanghai, China; read at 560 nm). CCO activity was assessed using the Mouse Cytochrome c Oxidase ELISA Kit (EM31160M, Wellbio; detection range 0.31–20.31 ng/mL, sensitivity 0.06 ng/mL, CV < 10%; read at 450 nm). NO was determined with the NO Assay Kit (ET0005M, Wellbio; range 0.313–20.313 µmol/L, sensitivity 0.2 µmol/L; read at 540 nm). ATP concentration was evaluated by luminescence using the ATP Assay Kit (JL-T0633-96T, Jonln, Shanghai, China). Tissues were homogenized in PBS (1:9, w/v), centrifuged at 3000–5000 g for 15–20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants collected. Concentrations were calculated from kit-specific standard curves. All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Transcriptome sequencing and data processing

RNA extraction and quality control

Total RNA was extracted from skull or cortex tissues using standard protocols (Biotree, Shanghai, China). Purity was assessed by NanoDrop (OD260/280, OD260/230), and integrity was evaluated with the Agilent 5400 Bioanalyzer. RNA integrity and yield served as the main quality indicators.

Library construction

Poly(A) + mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA with random hexamers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized, purified, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adapters. Fragments of 250–300 bp were selected using AMPure XP beads and amplified by PCR to generate libraries.

Library quality assessment

Library concentration was measured with Qubit, insert size with the Agilent 5400, and effective concentration by qPCR. Libraries with concentrations > 2 nM were deemed qualified.

Sequencing

Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (PE150). Sequencing was performed by the sequencing-by-synthesis method, where fluorescently labeled dNTPs release signals that were captured and converted into base calls.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.0, La Jolla, CA, USA), SPSS (v21.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (v4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, two-group comparisons were analyzed using Student’s unpaired two-tailed t-test, while multi-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. Non-normally distributed data were evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (multi-group) followed by Dunn’s post hoc correction. Parametric data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), while non-parametric data were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with adjustments for multiple comparisons where applicable.

Results

Effects of 50 mW IR on SBM: Localization, Safety, and immune modulation

50 mW IR confines photostimulation to the SBM layer while maintaining safe temperature

Ex vivo measurements confirm that tissue attenuation markedly reduces optical transmission with depth (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary_Data1). At 50 mW IR irradiation, the skull surface receives 7.54 ± 1.30 mW/cm², while cortical exposure remains limited (1.84 ± 0.15 mW/cm2), indicating that most of the optical energy is confined to the skull. In contrast, at 10 mW the power delivered to the skull is small (1.35 ± 0.11 mW/cm2), and at 30 mW the skull deposition decreases by nearly half compared with 50 mW (4.32 vs. 7.54 mW/cm2), whereas cortical exposure shows only a minor reduction (1.26 vs. 1.84 mW/cm2). At 100 mW, skull deposition increases further (12.14 ± 1.53 mW/cm2), but is accompanied by an increase in cortical exposure (3.56 ± 0.49 mW/cm2), compromising cortical sparing. Together, these comparisons highlight that 50 mW represents the most favorable regime, where optical energy is predominantly deposited in the skull, with only limited transmission to the underlying brain tissue.

To evaluate whether in vivo constituents (blood and cerebrospinal fluid) introduce systematic bias into ex vivo measurements, we use a CT-based forward model (Fig. 1b-c) parameterized by tissue-specific optical properties at 808 nm, namely the absorption coefficient (µa) and reduced scattering coefficient (µs′), which are assigned to HU-derived tissue classes based on literature values [29–34]. The calibration-free Ratio (Tskin/Ttotal) shows no significant difference between ex vivo and in silico data (median 3.72 vs. 3.50, p = 0.22) (Fig. 1d; Supplementary_Data2), indicating that the in silico ratios are consistent with the ex vivo measurements.

The temperature curve exhibits a biphasic pattern, with an initial steep rise followed by stabilization (Fig. 2a; Supplementary_Data3). For ≤ 50 mW IR, equilibrium is reached within 2–3 min, whereas higher powers take 3–4 min. Higher IR intensities lead to greater scalp heating. At 10, 30, and 50 mW, temperature increases remain modest, with a peak of 25.60 ± 0.41 °C and a temperature variation (ΔT) of 3.15 ± 0.06 °C at 50 mW, remaining within the physiological safe range (≤ 26 °C) and thus not expected to induce thermal injury. In contrast, at 500 mW, the peak temperature reaches 47.68 ± 0.62 °C (ΔT = 25.43 ± 0.49 °C), well above the threshold for tissue damage.

Unilateral IR is applied consistently to the right scalp of mice, with the contralateral hemisphere serving as a within-animal control (Fig. 2b-c). For histology, the DM is flat-mounted with its inner surface facing upward and brain sections are cut rostrocaudally, resulting in a left-right reversal: the photostimulated right hemisphere appears on the left side of both DM and brain section images.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) function as molecular chaperones that preserve protein homeostasis under stress. Among them, HSP70 is strongly inducible and serves as a marker of acute stress or cytoprotective responses, whereas HSP90 is constitutively expressed but undergoes modulation during severe stress [35, 36]. We therefore examined brain HSP70 and HSP90 expression as molecular indicators of potential thermal injury or proteostatic imbalance induced by IR.

In WT mice, HSP70 expression is predominantly localized to superficial cortical layers I-III (molecular, external granular, and external pyramidal), whereas HSP90 is enriched in deeper layers IV-VI (internal granular, internal pyramidal, and multiform) and hippocampus (Fig. 2d-f; Supplementary Fig. 2). At 50 mW, unilateral IR photostimulation maintains baseline HSP70 and HSP90 expression patterns across the IR-irradiated (L) and non-irradiated (R) cortex as well as hippocampus, indicating that 50 mW IR does not perturb baseline stress-protein distribution and confirming its safety threshold identified from temperature measurements. By contrast, 100 mW stimulation induces focal upregulation of HSP70 in IR-irradiated (L) cortical layer V and the hippocampus, accompanied by partial nuclear translocation (yellow arrows). A mild increase is also observed in non-irradiated (R) layer V and hippocampal regions. These findings indicate that 100 mW IR elicits a localized protective stress response in the cortex and hippocampus. At 300 mW, IR-irradiated (L) HSP70 expression is further enhanced in cortical layers II-III and V, and in the hippocampus (most pronounced in CA3), with clear evidence of nuclear translocation (yellow arrows). At 500 mW, pronounced HSP70 upregulation is observed in IR-irradiated (L) layers II-III, with strong nuclear positivity and cytoplasmic accumulation (yellow arrows). In parallel, HSP90 shows marked cytoplasmic upregulation across layers III-VI and hippocampus, accompanied by an almost complete loss of HSP70 staining in these deeper layers. This shift indicates a layer-dependent reprogramming of stress responses under high-intensity stimulation. Both 300 mW and 500 mW irradiation trigger widespread, strongly HSP70-positive cells within the superficial cortical layers, with staining intensity far exceeding baseline and spatially coinciding with the irradiated region (Fig. 2d, right panel).

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR does not induce inflammatory or thermal damage

At 500 mW IR, evident erythema and hemoglobin deposition are observed on the illuminated side of skull and brain tissue (Fig. 2c, white arrows), and are further confirmed by H&E staining (Fig. 3a-d, black arrows). In DM H&E staining, the illuminated side shows substantial inflammatory cell infiltration, which is quantified by hematoxylin %Area since nuclear counting is not applicable due to overlap and clumping; the hematoxylin %Area is significantly higher than that of the contralateral side (64.67% vs. 29.57%, P = 0.0034) (Fig. 3b-c; Supplementary_Data4).

Nissl staining reveals reduced neuronal density on the 500mw illuminated side, most notably in layer IV (internal granular layer) (P = 0.042) (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 3a; Supplementary_Data5). In contrast, 50 mW IR does not induce adverse effects on the skull, DM, or brain tissue. Moreover, IR irradiation (10–500 mW) exerts no impact on neuronal density in deep brain regions, including the CA1 and CA3 areas of the hippocampus (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3b-d; Supplementary_Data5). Given that Nissl staining intensity may vary with batch, results are based on quantitative neuronal density analyses.

50 mW IR induces macrophage polarization in the SBM and attenuates weight loss in SAE mice

To determine whether 50 mW IR is sufficient to influence the immune status of the SBM, irradiation is targeted to the parieto-occipital junction, where the SBM is most enriched (Fig. 4a-b). After daily 10-minute 50 mW IR irradiation for four consecutive days, quantification of CD206 expression in SBM reveals a marked increase in the LPSIR group compared with LPS (14.42 vs. 3.30, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4c-d; Supplementary_Data6). No significant difference are observed between PBS and LPS, or between PBS and PBSIR. In contrast, iNOS area fractions are significantly elevated in the LPS group compared with PBS controls (20.20 vs. 5.33, P < 0.001), but are reduced following IR treatment in the LPSIR group (10.06 vs. 20.20, p = 0.04) (Fig. 4c-d; Supplementary_Data7). Collectively, these findings suggest that 50 mW IR photostimulation promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype in the SBM.

To further explore whether the 50 mW IR-induced immunomodulation of the SBM can ameliorate SAE, we examine body weight changes. LPS-treated mice exhibit a marked weight loss compared with PBS controls (Day 2: −15.1%, Day 3: −22.6%, Day 4: −25.3%; all p < 0.001) (Fig. 4e; Supplementary_Data8). In contrast, PBSIR mice do not significantly differ from PBS, except for a modest reduction on Day 2 (PBS vs. PBSIR: +4.5%, p = 0.01). Relative to LPS, LPSIR mice display attenuated weight loss (Day 4: difference = 6.8%, p = 0.004), and a significant rebound from Day 3 to Day 4 (+ 2.08%, p = 0.031), indicating a partial recovery effect.

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR regulates neuroimmune responses and preserves neuronal integrity in SAE

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR modulates cortical microglial density, connectivity, and morphology in SAE

LPS injection increases microglial numbers while reducing area in the cortex, CA1, and CA3 (50.3 vs. 42.3, p = 0.004 in cortex counts; 14.2 vs. 18.6, p = 0.001 in cortex %Area; 43.0 vs. 38.5, p = 0.042 in CA1 counts; 12.8 vs. 17.7, p = 0.004 in CA1%Area; 44.8 vs. 34.2, p < 0.001 in CA3 counts; 14.0 vs. 19.8, p < 0.001 in CA3%Area) (Fig. 5a-b; Supplementary_Data9-10). Compared with LPS, LPSIR increases cortical microglial area (20.3 vs. 14.2, P < 0.001) while reducing microglial counts (37.4 vs. 50.3, P < 0.001). In the hippocampus, LPSIR restores microglial area (20.3 vs. 12.8, p < 0.0001 in CA1; 20.4 vs. 14.0, p < 0.0001 in CA3) without significantly altering microglial counts (44.0 vs. 43.0, p = 0.884 in CA1; 41.0 vs. 44.8, p = 0.167 in CA3) (Fig. 5a-b; Supplementary_Data9-10). These results indicate that LPS enhances microglial proliferation while reducing cell size across cortex, CA1, and CA3, whereas LPSIR effectively restores microglial area in all regions but attenuates cell number only in the cortex, which is likely attributable to region-specific changes in microglial morphology.

To examine whether IR modulates the spatial arrangement and intercellular interactions of microglia, end-to-end alignment events are quantified by counting head-to-tail contacts between adjacent Iba1 + cells per field of view. LPSIR reduces microglial end-to-end events in the cortex (4.0 vs. 14.0, P = 0.037) but shows no significant difference in the hippocampus (11.0 vs. 12.0, P > 0.05) (Fig. 5c-d; Supplementary_Data11), indicating a region-specific modulation that limits excessive microglial interactions.

The 4-day repeated LPS administration induces an adaptive shift of microglia from a highly phagocytic amoeboid state (0.34 vs. 0.37, P = 0.016 in the cortex; 0.31 vs. 0.38, P < 0.001 in the hippocampus) to a reactive hypertrophic state (0.18 vs. 0.14, P < 0.001 in the cortex; 0.19 vs. 0.12, P < 0.001 in the hippocampus) (Fig. 6a-c; Supplementary Fig. 4a-e; Supplementary_Data12). In contrast, LPSIR further reinforces this shift by increasing hypertrophic microglia (0.23 vs. 0.18, P = 0.002) while reducing ramified (surveillance) microglia (0.29 vs. 0.20, P = 0.013) in the cortex, representing a controlled remodeling of cortical microglia rather than an excessive activation. No significant differences in microglial morphologies are observed in the hippocampus between the LPSIR and LPS, consistent with unchanged end-to-end alignment and microglial numbers, suggesting that 50 mW IR photostimulation of the SBM exerts immune modulation confined to the cortex.

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR protects against inflammation-induced neuronal loss and structural disruption in SAE

To investigate whether IR mitigates inflammation-induced neuronal loss and structural disruption in SAE, we quantify neuronal density with Nissl staining, which clearly labels neuronal cell bodies and enables reliable assessment of cortical lamination across the six layers. H&E and NeuN staining are additionally applied qualitatively to corroborate the Nissl findings. Quantitative analysis based on Nissl staining reveals that after LPS injection, neuronal density significantly decreases across all cortical layers (Layer I: 951.88 vs. 665.62, P = 0.0013; Layer II: 2534.64 vs. 2152.83, P = 0.0154; Layer III: 2326.04 vs. 1939.55, P = 0.0062; Layer IV: 3080.71 vs. 2562.38, P = 0.006; Layer V: 1971.50 vs. 1504.23, P < 0.0001; Layer VI: 2497.74 vs. 2164.07, P = 0.0049) (Fig. 7a-c; Supplementary_Data13). Compared with LPS, the LPSIR group shows significant increases in neuronal density in Layer I, III-VI (Layer I: 985.04 vs. 665.62, P = 0.0003; Layer III: 2377.15 vs. 1939.55, P = 0.0016; Layer IV: 3012.54 vs. 2562.38, P = 0.021; Layer V: 1811.76 vs. 1504.23, P = 0.0004; Layer VI: 2450.84 vs. 2164.07, P = 0.0191), while PBSIR mice show higher density in Layers IV and VI relative to PBS (Layer IV: 3430.67 vs. 3080.71, P = 0.049; Layer VI: 2840.12 vs. 2497.74, P = 0.0037). Representative NeuN staining shows similar intergroup differences (Fig. 7a), supporting the validity of the Nissl-based counts.

In the cortex, H&E and Nissl staining reveal distinct morphological alterations. LPS induces significant structural disorganization, obscuring the six-layer architecture (Fig. 7a-b). H&E staining reveals pronounced neuronal swelling, rounded somata, and cytoplasmic vacuolization, especially in Layers III and V (Fig. 7a-b, yellow circles). The LPSIR group, however, maintains well-demarcated cortical lamination and regular neuronal morphology, resembling the PBS group. Nissl staining further supports these findings, showing intact Nissl body distribution and nucleolar prominence in the LPSIR group, while LPS-treated neurons exhibit chromatolysis and nucleolar shrinkage (Fig. 7a-b, black arrows). Since Nissl bodies mainly consist of rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) and ribosomes, TEM analysis provides ultrastructural validation: cortical neurons from PBS mice exhibit elongated RER cisternae densely studded with ribosomes, while those from LPS mice show markedly swollen, ribosome-depleted RER. Notably, LPSIR treatment partially restores the structural integrity of RER, further supporting the Nissl staining results (Fig. 7d).

In the hippocampus, the LPS group shows a significant reduction in neuronal number (CA1: 71.67 vs. 44.25, P < 0.0001; CA3: 54.17 vs. 29.50, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 7e-f; Supplementary_Data14), accompanied by disrupted CA1 and CA3 layers, neuronal soma atrophy, hyperchromatic cytoplasm, and blurred cell boundaries (Fig. 7e, black arrows). In contrast, the LPSIR group exhibits higher neuronal counts compared with LPS (CA1: 56.92 vs. 44.25, P < 0.0001; CA3: 48.08 vs. 29.50, P < 0.0001), along with preserved morphology characterized by clear cytoplasmic staining, sharp cellular margins, and intact laminar organization, closely resembling PBS controls. These findings indicate that LPSIR mitigates neuronal loss and structural damage in the SAE mice.

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR alleviates LPS-induced motor deficits but not cognitive impairments

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR alleviates LPS-induced motor deficits

LPS treatment induces significant motor impairments across multiple behavioral paradigms. In the grip strength test, LPS-treated mice exhibit reduced four-limb strength compared with PBS controls, both in absolute force (153.1 vs. 203.8 gf, p < 0.0001) and normalized values (9.76 vs. 10.76 gf/g, p = 0.041). These deficits are alleviated in the LPSIR group, which shows improved grip strength relative to LPS mice (absolute: 183.9 vs. 153.1 gf, p = 0.034; normalized: 11.38 vs. 9.76 gf/g, p = 0.0012), while PBSIR does not differ significantly from PBS in absolute force but displays slightly higher normalized values (12.14 vs. 10.76 gf/g, p = 0.046) (Fig. 8a-b; Supplementary_Data15).

In the 45° pole test, LPS mice exhibit prolonged T-turn (3.96 s vs. 1.71 s, p = 0.002), T-down (10.98 s vs. 6.03 s, p = 0.011), and T-total times (14.02 s vs. 7.92 s, p = 0.002) compared with PBS, indicative of motor slowing. No significant differences are observed between PBS and PBSIR mice, suggesting that photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR does not alter baseline motor performance (Fig. 8c-d; Supplementary_Data16).

Similarly, in the rotarod test, LPS mice show markedly reduced latency to fall compared with PBS (151.3 s vs. 270.3 s, p < 0.001), consistent with impaired motor coordination. This deficit is significantly improved in the LPSIR group (234.0 s vs. 151.3 s, p = 0.0047), whereas PBSIR performance remains comparable to PBS (Fig. 8e; Supplementary_Data17).

Collectively, these results indicate that LPS induces robust motor deficits, which are substantially mitigated by photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR, without affecting baseline performance.

Photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR fails to rescue cognitive impairments

In the Y-maze spontaneous alternation test, photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR fails to rescue LPS-induced working memory deficits. The LPS group exhibits lower SAP compared with PBS (51.9 vs. 66.1, p = 0.004), whereas no significant difference is observed between LPS and LPSIR (51.9 vs. 55.3, p = 0.169) or between PBS and PBSIR (66.1 vs. 61.3, p = 0.954). In contrast, total arm entries (TAE) and distance moved do not differ among groups, indicating that SAP differences are not confounded by locomotor activity (Fig. 8f, Supplementary Fig. 5a-b; Supplementary_Data18).

In the novel object recognition (NOR) test, LPS mice exhibit impaired recognition memory, which is not rescued by SBM photostimulation. At the test phase, discrimination index (DI) is significantly lower in LPS compared with PBS (0.186 vs. 0.343, p = 0.029), with no difference between LPS and LPSIR (0.186 vs. 0.054, p = 0.549). Preference for the novel object (PrefNew) shows the same pattern, being reduced in LPS compared with PBS (0.593 vs. 0.672, p = 0.029), with no difference between LPS and LPSIR (0.593 vs. 0.527, p = 0.549). By contrast, test total exploration time, distance moved, and velocity do not differ significantly among groups (all p > 0.05), suggesting that recognition deficits in LPS mice are not attributable to altered locomotor activity. Training phase measures (side bias, total exploration, distance, velocity) are also comparable across groups (all p > 0.05), excluding baseline differences in locomotor activity or exploration (Fig. 8g-h, Supplementary Fig. 5c-d; Supplementary_Data19).

In the PBS vs. PBSIR comparison, shared pathways related to protein synthesis, turnover, and antigen presentation (e.g., translation, proteasome assembly, amino acid activation, and cross-presentation of soluble exogenous antigens endosomes) are upregulated in skull but downregulated in the cortex (Fig. 13b-g). This bidirectional regulation suggests that IR photostimulation enhances metabolic and proliferative programs in the skull to support hematopoietic and immune readiness, while concurrently suppresses these processes in the cortex to reduce metabolic burden. Opposite regulation of cell-cycle pathways, such as Gtse1 in G2/M progression and Cdc25a degradation, further supports this dual role, promoting proliferation in marrow-derived cells while preventing aberrant cell-cycle entry in cortical cells.

Cortex-specific pathways echo this pattern: a few are upregulated, such as TGF-β production and mRNA deadenylation-dependent decay, consistent with enhanced transcript surveillance and anti-inflammatory tone, whereas a larger set-including mitochondrial translation/gene expression, tRNA and polyamine/ODC metabolism, and Wnt/DVL degradation-is downregulated (Fig. 13b-g), reflecting reduced mitochondrial protein synthesis and biosynthetic activity.

Skull-specific responses under PBS are broadly upregulated, encompassing RNA surveillance (NMD/NMD-EJC-independent), translation (synaptic initiation), rRNA processing, and multiple immune modules (neutrophil activation/degranulation, complement triggering, antigen presentation, phagocytosis regulation, IL-4 response, lymphoid-non-lymphoid interactions) (Fig. 13b-g). Only pyruvate decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA is downregulated among the top20 terms. These results indicate robust anabolic and immunoregulatory activation in skull marrow-upshifting protein synthesis/proteostasis and antigen presentation while engaging effector myeloid programs, in sharp contrast to cortical suppression.

In the LPS vs. LPSIR comparison, only two shared pathways-binding and uptake of ligands by scavenger receptors and IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling-are concordantly downregulated in both tissues (Fig. 13b-g). This indicates dampened phagocytosis, reduced inflammatory mediator production, and attenuation of cytokine-driven neurotoxicity.

Cortex-specific pathways are also uniformly downregulated, including animal organ regeneration, glycosyl compound biosynthesis, and complement triggering (Fig. 13b-g), suggesting suppression of reparative biosynthesis and dampening of innate activation within cortex during inflammatory challenge.

Skull-specific changes are bimodal: cell-cycle programs (DNA replication, G2/M checkpoint, E2F targets, NPC disassembly, heme signaling) are downregulated, while bioenergetic and lipid modules are upregulated (oxidative phosphorylation, electron transport, Complex I biogenesis, NADH dehydrogenase assembly, ATP synthesis, and cholesterol biosynthesis/homeostasis) (Fig. 13b-g). These shifts indicate that IR restrains proliferative cycling in skull marrow while reprogramming mitochondria toward oxidative metabolism and reinforcing lipid pathways required for immune cell function.

Together, these results demonstrate that IR photostimulation exerts opposing metabolic effects under basal conditions-enhancing anabolic activity in skull while suppressing biosynthesis in the cortex-yet enforces a coordinated anti-inflammatory program under systemic inflammation, with skull responses dominating in magnitude and breadth.

Consistent with previous work, IR regulates bone marrow metabolism and hematopoietic activity [38, 39], supporting skull marrow as a primary locus of PBM action. In contrast, our observation of cortical suppression of biosynthetic and mitochondrial programs diverges from earlier reports describing enhanced neuronal survival, mitochondrial function, and synaptic activity after IR exposure [21, 40–42]. This discrepancy implies that the cortical effects are unlikely to result from direct neuronal photostimulation and instead reflect secondary adaptations to skull marrow-derived signals.

Mechanistic anchor analysis identifies core driver genes of tissue-specific IR responses

Building upon the pathway-level landscape described above, we next perform a mechanistic anchor analysis to pinpoint the core pathways and driver genes mediating tissue-specific responses to IR photostimulation. This analysis integrates Camera enrichment and differential expression results, defining pathways with FDR ≤ 0.10 as pathway anchors. Their leading-edge genes are extracted using fgsea based on ranked t-statistics, with a fallback of the top 10 direction-consistent genes when necessary. Genes that recur across multiple enriched pathways and meet FDR ≤ 0.10, |log₂FC| ≥ 0.5, and consistent regulation direction are designated as gene anchors, ranked by a composite score combining recurrence, effect size, and significance.

Under physiological conditions (PBSIR vs. PBS), skull-derived anchors converge on coordinated activation of translational and immune programs, whereas cortical anchors emphasize suppression of biosynthetic and proteostatic processes (Fig. 14a-b), refining the bidirectional regulation pattern observed in Fig. 13. The most recurrent skull gene anchors (Syk, Btk, Tyrobp, Irf1, Nlrp3, and Fcgr1) link translation and antigen presentation to coordinated myeloid activation, reflecting a shift toward an immunometabolic state. In contrast, cortical gene anchors such as Psma/Psmb/Psmc/Psmd and Mrpl/Mrps define reduced protein turnover and mitochondrial translation (Fig. 14e-f), consistent with metabolic downscaling and anti-inflammatory adaptation.

Under inflammatory conditions (LPSIR vs. LPS), skull anchors shift toward oxidative and lipid metabolism while restraining proliferative programs, driven by genes including Hba-a1/a2, Inhbb, Pim1, Cpt1a, and Angptl4 (Fig. 14c, g). Discussions

In this study, ex vivo and in vivo penetration assays confirmed that 50 mW IR photostimulation is confined to the SBM, while temperature monitoring, HSP70/HSP90 expression, and H&E staining verified the absence of thermal damage or stress responses, thereby establishing both safety and spatial specificity of SBM-targeted photostimulation. In SAE mice, daily 10-min SBM photostimulation for four days promoted body weight recovery, improved motor function, and remodeled cortical microglia (shifting from ramified to hypertrophic states), thereby mitigating neuronal loss and structural disruption. At the DM level, photostimulation of SBM with 50 mW IR dilated mLVs, reduced scattered and aggregated neutrophils, and restored macrophage density, area, and morphology, reversing the LPS-induced shift toward enlarged, sparsely distributed macrophages. Paired skull-cortex transcriptomics revealed opposite tissue responses under basal conditions: 50 mW IR enhanced metabolic and proliferative programs in the skull, consistent with its established role in bone marrow metabolism and hematopoiesis [38, 39], thereby supporting skull marrow as a primary locus of IR. In contrast, IR suppressed biosynthetic and mitochondrial pathways in the cortex. This divergence from previous reports of direct neuronal photostimulation [21, 40–42] suggests that cortical changes do not arise from direct IR effects but instead reflect secondary adaptations mediated through the SBM-DM-B Axis. Finally, in SAE mice, paired transcriptomic comparisons demonstrated concordant suppression of scavenger receptor uptake and IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling in both skull and cortex, indicating reduced phagocytosis and inflammatory signaling. Together, these results highlight SBM-targeted photobiomodulation as a safe, precise, and effective approach to reprogram skull-meningeal immunity and indirectly protect the brain against systemic inflammation.

We systematically evaluated the optical power, light penetration, and thermal effects of 808 nm IR across a power gradient (10–500 mW). Ex vivo measurements revealed progressive attenuation of light through scalp, skull, and cortex, which was further confirmed by a CT-based forward model that incorporated in vivo constituents, such as blood and cerebrospinal fluid, thereby compensating for fluid absence in ex vivo preparations. This complementary ex vivo-in vivo approach demonstrated the spatial confinement of the 808 nm IR and enabled a reliable estimation of brain-surface exposure. At 50 mW, the optical power reaching the cortex is ≤ 2 mW/cm², corresponding to a cumulative fluence of 4.8 J/cm². According to prior in vitro reports, such a low dose is insufficient to elicit microglial or neuronal responses and is generally regarded as an ineffective or safety control condition [37, 43, 44]. Collectively, these findings establish a robust biophysical basis for interpreting downstream immunological and neuronal responses under SBM-restricted IR stimulation.

Beyond the optical considerations, we further assessed the thermal safety profile of 808 nm IR.Our findings demonstrate that 50 mW IR photostimulation represents a safe operating dose, avoiding thermal injury while preserving stress-protein homeostasis. At this power, scalp temperature remained below 26 °C, a range known to sustain normal metabolism, behavior, and immune function in mice [45, 46]. Correspondingly, HSP70/HSP90 expression in cortex and hippocampus showed baseline laminar patterns without aberrant activation. By contrast, 100–500 mW induced HSP70 upregulation and nuclear translocation, indicating stress responses. Thus, 50 mW provides safe tissue penetration without proteostatic disruption, defining an optimal window for IR photobiomodulation that minimizes thermal burden while enabling effective photobiomodulation.

At 50 mW, the optical power is insufficient to directly impact on neurons or glial cells, as confirmed by unchanged microglial and DM macrophage morphology between PBSIR and PBS groups. Although a statistical increase in neuronal density was observed in cortical layer I on the IR-treated side of 50 mW compared with WT and 300 mW, the sparse cellular distribution in this molecular layer makes such measurements highly sensitive to sectioning, ROI definition, and area normalization. We therefore consider this change as methodological variability rather than a genuine biological effect. By contrast, Immunofluorescence showed that 50 mW IR modulates the immune state of the SBM, with LPSIR mice exhibiting increased CD206 + and reduced iNOS + areas compared to LPS.

Given the close anatomical and immunological connection among the SBM, DM, and brain tissue [6–10, 20], we investigated whether local photobiomodulation of the SBM with 50 mW IR could indirectly modulate brain immunity, neuronal integrity, and function in SAE mice. Consistent with previous studies [26], LPS injection increased hypertrophic and decreased ameboid microglia, whereas IR further promoted hypertrophic while reducing ramified microglia in the cortex, indicating mild immune modulation without excessive activation. No significant hippocampal changes were observed, supporting the concept of an outside-in regulatory axis (SBM → DM → cortex). H&E and Nissl staining showed that LPS induced significant neuron loss and structural disruption, leading to motor and cognitive dysfunction. 50 mW IR effectively alleviated neuronal damage, and improved motor deficits, but failed to rescue cognitive impairments, suggesting stronger protection for cortical than hippocampal function.

The dissociation between motor recovery and persistent cognitive impairment may result from the spatially restricted effect of SBM-mediated photostimulation. The cortical surface, adjacent to the dura, receives stronger immunometabolic modulation via the SBM-DM interface, whereas the deeper hippocampus experiences weaker immune signaling. Consequently, cortical microglia and neurons show structural restoration supporting motor coordination, while hippocampal microglia remain in an inflammatory state. IR-induced restoration of cortical lamination and neuronal survival may further reestablish layer-specific corticospinal and corticostriatal circuits that support motor control [47–51], whereas cognition relies on hippocampal-prefrontal connectivity, synaptic plasticity, and network oscillations that are probably less responsive to such superficial modulation [52, 53].

To elucidate the role of the SBM-DM-B Axis in the indirect regulation of neuroimmunity and neuronal protection through photostimulation of the SBM with 50 mW IR, we examined DM immune responses. Through the measurement of mLV diameters, we found that LPS induced dilation of mLVs, which was further augmented by IR treatment. LPS also drove a shift of dural macrophages toward enlarged, sparsely distributed amoeboid forms with reduced density, whereas IR reversed these changes by suppressing amoeboid and hypertrophic cells, enriching ramified and rod-like phenotypes, and restoring macrophage density. Moreover, although both dural macrophages and microglia could be classified into four morphological types based on clustering and skeletal features, the specific morphological characteristics differed; for instance, rod-like dural macrophages displayed distinct branch complexity and soma size compared to microglia, highlighting region-specific morphological benchmarks.

Several studies have suggested that transcranial IR with photobiomodulatory effects directly on brain tissue is safe [14–22, 24]; however, we found that 500 mW IR caused significant thermal damage to the scalp, skull, and brain. At 100–300 mW, H&E and Nissl staining showed no histological damage, but HSP70 immunofluorescence revealed dose-dependent stress responses: mild upregulation with partial nuclear translocation at 100 mW (peak temperature 27.3 °C) and stronger induction across cortical layers and clear nuclear translocation at 300 mW (peak temperature 41.4 °C). At 500 mW (peak temperature 48.2 °C), the response shifted, with robust HSP70 accumulation in superficial layers and marked HSP90 cytoplasmic upregulation in deeper layers, accompanied by near-complete loss of HSP70. We did not further investigate the mechanisms underlying these changes, but these findings suggest that IR exposure between 100 and 500 mW triggers distinct temperature-dependent stress responses. Given that sunlight-induced migraine is characterized by acute headache attacks triggered by bright sunlight exposure and often exacerbated in hot weather [54–56], such temperature-dependent HSP-mediated stress responses may represent a mechanistic link between light exposure, cortical stress signaling, and light-triggered headache, warranting further investigation.

In summary, we demonstrate that 50 mW IR photostimulation confined to the SBM provides safe and effective neuroprotection by alleviating inflammation, improving motor function, restoring body weight, and sparing the brain parenchyma from direct photothermal stress. In addition to enhancing skull marrow immune and metabolic programs while reducing cortical metabolic burden, this approach suggests a shift of immune regulation hub from the central nervous system to the periphery. Accordingly, SBM-restricted photobiomodulation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy not only for SAE but also for acute brain injuries, including subarachnoid and intracerebral hemorrhage, where reducing cerebral metabolism and intracranial stress is critical.

From a translational perspective, the human skull is markedly thicker (4–10 mm vs. 0.1–0.7 mm in mice) [57–59] and exhibits greater optical scattering and absorption [60–62], thereby confining IR effects predominantly to the calvarial and marrow compartments. Moreover, the anatomically conserved SBM-DM-B Axis across species supports translational plausibility [6, 63, 64]. Ex vivo optical measurements on human skull specimens can establish baseline estimates of wavelength-dependent attenuation, while CT-based in silico simulations can model patient-specific light propagation. Together, this combined experimental-computational framework provides a quantitative basis for optimizing IR dosimetry and safety, supporting personalized clinical translation.

This study has several limitations. The proposed SBM-DM-B Axis was inferred from morphological, transcriptomic, and behavioral correlations rather than direct causal evidence, such as selective blockade of dural lymphatics, pharmacological inhibition, or targeted cellular manipulation. The paired skull-cortex transcriptomic analysis was limited by a small sample size and bulk-tissue resolution, preventing precise identification of cell-type-specific responses and inter-tissue communication. Future studies employing integrative multi-omics approaches, including single-cell or spatial transcriptomics combined with metabolomics, may better elucidate the immune-metabolic coupling underlying photostimulation. In addition, functional assessments using in vivo electrophysiology and two-photon imaging are warranted to visualize how SBM-confined photostimulation dynamically modulates cortical neuronal activity and immune-neural interactions.

Conclusions

In this study, we propose a IR photobiomodulation approach confined to the SBM to indirectly regulate neuroimmunity, without causing thermal or optical damage to brain parenchyma. In SAE mice, SBM-targeted IR alleviated inflammation, restored body weight, preserved neuronal integrity, and improved motor function through indirect modulation of cortical microglia and dural immune cells. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed tissue-specific effects-enhancing immune and metabolic programs in the skull while reducing cortical metabolic burden-supporting an SBM-DM-B regulatory axis.

Collectively, these findings establish SBM-restricted photobiomodulation as a safe and effective strategy to reprogram skull-meningeal immunity and indirectly protect the brain, with potential therapeutic applications not only in SAE but also in acute brain injuries where reducing cerebral metabolism and intracranial stress is critical. This work highlights the SBM-DM-B Axis as a novel pathway for CNS immune regulation and a promising target for non-invasive interventions.
