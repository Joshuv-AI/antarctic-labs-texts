Comparing Fibula and Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flaps: A 20‐Year Retrospective Review
Authors: Erich A Mildner, J Benjamin Gordon, Kyle S Hrabovsky, Samuel B Craig, Kenneth R Feehs, Brianna R Maniscalco, Gregory B Russell, James D Browne, Hafiz S Patwa, Joshua D Waltonen, Christopher A Sullivan, Allison A Slijepcevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)Fibula free flaps and osteocutaneous radial forearm flaps (OCRFFFs) may be used to reconstruct complex head and neck defects following cancer ablation and trauma. This study aimed to compare the reconstructive outcomes of defects rehabilitated with fibula or OCRFFFs.MethodsRetrospective chart review completed over 20 years of adult patients greater than 18 years of age undergoing reconstruction of head and neck defects with fibula or OCRFFFs.ResultsTotal patients: 52. Reconstruction type: 26/52 (50%) fibula, 26/52 (50%) OCRFFF. Mean surgical time: 697 min fibula, 576 min OCRFFF (p = 0.002). Charlson Comorbidity Index: 4.6 fibula, 4.7 OCRFFF (p = 0.43). There was no significant difference in flap survival rate. Survival rates of both fibula and OCRFFFs: 23/26 (88%) survived, 2/26 (8%) total loss, 1/26 (4%) partial flap loss (p > 0.99). There was no significant difference in post‐operative complications between flap types. Post‐operative complications: 10/26 (38%) fibula, 9/26 (35%) OCRFFF (p > 0.99). There was no significant difference in post‐operative infection rate. Post‐operative infection rate: 7/26 (27%) fibula, 7/26 (27%) OCRFFF (p > 0.99). There was no significant difference in post‐operative length of stay. Post‐operative length of stay: 11.6 days fibula, 14.0 days OCRFFF (p = 0.26).ConclusionOCRFFFs provide reconstruction of composite defects with significantly shorter operative times compared to fibula flaps. Post‐operative flap survival and complication rates were similar in both fibula and OCRFFF reconstructions. Although OCRFFFs may not undergo dental implantation as the bone stock is thin, the flap may optimally reconstruct composite defects in patients unable to undergo prolonged procedures.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: bone and soft tissue reconstruction, fibula free flap, free flap reconstruction, head and neck reconstruction, osteocutaneous radial forearm free flap

Introduction

Composite mandibular and midface defects caused by trauma or cancer ablation may lead to extensive bone and soft tissue deficits necessitating free flap reconstruction. The goals of reconstruction are to restore aesthetic form and function of the oral cavity and oropharynx to allow oral intake [1]. Facial rehabilitation may be optimally completed with vascularized free flaps [2]. Free flaps are the gold standard reconstructive method to treat bone and soft tissue defects as they promote wound healing in compromised recipient beds and allow for osteointegration of the flap bony tissue [2, 3].

The osteocutaneous radial forearm free flap (OCRFFF) is performed by removing a partial thickness segment of the radius distal to the insertion of the pronator teres while leaving at least 2 cm of the distal bone for plating and screw fixation following graft removal. OCRFFFs have gained popularity for their versatility, long vascular pedicle, ease of skin paddle manipulation, and the ability to harvest a sensory nerve for flap innervation [3].

Fibula free flaps involve removal of the full central segment of the fibula bone to allow access to the full vascular pedicle. The fibula provides at least 25 cm of bone to reconstruct extensive osseous defects. Additionally, the vascular pedicle often shows substantial length in diameter of the flap vessels to ease microvascular anastomosis [4]. Fibula flaps provide adequate bone stock to allow dental implantation [1]. However, the harvest of fibular free flaps is technically challenging, necessitating retraction of thick lower leg musculature and dissection of sometimes friable lower extremity vasculature.

Our study aims to compare the outcomes of fibula flaps and OCRFFFs in the reconstruction of composite head and neck defects. To our knowledge, there is a deficit in the current literature comparing the reconstructive capabilities of each flap type and post‐operative wound complications.

Materials and Methods

Our study is a retrospective review of patients 18 years old or greater who underwent fibula flap or OCRFFF reconstruction of head and neck defects at Atrium Health Wake Forest Baptist Hospital from 2018 to 2025. Institutional board approval was obtained with IRB#00088301.

Patients were identified using CPT codes and patient charts were reviewed for having undergone osteocutaneous reconstructions for head and neck defects. Patient charts were analyzed for outcomes of interest including comorbidity scores by the Charlson Comorbidity Index and intraoperative statistics, including operative time and postoperative reconstructive outcomes. Independent Tests were used to assess the strength of the association between reconstruction groups and independent measures; non‐parametric approaches were consistent with the findings observed using t‐tests. 95% confidence intervals around the observed differences were calculated. Fisher's Exact Tests were used for categorical measures, with 95% exact binomial confidence intervals calculated. A p‐value of 0.05 was assumed to be statistically significant. SAS (version 9.4, Cary, NC, USA) was used for all analyses.

Results

Demographics

52 patients underwent osteocutaneous reconstruction. 26/52 (50%) underwent fibula reconstruction, and 26/52 (50%) underwent osteocutaneous radial forearm (OCRFFF) reconstruction. The average age of patients undergoing OCRFFF reconstruction was 64 years old. The average age of patients undergoing fibula reconstruction was 61 years old.

Surgical Indication

The surgical indication included both cancer and cancer‐related issues. In the FFF group: 23/26 (88%) had a squamous cell carcinoma, 1/26 (4%) had osteoradionecrosis of the mandible, 1/26 (4%) had a mucoepidermoid carcinoma.

In the OCRFFF group: 22/26 (85%) had a squamous cell carcinoma, 2/26 (8%) had osteoradionecrosis of the mandible, and 2/26 (8%) had ameloblastoma.

Surgical Defect Location

The surgical defect location in the FFF group: 16/26 (62%) involved the mandible, 6/26 (23%) were composite or involved more than one location, 2/26 (8%) involved the floor of the mouth, and 2/26 (8%) involved the buccal mucosa.

In the OCRFFF group: 12/26 (42%) involved the mandible, 9/26 (35%) were composite, 4/26 (15%) involved the floor of the mouth, and 1/26 (3%) involved the buccal mucosa (Table 1).

Charlson Comorbidity Index

The Charlson Comorbidity score takes a patient's age and prior past medical history to calculate their estimated 10‐year survival rate. A score of 7 or higher results in an estimated 10‐year survival rate of 0%. This was a patient characteristic that may have differed between groups before surgery. The average Charlson Comorbidity score for patients undergoing fibula free flap reconstruction was 4.6 (standard error of mean = 0.4). The average Charlson Comorbidity score for patients undergoing OCRFFF was 4.7 (standard error of mean = 0.4). There was no statistical significance between the Charlson Comorbidity score for patients undergoing fibula versus OCRFFF reconstruction (estimated difference −0.1 (95% confidence interval of −1.2, 0.9 units), p = 0.78) (Figure 1).

Operative Time

The average operative time for patients treated with fibula flap reconstruction was 697 min (standard error of mean = 28). The average operative time for patients undergoing OCRFFF reconstruction was 576 min (standard error of mean = 16) (estimated difference of 122 (56, 187), p = 0.0004) (Figure 2).

Operative time. A bar graph comparing operative time between OCRFFFs (n = 26) and fibula flaps (n = 26) (95% CI: 56 to 187), p = 0.0004. Mean ±1 standard error. OCRFFF = Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flap.

Flap Survival Rate

There was no statistical significance in flap survival rate between the two reconstruction methods (p > 0.99) (Figure 3). Survival rates in fibula flaps: 23/26 (88%) survived, 2/26 (8%) total loss, and 1/26 (4%) partial flap loss. Survival rates in OCRFFF: 21/24 (88%) survived, 2/24 (8%) were total loss, 1/24 (4%) partial loss. The 95% confidence interval around the survival rate is (−20%, 22%). The results are unchanged if the data are analyzed as a time‐to‐event analysis.

Flap survival rate. A bar graph comparing Flap Survival Outcomes grouped into three categories (Survived, Total Loss, Partial Loss). OCRFFF = Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flap.

Post‐Operative Complications

There was no significant difference in post‐operative complications between flap types. We have separated our post‐operative complications into surgical and medical with major and minor complications in each. Major surgical complications included fistula, flap necrosis, and thrombectomy. Minor surgical complications included post‐operative wound dehiscence and post‐operative wound infection. Major medical complications included post‐operative sepsis, post‐operative death, post‐operative cardiac complications, and readmission within 30 days. Minor medical complications included post‐operative urinary tract infection, pulmonary complications, and alcohol withdrawal. Post‐operative complications: 10/26 (38%) fibula flaps, 9/26 (35%) OCRFFFs (difference of 3%, 95% CI (−22, 31%), p > 0.99) (Figure 4). All patients undergoing OCRFFFs had prophylactic plating of the radius bone at the time of flap harvest. Of this patient population, none demonstrated pathologic radius fracture in the post‐operative period.

Post‐operative complications. A bar graph comparing Percentage of Post‐Operative Complications between OCRFFFs (n = 26) and fibula flaps (n = 26). There was a difference of 3% (95% CI: −22% to 31%), p > 0.99. Mean ±1 standard error. OCRFFF = Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flap.

Post‐Operative Wound Infections

There was no significant difference in post‐operative infection rate between the two reconstruction methods. All surgical site infections occurred within 30 days of surgery. Post‐operative infection rate was 7/26 (27%) in fibula flaps, and 7/26 (27%) in OCRFFFs (p > 0.99) (Figure 5).

Post‐operative wound infection. A bar graph comparing post‐operative wound infection percentages between OCRFFFs and fibula flaps, p > 0.99. OCRFFF = Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flap.

Post‐Operative Length of Stay

The average length of stay from the time of reconstruction to discharge was 14.0 (standard deviation = 8.8) for patients undergoing OCRFFF reconstruction and 11.6 (standard deviation = 5.6) days for patients undergoing fibula flap reconstruction (estimated difference −2.3 (95% confidence interval of −6.4, 1.7 units), p = 0.26) (Figure 6).

Average post‐operative length of stay. A bar graph comparing average post‐operative length of stay between OCRFFFs and fibula flaps. Estimated difference of −2.3 days (95% CI: −6.4 to 1.7), p = 0.26. Mean ±1 standard error. OCRFFF = Osteocutaneous Radial Forearm Free Flap.

Discussion

Fibula flaps have traditionally been considered the gold standard in mandibular and midface reconstruction due to pedicle length, bone stock, and ability for two team flap harvest [1]. In contrast, OCRFFFs were pioneered in the 1970s as an alternative to fibula flaps. However, in the 1990s, OCRFFFs were less commonly used given the association with increased flap failure rates and pathologic fractures of the radius [5, 6]. While OCRFFFs were previously considered a less effective method of composite tissue reconstruction due to the limited bone thickness of the radius, over time, reconstructions have been completed successfully. Additionally, OCRFFFs demonstrated limited post‐operative comorbidities compared to fibula flaps with the introduction of prophylactic plating [5]. As a result of this, OCRFFF reconstructions of bony head and neck defects have increased. Of note, scapula flaps were not reviewed in this study as they provide an excessive amount of additional soft tissue, involve a more intense dissection, and are comparable to fibula free flap.

The results of this study demonstrate that mandibular reconstruction procedures using fibula flaps were significantly longer than those using OCRFFFs, shown by the mean operative time 697 min versus 576 min. Fibula flaps lasted on average 19% longer than OCRFFF cases. Our data contrast with a study completed by Dunlap in 2023 of 94 patients who underwent maxillomandibular reconstructions of head and neck defects that showed no statistical significance in operative time between fibula flaps and OCRFFFs [7]. Similarly, in a separate study by Bollig in 2022 of 886 patients undergoing fibula and OCRFFF reconstructions, they found a mean operative time of 592 min for fibula flaps and 527 min for OCRFFFs [8]. While these studies demonstrate no increased procedural time in fibula and OCRFFFs, at our institution fibula flaps are associated with increased operative length. These findings likely reflect differing operative technique and practice standards amongst different institutions. The difference in operative length found in our institution may likely reflect practice standards across the country. The dissection for the radial forearm free flap does not involve soft tissue retraction, the bone is more easily accessed in the forearm, and the bone stock is thinner in the forearm making it easier to cut, potentially resulting in decreased operative time. Our data suggest fibula flaps do take a significantly longer time to harvest and complete and should be used in healthy patients able to withstand prolonged operative length. Patients with higher comorbidity indices may be better candidates to undergo the OCRFFF. VSP is used on specific complex cases at our institution; however, it is not routinely used for cases with less complex anatomic defects or defects that are not easily predicted prior to surgery.

Our study showed that patients with moderate to high Charlson Comorbidity indices can undergo osteocutaneous flap reconstruction. In clinical practice, patients present with severe past medical histories and advanced age that may preclude the use of osteocutaneous flap reconstruction. Soft tissue flap reconstructions may be chosen for these patients to decrease operative length and prevent prolonged post‐operative length of stays. However, our data suggest OCRFFFs can be used to reconstruct composite facial defects with bone stock that provides optimal long term aesthetic form and oral function [9].

Flap survival was equal when comparing fibula flaps and OCRFFFs. The increased flap survival and reconstructive success seen in fibula flaps may be related to their increased use and making them routine in our practice. Given the equivalent reconstructive success provided by both flap types, the reconstructive method should be tailored to each patient. For patients with high frailty indices and low tolerance for prolonged procedures, the OCRFFF can provide bone reconstruction of mandible and midface defects to preserve facial form. However, the OCRFFF cannot undergo dental rehabilitation as the bone stock is too thin to hold osseointegrated implants. Healthy patients seeking dental rehabilitation may be offered fibula flap reconstruction for optimal repair of composite defects.

Our results demonstrate no difference in post‐operative complications including infection, wound dehiscence, or fistula when comparing fibula flaps and OCRFFFs. Additionally, our research demonstrates patients undergoing OCRFFFs showed no post‐operative pathologic fracture of the radius bone as all patients underwent prophylactic plating. Prior literature by Bollig in 2022 that included 886 patients undergoing head and neck reconstruction for cancer or trauma demonstrated an increased number of complications in fibula flap reconstructions [8]. The data in our study is unique as there was no difference in major complications in either reconstruction type. These findings suggest that the harvest and use of fibula free flaps have become more advanced and more routine in clinical practice. The preoperative work up for patients undergoing fibula flap reconstruction includes refined vascular imaging with CT angiograms that ensure fibula flaps have healthy vasculature prior to harvest. Additionally, clinicians at our institution screen patients for lower extremity immobility, neuropathy, and vasculopathy prior to suggesting fibula free flap reconstruction. These precautions promote the harvest of healthy fibula flaps with adequate vasculature for complex reconstructions, limiting post‐operative complications.

The results of our study show there was no statistically significant difference in post‐operative length of stay following fibula flap and OCRFFF reconstruction. Fibula free flap reconstruction is associated with increased donor site morbidity and limitations in post‐operative ambulation. Our data show patients with stable baseline health can undergo fibula flap reconstruction without significant morbidity or prolonged hospital lengths of stay.

One of the prominent reasons for selecting a fibula free flap over an OCRFFF is the ability to implant dental work into the thicker bone stock. OCRFFFs have thin bone and decreased reserve excluding the ability to undergo dental implantation. Dental rehabilitation is important for patients undergoing mandibulectomy as it restores mastication, articulation, and improves quality of life [10]. A study by Virgin in 2010 of 51 patients undergoing mandibulectomy and OCRFFF reconstruction showed dental implantation can be done in OCRFFFs supplemented with bone grafting [1]. In contrast, we recommend performing bone reconstructions with fibula flaps when able as this flap type provides adequate bone stock for dental rehabilitation and does not necessitate a second procedure for bone grafting. Otherwise healthy patients with no lower extremity mobility or vasculature limitations may undergo fibula free flap reconstruction for optimal long‐term mandible and dental rehabilitation. The need for dental implantations represents an important consideration when deciding which type of flap to use for reconstructive purposes.

This study contains several limitations. The sample size included in our study is limited as fibula and OCRFFFs represent two small populations of flap types used for composite bone reconstruction. A larger study group may have more clearly shown differences in operative length and subtleties of post‐operative outcomes. A post hoc power analysis was not performed as studies show that these calculations are not useful after a study is done and are logically invalid as well as practically misleading [11, 12]. In the future, a larger sample size would be sufficient to provide increased power, further substantiating our results. In addition, we followed our patients for post‐operative complications within 30 days. Furthermore, a future study of the donor site morbidity would allow comparisons between these two types of reconstructions. Future directions could also examine the rates of hardware exposure and removal through long‐term follow‐up. While we believe that this 30‐day period allowed us to capture most of the post‐operative complications, it is possible that some complications occurred outside of this window. Finally, our study is influenced by the clinical practices of the small number of surgeons contributing to the data set which likely influenced outcomes including operative time and patient selection for flap reconstruction.

Conclusion

Osteocutaneous radial forearm free flaps provide reconstruction of composite defects with significantly shorter operative times compared to fibula flaps. Post‐operative flap survival and complication rates were the same in both flap types. Although osteocutaneous forearm flaps may not undergo dental implantation, they may optimally reconstruct composite defects in patients unable to undergo excessively prolonged procedures.
