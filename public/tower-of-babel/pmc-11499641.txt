Response of growth and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of mulberry seedlings to waterlogging stress
Authors: Siyuan Li, Liangyi Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the adaptive mechanisms of mulberry (Morus alba) to waterlogged conditions, with a specific focus on the development of adventitious roots (ARs), alteration of growth strategies, and adjustment of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters. To achieve this goal, 4-year-old potted mulberry plants were selected for research, and a waterlogging simulation method was implemented. Four treatments were established to investigate the effects of varying water conditions on leaf waterlogging damage, the number of ARs, plant height, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, and proton motive force (pmf) parameters in mulberry plants. These treatments included the control group (CK), shallow submerged group (SS), half-submerged group (HS) and deep submerged group (DS). Our results showed that (1) The number of ARs in each group increased with increasing waterlogging time. (2) Waterlogging stress inhibited the height growth of mulberry, and the changes in plant height in the HS and DS groups were significantly lower than those in the CK and SS groups. (3) The maximum photochemical quantum yield (Fv/Fm) in the HS and DS groups decreased significantly under waterlogging stress. The nonphotochemical quenching (NPQt) of mulberry leaves in the submergence group increased significantly in the early stage of waterlogging stress, and the NPQt in the submergence group increased continuously with increasing waterlogging time. (4) Thylakoid conductivity to protons (gH+) in the leaves of mulberry decreased significantly under waterlogging stress, whereas the steady-state rate of proton flux (vH+) and total electrochromic shift (ECSt) increased significantly. The morphological, physiological, and ecological responses of mulberry plants to waterlogging stress include the timely generation of ARs at the stem base, the adjustment of plant growth strategies, and the repair of photosynthetic response centers in leaves through heat dissipation and thylakoid acidification mechanisms.Keywords: Waterlogging stress, Mulberry leaves, Adventitious roots, Chlorophyll fluorescence characteristicSubject terms: Plant physiology, Plant stress responses

Introduction

Water is an essential element for plant survival, but excess water has adverse effects on plant survival. In recent decades, the global waterlogging crisis caused by climate change has increased dramatically1,2. Waterlogging stress not only affects plant growth but also causes soil and water loss in ecologically fragile areas and reduces the stability of the ecosystem3. Waterlogging stress also affects the agricultural system and terminates the agricultural production process, leading to crop damage and reduced production and posing great hidden dangers to social food security4. Waterlogging-tolerant plants play important roles in maintaining biodiversity and reducing soil and water loss in similar areas. Therefore, studying the response mechanism of waterlogging-tolerant plants to waterlogging stress has become a research hotspot in ecology and other fields5.

The direct effect of waterlogging stress on plants is anoxic, and studies have shown that the diffusion rate of oxygen in water is approximately 10,000 times slower than that in the air6. A lack of O2 in the soil enhances anaerobic respiration and stunts plant growth7,8. Plants have also evolved two different growth strategies to cope with waterlogging stress9. One is the tolerant growth strategy to prolong the underwater survival time by maintaining minimum energy and carbon consumption (low-O2 quiescence strategy, LOQS). The second is the escape growth strategy, which allows the top leaves to obtain sufficient oxygen, light, and carbon dioxide through rapid elongation of the stem (low-O2 escape strategy, LOES)9. Waterlogging stress can also rapidly deplete oxygen in plant roots. To improve the oxygen delivery of plant roots, many plants can produce adventitious roots (ARs) on their stems during waterlogging10. The inner part of ARs contains aerenchyma, which promotes the diffusion of oxygen from the aboveground part of the plant to the root, improves the ventilation conditions inside the plant, and enhances the waterlogging resistance of the plant11.

Under flooded conditions, plant morphology and photosynthetic reaction processes also produce an acute response. On the one hand, owing to the anoxic or anaerobic growth environment created by waterlogging, mineral transport in plant roots is inhibited, resulting in the inability of the aboveground parts to absorb adequate nutrients and leaf wilting12. On the other hand, the photosynthetic reaction centers of plants can reduce or prevent the damage of excess light energy caused by waterlogging stress to photosynthetic reaction centers by regulating the absorption, distribution, and transformation of light energy13. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters are closely related to plant photosynthesis, and their dynamic changes can reflect changes in photosystem II (PS II) function14. The maximum photochemical quantum yield (Fv/Fm) reflects the maximum photochemical quantum yield of PS II and is highly sensitive to abiotic stress. Under nonstress conditions, the Fv/Fm of plants is generally maintained at approximately 0.80, indicating stable characteristics. However, when plants are subjected to stress, the Fv/Fm significantly decreases15. For example, the Fv/Fm of Phyllostachys rivalis significantly decreased after 360 days of submersion, which indicates that long-term flooding affects the activity of PS II reaction centers in Phyllostachys rivalis leaves16. The number of available photochemical reaction centers (qL) reflects the proportion of light energy captured by chloroplasts used for linear photochemical electron flow, and the greater its value is, the faster the electron flow in plants17. Nonphotochemical quenching (NPQt) reflects the portion of light energy captured by chloroplasts that cannot be used for linear electron flow and is lost in the form of heat energy. The greater its value is, the stronger the heat dissipation effect, which is a self-protection mechanism of plants in the face of stress18. When plants experience waterlogging stress, the increase in excess solar energy and heat dissipation in the plant body leads to a decrease in qL and an increase in NPQt19. The proton motive force (pmf) plays a central role in photosynthesis and regulates electron flow20. At a given proton flux, the pmf will be determined by the conductivity of the thylakoid membrane to protons (gH+), which is also utilized as a measure of chloroplast ATP synthase activity. The smaller the value of gH+ is, the larger the steady state pmf21. The steady-state rate of proton flux (vH+) reflects the rate of proton translocation through the entire electron transfer chain because all protons exit the lumen through the same pathways, usually predominantly through ATP synthase22. Importantly, based on the light‒dark difference in the amplitude of the ECS signal, the total electrochromic shift (ECSt) can be used to estimate the size of the pmf23. However, the regulation of pmf in leaves under waterlogging stress is less well understood.

Mulberry (Morus alba) is a perennial deciduous tree belonging to Morus L. in Moraceae that is widely cultivated24. Mulberry can be used as fodder, has high nutritional and economic value, strong stress resistance, and certain out-of-season waterlogging tolerance, and it can be a priority tree species for biological management in littoral zones25. Previous studies have shown that the germination rate of mulberry plants above 170 m in the littoral zone of the Three Gorges Reservoir area reached 62.6% after 90 days of deep submergence, and mulberry plants below 165 m in altitude can still germinate normally after 214 days of ultralong waterlogging26. Early studies on mulberry waterlogging stress focused mainly on how mulberry grows and germinates under waterlogging stress26. With further research, researchers have begun to pay attention to physiological and ecological factors. Waterlogging stress significantly increased peroxidase activity in mulberry plants24. The chlorophyll content of mulberry plants decreased significantly after 10 days of waterlogging25. Under waterlogging stress, the plasma membrane of mulberry seedling roots was damaged, and plasma membrane permeability increased27. The net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate of mulberry plants in the root submerged and deep submerged treatment groups decreased with increasing submerged time28. Only a few scholars have studied how waterlogging stress affects the chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics of mulberry plants29, and no research has been conducted on the variation in the pmf parameters of mulberry plants under waterlogging conditions.

This study used potted 4-year-old mulberry plants as test materials. By simulating waterlogging stress indoors, the effects of different waterlogging depths and different waterlogging durations on leaf waterlogging damage, plant height, the number of ARs, chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics, and thylakoid proton dynamics of mulberry plants were investigated to elucidate the response mechanism of forage mulberry plants to waterlogging stress from a physiological perspective.

Effects of waterlogging stress on the chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of mulberry plants

The contents of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, such as Fv/Fm, qL and NPQt, are significantly affected by the depth and duration of waterlogging and their interaction (Table 2). At 60 days, the Fv/Fm at all waterlogging severities reached a minimum, whereas the NPQt reached a maximum. Compared with those in CK, the Fv/Fm in SS, HS, and DS decreased by 11.71%, 28.20% and 35.82%, respectively (Fig. 3A). Moreover, NPQt in the SS, HS and DS treatments increased by 112.90%, 221.53% and 335.17%, respectively (Fig. 3C). qL presents a completely different trend of change. After 10 days, the qL in both the SS and DS reached a maximum. Compared with those in the control treatment, the qL values in the SS and DS treatments increased by 45.75% and 48.03%, respectively. As the waterlogging duration increased, the qL under all waterlogging severities continued to decrease. At 60 days, the qL in the SS, HS, and DS treatments increased by 22.78%, 9.58% and 8.54%, respectively, compared with that in the CK treatment (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

As an abiotic stress, waterlogging stress harms plants mainly due to the relatively low oxygen levels that limit the aerobic respiration of plants30;31. Waterlogging stress inhibits leaf formation and leaf expansion, leading to premature leaf senescence and abscission32. Under waterlogging stress, mulberry seedlings in the SS and HS groups began to develop yellow leaves after 20 days of waterlogging, and many healthy and intact leaves were still preserved at the end of summer waterlogging. After 10 days of waterlogging, the mulberry seedlings in the DS group began to exhibit yellow leaves. The effects of waterlogging stress on mulberry plants subjected to waterlogging in the four treatment groups decreased in the following order: DS group > HS group > SS group > CK group. This is basically consistent with the result that 1-year-old mulberry leaves showed mild symptoms after long-term root waterlogging stress but showed obvious symptoms under severe waterlogging stress on the 5th day of submergence28. At the same time, it is similar to the research results that only a few leaves of three-year-old mulberry seedlings began to curl and wilt slightly after 15–20 days of mild waterlogging, and after 10 days of severe waterlogging, yellow brown spots appear on the leaves of mulberry seedlings, and with the increase of waterlogging time, the wilting, yellowing, and shedding of leaves become increasingly severe29. This indicates that mulberry plants were adaptable to shallow waterlogging but could not tolerate prolonged high-water-level waterlogging.

Under waterlogging conditions, the root is the first organ in plants to face a decrease in oxygen partial pressure. Compared with parts such as the stem, the root undergoes significant surface changes33,34. The production of ARs during waterlogging is one of the landmark responses of flood-tolerant woody plants to waterlogging35. The formation of ARs maintains the growth of plants by providing them with water, minerals, and hormones, increasing their resistance to waterlogging36. The results showed that ARs were generated at the stem base of mulberry seedlings in each flooded group after 10 days of waterlogging, and with the extension of waterlogging, the number of ARs generated by each treatment group also increased, which was consistent with the result that Solanum dulcamara37 increased the number of ARs after 7 days of waterlogging, and Erythrina crista38 generated a large number of ARs at the stem base after 10 days of waterlogging. After 60 days of waterlogging, the number of ARs in HS was greater than that in DS, possibly because the ARs generated by HS can contact the water surface layer containing more oxygen, thus alleviating hypoxia, while the adventitious roots of DS cannot meet the needs of vigorous growth and metabolism of ARs due to long-term waterlogging stress and eventually cause severe damage to roots39. This finding is similar to the finding that a large number of ARs were generated by Triarhena lularioriparia at a flooding depth of 10 cm, but only a small number of adventitious roots were generated at a flooding depth of 50 cm40.

The growth morphology index can comprehensively reflect the degree of waterlogging stress and resistance ability of plants, which is the result of long-term adaptation to the environment. The morphological characteristics of plants under different environmental conditions will be continuously adjusted41. This study revealed that after 10 days of waterlogging, the height of mulberry plants in each flooded group increased to varying degrees, indicating that mulberry plants can maintain their normal growth under short-term waterlogging stress. This is the result of mulberry seedlings gradually acclimating to waterlogging stress environments. This is consistent with the research results that short-term waterlogging did not have a significant impact on the growth of poplar trees, and the flooded group showed a certain increase in plant height and good leaf morphology42. After 40 days of waterlogging, the change in plant height in the SS, HS, and DS treatments significantly decreased by 4.09%, 53.57% and 51.73%, respectively, compared with that in the CK treatment. Thereafter, the change in plant height of mulberry seedlings in the flooded group decreased with increasing time. This is similar to the research results that the plant height of poplar seedlings was significantly inhibited under waterlogging stress and the relative growth rate of plant height under shallow and deep waterlogging groups was lower than that of the control group43, which indicates that after 40 days of waterlogging, mulberry seedlings presented a very strong stress and unable to respond not by elongating nor changing their metabolism.

The ability of plants to absorb, transmit, and convert light energy is closely related to the strength of photosynthesis, including chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm, qL, NPQt). The experimental results indicate that the environment under waterlogging stress has an impact on the photochemical activity of mulberry seedling leaves44.

Fv/Fm indicates the maximum photochemical quantum yield of PS II and is extremely sensitive to the response to abiotic stress, and the Fv/Fm of plants under nonstress conditions is generally maintained at 0.80, indicating stability, but this parameter decreases significantly under stress conditions15. In the present study, it was found that the Fv/Fm of mulberry leaves in the flooded group decreased after 10 days of waterlogging. This is similar to the findings in Trollius chinensis that under waterlogging stress, the decrease in Fv/Fm already occurred on the first day after waterlogging14, suggesting this parameter is sensitive to the waterlogging stress and be used as an indicator. However, although the Fv/Fm of the SS group decreased during waterlogging, the decrease was more moderate. The Fv/Fm of the HS and DS groups significantly decreased under waterlogging stress and continued to decrease with increasing waterlogging time. This indicates that the photo response centers of mulberry seedlings in the SS group were only mildly damaged, while prolonged waterlogging caused a significant decrease in the photo response centers of mulberry seedlings in the HS and DS groups, which is similar to the findings of Liu et al.45, where the root-flooded and semi flooded groups showed a gradual decline after being subjected to waterlogging, while Cyperus rotundus in the fully flooded group showed a significant decline. This study also revealed that the Fv/Fm of mulberry seedling leaves was negatively correlated with waterlogging intensity and strongly negatively correlated with prolonged submergence stress, suggesting that the reduced waterlogging tolerance of mulberry plants under prolonged submergence stress was associated with a decrease in PS II photochemical activity.

Chlorophyll fluorescence bursts can be divided into qL and NPQt. qL is related to the photochemical reaction of PS II and reflects the redox state of the PS II primary electron acceptor QA, which can measure the electron transfer rate and the degree of opening of the PS II reaction center16,46. The results of this study showed that the qL of mulberry seedling leaves in the waterlogging group increased at the beginning of waterlogging stress. This is consistent with the research findings by Li et al.16, that the qL value of Phyllostachys rivalis leaves increased during the initial stage of waterlogging stress, which indicates that initial waterlogging facilitated the opening of the PS II reaction center and the electron transfer capacity of mulberry seedling leaves, which improved the light energy utilization efficiency. In the study of Niu et al.47, the qL value of Chionanthus retusus leaves decreased with the increase of waterlogging time, which was similar to the trend of qL variation in this study. The qL of SS and DS during waterlogging decreased significantly at 30 days of waterlogging, indicating that waterlogging caused the amount of light energy absorbed by mulberry leaves to be partially reduced due to photochemical reaction reduction, which resulted in excess light energy accumulating in PS II reaction centers and reduced photochemical activity48. The qL of mulberry seedling leaves in all the waterlogging groups showed a relatively smooth decreasing trend at the late stage of waterlogging, and the qL in the HS and DS groups was always significantly lower than that of the plants in the SS group, which indicates that the increase in waterlogging depth and the prolongation of waterlogging time both reduced the opening of the PS II reaction centre in mulberry seedling leaves. NPQt refers to the amount of light energy absorbed by PS II released in the form of heat dissipation, which is a self-protective mechanism of the plant photosynthetic reaction center49. In this study, we found that during the early stage of waterlogging stress, the NPQt of mulberry seedling leaves in all the waterlogging groups significantly increased. This is consistent with the research findings by Zhang et al.50, that the NPQ of Physocarpus opulifolius significantly increased from 10 days of waterlogging stress, which indicates that under waterlogging conditions, mulberry trees mitigate the effects of waterlogging on the PS II system by increasing NPQt to quickly dissipate excess light energy. It is likely that submergence stress acidifies the thylakoid lumen of mulberry leaves and that PsbS proteins sense this process and allow the excess excitation energy to be safely dissipated as heat51. As the duration of waterlogging increased, NPQt continued to increase in all groups, and heat dissipation protection was enhanced. This finding is consistent with research results showing that the NPQ of Phyllostachys rivalis increased with increasing waterlogging depth and waterlogging duration, and the NPQ of Sorghum bicolor increased significantly after 12 days of waterlogging stress16;52. This study also revealed that the NPQt of mulberry seedling leaves under waterlogging stress was positively correlated with waterlogging intensity and reached a very significant positive correlation in the late waterlogging period, indicating that mulberry plants improve their defense against waterlogging stress by increasing heat dissipation under prolonged stress.

The electrochromic shift (ECS) signal reflects changes in the electric field on the thylakoid membrane, which in turn reflects the accumulation of photochemical proton dynamics in thylakoids and their subsequent utilization by ATP synthesis53. The dark interval relaxation kinetics (DIRK) of electrochromic band shift was found to be effective in evaluating pmf (parameter ECSt) and the proton conductivity of thylakoids (parameter gH+), and their product can be used as an estimate of proton flux (vH+)21,54. Previous studies have shown a close relationship between ECS parameter values and effective regulation of electron transport and photoprotection under stress55;56. Our results showed that at the beginning of the waterlogging stress experiment, gH+ remained stable in the flooded groups, indicating that photosynthesis in mulberry seedlings was not inhibited at this time. With prolonged waterlogging time, gH+ appeared to significantly decrease, and vH+ and ECSt appeared to significantly increase in the flooded group, which indicated that mulberry plants initiated the pmf negative feedback regulatory mechanism to reduce the damage caused by photoinhibition to PS I and PS II. Mulberry plants reduce proton efflux by decreasing chloroplast ATP synthase activity and accumulating large amounts of pmf under stress conditions. This response stimulates acidification of the thylakoid lumen, which reduces photoinhibition damage to the photosynthetic machinery by initiating the NPQ heat dissipation mechanism, a process that consumes pmf21,57. Qian et al.58 also made similar findings in their study on the effects of salt alkali stress on chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics of Lycium barbarum seedlings. The gH+ in the saline alkali treatment group showed a significant decrease after 40 days of saline alkali stress. The change in ECSt was the opposite, showing a significant upward trend after 40 days. This study also revealed that the gH+ of mulberry leaves was negatively correlated with waterlogging intensity and that the ECSt was positively correlated with waterlogging intensity under waterlogging stress. Under waterlogging stress, mulberry plants improve their defense ability against waterlogging stress by reducing ATP synthase activity and accumulating pmf, which is a physiological response of forage mulberry plants to waterlogging stress. In addition, because the acidification mechanism of thylakoid consumes a large amount of pmf, the ECSt of flooded mulberry seedling leaves decreased in the later waterlogging stage, but the difference was not significant.

Materials and methods

The experimental materials

The materials for the experiment were Shengsang No. 1 4-year-old mulberry plants (Morus alba) that were artificially planted in a 4-gallon flowerpot. The experimental site is located in the Beijing Forestry University Sanqingyuan Garden nursery (115°33E, 40°10 ′N). The nursery is located in the northwestern North China Plain and has a warm temperate semihumid semiarid monsoon climate, four distinct seasons, adequate light, an annual average temperature of 12.5°C, and an annual average precipitation of 628.9 mm. Mulberry seedlings grew normally for more than 2 years in the Sanqingyuan Garden nursery. The compaction degree of nutrient soil is 1.96 MPa; the total porosity is 36.2%; the volumetric weight is 1.584 g/cm3; the mass fractions of total potassium, phosphorus, and nitrogen are 1.63%, 0.071%, and 0.104%, respectively; and the soil pH is 5.5–6.5. To ensure the normal growth of the plants, the saplings were watered once every 5 days, with 1 L of water per pot each time.

Experimental design

On July 1, 2021, mulberry seedlings with good growth and similar growth were selected for the waterlogging stress experiment. The average plant height and average ground diameter of the seedlings were 109.5 cm and 21.45 mm, respectively, and waterlogging stress ended on August 31, 2021.

The plants were divided into a control group (CK) and subjected to three types of waterlogging intensity treatments: shallow submergence treatment (SS), where the waterlogging depth exceeded 2 cm above the soil height in the pot; half-submergence treatment (HS), where the depth of accumulated water exceeded the height of the soil in the basin by 11 cm; and deep submergence treatment (DS), where the depth of waterlogging exceeded the height of the soil in the basin by 36 cm. In each treatment group, 8 mulberry trees were planted, and the waterlogging conditions were controlled by plastic water tanks of the same size (tank size: length, 98 cm; width, 76 cm; height, 68 cm). Four pots of mulberry seedlings were placed in each water tank, and a total of six water tanks were used. The flooded group drew a water level mark in the water tank, and then the lost water was replenished at 17:00 every day to stabilize each treatment within the set water level range. The 8 mulberry plants in the CK group were not placed in the water tank but in the open space next to the water tank for full sunshine conservation and management. To ensure water quality and reduce errors, the water in the water tank was changed every 5 days after the experiment began, and the plants in the control group were watered with 1 L of water.

Indicator determination

Determination of plant height and adventitious roots

The plant height and number of adventitious roots (ARs) of the mulberry plants were measured after 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 days of waterlogging. The plant height is the distance from the top of the plant to the soil surface, measured with a tape measure (accurate to 0.1 cm), and was measured three times for each seedling height measurement. The formation of ARs at the base of the stem of mulberry plants was carefully observed, and the data were recorded.

Determination of leaf waterlogging damage

According to the method of Zhou et al.59, the classification of the degree of leaf waterlogging damage is divided into the following five grades: Grade 1, very few leaves experience leaf chlorosis; Grade 2, approximately 20% of the leaves experience leaf chlorosis, and the leaves lose water, wither and droop; Grade 3, approximately 40% of the leaves have withered; Grade 4, more than 60% of the leaves have withered or dropped; Grade 5, more than 80% of the leaves have withered, and almost all of the leaves have fallen. The leaf waterlogging damage status of all mulberry plants in each treatment group was determined at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 days, and the leaf waterlogging damage rate and leaf waterlogging damage index were calculated. The leaf waterlogging damage rate is the ratio of the number of waterlogging-affected plants to the total number of plants. The leaf waterlogging index is obtained by the following formula:

Leaf waterlogging index = ∑ (injury level * the number of plants with corresponding damage level)/(highest level of damage * the total number of plants).

Determination of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and pmf parameters

The chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and pmf parameters were measured from 7:00 to 9:00 a.m. after 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 days of waterlogging. Fluorescence-based photosynthetic parameters and electrochromic shift evaluation were conducted in light-adapted leaves using MultispeQ V 2.0 devices connected to the photosynQ platform. The photosystem II parameters, such as the maximum photochemical quantum yield (Fv/Fm), nonphotochemical quenching (NPQt) and number of available photochemical reaction centers (qL), were estimated from the fluorescence yield changes derived from pulse-amplitude modulation (PAM) fluorometry using a pulse orange LED (605 nm) with far-red light at a wavelength of 730 nm. The total electrochromic shift (ECSt) reflects the energy storage level of thylakoid proton dynamics (pmf) and the activity of chloroplast ATP synthase during light–dark transition (dark region relaxation kinetics, DIRK), as evaluated by the absorbance change caused by the 300 ms dark region at 525 nm53. The initial decay rate of the ECS during rapid light–dark transitions from steady-state conditions generates the parameter vH+(steady-state rate of proton flux)22. The thylakoid conductivity to protons (gH+) was determined by the DIRK of the ECS at 520 nm60 and was utilized as a measure of chloroplast synthase activity. Eight mulberry plants were placed in each treatment group for chlorophyll fluorescence measurements.

Data processing

SPSS 24.0 was used to perform two-way ANOVA to detect the interaction between waterlogging depth and duration, and one-way ANOVA was used to compare physiological characteristics between treatments at each time point. The results are expressed as the means ± SD, and the differences between treatments were determined via Tukey’s HSD test. Pearson correlations between physiological parameters were analyzed via Origin 2024 (OriginLab, Northampton, USA). Origin2024 software was also used for mapping analysis.
