Performance of Pediatric Early Warning Score and physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill patients in an Emergency Department in Tanzania
Authors: Irene A. Kindole, Humphrey G. Medarakini, Alphonce N. Simbila, Christine A Kindole, Peter P Kunambi, Evelyne G. Mapunda, Said S. Kilindimo, Ellen J. Weber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCritically ill pediatric patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) are highly susceptible to adverse events if timely interventions are not initiated. To predict such events, ED physicians may use the Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) and/or clinical gestalt. PEWS has been validated in predicting deterioration in both high and low-middle income countries, but it has not been compared to physician gestalt in either setting.MethodsThis was a prospective cohort study of children aged 28 days to 14 years who presented to the ED of a tertiary teaching hospital in Tanzania from 1st May to 30th June 2024 and who were triaged as “emergency” (highest acuity). Physicians’ estimates of the probability (0–100%) of an adverse event (admission to the high dependent unit, Intensive care unit, cardiac arrest or death) occurring during the patient’s ED stay were recorded for each patient on arrival. PEWS was calculated by the researchers from the child’s vital signs, and not disclosed to the physician. The discriminatory ability of PEWS and physician gestalt for the prediction of an adverse event in the ED was compared using DeLong’s test. Area under the curve (AUC) sensitivity, specificity, positive (PPV) and negative predictive (NPV) values were calculated, using a cut-off of ≥3 for PEWS and ≥70% for physician gestalt.ResultsOf the 250 pediatric patients enrolled, 48 had an adverse event. PEWS (AUC = 0.869, 95% CI 0.809 to 0.930), at a cut-off of ≥3 achieved a sensitivity of 75.0% and a specificity of 80.7%, PPV of 48.0%, and NPV of 93.1%. Physician gestalt (AUC = 0.964; 95% CI 0.936 to 0.992), at a cut-off of ≥70% achieved a sensitivity of 77.1% and a specificity of 95.5%, PPV of 80.4%, and NPV of 94.6%. The AUC of physician gestalt was significantly higher than that of PEWS (p = 0.005).DiscussionPhysician gestalt demonstrated superior performance to PEWS in this setting, supporting its use as a risk stratification tool in low-resource emergency settings. However, as this study was performed at a tertiary referral and training hospital, further studies across diverse emergency care settings are needed to determine the generalizability of these findings.

Introduction

At the Emergency Department (ED), early recognition of deterioration among pediatric critically ill patients remains a challenge due to the ability of children to compensate physiologically until sudden clinical deterioration occurs [1]. In EDs in low- and middle-income countries with a high volume of sick pediatric patients and limited access to advanced monitoring and diagnostic tests, risk stratification tools are essential to identify those critically ill pediatric patients likely to develop adverse events early before their occurrence.

The Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) is a standardized risk stratification tool that has been validated in the ED in both high income countries (HIC) [2,3] and in low and middle-income countries (LMIC)[4] to identify pediatric patients at risk for clinical deterioration, need for intensive care unit (ICU) admission, or death. The discriminatory ability of PEWS has generally been good ranging from 0.75 to 0.86 but the sensitivity and specificity vary depending on whether the threshold is one, two or three points [[5], [6], [7]]. Thus, the utility of PEWS requires evaluation in a specific clinical setting and with a pre-determined cut-off before its implementation.

Physician gestalt guides management decisions by integrating information obtained from the patient’s history and physical examination with prior clinical experience [8,9]. This approach relies on the physician’s ability to recognize patterns of illnesses, such as fever, hypoxia, tachypnea, and to differentiate severe from non-severe illnesses [10]. Few studies have been conducted on physician gestalt predicting deterioration in children in the ED. One study in a high-income country found gestalt had a fair ability (AUC 0.747) to predict a complicated course in children presenting to the ED with suspected community-acquired pneumonia [11]. A study conducted in Tanzania in children under 5 presenting to the ED demonstrated a similar discriminatory ability (AUC 0.79) in predicting 24-hour mortality [12].

A previous prospective study on critically ill adults patients in our ED compared physician gestalt with lactate and the shock index, demonstrating gestalt had a strong predictive performance for adverse events among critically ill adults’ patients [13]. However, the performance of PEWS in children in comparison to physician gestalt at ED has not been tested in either high- or low-income settings. Using a similar methodology to the one previously conducted in our ED in adults [13], we compared the performance of PEWS to physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill pediatric patients in a low resource ED. Methods

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study comparing the performance of the PEWS and physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill pediatric patients presenting to the Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) ED in Tanzania, a large urban national referral hospital. The ED at MNH is a public ED with comprehensive emergency care capabilities, including 24 h emergency services, the capability for patient monitoring, critical care interventions, and specialist emergency care coverage. It is also one of the main sites for the emergency medicine residency training program in the country. The MNH ED attends about 150 – 200 patients daily, among them approximately 25% are children, excluding neonates who present directly to the maternity unit. The children are seen in a separate pediatric ED wing adjacent to the main ED. The unit has the ability to provide continuous cardiopulmonary monitoring, critical procedures, invasive ventilation and also has a full range of point of care tests including ultrasound, chemistry, blood gas and portable x-ray. The hospital has a general pediatric ward, pediatric intensive care unit (ICU) and high dependency unit (HDU).

Participants

Patient enrollment occurred between 1st May 2024 and 30th June 2024. Inclusion criteria were pediatric patients aged 28 days to 14 years, who were triaged as ‘emergency’ (highest acuity) using the local 3-tier triage tool adopted from the Emergency Severity Index (ESI), with the ‘emergency’ category considered equivalent to ESI level 1 and 2 [14]. Patients were excluded from enrollment if no informed consent was provided by the caregiver/guardian, or were pulseless on arrival at the ED. Although the ED has standard protocols for critically ill pediatric patients, investigations and treatment were performed at the discretion of the treating physician. The PEWS is not routinely used at triage or by treating physicians in this ED.

Study procedures

Research assistants were present to enroll patients consecutively on both day and night shifts. Each patient was assessed for eligibility shortly after arrival in the resuscitation area. The caregiver/guardian was informed about the study and provided consent for enrollment. For each patient, a structured case report form was used. The Brighton Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) (Supplemental Table 1) was calculated from the patient’s vital parameters and recorded by the research assistant on the form [15,16]. Within 10 min of patient arrival in the resuscitation area, the research assistant asked the treating physician to estimate the probability (from 0 to 100%) that the patient would have an adverse event during their stay in the ED. Physicians were not explicitly informed of the PEWS at the time of recording their gestalt estimation. After obtaining the physician’s estimate, the research assistant recorded the physician’s age, level of training, and, if a resident, year in training on a standardized case report form.

Outcome data were collected prospectively by the research assistant during the patient’s ED stay using a list of predefined adverse events. Research assistants were not blinded to either of the index test results, as they were responsible for recording both PEWS and physician gestalt scores.

Ethical clearance was granted by MUHAS IRB (MUHAS-REC-05-2024-2350) and permission to collect data by the MNH training and research unit.

Outcomes

The index tests were PEWS and physician gestalt; the reference standard was the occurrence of adverse events in the ED, defined as any of the following: cardiac arrest at ED, death at ED or admission to high dependency unit (HDU) or intensive care unit (ICU).

Analyses

Sample size estimation was based on the sensitivity and specificity of PEWS using the formula described by Buderer [17]. A multicenter study to validate PEWS, found a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 60% [3]. Using these test characteristics, with a 95% CI and assuming a 10% loss to follow up, we determined that a minimum of 252 participants was required.

The statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package SPSS, version 23. Descriptive statistics for patient demographic and clinical variables, as well as the qualifications of the physician attending the patients, are presented as either median (IQR) as the data were not normally distributed or frequency and percentage as appropriate.

Patients were excluded from analysis if the PEWS or gestalt were not recorded. The discriminatory performance for the index tests was evaluated by comparing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROC) of the two tests using DeLong’s test, with significance set at p < 0.05. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were calculated for each test. For PEWS, a threshold of ≥3 was used based on prior literature. For physician gestalt, an optimum cut-off of ≥70% probability was selected a priori based on methods of the previous prospective ED study conducted in our ED [13].

Results

A total of 252 pediatric patients were triaged as ‘emergency’ during the study period. After exclusions, 250 (99.2%) were eligible and included in the analysis (Fig. 1). A total of 48 (19.2%) patients had one or more adverse events at the ED: 18 (7.2%) required admission to the high dependent unit (HDU), 26 (10.4%) required admission to Intensive care unit (ICU), 8 (3.2%) had cardiac arrest and 2 (0.8%) died following cardiac arrest at ED before disposition.Fig. 1Patient flow diagram.

Discussion

In this ED in a LMIC, the PEWS had an AUC of 0.869 (0.809–0.930), considered a good discriminatory ability, consistent with prior studies conducted in both high- and low-income country ED settings [2,[4], [5], [6]]. However, physician gestalt with an AUC of 0.964 (0.936–0.992), demonstrated an excellent discriminatory ability for predicting adverse events, suggesting that gestalt has an important role in determining which children are likely to deteriorate and require rapid intervention. These findings are consistent with our previous prospective study of critical ill adults’ patients, conducted in the same ED with an AUC of 0.877 (0.833–0.922) [13], suggesting that gestalt may play an important role in risk stratification across different age groups in resource limited ED settings. Nevertheless, it is important to note that clinicians working in high-acuity resuscitation settings may develop enhanced pattern recognition skills through repeated exposures to critically ill children, potential contributing to the gestalt performance observed in our cohort. Furthermore, gestalt maybe influenced by level of training and subjective interpretation, which may result in interobserver variability and reduced reproducibility across providers in clinical settings.

In this study, the performance of physician gestalt in predicting adverse events was superior to two prior studies, one conducted in a HIC and one in an LMIC [11,12]. A single center study in a HIC including children who underwent a chest x-ray for suspicion of community acquired pneumonia found that physician gestalt had an AUC of 0.747 for discriminating which children would develop severe complications [11]. A previous study conducted at Muhimibili hospital ED asked physicians to categorize all children under 5 presenting to the ED into one of four categories (healthy to severely ill) and found an AUC of 0.797 for the ability to predict 24-hour mortality [12]. The discriminatory ability may be lower than what was found in the current study for several reasons. First, both included all children (rather than those deemed critically ill), they chose in-hospital outcomes, rather than the more proximal ED outcomes we looked at, and in both studies, physician gestalt was expressed categorically, rather than using a continuous estimate as probability.

The performance of PEWS in our study was also somewhat better than in a prior multicenter study validating the ED-PEWS, a modified PEWS developed for use in ED settings that used previously collected data from Gambia, Tanzania and Suriname [4]. Here the AUC for “high urgency” (which varied between cohorts) ranged from 0.80 to 0.62. As with the previous studies on gestalt, this study included children of various levels of illness (not just critically ill), and the outcome of interest varied between cohorts, including in some cases need for IV medications, need for oxygen, admission and referral, as well as death on the day of arrival.

None of the prior studies compared PEWS with physician gestalt. In the current study, gestalt had both a higher sensitivity and specificity than PEWS at the selected thresholds. The relatively higher sensitivity indicates that a greater proportion of children who experienced adverse events were correctly identified as high risk, while the higher specificity reflects fewer false positive classifications among children who did not deteriorate. However, the sensitivity of physician gestalt was suboptimal at 77.1%, indicating that a substantial proportion of children who subsequently experienced adverse events were not initially identified as high risk. Likewise, PEWS demonstrated a slightly lower sensitivity of 75.0%, suggesting that neither tool alone was sufficiently sensitive to reliably exclude the possibility of deterioration among critically ill pediatric patients.

PEWS and physician gestalt demonstrated similarly high negative predictive values of 93.1% and 94.6% respectively, whereas positive predictive values were low, particularly for PEWS, 48%. These high NPVs reflect the relatively low proportion of children (19.2%) who had an adverse event. Consequently, both tools were better at identifying children unlikely to deteriorate than for definitively predicting which children would experience adverse events. These findings highlight the importance of interpreting predictive values within the context of event prevalence and support the use of both approaches as risk stratification tools rather that definitive predictors of deterioration.

Importantly, these results should not be interpreted as support for replacing structured early warning scores with clinical gestalt. While PEWS provides objective and reproducible risk stratification based on predefined physiological parameters, its clinical value extends beyond prediction alone. The structured calculation of PEWS is a concise assessment of vital signs and clinical status, prompting a clinician to consider the child’s illness severity and potential for deterioration. In addition, PEWS is useful to identify trends over time and support early recognition of clinical deterioration. Also, since it is standardized and objective, PEWS can be applied by healthcare providers with varying levels of experience and may empower junior staff to recognize deterioration and escalate care appropriately. Moreover, it creates a shared language with other providers communicating patient severity. In contrast physician gestalt is often implicit, may not be formally documented or uniformly applied across providers, thus reliance on gestalt alone may limit consistency in patient assessment particularly in settings with varying level of clinician’s experience.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its single-center design may limit generalizability to other EDs with different patient populations, resource availability or levels of clinician expertise. Although Muhimbili ED receives patients from all over Tanzania, the ED is the site of a residency training program in emergency medicine and thus both residents in training or registrars with long experience in the ED may have greater acumen than non-specialist physicians who staff the majority of EDs in Tanzania. potentially reducing generalizability. No two physicians evaluated the same patient, so we were unable to assess interobserver variability in assignment of gestalt scores. Additionally, although clinician level of training/seniority was recorded, the study was not specifically powered to evaluate the association between clinician level/seniority and the accuracy of gestalt. The probability threshold used for physician gestalt was derived from a prior study in the same setting as there is no universally accepted standard; this may influence sensitivity and specificity estimates, although it does not affect the area under the curve. Likewise, physicians were also aware that they were participating in a study which may have introduced a Hawthorne effect. Also, the negative predictive value observed for both PEWS and gestalt are influenced by the underlying prevalence of adverse events, the negative predictive value may differ in settings with substantially higher or lower baseline event rates. Another limitation is ICU/HDU admission was part of the adverse event definition and may have been influenced by the same physician providing the gestalt assessment. However, objective outcomes such as cardiac arrest and death at ED were also included in the composite outcome to reduce this effect. Finally, the relative short duration may not have captured potential seasonal variations in pediatric disease patterns and adverse event rates.

Conclusion

Although both PEWS and physician gestalt performed well in our study, physician gestalt demonstrated superior discrimination compared with PEWS for predicting adverse events among critically ill pediatric patients in this setting. However, PEWS also had a good discrimination and has the advantage of being structured and more easily communicated. Integrating structured early warning scores with clinical gestalt may offer the most effective approach to early risk identification in similar low-resource settings. Given that neither approach demonstrated high sensitivity for adverse events when used alone, future studies should determine whether incorporating both PEWS and physician gestalt improves risk stratification in predicting adverse events in critically ill pediatric patients.
