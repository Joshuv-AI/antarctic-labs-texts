Multi-spatial scale analysis of cutting-slope houses to landslide hazard in granite regions: A case study in central China
Authors: Shuangshuang Wu, Guangjun Zou, Li Zhang, Boyu Zhou, Dingfang Xu, Chuncan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In landslide studies, vulnerable elements have received limited attention. In densely populated areas of China, slope cutting for house construction is widespread and significantly increases the risk of landslides behind houses, threatening both property and human safety. This problem is especially serious in China, where granite weathering crusts are widely distributed. Taking Yanling County as a case study, this study comprehensively investigated the current status and risks of cut-slope houses in granite areas. A total of 3,478 potential landslides were identified and 12,420 houses near slopes were sampled. A dataset was constructed using geological, geometric, and structural factors, and was combined with field surveys to support experiments and statistical analyses from the rock-soil scale to the regional scale. Results from 56 samples reveal significant granite strength degradation due to weathering. Analyses of three landslide cases and six slope units further clarify the characteristics of landslide hazards in the study area. Machine learning was used to assess the effects of multiple factors on landslide susceptibility and house vulnerability. Rainfall erosion showed a particularly strong effect on landslides in granite areas, distinguishing them from non-granite areas. For houses, slope-cutting height and distance from the slope toe were identified as the two most critical risk factors, as supported by both observed damage cases and regional-scale data mining. Two-dimensional thresholds were established to characterize the combined effects of these factors on disaster occurrence. Accordingly, three measures are recommended for landslide risk control in the study area: strengthening ecological restoration of granite slopes, moderately limiting slope-cutting height, and ensuring a sufficient safety distance from the slope toe during house construction.

Compared to geological disasters themselves, issues related to the damage caused by geological disasters to houses has not received the attention it deserves. These issues have traditionally been more prevalent in large-scale disaster events, such as the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake1 and the 2010 Zhouqu debris flow2. However, in recent years, these issues have become increasingly common in central and southeastern China, where large-scale hilly and low-mountainous terrain, humid and hot climates, abundant rainfall, and dense populations. It has created a unique structure where rainfall-induced small-scale landslides serve as the hazard-causing bodies and cutting slope house construction (CSHC) serves as the disaster-vulnerable entity3. Among these, small-scale landslide disasters in granitic regions, such as those found in Fujian, Guangdong, Hunan, and Jiangxi provinces in China4–7, are the most representative. It’s also reported in other economically developed regions, such as Japan8, South Korea9, Hong Kong, and Shenzhen City, China10,11. Such as, Wang et al. compared slope safety issues between Hong Kong and Chongqing, highlighting that both cities face slope instability challenges from densely populated hillside construction, with Hong Kong establishing a sustainable landslide prevention system while Chongqing confronting more complex natural hazards including earthquakes and flooding12. Dou et al. revealed the coupled failure mechanism of excavation and rainfall on a high cutting slope in Fujian using the finite element strength reduction method13. Sun et al. systematically investigated slope cutting for building in the central Sichuan Basin red bed area, identifying cut slope height, gradient ratio, and building-to-slope toe distance as key controlling parameters, and proposing three potential geological disaster formation patterns14.

Warm and humid granite regions are among the most severely affected areas where landslides are frequently triggered by CSHC. Extensive valuable studies have been conducted in landslide-prone granitic areas. The single-ring infiltration test and pulling test of bamboo above the slope have been designed to simulate the wind-driven rain conditions in a granitic slope field15. An 8 m*0.8 m model test chamber was used to conduct physical model tests on completely weathered granite landslide. The ‘sheet slip’ phenomenon discovered was consistent with many field observations16. The chemical analysis and the calculated Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA) indicate the significant weathering of granitic rock and soil samples and its possible influence on landslide occurrences in Western Ghats area17. In addition, the prediction accuracy of assessing the susceptibility of shallow or granite landslides has been improving, with the use of deep learning and machine learning18,19.

The progresses made in the assessment of building vulnerability to landslides in the past two decades has been summarizes20. A significant amount of research has focused on structural damage to buildings, stemming from an understanding of the intensity of landslide-induced disasters. In fact, many small and medium-sized landslides exhibit different disaster-causing mechanisms. While the load-bearing columns of buildings remain stable, wall damage also constitutes a disaster. This phenomenon is widely observed in landslides triggered by construction cutting-slope and rainfall3,21–23. The difference lies in the fact that the destructive intensity of landslides on buildings is between the allowable horizontal bearing capacity of load-bearing columns and walls.

This study focuses on the damage to houses caused by the above landslide strength, taking the granite area of Yanling County in central China as an example. Granite area accounts for 55.3% of Yanling County’s total area. 8,338 cutting slope house construction (CSHC) cases are recorded in the granite area. Furthermore, based on the analysis of rainfall records over the past 40 years, a correlation is identified between the number of landslide disasters and annual rainfall. Therefore, the landslide risk associated with CSHC in granite areas under heavy rainfall has emerged as a key concern in Yanling County. The study targets the CSHC issue instead the landslide disaster itself. Specifically, for the CSHC problem, five interrelated scales are presented:

1) A county-wide comprehensive coverage scale, involving statistical analysis of all CSHC cases;

2) An individual case scale, describing typical landslide events induced by CSHC;

3) A mountain slope unit scale, analyzing highlighted slope unit (HSU) in the county;

4) A material scale, summarizing geotechnical test results;

5) A sample statistical scale, conducting data mining on thousands of CSHC cases in the Granite area.

Among these multi scales, we have got the data: 12,238 CSHC cases, 3,478 landslides, 27 highlighted slope units, and 56 granite samples of different weathering degrees. Based on the dataset, various factors contributing to landslide susceptibility and house vulnerability are statistically analyzed. Factor analysis and threshold definition are conducted using Binary Logistic Regression, ROC curves, and Random Forests. It seeks to identify hidden key factors and thresholds to provide effective strategies for risk assessment in the study area.

Study Area

Tectonic characteristics and lithology

Yanling County is situated in the southeastern part of Hunan Province, within the Luoxiao Mountains area, Central China. The county boasts a terrain dominated by mountains and hills, higher in the southeast and lower in the northwest, with the Miling Mountains running through its territory, spanning a total area of approximately 2,030 km2. The main tectonic structures within the area are folds and faults. Based on their orientation, they can be divided into three tectonic systems: north-northeast, northeast-east, and northwest (Fig. 1).

The main exposed granites belong to the Caledonian I granite (γ13) and the Yanshan granite (γJ2), covering an area of 1,124 km² across the entire county, accounting for 55.3% of the total area. The rock bodies are predominantly complete to strongly weathered, occurring in scattered masses. The landscape is primarily erosional-weathering structural terrain, with well-developed residual slope deposits and slopes generally ranging from 30° to 40° (Fig. 2).

Rainfall and climate

Yanling County has a subtropical climate, featuring warmth, humidity, and abundant rainfall. According to 35 - year data (from 1981 to 2015) provided by the Meteorological Bureau, the annual rainfall generally ranges from 897.0 mm to 1883.0 mm. The multi-year average annual rainfall is 1590.4 mm. The maximum annual rainfall reaches 2447.2 mm, which occurred in 1997, while the minimum is 998.5 mm, recorded in 2009. In terms of monthly rainfall, the maximum monthly rainfall is 539.1 mm, observed in June 2002, and the minimum is only 0.1 mm, seen in October 2004. The maximum daily rainfall is 204.4 mm, which took place on June 12, 2012. Moreover, during the period from 2007 to 2015, the maximum hourly rainfall was 130.1 mm, measured at 9:00 am on January 2, 2009. Rainfall is concentrated from April to June, accounting for 30.4% − 50.7% of the annual total. The period from July to August follows as the next wettest period.

Relatively heavy rainfall zones are primarily distributed in the eastern, southeastern, and western directions of the study area (Fig. 3). Among these regions, the eastern and southeastern parts are dominated by granite outcrop areas, while partial granite outcrops are also present in the western part (see Fig. 1). Thus, the granite outcrop areas experience abundant rainfall conditions, which pose significant adverse impacts on the safety of CSHC in this region, and the associated risks merit close attention.

The distribution of the occurrence of 424 landslides in the area since 1981 to 2022, a total of 42 years, has shown by the average monthly rainfall in Fig. 4a. The concentration of the frequency of occurrence of the high time in April, May, June each year, and showed a continuous upward trend of the three months. The second quarter is the period of the highest occurrence of landslide events. In the other quarters, the average frequency of landslides basically no more than one time per year. From the annual rainfall distribution map (Fig. 4b), a positive correlation is observed between annual rainfall and hazard frequency, which satisfies the necessary but not sufficient condition: when the annual rainfall is significantly greater than the long-term average of 1590–1600 mm, the number of geological hazard points increases remarkably.

Methodology

In addition to the raw data set obtained from two years of field surveys, this study mainly employed the following methods. The study adopts a multi-scale research approach in its structure, which is an effective method for landslide risk assessment27,28. For the scale of granite outcrops in the study area, multiple statistical and machine learning methods are used. For the scale of slope units and landslide sites, field surveys, drones, and sampling tests are applied.

Varying degrees of weathering significantly affect the mechanical properties and decomposition of granite29,30. Focus on obtaining strength indicators for granite with different degrees of weathering, such as saturated compression and shear tests are conducted.

The risk assessment of the study area utilized statistical and machine learning methods, with both susceptibility and vulnerability target variables set as binary variables. The Categorical factor variables are preprocessed via label encoding. Data mining work is performed using machine learning algorithms such as Binary Logistic Regression (BLR), Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve, and Random Forest (RF)31–33. BLR can be used to determine the correlation between factors and target variables based on the significance levels of numerical and categorical factors. It can also measure the strength of the association between each factor and the target variable based on the Odds Ratio (OR) indicator. OR quantifies the association between a factor variable and a binary target variable (e.g., “event vs. non-event”), representing the relative change in the odds of the event occurring when the IV changes by one unit. OR > 1 means the tested factor variable increases the event odds; OR = 1 means no association; OR < 1 means the factor decreases the event odds. Landslide and slope

Representative landslide cases

Three typical granite landslides that have occurred in the study area in the last 20 years were selected. They are the Xianping road landslide, the Dayuan town landslide and the County Meteorological Station landslide, respectively, described below in terms of the basic characteristics, triggers and causes.

Case 1: The Xianping road landslide

The Xianping road landslide is situated in a granite valley region. The slope surface consists of strongly weathered granite, and the rock mass is fragmented (Fig. 5a). A high and steep slope was formed as a result of slope cutting during road construction. Affected by heavy rainfall, the rock and soil mass in the upper part of the slope slid in the same direction along the structural plane, giving rise to a steep scarp about 6 m in height. The landslide accumulation mass squeezed the Xianping road, which then led to the sliding of the lower slope of the road. The boundary of the landslide is distinct. The elevation of the rear edge of the landslide is 702 m, the elevation of the front edge is 681 m, the main sliding direction is 206°, the thickness ranges from 0.6 to 3.6 m, the average thickness is 2.6 m, and the volume is approximately 2,800 m3. As can be clearly seen in Fig. 5b, the outward-dipping structural plane of the granite, that is, the sliding surface, has an attitude of 210°∠33°. This structural plane is also exposed on the lower slope of the road.

The original natural slope had a moderate gradient. The cutting operation formed a relatively steep slope. The shallow layer of the slope body is composed of fragmented granite blocks and residual and colluvial sandy clay. The above-mentioned structural plane exists between the shallow rock and soil and the underlying bedrock, and the structural plane is filled with sandy clay. The underlying moderately weathered granite is relatively water-impermeable. Under the influence of continuous rainfall infiltration, rainwater is likely to accumulate on the structural plane. The filling material is extremely prone to softening after absorbing water, thus forming a weak sliding surface. The failure process is that the landslide body undergoes shear creep, the rear edge is tension-cracked and subsides downward, the front edge undergoes extrusion deformation, forming a whole pushing-type sliding. In summary, the main factors contributing to the landslide are heavy rainfall, unfavorable structural planes, and slope cutting for road construction. It is a small-sized pushing-type rock landslide.

Case 2: The Dayuan town landslide

The landslide, located on a hill behind the Dayuan Town, occurred on 30 July 2008 after several days of heavy rainfall. 2 houses were damaged, 1 house was cracked, the retaining wall of the slope was destroyed (Fig. 6a), and the roadbed was damaged, causing no casualties. The original natural gradient of the slope is 30°~40°, and an artificial slope at the foot of the hill with a height of about 10 m~18 m and a slope angle of 60°~70°. The slope body from top to bottom is residual slope deposit sandy clay soil and fully weathered and strongly weathered granite respectively. The landslide is in the shape of a lap chair, with an area of about 3375 m2, the average thickness is 3.3 m, the total volume is 11140m3, and the main sliding direction is about 140°. The elevation of the front and rear edges is about 1310.5 m and 1353.8 m, the relative elevation difference is about 43 m, the rear edge is dislocated by about 2.0 m (Fig. 6b and c).

After the artificial excavation of the slope, the equilibrium state of the soil body was changed. Rainwater infiltration during the rainy season caused the soil body to expand and soften and formed a potential slip surface. With the increase of water content and the increase of immersion time, the potential slip surface continued to soften, and the shear strength continued to decline. This landslide is also a small pushing-type soil landslide. The main triggering factors were artificial slope cutting and continuous rainfall.

Case 3: The County Meteorological Station landslide

Since the deformation of the County Meteorological Station (CMS) landslide appeared in 2010, collapses and slides have occurred several times, larger scale collapse and sliding have occurred at the leading edge in2016, 2017 and 2019 respectively, especially in the rainy season of 2017. The planform of the landslide has an overall semi-circular arc shape (Fig. 7a), with an area of about 8355 m2. the average thickness is about 8.4 m. The total volume is about 70,182 m3.

The sliding mass is residual gravelly clay and weathering fissure develops in residual soil. The landslide bed is strong weathering granite, relative water insulation, easy to form groundwater enrichment in the soil-rock interfaces. The thickness of the soil layer in front of the landslide is thicker, granite residual soil layer is sandy, cohesion is small, permeability is good, causing the rainfall rain easily to infiltrate into the slope body. The landslide in the residential area occurred because residents cut slopes to build houses, which formed a 10–20 m -high steep slope at the front edge and reduced the overall stability (Fig. 7b). Overall, the main factors of the landslide were also rainfall and slope-cutting. The landslide is currently undergoing engineering treatment and long-term monitoring and early warning contingency measures have been designed.

Each of the three cases above exhibits distinct characteristics. Case 1 involves a rock landslide, specifically the failure of highly weathered granite along structural planes. In the study area, this failure mode typically occurs on a larger scale than soil landslides. Case 2 refers to landslides in fully weathered granite layers, where slope surfaces are significantly affected by rainfall erosion. This type of landslide is very common in the study area and generally occurs on slopes disturbed by slope-cutting activities for housing or road construction. Case 3 highlights the characteristics of CSHC in densely populated areas: houses here act as high-risk disaster- affected bodies, while slopes composed of highly/fully weathered granite serve as hazard-causing bodies.

The selection criteria for HSU includes the five aspects: High population and building densities; Steep slopes; Complex geological structures within the slope unit; Poor vegetation coverage; Evident deformation signs (e.g., cracks, displacements) or existing landslide or collapse disasters. Based on these, a comprehensive survey of the Yanling county infiltrated 27 HSUs, 9 of which were of granite type, which were photographed by drone and documented. Slopes A and B on the figure below (Fig. 8) are the two linked HSUs, while slopes C and B belong to the same unit. In the HSU, based on geological surveys and investigations, (1) the landslide hazard sites were investigated and stability assessment was carried out, and (2) all the houses built near cut slopes were investigated, and they were graded in terms of risk according to high, medium, and low levels respectively.

In the study, it was found that houses located at the leading edge of natural landslides were not the main source of risk, mostly because of house extensions to the slope for land. A large number of houses were built on man-made cut slopes so that the houses were located at the foot of the cut slopes and the safe distance between the slope and the houses was less than 1 m. Slope failures, due to the lack of stability of the toe of the cut slopes themselves and the effects of rainfall, pose a risk to the houses and their inhabitants.

These fully weathered or strongly weathered granite slopes have some characteristics. At the time of slope cutting, fresh granite rock in dry state has good slope stability. Large angle of slope cutting, many slopes are nearly upright, no slope protection measures after slope cutting, and very close to houses. While after slope cutting, weathering is strong, and vegetation withers. Perennial rainfall scouring effect, the surface of the slope erosion trace is serious. The slope stability coefficient decreases at a fast rate, exacerbating the risk of slopes and disaster-vulnerable object.

The effect of weathering based on test and statistics

The granite of the above three typical landslide cases and six HSUs, at a total of nine sites, were sampled, with the maximum sampling depth not exceeding the 10 m below ground, and a total of 56 samples were taken. The range of weathering degree of the granite mainly includes medium weathering, strong weathering and full weathering, and a total of 8 full weathering samples were taken from Case 2, and 3–6 samples were taken from each of the other fields. By carrying out the compression and shear tests under saturated conditions, the saturated compressive strength and shear strength of almost samples at 9 sites were obtained respectively. The average value of the test results was used to represent the mechanical strength at each field and the following bar charts were plotted (Fig. 9).

By using the test results of 54 samples, the quantitative law of strength weakening under the influence of weathering degree of granite in the study area is statistically revealed, among which the attenuation of shear strength is noteworthy. It can be found that (i) the saturated compressive strength of strongly weathered granite is roughly 5–8 times smaller than that of medium weathering; in terms of shear strength; (ii) the cohesive force of strongly weathered granite is 4–10 times smaller than that of medium weathering, while comparing with the average value of the 8 samples in Case 2, the cohesive force of fully weathered granite is further reduced by about 50 times in comparison with that of strong weathering; and (iii) the angle of internal friction of strongly weathered granite has a maximum reduction of 33% in comparison with that of medium weathering, and also comparing the mean values of 8 samples in Case 2, the internal friction angle of fully weathered granite is further reduced by more than 50% compared to strongly weathered granite.

In addition, the failure mode of Case 1 is sliding along the structural surface of the rock mass, which led to the landslide, even though the obtained mechanical parameters of rock mass were better. Case 2 has the highest degree of weathering and the poorest mechanical parameters, which coincides with the strong deformation of the landslide as well as the complexity of the boundary conditions investigated in the field.

Slope stability of granite weathered crust is a key concern of scholars, and weathering has a significant impact on granite structural deterioration, strength weakening, and groundwater seepage. The present statistical analysis of the test results, in terms of mechanical strength parameters, reports the reduction of the mechanical strength of granite in the study area from medium weathering to strong weathering and total weathering, which is of some reference to related granite area.

Susceptibility and vulnerability

Using the dataset from Yanling County, three groups are established: the granite group, the non-granite group, and the mixed group. The original susceptibility and vulnerability factors were derived from 1:10,000 geological surveys, compiled through individual investigations of each landslide and each CSHC. The format of such geological survey forms is typically standardized by relevant authorities, including the classification levels of specific factors. For numerical values, they are generally obtained through on-site measurements or professional estimations by field personnel. Factor analysis can be conducted on the susceptibility of landslides in each group based on BLR. Factor analysis and threshold definition are performed on the vulnerability of slope cutting for housing construction based on ROC curves and RF. Accordingly, key causing factors can be selected for use in landslide risk assessment.

Factor analysis of the landslides

Through a comprehensive geo-survey in Yanling County, the potential 3478 landslides were registered, and 3478 data points were classified according to two classes: general and needing attention, among which 966 landslides needed attention. Among them, the highest number of landslides in the granite category, 631, occupies about 2/3 of the total number, so it is necessary to carry out the test of independence between granite category and non- granite category variables.

A Chi-square test was conducted to compare the distribution of landslide susceptibility between granite and non-granite categories and it was found that there was no statistical significance level between the two. Therefore, in the Binary Logistic Regression (BLR), granite group, non-granite group, and mixed group were designed for causal factor analysis with sample sizes of 631, 355, and 966, respectively. Nine causal factors are selected, there are Macroscopic Stability (MS): Qualitative (non-quantitative) slope stability status; Signs of Deformation (SD): Surface deformation signs of the slope, such as cracks, displacements, or collapses; Slope Surface Morphology (SSM): Geometric morphology of the slope-cut surface; Susceptibility to Rainfall Scouring and Erosion (ESE): Degree of the slope’s vulnerability to rainfall-induced scouring and erosion; Intensity of Engineering Interventions (IEI); Height Interval of Excavation (HIE); Slope Gradient (SG); Slope Height (SH); and Number of Houses (NH). Their data types (Categorical or Numerical), levels or values are shown in the following Table 2. In the Binary Logistic Regression Analysis (BLRA), the landslide susceptibility grade, serving as the target variable, is condensed into two classification levels: the low - level and the aggregated level encompassing medium and higher levels.

By comparing the performance of ESE in different groups, it reflects the significance of ESE for the susceptibility of granite-type landslides, which is in line with the results of the field survey. Granite-type landslides, especially in cut-slope excavations cases, are susceptible to repeated erosion by rainfall, as shown in the Fig. 10, which is typical slope erosion in granite areas. The importance of ESE factors for risk assessment of granite landslides is emphasized by the analysis.

The HIE factor has the most significant effect on the mixed group, with a level of significance for the non-granite group, but not for the granite group. This point also shows that the HIE factor is not suitable as an input variable for the risk assessment of landslides in the granite group. Due to the granite residual soil itself has good uprightness, high cut slope height and large cut slope area are quite common in Yanling County.

The IEI factor has a significant level for the granite group and mixed group, but not for the non-granite group, which reflects the fact that the cut slopes mainly appear in the granite area. The MS factor has a significant level for all groups.

Comparing the performance of the ESE factor and the HIE factor, a conjectural explanation can be that slopes in the granite area should have better uprightness and stability under dry conditions and slope-cutting, but are susceptible to the risk of instability under the continuous effect of rainfall and erosion. Therefore, for the landslide susceptibility in granite area, ESE factor needs to be focused on while reducing the dependence on HIE factor. Table 4 shows the effect of numerical input variables on the susceptibility of granite landslides. These variables are passed the Hosmer-Lemeschow test. SH, SG, and NH are all positively correlated with susceptibility, with Exp (B) of 1.005, 1.06, and 1.041, respectively. The above analyses clarified that the significant factors for granite-type landslides include ESE, MS, and IEI, and the three numerical factors.

Factor analysis of CSHC

1-D Threshold analysis.

During the geological survey in Yanling County, a total of 12,420 valid slope house samples were collected, including 12,238 cases of CSHC. Some characteristics were summarized from landslide cases (Fig. 11). Slope cutting damages the structure of rock and soil masses, and loosens surface materials, reducing slope stability. Such construction often lacks adequate engineering protection, exacerbating landslide risks. Houses located too close to the toe of cut slopes lead to higher disaster intensity if slope failure occurring.

The total sample set was divided by lithology. In the granite area, there are 8,338 samples, including 4,195 risky cases and 4,143 non-risky cases. In other lithology areas, there are 3,900 samples, with 1,785 risky cases and 2,115 non-risky cases. The distribution of positive and negative samples was basically balanced. A total of 9 vulnerability factors were screened for all samples, including 3 numerical factors, there are Slope cutting height (SCH), Slope cutting gradient (SCG), Toe distance of slope (TDS); six categorical factors, there are Slope Surface Morphology (SSM), Signs of Deformation (SD), Degree of Weathering (DW), Types of Retaining Walls (TRW), Types of Building Structures (TBS), Number of Floors in a Building (NFB). To investigate the characteristics of vulnerability factors in the study areas, three groups were tested: granite area, non - granite area and mixed area.

Based on the ROC curve, the numerical factors of SCH, SCG, and TDS have been tested, respectively. It is necessary to introduce these three numerical variables and how they are measured. SCH refers to the maximum height of the cutting slope, measured as the distance from the top of the cutting slope to the ground level. SCG denotes the average slope gradient of the slope post-cutting, obtained through distance measurement and trigonometric functions. TDS represents the closest horizontal distance from the toe of the cutting slope to the rear wall of an individual house; if a retaining wall exists at the slope toe, its position is used as a substitute, and this distance can be directly measured.

The results were evaluated through Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Youden’s index analysis (Fig. 12). The results revealed that SCH showed significant impacts on the vulnerability of CSHC, with AUC values exceeding 0.9 across all three groups. The critical SCH threshold (H₀) were determined to be 4.75 m, 4.95 m, and 4.75 m for the three groups, respectively. In contrast, neither SCG nor TDS demonstrated significant correlations with CSHC vulnerability.

The ROC curve performs well in single-factor detection, featuring high recognition efficiency and accuracy. However, vulnerability is typically influenced by multiple factors, and there may be interactions among these factors. For example, in actual slope-cutting and house-construction decisions, SCH, SCG, and TDS are mutually affected to a certain extent.

2-D Threshold analysis

Many machine learning algorithms are capable of simultaneously processing numerical and categorical variables, and the impact of factors on vulnerability can be inferred from the performance of regression or prediction. Among these algorithms, the Random Forest (RF) algorithm offers strong interpretability. Subsequently, the nine factors, involved in granite areas, non-granite areas, and mixed areas, were simultaneously tested using RF algorithm. Given that the primary focus was on analysis, only the training set (70%) and validation set (30%) was utilized, and no test set was established.

Tables 5, 6 and 7 illustrate the prediction accuracies of the three groups, all of which range from 0.85 to 0.90. Based on these results, the prediction importance of each factor was visualized in Fig. 13. Notably, in all three groups, the three numerical factors consistently ranked among the top three contributors. While it is not surprising that SCH ranked second, the fact that SCG emerged as the most influential factor, with TDS following in third place. The partial result seemingly contradicts their performance in the ROC analysis. Nevertheless, given their dominant positions in the rankings, these three factors warrant further investigation as key determinants of vulnerability. Since no significant differences were observed among the three groups in both ROC and RF analyses, to better highlight the factor characteristics specific to granite areas, subsequent analyses will focus solely on the vulnerability of CSHC within the granite group.

In addition to the RF algorithm, other machine learning methods such as C5.0, Bayes network, and Artificial Neural Network were applied to the three groups for comparison. However, the highest prediction accuracy achieved by these alternative algorithms was beyond 0.91, showing minimal improvement over the RF results. To maintain consistency in the analysis, subsequent discussions will focus exclusively on the random forest predictions. Confidence scores for the risky or non-risky (1- score) were assigned to all target data, enabling the creation of 2D factor contour maps. Given the consistent significance of SCH across both ROC and RF analyses, SCH was selected as the horizontal axis, while SCG and TDS were chosen as the vertical axes for separate contour maps to observe change trends in confidence score distributions.

From the SCG-SCH contour plot, it can be found (Fig. 14a) that SCH has the phenomenon of stepwise change, i.e., the value of isoline appears to change abruptly from close to 0 to 1, at a location roughly at 5 m. SCG has two cases, roughly above 40°, where the isolines are controlled by SCH, and below 40°, the value of isoline shows an asymptotic increase with the simultaneous increase of SCH and SCG. The analysis suggests that the critical state around 40° is related to the natural angle of repose of the weathered crust of granite, which is generally considered to be at 35°, 40° and 45° depending on the degree of weathering. The SCG should be greater than the angle of repose in order to exacerbate the risk of instability.

From the TDS-SCH contour plot, it can be found (Fig. 14b) that SCH still has the phenomenon of stepwise change, at a position close to 5 m. However, SCH>5 m, does not control all isolines, and within a certain range of SCH, the value of isolines shows an asymptotic decrease with the increase of TDS.

Accordingly, it can be concluded that the vulnerability of CSHC is effectively reduced even at SCH>5 m when the SCG is less than angle of repose or sufficient TDS is reserved. This is an effective strategy for the CBS problem. However, when this point of conclusion is verified, such data points only account for a very small portion of the sample size (Table 8). It shows that neither the SCG nor the TDS can be uniformly distributed on both sides of the threshold H0 as in the case of SCH. Therefore, the strategy does not solve the problem in most cases.

Thus, the data distributions of the three numerical factors were rechecked (Fig. 15). They are found to exhibit different degrees of skewed distributions, especially SCG showing a significant right skew and TDS showing a more significant left skew. Considering the above strategy applied to a small number of cases, data satisfying SCG < 40° or TDS>5 m were excluded. Of the remaining 8088 samples, they could not be analyzed by the 2-D contour plot because they were all in the red area of the plot (Fig. 14).

Normalized preprocessing of the selected 8088 samples, followed by summing and reassigning the values of SCH’, SCG’ and TDS’, allowed the plotting of a ternary phase diagram of the three factors (Fig. 16). New insights are revealed that, after eliminating a special small number of samples, the value of isolines is controlled by both SCH and TDS, and the influence of SCG is relatively weakened. There is a “triangle of impossibility” in the Fig. 16 where there are no isolines, but which has the connotation that an excessively large increase in SCH or a decrease in TDS must result in a sample that is 100% vulnerable. Both aspects reflect the fact that the vulnerability is highly dependent on SCH and TDS.

Based on the ternary phase distribution, the thresholds for SCH and TDS are defined. The vulnerability of the CSHC sample is high for SCH’∈(0.2, 0.6) and TDS’∈(0.4, 0.8), subject to SCH’ + SCG + TDS’ = 1. An even higher risk region is that of SCH’∈(0.4, 0.6) and TDS ‘∈(0.4, 0.6). With the above conclusions obtained for 8088 CSHC samples, it is believed that such 2-D thresholds can provide a risk-prevention decision basis for house construction under cutting slopes in granite areas in Yanling county.

Discussion

The landslide hazards in the granite study area are characterized by a high susceptibility of the completely weathered layer, larger failure scales when structural planes in rock mass are involved, and the predominance of CSHC as the main disaster-bearing element. While this study provides some valuable insights into cutting-slope houses and landslide hazards, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

Over 50% of Yanling County’s land area can be classified as the granite region and the number of slope-cutting house cases involved exceeds 10,000, providing an excellent study area and sample set for the study. Due to the large sample size, noise issues arise when creating isoline maps, whether using 2-D or 3-D thresholds, resulting in isolines that are not sufficiently smooth. Therefore, smoothing or noise reduction for non-time-series data is a limitation of this study. More detailed research should be conducted in the future, such as stricter data segmentation based on the geological eras and distribution areas of granite outcrops revealed in Fig. 1. Additionally, rainfall is also a key factor in landslides caused by slope cutting for housing construction in granite areas. The abundant rainfall in the study area reflects this, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. When analyzing the susceptibility of landslides in the study area, the erosive impact of rainfall on slope surfaces is emphasized. However, the absence of spatiotemporal data correlating rainfall information with landslide disasters hinders the derivation of more profound conclusions, particularly the characterization of rainfall-induced thresholds. Furthermore, the adoption of a multi-scale approach provides comprehensive data for the manuscript and multi-dimensional evidence to support the research findings. However, CSHC is an inherently complex issue: it inherently links hazard-causing bodies and hazard-affected bodies, making it difficult to separate them as independent entities. Unfortunately, the existing dataset still fails to fully reflect this interconnected characteristic. To address this limitation, incorporating numerical simulations to explore the physical processes of interaction in CSHC will supplement a new dimensional perspective to the research in future studies.

In the granite regions of Fujian, Guangdong, and adjacent southern Jiangxi, where residual soil and weathered layers are dominant, CSHC and engineering excavation are highly susceptible to shallow landslides and collapses. Existing studies and local geological disaster prevention plans consistently suggest an empirical threshold of 5 m: slope heights above this level are associated with a markedly increased risk, with many hazard cases concentrated in the 5–10 m range. Accordingly, reinforcement or stricter control is generally recommended for cut slopes exceeding 5 m. This threshold has also been explicitly adopted in several provincial and county-level prevention plans, indicating its practical relevance in regional hazard management36,37. In addition, there is one noteworthy aspect in the data analysis. The ROC analysis indicates that TDS is not significant, whereas RF identifies it as among the most important factors. In fact, relevant clues can also be found when we examine the data distributions of SCG and TDS (see Fig. 15). The data distribution of TDS is overly concentrated, with a mean value of 1.5 m. Therefore, when used as a univariate variable in ROC curve analysis, its influence is difficult to highlight. However, in the RF-based multivariate analysis, the significant impact of TDS is revealed. Based on this phenomenon, the authors refer to TDS as a “latent” feature, distinguishing it from the “explicit” feature of CSH. This result also indicates that when multiple factor variables jointly act on the target variable, univariate analysis, multivariate analysis, and multivariate interaction analysis are all indispensable. In this case, the categorical variables did not show significance; otherwise, conducting a two-way ANOVA would also be necessary. Finally, we aim to include rainfall data in future model training by improving its collection and processing. Although this remains challenging, incorporating rainfall as a factor into the model will provide valuable support for risk prevention and management in the study area.

Conclusions

Given the growing concern about landslide due to housing via cutting-slope in granite regions, this study provides critical insights into the interplay between landslide and house, particularly in hazard risk mitigation in Yanling County. The conclusions for derived from this study can be summarized as follows:

(1) The factors causing landslides in different lithology within the same area vary. For granite regions, rainfall erosion effect is one of the significant contributing factors. After cutting a slope, ecological restoration of the slope surface should be the focus of protection efforts.

(2) A 5-meter height of slope behind the house can be used as the critical threshold for the vulnerability of slope-cutting house construction in Yanling County. In general, the slope cutting height can be limited to within 5 m to reduce disaster risk.

(3) The slope-cutting height and toe distance to slope can be used to establish 2-D thresholds based on the isoline analysis. It allows to obtain a preliminary calculation of a relatively safe toe distance, especially in the case of the slope-cutting height exceeds 5 m.
