Stochastic Engrams for Efficient Continual Learning
Isabelle Aguilar, Luis Fernando Herbozo Contreras, Omid Kavehei

Abstract
The ability to learn continuously in artificial neural networks (ANNs) is often limited by catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon in which new knowledge becomes dominant. By taking mechanisms of memory encoding in neuroscience (i.e., engrams) as inspiration, we propose a novel approach that integrates stochastically-activated engrams as a gating mechanism for metaplastic binarized neural networks ( m BNNs). This method leverages the computational efficiency of mBNNs combined with the robustness of probabilistic memory traces to mitigate forgetting and maintain the model’s reliability. Previously validated metaplastic optimization techniques have been incorporated to further enhance synaptic stability. Compared to baseline binarized models and benchmark fully connected continual learning approaches, our method is the only strategy capable of achieving average accuracies over 70% in both class-incremental and domain-incremental MNIST benchmarks, matching full-precision state-of-the-art methods. Furthermore, we achieve a significant reduction in peak GPU and RAM usage, under 5% and 20%, respectively, as well as an ∼ 8 × \sim 8\times reduction in memory footprint compared to full precision counterparts. Our findings demonstrate (A) an improved stability vs. plasticity trade-off, (B) reduced memory intensiveness, and (C) enhanced performance in binarized architectures. By uniting principles of neuroscience and efficient computing, we offer new insights into the design of scalable and robust deep learning systems.

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) tend to lose previously acquired knowledge when training on new tasks. Additionally, while BNNs offer extreme energy efficiency, their rigid, discrete parameter space has left them fundamentally underutilized and underperforming in sequential learning scenarios.

II.1
Neurobiological Processes

Metaplasticity, or the plasticity of synaptic plasticity, is a neurobiological plasticity modulator, also described briefly as the ”plasticity of synaptic plasticity”
[
1
,
2
]
. This biological mechanism regulates plasticity by influencing synaptic states and the brain’s adaptive capacity to incoming information
[
16
]
. This ability is essential for balancing the varying strengths of synapses, stabilizing incoming knowledge in the brain while allowing for and adapting to new memories. This process is an attractive regularization or optimization approach for ANNs and has proven successful for continual learning compared to baseline feedforward networks
[
25
]
.

Another key mechanism in the brain is the formation and recall of engrams, which are sparse memory traces that encode, consolidate, and retrieve specific memories
[
19
]
. These neural ensembles are sparsely distributed across the brain and play a crucial role in recalling past information. Rodent-based experiments have observed the natural reactivation of these specific engrams during memory retrieval
[
55
,
69
]
. Furthermore, direct intervention studies using optogenetics have demonstrated that artificially activating these tagged neural ensembles is sufficient to causally cue memory retrieval
[
38
]
.

Engrams are also closely related to associative learning, a fundamental area of study in neuroscience and psychology, including the notable case of Pavlov’s dog in classical conditioning
[
76
]
. In neuroscience, associative learning interprets how stimulus representations in the brain (formed by cues, environmental contexts, temporal information, etc.) inform behavioral outcomes
[
70
]
. As memories and information continue to stream into the brain, associated contexts help form engrams, update our biological neural networks, and adapt to incoming representations
[
22
]
. Furthermore, the brain has long been viewed as a stochastic system, and more recent studies have even proposed that this stochasticity enhances associative memories and retention
[
17
,
37
]
.

II.2
Bio-inspired Continual Learning Strategies

These biological processes have inspired numerous methodologies in deep learning to address the discrepancy between artificial and biological networks in continual learning
[
26
,
20
,
25
]
. Several existing deep learning strategies share conceptual analogies with engram formation, although they are not explicitly modelled. For instance, the isolated subnetworks within an ensemble approach serve as a functional parallel to separate engrams
[
13
]
. Similarly, dropout and sparse neural networks exhibit analogous behaviors where specific groups of active neurons act as loosely inspired memory traces for a given task
[
41
,
67
]
. Transfer learning and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) also employ associative memory mechanisms that improve storage compared to traditional multilayer perceptrons (MLPs)
[
50
,
56
]
. Beyond structural analogies, stochasticity has been explored via probabilistic activations
[
68
]
and stochastic synapses sampled from Bernoulli distributions, which have demonstrated competitive continual learning performance while also addressing energy efficiency
[
60
]
.

Our method builds on this concept of stochastic modularity but extends it into a context-dependent framework. Rather than applying stochastic activations directly to the model’s weights, our stochasticity lies in the distinct gating mechanism applied to the backbone network. In terms of engram-based computational research, the use of context-dependent gating to allocate neuronal ensembles represents the most direct translation of associative learning into an architecture-based continual learning approach
[
45
,
71
]
. While previous biologically inspired models have explored computational efficiency, the specific integration of explicit engram gating mechanisms within extremely quantized binarized neural networks (BNNs) for severe resource-constrained environments remains unexplored. Other biologically inspired continual learning models typically focus on different strategy types, such as replay-based or distinct architecture-based models.

II.3
Related Architectures

In the ML field, binarized neural networks (BNNs) have emerged as a competitive alternative to their full-precision counterparts
[
11
,
10
,
23
]
. These one-bit networks show promise in producing a low-power solution to the catastrophic forgetting problem
[
30
]
, and are particularly attractive due to their hardware compatibility, although most of these works rely on replay-based mechanisms that impose more memory constraints
[
6
,
74
]
. Recent methods have continued to implement metaplasticity models in efficient architectures
[
81
,
9
]
. Our model leverages the metaplasticity function first introduced in
[
30
]
but differs from these methods by using a single classifier (rather than the ensemble network employed in
[
81
]
) and adding an engram module to incorporate context-dependent gating within the network.
Similarly, autoencoders and variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been previously employed for generative continual learning using latent spaces, such as brain-inspired replay
[
78
,
12
,
73
]
. However, these strategies are mainly replay-based, imposing significant memory demands that scale with increasing data volume. Additionally, modular architectures similar to biological neurogenesis have also been explored for continual learning, but they are difficult to scale as memory demands increase with the number of tasks
[
58
]
. We propose an alternative method that addresses the memory constraints these approaches overlook, and primarily utilizes an encoder module.

Methods

III.1
Binarized Neural Network

\label

sec:sub:bnn
The backbone of our model is an efficient BNN rather than a typical fully connected, full-precision network. These layers are shown as yellow blocks within the overarching architecture in Fig.
. BNNs are highly quantized, bitwise versions of standard MLPs that dramatically reduce memory and energy usage while achieving performance comparable to or even better than full-precision models
[
65
,
53
]
. Unlike typical feedforward networks, the activations and weights of a BNN are bounded to
\{-1
,
+1\}
. Backpropagation operates on a real-valued weight to influence gradient updates. However, the evaluation stage uses the binary weight, which equals the sign of the real-valued weight (i.e., the ”hidden weight”).

III.2
Engram Gating Block

\label

sec:sub:engram

Figure 2:
Engram block architecture.
The engram block processes the input data block through a two-layer encoder to compress features into a one-layer latent space. This compressed representation is then passed through a linear layer and subjected to stochastic activation, yielding a discrete gating vector. Finally, this gating vector modulates the mBNN via element-wise multiplication across its hidden layers, dynamically routing the forward pass.
\label
fig:fig2

To promote task-specific representations and mitigate interference during continual learning, we introduce a stochastic engram-gating block that modulates the activity of hidden neurons via context-dependent gating. This component is illustrated in blue and expanded upon in Fig.
.

For a given input sample, a single shared two-layer full-precision encoder projects it into a lower-dimensional latent space to extract essential spatial context. From this shared latent space, separate linear layers project outward to generate distinct, independent gating vectors (masks) for each corresponding hidden layer of the metaplastic BNN (
m
BNN). Because the gating is context-dependent, a unique mask is dynamically generated for every individual input on every forward pass.

While the internal encoder utilizes ReLU activations, the final projection layers instead apply a stochastic activation and a top-
k
mask to generate discrete
0,1
gating values. Both the encoder and linear layers are fully trainable, and their parameters are updated jointly with the rest of the network. While the architecture bears a structural resemblance to an autoencoder by distilling meaningful representations of the input, its functional objective diverges; the embedding drives task-specific modulation rather than input reconstruction.

Unlike Dropout

This mechanism encourages dynamic sparsity within the backbone architecture and modulates activity across tasks, thus reducing overlapping representations and catastrophic interference. Furthermore, Dropout does not use context to guide its masking, whereas the engram block employs embeddings to apply context-dependent gating to the backbone.

IV.1
Implementation Details

Our model utilizes a binarized backbone network. For standard experiments, this consists of two hidden layers, each containing 2048 neurons, with engram gating projected onto both linear layers. For Split CIFAR-100 experiments, a binarized ResNet-18 is employed. Here, engram gating is spatially broadcast to modulate the convolutional feature maps. More specifically, it is applied after the first batch normalization and again at the output of each residual block.
Experience replay
[
57
]
was implemented using a fixed buffer of
2000
samples. To ensure a fair and rigorous comparison, every CIFAR baseline receives this exact same replay buffer capacity and sampling budget. The buffer is updated via standard reservoir sampling, and during training, replay samples are mixed with current task data at a 1:1 ratio within a standard batch size of
128
, following standard preprocessing (outlined in Appendix
). All the methods we compared used the same backbone architecture, except for the state-of-the-art SDMLP, CH-HNN, and WSN, whose architectures are integral to their strategies.
In the Split MNIST setting, the metaplastic hyperparameter
m
is set to 5; in Split CIFAR-100, it is set to 250. For CORe50,
m
is set equal to 175. For more stable training, batch-normalization is added after each layer
[
11
]
. The real-valued hidden weights are initialized over a uniform distribution between the values
[-0.5,0.5]
. The learning rate was set to
\eta=10^{-4}
during training. Adam’s beta values
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
are
(0.9,0.999)
, respectively, as default.

In the engram block architecture, the computation graph is distinctly divided into a shared representation encoder and a gating projection network. For our standard MLP backbone, the shared encoder processes the input through a sequence of fully connected layers with decreasing dimensionalities (
\text{input}\to 512\to 128\to 64
). This bottleneck design enables the compression of arbitrary data types and facilitates robust representation learning in the latent space. Subsequently, the gating projection maps this condensed latent state to the exact width of the backbone layers (
64\to 2048\to 2048
) to generate the final masking probabilities.

The model is trained for 20 epochs per task for all incremental settings. For the CORe50 experiment, we increase the capacity of the backbone by widening the hidden layers from 2048 to 4096 neurons. Consequently, the mask width is scaled equivalently; the last engram projection layer is adjusted at 4096 neurons to match the wider backbone. Furthermore, to prevent cross-task statistical interference during continual learning, batch normalization statistics (i.e., running mean and variance) are isolated: they are explicitly saved at the end of each task’s training phase and reloaded during task-specific inference. The performance of the models is evaluated on the test set throughout training.

Our model, termed engramBNN, is tested and compared with a vanilla BNN baseline. Continual learning baselines are also compared with our model and applied to the BNN architecture. We examine the performance of BNNs with context-dependent gating (XdG)
[
45
]
, elastic weight consolidation (EWC)
[
29
]
, synaptic intelligence (SI)
[
80
]
, and learning without forgetting (LwF)
[
36
]
.

Additionally, recent biologically inspired state-of-the-art methods are tested, a hybrid neural network based on cortico-hippocampal recurrent loops (CH-HNN)
[
62
]
and a sparsely distributed memory multilayer perceptron based on the cerebellum (SDMLP)
[
8
]
. Winning Sub-Networks (WSN) is also considered a state-of-the-art method that learns and selects subnetworks within its model for each task
[
28
]
. The CH-HNN model is excluded from domain-incremental learning experiments because it requires pre-trained ANN models and CORe50 embeddings that were not available in the original experiments.

Serving as an ablation study comparison, we compare the performance, sparsity, and stability of the engramBNN, metaplastic BNN (
m
BNN)
[
30
]
without the gating module attached, and a BNN with Dropout regularization. This provides a fair comparison of the engram block’s benefits in our network. All analyses are conducted with the class-incremental Split MNIST experimental setting.

In this paper, we compare our model with conceptually similar approaches that focus on regularization and gating mechanisms to facilitate continual learning. However, our model efficiency comparison is done with a broader range of strategies. We compare our model with both replay and modular strategies, specifically brain-inspired replay (BI Replay)

IV.2
Metrics

In lifelong learning settings, earlier performance metrics are further specified in the cumulative and final point versions. Let
A_{j,k}
denote the model’s test accuracy on task
k
after having sequentially trained up to task
j
. Average accuracy (ACC) is reported. As shown in Equ.
, ACC takes the final accuracy,
A_{T,i}
, for each task
i
and averages them over the number of tasks seen. This gives a task-wise view of learning retention of the continual learning network.

{\rm ACC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}A_{T,i}\label{eq:acc}

(5)

A weighted accuracy value (
w
ACC) is also calculated. The standard ACC metric treats all tasks equally, which can obscure catastrophic forgetting if a model performs well on only the most recently learned tasks. To address this,
w
ACC prioritizes long-term memory stability and acts as a stress test rather than a balanced metric. It explicitly isolates and heavily penalizes the forgetting of the earliest tasks. As defined in Equ.
, the final accuracy for each task
i
after training on
T
total tasks (denoted as
A_{T,i}
) is scaled by a weight
w_{i}
, and then normalized over the sum of all weights.

The weighting factor is defined by an exponential function,
w_{i}=e^{\lambda t_{i}}
, where
t_{i}
represents the ”age” of the task relative to the end of training (calculated as
T-i
) with a decay rate hyperparameter set to
\lambda=0.5
. Because of this exponential scaling, the metric induces a deliberately steep gradient (e.g., in a 20-task sequence, the oldest task dominates the final score while the most recent tasks contribute marginally). In conjunction with ACC,
w
ACC acts as a lower-bound indicator of lifelong retention, ensuring that a high score cannot be achieved by merely excelling on the newest data at the expense of the oldest.

{\rm{\it w}ACC}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{T}w_{i}A_{T,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{T}w_{i}}\label{eq:w_{a}cc}

(6)

Forward Transfer (FWT) and Backward Transfer (BWT) are also continual learning metrics that evaluate learning and forgetting, respectively
[
40
]
. FWT examines forward knowledge transfer and evaluates a model’s ability to generalize learning from past tasks to future ones. It is defined as:

{\rm FWT}=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=2}^{T}A_{i-1,i}\label{eq:fwt}

(7)

where
A_{i-1,i}
is the accuracy on task
i
evaluated immediately after the network has finished training on task
i-1
(before any exposure to the training data of task
i
).

BWT quantifies how prior task knowledge is retained or forgotten during subsequent task learning. It is defined as:

{\rm BWT}=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\left(A_{T,i}-A_{i,i}\right)~.\label{eq:bwt}

(8)

A positive BWT signifies improved knowledge when learning new tasks, while a negative BWT signifies knowledge deterioration. As it is standard behavior for models to show catastrophic forgetting, we also add a modified metric based on BWT, known as Remembering (REM). Remembering or
{\rm REM}=1-|\min({\rm BWT},0)|
.

Continual learning encompasses various types of incremental learning: domain-incremental, class-incremental, and task-incremental. Each addresses different challenges in adapting to new data distributions. In domain-incremental learning, the input distribution shifts over time while the task and output targets remain unchanged. This differs from class-incremental learning, where new categories are introduced sequentially, and task-incremental learning, where new tasks require distinct boundaries. Here, we evaluate our model across class- and domain-incremental settings to assess the robustness of our model to various dataset biases and environmental shifts.

The most notable domain-incremental benchmark task is the Permuted MNIST task
[
80
]
. In this scenario, the model must learn various permutations of the MNIST image dataset sequentially. Each task has a unique and random spatial permutation of pixels. Here, we argue that although widely used in the continual learning field, Permuted MNIST has fundamental flaws that render it an unreliable indicator of continual learning ability. There have been previous criticisms of Permuted MNIST, as it does not translate to real-world scenarios and is, at best, a toy problem that does not capture the true nature of realistic domain shifts
[
15
]
. We present Permuted MNIST results in Sec.
to examine our model under a simple domain-incremental task.

Instead, we test our model on the more realistic CORe50-NI tasks (”NI” meaning ”New Instances”), a specific experimental setting of the CORe50 dataset, where each new instance is imposed by a domain shift defined by varying backgrounds, lighting, poses, and occlusions
[
39
]
. The CORe50-NI dataset was captured across 11 sessions with unique backgrounds and lighting conditions. For our configuration, the network targets the 10 broader category classes (aggregating the 50 underlying objects). The model is trained sequentially across eight sessions (treated as individual continuous learning tasks) and tested on the remaining three. Unlike task-incremental settings where each task
k
possesses a distinct test split, these three held-out sessions form a single, unified test set. Consequently, the performance metric after learning task
j
(analogous to
A_{j,k}
) is obtained by evaluating the model on the entirety of this fixed test set, yielding a global measure of domain generalization and retention. This dataset, in particular, has become more recognized as a benchmark in domain-incremental learning in recent years and has been curated with continual learning in mind
[
61
]
. As a supplementary experiment, we perform the domain-incremental version of Split MNIST, in which the model must classify digits
0
through
9
as odd or even across all tasks, which each contain two of the ten total classes.

As shown in Table
, in the CORe50-NI experiment, although vanilla BNN achieved the highest recorded ACC, our engramBNN model achieved the highest weighted ACC of
19.06\%
. Our model also had the highest FWT score of
16.49\%
. Interestingly, the baselines EWC and SI achieved the best BWT and REM scores, being the only models with positive BWT scores. In the Split MNIST domain-incremental experiments, our model achieved the highest ACC and weighted ACC of
75.34\%
and
79.47\%
, respectively. SI exhibited the best BWT and REM scores, and SDMLP had the highest FWT score. It should be noted that BWT exhibits higher variance than ACC across the six runs because the discrete nature of BNNs can cause sudden, localized task collapses that heavily penalize BWT, but are diluted in the overall ACC average.

Class-Incremental Experiments

\label

Another test for lifelong learning is class-incremental learning. Here, classes are introduced incrementally. The Split MNIST experiment divides the standard MNIST dataset into a sequence of tasks, each containing a distinct subset of classes. Following the typical baseline setup, our test consists of five tasks, each introducing two new classes that have not been previously seen. Similarly, Split CIFAR divides the CIFAR-100 dataset. Compared to MNIST, which consists of grayscale images of handwritten digits across one of 10 classes, CIFAR-100 is much more complex and contains RGB images of varied natural objects, such as animals, vehicles, and plants, that fall under one of 100 classes. Two experiments are conducted on this dataset: a 20-5 split (20 tasks with 5 unseen classes per task) and a 10-10 split. Similarly to domain-incremental experiments, although distinct tasks are set up, task IDs are not provided to the model, meaning our model is unaware when these tasks shift from one to the other.

Ablation and Analysis of the Engram Block

\label

Ablation experiments were conducted on the class-incremental Split MNIST setting. Test accuracy, sparsity, and mask stability are reported in Table
. Sparsity is measured during deterministic inference as the fraction of inactive neurons (masked or set to 0) in the second hidden layer of the network when evaluated on the test set. Mask stability was measured for the engramBNN to quantify how well the model preserved task-specific representations. For each task
i
, a binary task-level mask
M_{i}^{(j)}
is extracted, providing information on which neurons remain active for that task after learning task
j
. Because engram gating is input-dependent, this task-level mask is defined as the union of all active neurons across the entirety of task
i
’s evaluation set. The reference mask
M_{i}^{(i)}
corresponds to this union mask immediately after training on task
i
. Stability is computed as:

\text{Stability}(i,j)=\frac{|M_{i}^{(i)}\cap M_{i}^{(j)}|}{|M_{i}^{(i)}|}

(9)

where
|\cdot|
denotes cardinality (i.e., the total number of active neurons) in the given mask or intersection. To strictly measure retention and avoid artificially inflating the average, the baseline comparison at
j=i
(which guarantees a match of
1.0
) is excluded. Therefore, mask stability is computed exclusively across all subsequent tasks
j>i
. The resulting stability values were averaged within each task, and then averaged across all tasks to yield a singular metric, Average Mask Stability.

To quantify this clustering quality objectively, we calculated the Silhouette score (
S
) on the high-dimensional extracted features
[
51

k

[
21
]
to the features, setting
k=10
to correspond to the total number of digit classes. The silhouette score
s(i)
for a single sample
i

\label{eq:silhouette}s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),b(i)\}}

(10)

where
a(i)
is the average distance between sample
i
and all other points in the same cluster, and
b(i)
is the minimum average distance between sample
i
and all points in the closest neighboring cluster. The final score
S
is calculated as the average of all
s(i)
values across all samples, and is bounded between
[-1,1]
.

A higher overall silhouette score indicates that the natural clusters are dense and well-separated. As listed in Fig.

.

r_{ij}

d_{ij}

V
Discussion

\label

disc
Metaplasticity and probabilistic context-dependent gating inform artificial engrams in a resource-efficient BNN architecture. Our model was tested in a class- and domain-incremental learning setting using image-based data. Both tests demonstrated performance comparable to the previously validated metaplastic BNN, surpassing the GPU-based efficiency of the baseline and vanilla models.

The combination of context-dependent learning
[
22
]
and metaplasticity
[
25
]
provides a meaningful solution to continual learning by enabling more flexible and adaptive learning mechanisms. Context-dependent signals inform artificial engram modules
[
19
]
. Probabilistic activations induce these masks through their stochastic nature, enabling greater knowledge retention. The probabilistic activation of engram formation facilitated stable, scalable learning, contributing to the overall effectiveness of the model
[
72
]
. The metaplasticity mechanism further adjusts synaptic plasticity based on prior learning, enabling the model to more effectively prevent task interference. Together, these processes enable more stable learning and contribute to the development of power-efficient systems with binarized architectures, making them suitable for real-world lifelong learning applications with resource constraints
[
66
]
. Furthermore, our method is closely related to the recently published CH-HNN method
[
62
]
, which was compared in our experiments. Both approaches employ metaplasticity and induce some gating to modulate the signal. However, we argue that our approach is more efficient on the backend because it eliminates the need for pretraining and computing similarities between modulation signals. Unlike the CH-HNN, which requires hybridizing an ANN and an SNN, our method simplifies the process while achieving similar, if not improved, results.

Our model showed superior performance in the class-incremental Split MNIST experiment, outperforming vanilla models, baselines, and state-of-the-art continual learning methods, while significantly reducing resource usage, using less than half the GPU power of most other baselines and taking up only
3.02
MiB of memory in the inference stage of Split MNIST. In edge computing and real-time applications, consideration of resource efficiency is critical
[
64
]
.

How tasks are split in class-incremental scenarios is also critical to evaluating continual learning strategies. Different splits represent varying degrees of task similarity, which may influence degrees of forgetting and knowledge transfer. In the Split CIFAR-100 experiments, neither split demonstrated superior performance over the other, suggesting that more extensive experiments with backbones forming complex architectures are warranted. However, while almost all models exhibited final accuracies near or only marginally above random chance, the engramBNN achieved the best performance, with average accuracies between
8-10\%
. This strengthens the argument for contextual gating mechanisms and is remarkable, given that a binarized, highly quantized backbone can maintain performance that fully connected networks were unable to achieve.

In this study, we also focused on a domain-incremental setting to evaluate the model’s ability to learn and retain knowledge as the data distribution changes. We strongly believe that Permuted MNIST is ineffective as a continual learning benchmark. The reason it is referred to here is its prevalent use. We employed the CORe50 dataset
[
39
]
as a realistic alternative to address the need for a more realistic dataset. This benchmark posed challenges, as the images were more complex and had eleven different scenarios with varying backgrounds and lighting. Our model achieved higher weighted accuracy than other models and demonstrated forward transfer.

EngramBNN can continually learn in the domain-incremental setting, as evident in its FWT and BWT scores. However, it struggles to capture the complexity of RGB images of diverse objects. This may be addressed by employing different backbones for our model, such as low-power CNNs, SNNs, or energy-based models
[
35
]
. However, it should be acknowledged that there is a trade-off among efficiency, power consumption, and performance in meeting the low-power requirements of edge devices.

In the domain-incremental version of SplitMNIST, our model outperformed all other models, achieving a 10% improvement over
m
BNN and a 6% improvement over state-of-the-art methods SDMLP and XdG. This further validates the effectiveness of engramBNNs under domain shifts. To better maintain performance on the earliest tasks, the metaplastic
m
hyperparameter can be increased, though this also affects peak performance on recent tasks. As with many scenarios in both continual learning and the brain, there is a balance between the stability of old information and the plasticity needed to take in new information.

The domain- and class-incremental experiments are task-agnostic because they do not require a task ID to be explicitly provided to the model (except for the XdG method, which requires it in all scenarios). Although we did not explore new strategies for real-time label generation or incorporate a noisy student approach here, we acknowledge that this is an important direction for future studies. Incorporating methods such as noisy student labeling or weak- or pseudo-labeling
[
77
]
could potentially overcome this limitation, but it requires careful consideration of the trade-offs between computational efficiency and accuracy in such settings.

The semantic separation between class representations was also validated through feature visualization, quantifiable clustering, and overlap metrics. Qualitative analysis during the Split MNIST task showed that clusters were more well-established with the gating module attached, and this was further supported by a higher silhouette score than the other models. Ablation studies also revealed that our model exhibited greater Jaccard overlap in neuron masks across tasks, suggesting that the gating module encourages more relevant, shared representations while still maintaining distinct task clusters. Additionally, RDMs introduced higher overall dissimilarity in the engramBNN, which is analogous to pattern separation in the hippocampus
[
34
]
. This resembles how sparse representations and separate activations enable the brain to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

Notably, our model achieves competitive or superior performance compared to other fully connected models while consuming over
8\times
less memory. This result demonstrates the efficiency and practicality of our architecture, especially in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices. Not only does our model significantly reduce memory overhead, but it also retains the most informative and relevant representations in a multitask environment. Reduced power consumption and faster inference times are both critical factors for on-device learning. Unlike conventional fully connected networks, which scale linearly, our model leverages both architectural sparsity and binary representations. This makes it more suitable for long-term deployment. Additionally, the fact that our model’s memory advantage does not compromise performance has implications for sparser, quantized, and more efficient architectures in continual learning. It also aligns with biological insights that our brain encodes information through sparse neural codes.

Our model has demonstrated a promising approach to tackling the challenges of resource-efficient continual learning. By incorporating biologically inspired mechanisms, we achieved superior efficiency while maintaining competitive performance across class- and domain-incremental setups. While these results are encouraging, future work will address the limitations of supervised learning in these environments. In this work, we based our model on an established metaplastic binarized neural network that has been validated previously as a continual learning strategy. This provided a reliable and accepted backbone for introducing and validating the engram mechanism. However, more recent state-of-the-art binary architectures exist that have not been explored in a continual learning context. Integrating the lifelong learning mechanisms investigated here with such networks represents an important direction for further enhancing the performance and efficiency of our method. Further investigations will continue to focus on refining the gating mechanism and exploring its impact in more complex, real-world environments, such as with brain signals. This work also opens the door to scaling continuous learning systems to more complex environments or longer tasks without concern for memory cost. Since this architecture targets edge devices, future work will extend this research to more deployment-oriented settings by integrating inference engines to enable faster inference and lower energy consumption. Additionally, we plan to conduct inference benchmarking on edge devices to evaluate the proposed method’s performance under realistic edge conditions.

Ultimately, this work lays the foundation for more sustainable, adaptive, efficient, and scalable models suitable for deployment in edge computing and dynamic learning environments.

Appendix B
Benchmark Details

\label

secA2

B.1
Dataset Augmentation

Across all experiments (Split MNIST, Split CIFAR, and CORe50), we applied standard data augmentation to the training sets prior to optimization.

•

MNIST:
Each image is converted to a tensor and normalized using the standard dataset mean of
0.1307
and standard deviation of
0.3081
. Unlike the natural image datasets, no additional spatial augmentations or color perturbations are applied.

•

CIFAR:
Each image is zero-padded by 4 pixels on all sides and randomly cropped back to its original
32\times 32
spatial resolution. Next, a random horizontal flip is applied with a probability of
0.5
. Finally, we apply a color jittering transformation that randomly perturbs the image brightness by a factor chosen uniformly from
[-63/255,+63/255]
.

•

CORe50:
Each image is subjected to a random resized crop, which extracts a random patch of the original image and resizes it to
224\times 224
pixels (utilizing the default PyTorch scale and aspect ratio bounds). This is followed by a random horizontal flip (
p=0.5
) and a brightness perturbation identical to the CIFAR-10 setup, where the brightness factor is jittered by a factor chosen uniformly from
[-63/255,+63/255]
.
