Interpretable Embeddings of Speech Enhance and Explain Brain Encoding Performance of Audio Models
Riki Shimizu, Richard J. Antonello, Chandan Singh, Nima Mesgarani

Abstract
Self-supervised speech models (SSMs) are increasingly hailed as more powerful computational models of human speech perception than models based on traditional hand-crafted features. However, since their representations are inherently black-box, it remains unclear what drives their alignment with brain responses. To remedy this, we built linear encoding models from six interpretable feature families: mel-spectrogram, Gabor filter bank features, speech presence, phonetic, syntactic, and semantic Question-Answering features, and contextualized embeddings from three state-of-the-art SSMs (Whisper, HuBERT, WavLM), quantifying the shared and unique neural variance captured by each feature class. Contrary to prevailing assumptions, our interpretable model predicted electrocorticography (ECoG) responses to speech more accurately than any SSM. Moreover, augmenting SSM representations with interpretable features yielded the best overall neural predictions, significantly outperforming either class alone. Further variance-partitioning analyses revealed previously unresolved components of SSM representations that contribute to their neural alignment: 1. Despite the common assumption that later layers of SSMs discard low-level acoustic information, these models compress and preferentially retain frequency bands critical for neural encoding of speech (100–1000 Hz). 2. Contrary to previous claims, SSMs encode brain-relevant semantic information that cannot be reduced to lower-level features, improving with context length and model size. These results highlight the importance of using refined, interpretable features in understanding speech perception.



1
Introduction

Self-supervised models have emerged as increasingly popular tools for studying how the brain processes language and speech. Numerous studies have employed large language models (LLMs) and demonstrated their effectiveness in predicting neural responses to narrative stimuli by linearly probing their internal representations
(
Toneva and Wehbe 2019
;
Goldstein et al. 2022
;
Caucheteux and King 2022
;
Antonello, Vaidya, and Huth 2023
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
;
Mischler et al. 2024
)
. Similarly, self-supervised speech models (SSMs) have been used to investigate neural representations underlying speech perception. These studies commonly conclude that, like LLMs, SSMs are highly effective in predicting brain responses to speech, often outperforming their interpretable baselines
(
Vaidya, Jain, and Huth 2022
;
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
;
Goldstein et al. 2025
;
Tuckute et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, these findings are interpreted as evidence that SSMs are more powerful and brain-like models of speech perception, with their superiority often attributed to their ability to integrate contextual information over time
(
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
;
Goldstein et al. 2025
)
.

However, despite the apparent success of SSMs in predicting brain responses, several critical challenges remain in drawing meaningful neuroscientific conclusions from these results. First, the goal of cognitive neuroscience is not to build computational models with the best predictive power, but to understand the mechanisms underlying such neural computation. While there has been growing effort to investigate what these models encode, much of the existing work has focused on characterizing their internal representations
(
Pasad, Chou, and Livescu 2022
;
Pasad, Shi, and Livescu 2023
;
Pasad et al. 2024
;
Ashihara et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Choi and Yeo 2022
;
Choi et al. 2024
)
, an important but distinct goal from identifying which aspects of their representations are relevant for neural processing. Techniques such as Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA), and linear probing with interpretable embeddings are commonly used to probe the internal structure of SSMs. However, these methods typically assess aggregate similarity across representational spaces and do not isolate specific components that are mechanistically meaningful for brain function. As a result, even when combined with strong neural prediction performance, such findings offer limited guidance for understanding how model representations map onto neural processes and may be prone to misinterpretation. Second, the predictive performance of SSMs may potentially be explained by missed, yet simple confounds. In the context of LLMs,
Hadidi et al. 2025
showed that most of the neural variance explained by GPT2-XL can be explained by simple confounds such as word-rate and positional information
(
Hadidi et al. 2025
)
. This raises the concern that SSMs, too, might be over-credited with encoding unique brain-relevant contextual information when, in fact, their performance is driven by simpler, interpretable confounds. Collectively, these challenges underscore the importance of disentangling what properties drive the predictive performance of SSMs and assessing the extent to which their contextual representations capture unique neural variance beyond a broad set of interpretable features.

A detailed examination of the existing empirical evidence further highlights these concerns. While there is robust evidence that SSMs yield better alignment in higher-order brain regions than simple acoustic baselines
(
Vaidya, Jain, and Huth 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
)
, the nature of the representations driving this alignment remains unclear, particularly with regard to whether they encode brain-relevant semantic information. For example, Anderson et al.
(
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
)
found that combining representations from Whisper’s later layers with those of GPT-2 did not improve alignment with EEG data compared to Whisper alone, suggesting that Whisper already encodes lexical content relevant for neural processing. In contrast, Oota et al.
(
Oota et al. 2024
)
found that after regressing out low-level acoustic and speech features, the representations of the SSMs showed no significant alignment with high-level language regions of the brain, indicating that these models may lack higher-order semantic information. We argue that these contrasting findings may stem from the indirect nature of current methodologies, which often involve inferring properties of one black box (SSMs) using another (the brain) without the use of interpretable controls, making interpretation inherently challenging.

In contrast to previous work, this study directly addresses these open questions using a variance partitioning approach applied to hand-crafted, interpretable features spanning low-level acoustic to high-level lexical domains. Specifically, we show that: 1. Handcrafted interpretable features can outperform SSM representations in predicting brain responses. Combining interpretable features and SSM representations leads to the best performance. 2. SSM representations retain brain-relevant low-level acoustic information across layers, with acoustic features becoming increasingly compact to accommodate higher-level representations. 3. SSMs encode semantic information that is relevant to neural processing, which improves with context length and model size.

2.1
Datasets

All results were derived using open-source data contained in the Podcast ECoG Dataset
(
Zada et al. 2025
)
. It contains electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings from nine patients with epilepsy who listened to a 30-minute excerpt of the podcast
This American Life
(“So a Monkey and a Horse Walk Into a Bar: Act One—Monkey in the Middle”). Of the 1,330 intracranial electrodes originally implanted, 1,268 were retained after excluding contacts with imprecise anatomical localization or excessive noise in their power-spectral density. Electrode positions were projected onto a template cortical surface for visualization. Full pre-processing details are provided in
(
Zada et al. 2025
)
.

We focused on broadband high-gamma activity (70–200 Hz), extracted with a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth band-pass IIR filter and converted to analytic amplitude. The resulting envelopes were down-sampled to 4 Hz and z-scored.

2.2
Interpretable auditory features

Mel-spectrogram

We computed mel-spectrograms from 0–8 kHz with 32 mel bins and further converted them to decibels using
librosa

(
McFee et al. 2015
)
.

Gabor bank filter features (GBFB)

Spectro-temporal modulation features were extracted with the Gabor filter-bank implementation of Schädler
et al

(
Schädler, Meyer, and Kollmeier 2012
)
.

Speech presence

Speech probability was estimated with EfficientAT, a CNN-based audio tagger
(
Schmid, Koutini, and Widmer 2023
)
.

Phonetic features

Each word in the transcript was converted to its ARPAbet phoneme representations using
nltk.corpus.cmudict

(
Bird and Loper 2004
)
. Every phoneme was one-hot encoded over the 39 ARPAbet symbols.

Semantic features

Following
Benara et al. 2024

Benara et al. 2024
, we obtained 168-dimensional question-answering (QA) semantic embeddings by prompting GPT-4 (
gpt-4-0125-preview
;
OpenAI 2023

OpenAI 2023
) with 56 yes/no questions about each word’s meaning (e.g.,
“Does the word/sentence involve an expression of personal values or beliefs?”
) with 3 different context lengths (word-level, 1.5 s, and 3 s). For the 1.5/3-second context length, the model was additionally provided with the words spoken in the 1.5/3 seconds preceding each target word when answering the questions. Because of how this feature is constructed, its effective context window can extend up to approximately 4.75 seconds—accounting for the 3-second input context, the additional 0.5-second summing window, and the maximum word duration (about 1.25 seconds for words like “approximately”). A full list of questions is provided in

.

Syntactic features

Following

Zada et al. 2025

2.4
Encoding Model Construction

After processing, the high gamma signals we predict have shape
[T,N_{ch}]
where
T
is the number of time points (i.e., 7200) and
N_{ch}
is the number of channels (i.e., 1268). To estimate the model performance, we used a 5-fold cross-validation scheme, splitting the signals into 5 temporally contiguous chunks. For each fold, the model was trained on
80\%
of the data and tested on the remaining
20\%
. Within the training folds, we employed a 4-fold nested cross-validation scheme to optimize the model parameters. Unless otherwise noted—specifically in analyses examining the impact of context size on encoding performance—the optimal context size was selected and used for each speech audio model.

Banded ridge regression

To create a joint encoding model with features from different feature spaces and dimensionalities, we employed banded ridge regression, which has been shown to improve neural encoding performance when using diverse feature spaces
(
Nunez-Elizalde, Huth, and Gallant 2019
)
. Unlike ridge regression with spherical Gaussian priors, banded ridge regression optimizes the regularization parameter alpha separately for each feature space. We used the
himalaya
library by Dupre La Tour
et al.

(
Dupré la Tour et al. 2022
)
. Each feature was z-scored before applying the regression model. Unless otherwise specified, we varied alpha over 20 logarithmically spaced points between
10^{0}
and
10^{3}
.

Performance Metrics

To measure the performance of the encoding models, we used correlation (Pearson’s
r
) and the variance explained
R^{2}
, two standard metrics for evaluating encoding models
(
Vaidya, Jain, and Huth 2022
;
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
;
Goldstein et al. 2025
;
Tuckute et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. Generally, we used Pearson’s
r
to compare the encoding model performance of different representations, while the variance explained
R^{2}
was used to quantify the unique and shared contributions of different features for neural prediction. Negative
R^{2}
values were clipped to 0, since negative explained variance lacks interpretability.

Variance Partitioning

To quantify the shared and unique variance contributions of feature sets, we used the variance partitioning method established by de Heer et al.
(
Heer et al. 2017
)
. For details, refer to
Section

S4
or
Heer et al. 2017

Heer et al. 2017
.

Computational Resources

All experiments and analyses were conducted on AMD EPYC 7662 CPUs and NVIDIA L40 GPUs, except for the generation of QA semantic features, which was performed using the OpenAI API. Cumulatively, the experiments required approximately 500 GPU hours.

QA features encode genuinely semantic information relevant for brain responses

Before we assess SSMs, to evaluate whether our QA features actually encode semantic information, we computed the unique variance
R^{2}
by each interpretable feature set in predicting GPT-2 representations (

Speech probability also explains a statistically significant amount of unique variance, though its contribution is markedly smaller in comparison. In contrast, GBFB and mel-spectrogram features do not explain any significant unique variance, as expected—GPT-2 is not trained on acoustic input and thus should not encode such information. The significant unique variance explained by our semantic features supports the claim that they indeed capture meaningful semantic content.

4
Discussion

Recent papers
(
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
;
Goldstein et al. 2025
)
have claimed a paradigm shift from context-invariant handcrafted features to SSM embeddings in building computational models of speech perception. However, our findings caution against treating SSM embeddings as a standalone complete model for speech perception. We showed that interpretable models alone can outperform SSMs in predicting brain responses, especially in brain regions responsible for processing low-level acoustic information. Our results showed that the combination of SSM embeddings and interpretable features achieved the best predictive performance, highlighting the importance of the continued use of interpretable features in building computational models of human speech perception.

One of our main contributions is to explicitly demonstrate that SSMs preferentially preserve low-level acoustic information within the speech critical bands (100 – 1000 Hz). Previous studies
(
Pasad, Chou, and Livescu 2022
;
Pasad, Shi, and Livescu 2023
)
on the layer-wise representation in SSMs analyzed acoustic information more broadly, without examining frequency-specific selectivity. To the best of our knowledge, the closest related findings are: Wang
et al.

(
Wang and Yamagishi 2022
)
showed that speech anti-spoofing systems based on SSMs were relatively robust to band-stop filtering of high frequencies but not to filtering in the 0-0.8 kHz range. Our results build on these observations by providing direct evidence of frequency-selective representations within SSMs, showing that the preservation of low-frequency components is not only critical for performance but also intrinsically encoded in the model’s internal representations.

Whether SSMs encode brain-relevant semantics is debated, with evidence supporting
(
Pasad, Chou, and Livescu 2022
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Anderson, Davis, and Lalor 2024
)
and challenging
(
Choi et al. 2024
;
Oota et al. 2024
)
. Our method employs an interpretable semantic embedding to directly test for the shared neural variance with SSMs, while controlling for lower-level acoustic and linguistic information. We show that SSM representations significantly reduce the unique variance explained by the semantic embedding, providing strong evidence that they indeed encode brain-relevant semantic information. However, an important nuance remains: SSMs account for only about 40% of the unique variance explained by the semantic features, indicating that their semantic representations remain partial. Recent studies
(
Moussa, Klakow, and Toneva 2025
;
Vattikonda et al. 2025
)
have shown that fine-tuning SSMs on fMRI data can improve their alignment with language regions of the brain. Thus, while our findings affirm the presence of semantic information in SSM representations, they also suggest room for improvement.

Limitations

1. Our findings are based on data from nine clinical ECoG participants who listened to a single 30‑min English podcast. While the high temporal resolution of ECoG and broad electrode coverage across brain regions make this dataset particularly well-suited for the present study, replication using larger and more diverse datasets will be important for assessing the generalizability of these results. 2. While we controlled for several lower-level features in our analysis of semantic encoding in SSMs, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that unaccounted confounds contribute to the shared variance between SSM representations and our semantic features. However, the observed effect of context size provides some evidence against this interpretation, as lower-level features typically operate over shorter integration windows and are therefore less sensitive to extended context. 3. While our interpretable features account for most of the neural variance captured by SSM representations, a statistically significant portion remains unexplained. Identifying the nature of this residual variance is an important direction for future research.

Supplementary Materials for ”Interpretable Embeddings of Speech Enhance and Explain Brain Encoding Performance of Audio Models”

S1
SSM Feature Extraction

Representations from self-supervised speech models (SSMs) were extracted using the code provided by Antonello et al. (available at
https://github.com/HuthLab/encoding-model-scaling-laws
). The input audio file, totaling 1800 seconds, was divided into five contiguous 360-second segments before being fed into the SSMs. This segmentation was performed to prevent data leakage between training and test folds in the 5-fold cross-validation scheme.

We used the largest variants of each SSM (Whisper, WavLM, and HuBERT) because prior findings from Antonello
et al.

(
Antonello, Vaidya, and Huth 2023
)
showed that the encoding performance of the brain by SSMs increased logarithmically with the number of model parameters. The details of each model are shown in Table.
S1
.

S2
Interpretable auditory features

Full details of the processing procedures for each interpretable feature set are provided below.

Mel-spectrogram

We computed mel-spectrograms from 0–8 kHz with 32 mel bins and further converted them to decibels using
librosa

(
McFee et al. 2015
)
. To create a feature vector for time
t
s, we concatenated the frames spanning [
t-0.5
,
t
]s, yielding a 672-dimensional vector. Dimensionality was reduced by principal-component analysis (PCA), retaining 95% of the variance.

Gabor bank filter features (GBFB)

Spectro-temporal modulation features were extracted with the Gabor filter-bank implementation of Schädler
et al

(
Schädler, Meyer, and Kollmeier 2012
)
. The 455-dimensional features, computed at 100 Hz, were down-sampled to 4 Hz to align with the neural data; PCA (95% variance) was applied prior to modelling.

Speech presence

Speech probability was estimated with EfficientAT, a CNN-based audio tagger
(
Schmid, Koutini, and Widmer 2023
)
. To produce a feature scalar for time
t
s, we fed the model the audio spanning [
t-0.5
,
t
]s.

Phonetic features

Each word in the transcript was converted to its ARPAbet phoneme representations using
nltk.corpus.cmudict

(
Bird and Loper 2004
)
. Word intervals were evenly subdivided among their
N
phones, assigning on- and offset times to every phoneme. Every phoneme was one-hot encoded over the 39 ARPAbet symbols. To create a feature vector for time
t
s, the embeddings for every phoneme whose offset time lies in [
t-0.5
,
t
]s were summed, producing a
T\times 39
matrix.

Semantic features

Following
Benara et al. 2024

Benara et al. 2024
, we obtained 168-dimensional question-answering (QA) semantic embeddings by prompting GPT-4 (
gpt-4-0125-preview
;
OpenAI 2023

OpenAI 2023
) with 56 yes/no questions about each word’s meaning (e.g.,

t

,
t

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\mathcal{B}_{\textbf{unique}}}

\displaystyle=R^{2}_{\mathcal{(A,B)}}-R^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}

S5
Effect of context length on brain prediction performance

Among the context sizes tested, the context size with the highest average correlation was 30 seconds for Whisper and HuBERT, and 5 seconds for WavLM (
Fig.

S4
). This variability highlights the need for caution when interpreting the influence of context length on encoding performance. For instance, Anderson
et al.
found that EEG encoding performance with Whisper peaked at context lengths of 5–10 seconds and interpreted this as evidence that EEG preferentially reflects the encoding of speech within that temporal window. However, it is essential to distinguish whether such patterns reflect inherent properties of neural processing or are instead artifacts of model-specific biases.
