Educational quality of YouTube videos on robot-assisted thymectomy: a LAP-VEGaS-based evaluation
Authors: Okan Karataş, Ayşegül Güler, Nilay Çavuşoğlu Yalçın, Muharrem Özkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVideo-based learning has become an integral component of surgical training, especially with the growing implementation of minimally invasive techniques such as robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS). However, the educational value of freely accessible online videos remains unclear. We aimed to assess the educational quality of the most-viewed YouTube videos on RATS thymectomy using a structured evaluation framework and to analyze the relationship between video-related parameters and educational performance.MethodsA structured search of YouTube was conducted on March 11, 2026, using the predefined search term “robotic thymectomy.” The 100 most-viewed videos were screened, and 38 videos met the inclusion criteria. Videos were assessed by two experienced thoracic surgeons using the 9-item LAP-VEGaS (Laparoscopic Surgery Video Educational Guidelines) scoring system, which was applied as a structured framework for evaluating general surgical video educational quality. Video characteristics were recorded and analyzed in relation to educational quality.ResultsThe mean LAP-VEGaS score was 9.76 ± 3.42 (range: 4–16), and only 17 videos (44.7%) achieved a score ≥ 11, indicating acceptable educational quality. Most videos were uploaded by individual surgeons and were predominantly of 720p resolution. View count was positively correlated with likes and time since upload but showed no significant association with LAP-VEGaS score.ConclusionAmong highly viewed English-language YouTube videos on RATS thymectomy, a considerable proportion did not meet expected standards for structured surgical education. Common popularity metrics, particularly view count, are not reliable indicators of educational value. Structured and standardized approaches to video creation should be promoted to enhance the overall quality and trustworthiness of online surgical education materials.

Introduction

Thymic tumors are neoplasms that arise from thymic epithelial cells and are broadly categorized into thymomas and thymic carcinomas. Thymomas generally exhibit a slow growth pattern and are characterized primarily by local invasion; metastases are frequently confined to the pleura and pericardium. While complete (R0) surgical resection forms the cornerstone of treatment in early-stage disease, the therapeutic approach for advanced cases is more complex and necessitates multidisciplinary management [1, 2].

Thymectomy is the fundamental surgical approach indicated for the treatment of primary thymus pathologies, including thymoma, thymic cysts, and particularly myasthenia gravis (MG) [3].

Traditionally, the standard surgical approach for thymic pathologies has been total thymectomy via sternotomy (TORA); however, this approach is relatively invasive and may be associated with longer recovery times. In recent years, with advancements in minimally invasive techniques, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and subsequently robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) have become increasingly common alternative approaches in thymectomy. Consequently, multiple surgical options are currently available for thymectomy [3, 4].

The Da Vinci robotic surgical system offers enhanced surgical maneuverability through three-dimensional imaging, high-resolution magnification, tremor filtration, and instruments with seven degrees of freedom. Following FDA approval in 2000, it has been utilized across broad surgical fields. Due to its theoretical advantages over VATS, RATS is increasingly gaining prominence in thoracic surgery [5, 6].

In surgical education, video-based instruction has been demonstrated as an effective method supporting knowledge acquisition and technical skill development among medical students and surgical trainees. Recently, surgical education has evolved from a time-based approach toward a competency-based model, undergoing significant transformations in educational methodologies. Increasing requirements for patient safety and restrictions on resident working hours have intensified the need for extra-operative educational strategies. In this context, video-based learning emerges as a low-cost and easily accessible adjunctive tool. Particularly in robotic surgery, the absence of haptic feedback emphasizes learning based on visual cues, further increasing the importance of video content in training. Additionally, due to images obtained directly from the console, video can be utilized as a powerful e-learning tool in robotic surgery education [7, 8].

YouTube (Google, Mountain View, California, USA), widely used among surgical residents worldwide, stands out as one of the frequently consulted and easily accessible video-sharing platforms during the preparation process for surgical procedures. However, health-related content provided on online platforms exhibits significant heterogeneity, and existing studies reveal that YouTube videos often fail to meet standard educational criteria [9].

Accordingly, the Laparoscopic Surgery Video Educational Guidelines (LAP-VEGaS) consensus guide was developed with the contribution of an international and multidisciplinary group of instructors and residents to assess the educational quality of laparoscopic surgery videos [10].

Aim

This study aimed to assess the educational quality of the 100 most-viewed YouTube videos on robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) thymectomy using the LAP-VEGaS criteria.

Materials and methods

A structured search of YouTube was conducted on March 11, 2026, using the predefined search term “robotic thymectomy.” This term was selected because it represents a concise and commonly used phrase for the target procedure on YouTube. No additional keywords, synonymous terms, or Boolean operators were used in order to maintain a focused, reproducible, and user-oriented platform-based search strategy. This approach was intended to reflect the type of simple search that trainees or surgeons might perform when looking for RATS thymectomy videos on YouTube, rather than to provide a comprehensive systematic search of all possible online video content. To reduce algorithm-driven personalization, the search was performed in incognito mode without logging into any user account, following the clearing of browser cache. Standard YouTube settings were retained, with no changes made to location detection, language preferences, or regional filters.

The 100 most-viewed videos were selected as a pragmatic and reproducible visibility-based sampling strategy to capture content that users are most likely to encounter during a standard YouTube search. This strategy was not intended to represent all available RATS thymectomy videos on YouTube. View count was therefore considered a marker of platform visibility and user exposure rather than an indicator of educational quality. After application of the predefined exclusion criteria, 62 videos were excluded and 38 videos remained eligible for final analysis. Videos were assessed by two thoracic surgeons for relevance and eligibility. Cartoons, animations, non-procedural content, and videos in languages other than English were excluded.

The following variables were extracted for each video: title, URL, number of views, number of likes, time since upload (days), video duration (minutes), image quality (resolution), number of subscribers, uploader category, and LAP-VEGaS score.

Image quality was classified based on resolution (pixels) as reported on the YouTube platform, and no subjective visual assessment scale was used. Video duration was evaluated in minutes, and the duration online was evaluated in days.

Videos were assessed according to the LAP-VEGaS criteria [10], comprising nine items. Each item was scored as 0 points if not fulfilled, 1 point if partially fulfilled, and 2 points if fully fulfilled, yielding a maximum total score of 18. The nine scoring items and the operational definitions used for each score level are presented in Table 1. To improve consistency, two experienced thoracic surgeons independently evaluated all videos after a standardization session in which the scoring criteria were reviewed and adapted to the context of RATS thymectomy without modifying the original LAP-VEGaS scoring structure.

LAP-VEGaS Laparoscopic Surgery Video Educational Guidelines, RATS Robot-assisted thoracic surgery

The LAP-VEGaS scoring framework was based on the original criteria developed by Celentano et al. [10]. The original LAP-VEGaS structure and 0–2 scoring system were preserved. In the context of RATS thymectomy, relevant items were interpreted with attention to robotic access, port placement, docking, console-view dissection, and bedside assistant coordination when these elements were available in the video. No additional numerical items were added

Although the LAP-VEGaS tool was originally developed and validated for laparoscopic surgery videos, it was used in the present study as a structured framework to assess the general educational quality, transparency, and instructional completeness of robot-assisted surgical videos rather than as a robot-specific technical competency scale. Before formal scoring, the two reviewers conducted a standardization session to harmonize interpretation of the LAP-VEGaS items in the context of RATS thymectomy. During this process, general LAP-VEGaS domains such as case introduction, demonstration of relevant anatomy, procedural stepwise organization, operative field visualization, and reporting of outcomes were interpreted with attention to robotic thymectomy-specific elements when available, including robotic access, port placement and docking, mediastinal anatomical orientation, console-view dissection, and the role of the bedside assistant. No additional numerical items were added to the original LAP-VEGaS score in order to preserve comparability with previous studies using the same scoring system.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two experienced thoracic surgeons independently assessed all included videos after a standardization session designed to harmonize interpretation of the LAP-VEGaS criteria. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.826), demonstrating strong concordance. After independent scoring, discrepant item-level ratings were reviewed jointly by the two assessors and resolved through consensus discussion based on the predefined operational definitions of the LAP-VEGaS items. No third reviewer was required because all discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

The primary outcome measure was the educational quality of the included videos, assessed using the total LAP-VEGaS score. In addition, the proportion of videos achieving a LAP-VEGaS score of ≥ 11 was evaluated as a predefined indicator of acceptable educational quality, based on the previously validated LAP-VEGaS threshold.

Secondary outcome measures included video-related descriptive and popularity variables: number of views, number of likes, time since upload, video duration, image quality/resolution, number of channel subscribers, and uploader category. Secondary analyses also examined the relationships between LAP-VEGaS score and video-related variables, as well as the associations between view count and other popularity or technical characteristics.

Descriptive statistics were generated for all video-related variables and scores. Distribution normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Associations between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Because of the relatively small final sample size, correlation analyses were considered exploratory. Therefore, statistically significant associations were interpreted with caution, and no multivariable modelling was performed because of the risk of overfitting and unstable estimates.

Results

This study evaluated the educational quality of widely viewed YouTube videos on RATS thymectomy using the LAP-VEGaS criteria. The primary outcome was educational quality as assessed by the LAP-VEGaS score. Compliance with the LAP-VEGaS framework was assessed across 9 criteria. The mean LAP-VEGaS score was 9.76 ± 3.42, ranging from 4 to 16. A total of 17 videos achieved a LAP-VEGaS score ≥ 11.

Among the evaluated features, adequate visualization of the operative field with consistently clear image quality, together with smooth video flow at an appropriate pace, was the most commonly fulfilled characteristic. The least frequently fulfilled characteristic was the reporting of relevant procedural outcomes, including operative duration, postoperative complications, and histopathological findings. At the video level, the highest LAP-VEGaS score was obtained by video number 35, whereas the lowest scores were observed in videos number 29 and 32. The relationships between LAP-VEGaS scores and video characteristics were also analyzed (Table 3). No statistically significant correlation was found between view count and LAP-VEGaS score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.307, p = 0.061). However, a significant positive correlation was identified between the number of likes and the LAP-VEGaS score (ρ = 0.352, p = 0.030).

Discussion

The present study found that the educational quality of highly viewed YouTube videos on RATS thymectomy was heterogeneous and frequently below the expected standard for structured surgical education. The mean LAP-VEGaS score was 9.76 ± 3.42, and fewer than half of the included videos achieved the predefined threshold for acceptable educational quality. These findings suggest that many highly visible RATS thymectomy videos do not provide sufficient educational structure, particularly with respect to procedural context, stepwise explanation, and reporting of postoperative outcomes or learning points.

The contrast between video 35, which achieved the highest LAP-VEGaS score, and videos 29 and 32, which achieved the lowest scores, further illustrates that educational quality was determined primarily by content structure rather than image quality alone. Video 35 received high scores across several domains, including author/source information, operative setup, stepwise procedural demonstration, anatomical orientation, graphics, commentary, and image quality. In contrast, videos 29 and 32 lacked key educational components such as operative setup or port-placement information, demonstration of relevant anatomy, postoperative outcomes, graphics, and explanatory commentary. Notably, video 32 had adequate image quality but still received a low total score, indicating that visual clarity alone does not ensure educational value when structured narration, anatomical orientation, and outcome reporting are absent.

In this context, surgical content on YouTube has become a widely used and easily accessible educational resource for surgeons and trainees worldwide. The present study contributes to the existing literature by evaluating the educational quality of RATS thymectomy videos available on YouTube. A reproducible approach was used, focusing on the 100 most-viewed videos; video selection was based on view counts, with predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria implemented to support methodological robustness. This approach supports reproducibility and reflects the content most likely to be encountered by users; however, it should not be interpreted as a comprehensive assessment of all available robotic thymectomy videos on YouTube.

The use of view count as a selection criterion requires careful interpretation. View count was used as a measure of visibility rather than educational quality. This distinction is important because highly viewed videos may not necessarily be the most informative, technically current, or pedagogically complete. In addition, videos uploaded earlier have had more time to accumulate views, which may favor older content and potentially exclude recently uploaded videos with better image resolution, more contemporary robotic techniques, and improved educational structure. In the present study, this concern was supported by the positive correlation between view count and time since upload, as well as the negative correlation between view count and image quality. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect the quality of highly visible YouTube content rather than the overall quality of all available RATS thymectomy videos.

A notable finding was the significant positive correlation between the number of likes and LAP-VEGaS score, whereas view count was not significantly associated with educational quality. This may indicate that likes represent a more selective form of engagement than views. View count may largely reflect visibility, search ranking, duration of online availability, and algorithmic exposure, while likes may require a more deliberate viewer response. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the association was weak-to-moderate and may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors such as video age, audience composition, uploader visibility, audience reach, and platform exposure.

The negative correlation between video resolution and view count should also be interpreted in light of potential temporal confounding. Older videos have had more time to accumulate views and may also have been uploaded during periods when lower video resolutions were more common. Therefore, the inverse association between resolution and view count should not be interpreted as evidence that lower-resolution videos are more attractive or educationally superior. Rather, it likely reflects the combined effects of video age, accumulated platform exposure, and changes in video quality standards over time.

Various assessment tools with different purposes and strengths have been utilized in the literature to evaluate the educational quality of surgical videos on YouTube. Beyond LAP-VEGaS, several alternative assessment tools have been described, including the Critical View of Safety (CVS), JAMA criteria, and the Global Quality Score (GQS). Among these, LAP-VEGaS demonstrates closer alignment with the educational objectives of thoracic surgery, as it emphasizes the presentation of intraoperative anatomy, step-by-step procedural structure, and detailed technical aspects.

An important methodological consideration is the use of LAP-VEGaS in the assessment of RATS thymectomy videos. LAP-VEGaS was originally designed and validated for laparoscopic surgery videos; therefore, it does not fully capture several educational dimensions that are specific to robot-assisted surgery, including docking strategy, robotic arm configuration, instrument articulation, console ergonomics, camera control, bedside assistant coordination, and the lack of haptic feedback. Nevertheless, RATS thymectomy remains a minimally invasive video-based procedure, and many core educational requirements—clear operative field visualization, stepwise presentation, anatomical orientation, disclosure of patient and procedural details, and reporting of outcomes—are shared with laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgical education. For this reason, LAP-VEGaS was applied as a structured video-quality framework rather than as a comprehensive robot-specific skills assessment instrument. This approach allowed standardized comparison with previous surgical video-quality studies while acknowledging that a dedicated robotic surgery video assessment tool would provide a more procedure-specific evaluation.

In the original validation study of the LAP-VEGaS tool, ROC analysis demonstrated that a threshold score of ≥ 11 was strongly associated with expert recommendation for publication or conference presentation, with a sensitivity of 94% and specificity of 73% [10]. This validated cutoff, representing acceptable educational quality, was therefore adopted in the present study as a reference standard for evaluating the performance of RATS thymectomy videos.

The findings indicate that a notable proportion of videos may fall below the minimum standards for structured surgical education, with relatively low compliance across both CVS and LAP-VEGaS criteria. These results do not represent an isolated issue but rather reflect a broader trend regarding the overall quality of surgical video content available online. Indeed, similar findings have been reported across various surgical fields.

For instance, analyses of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, VATS segmentectomy, RATS lobectomy, and thoracoscopic segmentectomy videos have demonstrated low adherence to LAP-VEGaS criteria, with no significant association between view counts or overall popularity and educational quality [11–14]. Likewise, evaluations of other minimally invasive procedures, such as endoscopic choanal atresia repair and laparoscopic nephrectomy, have also reported low compliance rates [15, 16].

Consistent with previous studies, no significant relationship was identified between view count and LAP-VEGaS score [17, 18]. Although the correlation between view count and LAP-VEGaS score approached statistical significance, it did not meet the predefined significance threshold and should not be interpreted as evidence of a reliable association. This finding further supports the view that popularity metrics alone may be misleading when selecting videos for educational purposes. Standardized and validated tools such as LAP-VEGaS may therefore help assess existing video content and guide the development of higher-quality surgical videos aligned with formal educational standards.

Robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) represents an advanced form of minimally invasive surgery and requires substantial anatomical knowledge as well as three-dimensional dissection skills. In this context, the consistency and standardization of video-based educational materials are particularly important. However, our findings suggest that many videos lack key elements, such as preoperative information, surgeon identification, documentation of patient consent, discussion of potential complications, and a structured presentation of procedural steps. These limitations may reduce the educational value of the videos and could have implications for safe clinical practice.

An additional contextual factor is the probable heterogeneity in the intended purpose of the uploaded videos. Many YouTube surgical videos may not have been created as comprehensive educational modules, but rather as brief operative demonstrations, promotional materials, case highlights, or examples of technical feasibility. Such videos may reasonably omit elements that are central to LAP-VEGaS scoring, including detailed preoperative information, operative setup, stepwise explanation, discussion of complications, and postoperative outcomes. Therefore, low LAP-VEGaS scores should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of poor surgical performance or poor technical video quality. Instead, they may reflect a mismatch between the intended purpose of the uploaded content and the requirements of a structured educational assessment tool. This issue represents an important contextual limitation when evaluating open-access surgical videos on YouTube.

Overall, these findings suggest that, in their current form, online surgical videos may be insufficient to fully address existing educational needs. However, when developed in accordance with established standards and guidelines, they hold substantial potential as valuable educational resources. Therefore, content creators should be encouraged to adopt structured evaluation frameworks and prioritize educational quality in video production. Additionally, the selective and critical use of such content within surgical training programs may enhance learning outcomes.

Beyond confirming that popularity metrics do not reliably reflect educational quality, the present study provides procedure-specific insight into the educational gaps in publicly available RATS thymectomy videos. Based on the LAP-VEGaS domains that were least frequently fulfilled, RATS thymectomy videos intended for educational use should ideally include a minimum set of structured elements: author and institutional information, relevant patient and disease context, preoperative imaging or work-up, patient positioning, port placement and docking strategy, stepwise procedural narration, clear demonstration of mediastinal anatomy, explanation of key technical maneuvers and decision points, and postoperative outcomes or learning points. Incorporating such elements may improve the educational utility of videos uploaded to open-access platforms and help distinguish structured teaching material from brief operative demonstrations or promotional highlights.

As an open-access platform with user-generated content, YouTube hosts surgical videos with variable educational structure and limited standardization. In contrast, dedicated surgical education platforms and video journals may provide more structured formats through editorial oversight, peer-review processes, or standardized submission requirements. For example, WebSurg states that submitted videos are evaluated by a peer-review committee before publication [19], while the Journal of Medical Insight describes itself as a peer-reviewed surgical video journal publishing high-definition video articles [20]. Similarly, the AATS Surgical Video Library presents peer-reviewed surgical videos from JTCVS and AATS Journals [21]. Therefore, rather than suggesting that all non-YouTube platforms are uniformly superior, our findings support the need for clearer educational standards and structured reporting elements in surgical videos uploaded to open-access platforms such as YouTube.

This study has several strengths, including its focus on an advanced procedure such as RATS thymectomy and the application of a validated, widely recognized assessment tool (LAP-VEGaS). Nevertheless, certain limitations should be considered. Restricting the analysis to the 100 most-viewed videos is an important limitation. Although this strategy was chosen to reflect the videos most visible to users, it may have introduced selection bias toward older and highly visible content. Videos uploaded earlier had more time to accumulate views, whereas recently uploaded, lower-visibility, or niche academic videos with potentially higher educational quality may have been excluded. This limitation is particularly relevant because view count was positively associated with time since upload and negatively associated with image quality in the present analysis. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as an assessment of highly visible YouTube videos on RATS thymectomy rather than a comprehensive evaluation of all available educational videos on this topic.

The external validity of the study is limited by several design-related factors. The analysis included only English-language videos, was restricted to a single platform (YouTube), was conducted at a single time point, and focused on a highly procedure-specific topic, namely RATS thymectomy. These factors may introduce language-related, platform-specific, and selection biases and may limit the applicability of the findings to other surgical procedures, other video-sharing platforms, non-English content, or future time periods. This is particularly relevant because online video content is dynamic, and search results, view counts, likes, and available videos may change over time. To reduce the effect of temporal variability, all video characteristics were recorded at the time of data extraction. In addition, the intended purpose of each video could not be reliably determined. Some videos may have been uploaded primarily as promotional or highlight content rather than as formal educational material, which may have influenced their LAP-VEGaS scores. Although the scoring process involves a degree of subjectivity, this was addressed through independent evaluation by two reviewers.

Another limitation is that LAP-VEGaS was not originally validated for robot-assisted surgery. Although the scoring items were interpreted in the context of RATS thymectomy after reviewer standardization, the tool may not fully evaluate robotic-specific educational components such as docking, port geometry, robotic arm management, console ergonomics, and bedside assistant coordination. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as an assessment of general surgical video educational quality rather than a comprehensive evaluation of robotic surgical training content.

No formal sample size or statistical power calculation was performed because this was a descriptive cross-sectional analysis of publicly available online video content rather than a hypothesis-driven clinical study. The final sample size was determined by the number of videos meeting the predefined eligibility criteria. Therefore, the statistical analyses should be interpreted as exploratory, and the findings should not be overgeneralized beyond the sampled videos. The relatively small final sample size also limits the statistical power and robustness of the correlation analyses. In particular, the observed associations, including the weak-to-moderate correlation between likes and LAP-VEGaS score, should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

In conclusion, among the highly viewed English-language YouTube videos included in this analysis, a considerable proportion did not meet the expected standards for structured surgical education. Evaluation based on the LAP-VEGaS criteria revealed considerable heterogeneity in educational quality, with many videos lacking essential instructional components. Commonly used popularity metrics, particularly view count, were not found to reflect educational value. However, because the sample was limited to the 100 most-viewed videos identified at a single time point, the results should be interpreted as exploratory and reflective of highly visible content rather than all available RATS thymectomy videos on YouTube.

Beyond confirming that accessibility and popularity alone may not reliably indicate educational quality, the present study provides procedure-specific insight into educational gaps in robotic thymectomy videos and highlights the need for a minimum structured reporting framework for robotic surgical videos uploaded to open-access platforms. For robotic thymectomy videos, such a framework should include patient and disease context, preoperative planning, port placement and docking strategy, stepwise procedural explanation, anatomical orientation, technical decision-making, and postoperative outcomes or learning points. Therefore, both content creators and educators should prioritize the development and dissemination of standardized video content guided by structured assessment frameworks, while future research should focus on developing and validating robot-assisted surgery-specific video evaluation tools.

While video-based learning represents a valuable adjunct in surgical education, its effectiveness depends on the availability of standardized content, critical content selection, and adherence to established educational frameworks.
