Microfinger robot with inhaling channels for active sampling and analyzing gaseous and liquid substances toward source localization
Authors: Satoshi Konishi, Fumiya Sano, Yuki Fujimoto, Tomohiro Nakatsuka, Jin Iguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional humanoid technology has primarily focused on human tactile senses such as force and temperature. Previously, we introduced a microfinger robot integrated with force and temperature sensors for active sensing. In addition to sensors for mimicking human senses, artificial fingers can be functionalized with various sensors. This study proposes microfinger robot which is integrated with inhaling channels for actively sampling and analyzing gaseous and liquid substances. The microfinger can be even combined with an external advanced analyzer for analyzing samples. The microfinger (12 mm × 3 mm × 550 μm) incorporates pneumatic balloon actuators for upward and downward bending together with a strain sensor for detecting its motion. Inhaling microchannels, inspired by an elephant trunk, enable sample collection from an inlet at the microfinger tip. The active sensing performance is comprehensively examined, demonstrating that the microfinger can effectively search spatial distribution of samples diffused from chemical sources. Ethanol and colorant are used as gaseous and liquid source, respectively. The proposed smart microfinger, functionalized with sampling channels for active sensing beyond conventional sensor integration, offers a promising platform for advanced exploration and analysis of complex targets in microscale environments.

Introduction

In general, sensors enable the detection of various environmental information related to the atmosphere, infrastructure, and human body. Most high-performance analyzers are too large to be deployed on site for in situ measurements. In contrast, downsized microsensors can be set placed at desired locations of interest places or be integrated into targets for in-situ signal detection from targeted objectives. Such microsensors are also well suited for functionalizing robots and infrastructures without disrupting their fundamental functions, which is a common requirement with wearable sensors for human body. Wearable sensors can detect physiological parameters in real time1–4. Flexible and wearable sensors have been designed to monitor human vital signs, such as body temperature, heart rate, respiration rate, blood pressure, pulse oxygenation, and blood glucose levels1. Electronic skin, as well as epidermal and implantable medical sensing devices, utilize flexible, stretchable, and lightweight materials for biosensing. Notably, wearable sensors have limitations in sensing the information under the skin in comparison with biopsied sampling methods. Microneedle devices featuring electrochemical and optical sensors have been developed for point-of-care diagnostics and personalized medicine2. These sensors can detect various biomarkers, including electrolytes, metabolites, polysaccharides, nucleic acids, proteins, and drugs. By penetrating tissue and collecting samples such as blood and sweat, microneedles enable minimally invasive and real-time detection, including the measurement of the low concentrations of biomarkers3,4. Sensors integrated in limited spaces of wearable devices have restrictions in the number of diagnosis items. In comparison, hospital-based blood analysis instruments can precisely examine multiple analysis using biopsied blood. Conventional syringes enable the collection of sufficient amounts of blood, whereas wearable microneedle devices can acquire only a limited blood volume. Similarly, tissue sampling via endoscopy is restricted in the amount of sample that can be collected5. Although endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle biopsy and aspiration are effective diagnostic tools for pancreatic cancer. Biopsy is also the standard of care for the diagnosis of, as well as disorders of the stomach and small intestine, they present challenges in pediatric patients. Therefore, various non-endoscopic methods, including forceps, tubes, and capsules, have been developed to obtain biopsy samples6. Consequently, this study primarily aims at solving the drawbacks of integrating functions such as sensors into limited space. This study proposes microfinger robot which is integrated with inhaling channels for actively sampling and analyzing the samples by an external advanced analyzer beyond the conventional functions of microfinger robot limited to integrated sensors.

This study focuses on chemical sensing associated with smell and taste, senses which are not present in the human finger. In prior studies of chemical sensing functions for robots, mobile robots have been equipped with smell-sensing capabilities for the localization and mapping of odor or gas sources7,8. Unmanned ground vehicles have been widely used for field experiments with early research focusing on algorithms leveraging chemical gradients for localizing gas sources. Specifically, unmanned ground vehicles were used for the purpose of gas source localization and mapping. In general, unmanned ground vehicles have been applied for long-term measurements, such as mapping of the distribution of methane at landfill sites, detection of chemicals in underground mines, gas-pipeline leak detection, and near-ground gas source localization7. Robots with electronic-noses (E-noses) have been used for mapping distributions of multiple chemical compounds. Notably, these platforms are required to be sufficiently large to incorporate sensors such as pumped e-noses, LiDAR, ultrasonic anemometers, and global positioning systems. Recent studies have also reported increasing use of hardware platforms, such as quadruped robots and advanced sensor systems for practical odor source localization8. Recently, aerial mobile robots (drones) have also been applied to odor source localization in air for environmental and security monitoring9–14. Pocket-sized quadcopters have been evaluated for chemical plume tracing using odor sensing9. Studies have shown that the search success rate can be improved through exploration in a wide range or cooperative operation of multiple quadcopters. Electroantennogram (EAG) have also been applied for odor-tracking using mobile robots owing to their rapid response, prompt recovery, and high sensitivity11. In addition to mobile methods, the sensor array was reported for the visualization of the spatial distribution of the gas evaporating from an odor source15. The 3 × 3 gas sensor array was developed based on Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) technique. The detection area covered 15 mm × 15 mm area and 1225(35 × 35) Raman spectra were obtained from the sensor array for creating a heatmap graph of the spatial gas distribution. SEARS sensor is advantageous for biomarker detection in a complex biological matrix. Recent progress on the applications of SERS imaging in clinical diagnostics was reviewed in16. For example, imaging of a structurally complex 3D biological sample was demonstrated by using alternating layers of SERS tag-loaded fibroblasts17.

This study is interested in integrating chemical sensing function in a finger of robot, especially a microfinger robot. Humanoid robots are commonly equipped with various sensors to detect environmental signals for motion control and task control in industrial settings18–20. In contrast to macroscopic humanoid robots, microrobots are expected to operate in restricted spaces, such as the interiors of pipes for infrastructure inspection or within the human body for minimally invasive medical applications. In the literature of our soft microfinger robot, tactile sensors have been integrated with an artificial muscle actuator for exploring further potential applications. A pneumatic balloon actuator (PBA) composed of polymer materials was used as the artificial muscle21,22. Liquid metal strain gauges, as force sensors23–25, and thermocouples, as temperature sensor26, were integrated into the microfinger for tactile sensing. In general, a microfinger featuring tactile sensors can enable active sensing through motion driven by PBAs. The integrated sensors can be actively manipulated to approach and touch a target object, thereby improving signal intensity. Additionally, this microfinger can mechanically stimulate a target object through motion and contact. In our previous work27, contact between a microfinger and a pill bug induced a phenomenon in which the pill bug reacted and pushed back against the microfinger. Using a similar principle, when an organ is pushed by a microfinger, resulting deformation can be used to estimate tissue stiffness. A microfinger with tactile sensing has also been applied to evaluate distributed characteristics of a targeted object, where the microfinger was moved by a positioner and bent for touch sensing. For example, a microfinger with a temperature sensor was used to explore the position of a heat source in a temperature distribution field28. The position of the heat source on a flat surface was estimated using an algorithm based on the Poisson equation. Temperature touch sensing by the moving microfinger was repeatedly performed using this algorithm to localize the heat source. Furthermore, a multipoint touch-sensing system using a microfinger was developed to localize stiffness singularities within an object, assumed to represent tumors in tissue or organs29–31. Bending actuation of the microfinger allowed it to approach an object and perform push-in motions to enable touch-sensing. A bending PBA with a conversion mechanism was used owing to high force output19. A microfinger integrated with sensors can thus be used to localize a potential source or singular point following the coarse searching by other systems, such as a robot arm.

In this study, building on our previous work on microfinger robots for active sensing of force and temperature, we introduce a novel platform for the active sampling and analysis of chemical substances, endowing capabilities of smell and taste sensing20–23. Sampling by inhaling channels and analyzing the samples by an external advanced analyzer will contribute to solve the drawbacks of the conventional functions of microfinger robot limited to integrated sensors. A microchannel mimicking an elephant trunk is designed to collect samples at a distance (Fig. 1a). An artificial muscle drives the microfinger for searching motions, whereas the microchannels inhale gaseous or liquid samples. A microfinger implemented at the tip of a positioner can bend upward and downward to perform fine localization of a potential source, in combination with coarse positioning by the positioner, as shown in Fig. 1b and c. An advanced analyzer can be connected at the root of the microchannel, allowing a wider selection of analyzers beyond those than that be integrated into the microfinger.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of microfinger integrated with PBA and microchannels

Inhaling microchannels were integrated into a microfinger (12 mm × 3 mm × 550 μm) together with PBAs thorough the fabrication process shown in Fig. 2. PBAs were designed to enable upward and downward bending at both sides of the microfinger. Three structural layers, including balloon layers (Fig. 2a), a buffer layer (Fig. 2b), and channel layers (Fig. 2c), were prepared and bonded to form the final microfinger structure.

Balloon and channel films were fabricated by molding polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Silpot 184, Dow Corning Inc., USA) using photoresist (SU-8) molds on Si substrates (Fig. 2a–c). PDMS with a prepolymer-to–curing agent ratio of 12:1 was used for all films, where the ratio was selected for reducing the structural stiffness. SU-8 3050 and SU-8 2075 photoresists were used to fabricate molds for the balloon and channel PDMS structures, respectively. Five PDMS films, including two balloon films (one for each side), one buffer film, and two channel films, were bonded using surface activation by vacuum ultraviolet light, as shown in Fig. 2d–e. External connections for PBA pressure supply and sampling via inhaling channels were implemented to complete the fabrication process.

This fabrication process resulted in a 12 mm × 3 mm × 550 μm microfinger, as shown in Fig. 3a. PBAs and inhaling channels were integrated into the microfinger. The outlet of the inhaling channels and pressure supply ports for the PBAs were incorporated into the base structure at the root of microfinger (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b shows a magnified view of the inlets of the inhaling channels. Two PDMS films, each with six microchannels with a width of 120 μm and height of 90 μm, were stacked to obtain a total of twelve channels (Fig. 3c). Multiple channels with small cross-sectional areas were used to ensure sufficient stiffness and prevent collapse during microfinger bending. Double-layer structures were applied to increase the total cross-sectional area, thereby enhancing the inhalation capacity. As a result, twelve channels were arranged in double layers in 3 mm × 550 μm cross-sectional area in terms of inhaling function and structural strength.

Experimental setup for microfinger-based sampling and analysis

Figure 4 shows the experimental setup for the microfinger-based sampling and analysis. The setup was enclosed in a clean booth under conditions with minimal temperature variations and flow disturbances. Specifically, Fig. 4a illustrates the setup for gaseous sampling, which can also be adapted to liquid samples. The outlet of the inhaling microchannels was connected to a sampling syringe. The collected samples were supplied to the analytical system, which could be configured for either gaseous or liquid samples. Figure 4b shows a photograph of the experimental setup for sampling and sensing using a bending microfinger, whose position was controlled by an X–Y–Z positioning stage system. Specifically, an X–Y stage (KXL06050-N1, SURUGA SEIKI CO., LTD., Japan) and a Z stage (KHE06008, SURUGA SEIKI CO., LTD., Japan) were used for positioning of microfinger. Driving pressure for PBA of microfinger is supplied and controlled by a compressor (AS4P-6, Kobe Steel, Ltd.) and regulator (ITV0050-3MS, SMC Corporation, Japan). Figure 4c shows a magnified photograph in Fig. 4c shows a setup of gaseous source and microfinger. The gaseous source and the tip of microfinger was positioned at the zero point. Microfinger was coarsely moved by a positioning stage system and was pneumatically bent for fine positioning of the inhaling inlet at tip of microfinger. The smell intensity was evaluated 1 min later after microfinger completed bending motion in order to match the condition between measurements.

Gaseous source consisted of a reservoir of volatile liquid, such as ethanol connected to a tube with a diameter of 1 mm. Figure 4d shows the experimental setup for gaseous sampling and sensing. The gas sensing system included odor sensor (nose@MEMS, I-PEX Inc., Japan) with analytical software (nose@MEMS Viewer Simple, I-PEX Inc., Japan) and a syringe pump (YSP-201, YMC.CO., LTD., Japan). The sensitive membrane 200Y was used for our experiment using ethanol as the gaseous chemical source. Preliminary evaluation confirmed a good linearity in the response of the membrane for ethanol concentration. For liquid samples the system included microvolume ultraviolet–visible is microphotometer (Nano Drop Onec, Thermo Fisher Scientific™ Inc., USA) and pump (MP-4000, TOKYO RIKAKIKAI CO., LTD., Japan). For sensing, samples must be delivered to the sensitive part of the odor sensor. Consequently, the inhaling function of the microfinger is essential for collecting samples at a distance from the sensor. In fact, the smell intensity without inhaling suction showed approximately one-hundredth of that with the suction at the most sensitive membrane (200Y). The collected sample can be then transferred to the sensitive membrane of the odor sensor by switching the 3-way valve. In this study, gas was supplied from an exhaust mouth via a tube for 1 min. The sample was inhaled by the microfinger at a rate of 20 mL/min and measured for 20 s using the odor sensor. For liquid samples, concentrations of New coccine were evaluated using the spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Onec, Thermo Scientific™, USA). The gaseous smell intensity was evaluated based on that of the origin point, whereas liquid concentration was evaluated based on the optical absorbance of New coccine (5 wt%).

The actual suction flow rate reached 20 mL/min and stabilized around 19–21 mL/min in approximately 3 s. Limited slight ripples were transiently observed when the microfinger was bent. Therefore, stable inhaling flow through the channels showed the channel patency against bending motion of microfinger. The total dead volume including pump and tube amounted to approximately 354 mm³ for gaseous sampling. Therefore, the time for delivery of gaseous sample to the sensor was estimated to be approximately 1 s at 333 mm3/s of flow, which was much faster than transient time of 20 s in this experiment. For liquid sampling, the total dead volume amounted to approximately 2000 mm3 and delivery time was estimated to be approximately 40 s at 50 mm3/s (the fastest flow rate of the pump). High viscous fluid might affect the flow through inhaling channels whereas obvious influence was not observed in liquid sampling using aqueous solution in this study.

Results and discussion

Fundamental characteristics of bending microfinger for active sampling and sensing

The bending angle of the microfinger in both the upward and downward directions was evaluated as a function of applied pressure (Fig. 5). Individual data points represented the mean ± maximum and minimum of three independent measurements (n = 3). Bending motion began at approximately 40 kPa, and the bending angle increased with increasing pressure. A difference between upward and downward bending motion was observed: At 100 kPa, the bending angles in the upward and downward directions were 41° and 49°, respectively. This difference is likely attributable to gravitational effects owing to the dead weight of microfinger since the difference was not observed in the lateral motion. The motion trajectory of the bending microfinger was also evaluated. From the initial position of the microfinger tip, the upward motion led to displacements of 7.5 mm and 3.75 mm in the Z and X directions, respectively, with the corresponding values for downward motion being − 7.9 mm and 4.4 mm.

Directional characteristics of sampling and sensing by bending motion of the inhaling microfinger

The microfinger varied its bending angle θ while searching chemical source. Figure 6 shows the evaluation results of the directional characteristics of sampling and sensing. The gas inhaling rate was fixed at 20 mL/min. The bending characteristics of the microfinger followed the trend shown in Fig. 5. The microfinger tip was placed 5 mm from the odor source and bent downward by applying pressure. The directionality was evaluated based on the odor intensity measured at each bending angle. The odor intensity increased with increasing bending angle of the microfinger. This result indicated that the relative arrangement between the exhaust mouth of the gaseous source and inhaling inlet at the microfinger tip affects the inhaling efficiency. The most efficient inhaling occurred when the inhaling inlet directly faced the gaseous source.

Dependence of sampling and sensing characteristics on relative position to the gaseous source

We compared the distance dependence on the smell intensity between the sensor with/without suction by inhaling function of microfinger. The smell intensity was measured with/without the suction from 0 mm to 10 mm of distance between the odor source and inhaling inlet. The intensity changed from 993 to 25 for the sensor with inhaling function of microfinger, whereas the intensity by direct sensing showed from 1777 to 1512. As a result, the inhaling function could improve the distance sensitivity, which contributed to localization of chemical sources.

As shown in Fig. 7, the inhalation performance depends on the distance between the chemical source and inhaling inlet at the microfinger tip. In addition to the directivity, the dependence of sampling and sensing performance on the relative position between the gas source and inhaling inlet in the X–Y plane was evaluated (Fig. 7). The microfinger was moved and positioned along the first quadrant of the X–Y plane by taking account of symmetrical characteristics as shown in Fig. 7a. Measurement points extended up to 15 mm from the origin and were set at 5 mm intervals. Ethanol was used as the gaseous chemical source. Figure 7b shows the measured odor intensity in the X–Y plane. The intensity map indicates the position of the gaseous source and odor intensity distribution in the X–Y plane. The measured distributions were neither identical nor symmetric along the X and Y directions. The sampling and sensing performance in the Y direction was superior to that in the X direction. This difference could be attributed to the flat microfinger configuration, with 12 channels arranged in a double-layer manner horizontally along the Y direction.

The microfinger was inserted into the water-filled dish and moved using a positioning stage. In this test, the microfinger was not driven for bending motion. The colorant concentration was evaluated using a spectrophotometer. The sample was inhaled at 3 mL/min for 40 s. Figure 9b shows the measured colorant concentration at points within a 3 mm × 3 mm area in the Y–Z plane. The inhaling microfinger effectively collected the liquid sample, which could then be analyzed using an external advanced analyzer. The horizontal arrangement of microchannels in the flat microfinger led to differences in the measured concentration distribution along the Y and Z directions.

Conclusions

Sensing capabilities for smell and taste were incorporated into a microfinger, extending functionality beyond that of a human finger, which can typically realize tactile and temperature sensing. Specifically, we incorporated inhaling microchannels into a microfinger for collecting gaseous and liquid samples. Distributed chemical substances diffusing from a source were sampled by the inhaling microchannels. The collected samples are then transported through the microchannels to sensors or advanced analyzers located at the root of the microchannels. The collected samples were analyzed using sensors and advanced analyzers installed at the root of the channels, enabling smell- and taste-sensing taste without directly integrating sensors into the microfinger itself. The microfinger was bent upward and downward using PBAs as an artificial muscle. These motions were used for active searching and localization of chemical sources in combination with coarse positioning by an external positioner. Twelve channels (120 μm × 90 μm) arranged in double layers were fabricated within the microfinger (12 mm × 3 mm × 550 μm). The transporting time of gaseous sample through the channels was approximately 1 s, which is acceptable for searching a fixed gaseous source. Regarding a moving source might require faster sampling time, which could be improved by optimization of device design. Fundamental characterization showed the directional dependence of sampling and sensing performance. The distribution of chemical substances diffusing from a source was evaluated. Three-dimensional spatial distribution of ethanol vapor diffusing from a reservoir was characterized following in-plane evaluation. Additionally, the inhaling microfinger could effectively collect and analyze liquid samples. Furthermore, degree of freedom of microfinger robot32 can be developed for advanced searching based on this study. Investigation of application possibility to various viscous samples is also our interest.

These results highlight the potential of the microfinger for active sampling and analysis in microscale environments. In particular, the proposed microfinger can be incorporated into a sensory presentation robotic system capable of acquiring and presenting smell and taste information. As prospects following this study, the localization and mapping of gaseous or liquid sources is our interest as application of developed microfinger robot.
