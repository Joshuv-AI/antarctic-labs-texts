Mechanochemical bistability as a principle of robust morphogenesis
Authors: Shi-Lei Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbryogenesis is governed by diverse physical forces, yet how these forces coordinate with biochemical signals to ensure reproducible morphogenesis remains poorly understood. Using the developing vertebrate intestine as a model, recent studies integrating organoid systems, biophysical modeling, and pharmacological perturbations reveal that mechanochemical bistability, arising from feedback between actomyosin tension and luminal pressure, confers robust and irreversible crypt budding. This bistability ensures crypt morphology is tightly controlled during morphogenesis but becomes insensitive to fluctuations upon completion. In vivo, transient villus enterocyte swelling reduces epithelial tension and activates a cPLA2-dependent mechanosensitive pathway in stem cells, synchronizing contractility across neighboring crypts and preventing competitive divergence. Together, these findings establish a multi-tiered coordination strategy in which global tissue mechanics couple with local mechanosensation to buffer variability and enforce convergent morphogenesis. Mechanochemical bistability, coupled with tissue-level mechanical coordination, may represent a general principle underlying robust and reproducible patterning across developmental and organoid systems.Keywords: Mechanochemical bistability, Morphogenesis, Intestinal crypt, Tissue mechanics, Mechanosensation

Embryogenesis is governed by a diverse repertoire of physical forces,1,2 including active cytoskeletal tensions generated by the actomyosin network, compressive stresses arising from external constraints or differential tissue growth, and hydrostatic pressure. Despite the established functional significance of these forces in tissue shaping, the mechanisms by which they coordinate with one another and with concurrent biochemical signals, such as morphogen gradients, gene regulatory networks, and cell fate specification, remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap is particularly notable given that mechanical and chemical cues often operate in parallel, and their integration is likely critical for precise tissue patterning.3,4 Compounding this challenge, cells and tissues experience substantial noise at both biochemical and mechanical levels, stemming from stochastic gene expression, heterogeneous force generation, and variable cell-cell interactions. This raises a fundamental question in developmental biology: how does morphogenesis achieve reproducible and robust outcomes despite such inherent variability across scales?

The developing vertebrate intestine exemplifies this challenge, displaying a stereotypically folded architecture essential for efficient nutrient absorption. The small intestinal epithelium forms a continuous monolayer comprising two distinct structural compartments5: crypts, which serve as stem cell niches responsible for tissue renewal, and villi, which consist of differentiated enterocytes projecting into the intestinal lumen. This crypt-villus architecture is critical for intestinal function. Intestinal organoids have emerged as an ideal model system to dissect the emergence of this tissue architecture during development,6,7 as they overcome the limited accessibility of native intestinal tissues while enabling live imaging, genetic manipulation, and precise mechanochemical perturbations. Importantly, organoid crypts faithfully recapitulate both the morphology and cell fate composition of their in vivo counterparts, establishing them as a physiologically relevant platform for studying crypt morphogenesis. Crypt formation in organoids is driven by multiple coordinated mechanisms8: fate-dependent forces originating from distinct crypt and villus regions, actomyosin-mediated apical constriction that generates local curvature, and lumen osmotic forces that modulate global tissue pressure and geometry. However, it remains unclear whether lumen deflation and apical constriction are independently regulated or intrinsically coupled.

Previous studies suggested that lumen deflation accelerates budding but is not strictly required, provided apical constriction is sufficient.8 Yet experiments show that lumen reduction at the onset of morphogenesis is necessary for budding. This reveals a non-intuitive principle (Fig. 1): the timing of a mechanical event critically shapes its impact. The same volume change produces opposite outcomes before versus after budding, despite similar cell fate composition. Importantly, this temporal sensitivity could confer robustness, making crypt shape sensitive to forces during morphogenesis yet insensitive to fluctuations once budding is complete. In a recent study,9 Xue and colleagues combined biophysical modeling with pharmacological experiments to address how mechano-osmotic forces coordinate during intestinal organoid morphogenesis. Using a three-dimensional vertex model, the authors identified a mechanically bistable region in which both open and budded crypt states are possible for a given volume, with the outcome depending on the history of the system. Biochemical bistability is well known to confer robustness in cell fate decisions, and the authors hypothesize that lumen pressure acts as an analogous multicellular signal: mechanical bistability ensures that crypt shape is tightly controlled by pressure during morphogenesis yet becomes insensitive to fluctuations once budding is complete. This bistable regime is greatly expanded by mechanosensitive coupling between actomyosin tension and lumen pressure, a finding validated experimentally, enabling quantitative recapitulation of organoid morphogenesis and various perturbations.

The authors first modeled an intestinal organoid as a closed epithelial monolayer with crypt and villus regions enclosing an incompressible lumen. Using a three-dimensional vertex model,10,11 they found that differential tension and in-plane tension can produce two stable crypt configurations, open or closed, at the same lumen volume, a phenomenon known as morphological bistability. In the model, bistability arises from positive feedback: increased crypt curvature thickens the epithelium, which enhances the active bending moment and further drives deformation. This feedback eventually saturates due to bending stiffness. Experiments confirmed that bulged organoids have swollen lumens while budded ones are slightly shrunk, consistent with normal morphogenesis. However, with experimentally measured lumen volume, bistability in the model occurs only within a narrow tension range, requiring extreme fine-tuning. Given natural variability in myosin levels, this would compromise robustness. Indeed, measured tension asymmetries in budded crypts fall below the theoretical threshold for bistability. Thus, while the model qualitatively explains timing-dependent effects, additional mechanisms are required to ensure robust morphogenetic outcomes upon mechanical perturbations.

The authors initially assumed that lumen volume and actomyosin tensions are independent. However, luminal pressure directly impacts tissue geometry and stress, which can feedback on surface tension via mechanosensitive mechanisms.12 Moreover, crypt stem cells are mechanosensitive,13 and crypt formation can be biased by tissue curvature.14 Based on these findings, the authors considered two generic mechanotransduction mechanisms: stress-dependent feedback and curvature-dependent feedback. Given qualitatively similar predictions, they focused on curvature-dependent feedback, as curvature is easier to measure experimentally. In this framework, crypt differential tension depends on both an intrinsic value set by stem cell fate and on crypt curvature. Interestingly, this feedback functions distinctly in bulged versus budded crypts. Decreasing lumen volume in bulged organoids increases crypt differential tension through geometric effects; thus, lumen deflation contributes to crypt morphogenesis through both direct mechanical impact and indirect mechanosensitive consequences. However, once differential tension becomes sufficiently high for the crypt to bud, the system becomes mechanically trapped in the budded shape, and subsequent lumen volume changes no longer influence crypt morphology or differential tension. To test these predictions, the authors experimentally validated that differential tension depends on crypt geometry and lumen volume: volume inflation in bulged crypts lowered the apical-to-basal myosin ratio in a dose-dependent manner, whereas budded crypts showed no consistent change. This indicates that myosin changes are linked to crypt geometry rather than osmotic effects. Across all data, the authors further identified a consistent relationship linking myosin intensity to crypt curvature, allowing them to parameterize the mechanosensitive feedback. With this coupling, the theory predicted substantially broader regions of shape bistability, enabling robust and irreversible budding across various intrinsic tension levels. Consequently, budded organoids remain budded even upon large lumen inflation, whereas bulged crypts require a decrease in lumen volume to bud, both consistent with experimental observations.

In summary, the mechanosensing model reproduces bistable hysteresis in crypt morphology and cellular tensions. Lumen shrinkage drives budding through both passive mechanical effects and an active mechanosensitive increase in differential tension. Once budded, volume inflation does not revert morphology, and tensions are maintained. The model fits tension data from both bulged and budded samples with a single parameter. The authors further challenged the model with pharmacological perturbations. Inhibiting contractility in budded crypts causes opening, but when blebbistatin is washed out while maintaining a swollen lumen, crypts do not rebud, which is consistent with history-dependent bistability. After extended culture, lumen volume eventually decreases and budding resumes, confirming no irreversible loss of budding ability. Finally, the model predicts that crypt morphology changes little with increasing tension and decreasing volume until an abrupt transition. Indeed, opening angle measurements reveal rapid closure within 20 min, compared to the twenty-four-hour timescale of overall morphogenesis. This supports a snap-through bifurcation as organoids switch between stable energy minima.

A recent study further uncovered a critical coordination mechanism during in vivo morphogenesis of intestinal crypts15: transient swelling of villus enterocytes relaxes epithelial tension and increases crypt cell density. This density increase activates a mechanosensitive pathway in stem cells involving the calcium-dependent phospholipase cPLA2, which promotes apical myosin relocalization and synchronizes contractility across neighboring crypts, driving their morphological convergence. Disruption of this pathway leads to winner-takes-all dynamics, in which only the initially more mature crypts close, at the expense of others. These findings connect directly to the proposed framework of mechanochemical bistability. Whereas Xue and colleagues9 emphasized how bistability confers history-dependent robustness to individual crypts, this new study reveals how tissue-scale mechanical cues, via the shared villus environment, coordinate multiple crypts simultaneously. Together, these studies suggest a multi-tiered coordination strategy: global villus mechanics prevent competitive amplification of variability among neighbors, while cell-intrinsic bistability ensures that the morphogenetic outcome of each crypt is robust once initiated. Looking ahead, mechanochemical bistability, coupled with tissue-level mechanical coordination, may represent a general principle ensuring robust and irreversible morphogenesis across diverse developmental systems. Testing this hypothesis in other contexts remains a key goal for the field.
