On the Theoretical Foundation of Sparse Dictionary Learning in Mechanistic Interpretability
Yiming Tang, Harshvardhan Saini, Yizhen Liao, Dianbo Liu

Abstract
As AI models achieve remarkable capabilities across diverse domains, understanding what representations they learn and how they process information has become increasingly important for both scientific progress and trustworthy deployment. Recent works in mechanistic interpretability have shown that neural networks represent meaningful concepts as directions in their representation spaces and often encode many concepts in superposition. Various sparse dictionary learning (SDL) methods, including sparse autoencoders, transcoders, and crosscoders, address this by training auxiliary models with sparsity constraints to disentangle these superposed concepts into interpretable features. These methods have demonstrated remarkable empirical success but have limited theoretical understanding. Existing theoretical work is limited to sparse autoencoders with tied-weight constraints, leaving the broader family of SDL methods without formal grounding. In this work, we develop the first unified theoretical framework considering SDL as one unified optimization problem. We demonstrate how diverse methods instantiate the theoretical framwork and provide rigorous analysis on the optimization landscape. We provide the first theoretical explanations for some empirically observed phenomena, including feature absorption, dead neurons, and the neuron resampling technique. We further design controlled experiments to validate our theoretical results.

Preliminaries

In this section, we establish formal definitions of the foundational concepts: input spaces, model representations, the linear representation hypothesis and superposition.

2.1
Input Space and Model Representation

Definition 2.1

(Input Space)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the space of possible inputs to a neural network. For example,
\mathcal{S}=\{
images
\}
, or
\mathcal{S}=\{
text sequences
\}
.

Definition 2.2

(Model Representation)
.

For a given model representation
\mathbf{x}
, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be its dimensionality. For each
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the network produces a representation vector
\mathbf{x}(s)
, which is directly observable by running the model on
s
.

2.2
The Linear Representation Hypothesis and The Superposition Hypothesis

Empirical studies in mechanistic interpretability have observed that neural network representations encode meaningful concepts as linear directions, often in superposition
(
25
;
27
;
17
;
31
)
. Following
13
and
32
, we formalize the hypothesis as follows.

Assumption 2.3

(Linear Representation Hypothesis)
.

A model representation
\mathbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
satisfies the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) if there exists a
feature function

\mathbf{x}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a
feature matrix

W_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times n}
such that:

1.

Linear Decomposition
: For all
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)=W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s)

2.

Non-negativity
:
\mathbf{x}(s)\geq 0
component-wise for all
s\in\mathcal{S}

3.

Sparsity
: There exists
S\in[0,1]
such that for all
i\in[n]
,

\mathbb{P}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}(x_{i}(s)=0)=S

4.

Interpretability
: Each component
x_{i}
of
\mathbf{x}
corresponds to a human-interpretable concept

We refer to
\mathbf{x}(s)
as the
ground-truth features
, where each
x_{i}(s)
represents the activation level of interpretable concept
i
for input
s
. The matrix
W_{p}
encodes how these features are linearly combined to form the observed representation
\mathbf{x}_{p}
.

Assumption 2.4

(Superposition Hypothesis)
.

A representation
\mathbf{x}_{p}
satisfying the Linear Representation Hypothesis is said to be in
superposition
if
n>n_{p}
(more features than dimensions) and the feature matrix
W_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times n}
satisfies:

1.

Bounded Interference
: There exists
\epsilon\geq 0
such that for
i\neq j
,

|\langle W_{p}[:,i],W_{p}[:,j]\rangle|\leq\epsilon

where
W_{p}[:,i]
denotes the
i
-th column of
W_{p}
.

2.

Unit Norm
: For all
i\in[n]
,

\|W_{p}[:,i]\|_{2}=1

These conditions characterize a regime where many interpretable features are compressed into fewer dimensions, with feature directions arranged to have limited mutual interference. When
\epsilon
is small, features are nearly orthogonal; when
\epsilon=0
, they are exactly orthogonal.

3
A Unified Theoretical Framework for SDL

In this section, we present a unified theoretical framework for Sparse Dictionary Learning (SDL). We first define the general SDL architecture and objective, then demonstrate how various SDL methods instantiate our framework.

3.1
General SDL Framework

We formalize SDL as an optimization problem under the Linear Representation Hypothesis (Assumption
2.3
) and the Superposition Hypothesis (Assumption
2.4
).

Definition 3.1

(Sparse Dictionary Learning)
.

A SDL model maps an input representation
\mathbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
to a target representation
\mathbf{x}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{r}}
through a two-layer architecture:

(i)

An
encoder
layer that maps
\mathbf{x}_{p}
to a latent space:

\mathbf{x}_{q}=\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p})

(1)

(ii)

A
decoder
layer that maps the latents to
\mathbf{x}_{r}
:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{r}=W_{d}\mathbf{x}_{q}

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
,
W_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}\times n_{p}}
,
W_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{r}\times n_{q}}
, and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
is a sparsity-inducing activation function.

The SDL objective minimizes expected reconstruction error:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(3)

Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis (Assumption
2.3
), both representations admit linear decompositions
\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)=W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s)
and
\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)=W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)
in terms of ground-truth features
\mathbf{x}(s)
. The loss can thus be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\|W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s))\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(4)

3.2
Instantiations: Existing SDL Methods

We now demonstrate how existing SDL methods instantiate our framework through adopting different choices of input-target pairs
(\mathbf{x}_{p},\mathbf{x}_{r})
and activation functions
\sigma
, and proposing variants on the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}
.

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs).
SAEs
(
10
)
decompose polysemantic activations into monosemantic components through sparsity constraints. In our framework, SAEs are characterized by setting
\mathbf{x}_{r}=\mathbf{x}_{p}
(self-reconstruction). The encoder projects to a higher-dimensional sparse latent space (
n_{q}>n_{p}
), encouraging
\mathbf{x}_{q}(s)
to capture the underlying ground-truth features
\mathbf{x}(s)
.

Figure 1
:
Sparse Autoencoder: encoder
W_{e}
maps
\mathbf{x}_{p}
to sparse latents
\mathbf{x}_{q}
, decoder
W_{d}
reconstructs from
\mathbf{x}_{q}
.

Transcoders.
Transcoders
(
12
;
33
)
capture interpretable features in layer-to-layer transformations. Unlike SAEs that reconstruct a single layer, transcoders learn sparse mappings from one layer to the next. In our proposed theoretical framework, transcoders set
\mathbf{x}_{p}=\text{NN}_{\ell}(s)
and
\mathbf{x}_{r}=\text{NN}_{\ell+1}(s)
, where
\text{NN}_{\ell}
denotes the network’s representation at layer
\ell
.

Figure 2
:
Transcoder: encoder
W_{e}
maps layer
\ell
to sparse latents
\mathbf{x}_{q}
, decoder
W_{d}
predicts layer
\ell+1
.

Crosscoders.
Crosscoders
(
20
)
discover shared features across multiple representations simultaneously. In our framework, crosscoders concatenate representations from different sources, such as multiple model layers, different models, or base and fine-tuned pairs, setting
\mathbf{x}_{p}=[\mathbf{x}_{p}^{(1)};\ldots;\mathbf{x}_{p}^{(m)}]
and
\mathbf{x}_{r}=[\mathbf{x}_{r}^{(1)};\ldots;\mathbf{x}_{r}^{(m)}]
where each superscript denotes a different source.

Figure 3
:
Crosscoder: encoder
W_{e}
maps concatenated multi-layer input
\mathbf{x}_{p}
to
\mathbf{x}_{q}
, decoder
W_{d}
reconstructs multi-layer output
\mathbf{x}_{r}
.

Variants of SDL Methods.

Various SDL methods fit into our theoretical framework but differ in their choices of activation functions and loss designs.
5
and
47
use ReLU activation
\sigma_{\text{ReLU}}(z)=\max(0,z)
with
L_{1}
regularization on latents:

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma_{\text{ReLU}}(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}_{q}(s)\|_{1}\right]

(5)

36
propose to utilize JumpReLU as the activation function:

\sigma_{\text{JumpReLU}}(z)=z\cdot H(z-\theta)

(6)

combined with a smoothed Heaviside function to directly penalize the

norm.
23
introduce Top-
k
activation:

\sigma_{\text{Top-}k}(z)_{i}=\begin{cases}z_{i}&\text{if }z_{i}\text{ is among the }k\text{ largest components}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

6
extend this to Batch Top-
k
activation, which applies Top-
k
selection across a batch to allow different samples to have different numbers of activated features.
35
use ReLU activation with an additional Heaviside gating function.
14
employ Top-
k
activation and introduce an auxiliary loss using Top-

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}\Big[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma_{\text{Top-}k}(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|_{2}^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{aux}}\|\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)-W_{d}^{\prime}\sigma_{\text{Top-}k_{\text{aux}}}(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

to resurrect dead neurons.
7
and
46
use multiple

values with a multi-scale loss:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma_{\text{Top-}k_{i}}(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(9)

that sums reconstruction errors of different sparsity levels.

The variants described above demonstrate that diverse SDL methods can be unified under our general framework (Definition
3.1
) through specific choices of activation functions
\sigma
and loss modifications. This motivates our theoretical approach: rather than analyzing each variant separately, we establish results for the general framework and focus on representative scenarios. Specifically, our analysis primarily considers ReLU, JumpReLU, and Top-
k
activations, which capture the main sparsity enforcement mechanisms, and the multi-scale loss designs of the Matryoshka SAEs and Transcoders. These settings are both theoretically tractable and practically prevalent, and our results provide insights applicable to the broader family of SDL methods that instantiate our framework with different design choices.

4
Theoretical Results

In this section, we provide theoretical results characterizing the optimization landscape of our SDL framework. Section
4.1
gives one construction of global minima achieving perfect reconstruction. Section
4.2
derives necessary and sufficient conditions for these minima under different activation functions. Section
4.3
demonstrates the existence of spurious local minima and how they can lead to feature absorption. Section
further characterizes these local minima by deriving the necessary and sufficient conditions.

4.1
Global Minimum

We first establish the existence of an approximate global minimum under the Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Superposition Hypothesis (Assumption
2.3
and
2.4
).

Theorem 4.1

(Approximate Global Minimum)
.

Consider the SDL loss function

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\right\|^{2}

(10)

where
\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)
and
\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)
satisfy the Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Superposition Hypothesis with interference parameter
\epsilon
.

When
n_{q}\geq n
and
\sigma=\text{ReLU}
, the configuration

W_{d}^{*}=[W_{r},\mathbf{0}],\quad W_{e}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{p}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

(11)

achieves

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})=O(\epsilon^{2})

(12)

The proof proceeds by direct substitution and utilizes the non-positive interference and unit norm conditions. We provide the full proof of Theorem
4.1
in Appendix
A
.

This approximate global minimum
(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})
provided in Equation
11
justifies the effectiveness of SDL methods in recovering interpretable features: if this point is achieved, the encoder
W_{e}^{*}=W_{p}^{\top}
learns to invert the projection
W_{p}
, recovering the ground-truth features
\mathbf{x}(s)
, while the decoder
W_{d}^{*}=W_{r}
maps these features to the target representation.

4.2
Characterization of Global Minima

We now establish necessary and sufficient conditions for global optimality under extreme sparsity (
S\to 1
), which allows us to decompose the optimization problem into independent reconstruction constraints for each feature. In specific, we draw inspiration from a recent work on sparse autoencoder
(
9
)
and decompose the loss in the following way and further lead to Theorem
4.2
.

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}&=\sum_{m=0}^{n}P(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m)\\
&\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0}=m}\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|^{2}\right]\\
&=(1-S)\cdot\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}\\
&+O((1-S)^{2})\end{split}

(13)

where
M_{d}=\Theta(1)
(See Appendix
B
).

Theorem 4.2

(Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Global Optimality)
.

Consider the SDL loss under Assumption
2.3
and Assumption
2.4
with extreme sparsity (
S\to 1
). Then
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=\Theta(1-S)
in general. A pair
(W_{d},W_{e})
achieves
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=O((1-S)^{2})
if and only if

w_{r}^{d}=W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\quad\forall d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(14)

This condition requires perfect reconstruction for each feature when active in isolation. The characterization reveals that under extreme sparsity, the SDL optimization problem decomposes into
n
independent per-feature reconstruction constraints. Motivated by this decomposition, we define an approximate loss function that captures the dominant term:

Definition 4.3

(Approximate SDL Loss)
.

Under extreme sparsity (
S\to 1
), the approximate SDL loss is defined as:

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d},W_{e}):=\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}

(15)

where
M_{d}=\Pr(\mathbf{x}(s)=x_{d}(s)\mathbf{e}_{d})\cdot\mathbb{E}[x_{d}(s)^{2}\mid x_{d}(s)>0]
. We have
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=(1-S)\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}+O((1-S)^{2})
.

In real-world neural network embeddings where
S\approx 1
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}
are effectively equivalent. The approximate loss directly quantifies how well each feature
d
is mapped from its input representation
w_{p}^{d}
to its target representation
w_{r}^{d}
, decomposing the SDL objective into
n
independent per-feature reconstruction quality measures.

4.3
Local Minima

We now establish that SDL optimization can converge to spurious local minima with loss strictly greater than the global optimum. We also point out the connection between local minima and feature absorption.

Example 4.4

(Spurious Local Minimum)
.

Consider a simple situation where
n=n_{q}=2
,
\sigma=\sigma_{\text{Top-1}}
, and extreme sparsity (
S\to 1
). Let
W_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}(e^{1})^{\top}\\
(e^{2})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
and
W_{d}=[w_{d}^{1},w_{d}^{2}]
.

The approximate loss decomposes as:

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d},W_{e})=M_{1}\left\|w_{r}^{1}-W_{d}\sigma_{\text{Top-1}}(W_{e}w_{p}^{1})\right\|^{2}\\
+M_{2}\left\|w_{r}^{2}-W_{d}\sigma_{\text{Top-1}}(W_{e}w_{p}^{2})\right\|^{2}\end{split}

(16)

where
M_{1},M_{2}>0
are constants.

Consider a configuration where:

(e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{1}>(e^{2})^{\top}w_{p}^{1}\quad\text{and}\quad(e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{2}>(e^{2})^{\top}w_{p}^{2}

(17)

Under this condition, neuron 1 activates for both features while neuron 2 remains dead. The optimal decoder is:

w_{d}^{1,*}=\frac{M_{1}\left((e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{1}\right)w_{r}^{1}+M_{2}\left((e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{2}\right)w_{r}^{2}}{M_{1}\left((e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{1}\right)^{2}+M_{2}\left((e^{1})^{\top}w_{p}^{2}\right)^{2}}

(18)

Since
w_{r}^{1}\not\propto w_{r}^{2}
, this is a weighted average satisfying
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}^{*}>0
. This configuration is a local minimum: the loss is smooth within the region where (
17
) holds, but escaping requires crossing a non-differentiable boundary, which gradient descent cannot achieve.

The spurious local minimum in Example
4.4
corresponds precisely to the
feature absorption
phenomenon observed empirically in SAE training
(
8
;
5
)
: a single latent neuron (neuron 1) becomes responsive to multiple distinct interpretable features, while other neurons remain inactive as “dead neurons.” To characterize this phenomenon with a rigorous mathematical foundation, we provide the following definition:

Definition 4.5

(Feature Absorption)
.

Consider an SDL configuration
(W_{d},W_{e})
with activation function
\sigma
. For each neuron
i\in[n_{q}]
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq[n]
denote the set of features that activate neuron
i
when active in isolation:

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\left\{d\in[n]:\left(\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right)_{i}>0\right\}

(19)

We say that neuron
i
exhibits
feature absorption
if
|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\geq 2
. A configuration
(W_{d},W_{e})
exhibits feature absorption if there exists at least one neuron
i\in[n_{q}]
with
|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\geq 2
.

Definition 4.6

(Realizable Absorption Pattern)
.

Consider an SDL configuration with activation function
\sigma
and projection vectors
\{w_{p}^{d}\}_{d=1}^{n}
. An absorption pattern
(\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n_{q}})
forming a partition of
[n]
is
realizable
if there exists an encoder
W_{e}=[(e^{1})^{\top},\ldots,(e^{n_{q}})^{\top}]^{\top}
such that for all
i\in[n_{q}]
:

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\left\{d\in[n]:\left(\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right)_{i}>0\right\}

(20)

Equivalently, for each neuron
i
:

•

For all
d\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
:
(\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d}))_{i}>0
(neuron
i
activates for features in
\mathcal{F}_{i}
)

•

For all
d\notin\mathcal{F}_{i}
:
(\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d}))_{i}=0
(neuron
i
does not activate for other features)

Theorem 4.7

(Existence of Spurious Local Minima)
.

Consider
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d},W_{e})=\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}
under Assumptions
2.3
and
2.4
with
n\geq 2
features and
n_{q}\geq n
latent dimensions. Suppose
w_{r}^{i}\not\propto w_{r}^{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

For any realizable absorption pattern
(\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n_{q}})
with at least one
|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\geq 2
, there exists a local minimum
(W_{d},W_{e})
of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SDL}}
exhibiting this absorption pattern.

6
Limitations and Future Works

While our theoretical framework provides valuable insights into SDL methods, several limitations warrant discussion:

•

Assumption Violations.
Our analysis relies on the Linear Representation Hypothesis and Superposition Hypothesis (Assumptions 2.3 and 2.4), which may not hold perfectly in real neural networks.

•

Extreme Sparsity.
Our theoretical results primarily address the extreme sparsity regime (
S\to 1
), and the extent to which our conclusions generalize to moderate sparsity levels remains an open question.

•

Convergence Guarantees.
Our analysis characterizes the global and local minima of the SDL optimization landscape but does not provide convergence guarantees or sample complexity bounds in our theory.

Despite these limitations, we believe our framework establishes a rigorous foundation for understanding SDL methods and opens avenues for various future works.

7
Conclusion

We propose the first unified theoretical framework for Sparse Dictionary Learning in mechanistic interpretability, and demonstrate how various SDL methods instantiate our framework. Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Superposition Hypothesis, we established conditions for provable recovery of interpretable features and characterized the optimization landscape, pointing out the connection between spurious local minima and feature absorption, and providing explanations to empirically observed phenomena including dead neurons and feature resampling. We further validated our theoretical results on controlled experiments.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

We decompose the loss by the number of active features. Let
m=\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}
denote the number of non-zero components. Then:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}P(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0}=m}\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|^{2}\right]

(21)

For
m=0
(no active features), both
\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)=\mathbf{0}
and the reconstruction is zero, contributing zero loss.

For
m\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}\subseteq[n]
denote the indices of active features. Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)

\displaystyle=W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)w_{r}^{i_{j}}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)w_{p}^{i_{j}}

(23)

Substituting
(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})
from Equation (
11
):

W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s)=W_{p}^{\top}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)w_{p}^{i_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)W_{p}^{\top}w_{p}^{i_{j}}

(24)

The
k
-th component is:

(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)\langle w_{p}^{k},w_{p}^{i_{j}}\rangle

(25)

For
k=i_{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}
(an active feature):

(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))_{i_{\ell}}=x_{i_{\ell}}(s)\underbrace{\langle w_{p}^{i_{\ell}},w_{p}^{i_{\ell}}\rangle}_{=1}+\sum_{j\neq\ell}x_{i_{j}}(s)\underbrace{\langle w_{p}^{i_{\ell}},w_{p}^{i_{j}}\rangle}_{|\cdot|\leq\epsilon}=x_{i_{\ell}}(s)+\Delta_{i_{\ell}}

(26)

where
|\Delta_{i_{\ell}}|\leq\epsilon\sum_{j\neq\ell}|x_{i_{j}}(s)|\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}
.

For
k\notin\mathcal{I}
(inactive feature):

(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i_{j}}(s)\underbrace{\langle w_{p}^{k},w_{p}^{i_{j}}\rangle}_{|\cdot|\leq\epsilon}=\Delta_{k}

(27)

where
|\Delta_{k}|\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}
.

After ReLU activation
\sigma(\cdot)=\max(0,\cdot)
:

\sigma(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))_{i_{\ell}}=\max(0,x_{i_{\ell}}(s)+\Delta_{i_{\ell}})=x_{i_{\ell}}(s)+O(\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1})

(28)

and neurons
k\notin\mathcal{I}
may be weakly activated if
\Delta_{k}>0
.

The reconstruction is:

\displaystyle W_{d}^{*}\sigma(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))_{k}w_{r}^{k}

(29)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(x_{i_{\ell}}(s)+O(\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}))w_{r}^{i_{\ell}}+\sum_{k\notin\mathcal{I}}O(\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1})w_{r}^{k}

(30)

The reconstruction error is:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}^{*}\sigma(W_{e}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\right\|^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}x_{i_{\ell}}(s)w_{r}^{i_{\ell}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(x_{i_{\ell}}(s)+O(\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}))w_{r}^{i_{\ell}}-\sum_{k\notin\mathcal{I}}O(\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1})w_{r}^{k}\right\|^{2}

(32)

\displaystyle=O(m\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2})+O(n\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2})

(33)

\displaystyle=O(n\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2})

(34)

Taking expectation over all samples with
m
active features and summing:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})=\sum_{m=1}^{n}P(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m)\cdot O(nm\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2}\mid\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m])

(35)

This simplifies to:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})=O(n\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}])

(36)

When features are sparse with
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}]=O(1)
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2}]=O(1)
, we obtain:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}(W_{d}^{*},W_{e}^{*})=O(\epsilon^{2})

(37)

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
4.2

Proof.

We prove both directions of the if-and-only-if statement.

Loss Decomposition under Extreme Sparsity.

First, we establish how the loss decomposes when
S\to 1
. Let
W_{r}=[w_{r}^{1},w_{r}^{2},\ldots,w_{r}^{n}]
and
W_{p}=[w_{p}^{1},w_{p}^{2},\ldots,w_{p}^{n}]
denote the column representations of the reconstruction and projection matrices.

Under extreme sparsity (
S\to 1
), at most one component
x_{d}(s)
is active for any given input. The expectation decomposes over activation patterns:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left\|W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s))\right\|^{2}

(38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\Pr(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|^{2}\mid\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m]

(39)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=0)\cdot 0

(40)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{d=1}^{n}\Pr(\mathbf{x}(s)=x_{d}(s)\mathbf{e}_{d})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{d}(s)}\left[x_{d}(s)^{2}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}\mid x_{d}(s)>0\right]

(41)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m=2}^{n}\Pr(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{r}(s)-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}\mathbf{x}_{p}(s))\|^{2}\mid\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=m]

(42)

\displaystyle=(1-S)\cdot\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}+O((1-S)^{2})

(43)

where
M_{d}=\frac{\Pr(\mathbf{x}(s)=x_{d}(s)\mathbf{e}_{d})}{1-S}\cdot\mathbb{E}[x_{d}(s)^{2}\mid x_{d}(s)>0]=\Theta(1)
, and the
O((1-S)^{2})
term captures cases where
\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\geq 2
.

Necessity (
\Rightarrow
).

Suppose
(W_{d},W_{e})
achieves
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=O((1-S)^{2})
. From the decomposition above:

O((1-S)^{2})=\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}+O((1-S)^{2})

(44)

Since
M_{d}=\Theta((1-S))
, we can rewrite this as:

O((1-S)^{2})=\Theta((1-S))\sum_{d=1}^{n}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}+O((1-S)^{2})

(45)

Dividing by
(1-S)
and taking the limit as
S\to 1
:

\lim_{S\to 1}\frac{O((1-S)^{2})}{1-S}=\lim_{S\to 1}\left[C\sum_{d=1}^{n}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}+O(1-S)\right]

(46)

where
C>0
is a constant. The left side vanishes, and the
O(1-S)
term on the right also vanishes, so:

0=C\sum_{d=1}^{n}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}

(47)

Since
C>0
and each term is non-negative, we must have:

\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}=0\quad\forall d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(48)

Therefore,
w_{r}^{d}=W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})
for all
d
.

Sufficiency (
\Leftarrow
).

Suppose
(W_{d},W_{e})
satisfies
w_{r}^{d}=W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})
for all
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

For cases where
\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=1
, there exists unique
d\in[n]
such that
\mathbf{x}(s)=x_{d}(s)\mathbf{e}_{d}
. Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis:

\displaystyle W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)

\displaystyle=x_{d}(s)w_{r}^{d}

(49)

\displaystyle W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s)

\displaystyle=x_{d}(s)w_{p}^{d}

(50)

The reconstruction is:

\displaystyle W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}W_{p}\mathbf{x}(s))

\displaystyle=W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}x_{d}(s)w_{p}^{d})

(51)

\displaystyle=W_{d}\sigma(x_{d}(s)W_{e}w_{p}^{d})

(52)

\displaystyle=x_{d}(s)W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\quad\text{(by positive homogeneity of ReLU)}

(53)

\displaystyle=x_{d}(s)w_{r}^{d}\quad\text{(by assumption)}

(54)

\displaystyle=W_{r}\mathbf{x}(s)

(55)

Thus, all cases with
\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}=1
contribute zero loss:

\sum_{d=1}^{n}M_{d}\left\|w_{r}^{d}-W_{d}\sigma(W_{e}w_{p}^{d})\right\|^{2}=0

(56)

For cases where
\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\geq 2
, the bounded interference condition (Assumption
2.4
) introduces reconstruction errors. By the analysis in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, when the per-feature constraint is satisfied, the multi-feature interference contributes:

O(\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\mid\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\geq 2])

(57)

Under the sparsity assumption,
\Pr(\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\geq 2)=O((1-S)^{2})
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{1}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\mid\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|_{0}\geq 2]=O(1)
for fixed
\epsilon
. Therefore, the multi-feature contribution is:

O((1-S)^{2})

(58)

Combining both terms:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=0+O((1-S)^{2})=O((1-S)^{2})

(59)

Therefore, the condition is sufficient for achieving
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SDL}}=O((1-S)^{2})
.
∎
