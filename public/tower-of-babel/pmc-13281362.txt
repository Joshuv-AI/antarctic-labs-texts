Physical disorders, ADLs, cognition, and depression in nursing home residents: a parallel mediation analysis
Authors: Jinxin He, Yufeng Guo, Jiaqi Wang, Qi Wang, Gang Bai, Rong Fan, Yingying Geng, Yue Zhang, Zhengran Liu, Yueyang Hu, Siyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the relationships between physical disorders (visual impairment, hearing impairment, and chewing impairment) and depression among older adults in nursing homes in one city of China, and to examine the parallel mediating roles of activities of daily living (ADLs) and cognition.MethodsThe study conducted a cross-sectional study involving 591 older adults from 50 nursing homes in China. Participants provided informations on their vision, hearing, and chewing. Cognition was assessed using tests of memory and thinking skills. ADLs were evaluated through measures of daily task performance, and depression was assessed by epidemiologic studies depression scale. Multiple linear regression analysis and PROCESS macro were used for data analysis.ResultsThe overall detection rate of depression was 28.43%, and significant correlations were observed among physical disorders, ADLs, cognition, and depression (p < 0.05). Mediation analysis revealed that hearing impairment not only had a direct effect on depression (β = 2.298, p < 0.001) but also significant indirect effects mediated through ADLs (β = 0.630, p < 0.01) and cognition (β = 0.666, p < 0.001). The total indirect effect accounted for 36.06% of the total effect. Similarly, the effects of visual impairment on depression were mediated by ADLs (β = 0.301, p < 0.05) and cognition (β = 0.380, p < 0.01), with the mediation effect accounting for 68.10%. In contrast, chewing impairment showed a direct effect on depression (β = 1.354, p < 0.01), but no significant mediation was found through ADLs and cognition.ConclusionsOur findings indicated that hearing and visual impairments were associated with an increased risk of depression, with these effects operating both indirectly through ADLs and cognition. These findings highlight the necessity of comprehensive geriatric care approaches that integrating sensory rehabilitation with interventions with ADLs and cognitive support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07595-4.Keywords: Physical disorders, Depression, Activities of daily living, Cognition, Older adults, Parallel mediating effects

Background

Globally, the growth rate of aging population in developing countries has surpassed that of developed countries [1]. Demographic projections indicated a continuous upward trend in China’s older population [2–4]. Aging is inevitably accompanied by profound physiological alterations and shifts in social roles. Consequently, mental health problems like depression have emerged as prominent public health concerns that substantially compromise the quality of life among older adults. A 2023 global epidemiological study reported a depression prevalence of approximately 35.1% among older adults [5]. Tan et al. found 13.3% of older adults suffered from severe depression [6]. Geriatric depression was frequently characterized by impaired concentration and psychomotor slowing, although these symptoms typically improved following the remission of depressive episodes, they often failed to resolve completely [7–9]. Therefore, preventing and managing late-life depression is of critical importance. Older adults residing in nursing homes differ from their community-dwelling or home-cared counterparts regarding baseline health status, living environment, and daily care management [10, 11]. As the institutionalized older population continues to grow, evidence suggested that these individuals faced a substantially higher risk of depression. The prevalence of depression among nursing home residents was reported to be three to four times higher than that among community-dwelling older adults [12, 13]. The present study aimed to identify the multifaceted factors associated with depression among older adults in nursing homes. By doing so, this research sought to provide practical insights to inform targeted interventions and alleviate the depressive burden within this vulnerable population.

Previous studies have shown that declining physical disorders is a key risk factor for depression among older adults [14, 15]. As individuals aged, their physical capacity gradually deteriorated, often resulting in various functional limitations. These limitations may restrict social activities and negatively affect the quality of social relationships. This may explain why middle-aged and older adults with physical impairments were at an increased risk of developing depression [16]. Sensory impairments ranked among the most common chronic disabilities in older adults, with hearing impairment being the most prevalent sensory disability in this population, while visual impairment was one of the leading causes of disability [17]. Additionally, chewing impairment adversely affected overall health, accelerated functiona decline, and increased mortality risk among older adults [18–20]. This study focused on these key measures, specifically examining hearing impairment, visual impairment and chewing impairment to assess the extent to which physical disorders contribute to depression.

Clarke et al. demonstrated that older adults with long-term physical disorders participated less frequently in social and leisure activities. The onset of hearing or visual impairments was often accompanied by reductions in daily physical activity and social engagement, which subsequently increased the risk of limitations in ADLs. Furthermore, these sensory deficits could cause cognitive decline and elevate the likelihood of developing depression [21–23]. Similarly, chewing impairment hindered the consumption of solid foods, frequently resulting in inadequate nutritional intake. Poor nutrition, together with reduced physical activity, further exacerbated difficulties in performing basic daily tasks and accelerated cognitive decline among older adults [24, 25].

Cognitive impairment, characterized by a decline in mental function due to pathological changes in the cerebral cortex, affected multiple domains, including memory, language, executive function, and judgment skills. These deficits severely disrupted daily life. Yan et al. reported a strong association between depression and cognitive impairments among older adults, highlighting the close interrelationship between these conditions [22]. Older adults with cognitive impairment frequently experienced memory loss and difficulties in executing daily tasks. Concerns regarding the progression to dementia further exacerbated psychological distress, thereby increasing the risk of mental health problems [26, 27]. Additionally, ADLs were widely used by healthcare professionals to assess functional status represented a key indicator of independence. Impairments in ADLs often necessitate reliance on caregivers or assistive devices, which correlated with an increased risk of adverse health outcomes [28, 29]. Such ADLs limitations resulted in a loss of independence, reduced life satisfaction, and diminished self-esteem, all of which were critical contributors to depression among older adults [22–31].

Previous research predominantly focused on isolated relationships, with studies examining the effect of physical impairments on mental health, or ADLs/cognitive decline lead to depression. However, few studies integrated these elements into a comprehensive framework. Consequently, based on the reviewed theoretical framework and empirical evidence, the present study constructed a mediation model and proposed the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis A: Hearing, visual, and chewing impairments can directly affect depression levels in older adults.

Hypothesis A: Hearing, visual, and chewing impairments can directly affect depression levels in older adults.

Hypothesis B: Cognitive impairment mediated the relationship between hearing/visual/chewing impairments and depression.

Hypothesis B: Cognitive impairment mediated the relationship between hearing/visual/chewing impairments and depression.

Hypothesis C: ADLs mediated the relationship between hearing/visual/chewing impairments and depression.

Hypothesis C: ADLs mediated the relationship between hearing/visual/chewing impairments and depression.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the associations between hearing/visual/chewing impairments and depression among older adults living in nursing homes, and to examine whether ADLs and cognition mediate these relationships.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 in a city in China and included all 50 officially registered and operating nursing homes within the city. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) residing in the nursing home for at least three months; (3) able to communicate effectively and demonstrate sufficient cognitive capacity to complete the survey; and (4) provided informed consent after being informed about the purpose and procedures of the study.

The survey was administered by trained researchers through face-to-face interviews with each participant. A total of 627 questionnaires were collected. After data verification and quality control procedures, 591 questionnaires were deemed valid and included in the final analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 94.26%.

Ethical statement

The study design and procedures strictly followed the protocol of the Declaration of Helsinki, and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of our university (Ethical approval number: [202505] No. 4). One week prior to the survey assessment, nursing homes, guardians, and older adults were informed about the topic and purpose of the research. It was emphasized that participation was voluntary and anonymous, and that older adults had the right to withdraw from the survey at any point, even after it had started. After giving a briefing about study purpose and procedures to each nursing home, consent was obtained from older adults and their guardians. Before the formal survey, the researchers conducted a pre-survey to ensure level of understanding of the participants. The questionnaires were administered in paper-and-pencil format. To encourage honest responding, all older adults participated voluntarily and anonymously, and they were informed that their personal information would be kept strictly confidential.

Measures

Visual impairment, hearing impairment, and chewing impairment

We used self-report methods to measure visual and hearing impairments. Older adults were asked, “How is your eyesight?” and those who answered “nearsighted,” “farsighted,” or “blind,” were classified into the visual problem group. Participants were also asked, “Do you have difficulty hearing?” and those who responded “yes” were placed in the hearing impairment group. This measurement approach is widely used in aging research [32].

There is no single universally accepted method for defining chewing impairment; therefore, in this population-based survey, we used self-reports to identify it. Older adults were asked, “Do you have difficulty chewing food?” and those who responded “some difficulty” or “great difficulty,” were categorized into the chewing impairment group [33, 34].

Such self-report instruments are widely employed in epidemiological studies to assess functional health in older adults. Moreover, in the context of psychosocial outcomes such as depression, an individual’s subjective perception of functional limitation often holds more direct psychological relevance than objective clinical findings [35, 36].

Cognition

Cognition was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), a tool originally developed by Folstein and his colleagues. The MMSE is widely used to screen for cognitive impairment [37]. It assesses multiple cognitive domains, including memory, attention, calculation, recall, and spatial orientation, providing a comprehensive measure of overall cognition in older adults. Cognitive impairment was defined as an MMSE score ≤ 17. This cutoff was selected for two reasons: First, it is widely used to identify clinically significant cognitive deficits and demonstrates high diagnostic specificity [38–40]. Second, it ensures direct comparability of our findings with those of earlier major epidemiological studies [41]. For detailed information on the authorization of the MMSE, please see Additional File 1.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), a shortened version of the original scale developed by Radloff. The CES-D-10 evaluates a range of depressive symptoms, including depressed mood, negative affect, physical symptoms, and interpersonal difficulties [42]. Compared to the original, the CES-D-10 contains fewer items related to somatic complaints, which helps to differentiate between physical symptoms arising from illness and those stemming from depression itself. This feature enhances the interpretability of the results and makes the scale particularly suitable for use with older adults. According to standard scoring criteria, a total score of less than 10 indicates the absence of significant depressive symptoms, whereas a score of 10 or higher suggests the presence of probable depression [43].

Activities of daily living

Activities of daily living (ADLs) were assessed using a standardized instrument developed based on the Barthel Index and incorporating elements from the US Health and Retirement Study. The scale has been validated in the Chinese population, with evidence supporting its reliability and applicability in this demographic [44]. This approach ensured the reliability and stability of the measurements in the present study. Each item was scored as follows: 10 points for independent performance without assistance, 5 points for performing the activity with some difficulty but without assistance, and 0 points for inability to perform the activity. Participants were classified as having ADLs disability if they required assistance or were unable to perform at least one activity. Those who were able to complete all activities independently, even if they reported some difficulty, were considered to have no ADLs disability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.3. Descriptive analyses and Pearson correlation tests were performed to examine the relationships among visual impairment, hearing impairment, chewing impairment, ADLs, cognition, and depression.

To investigate the parallel mediating roles of ADLs and cognition in the association between sensory impairments (visual, hearing, chewing) and depression, we applied a regression-based path analysis approach using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) in R. This model enables the simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect (mediating) effects within a single integrative analysis. All analyses were adjusted for potential confounding by demographic covariates, including age, gender, education level, and marital status.

The significance of the indirect (mediation) effects was tested using the bias-corrected nonparametric percentile bootstrap method. We generated 5,000 bootstrap samples to derive robust 95% confidence intervals (CIs). An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if its 95% bootstrap CI did not include zero. In the results, we reported unstandardized regression coefficients (β), their standard errors (SE), and the bootstrap confidence intervals. The coefficient of determination (R²) was reported for each regression model to indicate the proportion of variance in the dependent variable accounted for by the predictors. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistically significant associations or differences.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

This study included 591 older adults with an average age of (79.30 ± 8.71) years. The sample consisted of 293 male (49.6%) and 298 female (50.4%). The majority of participants had a primary school education or below (54.1%), and were without a partner (81.6%). Regarding physical disorders, more than half of the participants experienced chewing (54.1%) and visual (52.3%) impairments, while 33.2% had hearing impairments. Furthermore, 41.3% exhibited cognitive impairment, and 23.3% reported limitations in ADLs. The overall prevalence of depressive symptoms among the participants was 28.43%. Notably, the prevalence of depression was particularly high among individuals with underlying health issues: 46.31% for those with cognitive impairments, 45.41% for those with hearing impairments, 35.28% for those with visual impairments, 34.06% for those with chewing impairments, and 31.16% with ADLs. Conversely, no significant mediating effects were observed in the relationship between chewing impairment and depression. Discussion

This study investigated depressive symptoms among 591 older adults residing in nursing homes. The prevalence of depressive symptoms was 28.4%, which is comparable to the 26.4% reported in the “China Aging Development Report 2024 — Mental Health Status of Chinese Older Adults“ [45]. Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between depressive symptoms and visual impairment, hearing impairment, ADLs limitations, and cognitive decline. Furthermore, mediation analysis indicated that ADLs and cognition partially mediators the relationship between both visual and hearing impairments and depression. However, no significant mediating effects of ADLs or cognition were found in the association between chewing impairment and depression.

Sensory and chewing impairments were associated with depression through various mechanisms. Decline in sensory functions such as vision and hearing significantly disrupts the lives of older adults. It not only impairs their ability to see clearly and communicate effectively, but also limits their mobility and overall independence. This often leads to a loss of independence and reduced social engagement, ultimately resulting in their inability to participate in group activities and being gradually marginalized from society [46, 47]. Chewing function plays a role in stress regulation. Chewing impairments can become a constant source of stress. This condition may activate the HPA axis. Activation of the HPA axis raises glucocorticoid levels. These changes may ultimately lead to depression [48]. Chewing impairments often lead to poor nutrition, which harms physical health. This often contributes to frailty [20]. These physical health declines further diminish older adults’ self-worth and sense of control over their lives, leading to feelings of loneliness and helplessness and increasing anxiety. Collectively, these factors significantly elevate their risk of depression.

This study confirms that ADLs and cognition serve as mediators in the relationship between visual and hearing impairments and depression. Specifically, ADLs mediate the relationship between sensory impairments and depression in older adults residing in nursing homes, as those with vision or hearing loss often depend on others for help. This dependence limits their daily activities. Thus, they cannot fulfill their expected social role of independent living [49]. This impairment undermines their sense of control and self-esteem. The resulting decline in social networks and support intensifies their isolation and loneliness, thereby increasing the risk of depression. They also make depression worse [50, 51].

Cognition serves as a mediator between sensory impairments and depression in older adults living in nursing homes. There are two explanations for this connection. First, older adults with visual or hearing impairments use extra neural effort for visual tasks. This extra effort helps maintain their performance. But it increases their cognitive load. Over time, this sustained effort reduces cognition. Second, severe hearing and visual impairments reduce the reception of environmental signals. So the brain’s hearing and visual centers get less stimulation. When this reduced stimulation persists over time, neural cell function declines. This neural decline then causes cognitive impairment [52]. Prolonged hearing impairment can lead to shrinkage of the brain’s temporal lobe. The temporal lobe is responsible for memory and language processing. This physical change harms thinking skills [53]. Older adults with cognitive decline often exhibit weaker executive function, which reduces their independence in self-care tasks [49, 54]. When aware of their cognitive decline, they frequently experience negative emotions such as frustration and helplessness, which exacerbate their depressive symptoms [55].

Although several studies have suggested that decreased chewing function may affect ADLs and cognition [13, 56], this mediating effect was not observed in the present study. This may be explained by the environment in which our research was conducted. Data were collected from nursing homes that provide centralized nursing services. These services include developing standardized meal plans and personalized dietary adjustments for residents with chewing difficulties, such as soft or liquid diets. This structured support ensures adequate nutrition and quality of daily life, buffering the health impact of chewing disorders.

Institutional care meets basic nutritional needs, helping to maintain cognition [57–59] and physical function [60, 61]. ADLs refer to the abilities required to perform basic tasks independently, and impairment often stems from systemic decline in neuromuscular, skeletal, or coordination functions. Chewing disorders are local functional limitations restricted to the oral feeding process. Under the premise of sufficient nutrition, chewing disorders do not affect ADLs. Therefore, chewing impairments may not significantly affect ADLs or reduce cognitive stimulation, thereby weakening this mediating pathway.

To reduce the risk of depression in nursing homes, multi-level supportive interventions are recommended. For older adults with visual impairments, environmental modifications such as adequate lighting, bright, easy-to-read signage, and the provision of reading glasses can help alleviate visual fatigue. For those with hearing impairments, maintaining a quiet environment, ensuring clear, normal‑paced for daily use are beneficial. Hearing aids should be provided to older adults, and their daily use should be encouraged [62]. To support chewing function, encourage and monitor oral hygiene. For residents with chewing difficulties, providing soft foods or drinks can help maintain adequate nutrition. Collaborate with doctors on regular health checks [63]. Additionally, reminding older adults to maintain regular meals and engage in physical activity may promote overall health and alleviate negative emotions [64]. Organizing diverse group activities can help combat loneliness. Staff should be trained to recognize early signs of depression and provide appropriate mental health support, such as cognitive therapy [65, 66].

Additionally, integrated interventions targeting both ADLs and cognition should be implemented. For ADLs, this includes providing age-appropriate assistive devices, offering personalized functional training, and establishing regular daily routines. In terms of cognition, organizing group activities such as daily brain games helps maintain mental agility, slow cognitive decline, and create social opportunities, thereby reducing loneliness. Empowering older adults to make choices in their daily lives, such as regarding meals or schedules and involving them in manageable tasks can strengthen their sense of control. Together, these approaches form a comprehensive support network that enhances mental well-being and life satisfaction, ultimately helping rebuild self-worth and build resilience against depression.

Older adults require broader social support to maintain their well-being. Studies show social support improves quality of life, while lack of financial support, social support, or medical care reduces it [67, 68]. Promoting health literacy and regular check-ups among older adults are essential. Communities should offer regular home screenings for early detection of age-related issues like vision, hearing, and oral decline. Accessible social centers provide crucial emotional support and companionship. Ultimately, collective efforts across sectors are needed to foster a supportive environment that enhances the well-being of the older adults.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a significant link between depression and physical impairments in older adults. The results indicate that visual, hearing, and chewing impairments, along with declines in ADLs and cognition, contribute to depressive symptoms. Furthermore, ADLs and cognition served as mediating variables between visual/hearing impairments and depression. In contrast, chewing impairment exerted a direct effect on depression without significant mediation through ADLs or cognition. These findings highlight the importance of improving sensory functions and providing appropriate assistive devices, while also preserving functional and cognitive abilities through rehabilitation and social activities. Although no mediating effect was found for chewing impairment, chewing function still influences depression in older adults. Therefore, prioritizing adequate nutritional intake, maintaining oral health, and strengthening routine oral examinations and treatment for this population remain essential. Ultimately, fostering a supportive social environment is crucial for safeguarding the physical and mental health of older adults.

Innovativeness

This study examined ADLs and cognition as parallel mediators. It provides a multi-level framework illustrating how physical disabilities lead to depression in older adults. This integrated approach offers new insights into these complex relationships and informs targeted mental interventions and health support plans. The study examines common hearing and visual impairments. It also includes chewing impairments, which receive less attention but still affect seniors’ lives. By examining their relationship with depression, the study expands our understanding of the causes of late-life depression. By screening all nursing home residents in a Chinese city, the findings accurately represent this population, providing evidence to improve care models and guide targeted interventions.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it cannot verify temporal sequences or establish causal relationships among variables. Future longitudinal studies from a life-course perspective are needed to examine depression trajectories and clarify its causes. Second, the study relied on self-reported data without objective measures. This may lead to reporting errors and memory biases. Future studies should incorporate objective tools and interview-based methods to enhance the trustworthiness of the results. Third, other variables, such as personal life experiences and biological conditions, are important contextual factors that may also be associated with physical impairments and depressive symptoms. Research should incorporate a broader range of variables to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of this issue. Fourth, a limitation of this study is the use of a uniform MMSE cutoff (≤ 17) without adjusting for education level. While this approach enhances diagnostic specificity and maintains comparability with similar studies, it may reduce sensitivity to cognitive impairment in populations with lower education levels. Due to potential dilution bias, the associations reported in this study may be underestimated. Therefore, the prevalence and related effect sizes reported here should be considered as conservative estimates. Future studies are encouraged to validate these findings using population-based, education-adjusted normative standards.

Future research should employ longitudinal tracking designs to examine the dynamic causal relationships between visual, hearing, and chewing impairments and depression, thereby clarifying their trajectories over time. Intervention studies are needed to verify whether improving sensory function—through measures such as providing hearing aids, improving lighting, and providing oral rehabilitation—can effectively alleviate depression, while also testing the mediating roles of ADLs and cognition in this process. In addition, future studies should incorporate psychosocial variables to examine their moderating or mediating effects on the relationship between sensory impairments and depression, thereby contributing to the development of a more comprehensive theoretical model.
