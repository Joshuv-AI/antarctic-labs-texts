An antimicrobial resistance gene situationer in the backyard swine industry of a Philippine City
Authors: Jerouen Paul D. Lumabao, Maria Catherine B. Otero, Joan T. Acaso, Pedro A. Alviola, Caroline Marie B. Jaraula, Lyre Anni E. Murao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as a result of antimicrobial overuse and misuse in agriculture is a growing concern, especially in the predominant but poorly regulated backyard or smallholder swine farms of the Philippines. A city-scale surveillance of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) was conducted through selected backyard swine farms to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the AMR situation in the backyard sector of Davao City, Philippines. The ARGs encoding resistance to four antimicrobial classes (β-lactams: blaCTX−M, blaOXA, blaSHV, blaTEM, mecA; quinolones: qnrS and qnr-A2; macrolides: ermA and ermB; tetracycline: tetM) were quantified in swine feces via real-time PCR, and point maps were generated to visualize the ARG geographic distribution. The ARGs bla-TEM, ermB, qnrS, and tetM were detected in all farms at relatively intermediate to high levels, consistently representing the four most used antimicrobial classes in Philippine farms. All the investigated ARGs were present in some local barangay units, indicating potential emergence of AMR hotspots. A 0.5 quantile regression analysis identified specific backyard farm attributes associated with ARG occurrence and diversity, which may influence the ecological spread of the ARGs. These findings provide support for evidence-based guidelines to reinforce a One Health framework for the Philippine National Action Plan to Combat AMR.

Introduction

The 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) Antimicrobial Surveillance Report highlights a concerning annual toll of at least 700,000 lives lost to drug-resistant diseases, emphasizing the critical global implications of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)1. Most antimicrobials are naturally-occurring or synthetically modified compounds produced by microorganisms like bacteria and fungi, while few are fully synthesized2. AMR emerges due to selective pressures in the microbial environments inside or outside of the host that promote protective mutations (that modify or inactivate antimicrobials, or prevent their entry into the microbial cell), and by gene transfer from antimicrobial-resistant bacteria (through conjugation and transformation, or transduction by bacteriophages). AMR emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistant genes (ARGs) are facilitated by human-human and human-animal interactions, and environmental transmission through soil and soil-associated products, surface water, agricultural, hospital and industrial wastewaters, and other polluted ecological niches3,4. Hence, ARGs are considered as emerging environmental pollutants5. Considering the multifaceted nature of AMR emergence and spread, a One Health strategy with coordinated programs that address human, animal, and environmental health is necessary.

AMR spread is widely attributed to anthropogenic activities that heighten antimicrobial use. In 2010, the global antimicrobial usage was approximately 63,151 tons, with an estimated 67% increase in antimicrobial consumption by the year 20306. Some of the agricultural practices on antimicrobial use include the unnecessary and wasteful utilization of antimicrobials for growth promotion, non-therapeutic usage, and treatment of clinically ill animals7. In Southeast Asia, livestock practices, combined with poor surveillance and control of infectious diseases in pig production contribute to the surge in antimicrobial-resistant bacteria8. Particularly concerning is the common practice in lower-middle-income countries of informally keeping livestock in the household’s backyard, leading to uncontrollable antimicrobial usage, and farm practices that may affect the dispersal of antimicrobial resistance9. This transforms livestock animals into focal points for harboring antimicrobial-resistant genes and facilitating the subsequent transfer of resistance.

The Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Program (ARSP) was established in the Philippines in 1988 to monitor antimicrobial resistance, albeit limited to the clinical setting10. Surveillance in the agricultural sector is not yet unified, and surveillance reports from private companies are not publicly available11, emphasizing the lack of coherent data on AMR and ARGs in livestock farms. Swine production has a predominant role in the livestock industry of the Philippines, including the Davao Region12,13. Given that backyard or smallholder farms constitute 71% of the Philippine hog industry14, the unregulated practices in these farms and heightened concerns about AMR fueled by factors such as over-the-counter access to antimicrobial drugs and inadequate veterinary guidance15 underscore the pressing need to examine the AMR situation in local backyard swine farms. An antimicrobial use surveillance identified the commonly utilized antimicrobial active ingredients in Philippine backyard poultry and swine farms15. Although AMR evidence is available using the traditional antimicrobial screening of bacterial isolates from backyard-raised animals16,17, ARG surveillance presents a unique platform for large-scale AMR monitoring and hotspot identification18. Furthermore, the detection of emerging resistance as an outcome of antimicrobial treatment in poultry emphasizes the need to review local farming policies and practices in the Philippines19.

This research examined ARG occurrence (detection, distribution, and diversity) in selected backyard swine farms from barangays (smallest political unit) in proximity to natural water sources of Davao City, Philippines. Farm attributes that act as potential drivers of ARG occurrence and spread were determined to formulate policy recommendations relevant to local backyard farms and future research on antimicrobial resistance in support of the One Health framework for the Philippine National Action Plan to Combat AMR.

Results

Backyard farm profile

A total of 27 selected backyard swine farms from barangays of Davao City located near rivers were profiled (Table 1). Fourteen (51.85%) of the backyard swine farmers had experience of more than five years. Nineteen (70.37%) of the farms raised small herd sizes of less than ten pigs and employed an all-in-all-out type of animal flow (81.48%). Additionally, eighteen (66.67%) of the swine farmers focused on a single farming activity, involving breeding, growing, or slaughtering of pigs. Fifteen (55.56%) of them used antimicrobials for either therapeutic or non-therapeutic purposes. Thirteen (48.15%) of the farms cleaned their vicinity without disinfection. In terms of waste disposal, 55.56% of the farmers disposed wastes in containers, while the remaining 44.44% discarded waste in open areas such as in canals or utilized it for composting or as fertilizer. Finally, 25 out of 27 farms (92.59%) were located more than 50 feet from a body of water and from the public well.

Antimicrobial resistance gene prevalence

All the 11 ARG targets were detected in the farms: the β-lactamase encoding genes (blaCTX−M, blaOXA, blaSHV,blaTEM, and mecA); quinolone resistance genes (qnrA); erythromycin ribosome methylase genes (ermA and ermB) (ermA and ermB), and a tetracycline resistance gene (tetM) (Fig. 1a). The ARGs bla-TEM, ermB, qnrS, and tetM were detected in all farms. Other ARGs were detected in 85.19% (23 out of 27) of the farms for ermA, 81.48% (22 out of 27) for bla-SHV and mecA, 74.07% (20 out of 27) for bla-OXA−58, 51.85% (14 out of 27) for bla-OXA−51, 59.26% (16 out of 27) for qnrA2 allele, and 29.63% (8 out of 27) for bla-CTX−M.

Figure 1b shows the mean normalized CT values for each ARG. One-way ANOVA with Welch homogeneity correction revealed statistically significant differences on the average CT values of the ARGs (Welch’s F(10, 74.168) = 47.782, p < .001). Post hoc comparison using the Games-Howell test identified which ARGs had statistically significant differences. The ermB and tetM genes had the lowest CT values, which were significantly different from most of the remaining ARGs.

A heatmap depicted the abundance of ARGs across farms based on a color gradient from white or blank cells indicating levels below detection up to dark blue cells representing low CT values, hence greater abundance of the detected gene (Fig. 1c). ARG levels were classified into three categories: low (white to yellow), intermediate (shades of green), and high (shades of blue-green to blue). The heatmap formed two clusters based on the ARG levels detected in the farms. The first cluster comprises ermB, tetM, qnrS, and blaTEM, all present in every farm. Within this cluster, ermB and tetM formed a sub-cluster, exhibiting the highest levels in most of the farms, while the qnr and blaTEM subcluster had intermediate to high levels of ARG across the farms. The second cluster is composed of the remaining ARGs at low to intermediate levels in most of the farms.

Geographical distribution of affected farms

The ARGs detected in selected backyard swine farms in Davao City were mapped according to barangay (Fig. 2). The four corresponding antimicrobial classes of the ARGs were represented in all barangays. All 11 ARGs investigated were detected in barangays Bato, Matina Biao, New Carmen, and Talandang. Furthermore, several of the farms in Talandang are noted to be in proximity to the Davao River.

Farm characteristics associated with ARG occurrence

Farm attributes associated with ARG occurrence were evaluated through 0.5 Quantile Regression in GRETL. Since the regression analysis cannot interpret variables with incomplete data or with a value of zero, only the four genes (blaTEM, ermB, qnrS, and tetM) that were detected in all farms were included in the analysis. These four genes conveniently represent the four classes of antimicrobials. Model specification was determined through an iterative process, using literature and variance inflation factors (VIF) as criteria in selecting the farm attributes to be associated with ARG occurrence. Independent variables with a VIF less than 10 were included in the model to ensure the absence of degrading multicollinearity20.

The number of detected ARG types in the farms was significantly associated with years of farming experience (p-value = 0.0963), farming type (p-value = 0.0900), antimicrobial use (p-value = 0.0867), and altitude (p-value = 0.0223) at α = 0.1 (Table 2).

The level of β-lactam resistance, represented by the mean CT values of blaTEM, was significantly associated with farming type (p-value = 0.0014) at α = 0.01, while the level of macrolide resistance, represented by the mean CT values of ermB, was significantly associated with the distance from body of water (p-value = 0.0001) and waste management practices (0.0166) at α = 0.01, as well as farming type (p-value = 0.0473) and animal flow (p-value = 0.0334) at α = 0.05 (Table 2).

Meanwhile, the level of tetracycline and quinolone resistance (reflected by the mean CT values of tetM and qnrS, respectively) were not significantly associated with any of the investigated farm attributes (data not shown).

Farms with more than five (5) years of experience were associated with a decrease in the number of ARG types by 2.4945 counts relative to farms with less than five (5) years of experience. Relative to farmers with one farming activity or mono-production, those that have mixed production strategies were associated with a 2.0522 increase in the number of ARG types detected, and a decrease of 2.2585 and 8.5830 in the mean CT values of ermB and blaTEM, respectively. Antimicrobial users had a 1.6418 increase in the number of ARG types detected relative to non-antimicrobial users. Farms with an all-in-all-out animal flow were associated with a 1.1218 increase in the CT value of ermB, relative to those with a continuous animal flow. Those that apply swine wastes for composting or fertilization were associated with a 1.4891 decrease in the CT value of ermB relative to those that dispose wastes in containers. Farms located 50 feet or more away from the body of water were associated with a 2.4757 decrease in the CT value of ermB relative to farms less than 50 feet away from a body of water. Every one (1) meter increase in the altitude above sea level of the farms was associated with a 0.003 decrease in the number of ARG types present in the farm.

Discussion

This study conducted a city-scale surveillance of ARGs among selected backyard swine farms in a Philippine city. The ARGs blaTEM, ermB, qnrS, and tetM were detected in all farms, consistently representing the four most used antimicrobial classes in Philippine farms15. In fact, the farmers in this study reported the use of Alamycin, Vetracin, and Aquadox, which are known to contain tetracycline as the active ingredient; Baytril, which contains enrofloxacin as an active ingredient; and β-lactam containing drugs such as Aquadox, Amoxicillin, Penicillin G Procaine, and Augmentin. None of the farmers in this study, however, indicated the use of macrolides or drugs containing erythromycin as an active ingredient. This could also suggest the pre-existence of macrolide resistance in the swine gut microbiome, high-level antimicrobial residues in farms, and the presence of naturally produced antimicrobials in the soil environment21. The erm, tet, and qnr ARGs have been detected in boiler farms in the Philippines and associated with antimicrobial use19. The tet genes are one of the most common ARGs found in animal manures and livestock lagoons22, probably due to their predominance in swine gut23. Interestingly, tet ARGs were also observed in water irrigation, bacterial isolates from fresh produce in outdoor markets, and clinical isolates in the Philippines24,25, highlighting the potential transmission of ARGs across the farm-environment-human interface.

The ARGs ermB and tetM were detected at relatively high levels across almost all farms, followed by blaTEM and qnrS. In China, a 100% detection rate for tetM and 97.14% detection rate for ermB were similarly reported in livestock farms26. Previous reports suggested the co-resistance of tetracycline and erythromycin in bacteria due to multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux pumps27 and mobile genetic elements28,29, which could explain their co-abundance. In fact, tetracycline has an analogous structure to erythromycin30. Co-abundance has also been observed for blaTEM and qnr genes in Salmonella enterica isolated from slaughtered pigs and Enterobacterales isolates from clinical samples31,32. This points to the potential of drug co-resistance in bacteria from local swine backyard farms, which should be a subject for future surveillance efforts.

Certain practices and environmental conditions in the selected backyard farms appear to facilitate the occurrence or variety of ARGs. Consistent with the widely recognized postulate, antimicrobial users (55.56% of the farms) were associated with an increase in ARG diversity in their farms. Antimicrobial use may be influenced by various farm characteristics. For example, longer farming experience (> 5 years), comprising 51.85% of the subjects, was associated with a decreased diversity of ARGs, highlighting the importance of established farm practices, which can restrain the random or experimental use of antimicrobials. Compared to new farmers who are still experimenting with practices, experienced farmers tend to adhere to effective management practices, including reducing antimicrobial use and adopting precautionary principles to limit the spread of antimicrobial resistance33,34. In contrast, the employment of a mixed production strategy as practiced by 33.33% of the farms was associated with increased ARG diversity and abundance, as this system can promote higher levels and broader range of antimicrobial use35,36. In fact, farms with mixed production strategies, such as breeder, grower, and feeder pigs, have been shown to have high levels of macrolide, fluoroquinolone, and β-lactam resistance genes30.

Cleaning and sanitation also play an important role in the perpetuation of ARGs. An all-in-all-out strategy, practiced by 81.48% of the farms in this study, ensures that the pens are completely cleaned and sanitized prior to the introduction of pigs. This has been associated with decreased levels of ARG in the investigated farms. Furthermore, farms that practiced open waste disposal, composting in ground, or the utilization of swine manure as fertilizers (44.44% of the farms) were associated with increased levels of ARGs. Consistent with this, farms that apply swine wastes for composting or fertilization were associated with increased levels of ermB relative to those that dispose wastes in containers. These practices facilitate environmental contamination and thereby pose risk on ARG transmission. The input of manure in soil has been reported to accelerate the evolution of resistance by inducing higher levels of antimicrobial resistant bacteria carrying ARGs, thereby posing higher risk of ARG transmission37,38. In support of this, metagenomic studies demonstrated that ARGs in the soil have a 100% homology to those from clinical isolates and constitute most of the antimicrobial classes39. Finally, farms located more than 50 feet away from a body of water (74.07% of the farms) were associated with increased ARG levels, probably due to the inaccessibility of a water source, leading to poor hygiene and sanitation.

Another important consideration is the ecological spread of AMR. Although the distribution of ARGs varied among the selected barangays in Davao City, all 11 investigated ARGs were detected in barangays Bato, Matina Biao, New Carmen, and Talandang. It has been reported that the mere existence of ARGs is an indication of higher levels of antimicrobial residues in backyard farms40. Hence, these barangays have the potential to emerge as geographical hotspots for antimicrobial residues and/or ARGs. Furthermore, the surveyed farms in Talandang were located near Davao River, the third largest river catchment in the Southern Philippines and the largest in Davao City. Davao River is the main natural reservoir of the city’s aquifer and flows southward through the populated and commercial areas before it reaches the Davao Gulf41. Wastewater from animal farms may also carry antimicrobial resistant bacteria and other elements that facilitate ARG exchange among microorganisms42, making agricultural runoff a contributing factor to the risk of AMR transmission43. Agricultural runoff from backyard farms to animal and human populations warrants AMR monitoring of the rivers. Farms at lower altitude were also at greater risk of a diverse presence of ARGs, potentially due to the surface and near surface runoffs that transport a variety of ARG-carrying microbes44. The tendency of microbes to accumulate at low altitudes after a rainfall enriches the resistome in these areas and increases the likelihood of infections and thereby use of antimicrobial drugs.

The Philippine National Action Plan to Combat AMR 2019–2023 (PNAP) is a nationwide framework addressing the control and prevention of AMR through a “One Health Approach.”10 Key strategies are proposed to reinforce the PNAP based on this study’s findings. For example, a strategic AMR surveillance using a One Health approach by tracing agricultural runoff from livestock farms to rivers and populations is recommended. Environmental monitoring for AMR determinants such as antimicrobials and ARGs in farm wastes and water systems will support contextualized interventions for smallholder farms, which are unlikely to have the appropriate structures for proper waste management and disposal of waste compared to large scale farms45.

This study has identified local farm practices that drive antimicrobial use and AMR spread. The PNAP has stated the need to strengthen the implementation and monitoring of good animal husbandry practices and biosafety in animal farms10. Thus, integrated guidelines on good farming practices for smallholder farmers such as quarantine and complete cleaning and disinfection during an outbreak, sentinel placement, diagnostic surveillance, and negative disease monitoring must be laid out to co-address AMR and other livestock diseases, providing a holistic approach to biological threats in the livestock industry.

Another key strategy according to the PNAP is the promotion of innovation and research on AMR10. Considering the significant role of antimicrobial use in AMR among the smallholder farms, interventions supporting non-antimicrobial alternatives for maintaining and promoting animal health must be strengthened. The reduction of unfavorable and harmful bacteria in the animal gut can be achieved through the application of probiotics, intestinal acidifiers, natural antimicrobials, and enzymes43,46.

Lastly, capacity building, education, awareness, and advocacy are one of the goals of the PNAP10. The PNAP has conducted an IAMResponsible campaign for farmers and provided pre-service training of health and agriculture professionals, including farm owners, on antimicrobial use47. With the detection of ARGs in backyard swine farms, educational campaigns and mentorship programs for smallholder farmers must be reinforced. For example, this study has demonstrated the importance of established farm practices acquired through experience, which can prevent the random or experimental use of antimicrobials. A mentorship program where experienced farmers can share good practices with the inexperienced would strengthen responsible farming.

These findings are delimited to a targeted set of ARGs within a backyard farm setting, hence a more comprehensive surveillance such as through metagenomic analysis of both the microbiome and resistome, estimation of antimicrobial use in the agricultural setting is needed to capture a holistic picture of antimicrobial resistance and their associated farm characteristics and practices. AMR is also a multifaceted problem requiring an investigation on the role of other players such as commercial farms, communities, local government, and industries. Climate change may also influence farm practices, environmental dynamics, disease transmission, and AMR prevalence. Therefore, understanding the AMR landscape in a One Health framework (farm to environment to populations) in a localized context opens opportunities to address this complex issue through a multidimensional, systematic, and evidence-based strategy.

Methods

Site selection

Fecal samples have been obtained from a previous study, which used stratified random sampling to select backyard farms in Davao City, Philippines48. Proximity to natural bodies of water (e.g. rivers) where environmental spillovers are possible, were selected for the current study due to the potential risk of spreading antimicrobial antibiotic resistance through wastewater and agricultural runoff in the main rivers of Davao City. Only 27 out of the 100 backyard farms previously surveyed from barangay units (Table 3) were located near the rivers. This sampling frame consists of 11 barangay units in three districts of the city. De-identified farm data and archived samples were used for the current study.

Sample collection and processing

Freshly defecated stool samples were previously collected from pig pens and stored at -80 °C. Only one stool sample per farm (coming from the pig pen with the most number of pigs) was collected48. No direct handling of animals was performed. De-identified data on farm herd size, type of farming, animal flow, antimicrobial utilization (type of antimicrobial, frequency of use, and source), cleaning method, waste management practices, and distance from a body of water and from a public well were also retrieved from the project records.

Extraction of DNA

DNA from swine fecal samples was extracted using the Nucleospin DNA Stool Extraction Kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co. KG, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted DNA samples were stored at -20 °C. To ensure non-degradation of samples, the quality (purity and quantity) of the samples were checked through a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Detection and quantification of ARG

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was conducted using commercially available PCR primers and probes. Pre-designed TaqMan Gene Expression quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used for the detection of ARGs, specifically: for β-lactams- blaCTX−M, blaOXA−58, blaOXA−51, blaSHV, blaTEM, and mecA; for quinolones- qnrS and qnr-A2; for macrolides- ermA and ermB; and for tetracycline- tetM. These represent the four (4) most used antimicrobial classes in backyard and commercial poultry and swine farms in the Philippines: fluoroquinolones (33%), tetracycline (13%), β-lactams (12%), and macrolides (12%).15 To normalize the data, a 16 S rRNA gene copy number was included. qPCR reactions were performed in duplicate in Quantstudio 5 Real-time PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using the cycling conditions of 95 °C for 5 min; 45 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 30 s at 62 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C; and the final step at 72 °C for 5 min.

Quantitative analysis of the qPCR results was assessed via the comparative cycle threshold or CT method49. The mean CT values and standard deviations of the two replicates for each sample were used in the ΔCT computation. For each sample, differences in the CT values of the tested ARG and 16s rRNA were calculated using the formula:

Statistical analysis

A One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Welch homogeneity correction was performed to compare the average ΔCT values among the ARGs using JASP v. 0.15.050. ARGs which are significantly different from each other were identified using the Games-Howell post hoc test.

QGIS mapping

The geographical distribution of the ARGs was mapped using Geographical Information System (GIS) Q-GIS Version 3.1651. Farm location coordinates and ARG data were encoded in a .XLS file which was inputted into the software to generate a .SHP file using the WGS 1984 coordinate system. The barangay boundary shapefile in Davao City was obtained from the Davao City Planning Development Office GIS Division of the City Government of Davao. A point map for the genes in each antimicrobial class was generated to visualize the distribution of the ARGs. The generated maps were exported into a PNG file.

Regression analysis

Collignon and co-authors analyzed how anthropological and socioeconomic factors predict AMR through regression52. Van Camp and colleagues utilized regression analysis to work on features that are correlated for multiple antimicrobial gene coverage53. Identification of backyard farm characteristics, practices, and environmental conditions associated with ARG abundance (Table 4) was done using 0.5 Quantile Regression in GNU Regression Econometric and Times-series Library (GRETL). The 0.5 Quantile Regression was used to provide a robust explanation and strong prediction of the explanatory variables towards the response variables in low sample sizes54. As the study had a small sample size with only 27 farms examined, the said regression analysis can provide a robust explanation on the association of farm characteristics, practices, and environmental conditions (explanatory variables) to ARG occurrence (response variable) in terms of number of ARG types (NumARGs) and CT values of the ARGs detected in all farms namely, blaTEM, ermB, qnrS, and tetM.
