Limits of biconditioned Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees
Vanessa Dan

Abstract
We study the limiting behavior of a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree conditioned to have a large number of vertices and either a fixed number of leaves or a fixed number of internal nodes. The first biconditioning gives a universal result with respect to the offspring distribution. In contrast, the second case leads to a variety of limiting behaviors, ranging from condensation phenomena to more elongated tree structures, depending on the properties of the offspring distribution. To prove these results, we use tools from conditioned random walk theory and from analytic combinatorics.

Introduction

In this article, we are interested in the asymptotic behavior of Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees (BGW trees) conditioned on having a large number of vertices with a fixed number of leaves or internal nodes.

Scaling limits of BGW trees were initiated by Aldous
[
Ald91a
,
Ald91b
,
Ald93
]
who proved that the rescaled critical BGW tree with finite-variance offspring distribution, conditioned on having a large total number of vertices, converges to a continuous random tree known as the Brownian Continuum Random Tree (CRT). The theoretical framework around this notion of convergence has since been well established (see the survey article
[
LG05
]
). This convergence result has been extended to more general settings. In particular, Duquesne
[
Duq03
]
(see also
[
Kor13
]
) studied the case where the offspring distribution has infinite variance, and showed that the scaling limit of a critical Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree whose offspring distribution lies in the domain of attraction of a stable law is a continuous random tree, known as the stable tree. It is worth noting that the case where the mean number of children is strictly greater than 1 is similar and leads to the same results.
However, if the mean is strictly less than 1, the geometry of a tree conditioned to be large can be very different and can lead to a condensation phenomenon
[
Kor15
]
. Often motivated by applications to other random combinatorial structures, many other types of conditioning have also been considered, such as maximum height
[
LG10
]
, maximum degree
[
He17
]
, generation sizes
[
ABD20
]
, or the total number of vertices with fixed degrees
[
Kor12
,
Riz15
,
Thé20
]
.

While the asymptotic behavior of conditioned BGW trees is now well understood, many biconditioned cases (i.e. conditioning on both the total number of vertices and the number of leaves), remain largely unexplored. Nonetheless, several works have addressed specific biconditioned models, beginning with Labarbe and Marckert who studied the case of the uniform distribution, in which both the number of leaves
k_{n}
and the number of internal nodes
n-k_{n}
tend to infinity
[

]
. They showed that, although the normalization factor must be adjusted depending on the number of imposed leaves, the scaling limit remains the same as in the single-conditioning case: the Brownian CRT. Then, Kargin considered BGW trees conditioned on both the total number of vertices

[

]
on the scaling limits of the Łukasiewicz walk of such trees. The results in

Techniques.

We now comment on the main tools involved in the proof of each case. On the one hand, the main tool in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, its generalization Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
1.2
is the encoding of conditioned BGW trees through their Łukasiewicz path, which, roughly speaking, correspond to a positive random walk conditioned to return to zero at a large time.
In fact, the key idea in the proof of Theorem
3.4
is to show that the tree tends to maximize its number of edges under a given constraint. The Łukasiewicz path encoding makes this strategy explicit, enabling a direct application of classical results on conditioned random walks. In the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we establish a condensation phenomenon by using a well-known result from the theory of random walks, often referred to as the ‘one big jump’ theorem. This result essentially states that a random walk conditioned to reach a very high value in a short time typically does so by making one large jump and smaller ones. On the other hand, the key result that serves as a common starting point for the proofs of Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
and
1.5
is Lemma
4.6
, which expresses the distribution of the reduced tree in terms of the coefficients of the generating function and its derivatives. This leads us to study the asymptotic behavior of these coefficients, and thus this part relies on complex-analytic methods.

Structure of the paper.

The paper is organized as follows. Section

recalls basic definitions and properties of Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees, as well as the coding function used to analyze their structure. Section

introduces the biconditioning by the total number of vertices and leaves, and presents the main result along with its proof. Section

presents the biconditioning by the total number of vertices and internal nodes and gives two different descriptions of a BGW tree, one through the Łukasiewicz path and the other via generating function. Finally, Sections

Lemma 2.7

We denote by

Lemma 3.3

Let

. We also denote by

Theorem 3.4

Let

As a result of the previous discussion, we can mention two interesting particular cases of the first part of Theorem

Moreover, it is clear that if

Proof.

We begin by proving that
R(T_{n}^{k})
converges in distribution as
n
tends to infinity to a uniform random tree in
\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mu)
. Let
(b^{\mathrm{max}}_{j})_{j\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)}
be the sequence maximizing
\sum_{j\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)}b_{j}
under the constraint
(\mathcal{C})
. Fix
a\in\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mu)
. Then,
\phi_{1}(a)=0
and we have

\mathbb{P}(R^{k}_{n}=a)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(R=a,T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}=\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}\frac{\mu(0)^{k}\mu(1)^{n-|a|}}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)}.

(3.1)

Indeed, the number of trees with
n
vertices and
k
leaves that have
a
as their reduced tree is equal to the number of ways to fill the
|a|
edges of
a
with
n-|a|
vertices with a single child, which is equal to
\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}
. So, the claim follows if we prove that
\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})
is asymptotically equivalent, when
n
tends to infinity, to

\big|\mathbb{T}^{k}({\mu})\big|\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}\mu(0)^{k}\mu(1)^{n-|a|}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)}.

By using the coding of
T
by its Łukasiewicz path (Proposition
2.5
), if
W

denotes the random walk starded from
W_{0}=0
and with i.i.d. increments
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
distributed according to
\nu
defined by
\nu(i)=\mu(i+1)
for all

, then
\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})
is equal to

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}=-1,W_{i}\geq 0\,\forall i\in[\![1,n-1]\!],\bigl|\{i\in[\![0,n-1]\!]\text{: }X_{i}=-1\}\bigr|=k\Bigr).

Then, applying Proposition
2.6
, we deduce that this probability is equal to
\mathbb{P}(W_{n}=-1,|\{i\text{ : }X_{i}=-1\}|=k)/n
, which is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[\![1,n]\!]\\
|I|=k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\Biggl(X_{i}=-1\text{ }\forall i\in I,\sum_{j\notin I}X_{j}=k-1,X_{i}\neq-1\text{ }\forall i\notin I\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[\![1,n]\!]\\
|I|=k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(X_{i}=-1\text{ }\forall i\in I\Bigr)\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\sum_{j\notin I}X_{j}=k-1,X_{i}\neq-1\text{ }\forall i\notin I\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\mu(0)^{k}(1-\mu(0))^{n-k}\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}X_{j}=k-1\,\Big|\,X_{i}\neq-1\text{ }\forall 1\leq i\leq n-k\Biggr),

which is equal to
\mathbb{P}(B_{n}=k)\mathbb{P}(W^{\prime}_{n-k}=k-1)/n
, where
B_{n}
is the sum of
n

\mu(0)
and
W^{\prime}
is a random walk starting at
0
with i.i.d. increments
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
such that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}=i)=\nu(i)/(1-\mu(0))
for all
i\geq 0
. In other words,
W^{\prime}
is equal in distribution to the random walk
W
conditioned to have only non negative steps. Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigl(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n}\bigr)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}\bigl(B_{n}=k\bigr)\mathbb{P}\bigl(W^{\prime}_{n-k}=k-1\bigr).

By summing over all possible increments, we get that
\mathbb{P}(W^{\prime}_{n-k}=k-1)
is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-k}\leq k-1\\
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n-k}=k-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n-k}=y_{n-k})=\frac{1}{(1-\mu(0))^{n-k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-k}\leq k-1\\
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n-k}=k-1\end{subarray}}\mu(y_{1}+1)\cdots\mu(y_{n-k}+1).

Therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})=\frac{\mu(0)^{k}}{n}\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-k}\leq k-1\\
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n-k}=k-1\end{subarray}}\mu(y_{1}+1)\cdots\mu(y_{n-k}+1).

(3.2)

The sum appearing in Equation (
3.2
) is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1}+\cdots+b_{k}=n-k\\
2b_{2}+3b_{3}+\cdots+kb_{k}=n-1\end{subarray}}\binom{n-k}{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}}\mu(1)^{b_{1}}\cdots\mu(k)^{b_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{2}+\cdots+b_{k}=p\\
b_{2}+2b_{3}+\cdots+(k-1)b_{k}=k-1\end{subarray}}\binom{n-k}{n-k-p,b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}}\mu(1)^{n-k-p}\mu(2)^{b_{2}}\cdots\mu(k)^{b_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(b_{j})_{j\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)\cap[\![2,k]\!]}\\
\sum b_{j}=p\\
\sum(j-1)b_{j}=k-1\end{subarray}}\binom{n-k}{n-k-p,b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}}\mu(1)^{n-k-p}\mu(2)^{b_{2}}\cdots\mu(k)^{b_{k}}

where
\binom{n-k}{n-k-\sum b_{j},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}}
is asymptotically equivalent to
(b_{2}!\cdots b_{k}!)^{-1}n^{b_{2}+\cdots+b_{k}}
and all the sums have a finite number of terms. Consequently, we have the following equivalent:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-k}\leq k-1\\
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n-k}=k-1\end{subarray}}\mu(y_{1}+1)\cdots\mu(y_{n-k}+1)\underset{n\to+\infty}{\sim}\frac{n^{p_{\mathrm{max}}}}{b^{\mathrm{max}}_{2}!\cdots b^{\mathrm{max}}_{k}!}\mu(1)^{n-k-p_{\mathrm{max}}}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{b^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}},

where
p_{\mathrm{max}}\coloneqq\sum_{j\geq 2}b^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
.
Recalling that the number of vertices in

is equal to
|a|=k+p_{\mathrm{max}}
for

, we deduce that

\mu(1)^{n-k-p_{\mathrm{max}}}\prod_{j=2}^{k}\mu(j)^{b^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}=\mu(1)^{n-|a|}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)}\quad\text{and}\quad\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}\underset{n\to+\infty}{\sim}\frac{k!}{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n^{p_{\mathrm{max}}}}{(n-|a|-1)!}.

Moreover by Equation (6.19) of
[
Pit06
]
giving the number of plane trees with prescribed degrees, we have that

\bigl|\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mu)\bigr|=\frac{(n-|a|-1)!}{k!b^{\mathrm{max}}_{2}!\cdots b^{\mathrm{max}}_{k}!}.

Consequently, we get that
\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})
is asymptotically equivalent to

\frac{\mu(0)^{k}}{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n^{p_{\mathrm{max}}}}{b^{\mathrm{max}}_{2}!\cdots b^{\mathrm{max}}_{k}!}\mu(1)^{n-k-p_{\mathrm{max}}}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{b^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}\underset{n\to+\infty}{\sim}\big|\mathbb{T}^{k}({\mu})\big|\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}\mu(0)^{k}\mu(1)^{n-|a|}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)},

so

converges in distribution as
n
tends to infinity to a uniform random tree in
\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mu)
.

Now, let us deduce Theorem
3.4
. Set
a\in\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mu)
. From the preceding, it is enough to prove that conditionally given the event
\{R^{k}_{n}=a\}
,

has the uniform distribution on

and then apply Lemma

to obtain the desired result. Fix

, and let us prove that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L^{k}_{n}=\ell\,\big|\,R^{k}_{n}=a\bigr)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}^{n}_{|a|,|a|}|}=\frac{1}{\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}}.

On the one hand, by Equation (
3.1
), we have that
\mathbb{P}(R^{k}_{n}=a)
is equal to

\binom{n-1}{|a|-1}\frac{\mu(0)^{k}\mu(1)^{n-|a|}}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)},

on the other hand we have that
\mathbb{P}(L^{k}_{n}=\ell,R^{k}_{n}=a)
is equal to

\frac{\mathbb{P}(L=\ell,R=a,T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}=\frac{\mu(0)^{k}\mu(1)^{n-|a|}}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}^{k}_{n})}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mu(i)^{\phi_{i}(a)},

so the proof is complete using
\mathbb{P}(L^{k}_{n}=\ell\mid R^{k}_{n}=a)=\mathbb{P}(L^{k}_{n}=\ell,R^{k}_{n}=a)/\mathbb{P}(R^{k}_{n}=a)
.

Lemma 6.6

6.2
The root has maximal degree

We now aim to prove Theorem
1.3
.2. Recall that we denote by
c^{o}_{i}(a)
the number of children of the
i
-th internal vertex, counted in lexicographic order, in a tree
a
.

Proposition 6.7
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that

\bigg|\frac{c^{o}_{0}(T_{n,k})}{n}-1\bigg|n^{\delta}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

Observe that this implies
n^{\delta-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0
. Moreover, Proposition
6.7
is in fact stronger than Theorem
1.3
.2. We will see in the proof why such a stronger statement is required.

In order to prove Proposition
6.7
, we start by proving that the largest degree concentrates around
n
.

Lemma 6.8
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that

\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\max_{0\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})-1\biggr)n^{\delta}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

Proof.

Since
\alpha>1
we may fix
0<\gamma<1
such that
1+2\gamma\leq\alpha
. Set
\delta\coloneqq(\alpha-1-2\gamma)/2\alpha>0
. Since
R_{n,k}=\ast_{k}
with probability tending to
1
, by Theorem
1.3
.1, it is enough to prove that
(\max_{0\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})/n-1)n^{\delta}
converges in probability to
0
under the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}(\cdot|R_{n,k}=\ast_{k})
, i.e. that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\max_{0\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})\leq(1-\epsilon n^{-\delta})n\,\Big|\,R_{n,k}=\ast_{k}\biggr)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

Set
\epsilon_{n}\coloneqq\epsilon/n^{\delta}
, so that
\epsilon^{2\alpha}n^{1-\alpha+\gamma}=\epsilon_{n}^{2\alpha}n^{-\gamma}
. Then this probability equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(c^{o}_{0}(T_{n,k})\leq(1-\epsilon_{n})n,\ldots,c^{o}_{k-1}(T_{n,k})\leq(1-\epsilon_{n})n\,\big|\,R_{n,k}=\ast_{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k})}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(c^{o}_{0}(T)\leq(1-\epsilon_{n})n,\ldots,c^{o}_{k-1}(T)\leq(1-\epsilon_{n})n,R=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{0},\ldots,i_{k-1}\leq n(1-\epsilon_{n})\\
\sum i_{j}=n-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(c^{o}_{0}(T)=i_{0},\ldots,c^{o}_{k-1}(T)=i_{k-1},R=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{0},\ldots,i_{k-1}\leq n(1-\epsilon_{n})\\
\sum i_{j}=n-1\end{subarray}}\binom{i_{0}}{k-1}\mu(i_{0})\mu(i_{1})\cdots\mu(i_{k-1})\mu(0)^{n-k}.

According to Lemma
4.6
and Equation (
6.6
), by setting
c_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\kappa(\ast_{k})}{(k-1)!\Gamma(k-\alpha-1)}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(R_{n,k}=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k})\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}c_{k}\mu(0)^{n-k}\ell(n)n^{k-\alpha-2}.

Fix
\eta>0
arbitrary. Then for
n
large enough, we get

\frac{\mu(0)^{n-k}}{\mathbb{P}(R_{n,k}=\ast_{k},T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k})}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\eta)c_{k}\ell(n)n^{k-\alpha-2}}.

Furthermore, we have
\binom{i_{0}}{k-1}\leq n^{k-1}
for every
i_{0}\leq n
and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{0},...,i_{k-1}\leq n(1-\epsilon_{n})\\
\sum i_{j}=n-1\end{subarray}}\mu(i_{0})\mu(i_{1})\cdots\mu(i_{k-1})=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(X_{0}+\cdots+X_{k-1}=n-1,\forall j\in[\![0,k-1]\!]\textbf{ }1\leq X_{j}\leq n(1-\epsilon_{n})\Bigr)

where the
X_{j}
’s are i.i.d. with law
\mu
. Observe that on the last event, at least two different
X_{j}
’s must be greater than
n\epsilon_{n}/2k
. Thus, we have for
n
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\biggl|\frac{1}{n}\max_{0\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})-1\biggl|\geq\epsilon_{n}\,\Bigl|\,R_{n,k}=\ast_{k}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(1-\eta)c_{k}}\frac{n^{\alpha+1}}{\ell(n)}\mathbb{P}\biggl(\exists i\neq j\text{ st }X_{i},X_{j}\geq\frac{n\epsilon_{n}}{2k}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k^{2}}{(1-\eta)c_{k}}\frac{n^{\alpha+1}}{\ell(n)}\mathbb{P}\biggl(X\geq\frac{n\epsilon_{n}}{2k}\biggr)^{2}.

Recall that
\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}-\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{-1}\ell(x)x^{-\alpha}
by Equation (
6.1
), then

\frac{n^{\alpha+1}}{\ell(n)}\mathbb{P}\biggl(X\geq\frac{n\epsilon_{n}}{2k}\biggr)^{2}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\Gamma(1-\alpha))^{2}}\frac{\ell(n\epsilon_{n}/2k)^{2}}{\ell(n)}\frac{n^{\alpha+1}}{(n\epsilon_{n}/2k)^{2\alpha}}.

Moreover, since
\ell
is slowly varying, we can apply Potter’s bounds: there exists
C>0
and
N>0
such that for
n>N
,
\ell(n\epsilon_{n}/2k)^{2}/\ell(n)\leq Cn^{\gamma}
. Therefore, we deduce that for every
n>N
,

\frac{n^{\alpha+1}}{\ell(n)}\mathbb{P}\biggl(X\geq\frac{n\epsilon_{n}}{2k}\biggr)^{2}\leq\frac{C}{(\Gamma(1-\alpha))^{2}}\frac{n^{\alpha+1+\gamma}}{(n\epsilon_{n}/2k)^{2\alpha}}=\frac{C}{(\Gamma(1-\alpha))^{2}(2k\epsilon)^{2\alpha}}n^{-\gamma}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

Finally,

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\biggl|\frac{1}{n}\max_{0\leq i\leq k-1}c^{o}_{i}(T_{n,k})-1\biggl|\geq\epsilon_{n}\,\Bigl|\,R_{n,k}=\ast_{k}\biggr)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0,

and the claim follows.
∎

We can now proceed to the proof of Proposition
6.7
.

Proof of Proposition
6.7
.

Set
\epsilon>0
and pick the same
\gamma
,
\delta
and
\epsilon_{n}=\epsilon n^{-\delta}
as in the proof of Lemma
6.8
. For every
i\in[\![0,k-1]\!]

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k},R=\ast_{k},c^{o}_{i}(T)\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})n\Bigr)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0}+\cdots+a_{k-1}=n-1\\

Using that

\frac{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k},R=\ast_{k},c^{o}_{0}(T)\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})n)}{\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathbb{T}_{n,k},R=\ast_{k},\exists i\geq 0\text{: }c^{o}_{i}(T)\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})n)}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}1.

Dividing both the numerator and denominator by

, we observe that the conditional probability in the denominator goes to

1.3
.1. ∎

.

satisfies (

H
α

Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma

.

The Dirichlet distribution is characterized by its moments, so it is enough to prove that for all integers
\lambda_{1}^{1},\ldots,\lambda_{1}^{k},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, we have the following equality

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\bigl(X_{1}Y_{1}\bigl)^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots\bigr(X_{1}Y_{k}\bigl)^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\Bigr)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(U_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots U_{k}^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{{}^{\prime}\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{{}^{\prime}\lambda_{n}}\Bigr),

where
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k},X^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n})\sim\mathbf{Dir}((\alpha_{1}/k)_{1\leq i\leq k},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.
As
Y
is independent of
X
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\bigl(X_{1}Y_{1}\bigl)^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots\bigr(X_{1}Y_{k}\bigl)^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\Bigr)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(Y_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots Y_{k}^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}\Bigl)\mathbb{E}\Bigl(X_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\Bigr),

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl(Y_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots Y_{k}^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma(\lambda_{1}^{i}+1)}{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_{1}^{i}+1)\bigr)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl(X_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\alpha_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{1}^{i}\bigr)\prod_{j=2}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\lambda_{j})}{\Gamma\bigl(\alpha_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{1}^{i}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\lambda_{j}+\alpha_{j})\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}.

As

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(U_{1}^{\lambda_{1}^{1}}\cdots U_{k}^{\lambda_{1}^{k}}X_{2}^{{}^{\prime}\lambda_{2}}\cdots X_{n}^{{}^{\prime}\lambda_{n}}\Bigr)=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma\bigl(\lambda_{1}^{i}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\bigr)\prod_{j=2}^{n}\Gamma\bigl(\lambda_{j}+\alpha_{j}\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\alpha_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{1}^{i}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\lambda_{j}+\alpha_{j})\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\bigr)\prod_{j=2}^{n}\Gamma\bigl(\alpha_{j}\bigr)},

using that for all real
z
,
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, we get the desired equality.
∎

8
Poisson case: Proof of Theorem
1.5

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
. We consider
\mu
an offspring distribution with generating function satisfying the assumption (

H
P

) defined by

\text{``}F_{\mu}(z)=c\exp{(P(z))}\text{ where }c>0,P(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}z^{i},a_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }\mathrm{gcd}\{j:a_{j}\neq 0\}=1\text{''}

(
\mathcal{H}_{P}
)

Theorem
1.5
shows that under assumption (

H
P

), the reduced tree of a
\mu
-BGW tree conditioned to have
n
vertices and
k
internal nodes converges in distribution to an explicit simply generated tree, along with the way the leaves are attached to it.

The third assertion of Theorem
1.5
is an immediate consequence of the first two and the definition of a BGW tree.

To establish the first point, it suffices to prove the following result.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mu
be an offspring distribution with generating function satisfying (

H
P

). Then for all
a,b\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
,

[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)}]G^{a}_{\mu}(z)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(b)}]G^{b}_{\mu}(z).

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.1.

Let us assume this result and see how it leads to the first point of Theorem
1.5
. Applying Lemma
4.6
, we get that for all
a\in\mathbb{T}_{k}

\mathbb{P}(R_{n,k}=a)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{\zeta_{k}(a)[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)}]G^{a}_{\mu}(z)}{\sum_{b\in\mathbb{T}_{k}}\zeta_{k}(b)[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(b)}]G^{b}_{\mu}(z)}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\frac{\zeta_{k}(a)}{\sum_{b\in\mathbb{T}_{k}}\zeta_{k}(b)},

so we get the result.
∎

Now, let us prove Proposition
8.1
. We divide the proof of Proposition
8.1
into two lemmas.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\mu
be an offspring distribution with generating function satisfying (

H
P

).

1.

For all
a\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
,

G^{a}_{\mu}(z)=Q_{a}(z)\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}F_{\mu}^{k-j}(z),

where
Q_{a}(z)\coloneqq z^{-\phi_{0}(a)}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{a}}q_{i}^{a}z^{i}=\sum_{i=-\phi_{0}(a)}^{d_{a}-\phi_{0}(a)}q_{i+\phi_{0}(a)}^{a}z^{i}
,
q_{i}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i
.

2.

For all
a\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
,
d_{a}=(k-1)(p-1)
so
Q_{a}(z)
has degree
(k-1)(p-1)-\phi_{0}(a)
and
q_{d_{a}}^{a}=(pa_{p})^{k-1}
.

Proof.

First, as
F_{\mu}
satisfies (

H
P

), the
j^{th}
-derivative of
F^{j}_{\mu}
can be written as
F^{j}_{\mu}(z)=P_{j}(z)F_{\mu}(z)
with
P_{j}
a polynomial function with degree
j(p-1)
. So, for all
a\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
, we have

\prod_{u\in I(a)}F_{\mu}^{(c_{u}(a))}(z)=F_{\mu}^{k-\phi_{0}(a)}(z)\prod_{u\in I(a)}P_{c_{u}(a)}(z),

\prod_{u\notin I(a)}\widetilde{F}_{\mu}(z)=z^{-\phi_{0}(a)}\bigl(F_{\mu}(z)-\mu(0)\bigr)^{\phi_{0}(a)}=z^{-\phi_{0}(a)}\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}F^{\phi_{0}(a)-j}_{\mu}(z),

thus by setting
Q_{a}(z)=z^{-\phi_{0}(a)}\prod_{u\in I(a)}P_{c_{u}(a)}(z)\coloneqq z^{-\phi_{0}(a)}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{a}}q_{i}^{a}z^{i}
we deduce that

G^{a}_{\mu}(a)=Q_{a}(z)\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}F^{\phi_{0}(a)-j}_{\mu}(z),

so we get the first point.

Then, as
\deg(P_{j})=j(p-1)
, we have that
d_{a}=\sum_{u\in I(a)}c_{u}(a)(p-1)=(k-1)(p-1)
so
Q_{a}(z)
has degree
(k-1)(p-1)-\phi_{0}(a)
. Moreover, the dominant term of
F^{j}_{\mu}
is equal to the dominant term of
(P^{\prime}(z))^{j}
which is equal to
(pa_{p}z^{p-1})^{j}
, consequently
q_{d_{a}}^{a}=\prod_{u=1}^{k}(pa_{p})^{c_{u}(a)}=(pa_{p})^{k-1}.

∎

For all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we define
e^{m}_{n}\coloneqq[z^{n}]F^{m}_{\mu}(z)
. Applying the first part of Lemma
8.2
we deduce the following equality

[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)}]G^{a}_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{a}-\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}q_{i+\phi_{0}(a)}^{a}e^{k-j}_{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)-i}.

(8.1)

The following lemma essentially tells that the coefficents in
F^{k}_{\mu}
decay fairly quickly, which is typically observed in cases where the generating function has an infinite radius of convergence.

Lemma 8.3
.

Fix
m\in[\![1,k]\!]
. Then,

\frac{e^{m}_{n+1}}{e^{m}_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}(mpa_{p})^{1/p}n^{-1/p}.

Proof.

Fix
m\in[\![1,k]\!]
. By applying Corollary VIII.2 of
[
FS09
]
to
F_{\mu}^{m}(z)=c^{m}\exp(mP(z))
, we get that

e^{m}_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{c^{m}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{n}}}\frac{\exp{(mP(r_{n}))}}{r_{n}^{n}},

where
r_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}
satisfy
r_{n}P^{\prime}(r_{n})=n/m
and
\lambda_{n}=m(n+r_{n}^{2}P^{(2)}(r_{n})).

Thus, we have the following estimate

\frac{e^{m}_{n+1}}{e^{m}_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n+1}}}\frac{r_{n}^{n}}{r_{n+1}^{n+1}}\exp{\bigl(m(P(r_{n+1})-P({r_{n}}))\bigr)}.

(8.2)

In addition, we have that
r_{n}P^{\prime}(r_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}r_{n}pa_{p}r_{n}^{p-1}
so we deduce that
r_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}(n/mpa_{p})^{1/p}
and
\lambda_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}mpn
. Therefore,

\lambda_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\lambda_{n+1}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}r_{n+1}.

(8.3)

To study the limit behavior of
e^{m}_{n+1}/e^{m}_{n}
, Equation (
8.3
) provides sufficient information for
\lambda_{n}
, but a more refined asymptotic expansion is required for
r_{n}
.
By setting
b_{i}\coloneqq(i+1)a_{i+1}
, we have

r_{n}=\frac{n}{mP^{\prime}(r_{n})}=\frac{n}{m}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}b_{i}r^{i}_{n}\biggr)^{-1}=\frac{n}{mb_{p-1}r^{p-1}_{n}}\biggl(1+\sum_{i=1}^{p-2}\frac{b_{i}}{b_{p-1}}r^{i-(p-1)}_{n}\biggr)^{-1},

so

r^{p}_{n}=\frac{n}{mb_{p-1}}\Bigl(1+\frac{b_{p-2}}{b_{p-1}}\frac{1}{r_{n}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{r_{n}}\Bigr)\Bigr)^{-1},

and by doing an asymptotic expansion and reinjecting the equivalent
r_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}(n/mpa_{p})^{1/p}
, we get that

r_{n}=\frac{1}{(mpa_{p})^{1/p}}n^{1/p}-\frac{m^{1/p}(p-1)a_{p-1}}{p^{2}a_{p}}+o(1).

In fact, one can show by iterating this bootstrapping argument, that
r_{n}
admits an asymptotic expansion of the following form

r_{n}=c_{-1}n^{1/p}+c_{0}+\frac{c_{1}}{n^{1/p}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{p-1}}{n^{(p-1)/p}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{n^{(p-1)/p}}\Bigr),

(8.4)

where
c_{-1}=(mpa_{p})^{-1/p}
,
c_{0}=m^{1/p}(p-1)a_{p-1}/p^{2}a_{p}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1}
are real numbers.
Consequently, using Equation (
8.4
), one can verify that
r_{n+1}^{n+1}/r_{n}^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}(mpa_{p})^{1/p}e^{-1/p}n^{-1/p}
and
\exp{(m(P(r_{n+1})-P({r_{n}})))}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}e
. The lemma follows by injecting these estimates in Equation (
8.2
) .
∎

We may now proceed to the proof of the Proposition
8.1
.

Proof of Proposition
8.1
.

Set
a,b\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
. By Equation (
8.1
) and then by applying Lemma
8.3
, we have

[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)}]G^{a}_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{a}-\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}q_{i+\phi_{0}(a)}^{a}e^{k-j}_{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)-i}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\sum_{j=0}^{\phi_{0}(a)}\binom{\phi_{0}(a)}{j}\mu(0)^{j}q_{d_{a}}^{a}e^{k-j}_{n-k-d_{a}}.

Fix
m\in[\![1,k]\!]
. By (
8.2
), we have

\frac{e^{m}_{n-k-d_{a}}}{e^{m+1}_{n-k-d_{a}}}=c\exp{\bigl(mP(r_{n-k-d_{a}})-(m+1)P(r_{n-k-d_{a}})\bigr)}=c\exp{\bigl(-P(r_{n-k-d_{a}})\bigr)}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

Therefore, we deduce that

[z^{n-k-\phi_{0}(a)}]G^{a}_{\mu}(z)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}q_{d_{a}}^{a}e^{k}_{n-k-d_{a}}

As
q_{d_{a}}^{a}e_{n-k-d_{a}}=q_{d_{b}}^{b}e_{n-k-d_{b}}
by the second part of Lemma
8.2
, the result follows.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of the second statement in Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.2.

The
X^{n}_{i}
’s are i.i.d. and satisfies (

H
P

) so the generating function of
X^{n}_{2}+\cdots+X^{n}_{k}
is equal to

F_{X^{n}_{2}+\cdots+X^{n}_{k}}(z)=F^{k-1}_{\mu}(z)=c^{k-1}\exp{\bigl((k-1)P(z)\bigr)}.

Let
\epsilon>0
and define
n_{x}\coloneqq(n-1)(1/k+x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Recalling that for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
e^{m}_{n}=[z^{n}]F^{m}_{\mu}(z)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\Big|\frac{X^{n}_{1}}{n-1}-\frac{1}{k}\Big|\geq\epsilon\,\Big|\,X^{n}_{1}+\cdots+X^{n}_{k}=n-1\Bigr)=\frac{\sum_{|i-(n-1)/k|\geq\epsilon(n-1)}e^{1}_{i}e^{k-1}_{n-1-i}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,j\leq n-1\\
i+j=n-1\end{subarray}}e^{1}_{i}e^{k-1}_{j}}=\frac{\sum_{|i-(n-1)/k|\geq\epsilon(n-1)}u^{i,k}_{n}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,j\leq n-1\\
i+j=n-1\end{subarray}}u^{i,k}_{n}},

where we define
u^{i,k}_{n}\coloneqq e^{1}_{i}e^{k-1}_{n-1-i}
.
We also define
A^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n},A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}
and
B^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n},B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}
by:

A^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}=\sum_{i\leq n_{-\epsilon}}u^{i,k}_{n},\quad A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}=\sum_{i\geq n_{\epsilon}}u^{i,k}_{n},\quad B^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}=\sum_{n_{-\epsilon}<i<(n-1)/k}u^{i,k}_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}=\sum_{(n-1)/k<i<n_{\epsilon}}u^{i,k}_{n}.

In particular, we have that

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\Big|\frac{X^{n}_{1}}{n-1}-\frac{1}{k}\Big|\geq\epsilon\,\Big|\,X^{n}_{1}+\cdots+X^{n}_{k}=n-1\Bigr)\leq\frac{A^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}}{B^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}}+\frac{A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}}{B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}},

(8.5)

so we now aim to prove that
A^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}/B^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}
and
A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}/B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}
tends to zero when
n
tends to infinity to conclude the proof. Fix
\eta>0.

By Lemma
8.3
, we have for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
:

\frac{e^{m}_{n+1}}{e^{m}_{n}}=(mpa_{p})^{1/p}n^{-1/p}\bigl(1+\delta(m,n)\bigr),

(8.6)

where
\delta(m,n)/n\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0
. Therefore, for each
m
, we choose
N_{m}
such that for all
n\geq N_{m}
,
|\delta(m,n)|\leq\eta
. Additionally, let
N^{\prime}_{m}
be an integer such that
(e^{m}_{n})_{n\geq N^{\prime}_{m}}
is a nonincreasing sequence. We first show that
A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}/B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0.
Let
n-1\geq\max{(kN_{1},N^{\prime}_{1}+N_{k-1})}
. We define
n_{\epsilon}\coloneqq(n-1)(1/k+\epsilon)
. Then, for every
i\in[\![n_{\epsilon/2},n-1-N_{k-1}]\!]
, we have by Equation (
8.6
)

\frac{u^{i+1,k}_{n}}{u^{i,k}_{n}}=\frac{e^{1}_{i+1}e^{k-1}_{n-2-i}}{e^{1}_{i}e^{k-1}_{n-1-i}}\leq\biggl(\frac{n-2-i}{(k-1)i}\biggr)^{1/p}\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta}.

As
x\mapsto(n-2-x/(k-1)x)^{1/p}
is a decreasing function, we deduce that

\frac{u^{i+1,k}_{n}}{u^{i,k}_{n}}\leq\biggl(\frac{n-2-n_{\epsilon/2}}{(k-1)n_{\epsilon/2}}\biggr)^{1/p}\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta},

where
0<(n-2-n_{\epsilon/2}/(k-1)n_{\epsilon/2})^{1/p}<1
so that for sufficiently small
\eta
, there exists
\alpha_{\epsilon}<1
such that

\frac{u^{i+1,k}_{n}}{u^{i,k}_{n}}\leq\alpha_{\epsilon}.

(8.7)

Now, for the remaining
N_{k-1}
values of
i\in[\![n-N_{k-1},n-1]\!]
, i.e.
N_{k-1}
values of
j\coloneqq n-1-i\in[\![0,N_{k-1}-1]\!]
, we notice that

\frac{u_{n}^{i,k}}{u_{n}^{n-1-N_{k-1},k}}=\frac{e^{1}_{i}e^{k-1}_{n-1-i}}{e^{1}_{n-1-N_{k-1}}e^{k-1}_{N_{k-1}}}=\frac{e^{1}_{n-1-j}e^{k-1}_{j}}{e^{1}_{n-1-N_{k-1}}e^{k-1}_{N_{k-1}}}

where
e^{k-1}_{j}/e^{k-1}_{N_{k-1}}\leq\max{\{e^{k-1}_{j}/e^{k-1}_{N_{k-1}}:0\leq j\leq N_{k-1}-1\}}\coloneqq c_{k}
and
e^{1}_{n-1-j}/e^{1}_{n-1-N_{k-1}}\leq 1
. Therefore, for every
i\in[\![n-N_{k-1},n-1]\!]
,
u_{n}^{i,k}\leq c_{k}u_{n}^{n-1-N_{k-1},k}\leq c_{k}\alpha_{\epsilon}^{n-1-N_{k-1}-n_{\epsilon}}u_{n}^{n_{\epsilon},k}\leq c_{k}u_{n}^{n_{\epsilon},k}
, where the second inequality comes from Equation (
8.7
).
Hence, applying (
8.7
) for
i\in[\![n_{\epsilon},n-1-N_{k-1}]\!]
, we get that

A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}=\sum_{n_{\epsilon}\leq i\leq n-1}u^{i,k}_{n}\leq\sum_{n_{\epsilon}\leq i\leq n-1-N_{k-1}}u^{n_{\epsilon},k}_{n}\alpha_{\epsilon}^{i-n_{\epsilon}}+\sum_{n-N_{k-1}\leq i\leq n-1}u^{i,k}_{n}\leq\frac{u^{n_{\epsilon},k}_{n}}{1-\alpha_{\epsilon}}+c_{k}N_{k-1}u_{n}^{n_{\epsilon},k}

and

B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}=\sum_{(n-1)/k<i<n_{\epsilon}}u^{i,k}_{n}\geq\sum_{n_{\epsilon/2}<i<n_{\epsilon}}u^{i,k}_{n}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}(n-1)u^{n_{\epsilon},k}_{n}.

The last inequality follows from the fact that, for
i\geq n_{\epsilon/2},

u^{i,k}_{n}
is nonincreasing in
i
as stated in Equation (
8.7
).
Consequently,

\frac{A^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}}{B^{\epsilon,k,r}_{n}}\leq\frac{2}{(1-\alpha_{\epsilon})\epsilon(n-1)}+\frac{2c_{k}N_{k-1}}{\epsilon(n-1)}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

Applying the same method, we obtain that

A^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}/B^{\epsilon,k,l}_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0
, and thus the result follows by inequality (
8.5
).
∎
