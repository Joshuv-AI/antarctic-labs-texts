Cyclic Equalizability of Words and Its Application to Card-Based Cryptography
Kazumasa Shinagawa, Koji Nuida

Abstract
Card-based cryptography is a research area to implement cryptographic procedures using a deck of physical cards. In recent years, it has been found to be related to finite group theory and algebraic combinatorics, and is becoming more and more closely connected to the field of mathematics. In this paper, we discuss the relationship between card-based cryptography and combinatorics on words for the first time. In particular, we focus on cyclic equality of words. We say that a set of words are cyclically equalizable if they can be transformed to be cyclically equal by repeated simultaneous insertion of letters. The main result of this paper is to show that two binary words of equal length and equal Hamming weight are cyclically equalizable. As applications of cyclic equalizability to card-based cryptography, we describe its applications to the information erasure problem and to single-cut full-open protocols.

1.2
Cyclic Equalizability from Card-Based Cryptography

In this section, we see that cyclic equalizability appears naturally in card-based cryptography by observing a protocol called the
five-card trick

[
1
]

random cut

A random cut is a shuffling operation that cyclically shifts a sequence of
face-down
cards a random number of times. For a sequence of
face-down
cards
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1})
, we obtain
(c_{r},c_{r+1},\ldots,c_{r+n-1})

is a secret number chosen uniformly at random and the subscript is modulo
n

Here, the secret number
r
is hidden from all players and nobody can guess it.
We use
\langle\cdot\rangle
to denote the application of a random cut to a sequence of cards.
Random cuts are considered to be the simplest shuffling operation proposed so far, since they can be easily performed by a well-known shuffling method called
Hindu shuffle
.

The five-card trick is a protocol for the AND function
a\wedge b
using a random cut.
A secure computation protocol for the AND function can be used in the following situation:

Alice and Bob are going to decide if they want to go on a date this weekend.
However, Alice and Bob are very shy and embarrassed to tell each other directly whether they want to go on a date or not.
This can be especially awkward if one of them wants to go on a date and the other does not.
Is there any way to know just whether they both want to go on a date or not?

If we assume that “1” means “I want to go on a date” and “0” means “I don’t want to go on a date”, and Alice and Bob each have inputs
a,b\in\{0,1\}

The security of the five-card trick follows from the fact that for any given input
(a,b)
such that
a\wedge b=0
, the resulting sequence is chosen uniformly at random from five patterns on the left side due to the effect of the random cut.
Indeed, if
a\wedge b=0
, a sequence of cards just after Step 2 is given as follows:

(a,b)=(0,0)
;

(a,b)=(0,1)
;

(a,b)=(1,0)
.

Then, a sequence of cards just after Step 3 is given as follows:

(a,b)=(0,0)
;

(a,b)=(0,1)
;

(a,b)=(1,0)
.

Since these sequences are cyclically equal (see Definition
2.1
), they become indistinguishable after a random cut is applied.
We get three cyclically equal sequences by inserting
in the middle.
This is the key to understanding the structure of the five-card trick.

Let us reconsider the above as a problem of combinatorics on words.
From now on, we consider words on
\Sigma
for a finite set of letters
\Sigma
.
We say that
k
words
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\in\Sigma^{n}
are
cyclically equalizable
if all words can be transformed into cyclically equal words by repeated simultaneous insertion of letters, i.e., by inserting the same letters at the same positions (see Definition
2.4
).
The five-card trick corresponds to three binary words
1001,1010,0101
with
\clubsuit=0
and
\heartsuit=1
, which are cyclically equalizable by inserting
1
in the middle.
In this paper, we consider a question of what is the necessary and sufficient condition for the cyclic equalizability.
This is a problem of combinatorics on words that arises naturally from card-based cryptography.

1.3
Our Contribution

In this paper, we show that two binary words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are cyclically equalizable if and only if the Hamming weights of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are equal (Theorem
4.1
).
To show the main theorem, we first show that the cyclic equalizability does not change before and after simultaneous insertion (Theorem
3.1
).
Thanks to this, in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we can assume that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
have no common letters on the same position.
This makes the discussion easy to understand.

As applications
of cyclic equalizability to card-based cryptography, we describe its applications to the information erasure problem and single-cut full-open protocols in Section
5
.
In particular, we discuss the fact that cyclic equalizability is equivalent to the possibility of information erasure (Section
5.1
), and we show a lower bound on the number of cards in single-cut full-open protocols (Section
5.2
).

For a letter

We identify

Definition 2.1

(Cyclically Equal)
.

Two words

(
i=1,2
) of equal length are said to be

if there exists an integer

We denote this relation by

(Note that

Similarly,

w_{i}\in\Sigma^{n}
(
i=1,2,\dots,k
) are said to be
cyclically equal
if any pair of words
w_{i},w_{j}
are cyclically equal.

\Delta
-deletion are all equal.

Remark 2.3
.

A
\Delta
-insertion means repetition of an insertion of a column vector
(cc\cdots c)^{\mathrm{T}}
(
c\in\Delta
) in the matrix representation of
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{k}
as follows:

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,0}&\cdots&a_{1,i}&a_{1,i+1}&\cdots&a_{1,n-1}\\
a_{2,0}&\cdots&a_{2,i}&a_{2,i+1}&\cdots&a_{2,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{k,0}&\cdots&a_{k,i}&a_{k,i+1}&\cdots&a_{k,n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}~\rightarrow~\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,0}&\cdots&a_{1,i}&c&a_{1,i+1}&\cdots&a_{1,n-1}\\
a_{2,0}&\cdots&a_{2,i}&c&a_{2,i+1}&\cdots&a_{2,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{k,0}&\cdots&a_{k,i}&c&a_{k,i+1}&\cdots&a_{k,n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Conversely, a
\Delta
-deletion means repetition of a deletion of a column vector
(cc\cdots c)^{\mathrm{T}}
(
c\in\Delta
) in the matrix representation of
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{k}
as follows:

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,0}&\cdots&a_{1,i}&c&a_{1,i+1}&\cdots&a_{1,n-1}\\
a_{2,0}&\cdots&a_{2,i}&c&a_{2,i+1}&\cdots&a_{2,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{k,0}&\cdots&a_{k,i}&c&a_{k,i+1}&\cdots&a_{k,n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}~\rightarrow~\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,0}&\cdots&a_{1,i}&a_{1,i+1}&\cdots&a_{1,n-1}\\
a_{2,0}&\cdots&a_{2,i}&a_{2,i+1}&\cdots&a_{2,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{k,0}&\cdots&a_{k,i}&a_{k,i+1}&\cdots&a_{k,n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Definition 2.4

(Cyclically Equalizable)
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma
.
We say that
k
words
w_{i}\in\Sigma^{n}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable
if there exist
w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k}\in\Sigma^{*}
that are cyclically equal such that
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\overset{{\Delta}}{\to}(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})
.
When
\Delta=\Sigma
, we simply say that they are cyclically equalizable.

Remark 2.5
.

In Definition
2.2
, we can assume without loss of generality that
u_{0}
is an empty word.
This is because whether
u_{0}a_{i,0}u_{1}a_{i,1}\cdots u_{n-1}a_{i,n-1}u_{n}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) are cyclically equal is not changed by moving
u_{0}
to the rightmost.

Given
k
words
w_{i}\in\Sigma^{n}
(
1\leq i\leq k
), the
\Delta
-cyclic equalizability problem
asks whether they are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable or not, and how to insert if it is possible.
In Section
5.1
, we will see that the cyclic equalizability problem is equivalent to the
information erasure problem
in card-based cryptography as an application of cyclic equalizability.

3
\Delta
-Insertion Preserves Cyclic Equalizability

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma
.
Suppose that we have
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\overset{{\Delta}}{\to}(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})
for
2k
words
w_{i},w^{\prime}_{i}\in\Sigma^{*}
(
1\leq i\leq k
).
Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable.

2.

w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k}
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable.

The following lemma is given for the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
w_{i}=a_{i,0}a_{i,1}\cdots a_{i,n-1}\in\Sigma^{n}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) are cyclically equal.
Then for any
c\in\Sigma
and
\mu\geq 0
,
\widetilde{w}_{i}=a_{i,0}c^{\mu}a_{i,1}c^{\mu}\cdots a_{i,n-1}c^{\mu}\in\Sigma^{(\mu+1)n}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) are also cyclically equal.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show the case where
k=2
.
From the assumption, there exists an integer
\delta
such that
a_{2,j}=a_{1,j+\delta\bmod n}
for all
0\leq j\leq n-1
.
Let
\widetilde{w}_{i}=b_{i,0}b_{i,1}\cdots b_{i,(\mu+1)n-1}
(
b_{i,j}\in\Sigma
).
Then
b_{i,j}=a_{i,j/(\mu+1)}
if
j
is a multiple of
\mu+1
and
b_{i,j}=c
otherwise.
Thus, for each
0\leq j\leq(\mu+1)n-1
,
b_{2,j}=b_{1,j+(\mu+1)\delta\bmod(\mu+1)n}
.
Therefore
\widetilde{w}_{i}
are also cyclically equal.
∎

Theorem
3.1
.

Since it is trivial that the latter condition implies the former, we show the converse direction.
It is sufficient to show that the latter condition follows from the former condition when
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\overset{{c}}{\to}(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})
for some letter
c\in\Delta
because any
\Delta
-insertion can be decomposed into a series of such insertions.

Let
w_{i}=a_{i,0}a_{i,1}\cdots a_{i,n-1}
(
a_{i,j}\in\Sigma
).
From Remark
2.5
, we can assume that
w^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i,0}c^{\mu_{0}}a_{i,1}c^{\mu_{1}}\cdots a_{i,n-1}c^{\mu_{n-1}}
for some common non-negative integers
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n-1}
.
Let
\nu:=\max\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n-1}\}
and
\widehat{b}:=bc^{\nu}\in\Sigma^{\nu+1}
for each
b\in\Sigma
.
Then for
(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})
, a
c
-insertion yields
(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
for
u_{i}=\widehat{a_{i,0}}\widehat{a_{i,1}}\cdots\widehat{a_{i,n-1}}\in\Sigma^{(\nu+1)n}
.
From the assumption that
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable and Remark
2.5
, we obtain
some common letters
d_{0,1},\dots,d_{0,\ell_{0}},\allowbreak\dots,d_{n-1,1},\dots,d_{n-1,\ell_{n-1}}\in\Delta
such that

a_{i,0}d_{0,1}\cdots d_{0,\ell_{0}}~a_{i,1}d_{1,1}\cdots d_{1,\ell_{1}}~\cdots~a_{i,n-1}d_{n-1,1}\cdots d_{n-1,\ell_{n-1}}

are cyclically equal.
Then, from Lemma
3.2
,

\widetilde{u}_{i}:=\widehat{a_{i,0}}\widehat{d_{0,1}}\cdots\widehat{d_{0,\ell_{0}}}~\widehat{a_{i,1}}\widehat{d_{1,1}}\cdots\widehat{d_{1,\ell_{1}}}~\cdots~\widehat{a_{i,n-1}}\widehat{d_{n-1,1}}\cdots\widehat{d_{n-1,\ell_{n-1}}}

are also cyclically equal.
Moreover, since
\widehat{d_{h,j}}\in\Delta^{\nu+1}
, we have

(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\overset{{\Delta}}{\to}(\widetilde{u}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{u}_{k})
.
As mentioned above, we have
(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})\overset{{\Delta}}{\to}(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
, thus we also have

(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})\overset{{\Delta}}{\to}(\widetilde{u}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{u}_{k})
,
therefore
(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable.
∎

The following corollary, which follows directly from Theorem
3.1
, is useful to reduce the
\Delta
-cyclic equalizability for a set of words to that of shorter words.

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose that
(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k})\in(\Sigma^{n^{\prime}})^{k}
is obtained from
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\in(\Sigma^{n})^{k}
by a
\Delta
-deletion for some subset
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma
.
Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable.

2.

w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k}
are
\Delta
-cyclically equalizable.

4
Cyclic Equalizability of Two Binary Words

Throughout this section, we assume that
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.
The following theorem gives a complete characterization on cyclic equalizability of two binary words.

Theorem 4.1
.

Two binary words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are cyclically equalizable if and only if
\mathsf{wt}(w_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w_{2})
.

Proof.

Since the only if part is trivial, we show the if part.
Suppose that
\mathsf{wt}(w_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w_{2})
. From Corollary
3.3
, we can assume that no more
\{0,1\}
-deletion can be applied to
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, i.e., each column in the matrix representation of the pair
(w_{1},w_{2})
is either
(10)^{\mathrm{T}}
or
(01)^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Furthermore, since a cyclic shift of the columns of the matrix does not affect the claim, we can also assume that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are of the following form:

\begin{split}w_{1}&=1^{\mu_{0}}0^{\mu_{1}}1^{\mu_{2}}0^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 1^{\mu_{2N-2}}0^{\mu_{2N-1}};\\
w_{2}&=0^{\mu_{0}}1^{\mu_{1}}0^{\mu_{2}}1^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 0^{\mu_{2N-2}}1^{\mu_{2N-1}},\end{split}

where each
\mu_{j}
is a positive integer and it holds
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\mu_{2j}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\mu_{2j+1}
from the assumption
\mathsf{wt}(w_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w_{2})
.
We assume
N\geq 2
since
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are already cyclically equal if
N=1
.

We say that a pair of binary words
(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})
is
admissible
if
\mathsf{wt}(w^{\prime}_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w^{\prime}_{2})
and they are of the following form:

\begin{split}w^{\prime}_{1}&=1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 0^{\nu^{\prime}_{2N-1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{2N-1}};\\
w^{\prime}_{2}&=1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 0^{\nu^{\prime}_{2N-1}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{2N-1}},\end{split}

for some parameters
\nu^{\prime}_{i}\geq 0
and
\mu^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1
(
0\leq i\leq 2N-1
).
The pair
(w_{1},w_{2})
in the claim is admissible with
\nu^{\prime}_{i}=0
and
\mu^{\prime}_{i}=\mu_{i}
.
For an admissible pair
(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})
and
0\leq j\leq 2N-1
, we say that the pair is
consistent
up to the
j
-th slot if the following holds for all
0\leq i\leq j
:

\nu^{\prime}_{i}+\mu^{\prime}_{i}=\mu^{\prime}_{(i+1)\bmod 2N}+\nu^{\prime}_{(i+2)\bmod 2N}.

For example, the following pair
(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})
is consistent up to the 1th slot if the underlined subsequences are equal:

\begin{split}w^{\prime}_{1}&=\underline{1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 0^{\nu^{\prime}_{2N-1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{2N-1}};\\
w^{\prime}_{2}&=1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}\underline{1^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 0^{\nu^{\prime}_{2N-1}}1^{\mu_{2N-1}}.\end{split}

Note that when
w^{\prime}_{1}
and
w^{\prime}_{2}
are consistent up to the
(2N-3)
-th slot, they are cyclically equal from the following reason.
If they are consistent up to the
(2N-3)
-th slot, they are also consistent up to the
(2N-2)
-th slot due to
\mathsf{wt}(w^{\prime}_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w^{\prime}_{2})
, and furthermore, they are also consistent up to the
(2N-1)
-th slot since they are of equal length.

In the following,
\{0,1\}
-insertions for
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are performed recursively to construct two cyclically equal binary words.

First, perform a
\{0,1\}
-insertion to be consistent up to the 0th slot. If
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are already consistent up to the 0th slot, i.e.,
\mu_{0}=\mu_{1}
, then nothing needs to be done. So we assume that
\mu_{0}\neq\mu_{1}
.
If
\mu_{0}>\mu_{1}
, then they will be consistent up to the 0th slot by the following
1
-insertion:

\begin{split}\widetilde{w_{1}}&=1^{\mu_{0}}0^{\mu_{1}}\underline{1^{\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}}}1^{\mu_{2}}0^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 1^{\mu_{2N-2}}0^{\mu_{2N-1}};\\
\widetilde{w_{2}}&=0^{\mu_{0}}1^{\mu_{1}}\underline{1^{\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}}}0^{\mu_{2}}1^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 0^{\mu_{2N-2}}1^{\mu_{2N-1}}.\end{split}

If
\mu_{0}<\mu_{1}
, then it is enough to do the following
1
-insertion:

\begin{split}\widetilde{w_{1}}&=\underline{1^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}}}1^{\mu_{0}}0^{\mu_{1}}1^{\mu_{2}}0^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 1^{\mu_{2N-2}}0^{\mu_{2N-1}};\\
\widetilde{w_{2}}&=\underline{1^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}}}0^{\mu_{0}}1^{\mu_{1}}0^{\mu_{2}}1^{\mu_{3}}\cdots 0^{\mu_{2N-2}}1^{\mu_{2N-1}}.\end{split}

In either case, the current pair
(\widetilde{w_{1}},\widetilde{w_{2}})
is admissible and consistent up to the 0th slot.

Next, for
1\leq j\leq 2N-3
, when the current pair
(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})
is admissible and consistent up to the
(j-1)
-th slot, we perform
\{0,1\}
-insertions to be consistent up to the
j
-th slot.
Let
k_{0}:=\nu^{\prime}_{j}+\mu^{\prime}_{j}-(\mu^{\prime}_{j+1}+\nu^{\prime}_{j+2})
and
k:=|k_{0}|
.
If
w^{\prime}_{1}
and
w^{\prime}_{2}
are already consistent up to the
j
-th slot, i.e.,
k_{0}=0
, then nothing needs to be done. So we can assume
k_{0}\neq 0
.
If
k_{0}>0
, perform a
0
-insertion of
0^{k}
just before
0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+2}}
when
j
is odd, and a
1
-insertion of
1^{k}
just before
1^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+2}}
when
j
is even.
The following is an example when
j
is odd:

\begin{split}\widetilde{w_{1}}&=\cdots 1^{\mu^{\prime}_{j-1}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+1}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{j+1}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+2}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{j+2}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+3}}\cdots;\\
\widetilde{w_{2}}&=\cdots 0^{\mu^{\prime}_{j-1}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{j+1}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+2}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{j+2}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{j+3}}\cdots.\end{split}

If
k_{0}<0
, for each
0\leq i\leq j
such that
i\equiv j\pmod{2}
,
a
0
-insertion of
0^{k}
just before
0^{\nu^{\prime}_{i}}
is performed when
j
is odd and a
1
-insertion of
1^{k}
just before
1^{\nu^{\prime}_{i}}
when
j
is even.
The following is an example when
j
is odd:

\begin{split}\widetilde{w_{1}}&=1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 1^{\mu^{\prime}_{j-1}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j}}\cdots;\\
\widetilde{w_{2}}&=1^{\nu^{\prime}_{0}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{0}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}1^{\nu^{\prime}_{2}}0^{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{3}}1^{\mu^{\prime}_{3}}\cdots 0^{\mu^{\prime}_{j-1}}\underline{0^{k}}0^{\nu^{\prime}_{j}}\cdots.\end{split}

In either case, the pair
(\widetilde{w_{1}},\widetilde{w_{2}})
is admissible and consistent up to the
j
-th slot.

By performing the above procedure up to
j=2N-3
, we can make
w_{1},w_{2}
cyclically equal.
This completes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

5
Application to Card-Based Cryptography

5.1
Information Erasure

Suppose that we have a sequence of face-down cards
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Sigma^{n}
.
We know that
\vec{x}\in S\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
but do not know which one.
Consider the protocol in the following.

1.

By inserting additional cards, arrange the following sequence:

\underset{x_{1}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{1}}\,\underset{x_{2}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{2}}\,\underset{x_{3}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{3}}\,\cdots\,\underset{x_{n}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{n}}\,,

where
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
.

2.

Apply a random cut.

3.

Open all cards.

We say that the above protocol is
information erasure
if the probability distribution of the opened symbols does not depend on
\vec{x}\in S
.
Given
S\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, the
information erasure problem
asks whether information erasure is possible, and how to insert if it is possible.

It is obvious that the information erasure problem can be solved if the cyclic equalizability problem for
k
words is solved for any
k
.
From Theorem
4.1
, the information erasure problem for
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
with
|S|=2
is completely answered: for
S=\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
, the information erasure problem is possible if and only if
\mathsf{wt}(w_{1})=\mathsf{wt}(w_{2})
and how to insert is given in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
The information erasure problems for
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
with
|S|\geq 3
and for
S\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
with
|\Sigma|\geq 3
are open problems.

5.2
Single-Cut Full-Open Protocols

Suppose that we have a sequence of face-down cards
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\overline{x_{1}},x_{2},\overline{x_{2}},\ldots,x_{n},\overline{x_{n}})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
, i.e.,
n
commitments to
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\in\{0,1\}
.
Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a function.
Consider the protocol in the following.

1.

Permute the order of the sequence by a known permutation
\pi\in S_{2n}
:

\underset{x_{1}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{\overline{x_{1}}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{x_{2}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{\overline{x_{2}}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{x_{n}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{\overline{x_{n}}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,~\rightarrow~\underset{y_{1}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{y_{2}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{y_{3}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underset{y_{4}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{y_{2n}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,,

where
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{2n})=\pi(\vec{x})
.

2.

By inserting additional cards, arrange the following sequence:

\underset{y_{1}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{1}}\,\underset{y_{2}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{2}}\,\underset{y_{3}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{3}}\,\cdots\,\underset{y_{2n}}{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,13.0){\bf?}}}_{u_{2n}}\,,

where
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2n}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
.
Define
s(\vec{x}):=(y_{1},u_{1},y_{2},u_{2},\ldots,y_{2n},u_{2n})
.

3.

Apply a random cut.

4.

Open all cards.

We say that the above protocol is a
single-cut full-open protocol
for
f
if there exist two binary words
z_{0},z_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
such that
z_{0},z_{1}
are not cyclically equal and two binary words
s(\vec{x})
and
z_{f(\vec{x})}
are cyclically equal.
The number of cards in a protocol is defined as
|s(\vec{x})|=2n+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|u_{i}|
.

In 1989, den Boer
[
1
]
proposed a single-cut full-open protocol for the AND function
x_{1}\wedge x_{2}
called the five-card trick (see Section
1.2
), which is the first card-based protocol in the history.
In 2014, Heather, Schneider, and Teague
[
3
]
proposed a single-cut full-open protocol for the three-bit equality function, which outputs
1
if
x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}
and
0
otherwise.
In 2018, Shinagawa and Mizuki
[
13
]
rediscovered the Heather–Schneider–Teague’s protocol and named it as the six-card trick.
In addition, they designed a single-cut full-open protocol for the two-bit equality function, which is equivalent to a protocol for
x_{1}\oplus x_{2}
, and posed an open problem of asking whether
there exists a single-cut full-open protocol for an
n
-bit equality function for
n\geq 4
.
So far, only these three single-cut full-open protocols are known.
It is an open problem to construct single-cut full-open protocols for other interesting functions.

Based on the cyclic equalizability, we can derive a lower bound on the number of cards in single-cut full-open protocols.
For
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
, define
N_{b}(f):=|\{x\mid f(x)=b\}|
.
From the definition, we have
N_{0}(f)+N_{1}(f)=2^{n}
for any function
f
.
First, we have a simple lemma as follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
k
different words
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\in\Sigma^{*}
are cyclically equal, then the length of the word
|w_{i}|
is at least
k
.

Proof.

Any word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
has at most
|w|
words that are cyclically equal to
w
.
∎

From Lemma
5.1
, we obtain a lower bound on the number of cards.

Theorem 5.2
.

The number of cards in a single-cut full-open protocol for a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is at least
\max(N_{0}(f),N_{1}(f))
.

Proof.

Let
T
be the truth table of
f
whose column corresponds to each symbol in
s(\vec{x})
, i.e.,
T
has
|s(\vec{x})|
columns and
2^{n}
rows.
From the definition of single-cut full-open protocols, all
N_{b}(f)
rows are different binary words and cyclically equal for each
b\in\{0,1\}
.
From Lemma
5.1
, the number of cards
|s(\vec{x})|
is at least
\max(N_{0}(f),N_{1}(f))
.
∎

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we show that two binary words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are cyclically equalizable if and only if the Hamming weights of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are equal.
We also give some applications of cyclic equalizability to the information erasure problem and single-cut full-open protocols.

Research on cyclic equalizability has just begun and there are many important open problems.
What is the necessary and sufficient condition for
k
words
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\in\Sigma^{n}
to be cyclically equalizable?
If they are cyclically equalizable, it is also important to minimize the length of the resulting words.
These general problems are obviously important, but the following more specific problems are also of interest.

•

What is the condition for two words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
to be cyclically equalizable?

•

Are there two binary words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of the same Hamming weight that are not
0
-cyclically equalizable?

•

Are there three binary words
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of the same Hamming weight that are not cyclically equalizable? (A counterexample exists for
0
-cyclic equalizability.
Indeed,
1001
,
1010
, and
1100
are not
0
-cyclically equalizable.)

•

Is there a single-cut full-open protocol for the three-bit AND function? In particular, is there a permutation
\pi\in S_{6}
such that seven binary words
\{\pi(x_{1}\overline{x_{1}}x_{2}\overline{x_{2}}x_{3}\overline{x_{3}})\mid(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\{0,1\}^{3}\setminus\{(1,1,1)\}\}
are cyclically equalizable?

•

Is there a single-cut full-open protocol for the four-bit equality function? In particular, is there a permutation
\pi\in S_{8}
such that 14 binary words
\{\pi(x_{1}\overline{x_{1}}x_{2}\overline{x_{2}}x_{3}\overline{x_{3}}x_{4}\overline{x_{4}})\mid(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\{0,1\}^{4}\setminus\{(0,0,0),(1,1,1)\}\}
are cyclically equalizable?

Appendix A
Concrete Example of Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
3.3

1.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{20}
be two binary words of the same Hamming weight as follows:

\begin{split}w_{1}&=1\dot{0}10\dot{\dot{1}}\ 001\dot{0}\dot{\dot{1}}\ \dot{0}1101\ \dot{0}1000;\\
w_{2}&=00011\ 11001\ 00010\ 00111.\end{split}

where they are separated by
5
bits for ease of reading, and the columns of
(00)^{\mathrm{T}}
are dotted at the top and the columns of
(11)^{\mathrm{T}}
are double-dotted at the top.

2.

By applying a
1
-deletion, we can remove the double-dotted columns as follows:

\begin{split}w^{\prime}_{1}&=1\dot{0}100\ 01\dot{0}\dot{0}1\ 101\dot{0}1\ 000;\\
w^{\prime}_{2}&=00011\ 10000\ 01000\ 111.\end{split}

3.

By applying a
0
-deletion, we can remove the dotted columns as follows:

\begin{split}w^{\prime\prime}_{1}&=11000\ 11101\ 1000;\\
w^{\prime\prime}_{2}&=00111\ 00010\ 0111.\end{split}

4.

Now we are ready to apply the proof of Theorem
4.1
. By applying a
1
-insertion, we can make it consistent up to the
0
th slot:

\begin{split}&\underline{\overline{\mathbf{1}}11}00\ 01110\ 11000;\\
&\mathbf{1}00\underline{11\ 1}0001\ 00111,\end{split}

where the bold columns are the columns inserted so far (including this time), the overlined columns are the columns inserted this time, and the underlined subsequences are the matching patterns. Hereafter, we use these notations.

5.

We notice that they are already consistent up to
1
th slot since the next sequences just after the underlined sequences are both
000
.
The underlines are updated as:

\begin{split}&\underline{\mathbf{1}1100\ 0}1110\ 11000;\\
&\mathbf{1}00\underline{11\ 1000}1\ 00111.\end{split}

6.

By applying a
1
-insertion, we can make it consistent up to the
2
th slot:

\begin{split}&\underline{\mathbf{1}1100\ 0111}0\ \overline{\mathbf{11}}110\ 00;\\
&\mathbf{1}00\underline{11\ 10001\ \mathbf{11}}001\ 11.\end{split}

7.

By applying a
0
-insertion, we can make it consistent up to the
3
th slot:

\begin{split}u_{1}&=\underline{\mathbf{1}11\overline{\mathbf{0}}0\ 00111\ \overline{\mathbf{0}}0}\mathbf{11}1\ 1000;\\
u_{2}&=\mathbf{1}00\mathbf{0}\underline{1\ 11000\ \mathbf{0}1\mathbf{11}0\ 0}111.\end{split}

Now
u_{1},u_{2}
are cyclically equal. Thus we can confirm that
w^{\prime\prime}_{1},w^{\prime\prime}_{2}
are cyclically equalizable. This corresponds to the end of the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

8.

Next we apply Corollary
3.3
.
Let
u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2}
be the binary words obtained by replacing
0
and
1
of
w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2}
by
\hat{0}:=01
and
\hat{1}:=11
, respectively, as follows:

\begin{split}u^{\prime}_{1}&=\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{0}\ \hat{0}\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{1}\ \hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{1}\ \hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0};\\
u^{\prime}_{2}&=\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{1}\hat{1}\ \hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0}\ \hat{0}\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0}\ \hat{1}\hat{1}\hat{1}.\end{split}

Then we have
(w_{1},w_{2})\overset{{1}}{\to}(u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2})
because
(w_{1},w_{2})
does not have two or more consecutive double-dotted columns
(11)^{\mathrm{T}}
.

9.

Let
u^{\prime\prime}_{1},u^{\prime\prime}_{2}
be the binary words obtained by replacing
0
and
1
of
w^{\prime\prime}_{1},w^{\prime\prime}_{2}
by
\tilde{0}:=\hat{0}\hat{0}\hat{0}=010101
and
\tilde{1}:=\hat{1}\hat{0}\hat{0}=110101
, respectively, as follows:

\begin{split}u^{\prime\prime}_{1}&=\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\ \tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{1}\ \tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0};\\
u^{\prime\prime}_{2}&=\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\ \tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\ \tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}.\end{split}

Now we notice that
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and
u^{\prime\prime}_{2}
are obtained by inserting
\hat{0}
’s in
u^{\prime}_{1}
and
u^{\prime}_{2}
because
(u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2})
has at most two consecutive columns
(\hat{0}\hat{0})^{\mathrm{T}}
. By combining this with
(w_{1},w_{2})\overset{{1}}{\to}(u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2})
in Step 8, we have
(w_{1},w_{2})\xrightarrow{\{0,1\}}(u^{\prime\prime}_{1},u^{\prime\prime}_{2})
.

10.

Finally, in the same operations to obtain
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
from
w^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and
w^{\prime\prime}_{2}
, the following binary words are obtained by inserting
\tilde{0}
’s and
\tilde{1}
’s in
u^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and
u^{\prime\prime}_{2}
as follows:

\begin{split}u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{1}&=\mathbf{\tilde{1}}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\mathbf{\tilde{0}}\tilde{0}\ \tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\ \mathbf{\tilde{0}}\tilde{0}\mathbf{\tilde{1}\tilde{1}}\tilde{1}\ \tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0};\\
u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2}&=\mathbf{\tilde{1}}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\mathbf{\tilde{0}}\tilde{1}\ \tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\ \mathbf{\tilde{0}}\tilde{1}\mathbf{\tilde{1}\tilde{1}}\tilde{0}\ \tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{1}.\end{split}

are cyclically equal of length
19\times 6=114

w_{1},w_{2}
are cyclically equalizable.

The length of the resulting binary words in the above construction depends on the order of the characters to be deleted. In fact, if we apply a
0
-deletion first and then a
1
-deletion, we have the resulting binary words of length

.
