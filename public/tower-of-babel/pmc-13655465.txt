Synergistic Effect of Bacteriophage vB_KpnS_Pey and Antibiotic Combinations against Clinical Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae Isolates
Authors: Parva Farmehr, Salehe Sabouri, Roya Ahmadrajabi, Saereh Mohammadpour, Hossein Hosseini-Nave, Sanaz Rastegar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rise of antimicrobial resistance, particularly among carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), presents a serious therapeutic challenge. This study aimed to isolate and characterize a bacteriophage effective against prophage-free CRKP and to evaluate its potential synergistic effects with ciprofloxacin, meropenem, gentamicin, and colistin.Materials and MethodsA total of 30 non-duplicate clinical blaNDM-positive CRKP isolates were collected from hospitalized patients in Kerman, Iran. Prophage-free isolates were identified using mitomycin C induction assays. A bacteriophage was isolated from hospital wastewater using enrichment and double-layer agar methods. Phage morphology was identified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Host range and efficiency of plating (EOP) were determined using spot assays. The optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) was assessed by optical density at 600 nm (OD600), and phage replication dynamics, including latent period and burst size, were evaluated using a one-step growth curve. Antibiotic susceptibility profiles and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for ciprofloxacin, meropenem, gentamicin, and colistin were determined by disk diffusion and broth microdilution. Phage–time-kill experiments were used to evaluate antibiotic interactions.ResultsTEM revealed that phage vB_KpnS_Pey is a member of the class Caudoviricetes, with an icosahedral head and a long, non-contractile tail. This phage produced clear, halo-form plaques and exhibited lytic activity against 13.3% of CRKP isolates. It showed high EOP against most susceptible strains, a latent period of approximately 30 minutes, and a burst size of 315 plaque-forming units (PFU) per infected cell. The optimal MOI was determined to be 1,000 PFU/colony-forming unit. Combination assays demonstrated phage–antibiotic synergy between vB_KpnS_Pey and subinhibitory concentrations of ciprofloxacin and meropenem, resulting in enhanced bacterial clearance in time-kill experiments. No synergistic effect was observed with gentamicin and colistin.ConclusionThese findings highlight the therapeutic potential of phage vB_KpnS_Pey, particularly when combined with ciprofloxacin and meropenem, as an alternative or combined strategy for treating infections caused by CRKP. Further research is required to fully understand the bacteriophage's mechanisms of action and its associated limitations.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a prominent Gram-negative bacterium frequently found in the gastrointestinal tract and nasopharynx of healthy individuals, as well as in the environment [1]. This bacterium causes opportunistic infections in both nosocomial and community settings. It is implicated in a broad spectrum of infections, including sepsis, pneumonia, liver abscesses, soft tissue infections, and urinary tract infections. Collectively, these infections contribute to an estimated 790,000 deaths annually [2]. The emergence and global spread of multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae, particularly carbapenem-resistant strains (CRKP), pose a significant challenge to effective antibiotic therapy. CRKP strains harbor resistance genes, such as blaKPC-2 and blaNDM-1, which provide the ability to hydrolyze nearly all β-lactam antibiotics. This mechanism causes CRKP infections to be severely challenging to treat [3]. Due to the serious public health concerns associated with carbapenem and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant K. pneumoniae, the World Health Organization has listed it as a high-priority pathogen in its 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List [4]. Given the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance and the concomitant emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains, there is an imperative need for alternative therapeutic strategies [5].

The application of bacteriophages presents a promising approach. Bacteriophages, or phages, are viruses that specifically target, infect, and lyse their host bacteria [6]. Bacteriophages are classified as lysogenic or lytic based on their life cycles. In therapeutic contexts, lytic phages are preferred as they selectively infect and rapidly lyse host bacterial cells without integrating their genome into the host chromosome. This ensures effective bacterial clearance while minimizing the risk of horizontal gene transfer, which can occur with lysogenic phages and potentially contribute to the spread of antibiotic resistance or virulence factors [7]. Studies have shown that antibacterial phages are effective against K. pneumoniae [8]. Bacteriophages can be used effectively alongside antibiotics to treat bacterial infections. Their distinct mechanisms of action could lead to synergistic antibacterial effects. This phenomenon, often termed phage-antibiotic synergy (PAS), significantly enhances inhibition of bacterial pathogen proliferation [9]. Furthermore, bacteriophages offer several distinct advantages over conventional antibacterial therapies when employed as therapeutic agents. These advantages include their high specificity towards bacterial hosts, which allows them to target pathogens without disturbing the normal flora; their ability to self-replicate at the site of infection; and their proven effectiveness against multidrug-resistant bacterial strains and biofilms. In addition, phages are considered safe, naturally occurring biological entities with the potential for genetic modification, making them promising candidates for the development of antimicrobial strategies [2]. Conversely, phage therapy faces several limitations, including a narrow host spectrum, the potential for the emergence of resistance in bacteria, and variable lytic efficacy. Therefore, the sustained progress and clinical application of phage therapy critically depend on the discovery and thorough characterization of novel phages [10]. Although PAS has been documented in numerous studies, growing evidence indicates that this phenomenon is highly context-dependent, strongly influenced by the specific bacteriophage employed, its biological and replication-related characteristics, and the genetic and phenotypic properties of the bacterial host strain [9]. Consequently, outcomes observed with a given phage or individual clinical isolate cannot be readily extrapolated to other phage–host systems. Therefore, a robust assessment of PAS necessitates the identification and comprehensive characterization of newly isolated phages to ensure accurate interpretation of phage–antibiotic interactions [7]. Previous studies have reported the presence of bla NDM-producing K.pneumoniae isolates in Kerman, Iran [11]. Accordingly, the present study focused on carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae harboring blaNDM to address a regionally relevant mechanism of resistance. This investigation aimed to isolate, comprehensively characterize, and evaluate, in vitro, the efficacy of a bacteriophage alone and in combination with antibiotics (meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and colistin) against blaNDM-positive CRKP clinical isolates.

Materials and Methods

1. Bacterial isolates

Non-duplicate clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae were collected consecutively from hospitalized patients admitted to Afzali-Pour Hospital, Kerman, Iran, between March and October 2023. Bacterial cultures were initially streaked onto MacConkey agar plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. The resulting colonies suggestive of K. pneumoniae were then validated through Gram staining and a series of standard biochemical and diagnostic tests. These tests included catalase, oxidase, urease, Simmons Citrate utilization, Triple Sugar Iron agar, and Sulfide Indole Motility medium. Subsequently, all confirmed K. pneumoniae isolates demonstrating resistance to at least one carbapenem antibiotic (e.g., meropenem, imipenem, or ertapenem), in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI, 2024), were subjected to Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) testing for the identification of the blaNDM gene [12]. From this screening, 35 K. pneumoniae isolates positive for the blaNDM gene were selected for further investigation.

2. Ethics statement

The project received ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (Kerman, Iran) under code IR.KMU.AH.REC.1403.119. All procedures were performed following the relevant guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Bacterial isolates were collected from teaching hospital laboratories as part of routine hospital procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from all the patients enrolled in the study.

3. Antibacterial susceptibility determination

In accordance with CLSI guidelines (2024), the antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the isolates were evaluated. Both Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion and microbroth dilution methods were employed. The disk diffusion method utilized ROSCO (Co, Denmark) antibiotic disks, including meropenem (10 μg), imipenem (10 μg), cefoxitin (30 μg), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefepime (30 μg), cefotaxime (30 μg), cefpodoxime (10 μg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (25 μg), amikacin (30 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), and ciprofloxacin (5 μg). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and colistin were determined using a modified colorimetric broth microdilution assay in 96-well microtiter plates [9]. Briefly, two-fold serial dilutions of each antibiotic were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth and mixed with an equal volume of a standardized bacterial suspension (106 colony-forming unit [CFU]/mL). Microplates were incubated at 37°C for 18 h. Subsequently, 20 µL of 0.1% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was added to each well, followed by an additional incubation for 2–3 h at 37°C to allow viable bacterial cells to reduce TTC to the red formazan. In this assay, viable bacterial cells reduce TTC to red formazan; therefore, the MIC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration that completely inhibited visible formazan formation, indicating suppression of bacterial metabolic activity [9].

To assess the effect of phage–antibiotic combinations, MICs were also determined in the presence of bacteriophage vB_KpnS_Pey (Pey) using the same assay conditions. For combination assays, a fixed phage concentration corresponding to an MOI of 0.1 (approximately 1 × 104 PFU/mL) was used. The bacteriophage and antibiotics were added simultaneously to each well, and the phage-to-antibiotic ratio was kept constant across all tested antibiotic concentrations. MIC values were determined for each antibiotic alone and in combination with the phage. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Quality control for susceptibility testing was performed using Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (CLSI, 2024).

4. Prophage detection

Prophage induction was performed on all CRKP isolates utilizing Mitomycin C (MitC) with minor modifications. Initially, overnight Luria-Bertani (LB) broth cultures were diluted at a ratio of 1:100 and incubated at 37°C with shaking (190 rpm). Once the cultures achieved an optical density (OD600) of 0.2 (approximately 2 hours), they were treated with MitC. A final concentration of 0.5 μg/mL was achieved by adding 5 μL of a 2 mg/mL MitC stock solution to 20 mL of the cultures. A parallel diluted culture lacking MitC served as the negative control. The cultures were monitored by measuring the OD600 at hourly intervals over a 24-hour incubation period. For supernatant isolation, a 1 mL aliquot was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C to pellet the bacterial cells. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, transferred to a sterile tube, and stored at 4°C until further analysis. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in fresh LB broth and incubated at 37°C for 3 hours to restore growth. To prepare the bacterial lawn, 10 µL of the 3-hour culture was combined with 4 mL of molten top agar (LB broth containing 0.7% agar) and overlaid onto solid LB agar plates. Once the agar had solidified, 10 µL aliquots of the filtrate collected from the induced culture were spotted onto the surface. The plates were incubated upright overnight at 37°C and subsequently examined for the presence of lysis zones (plaques) [13].

5. Phage isolation

Wastewater samples were obtained from Afzali-Pour Hospital in Kerman, Iran, between October and November 2023. 100 mL wastewater samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 minutes, and the supernatant was filtered with a 0.22-μm sterile disposable polyethersulfone (PES) membrane syringe filter (Startech, Taiwan) and subsequently stored at 4°C. Three randomly selected CRKP isolates (Kp1, Kp4, and Kp5) were cultured in LB broth for subsequent phage isolation. Ten mL of the sample supernatant was added to 100 mL of the host culture. These host cultures were in their early logarithmic phase (OD600 0.4-0.6) and contained 0.1 M calcium chloride (Merck, Germany). The combined cultures were incubated for 48 hours at 37°C with shaking (30 rpm). Following incubation, samples underwent centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes. Finally, the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 µm pore-sized membrane syringe filter to isolate phages from other potential contaminants. The obtained filtrate was utilized in the double-agar overlay method [9].

6. Plaque purification of phages and the double-agar overlay method

The double-agar overlay method was performed with minor modifications as follows: 100 µL of an early-logarithmic-phase bacterial culture (OD600 ≈ 0.4) was combined with 100 µL of the prepared phage filtrate. This mixture was incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes to facilitate phage adsorption to the host bacteria. Following adsorption, 4 mL of LB soft agar (0.7% agar) was added to the mixture, which was then immediately poured onto a base agar layer (2% agar) and incubated overnight at 37°C. The formation of clear zones of lysis, or plaques, indicated the successful isolation of lytic phages. For subsequent purification, individual clear plaques were selected. Each visible plaque represents a high concentration of phages, typically containing 105 to 106 phages [9]. The chosen plaque was suspended in 2 mL of LB medium. Following the established phage isolation methodology, the plaque-purification procedure was performed three times for each phage, thereby generating a homogeneous plaque. The final phage concentration active against CRKP isolates was determined by the double-agar overlay method using serial ten-fold dilutions of the purified phage lysate, and results were expressed as plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL).

7. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following the previously described method [14], a high-titer phage lysate (1012 PFU/mL) was prepared and then centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 60 minutes. The resulting phage pellet was washed with 0.1 M ammonium acetate (pH 7.0) and resuspended in SM buffer. For TEM, samples were deposited on carbon-coated Formvar films and negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate. Imaging was conducted using a ZEISS EM 900 TEM (Zeiss NTS, Oberkochen, Germany) at an acceleration voltage of 50 kV. Phage family classification was determined based on morphological characteristics observed by TEM, in accordance with the latest International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) report (https://ictv.global/taxonomy).

8. Host range investigation

The host ranges of the phages were assessed using spot tests against a panel of 30 clinical isolates of Carbapenem-Resistant K. pneumoniae, five clinical isolates each of Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii strains [14]. All S.aureus, A. baumannii, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa strains were obtained from the bacterial strain collection at the Microbiology and Virology Department of Kerman University of Medical Sciences. For the spot tests, a double-layer agar plate was prepared by mixing a 200 µL aliquot of an overnight bacterial broth culture (adjusted to 109 CFU/ml) with melted soft agar, which was then spread over a solidified agar base. Subsequently, 5 μl of phage stock was spotted onto each plate. Following overnight incubation at 37°C, plates were examined for zones of clearing, indicative of the phages' antibacterial activity.

9. The efficiency of plating (EOP)

Bacterial isolates demonstrating lytic activity in host-range screening underwent further characterisation using an EOP assay [15]. The EOP evaluates the productive infection of phage obtained from the tested bacterial strains relative to the primary host strain that produces the phage progenies. The experimental protocol involved preparing fresh bacterial cultures in LB medium and incubating them overnight at 37°C. Phage titers were subsequently determined by a double-layer plaque assay using serially diluted phage (103 to 109 PFU/mL). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 18 h, and all assays were performed in triplicate. After incubation, EOP estimation was performed. This involved deriving the ratio of the mean plaque-forming units obtained using the alternative host (PFUa) against those obtained using the production host (PFUp), mathematically represented as PFUa/PFUp. EOP thresholds were established to classify phage production efficiency as high (≥0.5), medium (>0.1 to <0.5), low (≥0.001 to <0.1), or inefficient (<0.001).

10. Determination of optimal multiplicity of infection

To identify the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI), 100 µL of the host strain (104 CFU/ml) was combined with 100 µL of various phage dilutions (ranging from 102 to 108 PFU/ml), yielding MOIs of 0.001 to 1,000. Absorbance at 600 nm (OD600) was measured using a microplate ELISA reader (ELx800, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours post-infection. To ensure the validity of the results, several controls were included. A negative control consisted solely of LB medium, providing baseline absorbance readings in the absence of biological activity. A positive control contained the host bacterial strain without the addition of any phage, serving to monitor normal bacterial growth and viability in the absence of infection. This experiment was conducted in triplicate [9].

11. One-step growth curve

A one-step growth experiment was performed to determine the phage's latent period and burst size [16]. The latent period is defined as the interval from phage adsorption to the bacterial cell until progeny phage release, while the burst size represents the ratio of the final number of released phage particles to the initial number of infected bacterial cells during the latent phase.

The host bacterium, Kp2, was grown in LB broth until it reached the logarithmic growth phase. Phage was then introduced to the bacterial culture at an MOI of 0.01 (108 CFU/mL bacteria and 106 PFU/mL phage). This mixture was incubated at 37°C for 10 minutes to facilitate phage adsorption. Following adsorption, the culture was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 minutes to pellet bacteria with adsorbed phages. The supernatant was decanted, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in fresh LB broth. Samples (100 µL) were collected at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 120, and 150 minutes post-resuspension. Each sample was subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions in 1×PBS, and phage titers were quantified using the double agar overlay method. The number of PFU/mL was recorded for each time point. The experiment included three technical and biological replicates (n=3).

12. Time-kill assay

To assess the synergistic potential of a phage with meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and colistin, the time-kill curve method was performed in a 96-well microtiter plate as previously described [17]. Bacterial growth was simultaneously monitored across different experimental combinations. These included: bacteria alone (5×108 CFU/mL) (control); bacteria exposed to antibiotics at sub-MIC concentrations; bacteria treated with the phage (MOI 10 PFU/CFU); and a combination of bacteria, the phage, and antibiotics (Sub-MIC). The effects of phage and/or antimicrobial agents on bacterial populations were quantified by measuring changes in optical density at 620 nm (OD620), a technique well-suited for high-throughput analysis. Absorbance at OD600 was recorded using a SpectraMax i3 microplate reader at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours of incubation at 37°C. The resulting data were subsequently utilised to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) through numerical integration, employing the following formula:

In the formula, ∆t represented 2 hours, and f(i × ∆t) corresponded to the OD600 measurements taken every 2 hours over the 24-hour experimental duration. To allow quantitative comparison between treatments, AUC values were normalized to the untreated control (C). Let SA, SB, and SAB represent the normalized AUC values obtained for bacteriophage alone, antibiotic alone, and the phage–antibiotic combination, respectively. Facilitation was defined as occurring when both of the following inequalities were satisfied:

Facilitation requires both SA/C > SAB/C and SB/C > SAB/C. Taking logarithms, these conditions can be expressed as:

Log(SA) - log(C) > log(SAB) - log(C)

or

log(SAB) - log(SA) < 0

Likewise,

Lo(SAB) - log(SB) < 0

The null model of no facilitation assumes that the effect of the combination equals that of the most effective single treatment.

Synergistic interaction:

Synergy was defined using a multiplicative interaction model, whereby the antibacterial effect of the combined bacteriophage–antibiotic treatment exceeds that expected from the independent action of each agent. Specifically, synergy was inferred when SA/C×SB/C > SAB/C. where C represents the normalized AUC of the untreated control, SA and SB denote the normalized AUC values obtained after treatment with bacteriophage alone and antibiotic alone, respectively, and SAB corresponds to the normalized AUC value of the combined treatment.

Taking logarithms, this inequality can be expressed as:

Log(C) - log(SA) - log(SB) + log(SAB) < 0

An additive interaction was defined as a combined treatment that was more effective than each monotherapy but did not meet the criteria for synergy. A neutral (indifferent) interaction was assigned when the effect of the combination (SAB) was comparable to that of the most effective single agent (SA or SB). An antagonistic interaction was defined when the combined treatment (SAB) was less effective than the most active monotherapy. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and interaction outcomes were determined based on statistical comparisons of normalized AUC values [18].

13. Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in at least triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), a standard approach for reporting variability in experimental studies. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Differences among treatment groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparisons test for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.050.

Results

1. K. pneumoniae isolates and antibiotic susceptibility

Out of 35 blaNDM-positive CRKP isolates, five were identified as prophage-positive and were excluded from the study; therefore, the remaining 30 isolates were used for the subsequent assays. All (100%, n=30/30) displayed resistance to imipenem, meropenem, and cefotaxime. Other notable high resistance rates were observed for cefoxitin (96.6%, n=29/30), ceftazidime and ceftizoxime (90.0%, n=27/30), cefpodoxime, cefepime, and ciprofloxacin (83.3%, n=25/30), gentamicin (76.6%, n=23/30), and amikacin (73.3%, n=22/30). Notably, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and tigecycline exhibited the lowest rates of resistance among all tested antibiotics, at 56.6% (n=17/30) and 36.66% (n=11/30), respectively. For the four CRKP isolates subsequently confirmed to be susceptible to the isolated phage, MIC values were determined for meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and colistin. 2. Phage morphology and plaques

The morphology of phage particles was determined by TEM. A head diameter and length of 64.72±1.6 nm and 78.4±3.9 nm, respectively, were observed, and a non-contractile tail length of 116.6±0.7 nm was measured (Fig. 1A). Consequently, the phage is classified morphologically as a member of the class Caudoviricetes, characterized by an icosahedral head and a long, non-contractile tail. The phage was named vB_KpnS_Pey, based on its morphology and host, K. pneumoniae. Halo-form plaques, with clear centers approximately 3-3.7 mm in diameter and turbid edges, were formed on bacterial lawns (Fig. 1B).

(A): Virion particle morphology of phage vB_KpnS_Pey observed by TEM. (B): Halo-form plaques, featuring clear centers and turbid edges, formed on Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae lawns. TEM, transmission electron microscopy.

3. Phage host range

The host range of vB_KpnS_Pey was tested against clinical isolates, including 30 isolates of CRKP and five isolates each of A. baumannii, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus. The phage's ability to infect these bacteria was determined by observing plaque formation. vB_KpnS_Pey was effective against only 4 out of the 30 (13.3%) CRKP isolates (Kp1-Kp4). No infectivity was observed against any of the clinical isolates of A. baumannii, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, or S. aureus.

4. EOP results

CRKP isolates that showed a positive result in the spot test subsequently underwent the EOP assay. Phage vB_KpnS_Pey demonstrated a highly productive infection, exhibiting an EOP value of ≥0.500 across three tested isolates. One isolate displayed medium efficiency (EOP values ranging from 0.1 to 0.5), and no isolate showed low efficiency (EOP values between 0.001 and 0.1).

5. Optimal MOI

The MOI was assessed across a range from 0.001 to 1,000 to identify the optimal concentration of phage particles required to eliminate or reduce host cell populations, given an initial bacterial concentration of 104 CFU/mL. A ratio of 1,000 for phage vB_KpnS_Pey to its host (K. pneumoniae Kp2) resulted in the lowest observed medium turbidity, and this ratio was subsequently designated as the optimal MOI. The determined optimal MOI was then employed in all subsequent experimental procedures, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Determination of optimal MOI for vB_KpnS_Pey against carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in vitro. K.pneumoniae isolate Kp2 was infected by vB_KpnS_Pey at MOIs of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 and cultured for up to 24 hours. 6. One-step growth curve

The one-step growth curve of phage vB_KpnS_Pey was analyzed, revealing a latent period of 28-30 minutes. The plateau phase commenced after 50 minutes, with the phage concentration reaching a maximum of 6×1010 PFU/mL. Furthermore, the burst size for this phage was determined to be 315 PFU per infected cell (Figure 3).

One-step growth curve of the phage vB_KpnS_Pey.

7. The combined effect of phage vB_KpnS_Pey and antibiotics against Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates

MICs of various antibiotics were determined for K. pneumoniae isolates, both alone and in combination with phage vB_KpnS_Pey. A summary of the MICs is presented in Table 1. For meropenem, the MIC range was 16 to 32 µg/mL. Upon combination with phage vB_KpnS_Pey, two isolates, Kp1 and Kp4, showed a 2-fold reduction in the meropenem MIC, whereas no change was observed in Kp2 or Kp3. Ciprofloxacin showed the most pronounced effect, with Kp1 exhibiting an 8-fold reduction, and Kp4 and Kp2 displayed a 2-fold decrease; Kp3 remained unaffected. Gentamicin MICs were reduced in two isolates; Kp3 showed a 2-fold reduction, and Kp4 demonstrated a 64-fold decrease, while Kp1 and Kp2 remained unchanged. For colistin, no difference was observed in three isolates, but Kp4 showed a 16-fold reduction.

In the time-kill assay, a PAS effect was specifically observed when carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae Kp4 isolates were combined with the phage vB_KpnS_Pey and a sub-inhibitory concentration (½×MIC) of ciprofloxacin and meropenem. This synergistic reduction in bacterial growth, as monitored by optical density at 600 nm, became evident 24 hours post-incubation. Quantitative OD600 values and statistical comparisons are presented in Table 2, while the overall growth trends are shown in Figure 4. In contrast, under the same experimental conditions, the time-kill curve method with the Kp4 isolate showed no significant synergistic effect when the phage vB_KpnS_Pey was combined with subinhibitory concentrations (½×MIC) of gentamicin and colistin. Bacterial populations in these combinations did not show enhanced reduction compared to phage or antibiotic treatment alone at any of the monitored time points, 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours, confirming the absence of consistent phage–antibiotic synergy in these treatments.

Discussion

The emergence of CRKP highlights a critical threat in infectious diseases, arising from the bacteria's ability to overcome carbapenem efficacy. K. pneumoniae is recognized as a key member of the ESKAPEE pathogens, a group known for promoting antimicrobial resistance in healthcare settings [1]. Carbapenems, including imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem, are a class of β-lactam antibiotics characterized by broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Their mechanism of action involves inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to transpeptidases, thereby causing cell lysis. Acquired resistance to carbapenems primarily results from carbapenemase production, acquisition of mobile genetic elements, and active efflux systems that transport the antibiotic out of bacterial cells [19]. The increasing prevalence of carbapenem-resistant strains is a critical public health concern, thereby diminishing therapeutic options and underscoring the need for effective antimicrobial stewardship and the evaluation of alternative therapies, such as phage therapy [11]. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of combining subinhibitory concentrations of meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and colistin with a bacteriophage specifically targeting blaNDM-positive K. pneumoniae, given the high prevalence of these isolates in Kerman, Iran [11]. Before starting the combination experiments, the isolates were screened for prophages. MitC was used to induce prophages by activating the bacterial SOS response. MitC damages DNA, causing RecA to cleave the CI repressor and shift the phage from the lysogenic to the lytic cycle. Although negative results can arise from defective prophages, this method is widely used to identify inducible phages [13].In this study, 5 of 35 K. pneumoniae isolates contained active prophages. Since the aim of this study was to isolate and evaluate a lytic bacteriophage, prophage-positive isolates were excluded from subsequent experiments. This exclusion was performed to minimize potential confounding effects. Inducible prophages may cause endogenous phage release and bacteriolysis independent of the tested phage, and antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin can further promote prophage induction by activating the bacterial SOS response [20]. Therefore, inclusion of only prophage-free CRKP isolates was necessary to ensure accurate interpretation of lytic phage activity and phage–antibiotic synergy [21]. Phage vB_KpnS_Pey, isolated from hospital wastewater, exhibited the ability to infect K. pneumoniae isolates and produced halo-form plaques, characterized by clear centers and turbid edges. The appearance of halos surrounding the plaques is often attributed to the production of phage-encoded polysaccharide depolymerases (endo-glycanohydrolases) [22]. These enzymes degrade capsular polysaccharides and biofilm-associated exopolysaccharides (EPS). Bacterial EPS play a crucial role in the inhibitory effects of biofilms on the host immune response. Additionally, polysaccharides are structural components attached to the bacterial surface, forming a dense scaffolding network. Notably, this biofilm matrix persists even after antibiotic treatment, often harboring dormant bacteria that are tolerant to conventional therapies. Consequently, antibiotics particularly reduce the overall bacterial load without fully eradicating the infection [23]. In contrast, bacteriophage-encoded depolymerases specifically target the glycosidic bonds of EPS, thereby disrupting the protective architecture of biofilms and enhancing phage penetration and antibacterial activity [24].

In the current study, phage vB_KpnS_Pey, identified as belonging to the class Caudoviricetes through TEM analysis, revealed a distinctive head-tail structure and exhibited a latent period of 28–30 minutes and an average burst size of 315 PFU/cell. Our results show that the phage replicates efficiently, yielding abundant progeny that are crucial for controlling K. pneumoniae infections. Such high reproductive potential significantly enhances the feasibility of its therapeutic use [25]. The latent period is defined as the time interval between phage adsorption to the host cell and the initiation of progeny phage release (burst), while burst size refers to the average number of new phage particles released per infected cell [26]. Notably, our findings closely align with those reported by Peng et al. for phage vB_KleS-HSE3, a member of the Siphoviridae, which exhibited a comparable latent period of approximately 30 minutes and a burst size of approximately 277 PFU/cell [25]. Similarly, Bai et al. also reported a latent period of 30 minutes for phage vB_kpnM_17-11; however, its burst size was notably lower at 31.7 PFU/cell [27]. A shorter latent period is a crucial determinant of phage effectiveness, enabling faster replication and host cell lysis, which in turn leads to more efficient bacterial population control [28]. The MOI for phages is variable; a lower optimal MOI indicates that fewer phage particles are required to lyse a given number of bacterial cells effectively [29]. For phage vB_KpnS_Pey, an MOI of 1,000 produced the greatest reduction in bacterial growth under the experimental conditions tested and was therefore designated the optimal inhibitory MOI. However, because phage progeny yield was not directly quantified, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of maximal replication efficiency or cost-effective large-scale phage production [2729].

This study revealed that the phage vB_KpnS_Pey exhibits a highly specific and narrow host range, being effective against only a small subset (13.3%) of CRKP clinical isolates. The absence of infectivity against other common multidrug-resistant pathogens, such as A. baumannii, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus, further underscores its limited spectrum. This specificity, while potentially advantageous in minimizing disruption to commensal flora, suggests that, like most isolated phages so far, vB_KpnS_Pey alone may not be a broad-spectrum therapeutic solution for infections caused by a diverse range of carbapenem-resistant organisms. Its potential application may be limited to highly targeted phage therapy, which may require a cocktail of phages to cover a broader spectrum of strains, and/or combination with antibiotics [30]. This observation of a narrow host range for vB_KpnS_Pey is consistent with findings for other phages, as exemplified by phage vB_kpnM_17-11, which similarly exhibited a restricted host spectrum, being able to infect only 4.35% (4 out of 92) of the bacterial strains tested [27]. A study by Zurabov et al. demonstrated that bacteriophages vB_KpnS_FZ10 and vB_KpnP_FZ12 exhibited broad lytic activity, effectively targeting a high percentage of the bacterial strains tested, 57% and 71%, respectively. In contrast, the lytic efficiency of phages vB_KpnS_FZ41 and vB_KpnM_FZ14 was comparatively lower, each displaying activity against only 29% of the strains. Despite these individual differences in host range, the observed overlapping activity among all four viruses suggests that their combined application could potentially achieve comprehensive efficacy against all investigated bacterial strains [31] The host range of a phage is inherently dependent on both the specific phage type and the bacterial strain. These differences in host range are frequently attributed to variations in phage tail structure, which is critically responsible for recognizing specific bacterial surface receptors and subsequent adsorption [27]. Despite its narrow host range, further investigation using the EOP assay on K. pneumoniae isolates susceptible to phage vB_KpnS_Pey revealed a generally high level of productive infection. A significant proportion of these isolates 3 out of the 4 demonstrated efficient phage replication, as indicated by EOP values of ≥0.500. This high efficiency is a critical characteristic for a therapeutic phage, suggesting that vB_KpnS_Pey can effectively lyse and propagate within these bacterial hosts. One isolate exhibited a moderate EOP, implying a somewhat less potent but still effective interaction. Importantly, the absence of any isolates with low EOP (0.001-0.1) underscores that when vB_KpnS_Pey is capable of infecting a K. pneumoniae strain, it does so with considerable lytic capability. Similarly, in the study conducted by Fayez et al., phage vB_Kpn_ZC2 demonstrated an EOP ≥1 against four out of seven tested isolates, confirming its effective lytic capability within their hosts [32]. Variations in EOP across studies could be due to differences in the specific phage and host strains utilized. As reported by Karthika et al., the EOP of individual phages showed considerable variation across different K. pneumoniae isolates, ranging from complete inactivity to high efficiency. In contrast, the phage cocktail exhibited broader host coverage and consistently higher efficiency, suggesting that combining phages can overcome the limitations of individual phages with a narrow host range [14].

The increasing prevalence of human infections caused by carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella strains has notably accelerated research into potential therapeutic phages for K. pneumoniae. Indeed, the application of identified bacteriophages as antimicrobial agents is a well-established practice [33]. Nevertheless, to effectively combat the multifaceted challenge of multidrug resistance, a single-agent approach often proves insufficient. PAS provides a potent strategy by simultaneously employing diverse antimicrobial mechanisms. This combinatorial approach enhances bacterial elimination, may restore antibiotic susceptibility, and can disrupt the development of resistance by applying distinct selective pressures [9]. The present study indicated that while phage vB_KpnS_Pey can synergistically interact with several antibiotics to lower their MICs against specific CRKP isolates, its most pronounced and consistent effect was observed in combination with ciprofloxacin. The more variable and strain-specific effects of meropenem and gentamicin, and the largely limited impact of colistin, except for a single isolate, highlight the nuanced nature of phage-antibiotic interactions. To confirm the synergistic effects, time-kill curve experiments were performed on K. pneumoniae isolate Kp4, the only phage-susceptible isolate that exhibited MIC reductions for all four tested antibiotics in the presence of phage vB_KpnS_Pey. Phage-antibiotic synergy was specifically observed when Kp4 was treated with phage vB_KpnS_Pey and a sub-inhibitory concentration (½×MIC) of ciprofloxacin and meropenem. This synergistic reduction in bacterial growth was evident 24 hours post-incubation. This time-dependent effect likely reflects delayed suppression of bacterial regrowth and the time required for effective phage amplification under the applied MOI (0.1). In contrast, under the same time-kill curve conditions for Kp4, no significant synergistic effect was detected when the phage was combined with sub-inhibitory concentrations (½×MIC) of gentamicin and colistin at any monitored time points. The absence of synergy with gentamicin and colistin, despite MIC reductions, may be attributed to antibiotic-specific effects on bacterial physiology that constrain productive phage infection, such as inhibition of protein synthesis by gentamicin or outer-membrane perturbation that affects phage adsorption in the case of colistin [34].

It should be noted that the phage–antibiotic synergistic effect confirmed by the time–kill assay was observed only in isolate Kp4. Given the pronounced heterogeneity among CRKP strains in terms of genetic background, antibiotic susceptibility, and phage receptor expression, these findings should not be generalized to all CRKP isolates. Consistent with our observations, Paranos et al. indicate varying degrees of PAS across different antimicrobial agents when tested against multidrug-resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae isolates. Specifically, PAS was observed for ciprofloxacin in (6 out of 15) isolates, meropenem in (10 out of 15), ceftazidime/avibactam in (11 out of 15), colistin in (8 out of 15), and amikacin in (9/15). These results consistently used all four phages (vB_KpP_AttikonH1, vB_KpP_AttikonH2, vB_KpP_AttikonH4, vB_KpP_AttikonH5) against the MDR K. pneumoniae strain [35]. Similarly, the study by Ziller et al. documented comparable results. They showed that phage vB_KpnD-LAPAZ, when co-administered with ciprofloxacin and meropenem, produced synergistic effects against MDR K. pneumoniae isolates [36]. Consistent with these observations, Karthika et al. also reported strong PAS involving ciprofloxacin and phages against K. pneumoniae isolates [14]. According to Zhao et al., while individual phage cocktails exhibited limited efficacy against MDR K. pneumoniae, their combination with last-resort antibiotics such as colistin or tigecycline produced a strong synergistic effect, potentiating bacterial inhibition. These findings emphasize the potential of PAS to improve treatment outcomes against multidrug-resistant pathogens [33] The findings by Rastegar et al. highlight the critical role of antibiotic concentration in phage-antibiotic synergism. Their time-kill experiments revealed a synergistic effect of phage SA1 and colistin at a ⅛ × MIC colistin concentration following 4 hours of incubation, an effect that was not observed at a lower concentration of 1/16 × MIC [9]. While our time-kill tests did not show synergy with gentamicin, Wang et al. reported conversely that in vitro co-administration of phage vB_KpP_HS106 and gentamicin resulted in synergistic bactericidal effects, which subsequently reduced the MIC of gentamicin [37]. An animal model further confirmed the therapeutic utility of phage vB_KpnM_P-KP2 against K. pneumoniae W-KP2 (K47 serotype) in lethal pneumonia. vB_KpnM_P-KP2 alone was as effective as gentamicin, and, crucially, co-treatment with gentamicin provided complete protection against infection in mice [38]. Wang et al. also demonstrated potent PAS against CRKP using bacteriophage vB_KpnP_23. Strong synergistic interactions were observed with meropenem, with a dramatic reduction in MIC; similar reductions were noted for amikacin and levofloxacin. Their findings demonstrated that PAS significantly reduced antibiotic MICs, with effects substantially greater than those of monotherapy. They further indicated that this efficacy could be optimized by adjusting MOI [39]. This variability in PAS results underscores the importance of adopting personalized medicine strategies in the management of infectious diseases, in which phage–antibiotic combinations are optimized based on the specific pathogen and its resistance profile. Moreover, the observation that MOI modulation can enhance synergistic interactions underscores the critical role of dosage optimization in phage therapy. Careful adjustment of the phage-to-bacterium ratio may therefore improve the overall effectiveness of combined therapeutic regimens [39]. PAS effects between phages and antibiotics have been attributed to several mechanisms, such as antibiotic-mediated phage release within bacterial hosts, antibiotic-induced cellular elongation, accelerated phage replication and enlarged burst size, diminished emergence of mutantation in phages and bacterial host, enhanced bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics in the presence of phages, reduction in antibiotic MIC when phages are combined with antibiotics, as demonstrated in the present study, and enzymatic degradation of bacterial polysaccharides by phage-derived enzymes [35]. This study has several limitations that should be considered. All experiments were performed in vitro; therefore, the observed PAS effects may not fully reflect in vivo conditions, particularly host immune responses and pharmacokinetic interactions. Only a limited number of clinical CRKP isolates were tested, which may not encompass the complete genetic diversity of prevalent strains. In addition, time-kill assays in the present study were based on OD600 measurements, which primarily reflect changes in bacterial biomass rather than direct quantification of viable cells. While this approach is used for high-throughput assessment of phage–antibiotic interactions and growth dynamics, it does not distinguish between live and non-viable bacteria. Therefore, the OD-AUC-based synergy results should be interpreted with caution, as they were not validated using CFU-based time-kill assays. CFU-based methods may provide more accurate confirmation of bactericidal activity and synergistic effects. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported that, particularly in phage–antibiotic combination assays, reductions in optical density generally correlate with decreases in bacterial cell numbers. Future studies incorporating the CFU assay would further strengthen the validation of the observed results [39].

In conclusion, the favorable outcomes of combining phage-antibiotics against CRKP highlight a promising direction in antimicrobial research, offering the potential to establish more efficient treatment strategies for diverse resistant pathogens and to mitigate the global challenge of antibiotic resistance. These findings could underscore the potential of phage-antibiotic combination therapy, particularly for restoring susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and meropenem, but a comprehensive understanding will require further studies. Specifically, dynamic in vitro systems that accurately simulate the human pharmacokinetics of both phage and antibiotic agents are essential. Concurrently, in vivo animal models are critical for evaluating the influence of complex host-related factors on therapeutic outcomes and for isolating specific evaluations to identify optimal therapeutic combinations.
