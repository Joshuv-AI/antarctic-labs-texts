Ultrasound-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration in parapharyngeal space lesions: a critical appraisal with illustrative cases
Authors: Rossella Catona, Salvatore Alessio Angileri, Giovanni Maria Rodà, Carolina Lanza, Chiara Luisa Bianchi, Stefano Fiori, Gianpaolo Carrafiello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParapharyngeal space (PPS) lesions represent a rare and heterogeneous group of deep neck entities whose diagnostic evaluation is often challenging because of their deep anatomical location and their close relationship with major neurovascular structures. Cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in lesion characterization and anatomical localization. However, in selected cases cytological confirmation may be required to refine the differential diagnosis and guide clinical management. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration (FNA) may represent a minimally invasive diagnostic option when an adequate acoustic window and a safe percutaneous access corridor can be identified. Nevertheless, PPS sampling differs substantially from routine cervical FNA because of the anatomical complexity of the region and the potential proximity to major vascular structures. This article provides a critical appraisal of ultrasound-guided percutaneous FNA for PPS lesions, focusing on anatomical considerations, technical workflow, and decision-making in patient selection. Three illustrative cases from our institutional experience demonstrate how imaging finding and ultrasound assessment guide the decision-making process in performing - or avoiding - percutaneous sampling of PPS lesions.Keywords: Parapharyngeal space (PPS), fine-needle aspiration (FNA), ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (ultrasound-guided FNA), interventional radiology, deep neck lesions, pleomorphic adenoma

Introduction

Parapharyngeal space (PPS) lesions represent a rare and anatomically complex group of deep neck entities, accounting for approximately 0.5% of all head and neck neoplasms (1,2). Their deep location and close relationship with major neurovascular structures make both diagnosis and treatment particularly challenging. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and typically related to mass effect rather than histological type (2). Patients may present with symptoms such as dysphagia, throat discomfort, or a sensation of pharyngeal fullness, frequently leading to delayed detection (1,2).

Cross-sectional imaging plays a central role in the diagnostic evaluation of PPS lesions (3). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allow accurate anatomical compartmentalization, characterization of lesion morphology, and assessment of relationships with surrounding vascular and neural structures (4,5). In many cases, imaging findings may strongly suggest a specific diagnosis based on typical displacement patterns and enhancement characteristics (5,6). Nevertheless, imaging alone does not always permit definitive differentiation between benign and malignant pathology, and tissue sampling may be required in selected cases to refine the differential diagnosis and guide clinical management (7,8).

Image-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration (FNA) represents a minimally invasive diagnostic option when a safe percutaneous corridor can be identified (8,9). In this setting, the procedure is often performed by Interventional Radiologists using ultrasound or CT guidance (10). However, sampling lesions within the PPS differs substantially from routine cervical FNA because of the depth of the target and the density of adjacent neurovascular structures (10,11). Careful imaging evaluation, procedural planning, and intra-procedural vascular assessment are therefore essential to minimize risk (11,12).

The aim of this article is to provide a critical appraisal of ultrasound-guided percutaneous FNA of parapharyngeal space lesions, focusing on anatomical considerations, lesion stratification, and technical workflow. In addition, selected illustrative cases from our institutional experience are presented to highlight practical challenges and decision-making in the management of PPS lesions.

Clinical anatomy of the parapharyngeal space

General boundaries and spatial configuration

The PPS is a deep neck compartment with an inverted pyramidal configuration extending from the skull base superiorly to the level of the hyoid bone inferiorly. Its base lies at the skull base, while the apex points inferiorly toward the greater cornu of the hyoid (2,4).

Medially, the PPS is bordered by the superior pharyngeal constrictor muscle and the pharyngeal mucosal space. Laterally, it is limited by the mandibular ramus and the deep lobe of the parotid gland. Posteriorly, it is bounded by the prevertebral fascia and musculature, while anteriorly it is related to the pterygomandibular raphe (2,5).

The PPS is predominantly a fat-containing space. On cross-sectional imaging, this fat provides a natural contrast that allows identification of displacement patterns produced by adjacent lesions (4). Recognition of these patterns is essential for determining the compartment of origin and for distinguishing primary PPS tumors from lesions extending from neighboring anatomical spaces (5,6).

Prestyloid compartment

The prestyloid compartment lies anterior and lateral to the styloid process and its associated fascia. It mainly contains adipose tissue and salivary gland elements, particularly the deep lobe of the parotid gland (2,5).

Because of this anatomical composition, most tumors arising in the prestyloid compartment are of salivary gland origin. Pleomorphic adenoma represents the most frequently encountered benign lesion in this region (1,2). On imaging, prestyloid lesions typically displace the carotid sheath structures posteriorly while maintaining smooth margins (5).

From a procedural standpoint, the relative paucity of major vascular structures in this compartment may allow a percutaneous approach when an adequate acoustic window and safe trajectory are present (10,11).

Poststyloid compartment

The poststyloid compartment is located posterior and medial to the styloid process. It contains critical neurovascular structures including the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, cranial nerves IX through XII, and the sympathetic chain (2,4).

Lesions arising in this compartment are more frequently neurogenic or vascular in origin (1,6). Schwannomas represent the most common neurogenic tumors (6,13), while paragangliomas arise from paraganglionic tissue associated with the carotid body or vagal chain (14,15).

Because of the high density of vascular and neural structures, lesions in the poststyloid space present greater technical challenges for percutaneous sampling. Careful imaging evaluation is therefore essential to assess vascular relationships and procedural feasibility (10,11).

Spectrum of parapharyngeal space lesions and diagnostic implications

Parapharyngeal space lesions represent a heterogeneous group of pathologies with different biological behaviors, vascular characteristics, and diagnostic implications (1,2). Accurate lesion stratification is essential because it directly influences both therapeutic planning and the indication for tissue sampling (3,5). In many cases, imaging findings alone may strongly suggest a specific diagnosis, whereas in others cytological confirmation may be useful to guide management (4,6,14).

For the Interventional Radiologist, the primary challenge lies in determining when percutaneous sampling is appropriate and when it may be unnecessary or potentially unsafe (10). The spectrum of PPS lesions includes salivary gland tumors, neurogenic tumors, and highly vascular lesions, each presenting distinct diagnostic considerations (1).

As will be illustrated in the clinical cases presented later in this article, careful integration of imaging findings with procedural planning is essential to determine whether fine-needle aspiration should be performed or avoided.

Salivary gland tumors

Salivary gland tumors represent the most common lesions arising in the prestyloid compartment of the PPS. These tumors frequently originate from the deep lobe of the parotid gland or from minor salivary gland tissue within the space (1,2).

Pleomorphic adenoma is by far the most common benign salivary tumor encountered in this region (1,2). On imaging, these lesions typically appear as well-circumscribed masses with smooth margins and characteristic displacement of adjacent structures rather than infiltration (4,5). MRI usually demonstrates low signal intensity on T1-weighted sequences and very high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, sometimes with internal heterogeneity in larger lesions (4,16).

In selected cases, cytological confirmation may assist in preoperative planning, particularly when imaging findings are not entirely characteristic or when malignancy cannot be excluded. One such scenario is illustrated in our series of illustrative cases, in which ultrasound-guided FNA provided cytological confirmation of pleomorphic adenoma (7,8).

Neurogenic tumors

Neurogenic tumors constitute the most frequent lesions of the poststyloid compartment. Schwannomas arising from cranial nerves IX–XII or from the sympathetic chain are the most commonly encountered entities.

These tumors typically present as well-defined masses that displace rather than invade surrounding structures (6,13). Imaging plays a key role in suggesting the nerve of origin based on characteristic displacement patterns of the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein (6,17).

Because of their close relationship with major vascular structures, percutaneous sampling of neurogenic tumors may be technically challenging (10,11). In many cases, imaging findings may already strongly suggest the diagnosis, and tissue sampling may not be required (6,13).

Highly vascular lesions

Highly vascular lesions represent a particularly important diagnostic category in the PPS. Paragangliomas arising from paraganglionic tissue associated with the carotid body or vagal chain are the most representative example (14,15).

On cross-sectional imaging, paragangliomas demonstrate intense contrast enhancement and may show characteristic vascular flow voids on MRI (14,15). Because of their hypervascular nature, these tumors have traditionally been considered relative contraindications to fine-needle aspiration (14).

In many cases, the diagnosis can be confidently established on the basis of imaging findings alone. As will be illustrated in one of our cases, recognition of imaging features suggestive of vascular lesions is critical, as percutaneous biopsy may expose the patient to unnecessary risk (14,15).

Pharyngeal lesions

A subset of masses presenting as parapharyngeal lesions actually originates from the pharynx and expands laterally toward the parapharyngeal space. In these cases, the lesion typically produces medial bulging of the oropharyngeal wall that is readily visible during endoscopic examination (18).

Because these lesions arise from the pharyngeal mucosa or submucosal structures, tissue sampling is usually performed through transoral or endoscopic approaches (18,19). Such procedures are generally carried out by otorhinolaryngologists and fall outside the scope of percutaneous image-guided biopsy.

For this reason, pharyngeal lesions are not addressed in detail in the present review, which focuses specifically on the radiological perspective and on percutaneous image-guided diagnostic approaches to parapharyngeal space lesions.

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration: technical workflow

Preprocedural imaging assessment and target planning

Careful review of second-level cross-sectional imaging is a mandatory step before attempting percutaneous sampling in the parapharyngeal space. In this anatomical region, imaging assessment should be considered an integral component of the procedure rather than a preliminary diagnostic step (10,11).

Contrast-enhanced CT and MRI allow accurate identification of the compartment of origin, characterization of lesion morphology, and evaluation of relationships with surrounding neurovascular structures (3,4). Particular attention must be paid to the spatial relationship with the carotid sheath structures, including the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein, as well as to the direction of vascular displacement (5,6).

Assessment of lesion vascularity is equally important. Lesions demonstrating intense contrast enhancement or other features suggestive of hypervascularity require particular caution, and in selected cases imaging findings alone may be sufficient to establish a diagnosis without tissue sampling (14,15).

Based on cross-sectional imaging, the operator should identify a potential percutaneous access corridor, estimate the skin-to-target distance, and anticipate possible anatomical constraints such as mandibular acoustic shadowing or a narrow lateral approach. These elements are essential for safe targeting in a region characterized by complex and variable anatomy (11,20).

Ultrasound evaluation and acoustic window

Following patient positioning in the supine position with mild neck extension and contralateral head rotation, a preliminary ultrasound examination is performed to assess the feasibility of a percutaneous approach (12).

High-frequency linear transducers are generally preferred because of their superior spatial resolution (12). However, the deep location of PPS lesions frequently limits sonographic visualization. Acoustic shadowing from the mandibular ramus and depth-related attenuation may reduce lesion conspicuity, and the lesion may appear smaller than on MRI (11,20).

The primary objective of this step is to confirm that the lesion can be visualized sufficiently to allow controlled needle advancement and that a safe trajectory can be established. If adequate visualization cannot be achieved, CT-guided biopsy may represent a more appropriate alternative (9,11,21).

Doppler vascular mapping

Systematic Doppler evaluation is a fundamental component of ultrasound-guided procedures in the PPS. Color or power Doppler imaging must be used to identify the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and other vascular structures along the projected needle trajectory (10,12).

Because PPS lesions typically displace rather than invade adjacent vessels, vascular structures may not occupy their expected anatomical position (5,6). Doppler mapping therefore provides essential real-time information for safe procedural planning (12).

Importantly, Doppler evaluation should not be performed only once at the beginning of the examination. Whenever probe orientation or needle trajectory is modified, Doppler reassessment should be performed to confirm that the intended access corridor remains free of major vascular structures.

Recognition of vascular abnormalities during Doppler evaluation may significantly alter procedural decision-making, as highlighted in one of the illustrative cases presented later in this article.

Needle advancement and sampling technique

Once a safe access corridor has been identified, percutaneous fine-needle aspiration can be performed under real-time ultrasound guidance (8,9). Thin needles ranging from 21 to 25 gauge are typically used for PPS lesions in order to balance diagnostic adequacy with procedural safety (7,8).

Smaller-caliber needles are generally preferred in this anatomical region because they reduce the risk of vascular injury and minimize hemorrhagic contamination of cytological samples (8).

Needle advancement should be performed slowly under continuous ultrasound visualization (12,22). Whenever possible, an in-plane approach is preferred because it allows the operator to monitor the entire needle shaft and tip during progression (22,23).

Loss of needle-tip visualization should prompt immediate interruption of advancement until the tip position is clearly identified (23). Once the needle tip is confirmed within the lesion, gentle aspiration can be performed. In deeply located PPS lesions, the number of passes should be limited in order to minimize procedural risk (7,8).

Published series evaluating fine-needle aspiration of PPS lesions have demonstrated that even limited cytological material may be sufficient for diagnostic interpretation when integrated with imaging findings (7,8,24).

Freehand versus needle-guided technique

Ultrasound-guided FNA may be performed either using a needle-guidance device mounted on the transducer or with a freehand technique.

Needle-guidance systems provide mechanical alignment between the needle and the ultrasound beam, facilitating visualization of the needle trajectory and improving procedural standardization (22,25). In experimental and clinical studies, these systems have been shown to improve needle visualization and reduce procedure time, particularly for operators with limited experience. Most available comparative data, however, derive from phantom models or from procedures performed in anatomical regions other than the parapharyngeal space, limiting direct extrapolation to PPS interventions (25-27).

However, the rigid trajectory imposed by a needle guide may limit the ability to make subtle adjustments in needle angulation. In anatomically complex regions such as the PPS, safe access often requires oblique trajectories and continuous adaptation to the spatial relationship between the lesion and adjacent vascular structures (11). For this reason, once the needle has been advanced through the superficial planes, the operator may detach the guidance system and proceed freehand to safely reach deeper and more angulated targets.

The freehand technique allows greater flexibility, enabling dynamic modification of the needle path based on real-time ultrasound and Doppler findings. This approach is particularly advantageous in the PPS, where vascular structures may be displaced and require continuous reassessment during needle advancement (22,27).

It should be noted that the initial phase of needle insertion may be associated with slightly greater patient discomfort, particularly during passage through superficial tissues where needle visualization is established.

Comparative studies have demonstrated similar diagnostic accuracy between guided and freehand techniques while preserving procedural adaptability in challenging anatomical scenarios (22,26,27).

For this reason, the choice between freehand and needle-guided techniques should be individualized and primarily based on anatomical feasibility rather than on a fixed procedural protocol.

Ultrasound versus CT-guided sampling

Ultrasound guidance

Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration provides continuous real-time visualization of both the needle and surrounding soft tissues. In the parapharyngeal space, this dynamic visualization is particularly valuable because of the close relationship between lesions and major vascular structures (10,12). The integration of Doppler imaging allows direct identification of the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein during both trajectory planning and needle advancement (11,12).

Another advantage of ultrasound guidance is the absence of ionizing radiation. This is particularly relevant in patients who may require repeated imaging examinations or diagnostic procedures (12). Furthermore, the use of thin needles—typically ranging from 21 to 25 gauge—often allows the procedure to be performed without local anesthesia, making it well tolerated in an outpatient setting (7,8).

However, ultrasound guidance is not universally feasible for all PPS lesions. The deep location of the space, combined with acoustic shadowing from the mandibular ramus or unfavorable cervical anatomy, may limit lesion visualization or prevent identification of a safe percutaneous corridor (9,11). In such circumstances, alternative image-guided techniques should be considered (9,20,21).

CT guidance

CT-guided biopsy represents an important alternative when ultrasound visualization is inadequate or when lesion depth prevents reliable sonographic targeting (9,11,21). The technique relies on detailed cross-sectional anatomical mapping to define a safe trajectory toward the target lesion (10,20).

During CT-guided procedures, a planning scan is first obtained to identify the optimal entry site and trajectory. The needle is then advanced incrementally with intermittent CT acquisitions to confirm tip position (11,20). This stepwise approach allows accurate targeting even for deeply located lesions or for masses located in anatomically complex regions of the suprahyoid neck (11,28).

In selected cases, CT guidance also allows alternative access routes, including transoral approaches for lesions that are not reachable through a safe percutaneous corridor. However, these approaches typically require general anesthesia to ensure adequate airway protection, patient immobility, and procedural safety, particularly in deep oropharyngeal and parapharyngeal locations (18,19).

Nevertheless, CT guidance differs fundamentally from ultrasound guidance in that it does not provide continuous real-time visualization during needle advancement (12). In addition, radiation exposure must be considered, particularly when multiple control scans are required during the procedure (10,20). Furthermore, the need for general anesthesia in selected cases may increase procedural complexity and resource utilization compared with ultrasound-guided techniques (18).

Modality selection

In the parapharyngeal space, the choice between ultrasound and CT guidance should be based primarily on anatomical feasibility and procedural safety rather than on operator preference (9,11).

When the lesion can be adequately visualized with ultrasound and a safe access corridor can be identified, ultrasound guidance offers several advantages, including real-time needle visualization, dynamic Doppler vascular assessment, and avoidance of ionizing radiation (10,12). Conversely, when acoustic windows are limited or lesion depth prevents reliable sonographic targeting, CT guidance provides a controlled alternative based on detailed cross-sectional imaging (20,21).

Transoral biopsy techniques are not addressed in the present appraisal, as they fall within the domain of otorhinolaryngological surgical practice rather than percutaneous image-guided procedures (18,19,29).

Illustrative cases

The following cases illustrate selected diagnostic scenarios encountered in our institutional experience at Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico di Milano, where ultrasound-guided percutaneous FNA of deep neck lesions is performed within the Interventional Radiology service.

These examples highlight different aspects of the clinical decision-making process in the management of parapharyngeal space lesions.

Through these cases we aim to demonstrate how the integration of cross-sectional imaging, ultrasound assessment, and Doppler evaluation plays a critical role in determining whether percutaneous sampling should be performed or avoided.

Pleomorphic adenoma of the parapharyngeal space

A 50-year-old male patient presented with persistent throat discomfort and a sensation of left-sided pharyngeal fullness. Otorhinolaryngological evaluation, including flexible fiberoptic endoscopy, revealed a left-sided oropharyngeal bulge suggestive of a deep mass effect without mucosal involvement.

MRI demonstrated a left parapharyngeal space lesion with well-defined margins and heterogeneous signal intensity. On T1-weighted sequences, the lesion showed predominantly low signal intensity. On T2-weighted and STIR sequences, it exhibited very high signal intensity with internal heterogeneity, a pattern commonly observed in larger pleomorphic adenomas (Figure 1A,1B). A peripheral rim of relatively lower signal intensity was appreciable on T2-weighted imaging, corresponding to the surrounding fibrous capsule. The lesion was located within the prestyloid compartment and displaced adjacent structures without evidence of infiltration.

(A,B) MRI showing a well-circumscribed prestyloid parapharyngeal lesion with low signal intensity on T1-weighted sequences and marked hyperintensity on T2-weighted images. (C) Ultrasound evaluation demonstrating a deep parapharyngeal mass (arrow) with reduced conspicuity compared with MRI because of acoustic attenuation. (D) Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration with visualization of the needle tip within the lesion (arrow). (E) Cytologic smear, Papanicolaou stain, 5× magnification. The aspirated material is composed of myoepithelial cells admixed with abundant myxoid matrix. (F) Histologic section, hematoxylin-eosin stain, 20× magnification. The lesion demonstrates a biphasic proliferation of epithelial and myoepithelial cells embedded within a variably myxoid and chondromyxoid stroma, consistent with pleomorphic adenoma. (G) Immunohistochemical staining for SOX10, 20× magnification. Diffuse nuclear positivity is observed within the myoepithelial cell component. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Given the imaging characteristics and the absence of definitive radiological criteria excluding salivary origin, the patient was referred for ultrasound-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration.

On ultrasound examination, the lesion was identifiable but appeared significantly smaller than on MRI because of depth-related acoustic attenuation (Figure 1C). Visualization was suboptimal with a high-frequency linear transducer, and adjacent vascular structures were not clearly delineated on grayscale imaging alone. The preprocedural MRI was therefore essential for anatomical orientation and trajectory planning.

A transparotid percutaneous approach was selected in order to access the prestyloid compartment through a controlled lateral corridor. Color Doppler evaluation was systematically performed to assess vascular relationships along the projected needle path. No intervening major vascular structures were identified within the selected access route.

Given the depth of the lesion and the relatively narrow acoustic window, a needle-guidance device was used to ensure stable alignment. A single pass was performed using a 22-gauge needle (Figure 1D). In light of the deep location and partial visualization constraints, aspiration was deliberately limited to minimize procedural risk.

Cytological analysis demonstrated epithelial and stromal elements consistent with pleomorphic adenoma (Figure 1E-1G).

This case illustrates the importance of integrating second-level imaging with cautious intra-procedural assessment in deep PPS lesions. Even when ultrasound visualization is limited, careful planning and controlled needle advancement may allow safe sampling and adequate diagnostic yield.

Internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysm mimicking a parapharyngeal mass

A 76-year-old female patient was referred for evaluation after the incidental detection of a right parapharyngeal mass during otorhinolaryngological examination. Initial clinical assessment revealed a bulging of the right lateral pharyngeal wall with medial displacement of the soft palate, while no cervical masses were palpable on physical examination. Cross-sectional imaging initially suggested a right parapharyngeal mass producing medial displacement of the lateral pharyngeal wall (Figure 2A).

(B) Doppler ultrasound demonstrating intense pulsatile vascular flow within the lesion. (C,D) Contrast-enhanced CT reconstructions confirming a large saccular aneurysm of the extracranial internal carotid artery with partially thrombosed lumen. CT, computed tomography; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Ultrasound evaluation performed during radiological assessment demonstrated an oval lesion in the right submandibular region measuring approximately 4.7 cm, characterized by calcified walls and intense internal Doppler flow (Figure 2B). Given the vascular signal detected during the examination, fine-needle aspiration was not performed and further cross-sectional imaging was recommended.

Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT of the neck revealed a large saccular aneurysmal dilatation of the extracranial right internal carotid artery located approximately 3–4 cm distal to the carotid bifurcation. The lesion measured approximately 4 cm in transverse diameter and 5 cm in craniocaudal extension, with partially calcified walls and a partially thrombosed lumen producing mass effect on the oropharynx (Figure 2C,2D).

The patient was therefore referred to vascular surgery. Surgical treatment consisted of laterocervical exposure of the carotid axis with isolation of the common, internal, and external carotid arteries. After systemic heparinization and carotid clamping, the aneurysmal sac was opened and partially resected. Carotid reconstruction was performed using end-to-end anastomosis between the proximal and distal segments of the internal carotid artery.

Histopathological examination of the resected vascular wall demonstrated extensive dystrophic calcification with chronic inflammatory changes and fibrin deposits, consistent with a degenerative pseudoaneurysm of the carotid artery.

This case highlights the importance of systematic Doppler evaluation in the assessment of suspected parapharyngeal masses. Recognition of vascular flow within the lesion allowed correct identification of the vascular nature of the mass and prevented a potentially hazardous percutaneous puncture.

Vagal Schwannoma of the parapharyngeal space

A 28-year-old male patient presented with an incidental right laterocervical mass detected during clinical evaluation. MRI demonstrated a well-circumscribed mass located along the expected course of the vagus nerve within the right parapharyngeal space, with signal characteristics compatible with a neurogenic tumor (Figure 3A,3B).

(C) Ultrasound image obtained during ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration of the lesion (arrow). (D) Cytoblock section, hematoxylin-eosin stain, 10× magnification. The specimen shows a spindle-cell proliferation composed of elongated cells with wavy nuclei arranged in fascicular aggregates. (E) Immunohistochemical staining for SOX10, 10× magnification. Diffuse strong nuclear positivity is observed in the neoplastic spindle-cell population. (F) Histologic section, hematoxylin-eosin stain, 20× magnification. The lesion is composed of spindle cells with elongated, wavy nuclei arranged in intersecting fascicles, with alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration was performed using a 22-gauge needle under real-time ultrasound guidance (Figure 3C). The procedure was completed without complications and cytological samples were obtained for pathological analysis.

Cytopathological examination demonstrated spindle-cell morphology with diffuse S100 positivity, consistent with schwannoma (Figure 3D-3F).

The patient subsequently underwent elective surgical excision. Through a right laterocervical approach, a well-circumscribed mass measuring approximately 3.5 cm was identified in close relationship with the vagus nerve and located between the internal jugular vein superficially and the internal carotid artery medially. The tumor was carefully dissected from surrounding structures and excised while preserving the major neurovascular structures.

Definitive histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of vagal schwannoma, with focal ancient-type features and low proliferative activity (Ki-67 <5%). The lesion was largely encapsulated and showed no features of malignancy.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in stable clinical condition following routine postoperative care.

This case demonstrates the diagnostic value of ultrasound-guided sampling in neurogenic tumors of the parapharyngeal region and its role in guiding subsequent surgical management.

Discussion

Percutaneous sampling of lesions arising within the parapharyngeal space (PPS) remains technically demanding because of the deep anatomical location of the compartment and its close relationship with major neurovascular structures. In contrast to routine cervical fine-needle aspiration (FNA), biopsy of PPS lesions requires careful integration of cross-sectional imaging, ultrasound evaluation, and real-time procedural planning. Contrast-enhanced CT and MRI play a central role not only in lesion characterization but also in procedural feasibility assessment, allowing accurate identification of the compartment of origin, evaluation of vascular displacement patterns, and recognition of imaging features suggestive of salivary, neurogenic, or vascular lesions (3-5).

In many PPS lesions, imaging findings may already provide strong diagnostic orientation and significantly influence the decision of whether tissue sampling is truly necessary. Typical displacement patterns of the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein, together with enhancement characteristics and MRI signal features, may strongly suggest the underlying pathology, particularly in pleomorphic adenomas, schwannomas, and paragangliomas (6,13,14). In this setting, imaging should not be regarded solely as a preliminary diagnostic step but rather as an integral component of procedural planning and patient selection.

When tissue sampling is required, ultrasound-guided FNA offers several practical advantages. Real-time visualization of the needle allows continuous monitoring of needle progression, while Doppler imaging enables dynamic identification of vascular structures along the projected trajectory. In addition, ultrasound guidance avoids ionizing radiation and may often be performed in an outpatient setting using small-caliber needles with limited patient discomfort (10,12). These advantages are particularly relevant in the PPS, where vascular displacement may be highly variable and where real-time Doppler reassessment may significantly improve procedural safety.

However, the feasibility of ultrasound guidance is highly dependent on lesion depth, acoustic window availability, and cervical anatomy. Acoustic shadowing from the mandibular ramus, depth-related attenuation, and limited lateral access may impair lesion conspicuity and prevent safe needle advancement. In such cases, CT-guided biopsy represents an important alternative, allowing accurate targeting based on detailed cross-sectional anatomical mapping (11,20,21). CT guidance may be particularly useful for deeply located suprahyoid lesions or when ultrasound visualization is inadequate. Nevertheless, unlike ultrasound guidance, CT-guided procedures do not provide continuous real-time needle visualization during advancement and may require repeated acquisitions with associated radiation exposure. Furthermore, selected transoral CT-guided approaches may require general anesthesia and increased procedural resources (9,28).

Despite its diagnostic utility, FNA also presents important intrinsic limitations that should be recognized. Cytological samples obtained from deep PPS lesions may occasionally be nondiagnostic or insufficient because of limited cellularity or hemorrhagic contamination. In addition, FNA provides limited evaluation of tissue architecture and may therefore reduce diagnostic confidence in selected salivary gland and neurogenic tumors. False-negative or indeterminate cytological results may also occur, particularly in lesions with overlapping cytomorphological features or marked internal heterogeneity (7,8,24). For these reasons, cytological findings should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and imaging data rather than in isolation.

Core needle biopsy (CNB) may provide larger tissue samples and improved architectural assessment compared with FNA, potentially increasing diagnostic accuracy in selected head and neck lesions. However, in the PPS, the use of larger-caliber needles may theoretically increase the risk of hemorrhagic complications because of the close proximity of major vascular structures and the limited maneuverability within deep anatomical corridors (10,21). Consequently, while CT-guided CNB may be feasible in selected cases, particularly in experienced centers, FNA remains an attractive minimally invasive option when imaging findings and procedural anatomy are favorable. Since the present article specifically focuses on ultrasound-guided FNA, a detailed analysis of core biopsy techniques falls outside the primary scope of this review.

The present appraisal also highlights the importance of careful Doppler evaluation before needle insertion. As demonstrated in the case of internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysm, vascular lesions may clinically and radiologically mimic solid PPS masses. Recognition of intense internal Doppler flow fundamentally altered procedural decision-making in our case and prevented a potentially hazardous percutaneous puncture. This observation reinforces the concept that the most important procedural decision may sometimes be not how to perform the biopsy, but whether the lesion should be sampled at all (14,15).

The comparison between freehand and needle-guided techniques represents another relevant procedural consideration. Needle-guidance systems may improve needle visualization and procedural standardization, particularly during the initial phases of needle advancement. However, their fixed trajectory may reduce flexibility in anatomically complex regions such as the PPS, where continuous trajectory adjustment may be required to avoid displaced vascular structures. Importantly, currently available comparative evidence regarding freehand versus needle-guided techniques derives predominantly from breast, liver, phantom, or non-PPS interventional studies and may not be directly generalizable to PPS procedures (22,25,27). Consequently, the choice between freehand and needle-guided approaches should primarily depend on operator experience, lesion anatomy, and procedural feasibility rather than on rigid technical preference.

Taken together, the illustrative cases presented in this study emphasize that the role of the Interventional Radiologist extends beyond the technical execution of the FNA itself. Careful interpretation of imaging findings, appropriate patient selection, and meticulous procedural planning are fundamental components of safe clinical practice in this anatomically complex region. In many situations, the most critical decision is not how to perform the FNA, but whether percutaneous sampling is indicated at all.

Conclusions

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration represents a valuable diagnostic option for selected parapharyngeal space lesions when an adequate acoustic window and a safe percutaneous access corridor can be identified. The technique offers several advantages, including real-time needle visualization, Doppler assessment of vascular structures, and avoidance of ionizing radiation.

However, due to the deep location of the parapharyngeal space and the proximity of major neurovascular structures, careful procedural planning is essential. Integration of cross-sectional imaging with ultrasound evaluation plays a critical role in determining the feasibility and safety of percutaneous sampling.

Equally important is the recognition that not all parapharyngeal lesions require tissue sampling. In many cases, characteristic imaging findings may strongly suggest a specific diagnosis and help avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.

In this context, the interventional radiologist plays a key role not only in performing image-guided FNA safely but also in guiding the diagnostic pathway and contributing to multidisciplinary clinical decision-making.
