1.
Introduction

The semiconductor industry faces a critical sustainability challenge with AI-assisted design flow. Today, the industry is increasingly turning to AI for productivity gains. However,
LLM
-based design automation comes at a substantial cost: GPT-4 consumes
\$0.0012
–
\$0.0016
kWh per 1K tokens
27
, translating to millions in operational expenses for large projects. At
\$0.03
per
1K
output tokens, processing a medium-sized SoC through iterative refinement costs
\$15K
–
\$45K
for inference alone. The semiconductor industry already accounts for 2–3% of global electricity
30
; layering power-hungry
LLM
onto design workflows exacerbates this burden precisely when sustainability pressures mount. As models scale toward trillion-parameter regimes, these costs will only intensify. This paper explores whether local, efficient
SLM
viably replace or complement centralized
LLM
, thus effectively redistributing demand and reducing the industry’s energy and cost footprint. Thus, the question emerges:
do we always need Goliath-sized models, or can David win with the right strategy?

Organizational Analogies for AI-Assisted Design:

Figure 1
outlines how typically a fabless semiconductor chip design industry operates. Experienced engineers (
>5
years of experience) focus on architectural decisions, technology selection, and complex timing closure – tasks that require deep expertise. Junior engineers handle well-defined implementations: instantiating modules, connecting busses, implementing straightforward state machines, and debugging syntax errors. They succeed through clear specifications, relevant examples, immediate feedback, and quality oversight. Not every task demands senior expertise; a junior engineer perfectly implements a FIFO given clear specifications, even if unable to architect a cache coherence protocol. This organizational wisdom is lost in current automation based on
LLM
. Existing systems uniformly deploy expensive large models even for routine tasks manageable by smaller alternatives, analogous to assigning principal engineers to debug syntax errors. Just as organizations cannot staff entirely with principals, sustainable AI-assisted design cannot rely exclusively on frontier
LLM
.

Research Gap:
Despite the clear need for efficient alternatives, no previous work systematically evaluates
SLM
(<20B parameters) within sophisticated agentic frameworks for hardware design. Research falls into two camps: (1) Single-shot
SLM
evaluation showing 35–45% pass rates versus GPT-4’s 58%, concluding smaller models inadequate
20
, and (2) Sophisticated multi-agent systems demonstrating framework benefits but exclusively employing large commercial models
40
;
10
. This gap prevents understanding whether observed
SLM
performance ceilings reflect inherent limitations or simply inadequate scaffolding.

Core Hypothesis:
We hypothesize that hardware design tasks, when appropriately decomposed through agentic frameworks, create subtasks whose complexity falls within
SLM
capability thresholds. Just as junior engineers succeed with proper mentorship,
SLM
can achieve near-
LLM
performance when equipped with sophisticated task decomposition, iterative refinement, and targeted validation. Three mechanisms enable
SLM
success: (1) task decomposition breaking complex designs into subtasks, (2) structured guidance providing explicit instructions and examples, and (3) iterative refinement enabling convergence despite initial imperfections.

We derive two key observations from our evaluation. First, agentic task decomposition yields substantial gains for

The largest boost occurs in cid007, where models such as DeepSeek-R1 and Granite-4 more than double their single-shot success rates. Second, SLMs offer favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-offs in code-comprehension tasks, with models like phi-3.5-mini-instruct and DeepSeek-R1 achieving performance comparable to larger LLMs while requiring significantly fewer computational resources

Background and Related Work

2.1.
LLM-Based Hardware Design Automation

The application of
LLM
to hardware design has evolved through three generations. Early work focused on single-shot RTL generation from natural language. VerilogEval
20
demonstrated GPT-4 achieves 58.9% pass@1 on basic tasks but struggles with complex, multi-module designs requiring architectural planning. CodeV
41

33
and VeriGen
31

Domain-adapted models
39

19
demonstrated 72.3% pass@1 on internal benchmarks through continued pre-training on proprietary hardware data, while RTLCoder
21
achieved 82.3% on RTLLM benchmark through open-source fine-tuning.
17
;
22
and
28
are some of the works in LLM-aided verification domain. However, these models remain large with expensive training and deployment costs.

Current state-of-the-art employs multi-agent systems: Spec2RTL-Agent
40
reduces human intervention by 75% through iterative understanding, progressive coding, and adaptive reflection, but exclusively uses GPT-4 at substantial cost ($300+ per implementation). UVLLM
10
achieves 86.99% syntax error fix rate through UVM integration. VerilogCoder
9
, GPT4AIGChip
7
, Chipmind
6
, ChipAlign
5
and ChatCPU
34
demonstrate human-LLM collaboration benefits.
35

38
;
26
summarize latest works in
LLM
-aided design automation.

2.2.
Small Language Models and Agentic AI

Recent work
29
introduces the metric of
"intelligence per watt"
: task accuracy per unit of power consumption—as a unified measure of both capability and efficiency for local AI inference, demonstrating that SLMs on local accelerators can now handle the majority of real-world queries at a fraction of the energy and cost of frontier models.
SLM
(
\leq
20B parameters) offer compelling advantages: 8–10× lower energy consumption
14
;
32
, faster inference, reduced memory footprint, and lower operational costs. Recent models demonstrate impressive capabilities: Phi-3.5 (3.8B)
23
achieves competitive reasoning through quality training data; DeepSeek-R1 (7B)
4
incorporates reinforcement learning for code generation; Qwen-Coder
1
specializes in programming languages including Verilog; SmolLM2 (1.7B)
12
targets edge deployment. However,
SLM
face limitations: reduced reasoning capacity for multi-hop tasks, shorter context windows, and lower out-of-distribution performance
15
. Granite4
13
is IBM’s next-generation
SLM
featuring a hybrid Mamba/Transformer architecture that reduces memory and compute costs.

Agentic AI systems decompose complex tasks into manageable subtasks through iterative refinement and tool use
37
;
11
. Effective frameworks incorporate task decomposition, feedback incorporation, external tool integration, multi-agent collaboration, and rollback mechanisms
3
. AgentCoder
11
demonstrates 7–15% improvements in software through multi-agent approaches. AutoSafeCoder
24
integrates static analysis and fuzz testing. However, no work systematically evaluates whether agentic scaffolding can elevate
SLM
performance for hardware design.

2.3.
CVDP hardware design tasks

The Comprehensive Verilog Design Problems (CVDP) benchmark
25
, developed by NVIDIA, provides rigorous evaluation with 336 problems across arithmetic operations, control logic, memory systems, and miscellaneous designs. Each includes natural language specification, module interface, functional requirements, and CocoTB-based test suites. Derived from production IP blocks, it represents realistic complexity. State-of-the-art achieves only 26.5% pass rate (GPT-4o-mini, single-shot), highlighting substantial improvement opportunity. Sec.
5
provides detailed breakdown on the benchmark.

3.
Problem Formulation

We formalize our investigation into the effectiveness of
SLM
for hardware design when paired with sophisticated agentic-AI frameworks. Our central question is:

Can a strategically designed, SLM-aware agentic-AI framework enable resource-efficient models to achieve practical performance in hardware design automation?

To this end, we evaluate two core research questions through an agentic-AI based benchmarking study:

RQ1: Can agentic frameworks elevate SLM performance to practical levels?

Using our comprehensive evaluation pipeline on state-of-the-art CVDP benchmarks
25
, we examine whether advanced, SLM-aware scaffolding can approach or surpass the single-shot performance of larger
LLM
. We define “practical levels” as achieving functional correctness and performance sufficient for real-world deployment at attractive cost–efficiency tradeoffs.

2: For which specific tasks does agentic scaffolding enable SLMs to reach LLM-level performance?

We investigate which categories of hardware-design tasks allow an
SLM
equipped with sophisticated scaffolding to match or exceed
LLM
performance, and which categories remain challenging where
SLM
continue to underperform even with agentic support.

4.
SLM-aware agentic AI framework

This section presents a structured agentic framework designed to compensate for the inherent limitations of small language models (
SLM
) in hardware design automation. The framework provides the scaffolding that junior hardware engineers typically receive from senior mentors: context preparation, structured instructions, curated examples, iterative validation, and targeted feedback.
Figure
2
illustrates the system architecture, consisting of five cooperating agents that transform design intent from the CVDP dataset into verified
RTL
(
RTL
) implementations.

Figure 2.
Proposed SLM-aware agentic AI framework. (1) PPA retrieves and structures context from the dataset; (2) SPEA constructs SLM-aware prompts using keyword injection, in-context examples, and token budgeting; (3) CA generates candidate RTL implementations; (4) VA performs syntax checking, I/O port usage analysis, and functional testing; and (5) AFA categorizes errors, evaluates quality, and produces structured refinement prompts. These agents form a closed-loop iterative workflow.

4.1.
Planning and Pre-processing Agent (
PPA
)

The
Planning and Pre-processing Agent
is the pipeline entry point (leftmost block in Figure
2
) and prepares task-relevant context and metadata for effective
SLM
operation. It has three coordinated components:

•

Context Retriever
: Extracts relevant design descriptions, interface specifications, and auxiliary documentation from the CVDP dataset using keyword- and structure-based retrieval.

•

Planner
: Decomposes the design problem into sub-goals behavioral intent, interface semantics, and corner-case conditions—to provide structured guidance to downstream agents.

•

Database Generator
: Packages task descriptions, retrieved context, and planning metadata into a unified context bundle.

This agent grounds downstream prompts in complete, accurate context while keeping
SLM
lightweight.

4.2.
SLM-aware Prompt Engineering Agent (
SPEA
)

The
SLM-aware Prompt Engineering Agent
, represented in Figure
2
, constructs prompts that maximize
SLM
reliability through strategic structuring and explicit instructional cues. It addresses
SLM
challenges in instruction following, long-context reasoning, and token efficiency. The agent integrates three submodules:

•

SLM-Aware Keyword Injector
:
Injects deterministic structural keywords—

ROLE
(establishes expert identity),
TASK
(delineates objective),
REQUIREMENTS
(mandatory constraints),
CONSTRAINTS
(technical limitations), and
GENERATE
(requests code output). We introduced these optimized prompts motivated by
16
;
18
to optimize tokens consumed. These serve as attention anchors for
SLM
, enhancing hierarchical instruction parsing.

•

In-Context Learner
: Selects few-shot examples (e.g., counter, FIFO, FSM) matched by task similarity. Examples are incorporated into the prompt to serve as pattern templates analogous to senior engineer design references.

•

Token Budget Manager
: Allocates limited context capacity (typically
8
K–
32
K tokens) across task descriptions (
40\%
), context files (
40\%
), and examples (
20\%
). Large files are truncated using structure-preserving heuristics to retain essential information.

We carefully engineered this agent to generate a fully structured, SLM-optimized prompt that guides subsequent code generation with minimal ambiguity and fairly deterministic output.

4.3.
CodeGen Agent (
CA
)

The
CodeGen Agent
performs reasoning-guided Verilog generation using the structured prompt produced by the SLM-aware prompt engineering agent. Its responsibilities include: 1) Executing deterministic
SLM
-based code generation. 2)
Suppressing verbose natural-language reasoning to maximize available output tokens for code generation. 3) Extracting candidate Verilog modules using pattern-matched parsing. and 4) Preparing the candidate implementation for multi-stage validation. This agent embodies the core generative function of the framework while relying on external scaffolding to steer
SLM
behavior.

4.4.
Validation Agent (
VA
)

The
Validation Agent
enforces correctness through a three-stage pipeline, emulating incremental checks typically performed by senior engineers during code review:

•

Syntax Validation
: Detects parse errors, type mismatches, undeclared identifiers, and structural inconsistencies using an RTL code linter.

•

I/O Port Usage Checker
: Identifies “zombie ports”—declared but unused I/Os—by (1) extracting all port declarations via regex (ANSI, non-ANSI, SystemVerilog, Verilog-2001), (2) scanning the module body for input/output usage, and (3) generating targeted feedback for unused or unassigned ports.

•

CocoTB Test Runner
: Executes functional tests across nominal and corner-case scenarios. Success terminates the pipeline; failure triggers corrective feedback via Adaptive Feedback Agent.

4.5.
Adaptive Feedback Agent (
AFA
)

The
Adaptive Feedback Agent
generates structured refinement signals and enables error-aware iteration. Its components include:

•

Error Categorizer
: Maps simulator and validator messages to one of seven categories:
syntax, undeclared, type, width, latch, timing, or novel. This structured categorization ensures targeted refinement instructions for
SLM
.

•

Quality Scorer
: Produces a composite score combining functional correctness (
60\%
), compilation quality (
20\%
), port completeness (
10\%
), and code structure (
10\%
). Lower-scoring updates trigger rollback to the highest-scoring version.

•

Contextual Error Gatherer
: Synthesizes concise error summaries (e.g., line-level syntax issues, missing assignments) and integrates them into refinement prompts, including “damage repair” history to prevent repeated failed attempts.

This agent enables targeted, context-dependent correction, analogous to senior engineer feedback during iterative design refinement.

4.6.
Putting It All Together

Figure
2
shows the full agentic AI workflow as a closed-loop pipeline. The
PPA
agent retrieves and structures task context from the dataset and generates a plan. The
SPEA
agent builds an SLM-aware prompt with keyword injection, curated examples, and context constraints. The
CA
agent produces initial Verilog implementations. The
VA
agent evaluates candidates for syntax, port usage, and functional correctness. Syntax or port errors trigger structured error prompts; functional test failures invoke the
AFA
agent to generate category-specific corrective prompts.

This pipeline iterates prompt engineering, code generation, and validation for up to five rounds, guided by scoring, rollback, and error-awareness, until all checks pass, the iteration limit is reached, or early termination criteria are met. This coordinated loop enables
SLM
to produce high-quality, functionally correct
RTL
despite limited internal reasoning capabilities.

5.
Experimental Evaluation

5.1.
Experimental Setup

We now describe the benchmark suite, task selection criteria, and model configurations used in our evaluation.

5.1.1.
Benchmark Suite

Our experiments are conducted on the
Comprehensive Verilog Design Problems (CVDP)
benchmark suite
25
, a recently released and systematically curated dataset targeting RTL design, debugging, and verification tasks. CVDP organizes problems into three high-level categories:

•

Non-Agentic Code Generation:
Includes RTL module creation, logic completion, micro-architectural construction, and code repair. Subtasks cover combinational/sequential design, FSM generation, structural code fixing, signal-width correction, latch removal, and general debugging.

•

Non-Agentic Code Comprehension:
Focuses on extracting functional intent from HDL artifacts. Representative subtasks include RTL/Testbench correspondence, identifying behavioral mismatches, natural-language question answering about design intent, and summarization of module functionality.

•

Agentic Code Generation:
Involves multi-step tasks requiring planning, iterative correction, and tool-driven refinement such as synthesis-guided debugging and simulation-based repair.

CVDP is specifically designed to probe reasoning, syntax discipline, hardware-awareness, and tool-aligned correctness. As reported in
25
, state-of-the-art LLMs achieve only
34% pass@1
on Non-Agentic Code Generation and perform significantly worse on several Code Comprehension subtasks, highlighting the inherent difficulty of the benchmark.

For a controlled and meaningful evaluation of our agentic framework, we limit our experiments to the
Non-Agentic Code Comprehension
and
Non-Agentic Code Generation
categories (
Table 1
). We intentionally exclude CVDP’s Agentic Code Generation tasks because even frontier LLMs were shown to underperform on these tasks in single-shot settings, making it difficult to isolate the gains attributable to our agentic scaffolding. For Code Comprehension tasks, we evaluate both
LLM
and
SLM
strictly in single-shot mode. For Code Generation tasks, we report: 1) Single-shot (SS) correctness (
pass@1
with
n=5
completions), and 2) Agentic (AG) performance using our full refinement loop (iteration cap = 5).

5.1.2.
Models Evaluated

We benchmark four resource-efficient
SLM
widely used for code generation tasks: SmolLM2 (1.7B), Nemotron-Mini (4B), Granite-4 (3B), and DeepSeek-R1 (7B). For code comprehension, we additionally evaluated with GPT-OSS (20B) and Phi3.5-mini-construct. For comparison against a frontier model, we include GPT-o4-mini as the
LLM
baseline. All models use temperature
0.7
for generation-oriented tasks to preserve architectural diversity and temperature
0.3
for interface-heavy outputs to prioritize determinism and structural precision. A global timeout of
300
seconds is enforced per test case. To measure the relative importance of framework components, we evaluate two configurations:

(1) single-shot without scaffolding
,

(2) full agentic framework with adaptive refinement
.
Experiments were executed on an AMD EPYC 7763 (2.45 GHz) server with 512 GB RAM and NVIDIA L40S GPUs.

Conclusion

In this work, we present the first systematic study of SLM-centric agentic AI systems applied to state-of-the-art hardware design tasks from the CVDP benchmark. Framing hardware design workflows through the lens of organizational hierarchy, we show that certain well-defined subtasks—similar to work handled by beginners—can be effectively performed by compact, energy-efficient SLMs.

Our experiments demonstrate that, with appropriate scaffolding via agentic frameworks—including task decomposition, structured guidance, and iterative refinement—SLMs can achieve near-LLM performance on both code generation and comprehension tasks. These findings confirm that “strategy over scale” is a viable approach for AI-assisted hardware design: properly architected agentic frameworks allow smaller models to succeed on tasks traditionally reserved for large models. Beyond immediate performance gains, this opens the door to sustainable, task-specific SLMs that maintain high accuracy and reduce the AI energy footprint.
