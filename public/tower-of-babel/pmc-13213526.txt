High-Dose vs Standard-Dose Influenza Vaccines in Older Adults
Authors: Kristoffer Grundtvig Skaarup, Mats C. H. Lassen, Kaveh Hosseini, Niklas Dyrby Johansen, Matthew M. Loiacono, Rebecca C. Harris, Sandrine I. Samson, Arto A. Palmu, Kevin McConeghy, Stefan Gravenstein, Orly Vardeny, Brian Claggett, Scott D. Solomon, Federico Martinón-Torres, Tor Biering-Sørensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAmong adults 65 years and older, does high-dose inactivated influenza vaccine (HD-IIV) provide superior protection against hospitalization and mortality compared with the standard dose?FindingsIn a meta-analysis of 8 randomized trials including 605 098 participants, HD-IIV was associated with significantly reduced hospitalizations for influenza, laboratory-confirmed influenza, pneumonia or influenza, cardiorespiratory disease, and all causes compared with the standard-dose vaccine; however, HD-IIV was not associated with reduced all-cause mortality. Relative vaccine effectiveness estimates were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses.MeaningThese findings suggest that HD-IIV offers clinically relevant improvements in prevention of hospitalization outcomes compared with standard-dose in older adults, which may inform decision-makers in developing vaccine recommendations and policies.

Introduction

High-dose inactivated influenza vaccine (HD-IIV), containing 4 times the antigen of standard-dose inactivated influenza vaccine (SD-IIV), that is, 60 µg hemagglutinin antigen per strain instead of 15, was developed to enhance protection in older adults. This population exhibits a diminished immune response to SD-IIV1 and is at increased risk of severe influenza-related complications.2,3 HD-IIV has previously been demonstrated to induce higher antibody responses and superior protection against laboratory-confirmed influenza (LCI) with a relative vaccine efficacy of 24.2% (95% CI, 9.7%-36.5%) compared with SD-IIV in individuals 65 years or older.4 However, whether this increased immunogenicity compared with SD-IIV and reduction in infections translates into reductions in severe clinical outcomes, such as influenza-related hospitalizations and mortality, had until recently not been demonstrated in an adequately powered individually randomized trial. Prior randomized trials of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV have evaluated these outcomes in post hoc analyses,5 in selected high-risk populations including individuals with cardiovascular disease,6 and in clusters of nursing homes,7 with mixed findings. The recently completed FLUNITY-HD study, a prespecified pooled analysis of the methodologically harmonized,8 individually randomized DANFLU-2 and GALFLU trials,9,10 demonstrated superior protection of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV in older adults against hospitalizations for pneumonia or influenza, cardiorespiratory disease, LCI, and all causes. These studies were powered to evaluate the relative vaccine effectiveness (rVE) of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV in preventing severe clinical events in broadly representative populations of older adults. In a meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials including 105 685 participants, HD-IIV was associated with reductions in hospitalizations for pneumonia or influenza and for all causes compared with SD-IIV.11 However, that analysis preceded the publication of the DANFLU-2,9 GALFLU,10 and Finnish Influenza High‐Dose (FinFluHD) trials,12 which together contributed nearly 500 000 additional randomized participants, substantially expanding the available evidence base. The objective of this updated meta-analysis was to summarize the findings from randomized clinical trials comparing HD-IIV with SD-IIV for prevention of hospitalization events and mortality among older adults.

Methods

This meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251040175). Results are reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline.

Data Sources and Searches

We systematically searched PubMed and Embase without language restriction. The prespecified search strategy was (influenza or flu) and (vaccine or vaccines or vaccination or immunization) and (high-dose or high dose or high dose influenza or Fluzone high dose or Flu-zone high-dose or high dose quadrivalent or high-dose quadrivalent or high dose trivalent or high-dose trivalent or HD or HD-IIV or HD-TIV or HD-QIV or HD-IIV3 or HD-IIV4) and (clinical trial or randomized or randomised). All search fields were used. Two investigators (K.G.S. and M.C.H.L.) independently searched and screened titles, abstracts, and full texts against the eligibility criteria.

Study Selection

Randomized clinical trials comparing HD-IIV with SD-IIV and reporting severe clinical outcomes (hospitalization or mortality) in adults during at least 1 influenza season were eligible. Trials were required to be published between December 31, 2009 (following the initial licensure of HD-IIV), and September 15, 2025. Trials were eligible regardless of whether they were powered for the outcomes of interest and could include adults of all ages. Trials conducted during influenza pandemics were excluded because both seasonal vaccines would be mismatched to the circulating pandemic strain, such that any observed effects would not reflect differences between HD-IIV and SD-IIV.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Two investigators (K.G.S. and M.C.H.L.) independently extracted study data, including event counts and sample sizes, using a standardized form. Disagreements were resolved by a third investigator (N.D.J.). When end point data were not publicly available, corresponding authors or study sponsors were contacted, and additional data were requested. Intention-to-treat datasets were preferred over as-treated datasets if both were available, and a 14-day postvaccination blanking period was preferred when available. The same process was used to obtain summarized data for subgroup and sensitivity analyses. A detailed explanation of extracted data is provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1. For studies spanning multiple influenza seasons, summarized data that combined seasons were selected to ensure independence of each observed effect size.

The Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool was used to assess risk of bias in each of the included randomized trials. The tool covers 5 domains: (1) randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) outcome measurement, and (5) selection of the reported result. For each trial, all reported outcomes were considered for the fifth domain, and the overall assessment was determined by the outcome with the highest risk of bias.

The outcomes investigated in this meta-analysis were (1) hospitalization for influenza (defined by each trial; eg, based on diagnosis codes, other clinician-documented diagnosis of influenza, or laboratory confirmation), (2) hospitalization for LCI, (3) hospitalization for pneumonia or influenza (a broader composite), (4) hospitalization for cardiorespiratory disease, (5) all-cause hospitalization, and (6) all-cause mortality as defined in each individual trial protocol. Influenza hospitalization was included to capture a broader clinical influenza outcome across trials because LCI hospitalization may not be consistently ascertained or reported. The pneumonia or influenza hospitalization outcome was included to capture influenza-associated pneumonia, including cases coded as pneumonia that may have been triggered by influenza but not recorded as influenza.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analysis population included adults 65 years or older from trials comparing HD-IIV with SD-IIV. Secondary analyses were conducted for all adults (aged ≥18 years), adults aged 65 to 79 years, adults 80 years or older, adults with cardiovascular disease (heart failure and/or coronary heart disease), and adults without cardiovascular disease.

Sensitivity analyses in the primary population excluded trials restricted to high-risk disease groups, excluded data collected during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic (defined as data collected after the first reported COVID-19 case in the respective trial country during the first pandemic wave in winter and spring 2020) to reduce potential distortion of outcomes during the initial COVID-19 surge, and excluded trials with fewer than 100 outcome events. Two additional sensitivity analyses repeated the analysis conducted in the primary analysis set using random-effects models instead of the prespecified fixed-effects model.

Study weights and combined effect sizes were estimated using fixed-effect models with the inverse variance method. Fixed-effect models were preferred because they place greater weight on larger, more precise trials and provide more conservative estimates in the presence of small-study effects.13 Combined rVE estimates were reported with 95% CIs. Random effects sensitivity analyses in the primary population were conducted using both the DerSimonian and Laird14 and Hartung-Knapp-Sidik Jonkman15 methods. Potential small-study effects were assessed through funnel plot inspection and the Egger test for the primary population. Heterogeneity was evaluated with the Cochran Q test, and an I2 value was reported to describe between-study variability. Numbers needed to vaccinate (NNV) with HD-IIV instead of SD-IIV were calculated as the inverse of the absolute risk difference based on summed event counts divided by summed participants in each study arm across included trials.

All statistical analyses were performed with Stata SE, version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC). Two-sided P < .05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

The systematic literature search yielded 640 unique records. After screening title and abstracts, 9 full-text articles remained. One trial was excluded during full-text screening as it had been conducted during the 2009 to 2010 H1N1 pandemic16; therefore, 8 trials met eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Figure 1).5,6,7,9,10,12,17,18 Unpublished aggregate-level data were obtained from all eligible studies. All data extracted were reported according to the intention-to-treat principle.

The Table summarizes the characteristics of the included studies. All trials were conducted in Western Europe or North America and comprised 6 individually randomized clinical trials and 2 cluster randomized clinical trials. Five trials5,9,10,12,18 enrolled older adults from the general population (aged ≥65 years), 2 trials7,17 enrolled nursing home residents (aged ≥65 years), and 1 trial6 was conducted among individuals with heart failure or history of myocardial infarction (no age restriction; mean [SD] age, 65.5 [12.6] years). Some concerns in domain 2 reflect the open-label design of several trials; however, all outcomes were hospitalization or death, which are unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of vaccine assignment. Some concerns in domain 3 arose when outcomes were available only for a subset of the randomized sample rather than the full randomized sample (Gravenstein et al7,17), which may reduce precision but is unlikely to introduce systematic differences between study groups. Diagram of Risk of Bias Assessment Among Included Randomized Clinical Trials

Risk of bias for each included trial was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. The tool evaluated 5 domains: (1) randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome, and (5) selection of the reported result. Each domain includes as many as 7 signaling questions. No domain for any trial was identified as having a high risk of bias.

Combined rVE

Influenza Hospitalizations

In the primary population of adults 65 years or older, HD-IIV was associated with a significantly reduced risk of influenza hospitalization compared with SD-IIV. The combined rVE was 38.5% (95% CI, 26.5%-48.5%) (Figure 3A), corresponding to an NNV with HD-IIV instead of SD-IIV of 2429 to avert 1 influenza hospitalization (eTable in Supplement 1). Effect estimates were consistent across subgroups by age and cardiovascular disease status and in sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of trials restricted to high-risk groups, exclusion of data from the early COVID-19 pandemic, exclusion of trials with fewer than 100 outcome events, and when using a random effects model (eFigures 1-10 in Supplement 1). Funnel plot inspection and Egger test did not suggest small-study effects (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

LCI Hospitalizations

HD-IIV was associated with a significantly reduced risk of LCI hospitalization compared with SD-IIV, with a combined rVE of 31.2% (95% CI, 19.3%-41.4%) (Figure 3B), and an NNV of 2075 (eTable in Supplement 1). Results were consistent across most subgroups and sensitivity analyses, although analyses restricted to individuals with cardiovascular disease suggested possible mildly attenuated effectiveness (eFigures 1-10 in Supplement 1). No indication of small-study effects was observed (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

Pneumonia or Influenza Hospitalizations

HD-IIV was associated with a lower risk of pneumonia or influenza hospitalization compared with SD-IIV. The combined rVE was 11.5% (95% CI, 5.9%-16.8%) (Figure 3C), with an NNV of 1216 (eTable in Supplement 1). Results were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses (eFigures 1-10 in Supplement 1), with no evidence of small-study effects (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

Cardiorespiratory Hospitalization

HD-IIV was associated with a significantly reduced risk of cardiorespiratory hospitalizations in adults 65 years or older with a combined rVE of 7.5% (95% CI, 4.7%-10.3%) (Figure 4A) and an NNV of 479 (eTable in Supplement 1). Effectiveness was consistent across all subgroup and sensitivity analyses (eFigures 1-10 in Supplement 1), and some small-study effects were detected (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

All-Cause Hospitalization

HD-IIV was associated with reduced risk of all-cause hospitalization compared with SD-IIV, with a combined rVE of 3.3% (95% CI, 1.8%-4.8%) (Figure 4B) and an NNV of 335 (eTable in Supplement 1). The effects were consistent in subgroup and sensitivity analyses (eFigures 1-10 in Supplement 1). Some small-study effects were observed (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

All-Cause Death

There was no significant difference in all-cause mortality between the HD-IIV and SD-IIV groups. The combined rVE was 0.9% (95% CI, −2.1% to 3.8%) (Figure 4C). Findings were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses (eFigures 1-9 in Supplement 1), with some evidence of small-study effects (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This meta-analysis of 8 randomized clinical trials involving more than 600 000 participants found that HD-IIV was associated with reduced risk of hospitalization for various causes, from the most specific of LCI hospitalizations to all causes, compared with SD-IIV. Effect sizes were numerically largest for LCI and influenza hospitalizations and more moderate for pneumonia or influenza, cardiorespiratory disease, and all-cause hospitalizations. No significant reduction in all-cause mortality was observed. These effects were overall consistent by age and cardiovascular disease status.

To our knowledge, this updated meta-analysis provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of evidence on the rVE of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV, incorporating data from the recently completed DANFLU-2 and GALFLU randomized clinical trials and previously unpublished outcome and subgroup data from all 8 prior trials. The inclusion of these data increased statistical power, strengthened subgroup analyses, and ensured completeness. This work builds beyond the FLUNITY-HD prespecified pooled individual participant analysis of DANFLU-2 and GALFLU,8 which demonstrated superior protection of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV against hospitalizations across 466 320 participants. The findings of the present meta-analysis may inform decision-makers and guideline committees in developing vaccine recommendations and policies.

Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations with effectiveness across all outcomes in the overall population, in those aged 65 to 79 years, and in participants without cardiovascular disease. These 3 groups had the largest sample sizes and highest event rates, supporting the robustness of the combined estimates. In individuals 80 years or older, effect estimates were consistent with the overall findings, but analyses of influenza and LCI hospitalizations were limited by the small number of events in most trials; only DANFLU-2, by virtue of its large size, contributed substantial data. Despite this limitation, effect estimates for influenza and LCI hospitalizations were comparable to those in individuals aged 65 to 79 years and those of all ages, with confidence intervals clearly above the null. For cardiorespiratory and all-cause hospitalizations in this age group, point estimates were also comparable and indicative of greater protection, but with wider confidence intervals reflecting less precision. Nevertheless, these findings illustrate that the enhanced antibody response and superior efficacy of HD-IIV relative to SD-IIV translates into a significantly reduced risk of influenza hospitalization even among the oldest adults.4,19

Following the neutral results of the Influenza Vaccine to Effectively Stop Cardio Thoracic Events and Decompensated Heart Failure (INVESTED) trial for its primary outcome of cardiopulmonary hospitalization or death,6 the authors concluded that HD-IIV offered minimal protection compared with SD-IIV in individuals with cardiovascular disease. In the present analysis, in the subgroup with cardiovascular disease at enrollment, combined rVEs showed significant protection against both influenza and LCI hospitalizations, with effect estimates comparable, albeit slightly lower, than in the overall population. For pneumonia or influenza and cardiorespiratory hospitalizations, combined estimates likewise suggested improved protection, although INVESTED diverged from the combined findings. The neutral findings of INVESTED likely reflect its enrollment of recently hospitalized patients with myocardial infarction and heart failure, in whom influenza may contribute only modestly to overall hospitalization risk and outcomes may be driven by a severe clinical trajectory, unalterable by influenza vaccination.6 In contrast, the remaining trials included samples with heart failure or coronary heart disease from the general population or from nursing homes, with greater breadth of baseline risk, in whom influenza may represent a proportionally greater hazard. Overall, these findings suggest that HD-IIV offers improved protection compared with SD-IIV in the general population of patients with cardiovascular disease. Additional analyses from the DANFLU-2 trial have also reported consistent rVE of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV in participants with and without diabetes.20

In the randomized clinical trials we reviewed, when compared with SD-IIV, HD-IIV was associated with improved protection against multiple hospitalization outcomes in adults 65 years or older, whereas no significant difference was observed for all-cause mortality. Evidence for greater rVE of HD-IIV was most precise in individuals aged 65 to 79 years and in participants without cardiovascular disease, driven by the large sample size in these populations. In individuals 80 years and older and those with cardiovascular disease, effect estimates were less precise but remained comparable and indicative of greater protection of HD-IIV compared with SD-IIV. The relative effect estimates should always be interpreted alongside the corresponding estimated NNV when assessing the clinical value of HD-IIV compared with SD-IIV in vaccination practice. Overall, these findings demonstrate the clinical benefits of HD-IIV in lowering hospitalization risk, underscoring its value in reducing influenza burden among older adults and the corresponding impact across health systems.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. We observed small-study effects for pneumonia or influenza hospitalization, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause death. However, tests for small-study effects are unreliable when few trials are available, as statistical power is limited and findings may be disproportionately influenced by single small outlying studies due to random variability.21 Combined rVE estimates for influenza and LCI hospitalizations were largely driven by the DANFLU-2 and GALFLU trials, encompassing populations from Denmark and Galicia, Spain, respectively. While this may impact generalizability, as large-scale pragmatic trials with inclusion of a broad, general population conversely may improve generalizability as opposed to often strict inclusion or exclusion criteria of traditional smaller-scale randomized trials. Medicare reporting rules prohibited the presentation of outcome cells with fewer than 10 events in the nursing home trials,7,17 which limited inclusion of the US-based nursing home trials in some subgroup analyses for influenza and pneumonia or influenza outcomes. However, as these trials contributed only few events for these outcomes, combined estimates are unlikely to be affected. Finally, because influenza risk and vaccine performance vary across seasons and populations, combined effect estimates represent average population-level estimates and may not directly reflect individual-level protection. However, the literature indicates clear improved protection of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV, with relative effects in individuals likely similar to the combined estimate.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials of HD-IIV vs SD-IIV in older adults, HD-IIV was associated with reduced risk of hospitalization for influenza, LCI, pneumonia or influenza, cardiorespiratory disease, and all causes, whereas no significant reduction in all-cause mortality was observed. These findings may inform decision-makers in developing vaccine recommendations and policies.
