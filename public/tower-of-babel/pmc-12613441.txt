Rapid prognostic tools in disaster medicine: C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) and bicarbonate-to-lactate ratio (BILAR) in crush syndrome
Authors: Nazmiye Serap Biçer, Mehmet Biçer, Davut Eren, Hilal Sipahioğlu, Belgin Zeybek Oral, Banu Açmaz, Sümeyra Koyuncu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCrush syndrome is a life-threatening complication frequently observed following earthquakes and other natural disasters, often leading to multi-organ failure and high mortality. Rapid and reliable prognostic tools are critical for early risk stratification and management of affected patients, particularly in resource-limited disaster settings. In this context, inflammatory and perfusion-based indices such as the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) and the bicarbonate-to-lactate ratio (BILAR) have recently gained attention as potential predictors of adverse outcomes. The aim of this retrospective study was to evaluate the prognostic value of CAR and BILAR in patients with crush syndrome and to determine their role in predicting mortality and major clinical outcomes.Materials and methodsThis study included patients over 18 years of age who were diagnosed with crush syndrome following the February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were analyzed. The CAR was calculated using the formula CRP (mg/L)/Albumin (g/L), while the BILAR was derived as the ratio of HCO₃ (mmol/L) to lactate (mmol/L). The primary endpoint was defined as mortality, and the secondary endpoints included amputation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, stage 3 acute kidney injury (AKI), and the need for renal replacement therapy. The prognostic impact of CAR and BILAR indices on mortality was assessed using statistical analyses.ResultsA total of 231 patients (mean age 36.63 ± 12.35 years, 51.1% female) were included, of whom 18 (7.8%) died. Mortality was significantly associated with elevated levels of BUN, creatinine, potassium, phosphorus, AST, ALT, creatine kinase, lactate, and the CAR index, as well as decreased albumin, calcium, HCO₃⁻, hemoglobin, and BILAR index values (p < 0.05). ROC analysis revealed that CAR showed moderate-to-good discriminative ability for mortality (AUC = 0.64), stage 3 AKI (AUC = 0.73), ICU admission (AUC = 0.75), amputation (AUC = 0.65), and the need for hemodialysis (AUC = 0.72). According to the Youden index, the optimal CAR cut-off for predicting mortality was 2.37 (sensitivity = 83%, specificity = 44%). Low BILAR values were inversely associated with all endpoints, with BILAR < 5 showing a particularly strong correlation with mortality (p < 0.001). Importantly, 71 patients (30.7%) required renal replacement therapy (RRT), with a significantly higher mortality rate compared to non-RRT patients (19.7% vs. 2.5%, p < 0.001). In multivariate logistic regression analysis including CAR, BILAR, age, gender, comorbidity, AKI, and RRT, low BILAR values, advanced age, and receipt of RRT were independently associated with increased mortality, whereas CAR, AKI, sex, and comorbidity did not retain independent significance.ConclusionsCAR and BILAR are rapid and easily calculated prognostic indices in crush syndrome. In this study, low BILAR values, advanced age, and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) were independently associated with increased mortality. These findings suggest that perfusion-based markers such as BILAR may be more reliable than inflammatory indices like CAR for early mortality risk prediction. Incorporating BILAR into clinical assessment could support timely risk stratification and treatment planning in disaster-related mass-casualty settings.Keywords: Crush syndrome, Mortality, Earthquake, Prognostic index

Introduction

On February 6, 2023, two major earthquakes struck Türkiye, resulting in the collapse of 38,901 buildings and causing 53,537 fatalities. This disaster led to hundreds of thousands of injuries, with a substantial proportion of survivors developing crush syndrome (CS) and associated complications. Following trauma-related deaths, the leading causes of mortality were acute kidney injury (AKI), AKI-related complications, and crush syndrome itself [1].

Predicting which patients with post-earthquake CS will develop AKI or experience fatal outcomes remains clinically challenging. Several factors have been investigated as mortality predictors in such mass-casualty scenarios. Dysnatremia, duration of entrapment, elevated APACHE II scores, direct ICU admission, hemodynamic support requirements, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, elevated creatine kinase (CK), CRP, and abnormal BUN levels have been identified in previous studies as strong predictors of mortality [2–5].

Early prognostic indices with high sensitivity and specificity, along with robust risk scoring systems, can play a crucial role in triage and treatment planning. However, the literature on such tools in earthquake-related crush syndrome remains limited [6].

In this study, we evaluated the clinical value of alternative biomarkers that may aid in predicting mortality among individuals affected by this disaster. Specifically, we investigated the prognostic performance of the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) and the bicarbonate-to-lactate ratio (BILAR). CAR is an inflammatory index with documented prognostic value in malignancies, sepsis, and acute pancreatitis. Elevated inflammatory burden reflected by a high CAR has been associated with adverse outcomes and increased mortality [7, 8].

BILAR is defined as the ratio of serum bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) to lactate obtained from arterial or venous blood gas analysis. It serves as a marker of tissue perfusion, particularly in critical illnesses such as shock and sepsis. A BILAR value < 5 has been linked to hypoperfusion, shock, multi-organ failure, and increased mortality, whereas values >10 are considered indicative of compensated metabolic status and adequate oxygenation [9].

The demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters of patients admitted to our hospital were analyzed, and groups with and without acute kidney injury (AKI) were compared. In addition, among patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission and those with fatal outcomes, these indices were evaluated alongside other established mortality predictors to develop a novel and rapid risk stratification approach.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective observational study included patients diagnosed with crush syndrome who were treated at Kayseri City Training and Research Hospital following the February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. Crush syndrome was diagnosed based on a combination of clinical features and laboratory abnormalities consistent with muscle necrosis and renal involvement, including serum creatine kinase (CK) > 1000 U/L, oliguria (urine output < 400 mL/day), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) > 40 mg/dL, serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL, serum potassium > 6 mmol/L, serum phosphorus > 8 mg/dL, and serum calcium < 8 mg/dL. Specifically, AKI was defined according to KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) criteria, with stage 3 AKI defined as a serum creatinine ≥ 3.0 times baseline or ≥ 4.0 mg/dL, or initiation of renal replacement therapy.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, confirmed diagnosis of crush syndrome, and availability of complete laboratory data at admission. Exclusion criteria included: incomplete laboratory data, transfer to another hospital before outcome assessment, loss to follow-up, and patients younger than 18 years.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities), clinical outcomes (ICU admission, amputation, stage 3 acute kidney injury [AKI], renal replacement therapy [RRT], and mortality), and laboratory findings (CRP, albumin, HCO₃⁻, lactate, electrolytes, complete blood count, and liver and kidney function tests) were retrieved from hospital electronic records. The C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) was calculated using the formula CRP (mg/L) divided by albumin (g/L). The bicarbonate-to-lactate ratio (BILAR) was defined as HCO₃ (mmol/L) divided by lactate (mmol/L). The primary endpoint was defined as in-hospital mortality during the index hospitalization. Patients were followed until discharge or death.Secondary endpoints included amputation, ICU admission, stage 3 AKI, and the requirement for renal replacement therapy (hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy).

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kayseri City Training and Research Hospital (Approval No. 448, Date: May 13, 2025) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants involved in the study.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation, median (min–max), or frequency and percentage, as appropriate. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were made using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate) for categorical variables.

The prognostic performance of CAR and BILAR for primary and secondary outcomes was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and area under the curve (AUC) values. Optimal cut-off points were determined with the Youden index.

To adjust for confounders, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed including age, sex, comorbidities, AKI, RRT, CAR, and BILAR. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 248 patients were initially evaluated. After excluding 17 patients with incomplete data, the final analysis included 231 patients. Of the 231 patients included in the study, 118 (51.1%) were female and 113 (48.9%) were male. The mean age was 36.63 ± 12.35 years (range: 18–65). The most common comorbidities were hypertension (n = 22) and diabetes mellitus (n = 11). Sixteen patients (6.9%) had a single comorbidity, 41 (17.7%) had two or more, and 174 (75.3%) had no chronic conditions. During follow-up, 18 patients (7.8%) died while 213 (92.2%) survived. No significant association was found between mortality and either sex or the presence of chronic diseases (Table 1).

At admission, the most frequent injuries were extremity fractures and compartment syndrome. Ninety-six patients required intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and 70 underwent fasciotomy. Acute kidney injury (AKI) developed in 69 patients (Table 2). Mortality was significantly higher in patients with abdominal trauma, amputation, mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, inotropic support, AKI, and RRT. Among RRT patients, 14 died while 57 survived (Table 2). Analysis of continuous variables revealed that non-survivors had significantly higher median values of BUN, creatinine, potassium, phosphorus, AST, ALT, creatine kinase, lactate, CAR, and age, while albumin, calcium, HCO₃⁻, hemoglobin, and the BILAR index were significantly lower. The prognostic performance of CAR and BILAR for primary and secondary outcomes was evaluated using ROC analysis. For mortality, the area under the curve (AUC) for CAR was 0.64, with an optimal cut-off of 2.37 (sensitivity = 83%, specificity = 44%). For BILAR, lower values were strongly associated with mortality, with BILAR < 5 showing a highly significant correlation (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). CAR also demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for stage 3 AKI (AUC = 0.73), ICU admission (AUC = 0.75), amputation (AUC = 0.65), and the need for hemodialysis (AUC = 0.72) (Table 4). Overall, CAR showed moderate prognostic performance across outcomes, while low BILAR values consistently emerged as a marker of poor prognosis. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis including CAR, BILAR, age, sex, comorbidity, AKI, and RRT, low BILAR values, advanced age, and the use of RRT were independently associated with increased mortality. Specifically, BILAR remained a significant inverse independent predictor of mortality (p = 0.017), while age (p = 0.033) and RRT (p = 0.016) showed a positive association. In contrast, CAR, AKI, sex, and comorbidity were not independently associated with mortality. These results are presented in Table 5. These prognostic factors and suggested clinical interventions for patients with low BILAR values are summarized in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes resulted in thousands of individuals being trapped under rubble, leading to severe systemic complications associated with crush syndrome (CS). This syndrome, driven by mechanisms such as rhabdomyolysis, hypovolemia, and reperfusion injury, significantly increases early mortality risk and represents a clinical scenario requiring urgent intervention [10]. Recently, a nationwide cohort analysis from the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes was reported in Kidney International (2024), identifying older age, delayed rescue, hyperkalemia, and acute kidney injury as key predictors of mortality after crush injury. In our cohort, similar clinical associations were observed, particularly advanced age, renal replacement therapy, and severe metabolic derangements. Unlike the Kidney International report, our study additionally assessed two rapid biochemical ratios—CAR and BILAR—as novel prognostic tools for early risk stratification in disaster settings [11]. In our study, factors significantly associated with mortality included abdominal trauma, amputation, mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, inotropic support, acute kidney injury (AKI), and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT). Notably, the mortality rate was substantially higher among patients receiving RRT (19.7%) compared to those who did not (2.5%). Although RRT is typically initiated for patients with severe AKI, in our cohort, some patients were dialyzed primarily due to critical metabolic indications such as life-threatening hyperkalemia and profound lactic acidosis, independent of formal AKI criteria. This may explain why the number of RRT cases exceeded those classified as stage 3 AKI. Delivering RRT in disaster conditions, however, presents substantial logistical challenges, as hospitals may themselves be damaged or overwhelmed. In such circumstances, mobilization of dialysis resources, referral to unaffected regional centers, and prioritization of patients with life-threatening metabolic derangements such as hyperkalemia or severe acidosis are essential strategies to ensure feasibility of this intervention.

In crush syndrome, alongside traditional biomarkers such as creatine kinase, myoglobin, and potassium, the identification of rapid and accessible biochemical indices capable of predicting mortality is of critical importance for optimizing clinical decision-making. Early identification of high-risk patients is essential for timely interventions, including fluid management and renal replacement therapy.

In recent years, the use of simple, readily available biochemical ratios with a clear pathophysiological basis has been investigated as a means of predicting mortality. In this context, our study evaluated the prognostic value of the bicarbonate-to-lactate ratio (BILAR) and the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) in patients with crush syndrome.

The C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), derived from the relative levels of CRP and albumin, reflects both the severity of systemic inflammation and the nutritional status of the patient. Owing to these properties, CAR has been widely utilized as a prognostic marker of mortality in numerous critical illnesses, particularly in sepsis [12].

CAR serves as a bidirectional indicator of systemic inflammation: elevated CRP represents the positive acute-phase response and heightened proinflammatory activity, which can also contribute to vascular imbalance, vasoconstriction, and a prothrombotic state. Conversely, decreased albumin levels reflect capillary leak syndrome, malnutrition, or a catabolic response, and may exacerbate renal dysfunction through oxidative stress-induced injury. Given the inverse relationship between albumin and inflammation, this combination results in an elevated CAR in severe disease states.

Accordingly, CAR has been proposed as a potential prognostic biomarker in trauma, sepsis, cardiovascular events, and oncologic surgery [13–15]. Several studies have reported associations between elevated CAR values and increased mortality, with area under the curve (AUC) values frequently exceeding 0.70 [16–18]. In our cohort, CAR was significantly higher among non-survivors in univariate analysis, although it did not retain independent predictive value after adjustment for confounders. Nevertheless, CAR may still provide complementary information on systemic inflammation and nutritional status in the context of crush syndrome. The BILAR index provides a comprehensive assessment of tissue perfusion and metabolic balance by simultaneously reflecting the degree of metabolic acidosis through low bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) levels and the extent of hypoperfusion via elevated lactate concentrations. In crush syndrome, systemic perfusion impairment driven by muscle necrosis, hypovolemia, and inflammatory mediators frequently leads to lactate accumulation, which is associated with adverse outcomes.

In the literature, particularly in cases of sepsis and trauma, low BILAR values have been shown to correlate significantly with increased mortality [19]. For instance, conditions with bicarbonate levels ≤ 18 mmol/L and lactate ≥ 4 mmol/L have been reported to markedly increase the risk of death [20]. When assessed as a ratio, the bicarbonate-to-lactate balance has been proposed as a dynamic parameter integrating systemic hypoperfusion and metabolic reserve, with reduced values consistently linked to higher mortality across critical illness cohorts.

In a study of 60 patients with acute mesenteric ischemia, an HCO₃⁻/lactate ratio ≤ 10 was a strong predictor of 30-day mortality, alongside lactate, bicarbonate, and neutrophil count [16]. Similarly, a study including 492 pediatric patients with dengue shock syndrome (DSS) in southern Vietnam evaluated the clinical utility of the lactate-to-bicarbonate (L/B) ratio, demonstrating that the L/B index provided higher prognostic value compared to lactate alone (odds ratio [OR] = 8.66, 95% CI 1.96–38.3; P < 0.01 vs. OR = 1.35, 95% CI 1.15–1.58; P < 0.001). Both the L/B and lactate models showed comparable performance [21].

In line with these findings, our study observed significantly increased mortality in patients with low BILAR values, with the index demonstrating strong discriminative ability in ROC analysis. These results, together with existing evidence, emphasize the prognostic utility of inflammatory indices such as CAR and BILAR and highlight the value of biomarker-based risk stratification in critical care settings. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, a low BILAR value remained independently associated with mortality, even after adjusting for confounders such as age, gender, comorbidities, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Although CAR showed statistical significance in univariate analysis, it did not retain independent predictive value in the adjusted model. These findings suggest that tissue perfusion markers such as BILAR may be more reliable than inflammatory markers like CAR in early mortality risk prediction following crush syndrome. Our study has several limitations. First, CAR and BILAR indices were calculated based solely on laboratory values obtained at admission; therefore, their dynamic changes over time were not assessed. Second, data regarding patients’ pre-disaster nutritional status, comorbidity burden, and pre-existing organ function were insufficient, which may have introduced potential bias. Additionally, factors that could influence mortality, such as sheltering conditions, transfer times, and delays in search and rescue operations, were not available in the dataset.

Moreover, although mortality data were recorded, detailed clinical information on causes of death could not be obtained. This reflects the practical challenges of data collection in disaster settings. Furthermore, our hospital was not a primary receiving center during the initial phase of the disaster. A considerable number of patients were referred after initial triage and treatment at other facilities. This may have introduced a selection bias and contributed to the relatively low observed mortality rate in our cohort.

Finally, as this study was conducted using single-center data, the generalizability of the findings to other institutions and to non-disaster-related traumatic scenarios is limited. Consequently, multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to validate these results and to more robustly establish the prognostic role of CAR and BILAR indices in predicting mortality. Due to the retrospective nature of our study and the challenges of data collection under disaster conditions, scoring systems such as APACHE II and SOFA, as well as myoglobin levels, were not consistently available. Future prospective studies should aim to include these indicators to strengthen external validity.

Conclusion

CAR and BILAR are rapid and inexpensive biochemical ratios that may assist early prognostic evaluation in crush syndrome. In this study, both elevated CAR and decreased BILAR values were associated with mortality; however, after multivariate adjustment, only low BILAR, advanced age, and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) remained independent predictors.

While CAR reflects systemic inflammation and nutritional status, its prognostic contribution appears to be secondary to the more direct impact of perfusion and metabolic parameters captured by BILAR. These complementary indices may together enhance risk assessment and guide early management in disaster-related crush syndrome. Clinically, a low BILAR value at admission may serve as an early warning signal, guiding fluid resuscitation, correction of acidosis and hyperkalemia, and timely initiation of RRT, whereas elevated CAR values may indicate an enhanced inflammatory response that could further complicate recovery. Integrating both indices into disaster triage algorithms could help identify high-risk patients earlier and improve outcomes in mass-casualty, resource-limited settings.
