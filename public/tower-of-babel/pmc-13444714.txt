When Joint Aspiration Fails: Point‐of‐Care Ultrasound for Diagnosing Chronic Gout in Prolonged Monoarthritis

Case Presentation

A 34‐year‐old man presented to our primary care clinic with a 3‐month history of pain and swelling in the right first metatarsophalangeal joint. Three months earlier, he had visited another clinic where hyperuricemia (serum uric acid, 8.7 mg/dL) was noted and gout was suspected. He was prescribed febuxostat, but was instructed not to take it during episodes of active inflammation. Consequently, he relied solely on loxoprofen for symptomatic relief, with little improvement in persistent joint swelling.

At the initial evaluation, it was uncertain whether the clinical course represented recurrent gout flares or a different pathology underlying prolonged monoarthritis. The differential diagnoses included septic arthritis, including indolent infection caused by organisms such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis , calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (although the course was not typical of acute pseudogout), tumorous lesions, and reactive arthritis (although no clear antecedent infection was identified). Given the potential for irreversible joint destruction if septic arthritis is missed, excluding infection was prioritized.

Ultrasound‐guided joint aspiration was attempted to obtain synovial fluid for crystal identification and microbiological analysis. However, the procedure yielded a dry tap, with only trace amounts of thick, chalky material that could not be analyzed. Faced with this diagnostic impasse, point‐of‐care ultrasound of the joint was performed. This examination revealed extensive findings consistent with chronic tophaceous gout. Longitudinal imaging demonstrated diffuse hyperechoic material filling the joint cavity, representing a large intra‐articular tophus (Figure 1A). Over the dorsal aspect of the metatarsal head, a thick hyperechoic band was visualized along the cartilage surface, and interpreted as a double contour sign, although the anechoic cartilage layer beneath it was not clearly delineated and was likely obscured by the massive crystal burden (Figure 1B). Color Doppler imaging showed no synovial hypervascularity, effectively helping to rule out acute septic arthritis. Based on these ultrasound findings and the clinical context, the patient was diagnosed with chronic tophaceous gout.

(A) Longitudinal view showing diffuse hyperechoic material (asterisk) filling the joint cavity, consistent with a large intra‐articular tophus (arrows). (B) Longitudinal view demonstrating a thick hyperechoic band overlying the dorsal aspect of the metatarsal head (arrowheads), interpreted as a double contour sign with possible tophaceous aggregate. The anechoic cartilage layer is not clearly visible, likely obscured by extensive crystal deposition. Color Doppler imaging (not shown) revealed no synovial hypervascularity, helping rule out acute septic arthritis.

The patient was treated with colchicine 1 mg/day with a short course of oral corticosteroids. Erythema and pain had resolved by the 4‐week follow‐up visit, at which point febuxostat was initiated. Follow‐up ultrasound revealed that the tophus remained unchanged. His symptoms improved dramatically within 1 week following presentation and did not recur during early follow‐up.

Discussion

Prolonged monoarthritis lasting several months presents a diagnostic challenge in primary care as the differential diagnosis extends beyond crystal arthropathy to include septic arthritis, particularly indolent infections such as tuberculosis, tumorous lesions, and reactive arthritis. In the present case, the patient had been labeled as having “suspected gout” at another clinic for 3 months, yet his symptoms persisted without resolution. This course illustrates the risk of diagnostic anchoring bias and the need to periodically reassess working diagnoses when the clinical course deviates from expectations.

Synovial fluid analysis with identification of monosodium urate crystals remains the gold standard for diagnosing gout; however, joint aspiration is not always feasible. In the present case, a dry tap occurred because of the highly viscous, chalky nature of the tophaceous material. Point‐of‐care ultrasound proved particularly valuable in this setting, demonstrating clear features of chronic gout, including a double contour sign and an intra‐articular tophus, which provided sufficient diagnostic confidence to initiate treatment. Ultrasound evidence of urate deposition, particularly the double contour sign, is included in the 2015 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism gout classification criteria [1]. Recent EULAR recommendations also recognize ultrasound findings such as the double contour sign, tophi, and aggregates as key imaging features in crystal‐induced arthropathies [2, 3].

The observed hyperechoic band could represent either a true double contour sign or a tophaceous aggregate overlying the cartilage surface. Dynamic ultrasound assessment using probe maneuvers may help to distinguish a true double contour sign from calcium pyrophosphate deposition or overlying crystal aggregates and may improve diagnostic precision in difficult cases [4]. In addition, identifying intra‐articular tophi alongside the double contour sign may improve the diagnostic performance of ultrasound for gout [5].

Crystal‐induced arthritis and septic arthritis can coexist, and a convincing diagnosis of gout does not completely exclude the possibility of joint infection [6, 7]. Thus, even when ultrasonography strongly supports gout and color Doppler imaging shows no hypervascularity, bacterial infection should not be completely ruled out. In the present case, careful clinical follow‐up was essential for diagnostic safety. After colchicine was started, the patient was monitored closely, and erythema and pain had resolved by the 4‐week follow‐up visit, without any findings suggestive of persistent infection. This sustained improvement, together with the absence of any clinical features indicating infection, supported our interpretation that septic arthritis was unlikely in the present case.

Another important lesson from this case is that delayed initiation of urate‐lowering therapy (ULT) may have contributed to the prolonged disease course. At the previous clinic, febuxostat was withheld because of ongoing inflammation, possibly prolonging active disease and persistent crystal burden. The 2020 American College of Rheumatology guideline conditionally recommends initiating ULT during an acute gout flare in cases where ULT is indicated, rather than delaying initiation until flare resolution [8]. In our patient, erythema and pain resolved following colchicine treatment, and febuxostat was started at the 4‐week follow‐up visit; however, the tophus remained unchanged on follow‐up ultrasound. The earlier initiation of ULT may have shortened the duration of disease activity and could have helped to prevent persistent crystal deposition.

Conclusion

Overall, this case highlights how integration of point‐of‐care ultrasound into primary care practice can improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce reliance on invasive procedures in patients with prolonged inflammatory monoarthritis. Simultaneously, clinicians should recognize the learning curve required for the confident interpretation of musculoskeletal ultrasound findings and the importance of clinical correlation. In resource‐limited or time‐constrained settings, point‐of‐care ultrasound offers a pragmatic, noninvasive alternative when synovial fluid analysis is unsuccessful or contraindicated.
