Association of the uric acid-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio with vitamin D deficiency in postmenopausal women: evidence from NHANES 2005–2016
Authors: Xueping Huang, Xiukui He, Jianhong Xia, Limei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between vitamin D deficiency and the uric acid-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) in postmenopausal women remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between these variables and to evaluate the potential interaction between UHR and waist circumference (WC).MethodsData from 5,155 postmenopausal women who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between 2005 and 2016 were included in this cross­sectional investigation. Weighted regression analysis served as the primary methodology employed to examine the association between UHR and vitamin D deficiency. Furthermore, interaction analysis, sensitivity analysis, and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were also conducted.ResultsAccording to multivariate adjusted analysis, vitamin D deficiency was positively correlated with UHR (OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.02–1.07). The risk of vitamin D deficiency in the highest quartile population of UHR was significantly higher than that in the lowest quartile population (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.36–2.27). Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was negatively correlated with UHR (β= -0.74, 95% CI: -1- -0.47). Vitamin D deficiency has a linear dose-response relationship with UHR. In addition, the study also discovered the interaction between UHR and WC.ConclusionsThe UHR level in postmenopausal women is positively associated with vitamin D deficiency. Managing WC may contribute to reducing the risk of vitamin D deficiency in individuals with elevated UHR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02783-4.

Introduction

Vitamin D, an essential micronutrient for human health, plays a critical role in maintaining bone integrity and regulating calcium and phosphorus homeostasis [1]. Recent research has demonstrated that vitamin D exerts multiple physiological functions, including modulation of immune responses, suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators, improvement of vascular endothelial function, reduction of insulin resistance, and protection of reproductive health [2–6]. Vitamin D deficiency is typically characterized by a serum level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) that falls below 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) [7]. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 47.9% of the global population is deficient in vitamin D, with the prevalence in the United States being 32.1% [8, 9]. Postmenopausal women are more prone to vitamin D deficiency [10], which increases the prevalence of osteoporosis [11]. Furthermore, these women demonstrate a greater susceptibility to metabolic disorders such as insulin resistance, central obesity, and diabetes; the absence of adequate vitamin D exacerbates this condition [10, 12, 13]. Cognitive decline and mood disturbances are two additional symptoms commonly associated with both menopause and vitamin D insufficiency [14, 15]. Consequently, the effective supplementation of vitamin D holds significant importance for enhancing the quality of life in postmenopausal women.

The uric acid-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) has attracted increasing attention as a potential biomarker. This innovative metric effectively integrates two important markers: high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and uric acid (UA) [16]. This comprehensive indicator effectively reflects systemic oxidative stress and inflammatory response, and serves as a sensitive marker for dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risks [17]. In addition to its strong association with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [18], elevated UHR levels are also linked to insulin resistance (IR), abdominal obesity [19], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [20], and female infertility [21].

Waist circumference (WC) serves as the primary clinical indicator of central obesity, which is characterized by pathological accumulation of visceral fat. In postmenopausal women, the accumulation of abdominal fat may disrupt metabolic homeostasis of vitamin D through an oxidative-inflammatory response [22, 23]. Given that this mechanism aligns closely with the pathological process associated with elevated UHR, we hypothesize that elevated UHR and central obesity may interact synergistically collectively contribute to vitamin D deficiency. In addition to investigating the relationship between UHR and vitamin D deficiency, this study also aims to analyze the potential interaction between UHR and WC.

Materials & methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a nationwide research project of the American population that adheres to strict standards and collects data through questionnaires, interviews, laboratory tests, and physical examinations, providing an important basis for health and epidemiological research. All participants have given their informed permission, and the study procedure has been approved by ethics.

This study is based on the data of a total of 60,936 participants in the NHANES cycle from 2005 to 2016. The screening steps for the research subjects are as follows: Firstly, male participants (n = 30,152) and non-menopausal or non-naturally menopausal women (n = 24,307) were excluded; Secondly, individuals without UA (n = 390), without HDL-C (n = 2), and without vitamin D (n = 138) data were excluded; Ultimately, those without covariate data (n = 636) and those without measured WC (n = 156) were further excluded. In the end, the analysis included 5,155 eligible postmenopausal women (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The hierarchical screening process diagram of the research population

The hierarchical screening process diagram of the research population

Menopause judgment

The verified reproductive health questionnaire was used to determine the postmenopausal status: (1) No menstrual period in the past 12 months; (2) The reason for the absence of menstruation is natural menopause. Individuals who concurrently fulfill both of the aforementioned criteria are classified as postmenopausal.

Measurement of serum 25(OH)D

Prior to 2007, radioimmunoassay (RIA) was employed to measure serum concentrations of 25(OH)D. However, this method is associated with accuracy deviations that represent a significant limitation. Since 2007, the methodology has been completely upgraded to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), which offers enhanced sensitivity and specificity [9, 24]. The criterion employed in this research is 25 (OH) D levels below 50 nmol/L, which aligns with the definition of vitamin D deficiency as outlined in the clinical practice guidelines established by the Endocrine Society [7].

The measurement of UHR and WC

According to previous research, the levels of HDL-C and UA were assessed using various analyzers for each cycle of the NHANES. UHR (%) is calculated as follows: (UA [mg/dL] ÷ HDL-C [mg/dL]) × 100 [17]. Health technicians with training took measurements of WC. A WC ≥ 88 cm was identified in this investigation as a primary marker of central obesity [25].

Covariates

This study considered potential confounding factors. Researchers collected demographic data (age, race, education level, marital status, and poverty income ratio (PIR)), smoking status (never/former/now), and alcohol (≥ 12 cups/year) through interviews. The mobile physical examination facility evaluated the body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Obtain the history of chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (CVD)), and menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) from the health questionnaire. Laboratory data include serum calcium (mg/dL), phosphorus (mg/dL), and creatinine (mg/dL). Hypertension is defined as being diagnosed by physicians ≥ 2 times; diabetes is diagnosed by doctors or the use of insulin; CVD includes self-reported congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, or angina pectoris. It is important to note that the “NHANES survey cycle” was incorporated as a covariate in the model for adjustment in this study, aimed at correcting for deviations introduced by changes in the vitamin D measurement technique.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was weighted in accordance with the NHANES guidelines. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) along with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables report their frequency and percentage. The chi-square test, one-way analysis of variance, and Kruskal-Wallis H test were selected for the assessment of differences between groups.

To explore the association between UHR and vitamin D deficiency as well as serum 25(OH)D, a weighted regression model was constructed, and the odds ratio (OR), its 95% confidence interval (CI), regression coefficient (β), and its 95% CI were calculated. Furthermore, UHR was divided into quartiles into groups Q1-Q4 to investigate the potential dose-response relationship. The model construction adopts a stepwise adjustment strategy: Model 1 does not include any covariates; Model 2 adjusts for demographic characteristics and BMI; and Model 3 further incorporates smoking, alcohol, MHT, history of chronic diseases, blood calcium, blood phosphorus, creatinine, and the NHANES survey cycle on the basis of Model 2. Meanwhile, the relationships among UA, HDL-C, and vitamin D deficiency were analyzed individually. A three-node restricted cubic spline (RCS) was employed to further investigate the potential nonlinear relationship between UHR and vitamin D deficiency, as well as serum 25(OH)D levels.

The interaction terms between UHR (binary classification, bounded by the median) and WC (binary classification, < 88 cm or ≥ 88 cm) were constructed. Further analysis was done on the relationship between UHR and vitamin D insufficiency under various WC conditions. To further confirm the stability of the study findings, sensitivity analysis—which included multiple imputation and subgroup analysis—was carried out. R (v4.2.2; R Foundation) and the Free Statistics platform (v1.9.2), which was created in China, were used for data processing and modeling.

Results

Baseline attributes

A total of 5,155 postmenopausal women were included in this study; the average age of the subjects reached 63.9 ± 10.6 years old. The average concentration of serum vitamin D was 68.9 ± 30.5 nmol/L, among which 29.4% of the individuals had vitamin D deficiency. Data analysis shows that compared with participants in the lowest quartile, those in the highest quartile have higher BMI and creatinine levels and also have higher prevalence rates of vitamin D deficiency, hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Discussion

A total of 5,155 postmenopausal women participated in this study. The findings indicated a significant correlation between vitamin D deficiency and lower blood levels of 25(OH)D with elevated UHR. This consistent positive relationship between vitamin D deficiency and UHR was further substantiated through sensitivity analysis. It is noteworthy that WC status may serve as a moderating factor in the association between UHR and the risk of vitamin D deficiency.

A study utilizing Mendelian randomization indicates that serum UA exerts a negative causal effect on 25(OH)D. However, the reciprocal relationship between these two variables is not substantiated [26]. Elevated UA levels can enhance the production of reactive oxygen species, directly down-regulate the expression of proteins and genes associated with 1-α hydroxylase in renal tubular cells, and inhibit the synthesis of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [27, 28]. Meanwhile, urate crystals can stimulate the release of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-1β. This process subsequently leads to a reduction in the expression of vitamin D receptors and interferes with their signal transduction pathways [29, 30]. Several additional results from multiple regression analyses have demonstrated a significant positive correlation between vitamin D deficiency and hyperuricemia [31, 32]. In this study, no significant association was identified between UA and vitamin D deficiency. One potential explanation for this finding is that when UA operates in isolation, its pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative effects may be mitigated or obscured by other intricate metabolic factors present in postmenopausal women.

In numerous metabolic disorders, low levels of HDL-C and vitamin D insufficiency frequently coexist and may exert reciprocal effects on one another. HDL-C can inhibit the expression of endothelial cell adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory factors, exerting anti-inflammatory effects [33]. Low levels of HDL-C are indicative of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which can impair the function of vitamin D-activating enzymes and disrupt their metabolism [34, 35]. Vitamin D also regulates lipid metabolism and affects the expression of lipid balance genes through nuclear receptors [36]. Its deficiency can impair HDL metabolism as well as the transcription and function of apolipoproteins, such as ApoA-I, thereby establishing a detrimental cycle [37]. A meta-analysis involving 7,952 individuals further substantiated that this association was more pronounced in overweight and obese populations [38]. Therefore, low levels of HDL-C and vitamin D may share a common pathophysiological basis rather than exhibiting a direct causal relationship.

A cross-sectional study indicated that in healthy young adults aged 22 to 35, there is a positive correlation between UHR and vitamin D deficiency [39]. The results of our study have shown for the first time that this association persists throughout the particular physiological and metabolic phase of postmenopause. As a composite indicator, UHR can reveal the essence of systemic metabolic imbalance that cannot be reflected by a single component. There exists a metabolic disorder characterized by an inflammatory-oxidative stress state within the body, which is associated with elevated levels of the UHR marker [16, 40]. This environment may collaboratively impede the activity of vitamin D-activating enzymes and disrupt their downstream signal transduction pathways, consequently elevating the risk of vitamin D deficiency [39]. Among postmenopausal women, aging and decreased estrogen are associated with an increased incidence of hyperuricemia and dyslipidemia [41, 42], and the pathophysiological imbalance represented by UHR may be more significant. It is recommended that clinical intervention measures include increasing physical exercise and optimizing dietary structure (such as limiting purine intake and increasing dietary fiber and monounsaturated fatty acids).

The findings of this investigation suggest that visceral obesity (WC ≥ 88 cm) and UHR exhibit an interactive relationship. First of all, excessive belly fat can lead to chronic inflammation, which releases inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6, promoting the production of UA [43, 44]. Second, IR, which may be induced by non-esterified fatty acids and resistin released by adipose tissue, is intimately linked to the development of hyperuricemia [45]. Meanwhile, the above-mentioned inflammatory factors can also inhibit the synthesis of apolipoprotein A-I in the liver. Coupled with the fact that IR can accelerate the clearance of HDL, they jointly lead to a decrease in the circulating HDL-C level [46, 47]. These pathological changes acted in synergy, increasing the level of UHR. Menopause is closely linked to the accumulation of visceral fat, dyslipidemia, and the onset of IR, which may further aggravate the previously mentioned metabolic imbalances [42, 48]. Therefore, the link between vitamin D deficiency and UHR as well as visceral obesity is complicated.

Strengths and limitations

The NHANES database, a large sample with national representativeness, serves as the cornerstone for this investigation. By constructing a weighted regression model, potential confounding variables such as demographic characteristics, BMI, history of chronic diseases, and lifestyle were corrected, and subgroup analysis was supplemented to confirm the robustness of the results. This research does, however, possess certain limitations. The association observed in this study cannot be definitively interpreted as a causal or co-causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency and UHR, owing to the constraints inherent in the cross-sectional study design. Future studies are required to elucidate this. It is still hard to totally rule out the influence of other probable variables, such as dietary vitamin D intake, the duration of sun exposure, and the use of certain medications, on the findings, even when other confounding factors have been addressed. The assessment of UHR levels and vitamin D status relies on a single measurement and may be affected by short-term fluctuations and seasonal variations. Finally, the research population focused on postmenopausal women, so the conclusion may be difficult to directly generalize to other groups.

Conclusions

The UHR level in postmenopausal women is positively correlated with vitamin D deficiency. Managing WC may contribute to reducing the risk of vitamin D deficiency in individuals with elevated UHR. However, this conclusion requires validation through future prospective studies or randomized controlled trials.
