Selective degradation of platelet BTK by PROTAC NX-5948 provides antithrombotic benefits without affecting hemostasis
Authors: Carl J. May, Justin S. Trory, Connor E. Webb, Hetty S. Walker, Yong Li, Jonathan A. Furniss, Johannes A. Eble, Alastair W. Poole, Jordan Vautrinot, Jay Tromans, Ingeborg Hers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•NX-5948 selectively removes platelet BTK and prevents thrombosis without affecting bleeding.•BTK degradation could provide safer clot prevention for patients who cannot tolerate standard antiplatelet drugs.

Introduction

The abnormal formation of thrombi can lead to severe and potentially life-threatening consequences. By obstructing blood vessels and impairing circulation, thrombi can precipitate conditions such as myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, ischemic stroke, and deep vein thrombosis.1 Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and a P2Y12 antagonist effectively reduces recurrent thrombotic events but also increases bleeding risk, limiting clinical use and highlighting the need for antithrombotic therapies that prevent pathological thrombosis without impairing hemostasis.

The collagen receptor glycoprotein VI (GPVI) and its downstream effector Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) have been proposed as promising antithrombotic targets because they are critical contributors to thrombosis but are largely redundant for hemostasis. Their role is supported by impaired thrombus formation on collagen and protection from arterial thrombosis in GPVI-deficient models and humans with minimal bleeding.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 BTK inhibitors impair atherosclerotic plaque–induced thrombus formation in humans9, 10, 11 and reduce venous thrombosis in mice.12 However, their therapeutic window remains narrow because of off-target bleeding.13,14 Interestingly, patients with X-linked agammaglobulinemia who lack functional BTK do not exhibit an increased risk of bleeding, which suggests that the selective inhibition of BTK is unlikely to affect haemostasis.15,16

To address the limitations of BTK inhibitors, such as their narrow therapeutic windows and off-target effects, BTK degraders present a promising new avenue for antithrombotic treatments, with the added benefit of eliminating both the kinase and scaffolding functions of BTK.17 Over the last few years, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have shown great promise as targeted protein degraders. These bifunctional molecules recruit an E3 ligase to ubiquitinate and degrade the target via the ubiquitin-proteasome system.18 We previously demonstrated that human platelets are highly susceptible to PROTAC-mediated BTK degradation using the BTK degraders DD-03-171 and DD-04-15, with tandem mass tag (TMT)–based proteomic analysis confirming superb specificity.19

Because BTK also plays a central role in B-cell receptor signaling and sustained activation of BTK is a defining feature of many B-cell malignancies, significant industrial effort has been directed toward developing BTK PROTACs for the treatment of B-cell lymphomas. Several of these, including NX-2127 and NX-5948, are currently undergoing early-phase clinical trials. In addition to degrading BTK, NX-2127 also acts as a molecular glue, resulting in the degradation of the transcription factors Ikaros (IKZF1) and Aiolos (IKZF3),20 which enhances the antitumor properties of T cells.21 NX-5948 is a related compound developed for similar indications but selectively targets BTK without affecting the transcription factors.22

At present, the prospective application of these PROTACs as antithrombotic agents by targeting platelets remains to be explored. Given their potential to preserve hemostasis, we investigated the effects of NX-2127 and NX-5948 on platelets. Specifically, we sought to characterize the ability of these clinical-stage BTK degraders to eliminate BTK from human and mouse platelets to demonstrate their mechanistic basis and to assess the functional consequences on platelet activation, aggregation, thrombus formation, and hemostasis.

Methods

Human platelet isolation

Blood from healthy, drug-free volunteers was collected into 4% (weight-to-volume ratio) trisodium citrate after National Health Service Research Ethics Committee approval (20/SC/0222) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Platelets were isolated as described previously. Briefly, blood was acidified with acid citrate dextrose at a 1:7 volume-to-volume ratio and centrifuged at 150 × g for 17 minutes at room temperature (RT), and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was collected. Platelets were pelleted at 800 × g for 10 minutes in the presence of 2μM prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) and 0.02 U/mL apyrase, washed in citrate-glucose-saline buffer (13mM trisodium citrate, 22mM glucose, 120mM sodium chloride [NaCl]) containing apyrase, and resuspended in HEPES (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid) Tyrode’s buffer (145mM NaCl, 3mM potassium chloride, 0.5mM disodium hydrogen phosphate, 1mM magnesium sulfate, 10mM HEPES; pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.1% (∼5.5mM) d-glucose and apyrase to yield 4 × 108 platelets per mL.

Incubation with PROTACs

Platelets in whole blood, PRP, or washed suspensions were incubated at 30°C with NX-2127 (HY-153220, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, or NX-5948 (8856, Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, United Kingdom) at the indicated concentrations and durations, then isolated as described earlier for human or mouse platelets.

Western blotting

Washed platelets were lysed in 4× NuPAGE Sample Buffer containing 0.1% (4% weight-to-volume ratio) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). Lysates were analyzed by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and immunoblotting using primary antibodies against talin (TA205, MA528133, Thermo Fisher Scientific Invitrogen Waltham, MA), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (6C5, SC-32233 Santa Cruz Dallas, TX), BTK (D3H5, 8547, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), and tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC) (A12533, Antibodies.com, Cambridge, United Kingdom), as described previously.19

In vitro thrombus formation assay

PRP was incubated for 4 hours with vehicle (dimethyl sulfoxide), 3μM NX-2127, or 100nM NX-5948, then labeled with 2μM DiOC6 (3,3ʹ-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide) for 30 minutes. PRP was recombined with erythrocytes and perfused through Vena8 GCS biochips coated with 50 μg/mL fibrillar collagen at 3.07 mL/h (shear rate, 1000 s˗1) for 5 minutes. Thrombus formation was imaged by confocal microscopy (2 fields per channel). Images were analyzed using a custom ImageJ plugin; data were processed in Microsoft Excel, and statistics were performed in GraphPad Prism.

Platelet spreading assay

Washed platelets (5 × 106/mL) were incubated for 75 minutes at 30°C on coverslips coated with fibrinogen (20 μg/mL) and collagen (10 μg/mL). Platelets were fixed with 8% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained with ActinGreen 488 ReadyProbes (Invitrogen). Samples were mounted in ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant and imaged using an EVOS-FL microscope (40× objective). Five fields per coverslip were captured per condition. Platelets were categorized into 3 spreading stages (stage 1, resting; stage 2, filopodia; stage 3, lamellipodia and actin-rich membrane) using a macro developed by Stephen Cross (University of Bristol). Data were analyzed in Excel and plotted in GraphPad Prism.

Mice

Male and female C57BL/6 mice (9-10 weeks old; Charles River, Wilmington, MA) were used under UK Home Office project license PP5643338. All procedures were approved by the University of Bristol Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body and conducted in accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986. Mice were randomized to receive NX-5948 (30 mg/kg, oral gavage) or vehicle control (n = 3-5 per group). Each animal constituted an independent experimental unit. Sample size was informed by previous thrombosis studies providing 80% power to detect ≥30% differences. Imaging and analyses were performed blinded to treatment. Mice were euthanized by rising CO2 inhalation 24 hours after treatment, and blood was collected by cardiac puncture into 4% trisodium citrate at a 1:9 volume-to-volume ratio.

Mouse platelet isolation

Blood was collected by cardiac puncture using a 23-gauge needle preloaded with acid citrate dextrose. Samples were diluted with HEPES-Tyrode’s buffer containing glucose (HT+G) and 10mM EGTA to 3 mL. PRP was obtained by centrifugation at 200 × g for 5 minutes (RT). The red cell layer was resuspended in HT+G plus EGTA, then centrifuged again, and the pooled PRP was collected. Combined PRP was centrifuged with 0.5μM PGE1 (and 0.2 U/mL apyrase if required) at 800 × g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was washed once in HT+G with PGE1, recentrifuged, and resuspended in a small volume of HT+G. Platelet concentration was measured and adjusted before use.

Mouse B-cell isolation

Spleens were harvested from mice and placed immediately into cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Single-cell suspensions were generated by mechanical dissociation through a 70 μm cell strainer (Falcon, Corning Inc, Corning, NY) using the plunger end of a syringe. The strainer was rinsed with cold PBS to maximize cell recovery. Cell suspensions were centrifuged (300-400 × g, 5 minutes, 4°C), and red blood cells were lysed using ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer for 1 to 3 minutes at RT. Lysis was quenched with excess PBS, followed by centrifugation and resuspension in sorting buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum and 2mM EDTA).

B cells were isolated from splenocyte suspensions using a MojoSort Mouse B Cell Isolation Kit (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This negative selection strategy yields untouched B cells by depletion of non–B-cell populations using magnetic separation. Briefly, cells were incubated with a cocktail of biotinylated antibodies targeting non–B-cell lineages, followed by magnetic streptavidin nanobeads. Labeled cells were retained using a magnetic separator, and the unlabeled fraction containing B cells was collected.

Isolated B cells were washed and lysed ready for western blotting.

Mouse megakaryocyte isolation

Megakaryocytes were enriched from mouse bone marrow by size-based filtration. Femurs and tibias were dissected, and bone marrow was flushed using a 25-gauge needle with CATCH buffer. Cells from each mouse were processed separately. After centrifugation (300 × g, 5 minutes, RT), pellets were subjected to red blood cell lysis (ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer, 10 minutes, RT), washed, and resuspended in complete CATCH buffer supplemented with bovine serum albumin and fetal calf serum.

Cell suspensions were passed through a 70-μm strainer to remove debris, followed by sequential filtration through 20-μm and 15-μm nylon meshes. For each step, flow-through fractions were collected, filters were washed, and retained large cells were recovered by reverse flushing. Fractions enriched from sequential filtration steps were combined and centrifuged (300 × g, 10 minutes), resuspended in a reduced volume, and pelleted before downstream applications.

Enriched megakaryocytes were washed and lysed, ready for western blotting.

In vivo arterial thrombosis and tail-bleeding assays

Mice were anaesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg; Vetalar V, Pfizer New York, NY) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Rompun, Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany). Platelets were labeled IV with DyLight-488-anti-GPIbβ (100 μg/kg; ×488, Emfret, Emfret Analytics GmbH & Co KG, Eibelstadt, Germany) 10 minutes before application of 12% ferric chloride to the carotid adventitia for 3 minutes. Fluorescence images were acquired for 20 minutes and analyzed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) as integrated thrombus fluorescence after background subtraction. For tail-bleeding assays, bleeding time was assessed using a warm-saline immersion method to minimize vasoconstriction and provide standardized physiological conditions. Mice (C57BL/6) were anaesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg; Vetalar V, Pfizer) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Rompun, Bayer) and maintained on a heating pad to prevent hypothermia. A full-thickness transverse incision was made 5 mm from the tip using a fresh scalpel blade. Immediately after incision, the tail was immersed 1 to 2 cm into sterile saline prewarmed to 37°C in a 50-mL tube. Bleeding was monitored continuously, and the time to first cessation of bleeding (defined as no visible blood for 30 seconds) was recorded. Any subsequent rebleeding events were documented, and total bleeding time was measured up to a 10-minute maximum.

Flow cytometry

Integrin αIIbβ3 activation and α-granule secretion were assessed in human PRP (1:20 dilution) using PAC1–fluorescein isothiocyanate and anti–CD62P-PE antibodies, and in washed mouse platelets using Jon/A-PE and WugE9–fluorescein isothiocyanate. Platelets were stimulated with cross-linked collagen-related peptide (CRP; 5 μg/mL; Triple Helical Peptides, Cambridge, United Kingdom), thrombin receptor-activating peptide 6 (TRAP-6; 10μM; Bachem, Bubendorf, Switzerland), thrombin (1 U/mL) for 10 minutes, ADP (adenosine diphosphate; Cambridge Bioscience, Cambridge, United Kingdom), protease-activated receptor (PAR) 4 activating peptide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), or rhodocytin (synthesized by Prof. Johannes Eble), then fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and analyzed on a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (FL1/FL2 channels), capturing 10 000 events per sample. Results are presented as median fluorescence intensity.

Calcium mobilization assay

Calcium mobilization was measured as described previously.23 Briefly, PRP was prepared as outlined earlier and adjusted to 4 × 108 platelets per mL in citrate-glucose-saline buffer (13mM trisodium citrate, 120mM NaCl, 22mM d-glucose; pH 6.5) containing 0.02 U/mL apyrase. Platelets were loaded with 4μM Fura-2 AM in the presence of 0.35% fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin for 30 minutes at 30°C. After loading, samples were centrifuged at 520 × g for 10 minutes (RT) and resuspended in HT supplemented with 0.02 U/mL apyrase and 5.5mM d-glucose. Platelet counts were verified using a Coulter counter (Beckman Coulter Z1).

For agonist stimulation, partial half-log concentration-response curves were generated for cross-linked CRP and TRAP-6, with ionophore A23187 included as a positive control. Immediately before loading, washed platelets (4 × 108/mL) were diluted 1:1 in HT and recalcified with 1mM calcium chloride. Aliquots (95 μL; 2 × 108/mL) were added to a 96-well clear-bottom black microplate (Corning, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and preincubated for 5 minutes at 37°C. Intracellular calcium (Ca2+) mobilization was recorded in real time using a Tecan Infinite 200 PRO multimode plate reader (Männedorf, Switzerland) at 340/380 nm excitation and 510-nm emission, with 1-second shaking intervals between reads. Data were expressed as the 340/380 nm fluorescence ratio.

Proteomic analysis of platelet samples

Washed platelets were lysed in 1× radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (25mM HEPES, 200mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, 2% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS; pH 7.4) supplemented with PhosSTOP and cOmplete Mini EDTA-free inhibitors (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no. 23225) and normalized. Proteins were digested with trypsin, labeled with TMTpro, pooled, and desalted using SepPak cartridges. Peptides were fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase chromatography into 20 fractions and analyzed by nano-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (SPS-MS3 mode). Data were searched against UniProt human and contaminant databases (Sequest; 5% false discovery rate). Log2-transformed protein abundances were compared between PROTAC- and dimethyl sulfoxide–treated samples by paired t test and visualized as volcano plots in R (ggplot2 version 4.2.3). Single-peptide identifications were manually inspected to understand isoform assignments and investigated by western blotting. Apparent degradation of the adaptor-associated kinase 1 (AAK1) annotated protein group by NX-2127 was driven by a single unique peptide mapping to a poorly annotated noncanonical AAK1-like isoform (A0A096LP25; 3% coverage) and no peptides uniquely identifying canonical AAK1 (Q9BUD9). Western blotting of NX-2127–treated platelets for AAK1 showed no evidence of AAK1 degradation (supplemental Figure 2).

96-well plate aggregation

Platelet aggregation was assessed in a 96-well format. PRP was prepared and incubated with PROTACs, then dispensed into half-area plates alongside platelet-poor plasma (PPP) controls representing 100% aggregation. Plates were warmed to 37°C, stimulated with agonists, and shaken at 1200 rpm for 5 minutes. Absorbance (595 nm) was measured on a Labtech LT-4500 reader. Percentage aggregation was calculated as the normalized absorbance of stimulated PRP minus that of unstimulated PRP, each relative to PPP (response PPP).

Whole blood count

Male and female C57BL/6 mice (9-10 weeks; Charles River) were treated with vehicle (composition) or NX-5948 (30 mg/kg) by oral gavage (5 mL/kg). At 24 hours after dosing, whole blood was collected via venipuncture of the inferior vena cava into K2-EDTA–coated tubes. Samples were gently inverted and analyzed within 6 hours using a Siemens ADVIA 2120 hematology analyzer operating in veterinary (murine) mode. Complete blood counts, including erythrocyte indices, platelet counts, and leukocyte differentials, were obtained.

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using an unpaired 2-tailed Student t test in GraphPad Prism, with significance defined as P < .05.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (version 9). Welch’s t test was used for 2-group comparisons, and 1-way analysis of variance for analyses involving >3 groups. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P ≤ .05, ∗∗P ≤ .01, ∗∗∗P ≤ .001, and ∗∗∗∗P ≤ .0001.

Ethics

Human blood was obtained from healthy volunteers after approval by the National Health Service Research Ethics Committee (reference number 20/SC/0222) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent. All animal experiments were approved by the University of Bristol Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body and conducted under a UK Home Office project license (under the name of Professor Alastair Poole), in accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986.

Results

NX-2127 and NX-5948 efficiently and selectively degrade BTK in human platelets

We examined whether the cereblon-recruiting BTK degraders NX-2127 and NX-5948 could eliminate BTK in human platelets, a cell type only recently shown to be susceptible to PROTAC-mediated targeted protein degradation.19 Concentration-response analyses in washed platelets and PRP revealed that both compounds robustly degraded BTK, but with striking differences in potency and cooperativity.

In washed platelets, NX-2127 produced a concentration-dependent decline in BTK with a half-maximal degradation concentration (DC50) of 12nM and a classic “hook effect” at high concentrations, consistent with nonproductive ternary complex formation when a PROTAC binds to either the E3 ligase or the target protein, but not both at the same time (Figure 1A). In PRP, its potency was reduced roughly 10-fold (DC50 ≈ 98nM; Figure 1C), suggesting partial sequestration by plasma proteins. NX-5948 achieved a DC50 of 5.3nM in washed platelets (Figure 1B) and maintained near-complete degradation without any hook effect, suggestive of a highly cooperative target engagement. The potency shift between washed platelets and PRP was modest (DC50 = 12.8nM; Figure 1D), implying that NX-5948 remains active even in plasma, possibly because of reduced susceptibility to protein binding than NX-2127. The relative resistance of NX-5948 to plasma sequestration represents a favorable pharmacological property for in vivo use. Kinetic degradation profiling reinforced these distinctions. NX-2127 required ∼150 minutes to reach 50% BTK loss and 250 minutes for maximal degradation, whereas NX-5948 achieved the same end points within 30 minutes and 150 minutes, respectively (Figures 1E-F). This rapid and near-complete loss of BTK establishes NX-5948 as a highly potent and kinetically efficient degrader.

NX-2127 and NX-5948 have limited effect on TEC

Quantitative TMT proteomics revealed high substrate specificity of the 2 degraders. Under matched conditions (3μM NX-2127, 100nM NX-5948), NX-2127 and NX-5948 induced degradation of BTK with high efficacy (Figure 2A-B). Although a reduction in the level of noncanonical AAK1 (A0A096LP25) was detected after NX-2127 treatment, western blotting confirmed that AAK1 expression levels were unaltered (supplemental Figure 2). Both compounds achieved near-total BTK loss, but NX-5948 did so at submicromolar concentrations (500nM vs 20μM for NX-2127), underscoring a >40-fold pharmacological advantage. Neither degrader affected the related TEC kinase by proteomics or immunoblotting (Figure 2C). BTK degradation abolishes GPVI- but not PAR-1–dependent Ca2+ signaling

Because BTK operates downstream of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM)–linked receptors, such as GPVI, but not of G protein–coupled receptors like PAR-1, we measured cytosolic Ca2+ mobilization after BTK degradation. TRAP-6–induced PAR-1 signaling remained unchanged after treatment with either degrader (Figure 4A). In contrast, Ca2+ mobilization in response to the GPVI agonist CRP was almost completely abolished across agonist concentrations up to 10 μg/mL, with only minimal residual flux at 20 μg/mL (Figure 4A). NX-5948 produced the most profound inhibition, achieving full suppression at doses that preserved overall platelet viability. These data support BTK as a nonredundant mediator of GPVI-mediated PLCγ2 activation and Ca2+ release but dispensable for PAR-1 signaling. The preservation of PAR-1 responses indicates that overall platelet viability and signaling competence are maintained. NX-5948 selectively suppresses GPVI-dependent activation and aggregation

Flow cytometric analyses revealed that BTK degradation translated directly into impaired functional activation. CRP-stimulated integrin αIIbβ3 activation and P-selectin expression were sharply reduced by both degraders, with NX-5948 producing near-complete inhibition (Figure 4B). In contrast, TRAP-6–induced responses were unaffected, confirming that PAR-1–mediated platelet function was intact. Aggregometry mirrored these results: CRP-induced aggregation was strongly suppressed, whereas thrombin and TRAP-6 responses were unchanged. These functional data show that NX-2127 and NX-5948 selectively impair GPVI-dependent, but not PAR-1–dependent, platelet activation.

Because GPVI also contributes to platelet spreading on a collagen surface,25 we assessed platelet adhesion and spreading under static conditions. On fibrinogen, NX-5948 significantly reduced the lamellipodia-to-filopodia ratio (Figure 5B), consistent with impaired integrin αIIbβ3-dependent spreading. Together, these results establish NX-5948 as a first-in-class orally active BTK degrader that achieves rapid, complete, and selective degradation of BTK in platelets, suppressing GPVI-dependent activation while preserving PAR-1–mediated function. This pharmacological separation between thrombosis and bleeding represents a major conceptual advancement for antithrombotic therapy and validates targeted protein degradation as a viable strategy in anucleate cells.

Discussion

BTK has emerged as a promising antithrombotic target, and here we compared the degraders NX-2127 and NX-5948 to define their effects on platelet BTK, function, and thrombosis. Both compounds efficiently degraded BTK in human and mouse platelets, but NX-5948 showed greater potency, faster kinetics, and deeper degradation, consistent with cooperative binding. Proteomics confirmed the high selectivity of both degraders. Functionally, both compounds suppressed CRP-induced integrin αIIbβ3 activation, P-selectin expression, and aggregation, but NX-5948 produced near-complete inhibition of GPVI signaling. TRAP-6–mediated PAR-1 signaling remained intact, confirming pathway specificity. In vivo, oral NX-5948 markedly reduced ferric chloride–induced arterial thrombosis in mice without prolonging bleeding. Together, these data establish NX-5948 as a potent and selective BTK degrader that prevents thrombosis while preserving hemostasis, supporting its further development as a next-generation antithrombotic.

We previously demonstrated that BTK degraders such as DD-03-171 and DD-04-15 target platelet BTK but also reduce TEC by ∼75%.19 In contrast, NX-2127 and NX-5948 showed minimal effects on TEC, providing more selective tools to dissect BTK function. Among these, NX-5948 was clearly superior, showing greater potency, more rapid kinetics, and near-complete BTK degradation. BTK, a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase of the TEC family, mediates platelet activation downstream of GPVI, C-type lectin-like receptor 2, and FcγRIIa through ITAM or hemITAM motifs. After GPVI ligation, Lyn phosphorylates FcRγ, recruiting spleen tyrosine kinase and the linker for activation of T cells signalosome, where BTK becomes activated to phosphorylate PLCγ2 and drive Ca2+ mobilization, key events in integrin αIIbβ3 activation, secretion, and thrombus growth. Although TEC can compensate for BTK deficiency in mice,26 our findings confirm BTK as the principal mediator of GPVI-dependent Ca2+ signaling, consistent with observations from patients with X-linked agammaglobulinemia whose platelets fail to mobilize Ca2+ in response to CRP.15

Both degraders markedly inhibited CRP-mediated integrin αIIbβ3 activation and aggregation, yet TRAP-6 responses were unaffected, demonstrating pathway specificity. Consistent with this, responses to rhodocytin were similarly impaired, whereas responses to ADP and PAR4 peptide were preserved, further reinforcing that BTK degradation selectively disrupts ITAM-mediated platelet signaling while sparing pathways dependent on the G protein–coupled receptor. In contrast to the near-complete suppression of both CRP-mediated integrin αIIbβ3 activation and P-selectin expression in human platelets, mouse platelets retained partial P-selectin expression despite equivalent inhibition of integrin activation, indicating that α-granule secretion may be less dependent on BTK in mice.27

Despite BTK’s key role in GPVI signaling, NX-5948 did not significantly alter platelet spreading stage on collagen under static conditions, whereas it reduced thrombus volume under flow. Because thrombus formation reflects both adhesion and platelet-platelet aggregation, these data do not establish whether primary adhesion to collagen under flow is preserved, consistent with previous inhibitor studies.28,29 Although both compounds modestly reduced spreading on collagen, this did not reach significance. The absence of a clear spreading defect may reflect residual GPVI- or BTK-independent signaling, or compensation by TEC, although the lack of effect of dual BTK/TEC inhibitors on adhesion and spreading would argue against the latter.28 Other receptors such as integrin α2β1 and GPIb may furthermore contribute under these conditions.26 Integrin αIIbβ3-mediated outside-in signaling is also linked to PLCγ2 and calcium dynamics through BTK.26,30 On fibrinogen, NX-5948 indeed significantly reduced the filopodia-to-lamellipodia transition. Collectively, these data indicate that BTK contributes to platelet spreading and cytoskeletal reorganization on fibrinogen, but not collagen, which may help explain the preserved hemostatic function despite potent antithrombotic activity.

Given its superior pharmacological profile, NX-5948 was selected for in vivo evaluation. A single 30 mg/kg oral dose eliminated platelet BTK within 24 hours and significantly impaired GPVI-mediated activation and P-selectin expression ex vivo. Strikingly, NX-5948 markedly suppressed arterial thrombosis without prolonging bleeding, achieving a clear separation of antithrombotic efficacy from hemostatic safety.

BTK degradation may have additional therapeutic implications beyond thrombosis. Patients with B-cell malignancies, where NX-5948 is already in clinical development, exhibit elevated thrombotic risk, particularly for deep vein thrombosis. Experimental data identify platelet Btk as a key driver of deep vein thrombosis,12 suggesting NX-5948 could confer dual benefit: antineoplastic and antithrombotic. A second potential indication is immune thrombocytopenic purpura, where BTK mediates both B-cell–driven autoantibody production and Fcγ-dependent platelet clearance. BTK inhibitors have shown efficacy in immune thrombocytopenic purpura,31, 32, 33 and degraders such as NX-5948 could enhance this effect by simultaneously limiting antibody production, macrophage phagocytosis, and thrombotic risk, particularly in patients treated with thrombopoietin receptor agonists who experience paradoxical hypercoagulability.34

Although platelets retain limited translational capacity, recovery of BTK after degradation is likely to depend predominantly on platelet turnover; BTK loss within platelets is therefore expected to persist for the life span of the affected platelet. Mice treated with NX-5948 for 24 hours furthermore showed ∼95% degradation of BTK in megakaryocytes. Although BTK is degraded in megakaryocytes, new platelets will be produced that already have significantly reduced levels of BTK, thereby potentially prolonging the platelet effect of NX-5948. BTK degradation in megakaryocytes may thus be advantageous because it prolongs the presence of BTK-depleted platelets and thereby extends NX-5948 activity. Dose optimization could further exploit this effect while potentially minimizing BTK degradation in other cells, including B cells. This pharmacological profile could benefit patients at high thrombotic risk who are intolerant of dual antiplatelet therapy because of bleeding. By reducing thrombus formation without impairing hemostasis, NX-5948 addresses a central limitation of current antiplatelet strategies.35 A key next step will be to define how platelet BTK degradation interacts with established antiplatelet agents such as aspirin because combined inhibition of GPVI-dependent signaling and thromboxane-dependent amplification may further enhance antithrombotic efficacy but could potentially narrow the hemostatic safety margin.

In summary, NX-5948 is a potent and selective BTK degrader that suppresses GPVI-mediated, but not PAR-1–mediated, platelet activation, effectively limiting thrombosis without prolonging bleeding. Its mechanism of action, safety profile, and oral bioavailability highlight its potential as a next-generation antithrombotic that dissociates efficacy from bleeding risk and may offer additional clinical benefit in thromboinflammatory and autoimmune disease. By separating thrombosis from hemostasis via degradation, rather than inhibition, of BTK, NX-5948 defines a mechanistically precise antiplatelet strategy. These data validate targeted protein degradation in platelets and motivate formal dose- and exposure-response and durability studies toward the development of a first-in-class antithrombotic.
