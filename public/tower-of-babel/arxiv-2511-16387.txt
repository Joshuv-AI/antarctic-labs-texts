The analysis of resonant frequencies and blow-up estimates of close-to-touching subwavelength resonators in the two-dimensional Helmholtz system
Hongjie Dong, Hongjie Li, Longjuan Xu

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate wave scattering by a pair of closely spaced inclusions embedded in a homogeneous medium, characterized by a high contrast physical parameters. The system is modeled by the two-dimensional Helmholtz equation. We show that this configuration exhibits two sub-wavelength resonant modes, whose frequencies display distinct leading-order asymptotic behaviors. These findings differ significantly from those in the three-dimensional Helmholtz setting. Furthermore, we provide a quantitative analysis of the gradient blow-up rates for the wave field localized between the two resonators. Keywords: subwavelength resonances, Helmholtz equation, high contrast materials, gradient estimates AMS Subject Classification: 35J05, 35C20, 35P20

Introduction

When inclusions are embedded in a matrix material, where their physical parameters share a high contrast, they exhibit strong oscillations under a certain impinging wave. This phenomenon is known as the subwavelength resonance. The subwavelength denotes that the size of the embedded inclusions is smaller than the wavelength of the incident wave. Subwavelength resonances occur in various systems, including air bubbles in water
[
6
,
20
]
, air bubbles in soft elastic materials
[
14
,
37
]
, hard inclusions in soft elastic materials
[
41
,
42
,
45
]
and silicon particles in the air within electromagnetism
[
9
,
31
]
. Due to their resonant properties, even a small volume fraction of these particles can significantly alter wave propagation. Consequently, subwavelength resonators enable many important applications such as invisibility cloaking
[
2
,
48
,
50
,
38
,
43
]
, super-resolution
[
13
,
10
,
18
,
24
]
, and super-absorption
[
32
,
39
]
.

When two resonators are brought close together, it is of significant interest to analyze their behavior, particularly the subwavelength resonant frequencies and the blow-up estimates of the wave field between them.
For related physical studies, we refer the reader to
[
25
,
29
,
51
]
and the references therein. Mathematically, the work
[
4
,
34
,
17
]
studied two adjacent bubbles in a liquid, modeled by the three-dimensional (3D) Helmholtz system.
The authors in
[
41
]
investigated the case of two hard inclusions embedded in soft elastic materials governed by the 3D elastic system. However, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies on two-dimensional (2D) cases involving both the Helmholtz and elastic systems.
In this paper, we focus on two adjacent resonators in the 2D Helmholtz system. The corresponding analysis in the 2D elasticity system is left for future work.
Indeed, the 2D Helmholtz equation models acoustic wave propagation scattering from long cylinders or the transverse electromagnetic wave propagation corresponding to long cylindrical structures
[

,
40
]
.
Related physical studies on pairs of cylinders in electrostatic and optical resonances can be found in
[
47

.
Hence, it is both necessary and important to develop rigorous mathematical theories for two adjacent resonators governed by the 2D Helmholtz equation.

In this paper, we consider the case that the two high-contrast particles are embedded in a matrix. The wave propagation is governed by the 2D Helmholtz equation.
As mentioned before, the model we considered here can apply to both acoustic and transverse electromagnetic wave propagation.
Let
\delta
denote the high-contrast physical parameter between the embedded inclusions and the surrounding matrix. Let
\varepsilon
represent the distance between the two inclusions. In the 3D Helmholtz system, the corresponding configuration exhibits two subwavelength resonant frequencies
[
4
]
.
One resonant frequency is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta\ln(1/\varepsilon)})
and the other is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
. In the 2D Helmholtz system, there are still two subwavelength resonant frequencies.
One resonant frequency is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta/\sqrt{\epsilon}})
.
However, the other resonant frequency
\omega
satisfies
\omega^{2}\ln\omega=\mathcal{O}(\delta)
, which is a nonlinear equation and significantly different from the 3D case.
Moreover, the analysis is more intricate due to the greater complexity of the asymptotic expansion of the 2D Helmholtz fundamental solution compared to the 3D case. See Theorem
4.1
for the details and Remark
4.3
for further discussion.
This analysis of resonant frequencies has important applications for designing metamaterials with multifrequency or broadband functionality in both acoustics and electromagnetics.

Furthermore, we investigate the behavior of the eigenmodes as the resonators are brought closer. The work
[

,
34

studied the the 3D Helmholtz system and showed that the maximum gradients for two wave fields are of the order
\mathcal{O}(1/{(\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon)})
and
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
, respectively. The authors in
[
41
]
proved the corresponding gradient estimates for the case of two hard inclusions embedded in soft elastic materials governed by the 3D elastic system. Therefore, it is natural to explore the gradient estimates for the wave fields localized between the two resonators in the 2D Helmholtz system. Prior studies in 2D electrostatics
[

,
7
,
11
,
16
,
21
,

,

,
30

and linear elasticity
[

,

,
36
,
33
,
35
]
have demonstrated that, when the material parameters of the inclusions tend to infinity, the electric field or stress field exhibits a characteristic blow-up rate of
1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. However, the blow-up mechanism in our present study differs fundamentally from these previous cases due to the resonant nature of the interaction.
Unlike the static or non-resonant scenarios, where blow-up arises from extreme material contrasts, our analysis reveals a distinct singular behavior driven by the excitation of subwavelength resonances. We derive rigorous gradient estimates in Theorems
5.1
and
5.2

\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
at the narrow region between two resonators. This phenomenon highlights the unique interplay between geometric proximity and resonant coupling, leading to enhanced field concentrations.

2
Mathematical set-up

In this section, we present the mathematical set-up of the considered problem. Then, the definitions of the resonant phenomenon and eigenmodes are provided from a mathematical perspective.

We consider two
C^{2,\alpha}
inclusions (resonators)
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
embedded in a matrix, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Denote
D:=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
. Let
\rho_{b}
and
\kappa_{b}
signify the density and bulk modulus of the resonators in acoustics, respectively. The corresponding parameters in the matrix are
\rho
and
\kappa
. We then introduce the following auxiliary parameters

v=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\rho}},\quad v_{b}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{b}}{\rho_{b}}},\quad k=\frac{\omega}{v},\quad k_{b}=\frac{\omega}{v_{b}},

(2.1)

which are wave speeds and wave numbers in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}
and in
D
, respectively. We also introduce the dimensionless contrast parameters

\delta=\frac{\rho_{b}}{\rho},\quad\tau=\frac{k_{b}}{k}=\frac{v}{v_{b}}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{b}\kappa}{\rho\kappa_{b}}}.

(2.2)

We assume that
v
,
v_{b}
, and
\tau
are all
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and there is a high contrast between the densities, namely

\delta\ll 1.

Indeed, when bubbles are immersed in liquids, the related parameters satisfy the assumptions here
[
20
]
. For the corresponding physical meaning of the parameters in the transverse electromagnetic, we refer the reader to
[
15
,
40
]
.

Let
u^{in}(\mathbf{x})
be an incident wave satisfying the equation

(\Delta+k^{2})u^{in}=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then the total wave produced by the above system is controlled by the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\Delta+k^{2})u=0&\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\
(\Delta+k_{b}^{2})u=0&\quad\text{in}\quad D,\\
u|_{+}-u|_{-}=0&\quad\text{on}\quad\partial D,\\
\delta\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{\nu}}|_{+}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{\nu}}|_{-}=0&\quad\text{on}\quad\partial D,\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
\bm{\nu}
denotes the exterior unit normal vector to
\partial D
, and the subscripts
\pm
indicate the limits from outside and inside of
D
, respectively. In the above system, the scattering wave
u^{s}:=u-u^{in}
satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition, namely

\lim_{\left|\mathbf{x}\right|\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\left|\mathbf{x}\right|}\left(\frac{\partial u^{s}}{\partial\left|\mathbf{x}\right|}-\mathrm{i}ku^{s}\right)=0,

where
\mathrm{i}
denotes the imaginary unit.

We will use the potential theory to analyze the resonant phenomenon of the system (
2.3
). Thus, we first present the related results for the subsequent use.
The fundamental solution of the operator
\Delta+k^{2}
is given by

G^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|),

(2.4)

where
H_{0}^{(1))}
is the Hankel function of the first kind and zero order
[
15
,
52
]
. Then the single-layer potential operator associated with the domain
D
is defined by

S^{k}_{D}[\varphi](\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\partial D}G^{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\varphi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial D,~\varphi\in L^{2}(\partial D).

On the boundary
\partial D
, the conormal derivative of the single-layer potential satisfies the following jump formula

\partial_{\bm{\nu}}S_{D}^{k}[\varphi]|_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}I+K_{D}^{k,*}\right)[\varphi](\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial D,

(2.5)

where

K_{D}^{k,*}[\varphi](\mathbf{x})=\mbox{p.v.}\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\bm{\nu}_{\mathbf{x}}}G^{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\varphi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})

with
p.v.
standing for the Cauchy principal value. It is noted that the operator
K_{D}^{k,*}
below (
2.5
) is called the Neumann-Poincaré (N-P) operator.

With the above preparations, the solution to the system (
2.3
) can be written as

u(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}u^{in}+S_{D}^{k}[\psi]\quad&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\
S_{D}^{k_{b}}[\varphi]\quad&\mathbf{x}\in D\end{cases}

for some surface potentials
(\varphi,\psi)\in L^{2}(\partial D)\times L^{2}(\partial D)
.
From the third and fourth conditions in (
2.3
), the surface potentials
(\varphi,\psi)
should satisfy

\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)\begin{bmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{in}\\
\delta\frac{\partial u^{in}}{\partial\bm{\nu}}\end{bmatrix},

(2.6)

where the operator
A(\omega,\delta)
is given by

\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)=\begin{pmatrix}S_{D}^{k_{b}}&-S_{D}^{k}\\
-\frac{1}{2}I+K_{D}^{k_{b},*}&-\delta(\frac{1}{2}I+K_{D}^{k,*})\end{pmatrix}.

In view of (
2.6
), we can define the resonance of the system (
2.3
).

Definition 2.1
.

We say that the resonance occurs for the system (
2.3
), if there exists a resonant frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
with positive real part and negative imaginary part such that the operator
\mathcal{A}
has a nontrivial kernel, where the operator
\mathcal{A}
is given in (
2.6
). In other words, there exists a nontrivial solution
(\varphi,\psi)\in L^{2}(\partial D)\times L^{2}(\partial D)
such that

\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)\begin{bmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{bmatrix}=0.

The functions
(\varphi,\psi)
are called the resonant eigenfunctions.
For such a resonant frequency, we define the corresponding resonant mode (or eigenmode) as

u(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}S_{D}^{k_{b}}[\varphi]\quad&\mathbf{x}\in D,\\
S_{D}^{k}[\psi]\quad&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}.\end{cases}

(2.7)

Remark 2.1
.

The resonant mode defined in (
2.7
) is normalized in the sense that

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}=\mathcal{O}(1).

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we provide some preliminaries and derive some auxiliary results for our subsequent use.

The fundamental solution
G^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
defined in (
2.4
) has the asymptotic expansion for
k\ll 1

[
6
]
,

G^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|+\eta_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(b_{j}\ln k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|+c_{j})(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|)^{2j},

(3.1)

where

\eta_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}(\ln k+\gamma-\ln 2)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4},\quad b_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2^{2j}(j!)^{2}},\quad c_{j}=b_{j}\left(\gamma-\ln 2-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\sum_{n=1}^{j}\frac{1}{n}\right),

(3.2)

and
\gamma
is the Euler constant. In particular,

b_{1}=-\frac{1}{8\pi},\quad c_{1}=-\frac{1}{8\pi}(\gamma-\ln 2-1-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}).

Thus, from (
3.1
) the following asymptotic expansion holds for the single-layer potential

S_{D}^{k}=\hat{S}_{D}^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(k^{2j}\ln k\right)S_{D,j}^{(1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}k^{2j}S_{D,j}^{(2)},

(3.3)

where

S_{D}[\psi](\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\psi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y}),\quad\hat{S}_{D}^{k}[\psi](\mathbf{x})=S_{D}[\psi](\mathbf{x})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D}\psi(\mathbf{y})\,ds(\mathbf{y}),

(3.4)

and

S_{D,j}^{(1)}[\psi](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\partial D}b_{j}|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2j}\psi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y}),

S_{D,j}^{(2)}[\psi](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\partial D}|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2j}(b_{j}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|+c_{j})\psi(\mathbf{y})\,ds(\mathbf{y}).

For the N-P operator
K_{D}^{k,*}
defined below (
2.5
), the following asymptotic expansion holds

K_{D}^{k,*}=K_{D}^{*}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(k^{2j}\ln k\right)K_{D,j}^{(1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}k^{2j}K_{D,j}^{(2)},

(3.5)

where

\begin{split}K_{D,j}^{(1)}[\psi](\mathbf{x})&=\int_{\partial D}b_{j}\frac{\partial|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2j}}{\partial\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{x})}\psi(\mathbf{y})\,ds(\mathbf{y}),\\
K_{D,j}^{(2)}[\psi](\mathbf{x})&=\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2j}(b_{j}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|+c_{j}))}{\partial\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{x})}\psi(\mathbf{y})\,ds(\mathbf{y}).\end{split}

(3.6)

Lemma 3.1
.

[
6
]

The following estimates hold in
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\partial D),H^{1}(\partial D))
and
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\partial D),L^{2}(\partial D))
, respectively :

\begin{split}S_{D}^{k}&=\hat{S}_{D}^{k}+k^{2}\ln k\,S_{D,1}^{(1)}+k^{2}S_{D,1}^{(2)}+O(k^{4}\ln k),\\
K_{D}^{k,*}&=K_{D}+k^{2}\ln k\,K_{D,1}^{(1)}+k^{2}K_{D,1}^{(2)}+O(k^{4}\ln k).\end{split}

Based on the asymptotic expansions of the single layer potential operator and the N-P operator
S_{D}^{k}
and
K_{D}^{k,*}
in Lemma
3.1
, we have the asymptotic expansion of the operator
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For the operator
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)
from
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\partial D)\times L^{2}(\partial D)
to
H^{1}(\partial D)\times L^{2}(\partial D))
defined in (

), we have

\mathcal{A}(\omega,\delta)=\mathcal{A}_{0}+\omega^{2}\ln\omega\,\mathcal{A}_{1,1,0}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{A}_{1,2,0}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{0,1}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}\ln\omega)+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{4}\ln\omega),

where

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{S}_{D}^{k_{b}}&-\hat{S}_{D}^{k}\\

\mathcal{A}_{1,2,0}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{b}^{-2}(-\ln v_{b}\,S_{D,1}^{(1)}+S_{D,1}^{(2)})&-v^{-2}(-\ln v\,S_{D,1}^{(1)}+S_{D,1}^{(2)})\\

and

\mathcal{A}_{0,1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\left(\frac{1}{2}I+K_{D}^{*}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
8
]

For some
\varphi\in L^{2}(\partial D)
with
\int_{\partial D}\varphi=0
, if it holds that
S_{D}[\varphi](\mathbf{x})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\partial D
, then
\varphi=0
.

If functions
\varphi\in L^{2}(\partial D)
do not satisfy
\int_{\partial D}\varphi=0
, i.e.,
\int_{\partial D}\varphi\neq 0
, the operator
S_{D}
may have a nontrivial kernel
[
8
]
. Moreover, we can show that the dimension of the

is at most

.

Lemma 3.4
.

For the single-layer potential operator
S_{D}
from
L^{2}(\partial D)
to
H^{1}(\partial D)
, there holds that

\dim\ker S_{D}\leq 1.

Proof.

Assume that two functions
\varphi,\psi\in\ker S_{D}
. Then we define a new function

\phi=\frac{\varphi}{\int_{\partial D}\varphi}-\frac{\psi}{\int_{\partial D}\psi}.

It is obvious that
S_{D}[\phi]=0
and
\int_{\partial D}\phi=0
. From Lemma
3.3

, we can obtain
\phi=0
. The proof is completed.
∎

From the discussion above, the operator
S_{D}
may not be invertible. Nevertheless, we then show that the operator
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
is always invertible.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Im\ln k\neq\pi/2
, the operator
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
defined in (
3.4
) is invertible from
L^{2}(\partial D)
to
H^{1}(\partial D)
.

Proof.

Since the operator
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
is a Fredholm operator
[
8
]
, we just need to consider the kernel of the operator
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
. Assume that the function
\varphi\in L^{2}(\partial D)
satisfies

\hat{S}_{D}^{k}[\varphi]=S_{D}[\varphi](\mathbf{x})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D}\varphi(\mathbf{y})\,ds(\mathbf{y})=0.

(3.7)

From the discussion below Lemma

, the operator

may have a nontrivial kernel. Thus we first consider the case that there exists a nontrivial

\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2}).

For the parameters
\alpha_{ij}
given in (
4.2
), it holds that

\alpha_{21}=\alpha_{12}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha_{11}=\alpha_{22}.

Proof.

From the definition of
\zeta_{1}
in (
3.8
) and using (
3.4
), we have that

\displaystyle\chi_{\partial D_{1}}=\hat{S}_{D}^{k}[\zeta_{1}]=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ \mathrm{d}s(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D_{1}}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial D_{2}}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y}).

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D_{1}}\ln\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{y}_{2})^{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D_{2}}\ln\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{x}_{2}-(-\mathbf{y}_{2}))^{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D_{2}}\ln|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})ds(\mathbf{y}),

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},-\mathbf{x}_{2})
. Following the same discussion, we have that

\begin{split}&\int_{\partial D_{2}}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\ ds(\mathbf{y})\\
&=\int_{\partial D_{1}}\ln|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}|\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})\ ds(\mathbf{y})+\eta_{k}\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})\ ds(\mathbf{y}).\end{split}

From the last three identities, we obtain that

\hat{S}_{D}^{k}[\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})]=\chi_{\partial D_{2}}.

Thus, we conclude that
\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})
as
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
is invertible. Similarly, we prove
\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})
.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{21}=\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})=\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\alpha_{12},

\displaystyle\alpha_{22}=\int_{\partial D_{2}}\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},-\mathbf{y}_{2})=\int_{\partial D_{1}}\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=\alpha_{11}.

The proof is completed.

The following lemma provides a more detailed estimate of

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
C
denote the norm of the operator
\mathcal{T}
defined in Lemma
3.6

\tau=\frac{1}{2\pi}(\gamma-\ln 2)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}.

If

\left|2\pi(C+\left|\tau\right|)/\ln k\right|<1

the parameter
\alpha_{1}=\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1}=\alpha_{11}+\alpha_{12}

\alpha_{1}=\frac{\pi}{\ln k}(1+o(1)).

(4.7)

Proof.

From
\zeta_{1}=(\hat{S}_{D}^{k})^{-1}[\chi_{\partial D_{1}}]
and the definition of
\hat{S}_{D}^{k}
in (
3.4

\hat{S}_{D}^{k}[\zeta_{1}]=\frac{\ln k}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1}+\tau\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1}+S_{D}[\zeta_{1}]=\chi_{\partial D_{1}}.

(4.8)

Next, we prove that the function
\zeta_{1}
has the following asymptotic expansion:

\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\zeta_{1,0}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\ln k)^{j}}\zeta_{1,j}(\mathbf{y}).

(4.9)

Substituting the last equation into (
4.8
) and comparing the order of the parameter
\ln k
, we have that

\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,0}=0,\quad\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,1}+S_{D}[\zeta_{1,0}]=\chi_{\partial D_{1}},

(4.10)

and for

,

\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,j+1}+\tau\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,j}+S_{D}[\zeta_{1,j}]=0,\\
\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,j}=t_{j},\end{cases}

(4.11)

where
t_{j}
are constants.

The existence of the solution
\zeta_{1,0}
in (

) is guaranteed by Lemma
3.6
.
Next, we provide the detailed estimate of the parameter
t_{1}=\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,1}

4.1

\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{2,0}=0,\quad\frac{1}{2\pi}t_{1}+S_{D}[\zeta_{2,0}]=\chi_{\partial D_{2}}.

(4.12)

Adding (

) and (

\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,0}+\zeta_{2,0}=0,\quad S_{D}[\zeta_{1,0}+\zeta_{2,0}]+\frac{1}{\pi}t_{1}-1=0.

(4.13)

By Lemma
3.6
, we conclude

Next, we consider the system (

). By Lemma
3.6
, the solution
\zeta_{1,1}
exists and satisfies the following estimate

\|\zeta_{1,1}\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}+\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D}\zeta_{1,2}+\tau t_{1}\right|\leq C|t_{1}|,

is the norm of the operator

(4.15)

4.3

Remark 4.3
.

The derivation of the 2D resonant frequencies is more involved than in the 3D case. For the 3D Helmholtz system, both resonant frequencies are obtained by just expanding the N-P operator to the first order. However, for the 2D Helmholtz system, the first resonant frequency is obtained by expanding the N-P operator to the order
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\ln k)
. The other resonant frequency is derived by expanding the N-P operator to the next order
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
. That is why the two resonant frequencies in 2D have different leading orders.

As shown in Theorem
4.1
, one resonant frequency satisfies

\omega=v_{b}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{12}-\alpha_{11}}{|D_{1}|}}\sqrt{\delta}(1+o(1)).

In Remark
4.2
, we have shown that
\alpha_{12}-\alpha_{11}
is positive. Moreover, as shown in Appendix
A
, when the distance
\varepsilon
between the two resonators tends to zero, we can prove the following result.

Corollary 4.2
.

The term
\alpha_{12}-\alpha_{11}
appeared in the last formula has the following estimate

\displaystyle\alpha_{12}-\alpha_{11}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\mathcal{O}(1),

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\lambda
is the curvature of
\partial D
at
(0,\varepsilon/2)
and
(0,-\varepsilon/2)
. Thus, the corresponding resonant frequency is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{1/2}/\varepsilon^{1/4})
. If the parameter
\varepsilon
is chosen
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\beta})
with
0<\beta<2
, the resonant frequency is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{(1-\beta/2)/2})
.

5
Blow-up analysis

In this section, we analyze the blow-up behavior of the resonant modes as the two resonators get closer to each other. For this, we first take a large constant
R
such that
\overline{D_{1}\cup D_{2}}\subset B_{R}
. In what follows, we denote
\Omega:=B_{R}\setminus\overline{D}
for simplicity and let
\Omega_{r}
(defined in (

and

.

From Theorem

where

,

, and

with
\psi
defined in (

).

where

with
\psi
defined in (
4.15
).

In order to prove the gradient estimates of solutions of (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we first present more characteristics of the two domains

and

.
By a translation and rotation of coordinates (if necessary), there exists a constant
R_{0}
independent of
\varepsilon
, such that the sections of
\partial D_{1}
and
\partial D_{2}
near the origin, respectively, can be represented by

\displaystyle x_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\mathcal{H}_{1}(x_{1})\quad\mbox{and}\quad x_{2}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\mathcal{H}_{2}(x_{1})\quad\mbox{for}~|x_{1}|<2R_{0},

where

. In (
5.3
), the functions
\mathcal{H}_{i}\in C^{2,\alpha}(-2R_{0},2R_{0})
,
i=1,2
, have the expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{i}(x_{1})=(-1)^{i+1}\frac{\lambda}{2}|x_{1}|^{2}+O(|x_{1}|^{2+\alpha}),

and

\|\mathcal{H}_{i}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(-2R_{0},2R_{0})}\leq C,

where
C
is a positive constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
\lambda
is the curvature of
\partial D
at
(0,\varepsilon/2)
and
(0,-\varepsilon/2)
. Throughout the paper, the constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
, and may vary from line to line in various inequalities. Here we would like to remark that our method can be applied to deal with the
more general inclusions case, say,
\mathcal{H}_{i}(x_{1})=(-1)^{i+1}\lambda_{i}|x_{1}|^{2}+O(|x_{1}|^{2+\alpha})
with two positive constants
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
For

, we define the narrow region between
\partial{D}_{1}
and
\partial{D}_{2}
as follows:

\Omega_{r}:=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\mathcal{H}_{2}(x_{1})<x_{2}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\mathcal{H}_{1}(x_{1}),~|x_{1}|<r\right\},

(5.4)

and the vertical distance between

and
\partial{D}_{2}
is denoted by

\delta(x_{1}):=\varepsilon+\mathcal{H}_{1}(x_{1})-\mathcal{H}_{2}(x_{1})=\varepsilon+\lambda|x_{1}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|x_{1}|^{2+\alpha}).

(5.5)

The behavior of the gradients of the resonant modes as the two resonators are moved close together is stated as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
