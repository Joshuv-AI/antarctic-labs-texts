Dolomitic Microfossil Preservation in a Cryogenian Microbial Reef, Arkaroola (South Australia)
Authors: France Champenois, Maree L Corkeron, Annette D George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobial dolostones that now form the Arkaroola reef, northern Adelaide Rift Complex, and were deposited during the Cryogenian interglacial period, display a wide range of meso‐scale stromatolite morphotypes in outcrop. Microcharacterisation involving optical microscopy, combined scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM‐EDS) and nanoscale secondary ion mass spectroscopy (NanoSIMS), was undertaken to identify microbial textures and relics in stromatolite samples. Microbial textures identified are micritic streaky and micropeloidal (grumous) microstructures as well as microcolumns, micritic threads and micritised tubular outlines resembling the Girvanella calcimicrobe. Within these microbial textures, multiple biotic components are discerned, including intact and flattened tubular bacterial sheaths, smooth and nanogranular non‐crystalline extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), filamentous and spherical bacteriomorphs, as well as coccobacilli‐like moulds. The biotic‐mineralogical interface is characterised by microcrystalline rhombic dolomite, nano‐sized pyrite spheroids and nano‐sized flaky silicates in direct contact with biotic components. Interpreted as a mixed community of filamentous and coccoidal (cyano)bacteria, these microbes were important contributors to reef building. Preservation of microfossils was likely facilitated by synsedimentary silicification of EPS and bacterial sheaths. Microbial metabolic processes lowered kinetic barriers for authigenic precipitation of clay minerals on bacterial surfaces serving as nucleation sites. These synsedimentary clays strengthened initially soft microfabrics and potentially helped establish chemical conditions that promoted early diagenetic precipitation of protodolomite. The findings of this study show that carbonates may intricately preserve microfossils where associated with synsedimentary authigenic clays and microcrystalline dolomite. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of the formation and growth of microbial reefs during the Cryogenian interglacial period.Keywords: Cryogenian, grumous microfabric, microbialite growth model, microbially mediated authigenic clays, organomineralisation, protodolomite, stromatolite accretion

Introduction

The Cryogenian reef complexes of the Balcanoona Formation, northern Adelaide Rift Complex (Figure 1a), preserve a variety of organosedimentary structures (Giddings et al. 2009; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). Abundant microbial mats and stromatolites are described, the morphology of which varies between different reefal environments (Preiss 1973; Wallace et al. 2015; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). For example, large domal stromatolites are associated with the reef crest whereas turbinate columnar and branching forms are associated with the reef flat (Preiss 1973; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). Despite the development of common macroscale bioherms, it has proved challenging to find micro‐scale evidence of the microbial communities responsible for their initiation and growth.

Structural, metamorphic and diagenetic processes adversely affect preservation of Precambrian microbial components by overprinting the original microfossils (Grotzinger and Knoll 1999). However, microbial contributions (both through metabolic influence and as organic nucleation points for mineral preservation) have been reported to preserve microfossils in chert (Francis et al. 1978; Knoll 1985; Orange et al. 2009; Benning et al. 2004; Sergeev et al. 2012; Demoulin et al. 2019; Slagter et al. 2021, 2022; Moore et al. 2021, 2023 and references therein) and limestones (Lyons et al. 1984; Schneider and Le Campion‐Alsumard 1999; Visscher et al. 2000; Arp et al. 2001, 2003; Sprachta et al. 2001; Zavarzin 2002; Braissant et al. 2003; Van Lith et al. 2003; Bosak and Newman 2005; Chekroun et al. 2004; Dupraz and Visscher 2005; Visscher and Stolz 2005; Gautret et al. 2006; Zaitseva et al. 2006; Ercole et al. 2007; Kempe and Kaźmierczak 2007; Dupraz et al. 2004, 2009; Kranz et al. 2010; Cutts 2022; Jarwar et al. 2022). Preservation of microorganisms and associated biofilm elements in Precambrian microbialites (e.g., Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004; German and Podkovyrov 2011; Moore et al. 2023) has been attributed to factors such as seawater supersaturation (e.g., Knoll et al. 1993), hydrodynamic conditions (e.g., Lan et al. 2020), or environmental variations in cementation and diagenesis (e.g., Knoll and Semikhatov 1998). Past studies reporting carbonate‐hosted microfossils across the Proterozoic, and in particular the Cryogenian, are mainly focussed on limestones (Pruss et al. 2010; Dalton et al. 2013; Cohen and MacDonald 2015; Moore, Bosak, MacDonald, Lahr, et al. 2017; Moore, Bosak, MacDonald, Du, et al. 2017). However, the limited attention given to dolostones may underestimate the potential for microfossil preservation in these abundant rocks in the search for relics of ancient microbial life.

During biomineralisation, microorganisms precipitate minerals either ‘biologically induced’ (or mediated) where inorganic mineralisation is facilitated by the by‐products of microbial processes, or ‘biologically controlled’ that is active precipitation of physiologically essential minerals (Dove et al. 2018). In modern microbialites, microbes and/or organotemplates such as extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and microbial sheaths provide a nucleating template for inorganic precipitation of metal‐bearing minerals (e.g., Konhauser 1998), silica (Renaut et al. 1998; Konhauser et al. 2001) and clay silicate minerals (e.g., Douglas and Beveridge 1998; German and Podkovyrov 2011; Cuadros 2017). Mg‐silicates have been observed in EPS driven by microenvironmental chemical changes induced by microbial metabolic reactions and/or degradation (e.g., Souza‐Egipsy et al. 2005; Bontognali et al. 2010; Pace et al. 2016; Wacey et al. 2018).

Despite these observations from modern microbialites, evidence for similar relationships between microbial surfaces and authigenic clays in Precambrian microbialites is scant, hindered by the perceived low preservation potential of microbial elements in ancient carbonate rocks (Knoll et al. 1993; Grotzinger and Knoll 1999; Riding 2000). However, recent studies of Neoproterozoic carbonate rocks have shown relics, barely recognisable with conventional microscopy, to be observable using electron microscopy (Knoll and Golubic 1979; Oehler 1978; Hofmann and Altken 1979; Aitken and Narbonne 1989; Knoll and Semikhatov 1998; Knoll et al. 1993; Turner et al. 1993; Bartley et al. 2000; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004; Kah and Riding 2007; Mettraux et al. 2015; Lan et al. 2020; Moore et al. 2023 and references therein). These results offer potential for recognition of microbial evidence in other Precambrian carbonate rocks.

This study applies macro‐ to nano‐scale visualisation and geochemical analysis to identify and assess the role of microbes as bioconstructors of Precambrian dolomitic microbialites. The morphology, chemistry and preservation state of microfossils identified within dolostones of the Arkaroola microbial reef of the Balcanoona Formation are analysed in the context of their paleoenvironments to interpret the key processes that operated at the biotic‐mineralogical interface. Furthermore, relationships between microbial growth and biomineralisation were investigated to develop a paragenetic model aligned with biogenic mechanisms considered in the growth of domal stromatolites with grumous (clotted) microfabric. A variety of potential microfossils and their taphonomy are described and we propose biologically mediated mineral precipitation mechanisms to explain the observed microtextures. The characterisation of Cryogenian microfossils expands our understanding of the evolution of the biosphere surrounding the Snowball Earths Events as well as the taphonomic processes that preserved soft‐bodied organisms and communities during the Neoproterozoic.

Geological Setting

The Cryogenian dolostone unit, the Balcanoona Formation (Umberatana Group of the Adelaide Rift Complex, South Australia), is interpreted as a regionally extensive prograding barrier reef system, comprising several reef complexes (Preiss 1993; Giddings et al. 2009) and featuring extensive stromatolite bioherms and other interbedded microbialites (Wallace et al. 2015; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). The reef complexes were deposited in the non‐glacial interval between the Sturt and Elatina glaciations (Preiss et al. 1998), globally correlated with the Sturtian and Marinoan ‘Snowball’ glaciations respectively (Hoffman and Schrag 2002; Williams et al. 2008). The age of the Balcanoona Formation is constrained between a tuffaceous horizon within a Sturtian glacial unit dated at ~663 Ma based on zircon U–Pb dating (Cox et al. 2018), as well as the underlying Tapley Hill Formation (specifically the Tindelpina Shale Member) with Re‐Os ages of 647 ± 10 and 645.1 ± 4.8 Ma (Kendall et al. 2006) and the stratigraphically younger Marinoan deglaciation deposits of the Elatina Formation (correlated with the upper Cottons Breccia, CA‐TIMS U–Pb zircon 636.41 ± 0.45 Ma; Calver et al. 2013). This reasoning results in an estimated age of ~650 Ma for the Balcanoona Formation.

The Arkaroola reef is a geomorphic element of the Balcanoona Formation. A generalised depositional model for the Arkaroola reef system comprises the forereef slope, reef‐margin, reef‐flat and back‐reef environments (Wallace et al. 2015; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). It comprises extensive microbialites including well‐developed bioherms, the morphology of which varies between different reef sub‐environments (Giddings et al. 2009; Wallace et al. 2015; Corkeron and Slezak 2020). Macroscale microbialite morphology is characteristic for each environment: minor encrusting boundstones and large domal stromatolites are linked to the upper reef slope to reef margin; turbinate columnar, branching, pseudocolumnar/cumulate and digitate stromatolites characterise the reef flat; and microbial oolitic boundstones are associated with the higher energy parts of the back‐reef lagoon (Corkeron and Slezak 2020).

The Balcanoona reefs are composed of limestones and dolostones. Debrites containing both dolomite and limestone clasts, and allochthonous blocks composed of limestones with poorly preserved sedimentary fabrics from the nearby Oodnaminta reef, suggest that dolomitization occurred continuously during synsedimentary diagenesis affecting the Balcanoona reefs from the tidal platform facies down to the reef slope (Hood et al. 2011; Hood and Wallace 2012, 2018; Wallace et al. 2015; O'Connell et al. 2020). The Balcanoona Formation developed at the interface between an oxidising platform environment (in contact with an oxygenated atmosphere) with ferruginous, anoxic, high Mg seawater (Hood and Wallace 2012, 2014, 2015; O'Connell et al. 2020). The ferruginous subtidal marine setting of the Balcanoona Formation is associated with Fe–Mn‐oxides precipitating and dissolving at this shallow marine chemocline (O'Connell et al. 2020). The Balcanoona reef complexes developed within a shallow‐marine environment characterised by redox heterogeneity and redox fluctuations during the transitional phase from an oxidised atmosphere and ferro‐sulfidic oceans dominated by widespread dolomite precipitation to more oxidised and ventilated oceans (O'Connell et al. 2020).

Materials and Methods

Sample Selection and Preparation for Microscale Characterisation

Hand samples with distinguishable microbial features were collected from different facies previously defined through detailed stratigraphic, sedimentological and facies mapping by Corkeron and Slezak (2020) in the northern Arkaroola reef around a gorge incised by Kingsmill Creek (Figure 1c). Fifty‐five thin sections (30 μm thick)—11 from the back reef, 29 from the reef flat, eight from the forereef slope and seven from the reef crest (Table S1)—were examined under bright‐field light with a Nikon Eclipse 50i POL optical microscope to characterise the facies and identify any micro‐scale microbial features and textures.

Microscale Characterisation

A suite of samples with likely microbial features identified from petrographic analysis was selected for detailed study (Table S1). Twenty‐eight small (~3 mm long) rock fragments were chipped under dry working conditions using a hammer from matching thin section blocks giving fresh surfaces to avoid surface contamination. Additionally, sampling near fractures was avoided to limit potential in situ contamination from burial fluids, soil‐derived organic matter and endoliths during exhumation.

The rock chips were set on SEM mounts using sterile equipment and copper tape for grounding, then gold coated to increase conductivity. Acid treatment was avoided in this study as acid etching has been associated with leaching of bacterial cells (Folk 1993) and production of bacteria‐like artefacts (Lan et al. 2020). Compressed air in a laminar flow cabinet prevented contamination from dust created during rock chip production. SEM mounts were stored in sterilised airtight storage boxes. Additionally, sample production, mounting and coating was undertaken in batches of 6 or less with SEM‐EDS analysis undertaken within 24 h. Every SEM mount was revisited 3 months after the first analysis to assess for contamination during production and storage. Rock chips were imaged using a JEOL JCM‐7000 NeoScope Benchtop SEM at the Centre for Microscopy, Characterisation and Analysis (CMCA) at UWA. Both secondary electron (SE) and backscattered electron (BSE) images were collected under high vacuum. SEM beam conditions and working distance are sample specific due to the irregularity of the rock chip surfaces.

Where microbial elements were identified from SEM analysis, five associated thin sections (Table S1) were selected for imaging under cathodoluminescence (CL) using a hot cathode. Hot CL microscopy enables differentiation of microbial components from carbonate cements in a broad field of view. Thin‐sections were analysed under vacuum in the CITL CL8200 Mk5‐2 Optical Cathodoluminescence Microscope Stage at CSIRO, Kensington, Western Australia. Analyses were conducted using an electron gun current of 450–550 μA, vacuum of 0.01 Torr and voltage of 12–15 kV. Samples were analysed at a fixed 10× magnification with 5 mm WD for objectives with 2.5× and greater magnification. Images were captured with a Nikon camera mounted on top of the microscope using exposure times of 253 ms for conventional microscopy and 51.1 s for hot CL microscopy.

Geochemical Characterisation

Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) available on the JEOL JCM 7000 Neoscope Benchtop SEM enabled spot geochemical analyses of likely microbial relics observed under SEM. Additionally, inorganic phases (carbonate phases and authigenic and detrital clays) were analysed for geochemical comparison. Due to the irregular surface of the mounted rock cuttings, the EDS spot analyses yielded qualitative chemical data. The Au‐Lα peak in EDS spectra originating from the Au coating was subtracted from each spectrum using the software Aztec.

One polished thin section from a large domal stromatolite with established presence of abundant microfossils under SEM‐EDS (Figure 2) was selected for further high‐resolution imaging using the CAMECA NanoSIMS 50 L at the CMCA to map elemental distribution. NanoSIMS analysis allowed for: (1) targeting of nano‐sized biotic and abiotic phases indistinguishable via SEM, and; (2) analysis for elements indicative of organic phases (e.g., N, S, P) where concentrations were below the EDS detection limit. The thin section was cut in half (25 mm × 30 mm) to fit the NanoSIMS holder and was gold coated (10 μm thickness) prior to imaging. NanoSIMS enables simultaneous analysis of seven isotopes. The first scan targeted 16O, 12C2, 12C14N, 32S, 28Si, 56Fe16O and 31P, commonly considered indicative of organic matter (Wacey et al. 2010). A Cs+ primary ion beam was used because of the high electron affinity of these elements. Coupling of N and C is used because CN− ionises better during SIMS sputtering than N on its own (Hoppe et al. 2013) and because it allows distinction of N bound in organic matter from N associated with, or bound in, minerals. Mapping Ca using the Cs+ beam was more challenging because of its strong positive secondary ion yield. The second scan focused on 24Mg, 40Ca, 27Al, 39K, 55Mn, 56Fe and 48Ti. Aluminium, potassium, manganese and iron are associated with metabolic processes (e.g., Konhauser 2007; Loutet et al. 2015) and degradation processes (e.g., Kaźmierczak et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2019).

Results

Microbial Textures From Petrographic Analysis

Microbial features from different reef facies were identified through conventional petrography and CL microscopy. The most recognisable microbial features were observed in domal stromatolites from the reef crest, microbial intraclastic breccia from the forereef slope, as well as in turbinate columnar stromatolites from the reef flat and encrusting microbial boundstone from the back reef. Under conventional microscopy, darker potentially microbial material contrasts with lighter micrite. CL microscopy especially aided in the recognition of cement paragenesis, particularly dolomite phases. Variable dolomite crystal sizes were observed in this study and are differentiated by size as follows: (1) microcrystalline rhombic dolomite as framework micrite (< 4 μm); (2) very finely crystalline (4–20 μm); (3) finely crystalline (20–50 μm); (4) medium‐crystalline (50–100 μm); (5) coarse crystalline dolomite (> 100 μm).

The domal stromatolite (Figure 2a) displays two different microfabrics. A sample from close to the core of the domal stromatolite (Figure 2b) has a laminated mesostructure characterised by primary and secondary laminae each comprising micritic clots thus giving it a grumous microfabric texture (Figure 3a). The primary laminae (~1–2 mm thick) are parallel to the domal shape, whereas the secondary laminae form micro‐columns orthogonal or oblique to the primary laminae. At higher magnification under parallel polar light, the micritic clots comprise dark brown micropeloidal aggregates in a lighter brown micritic background. Under CL, the grumous fabric has dull red luminescence at its core and a fine bright red luminescent outer rim. The grumous laminae form the framework of the domal stromatolite whereas a drusy cement fills the framework porosity (Figure 3b). The drusy cement is non‐equant with crystal size ranging from very fine (typically forming an isopachous rim around the grumous fabric), to fine and medium crystalline anhedral dolomite with lobate crystal boundaries (Figure 3b). The drusy cement has dull red luminescent cores and bright red luminescent outer crystal rims. A non‐luminescent blocky cement comprising subhedral coarse crystalline dolomite fills remaining pore space (Figure 3b,c).

A sample from the outer section of the domal stromatolite shows subtle lamination with a tubular microfabric (Figure 3d). Each lamina (~1–2 mm thick) comprises densely packed, short, fine threads that resemble tubes (Figure 3d). Unlike the micritic threads observed in the inner section of the domal stromatolites, these threads comprise anhedral to subhedral fine crystalline dolomite appearing pale brown in thin section (Figure 3e). These threads form a disorganised meshwork within drusy cement characterised by anhedral medium crystalline dolomite with irregular to lobate crystal boundaries (Figure 3e). Both finely crystalline dolomite and drusy cement have dull cores and bright luminescent outer rims suggesting both grew under similar conditions (Figure 3f). Remaining pore space was filled by non‐luminescent, subhedral, coarse crystalline dolomite (Figure 3f).

The turbinate columnar stromatolite has a banded microfabric (Figure 4a). Dark brown, dense micritic laminae (~1 mm thick) are interlayered with mixed micrite and dolomite cement bands (~3 mm thick) that display a grumous (clotted) streaky (alternating bands) fabric (Figure 4b). The dense micritic laminae comprise disrupted micritic sub‐laminae with micropeloids, condensed siliciclastic silt and few small vugs filled with anhedral to subhedral, fine crystalline, dolomite cement (Figure 4c). In contrast, thicker laminae comprise dark brown micropeloids, micritic threads resembling filamentous relics and more dispersed siliciclastic silt in brown framework micrite. Framework porosity is filled with drusy cement comprising non‐equant fine to medium crystalline, anhedral to subhedral dolomite (Figure 4c). Under CL, minor fine, bright violet‐luminescent quartz and bright green‐luminescent feldspar silt are hosted in the bright red dull luminescent micrite (Figure 4d). The fine to medium crystalline drusy dolomite cement has dark dull luminescent cores with bright red luminescent outer rims.

The encrusting microbial boundstone typically displays a fenestral fabric containing dark micritic bedding‐parallel wrinkly laminae 0.5–1.5 mm thick (Figure 4e). Laminae incorporate peloids, intraclasts and meniscus‐like clay stringers (Figure 4f). It is difficult to identify distinct microbial features in the dense laminae but some fine micritic threads are visible, especially under CL where they have bright orange‐red luminescence (Figure 4g,h). Very fine crystalline dolomite with bright dull orange‐red luminescence lines the fenestral pores. Larger voids within the micritic laminae are occluded by drusy cement characterised by non‐equant anhedral to subhedral, fine to medium crystalline dolomite with dull luminescent cores and thin bright red luminescent outer rims (Figure 4g,h). Under CL, non‐luminescent thin flakes appear within the bright orange‐red luminescent micritic background (Figure 4h).

The microbial intraclastic breccia (Figure 5a) displays a disorganised microfabric characterised by subangular to subrounded dark micritic intraclasts (0.5 mm to 2 dm across) in a ‘dirty’ drusy cement comprising a mosaic of anhedral to subhedral, non‐equant, fine, medium to coarse crystalline dolomite with irregular and lobate crystal boundaries (Figure 5b). In the field, the microbialite component of this breccia is a buff‐coloured dolomite hosting grey calcite breccia clasts (Figure 5a). A coarsely crystalline dolomite cement fills fractures that crosscut the microfabric (Figure 5b). Some irregular shaped patches comprise concave wrinkly brown micritic threads that resemble microbial filaments embedded in dolomite cement (Figure 5c). Under CL, the micritic threads itself are difficult to distinguish. Dull luminescent very fine crystalline dolomite often lines the “outer” side of the concave micritic thread feature, whereas larger and ‘cleaner’ subhedral coarse crystalline dolomite with alternating bright to dull red‐orange luminescence crystal zonation line the “inner” part of the feature (Figure 5c,d). As in other facies, drusy cement of fine to medium crystalline dolomite has dull luminescent cores with bright orange‐red luminescent outer rims. In contrast, the coarse crystalline calcite cement inside the clasts has bright orange‐red luminescence with crystal zonation.

The following paragenesis appears consistent between the different reef facies: (1) microbial colonisation/material building a soft organic framework; (2) microcrystalline dolomite (micrite as framework cement) directly on organic phases; (3) drusy cement initially surrounding the clotted micrite and micritic threads as an isopachous rim of very fine crystalline dolomite, later filling framework porosity as a mosaic of anhedral to subhedral, largely non‐planar/lobate dolomite ranging from fine crystalline at the edges of the pores to medium crystalline towards the middle of the pore; (4) a ‘clean’ non‐equant blocky cement with subhedral coarse crystalline dolomite crystals fills up the remaining pore space.

Compositional and Textural Characterisation of the Biotic‐Mineralogical Interface

SEM‐EDS analysis of samples from different microbial facies resulted in the textural and compositional distinction of potential microfossils. The assemblage of microfossils, including tubular features, spheroidal features, non‐crystalline smooth planar coatings as well as nanogranular planar coatings varies between facies with a greater abundance and variety of microfossils in reef crest and forereef slope stromatolites, as outlined below.

The domal stromatolite (reef crest) displays abundant, randomly arranged, well‐preserved tubular features embedded in micrite (Figure 6a,b). Tubes are 3–5 μm across and at least 30 μm long featuring branching nodes in longer specimen (Figure 6b). They have a smooth outer surface, are segmented, hollow and typically appear flattened (Figure 6b,c). Chemically, these tubes have higher C:Ca ratios than the surrounding dolomite phases as well as minor amounts of Mg, Mn, Fe, K and significant Al, O and Si (Figure 6c,d). These tubular features are commonly seen overlying or enveloping rhombic microcrystalline dolomite with comparably low C:Ca ratio (Figure 6b–d). Some compacted tubular features displaying elongated segments are also found embedded in very fine crystalline dolomite (Figure 6e). These generally have similar C:Ca ratios and major elements as the other deflated hollow tubes with the addition of Cl and lack of Fe, Mn and Al (Figure 6e). These tubes are often closely associated with irregularly shaped, non‐crystalline smooth planar coatings (Figure 6c,d). The planar coating appears smooth with nano‐sized circular moulds (~1 μm across) composed of moderate amounts of Si, Al, O, Mg and Mn as well as a minor amount of Fe, P, Ba and K with very high C:Ca ratios (Figure 6d). Aggregates, up to ~50 μm across, of nano‐sized flaky minerals are commonly overlain by hollow tubes and these irregularly shaped non‐crystalline planar coatings (Figure 6d). Chemically, they are characterised by low C:Ca ratios, moderate amounts of Si, Al, Mn, O, Mg and minor amounts of Fe, Ba and K (Figure 6d). Deflated spheroidal cell‐type features (~2 μm across) also occur within very fine crystalline dolomite (Figure 6f). Like the deflated tubes, they have high C:Ca ratios, significant amounts of Al, O, Mg and Si, and minor amounts of K and Fe (Figure 6f).

Discussion

Interpretation of Microtextures

The samples show a range of microfabrics from which microbial features were interpreted prior to microcharacterisation. A summary of the interpreted microbial features as biotic components is given here. Fine micritic threads (Figures 2E, 3A,D, 4B,G, 5B,C) are most common and are interpreted as filamentous bacterial sheaths because the dimensions and style of their longitudinal and circular cross‐sections resemble the circular outline of filamentous Girvanella. The tubular microstructure is very similar to the filament‐enriched laminae described from Neoproterozoic stromatolites elsewhere (Knoll et al. 1993, their figure 8; Knoll and Semikhatov 1998, their figure 5).

The grumous micritic streaks (Figure 4b) and microcolumns (Figure 3a) are interpreted as bundles of closely packed parallel filamentous bacterial sheaths orientated parallel or orthogonal to the growing surface. The streaky microtexture (Figure 4b) resembles laminae composed of calcified filaments in the Neoproterozoic Draken Formation (Knoll et al. 1993, their figure 7). The observed microscale texture of primary and secondary laminae composed of horizontal micritic threads and vertical micritic grumous microcolumns, respectively, resembles mineralised cyanobacterial filaments observed growing upright in modern (e.g., Perri et al. 2012) and ancient microbialites (e.g., Knoll 2003).

The grumous (peloidal/clotted) microtexture (Figure 3a) is interpreted as aggregated degraded extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and authigenic silicate minerals. This microtexture is very similar to microbially mediated peloidal textures described from Early Cretaceous stromatolites (Samankassou et al. 2005, their figure 10; Riding and Tomás 2006, their figure 7), and modern stromatolites in Brazil (Spadafora et al. 2010; their figures 4 and 10a) and South Australia (Perri et al. 2012, their figure 2c,d). Destructive diagenetic processes have been proposed to form secondary textures that mimic grumous (peloidal and clotted) textures in Pleistocene aragonitic crusts (Jaramillo‐Vogel et al. 2023). However, the grumous microfabric observed in this study has a different, more laminated configuration within a microscale texture of primary and secondary laminae suggesting it to be the result of microbially mediated precipitation.

Identification of Potential Microfossils in Neoproterozoic Dolostones

The contextual approach of this study using petrography to recognise microbial fabrics in microtextures of dolomitic stromatolites from various microbial facies of the Arkaroola reef facilitated identification of biotic components through SEM accompanied by spatial‐geochemical analysis with EDS and NanoSIMS. Potential microfossils are identified based on morphological and geochemical comparison with analogues in ancient and modern microbialites, mostly from a similar shallow marine setting. Characterisation at all scales is here integrated in the identification and interpretation of microfossils.

The tubular features (Figures 6b–e and 7a–c) are interpreted as filamentous bacterial sheaths. Filamentous cyanobacteria typically form a chain of cells surrounded by a mucilaginous sheath that protects the bacterial cells from unfavourable conditions and maintains moisture (Castenholz and Waterbury 2001). The elongated segments are consistent with an initial chain of bacterial cells that were enclosed by the sheath. The hollow tubular features from the forereef slope (Figure 7a–c) closely match silicified bacterial sheaths of the cyanobacterial Calothrix sp. (Renaut et al. 1998; their figure 7e,f), calcified filamentous cyanobacteria in Neoproterozoic turbinate columnar stromatolites (Lan et al. 2020; their figure 6), ancient cyanobacterial filaments documented from the Neoproterozoic Svanbergfjellet Formation (N. J. Butterfield 2009), empty sheaths of either Leptothrix‐type bacteria or oscillatoriacean filamentous cyanobacteria in the Precambrian Bitter Springs Formation (Knoll and Golubic 1979; their figure 4A), and the silicified Siphonophycus sp. in the Neoproterozoic Eleonore Bay Group, East Greenland (N. J. Butterfield 2015; Figure 4c) as well as in the latest Archean Gamohaan Formation, South Africa (N. J. Butterfield 2015; Figure 5). Siphonophycus is an unbranched, non‐septate and empty smooth‐walled filamentous sheath (Demoulin et al. 2019) which exhibits an alternating vertical/horizontal orientation typical of mat‐building photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

In contrast, the flattened and segmented tubular features observed in the domal stromatolites (Figure 6b–e) are interpreted as a remnant of a chain of elongated cells enveloped by a bacterial sheath. Their dimensions and segmented character resemble Primaevifilum amoenum Schopf observed in Archean carbonaceous cherts from the Warrawoona Group, Western Australia (Schopf and Klein 1992; their figure 1.5.5) but shares most of the morphological characteristics common for Oscillatoria (Schopf and Klein 1992; their figures 4 and 6), more specifically Oscillatoriopsis, an unsheathed cellular filament with more or less isodiametric cells (Demoulin et al. 2019 and references therein).

The cell envelope of cyanobacteria consists of a plasma membrane, a peptidoglycan layer (an outer membrane), and an outermost surface layer called the bacterial sheath (Konhauser 2007). However, not all filamentous cyanobacteria species have a mucilaginous bacterial sheath which surrounds the chain‐like arrangement of cells (Castenholz and Waterbury 2001). As with EPS, bacterial sheaths can be preserved due to their negatively charged surface. A process such as silicification would be a plausible taphonomic control on their preservation (Renaut et al. 1998; Konhauser et al. 2001; Souza‐Egipsy et al. 2005; Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008). Elevated amounts of Si were measured for the filamentous bacterial sheaths observed in this study (Figures 6c–e and 7b,c) suggesting they underwent silicification. These flattened and deformed tubes are similar to decaying filamentous cyanobacteria in the lagoonal microbialites of Tikehau atoll (Sprachta et al. 2001; their figures 7 and 9), as well as desiccated organic matter in modern subtidal microbialites on the Great Barrier Reef (Webb et al. 1999; their figure 9E,F).

Without quantitative geochemical evidence and genomic analysis, we cannot confidently conclude the observed filamentous features are cyanobacterial; however, it is a reasonable interpretation based on comparison of micro‐ and macro‐scale structures with modern and other ancient analogues. An interpreted distribution along the reef crest and forereef slope (Corkeron and Slezak 2020) is also consistent with cyanobacterial growth, which is constrained largely to the photic zone (depths up to 200 m; Davies and Smyth 2025). In an interpreted deeper depositional setting (Giddings et al. 2009; Wallace et al. 2015), cyanobacteria would be less likely the microbial agents of growth.

The fine smooth filaments associated with nanogranular planar EPS coatings (Figure 8c) may be bacterial or fungal filaments but filaments of such size and nature are limited in the Precambrian carbonate rock record. Alternatively, these fine filaments could represent fibrillar EPS. Filaments and fibrillar EPS were found closely associated in Miocene lacustrine microbial mats (Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008 their figures 13a and 14). Fibrillar EPS is associated with the curling of EPS mucus manifesting as a layer of fibres (Renaut et al. 1998). The rarity of fine filaments in these Neoproterozoic dolostones may be attributed to the reduced preservation potential of bacterial cells and filaments compared to bacterial sheaths and EPS (Bartley 1996). Microbial elements best preserved in Precambrian rocks are filamentous bacteriomorphs, trichomes and bacterial sheaths although it remains difficult to distinguish between them (Wacey et al. 2010, 2019; Moore et al. 2023 and references therein). Cells can be preserved under specific environmental conditions leading to rapid cell encrustation (Kanaparthi et al. 2026).

The spheroidal cell‐type features (Figure 6f) could be interpreted as colonial bacteria that may form aggregates of individual spheroidal cells (Castenholz and Waterbury 2001). Their distorted state resembles the collapsed cell walls of empty spheroids ∼1.0 μm across from Triassic stromatolites described and interpreted by Perri and Tucker (2007) as mineralised coccoid bacteria. They also resemble degradational variants of chroococcoid unicells from the Late Precambrian Bitter Springs Formation interpreted as Eoentophysalis, a coccoid cyanophyte (Knoll and Golubic 1979; their figures 6 and 7). Similar to the tubular features previously discussed, the Si peak measured with EDS (Figure 6f) suggests preservation was likely facilitated through silicification. The flattened filamentous and deflated spheroidal features are interpreted as cell breakdown which took place before these bacterial surfaces fully silicified.

The smooth non‐crystalline coatings (Figures 6c,d and 7d) are interpreted as planar sheaths of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). The moderate to high levels of Si and O measured with EDS (Figures 6d, 7d, 8e) suggest the EPS might have silicified facilitating its preservation. Extracellular polymeric (or exopolymeric) substances comprise a highly hydrated matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids excreted by microorganisms into their extracellular environment allowing organisms to interact with the ocean geochemistry (Flemming and Wingender 2010; Decho and Gutierrez 2017; Costa et al. 2018). EPS serves as a protective envelope buffering individual cells against environmental stresses (e.g., desiccation, UV radiation, osmotic fluctuations) while facilitating nutrient trapping and stabilising cellular structural integrity (Flemming and Wingender 2010; Decho and Gutierrez 2017; Costa et al. 2018), At the community level, EPS acts as a glue holding microbial mats together, mediating cell‐to‐cell adhesion and defining the micro‐spatial biofilm community architecture (Flemming and Wingender 2010; Stal 2012; Decho and Gutierrez 2017). The minor presence of Al, Cl, K, Ba, Fe (Figures 6d, 7d, 8e) detected with EDS reflects its capacity of entrapping nutrients through the absorption of cations onto its template. The smooth non‐crystalline planar coatings observed in this study resemble EPS relics in modern stromatolites of South Australia (Perri et al. 2012; their figure 7c).

Circular moulds in the smooth non‐crystalline planar coatings (Figure 6d) may be textural imprints of now absent coccoidal cells or cavities. Alternatively, they can be interpreted as moulds of coccoid bacteria or other spherical bacteria‐like forms like those observed in modern lacustrine stromatolites (Perri and Spadafora 2011; their figure 9). Their dimensions also fit those of calcified coccoid bacterial fossils (1–4 μm across) described from Neoproterozoic peritidal stromatolites (Lan et al. 2020; their figure 7a–d). In a similar way, the tear‐shaped moulds ~2–3 μm across observed in smooth non‐crystalline planar coatings (Figure 8e) could be interpreted as moulds of another coccobacilli‐type bacteria. Submicron‐sized, circular moulds in this coating are too small to be coccoid‐type of bacteria and may, therefore, be moulds of previously present nanobacteria, viruses, or tiny gas bubbles. Given that the EPS sheet is intact, and the size of the moulds is not uniform, it is possible they are preserved gas bubbles.

In shallow marine settings, there are a few abiotic ways of producing textures which mimic the alveolar, filamentous or sheet‐like appearance of EPS as observed in this study. Dehydration of magnesium‐silicate or magnesium‐carbonate gels can create a similar web‐like meshwork and clotted filamentous networks (Kaźmierczak et al. 2015; their figure 3), or through the self‐assembled coprecipitation of silicate–carbonate biomorphs yielding smooth membrane‐like sheets (Islas and Cuellar‐Cruz 2021; their figures 3 and 4). Though there are chemical resemblances between the geochemical signature of the potential EPS observed in this study and the Mg‐silicate/carbonate gels described in Kaźmierczak et al. (2015), the environmental circumstances in which these abiotic biomorphs developed do not match the shallow marine reef environment in which the putative microfossils were observed in this study. Though these abiotic ways of producing EPS‐like textures exist, the geochemical profile, textural intricacy and close spatial association with filamentous bacterial sheaths in this study point towards a biotic origin. Further geochemical analysis focussing on organic biomarkers like lipid fragments and a distinct clumped isotope profile with δ18O and δ13C fractions driven by metabolic processes are necessary to exclude any abiotic influences.

Nanogranular non‐crystalline planar coatings (Figure 8d) are interpreted as remineralised EPS. As a hydrated substrate, EPS is known to be a ‘cation sponge’ having a strong cation binding property (Dupraz et al. 2004; Decho 2010). It provides an environment for both ion and molecule accumulation that are released into the immediate microenvironment upon EPS degradation (Dupraz et al. 2004; Bontognali et al. 2010). Therefore, EPS serves as an ideal organotemplate for the nucleation of minerals such as silica (Renaut et al. 1998; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004; Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008) and authigenic clays (Bontognali et al. 2010; Perri et al. 2021). The nanogranular planar coatings in this study (Figure 8c,d) resemble dehydrated EPS with nanospherical mineral units later forming micrite documented from South Australian stromatolites formed on modern marginal flats (Perri et al. 2012; their figure 7b,d). Alternatively, they also resemble microgranular silicate precipitates in Neoarchean and modern microbial mats (Kaźmierczak et al. 2009; their figure 7). Alternatively, these nanograins are also interpreted as mineralised bacterial bodies (Pedone and Folk 1996, their figure 2c; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004, their figure 2e–h).

Abiotic pathways in silica‐carbonate biomorph systems can synthesise nanogranular EPS matrices hosting in situ mineral nucleation. Self‐assembled systems couple non‐crystalline (amorphous), nanogranular silica polymerisation with the concurrent, localised nucleation of nanoscale carbonate crystals, closely mimicking early‐stage biomineralisation as sheet‐like structures comprising nanospheres and nanorods documented by Kellermeier et al. (2012; their figures 8b and 10) and sheets of elongated nanocrystals observed by García‐Ruiz et al. (2009) but none of these closely resemble the EPS textures seen in this study. Furthermore, there is a general lack in literature describing abiotic EPS‐like textures observed in shallow marine setting. Therefore, based on a strong morphological resemblance between the laminar non‐crystalline coatings observed in this study and the EPS observed in both ancient and modern material the smooth to nanogranular planar coatings in this study are interpreted as variably preserved EPS, for example, intact, dehydrated or remineralised. Considering the proximity of most of these EPS remnants to filamentous bacterial sheaths, it is likely filamentous bacteria excreted the EPS though contribution from coccoidal bacteria observed in this study cannot be excluded. Due to the Precambrian nature of these putative microfossils, which imposes a hard limit on DNA survival, no metagenomic sequencing data is available to finetune microbial identification. Therefore, no confident claims can be made regarding the primary metabolic pathways resulting in organomineralisation.

The Biotic‐Mineralogical Interface

In this study, the biotic‐mineralogical interface is characterised by aggregates of nano‐sized flaky minerals in between planar EPS coatings and bacterial sheaths in the domal stromatolite of the reef crest (Figure plate 6), and nano‐sized grains coating bacterial sheaths in the microbial intraclastic breccia of fore‐reef slope facies (Figure plate 7). Aggregates (Figure 6b–d) consisting of nano‐sized flaky silicate minerals with moderate amounts of Mg, Al and Si, and minor amounts of K, Cl and Ba are observed in direct contact with well‐preserved smooth planar EPS and filamentous bacterial sheaths. Nano‐sized granular silicate minerals coating the bacterial sheaths (Figure 7b,c) are similar to Perri et al.'s (2018) microbially mediated nano‐sized clay crystals (< 100 nm) within the non‐crystalline EPS surrounding the cells. Nano‐sized Mg‐silicates coating organic sheaths are mostly reported for (hyper)saline modern settings (Pace et al. 2016; Perri et al. 2018; Mercedes‐Martín et al. 2025) but rarely reported for shallow marine conditions similar to this study. The nano‐sized silicate grain coatings may, therefore, be the mineralisation product of the mucus‐like EPS that enclosed the bacterial sheath. Though the proximity to bacterial surfaces is not inherently indicative of authigenic clay mineralisation, their size, crystallography, organisation and geochemistry are all consistent with an authigenic source. Detrital clays are usually larger, well organised and densely packed (limited intergranular pore space), exhibiting discrete grain boundaries and signs of mechanical weathering (Burne et al. 2014; Arizaleta et al. 2026) which are lacking in this study. In contrast, authigenic clays are usually smaller in size, have random orientation, clean textures, occur in aggregates and fill in pore spaces (Kang et al. 2024) which corresponds to the characteristics of the clay aggregates observed in this study. Neoformed authigenic clays generally have very low Rare Earth Elements (REE) and Transition Trace Element (TTE) contents, while detrital‐derived or transformed clays carry low‐to‐moderate trace element contents (Pozo and Calvo 2018), so further trace element studies should be conducted to confidently exclude a detrital origin. The characteristics of the aggregates of nano‐sized flaky clays observed in this study are consistent with an authigenic origin. There are both biotic and abiotic processes that could lead to authigenic clay precipitation in shallow marine settings. Abiotic authigenic clay precipitation occurs primarily through chemical neoformation (Pozo and Calvo 2018), or reverse silicate weathering when localised evaporation increases pH, or the mixing of incompatible fluids drive porewater fluids to supersaturation with respect to clay minerals (Baldermann et al. 2022, 2025). Abiotic authigenic clays grow unhindered by biological templates as a poorly crystalline structure (Burne et al. 2014) or as delicate needles or fibrous networks with a widespread, uniform distribution controlled by physical pore space geometry (Pozo and Calvo 2018). In contrast, microbially induced authigenic clays precipitate localised around the fluctuating geochemical microenvironment of a decaying bacterial cell (wall) or EPS resulting in patchily distributed, poorly crystalline aggregates including trapped organic matter (Gadd 2010). The sole presence of these flaky silicate aggregates alongside microbial filament and EPS relics in this study is a strong indicator towards a microbially mediated authigenic origin.

Similar textures are observed in modern calcareous microbialites where an advanced stage of EPS degradation is characterised by a coarse grained authigenic Al–Mg–Si phase (Kremer et al. 2019, their figure 4d). The nano‐sized flaky clays observed in this study were likely formed in a microenvironment induced by EPS‐excreting microorganisms and are therefore interpreted to be microbially mediated clays. Clays (e.g., Kremer et al. 2019; Perri et al. 2021) can replace EPS structures during decay demonstrating microbially mediated authigenic crystal nucleation starts in the EPS at nanoscale (Konhauser 2007). Microbially mediated clays typically begin to form as nanoparticles (< 100 nm) within the non‐crystalline or poorly ordered EPS ultrastructure (Perri et al. 2012, 2018). Bacteria provide interfaces for sorption of alkali‐metal cations (Ca, Mg and Ba) and other elements from their surrounding aqueous environment due to the anionic charge of their surfaces and excretions (Douglas and Beveridge 1998; Renaut et al. 1998; Perri et al. 2018). Once metal ions have interacted with the electronegative sites, clay minerals can nucleate (Konhauser 2007). Production of these flaky silicate minerals may have been promoted by an increase in pH and supersaturation within the irregularly distributed microdomains of the EPS during its degradation (e.g., Dupraz et al. 2009; Perri et al. 2021). Al–Fe silicate precipitation is thus a form of biomineralisation on bacterial surfaces (Konhauser 2007).

Clays are found to be advantageous to both individual bacteria as well as the collective biofilm by providing micro‐nutrients while eliminating waste products through adsorption onto clay surfaces (Ransom et al. 1999), offering protection against desiccation and UV exposure (Alimova et al. 2009), adding biomass to the biofilm leading to greater stability, and facilitating quicker mass transfer throughout the biofilm (Vieira and Melo 1995). Microbially mediated minerals are typically more diverse in composition and morphology, less crystalline, smaller and more abundant than those that were abiotically formed (Urrutia and Beveridge 1994).

The Al, Mg, Si, Cl, K and Ba measured for nano‐sized flaky silicate minerals and granular silicate mineral coatings in this study where likely absorbed from the water column onto the EPS template from which these nano‐sized flaky clays precipitated through metabolic and degradational processes. Seeing the Balcanoona Reefs developed during a Cryogenian interglacial, elements such as Al, Si, K and Mg could represent a runoff suite of continental rocks heavily weathered during Snowball Earth glacial periods (Huang et al. 2016). Cycling of these elements into early oceans has been associated with the formation of authigenic clays on the seafloor (Zheng et al. 2024). Elements such as Fe, Mn and Ba could have been sourced from hydrothermal ridges on the seafloor related to shallow marine volcanic activity (Hoffman et al. 2011; Gernon et al. 2016). The lack of Fe, Mn in these potentially authigenic clays could be explained by its preferential incorporation into Fe‐Mn‐oxides, like the oxides observed in this study (Sites 1–3 Figure 9). Lastly, Cl could have been mobilised into the ocean through continent evaporite remobilisation during the interglacial periods (Hussain et al. 2021) or volcanic outgassing (Gernon et al. 2016). In this study, microcrystalline dolomite rhombohedra have been found in direct contact with planar EPS coatings. EPS has been shown to provide a suitable substrate for the development of protodolomite though predominantly (sub)spherical, or dumbbell‐shaped (e.g., Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008; You et al. 2013; Al Disi et al. 2019). In existing literature, rhombohedral forms of protodolomite nucleated from EPS is mostly associated with hypersaline settings like sabkhas (e.g., DiLoreto et al. 2019) or lacustrine lakes (Dunham et al. 2020). For the precipitation of low‐temperature protodolomite, these authors (Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008; You et al. 2013; Al Disi et al. 2019; DiLoreto et al. 2019; Dunham et al. 2020) propose a cooperative model where various active microbial metabolisms generate alkalinity and strip away kinetic inhibitors while the highly organised, negatively charged functional groups within the EPS matrix simultaneously bind and dehydrate Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions providing a crystalline template for protodolomite nucleation.

The microcrystalline dolomite rhombohedra in this study resemble euhedral dolomite rhombohedra (~5 μm) that nucleated on EPS in Late Triassic stromatolitic dolostones (Perri and Tucker 2007), and dolomite rhombohedra observed in Holocene carbonate sediments (Teal and Bischoff 2000). Microcrystalline dolomite rhombohedra have also been observed embedded in Al‐K flaky silicate aggregates in this study. Laboratory carbonation experiments (Liu et al. 2019) showed that highly negatively charged clay minerals (such as illite) can aid the precipitation of abiotic protodolomite under ambient conditions. Dolomite crystallisation might have been catalysed by clay minerals via electrostatic binding of Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions and simultaneous dissociation of these strongly hydrated cations resulting in protodolomites with similar morphologies, textures and structures to those of biogenic dolomite (Liu et al. 2019). Wanas and Sallam (2016) suggested that smectitic clays would play an important role in the formation of an abiotic protodolomite within an evaporative alkaline‐saline marginal lake system, in the absence of a carbonate precursor and microbes. Therefore, these authigenic clays could have played an important role in overcoming the kinetic barrier for the precipitation of early dolomite.

Few studies utilise the NanoSIMS to characterise nanoscale microbial features in carbonate materials so there are limited ancient or modern analogues to help identify biotic components. Geochemical evaluation of biotic versus mineralogical components with NanoSIMS is mostly consistent with SEM‐EDS results. The fragments observed in Sites 1, 2 and 3 (Figure 9) with elevated S, C and N contents are interpreted as fragments of EPS, and the irregularly shaped nanoscale features of varying size represent nanocrystals nucleating within the EPS microenvironment.

The nanoscale flaky silicate minerals observed with NanoSIMS are interpreted to be the same as the nanoscale flaky silicate minerals forming aggregates in direct contact with EPS observed with SEM‐EDS. The lack of other elements measured with NanoSIMS compared to the acquired EDS spectra for certain biotic and mineralogical phases could be due to their genuine absence in that specific chemical profile or due to content values below the detection limit.

The oval feature at Site 4 could be interpreted as a tubular bacterial filament in cross‐section, but the lack of NanoSIMS studies focused on Precambrian microbialites renders confident interpretation difficult. The flaky silicate minerals around and in direct contact with the tubular filament suggest that this bacterial surface may have provided a suitable substrate for their nucleation. Mg‐silicate minerals have been described attached to EPS (Ransom et al. 1999), as well as cyanobacterial cells in modern microbial mats (Douglas and Beveridge 1998), and experimental culture (Newman et al. 2017). The precipitation of authigenic clays is commonly associated with the presence of bacteria providing nucleation sites (Douglas and Beveridge 1998; Konhauser 2007; Newman et al. 2017; Spadafora et al. 2010; Perri et al. 2012, 2018, 2021; Perri and Spadafora 2011; Cuadros 2017).

Combined SEM‐EDS and NanoSIMS data suggest that the nano‐ to micron‐sized silicate minerals nucleated on bacterial surfaces (EPS and bacterial sheaths) and are probably microbially mediated authigenic clays.

Organomineralisation and Authigenic Clay Nucleation Within the Microbial Framework

Findings of this study suggest that organomineralisation of biotic components resulted in their early preservation, facilitating their structural integrity during growth and before compaction. A range of planar EPS preservation states were observed: (i) non‐crystalline smooth and textured most closely resembling its undisturbed state; (ii) non‐crystalline smooth with abundant moulds and few nanograins indicating an early stage of cell and EPS degradation with enzymatic breakdown, gas bubble formation and shrinkage due to dehydration (e.g., Arp et al. 1999); and (iii) non‐crystalline with plenty of nanograins enriched in Si, O and other elements reflecting an early stage of organomineralisation that progressively replaces the EPS structure (e.g., Perri et al. 2012) within a microenvironment altered by both microbial metabolic and degradational processes.

Bacterial sheaths observed in this study are preserved as: (i) moderately deflated and/or flattened sheaths with smooth surfaces consisting of Si and O among other elements (Figure 6d); and (ii) slightly deflated coated with nano‐sized granular silicate minerals composed of Si, O and other elements (Figure 7b,c). Silicification, a form of organomineralisation, likely played an early role in the preservation of these microfossils (Figure 10a). Intracellular and extracellular microbial material acts as organotemplates for the nucleation and growth of silica and other silica‐rich products such as clays (e.g., Renaut et al. 1998; Handley et al. 2008; Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008). Silicification begins with the chemical interaction between dissolved silica and organic functional groups present on the microbial cell walls, sheaths and EPS under silica‐undersatured conditions, with time coalescing into a siliceous matrix if silicification is sustained (Moore et al. 2020, 2021, 2023 and reference therein). Moore et al. (2021, 2023) suggested silicification in the Proterozoic would have been driven by photosynthetic pH increases and mediated by ambient magnesium ions acting as critical chemical bridges resulting in localised, nanoscopic coatings of magnesium‐rich amorphous silica preserving cellular structures and microbial mat textures. As such, bacterial sheaths with smooth surfaces enriched in Si and O represent an early stage of silicification and bacterial sheaths coated with nano‐sized silicate minerals are considered an advanced stage of silicification.

(b) Proposed paragenetic model for microbially mediated accretion of grumous microstructure and layered mesostructured in the Cryogenian domal stromatolites of the subtidal reef crest facies of the Arkaroola reef.

Silicification of cell membranes and EPS ultrastructure happens during life processes in microbial mat cementation (Renaut et al. 1998; Konhauser et al. 2001; Sanz‐Montero et al. 2008). The most favourable conditions for silicification are post‐mortem because inactive and dead cells have a higher capacity for binding metal ions as no more protons are released that would then occupy negatively charged sites on their surfaces (Renaut et al. 1998). With filamentous cyanobacteria, silica is preferentially precipitated on the outer bacterial sheath (Douglas and Beveridge 1998; Moore et al. 2020), which facilitates sheath preservation and not the trichomes they envelop. The structural polymers of bacterial sheaths are chemically more resistant to decay than internal cellular proteins and nucleic acids, resulting in a slowed decay rate allowing ample time for silica to nucleate and petrify the sheath before it loses its morphological integrity (Moore et al. 2023). Early silicification of the Cryogenian microfossils observed in this study is consistent with high silica concentrations present in Proterozoic oceans driving early diagenetic precipitation of non‐crystalline (amorphous) silica directly from the water column, resulting in silica encapsulation before further decay and compaction processes could destroy them (Moore et al. 2023).

The preservation state of tubular sheaths helps determine timing of silicification. Intact round tubes suggest that organomineralisation started during bacterial life or at the latest postmortem but before cellular degradation. The fine granular coating around these tubes leaves an imprint in the surrounding dolomitic microspar suggesting authigenic mineralisation took place prior to dolomite precipitation. Such silicification requires continuous delivery of siliceous fluids (Renaut et al. 1998). In contrast, distorted, flattened and deflated tubular sheaths indicate some degree of burial and compaction of the cells took place before silicification.

EDS data in this study show that EPS comprises certain metal ions like Al3+ and Mg2+ that could have further facilitated its long‐term preservation. The capacity of EPS to absorb metal cations such as aluminium inhibits the activity of autolytic enzymes, thus protecting cells and preventing the complete degradation of cellular substances (e.g., Ferris et al. 1988; Douglas and Beveridge 1998; Decho 2000). Simultaneously, metal binding would alter the surface chemistry of the EPS and cell walls, reducing the thermodynamic kinetic barrier for silica precipitation from solution (Konhauser 1997; Moore et al. 2023). Furthermore, negatively charged functional groups within EPS (such as carboxyl group) actively bind Mg2+ out of the ambient water column creating localised hypersaturation micro‐environments promoting carbonate mineralisation (Robles‐Fernández et al. 2022). Acidic organic substrates within the EPS act as catalysts to dehydrate Mg2+ complexes, facilitating the heterogeneous nucleation of high‐Mg dolomite rhombs that encapsulate the biofilm structure (Hoffmann et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2023). Dampening of organic degradation relative to the rate of authigenic mineral precipitation and rapid burial could have further facilitated long‐term preservation of the putative microfossils within the studied Precambrian dolostones.

Protodolomite and Microfossil Preservation in Neoproterozoic Microbial Carbonates

Microcrystalline dolomite and authigenic clays are texturally associated with preserved microfossils implying a paragenetic relationship. In this study, exceptionally well‐preserved microfossils were predominantly found in domal stromatolites and in microbial intraclastic breccia from the reef crest and forereef slope, respectively. Coincidently, authigenic clays (e.g., flaky clay aggregates, coating of very fine clays) were found in both microbialite morphologies, whereas microcrystalline dolomite was primarily distinguished in the domal stromatolites. Both mineral phases are spatially closely associated with the microfossils implying they played an important role in facilitating their long‐term preservation within microbial carbonates. Other stromatolite morphologies from back reef and reef flat facies were analysed but generally lacked microfossils. This is likely associated with prevalent hydrodynamic energy as well as nutrient and ion flux present within those reef facies. The upper fore‐reef slope and reef crest receive the highest volume of nutrients and oceanic ions through wave pumping and upwelling as deep, cold and nutrient‐rich oceanic waters are driven upwards against the reef topography by internal waves and tides. The sticky EPS present within active biofilms on the domal stromatolites and microbial intraclastic breccia would capture bulk ions flushing through before passing on to the reef flat and back‐reef. This continued influx of ions would facilitate synsedimentary organomineralisation of bacterial surfaces before they fully degraded. Moore et al. (2023) described a mat stratification model where silica and dolomite could precipitate simultaneously. Dolomite would form in anoxic sections of the biofilm, whereas silica would precipitate in the biofilm section dominated by actively photosynthesizing cyanobacteria. Alternatively, dolomite would precipitate in deeper parts of the microbial mat where bacterial surfaces degrade by heterotrophic processes, whereas silica precipitates in the top part of the microbial mat dominated by photosynthesizing cyanobacteria and evaporation. Within the domal stromatolites formed on the exposed reef crest, such mat stratification could explain the observed abundance in silicified microfossils and development of authigenic clays as an advanced stage of organomineralisation. Essentially, the active hydrodynamics of the reef crest and upper fore‐reef slope create a set of chemophysical conditions that win the taphonomic race against degradational breakdown through rapid lithification via silicification and authigenic clay precipitation, quickly followed by the precipitation of microcrystalline dolomite.

A mechanism for precipitation of early microcrystalline dolomite, or protodolomite, may be related to microbial degradation. In particular, degradation of EPS and bacterial sheaths lowers pH enabling carbonate nucleation (Dupraz et al. 2004). This can be achieved via: (1) externally increasing ion concentration to saturate the acidic binding sites with metal ions (Mg2+, Ca2+); (2) hydrolytic destruction of the EPS matrix which releases bound cations, and/or; (3) organisation into an acidic template for organomineralisation, that is, non‐biologically driven precipitation where organic substrates act as a template for dolomite nucleation (Trichet and Défarge 1995; Dupraz et al. 2004; Bontognali et al. 2010).

In the first case, increased ion concentration by external factors could be achieved through evaporation. Evidence for evaporation has been observed in certain biolithofacies of this study (distorted & shrivelled bacterial sheaths, early diagenetic silicification) and is associated with the shallow marine platform facies. Evaporation is unlikely, however, on the subtidal reef crest and forereef slope where the greatest variety and abundance of microfossils were observed in this study. An alternative way to increase ion concentration within the water column is through nutrient upwelling and recycling, associated with upwelling hydrodynamics at the forereef slope and wave action at the reef crest.

In the case of hydrolytic destruction, degradation of organic tissues involving decarboxylation releases large amounts of ions like Mg2+ from decaying Mg‐enriched exopolymers (Souza‐Egipsy et al. 2005; Bontognali et al. 2010). This increases cation concentration in solution permitting precipitation of dolomite. EPS degradation may happen through either heterotrophic metabolism (fermentation) or destruction by UV radiation (Dupraz et al. 2004). The latter requires shallow marine conditions. In deeper marine settings, turbidity adsorbs UV light, preventing decarboxylation.

The third mechanism involving acidic template organisation relates to the organomineralisation of EPS as it decays which is consistent with observations from this study. For the domal stromatolites of the Arkaroola reef, it is likely that a combination of all three mechanisms facilitated the nucleation of microcrystalline protodolomite.

In summary, this study proposes several pathways for protodolomite precipitation within a microenvironment, wherein authigenic clay formation linked to microbial and decay processes effectively lowered the kinetic barrier to dolomite precipitation, ultimately constructing the grumous microfabric of the reef crest domal stromatolites. Biofilms are highly structured systems actively manipulated by microbial cells to create microenvironments allowing the co‐existence of diverse metabolism types (Decho 2000). It is likely the combination of microbial degradational and metabolic processes within biofilms comprising a mixed community of predominantly filamentous (cyano)bacteria and few coccoidal bacteria facilitated synsedimentary nucleation of microcrystalline protodolomite within the reef crest domal stromatolites.

High‐resolution imagery links exceptional preservation of microfossil relics and the presence of nanogranular and microcrystalline mineral phases that precipitated during synsedimentary to early marine diagenetic stages. We propose that extensive early marine dolomitisation of calcite and aragonite precursors in the anoxic, Mg‐rich Neoproterozoic oceans (Hood et al. 2011; Hood and Wallace 2012, 2018) happened at an extremely fine scale. This synsedimentary dolomite crystal growth did not obliterate bacterial sheaths and EPS; rather, it facilitated their long‐term preservation in the geological record.

Mechanisms of Domal Stromatolite Accretion

High‐resolution imaging offers insights into how microbially mediated accretion (precipitation) produced domal stromatolites with a grumous (micropeloidal) microstructure and laminated mesostructure (Figure 10b). Nano‐ to microscale analysis of the biotic‐mineralogical interface suggests that decay processes of filamentous microorganisms resulted in organomineralisation (e.g., authigenic clays) of organotemplates (Figure 10a). Upward (light‐ or nutrient‐seeking) or horizontal growth of filaments resulted in a soft microstructure that developed into a solid primary microframework upon authigenic organomineralisation (Figure 10b). Organomineralisation lowered the kinetic barrier for the synsedimentary precipitation of early marine diagenetic protodolomite (with precursor aragonite or high‐magnesium calcite) on organotemplates, thus providing structural integrity to this primary microframework. During diagenesis, this microbially mediated microframework provided a template for precipitation of early diagenetic marine carbonate cements and later diagenetic dolomite in pore spaces (Figure 10b). Paragenesis of marine and diagenetic cements are well documented from the nearby Cryogenian Oodnaminta reef (Hood et al. 2011; Hood and Wallace 2012, 2014, 2018).

Conclusions

The Arkaroola reef microbialites offer new insights into processes that facilitate long‐term preservation of microfossils in Neoproterozoic microbial carbonates, as well as the accretion mechanisms responsible for the precipitation of grumous (micropeloidal) microstructures and layered mesostructures that make up subtidal domal stromatolites from the reef crest. Mixed microbial communities comprising filamentous and coccoidal bacterial morphotypes played a principal role in bioherm construction in this Cryogenian interglacial reef. The integration of outcrop‐scale observations of microbial dolostone to high‐resolution microscopy down to nanoscale, enables textural and geochemical characterisation of in situ microfossils, providing new evidence of ancient authigenic organomineralisation. This study proposes a paragenetic model for syndepositional Precambrian microbialite growth in which microbial metabolic and degradational processes resulted in organotemplate silicification and precipitation of authigenic clays on organotemplates within biofilms as the primary driver for the long‐term preservation of observed microfossils (e.g., bacterial sheath, planar EPS) in dolostones. Subsequently, authigenic clays would have assisted in lowering the kinetic barrier for the synsedimentary/early diagenetic marine precipitation of microcrystalline protodolomite crystals on these organotemplates providing structural integrity to microbial structures. This reinforced microframework becomes an important early phase in the accretion of non‐clastic Precambrian domal stromatolites. As such, this study offers a novel insight into the formation of microbialites with grumous microfabric. SEM identification of a suite of microbial relics from freshly fractured dolostone chips challenges the long‐held assumption of taphonomic limitations on microbial microfossil preservation in Precambrian dolomites. While back‐reef and reef‐flat environments lack microfossil evidence, the high‐energy hydrodynamics and associated ion pumping of the upper fore‐reef and reef crest drove a rapid taphonomic ‘flash‐freeze’ via authigenic clay precipitation and subsequent dolomitisation. Similar high‐resolution imaging studies should be undertaken on microbial carbonates from other Balcanoona Formation reefs to assess whether the proposed paragenetic sequence can be recognised across these different interglacial Cryogenian reefs. We propose revisiting the sedimentological record, targeting the global Precambrian carbonate rock inventory in the search for previously concealed microfossils that could provide new insights into Precambrian microbes and their bio‐physicochemical interactions in the marine realm. Ultimately, decoding the mineralogical and hydrodynamic conditions that were conducive to microfossil preservation provides a predictive framework for targeting lithologies in the search for ancient life on Mars and other planetary bodies.
