Balanced Component and Whole-Blood Transfusion Practices in US Trauma Centers
Authors: Pawan Acharya, Russell Griffin, Molly P. Jarman, Chandler A. Annesi, Jan O. Jansen, Jeffrey D. Kerby, John B. Holcomb, Zain G. Hashmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow have balanced component and whole-blood transfusion practices changed in US trauma centers, and to what extent do hospitals differ in adoption over time?FindingsIn this national cohort study of 71 997 trauma patients treated at 650 hospitals from 2018 to 2024, balanced transfusion use increased over time, largely driven by the adoption of whole-blood, but not component, transfusion. Significant between-hospital variation persisted after accounting for patient and hospital factors.MeaningBalanced transfusion has increased in US trauma centers, predominantly due to adoption of whole blood, yet significant between-hospital variation highlights the need to understand its mechanistic underpinnings.

Introduction

Hemorrhage remains a leading cause of early potentially preventable death after traumatic injury, and timely, balanced resuscitation is central to reducing mortality in patients with severe bleeding.1,2,3,4 Balanced component transfusion, typically delivered using a 1:1:1 ratio of plasma, platelets, and red blood cells (RBCs), is a cornerstone of damage control resuscitation and has been associated with improved outcomes compared with lower plasma and platelet exposure.5,6

There is increasing interest in cold-stored, low-titer group O whole blood (WB) as an alternative approach to component-based balanced resuscitation.7,8 WB simplifies administration logistics, shortens delivery of hemostatic products, and reduces transfusion complexity when speed is critical.9,10 Accordingly, military and civilian trauma guidelines increasingly endorse WB for early resuscitation of patients suffering hemorrhagic shock.11,12,13

However, despite increasing use, national uptake of balanced transfusion and WB programs across US trauma centers remains incompletely characterized. The existing literature largely reflects single center experiences, deployed military use, or regional systems.14,15,16,17 Systemic adoption requires institutional infrastructure, blood bank capability, regulatory oversight, and multidisciplinary coordination, which may contribute to variation in delivery of hemorrhage care.18,19 Such variation may have implications for trauma system performance and equity and underscores the importance of quantifying between-hospital differences to better understand adoption of evidence-informed transfusion practices.20,21

The aim of this study was to evaluate temporal trends in balanced component and WB transfusion, assess factors associated with their use, and quantify between-hospital variation in adoption. We hypothesized that adoption would increase over time and that substantial hospital-level variation would persist even after adjustment for patient and hospital characteristics. Although an evaluation of the impact of hospital-level differences in blood transfusion practices on patients outcomes is important, this exceeds the scope of this current study and, therefore, will be pursued as a future investigation.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the American College of Surgeons (ACS) Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) 2018 to 2024 datasets, a national trauma registry with standardized patient, injury, and hospital-level data. In 2024, TQIP included 845 participating US trauma centers.22 This study used publicly available, deidentified data and was exempt from institutional review board review and informed consent requirements.23 We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Study Population

We included trauma patients aged 16 years and older with physiologic evidence of hemorrhagic shock, defined as systolic blood pressure less than or equal to 90 mm Hg with heart rate greater than or equal to 108 beats per minute, systolic blood pressure less than or equal to 70 mm Hg, or shock index 1 or higher. These criteria were applied using initial physiologic measurements recorded after hospital arrival and reflect conservative enrollment parameters used in multiple large multicenter resuscitation studies.24,25,26

Patients were required to receive at least 1000 mL of blood products within 4 hours of arrival. This threshold was incorporated to reduce inclusion of patients receiving only small-volume transfusions. Patients transferred between institutions, arriving without signs of life, or missing systolic blood pressure or heart rate were excluded (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).

Outcome and Exposure Definitions

The main outcome was balanced transfusion and WB use within 4 hours of arrival. Among patients receiving RBCs, plasma-to-RBC and platelet-to-RBC ratios were calculated as plasma or platelet units divided by RBC units transfused during this period.27,28,29,30

TQIP reported transfusion data as units in 2018 and 2019 and as volumes in 2020 to 2024. The unit-based values were converted to milliliters using TQIP hospital-specific conversion variables and then standardized as 300 mL for RBCs, 250 mL for plasma, and 50 mL for platelets. These conversions were applied for data harmonization and do not represent product-specific administered volumes.

Balanced component and WB use were defined using prespecified component ratio, low volume exception, and WB criteria applied within 4 hours of arrival (definitions in eTable 1 in Supplement 1). The low-volume exception criteria were included to reduce misclassification of early balanced resuscitation among patients receiving fewer than 4 RBC units, and WB-based resuscitation was defined as receipt of WB comprising at least 50% of total transfused volume.10 The 50% threshold was selected as an operational definition to identify predominantly WB-based resuscitation rather than to imply biologic equivalence to component-based balanced transfusion.

Patient covariates included age group, sex, race and ethnicity, primary payer, trauma type (blunt vs penetrating), injury mechanism, helicopter transport, and severe injuries (Abbreviated Injury Scale score ≥3) across major body regions. Race and ethnicity were obtained from the TQIP registry and were included because they may influence trauma care delivery and access to resources. Hospital characteristics included ACS verification level, bed size, and teaching status.

Statistical Analysis

We described annual trends in balanced component and WB transfusion from 2018 to 2024. Multilevel, mixed-effects logistic regression models were used to evaluate patient- and hospital-level factors associated with balanced component and WB use.31 In the primary models, calendar year was modeled categorically to estimate year-specific associations. To evaluate hospital-specific adoption trajectories, separate mixed-effects models included study year as a continuous variable centered at 2020, corresponding to the initiation of standardized WB reporting in TQIP. These models included hospital-level random intercepts to account for baseline differences in transfusion practices and random slopes for study year to capture heterogeneity in temporal adoption trajectories. Random-intercept models were used to estimate average adjusted associations, whereas random-slope models were used to assess between-hospital variability in adoption over time. Model fit was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test.

Fixed effects are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Random effects were summarized using hospital-level variance, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and median OR (MOR).32 The ICC quantified residual variation attributable to between-hospital differences, The MOR was calculated as exp[√(2 × σ2) × 0.6745], where σ2 denotes the hospital-level random intercept variance and 0.6745 is the 75th percentile of the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.31,33,34 The MOR represents the median increase in odds of balanced component and WB use for otherwise similar patients treated at randomly selected hospitals.

Balanced component and WB use, including temporal trends and component distributions, were also examined using a more permissive component ratio threshold (≥0.8 for plasma-to-RBC and platelet-to-RBC ratios) as a sensitivity analysis. A 2-sided α = .05 defined statistical significance. Data were analyzed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and Stata statistical software version 18 (StataCorp).

Results

Trend in Balanced Component and WB Use

This study included 71 997 patients treated at 650 hospitals, including 197 ACS-verified level I trauma centers (30.3%). The median (IQR) age was 36.0 (26.0-52.0) years, 54 889 patients (76.2%) were male, 45 185 patients (62.8%) sustained blunt injuries, and the median (IQR) Injury Severity Score was 26 (17-35). Patients receiving balanced component and WB transfusion were more often male, transported by helicopter, and treated at ACS level I and teaching hospitals, and were less likely to have severe abdominal injury or undergo major cavitary operations (Table 1).

Balanced transfusion increased from 12.4% (918 transfusions) in 2018 to 37.6% (4244 transfusions) in 2024 (OR, 5.33; 95% CI, 4.88-5.81) (Figure 1). Component-based balanced transfusion remained relatively stable (approximately 12%-16%), whereas WB-based resuscitation increased from 8.1% (826 transfusions) in 2020 to 25.1% (2838 transfusions) in 2024 (Figure 1; eFigure 2 and eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Among hospitals with eligible patients, WB adoption increased from 173 of 525 hospitals (33.0%) in 2020 to 380 of 579 hospitals (65.6%) in 2024. WB-based classifications were unchanged under the more permissive definition; differences were limited to component-based transfusion recipients who did not meet the optimal 1:1 ratio threshold (eFigure 2 and eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Variation in Balanced Component and WB Use

Substantial and widening hospital-level variation persisted after adjustment for patient and hospital characteristics. Adjusted probability of balanced component and WB transfusion increased over time and remained higher among ACS-verified level I centers. The ICC increased from 0.22 (95% CI, 0.17-0.27) in 2018 to 0.35 (95% CI, 0.31-0.39) in 2024 (Table 2 and Figure 3), indicating that an increasing proportion of residual variation in balanced transfusion was attributable to differences between hospitals. Overall, hospital-level differences accounted for 24% of variation in balanced component and WB use (ICC, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.21-0.26). In 2024, the corresponding MOR was 3.56 (95% CI, 3.18-4.03), further supporting substantial between-hospital variation.

In models allowing facility-specific random slopes, both baseline utilization and adoption trajectories varied substantially across hospitals. The random intercept variance of 1.12 (95% CI, 0.95 to 1.32) indicated substantial between-hospital variation in baseline balanced component and WB utilization after adjustment. The random slope variance of 0.43 (95% CI, 0.35 to 0.54) suggested that adoption of balanced component and WB transfusion practices occurred at markedly different rates across hospitals over time. The small negative intercept-slope covariance (–0.05; 95% CI, –0.15 to 0.05) indicated no consistent association between a hospital’s baseline utilization and its subsequent adoption trajectory (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Factors Associated With Balanced Component and WB Transfusion

In adjusted analyses, balanced component and WB use was more likely among male patients, those with public or private insurance, and those transported by helicopter. Older age (55-74 years), gunshot or assault-related injuries, serious injuries (Abbreviated Injury Scale score ≥3) across multiple body regions, and hemorrhage control procedures were negatively associated. Additional adjustment for shock index (continuous variable) yielded similar estimates and did not meaningfully alter the primary associations, although higher shock index was associated with lower odds of balanced component and WB transfusion (OR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.75-0.82) (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). In subgroup analyses, patients undergoing operative hemorrhage control received greater transfusion volumes, and among these patients, those not meeting balanced transfusion criteria received significantly higher overall blood volumes within the first 4 hours (eTable 7 in Supplement 1). Larger hospitals demonstrated progressively higher odds of balanced component and WB use, whereas ACS verification level and teaching status were not independently associated. The odds of balanced component and WB use increased steadily across calendar years (Table 2).

Discussion

In this national, retrospective cohort analysis of trauma patients receiving transfusion for hemorrhagic shock, balanced component and WB use increased from 12.4% in 2018 to 37.6% in 2024. This increase was largely driven by an increased adoption of WB, whereas balanced component transfusion remained relatively unchanged over time. Substantial between-hospital variation persisted despite adjustment for patient and hospital characteristics, with increasing clustering over time as reflected by an increasing ICC, indicating heterogeneous adoption across trauma centers. Balanced component and WB use was also associated with sex, injury severity, hemorrhage control procedures, helicopter transport, insurance status, and hospital bed size.

The marked increase in WB use after 2020 likely reflects converging developments in trauma resuscitation, including experience from military and civilian trauma systems, increasing availability of low-titer group O WB, growing observational evidence, and broader institutional implementation.5,6,9,11,12,14,24 Interpretation of temporal trends should also consider that TQIP began capturing WB administration in 2020, defining the earliest period available for national evaluation.22 In contrast, component-based balanced transfusion had already become widely adopted following evidence supporting damage-control resuscitation, which may explain its relatively stable utilization over the study period.5,35,36 Together, these findings suggest that recent changes in balanced transfusion practice primarily reflect adoption of WB rather than changes in component-based transfusion practices.

Despite overall growth in balanced transfusion, significant hospital-level variation persisted after adjusting patient and hospital factors. In 2024, 2 otherwise similar patients could have more than a 3-fold difference in the likelihood of receiving balanced component and WB use based solely on treatment facility.31 Hospitals with higher baseline utilization were not consistently more likely to demonstrate faster adoption over time, suggesting that diffusion of balanced component and WB transfusion practices occurred through heterogeneous pathways rather than a uniform pattern in which early adopters continued to outpace lower-utilizing centers, highlighting substantial institutional variation in implementation across trauma systems. Substantial variation persisted even within the same ACS verification level, suggesting that structural capability alone does not ensure consistent balanced resuscitation. Because trauma center designation defines resources rather than operational transfusion practices and outcomes, more consistent definitions and reporting within TQIP may help better characterize balanced component and WB utilization across trauma systems.

Male sex and helicopter transport were associated with greater use of balanced component and WB transfusion. Historically, some centers have adopted more restrictive approaches to Rh-positive WB administration among female patients of childbearing potential, which may contribute to differential WB exposure by sex. Consistent with this possibility, exploratory analyses showed that the higher odds observed among male patients were driven primarily by differences between male and female individuals aged 15 to 50 years, whereas female individuals younger than 15 years or older than 50 years had similar odds of receiving balanced component and WB transfusion (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Patients with private or public insurance had higher odds, suggesting that WB and ratio-based protocols may cluster in institutions with greater operational resources. Because payer mix influences reimbursement and infrastructure capacity, these associations likely reflect institutional readiness rather than clinical need alone. Lower odds among Asian and American Indian patients should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect geographic distribution, institutional adoption patterns, or residual confounding.

Patients with serious abdominal injury and those undergoing major cavitary or extremity and vascular procedures were less likely to meet component-based and WB-based balanced transfusion criteria despite receiving larger transfusion volumes. This likely reflects challenges in achieving predefined transfusion ratios during high-acuity resuscitation, especially at the 4-hour time point. For example, patients who received lower-volume yet balanced resuscitation may have been closer to meeting their resuscitation end points at the 4-hour time point vs patients who received larger-volume yet imbalanced resuscitation, with ongoing hemorrhage control and resuscitative needs.

Our findings expand prior work demonstrating substantial variation in trauma care processes across hospitals.37,38,39,40,41 We observed similar heterogeneity in balanced component and WB utilization practices, extending prior reports of variation in hemorrhage resuscitation and blood product utilization.42,43,44 However, this between-hospital variation should not be interpreted as reflecting implementation differences alone. Variation may arise from difference in institutional transfusion protocols, massive transfusion activation thresholds, blood product availability, prehospital transfusion practices, and data capture or reporting processes that are not measured within TQIP.45,46,47 Patients receiving plasma or WB prior to hospital arrival may enter care with partially balanced resuscitation that is not fully captured by registry definitions beginning at hospital admission. Institutional factors, including clinician practice patterns, transfusion infrastructure, and coordination between prehospital emergency services, trauma, and transfusion services, likely contribute to these differences.48,49,50,51

Although balanced component and WB transfusion practices increased substantially during the study period, the present analysis was designed to characterize adoption patterns and hospital-level variation rather than evaluate associations with clinical outcomes. In lieu of large randomized studies, future studies using designs better suited to address treatment timing, confounding by indication, survivorship bias, and immortal time bias are needed to determine whether increasing adoption of these practices translates into improved patient outcomes. Nonlinear modeling approaches, such as spline functions or segmented regression, may also better characterize temporal adoption patterns and periods of accelerated implementation.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. As a retrospective, registry-based observational analysis, we cannot determine whether higher or lower balanced component transfusion or WB utilization represents optimal practice. Residual confounding at both the patient and facility level is possible, including unmeasured factors, such as coagulopathy, physician decision-making, institutional transfusion protocols, and resource availability.

TQIP reports aggregate blood product administration within the first 4 hours and do not capture the timing or sequence of transfusion delivery, changes in transfusion ratios beyond 4 hours, or local massive transfusion activation practices. Consequently, balanced transfusion or WB utilization was defined using an operational classification that may not fully reflect dynamic resuscitation practices. Patients may have been misclassified if balanced transfusion or WB use was achieved after the reporting window, and the classification should be interpreted as an operational measure of transfusion practice rather than a direct measure of resuscitation quality. The low-volume exception criteria may have further introduced misclassification among patients receiving relatively smaller transfusion volumes. Alternative cohort definitions, including inclusion of all transfused patients, may also produce different estimates of balanced transfusion utilization.

Conversion of blood products from reported units to estimated volumes required assumptions regarding standard product volumes. Because TQIP does not capture detailed product characteristics, including leukoreduction status, platelet product type, platelet function, or storage age, biologic equivalence between WB and component-based balanced transfusion could not be determined, and misclassification is possible. Similarly, WB classification was based on the proportion of total transfused volume rather than product-specific composition.

This study is susceptible to survivorship bias. Patients who died rapidly, underwent immediate operative intervention, or did not survive long enough to receive plasma, platelets, or WB within the 4-hour observation window may have been classified as not receiving balanced transfusion despite aggressive resuscitation efforts. As a result, balanced transfusion or WB utilization rates may be underestimated among the highest-acuity patients. Although the magnitude of this bias cannot be directly quantified using TQIP data, its overall impact may be limited because the study cohort was restricted to patients receiving at least 1000 mL of blood products within 4 hours, thereby excluding patients with minimal transfusion exposure.

Furthermore, hospital-level variation may reflect differences in documentation, reporting, or measurement rather than true differences in practice. Because TQIP does not capture local transfusion protocols, massive transfusion activation thresholds, or data validation procedures, the relative contributions of practice variation and measurement heterogeneity could not be determined. In addition, because TQIP predominantly includes level I and II trauma centers, findings may not be generalizable to lower-resource hospitals or nonparticipating trauma systems. We also did not evaluate other resuscitation components, including crystalloid administration, that may influence transfusion practices.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of trauma patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock, balanced transfusion increased substantially from 12.4% in 2018 to 37.6% in 2024, largely corresponding to increasing WB adoption. Substantial between-hospital variation persisted and increased over time, likely reflecting a combination difference in clinical practice, institutional protocols, blood product availability, and unmeasured practices or reporting heterogeneity. This analysis was designed to characterize adoption patterns and hospital-level variation, rather than evaluate associations with mortality or other clinical outcomes, providing a foundation for future implementation studies and comparative effectiveness research.
