Valproic acid boosts hair follicle stem cell resilience to oxygen-glucose deprivation through autophagy induction and AKT/mTOR suppression
Authors: Fatemeh Keshavarzi, Mohammad Saied Salehi, Sareh Pandamooz, Sanaz Dastghaib, Morvarid Siri, Mehdi Dianatpour, Afshin Borhani-Haghighi, Zohreh Mostafavi-Pour, Pooneh Mokarram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stem cell-based therapy represents a promising strategy for the treatment of ischemic stroke. However, its therapeutic efficacy is limited by the poor survival and migration of transplanted stem cells within the hostile ischemic microenvironment. In this study, we investigated whether preconditioning human hair follicle–derived stem cells (HFSCs) with valproic acid (VPA) could enhance their survival and migration under ischemic-like conditions, with a particular focus on the roles of autophagy and the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. HFSCs were pretreated with 1 mM VPA for 24, 72, or 168 h and then exposed to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) as an in vitro model of ischemic injury. Rapamycin (RAPA), a known autophagy inducer, served as a positive control. VPA pretreatment significantly enhanced autophagic activity, as indicated by increased expression of Beclin 1 and LC3-II, decreased p62 accumulation, and augmented lysosome biogenesis. Concurrently, VPA suppressed the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. These effects conferred protection against OGD-induced apoptosis and preserved cell viability. Notably, 72 h VPA preconditioning markedly improved the migration capacity of HFSCs under OGD conditions. Importantly, the cytoprotective and pro-migratory effects of VPA were abolished by chloroquine (CQ), an autophagy inhibitor, highlighting the essential role of autophagy in mediating these benefits. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that VPA preconditioning enhances the survival and migration of HFSCs through autophagy activation and inhibition of the AKT/mTOR pathway. These results suggest that autophagy-based preconditioning strategies may improve the efficacy of stem cell therapies for ischemic stroke and warrant further translational research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32125-4.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide. This is largely due to the brain’s limited capacity for self-repair and the narrow therapeutic window of current treatments such as thrombolysis and thrombectomy1. These limitations highlight the urgent need for new regenerative therapies to restore neural function after stroke. Stem cell-based strategies are among the most promising options. However, their clinical use is restricted by the lack of accessible cell sources with robust neurogenic potential2.

Hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs), residing in the bulge region of adult hair follicles, offer a compelling alternative. These cells are readily obtainable, exhibit self-renewal capacity, and possess multipotent differentiation potential toward both ectodermal and mesodermal lineages, including neuronal and glial phenotypes3,4. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that HFSCs can home to injury sites and promote tissue repair in models of cutaneous wound healing5, spinal cord injury6, and neurodegenerative disorders7. Despite this therapeutic promise, their application in ischemic stroke remains underexplored8–10. The efficacy of stem cell therapies is frequently compromised by the hostile microenvironment of the ischemic brain, characterized by elevated oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic signaling, all of which detrimentally affect transplanted cell survival, homing, and function11. Consequently, enhancing stem cell resilience prior to transplantation has become a critical focus to improve therapeutic outcomes in stroke.

Pharmacological preconditioning represents a promising strategy to bolster stem cell viability and function12. Among candidate agents, valproic acid (VPA)—a well-established antiepileptic and mood stabilizer—has garnered considerable attention due to its multifaceted biological effects. As a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, VPA modulates chromatin architecture and gene transcription, thereby promoting the expression of cytoprotective and pro-survival and regenerative genes13. Beyond epigenetic regulation, emerging evidence indicates that VPA can induce autophagy, a vital catabolic process essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis under conditions of metabolic and oxidative stress14. Autophagy facilitates the clearance of damaged organelles and protein aggregates, preserves mitochondrial function, and supplies metabolic substrates—functions particularly critical in the ischemic milieu where energy depletion and oxidative injury threaten cell viability. The induction of autophagy thus emerges as a promising target for enhancing stem cell survival and therapeutic efficacy15. Indeed, pharmacological autophagy inducers such as rapamycin (RAPA) have demonstrated the ability to improve stem cell viability, migration, and paracrine signaling in ischemia models, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of this pathway16.

Despite these promising findings, the precise molecular mechanisms by which VPA regulates autophagy remain incompletely defined. Notably, VPA appears to modulate multiple intracellular signaling cascades, with the AKT/mTOR axis—a central regulator of cell growth, survival, and autophagy—playing a key role. Under normal physiological conditions, AKT activation stimulates mTOR signaling, suppressing autophagy, whereas inhibition of this pathway promotes autophagy17. Thus, VPA’s modulation of the AKT/mTOR pathway may be integral to its cytoprotective and pro-autophagic effects. While such mechanisms have been reported in various cell types and disease models, their relevance in ischemic brain injury and HFSC-based therapies is yet to be fully elucidated.

To address this gap, the present study aims to investigate the effects of VPA preconditioning on autophagic activity and AKT/mTOR signaling in HFSCs subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), a well-established in vitro model that mimics ischemic injury. We will evaluate whether VPA-induced autophagy enhances HFSC survival under these adverse conditions. Furthermore, considering that efficient migration is essential for stem cells to home to and repair injured tissue18, we will examine the influence of VPA on HFSC migration using a scratch wound assay under OGD conditions. Finally, the role of autophagy in modulating migratory behavior will be assessed to clarify its functional significance. Collectively, these investigations aim to provide mechanistic insights that may inform the optimization of VPA-preconditioned HFSC therapies for ischemic stroke.

Materials and methods

Materials

Valproic acid and Rapamycin (R-5000, 50 mg/mL) were obtained from RAHA Pharmaceutical Co. (Esfahan, Iran) and LC Laboratories (USA), respectively, while Chloroquine (CQ) and mitomycin C were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Before conducting the experiments, prepared RAPA in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted with complete media. VPA and CQ were directly prepared in complete media. The culture media used was alpha-modified Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS) obtained from Bioidea, Iran. Penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml) was sourced from Gibco (Life Technology, US). Primary polyclonal antibodies, including rabbit anti-nestin (#19483-1-AP), anti-SOX10 (#10422-1-AP), and anti-SOX2 (#11064-1-AP), were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Germany). The GAPDH antibody (Cat. #sc-47724) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA), and the p-mTOR (Ser2448; Cat. #610301) antibody from BioLegend (USA). Antibodies against Beclin 1 (Cat. #3495), LC3-β (L7543), p62 (Cat. #88588S), AKT (Cat. #4691), and p-AKT (Ser473; Cat. #9271) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA). Secondary antibodies, including peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (whole molecule) and anti-mouse IgG (Fab specific), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibody was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). The high-sensitivity enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blot substrate kit (ab133406) was also purchased from Abcam. Acridine Orange and Lyso Tracker Red were provided by Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). Thermo Fisher Scientific (Winnipeg, MB, Canada) and MabTag (GmbH, Germany) provided the bicinchonic acid (BCA) protein assay kit and Annexin-V apoptosis detection kit, respectively. For gene expression analysis, the Tripure RNA extraction reagent (Zhejiang, China), cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Canada), and SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) were used. Detailed protocols and primer sequences for qPCR are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Isolation and culture of HFSCs

According to a previously established protocol8, hair follicles were isolated from the pubic skin punches of a 22-year-old brain-dead donor, with written informed consent obtained from the legal guardian. The hair follicles were carefully dissected into small fragments and placed onto collagen-coated four-well plates (1 mg/mL). The culture medium consisted of α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10% chick embryonic extract, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Following explantation, stem cells migrated from the follicles and proliferated over the subsequent days. HFSCs at passages 4–6 were utilized for all experiments.

Verification of HFSCs

Immunostaining was performed to confirm the identity of cultured HFSCs using specific antibodies against nestin (a neural crest stem-cell marker), SOX10 (a neural crest cell marker), and SOX2 (a pluripotency marker)19–21. After fixing the migrated cells with paraformaldehyde, they were blocked with normal goat serum and incubated overnight with primary rabbit polyclonal antibodies against nestin (1:50), SOX10 (1:100), and SOX2 (1:100). To ensure accurate results, separate immunofluorescent staining was conducted for each protein. After washing and blocking, the cells were exposed to a goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342, and immunofluorescent images were captured using a ZOE™ Fluorescent Cell Imager.

Cell viability assay

To determine an optimal VPA preconditioning dose for HFSCs, an MTT assay was conducted. HFSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated with varying concentrations of VPA (0.01–10 mM) for 24, 72, or 168 h. For the 168 h incubation period, the medium was refreshed on days 3 and 5 to maintain nutrient availability and drug stability. In parallel, a subset of cells was treated with the autophagy inducer RAPA (20–2000 nM), either alone or in combination with VPA, to assess potential synergistic effects on viability. At the end of each time point, MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS) was added and cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the medium was removed and formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the untreated control group. Data represent the mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

Oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) model

To simulate ischemic-like conditions in vitro, HFSCs were subjected to OGD as previously described with minor modifications. Briefly, cells were washed twice with glucose-free α-MEM (Bioidea, Iran) and then incubated in the same glucose-free medium inside a modular hypoxia chamber (Billups-Rothenberg) pre-flushed with a gas mixture containing 1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂. The chamber was sealed and maintained at 37 °C for 24 h to induce OGD. Following OGD, cultures were returned to normoxic conditions (21% O₂, 5% CO₂) and replenished with normal α-MEM containing 4.5 g/L glucose for recovery, when applicable. Control (non-OGD) cells were maintained in normoxic, glucose-containing α-MEM under identical temperature and CO₂ conditions.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed as described in our previous publication22. In summary, stem cells were extracted and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. A BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to determine protein concentrations. Fifteen to thirty-five micrograms (15–35 µg) of total protein were electrophoresed on SDS–PAGE gels (15% for proteins with molecular weight ≤ 60 kDa and 10% for > 60 kDa) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (0.2 μm). After blocking for 2 h with 5% non-fat dry milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-mTOR, LC3β, Beclin 1, p62, AKT, p-AKT, and GAPDH (diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) was used to visualize protein bands following treatment with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Finally, Image Lab software (Bio-Rad) was used to semi-quantify protein band intensities, which were normalized to GAPDH.

Autophagy acidic vesicle detection

To qualitatively assess autophagic flux in HFSCs, two acidotropic fluorescent dyes—acridine orange (AO) and LysoTracker™ Red DND-99—were used to visualize acidic vesicular organelles (AVOs), such as autolysosomes, indicative of active autophagy. For AO staining, HFSCs were cultured in 6-well plates and subjected to the designated preconditioning treatments. After treatment, cells were incubated with AO (1 µg/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were gently washed with PBS and immediately examined under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX50). AO accumulates in acidic compartments, shifting its fluorescence from green (cytoplasmic/nuclear) to red in acidic vesicles, thereby allowing visualization of AVOs.

For LysoTracker™ Red staining, HFSCs were incubated with LysoTracker Red DND-99 (50 nM) for 40 min at 37 °C following the preconditioning phase. To visualize nuclei, cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1 µM) for 15 min in the dark. After staining, cells were washed with PBS, and fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. The accumulation of red fluorescent puncta indicated increased lysosomal activity and autophagosome–lysosome fusion.

Flow cytometry

To assess the effects of VPA preconditioning on HFSC apoptosis under ischemic stress, flow cytometric analysis was performed following OGD exposure. Based on preliminary MTT results, HFSCs were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to 72 h of preconditioning with VPA, either alone or after a 4 h pretreatment with chloroquine (CQ, 10 µM), which was subsequently removed by washing with PBS. After completion of the preconditioning phase, all groups were exposed to OGD for 24 h. Cells were then harvested using a non-enzymatic dissociation buffer, centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min, and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. The cell pellets were resuspended in 90 µL of Annexin V binding buffer and stained with 5 µL of FITC-conjugated Annexin V and 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, 200 µL of binding buffer was added to each sample to stabilize the suspension. Apoptotic cell populations were analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were processed with FlowJo software (version 7.6).

Cell migration assay

To evaluate the impact of VPA preconditioning on the migratory behavior of HFSCs under ischemic-like stress, a scratch-wound assay was conducted in conjunction with OGD exposure. To investigate the role of autophagy in VPA-induced migration, both autophagy-activating (RAPA) and autophagy-inhibiting (CQ) conditions were included as controls. HFSCs were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to 72 h of preconditioning with VPA, RAPA, or their combination (VPA + RAPA). Where indicated, CQ (10 µM) was added 4 h prior to the onset of preconditioning and removed by washing with PBS to transiently inhibit autophagic flux without inducing cytotoxicity. For the CQ-alone control group, cells received a 4 h CQ pretreatment followed by PBS washing and 72 h of incubation in standard medium, thereby ensuring equal incubation times across all groups.

To prevent proliferation-driven wound closure, all wells were treated with mitomycin C (10 µg/mL) 2 h before scratch induction. At the end of the preconditioning period, a uniform linear scratch was introduced across the cell monolayer using a sterile 100-µL pipette tip. The wells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove debris and immediately subjected to OGD conditions. Wound healing was monitored over a 24 h OGD period to directly assess cell migration under ischemic stress. Images were captured at 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h post-scratch using a MicroOptix phase-contrast microscope equipped with a digital camera (10× magnification), with three predefined fields imaged per well. The percentage of wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software and compared across treatment and control groups.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from two or three independent biological replicates, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Each biological replicate represents an independently cultured and treated HFSC population derived from a distinct donor sample. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.01; San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Optimization of RAPA, VPA, and combination preconditioning doses on HFSC viability

The experimental timeline, including preconditioning, OGD exposure, and subsequent assays, is shown in Fig. 2A to guide interpretation of the results.

To establish suitable preconditioning concentrations that maintain HFSC viability, an MTT assay was performed across a broad range of RAPA and VPA concentrations. Neither single-agent treatment with RAPA (20–1000 nM) nor VPA (0.01–1 mM) significantly affected cell viability at 24, 72, or 168 h (Figs. 2B–D). In contrast, higher doses of RAPA (≥ 1500 nM) and VPA (≥ 2 mM) induced dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity (Figs. 2C, D).

Similarly, combination treatments at lower concentrations (500–1000 nM RAPA with 0.5–1 mM VPA) were well tolerated, whereas the highest combination dose (1000 nM RAPA with 1 mM VPA) moderately reduced cell viability at later time points (72 and 168 h; p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) (Figs. 2B–D). Based on these findings, 1000 nM RAPA and 1 mM VPA were selected for single-agent preconditioning, while a submaximal combination (500 nM RAPA + 1 mM VPA) was used to minimize potential cytotoxicity in combination studies.

VPA preconditioning induces autophagy in HFSCs

To investigate whether VPA preconditioning activates autophagy in HFSCs, we examined the protein levels of key autophagy markers—Beclin 1, LC3, and p62—after 24, 72, and 168 h of treatment using western blotting. Representative western blot bands for these markers are shown in Fig. 3A. RAPA was used as a positive control to benchmark canonical autophagy induction and to assess potential additive effects in combination with VPA. Quantitative real-time PCR analyses for these markers were also performed, with methodological details and primer sequences provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S1). After 24 h of preconditioning, Beclin 1 expression markedly increased by 4-fold (P < 0.05) with RAPA, 4.1-fold (P < 0.05) with VPA, and 6-fold (P < 0.01) with RAPA–VPA (Fig. 3B). LC3-βII/βI ratios showed a modest rise at this time point—1.7-fold (P < 0.05) for RAPA, 1.1-fold (P > 0.05) for VPA, and 1.3-fold (P > 0.05) for RAPA–VPA—whereas p62 levels exhibited only a slight, non-significant reduction (Fig. 3C–D). At 72 h, autophagic signaling became more prominent. Beclin 1 levels remained significantly elevated (2.2-fold, P < 0.05 for RAPA; 3.4-fold, P < 0.01 for VPA; and 3.8-fold, P < 0.01 for RAPA–VPA) (Fig. 3B), accompanied by enhanced autophagosome formation as indicated by increases in LC3-βII/βI ratios (2.3-fold, P < 0.01 for RAPA; 1.4-fold, P > 0.05 for VPA; 3.5-fold, P < 0.001 for RAPA–VPA) (Fig. 3C). Concurrently, autophagic degradation was confirmed by decreased p62 protein levels—0.6-fold (P < 0.01), 0.3-fold (P < 0.001), and 0.3-fold (P < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 3D). Following 168 h of preconditioning, sustained autophagic activation was evident. Beclin 1 expression remained elevated (2.6-fold, P < 0.05 with RAPA; 4-fold, P < 0.05 with VPA; 5-fold, P < 0.01 with RAPA–VPA) (Fig. 3B), accompanied by consistent increases in LC3-βII/βI ratios (~ 2-fold, P < 0.05 for all treatments) and further p62 reduction (0.5-fold, P < 0.01 with RAPA; 0.7-fold, P > 0.05 with VPA; 0.3-fold, P < 0.01 with RAPA–VPA) (Fig. 3C-D). Collectively, these results indicate that VPA effectively induces autophagy in HFSCs in a time-dependent manner, comparable to RAPA, with the combined treatment producing the most pronounced activation of the autophagic pathway.

Qualitative assessment of autophagic flux using LysoTracker Red and Acridine Orange staining further supported western blot findings. While no significant fluorescence changes were detected after 24 h of treatment (data not shown), increased AVO formation was evident at 72 h in HFSCs preconditioned with RAPA, VPA, and their combination, with the co-treatment group exhibiting the most pronounced fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3E and F). After 168 h, fluorescence signals plateaued with similar intensities across all treatment groups (data not shown).

VPA preconditioning inhibits the AKT/mTOR pathway in HFSCs

Building upon the observed autophagy induction by VPA, we investigated whether this effect involves modulation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, a well-known regulator targeted by RAPA. Western blot analysis was performed to quantify phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT), phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR), and total AKT protein levels in HFSCs preconditioned with VPA, RAPA, or their combination.

After 24 h of preconditioning HFSCs with RAPA, VPA, and RAPA-VPA, no significant alteration was noted in the total AKT, p-AKT, and p-mTOR protein amounts (Figs. 4A-D).

However, HFSCs preconditioned with RAPA, VPA, and RAPA-VPA induced a time-dependent decrease in p-AKT (Figs. 4A, C) following 72 h (P < 0.05, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, respectively) and 168 h (P < 0.01) without affecting the protein level of total AKT compared to time-matched control (Figs. 4A, B). RAPA, VPA, and RAPA-VPA pretreated cells were observed to induce a significant time-dependent decrease in the phosphorylation of mTOR following 72 and 168 h, compared to time-matched control (P < 0.001) (Figs. 4A, D).

Therefore, our results suggest that RAPA and VPA, despite having distinct targets, may induce autophagy through a similar pathway, the AKT/mTOR, in HFSCs.

VPA preconditioning promotes cytoprotection under OGD stress via autophagy activation

To evaluate whether VPA preconditioning enhances HFSC resistance to ischemic stress, cell viability was assessed by MTT assay following 24 h of OGD exposure after different preconditioning durations (24, 72, and 168 h). RAPA was included as a reference autophagy inducer to compare mechanistic outcomes. As expected, OGD markedly reduced cell viability to approximately 55%, confirming effective induction of ischemic-like injury (Fig. 5A). Short-term preconditioning (24 h) with either VPA or RAPA did not significantly improve survival (data shown in supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, 72 h preconditioning significantly increased viability in both groups (P < 0.05), with the effect persisting—albeit slightly attenuated—at 168 h. The combination of VPA and RAPA produced the greatest protection, synergistically enhancing survival under OGD stress (P < 0.001, Fig. 5A). Based on these findings, the 72 h time point was selected for subsequent mechanistic analyses.

To determine whether autophagy mediates this cytoprotective effect, autophagy was pharmacologically inhibited using chloroquine (CQ, 10 µM, 4 h prior to VPA or RAPA). Western blot analysis showed that CQ alone increased LC3-βII and p62 levels compared with the OGD group (P < 0.05), consistent with lysosomal blockade and accumulation of undegraded autophagosomes. Importantly, cells preconditioned with VPA or RAPA in the presence of CQ exhibited a further increase in LC3-βII and p62 relative to CQ alone (P < 0.01), indicating that both agents enhanced autophagic flux upstream of the CQ-induced block (Fig. 5B–D). The selected CQ concentration was validated using MTT, confirming its non-cytotoxicity under normoxic conditions (Supplementary Figs. S3). These results support that the enhanced apoptosis observed under OGD + CQ conditions was specifically due to autophagy inhibition rather than off-target toxicity.

Flow cytometric analysis further supported these results. VPA preconditioning markedly reduced OGD-induced apoptosis—from 24% in untreated OGD cells to 8.6%—whereas CQ co-treatment partially reversed this effect, increasing apoptosis to 12.7%. CQ alone produced no significant toxicity at this concentration (apoptosis ~ 30%) (Fig. 5E, F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that VPA preconditioning confers robust cytoprotection against ischemic-like injury through activation of autophagy, with optimal efficacy achieved after 72 h of treatment. The loss of protection upon autophagy blockade underscores the essential role of intact autophagic flux in mediating VPA-induced ischemic tolerance in HFSCs.

VPA preconditioning enhances HFSC migration during OGD stress via autophagy activation

The scratch migration assay was conducted to investigate the effect of VPA preconditioning on the migratory capacity of HFSCs under OGD stress. As shown in Figs. 6A-B, HFSCs preconditioned with VPA, RAPA, and VPA-RAPA demonstrated significantly increased wound closure at 24 h, achieving approximately 60% (p < 0.05), 67% (p < 0.01), and 77% (p < 0.01) closure, respectively, compared to the non-preconditioned control group (42%). In contrast, treatment with CQ alone significantly impaired migration, reducing wound closure to 32% (p < 0.05 versus control). Importantly, the migration-enhancing effects of both VPA and RAPA were substantially inhibited by CQ pretreatment, which was administered 4 h prior to preconditioning and then removed, resulting in wound closure rates near 42%, similar to the control group (p < 0.05 compared to VPA or RAPA alone) (Fig. 6A, B). These findings indicate that autophagy plays a critical role in mediating the pro-migratory effects of VPA and RAPA under ischemic stress.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive mechanistic evidence that pharmacological preconditioning with VPA significantly enhances the survival and migratory capacity of HFSCs under ischemia-mimicking OGD conditions. These beneficial effects are predominantly mediated through the induction of autophagy and concurrent suppression of the AKT/mTOR signaling cascade, two interconnected pathways that govern cellular metabolism, stress response, and survival.

Autophagy has emerged as a pivotal process in improving the stress resilience of stem cells in hostile environments, such as the ischemic brain23. Despite the growing promise of HFSCs in stroke therapy due to their accessibility and neurogenic potential8,22, their efficacy remains limited by poor survival in the post-ischemic microenvironment. Various preconditioning approaches, including hypoxic and pharmacological strategies, have been shown to enhance stem cell survival and function by modulating autophagy24,25. In this context, we investigated the effects of VPA preconditioning on autophagy in HFSCs. VPA has been reported to induce autophagy in multiple cell types; for example, in glioma cells, it increased LC3-βII and Beclin 1 expression, indicative of enhanced autophagosome formation26. Similar effects have been observed in prostate cancer cells27 and in neuronopathic mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS) models, where VPA, alone or combined with lithium chloride, promoted lysosomal clearance and restored intracellular homeostasis28. Consistent with these observations, our results demonstrate a time-dependent autophagic response in HFSCs, with upregulation of Beclin 1 and LC3-βII, reduced p62 accumulation, and increased lysosomal activity, becoming prominent after 72 and 168 h of preconditioning. This gradual activation contrasts with the more rapid autophagic responses often seen in cancer cells or neuronal cultures. Such temporal differences may reflect the inherently lower basal metabolic rate and autophagic turnover in quiescent stem cells, which require prolonged pharmacologic stimulation to initiate downstream events29.

Regarding molecular signaling, recent evidence highlights variable effects of VPA on the AKT/mTOR pathway. While some reports suggest VPA can activate this pathway in specific contexts30,31, accumulating evidence supports its inhibitory effects, particularly in settings of autophagy induction26,32. Our data corroborate these latter findings by demonstrating significant reductions in phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR protein levels in HFSCs over 72 and 168 h VPA preconditioning, with no change in total AKT, suggesting a specific modulation of upstream signaling rather than nonspecific cytotoxic effects. Mechanistically, as an HDAC inhibitor, VPA may facilitate PTEN acetylation and activation, thereby enhancing its negative regulation of PI3K/AKT signaling and suppressing downstream mTOR activity27,33. In parallel, VPA has also been reported to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which further contributes to mTOR inhibition and autophagy induction34. These convergent mechanisms collectively explain the attenuation of AKT/mTOR signaling and the consequent cytoprotective phenotype induced by VPA preconditioning under OGD stress. Although, the present study did not directly manipulate AKT or mTOR activity, future experiments employing pharmacological inhibition or constitutive activation of these proteins will be critical to confirm their causal involvement in VPA-mediated autophagy and cytoprotection. To our knowledge, this is the first study to propose and experimentally support such an integrated signaling mechanism underlying VPA-induced ischemic tolerance in HFSCs.

To strengthen the mechanistic interpretation that VPA-induced autophagy in HFSCs is mediated via suppression of the AKT/mTOR pathway, we employed RAPA, a well-established autophagy inducer and direct mTOR inhibitor, as a pharmacological reference. RAPA was used for two main purposes: first, to confirm that the autophagic signature induced by VPA aligns with canonical mTOR-dependent autophagy; and second, to assess whether combined treatment with both agents would produce additive effects. Pretreatment of HFSCs with RAPA for 24 h markedly increased protein levels of Beclin 1 and LC3-βII/LC3-βI, while p62 exhibited a slight but non-significant decrease. These observations are consistent with Wet et al., who reported no significant reduction in p62 protein in mouse embryonic fibroblasts treated with 1 µM RAPA for 24 h35. At later time points (72 and 168 h), RAPA replicated VPA’s effects by enhancing autophagy markers and lysosomal activity, concomitant with suppression of AKT and mTOR phosphorylation. This temporal pattern aligns with Sotthibundhu et al., who demonstrated time-dependent autophagy activation in RAPA-treated induced pluripotent stem cells over 3 to 6 days36. Interestingly, when HFSCs were co-preconditioned with both RAPA (500 nM) and VPA (1 mM), we observed effective suppression of p-AKT and p-mTOR, along with significant elevations in LC3-βII and Beclin 1 expression and p62 degradation. However, the magnitude of these changes did not exceed those observed with either agent alone. This lack of additive effect suggests convergence of both compounds on a shared signaling axis—most likely mTOR—beyond which further inhibition does not enhance autophagic output. Collectively, these findings establish RAPA as a mechanistic benchmark and reinforce that VPA-induced autophagy in HFSCs is predominantly mediated via AKT/mTOR inhibition.

To further elucidate the functional relevance of autophagy activation, we investigated whether VPA preconditioning enhances HFSC resistance to ischemic stress. HFSCs preconditioned with VPA for 72 and 168 h exhibited significantly improved viability following OGD, accompanied by a marked reduction in apoptotic cell death. Notably, RAPA preconditioning elicited comparable cytoprotective effects, reinforcing the notion that autophagy activation—regardless of the upstream pharmacological trigger—confers ischemic resilience to stem cells. Crucially, pharmacological blockade of autophagy using CQ abolished these protective effects and increased apoptosis in preconditioned cells, confirming that intact autophagic flux is required for VPA-mediated cytoprotection.

These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence demonstrating that pharmacologically induced autophagy supports cellular survival under ischemic or oxidative stress. Chen et al. reported that VPA attenuates traumatic brain injury (TBI) by enhancing autophagy and activating the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant axis, thereby reducing neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation14. Similarly, Tribble et al. showed that VPA modulates glial activation and protects retinal ganglion cells via autophagy induction in an optic nerve axotomy model37. Comparable observations were reported for RAPA, where Li et al. demonstrated that short-term RAPA exposure in human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) inhibited caspase-3 activation and enhanced cell survival under OGD38. Likewise, autophagy activation has been shown to protect mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) against apoptosis triggered by hypoxia/serum deprivation or tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), highlighting the conserved cytoprotective role of autophagy across different stem cell populations39,40. VPA preconditioning may further enhance HFSCs survival in vivo by autophagy-dependent mitophagy, facilitating the clearance of ROS-damaged mitochondria and limiting pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Consistent with our in vitro results, several in vivo studies have established that autophagy activation promotes stem cell persistence and functionality under ischemic conditions. Li et al. demonstrated that RAPA-induced autophagy improved MSC survival and differentiation in infarcted myocardium41, while Sun et al. reported that VX-765 activated autophagy via AMPK/mTOR in human umbilical MSCs, protecting them from stroke-induced apoptosis and inflammation42. Similarly, Lv et al. showed that HIF-1α overexpression enhanced bone MSC survival in a rat MCAO stroke model through autophagy activation43. Together, these findings strengthen the concept that autophagy constitutes a central mechanism of stem cell resilience in ischemic environments and corroborate our observation that VPA-induced autophagy enhances HFSC survival under OGD stress.

Beyond cytoprotection, VPA preconditioning also conferred a marked enhancement of HFSC migratory capacity under OGD stress, as shown by the scratch wound assay (Fig. 6). This migration-promoting effect was comparable to that observed with RAPA and was significantly diminished following autophagy inhibition with CQ, further emphasizing the functional role of autophagic flux in regulating cell motility. Interestingly, co-activation of autophagy through combined VPA and RAPA preconditioning produced the greatest improvement in migration, reaching nearly a 35% increase compared with control, surpassing the effects of either agent alone. Although the role of autophagy in migration appears to be context-dependent—sometimes suppressive in malignant cells44—our findings align with reports suggesting that autophagy supports stem cell migration by facilitating cytoskeletal remodeling, focal adhesion turnover, and metabolic adaptability45. Zheng et al. similarly demonstrated that RAPA-induced autophagy enhanced the migratory ability of mesenchymal stem cells during ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice46. In parallel, VPA has been shown to promote central nervous system homing of bone marrow–derived MSCs via CXCR4 upregulation, suggesting that its migration-promoting actions may involve both autophagy-dependent and chemokine-mediated mechanisms47.

In conclusion, this study provides the first mechanistic evidence that VPA preconditioning enhances survival and migration of HFSCs under OGD via autophagy activation and AKT/mTOR suppression, highlighting its potential to improve stem cell-based therapies for ischemic stroke. Experiments were conducted using HFSCs from a single donor, representing proof-of-concept that requires validation across multiple donors and in vivo models to ensure translational relevance. While pharmacological and protein-level analyses robustly supported autophagy-mediated cytoprotection, complementary genetic approaches (e.g., ATG5 silencing, AKT overactivation) and assessment of upstream regulators (PTEN/PI3K) could further clarify mechanism. CQ use was optimized to minimize off-target effects, though verification of lysosomal integrity and inclusion of early-stage inhibitors like 3-MA would reinforce interpretation. In addition, although migration assays revealed enhanced motility following VPA preconditioning, integrating time-lapse imaging and EdU incorporation could further elucidate the underlying mechanisms and proliferative dynamics. Importantly, HFSCs—also known as epidermal neural crest–derived stem cells (Epi-NCSCs)—possess intrinsic neurogenic potential and have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in experimental stroke models8,48. Although neural differentiation was not assessed under ischemic conditions in this study, our previous work showed that HFSCs generate β-III-tubulin–, GAD67–, and GFAP-positive cells and improve stroke outcomes8. Given that VPA promotes early neuronal differentiation in various progenitor cell types49, it is plausible that VPA preconditioning may not only enhance HFSC survival via autophagy and AKT/mTOR modulation but also facilitate neuroglial differentiation during recovery, warranting future in vivo validation.
