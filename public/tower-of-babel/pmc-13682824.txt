Current status and challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of psittacosis
Authors: Long Xu, Chi Zhang, Wanwan Shi, Jiaxi Ma, Guiqiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsittacosis is a zoonotic infectious disease caused by the obligate intracellular Gram-negative bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. Despite over a century of research, its global burden remains underestimated owing to non-specific symptoms, poor clinical recognition, and limited diagnostic capacity. This review synthesizes epidemiological evidence from 1879 to 2024, outlining transmission routes, clinical manifestations, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic strategies, with a focus on advances in molecular diagnostics and the management of high-risk populations and complications. Surveillance data document recurrent outbreaks across Europe, North America, and Asia, with a notable increase in reported cases in China and Europe since 2020. The disease spectrum spans from atypical pneumonia to life-threatening systemic complications, including neurological, cardiac, and pregnancy-related involvement. Conventional methods, such as culture and serology, are constrained by biosafety requirements and cross-reactivity, while polymerase chain reaction, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, and targeted next-generation sequencing provide superior sensitivity but face the challenges of cost and standardization. Tetracyclines remain the mainstay of therapy, macrolides are recommended for pregnant women and children, and newer tetracycline derivatives show promise in severe or refractory cases. Psittacosis should be recognized as a systemic disease of both clinical and public health importance. Future efforts must prioritize standardizing molecular diagnostic workflows, validating novel antimicrobials in clinical practice, and incorporating psittacosis surveillance into public health systems while raising awareness, broadening diagnostic access, and optimizing therapeutic strategies to enable early detection, precision treatment, and effective control.

1. Introduction

Psittacosis is a systemic zoonotic disease caused by Chlamydia psittaci, a Gram-negative obligate intracellular bacterium divided into 10 genotypes (A–G, WC, E/B, and M56), among which genotype A predominates in human infections.[1,2] The pathogen replicates within host mononuclear phagocytes as elementary and reticulate bodies, subsequently spreading via the bloodstream to multiple organs. Although the lungs are the main site of infection, extrapulmonary involvement—including the liver, central nervous system, heart, kidneys, joints, and eyes—has been reported, occasionally leading to keratoconjunctivitis or ocular adnexal lymphoma.[3] Human infection usually results from close contact with infected birds or poultry in farms, markets, and veterinary settings. Notably, recent evidence indicates that avian Chlamydia abortus, a novel strain group first identified in wild birds in 2021, is capable of infecting humans may allow human-to-human transmission.[4] However, due to non-specific respiratory manifestations and limited diagnostic capacity in many countries, the global burden of psittacosis remains underestimated.[5,6]

2. Epidemiology of psittacosis

2.1. Overview

Psittacosis was first reported in Europe in 1879, indicating that the outbreak of fatal respiratory diseases in humans could be linked to contact with parrots and finches. In 1893, researchers isolated the pathogen from diseased parrots for the first time, and it was confirmed as the source of infection the following year, named psittacosis.[7] Between 1929 and 1930, psittacosis outbreaks occurred across Europe, Africa, South America, and North America, affecting over 800 individuals and raising significant public concern.[8] In the early 1950s, C. psittaci was detected in poultry farm workers in the United States.[9] From 1975 to 1980, the United Kingdom reported a male-to-female ratio of 3:2 for psittacosis cases, with the majority of patients aged 35–44 years.[10] Between 1988 and 2003, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States reported 935 cases of psittacosis, ranging from asymptomatic infections to severe cases.[5] From 2000 to 2015, the Netherlands reported 700 cases of psittacosis, with infection peaks in the spring and summer months.[11] In Belgium, between 2015 and 2017, 55% of confirmed and suspected cases were men, whose average age was higher than that of women; children under 15 accounted for only 4% of cases.[12] In 2020, a secondary and tertiary human-to-human transmission of psittacosis occurred in a duck processing plant in Shandong Province, China.[13] A multicenter study and regional survey revealed that C. psittaci infection primarily affects older adults, occurs more frequently in winter and spring, and occasionally leads to localized outbreaks. No significant differences were found between genders or regions, and individuals with underlying health conditions tended to develop more severe illness.[14151617] Between January 2021 and June 2024, 148 cases of psittacosis were reported in Guangzhou,[18] China, showing a steady increase over time. Most cases were sporadic, as confirmed by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), and occurred in men aged 40–79 years, with fever and pneumonia as the main clinical features. Seasonal peaks were observed in December and March. Among local cases, 87% originated from rural areas, with 67.7% reporting direct contact with birds or poultry and 17.7% reporting environmental exposure. Parrots were the predominant suspected source in urban areas, whereas poultry predominated in rural settings. During the same period, several European countries noted increasing case numbers[19]: Austria reported 14 cases in 2023 and four in early 2024 (vs. a median of two cases annually in the previous eight years); Denmark reported 23 cases between late 2023 and early 2024, including 17 hospitalizations, 15 pneumonia cases, and four deaths, mostly linked to wild birds or droppings; Germany reported 14 cases in 2023 and five in early 2024, mostly pneumonia and some associated with poultry exposure; Sweden reported 39 cases between November 2023 and February 2024, mainly in the southern regions and often related to droppings from small birds; and the Netherlands reported 21 cases from late December 2023 to February 2024 (mean age 67 years, 16 male), all hospitalized with one death, with some but not all reporting exposure to bird or poultry droppings.

2.2. Source of infection, transmission routes, and biphasic developmental cycle

The primary sources of C. psittaci infection are birds, such as parrots, and poultry, including chickens, ducks, geese, and pigeons. Additionally, wild birds and even mammals, such as horses, cattle, and koalas, may also serve as potential sources of infection.[20] Studies have reported that 467 species of birds from 30 avian orders can be infected with C. psittaci.[21] Although human-to-human transmission is rare, it has been documented. For instance, a 2013 outbreak in Sweden involved one index case linked to wild bird feces and subsequent transmission to 10 individuals, including healthcare workers and family members.[22]

Infection mainly occurs through inhalation of aerosolized particles from contaminated bird excreta or respiratory secretions or through direct contact. Contaminated environments and exposure to feathers, tissues, or secretions can also transmit the pathogen through mucous membranes, broken skin, or ingestion.[23] All age groups are susceptible, and immunity after infection is typically not long lasting, leading to possible reinfections.[24]

C. psittaci is capable of infecting a wide variety of cell types, often resulting in systemic infections. In addition to its broad infection range, C. psittaci undergoes a unique biphasic developmental cycle. During this cycle, the elementary body (EB) and the reticulate body (RB) appear sequentially. The EB is highly infectious and uses surface adhesion factors, such as major outer membrane protein (MOMP), polymorphic membrane proteins, lipopolysaccharide, and protein disulfide isomerase, to interact with the host cell membrane, facilitating entry through phagocytosis or endocytosis.[25,26] Inside host cells, the type III secretion system and Inc proteins facilitate the formation of inclusion bodies in which RBs replicate. Within 48–72 h, RBs convert back into EBs, which then spread to new cells or hosts via cell lysis.[27] Figure 2 illustrates the biphasic developmental cycle of C. psittaci, including the transition from elementary to reticulate bodies, host cell entry by elementary bodies, intracellular replication within inclusion bodies, and the subsequent release of infectious progeny to propagate new cycles of infection.

3. Clinical manifestations

C. psittaci is frequently misdiagnosed not only due to the low sensitivity of current diagnostic methods but also to the non-specific nature of its clinical manifestations. The incubation period for C. psittaci infection typically ranges from 5 to 14 days, but it can be as short as 3 days or as long as 45 days. The infectious period usually begins 7–8 days after symptom onset.[28] While the disease can occur at any age, it is most common in young and middle-aged adults. The clinical manifestations are non-specific, as C. psittaci can infect multiple tissues and organs. The severity of the illness varies, ranging from subclinical infections to fulminant septicemia. Symptoms differ depending on disease severity, the organs involved, and the presence of comorbidities or complications. Common symptoms include sudden chills, fever, sweating, severe headache, and a mild dry cough.[29] Some patients may also experience myalgia, fatigue, sputum production, diarrhea, or sore throat, although a small number may lack respiratory symptoms altogether.[30,31] In certain cases, relative bradycardia may occur, providing a useful diagnostic clue.[32]

After replicating in respiratory epithelial cells and macrophages, C. psittaci can disseminate systemically, leading to severe complications, multi-organ failure, or death in severe cases[13]. Urinary and gastrointestinal infections caused by C. psittaci may result in acute interstitial nephritis, acute renal failure, glomerulonephritis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, and systemic peritonitis.[33] When the nervous system is affected, C. psittaci may cause meningitis, cerebellar ataxia, cranial nerve palsy, transverse myelitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, or status epilepticus.[34] Infections during the second or third trimester of pregnancy can result in life-threatening complications for pregnant women, including respiratory failure, liver dysfunction, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Moreover, C. psittaci can cross the placental barrier, multiply extensively within the placenta, and cause uteroplacental perfusion damage, leading to intrauterine fetal distress or miscarriage. Reports have indicated that C. psittaci infections during pregnancy are associated with a fetal mortality rate of 82.6% and a maternal mortality rate of 8.7%.[35]

In rare cases, psittacosis can lead to complications, such as hemophagocytic syndrome, organizing pneumonia, tracheobronchomalacia, or rhabdomyolysis.[363738] Fulminant psittacosis may manifest as rapidly progressing severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, or multi-organ failure.[39] Moreover, C. psittaci infections have been associated with reactive arthritis and lymphoma.

4. Laboratory diagnostics: Challenges and translational perspectives

4.1. Current limitations in the clinical application of diagnostic techniques

The clinical manifestations of psittacosis are predominantly respiratory but are non-specific and lack characteristic features, making delayed or missed diagnoses common. This may result in treatment delays and, in severe cases, life-threatening complications.[404142] Routine laboratory examinations often reveal normal or mildly elevated leukocyte counts, neutrophilia, and lymphopenia. Inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, are frequently elevated, often accompanied by increased lactate dehydrogenase and creatine kinase levels, hypoalbuminemia, elevated transaminases, hyponatremia, and hypoxemia.[40] Severe cases may progress to respiratory failure, underscoring the potential life-threatening nature of the disease.[41,42] Nevertheless, these laboratory findings lack specificity and cannot serve as standalone diagnostic criteria.

Regarding imaging examinations, chest CT (Computed Tomography) or X-ray typically reveals segmental or lobar consolidation, with some cases involving multiple lobes or even both lungs, and the extent of involvement correlating positively with disease severity.[43] However, as these imaging findings resemble those of other community-acquired pneumonias and lack distinguishing characteristics, they may only provide adjunctive value and cannot serve as definitive diagnostic criteria.[44,45]

Pathogen isolation and culture are regarded as the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, offering the most direct diagnostic evidence. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), sputum, and throat swabs are commonly used specimen types, which generally yield relatively high positivity rates.[46] However, the culture of C. psittaci is time consuming, typically requiring several days to weeks, and must be performed in a biosafety level 3 laboratory to minimize the risk of laboratory-acquired infections.[42] Thus, although the diagnostic accuracy of culture remains unsurpassed, its clinical utility is limited by technical complexity, biosafety risks, and prolonged turnaround times.

Serological testing remains the most frequently employed specific laboratory method, but its clinical utility is constrained by several factors. First, C. psittaci cross-reacts antigenically with other members of the genus, such as Chlamydia trachomatis and Chlamydia pneumoniae, thereby limiting specificity. Second, prior antibiotic therapy may delay or attenuate antibody responses, compromising diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy.[47] In most cases, a single serum specimen is insufficient for confirmation; instead, paired sera from the acute and convalescent phases must be compared. A ≥4-fold rise in antibody titers, in conjunction with clinical manifestations, is considered diagnostic.[27,39] As a result, serological testing fails to meet the requirements for early diagnosis.

The most commonly employed serological methods include the complement fixation test (CF), micro-immunofluorescence (MIF), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).[46,48] Among these, MIF demonstrates superior sensitivity and specificity compared with CF and is internationally recognized as the standard serological method for chlamydia diagnosis. ELISA is convenient and easy to perform, but it suffers from limited specificity due to antigen cross-reactivity. To minimize false positives, researchers have developed monoclonal antibody-based modified ELISA kits. For example, MOMP-ELISA, which uses MOMP as an antigen, and polymorphic membrane protein D (PmpD)-NELISA, which utilizes the N-terminal fragment of PmpD, achieved remarkably high sensitivity (97.9%), specificity (100%), and accuracy (98.1%) in detecting C. psittaci, indicating substantial clinical potential.[49] Moreover, Pmp20G-NELISA, based on polymorphic membrane protein 20G, has also shown promising diagnostic performance, but clinical validation remains insufficient and requires further investigation.[50] Overall, serological testing is more valuable for retrospective epidemiological investigations during outbreaks than for real-time diagnosis.[51]

4.2. Translational barriers to emerging technologies

Advances in molecular biology have substantially advanced diagnostic capabilities for psittacosis. Among these, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has become a widely used diagnostic tool in clinical practice because of its rapid turnaround, high sensitivity, and relatively low cost. PCR can target multiple specific gene fragments of C. psittaci, including 16S rRNA, 23S rRNA, ompA, and the inner membrane protein gene.[7] With regard to specimen selection, BALF and sputum yield the highest positivity rates and are regarded as the optimal samples. By contrast, blood and throat swabs are commonly used in clinical settings due to ease of collection but show relatively lower sensitivity.[46] Nevertheless, PCR performance remains constrained by sample quality and pathogen burden, and false-negative results are common in mild or early infections due to low bacterial loads.

To overcome the limitations of conventional PCR, several novel molecular diagnostic approaches have been developed in recent years. Probe-based recombinase polymerase amplification, quantitative competitive PCR with quenched probes, and droplet digital PCR have all demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional PCR. Nonetheless, these methods remain in the early stages of clinical implementation, with no commercially available reagent kits or standardized operating protocols, and thus require further validation.[525354] Studies have also suggested that integrating serological testing with PCR can markedly increase the early detection rate of C. psittaci, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.[55]

mNGS has emerged as a novel molecular diagnostic approach and has seen increasing application in the detection of psittacosis in recent years. Its principal advantages lie in its high throughput and broad coverage, comprehensive pathogen profiling of clinical samples, and rapid identification of rare or difficult-to-detect pathogens.[56,57] Evidence has shown that mNGS offers superior sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing rare pathogens.[585960] Since 2019, it has been used for the diagnosis of C. psittaci infections, particularly valuable for patients with atypical symptoms, enabling earlier diagnosis, shortening the disease course, and improving therapeutic outcomes.[616263] BALF is regarded as the optimal specimen, although sputum, blood, and throat swabs may serve as alternatives when bronchoscopy is not feasible.[58] By sequencing the ompA gene and integrating nucleic acid sequence data with microbial community and clinical information, mNGS can deliver results within 48–72 h, which is considerably faster than the 5–7 days required for conventional culture.[4,64,65] Nevertheless, its widespread adoption in routine clinical laboratories remains constrained by high costs, the need for specialized equipment and expertise, and the absence of standardized bioinformatics workflows.[58] Despite these limitations, ongoing advances in automation and miniaturization, together with declining costs, suggest that mNGS continues to hold significant promise for clinical application.[55,66]

Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) integrates multiplex PCR amplification with high-throughput sequencing, enabling simultaneous pathogen identification and detection of resistance and virulence genes within a single assay. Unlike mNGS, tNGS targets only specific genomic regions, thereby lowering analytical complexity and costs while enhancing sensitivity and specificity.[67] Across sample types such as BALF, sputum, blood, and throat swabs, tNGS has demonstrated high stability and reliability and is thus regarded as more clinically feasible.[68,69] Nevertheless, clinical evidence for tNGS remains limited, and its effectiveness and broader applicability require further validation through large-scale multicenter studies.

In addition, a recent study[70] reported the ERA/Cas12f1_ge4.1 diagnostic platform, which integrates CRISPR-based detection with enzymatic recombinase amplification, enabling C. psittaci identification within 50 minutes using either fluorescence or lateral flow readouts. The assay demonstrated detection limits of 10 copies/μL (fluorescence) and 100 copies/μL (lateral flow). Veterinary validation in 93 parrot samples achieved a sensitivity of ≥ 92% and a specificity of 100%. These findings underscore the promise of CRISPR–isothermal amplification assays for point-of-care applications. However, clinical validation in humans and the development of standardized protocols remain essential before widespread clinical adoption.

4.3. Strategies to enhance accessibility

Despite the diversity of available diagnostic methods, limited accessibility remains a challenge in clinical practice, necessitating improvements across multiple dimensions. First, the development of point-of-care rapid testing platforms is critical for shortening diagnostic turnaround and facilitating timely intervention. For example, portable PCR devices and isothermal amplification technologies can markedly reduce the interval from sampling to result reporting, thereby supporting rapid clinical decision-making in critically ill patients.

Second, efforts should be made to standardize and integrate mNGS into routine practice. Centralized reagent procurement, the establishment of cloud-based or automated bioinformatics pipelines, and the implementation of standardized interpretation criteria could collectively reduce testing costs while improving inter-laboratory comparability.[58]

Third, the development of a tiered diagnostic strategy is particularly important. For suspected cases, serological testing and PCR may be prioritized for rapid screening, while mNGS or tNGS should be reserved for diagnostically challenging cases or those with negative results from conventional methods, thus achieving a rational balance among speed, accuracy, and cost.[55]

Finally, large-scale clinical validation of emerging diagnostic approaches should be conducted through multicenter collaborative networks, accompanied by the establishment of external quality control systems and the evaluation of diagnostic performance in relation to treatment outcomes. These measures will not only generate robust evidence but also accelerate the efficient translation of laboratory advances into clinical practice.[55,66]

5. Therapy

5.1. Symptomatic supportive therapy

Supportive therapy in psittacosis is not only an adjunctive measure but also a critical determinant of clinical outcomes, particularly in patients with severe disease or complications. A quiet, clean, and well-ventilated environment, together with adequate rest, reduces metabolic demands and mitigates the risk of secondary respiratory impairment. Nutritional support with easily digestible, nutrient-dense food supplies energy for immune response, while sufficient fluid intake helps maintain electrolyte balance and renal perfusion, thereby lowering the risk of acute kidney injury and multi-organ dysfunction.[71]

Meticulous oral and skin care reduces the likelihood of secondary bacterial or fungal infections, which often mask the underlying disease course and worsen the prognosis. In critically ill patients, continuous monitoring of consciousness, respiratory function, hemodynamic parameters, and urine output is essential for the early detection of severe complications, such as septic shock, acute respiratory failure, or acute kidney injury. These measures underscore the vital role of supportive therapy in maintaining physiological stability, augmenting the efficacy of antimicrobial agents, and reducing the need for advanced interventions, such as mechanical ventilation or renal replacement therapy.[727374]

The effectiveness of supportive therapy relies heavily on the timely recognition of disease severity and individualized management. Although these strategies do not directly target C. psittaci, their contribution to enhancing host resilience and preventing secondary complications establishes them as a cornerstone of comprehensive psittacosis management rather than a passive adjunct.[71]

5.2. Targeted antimicrobial drug therapy

Tetracycline antibiotics remain the foundation of psittacosis treatment, with doxycycline being the preferred agent due to its excellent intracellular penetration and inhibition of protein synthesis, which aligns well with the pathogen’s intracellular lifestyle. Its clinical efficacy is well established, with more than 90% of patients achieving defervescence within 48 h of treatment, underscoring its therapeutic superiority.[30] In severe cases, newer tetracycline derivatives, such as tigecycline and omadacycline, are reasonable alternatives. Tigecycline provides broad-spectrum antibacterial activity suitable for critical infections, while omadacycline achieves stable distribution in lung tissue and requires no dose adjustment in patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction or in the elderly, making it particularly valuable for vulnerable populations.[75]

The availability of alternative agents highlights both the diversity and limitations of therapeutic choices. Macrolides are considered safe and effective in populations where tetracyclines are contraindicated, such as pregnant women, and they also provide clinical utility in children under eight years of age.[46] By contrast, fluoroquinolones show limited efficacy and delayed defervescence; their pharmacodynamic properties restrict their effectiveness against C. psittaci, confining their use to salvage therapy or combination regimens.[59767778]

The use of glucocorticoids underscores the uncertainty of current therapeutic approaches. Observational studies have suggested that they may attenuate excessive inflammatory responses in severe cases and improve outcomes, but their use remains empirical rather than evidence based in the absence of standardized protocols and given the risk of secondary infections.[71] Further mechanistic research is needed to clarify their immunomodulatory role in atypical pneumonia.[727374]

The management of special populations shows the discrepancy between theoretical efficacy and clinical feasibility. In pregnancy, macrolides are the mainstay because of the contraindication of tetracyclines, and case reports have suggested that azithromycin combined with tigecycline may improve maternal outcomes without increasing fetal risk, although a systematic evaluation is warranted.[71] In children, concerns over bone and dental toxicity limit tetracycline use, leaving macrolides as the principal alternative.[46] Elderly patients with reduced immunity and substantial comorbidity burdens are over three times more likely to develop severe pneumonia than younger adults; tetracyclines remain the first-line option, while fluoroquinolones are inadequate for frontline therapy.[79] In immunocompromised individuals, treatment is particularly challenging, often necessitating prolonged courses and close monitoring to prevent relapse, thus underscoring the limitations of uniform treatment strategies.[34]

Antimicrobial resistance adds further complexity to management. Although tetracycline resistance in C. psittaci remains rare, sporadic clinical cases and in vitro studies indicate emerging risks. Resistance compromises the broad applicability of traditional tetracyclines and highlights the value of stabilized formulations (e.g., anhydrous tetracycline) and newer agents (e.g., omadacycline).[80] Moving forward, therapy should transition from empirical regimens toward precision treatment informed by molecular diagnostics and resistance surveillance.

5.3. Treatment of complications

The development of complications highlights the systemic pathogenicity of C. psittaci and the limitations of standard therapeutic regimens. Meningitis and endocarditis are representative examples that are often refractory to empirical therapy and require metagenomic sequencing for early etiological confirmation. The effective treatment of meningitis typically necessitates combination antibiotic therapy to overcome the poor CNS (Central Nervous System) penetration of single agents and to mitigate the high mortality associated with delayed recognition.[81] Endocarditis carries a mortality rate approaching 50%, and antimicrobial therapy alone is often insufficient. In patients with vegetations larger than 1 cm, early surgical intervention can markedly reduce the risk of distal embolism and infarction.[82,83]

Rhabdomyolysis and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) further illustrate systemic injury resulting from uncontrolled infection. Rhabdomyolysis, more common in patients with diabetes or hyperglycemia, is associated with mortality rates approaching 50%. The early application of the McMahon score is essential to assess the risk of acute kidney injury, and the prompt initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy is recommended when indicated.[79,84,85] Although rare, MODS signifies failure of infection control, often presenting with respiratory failure, DIC, and shock in which aggressive antimicrobial combinations must be administered alongside intensive supportive care.[86,87]

The management of psittacosis complications emphasizes two key principles. First, psittacosis should not be regarded solely as a localized pulmonary infection but as a systemic disease with potential multi-organ involvement. Second, optimal outcomes depend on early molecular diagnosis, aggressive antimicrobial therapy, and, when necessary, the integration of supportive care, surgical intervention, and critical care within a multidisciplinary framework.[34,71]

6. Summary and outlook

As a zoonotic disease, psittacosis is characterized by non-specific clinical manifestations that are often mistaken for other respiratory infections or systemic disorders, leading to delayed or missed diagnoses. Some patients initially present with only mild upper respiratory symptoms, but disease progression can be rapid, resulting in a missed window for optimal diagnosis and treatment. Despite continuous advances in diagnostic technology, significant challenges remain. Serological testing fails to meet the requirements of early diagnosis due to cross-reactivity and delayed antibody responses.[2841464748] Although molecular methods, such as PCR, are highly sensitive, their clinical performance depends heavily on sample quality and standardized procedures, with a considerable risk of false-negative results.[746525354] mNGS and tNGS offer greater sensitivity and broader pathogen coverage, but their translation into routine clinical practice remains constrained by high costs, a lack of standardized interpretation criteria, and reliance on specialized technical expertise, limiting their applicability in primary and routine laboratories.[55,56,58,66] Therefore, future research should not only focus on improving the performance metrics of novel technologies but also address the standardization of sample preprocessing, the normalization of testing workflows, and issues of affordability to achieve true clinical feasibility for early diagnosis.

In terms of treatment, tetracyclines (e.g., doxycycline) remain the first-line option, and most patients experience rapid symptom relief following therapy.[15,30] However, the emergence of antimicrobial resistance and the presence of contraindications in certain populations have made treatment increasingly challenging.[75,80] In particular, therapeutic choices are restricted in pregnant women, children, and patients with hepatic or renal impairment, requiring clinicians to carefully balance efficacy and safety.[46,59,76,79] Macrolides and newer tetracycline derivatives (e.g., omadacycline) have demonstrated promising activity in in vitro studies and early clinical trials,[75] but their clinical use remains unsupported by large-scale evidence-based studies. In addition, glucocorticoids may serve as adjunctive therapy for severe cases,[727374] although there are no standardized recommendations regarding the timing, dosage, or duration of treatment. The central challenge for future therapeutic research is in validating laboratory susceptibility data and novel drug efficacy through multicenter clinical trials and translating these findings into individualized treatment strategies tailored to different patient populations and disease stages.

From a public health perspective, significant gaps remain in the prevention and control of psittacosis. In certain countries and regions, awareness of the disease remains limited, particularly within the poultry farming and pet trade industries, in which the absence of standardized health management and surveillance measures leads to delays in the identification and reporting of potential cases, thereby increasing the risk of human transmission. This not only undermines epidemiological surveillance and disease control but also poses a risk of outbreaks. A major challenge for future prevention and control efforts lies in accelerating the integration of laboratory-confirmed case data into public health systems and in establishing a rapid diagnostic network that extends to primary healthcare institutions. Large-scale nationwide tNGS data (2022–2024)[88] demonstrate that the true burden of psittacosis in China is likely underestimated, underscoring the need to incorporate psittacosis into the notifiable infectious disease system.

Overall, although considerable clinical experience has been gained in the diagnosis and treatment of psittacosis, the absence of large-scale clinical studies and standardized treatment guidelines has resulted in substantial heterogeneity in clinical practice across regions and healthcare institutions. Future research should prioritize three key areas: first, advancing the standardization and broader clinical adoption of molecular diagnostic technologies, particularly mNGS and tNGS, to facilitate their translation from research findings into routine clinical practice; second, strengthening investigations into resistance mechanisms and the clinical efficacy of novel antibiotics, with multicenter clinical trials aimed at developing evidence-based, individualized treatment strategies for special populations; and third, establishing a multidisciplinary collaborative framework that integrates laboratory research, clinical trials, and public health surveillance to accelerate the translation of basic research into clinical application. By optimizing diagnostic approaches, updating therapeutic strategies, and strengthening prevention and control systems, psittacosis can be more effectively detected early, treated precisely, and prevented.
