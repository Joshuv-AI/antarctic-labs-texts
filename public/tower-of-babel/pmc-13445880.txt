Health behavior risk prediction in metabolic syndrome patients: development and validation of an interpretable machine learning model via multisource heterogeneous data integration
Authors: Zhihong Ni, Xin Deng, Keyi Li, Xinxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MetS) represents a major public health challenge in rural populations, particularly in resource-limited regions such as southern Xinjiang, China. Unhealthy behaviors serve as key modifiable drivers of MetS progression; however, existing models fail to integrate psychosocial determinants within a comprehensive social ecological framework. Interpretable machine learning (ML) presents an opportunity to address this gap by identifying potentially modifiable predictive factors through multisource data integration.ObjectiveTo develop and validate an interpretable ML model that integrates biomedical, psychological, and social data for predicting health behavior risk in MetS patients and exploring candidate predictive factors for future intervention research.MethodsIn a cross-sectional study of 906 MetS patients from southern Xinjiang, China, six core predictors were sequentially identified through Lasso regression and subsequent logistic regression. Eight ML models were evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, Brier score, and decision curve analysis (DCA), with interpretability enhanced by SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsXGBoost achieved optimal performance (AUC = 0.852, sensitivity = 0.865, specificity = 0.908). DCA confirmed clinical utility across threshold probabilities. SHAP analysis demonstrated that perceived stress ranked as the top predictor for model output, with perceived stress, self-efficacy, and social support jointly forming a core psychosocial cluster associated with health behavior risk, showing greater predictive importance than biomedical indicators. A statistical inflection range of TC (4.0-5.2mmol/L) was suggestively linked to dual metabolic-neurobehavioral stabilization.ConclusionsThis validated interpretable ML model highlights psychosocial factors as central to health behavior risk in MetS patients. The identified thresholds provide data support for potential precision-based interventions and offer insights for chronic disease management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03600-3.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Health behavior, Machine learning, Interpretable artificial intelligence, Risk prediction, Precision public health

Metabolic Syndrome (MetS), characterized by abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia [1], is a critical driver of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality [2]. Its prevalence exhibits marked age dependence and geographic variability: 41.8% of US adults are affected, escalating to 60.9% among individuals aged ≥ 60 years [3], while China reports 31.1% overall prevalence rising to 36.9% in elderly populations [4, 5]. This accelerating global burden necessitates a paradigm shift toward data-driven precision prevention through digital health technologies.

Effective MetS management depends on modifying core health behaviors, including physical activity, nutrition, and stress management [6, 7]. However, these behaviors do not occur in isolation. They arise from a complex interplay of biomedical, psychological, cognitive, and socioecological factors, as described in theoretical frameworks such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) [8–10]. This multifactorial nature leads to substantial interindividual heterogeneity, which limits the effectiveness of conventional one-size-fits-all interventions due to poor adherence and low sustainability [11]. In current practice, risk assessment relies primarily on traditional parametric models such as logistic regression. These models are constrained by their inability to capture nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional interactions inherent in integrated data sources, thereby impeding the development of practical digital decision-support tools capable of delivering individualized predictions [12].

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful approach for health behavior prediction, demonstrating superior performance in processing large-scale, high-dimensional, and heterogeneous data [13]. For example, Sirapangi and Gopikrishnan [12] developed a Medical Internet-of-Things (MIoT)-based framework that integrates physiological, behavioral, and environmental data from wearable sensors to enable real-time forecasting. Similarly, Aguilar et al. [14] applied ensemble learning to multi-omics data from over 488,000 UK Biobank participants, showing that multimodal integration improves latent risk detection. Notably, metabolomic features contributed modestly to model performance, highlighting the importance of evaluating the incremental value of each data domain.

Despite these advances, significant translational gaps remain. Most ML applications focus on single behaviors or general populations, with limited attention to chronic disease cohorts like MetS patients. Crucially, no prior study has comprehensively integrated five essential data domains: clinical biomarkers, genetic and demographic traits, cognitive function, psychological parameters, and socioecological determinants into a validated, interpretable risk prediction model. This fragmentation hinders accurate identification of high-risk individuals and obscures potentially modifiable predictive factors during critical windows for intervention.

To address this unmet need, we developed an interpretable machine learning model grounded in socioecological theory. We integrated five heterogeneous data domains: clinical parameters (waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose, lipids), genetic/phenotypic traits (age, sex, family history), health literacy indicators (education, disease knowledge), psychosocial factors (self-efficacy, perceived stress), and socioecological determinants (income, residential environment, social support). Key predictors were selected using Lasso regression, and multivariable logistic regression was used to assess independent contributions. Eight ML algorithms, including XGBoost, SVM, and LightGBM, were trained and compared. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were employed to quantify feature-specific contributions and enhance model interpretability. The resulting decision-support tool combines high predictive accuracy with clinical transparency, enabling precise identification of at-risk individuals and exploration of modifiable predictive factors. By advancing individualized health management paradigms, this study provides methodological and practical foundations for future behavioral intervention research in MetS patients.

Study population and methods

Study population

A total of 906 MetS patients were identified from the 2022 regional universal health examination database in southern Xinjiang, China, based on predefined eligibility criteria. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Medical College of Shihezi University (Approval No. KJX-2021-028-01), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria required: diagnosis of MetS according to the 2009 Joint Interim Statement (JIS) [1] criteria; age ≥ 18 years; permanent residency in the region for at least six months; preserved cognitive capacity for independent questionnaire completion; and voluntary participation with documented informed consent.

Exclusion criteria comprised severe comorbidities including malignancies or decompensated heart failure; history of cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorders, or sensory deficits (hearing or visual) impairing communication; or pregnancy at enrollment.

Diagnostic criteria for MetS were defined per the 2009 JIS harmonized criteria, with diagnosis established when three or more of the following components were present: elevated waist circumference (WC; ≥85 cm in men or ≥ 80 cm in women); hypertriglyceridemia (serum triglycerides, TG; ≥1.70 mmol/L or current lipid-lowering therapy); low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; <1.00 mmol/L in men or < 1.30 mmol/L in women); elevated blood pressure (systolic ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 85 mmHg, or ongoing antihypertensive treatment); elevated fasting plasma glucose (FBG; ≥5.6 mmol/L or current treatment for type 2 diabetes) [1].

Study indicators

General information questionnaire

A structured questionnaire collected demographic characteristics (sex, age, education level, marital status, occupation, place of residence, living status, family monthly income per capita, payment method of medical expenses, annual hospitalizations) and disease-related information (comorbidities, family history of cardiometabolic disorders).

Clinical biomarkers

Clinical biomarkers were categorized into:

Physical examination parameters: weight (W), waist circumference (WC), body roundness index (BRI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Biochemical indicators: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride-glucose index (TyG).

Knowledge of MetS scale (K-MetS)[15]

Developed by See et al. evaluates knowledge across three domains: disease definition, health consequences, and preventive strategies. This 10-item instrument uses a dichotomous scoring system (correct response = 10 points; total score range 0–100), with higher scores indicating greater knowledge of MetS. The original Cronbach’s α was 0.690; in this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.457, KMO = 0.681, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001, suggesting acceptable sampling adequacy but limited item homogeneity.

Self-efficacy scale for chronic illness (SSC)[16]

Developed by Lorig et al. comprises six items measuring two dimensions: symptom management efficacy and disease-specific self-efficacy. Responses are rated on a 10-point Likert scale (total score 6–60), with higher scores reflecting stronger perceived self-efficacy. Original Cronbach’s was 0.910; in this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.949, KMO = 0.933, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001).

Self-management behavior scale for MetS patients(SMB-MetS)[17]

Developed by Ni Zhihong, the 36-item SMB-MetS evaluates behaviors in seven domains: dietary habits, physical activity, other lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep quality, sedentary behavior), medication adherence, disease self-monitoring, emotional regulation, and communication with healthcare providers. Using a 5-point Likert scale (total score 36–180), higher scores indicate better health behaviors. Original Cronbach’s α was 0.870; in this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.859, KMO = 0.945, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001.

Chinese perceived stress scale (CPSS)[18]

Adapted by Yang Tingzhong et al. contains 14 items assessing tension and loss of control. Responses are scored on a 5-point Likert scale (total score 14–70), with higher scores indicating greater psychological stress. Original Cronbach’s α was 0.780; in this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.880, KMO = 0.906, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001.

Social support rating scale (SSRS)[19]

Developed by Xiao Shuiyuan, measures three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and support utilization through 10 iteMetS. Using cumulative positive scoring (total score 12–66), higher scores reflect greater perceived social support. Original Cronbach’s α was 0.90; in this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.830, KMO = 0.843, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001.

Data collection procedures: Clinical biomarkers were measured by trained nurses at local hospitals following standardized operating procedures, with fasting venous blood samples collected after ≥ 8 h overnight fast. All other data were collected in person by researchers and 10 uniformly trained chronic disease managers from community health service centers.

Data processing and analysis

Data preprocessing

Data quality was ensured through double data entry with independent verification of discrepancies, and random cross-checking of 5% entries. The complete dataset (n = 906) was randomly split into a training set (70%, n = 634) and an independent test set (30%, n = 272) using the createDataPartition function with set.seed = 1234 to ensure reproducibility. All subsequent data preprocessing, feature selection, and model training were performed exclusively on the training set; the test set was not involved in any training-related steps to avoid data leakage. Continuous variables were retained in their original units without normalization to preserve clinical interpretability and avoid distorting clinical meaning for SHAP-based interpretability analysis. The raw dataset underwent systematic quality assessment to identify missing values and outlier distributions. Missing data were imputed using random forest imputation, while outliers were detected and removed based on box plots and the 3-standard deviation rule. To construct a clinically interpretable binary prediction model, the total health behavior score (continuous variable) was converted into a binary outcome. Kernel density estimation (KDE) was used to examine the distribution of health behavior scores in 906 patients. KDE results showed a distinct trough at the x-coordinate of 102 points, indicating a natural boundary between subgroups with high and low behavioral capacity. Additionally, the 65th percentile of the scores was 108 points, and A sharp decline in density was observed within the 108–116 score range in the KDE curve, which further verified the objectivity of selecting 108 points as the cutoff value (Multimedia Appendix 1). Notably, this cutoff is consistent with the behavioral capacity stratification thresholds reported in previous studies [17]. Accordingly, scores < 108 points were classified as the poor health behavior group, and scores ≥ 108 points as the good health behavior group. Using this binary variable as the dependent variable, 28 independent variables constructed based on the socioecological theory model (Multimedia Appendix 2) were incorporated.

Feature selection

Lasso regression (alpha = 1) was performed exclusively on the training set using the glmnet package to mitigate multicollinearity and select optimal predictive features. The optimal penalty parameter λ (lambda.min) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation on the training set, and variables with non-zero coefficients in the optimal Lasso model were retained as candidate features. A total of 13 features with non-zero coefficients were retained and further analyzed by multivariable logistic regression, yielding 6 significant variables (P < 0.05) for the final model. Stability was validated using 10 random data splits, confirming that the 6-variable set could be stably reproduced and was not driven by a single random partition. This sequential approach focused on linearly independent predictors to achieve a balance between prediction performance and clinical interpretability.

Model development and validation

Eight predictive models for assessing health behavior risks in MetS patients were constructed using ML algorithms in R4.4.2 software: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Adaptive Boosting (ADA), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, XGB), Lightweight Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM, LGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost, CATB), and Neural Network (NNET). To optimize model performance, grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation was employed for systematic tuning of key hyperparameters across all models, which was performed exclusively on the training set. For linear models like logistic regression, L1 regularization (Lasso) was applied to reduce model complexity. The optimal penalty coefficient λ was determined through cross-validation; as log(λ) increases, regression coefficients gradually shrink to zero, enabling automatic feature selection and effectively preventing overfitting. All model training and parameter optimization were performed using the train() function from the caret package, balancing overfitting control with the capture of potential feature interactions (Multimedia Appendix 3). Model performance evaluation was conducted on both the training set and the independent test set. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated across three dimensions: discriminatory power, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination metrics included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). ROC curves for both training and testing sets visually demonstrated model discrimination capabilities. Calibration curves assessed consistency between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) evaluated the model’s net benefit at different threshold probabilities, thereby measuring its practical value in clinical practice.

SHAP model interpretation

The “shapviz” package in R4.4.2 was employed to conduct interpretability analysis on the ML model’s predictions, enabling visual interpretation of the health behavior risk prediction model based on SHAP values. SHAP values quantify each feature’s contribution to the model output for an individual sample prediction: positive values indicate the feature drives the model toward predicting “unhealthy behaviors” (positive impact on risk), while negative values indicate a protective effect, reducing the predicted risk probability (negative influence). Feature importance is ranked by the average |SHAP| value (i.e., the mean of the absolute SHAP values) for each feature and presented as a bar chart, reflecting the overall influence of each variable on model decisions across the entire dataset. To further reveal the model’s decision-making mechanism for individual predictions, SHAP plots were generated for two representative samples, visually illustrating how features synergistically influence the final prediction outcome and enabling interpretable analysis of individual prediction pathways.

Statistical analysis methods

Data analysis was performed using R4.4.2. The “tidyverse” package was employed for data cleaning and organization, the “pROC” package was used to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculate area under the curve (AUC), the “CBCgrps” package was utilized for subgroup analysis, and the “rms” and “rmda” packages were used for constructing regression models and decision curve analysis (DCA), respectively. All continuous variables underwent normality testing: those meeting normality were expressed as mean ± standard deviation(±s), while non-normally distributed variables were described using median (interquartile range, P25-P75). Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage [n (%)]. Intergroup comparisons employed tests selected based on variable type and distribution characteristics: two-sample t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and χ² tests for categorical variables (replaced by Fisher’s exact test when theoretical frequency < 5). All tests were two-tailed with a significance level of α = 0.05; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A detailed flowchart of the entire analytical procedure is presented in Multimedia Appendix 4.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of all patients between the training and validation cohorts

The study included 906 MetS patients with a mean age of 53.50 ± 10.65 years; 457 (50.4%) were female. Mean body weight was 74.93 ± 13.52 kg, with TC at 4.86 ± 1.18 mmol/L. The health behavior score averaged 102.03 ± 22.60 points (range: 36–177). Among them, 583 patients (64.35%) scored below the 60% threshold (< 108 points), classified as poor health behaviors. No significant differences were observed in baseline variables including demographic characteristics, clinical indicators, and health behavior scores (P > 0.05), indicating good comparability between groups. Detailed baseline data are presented in Multimedia Appendix 5.

Lasso regression for variable selection

The 28 candidate variables were incorporated into Lasso regression analysis, and the model achieved minimal error and optimal performance when λmin was set to 0.011. A total of 13 predictor variables had non-zero coefficients, among which living status had the highest coefficient at 0.392, followed by TC, perceived stress, family monthly income per capita, gender, annual hospitalizations, place of residence, social support, self-efficacy, W, knowledge of MetS, DBP, and SBP (Fig. 1).

These 13 variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to further evaluate their contributions. The results indicated that W, TC, knowledge of MetS, self-efficacy, perceived stress, and social support were statistically significant predictors of health behaviors in MetS patients (P < 0.05; Multimedia Appendix 6). Specifically, TC, knowledge of MetS, self-efficacy, and social support served as protective factors for good health behavior, whereas W and perceived stress were identified as risk factors for poor health behaviors. The Stability results showed that the 6 core features were consistently retained in more than 80% of the splits, indicating that the selected features were stable and not affected by random data splitting.

Model performance evaluation

The six finalized key predictor variables were incorporated into LR, SVM, GBM, NNET, XGB, ADA, LGB, and CATB models, with ROC curves plotted (Fig. 2A, B). Performance metrics for the eight models are summarized in Table 1. Comprehensive evaluation revealed that the XGB model significantly outperformed other models in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. On the training and test sets, the XGB model achieved AUC values of 0.889 (95% CI: 0.864–0.915) and 0.852 (95% CI: 0.808–0.906), respectively, while demonstrating excellent sensitivity (training set: 0.881; test set: 0.865), specificity (training set: 0.916; test set: 0.908), F1 score (training set: 0.804; test set: 0.813), accuracy (training set: 0.775; test set: 0.781), and precision (training set: 0.716; test set: 0.736), indicating stable generalization capability and clinical applicability. In calibration assessment, the XGB model’s calibration curve most closely approximated the ideal diagonal (Fig. 2C, D), exhibiting the lowest Brier score (training set: 0.133; test set: 0.154), confirming high consistency between predicted probabilities and actual observations. Decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that compared to other models, the XGB model yielded greater net benefit across the threshold probability range of 2% to 85% (Fig. 2E, F).

SHAP methodology explores the interpretability of the optimal model

The SHAP algorithm was applied to conduct an interpretability analysis of the optimal predictive model XGB. Figure 3A illustrates the direction and strength of each feature’s contribution to individual predictions, where each point represents a sample and the x-axis denotes the SHAP value for that feature. Higher SHAP values indicate greater risk of poor health behaviors for that individual. Results show that self-efficacy, social support, knowledge of MetS, and TC levels are associated with lower predicted risk health behaviors (SHAP < 0), while perceived stress and W are associated with higher predicted risk of poor health behavior (SHAP > 0). Figure 3B presents the average absolute SHAP values for each feature, assessing their overall influence on model output. Results indicate that perceived stress most significantly impacts predictions, followed by self-efficacy, social support, TC levels, knowledge of MetS, and W. Nonlinear relationships between key features and predicted risk were further explored using SHAP dependency plots (Fig. 3C-G). SHAP values turned positive under the following conditions, indicating the XGB model’s tendency to predict poor health behaviors: weight > 75 kg, Perceived Stress Scale score > 45, TC level < 3.75 mmol/L, knowledge of MetS score < 30, self-efficacy score < 40, and social support score < 32.

To visually illustrate the decision-making mechanism of the XGB model for individual patients, this study utilized SHAP plots to analyze the prediction processes of two representative patients (Fig. 3H, I). In the SHAP plot, yellow segments indicate positive contributions of the feature to predicting “poor healthy behaviors” (increasing the model’s predicted risk, pushing f(x) rightward and increasing SHAP values); purple segments indicate negative contributions (lowering the model’s predicted risk, pushing f(x) leftward and decreasing SHAP values). Segment length reflects the absolute strength of the feature’s contribution. E[f(x)] denotes the baseline expectation (mean of all sample predictions), and f(x) represents the final prediction for the current individual. For a true negative patient correctly identified as low-risk (Fig. 3H), f(x) = 0, lower than E[f(x)], indicating multiple factors associated with lower predicted risk (e.g., low perceived stress, high social support) collectively reduced the model’s risk judgment. For a true positive patient (Fig. 3I), f(x) = 0.719, significantly exceeding the baseline threshold. The model assesses their health behaviors as poor, primarily due to the synergistic effects of factors associated with higher predicted risk (e.g., low social support and high perceived stress).

Discussion

Clinical implications of model performance and variable importance

This study developed and validated eight ML models for predicting health behavior risk in MetS patients using six key predictors: W, TC, knowledge of MetS, social support, self-efficacy, and perceived stress. Comprehensive evaluation demonstrated XGB significantly outperformed all others in discriminative ability, calibration performance, and clinical net benefit (Fig. 3). The XGB model achieved AUC values of 0.889 (95% CI: 0.864–0.915) in training and 0.852 (95% CI: 0.808–0.906) in testing, with sensitivity (0.881/0.865), specificity (0.916/0.908), F1 score (0.804/0.813), and accuracy (0.775/0.781). Notably, XGB exhibited the lowest Brier scores (training set: 0.133; test set: 0.154) and calibration curves closely approximating the ideal diagonal (Fig. 2C, D), confirming that the model’s probability estimates are highly reliable and accurately reflect individual risk levels for adverse health behaviors. DCA showed superior clinical net benefit across threshold probabilities of 2%–85% (Fig. 2E, F), supporting prioritized deployment in resource-constrained settings. This approach identifies individuals at higher predicted risk of adverse health behaviors, which may provide supportive evidence for optimizing resource allocation in community screening.

Although dichotomizing the continuous health behavior score may lead to minor information loss, it improves clinical interpretability and practical utility for community screening by providing a clear classification. A cutoff of 108 points enables rapid identification of patients requiring intensive intervention, supporting efficient risk stratification in routine chronic disease management.

Potential MechanisMetS underlying metabolic-psychosocial-behavioral interactions

The XGB model’s advantage steMetS from its capacity to capture complex nonlinear interactions. Feature importance analysis revealed perceived stress (54.09% contribution), self-efficacy (15.02%), and social support (13.67%) as the top predictors, collectively accounting for 82.78% of variance (Fig. 3A, B).

These factors constitute core psychosocial drivers for health behavior risks, supporting an integrative pathophysiological model where chronic stress impairs behavioral regulation and physiological function [20]. Stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, causing cortisol elevation that promotes visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and glucose-lipid metabolism dysregulation [21]. Concurrently, it impedes prefrontal-striatal neural circuit function, reducing patients’ capacity to translate health knowledge into consistent behavior [22]. Low self-efficacy further diminishes motivational resilience, making healthy practice maintenance difficult even among knowledgeable individuals [23]. Inadequate social support intensifies psychological isolation, perpetuating a vicious cycle of psychological burden, behavioral impairment, and metabolic deterioration.

Notably, TC demonstrated a significant protective association with health behaviors, identified as a key negative predictor (OR = 0.749, 95% CI: 0.635–0.883; P < 0.001). This provides new insights beyond the conventional view of TC merely as a cardiovascular risk factor, highlighting its potential role in modulating neurobehavioral processes. TC may indirectly influence health behavior decision-making by affecting central nervous system functions (e.g., hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex), including energy homeostasis perception and reward regulation [24, 25]. For example, moderately elevated TC levels may reduce satiety sensitivity through disruption of leptin signaling and suppress dopamine-mediated motor motivation pathways, thereby impairing behavioral regulation [25]. Concurrently, obesity may exacerbate neurofunctional impairment via chronic low-grade inflammation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, further increasing maladaptive behavior risk [26].

Evidence indicates [27–29] that TC levels are nonlinearly associated with central nervous system function. Within an appropriate range, TC levels are associated with better neurobehavioral function. Moderate TC concentrations are associated with more favorable neurological outcomes, whereas both low and high levels increase vulnerability to neuronal dysfunction. Therefore, TC values within the range of 4.0–5.2 mmol/L may reflect a more balanced metabolic and neurological profile, with lower likelihood of cognitive and behavioral regulation impairments without substantially increasing atherosclerosis risk. This approach is particularly relevant for MetS patients without established cardiovascular disease but exhibiting poor adherence to healthy behaviors [30, 31], and may inform comprehensive clinical assessment of metabolic and behavioral health. We explicitly emphasize that this finding should not be misconstrued as support for maintaining higher cholesterol levels in routine clinical care for MetS patients; on the contrary, clinical management of TC should still follow current guidelines.

Application value and implementation pathways of the model

Based on the exploratory statistical inflection points identified from SHAP dependency plots, we propose a preliminary stratified behavioral intervention direction for research exploration, laying a foundation for future clinical application. Thus, the proposed intervention strategies are preliminary references for future research: High-risk individuals (≥ 3 abnormal tentative indicators) may benefit from comprehensive interventions (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy to improve self-efficacy to ≥ 40, social support enhancement to achieve SSRS ≥ 32, tentative TC modulation to 4.0-5.2 mmol/L under clinical supervision), and moderate-risk individuals from tailored strategies (targeting CPSS ≤ 45 and weight ≤ 75 kg).As these thresholds are derived from a single internal dataset, they carry inherent uncertainty for direct clinical use and only provide testable hypotheses, requiring external and prospective validation prior to definitive clinical application.

Notably, the MetS knowledge scale showed relatively low internal consistency. After item diagnosis, removing Item 5 with a low item-total correlation improved Cronbach’s α to 0.521. We subsequently performed sensitivity analyses with this revised 9-item scale, which confirmed that low reliability did not substantially distort core findings. Key predictors, SHAP importance rankings, and model AUC (0.899 vs. 0.856) remained stable, supporting the robustness of our results.

Study limitations and future directions

Despite positive findings, limitations warrant consideration: (1) Sampling bias limited population coverage, particularly for MetS patients < 40 years or with non-obese MetS, potentially compromising model generalizability in these subgroups; (2) Static single-timepoint measurements failed to capture individual behavioral and physiological fluctuations, limiting dynamic characterization of metabolism-behavior interactions; (3) Psychosocial dimension assessment remained incomplete due to lack of standardized scales for anxiety, depression, social isolation, and loneliness, resulting in less systematic analysis of health behavior drivers.

Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies to validate dynamic predictive validity across life stages and phenotypic subtypes. Further exploration of multi-modal medical image fusion techniques, advanced transformer-based architectures, and interpretable deep learning with multi-channel attention mechanisms is warranted to integrate heterogeneous clinical data, enhance model transparency and interpretability, and enable more comprehensive and robust risk stratification [32–34]. Concurrently, integration of wearable device data is recommended to develop time-resolved dynamic prediction models capturing temporal associations between metabolism and behavior. Multicenter RCTs should evaluate risk-stratified intervention effectiveness on clinically relevant outcomes like HbA1c reduction and cardiovascular event prevention, advancing MetS management from passive risk prediction to proactive precision-based prevention.

Conclusion

This study developed ML models integrating clinical, behavioral, and social data within a socioecological framework to predict health behaviors in MetS patients. The XGB model demonstrated superior discriminative ability, calibration, and potential clinical utility. Perceived stress, low self-efficacy, and poor social support emerged as key drivers of unhealthy behaviors, underscoring the centrality of psychosocial factors. Moderately elevated total cholesterol levels were associated with reduced unhealthy behavior risk, which may suggest potential link with neurobehavioral regulation via prefrontal synaptic plasticity and reward regulation pathways. SHAP analysis identified potential thresholds, such as W > 75 kg and CPSS > 45; these thresholds delineate potential high-risk profiles and reveal nonlinear metabolic-psychological-behavioral interactions. As the first interpretable ML model grounded in socioecological theory, this tool enables precise identification of high-risk individuals and exploration of modifiable predictive factors. Multicenter validation is ongoing, supporting a paradigm shift from experience-based to data-driven, mechanism-informed prevention strategies.
