Protocol for modeling injury-induced regeneration in retinal organoids and isolating hNRSCs for subretinal transplantation in rd10 mice
Authors: Ming Chen, YunXi Zhang, Na Gao, WenMin Pan, Chen Cheng, Xiaoyu Liu, ShaSha Li, JianZhong Su, Hui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman retinal organoids (hROs) provide a platform for modeling injury and assessing regenerative capacity in vitro. Here, we present a protocol for stepwise generation of hROs from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs). We describe steps for microsurgical induction of neural retina (NR) injury, isolation of CPAMD8+ human neural retinal stem-like cells (hNRSCs) using flow cytometry, and their subretinal transplantation into rd10 mice. By isolating and subretinally injecting hESC-derived neural retinal stem-like cells, this protocol enables subsequent functional studies in retinal degeneration models.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Liu et al.1

Before you begin

Retinal degenerative diseases, such as retinitis pigmentosa and age-related macular degeneration, are leading causes of irreversible vision loss, primarily resulting from the progressive degeneration of photoreceptors and retinal neuroepithelial cells.2 Due to the limited intrinsic regenerative capacity of the adult mammalian retina, current clinical interventions are primarily aimed at slowing disease progression or offering visual support, rather than restoring retinal function.3,4

Stem cell-based retinal regeneration represents a promising therapeutic strategy for treating degenerative retinal diseases. Among the various candidate cell sources, retinal stem cells (RSCs) are particularly attractive due to their ability to differentiate into multiple neural retina cell types.3,5

In our previous work, we identified a population of hNRSCs residing in the ciliary marginal zone–like (CMZ-like) region of hROs.1 These cells exhibit molecular characteristics similar to those found in human fetal retinas. Although hNRSCs are present in the CMZ-like region, they are relatively quiescent under steady-state conditions, making direct isolation challenging and yielding limited cell numbers. Using this organoid-based injury-repair model, we activate the hNRSC population to facilitate efficient isolation. We then demonstrated the regenerative potential of hNRSCs and systematically characterized their dynamic cellular and molecular responses during the repair process.

Furthermore, hNRSCs derived from hROs can be expanded in vitro and transplanted into the subretinal space of rd10 mice, where they contribute to the preservation of retinal structure and lead to significant improvement in visual function.

To enable consistent isolation and functional assessment of hNRSCs, we established a standardized protocol that integrates precise organoid dissection with optimized flow cytometry. This approach provides a reproducible platform for investigating hNRSCs, elucidating their regenerative mechanisms, and exploring their potential in cell-based therapies.

Innovation

The existence of human RSCs and their regenerative potential has long been debated. In our previous studies, we identified hNRSCs in the ciliary margin region of both human fetal retina and hROs, and further demonstrated their regenerative capacity using a retinal injury-repair model and transplantation into rd10 mice.1 This protocol presents a systematic and reproducible workflow for the identification and isolation of hNRSCs, enabling direct experimental validation of their existence and regenerative capacity. The stepwise procedures cover cell recognition and application to regenerative assays, establishing hNRSCs as a practical cellular source for retinal repair. By integrating careful experimental design with methodological innovation, this protocol resolves a long-standing debate and offers a broadly applicable framework for studying endogenous neural stem cells. It provides researchers with a reliable platform to explore human retinal repair and to advance stem cell–based therapies for degenerative retinal diseases.

Preparation for coating culture plates with Matrigel

Timing: 2 h 7.Use 12 mL of ice-cold DMEM/F12 medium to thaw and resuspend each Matrigel aliquot (final concentration of 0.16 mg/mL).8.Add 1 mL of the diluted Matrigel to each well of a 6-well plate, ensuring the entire surface is evenly coated.9.Incubate the plate at 37°C for 60 min. Note: Coated plates can be stored at 4°C for up to 2 weeks. Before use, incubate plates at 37°C for at least 30 min and no longer than 120 min. Expected outcomes

This protocol enables the generation of morphologically defined and structurally intact hROs using a standardized differentiation system, followed by the establishment of an injury-induced repair model and isolation of hNRSCs for downstream in vitro culture and in vivo transplantation.

During hRO differentiation, translucent, spherical aggregates with well-defined borders typically appear by D18, progressively developing surface features reminiscent of early neuroepithelium. By approximately D30, the peripheral regions of organoids display densely packed neuroretina-like structures expressing early retinal markers such as VSX2 and PAX6. Between D60 and D75, organoids exhibit advanced structural organization, including lamination and compartmentalization, accompanied by the expression of retinal progenitor and photoreceptor lineage markers such as CRX, OTX2, and Recoverin.

Following surgical excision of the central NR region using a V-Lance knife, the residual CMZ-like domain within the organoid enters a regenerative phase. In the days following injury, cells at the organoid margin show centripetal migration and proliferation. This regenerative process is characterized by a wound closure-like morphology, and upregulated expression of marker genes including MECOM, CPAMD8, SOX2, and LHX2.

During FACS-based isolation, hNRSCs are labeled using specific surface markers. The yield depends on the developmental stage and quality of the organoids. Using well-developed organoids at R5, we typically isolate approximately 100,000–200,000 hNRSCs from every 10 organoids. The sorted cells exhibit high viability (>90%) and provide sufficient numbers for downstream applications, including transplantation or in vitro analyses.

Upon subretinal transplantation into rd10 mice, hRO-derived hNRSCs integrate into the outer retinal layers within 8-12 weeks. As we have reported previously, immunostaining of host tissue sections reveals human-origin cells co-expressing neural retinal markers such as VSX2 and CRX. Functional restoration is supported by electroretinography (ERG), with transplanted mice showing significantly improved light-evoked responses compared to sham-treated controls. Detailed procedures for these downstream analyses can be found in our earlier publication.1

Overall, this protocol yields highly reproducible results across batches and supports large-scale production of retinal organoids and regenerative evaluation. It provides a reliable model and cellular platform for studying human retinal stem cell biology and assessing their therapeutic potential in degenerative retinal diseases.

Limitations

Although this protocol provides a robust and reproducible strategy for generating hROs, establishing an injury-repair model, and isolating functional hNRSCs, several limitations should be taken into consideration.

First, the efficiency of organoid differentiation and the structural integrity of hROs are highly dependent on the quality and physiological state of the starting hESC population. Variables such as pluripotency level, passage number, and recovery duration post-thawing can significantly impact the consistency of organoid formation. Suboptimal cell quality often results in morphologically heterogeneous organoids, which may compromise the reproducibility of injury modeling and stem cell isolation. In addition, some critical reagents, such as BMP4 and Matrigel, can be obtained from different suppliers; however, both batch-to-batch and brand-to-brand variations may influence retinal tissue development. Users are advised to carefully consult the manufacturer’s certificate of analysis and adjust experimental conditions accordingly to ensure reproducibility.

Second, the injury model is based on manual microsurgical dissection using a V-lance knife. This step is inherently sensitive to the operator’s technique. Inconsistent dissection angle or depth may lead to unintended damage to the CMZ or incomplete removal of the neural retina, thereby affecting both the regenerative potential and the interpretability of downstream analyses.

Third, the current strategy for isolating hNRSCs relies on the surface marker CPAMD8, which may have limited specificity and may not encompass the full spectrum of regenerative stem-like subpopulations. In addition, flow cytometry-based gating and sorting require technical expertise to optimize cell purity and yield. Inexperienced handling may result in low recovery or contamination with non-target cells.

Lastly, subretinal transplantation into rd10 mice introduces further variability. Surgical outcomes are influenced by factors such as the accuracy of the injection site, the operator’s microsurgical proficiency, and the post-transplantation care. These variables critically affect graft survival, tissue integration, and functional restoration. Furthermore, the rd10 model undergoes rapid retinal degeneration, which limits its utility in evaluating long-term regenerative durability.

In summary, we strongly recommend rigorous quality control of hESC input, standardized training for surgical and sorting procedures, and preliminary pilot experiments to ensure consistency before large-scale implementation of this protocol. This protocol focuses on the isolation and subretinal injection of neural retinal stem-like cells; functional rescue of vision is not assessed here. For studies evaluating visual function, readers may refer to previously published work.1
