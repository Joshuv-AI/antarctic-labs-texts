Circadian clock control of breast cancer hallmarks: molecular mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Luis Ricardo Hernández-Barrientos, Lorena Aguilar-Arnal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms regulate physiological functions and critical biological processes in almost all living forms. The circadian system enables organisms to anticipate and adapt to environmental fluctuations, thereby optimizing physiological responses. Proper synchronization between the master pacemaker in the brain and peripheral clocks throughout the organism is essential for maintaining homeostasis and promoting health. Conversely, circadian misalignment is increasingly recognized as a contributing factor in the pathogenesis of various diseases. Emerging evidence implicates circadian disruption as a driver of carcinogenesis. Indeed, epidemiological studies have associated night-shift work and chronic jet lag with an increased risk of cancer. At the molecular level, the biological clock comprises a complex network of transcriptional-translational feedback loops that generates approximately 24-h cycles in gene expression across all tissues. Notably, many clock-regulated genes participate in key pathways relevant to tumorigenesis, including cell cycle regulation, DNA damage repair, angiogenesis, and metabolism. Moreover, the circadian clock represents a promising therapeutic target in oncology. Numerous clinical trials have demonstrated that chronomodulation of chemotherapeutic agents can enhance treatment efficacy while mitigating adverse effects, thereby improving the quality of life for cancer patients. Hence, advancing our understanding of the molecular interplay between circadian regulation and cancer development may elucidate the role of circadian rhythms in tumorigenesis, facilitate the identification of novel prognostic markers and therapeutic targets, and ultimately improve outcomes in breast cancer patients.Keywords: Circadian rhythms, Breast cancer, Hallmarks of cancer, Circadian disruption, Transcription, Chronotherapy

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide and ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with an estimated number of 2.3 million new cases reported globally according to GLOBOCAN 2022 [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines breast cancer as a heterogeneous disease characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal breast cells leading to tumor formation. Breast cancer can be further classified into subtypes based on histological features, tumor grade, lymph node status, and the expression of specific biomarkers, including estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [2].

Based on receptor status, breast cancer is categorized into at least four major subtypes: luminal A (ER+, PR+/−, HER2−), luminal B (ER+, PR+/−, HER2+), HER2-enriched (ER−, PR−, HER2+), and basal-like (ER−, PR−, HER2−) (Fig. 1). Basal-like tumors are commonly known as “triple-negative” due to the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression [2, 3]. Interestingly, pathways such as estrogen signaling, which play a crucial role in breast cancer development, are regulated by circadian rhythms. Consequently, disruption of the biological clock has been implicated as a contributing factor in tumorigenesis [4].

In this review, we discuss the molecular mechanisms linking the circadian clock to breast cancer development, focusing on the interplay between clock machinery and the hallmarks and enabling capabilities of cancer. Furthermore, we explore the therapeutic potential of targeting the circadian clock in breast cancer treatment through chronotherapeutic approaches.

The circadian clock

Functional aspects of the circadian clock

Circadian rhythms are endogenous temporal systems with a period of approximately 24 h, shaped by the rotation of the Earth around its own axis. They regulate physiological functions, including body temperature, blood pressure, and muscular efficiency, behavioral patterns such as feeding-fasting cycles, sleep-wake cycles, or mood, and biochemical processes as hormone release and circulating levels of glucose and lipids, in almost all living forms. This regulation allows organisms to anticipate and adapt to environmental changes for optimal physiological responses [5–8]. Circadian rhythms require daily synchronization through specific environmental cues known as Zeitgebers (time givers). Most organisms have evolved to use light as the primary Zeitgeber [9], and in mammals, this synchronization is mediated by specific photoreceptors in the retina that detect particular changes in light intensity, quality, and composition [10].

In mammals, the circadian system clock is hierarchically organized, with multiple and interrelated oscillators throughout the body. The master clock is located in a tiny area in the brain, identified as the suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) of the hypothalamus. It is composed of approximately 20,000 pacemaker neurons in rodents [11, 12], 80,000 in humans, and particular to this nucleus is that it directly receives the environmental light signaling via the retinohypothalamic tract, originating from intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). These specialized cells respond to short-wavelength light through the photopigment molecule melanopsin [13–15]. This light cue synchronizes the pacemaker neurons in the SCN, which in turn relays the photic information to peripheral clocks allocated within different tissues and other areas in the brain. Hereby, the SCN, through humoral, hormonal, and neural signaling, ensures optimal alignment of the body to day/night cycles and coordinates physiological responses throughout the day [16–18].

The organization of the molecular clock

At molecular level, there is an intrinsic clock in almost every cell of the organism. Interestingly, this molecular clock is composed of a number of regulators of gene expression which operate by interconnected and self-regulated transcriptional-translational feedback loops [19] (Fig. 2A). Specifically, eight core clock genes participate in this mechanism. The transcriptional activator elements are Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Kaput (CLOCK) [20] and Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like Protein-1 (BMAL1) [21, 22]. These proteins heterodimerize via their basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) domains and bind to specific DNA sequences known as Enhancer-box (E-box) elements (5’-CACGTG-3’) [23] located in the promoter and regulatory regions of clock-controlled genes (CCG). In the negative arm of the loop, the Cryptochrome (CRY 1 and CRY 2) [24–26] and Period (PER1, PER2, and PER3) [27–31] proteins heterodimerize and are activated by phosphorylation by Casein kinase 1 family members (CK1α, CK1ε) [32, 33]. This repressor complex translocates to the nucleus where it destabilizes the BMAL1/CLOCK heterodimer, evicting it from chromatin and leading to repression of CCGs [34, 35]. Additionally, a complementary regulatory loop exists in the molecular clock involving Nuclear Receptor subfamily 1 group D members 1 and 2 (NR1D1 and NR1D2, also known as RevErbα and RevErbβ), which act as repressors of BMAL1 and CRY1 expression, while Retinoic Acid-Related Orphan Receptors (RORα, β, and γ) function as activators by binding to ROR response elements (ROREs) [36, 37].

Because of the operation of this molecular machinery, thousands of genes appear transcribed with an oscillatory pattern across the day. Some of these are master transcription factors that impose rhythmic expression on subordinate groups of genes. Intriguingly, many oscillatory transcripts are associated with metabolic pathways that regulate the pace of various biosynthetic and catabolic processes. Through this mechanism, the clock machinery ensures the temporal compartmentalization of cellular functions, assigning distinct times of day to incompatible processes, for example, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis.

Circadian disruption and its contribution to cancer hallmarks in breast cancer

Circadian disruption, defined as misalignment between internal rhythms and environmental cues, alters essential physiological functions and has been increasingly linked to disease [38, 39], including cardiovascular diseases [40], sleep disorders [41], metabolic disorders [42–44], neurodegenerative diseases [17, 41], and cancer [45, 46] (Fig. 2B).

Because photic cues are the primary zeitgebers of circadian rhythms, exposure to abnormal light-dark cycles can disrupt circadian rhythmicity and alter sleep-wake patterns. Remarkably, exposure to light at night (LAN) has been strongly associated with increased risk of breast cancer [47–49]. LAN exposure commonly occurs in individuals engaged in night-shift work, which is prevalent in healthcare, manufacturing, transportation, retail, and service sectors, as well as through lifestyle habits that involve prolonged exposure to LED light sources, such as those emitted by electronic screens [50]. The most significant consequence of LAN exposure is the misalignment between internal circadian rhythms and the external environmental time [51]. At the molecular level, LAN influences the neurons in the SCN which in turn lead to suppression of nocturnal melatonin release from the pineal gland [52, 53]. This disruption might significantly impair cancer control mechanisms, given that melatonin exerts potent oncostatic actions at its physiological nocturnal levels, promoting apoptosis in cancer cells, suppression of tumor growth, and antiestrogenic effects [54, 55]. In this regard, many epidemiological studies have demonstrated a strong association between night-shift work and increased cancer incidence [56–58]. Hereby, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies night-shift work as a probable human carcinogen for cancers of the breast, prostate, colon, and rectum (group 2 A).

Recently, non-photic synchronizers such as food intake and physical activity have been extensively explored to understand their contribution to circadian function and disease. These cues contribute to coordinating circadian timing with sleep-wake, feeding-fasting, and activity-rest cycles, because they function as strong zeitgebers for peripheral clocks and can override signals originating from the SCN in peripheral tissues [59–61]. Recent research suggests that these non-photic zeitgebers may play an intriguing role as potential therapeutic agents to reduce adverse effects induced by chemotherapy and improve the lifestyle of cancer patients, as we summarize later.

Several biological processes driving tumorigenesis, collectively defined as the hallmarks of cancer, have been extensively characterized [62]. These include sustained proliferative signaling, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to cell death, replicative immortality, deregulated cellular energetics, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, and tumor-promoting inflammation, along with enabling characteristics such as genomic instability and epigenetic reprogramming. As shown in Fig. 3, these hallmarks can be conceptually grouped according to their functional contributions to tumor development. The circadian clock interacts with these processes and modulates multiple molecular pathways underlying them, such as cell cycle progression, DNA damage response, metabolism, and immune function [4, 63]. Disruption of circadian regulation may therefore contribute to the acquisition or reinforcement of cancer hallmarks, influencing breast cancer initiation and progression [64–67]. Notably, these interactions may differ across breast cancer subtypes, such as luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative tumors, which display distinct molecular dependencies and may exhibit differential sensitivity to circadian disruption.

The circadian clock and proliferative axis: initiation of tumorigenesis

Genomic instability and mutation

Circadian regulation plays a key role in maintaining genomic stability, and its disruption contributes to the accumulation of mutations characteristic of cancer cells. Genomic instability is an enabling capability for the development of other cancer hallmarks, and mutations in DNA repair genes can lead to a mutator phenotype, promoting genomic instability [68–70]. Specifically, mutations in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, CDH1, PIK3CA, MAP3K1, GATA3, PTEN, PIK3R1, AT1, and RB1 represent prominent driver mutations in breast cancer. Many of these genes function as gatekeepers (TP53, CDH1) or caretakers (BRCA1/2), whose dysregulation confers proliferative, survival, and metastasis advantages to tumor cells [68]. These alterations are differentially distributed across breast cancer subtypes, with BRCA1/2 mutations enriched in basal-like tumors, PIK3CA and GATA3 alterations more frequent in luminal subtypes, and HER2-driven tumors displaying distinct genomic profiles [71]. Notably, several of these driver genes interact with circadian clock components. For example, PER2 opposes p53 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation to enhance DNA damage response [72, 73]; CRY1/2 directly interact with ATR-CHK1-p53 complexes to regulate cell cycle checkpoint activity in a circadian manner [74], and CLOCK/BMAL1 transcriptionally control the key cell cycle regulators c-Myc, Cyclin D, and Wee1, through binding to E-box elements in the regulatory elements of these genes [64, 75–77]. Thus, circadian regulation operates to dictate time-dependent activity of critical actors in the cell cycle control.

Finally, a critical regulator of the circadian clock is phosphorylation by casein kinases (CK), a family of proteins [78], which control the stability and nuclear translocation of the circadian machinery, leading to changes in period length. Particularly, CK1δ is also a mayor regulator of mitotic checkpoints, and its mutation can promote genomic instability. Interestingly, CK1δ is frequently overexpressed in BRCA1-mutated triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and invasive breast carcinomas, while CK1ε expression is more restricted to the basal-like subclass and not associated with invasive breast carcinoma. Thus, CK1δ inhibition has been proposed as an attractive therapeutic approach with potential benefit for HER2+ breast cancer and TNBC patients aberrantly expressing CK1δ [79].

Sustaining proliferative signaling and evading growth suppressors

In breast cancer, the intricate coupling between the circadian clock and the cell cycle is often dysregulated, leading to disrupted cell proliferation, which is considered a hallmark of cancer. During tumorigenesis, malignant cells exhibit sustained proliferative signaling and evasion of growth suppressor signals, driving continuous and excessive rounds of cell division [80]. Notably, these regulatory mechanisms are differentially altered across breast cancer subtypes, with luminal tumors often driven by cyclin D1/CDK4/6 signaling and basal-like tumors frequently exhibiting defects in TP53 and DNA damage response pathways [81, 82].

The circadian clock is functionally intertwined with cell cycle progression, coordinating the temporal activation of key regulators that govern proliferative signaling in breast cancer. Furthermore, both cycles are controlled by transcriptional and translational feedback loops, with their protein products displaying periodic activation and repression to temporally segregate physiological processes [19, 83]. In breast cancer, dysregulation of the cell cycle involves alterations in cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), cyclins (Cyc), and CDK inhibitors [84, 85], and specific pathways such as the Ink4 family (p15, p16, p18, p19) and the Cip1/Kip family (p21, p27, p57), which normally operate to induce cell cycle arrest to prevent the accumulation and propagation of genetic alterations during cell divisions [80, 86]. In a time-of-day–dependent manner, DNA damage response pathways are coordinated with the cell cycle phase through two mayor pathways: during the G1 phase, the ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) kinase phosphorylates checkpoint kinase 2 (CHK2) and activates p53, preventing cells from entering DNA replication, while in S/G2 phase, the response is mediated by ATM- and RAD3-related (ATR) kinase, signaling through checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) and p53, leading to cell cycle arrest [87–89]. Subsequently, BRCA1 and BRCA2 act downstream of ATM/ATR signaling to promote homologous recombination-mediated DNA repair.

Importantly, these pathways are not only structurally conserved but are also temporally regulated by the circadian clock. The cell cycle and DNA damage response are coupled to the circadian clock, for example through temporal control of transcription of key regulators or by protein-protein interactions with the clock machinery. Specifically, CLOCK and BMAL1 regulate the transcription of cell cycle activators such as the c-Myc oncogene [90], which participates in the G0-G1 transition for cell entry into replication, and is a key oncogenic driver frequently dysregulated in breast cancer. Conversely, c-Myc can also affect circadian function through at least two mechanisms: in an E-box-independent manner, c-MYC forms a complex with MIZ1 to repress BMAL1 and CLOCK expression [76]; in an E-box-dependent manner, c-MYC upregulates REVERBs genes, which in turn repress BMAL1 expression [91]. Furthermore, c-MYC can interact with BMAL1, therefore interfering with CLOCK/BMAL1 transcriptional activity [4]. During the G1 phase, transcription of Cyclin D1, CDK 4/6 activators, and the p21 inhibitor is controlled by the circadian clock [77, 92, 93]. Moreover, the p21 gene contains RORE elements in its promoter, making it susceptible to regulation by ROR and REVERB genes. Circadian regulation of the p16 inhibitor occurs via the RNA-binding protein NONO in a PER-dependent manner [94]. At the G2/M transition, the BMAL1/CLOCK complex exerts dual regulation by acting on the Wee1 kinase inhibitor and the Cyclin B activator promoter regions [64, 75].

In the DNA damage response, molecular interactions between the cell cycle and the circadian clock occur through PER and CRY genes, which interact directly with protein complexes ATM-CHK2-p53 and ATR-CHK1-p53, respectively [74, 95]. Particularly, p53, a key regulator in proliferation, is potentially under circadian regulation through BMAL1 binding to its promoter [96, 97]. PER2 has been shown to regulate p53 stability by blocking MDM2-mediated ubiquitination, thereby enhancing activation of p53 target genes such as p21 [73, 87, 98, 99]. Finally, the circadian clock protein REVERBα acts as a transcription factor repressing DNA damage repair gene expression, increasing cell susceptibility to DNA damage at night, which may contribute to cancer development [89]. Together, these findings indicate that circadian regulation is embedded within the molecular circuitry controlling cell proliferation and genome integrity, and that its disruption may directly contribute to sustained proliferative signaling in breast cancer.

The circadian clock and surveillance axis: promotion of tumorigenesis

Resisting cell death

The circadian clock plays a critical role in regulating apoptotic pathways, and its disruption contributes to resistance to cell death in breast cancer. In cancer cells, evasion of apoptosis is frequently achieved through loss-of-function mutations in the TP53 gene. In breast cancer, TP53 mutations are reported in approximately 25–30% of cases on average, although the frequency of these mutations is highly subtype-dependent, with the lowest rates in Luminal A tumors (approx. 17%) and the highest in basal-like tumors, reaching up to 80–88% [81]. This leads to the downregulation of pro-apoptotic factors and the induction of anti-apoptotic proteins [81].

Apoptosis is mediated by intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, both of which are subject to circadian regulation. The intrinsic pathway is triggered by pro-apoptotic members of the BCL-2 family, while anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) family members can inhibit apoptosis by sequestering activators [100]. The extrinsic pathway is mediated by the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily of membrane receptors, including Fas, TNFR1, or TNFR2. In addition, TNFRs can activate the NF-kB pathway, which subsequently activates caspase-8 followed by executioner caspases such as caspase-3 [100]. These pathways are tightly interconnected with circadian regulation, which modulates both pro- and anti-apoptotic signaling in a time-dependent manner. For example, expression of BCL-2 family proteins is regulated by CDKs and p53 activity, both under circadian clock control. The connection between apoptosis evasion and the circadian clock is largely mediated by dysregulation of the p53 signaling pathway and has been linked to BMAL1, PER, and CRY proteins [101]. For example, two orthologous mutations of mouse CRY2 that appear frequently in human cancers lead to increased cell growth and suppressed expression of P53 target genes [102]. Furthermore, a Cry1/Cry2 double mutation delays cancer onset in p53 mutant mice hereby increasing the median lifespan of these mice [103]. Mechanistically, Cry downregulation may promote hyperphosphorylation of glycogen synthase kinase-3b (GSK3b), which becomes inactivated and unable to phosphorylate and activate the anti-apoptotic effect of NF-κB, making cells more sensitive to TNFα-induced apoptosis [100]. Efforts oriented to understand the role of CRY proteins in breast cancer raise the notion that certain genetic variants of the CRY2 gene might be associated with breast cancer risk, particularly with ER- and PR-negative tumor types. Concomitantly, a decreased expression of CRY2 in breast tumor tissue, especially in ER/PR-positive cancers, has been described, and CRY2 promoter methylation, which may lead to decreased gene expression, was found elevated in breast cancer cases relative to controls [104]. In MCF-7 breast epithelial cultured cells, reduced CRY2 levels lead to higher DNA damage after exposure to chemical mutagens and increased genome instability [105]. Interestingly, the specific CRY inhibitor KS15 can inhibit proliferation and increase chemosensitivity of MCF-7 breast cancer cells, suggesting that CRY proteins might be considered as therapeutic targets for treatment of breast cancer [106].

BMAL1 binds to the p53 promoter and regulates its expression, while PER genes have a tumor-suppressive role in breast cancer [107]. Specifically, PER2 appears to play an anti-tumorigenic role in breast cancer, particularly in ER-positive tumors, where it directly interacts with ERα and promotes its degradation, thereby attenuating estrogen-driven proliferation. Accordingly, loss of PER2 may enhance ERα stability and signaling in luminal breast cancer, whereas in ER-negative subtypes, PER2 is likely to exert tumor-suppressive effects through ER-independent mechanisms [108]. Similarly, CLOCK protein interacts with ERα in MCF7 and T47D cells, and sumoylation of CLOCK, which can be stimulated by estrogen, increases ERα-dependent transcription of cyclin D1, supporting cell growth [109]. Altogether, this evidence suggests that circadian disruption actively contributes to apoptosis resistance in breast cancer through modulation of subtype-specific molecular pathways, rather than being a secondary or indirect factor.

Tumor-promoting inflammation and avoiding immune destruction

The circadian clock plays a central role in regulating immune function and inflammatory processes, and its disruption contributes to tumor-promoting inflammation and immune evasion in breast cancer [110]. Both innate and adaptive immune responses are under circadian control, influencing the function of multiple immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes [111–114]. In this context, disruption of circadian machinery alters the tumor microenvironment by modulating immune cell infiltration and inflammatory signaling, including pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, IL-19) and chemokines (TGF-b, TNF-α, MMP9, IFN-γ, CCL2, CXCL6), leading to a pro-inflammatory state or contributing to immunosuppression, thereby influencing breast cancer progression and metastasis [115].

At molecular level, circadian regulators such as BMAL1 and REVERBα modulate the expression of inflammatory genes, including CCL2, CCL8, IL6, and TNF, in macrophages and dendritic cells [116]. Thereby, downregulation of these clock genes enhances expression of pro-inflammatory chemokines, promoting a tumor-supportive inflammatory environment [98]. Also, the NLRP3 (NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3) inflammasome exhibits circadian oscillations regulated by clock genes, influencing secretion of IL-1b and IL-18, and shaping the inflammatory milieu within breast tumors [117–119].

These circadian–immune interactions extend to key inflammatory signaling pathways. The interaction between clock machinery and NF-kB, a key regulator of inflammation, links inflammatory processes to breast cancer development. BMAL1 and REVERBα exert anti-inflammatory effects through regulation of NF-kB activation, whereas CLOCK displays pro-inflammatory properties through complex formation with the NF-kB subunit p65 [120, 121]. Consistently, circadian disruption through a jet-lag protocol in a mouse model of breast cancer shows an immunosuppressive pro-tumor microenvironment, facilitating the dissemination of mammary cancer cells and promoting lung metastasis [115]. Hereby, circadian regulation appears tightly embedded within immune and inflammatory networks, and its disruption promotes a tumor-supportive microenvironment and immune evasion in breast cancer.

The circadian clock and energetics axis: conversion of tumorigenesis

Nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming

The circadian clock plays a central role in regulating epigenetic processes, and its disruption contributes to non-mutational epigenetic reprogramming in breast cancer. In this context, chromatin remodeling and gene silencing, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs, are tightly regulated by circadian mechanisms and are frequently dysregulated in breast tumor cells [122, 123]. Notably, epigenetic alterations are particularly prominent in specific breast cancer subtypes, such as basal-like/triple-negative tumors, which exhibit widespread chromatin remodeling and distinct DNA methylation signatures [124].

At the molecular level, the circadian clock is a transcriptional regulatory mechanism tightly controlled by epigenetic mechanisms [125]. Therefore, epigenetic regulation represents a common mechanistic link between circadian rhythms and tumorigenesis [122]. For example, circadian disruption has been linked to aberrant DNA methylation patterns that contribute to breast cancer through dysregulating gene expression, including the silencing of tumor suppressor genes such as BRCA1 and CDH1 [126–128]. On one hand, silencing of tumor suppressor genes via promoter CpG island hypermethylation has been identified as a driver of breast tumorigenesis [127, 129]. On the other hand, oncogene hypomethylation is observed in approximately 50% of breast cancers and is associated with altered activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and DNA demethylases (TET) [130, 131]. Regarding the circadian clock, aberrant methylation of promoter regions in key clock genes such as PER, CRY, BMAL1, and CLOCK has been reported in breast cancer samples [132–136]. Conversely, the molecular clock modulates DNA methylation dynamics, and its disruption may contribute to the epigenetic reprogramming observed in breast tumors. Several mechanisms have been described, such as the BMAL1-dependent rhythmic expression of the de novo DNA methyltransferase Dnmt3b in mouse liver [137]. Together, these findings suggest a bidirectional relationship between circadian regulation and the cancer epigenome.

Histone modifications further illustrate this interplay, as many histone-modifying enzymes are under circadian control and implicated in cancer development. Histone methylation, regulated by histone-methyltransferases (KMT) and demethylases, contributes to circadian gene expression through modulation of CLOCK/BMAL1 binding to chromatin [138]. For example, the KMTs mixed lineage leukemia (MLL) proteins 1 and 3 contribute to circadian gene expression by regulating CLOCK/BMAL1 binding to clock-controlled gene (CCG) promoters [139, 140]. Conversely, histone demethylases such as LSD1 and JARID1A are key regulators of circadian transcriptional programs [141]. Histone acetylation is also tightly coupled to circadian regulation, with CLOCK itself exhibiting intrinsic HAT activity and cooperating with additional acetyltransferases to sustain rhythmic chromatin states [142, 143]. Furthermore, the circadian regulator SIRT1 deacetylates various cancer-related proteins including MYC, p53, HIF, TGFB, and WNT, acting as both a tumor suppressor and an oncogene depending on context [144].

Importantly, the circadian clock modulates chromatin remodeling through fluctuations in cofactor availability, such as the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) for SIRT1 and S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) for methyltransferase [145, 146]. Finally, non-coding RNA, particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), contribute to the epigenetic landscape of breast cancer and are themselves subject to circadian regulation, either by controlling clock gene expression or through oscillatory transcription driven by E-box elements [147, 148]. Altogether, these findings indicate that circadian regulation is embedded within epigenetic mechanisms and that its disruption contributes to the reprogramming of the breast cancer epigenome, with potential subtype-specific consequences [149].

Enabling replicative immortality

The circadian clock contributes to the regulation of replicative potential by controlling telomerase activity and telomere maintenance, and its disruption may promote replicative immortality in breast cancer. Normal cells possess limited replicative potential, determined by telomere shortening. However, cancer cells are capable of overcoming this limitation, reaching replicative immortality. This hallmark of cancer involves upregulation of telomerase, a process that is subject to circadian regulation [150]. Telomerase is formed by three main components: the telomerase RNA (TERC), which provides the RNA template for telomere synthesis; the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), which participates as the catalytic subunit and rate-limiting factor for enzymatic activity; and accessory telomerase-associated regulatory proteins. While TERC and associated proteins are constitutively expressed, TERT expression is tightly regulated [151].

In breast cancer, TERT is often overexpressed, correlating with poor prognosis [152]. Increased TERT expression has been associated with the most aggressive subtype TNBC, where replicative potential and telomere maintenance mechanisms are highly activated, allowing cancer cells to achieve immortality and evade senescence [153]. Interestingly, TERT is a clock-controlled gene and its transcription is regulated by BMAL1/CLOCK heterodimers; therefore, circadian disruption may directly influence telomerase activity in tumor cells [154]. This establishes a direct link between circadian regulation and telomere maintenance in cancer cells, indicating that disruption of circadian rhythms may facilitate replicative immortality in breast cancer through dysregulation of telomere maintenance probably in a subtype-dependent manner. In addition, TERT has telomere-independent functions, affecting cellular pathways that contribute to malignant phenotype, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell adhesion, migration, and cell cycle regulation [155] (Fig. 3).

Deregulating cellular energetics

The circadian clock plays a central role in regulating cellular metabolism, and its disruption contributes to metabolic reprogramming in breast cancer. Under aerobic conditions, normal cells process glucose to pyruvate via glycolysis, which enters the mitochondria, leading to ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation. In breast cancer, circadian disruption can promote metabolic reprogramming, leading to a shift toward aerobic glycolysis, commonly known as the Warburg effect, which fuels rapid, uncontrolled proliferation [156, 157].

In breast cancer, metabolic rewiring is driven by two main pathways: PI3K/AKT signaling, frequently activated in luminal tumors, and HIF-1α stabilization under hypoxic conditions in the tumor microenvironment [158]. Interestingly, the circadian clock imposes rhythmicity on HIF1A gene expression [159], while HIF-1α can in turn regulate the expression of clock genes [160]. Moreover, low oxygen levels in solid tumors stabilize HIF-1α leading to increased anaerobic glycolysis and acidification of the tumor microenvironment. This acidic environment suppresses mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling, leading to decreased translation of clock proteins including BMAL1, CLOCK, and PER2 [161].

The NAD+ pathway is considered a major and direct connection between the circadian clock and metabolism [162]. In cancer cells, the NAD+/NADH balance reflects the cellular energetic state [163]. Importantly, nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT), the rate-limiting enzyme in NAD+ biosynthesis through the salvage pathway, is a clock-controlled gene [164, 165]. Circadian oscillations of NAMPT modulate NAD+ levels, which in turn determine the activity of SIRT1, a class III NAD+-dependent deacetylase [165, 166]. Notably, in triple-negative breast cancer, tumor metastasis is suppressed by NAD+ supplementation [167]. Conversely, reduction in NAMPT expression enhances metastatic aggressiveness in breast cancer [168], while SIRT1 overexpression has been associated with significantly increased breast cancer growth [169]. Interestingly, the NAD+-dependent HDAC SIRT7 targets CRY1 in mice for its deacetylation, leading to degradation which can affect glucose homeostasis [170]. In breast cancer cultured cells of the luminal subtype, acetylation of the homologous protein CRY2 by p300 stabilizes it, leading to increased proliferation via NF-κB pathway [171]. Hereby, metabolic adaptations vary across breast cancer subtypes, with triple-negative tumors displaying enhanced glycolytic and NAD+-dependent pathways, while luminal tumors exhibit distinct metabolic dependencies, often relying on oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and signaling pathways linked to hormone receptors [172].

Lipid metabolism is another circadian-regulated process involved in breast cancer metabolic reprogramming. The circadian clock regulates key enzymes responsible for fatty acid synthesis, transport, and degradation. Specifically, core clock proteins control the expression of transcription factors like SREBP1c, which in turn regulate lipogenic enzymes including fatty acid synthase (FASN), ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) [173]. Dysregulation of circadian rhythmicity disrupts fatty acid synthesis, conferring metabolic advantages to malignant cells. Tumor cells are known to enhance de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid uptake, and fatty acid oxidation for energy production and lipid storage [174]. This suggests a strong connection between lipid metabolism and the circadian clock, highlighting it as a potential target for cancer therapy.

The circadian clock and metastasis axis: progression of tumorigenesis

Polymorphic microbiomes

The circadian clock plays an important role in shaping host-microbiome interactions, and its disruption may contribute to microbial dysbiosis associated with breast cancer [175, 176]. There is evidence that these microbiomes have a profound impact on cancer phenotypes, affecting cancer development, malignant progression, and response to therapy; hence, polymorphic microbiomes have been proposed as an emerging hallmark of cancer [123, 177]. Microbial dysbiosis plays an important role in chronic inflammation, inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis processes, and immune evasion. In this sense, specific microbes are associated with the development of particular cancers, such as Helicobacter pylori in gastric cancer and Fusobacterium spp. in colorectal cancer [178].

Breast tissue similarly harbors a diverse and unique microbial community. Importantly, significant differences in bacterial composition between healthy and tumor breast tissues have been identified. Specifically, dysbiosis involving bacteria such as Streptococcus spp., Sphingomonas spp., Enterococcus spp., Ralstonia spp., and Fusobacterium spp. correlates with breast cancer progression, with microbial abundance varying by tumor stage [179, 180]. Furthermore, dysbiosis in the gut microbiome is associated with breast cancer development and progression through regulation of the systemic inflammatory response, hormone metabolism, and immune system modulation [181]. Circadian rhythms regulate intestinal microbiota composition and function, and their disruption can lead to dysbiosis that may influence breast cancer development. It is well established that intestinal microbiota populations and their functions exhibit diurnal fluctuations, which are driven by the host via time of eating, diet composition, and circadian status. Therefore, disruptions such as irregular eating patterns and a high-fat diet can perturb microbiome rhythmicity, potentially influencing cancer risk [182].

Inducing angiogenesis

The circadian clock regulates angiogenic processes, and its disruption contributes to tumor vascularization in breast cancer, which involves the formation of new tumor-associated neo-vasculature to supply oxygen and nutrients [183]. Tumor cells activate an angiogenic switch by altering the balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic factors. Circadian rhythms also regulate angiogenesis through the control of the cell cycle of endothelial cells, which constitute the building blocks of neo-vasculature formation [184]. However, the direct molecular links between circadian clock components and angiogenic signaling in breast cancer remain less well characterized. Further studies are needed to clarify how circadian mechanisms influence angiogenesis and whether they may represent therapeutic opportunities in this context.

Activating invasion and metastasis

One of the typical alterations in cancer cells occurs in their shape and attachment to other cells and to the extracellular matrix (ECM). These alterations are related to higher levels of malignancy and confer cells with abilities to invade, resist apoptosis, and disseminate. This is a multi-step process known as the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [185]. Frequently, invasive carcinoma cells are characterized by the loss of E-cadherin, a key cell-to-cell adhesion molecule, or the upregulation of N-cadherin, which is normally expressed in migrating neurons and mesenchymal cells during organogenesis. Additionally, EMT is orchestrated by specific transcription factors, including SNAIL, SLUG, TWIST, and ZEB 1/2 [186].

Interestingly, E-cadherin expression is regulated by the Wnt/b-catenin signaling pathway, which is linked to the circadian clock via melatonin regulation of glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3b), a regulator of b-catenin expression [187]. Many transcription factors involved in EMT are also regulated by the molecular clock. For example, BMAL1 has been reported to promote metastasis by activating transcription of matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) [188], while downregulation of PER2 prevents OCT1 binding to promoters of the EMT genes Twist1, Slug, and Snail, allowing their expression and promoting migration and invasiveness in breast cancer [189]. Additionally, the CLOCK/BMAL1 complex regulates metastasis-associated protein 1 (MTA−1) expression, controlling the EMT process [190]. Consistent with these observations, experimental disruption of circadian rhythms through light-dark shift paradigms in mouse models of breast cancer promotes tumor progression and metastasis, accompanied by downregulation of PER2 and upregulation of pro-migratory factors such as PAI-1 [191]. Furthermore, clinical evidence supports a role for circadian regulation in metastatic progression. Higher expression of multiple clock genes, including PER1-3, CRY2, CLOCK, NPAS2, and RORC, is associated with improved metastasis-free survival in breast cancer patients. These associations are subtype-specific, and coordinated clock gene expression is preserved in less aggressive tumors but disrupted in metastasis-prone cancers [192]. Together, these findings link circadian dysfunction to increased metastatic potential and poorer clinical outcome.

In addition, ARNTL2, a circadian transcription factor, has been identified as a metastasis susceptibility gene, with regulatory variants modulating its expression and impacting metastatic burden. Importantly, ARNTL2-associated eQTLs correlate with disease-free survival in breast cancer patients, particularly in estrogen receptor-negative tumors, providing genetic evidence linking circadian regulation to metastatic progression in a subtype-specific manner [193]. Supporting a subtype-specific role for the circadian clock, a recent study showed that luminal A breast cancer patients with tumors exhibiting higher circadian rhythm strength present reduced 5-year survival. In these tumors, EMT genes display enhanced rhythmicity, and disruption of the molecular clock in luminal A models of 3D cultures reduces invasive capacity [194]. Together, these findings link circadian dynamics to EMT regulation, metastatic potential, and clinical outcome in a subtype-dependent manner.

Finally, the release of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and metastatic dissemination have been shown to follow circadian patterns. In both breast cancer patients and mouse models, CTC generation is largely restricted to the rest phase, and these CTCs exhibit enhanced proliferative capacity and metastatic potential compared to those released during the active phase. This increased metastatic competence appears to be driven by time-dependent proliferation programs and regulated by circadian hormones, including melatonin, glucocorticoids, testosterone, and insulin. These findings highlight the importance of temporal regulation in metastatic dissemination and suggest that circadian timing may influence both the detection and therapeutic targeting of breast cancer [195].

Therapeutics implications of circadian rhythms in breast cancer

Recently, the new insights into cancer therapies have highlighted the molecular understanding of the circadian clock and its relevance to breast cancer development, enabling researchers to focus on components of the biological clock as potential therapeutic targets. Therefore, pharmacological modulation of the circadian clock and chronotherapy have emerged as two promising strategies for cancer management [4, 196–198]. In addition, non-pharmacological interventions aimed at circadian rhythm realignment offer a complementary approach for cancer treatment [61, 199].

As proposed by Sulli et al. [200, 215], therapeutic approaches involving the circadian clock can be classified into three categories: (I) “Training the clocks,” which refers to interventions to maintain a robust circadian rhythm in feeding-fasting, sleep-wake, or light-dark cycles; (II) “Drugging the clock,” which refers to using small-molecule agents that directly target a circadian clock component or its regulators; and (III) “Clocking the drugs,” which refers to optimizing the timing of the drugs to improve efficacy and reduce adverse side effects.

Enhancing the circadian clock for breast cancer therapy

As we have shown, disruption of circadian rhythms acts as a trigger for breast cancer development. Therefore, enhancing or restoring the circadian clock holds potential for cancer therapy by optimizing treatment timing and modulating tumor biology through lifestyle interventions, such as sufficient sleep, time-restricted feeding (TRF), daylight exposure, and optimal timing of exercise. This strategy involves entrainment of the circadian clock by various zeitgebers to effectively reduce cancer risk and comorbidities or to improve lifestyle in cancer patients [200, 201].

In this context, enhancing the sleep/wake cycle depends on the proper regulation of sleep timing and quality. This intervention requires consistent light hygiene, including exposure to natural daylight during the day, avoidance of bright indoor light for 2–3 h before habitual bedtime, maintenance of darkness during sleep, and an average nightly sleep duration of 7.5 h [199, 202]. These circadian lifestyle interventions are considered effective in supporting restorative sleep at night, are associated with reduced comorbidities, and have shown benefits in reducing fatigue and sleep disturbances in breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy [203, 204].

Conversely, enhancing the fasting/feeding cycle represents a promising strategy, as dietary patterns can synchronize circadian gene expression in multiple peripheral tissues [60]. Time-restricted feeding (TRF) is a dietary regimen limiting daily food intake to a consistent 8–12 h window, aligning food consumption with the active phase without explicit caloric restriction, thereby improving metabolic and immune functions [205]. This intervention has been demonstrated to inhibit tumor growth, delay tumor initiation, and reduce metastasis in breast cancer models [206]. At the molecular level, fasting modulates nutrient availability for tumor metabolism, enhancing the antitumor efficacy of chemotherapy, while reducing side effects [61].

Furthermore, enhancing the activity/rest cycle has potential therapeutic effects for cancer prevention and treatment. The timing of physical activity is important in regulating clock gene expression; however, exercise is a less dominant zeitgeber than feeding and thus may be less effective for entrainment if it is not in phase with mealtimes [61, 207]. Additionally, exercise has been associated with decreased cancer incidence, improved survival outcomes across multiple cancer types, and enhanced immune surveillance against tumors, with anti-inflammatory effects mediated by exerkines [208, 209]. Moreover, the timing of exercise affects which metabolic pathways are activated in response to physical activity. Accordingly, exercising at an optimal time can regulate metabolism and modulate nutrient availability, a crucial factor for cancer cell surveillance [199, 210].

Targeting the circadian clock for breast cancer therapy

Currently, targeting the circadian clock has attracted much attention as a novel therapeutic approach for chronic diseases such as metabolic syndrome and cancer. In this sense, two types of drugs have been developed: those that directly modulate the activity of core circadian genes and those targeting their regulatory factors. However, targeting core clock proteins BMAL1 and CLOCK remains challenging due to their nature as transcription factors. Consequently, drug design has focused on agonists and antagonists of the clock’s negative regulators in order to enhance or suppress the transcriptional activity of the BMAL1/CLOCK complex [4, 8, 200].

Particularly, components such as REV-ERBs and RORs have been extensively studied as drug targets, since they inhibit or activate BMAL1 expression, respectively [211, 212]. In addition, CRY components are widely explored as drug targets, because they inhibit clock-controlled gene transcription through direct interaction with BMAL1/CLOCK complexes [213]. The family of casein kinases, which regulate components of the core circadian clock, has also emerged as a novel therapeutic target in breast cancer treatment [201].

In the case of REV-ERBα/β, commonly used agonists are SR9009 and SR9011 [214], which have been reported to exhibit anti-cancer activity in preclinical models against several tumor types, including breast cancer, by suppression of autophagy and de novo lipogenesis pathways, without adverse effects on normal cells [215]. However, the selectivity and mechanisms of action of some REV-ERB agonists remain under debate, and off-target effects have been reported [216, 217]. Conversely, RORc-selective antagonists such as XY018, SR 2211, and GSK805 have shown inhibitory effects on tumor growth and improved survival in breast cancer models [218]. These agents have also exhibited anti-cancer effects, including downregulation of cholesterol-biosynthesis pathway genes, suppression of pathways driving pro-metastatic EMT, and the acquisition of stemness in triple-negative breast cancer subtype [218, 219]. Moreover, RORa has demonstrated therapeutic potential in cancer treatment; for example, the agonist SR1078 enhances apoptosis in liver cancer cells through p53 stabilization, although its effects in breast cancer remain unexplored [220, 221].

Furthermore, pharmacological targeting of CRY1/2 proteins by KS15 reduces proliferation and increases the sensitivity of MCF-7 breast cancer cells to doxorubicin and tamoxifen treatment [106]. Additionally, inhibition of CK1δ/CK1ε by SR-3029 induces apoptosis in CK1δ-expressing breast tumor cells ex vivo, leads to tumor regression in orthotopic models of triple-negative breast cancer (including patient-derived xenografts), and inhibits tumor growth in HER2+ breast cancer models, likely through modulation of the Wnt/b-catenin signaling pathway [222]. Similarly, inhibition of CK2 by CX-4945 has demonstrated antiproliferative and anti-angiogenic effects against cancer cells [223].

Recently more refined, clinically viable options have been developed in order to overcome severe limitations in selectivity and toxicity of clock-targeting compounds. For example, while previous CRY modulators such as KS15 showed antiproliferative effects in breast cancer models, the novel CRY2-selective stabilizer SHP1705 exhibits improved specificity and has advanced to early-phase clinical evaluation [224]. Notably, in mesenchymal stem-like TNBC models, SHP1705 selectively repressed BMAL1/CLOCK-driven circadian transcription but had limited effects on proliferation as a single agent. However, combinatorial targeting through co-activation of REV-ERB signaling, using the novel compound SR29065, has shown synergistic effects, further reducing tumor cell viability [225]. These findings suggest that subtype-specific circadian dependencies may be more effectively approached through multi-target strategies rather than single-agent therapies.

Chrono-therapeutical approaches for breast cancer treatment

In recent years, the timing of drug administration has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach in cancer treatment. This strategy aims to reduce side effects (chronotoxicity) and improve efficacy (chronoefficacy) of therapeutic interventions by administering them at specific times of the day. Chronotherapy in cancer involves the chronomodulation of radiation therapy, endocrine therapy, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy [226, 227]. The circadian clock plays a critical role in chronotherapy by influencing the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of drugs [228, 229]. This influence arises from the circadian regulation of molecular drug targets involved in several biological functions (such as cell cycle, DNA repair, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and metabolism), as well as circadian control over drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) processes [230, 231] (Fig. 4).

Chronomodulation is a promising therapeutic approach for breast cancer treatment. A Chronomodulation of the circadian system involves three main strategies: (top) enhancing the circadian clock through external cues such as light exposure, feeding schedules, and physical activity to maintain a robust circadian system; (middle) targeting the circadian clock using small-molecule agents directed at specific clock components; and (bottom) chronomodulating drug administration, which involves optimizing the timing of drug delivery to improve therapeutic efficacy and minimize side effects. B Chronomodulation, applied through these approaches, represents a promising strategy for breast cancer treatment. Its integration into personalized cancer chronotherapy may become a key component of future clinical oncology practice

Radiation therapy efficacy mainly depends on its ability to induce DNA damage, making it suitable to chronomodulation since DNA damage response mechanisms, including DNA repair, DNA damage checkpoints, and apoptosis, are regulated by the circadian clock [89]. In breast cancer, clinical studies on radiation therapy have reported opposite effects, with some indicating higher incidences of adverse reactions in either the morning or the afternoon [232–234].

Endocrine signaling pathways are crucial in breast cancer development; hence, the endocrine therapy has been adopted as a widely used treatment for mammary tumors. This intervention focusses on the inhibition of estrogen receptors and aromatase enzymes, using drugs such as tamoxifen, letrozole, anastrozole, and exemestane [235]. In breast cancer, chronotherapeutic considerations are gaining attention in the context of endocrine therapy, where treatment adherence and toxicity remain major clinical challenges. A systematic evaluation of available evidence proposed that modifying the timing of tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitor intake could represent a simple strategy to mitigate adverse effects and improve compliance, although prospective trials are still needed [236]. Interestingly, tamoxifen pharmacokinetics exhibit circadian variation, with increased absorption following morning administration (8:00 a.m.) in breast cancer patients [237]. In this context, a study assessing the timing of endocrine adjuvant therapy intake in high-risk early breast cancer patients found that evening or nighttime tamoxifen administration significantly prolonged disease-free survival, whereas no timing effects were observed for aromatase inhibitors [238]. Conversely, a pragmatic, multicenter and randomized study comparing morning versus evening dosing of adjuvant endocrine therapy in early breast cancer observed no statistically significant differences between groups [239].

Immunotherapy is a complementary therapeutic strategy for breast cancer, often combined with endocrine therapy and chemotherapy depending on hormone receptor status [235]. Immunotherapeutic agents include monoclonal antibodies targeting the HER2 protein on the surface of cancer cells, such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and margetuximab, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the Programmed Cell Death receptor (PD-1) and ligand (PD-L1), such as pembrolizumab and atezolizumab, respectively [240]. Although chrono-immunotherapy represents a promising approach under active investigation, direct clinical trial evidence on the timing of monoclonal antibodies administration in breast cancer patients remains lacking [241]. However, retrospective clinical studies across multiple cancers, including metastatic melanoma, lung, kidney, bladder, esophageal, stomach, and liver cancer, have reported that early-day infusion of ICIs may enhance progression-free and overall survival up to fourfold compared to late-day dosing, highlighting a previously underappreciated role of circadian biology in antitumor immunity [242]. Furthermore, a clinical study in advanced melanoma patients demonstrated shorter overall survival following evening ICI infusions, potentially due to a less robust adaptive immune response [243].

Chemotherapy is one of the most commonly used treatments against tumor cells. However, chemotherapy agents often exhibit high toxicity to both cancerous and healthy cells due to their effects on the cell cycle, which is under circadian regulation. Consequently, chronotherapy has been proposed as a promising strategy for reducing side effects and enhancing drug efficacy [197, 244]. In mammary tumors, chemotherapy is predominantly used for the triple-negative subtype and includes alkylating agents (such as cyclophosphamide, mitomycin, doxorubicin, epirubicin) and platinum-based drugs (such as cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin), which induce DNA crosslinks leading to DNA destabilization and breakage; antimetabolites (such as 5-fluorouracil, gemcitabine, capecitabine), which inhibit DNA, RNA, or precursor synthesis; topoisomerase inhibitors (such as irinotecan, etoposide, doxorubicin), which block DNA unwinding enzymes; and mitotic inhibitors (such as paclitaxel, docetaxel, vinorelbine), which act on tubulin, impeding the mitotic spindle and stalling cell division [245, 246].

Although many chemotherapy drugs have not been extensively evaluated for chronotherapy in breast cancer clinical trials, chronomodulation has been studied in other cancer types and animal models. For instance, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), commonly used in gastrointestinal cancer treatment, shows decreased toxicity and enhanced antitumor activity when administered during the early rest phase [247]. At the molecular level, this effect corresponds to circadian oscillations in dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) activity, the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for 5-FU elimination, with peak activity occurring around 4 pm [248]. Platinum-based drugs (cisplatin, oxaliplatin) show reduced toxicity when administered in the afternoon (~ 4 pm), coinciding with peak glutathione (GSH) levels, an antioxidant tripeptide involved in drug detoxification [249]. Similarly, irinotecan administration between 2 and 8 am results in less severe diarrhea and reduced interpatient variability [250]. For its part, doxorubicin exhibits improved efficacy with morning administration [251]. For taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel), nighttime administration (9 pm) of paclitaxel (PTX) is associated with lower plasma concentrations after 12 h of administration and faster recovery from mechanical allodynia, a common adverse effect of PTX treatment [252].

Interestingly, chemotherapy drugs such as irinotecan, paclitaxel, and oxaliplatin display higher efficacy when administered near the peak expression of BMAL1 across multiple cancer types [253]. Moreover, peak PER2 expression correlates with enhanced efficacy of DNA-damaging agents like oxaliplatin [254]. Along the same lines, circadian modulators such as melatonin have been shown to influence tumor growth, oxidative stress responses, and treatment sensitivity, further linking systemic circadian cues with cancer therapeutic responses [255]. Collectively, this evidence suggests that integrating circadian principles into therapeutic scheduling represents a feasible and low-cost strategy to enhance breast cancer treatment efficacy and patient well-being.

Conclusions

Breast cancer development is significantly influenced by dysregulation of the circadian clock. Key signaling pathways driving tumorigenesis are intricately controlled by circadian mechanisms. Consequently, elucidating the molecular pathways that connect circadian disruption with the acquisition of cancer hallmarks is crucial for the discovery of novel prognostic markers. Furthermore, the dynamic interplay between circadian biology and breast cancer therapy is a rapidly evolving field. Strategies integrating behavioral rhythm optimization, clock-targeted pharmacological interventions, and chronotherapeutic scheduling offer promising options to enhance treatment efficacy, minimize side effects, and potentially overcome resistance to therapy. The circadian clock plays a critical role in breast tumorigenesis, as it regulates the molecular mechanisms responsible for the establishment of cancer hallmarks. It is also involved in multiple biological processes underlying breast cancer development. Altogether, current evidence supports a significant role for circadian disruption in breast cancer, contributing to multiple processes underlying tumor progression.

Indeed, accumulating evidence positions the circadian clock as a fundamental regulator of multiple hallmarks of breast cancer, influencing tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response. Clinically, these findings highlight the potential of circadian-based strategies, including chronotherapy, clock-targeting pharmacological interventions, and behavioral or metabolic timing approaches, to improve treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity. However, important controversies remain, particularly regarding the heterogeneity of circadian disruption across tumor subtypes, the extent to which tumor versus host clocks drive disease progression, and the challenges of translating preclinical chronobiology into standardized clinical protocols. Key gaps include the limited availability of longitudinal human datasets, insufficient biomarkers to assess circadian phase in patients, and the need to integrate temporal variables into clinical trial design. Future research should prioritize multi-omic temporal profiling, patient stratification based on circadian phenotypes, and prospective trials explicitly testing time-of-day–dependent therapeutic interventions. Addressing these challenges will be essential to fully incorporate circadian biology into precision oncology frameworks and to realize its translational potential in breast cancer management.
