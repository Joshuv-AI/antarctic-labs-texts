Radiological diagnosis of cochlear incomplete partition type 3 (IP-III) in a 4-year-old male: A case report
Authors: Nilesh Kumar Sinha, Gaurav Raj, Shailly Sonker, Shubhlaxmi Srivastava, Rudra Prasad Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Incomplete Partition Type 3 (IP-III) is a rare congenital cochlear malformation and is associated with X-linked deafness radiologically characterized by the absence of the modiolus, absence of cribriform plate and an enlarged internal auditory canal (IAC) with preserved interscalar septa. Preoperative recognition of this anomaly is essential, as cochlear implantation (CI) is challenging due to the high risk of perilymphatic gusher.We present the case of a 4-year-old male with delayed speech development and bilateral severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). Otoacoustic emission (OAE) and BERA reported severe to profound bilateral hearing loss with probable cochlear origin. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the temporal bone revealed a cochlea with corkscrew appearance, absence of the modiolus, and an enlarged IAC, consistent with IP-III. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) showed a normal cochlear nerve with no additional labyrinthine malformations. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of IP-III, guiding preoperative evaluation for cochlear implantation, given the potential surgical risks.This case highlights the critical role of HRCT and MRI in diagnosing IP-III, a rare but clinically significant cochlear anomaly. Recognizing its distinct radiological features is essential for differentiating it from other incomplete partition anomalies (IP-I, IP-II) and for anticipating intraoperative complications during cochlear implantation.

Introduction

Congenital inner ear malformations account for approximately 20% of congenital sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), with incomplete partition (IP) anomalies being a significant subset. Incomplete partition type 3 was first described by Nance et al. (1971) as X linked deafness and its HRCT findings were first described by Phelps et al [[1], [2]]. Sennaroğlu (2006) first classified Incomplete Partition Type 3 (IP-III) as a distinct cochlear anomaly, setting it apart from IP-I and IP-II based on radiological findings [3].

IP-III is seen in X linked deafness or X linked stapes gusher syndrome caused by mutations in POU3F4, an X-linked gene essential for inner ear development [4]. Unlike IP-II (Mondini malformation), where the modiolus is partially formed, IP-III is characterized by complete modiolar absence, a corkscrew like cochlea lacking cribriform plate and an enlarged IAC [5]. This abnormal communication between the cochlea and IAC predisposes patients to perilymphatic gusher during cochlear implantation, making preoperative imaging essential [6].

Case presentation

A young child presented to the ENT department with delayed speech development and bilateral profound hearing loss, first noted around 1.5 years of age. There was no family history of hearing loss or consanguinity. The child had no history of neonatal hypoxia, infections, or syndromic features.

The Otoacoustic Emissions (OAE) test results for both ears showed a “REFER” outcome, indicating absent or significantly reduced outer hair cell function. This finding suggests a possible cochlear pathology.

Brainstem Evoked Response Audiometry (BERA) revealed the presence of Wave V at 90 dB nHL in both ears, with no response at lower intensities. This confirms bilateral severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), suggesting a cochlear origin of hearing loss.

HRCT of the temporal bone showed normal sized cochlea bilaterally. The base of bilateral cochlea including modiolus and lamina cribrosa were completely absent, resulting in direct communication between the cochlea and an abnormally enlarged bulbous IAC (>10 mm in diameter) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve was seen more superior in position in relation to the cochlea (Fig. 3). Angle between first and second part of the intratemporal facial nerve canal was less acute than normal. Vestibular aqueduct is also irregularly dilated (Fig. 4). Discussion

Incomplete partition (IP) anomalies represent a spectrum of cochlear malformations resulting from arrested development during embryogenesis. They are classified into three main types—IP-I, IP-II, and IP-III—based on the degree of cochlear development and associated anatomic abnormalities (Fig. 7) [7].Fig. 7Diagrammatic representation of incomplete partition anomalies (IP-I, IP-II, and IP-III), highlighting key differentiating anatomical features.Fig 7 – dummy alt text

Incomplete Partition Type I (IP-I) is the most severe form and is characterized by a cystic cochlea devoid of any internal architecture, including the modiolus and interscalar septae. The vestibule is also markedly dilated and dysplastic. The internal auditory canal (IAC) may appear normal or slightly enlarged. Due to the complete absence of the modiolus and the basal turn differentiation, cochlear implantation can be particularly challenging. Incomplete Partition Type II (IP-II), also known as the Mondini deformity, is the most commonly encountered type and is less severe than IP-I. Radiologically, IP-II is characterized by a normal basal turn of the cochlea with fusion of the middle and apical turns into a cystic cavity, often associated with an enlarged vestibular aqueduct (EVA). The modiolus is partially formed, and the IAC typically appears normal in size [7].

Incomplete Partition Type III (IP-III) as was seen in our case is a distinct and rare cochlear malformation associated with an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern, most notably seen in X-linked deafness. First described by Nance et al., IP-III is unique in its radiologic features: a complete absence of the modiolus and a markedly enlarged internal auditory canal (IAC) [8]. The enlarged IAC in IP-III allows direct communication between the cochlear basal turn and the subarachnoid space, increasing the risk of intraoperative perilymphatic gusher during cochlear implantation. The absence of the modiolus eliminates the natural bony barrier between the cochlea and IAC, which poses a significant surgical challenge. A gusher can obscure the surgical field and complicate electrode insertion. Management and prevention of CSF gusher during cochlear implantation rely on careful preoperative imaging to anticipate high-risk inner ear malformations and appropriate patient counseling. Intraoperatively, measures such as reverse-Trendelenburg positioning and controlled hyperventilation help reduce CSF pressure. Continuous suction is used to maintain visualization, allowing safe electrode insertion. The key step is achieving a watertight seal at the cochleostomy site by packing around the electrode with fascia or muscle, which is usually sufficient to control the leak and prevent complications like persistent CSF rhinorrhea, meningitis, and electrode extrusion. Techniques such as the use of round window insertion (instead of cochleostomy), meticulous soft surgical approaches, and the use of perimodiolar or slim straight electrodes help minimize CSF egress [9,10].

Differentiating IP-III from other inner ear malformations is imperative. On imaging, IP-I appears as a cystic cochlea with complete absence of the modiolus and interscalar septae, along with a dilated vestibule. IP-II (Mondini deformity) shows a normal basal turn with fusion of the middle and apical turns, a partially formed modiolus, and is often associated with an enlarged vestibular aqueduct. In contrast, IP-III demonstrates a normal external cochlear outline with complete absence of the modiolus and lamina cribrosa, resulting in direct communication with a markedly enlarged internal auditory canal, while the vestibule and semicircular canals remain normal [5].

Conclusion

Precise radiologic classification of incomplete partition (IP) anomalies is essential for guiding surgical decisions and anticipating potential complications. High-resolution CT (HRCT) and MRI play a pivotal role in diagnosing these anomalies, with early identification being particularly crucial in rare entities like IP-III. This congenital cochlear malformation poses a significant surgical challenge due to the high risk of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak or perilymphatic gusher during cochlear implantation. Recognizing the distinct radiological features of IP-III is vital for differentiating it from IP-II and other inner ear anomalies. While IP-II typically allows for safe and effective cochlear implantation, IP-III requires heightened surgical caution. A multidisciplinary approach involving otologists, radiologists, and audiologists is key to minimizing intraoperative risks and optimizing postoperative hearing outcomes. Preoperative imaging thus plays a central role in ensuring successful hearing rehabilitation by enabling tailored surgical planning.
