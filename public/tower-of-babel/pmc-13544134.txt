Serum Neuritin is a Predictive Biomarker of Poor Neurological Recovery After Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Tian Yan, Wenbiao Zheng, Youyou Hu, Tianming Mao, Yuan Chen, Hailong Ji, Zhenyu Li, Yong Jin, Kaiyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTraumatic spinal cord injury (TSCI) is a severe neurological urgency. Neuritin is a neurotrophic factor. This study aimed to explore prognostic predictive value of serum neuritin levels in TSCI.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, serum neuritin levels were quantified at admission of 126 patients and at study entry of 126 controls. The American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) was used as the severity index. Poor prognosis was defined as no improvement in AIS grade at six months post TSCI versus admission. The results were analyzed using multivariate regression method.ResultsSerum neuritin levels were significantly higher in patients with TSCI than in controls. No significant departure from linearity was detected between serum neuritin levels, AIS grade and poor prognosis in the restricted cubic spline. AIS grade was independently correlated with serum neuritin levels. Age, AIS grade, and serum neuritin levels were independently associated with poor prognosis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed that poor prognosis was effectively predicted by serum neuritin levels. The results of the regression analysis were robust via sensitivity analysis, variance inflation factor estimation, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and Brier score calculation. The prognosis model incorporating AIS grade, age, and serum neuritin levels was visualized by nomogram, had satisfactory goodness of fit under the calibration curve, showed substantially high discrimination efficiency through the ROC curve approach, and displayed good clinical validity as demonstrated by the decision curve through internal validation. Serum neuritin levels partially mediated the association between AIS grade and poor prognosis.ConclusionsElevated serum neuritin levels are closely associated with TSCI severity and poor prognosis following TSCI, indicating that serum neuritin may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker of TSCI.

Introduction

Traumatic spinal cord injury (TSCI), a serious debilitating disease, clinically manifests as neurological dysfunction, loss of independence, respiratory failure, and mental illness, and therefore has a high disability rate and mortality.1,2 The incidence of TSCI ranges from 250,000 to 500,000 individuals per year worldwide.3 Its pathophysiological mechanisms include oxidative stress, inflammatory reactions, cellular apoptosis and so forth.4–6 The American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS), a favorable method for assessing TSCI severity, represents a robust prognostic factor for TSCI.7 Because of easy accessibility of blood specimens and objectivity of laboratory data, some biomarkers or indices, such as neurofilament light chain, glial fibrillary acidic protein, S100B, neuron-specific enolase, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio and glucose/potassium ratio, have garnered broad attention with respect to severity appraisal and prognosis prediction of TSCI in recent years.8–14

Neuritin, a neuron-specific nutritional factor, has anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties and potently promotes neural plasticity and nerve regeneration.15–19 Interestingly, higher serum neuritin levels were independently associated with poor prognosis in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage.20 Moreover, significantly elevated serum neuritin levels are closely related to poor prognosis in patients with traumatic brain injury combined with TSCI.21 Thus, neuritin may be a biomarker of central nervous system injury. However, there is limited knowledge regarding prognostic meaningfulness of serum neuritin levels in humans after isolated TSCI. This prospective cohort study was conducted to investigate alterations in serum neuritin levels following TSCI and their prognostic implications.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participant Selections

This prospective cohort study was conducted with patients with TSCI who were consecutively recruited at Taizhou Municipal Hospital from March 2019 to December 2023. All patients met the following enrollment criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) collaboration to complete neurologic examination; (3) AIS grade from 1 to 4 on admission; (4) no concomitant craniocerebral trauma; and (5) hospital admission within 24 h post trauma. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) other previous or coexistent neurological diseases, such as spinal cord disorders or spinal surgery before TSCI, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, moyamoya disease, intracranial aneurysms, etc.; (2) severe diseases in other organs, such as malignancies, uremia, liver cirrhosis, myocardial infarction, etc.; and (3) other peculiar conditions, such as missed visits, inadequate information, unwillingness to take part in this study, and unqualified blood specimens. Physical examinees from the hospital were recruited as controls. All procedures in this study were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later updates. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Taizhou Municipal Hospital (Opinion No: LWYJ2019139) (Opinion No: LWYJ2026353). Patients themselves, controls themselves, or patients’ statutory representatives were acquainted with the study details and signed informed consent forms to participate in this study.

Information Acquisition

Information was collected on demographic data (age, sex, height, and weight), adverse lifestyle habits (tobacco smoking and alcohol drinking), and common chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia). The interval between trauma and hospital admission and the period between trauma and sampling were recorded. The traumatic locations included the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spines. There were three types of injury, caused by falls, motor vehicle accidents, or others. A spinal fracture was diagnosed radiologically. TSCI severity was assessed using the AIS grade. Surgical decompression was performed as necessary. Six months after TSCI, the AIS grade was repetitively assessed. No improvement in AIS grade was deemed a poor prognosis.22

Blood Test

Blood samples from controls and patients were obtained separately at their study entry and hospital admission. Upon clot formation, venous blood samples were centrifuged and later transferred to Eppendorf tubes for storage at −80 °C for subsequent testing. To avoid protein breakdown, the collected specimens had to be melted within three months post drawing to measure serum neuritin levels using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The kit was purchased from Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd (Article Number: EH0944), and all detections were performed in duplicate by the same skilled personnel under blinded means as per the specifications in the kit manual. The sensitivity of the kit was 18.75 pg/mL, and the detection range was 31.25–2000 pg/mL, with both intra-assay and inter-assay variabilities below 10%. Two measurements were converted to the average value for the final analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Four statistical software packages were used to analyze the data and draw graphs: SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), R 4.2.1 (www.r-project.org), GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California, USA), and MedCalc 20.0 (MedCalc Software, Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). Statistical approaches for normality examination of measurement data were inclusive of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Shapiro–Wilk test; thus, the mean ± standard deviation or median (lower-upper quartiles) was presented, and the independent-sample t-test, Mann–Whitney test, or Kruskal–Wallis H-test was conducted for intergroup comparisons. Counts (proportions) were provided to express qualitative variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for statistical analysis. Bivariate correlations were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficient. Statistically significant variables (P<0.05) from univariate analysis were integrated into the multivariate model to discern independent factors. A multivariate linear regression model was configured to identify the variables that were independently associated with serum neuritin levels, and a binary logistic regression model was established to determine the factors that were independently related to poor prognosis.

Other auxiliary statistical tools included the E value for sensitivity analysis,23 receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for discrimination efficiency assessment, the variance inflation factor for multicollinearity assessment,24 restricted cubic spline for linearity estimation,25 as well as the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Brier score for stability test of regression analysis. A model encompassing independent predictors of poor prognosis was created, which was graphically mirrored by the nomogram, and calibration curve and decision curve analyses were supplementary means for judging the goodness of fit and clinical benefit of the combined model. The optimism-corrected area under the ROC curve and shrinkage factor of the nomogram model were obtained from bootstrap resampling for internal validation.

Dual measurements of serum neuritin levels were assessed for concordance by adopting the Bland–Altman plot and intraclass correlation coefficient. The mediating effect of serum neuritin level on the association between AIS grade and poor prognosis was determined. The sample size to perform statistical analysis related to serum neuritin levels was computed using type 1 error (alpha) of 0.05, test power (1 – beta) of 0.95, and effect size of 0.8. Sample size estimation was validated for its accuracy by performing a priori power analysis supplied by G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich-Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Germany).26 All variables were available for enrolled participants; therefore, complete-case analysis was applied for handling missing values. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Relationship Between Serum Neuritin Levels and Poor Prognosis After SCI

In contrast to patients with good prognosis, those with poor prognosis displayed pronounced heightened serum neuritin levels (P < 0.001; Supplemental Figure 6). In addition, poor prognosis of TSCI patients could be effectively indicated by serum neuritin levels in the ROC analysis paradigm, and serum neuritin levels above 99.1 pg/mL distinguished patients with development of poor prognosis at maximum Youden index of 0.518 (Figure 1). In addition, serum neuritin levels correlated with the probability of poor prognosis in a dose–effect relationship (P for nonlinear >0.05; Supplemental Figure 7). As shown in Table 4, patients with poor prognosis were significantly older, had higher blood glucose levels, and presented lower AIS grade than those with good prognosis (all P<0.05), and significant differences were found in terms of the location of injury and surgical decompression (both P<0.05). Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that age, AIS grade, and serum neuritin level were independent predictors of poor prognosis after TSCI (all P<0.05; Table 5). Events-per-variable reached 17.3 for logistic prediction modelling. Using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, the P-value was greater than 0.05, and the Brier score was 0.231. Even after correcting for other baseline parameters, such as demographic data, lifestyle habits, and chronic diseases, serum neuritin levels were strongly associated with poor prognosis in TSCI patients (all P<0.05; Table 6). The E value for sensitivity appraisal reached 1.23 (Figure 2). As shown in Figure 3, the ability of serum neuritin levels to predict poor prognosis was equivalent to that of AIS grade (P>0.05) and was significantly increased as compared with age (P<0.05). Serum neuritin levels in part mediated the association between AIS grade and poor prognosis (Figure 4), and this partial association was comparatively significant (Figure 5). Mediating effect was 30.6% (95% confidence interval: 18.6–42.5%). A nomogram was constructed to visualize the combined model, which consolidated age, AIS grade, and serum neuritin level (Figure 6). The model exhibited significantly higher area under the ROC curve than any individual metrics (all P<0.05, Figure 3), and its optimism-corrected area under the ROC curve reached 0.793 (95% CI, 0.715–0.871). The shrinkage factor was 0.925. Discussion

This study determined the relationship between serum neuritin levels, disease severity, and TSCI prognosis by measuring serum neuritin levels at admission in patients. Subsequently, it was affirmed that serum neuritin levels, in close correlation with AIS grades, were independently associated with poor prognosis of human TSCI. Moreover, the model integrating serum neuritin levels had a good performance for prognosis prediction of TSCI. Thus, serum neuritin may be an alternative prognostic biomarker of TSCI.

Prognostic assessment of neurological recovery after TSCI is central to clinical decision-making and involves injury characteristics, individual factors, and intervention timing. The AIS grade is widely recognized as the strongest prognostic factor.22 Age is another independent predictor; younger patients exhibit greater neurological recovery potential and fewer complications, likely due to enhanced synaptic plasticity, higher neurotrophic factor levels, and reduced inflammatory and age-related burdens.27 Injury level also influences prognosis. Although cervical injuries cause the most severe functional loss; their neurological improvement is often greater than that of thoracic injuries, as the cervical enlargement contains a denser intrinsic spinal neuronal network that provides the anatomical substrate for segmental reflexes and limited motor output.22 Additionally, the time from injury to surgical decompression is critical. Early decompression (typically within 24 hours) can limit secondary injury cascades, reduce ischemia, edema, and inflammation-mediated damage, and preserve potentially recoverable neural substrate.28 Other relevant factors include intramedullary hemorrhage or edema extent, admission motor scores, and hemodynamic instability.22,29,30 This study found that post-TSCI neurological prognosis was significantly associated with age and AIS grade but not with other known factors such as injury level or surgical timing. The discrepancy between these findings and some published literature may be explained as follows. First, the relatively small sample size may have yielded insufficient statistical power to detect factors with small effect sizes; compared with the substantial prognostic variance explained by AIS grade and age, the independent contribution of variables such as surgical timing may be relatively weak, making statistical significance difficult to achieve in a small sample rather than indicating a lack of biological effect. Second, the cohort may have had a biased injury characteristic distribution; for instance, a narrow surgical time window with most patients decompressed within 24 hours would limit variability in this variable, preventing demonstration of its prognostic stratification value. Furthermore, the effect of surgical timing may be confounded by initial injury severity. Patients with complete injuries are often more critically ill and their surgery may be delayed, causing this variable’s prognostic effect to be masked by AIS grade. Future investigations should employ a multicenter, prospective design with a larger sample size to ensure adequate prognostic factor variability, together with refined outcome assessment tools, to further clarify the true contribution of these factors to TSCI prognosis.

Ultimate TSCI prognosis depends not only on destructive processes but also on the limitations and regulation of endogenous repair.31 Post-injury repair mechanisms are extremely limited, primarily involving surviving axonal sprouting and collateral formation, synaptic plasticity reorganization, and limited remyelination by oligodendrocyte precursor cells.32 However, molecules that inherently inhibit axonal growth in the adult central nervous system, such as Nogo-A and myelin-associated glycoproteins, together with the physicochemical barrier of the glial scar, prevent these regenerative responses from translating into meaningful neurological recovery.33 In this context, molecules that promote axonal growth and synaptic remodeling have attracted attention for their therapeutic and prognostic potential.

Neuritin belongs to the neurotrophic factor family, is activated by neural activity and neurotrophic factors, and can promote neuronal survival, differentiation, function, and repair.34 It is expressed in punctate form within the cell body, dendrites, and axons of neurons, and expression is particularly intense at axon terminals.35 Neuritin can create an environment that promotes the survival of nerve cells and regeneration of neurites after TSCI, which is conducive to nerve regeneration and recovery of motor function.19 Beyond its general neuroprotective and regenerative properties, neuritin also functions at the molecular and structural level to guide specific repair processes. Neuritin can promote axonal branching and synaptic maturation by activating local focal adhesion kinase and extracellular signal-regulated kinase pathways.15 Accordingly, the level or dynamic change of endogenous neuritin may reflect the activation degree of endogenous repair, serving as a predictive indicator of neural plasticity potential.36 Thus, neuritin can offer unique insight into the “injury–repair balance” and complement prognostic models by adding a positive dimension of compensatory repair.11,21,36

These experimental and theoretical foundations have prompted clinical investigations into neuritin expression patterns across various central nervous system disorders. Animal studies demonstrate that post-TSCI upregulation of neuritin enhances corticospinal tract lateral branching and correlates with improved motor function.37 Neuritin expression in brain tissues is markedly upregulated in central nervous system diseases.38–40 In humans with intracerebral hemorrhage, serum neuritin levels are increased compared to controls.20 Neuritin is expressed in neurons in spinal cord tissues.37 In a group of patients with traumatic brain injury combined with TSCI, blood neuritin levels were obviously enhanced compared to controls.21 However, it is difficult to distinguish whether blood neuritin is derived from brain or spinal cord tissues, considering that these patients were inflicted by multiple traumas. Here, a cohort of patients with isolated TSCI were enrolled, and it was revealed that serum neuritin levels were profoundly higher in patients with TSCI than in controls. Considering the neuronal specificity of neuritin, serum neuritin may originate from spinal cord tissue. Moreover, considering that neuritin possesses neuroprotective properties, it is presumed that elevated neuritin expression may be a compensatory response to nerve injury after TSCI.

Our study showed that serum neuritin levels were independently associated with AIS grade and poor prognosis six months after TSCI. Moreover, serum neuritin levels partially mediated the relationship between the AIS grade and poor prognosis. In terms of predictive ability based on the ROC curve, serum neuritin levels exhibited satisfactory discrimination effectiveness for poor prognosis following TSCI. The combined model containing age, AIS grade, and serum neuritin levels was demonstrated to be feasible in predicting poor prognosis of patients with TSCI based on numerous statistical methods. Furthermore, relative to odds ratio value of 1.035, E-value at 1.225 was comparatively high. In order to demonstrate robustness of binary logistic regression results, adjustments were done for other variables, such as demographical data, lifestyle habits, chronic diseases and so on. Consistently, the two approaches of sensitivity analysis were supportive of the notion that serum neuritin might be a potential prognostic biomarker of TSCI. Overall, serum neuritin, a neuron-specific factor, may be an appealing biomarker for TSCI.

Serum neuritin testing may enable risk stratification and prognostic classification of TSCI. This molecular stratification is likely to complement traditional imaging-based and AIS impairment classifications of TSCI severity and prognosis. And then, such stratification can guide treatment decisions and resource allocation during clinical management of TSCI. Therefore, translating serum neuritin measurements into clinical practice has the potential to shift spinal cord injury management from a passive, expectant approach to an active, biomarker-guided strategy. However, rigorous prospective validation with standardized assays and well-defined cutoff values remains necessary before widespread adoption.

The weaknesses of this study must be stated here. First, control group selection bias may be generated because hospital-based controls may not be fully representative. Second, although a sufficient sample size has been confirmed for statistical analysis with respect to serum neuritin as a prognostic biomarker of TSCI, further validation in multicenter, independent external cohorts is still required before its clinical application can be considered. Furthermore, although the predictive model in this study has undergone internal validation, it was developed based on a single-center cohort; so, its generalizability still needs to be further validated. Finally, it may be beneficial to determine dynamic trends of serum neuritin levels following TSCI for obtaining further clinical information, therefore necessitating this aspect of study in the future.

Conclusions

Serum neuritin levels, which are closely correlated with AIS grade, effectively predict poor prognosis of TSCI and in part mediate the pertinence of the AIS grade to poor prognosis. Serum neuritin levels and the combined model perform comparatively well for predicting poor prognosis of TSCI patients. Thus, serum neuritin may emerge as a candidate prognostic biomarker of TSCI pending external validation, when acknowledging the single-center, internally-validated nature of the model.
