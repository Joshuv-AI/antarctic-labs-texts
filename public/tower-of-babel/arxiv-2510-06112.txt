Lagrangian Dual Sections: A Topological Perspective on Hidden Convexity
Venkat Chandrasekaran, Timothy Duff, Jose Israel Rodriguez, Kevin Shu

Abstract
Hidden convexity is a powerful idea in optimization: under the right transformations, nonconvex problems that are seemingly intractable can be solved efficiently using convex optimization. We introduce the notion of a Lagrangian dual section of a nonlinear program defined over a topological space, and we use it to give a sufficient condition for a nonconvex optimization problem to have a natural convex reformulation. We emphasize the topological nature of our framework, using only continuity and connectedness properties of a certain Lagrangian formulation of the problem to prove our results. We demonstrate the practical consequences of our framework in a range of applications and by developing new algorithmic methodology. First, we present families of nonconvex problem instances that can be transformed to convex programs in the context of linear inverse spectral problems – which include quadratically constrained quadratic optimization and Stiefel manifold optimization as special cases – as well as unbalanced Procrustes problems. In each of these applications, we both generalize prior results on hidden convexity and provide unifying proofs. For the case of the linear inverse spectral problems, we also present a Lie-theoretic approach that illustrates connections with the Kostant convexity theorem. Second, we introduce new algorithmic ideas that can be used to find globally optimal solutions to both Lagrangian forms of an optimization problem as well as constrained optimization problems when the underlying topological space is a Riemannian manifold.



Keywords:

hidden convexity, Lagrangian, quadratically constrained quadratic programming, Stiefel manifold optimization, unbalanced orthogonal Procrustes, Kostant convexity

1
Introduction

In global optimization, a major distinction has been made between ‘convex’ and ‘nonconvex’ optimization problems for decades
[
26
]
.
While there is a rich theory for how to solve convex optimization problems to global optimality, the analogous theories for nonconvex optimization are distinctly weaker and more ad hoc.
However, convexity is not invariant under changes of variables, meaning that a problem may be convex in one presentation, but not in another.
Hence, it is of interest to identify situations in which a given problem can be
reparametrized
in such a way that the resulting problem is a convex optimization problem in the new parameters. When this is possible, and the resulting convex optimization problem is tractable, we say that the problem has a convex reparametrization. This phenomenon of problems having such a convex reparametrization is also referred to as ‘hidden convexity’ in the literature.

In studying this phenomenon, it is helpful to identify properties of convex optimization problems which will continue to hold under reparametrization. One such property is a tendency for
parametric families
of convex optimization problems to have solutions which vary continuously with the parameter. This property is prominently exploited by a number of convex optimization algorithms, such as interior point methods and augmented Lagrangian methods
[
23
]
. These methods associate a parametric family of optimization problems to a problem – such as a Lagrangian formulation of a nonlinear program – and then change that parameter slowly as the algorithm progresses. The continuity of the solutions of the problems in the family with respect to the underlying parameter then yields effective algorithms based on solution-tracking.

In this paper, we show a sort of converse to this property – if there exist continuously-varying solutions to a certain parametric family of problems associated to a constrained nonlinear program, then we can provide a natural convex reformulation of the constrained program. In particular, this property can be defined over an arbitrary topological space, as the definition will only involve topological aspects of the problem. We will show the practical utility of our results by exhibiting many examples satisfying our criterion arising from fundamental optimization problems, such as quadratically constrained quadratic optimization problems and Stiefel manifold optimization problems. Our methods yield new results on the exactness of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations for these problems. We also give applications to the less well-understood unbalanced Procrustes problem.

We will formalize the notion of a continuous family of solutions to an optimization problem using the notion of a
bundle
, which is ubiquitous in topology and geometry. To a constrained (possibly nonconvex) optimization problem, we will associate a
Lagrangian dual bundle
, which describes how the maximizer of a Lagrangian formulation of the problem varies as the Lagrange multipliers change. We then show how properties of that bundle can have geometric consequences for convex reparametrizations and the development of effective algorithms. We present one such algorithm which recovers global optima of sectioned problems via a novel combination of homotopy continuation and optimization on Riemannian manifolds. We also outline how the ellipsoid algorithm applies in this setting.

1.1
Preliminaries and Main Result

We give here the general setting of our analysis, and state our main result. We fix a topological space
{\mathcal{M}}
to be the domain of our optimization problem.
As examples,
{\mathcal{M}}
may be
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean topology, a manifold, or an algebraic variety.
For our purposes, a constrained optimization problem on
{\mathcal{M}}
is specified by a continuous function
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a constraint vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

V_{f}(c)=\max\{f_{0}(x):f_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,f_{k}(x)=c_{k},x\in{\mathcal{M}}\}.

(opt-Main)

We refer to
V_{f}
as the
optimal value function
of
f
. We assign the value
-\infty
to
V_{f}(c)
in case
\{x\in{\mathcal{M}}:f_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,f_{k}(x)=c_{k}\}
is empty.

The continuous function

f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
also induces the
Lagrangian
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{L}(t,x)=\langle t,f(x)\rangle,

(1.1)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual inner product on Euclidean space
and

\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}:t_{0}\geq 0,t\neq 0\}.

The
Lagrangian dual bundle
is defined to be following subset of the domain of
\mathcal{L}
:

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{(t,x):x\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{z\in{\mathcal{M}}}\langle t,f(z)\rangle\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}\times\mathcal{M}.

(1.2)

We define a
Lagrangian dual section of
\mathcal{B}
to be a continuous function
D:\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
with the property that
(t,D(t))\in\mathcal{B}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
. That is, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
,
D(t)
is a maximizer of the function

\ell_{t}:{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\ell_{t}(x)=\langle t,f(x)\rangle,

(1.3)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
.

\pi:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
, and a section would be right inverse of

. Our setup is equivalent, but simplifies the concept for our purposes.
In

, a related notion was defined, where it was termed ‘continuous maximization’.

If a Lagrangian dual section exists, we say the function

is
Lagrangian dual sectioned
, or simply
sectioned
.

Our main theorem shows a connection between the property of
f
being sectioned and a convex reparametrization of the optimization problem defined by
V_{f}
:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
{\mathcal{M}}
is compact and
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is Lagrangian dual sectioned, then
the optimal value function
V_{f}
is concave.

We prove this result in

.
An easy corollary of this result is the existence of a reformulation of the optimization problem (
opt-Main
) as a convex optimization problem; we will include a proof in
Appendix

Corollary 1.1
.

If
{\mathcal{M}}
is compact and
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is Lagrangian dual sectioned, then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

V_{f}(c)=\max\{y_{0}:y_{1}=c_{1},\dots,y_{k}=c_{k},y\in\conv(f({\mathcal{M}}))\}.

The concavity for
V_{f}
enables us to use the tools of convex optimization to approach the solution of (
opt-Main
).
Depending on the particular application, it can be possible to reformulate the optimization problem (
opt-Main
) as a semidefinite program or to use methods such as the ellipsoid algorithm. We illustrate these possibilities in the examples given throughout the introduction.

Our broader aim is to illustrate deeper connections between topology and convexity. For example, we note that
Theorem

2.1
, which has the same conclusion as

Theorem

1.1
when
k=1
, gives a topological condition on
\mathcal{B}
which is simpler to check in some cases than the existence of a Lagrangian dual section. We also note that our results on convex reformulations for linear inverse spectral problems show connections between convex reformulations for linear inverse spectral problems and the Radon-Hurwitz numbers which appear prominently in algebraic topology.

For the rest of the introduction, we will give examples of fundamental optimization problems in
Sections

1.2
and
1.3
.
We will then preview our main results on whether these optimization problems correspond to sectioned functions.
In
Section

1.4
, we will also consider algorithms which can be used to solve sectioned problems, both in their constrained and Lagrangian formulations.

1.2
Linear Inverse Spectral Problems

We will outline two common classes of optimization problems involving matrices:
quadratically constrained quadratic programs
and
linear Stiefel manifold optimization problems
. Both are known to be NP-hard in general
[
30
,
4
]
. We will regard both of these problems as being special cases of more general ‘linear inverse spectral problems’, which involve optimization over matrices with fixed eigenvalues or singular values. Indeed, it is possible to view these problems in an even broader context as optimization problems over the orbits of a Lie group action. For the sake of keeping our exposition elementary, we will only outline the connections to Lie theory in
Section

3.4
.

We will give a criterion for natural convex relaxations of these problems to be tight using the notion of
noncrossing
subspaces, which we will define later in this section.
We defer the proofs, which use the notion of Lagrangian dual sections, to
Section

3
.

1.2.1  QCQPs and Linear Inverse Eigenvalue Problems

Quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQPs) are found in a wide range of applications including engineering
[
18
]
, statistics
[
7
]
, and combinatorics
[
14
]
.
They are formulated as follows:
For
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, consider

\max\{x^{\intercal}A_{0}x:x^{\intercal}A_{1}x=c_{1},\;\dots,\;x^{\intercal}A_{k}x=c_{k},x\in S^{n-1}\}.

(opt-QCQP)

This is a special case of (
opt-Main
) over the space
S^{n-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{\intercal}x=1\}
, where the function
f
is
f(x)=(x^{\intercal}A_{0}x,\dots,x^{\intercal}A_{k}x)
.

A QCQP can also be expressed in terms of rank one matrices: (
opt-QCQP
) is equivalent to the optimization problem

\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{(1,0,\dots,0)}\},

(1.4)

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{(1,0,\dots,0)}=\{xx^{\intercal}:x\in S^{n-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
.
The notation
{\mathcal{M}}_{(1,0,\dots,0)}
is meant to suggest an alternative characterization of this set as the set of matrices whose eigenvalues are
1
with multiplicity 1 and 0 with multiplicity
n-1
. This motivates us to consider the more general domain
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, which is the set of
n\times n
symmetric matrices with prescribed eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
.
A
linear inverse eigenvalue problem
is a problem of the form

\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\},

(opt-LIEP)

where
\lambda
is
fixed in advance. Such problems are studied in their own right
[
8
,
20
]
.

A standard tool for understanding these problems is that of taking a convex relaxation, as these produce upper bounds on the optimal value of the problem.
The natural convex relaxation of (
opt-LIEP
) is given by

\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in\conv({\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}),

(cr-LIEP)

where
\conv({\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda})
is known as the
Schur-Horn orbitope
, which has an efficient spectrahedral representation
[
28
]
. For example,

\conv({\mathcal{M}}_{(1,0,\dots,0)})=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}:\mathrm{Tr}(X)=1,X\succeq 0\}.

It is of considerable interest to understand when this convex relaxation is tight,
We will give a criterion for the convex relaxation to recover the true optimal value of the problem using the notion of a
noncrossing subspace
, which was originally studied in
[
13
]
.
To define a noncrossing subspace, first recall that an eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix is degenerate if its corresponding eigenspace has dimension greater than one, and otherwise we say that the eigenvalue is nondegenerate.

Definition 1.1
.

A linear subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{n\times n}
is
noncrossing
if every nonzero matrix in
\mathcal{S}
has only nondegenerate eigenvalues.
More generally, we will say a linear subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{n\times n}
is
\ell
-weakly noncrossing
if every nonzero matrix in
\mathcal{S}
has the property that its
\ell
largest eigenvalues are all nondegenerate.

Now we state our result for linear inverse eigenvalue problems.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{n\times n}
span a
\ell
-weakly noncrossing subspace, and
\lambda
satisfies the condition that
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{\ell+1}
for all
i>\ell
.
Then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the values of (
cr-LIEP
) and (
opt-LIEP
) agree, i.e. the convex relaxation of the linear inverse eigenvalue problem is tight.

We prove this in
Section

3.2
.
This result is interesting to a practitioner because it provides a new toolkit for showing that convex relaxations of structured optimization problems give a tight answer.
It also recovers
known results when
k=1

[
2
]

and
\lambda=(1,0,\dots,0)
(
[
15
]
).

1.2.2  Linear Inverse Singular Value Problems and Stiefel Manifold Optimization

Analogously to the previous subsection on linear inverse eigenvalue problems, we will consider the inverse singular value problem. For
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
and
n\geq m
, we will let
{\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma}
be the space of
n\times m
real matrices whose
m
singular values are the entries of
\sigma
. If
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is not full rank, then we consider 0 to be a singular value of
X
.

A linear inverse singular value problem for

A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is defined as

\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in{\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma}\}.

(opt-LISV)

Many important problems are special cases of this one.
For example, if
\sigma=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the all one’s vector, then
{\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma}
is the Stiefel manifold of matrices
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
such that
X^{\intercal}X=I
.
For this special case, we will use the notation

\St^{n,m}={\mathcal{M}}_{(1,\dots,1)}^{(n,m)}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}:X^{\intercal}X=I\}.

Optimization over
\St^{n,m}
is used extensively in robotics
[
24
,
10
,
27
]
and chemistry
[
5
]
.

The linear inverse singular value problem also includes bilinear optimization, i.e. optimization problems of the form

\max\{x^{\intercal}A_{0}y:x^{\intercal}A_{1}y=c_{1},\dots,x^{\intercal}A_{k}y=c_{k},x\in S^{n-1},y\in S^{m-1}\},

(1.5)

as the special case where
\sigma=(1,0,\dots,0)
.

Once again we can consider the convex relaxation,

\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in\conv({\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma})\},

(cr-LISV)

and ask when it is tight. The set
\conv({\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma})
is the
Fan orbitope
in the language of
[
28
, Section 4.3]
, and has a spectrahedral representation.

In line with the previous section, we define some notions of singularly noncrossing subspaces.
For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m\leq n
,
we say that a singular value
\sigma\geq 0
of
A
is degenerate if
\sigma^{2}
is a degenerate eigenvalue of
A^{\intercal}A
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say a linear subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing
if the
\ell
largest singular values of every nonzero matrix in
\mathcal{S}
are nondegenerate.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
m\leq n
, and suppose that
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}
span a
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
for
\ell<m
.
If either of the following two conditions hold, then (
opt-LISV
) and (
cr-LISV
) agree, i.e. the convex relaxation for the inverse singular value problem is tight.

•

If
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a vector with entries in descending order so that
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i>\ell
.

•

If
\text{span}\{A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\}
in addition to being
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing contains no nonzero matrix of rank less than
m
, and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a vector with entries in descending order so that
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{\ell+1}
for
i>\ell
.

We prove this in
Section

3.3
.
In
[
30
]
, it was shown that if
k\leq n-m
, and if
\sigma=(1,\dots,1)
, then for any
A_{0},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, for
f=(\langle A_{0},X\rangle,\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle)
, then the convex relaxation is tight. This result can be seen to follow from
Theorem

1.3
in the case in which
\sigma=(1,\dots,1)
, as we note in
Remark

4
. In
[
21
, Theorem 11.5]
, the result when
k=1
was also shown.

The applicability of our results depends on how commonplace (singularly) noncrossing subspaces are. There are many interesting examples, as we will discuss in the following remark.

Remark 1

(Comments on the generality of our results)
.

Whether or not there exists a (singularly) noncrossing subspace of dimension
k+1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
is an interesting question characterized in
[
13
]
. If
k=1
, then the Von Neumann-Wigner crossing rule
[
32
]
states that almost all (i.e. all but a set of measure 0) pairs
A_{0},A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
span a noncrossing subspace. If
n
is a power of
2
, then such
k
dimensional subspaces only exist when
k\leq\log_{2}(n)
, although the largest dimension of a noncrossing subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
dimension is not monotonic in
n
, and is given by the so-called Radon-Hurwitz number.
On the other hand, there exists a
k
-dimensional 1-weakly noncrossing subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
if and only if
k<n

[
12
]
, and this is the case which is relevant for QCQPs.
Moreover, the set of
d
-dimensional
k
-weakly noncrossing subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
can be thought of as an open subset of an appropriate Grassmannian, and so this phenomenon is not restricted to a set of measure zero.

Remark 2

(Connection to Kostant convexity theorem)
.

We can generalize our results to domains
{\mathcal{M}}
which arise as the orbit of a single point under the action of a polar Lie group orbit, using Kostant’s convexity theorem
[
19
]
, which we discuss in greater detail in
Section

3.4
.
This has applications to problems related to these matrix optimization problems over domains such as the special orthogonal group or settings involving complex numbers, and it is possible to generalize our results using appropriately generalized notions of noncrossing subspaces. We will not write out the analysis in these settings here for the sake of brevity, though convex reparametrizations in the context of the special orthogonal group was analyzed in
[
25
]
.

1.3
Unbalanced Orthogonal Procrustes Problems

The Unbalanced Procrustes Problem (UPP) is well studied
[
34
,
33
]
,
and has resisted attempts to understand its computational complexity. In particular, it not known whether this problem is NP-hard or if it admits a polynomial time algorithm.
If
n>m
, then a pair of matrices,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, give an instance of the UPP:

\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\left\{\|U^{\intercal}X-W^{\intercal}\|^{2}:X\in\St^{n,m}\right\}.

(opt-UPP-orig)

Here,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Frobenius norm on matrices.

This problem has a geometric description:
if we interpret the columns of
U
as being points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then (
opt-UPP-orig
) asks us to find a rotation and projection of these points so that their images are as close as possible to the points corresponding to the columns of
W
.

Figure 1
:
An example of the UPP with
(n,m)=(3,2)
: the columns of
U
can be thought of as elements of a point cloud in 3D, and the columns of
V
can be thought of as elements of a 2D point cloud. The goal of the UPP is to find an orthogonal projection of the 3D point cloud that best matches the 2D point cloud.

We will reparametrize this problem in terms of two matrices
A
and
B
, where
A=-UU^{\intercal}
and
B=2UW^{\intercal}
.
Lemma

4.1
shows that the UPP is equivalent to

\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\langle B,X\rangle+\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle.

(opt-UPP)

This reformulation highlights the fact that we can view the objective as being an instantiation of the Lagrangian associated to the function

f:\St^{n,m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\;f(X)=(\langle B,X\rangle,\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle).

In particular, the optimal value of the UPP problem is
\displaystyle\max_{X\in\St^{n,m}}f_{0}(X)+f_{1}(X)
.

When
m=1
, it is well known that this problem is equivalent to the
generalized trust region subproblem
of minimizing an inhomogeneous quadratic polynomial on the sphere, which has a tight convex reformulation. We will discuss how we can view this convex reformulation as a consequence of our results in
Example

4.1
, in particular showing that
f
is always sectioned when
m=1.

It is not the case that all instances of the UPP are sectioned, but we will give a criterion in
Theorem

4.1
for which the UPP with
(n,m)=(3,2)
is sectioned. Numerical experiments indicate that if
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
are sampled uniformly at random from the set of matrices with unit Frobenius norms, then
95\%
of these instances correspond to cases in which
f
is sectioned.

1.4
Algorithms

Our results illustrate that the existence of Lagrangian dual sections has strong implications for the geometry of optimization problems such as (
opt-Main
). Assuming that there exists a Lagrangian dual section, it is natural to ask two algorithmic questions: (1) how can we solve problems of the form
\max_{x\in{\mathcal{M}}}\langle t,f(x)\rangle
for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
, and subsequently (2) how can we solve the constrained optimization problems (
opt-Main
), assuming that we can solve (1) efficiently?

When a problem admits a Lagrangian dual section
D,
question (1) above asks for a method to compute
D(t)
for any given value of
t
. One natural approach leverages the continuity of
D
: start with the value of
D(t^{(0)})
for some
t^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
, then track the value of
D(t)
as
t
varies from
t^{(0)}
to its target value. Similarly, there is a natural approach to question (2) about (
opt-Main
): leverage the convexity of
V_{f}
to apply the ellipsoid algorithm from convex optimization.
While these are natural approaches to problems (1) and (2) enumerated above, we will need additional structure to rigorously analyze concrete instantiations of these approaches. This is because there is no way to quantify distances between points in a general topological space
{\mathcal{M}}
, and we will need quantitative bounds on distances to control the error incurred by our algorithms. To this end, for our algorithmic results, we will assume that
{\mathcal{M}}
is in fact a Riemannian manifold, and that we can bound the first and second derivatives Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(t,x)
. It turns out that this is enough structure for us to give both algorithms to solve (1) and (2), as well as rigorous mathematical analyses of those algorithms.

In
Section

5
, we will give some background on the theory of Riemannian manifolds and manifold optimization, and then use the notion of Lagrangian dual sections to address problems (1) and (2) above. Our main novelty is our ‘path tracking algorithm’ for solving problems of the form
\max_{x\in{\mathcal{M}}}\langle t,f(x)\rangle
, which we call CHORD.
This algorithm is similar in spirit to existing path tracking / homotopy continuation methods, yet differs significantly from them—in stark contrast to existing methods which either lack convergence guarantees or track paths for all first-order critical points, we can recover the global optimum of a sectioned problem by tracking a single path.
Our approach hinges on the special properties of sectioned problems, and bootstraps a mature convergence theory for Riemannian gradient descent (RGD).
In particular, while our methods ensure a global optimum for a sectioned problem, RGD does not, and it may become trapped in local optima. We give an example for the UPP described above in which CHORD returns a near-global optimizer, while RGD does not in
Example

5.1
.

2
Convexity and Topology

The main purpose of this section is to lay out some fundamental connections between elementary notions from topology and convexity.
In particular, we will emphasize the topological properties of the Lagrangian dual bundle and how they imply the convexity of the optimal value function
V_{f}(c)
defined by (
opt-Main
).

Our first topological property is the existence of a Lagrangian dual section, which our main result
Theorem

1.1
shows implies the existence of a natural convex reformulation for the problem. We prove this in
Section

2.1
.
In
Section

2.2
, we also show that if the maximizer of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(t,x)
is unique for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
, then
f
is sectioned. We also show that this property of having unique maximizers is preserved under a natural operation in
Lemma

2.5
.
Finally, in
Section

2.3
the connectedness of the fibers of the Lagrangian bundle are also shown to imply the existence of a natural convex relaxation. Here, the fibers of the Lagrangian bundle are the sets of the form
{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\{(t,x):x\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\langle t,f(x)\rangle\}
.

2.1
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

In finite dimensions, the convex hull of a compact set is compact
[
3
, Corollary 2.4]
, and in particular, this implies that if
{\mathcal{M}}
is compact and
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is continuous, then
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
is compact. We may also assume that
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
is full dimensional, i.e. that it contains an interior point, as otherwise, it would be contained in an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
, and we could just treat
f
as a map from
{\mathcal{M}}
to that affine subspace.

In this section, we also use the notation
f_{\operatorname{CM}}(x)
for
(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{k}(x))
, where
\operatorname{CM}
is meant to abbreviate ‘constraint map’.

We aim to prove that the Lagrangian dual of (
opt-Main
)
satisfies a version of
strong duality
. To be precise, we define the function

\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},x\right)-\langle c,\lambda\rangle,

and show that for all
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=V_{f}(c).

(2.1)

This function
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
is
analogous to the double Fenchel conjugate of
V_{f}
, and since it is the pointwise minimum of linear functions in
c
, it is concave.
Thus, proving
the equality
(
2.1
) implies that
V_{f}(c)
is concave.

At a high level, we will prove
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=V_{f}(c)
by showing that

V_{f}(c)
is bounded above and below by
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.
The bound
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\geq V_{f}(c)
is a standard consequence of weak duality.
Proving the second bound,
V_{f}(c)\geq\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
, involves using the Lagrangian dual section to show the existence some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
where the inner maximization gives a value of at most
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
. In order to show the existence of such a
\lambda
, we will employ some ideas from algebraic topology.

A lower bound on
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.

We may assume that assume that
V_{f}(c)\neq-\infty
.
By compactness of
{\mathcal{M}}
there exists a point
x_{c}\in{\mathcal{M}}
such that

f(x_{c})=\begin{pmatrix}V_{f}(c)\\
c\end{pmatrix}.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},x_{c}\right)-\langle c,\lambda\rangle\;=\;

\displaystyle f_{0}(x_{c})+\langle f_{\operatorname{CM}}(x_{c}),\lambda\rangle-\langle c,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle V_{f}(c).

Since this holds for any
\lambda
, the infimum over
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfies the upper bound:

\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},x\right)-\langle c,\lambda\rangle\geq V_{f}(c).

Towards an upper bound on
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.

Let
D(t)
be the Lagrangian dual section for
f
, guaranteed in the hypotheses of
Theorem

1.1
.
We want to show that for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for which
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\neq-\infty
we have
V_{f}(c)\geq\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
. The following lemma reduces this problem to that of finding some
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
so that
f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t))=c
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
such that
f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t))=c
,
then
V_{f}(c)\geq\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.

Proof.

If such a
t
exists, then we are in one of two cases.
In the first case,
t_{0}>0
. In this situation, we let

\lambda=\frac{1}{t_{0}}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})}

and get the desired inequality:

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{f}(c)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},x\right)-\langle c,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},D(t)\right)-\langle c,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=f_{0}(D(t))+\langle f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t)),\lambda\rangle-\langle c,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle\leq V_{f}(c).

For the case
t_{0}=0
, we can apply a sequence of the same inequalities with

\lambda^{(j)}=j\cdot(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Letting
j\to\infty
gives the desired lower bound on
V_{f}(c)
.
∎

Proving surjectivity.

Completing the proof
reduces to showing that for any
c
such that
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\neq-\infty
, there exists some
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}

with

f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t))=c
.
The separating hyperplane theorem implies that

\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\neq-\infty\}=\conv f_{\operatorname{CM}}({\mathcal{M}}).

Letting
\mathcal{C}=\conv f_{\operatorname{CM}}({\mathcal{M}})
, the main theorem follows from the next result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
H_{k}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}_{0+}:\|t\|=1\}
. The map
\phi:H_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
defined by
\phi(t)=f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t))
is surjective.

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving
Proposition

2.1
. We will be using some topological notions in order to accomplish this.
Note that
H_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}
are each homeomorphic to the closed ball of dimension
k
,
B^{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\|x\|\leq 1\}
, so the map
\phi
can be regarded, up to homeomorphism, as a continuous map from the unit ball to itself. This is the setting of
Lemma

2.2
from algebraic topology, which will be the way we ultimately show the surjectivity of
\phi
.

Before stating
Lemma

2.2
, we will need to recall the notion of a homotopy equivalence, which is also defined in
[
16
, Chapter 0]
.
If
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are topological spaces, and
g_{1},g_{2}:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are continuous maps, then a
homotopy
from
g_{1}
to
g_{2}
is given by a continuous map
H:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\times[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
such that
H(x,0)=g_{1}(x)
and
H(x,1)=g_{2}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. A
homotopy inverse
for a map
g:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a map
h:{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
so that there is a homotopy from the composition
g\circ h
to the identity on
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and there is a homotopy from the composition
h\circ g
to the identity on
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Finally, we say that
g:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a
homotopy equivalence
if there exists a homotopy inverse for
g
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
\phi:B^{k}\rightarrow B^{k}
is a continuous map. Let
\partial\,\phi
be the restriction of
\phi
to the boundary
\partial B^{k}
and suppose that the image of
\partial{\phi}
is contained in
\partial B^{k}
. If
\partial{\phi}:\partial B^{k}\rightarrow\partial B^{k}
is a homotopy equivalence, then
\phi
is surjective.

The proof of
Lemma

2.2
is very similar to the proof to the proof of the Brouwer fixed point theorem, and we will include it for completeness in
Appendix

A
.

We apply
Lemma

2.2
to
\phi=f_{\operatorname{CM}}\circ D
to show
Proposition

2.1
. We first need to show that the map
\partial{\phi}:S^{k-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
maps onto the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
(where
S^{k-1}=\partial H_{k}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}:t_{0}=0,\|t\|=1\}
). This is clear because if
t\in S^{k-1}
, then
D(t)
maximizes the linear function

\langle t,f(x)\rangle=\langle(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}),f_{\operatorname{CM}}(x)\rangle

over
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
, and thus
f_{\operatorname{CM}}(D(t))
must be on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
.

It remains to show that
\partial{\phi}
is a homotopy equivalence between
S^{k-1}
and
\partial\mathcal{C}
. We do this in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a full dimensional convex set, and that
\partial{\phi}:S^{k-1}\rightarrow\partial\mathcal{C}
satisfies the property that for any
t\in S^{k-1}
,

\partial{\phi}(t)\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{y\in\mathcal{C}}\langle t,y\rangle.

Then
\partial{\phi}
is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

Fix some
y_{0}
in the interior of
\mathcal{C}
, and let
\psi:\partial\mathcal{C}\rightarrow S^{k-1}
be defined by
\psi(y)=\frac{y-y_{0}}{\|y-y_{0}\|}
. This map
\psi
is clearly continuous, and standard results from convex geometry show that
\psi
is a bijection. Since
S^{k-1}
is a compact Hausdorff space, this implies that
\psi
is a homeomorphism. We claim that
\psi
a homotopy inverse to
\partial{\phi}
.

To see this, we first define the map

H:S^{k-1}\times[0,1]\rightarrow S^{k-1},\quad(t,s)\mapsto\frac{st+(1-s)\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))}{\|st+(1-s)\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))\|},

which projects a convex combination of
t
and
\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))
onto the sphere.
To show that
H
is defined, we must show
st+(1-s)\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))\neq 0
. To see this, note first the equality

\langle t,st+(1-s)\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))\rangle\,=\,s\|t\|^{2}+(1-s)\frac{\langle t,\partial\phi(t)-y_{0}\rangle}{\|\partial{\phi}(t)-y_{0}\|}.

(2.2)

Since
\partial{\phi}(t)
maximizes
\langle t,y\rangle
on
\mathcal{C}
and
y_{0}
is in the interior of
\mathcal{C}
, we have
\langle t,\partial{\phi}(t)-y_{0}\rangle>0
for all
t\in S^{k-1}
. Thus, (
2.2
) is positive for all
t,s
, and in particular,
st+(1-s)\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))\neq 0
.
Note that
H(t,0)=\psi(\partial{\phi}(t))
and
H(t,1)=t
, so this shows that
H
is a homotopy from
\psi\circ\partial\phi
to the identity map.

Since
\psi
is a homeomorphism, we can conjugate
H
by the map
\psi
to obtain a homotopy from
\partial{\phi}\circ\psi
to the identity, i.e. we may set

H^{\prime}:\partial\mathcal{C}\times I\rightarrow\partial\mathcal{C},\;H^{\prime}(y,s)=\psi^{-1}(H(\psi(y),s)).

This is also continuous, and it satisfies the property that

H^{\prime}(y,0)=\psi^{-1}(\psi(\partial\phi(\psi(y))))=\partial{\phi}(\psi(y))

and
H^{\prime}(y,1)=y
.
This shows that
\partial{\phi}
is a homotopy equivalence.
∎

2.2
Unique Maximization and Lagrangian Dual Sections

Here, we will give a simple criterion for a function to be sectioned.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that

f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is
uniquely maximized
, if for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
,
the function
\ell_{t}:{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R}
,

x\mapsto\langle t,f(x)\rangle

has a unique maximizer.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix a compact metric space
{\mathcal{M}}
and a continuous function
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
and its associated Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(t,x)
. If
f
is uniquely maximized, then
f
is sectioned.

Proof.

Let
D(t)
be the unique maximizer of the function
\langle t,f(x)\rangle
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
.
Because
{\mathcal{M}}
is a metric space, it suffices to show that if
\left(t^{(i)}\right)_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
and
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}t^{(i)}=t_{*}
, then
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}D(t^{(i)})=D(t_{*})
.
We may assume that
\{D(t^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
in fact has a limit
x_{*}
by passing to a subsequence and using the compactness of
{\mathcal{M}}
. We need to prove that
x_{*}=D(t_{*})
.

Since
D(t_{*})
is the unique maximizer of
\langle t_{*},f(x)\rangle
, it suffices to establish the claim that
x_{*}
also maximizes this function.
That is, for any
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we wish to argue that

\langle t_{*},f(x_{*})-f(x)\rangle\geq 0.

For this, note that

\langle t_{*},f(x_{*})-f(x)\rangle=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\langle t^{(i)},f(D(t^{(i)}))-f(x)\rangle.

We have that
\langle t^{(i)},f(D(t^{(i)}))-f(x)\rangle>0
for all
i
by the definition of
D(t^{(i)})
, so that the limit is also nonnegative. This proves the claim.
∎

A property of uniquely maximized functions is that they behave well under composition with monotonic increasing functions.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
\tilde{f}=(\tilde{f}_{0},\tilde{f}_{1},\dots,\tilde{f}_{k+1}):{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is uniquely maximized. If
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is any monotonically increasing strictly concave function, then the function
f(x)=(g(\tilde{f}_{0}(x)),\tilde{f}_{1}(x)\dots,\tilde{f}_{k+1}(x))
is uniquely maximized.

Proof.

We have seen that the function

V(c)=\max\{\tilde{f}_{0}(x):\tilde{f}_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,\tilde{f}_{k}(x)=c_{k},x\in{\mathcal{M}}\}

is concave because
f
is sectioned.
If we let

\widetilde{V}(c)=\max\{g(\tilde{f}_{0}(x)):\tilde{f}_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,\tilde{f}_{k}(x)=c_{k},x\in{\mathcal{M}}\},

then it follows from the monotonicity of
g
that
\widetilde{V}(c)=g(V(c))
. Since
g
is both monotonically increasing and strictly concave, the function
\widetilde{V}(c)
is also strictly concave.

Fix a
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
, and consider the maximum of
\langle t,f(x)\rangle
. This is equal to the maximum value of the function

p(c)=t_{0}g(V(c))+\langle(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}),(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\rangle,

over
c
.
Because
g(V(c))
is strictly concave,
p(c)
is strictly concave, and in particular,
p(c)
has a unique maximizer, say
c^{*}
.

We now want to claim that there is a unique
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
so that
f(x)=(V(c^{*}),c^{*})
. To see this, note that
(V(c^{*}),c^{*})
is on the upper boundary of
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
, and therefore, by the separating hyperplane theorem, there exists a
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\max\langle t^{\prime},f(x)\rangle=\langle t^{\prime},(V(c^{*}),c^{*})\rangle
. By unique maximization of
f
, we have therefore that there is a unique
x^{*}
maximizing
\langle t^{\prime},f(x)\rangle
on
{\mathcal{M}}
, and therefore there is a unique
x^{*}
mapping to
(V(c^{*}),c^{*})
, which is also therefore the unique maximizer of
\langle t,\tilde{f}(x)\rangle
.
∎

2.3
Connectedness of the Fibers of the Lagrangian Bundle

In the special case of
k=1
constraint, there is a simple topological condition on the fibers of the Lagrangian bundle of
f
which implies the existence of a convex reparametrization: namely, that all of the fibers are connected.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a compact topological space, and
\mathcal{B}
be the Lagrangian bundle associated to
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as defined in (
1.2
). If for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{2}
the fiber

{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\{x\in{\mathcal{M}}:(t,x)\in\mathcal{B}\}
is connected, then the function
V_{f}(c)
is concave.

Proof.

Let

\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=\max\{y_{0}:y_{1}=c_{1},y\in\conv f({\mathcal{M}})\}.

We will again argue that
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)=V_{f}(c)
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We note that
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\geq V_{f}(c)
for all
c
, since
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})\supseteq f({\mathcal{M}})
.
Fix some
c
so that
\widehat{V}_{f}(c)\neq-\infty
, and
\hat{y}\in\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
satisfying
\hat{y}_{1}=c_{1}
and also
\hat{y}_{0}=\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.
It is clear that if
\hat{y}
cannot be in the interior of
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
and still maximize
y_{0}
subject to
y_{1}=c_{1}
. It follows that
\hat{y}
must be on the boundary of
{\mathcal{M}}
, and therefore, it is contained in a face of
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
of dimension at most one. If
\hat{y}
is contained in a zero dimensional face of
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
, then it is an extreme point, and hence in
f({\mathcal{M}})
, and this implies that
V_{f}(c)\geq\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.

Assume now that
\hat{y}
is contained in a one dimensional face of
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
, which must be a line segment
[y^{(1)},y^{(2)}]
spanned by two extreme points
y^{(1)},y^{(2)}\in f({\mathcal{M}})
.
Thus, there exists some
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}
so that the linear function
\langle t,y\rangle
on
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
attains its maximum value
v
on
[y^{(1)},y^{(2)}]
.
Since
{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\{x\in{\mathcal{M}}:\langle t,f(x)\rangle=v\}
is connected, so is the set
f({\mathcal{M}}_{t})\subseteq[y^{(1)},y^{(2)}].

Moreover, since
y^{(1)},y^{(2)}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{t},
we have
f({\mathcal{M}}_{t})=[y^{(1)},y^{(2)}].

In particular,
\hat{y}\in f({\mathcal{M}}_{t})
, so that
V_{f}(c)=\widehat{V}_{f}(c)
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Theorem

2.1
can be used to give a simple proof of the Schur-Horn theorem for
3\times 3
symmetric matrices.
It also gives a simple proof of the fact that, for example, if
f:S^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a quadratic map
f(x)=(x^{\intercal}A_{0}x,x^{\intercal}A_{1}x)
for symmetric matrices
A_{0},A_{1}
, then
V_{f}(c)
is concave. In this case, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is not hard to see that the fiber of the Lagrangian bundle at
t
is given by

\{(t,x):x\in S^{n-1},x\text{ is an eigenvector of }t_{0}A_{0}+t_{1}A_{1}\text{ with maximum eigenvalue}\},

which is clearly connected. Similar simple proofs also apply in the settings of inverse eigenvalue and singular value problems with one constraint.

3
Lagrangian Dual Sections for Linear Inverse Spectral Problems

This section explains connections between the various notions of noncrossing subspaces and Lagrangian dual sections. These noncrossing subspaces
are related to representations of Clifford algebras and have been studied in physics
[
13
,
32
]
.
We show that these noncrossing subspaces are intimately connected to the exactness of certain SDP for linear inverse spectral problems.

We prove
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
by showing that certain maps associated to the linear inverse eigenvalue and singular value problems are sectioned. We prove these results in
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
in
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
respectively.

We primarily focus on the examples of linear inverse spectral problems because they are of the greatest interest in optimization, and also because these examples can be proven using only well known facts about linear algebra. We will note though that we can give a unified proof of both of these results in the context of polar representations of Lie groups using the Kostant convexity theorem (and its variants). We will discuss this approach and the common generalization of our results which is afforded by this approach in
Section

3.4
.

3.1
Convex Reparametrizations of Linear Inverse Spectral Problems

We recall from
Definition

1.1
the notion of an (
\ell
-weakly) noncrossing subspace, and from
Definition

1.2
its singular-value analogue.
If
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, we will let
\lambda_{1}(X)\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}(X)
denote the eigenvalues of
X
(accounting for multiplicity). Similarly, for
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m\leq n
, let
\sigma_{1}(X)\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{m}(X)
denote the singular values of
X
.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we recall

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}:\forall i=1,\dots,n,\;\lambda_{i}(X)=\lambda_{i}\}.

Similarly, for
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

{\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}:\forall i=1,\dots,m,\;\sigma_{i}(X)=\sigma_{i}\}.

The following proposition shows that if
f
is a linear map, then the concavity of
V_{f}
is sufficient to show that replacing
{\mathcal{M}}
with
\conv({\mathcal{M}})
in the constrained optimization problem (
opt-Main
) gives the same optimal value function. Thus,
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
reduce to showing that
V_{f}
is concave under the hypotheses of the respective theorems, which we will ultimately do by showing that
f
is sectioned.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a compact set and that
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is a linear map. If
V_{f}(c)=\max\{f_{0}(x):f_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,f_{k}(x)=c_{k},x\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
is concave, then

\displaystyle V_{f}(c)=\max\{f_{0}(x):f_{1}(x)=c_{1},\dots,f_{k}(x)=c_{k},x\in\conv({\mathcal{M}})\}.

Proof.

This follows from
Corollary

1.1
by simply noting that if
f
is linear, then
\conv f({\mathcal{M}})=f(\conv({\mathcal{M}}))
.
∎

With
Proposition

3.1
in hand, we may now prove
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
. The arguments follow a similar structure, and are split into the following two subsections.

3.2
Noncrossing Subspaces and Sectioned Linear Inverse Eigenvalue Problems

We will use the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
be linearly independent elements of a
\ell
-weakly noncrossing subspace. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{\ell+1}
for all
i\geq\ell+1
, the map
f:{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
defined below is sectioned:

f(X)=(\langle A_{0},X\rangle,\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle).

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Fix any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k+2}=\dots=\lambda_{n}
, and an arbitrary
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
.
By
Lemma

2.4
, it suffices to show that the function

\ell_{t}(X)=\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{i}A_{i},X\right\rangle

has a unique maximizer in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
.
Since
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
is invariant under orthogonal change of basis, we may assume that the matrix
\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{i}A_{i}
is diagonal, i.e.
\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{i}A_{i}=\text{Diag}(\mu)
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Having assumed that
\{A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\}
span a
\ell
-weakly noncrossing subspace, we may further assume that

\mu_{1}>\dots>\mu_{\ell}\geq\mu_{\ell+1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{n}.

We have

\ell_{t}(X)=\langle\text{Diag}(\mu),X\rangle=\langle\mu,{\rm diag}(X)\rangle,

where
{\rm diag}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the vector of diagonal entries of the matrix
X
.

By the Schur-Horn theorem, the extreme points of
\{{\rm diag}(X):X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}
are vectors of the form
\tau\lambda
, where
\tau
is some permutation of the indices
1,\ldots,n
and
\tau\lambda=(\lambda_{\tau^{-1}(i)})_{i=1}^{n}
. It follows that
\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{y}\{\langle\mu,y\rangle:y={\rm diag}(X),X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}=\lambda
, where

\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell+1}=\dots=\lambda_{n}.

In particular, we have that any maximizer of
\ell_{t}
over
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
must satisfy
{\rm diag}(X)=\lambda
, which clearly implies that
X=\text{Diag}(\lambda)
is the unique maximizer of
\ell_{t}
.
∎

Combining
Proposition

3.1
and
Lemma

3.1
, we deduce
Theorem

1.2
below.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Let
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
span a
\ell
-weakly noncrossing subspace, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy
\lambda_{\ell+1}=\lambda_{i}
for
i\geq\ell+1
.
We may reduce to the case where
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}
are linearly independent, by linear change of coordinates for both the original and relaxed problems.

Lemma

3.1
then implies that the map
f:{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
defined by
f(X)=(\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}X),\dots,\mathrm{Tr}(A_{k}X))
is sectioned.
We may therefore conclude, by
Proposition

3.1
, that

V_{f}(c)=\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in\conv({\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda})\}.

∎

3.3
Singularly Noncrossing Subspaces and Sectioned Linear Inverse Singular Value Problems

Here is a singular-value analogue of
Lemma

3.1
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq m
, and
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}_{sym}
be linearly independent elements of a
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing subspace.
If either of the following two conditions hold, then the map
f:{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
below is sectioned:

f(X)=(\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}X),\dots,\mathrm{Tr}(A_{k}X)).

•

If
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a vector with entries in descending order so that
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i\geq\ell
.

•

If
\text{span}\{A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\}
, in addition to being
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing, contains no nonzero matrix of rank less than
m
, and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a vector with entries in descending order so that
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{\ell+1}
for
i>\ell
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.2
.

This proof is almost identical to that of
Lemma

3.1
, where the relevant facts about eigenvalues are replaced by singular values.
We now fix
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying the assumptions of the theorem. We want to apply
Lemma

2.4
, which requires showing that the function

\ell_{t}(X)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{i}A_{i}X\right)

has a unique maximizer over the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
.
Since
M_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}
is invariant under the bi-orthogonal action

(U,V)\cdot X=UXV^{T}\quad(U,V)\in O(n)\times O(m),\,\,X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{(n,m)},

we may assume
\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{i}A_{i}=\text{Diag}(\mu)
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Because
\{A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\}
span a
\ell
-weakly singularly noncrossing subspace, we may assume that

\mu_{1}>\dots>\mu_{\ell}>\mu_{\ell+1}\geq\dots\mu_{m}\geq 0.

As in the previous proof, we have that

\ell_{t}(X)=\langle\text{Diag}(\mu),X\rangle=\langle\mu,{\rm diag}(X)\rangle.

We may now apply Fan’s theorem
[
11
]
, which implies that the extreme points of

\{{\rm diag}(X):X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}\}

are precisely those vectors lying in the set

\{S\tau\sigma:S\in\text{Diag}(\{-1,1\}^{m}),\tau\text{ is a permutation of indices }1,\ldots,m\},

where
\tau\sigma=(\sigma_{\tau^{-1}(i)})_{i=1}^{m}
.

If either of the conditions in the statement of the theorem are satisfied, then

\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{y}\{\langle\mu,y\rangle:y={\rm diag}(X),X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}\}=\sigma.

Hence, we have that for any maximizer
X
of
\ell_{t}
,
{\rm diag}(X)=\sigma
, which clearly implies that
X=\text{Diag}(\sigma)
is the unique maximizer of
\ell_{t}
.
∎

Remark 4
.

In the special case of the Stiefel manifold, where
\sigma=(1,\dots,1)
,
our assumption simplifies to requiring that the matrices

.
Therefore, if

is concave for all choices of

provided

, recovering a result of
[
30
]
.

There may exist
k+1

\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
containing no nonzero matrix of rank less than
m
, even when
k
is larger than
n-m

(n,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
for which there exists such a

-dimensional subspace of

containing no nonzero singular matrix is closely related to the Radon-Hurwitz numbers
[
1
]
.

Proof of

.

Let

We may therefore conclude by

3.4
A Lie-theoretic Generalization

We will describe how the linear inverse spectral problems already analyzed in this section can be proven in a more general setting involving the orbits of Lie groups.
To avoid introducing too many Lie-theoretic notions, we will only give a high level overview of these ideas, and simply indicate how the proofs of
Lemma

3.1
and
Lemma

3.2
generalize to the setting of Lie groups, and reference the works
[
19
,
9
,
17
]

for further reading on Kostant’s convexity theorem.

First let us describe how the manifolds
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}^{(n,m)}
can be thought of as the orbits of group actions.
In the case of the
linear inverse eigenvalue problem, the manifold
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
can be thought of as the orbit of the matrix
\text{Diag}(\lambda)
under the action of the group
\text{SO}(n)
, acting by letting
U\cdot X=UXU^{\intercal}
.
For the linear inverse singular value problem with
m<n
, the manifold
{\mathcal{M}}^{(n,m)}_{\sigma}
is the orbit of the matrix
\text{Diag}(\sigma)
under the action of the group
\text{SO}(n)\times\text{SO}(m)
acting by letting
(U,V)\cdot X=UXV^{\intercal}
.

Thus, we see that in both of these cases, there is an underlying group
G
acting orthogonally on some representation
V
for which the domain
{\mathcal{M}}
is the orbit of an element under the group action. Indeed, in both of these examples, the underlying group representation is a ‘polar representation’, which were defined in
[
9
]
. While we will not define a polar representation precisely, we will note that a polar representation involves two pieces of information in addition to the group action: a linear subspace of
V
called the Cartan subspace and a group
W
acting on the Cartan subspace called the Weyl group. In the case of
\text{SO}(n)
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, the Cartan subspace is the space of diagonal matrices, and the group
W
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the permutation group on
n
letters. In the case of
\text{SO}(n)\times\text{SO}(m)
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, the Cartan subspace is the set of diagonal matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and the Weyl group is a semidirect product
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{n}
known as the hyperoctahedral group.

For polar representations, there is a common generalization of the Schur-Horn theorem and Fan’s theorem known as Kostant’s convexity theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Kostant Convexity
[
19
]
)
.

If
(V,\cdot)
is a polar representation of a compact connected Lie group
G
,
\Sigma
is the Cartan subspace, and
W
is the Weyl group, then for any
v\in\Sigma
,

\pi_{\Sigma}(G\cdot v)=\conv(W\cdot v),

where
\pi_{\Sigma}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
\Sigma
.

If we consider the proofs of
Lemma

3.1
and
Lemma

3.2
, we see that we can replace the usage of the Schur-Horn theorem and Fan’s theorem with this result immediately.
It may be noted that our proofs only use use the ‘easy’ part of the Kostant convexity theorem that
\pi_{\Sigma}(G\cdot v)\subseteq\conv(W\cdot v)
, which can be proven using elementary calculus.

In order to fully generalize
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
to the context of polar representations, we would need to define the relevant version of a noncrossing subspace.
To derive this generalization, we will need to recall that if
v\in\Sigma
, then the stabilizer of
v
inside
W
is the subgroup
Stab(v)\subseteq W
of group elements fixing
v
.
We will also require the notion of a closed Weyl chamber. One way to define a closed Weyl chamber in the context of convex geometry is that it is the normal cone to
\conv(W\cdot v)
at
v
for any
v\in\Sigma
which has trivial stabilizer in
W
. For a fixed closed Weyl chamber
C\subseteq\Sigma
, for any
A\in v
there is a unique
\sigma(A)\in C
so that there exists some
g\in G
with
A=g\cdot\sigma(A)
. In our running example of the action of
\text{SO}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, a Weyl chamber inside the set of diagonal matrices is simply the set of diagonal matrices with nonincreasing entries along the diagonal.

With these notions in mind, fix a polar representation
V
with a Cartan subspace
\Sigma
, a Weyl chamber
C\subseteq\Sigma
, and Weyl group
W
. If
H
is a subgroup of
W
, then we will say then that a linear subspace
S\subseteq V
is
H
-noncrossing
if
Stab(\sigma(A))\subseteq H
for every nonzero
A\in S
. In our running example of the action of
\text{SO}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, if
H=\mathfrak{S}_{n-\ell}
is the subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that fixes the first
\ell
elements of
[n]
, then
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
is
H
-noncrossing if and only if
S
is
\ell
-weakly noncrossing. Note that in this example,
H=Stab((v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell},0,\dots,0))
whenever
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
are distinct.

Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
on sectioning can be generalized to the following:

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix a polar representation
V
with a Cartan subspace
\Sigma
, a Weyl chamber
C\subseteq\Sigma
, and Weyl group
W
. Also fix some
v\in C
and let
{\mathcal{M}}=G\cdot v
.
If
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in V
are linearly independent elements of a
Stab(v)
-noncrossing subspace, then the linear map
f(x)=(\langle A_{0},x\rangle,\dots,\langle A_{k},x\rangle)
is sectioned on
{\mathcal{M}}
.

The proof is essentially identical to those given for
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
, but we replace the Schur-Horn and Fan’s theorem with Kostant’s convexity theorem.

Finally, we note that
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
also can be generalized by combining
Theorem

1.1
with
Lemma

3.3
. Indeed, it is even known that in the context of polar representations that
\conv(G\cdot v)
is in fact spectrahedral, and therefore, this convex reformulation can be expressed as a semidefinite program.

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix a polar representation
V
with a Cartan subspace
\Sigma
, a Weyl chamber
C\subseteq\Sigma
, and Weyl group
W
. Also fix some
v\in C
and let
{\mathcal{M}}=G\cdot v
.
If
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}\in V
are linearly independent elements of a
Stab(v)
-noncrossing subspace of
V
, then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\displaystyle\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in{\mathcal{M}}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\langle A_{1},X\rangle=c_{1},\dots,\langle A_{k},X\rangle=c_{k},X\in\conv({\mathcal{M}})\}.

4
Unbalanced Procrustes Problems

This section will analyze the UPP from the perspective of Lagrangian dual bundles.
To restate the UPP, we are given two matrices
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and we want to find the
X\in\St^{n,m}
minimizing the objective
\|U^{\intercal}X-W^{\intercal}\|^{2}.

We often make use of an alternative formulation of this problem in terms of two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
let
A=-UU^{\intercal}
and
B=2UW^{\intercal}
.
Then a matrix
X\in\St^{n,m}
minimizes
\|U^{\intercal}X-W^{\intercal}\|^{2}
over
\St^{n,m}
if and only if
X
maximizes
\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle+\langle B,X\rangle
over
\St^{n,m}
.

Proof.

Note that

\|U^{\intercal}X-W^{\intercal}\|^{2}=\|U^{\intercal}X\|^{2}-2\langle UW^{\intercal},X\rangle+\|W\|^{2}=-\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle-\langle B,X\rangle+\|W\|^{2},

and that the term
\|W\|^{2}
is a constant independent of
X
.
∎

Example 4.1

(UPP and the generalized trust region subproblem)
.

When
m=1
, the UPP is equivalent to the generalized trust region subproblem of minimizing an inhomogeneous quadratic function on the sphere. We will show how this case can be analyzed with our theory.

In the case
m=1
,
St^{n,1}=S^{n-1}
and we wish to maximize

\max_{x\in S^{n-1}}\;\langle B,x\rangle+x^{\intercal}Ax,

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
are given.
We want to show that the map

f:S^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\mapsto(\langle B,x\rangle,x^{\intercal}Ax)

is sectioned as long as
\{A,BB^{\intercal}\}
spans a 1-weakly noncrossing subspace. (Note that this condition is very weak, as there is a dense set of pairs
(A,B)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
satisfying this condition.)
To do this, we first analyze the modified function
\hat{f}(x)=(\langle B,x\rangle^{2},x^{\intercal}Ax)
. This modified function is convenient, because
\langle B,x\rangle^{2}=x^{\intercal}BB^{\intercal}x
, and so the function
\langle t,\hat{f}(x)\rangle=x^{\intercal}(t_{0}A+t_{1}BB^{\intercal})x

is a quadratic form for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
.

The largest eigenvalue of the matrix
t_{0}A+t_{1}BB^{\intercal}
is non-degenerate for all
t_{0}\geq 0
and
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
when
\{A,BB^{\intercal}\}
spans a 1-weakly noncrossing subspace. It follows that the function
\hat{f}
is uniquely maximized.
Since
\sqrt{\cdot}
is strictly concave, we may apply
Lemma

2.5
to see that
f
is also uniquely maximized, and thus sectioned.

4.1
Lagrangian Dual Sections for UPP when
(n,m)=(3,2)

We aim to provide a
condition on
A\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{3\times 3}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
which ensures that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\langle t,f(X)\rangle=t_{0}\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle+t_{1}\langle B,X\rangle

has a unique maximizer over
X\in\St^{3,2}
.
Lemma

2.4
then implies that
f(X)=(\langle B,X\rangle,\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle)
is sectioned in this case.

We will require a technical definition in order to state our claim.

Definition 4.1
.

Fix a pair
(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
.
Let
w\in S^{2}
be an
eigenvector of
A_{0}
associated to the largest eigenvalue.

If
(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
satisfies three
conditions, we will say that this pair is
well-positioned
.

1.

A_{0},A_{1}
span a 2-dimensional 1-weakly noncrossing subspace.

2.

A_{1}
has no eigenvector orthogonal to
w
(and in particular, does not have
w
as an eigenvector).

3.

The matrix
A_{0}+t^{*}A_{1}-\lambda^{*}I
has a positive eigenvalue, where

z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is any nonzero solution to the linear equations
w^{\intercal}A_{1}z^{*}=w^{\intercal}z^{*}=0
,
and where
t^{*},\lambda^{*}
are scalars uniquely determined by
z^{*}
and the following equations:

(A_{0}+t^{*}A_{1}-\lambda^{*}I)z^{*}=0.

Remark 5

(Remark on
Definition

4.1
)
.

Conditions 1 and 2 above are generic in the sense that they hold for all
(A_{0},A_{1})
in a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
.
Condition 3, however, is not generic in this sense.

Assuming that Condition 2 holds, we can see that there is a one dimensional space of solutions to the linear equations
w^{\intercal}A_{1}z=w^{\intercal}z^{*}=0
by noting that
A_{1}w
and
w
are only linearly dependent if
w
is an eigenvector of
A_{1}
, which we have forbidden. For a fixed
z^{*}
, we note that the equation
(A_{0}+t^{*}A_{1}-\lambda^{*}I)z^{*}=0
can be viewed as a system of 3 equations in 2 variables, but where the one of the equations is redundant since
w^{\intercal}(A_{0}+tA_{1}-\lambda I)z^{*}=0
for any
t,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. To see that there is exactly one solution to the linear system, note that if there were infinitely many or zero, then there would need to be a solution to the homogenized system
(tA_{1}-\lambda I)z^{*}=0
, which implies that
z^{*}
is an eigenvector of
A_{1}
. Since
z^{*}
is orthogonal to
w
by definition, this would be a violation of the second assumption.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
, and

B=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
, where
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}\geq 0
.

Suppose the pair
(-BB^{\intercal},A)
is well-positioned, and the linear systems

(tA_{1}+\lambda I-BB^{\intercal})\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}}\\
0\\
\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\end{pmatrix}=2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

(tA_{1}+\lambda I-BB^{\intercal})\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{1-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}}\\
0\\
\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\end{pmatrix}=2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

have no solutions in
t
and
\lambda
.
Then the function

f(X)=(\langle B,X\rangle,\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle)

is uniquely maximized on
\St^{3,2}
. In particular, if
(A,BB^{\intercal})
is well-positioned, then
f
is sectioned.

Note that, for arbitrary
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
, we can put
B
in the form required by
Theorem

4.1
by expressing
A
and
B
in a basis of singular vectors for
B
.

At a high level, the proof of
Theorem

4.1
reduces to proving an analogous property for an inhomogeneous quadratic maximization problem on the sphere. More precisely, we first reduce UPP for well-positioned inputs to an optimization problem over the Grassmannian.
Subsequently, we reduce the
(n,m)=(3,2)
Grassmannian problem to optimization over the real projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}.
We will define these topological spaces when they become relevant to our analysis.

We will establish our results in the order in which they are necessary to prove
Theorem

4.1
, before presenting a proof in
Section

4.4
.

4.2
A Quadratic Maximization Problem

We recall the description of real projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}=S^{n-1}/\{z\sim-z\}
obtained by identifying antipodal points
z
and
-z
on the sphere.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
Q
has no repeated eigenvalues and
w
is not orthogonal to the eigenvector of
Q
with largest eigenvalue,
then the function
\ell(z)=z^{\intercal}Qz+|w^{\intercal}z|
has a unique maximum over
z\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.

Proof.

We aim to analyze the maximizer(s) of the function

\ell(z)=z^{\intercal}Qz+\sqrt{z^{\intercal}ww^{\intercal}z},

where
z\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
Define the set

\mathcal{C}=\{(z^{\intercal}Qz,z^{\intercal}ww^{\intercal}z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:z\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\}.

By Brickman’s Theorem
[
3
, Theorem 14.1]
,
\mathcal{C}
is convex.
We let
\hat{\ell}(c)=c_{0}+\sqrt{c_{1}}
.
Then
\ell(z)=\hat{\ell}(c)
for
c=(z^{\intercal}Qz,\sqrt{z^{\intercal}ww^{\intercal}z})\in\mathcal{C}
.

Note that
\hat{\ell}(c)
is strictly concave in
c_{1}
. Therefore,
the maximizers
(c_{0}^{*},c_{1}^{*})
of
\hat{\ell}(c)
over
\mathcal{C}
must all have a unique value of
c_{1}^{*}
.
It follows that any
z^{*}
maximizing
\ell(z)

satisfies

z^{*\intercal}ww^{\intercal}z^{*}=(w^{\intercal}z^{*})^{2}=c_{1}^{*}
.

We may consider two cases:
if
c_{1}^{*}=0
, then the second term in
\ell(z)
vanishes as

w^{\intercal}z^{*}=0
.
This implies
z^{*}
is a maximizer of the quadratic form
z^{\intercal}Qz
, and therefore an eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
Q
.
If there are multiple maximizers of
\hat{\ell}(c)
, then
Q
must have degenerate maximum eigenvalue, a contradiction.

If
c_{1}^{*}\neq 0
, then
\hat{\ell}(c)
is differentiable at any maximizer
c^{*}
, and we may compute
\nabla\hat{\ell}(c)=\left(1,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c_{1}^{*}}}\right)
.
The first order optimality condition on
\hat{\ell}(c)
implies that any maximizer
c^{*}
of
\hat{\ell}(c)
must in fact maximize the linear function
c_{0}+\frac{c_{1}}{2\sqrt{c_{1}^{*}}}
on
\mathcal{C}
. This implies that
z^{*}
must be an eigenvector of the matrix

M=Q+\frac{ww^{\intercal}}{2\sqrt{c_{1}^{*}}}

associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda
, the largest eigenvalue of
M
.
If we choose the representative of
z^{*}
so that
w^{\intercal}z^{*}=\sqrt{c_{1}^{*}}
,
then the eigenvector equation becomes

Mz^{*}=Qz^{*}+\frac{w}{2}=\lambda z^{*},

and we see that for any maximizer
z^{*}
of
\ell(z)
, we have that

(Q-\lambda I)z^{*}=-\frac{w}{2}.

In order for this to have multiple solutions, we see that
Q-\lambda I
must be singular, i.e. that
\lambda
must also be an eigenvalue of
Q
, and we must also have that
w
is in the image of
(Q-\lambda I)
, i.e.
w
must be orthogonal to all eigenvectors of
Q
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Note that
\lambda
must in fact be the largest eigenvalue of
Q
, since it is the largest eigenvalue of
M
and
M\succeq Q
. This is a contradiction, as we have assumed that
w
is not orthogonal to the eigenvectors of
Q
with largest eigenvalue.
∎

We will need to understand when inhomogeneous quadratic optimization problems have a unique maximum
for all
Q
in a linear matrix pencil
. This has a connection to our notion of a well positioned pair of matrices, as we show in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
is well-positioned, and that
w
is the eigenvector of
A_{0}
associated to the largest eigenvector of
A_{0}
.
For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
Q(t)=t_{0}A_{0}+t_{1}A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
.
For all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
,
the function
\ell_{t}(z)=z^{\intercal}Q(t)z+|w^{\intercal}z|

has a unique maximizer over
z\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

Proof.

We argue that if
(A_{0},A_{1})
are well-positioned, then
Q(t)
has no repeated eigenvalues for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
, and
w
is not orthogonal to the eigenvector of
Q(t)
with maximum eigenvalue. This implies by
Lemma

4.2
that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
, the function
\ell_{t}
has a unique maximizer on the sphere. Note that the matrices
Q(t)
have no repeated eigenvalues, since
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
span a noncrossing subspace.

We will first consider the set of
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
so that
w
is orthogonal to some eigenvector of
Q(t)
. This is equivalent to the condition that there exists
z\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
so that

\displaystyle Q(t)z=\lambda z

(4.1)

\displaystyle w^{\intercal}z=0.

Note that this in particular implies that
z
solves the equation

w^{\intercal}Q(t)z=t_{0}w^{\intercal}A_{0}z+t_{1}w^{\intercal}A_{1}z=t_{1}w^{\intercal}A_{1}z=0.

For the second equality, we used the fact that
w
is an eigenvector of
A_{0}
to conclude that
w^{\intercal}A_{0}z=w^{\intercal}z=0
.
Hence, we see that either
t_{1}=0
, or
w^{\intercal}A_{1}z=0
. In case
t_{1}=0
, we see that
Q(t)=t_{0}A_{0}
, where
t_{0}>0
. Since
w
is the eigenvector associated to the largest eigenvalue of
A_{0}
, we see in particular that
w
is not orthogonal to the largest eigenvector of
t_{0}A_{0}
.

If
w^{\intercal}A_{1}z=w^{\intercal}z=0
, then this implies that
z
is a multiple of
z^{*}
appearing in the definition of ‘well-positioned’ (
Definition

4.1
.) Equations (
4.1
) thus are equivalent to

\displaystyle(t_{0}A_{0}+t_{1}A_{1}-\lambda I)z^{*}=0.

Up to scaling, we may assume
t_{0}=1
, which then implies
t_{1}=t^{*}
and
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
in
Definition

4.1
.
We then see from this definition that
Q(t)-\lambda I
has a positive eigenvalue, implying that
\lambda
is not the largest eigenvalue of
Q(t)
.
Thus, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
, the conditions of
Lemma

4.2
hold.

We conclude that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
,
\ell_{t}
has a unique maximizer, as desired.
∎

4.3
A Grassmannian Optimization Problem

We consider the Grassmannian to be the set of symmetric orthogonal projectors of rank
m
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
:

\Gr^{n,m}=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}:Z^{2}=Z,\,\mathrm{Tr}(Z)=m\}.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{sym}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
Consider the function

\hat{f}:\Gr^{n,m}\to\mathbb{R},\quad Z\mapsto(\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1},\langle A,Z\rangle),

(4.2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}

denotes the trace norm (the sum of a matrix’s singular values).

Our aim is to show
the function in (
4.2
)
is uniquely maximized when
(n,m)=(3,2)
and
(-BB^{\intercal},A)
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

4.1
.
To do this, we will apply
Lemma

2.5
to the following function, which we show is uniquely maximized on
\Gr^{3,2}
:

\tilde{f}(Z)=(\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}^{2},\langle A,Z\rangle).

(4.3)

Before we prove these results, we will need some algebraic lemmas.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
, and that
\Pi_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
is the matrix of left singular vectors of
B
.
If
Z\in\Gr^{n,2}
, then

\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(BB^{\intercal}Z)+2\sigma_{1}(B)\sigma_{2}(B)\sqrt{\det(\Pi_{B}^{\intercal}Z\Pi_{B})}}.

(4.4)

Proof.

Throughout the proof, for a
n\times 2
matrix
M
, we let
\sigma_{1}(M)
and
\sigma_{2}(M)
denote the first and second singular values of
M
.
By definition,

\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}=\sigma_{1}(B^{\intercal}Z)+\sigma_{2}(B^{\intercal}Z).

Squaring each side, we get

\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{1}(B^{\intercal}Z)^{2}+\sigma_{2}(B^{\intercal}Z)^{2}+2\sigma_{1}(B^{\intercal}Z)\sigma_{2}(B^{\intercal}Z).

We note that

\sigma_{1}(B^{\intercal}Z)^{2}+\sigma_{2}(B^{\intercal}Z)^{2}=\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}(B^{\intercal}ZB),

where we
used the fact that
Z^{2}=Z
.

We also note that

\sigma_{1}(B^{\intercal}Z)\sigma_{2}(B^{\intercal}Z)=\sqrt{\det(B^{\intercal}ZB)}.

Finally, we apply singular value decomposition of
B
in order to write

B=\Pi_{B}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(B)&\\
&\sigma_{2}(B)\end{pmatrix}V^{\intercal},

where
V\in O(2)
and note using the multiplicative property of determinants that

\det(B^{\intercal}ZB)=\sigma_{1}(B)^{2}\sigma_{2}(B)^{2}\det(V)^{2}\det(\Pi_{B}^{\intercal}Z\Pi_{B}).

The result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)}
is a matrix such that

\det(\Pi^{\intercal}\Pi)=1
.
If
z\in S^{n-1}
, then

\det(\Pi^{\intercal}(I-zz^{\intercal})\Pi)=z^{\intercal}(I-\Pi\Pi^{\intercal})z.

Proof.

This follows from the Matrix Determinant lemma, which implies that

\det(\Pi^{\intercal}(I-zz^{\intercal})\Pi)=\det(\Pi^{\intercal}\Pi)(1-z^{\intercal}\Pi\Pi^{\intercal}z)=z^{\intercal}(I-\Pi\Pi^{\intercal})z.

∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
satisfy the property that
(-BB^{\intercal},A)
is well-positioned. Then the function
\tilde{f}
defined in (
4.3
) is uniquely maximized.

Proof.

Because
k=1
in this setting, it suffices to show that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
, we have that the function

\ell_{t}(Z)=\langle t,\tilde{f}(Z)\rangle=t_{0}\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}^{2}+t_{1}\langle A,Z\rangle

has a unique maximizer in
\Gr^{3,2}
.

Note that any matrix
Z\in\Gr^{3,2}
can be uniquely represented as
I-zz^{\intercal}
for
z\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
.
Combining
Lemmas

4.5
and
4.4
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(I-zz^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=t_{0}\|B^{\intercal}(I-zz^{\intercal})\|_{1}^{2}+t_{1}\langle A,I-zz^{\intercal}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle t_{0}BB^{\intercal}+t_{1}A,(I-zz^{\intercal})\rangle+2\sigma_{1}(B)\sigma_{2}(B)t_{0}\sqrt{z^{\intercal}(I-\Pi_{B}\Pi_{B}^{\intercal})z}

Note that because
\Pi_{B}\Pi_{B}^{\intercal}
is a rank 2 orthogonal projector, we have
I-\Pi_{B}\Pi_{B}^{\intercal}=ww^{\intercal}
for some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
w
is orthogonal to the image of
B
.

If we let
Q(t)=-t_{0}BB^{\intercal}-t_{1}A
, then the maximizers of
\ell_{t}(I-zz^{\intercal})
are in 1-1 correspondence with the maximizers of the function

z^{\intercal}Q(t)z+2\sigma_{1}(B)\sigma_{2}(B)t_{0}|w^{\intercal}z|.

Lemma

4.3
implies that the
\ell_{t}
has a unique maximizer for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0+}
. This shows that
f
is uniquely maximized, as desired.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
satisfy the property that
(-BB^{\intercal},A)
is well positioned, then the function
\hat{f}(Z)=(\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1},\langle A,Z\rangle)
is uniquely maximized over the Grassmannian.

Proof.

Note that

(\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1},\langle A,Z\rangle)=(\sqrt{\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}^{2}},\langle A,Z\rangle),

and so since
\sqrt{\cdot}
is a strictly concave continuous function,
Lemma

2.5
yields that
\hat{f}(Z)
is uniquely maximized as long as
\tilde{f}(Z)
is. The fact that
\tilde{f}(Z)
is uniquely maximized is precisely
Lemma

4.6
.
∎

4.4
Proof of Unique Maximization when
(n,m)=(3,2)

When
A
and
B
satisfy the conditions of
Theorem

, we will show that the function
f(X)=(\langle B,X\rangle,\langle A,XX^{\intercal})
is uniquely maximized over
\St^{3,2}.

We will first note that there is an action of the group
O(2)
on
\St^{3,2}
by taking
(X,U)\in\St^{3,2}\times O(2)
to
XU
.
We can characterize the orbits of this action using the Grassmannian
\Gr^{3,2}
: the relation
X=X^{\prime}U
for some

is equivalent to the claim that
XX^{\intercal}=X^{\prime}X^{\prime\intercal}
.
This action preserves the expression
\langle A,XX^{\intercal}\rangle
, so maximizing
f(XU)
and
\langle B,XU\rangle
over

are equivalent.

Lemma 4.8
.

For any
B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
and
X\in\St^{3,2}
, let

. We have

\max_{U\in O(2)}\langle B,XU\rangle=\|B^{\intercal}Z\|_{1}.

(4.5)

If further,
B=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
(i.e. we are the singular vector basis for

), then there is a unique
U
achieving this maximum value as long as
Z
is not of the form

I-\begin{pmatrix}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{1}}\\
0\\
\pm\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{1}}\\
0\\
\pm\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}.

Proof.

We can once again apply Fan’s theorem to state

\max_{U\in O(2)}\langle B,XU\rangle=\max_{U\in O(2)}\langle X^{\intercal}B,U\rangle=\sigma_{1}(X^{\intercal}B)+\sigma_{2}(X^{\intercal}B).

Note that because

(X^{\intercal}B)^{\intercal}X^{\intercal}B=B^{\intercal}ZB=(ZB^{\intercal})^{\intercal}ZB,

we have that the singular values of
X^{\intercal}B
are the same as those of
B^{\intercal}Z
, giving (

).

Fan’s theorem also implies that the optimal

4.5
) is unique as long as the singular values of

B^{\intercal}ZB
are distinct. Since

is a

matrix, the eigenvalues of this matrix are not distinct if and only if

for some

.
Assume this holds.

We can write

for a unique

, from which it follows that

\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}z_{1}\\
\sigma_{2}z_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}z_{1}\\
\sigma_{2}z_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}-c&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}^{2}-c\end{pmatrix}.

Since the left hand side is a rank one PSD matrix, the right hand side would need to be as well, i.e.

, and we see that

z_{1}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{1}}
,
z_{2}=0
, and
z_{3}=\pm\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}
.
The conclusion of the theorem follows.
∎

Proof of

.

We have seen that if

5
Algorithms for Riemannian Manifolds

Given a topological space
{\mathcal{M}}
and a function
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
that
has a Lagrangian dual section
D(t)
, two algorithmic questions immediately arise from our study. First, we may ask whether it is possible to maximize the Lagrangian
\langle t,f(x)\rangle
for arbitrary
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
. Second, we may ask whether it is possible to solve the constrained optimization problem (
opt-Main
), given an oracle for maximizing Lagrangians for arbitrary choices of
t
.

For the first question, let us assume that we have access to the value of
D(t^{(0)})
for some fixed
t^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
,
and that we want to find
D(t^{(1)})
for some
t^{(1)}\neq t^{(0)}
. Given that
D(t)
is a continuous function, it is natural to attempt a ‘path tracking’ approach to solving this problem, in which we slowly adjust the parameter
t
from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
, while repeatedly finding
D(t)
using some local search procedure.

For the second question, let us assume that
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is sectioned and that we can find
D(t)\in{\mathcal{M}}
maximizing
\ell_{t}(x)
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
. We want to find some
x^{*}\in{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying
f_{1}(x^{*})=c_{1},f_{2}(x^{*})=c_{2},\dots,f_{k}(x^{*})=c_{k}
and which maximizes
f_{0}(x^{*})
, i.e. we want to find an
x^{*}
which solves
opt-Main
.
Indeed, we will see that it is at least possible to compute the value of
V_{f}(c)
to arbitrary precision using oracle access to
D(t)
for arbitrary values of
t
, using the ellipsoid algorithm. However, it is unclear whether we can recover the optimizer
x^{*}
which witnesses the value of
V_{f}(c)
in the same way, as we are only able to solve a ‘dual’ problem, in a sense we will make precise in
Section

5.3
.

In order to get quantitative approximation guarantees for both the algorithmic tasks of finding
D(t)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
and solving (
opt-Main
) given access to the value of
D(t)
,
it will be necessary to be able to bound how quickly
D(t)
changes depending on its input. The speed at which
D(t)
changes depending on
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
will control how quickly we can make progress in a path tracking algorithm for computing
D(t)
, and also how closely we can approximate a solution to the constrained optimization problem (
opt-Main
) using the ellipsoid algorithm.
To obtain such quantitative bounds, we will assume that the domain
{\mathcal{M}}
carries not merely the structure of a topological/metric space, but that of a
Riemannian manifold.
This will allow us to control the Lipschitz parameter of the function
D(t)
using calculus.

We will first provide the basic tools we will need from Riemannian geometry and optimization, based on the reference
[
6
]
, and in particular, we will state a result bounding the Lipschitz constant of
D(t)
given control of certain condition numbers of the Riemannian Hessian of the Lagrangians.
In
Section

5.2
, we demonstrate an algorithm, which we call CHORD, that implements a path tracking methodology to solve Lagrangian dual problems.
In
Section

5.3
, we show how to make use of the ellipsoid algorithm to solve constrained problems of the form (
opt-Main
) given access to an oracle solving the Lagrangian problems.
The CHORD method improves upon standard ideas from Riemannian optimization because it uses properties of the Lagrangian dual section to ensure that the output is close to a global optimizer.
By contrast, Riemannian gradient descent may become trapped in a local minimum with suboptimal objective value.

Informally, CHORD is able to get global optimality guarantees because the Lagrangian dual section ensures that the global optima for nearby settings of the Lagrange multipliers are nearby. This implies if RGD run on an objective defined by
\langle t,f(x)\rangle
, and it is initialized at
D(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}
close to
t
, then it will converge to
D(t)
. Formalizing this notion will require a manifold structure on
{\mathcal{M}}
and control over the Lipschitz parameter on
D(t)
.

5.1
Background on Riemannian Optimization

In
Sections

5.3
and
5.2
, we analyze two algorithms that allow us to solve optimization problems with Lagrangian dual sections.
A shared requirement for these results is the need to quantify the continuity of the Lagrangian dual section
D
, i.e. its Lipschitz constant.

Recall that a
smooth embedded submanifold

{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
of dimension
d
may be defined as follows
[
6
, Def 3.10]
: for every
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a neighborhood
U
and a smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N-d}
so that
{\mathcal{M}}\cap U=U\cap\{g(x)=0\}
, and in such a way that the derivatives of coordinate functions
\nabla g_{1}(x),\dots,\nabla g_{N-d}(x)
are linearly independent on
U
. When this holds, we denote by
T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
the
tangent space
of
{\mathcal{M}}
at
x
, defined as the
d
dimensional vector space
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\forall i=1,\dots,N-d,\;\langle\nabla g_{i}(x),y\rangle=0\}
.
Equipping each tangent space with the restriction of the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
gives
{\mathcal{M}}
the structure of a
Riemannian manifold.

Our key examples
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda},{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma},
and
\St^{n,m}
are all embedded Riemannian submanifolds of Euclidean matrix spaces.
Some of the results that follow could be generalized to the setting of abstract Riemannian manifolds, e.g. by invoking the famous Nash embedding theorem
[
22
, Theorem 1, p52]
.
Working with embedded manifolds affords us simpler descriptions of some technical notions from Riemannian geometry, such as the Riemannian Hessian defined below.

We will also assume that our function
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
arises by restricting some smooth extension map
\overline{f}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}
. We may therefore also consider a smooth extension of the Lagrangian of
f
,
\overline{\mathcal{L}}:\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
\overline{\mathcal{L}}(t,x)=\langle t,\overline{f}(x)\rangle
. Finally, we will let
\overline{\ell_{t}}(x)=\overline{\mathcal{L}}(t,x)
.

We may define the
Riemannian gradient
of
\ell_{t}
,

As a

\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\ell_{t}(x)=0,

\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ell_{t}(x)\preceq 0.

Lemma 5.1

With notation as above, let

5.2
A Path Tracking Algorithm for Solving Lagrangian Optimization Problems

Given
\hat{x}_{t}\approx D(t)
and a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0,
the method of Riemannian Gradient descent (RGD) can be used to refine
\hat{x}_{t}
as an approximation of
D(s)
when
\|s-t\|<\epsilon.

We explain how to make this effective.
In general, RGD depends on the choice of a
retraction
on
{\mathcal{M}}
: this is a smooth map

\displaystyle R:T{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle(x,v)

\displaystyle\mapsto R_{x}(v)

such that every curve
c(\lambda)=R_{x}(\lambda v)
satisfies
c(0)=x
and
c^{\prime}(0)=v.

More generally,
R
may be defined on a neighborhood of
{\mathcal{M}}
in
T{\mathcal{M}}.

Fix a retraction
R
on
{\mathcal{M}}
. We may define the
R
-injectivity radius

\operatorname{inj}_{R}(x)
at a point
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
is the supremum over all radii
r>0
such that
R_{x}
restricts to a diffeomorphism on

B(x,r)=\{v\in T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}\mid\|v\|<r\}.

(5.7)

The inverse function theorem implies
\operatorname{inj}_{R}(x)>0
—see
[
6
, Corollary 4.17]
.

Fix
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
r<\operatorname{inj}_{R}(x)
, and consider the neighborhood

U:=R_{x}(B(x,r))\ni x.

For
i=1,2,
take
x_{i}\in U,
and

v_{i}=R_{x}^{-1}(x_{i})
.
A convexity condition holds:

R_{x}((1-\lambda)v_{1}+\lambda v_{2})\in U\quad\forall\lambda\in[0,1].

(5.8)

When
R
is the
exponential map
on
{\mathcal{M}},
the quantity
\operatorname{inj}_{R}(x)
reduces to the standard notion of the injectivity radius, simply denoted
\operatorname{inj}(x)

The condition (

is a
geodesically convex set
(
[
6

.)
Furthermore, our lower bound on the Riemannian Hessian implies that

restricts to a
geodesically convex function
on

(cf.
[
6
, Theorem 11.23]
.)

Let

be a Lipschitz constant for the Riemannian gradient (
[
6
, Corollary 10.47]

L=\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\|\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\ell_{\lambda}(D(\lambda))\|_{op}.

(5.9)

As long as we have

\epsilon<(1/2)\max_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\operatorname{inj}(D(\lambda)),

(5.10)

(1/L)

Taking

A natural test case for the CHORD algorithm is the UPP, which we have analyzed in detail for the

To recall, the UPP asks us to find, for a set of points represented by the columns of a matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
and a corresponding set of points represented by
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, a rotation and projection which best maps
U
onto
V
. In practice,
V
is often a noisy version of a true projection of

and the goal is to recover the rotation + projection matrix. Formally, we want to minimize
\|UX-V\|^{2}
for
X\in\St^{n,m}

In the case
(n,m)=(3,2)

and

We can put this problem into our framework by noting that the minimizers of

are identical to the maximizers of

, where

is the singular value decomposition of

. Thus, in the case that

5.3
From Lagrangians to Constrained Optimization via the Ellipsoid Algorithm

Suppose that
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is sectioned, and let
\mathcal{C}=\conv f({\mathcal{M}})
.
We have seen that the constrained optimization problem (
opt-Main
) may be reformulated as the convex optimization problem

\max\{y_{0}:y_{1}=c_{1},\dots,y_{k}=c_{k},y\in\mathcal{C}\}.

(cr-Main)

We will give a brief exposition about how to use the ellipsoid algorithm to solve a ‘dual’ version of this problem, which only requires the ability to compute the value of
D(t)
for some Lagrangian dual section of
f
; in particular, to solve the dual problem, no manifold assumption on
{\mathcal{M}}
is needed. It is only when we go to recover the actual primal solution to the optimization that we will need to assume that
{\mathcal{M}}
is a manifold.

If
0\in\mathcal{C}
, then define the polar of
\mathcal{C}
as

\mathcal{C}^{o}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}:\forall y\in\mathcal{C},\;\langle t,y\rangle\leq 1\}.

Similarly, we define the
dual optimization problem
to be

\min\left\{\frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}t_{i}}{t_{0}}:t\in\mathcal{C}^{o},t_{0}>0\right\}.

(opt-polar)

A consequence of
the bipolar theorem
[
3
, Ch. IV, (1.2)]

is that we have equality between (
cr-Main
) and (
opt-polar
), namely

\max\{y_{0}:y_{1}=c_{1},\dots,y_{k}=c_{k},y\in\mathcal{C}\}=\min\left\{\frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}t_{i}}{t_{0}}:t\in\mathcal{C}^{o},t_{0}>0\right\}.

A
separation
oracle for set
\mathcal{C}^{o}
is an algorithm, which on an input
t
outputs either the fact that
t\in\mathcal{C}^{o}
or produces some
y\in\mathcal{C}
so that
\langle t,y\rangle>1
.
We can see that having access to an oracle for computing
D(t)
is enough to produce a separation oracle for
\mathcal{C}^{o}
; on an input
t
, either output
t\in\mathcal{C}^{o}
if
\langle t,f(D(t))\rangle\leq 1
, and output
f(D(t))
otherwise.
Given a separation oracle for a convex set, it is possible to approximately minimize an arbitrary convex function on that set in polynomial time using the ellipsoid algorithm, under some additional mild technical assumptions that we will not enumerate here—see e.g.
[
4
, §5.2]
.

We now turn to the primal problem of finding
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying
f(x)=\begin{pmatrix}V_{f}(c)\\
c\end{pmatrix}
.
Indeed, if we could find an exact minimizer
t^{*}
minimizing
\frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}t_{i}}{t_{0}}
over
\{t\in\mathcal{C}^{o}:t_{0}>0\}
, then we have seen that
x^{*}=D(t^{*})
solves (
opt-Main
). However, in practice, we are not able to find an exact solution
t^{*}
, but rather some point
\hat{t}
so that
\|t^{*}-\hat{t}\|\leq\epsilon
for an
\epsilon
of our choosing. We would like to find a bound on
\epsilon
such that the inequality
\|t^{*}-\hat{t}\|\leq\epsilon
implies that
\|D(t^{*})-D(\hat{t})\|\leq\delta
. The following lemma shows that when
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Riemannian manifold and
\ell_{t}
satisfies some derivative bounds, we can obtain such a bound on
\|D(t^{*})-D(\hat{t})\|
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a Riemannian manifold, and that
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
is smooth.
Suppose that we are able to find
\hat{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
satisfying
\|\hat{t}-t^{*}\|\leq\epsilon
.
Further suppose for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}
with
\|t-\hat{t}\|\leq\epsilon
, that

\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}^{2}\ell_{t}^{\prime}(D(t))\preceq\mu I,

and

\|\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}\ell_{t}^{\prime}(D(t))\|\leq M.

We then have that
\|D(\hat{t})-D(t^{*})\|\leq\frac{M}{\mu}\epsilon
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

5.1
.
∎

6
Discussion and Future Directions

We have given a general topological framework for analyzing whether a (possibly nonconvex) optimization problem has a natural convex reformulation. Using this framework, we showed broad connections between algebraic topology and convexity, and demonstrated the effectiveness of our notions in the context of some canonical nonconvex optimization problems. In particular, we showed connections between some classic optimization problems, such as QCQPs and Stiefel manifold optimization, and algebraic concepts such as noncrossing subspaces and Lie group theory. We also give some new results on the unbalanced orthogonal Procrustes problem, and some novel algorithmic ideas for tackling that problem.

In the future, we would like to extend the applicability of these results.

Approximate convexity

Our results in this paper are only concerned with whether certain problems have exact convex reparametrizations.
We do not expect to find exact convex reparametrizations for all applications of interest.
Thus, a future direction is to obtain

quantitative results
which control the
approximation quality
of a convex relaxation.
For example,
a first step would be
to show how
Theorem

1.1
can be generalized to the setting where the continuous function

D:\mathbb{R}_{0+}^{k+1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
only approximately maximizes the Lagrangian. Further steps can be taken by understanding classic approximation results for convex relaxations through the Lagrangian dual section lens.

Convex hierarchies

A classic tool for strengthening a convex relaxation is to consider lifting the problem to a larger number of variables and then imposing more constraints. For example, the sum-of-squares hierarchy or the Sherali-Adams hierarchy are well known approaches for this task. A natural question is to ask whether our approach has any implications for how to choose such an extension, and whether or not these topological notions are helpful for making such a choice.
