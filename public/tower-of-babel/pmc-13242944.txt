Verification of the value of optimization of the medication-related clinical decision support system in China, a hospital-based study
Authors: Shan Ran, Dongshi Gu, Jing Xie, Kang Jiang, Nannan Yao, Zhonghong Fang, Tetsuya Asakawa, Ning Zhang, Huan Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluated the optimization of a medication-related clinical decision support system (MRCDSS) in a 1000-bed general hospital in China by analyzing clinician responses.MethodsThe indices “Alert generation rate,” “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate,” “Prescription appropriateness,” and “Override rates” were assessed for senior and junior clinicians before (March–May 2021) and after optimization (March–May 2023).ResultsSignificant reductions in “Alert generation” and “Total override rates,” and significant increases in “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate” and “Prescription appropriateness rate” were observed across all clinician levels. MRCDSS optimization improved prescription appropriateness more efficiently in junior clinicians, whereas senior clinicians had a greater impact on MRCDSS decision making.DiscussionThe findings confirm the value of continuous MRCDSS optimization in enhancing prescription quality, reducing alert generation and override rates, and improving the consensus between clinicians and pharmacists.ConclusionWe confirmed the importance of constant optimization in the application of the MRCDSS in clinical settings.Keywords: medication-related clinical decision support system (MRCDSS), alerts, prescription quality, alert appropriateness, override

Introduction

Medication errors frequently occur in clinical settings, with prescription errors being the most prevalent due to insufficient professional knowledge and high workloads. These errors must be promptly identified and corrected to prevent further damage. To address this issue, a medication-related clinical decision support system (MRCDSS) was implemented in clinical practice. MRCDSS is a crucial component of the hospital information system (HIS) and has been shown to reduce medication errors and adverse drug events (ADEs),1–4 improve medical standards and care quality,5–9 and and enhance drug-related clinical outcomes. In China, the MRCDSS has been mandated nationwide by the National Health Commission of China.10

However, clinician acceptance of the MRCDSS in China remains low.11 Many clinicians view MRCDSS as ineffective, exhausting, and a waste of resources.12,13 Our previous study indicated a high override rate and low alert appropriateness, highlighting the need for continuous optimization and maintenance of the system.11 Indeed, optimizing MRCDSS improves its performance, reduces inappropriate alerts, alleviates “alert fatigue,” and increases clinician acceptance. The initial MRCDSS knowledge base (KB) includes only commercial drug instructions and requires constant optimization for clinical use, which involves updating clinicians’ feedback, prescription review, new drug knowledge, and new rules. Accordingly, routine optimization is crucial for developing a satisfactory MRCDSS, characterized by low alert override rates, high acceptance,11,14 and high specificity and sensitivity, ultimately enhancing medication safety and efficiency.15,16

Based on the findings of our previous study,11 the present study investigated the impact of MRCDSS optimization on clinicians. We compared several key indices of the MRCDSS before and after its optimization. We hypothesized that such optimization measures may reduce alert generation, improve the review appropriateness of pharmacists, appropriateness of prescription, and reduce override rates. We believe that the findings of the present study are useful for better understanding the importance of MRCDSS optimization and thereby better using the MRCDSS per se.

Methods and materials

The MRCDSS system

We used the MRCDSS software (V3.5.0, Beijing Puhua Health Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) integrated with the HIS. Alerts were categorized into three levels based on the danger according to the characteristics of a certain drug, such as indications, dosage, and related adverse events: (i) low danger, which only prompted without disrupting the prescription; (ii) general danger, which interrupted and required clinical pharmacist review; and (iii) high danger, which halted the prescription and prevented the clinician from overriding. In this MRCDSS, the alert generation rate includes all alert types (low, general, and high). The difference among the alert types is the prescription procedure. Adjustments to the alert levels do not change the alert generation rate. This is because alerts of all levels were included in the calculation. Indeed, adjustments to alert levels are rare in practice. Most alerts were preset as general dangers. The procedure for handling a generally dangerous prescription is illustrated in Figure 1. Pharmacists approved passing if the prescription was judged manually as appropriate. If the pharmacist determined that the alert was appropriate, but the prescription was not, then he had two options: “not pass and return” or “refuse the prescription.” “Refuse the prescription” amounted to a manual prohibition against overriding an alert. The prescription marked as “not pass and return” was returned to clinician, who also had two options: “modify the prescription according to alert” or “override the alert.” Subsequently, the prescription process was continued, and the medication was dispensed to the patient (Figure 1). Here, the definition of “appropriate” prescriptions consisted of two parts: (i) prescriptions complying with the medication rules of the MRCDSS and did not trigger alerts; (ii) prescriptions conflicting with the MRCDSS (thus triggering alerts) but were determined as “reasonable” by the discussion between clinicians and pharmacists. Drug instructions, pharmacopoeia, formulary, clinical pathway, clinical guidelines, expert consensus, and evidence from well-designed randomized controlled trials were used as gold standards.

The processes of optimization of the MRCDSS.

In this study, “Alert generation rate” was calculated as “System pre-audit inappropriate” frequency/(“System pre-audit appropriate” frequency + “System pre-audit inappropriate” frequency); “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate” was calculated as “Approvals by pharmacists” frequency/(“Approvals by pharmacists” frequency + “Unapproved by pharmacists” frequency); “Appropriate prescription” frequency was calculated as “System pre-audit appropriateness” frequency + “Pharmacist reviewed appropriateness” frequency; “Prescription appropriateness rate” was calculated as “Appropriate prescription” frequency/(“Appropriate prescription” frequency + “Inappropriate prescription” frequency); “Overriding rate” was calculated as “Prescriptions overridden by physicians” frequency/(“Prescriptions overridden by clinicians” frequency + “Prescriptions NOT overridden by clinicians” frequency).

Optimization of MRCDSS system

The initial optimization steps, including (i) prescriptions of all clinical departments covering all daily medications, were extracted from MRCDSS, and daily feedbacks from clinicians were also collected. (ii) Prescription data, such as inappropriate alert rules, including system misjudgment (false positive) and system missed judgment (false negative), were reviewed and evaluated by clinical pharmacists. False-positive rules are mainly obtained through feedback from clinicians, and false-negative rules are usually found in prescription reviews conducted by clinical pharmacists. (iii) Corrected alert rules were sent to all directors of the clinical departments, and their suggestions were sent back to the clinical pharmacists for further optimization. (iv) Senior clinical pharmacists were responsible for further revision based on the clinician’s opinions. In these optimization processes, the pharmacopoeia, formulary, clinical pathway, clinical guidelines, expert consensus, and evidence from well-designed randomized controlled trials were successively imported for clinical application in all clinical departments. The optimization processes were sustained for two years (From March 2021 to May 2023). To reduce the override rate of clinicians, updating alert rules based on clinician feedback and overriding situations is indispensable. In this regard, routine maintenance, as well as long-term and daily optimization according to the results of prescription reviews, clinicians’ feedback, and the laws, regulations, and rules of medicine management were extremely important and were conducted by clinical pharmacists. Routine maintenance was performed daily and included the following: (i) revising the alert rules according to the updates of national regulations and instructions; (ii) allowing certain off-label medications under strict control in light of clinical needs; (iii) updating the alert rules according to the newest acknowledged evidence; (iv) adding alert rules in light of clinical practice but not mentioned in the available instruction manual; (v) adding alert rules regarding the cross-departmental medication duplication review; and (vi) adding alert rules for the maximum administration days for special controlled drugs.

Participants and investigations

All clinicians with the right to prescribe drugs in a 1000-bed general hospital were included in this study. They were divided into two groups according to their experience with medication: senior clinicians had over ten years of medication experience, whereas junior clinicians had less than or equal to ten years of medication experience. Their records were analyzed using the MRCDSS. We set the “Alert generation rate,” “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate,” “Prescription appropriateness rate,” and “Override rate” as the indices to evaluate the efficiency of prescription appropriateness and the impacts of MRCDSS on the decision making for prescription. The timing of “Before optimization” was set as March to May in 2021 when the MRCDSS was initially and fully conducted to HIS, and “After optimization” was set as March to May 2023 when the optimization processes were completed in all clinical departments. This study was conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association (2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinshan Hospital of Fudan University (approval number: JIEC 2024-S66).

Statistics

All indices (rates) are presented as percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (V25.0, IBM, IL, USA).

Results

Table 1 shows the improvement in prescription appropriateness after the MRCDSS optimization. The index of “alert generation” was significantly reduced, indicating less alert generation. Meanwhile, indices including “Pharmacist review appropriateness,” “Prescription appropriateness,” “Final prescription appropriateness,” “Prescription appropriateness of senior clinicians,” and “Prescription appropriateness of junior clinicians” were significantly increased. These data suggest that the optimization of the MRCDSS achieved better prescription appropriateness. In addition, when comparing the difference in rates of prescription appropriateness between senior and junior clinicians before optimization, there was a significant difference between senior and junior clinicians (82.49% vs 81.89%, P < .05). However, after optimization, no significant difference was found between the two groups of clinicians (94.75% vs 94.83%, P > .05). These results imply that optimization of MRCDSS might achieve better efficiency in terms of prescription appropriateness in junior clinicians, who have less medication experience than senior clinicians (Table 1).

Discussion

In the present study, we verified the efficiency of MRCDSS optimization for clinicians. We found that optimization of the MRCDSS contributed to a significant reduction in “Alert generation” and “Total override situation,” and significant enhancement of the “Pharmacist review appropriateness,” and “Prescription appropriateness” (final, in senior clinicians, and in junior clinicians). Moreover, optimizing the MRCDSS might achieve better efficiency in terms of prescription appropriateness for junior clinicians, whereas it has a greater impact on decision-making for senior clinicians. These findings imply that the optimization of the MRCDSS reduced medication errors and improved prescription quality. This discrepancy between senior and junior clinicians warrants further investigation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report based on the working environment of hospitals in China. We believe that the findings of the present study provide useful evidence for the importance of optimizing MRCDSS, as well as the value of MRCDSS per se.

Regarding the “optimization” and its value

It is widely accepted that the initial MRCDSS requires further optimization. An unoptimized MRCDSS may generate meaningless interference alerts, leading to alert fatigue and an increase in the override rate of clinicians.17–19 It is also known that inappropriate alert overrides may lead to ADEs causing patient harm20–22 and resulting in significant costs.23 Our previous study also reported that an unoptimized MRCDSS had high alert generation and override rates. They were prone to generating “alert fatigue,” thereby reducing the acceptance of clinicians.11 Therefore, we recommend timely and constant optimization by clinical pharmacists.11 The optimization includes two domains: (i) Keep the KB staying up-to-date. This requires that the newest information, such as pharmacopoeia, formulary, clinical pathway, clinical guidelines, expert consensus, and evidence from well-designed randomized controlled trials, is constantly updated. (ii) Strengthen communication between clinicians and pharmacists via the MRCDSS. Therefore, improving alert generation to meet clinical reality is extremely important.23 However, this is difficult to achieve. Some divergent rules must be communicated among clinicians and pharmacists via the MRCDSS for several rounds (Figure 1). Inappropriate alert rules cannot be completely identified in a short time, which is why the entire optimization process lasted approximately two years in our hospital.

After two years of running, the “Final prescription appropriateness rate” of hospital significantly enhanced from 83.66% to 95.71%, whereas the “Alert generation rate” decreased from 17.73% to 5.22% (Table 1). These data align with previous studies5–9 indicating that optimization of the MRCDSS significantly improved alert generation and prescription quality. The “Pharmacist review appropriateness” index, which indicates consensus between clinicians and pharmacists regarding medication alert issues, increased significantly (from 77.79% to 84.42%). This shows that clinicians and pharmacists reached a better consensus after optimization, proving that a reduced “alert generation rate” means improved alert quality. The “Override situation” index, which indicates inconsistency between clinicians and pharmacists, was reduced after optimization (Table 2). Our findings suggest that optimizing the MRCDSS improves prescription quality, enhances consensus, and reduces inconsistency between clinicians and pharmacists.

The discrepancy between senior and junior clinicians

The initial prescription appropriateness rate for junior clinicians was significantly lower than that for senior clinicians; however, after optimization, they reached the same higher rates. This suggests that MRCDSS optimization might achieve better prescription appropriateness efficiency for junior clinicians. Interestingly, override rates showed a converse trend; senior doctors seemed more affected by the MRCDSS prescription. The reasons for this require further investigation. One plausible interpretation is that senior clinicians have more clinical experience. On the one hand, their prescription quality was higher than that of junior clinicians; thus, their initial rate of alert appropriateness was higher than that of junior clinicians. After optimizing the MRCDSS, they achieved the same prescription quality. On the other hand, senior clinicians have more self-confidence regarding their clinical experience, which might be prone to neglect/disagree with the alerts generated from MRCDSS and generate more overrides. However, after optimization, they found that the MRCDSS could provide more useful alerts and their override rate was significantly reduced (Table 2). Here, senior and junior clinicians have the same autonomy in the MRCDSS. In a clinical setting, professional experience is closely associated with clinical ability; therefore, we selected “senior and junior” as a parameter to classify the clinicians. We deduced that clinical experience and skills might play crucial roles in the decision to override. Although the exact reasons for the discrepancy between senior and junior clinicians require further investigation, the value of constant optimization was confirmed.

This study had several limitations. First, the index of “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate” can only reflect the clinician–pharmacist consensus (which is mainly influenced by clinician behavior) rather than directly representing algorithmic performance. Second, this study could not distinguish between cases where the pharmacist approved because the alert was a false positive and those where the clinician modified the prescription following an alert, although these situations have different implications for evaluating system accuracy. Third, to provide a more objective assessment of the system’s ability to detect inappropriate prescriptions while minimizing unnecessary alerts, metrics based on true positives, false positives, false negatives, true negatives, and report-derived measures such as sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value are required. Fourth, although this study attempted to elucidate a causal relationship between MRCDSS optimization and changes in prescription appropriateness, we did not observe many external factors that might influence the results (such as psychological factors, economic factors, and reward and punishment mechanisms). Future studies should address these limitations.

Conclusions

This study verified the changes in several indices, including the “Alert generation rate,” “Pharmacist review appropriateness rate,” “Prescription appropriateness rate,” and “Total override situation” before and after MRCDSS optimization. We found that optimization of MRCDSS contributed to the improvement of the quality of prescription and alert generation, enhancement of the consensus, and reduction of the inconsistency between clinicians and pharmacists, which might be beneficial for improving the acceptance rate of MRCDSS in clinicians. Although there was a discrepancy between senior and junior clinicians, the findings of this study confirmed the value and importance of constant optimization in the application of MRCDSS in clinical settings.
