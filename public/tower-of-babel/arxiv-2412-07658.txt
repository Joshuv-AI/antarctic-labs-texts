Rethinking Negative Prompting for Concept Suppression: Unconditional Anchoring and Localized Guidance
Anubhav Jain, Takashi Shibuya, Yuya Kobayashi, Yuhta Takida, Nasir Memon, Julian Togelius, Yuki Mitsufuji

Abstract
Text-to-image diffusion models have rapidly transitioned from research prototypes to widely deployed tools. Along with their impressive capabilities, these models can also generate harmful or undesirable content (e.g., NSFW imagery), motivating practical mechanisms for removing such concepts. Negative prompting, which uses the negative-prompt prediction as the guidance anchor, is among the most widely used mechanisms for steering sampling trajectories away from unwanted concepts. However, for jailbreak prompts, it can fail in a regime we refer to as guidance collapse , where the model’s denoising predictions for the prompt and negative prompt become similar, so that the guidance difference driving negative prompting vanishes and the guided prediction degenerates to the negative-prompt prediction, undermining suppression. To fix this issue, we propose TraSCE ( Tra jectory S teering for C onc E pt Suppression), a training-free inference-time method that makes negative prompting reliable under guidance collapse. TraSCE combines two complementary components: (i) unconditional anchoring, which makes the fallback under guidance collapse a neutral unconditional prediction rather than the negative-prompt prediction, and (ii) an alignment-localized steering step, which deliberately induces this collapse for adversarial prompts and steers the sample to the unconditional fallback, while remaining minimally intrusive on benign prompts. We evaluate TraSCE on adversarial prompt benchmarks targeting nudity and violence, and further demonstrate its applicability to suppressing artistic styles and objects. Across these settings, TraSCE substantially improves the robustness of negative prompting to jailbreak prompts without any training, weight updates, or pre-collected concept-specific data (prompts or images), while keeping the impact on general image quality small. Our codebase is publicly available at https://github.com/SonyResearch/TraSCE/ .

Introduction

Text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al., 2022
;
Song et al., 2020
)
have made high-quality image generation widely accessible.
At the same time, these models can generate harmful content (e.g., nudity or violence) and legally problematic content (e.g., copyrighted artistic styles), since they are trained on large-scale web data that is difficult to fully curate.
As a result, there is an increasing demand for safe image generation.
A straightforward solution is to retrain a model after removing problematic data, but this is often impractical due to the scale of modern datasets and training costs.
Consequently, many concept erasure methods modify model weights
(
Gandikota et al., 2024
;
Pham et al., 2024
;
Lu et al., 2024
;
Gong et al., 2024
;
Gandikota et al., 2023
;
Kumari et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2024a
;
Heng & Soh, 2024
)
.
While effective in some settings, weight-update approaches typically require concept-specific prompts or images, can introduce collateral damage to unrelated concepts, and are not easily reversible once deployed.
These limitations motivate inference-time approaches that require no training, no weight updates, and no pre-collected concept-specific data.

Among inference-time tools, negative prompting is arguably the most widely used mechanism in practice: it is widely supported in existing diffusion pipelines and is routinely used to discourage unwanted attributes.
However, in the context of concept suppression, the relevant threat model differs from typical “quality-improving” uses of negative prompts.
An attacker can craft
jailbreak prompts
that deliberately elicit the very concept being suppressed.
In this work, we revisit negative prompting for a jailbreak threat model and show that its standard formulation can exhibit a critical failure mode we refer to as
guidance collapse
.
As the prompt-conditioned and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions become more similar, the guidance difference that drives negative prompting shrinks continuously, and in the limiting case where the two predictions coincide, it vanishes entirely, so the guided prediction degenerates to the negative-prompt prediction and suppression is lost.
In this setting, what matters is not whether the target concept appears verbatim in the text, but whether the model’s prediction under the prompt becomes similar to its prediction under the negative prompt during sampling.
This motivates a robustness-oriented design requirement for concept suppression: when guidance collapses, the update should avoid degenerating to the negative-prompt prediction, and instead fall back to a conservative reference.

We propose
TraSCE
(
Tra
jectory
S
teering for
C
onc
E
pt Suppression), a training-free inference-time approach that improves the reliability of negative prompting by steering the diffusion trajectory.
TraSCE consists of two components.
First, we adopt an
unconditional-anchored
formulation of negative prompting that provides a conservative fallback when guidance collapses, avoiding degeneration to the negative-prompt prediction.
Second, we introduce an
alignment-localized trajectory steering
step that intervenes primarily near guidance collapse, i.e., when the prompt-conditioned and negative-prompt-conditioned denoising predictions become similar.
This similarity can be induced by jailbreak prompts, including semantically ill-formed ones (e.g., random token sequences), so we steer the sampling trajectory toward the unconditional fallback; otherwise, we keep the intervention negligible to preserve benign behavior.

We evaluate TraSCE on adversarial prompt benchmarks targeting nudity and violence generation, and further demonstrate its applicability to suppressing artistic styles and objects.
Our approach requires no training, no weight updates, and no pre-collected concept-specific data (prompts or images), making it easy to deploy and to update the suppression list by adding new target concepts or dropping existing ones.

Our main contributions are:

•

Failure analysis of standard negative prompting under guidance collapse.

We formalize
guidance collapse
and show that the suppression signal of standard negative prompting degrades continuously as the prompt- and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions align, and degenerates to the negative-prompt prediction in the limit, undermining concept suppression for jailbreak prompts.

•

Defensive anchoring with alignment-localized trajectory steering.

We propose a training-free inference-time procedure that (i) uses the unconditional prediction as a conservative
defensive anchor
when guidance collapses, and (ii) applies an alignment-localized steering step as a soft gate to reduce adversarial steering while limiting impact on benign prompts.

•

Broad empirical validation.

We show that TraSCE substantially strengthens negative prompting against jailbreak prompts on established nudity and violence benchmarks, and that the same procedure extends to artistic style and object erasure, without training, weight updates, or pre-collected concept-specific data, and with a small impact on general image quality.

Related Work

Concept Erasure.

Prior work on concept erasure broadly falls into three categories: (i)
training-based fine-tuning
methods that require training and weight updates
(
Kumari et al., 2023
;
Gandikota et al., 2023
;
Kumari et al., 2023
;
Lu et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024a
)
; (ii)
training-free weight-editing
methods that update weights without training
(
Gandikota et al., 2024
;
Gong et al., 2024
)
; and (iii)
inference-time
methods that operate only during sampling without any weight update
(
Schramowski et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2024
;
Yoon et al., 2024
)
.

Many approaches perform concept erasure by modifying model parameters.

Kumari et al. (2023)
proposed minimizing the KL divergence between a set of prompts defining a concept and an anchor concept.

Gandikota et al. (2023)
updated diffusion model weights to minimize the likelihood of generating particular concepts based on an estimated distribution from a set of collected prompts.

Lu et al. (2024)
used LoRA (low-rank adaptation) for fine-tuning the base model along with a closed-form expression of the cross-attention weights.

Heng & Soh (2024)
updated model weights to forget concepts inspired by continual learning.

Zhang et al. (2024a)
proposed cross-attention re-steering, which updates the cross-attention maps in a UNet model to erase concepts.

Gandikota et al. (2024)
derived a closed-form weight update for one-shot erasure.

Gong et al. (2024)
proposed using a closed-form solution to find target embeddings corresponding to a concept and updated the cross-attention layer accordingly.

In contrast, inference-time approaches aim to suppress concepts during sampling without updating model weights.

Schramowski et al. (2023)
proposed a modified version of negative prompts to guide the diffusion model away from generating unsafe images.

Li et al. (2024)
proposed a self-supervised approach to find latent directions pertaining to particular concepts and then used these to steer trajectories away from them.

Yoon et al. (2024)
found subspaces in the text embedding space corresponding to particular concepts and filtered text embeddings based on this to erase concepts. They additionally applied a re-attention mechanism in the latent space to diminish the influence of certain features.

Li et al. (2025)
and
Lee et al. (2025)
use a detect-and-erase paradigm that activates editing only when a target concept is detected.

Jailbreak Attacks on Concept Erasure.

Red-teaming efforts have focused on circumventing concept erasure methods by finding jail-breaking prompts via either white-box

(
Chin et al., 2024
;
Pham et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2024c
;
Yang et al., 2024
)

or black-box

(
Tsai et al., 2023
;
Yang et al., 2024
)

adversarial attacks.

Pham et al. (2023)
used textual inversion to find adversarial examples that can generate erased concepts.

Tsai et al. (2023)
used an evolutionary algorithm to generate adversarial prompts in a black-box setting.

Zhang et al. (2024c)
found adversarial prompts using the diffusion model’s zero-shot classification capability for guidance.

Chin et al. (2024)
proposed optimizing the prompt to minimize the distance of a diffusion trajectory from an unsafe trajectory.

Yang et al. (2024)
proposed both white-box and black-box attacks on both the prompt and image modalities to bypass prompt filtering and image safety checkers.

Our work targets this jailbreak threat model and strengthens negative prompting for inference-time concept suppression when prompts do not explicitly mention the target concept.

Preliminaries

Diffusion models
(
Song et al., 2020
;
Rombach et al., 2022
)
learn a denoiser
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
that predicts the noise added to a noisy input at timestep
t
(see Appendix
A
for details).

Classifier-free guidance (CFG).

Text-to-image diffusion models commonly perform conditional generation via classifier-free guidance (CFG)
(
Ho & Salimans, 2022
)
:

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}\leftarrow\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\emptyset})+s\big(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}})-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\emptyset})\big),

(1)

where
\bm{e}_{\emptyset}
and
\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}}
denote the empty and prompt embeddings, and
s
is the guidance scale.

Negative prompting.

A standard negative prompting rule replaces the unconditional embedding
\bm{e}_{\emptyset}
in Eq. (
1
) with a negative-prompt embedding
\bm{e}_{\textnormal{np}}
, using it both as the anchor and as the reference in the guidance difference:

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}\leftarrow\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{np}})+s\big(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}})-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{np}})\big).

(2)

We revisit this formulation for concept suppression in Sec.
4
.

Notation.

In what follows, we abbreviate the denoising predictions as

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}:=\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\emptyset})
,

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}:=\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}})
, and

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}:=\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{np}})

for the unconditional, prompt-, and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions, respectively.
We use
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}
for the final guided prediction used in the sampling update.

.

under adversarial prompts.

Unconditional-Anchored Negative Prompting.

Jailbreak prompts can drive the prompt- and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions to become similar,
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}\approx\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
, even when the prompt and negative-prompt text strings differ.
To quantify how close they are, we define the
alignment gap

\delta\;:=\;\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2},

so that the two predictions align as
\delta
shrinks, with exact alignment at
\delta=0
.
Intuitively, as
\delta
shrinks, the guidance difference
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
vanishes and Eq. (
2
) drifts toward the negative-prompt prediction.
The following proposition makes this degradation precise.

1
Degeneracy of standard negative prompting as the predictions align.

Let
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}
denote the guided prediction obtained by standard negative prompting (Eq.(
2
)), which can be rewritten as
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}+s\,(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}})
.
Hence, the repulsion from the negative-prompt prediction satisfies

\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}\;=\;s\,\delta,

which decreases linearly to
0
as the alignment gap
\delta\to 0
.
In particular, under exact alignment
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(i.e.,
\delta=0
), we have
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
, i.e., the guided prediction degenerates to the negative-prompt prediction and provides no repulsion.

Proof.

The standard negative prompting rule in Eq. (
2
) is

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}+s\,(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}})
.
Subtracting the anchor
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
from both sides gives

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}=s\,(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}})
.
Taking the Euclidean norm of both sides and using the definition of the alignment gap
\delta=\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}
, we obtain

\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}=|s|\,\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}=s\,\delta,

where the last equality uses
s>0
.
This expression decreases linearly in
\delta
and vanishes as
\delta\to 0
.
In particular, at exact alignment
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(i.e.,
\delta=0
), the guidance difference
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
is the zero vector, so
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{std}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
and the repulsion is zero.
∎

Proposition
1
shows that as
\delta
shrinks, the guidance difference driving negative prompting vanishes and the effective repulsion
s\delta
decreases, so standard negative prompting degenerates to the negative-prompt prediction
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
.
We refer to this failure mode (where the guidance signal vanishes and the update degenerates to the anchor) as
guidance collapse
.
Jailbreak prompts can collapse the guidance and undermine concept suppression by driving
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}\to\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(small
\delta
), even when the prompt text does not contain the target concept explicitly.

To obtain a conservative fallback when guidance collapses, we instead anchor the update at the unconditional prediction:

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}\leftarrow\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}+s\big(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\big),

(3)

where
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}
is the unconditional prediction.
While unconditional anchoring itself has appeared in compositional generation
(
Liu et al., 2022
)
, where it is used to compose multiple conditions, our contribution is to reinterpret and motivate it specifically as a
defensive fallback
for concept suppression.
We identify guidance collapse as the failure mode of standard negative prompting under jailbreak prompts, and show that unconditional anchoring turns this collapse into a conservative, prompt-agnostic default rather than a degeneration to the suppressed concept.

1
Unconditional fallback as the predictions align.

Consider the unconditional-anchored rule in Eq. (
3
), and recall the alignment gap
\delta=\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}
.
The deviation of the guided prediction from the unconditional anchor is exactly

\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}\|_{2}=s\,\delta
.
In particular, under exact alignment
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(i.e.,
\delta=0
), the guidance difference vanishes and the rule reduces to

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}\;=\;\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset},

i.e., the guided prediction falls back to the unconditional prediction rather than to the negative-prompt prediction.

Proof.

The unconditional-anchored rule in Eq. (
3
) is

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}+s\,(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}})
.
Subtracting the anchor
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}
from both sides gives

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}=s\,(\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}})
.
Taking the Euclidean norm and using
s>0
together with the definition
\delta=\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}
, we obtain

\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}\|_{2}=|s|\,\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}=s\,\delta.

In particular, at exact alignment
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(i.e.,
\delta=0
), the guidance difference is the zero vector, so the rule reduces to
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}=\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}
, i.e., the guided prediction falls back to the unconditional prediction.
∎

The corollary makes the design rationale explicit by contrasting the two rules as the two predictions align.
As the alignment gap
\delta
shrinks, both rules lose their guidance term at the same rate
s\delta
, but they fall back to
different
anchors.
Standard negative prompting drifts toward the negative-prompt prediction
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
(Proposition
1
), which is exactly the prediction associated with the concept we wish to suppress; in the limit
\delta=0
, it reproduces
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
and provides no suppression whatsoever.
By contrast, unconditional anchoring drifts toward the unconditional prediction
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}
(Corollary
1
), which carries no explicit conditioning on the (possibly adversarial) prompt and therefore acts as a conservative, prompt-agnostic default.
In other words, when guidance collapses, standard negative prompting falls back to the worst-case reference, whereas unconditional anchoring falls back to a neutral one.
This is precisely the behavior we want from a defensive fallback, and it motivates the adoption of unconditional anchoring as the first component of TraSCE.

characterize the fallback behavior of the two anchoring rules as the alignment gap
\delta
shrinks, but they do not by themselves establish that adversarial prompts actually drive
\delta
toward zero.
To verify this empirically, we measure the per-timestep alignment gap
d_{t}=\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}
for benign prompts and for the adversarial jailbreak prompts from Ring-A-Bell K16

.
As shown in Fig.

(left), adversarial prompts yield a consistently smaller alignment gap than benign prompts across the entire denoising trajectory, and Fig.

(right) shows that adversarial prompts fall below every threshold

with substantially higher probability (e.g.,
P(d_{t}<1.0)
is
0.39
for adversarial versus
0.10
for benign prompts).
This confirms that jailbreak prompts operate precisely in the small-
\delta
regime where standard negative prompting degenerates (Proposition
1
), providing direct empirical support for the guidance-collapse mechanism.
The fact that this collapse is concentrated in a specific
\delta
range further motivates an intervention that is localized around small alignment gaps, which we introduce next.

Alignment-localized Steering.

Even with Eq. (
3
), some adversarial prompts can still bypass suppression when the residual term,
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}
, remains non-negligible.
Since Eq. (
3
) uses this difference to steer the trajectory, a jailbreak prompt can still influence sampling as long as this discrepancy is sufficiently large.

To reduce the adversarial prompt’s effective steering signal, we introduce an additional
trajectory steering
step that directly updates the current latent state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
during sampling.
This steering step is designed to pull the final prediction toward its unconditional anchor when the prompt-conditioned prediction is close to the negative-prompt prediction, i.e., when an adversary is indirectly implying the concept.
This step is intended to make the residual term small, thereby limiting the adversarial prompt’s influence during sampling.
However, applying such a steering step uniformly for every prompt can be overly intrusive, degrading prompt adherence and general image quality for benign prompts that are unrelated to the suppressed concept.
We therefore apply the steering step
selectively
: we intervene primarily when the current denoising state is near
guidance collapse
, i.e., when the two predictions are already similar.
This is precisely the regime where jailbreak prompts are most likely to partially align with the suppressed concept, and where a small additional steering update can effectively reduce adversarial steering.

Concretely, we use a Gaussian-shaped loss as a smooth locality gate and take a gradient step

\mathbf{x}_{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}_{t}-\lambda\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{t},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{L}_{t}=-\exp\left(-\frac{\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(4)

This exponential weighting acts as a
soft gate
.
In particular, the gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{t}
is scaled by
\exp\!\left(-\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\delta^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\right)
(which follows directly from differentiating
\mathcal{L}_{t}
), so the update becomes negligible when the two predictions are far apart (large
\delta
) and activates primarily near guidance collapse (small
\delta
).
The bandwidth
\sigma
controls the activation region of this gate, while
\lambda
controls the update strength once activated.
Crucially, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{t}
decreases the alignment gap
\delta
; by Corollary
1
, driving
\delta\to 0
makes the guided prediction fall back to the neutral unconditional prediction
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\emptyset}
.
Thus, rather than avoiding guidance collapse, the steering step deliberately induces it for adversarial prompts, converting a failure mode of standard negative prompting into a controlled defensive fallback.

The Gaussian-shaped loss in Eq. (
4
) also arises in the diffusion model unlearning framework of
Novello et al. (2026)
, where it corresponds to the closed-form objective derived from the squared Hellinger distance between two Gaussians (their H-DMU instance). In that work, the loss is analyzed from a gradient-dynamics perspective and shown to act as a soft weighting that attenuates the influence of samples with a large discrepancy, a behavior consistent with the localized, collapse-focused steering we rely on here. The two settings differ fundamentally in where and how the loss is applied.
Novello et al. (2026)
employ it during
fine-tuning
to update the model weights and permanently erase a concept, whereas we apply it at
inference time
as a trajectory-steering signal that modifies the generated sample on the fly, leaving the model weights unchanged. While the functional form of the objective coincides, our use of it as a training-free, inference-time gate for robust concept suppression under jailbreak prompts is distinct.

Practical Advantages.

TraSCE operates entirely at inference time: it requires no training, no weight updates, and no pre-collected concept-specific data (prompts or images).
The erased suppression is specified directly via a negative prompt, making TraSCE easy to deploy and to update (or roll back) by simply changing prompts at inference time.

Experiments

We evaluate TraSCE on concept suppression under jailbreak prompting.
Our evaluation is guided by three questions:
(i) Can TraSCE improve the reliability of negative prompting for safety-related concepts (nudity and violence) under established jailbreak prompt benchmarks, while preserving general image quality?
(ii) Does TraSCE generalize beyond safety concepts to suppress concepts such as artistic styles and objects?
(iii) How do the two components of TraSCE, namely unconditional-anchored negative prompting and alignment-localized steering, contribute to robustness and the robustness–quality trade-off?
Beyond these questions, we further analyze TraSCE’s suppression behavior (its specificity, generalization, and extension to multiple concepts) in Appendix
D.2
.

)
.

.

5.1
Robustness to Jailbreak Prompts

We evaluate TraSCE under jailbreak prompting, where an attacker crafts prompts to elicit suppressed concepts.
Since many defenses already perform well when the prompt explicitly contains the target concept, we focus on established jailbreak benchmarks that are designed to bypass suppression for NSFW content such as nudity and violence.

Threat Model.

We assume black-box access to the deployed generator: the attacker can query the model with arbitrary prompts and seeds.
This setting matches the construction of widely used jailbreak prompt sets
(
Tsai et al., 2023
;
Yang et al., 2024
)
and reflects practical deployment scenarios, where model weights are not exposed.

For nudity, we use Ring-A-Bell

)
, MMA-Diffusion
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
, P4D
(
Chin et al., 2024
)
, UnLearnDiffAtk
(
Zhang et al., 2024c
)

Results on Nudity.

We additionally note an edge case common in jailbreak benchmarks: many adversarial prompts are semantically ill-formed, often dominated by seemingly random token sequences, for which the intended output is not well-defined.

We also consider object erasure, i.e., suppressing the generation of an entire object category.

A simpler alternative to our soft gate is a hard threshold on the alignment gap
\delta

\delta\geq\tau

\delta<\tau

We find this hard-gate variant is sensitive to the threshold
\tau
and introduces discontinuous switching across timesteps, yielding a worse robustness–quality trade-off than our Gaussian soft gate, which intervenes continuously and localizes the update around small
\delta

5.7
Limitations

Inference-time overhead and possible speedups.

TraSCE introduces additional computation at inference time.
Compared to standard negative prompting, which evaluates the prompt- and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions, TraSCE additionally requires (i) an unconditional prediction per timestep, and (ii) a gradient computation at each timestep for the alignment-localized steering step.
This overhead is comparable in spirit to other inference-time guidance approaches that require extra model evaluations (e.g., SLD
(
Schramowski et al., 2023
)
), but it remains a practical limitation.
In our implementation, image generation using SDv1.4 takes 5.45 seconds on average, while TraSCE takes 14.29 seconds on average across 100 generations with 50 denoising steps on one A100 GPU (see Table
7
).
For FLUX.1-dev, generation takes 29.62 seconds on average for one image, whereas with TraSCE it takes 47.58 seconds on one H100 GPU.
Reducing this overhead (e.g., via gradient approximation, intermittent/timestep-scheduled updates, or detector-based gating that activates TraSCE only when needed) is an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

We revisited negative prompting from the perspective of inference-time concept suppression under adversarial (jailbreak) prompting.
Motivated by the observation that standard negative prompting can fail through
guidance collapse
, where the prompt-conditioned and negative-prompt-conditioned predictions become similar and the guidance difference vanishes, we proposed TraSCE, a training-free inference-time approach that strengthens negative prompting by steering the diffusion trajectory.
TraSCE combines an unconditional-anchored formulation that provides a conservative unconditional fallback when guidance collapses with an alignment-localized steering step that reduces the adversarial prompt’s effective steering signal while remaining minimally intrusive on benign prompts.
Experiments on adversarial prompt benchmarks for nudity and violence, as well as style and object suppression, show that TraSCE reduces jailbreak success while largely preserving general image quality.
We further show in Appendix
D.2
that TraSCE is specific (preserving nearby benign concepts), generalizes across semantically related negative prompts, and extends to multi-concept suppression.
As TraSCE specifies suppressed concepts directly via negative prompts, the suppression set can be updated, expanded, or reverted at deployment time without retraining or weight updates.
Overall, TraSCE provides a practical, deployment-friendly alternative to weight-editing approaches when training, data collection, or irreversible updates are undesirable.

Broader Impact.

This work targets a safety application of text-to-image diffusion models. We note three considerations. (a)
Dual use
: our characterization of guidance collapse explains how negative prompting fails under jailbreaks, which could in principle inform stronger attacks; we believe the defensive value outweighs this risk, as the mechanism is already implicit in existing attack successes. (b)
Residual leakage
: TraSCE reduces but does not eliminate unsafe generations on some benchmarks, and should not be treated as a complete safety guarantee. (c)
Explicit content handling
: all model-generated explicit imagery in this paper is redacted with black boxes, and a content warning appears on the first page.

Appendix A
Preliminaries on Diffusion Models

During training, the forward diffusion process is a Markov chain with
T
timesteps. Given a data sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})
, Gaussian noise with variance
\beta_{t}
is added at each step:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\,\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}\right),\quad t\in\{1,\ldots,T\},

so that
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
. This admits the closed-form expression

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\bm{\epsilon},\quad\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),

(5)

where
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
.

The reverse process is learned by training a noise-prediction network
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
to estimate the injected noise at timestep
t
conditioned on a text embedding
\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}}
:

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(\{1,\ldots,T\}),\,\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(6)

Using the learned predictor, one can compute
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
from
\mathbf{x}_{t}
(e.g., DDPM sampling) as

\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}-\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}-1}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}-1}\right)\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}}).

(7)

Loss design.

We compare the localized exponential loss used in our alignment-localized steering step (Eq. (
4
)) with directly minimizing the unlocalized MSE discrepancy,
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{p}})-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\bm{e}_{\textnormal{np}})\|_{2}^{2}

\exp\!\left(-\|\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{p}}-\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\textnormal{np}}\|_{2}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\right)
,
so the update becomes negligible when the two predictions are far apart and activates primarily near guidance collapse.
In contrast, the unlocalized MSE produces non-negligible gradients broadly, which can over-intervene on benign prompts and degrade perceptual quality.

Results and discussion.

Table
11
summarizes the results.
TraSCE achieves near-zero accuracy on the erased class across targets, indicating effective object suppression under this protocol, while maintaining reasonable accuracy on the remaining classes.
Unlike ESD/UCE/RECE, TraSCE performs suppression purely at inference time without weight updates or an explicit preservation set, which can make the robustness–quality trade-off more challenging for some classes.

We note that the ResNet-50 classifier inherits biases from ImageNet; for example, aerial scenes may be over-predicted as “parachute” even when a parachute is not clearly present.

D.2
Analysis of Suppression Behavior

This section analyzes the behavior and properties of TraSCE beyond aggregate benchmark scores, covering its hyperparameter sensitivity, its specificity and generalization with respect to the suppressed concept, and its extension to multiple concepts.

D.2.1
Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis

TraSCE introduces two hyperparameters in the alignment-localized steering step (Eq. (
4
)): the update strength
\lambda
and the Gaussian bandwidth
\sigma
, which control how narrowly the steering is localized to the near-collapse regime.
We conducted an empirical study to show their impact and how they can be used to control the ASR-quality trade-off.
We report results in Fig.
8
, which demonstrates that increasing
\lambda
lowers ASR with mostly stable FID (except for very large
\lambda
), while
2\sigma^{2}
modulates the sensitivity of this trade-off across
\lambda
.
This makes the ASR-quality trade-off easier to anticipate and helps identify a stable operating region for a given task.

Hyperparameter Selection.

We use different values across tasks because they differ in how easily guidance collapse is induced and in detector sensitivity
Nudity requires stronger, more broadly activated steering, whereas style and object suppression are easier to suppress and use smaller
\lambda
and a narrower
\sigma
to limit collateral impact.
We select (
\lambda
,
\sigma
) per task using a small set of held-out prompts that are disjoint from the evaluation benchmarks, guided by the ASR–quality trade-off in Fig.

, and then fix a single (
\lambda
,
\sigma
) per task for all reported results.
As Fig.
8
shows, the trade-off is smooth in
\lambda
and stable across a range of
\sigma
, so performance is not sensitive to the precise setting.

D.2.2
Specificity: Preserving Benign Concepts near the Suppressed Concept

A common criticism of concept erasure approaches is that they often degrade closely related benign concepts
(
Amara et al., 2025
)
.
This concerns
specificity
: whether suppressing a target concept leaves nearby benign concepts intact.
We conduct a study following
Amara et al. (2025)
, and show that TraSCE is minimally invasive: it suppresses the target concept while largely preserving closely related benign concepts.

Figure

(

(

On prompts that explicitly contain the suppressed concept (Rows 1 and 3), all methods successfully suppress the target.
The key difference emerges on the benign prompts (Rows 2 and 4): SLD and SAFREE tend to over-suppress, distorting the closely related benign concept. For example, the sloth is distorted, and the moon is noticeably altered.
In contrast, TraSCE preserves these benign concepts, producing outputs closest to the original SDv1.4 generations while still suppressing the target.
This indicates that TraSCE achieves high specificity, suppressing the target concept with minimal collateral damage to nearby benign concepts.

D.2.4

We provide qualitative examples showing that TraSCE can suppress multiple target concepts.
In practice, we aggregate multiple targets by concatenating their descriptions into a single negative prompt, which is then used as
