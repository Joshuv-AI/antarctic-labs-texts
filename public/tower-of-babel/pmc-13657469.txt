Hydrocele stone presentation and crystallographic analysis: a unique case report
Authors: Eva Malik, Abdallah El Kasti, Oussama G. Nasrallah, Jad Abdul Khalek, Riad Khouzami, Bassel G. Bachir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Hydroceles are typically benign fluid collections around the testes. Rarely, scrotal calculi, or “hydrocele stones,” form within the tunica vaginalis, possibly due to chronic inflammation, torsion of the appendix testis, minor trauma, or other mechanisms. These stones are infrequently reported, and cases with detailed crystallographic analysis are exceptionally rare.Case presentation:We present a 43-year-old male with bilateral hydroceles, a history of psoriasis and dyslipidemia, and prior right inguinal hernia repair. Ultrasound revealed a moderate right hydrocele and a small left hydrocele containing a 3 mm echogenic hydrocele stone, later found to measure 5 mm on direct intraoperative measurement. The patient underwent bilateral hydrocelectomy, during which the left hydrocele stone was retrieved. Crystallographic analysis revealed that the stone was composed of 80% calcium carbonate phosphate and 20% calcium carbonate. Postoperative recovery was uneventful.Clinical discussion:Scrotal calculi are generally asymptomatic and often discovered incidentally during imaging or surgery. Their formation may involve inflammation, torsion, trauma, or parasitic infections. Crystallographic analysis provides valuable insights into stone composition, helping to distinguish these rare lesions from other scrotal pathologies.Conclusion:Although hydrocele stones are rare and usually benign, further studies are needed to elucidate their pathogenesis, composition, and prevalence. Reporting such cases contributes to the understanding of this uncommon entity and aids in clinical recognition.

Introduction

A hydrocele is a benign collection of serous fluid between the two layers of the tunica vaginalis surrounding the testicle and spermatic cord[1]. Their sizes can vary and fluctuate, with surgical management (hydrocelectomy) being the definitive treatment. While hydroceles are mostly fluid-filled, there have been reports of intrascrotal calculi retrieved intraoperatively, with the first described by Kickham et al in 1935 and the most recent cited by Korrapati et al in 2025[2,3]. Due to their rarity, scrotal pearl findings in hydroceles have not been well documented in the literature. It is hypothesized that these stones form due to chronic inflammation, torsion of the appendix testis, or minor scrotal trauma[3]. According to a study conducted by Artas et al, the prevalence of scrotal calculi among 868 patients was only 2.65%, reflecting the rare nature of this occurrence[4]. Here, we present a case of a scrotal pearl obtained from a hydrocelectomy in a 43-year-old male patient at our institution, with the aim of further expanding the reported cases of this pathology. Clinical case presentation

Our patient was a 43-year-old male known to have psoriasis and dyslipidemia controlled with lifestyle modifications who presented for surgical treatment of bilateral varicoceles. He first started noticing right scrotal swelling after an open hernia repair performed at the end of 2023, with progressive worsening of swelling over the following months. Approximately 9 months after the hernia repair, he underwent testicular ultrasound. The testicular ultrasound showed a moderate right hydrocele and a small left hydrocele, with a 3 mm scrotal pearl, as shown in Figures 1 and 2. A decision was made to observe his progression for a few months, after which repeat ultrasound showed a moderate right septated hydrocele containing echogenic foci or debris, reactive and inflammatory, and a small septated left inflammatory hydrocele with the same scrotal pearl. On ultrasound, the scrotal pearl appeared as a mobile, well-defined echogenic focus without posterior acoustic shadowing or increased vascularity on color Doppler, and there was no associated solid intratesticular mass. These features, together with the mobility of the lesion within the hydrocele fluid, argued against a testicular tumor, sperm granuloma, or calcified hematoma, and malignancy was confidently excluded preoperatively. Serum calcium and phosphate levels were not obtained. According to the patient, the hydroceles were increasing in size, causing discomfort, but he denied any erythema, warmth, or fluctuation in size during the day. He subsequently underwent bilateral hydrocelectomy using the Jaboulay eversion technique under general anesthesia, with retrieval of the left scrotal pearl as demonstrated in Figure 3. Intraoperatively, aside from retrieval of the left scrotal pearl, no other abnormal findings were noted. The procedure lasted approximately 35 minutes with minimal blood loss. His post-operative course was uncomplicated and smooth. The patient was followed clinically and by ultrasound at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, with no evidence of recurrence on either assessment. Discussion

Scrotal stones, described as either freely floating or fixed calcifications located between the layers of the tunica vaginalis, are benign lesions that are usually found incidentally on ultrasound or during scrotal surgery[4]. Scrotal calculi are generally asymptomatic, although they may cause pain. In 1935, Kickham described them as “calcified hydroceles mimicking tumors.” These formations are typically round, pearly white, and rubbery, with a core primarily composed of hydroxyapatite, a mineral commonly found in bones and teeth[2].

The incidence has been described by several authors. In a retrospective study conducted by Tan et al, a scrotal calculus was identified in 1.95% of 3435 patients[6]. Additionally, a prospective study by Artaş and Orhan examined 868 patients and reported an incidence rate of 2.65%, whereas another prospective analysis by Aslan et al found that 73 out of 758 patients had scrotal calculi on ultrasound (9.6%)[4,7].

These stones might also be accompanied by a hydrocele and hence be accidentally seen during hydrocelectomy, as in our case. Namjoshi observed calculi in 32 of 78 patients (40.1%) with hydroceles[8]. In a retrospective study, Tan et al identified hydroceles in 25 out of 44 patients (56.8%)[6]. Meanwhile, Artaş and Orhan found hydroceles in 3 of 23 patients (13%) with scrotal calculi[4]. In addition, Aslan et al found that hydrocele was the most frequent scrotal anomaly associated with scrotal calculi (29.8%)[7].

The etiology of scrotal calculi remains unclear. It is theorized that the development of scrotal calculi may result from inflammation of the tunica vaginalis or from torsion of the appendix testis or the epididymis. Inflammatory processes can lead to tissue damage, fibrosis, and the accumulation of substances such as calcium, fibrin, and cholesterol, ultimately contributing to the formation of calculi. Another suggested mechanism involves the calcification of residual tissue following torsion[9]. In addition, Hussein et al found that parasites, especially filarial worms, are among the causes of calculi in hydroceles[10]. Frauscher et al found that 81% of extreme mountain bikers exhibited scrotal calculi, likely due to the repeated shocks and vibrations experienced while riding on rough terrain[11]. These mechanical forces may contribute to tissue irritation, promoting calcification over time.

Regarding composition, only a few reports have included the results of crystallography of hydrocele stones. Merino et al identified three calculi in two patients with hydrocele. Scanning electron microscopy and X-ray dispersion analysis revealed that all three stones had a core composed of hydroxyapatite (calcium phosphate ceramic) and white organic matter, while their outer layers consisted of yellow organic matter. The mineral composition of the center of the 6 mm calculus and one of the 2 mm calculi was found to be 33% phosphorus and 67% calcium, whereas the other 2 mm calculus contained 32% phosphorus and 68% calcium, as determined by X-ray dispersion analysis[12]. Li et al reported on an intrascrotal calculus that was large (8.7 cm) and was composed of magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite), also using scanning electron microscopy and X-ray dispersion studies[13]. Furthermore, Vahlensieck et al reported on a 2 g, white, rough hydrocele stone composed of 100% carbonate apatite, as was also seen in our patient, using infrared spectroscopy[14]. Finally, Zaitsu et al performed crystallographic analysis of a hydrocele stone using an infrared spectrophotometer, which showed that the center was composed of 64% calcium carbonate (calcite) and 36% calcium phosphate, while the outer portion was protein[15]. Our case is the fifth in which crystallographic analysis was done for a hydrocele stone, and its composition differs from those of previously reported cases, as it is composed of 80% calcium phosphate apatite (carbonate apatite) and 20% calcium carbonate (calcite). The mechanism of carbonate apatite formation in scrotal calculi likely mirrors that described in other biological settings, where local supersaturation of calcium and phosphate around an organic nucleus – here presumably related to chronic inflammatory debris within the tunica vaginalis – drives progressive mineral precipitation[14]. Unlike urinary carbonate apatite stones, which are strongly associated with urease-producing bacterial infection and consequent urinary alkalinization, no comparable infective process applies to the scrotal environment, suggesting that local tissue inflammation and protein nucleation are more relevant here. Compared with the pure carbonate apatite composition reported by Vahlensieck et al, our stone’s mixed composition (80% carbonate apatite and 20% calcium carbonate) may reflect differences in the degree or chronicity of local inflammation, fluid pH, or nucleation kinetics, although the precise determinants remain speculative given the paucity of comparative biochemical data[14].

Conclusion

To conclude, although scrotal calculi are rare and generally benign, more studies are needed to better understand their pathogenesis, crystallography, and prevalence in the general population.
