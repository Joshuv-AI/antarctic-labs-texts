On the onset of filamentation on two-dimensional vorticity interfaces
Adrian Constantin, David Dritschel, Pierre Germain

Abstract
. We study an asymptotic nonlinear model for filamention on two-dimensional vorticity interfaces. Different re-formulations of the model equation reveal its underlying structural properties. They enable us to construct global weak solutions and to prove the existence of traveling waves.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The equation

We consider complex functions
u
on the torus
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
with positive spectrum
\mathbb{P}_{+}u=u
, where
\mathbb{P}_{+}
is the projector defined by
\mathbb{P}_{+}e^{ikx}=\mathbf{1}_{[1,\infty)}(k)e^{ikx}
. We add a time dependence
u=u(t,x)
and evolve such functions by the following equation

(
\star
)

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[u]

where the cubic term is given by

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[u](x)=\mathbb{P}_{+}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\sigma|u(x)|^{2}u(x)\right],

for a coefficient
\sigma
equal to
0
or
1
. Equation (

⋆

‣
1.1
) is referred to as the ‘filamentation equation’.

•

\sigma=0
corresponds to the periodic planar case (a linear vorticity interface in the plane)

•

\sigma=1
corresponds to the spherical case (a zonal vorticity interface on the sphere)

1.2.
Known results

The filamentation equation (

⋆

‣
1.1
) was first derived by Dritschel
[
7

in a different form, as an asymptotic model describing the onset of filamentation on two-dimensional vorticity interfaces. To be more specific, this equation arose in
[

as the normal form (valid for small wave-slope solutions) of a weakly nonlinear expansion of the two-dimensional Euler equation, specifically the
contour dynamics

.

The filamentation equation for
\sigma=0

[
1
]
. These authors introduced the Burgers-Hilbert equation as a universal model for constant-dispersion Hamiltonian systems, including in particular two-dimensional vorticity interfaces
1

1

Though it appears that, in the regime of small solutions, the correct equation is the one derived in
[

]
, since cubic terms cannot be neglected.
. The Burgers-Hilbert equation was first written down by Marsden and Weinstein

In the present article, we revisit the properties and analysis of the filamentation equation. The key observation is that the nonlinearity can be written in the simple form appearing in (

⋆

). Combining this observation with its spectral formulation, we are able to uncover some interesting properties. In the companion paper

2.
Structure of the equation

2.1.
The energy

The energy associated to (

⋆

‣
1.1
) is

\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{4}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\frac{2\sigma}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|u(x)|^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The second summand is obviously the
L^{4}
norm raised to the power
4
, while the first summand is equivalent to the norm of the fractional Sobolev space
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}=B^{\frac{1}{4}}_{4,4}
raised to the power 4 (it is the so-called Gagliardo norm). Namely, if
u
has mean zero, denoting
P_{j}
for the usual Littlewood-Paley projection,

\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{4}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\sim\|u\|_{B^{\frac{1}{4}}_{4,4}}^{4}=\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{j}\|P_{j}f\|_{L^{4}}^{4},

see Triebel
[
14
]
, Section 2.6.1.

The equation (

⋆

‣
1.1
) can then be interpreted as the symplectic flow associated with this energy through the symplectic form
\omega(u,v)=\operatorname{Re}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{x}^{-1}\overline{u}\,v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, see Appendix
A
.

2.2.
Form of the filamentation equation in Fourier space

By Lemma
B.2
, the cubic term can be written

(2.1)

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[u]=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}|u|^{2}\Lambda u-\frac{1}{2\pi}u\Lambda|u|^{2}-\sigma|u|^{2}u\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 1\end{subarray}}e^{ipx}\left(k-|k-p|-\sigma\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}},

where
\Lambda=|\partial_{x}|
corresponds to the Fourier multiplier
2\pi|k|
.
Symmetrizing in
k
and
\ell
and using the remarkable identity

\frac{1}{2}(k+\ell-|k-p|-|\ell-p|)=\min(k,\ell,m,p),

already noted by
[
1
]
, we obtain yet another formulation

(2.2)

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[u]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 1\end{subarray}}e^{ipx}\left(\min(k,\ell,m,p)-\sigma\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}.

By Lemma
B.3
, the energy can be rewritten using the Fourier multiplier
\Lambda
as

(2.3)

\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}[u]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[-4|u|^{2}\Lambda|u|^{2}+2|u|^{2}(\overline{u}\Lambda u+u\Lambda\overline{u})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z-\frac{2\sigma}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|u(x)|^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

This form is more amenable to the subsequent analysis.

2.3.
Symmetries and invariants

The symmetries of the filamentation equation are:

•

space translation
u\mapsto u(x+x_{0})
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
;

•

phase rotation
u\mapsto e^{i\theta}u
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

scaling
u(t,x)\mapsto\lambda u(\lambda^{2}t,x)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
; and

•

space-time reversal
u(t,x)\mapsto u(-t,-x)
.

The following quantities are conserved:

•

if
\sigma=1
, the first Fourier mode
\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(x)e^{-ix}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
;

•

the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)
of course;

•

the ‘momentum’ (
L^{2}
norm)
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(u)=\int|u(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
; and

•

the ‘mass’ (
\dot{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
norm)
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u)=\int\left|\partial_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
(only defined if
a_{0}=0
, which can be assumed to hold without loss of generality, see
[
5
]
)

Let us briefly justify these conservation laws. The conservation of
a_{1}
if
\sigma=1
follows immediately from the right-hand side of equation (
2.1
), which vanishes if
p=\sigma=1
and
k\geq 1
. The conservation of the mass and momentum are most easily obtained from (
2.2
) and symmetrization:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=2\operatorname{Re}i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 1\end{subarray}}p\left(\min(k,\ell,m,p)-\sigma\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 1\end{subarray}}p\left(\min(k,\ell,m,p)-\sigma\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}=0,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=2\operatorname{Re}i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\min(k,\ell,m,p)-\sigma\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}=0.

As was already noticed in
[
1
]
, the conserved quantities and symmetries are related via Noether’s theorem; see Appendix
A
for details.

3.
Local strong solutions

Theorem 3.1
.

The filamentation equation is locally well-posed in
H^{s}
if
s>\frac{3}{2}
. More precisely, if
u_{0}\in H^{s}
, there exists
T>0
and a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],H^{s})
to the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[u]\\
&u(t=0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We shall focus on the case
\sigma=0
for simplicity, and only prove the
a priori
estimate. Using the identity (
2.2
) and symmetrizing in
k,\ell,m,p
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2s+1}\operatorname{Re}i\sum_{k+\ell=m+p}p^{2s+1}\min(k,\ell,m,p)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2s+1}\operatorname{Re}i\sum_{k+\ell=m+p}(p^{2s+1}+m^{2s+1}-k^{2s+1}-\ell^{2s+1})\min(k,\ell,m,p)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}.

Without loss of generality, we can restrict the summation above to
\ell\leq k
and
m\leq p
; as a result,
p\sim k
.
Then repeated applications of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality combined with the hypothesis that
s>\frac{3}{2}
give

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+\ell=m+p\\
\ell\leq k,\,m\leq p\end{subarray}}\ell^{2s+1}\min(k,\ell,m,p)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}\right|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+\ell=m+p\\
\ell\leq k,\,m\leq p\end{subarray}}k^{s}p^{s}\ell k\,a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}\lesssim\|k^{s}a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\|ka_{k}\|_{\ell^{1}}^{2}\lesssim\|k^{s}a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}.

Symmetrically,

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+\ell=m+p\\
\ell\leq k,\,m\leq p\end{subarray}}m^{2s+1}\min(k,\ell,m,p)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}\right|\lesssim\|k^{s}a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}.

There remains to bound

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+\ell=m+p\\
\ell\leq k,\,m\leq p\end{subarray}}(p^{2s+1}-k^{2s+1})\min(k,\ell,m,p)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{p}}\right|.

Using the fact that
(p^{2s+1}-k^{2s+1})\min(k,\ell,m,p)\lesssim p^{s}k^{s}(\ell+m)\min(k,\ell,m,p)
, and proceeding as earlier, this can be bounded by

\dots\lesssim\|k^{s}a_{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{4}.

Overall, we have proved the
a priori
estimate

\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{4},

which gives an
a priori
local bound on
\|u\|_{H^{s}}
.
∎

4.
Global weak solutions

For
u
and
\varphi
sufficiently regular and with positive spectrum, symmetrization gives the following identity

\int\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(u)\overline{\varphi(x)}\d{x}=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\iint\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\sigma\int|u(x)|^{2}u(x)\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Notice that the right-hand side is well defined if
\varphi
is smooth and
u\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
; indeed,

\left|\iint\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|\lesssim\left\|\frac{\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-\partial_{x}\varphi(y)}{|x-y|}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,y}}\left\|\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{3}}{|x-y|}\right\|_{L^{1}_{x,y}}.

This leads to the following definition of a weak solution.

Definition 4.1
.

If
T\in(0,\infty]
, a function
u\in L^{\infty}_{t}([0,T],W^{\frac{1}{4},4})
is a weak solution of (

⋆

‣
1.1
) with data
u_{0}\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4})
if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}u_{0}(x)\overline{\varphi(t=0,x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(t,x)\overline{\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\sigma\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|u(x)|^{2}u(x)\overline{\varphi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

for any
\varphi(t,x)
which is smooth, is compactly supported on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}
and has a positive spectrum
\mathbb{P}_{+}\varphi=\varphi
(in the second line, we omitted the dependence on
t
for sake of clarity).

Theorem 4.2
.

If
u_{0}\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a global weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
of (

⋆

‣
1.1
).

Proof.

Step 1: regularization of the equation.
Define the regularized energy

\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{N}(u)=\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(Q^{N}u),\qquad\mbox{where $Q^{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}$.}

It generates the Hamiltonian flow

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}Q^{N}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(Q^{N}u).

The solution associated with the data
u_{0}
will be denoted
u^{N}
. Observe that
(\operatorname{Id}-Q^{N})u
is constant, so that
u^{N}
is effectively finite-dimensional, and therefore global in time by virtue of the global bound provided by conservation of the energy.

Step 2: compactness.
By energy conservation,
u^{N}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,\infty),W^{\frac{1}{4},4}(\mathbb{T}))
. Furthermore, we claim that

\|\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(u)\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim\|u\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}^{3}.

By symmetrization, Hölder’s inequality, the Gagliardo characterization of
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
, and Sobolev embedding, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int\mathcal{C}_{0}(u)(x)\overline{g(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{8\pi}\iint\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(g(x)-g(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{3}}{|x-y|^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\left\|\frac{g(x)-g(y)}{|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim\|u\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}^{3}\|g\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}\lesssim\|u\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}^{3}\|g\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Here,
H^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the classical
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space, which we fall back to in order to avoid technicalities linked to fractional Sobolev spaces with
p\neq 2
.

Interpreting the above inequality by duality and using
L^{p}
boundedness of the Fourier multiplier
Q^{N}
, we have

\|\partial_{x}Q^{N}\mathcal{C}_{0}(Q^{N}u)\|_{H^{-{\frac{3}{2}}}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{C}_{0}(Q^{N}u)\|_{H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}}\lesssim\|Q^{N}u\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}^{3}.

While we have focused on
\mathcal{C}_{0}
for notational simplicity, the bound above remains true for
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

By the equation satisfied by
u^{N}
, this gives a uniform bound for
\partial_{t}u^{N}
in
L^{\infty}H^{-{\frac{3}{2}}}
; recall furthermore the uniform bound in
L^{\infty}W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
. By the Rellich-Kondrakov theorem,
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
embeds compactly in
L^{10}
, and by the Sobolev embedding theorem,
L^{10}
embeds continuously in
H^{-\frac{3}{2}}
. Thus, the Aubin-Lions lemma gives compactness of
(u^{N})
in
L^{\infty}L^{10}
. We select a subsequence converging in
L^{\infty}L^{N}
, and still denote it
(u^{N})
for simplicity.

Finally, the sequence
(u^{N})
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}_{t}W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
. Therefore, by weak compactness of this space, we can also assume (up to extracting a new subsequence) that
(u^{N})
converges to
u
weakly in
L^{\infty}_{t}W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
. This follows by reflexivity of
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
, see
[
11
,
14
]
.

Step 3: passing to the limit in the equation.
The weak form of the equation satisfied by
u^{N}
is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}u_{0}(x)\overline{\varphi(t=0,x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u^{N}(t,x)\overline{\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|Q^{N}u^{N}(x)-Q^{N}u^{N}(y)|^{2}(Q^{N}u^{N}(x)-Q^{N}u^{N}(y))\overline{(Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(Once again, we have focused on the case
\sigma=0
for the sake of clarity.) Passing to the limit in the first line is immediate, so that we turn to the second line, which we split into two pieces:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|Q^{N}u^{N}(x)-Q^{N}u^{N}(y)|^{2}(Q^{N}u^{N}(x)-Q^{N}u^{N}(y))\overline{(Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{S_{\delta}}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{T}\setminus S_{\delta}}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
S_{\delta}
is the
\delta
-neighbourhood of the diagonal:
S_{\delta}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{2},\,|x-y|<\delta\}
. The first piece goes to zero with
\delta
, as follows by Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle|I_{\delta}|\leq\left\|\frac{|Q^{N}u^{N}(x)-Q^{N}u^{N}(y)|^{3}}{|x-y|^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\left\|\frac{Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\|_{L^{8}}\|\mathbf{1}_{S_{\delta}}\|_{L^{8}}\lesssim\|u^{N}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}\|\partial_{x}\varphi\|_{W^{\frac{3}{8},8}}\delta^{\frac{1}{8}}.

By compactness in
L^{\infty}L^{10}
and convergence of
Q^{N}\partial_{x}\varphi
to
\partial_{x}\varphi
in
L^{p}
, the second piece has the desired limit as
N\to\infty
:

\displaystyle II_{\delta}\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{0}^{\infty}\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(\partial_{x}\varphi(x)-\partial_{x}\varphi(y))}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Combining the bound on
I_{\delta}
and the limit of
II_{\delta}
gives the desired result.
∎

5.
Variational aspects

5.1.
Sign of the energy

Lemma 5.1
.

(i)

If
u
has a non-negative spectrum (
\mathbb{P}u=u
), then
\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)\geq 0
, and it is only zero if
u=C
,
C\in\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

If
u
has a positive spectrum (
\mathbb{P}_{+}u=u
), then
\mathcal{E}_{1}(u)\geq 0
, and it is only zero if
u=Ce^{ix}
,
C\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

The first assertion is obvious. We learn from
(
2.3
) that

\mbox{if}\;\mathbb{P}_{+}u=u,\qquad\mathcal{E}_{0}(e^{-ix}u)=\mathcal{E}_{1}(u),

which implies the second assertion.
∎

5.2.
Weak compactness and lower semi-continuity of the energy

We provide a direct proof of weak compactness and lower semi-continuity for the energy which avoids any functional analytic prerequisite, except for the characterization
\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)\sim\|u\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that
(u_{n})
is a sequence in
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{0}(u_{n})
is decreasing. Then there exists a subsequence (which we still denote
(u_{n})
for simplicity) and a function
u\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
such that, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\int u_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int u(x)\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

and furthermore

\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{0}(u_{n}).

Proof.

Step 1: weak compactness.
Recall that
Q^{N}
is the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\mathbf{1}_{[1,N]}(k)
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
u^{N}_{n}=Q^{N}u_{n}
is compact in
L^{4}
; hence, by a diagonal argument, we can find Fourier coefficients
(a_{k})
and a subsequence (in
n
) such that
u^{N}_{n}\rightarrow\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}e^{ikx}
as
n\to\infty
for any
N
. This implies that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}e^{ikx}
is uniformly (in
N
) bounded in
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
. From this, it is not hard to deduce that
u=\sum_{k=1^{\infty}}a_{k}e^{ikx}
belongs to
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
.

Splitting
u_{n}
into
Q^{N}u_{n}
and
(1-Q^{N})u_{n}
, one readily establishes that

\int u_{n}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int u(x)\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Step 2: lower semi-continuity.
We will now split
u_{n}
into a low, a middle, and a high frequency part

\displaystyle u_{n}=Q^{N}u_{n}+(Q^{M}-Q^{N})u_{n}+(1-Q^{M})u_{n}=u_{n}^{\operatorname{low}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{mid}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}},

with a similar decomposition for
u
. This decomposition depends on two parameters,
N and M,
which we fix as follows. First, we suppose we have an
\epsilon>0
, and
N
such that

(5.1)

\|u-u^{\operatorname{low}}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}<\epsilon,

and finally
M
such that

(5.2)

\iint\frac{|u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)|^{2}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\operatorname{Re}\left[(\overline{u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)})(f^{\operatorname{high}}(x)-f^{\operatorname{high}}(y))\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y<\epsilon\qquad\mbox{if $\|f\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}<1$}

(the existence of such an
M
is a consequence of the fact that
\frac{|u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)|^{2}(\overline{u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)})}{1-\cos(x-y)}
is a given smooth function after
N
has been fixed).

Once
N
and
M
have been fixed, we choose a constant
K
such that, if
n>K
, then

(5.3)

\begin{split}&\|u^{\operatorname{low}}_{n}-u^{\operatorname{low}}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}<\epsilon\\
&\|u^{\operatorname{mid}}_{n}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},4}}\lesssim\epsilon\end{split}

(such a
K
exists since
u^{\operatorname{low}}_{n}
and
u^{\operatorname{mid}}_{n}
converge to
u^{\operatorname{low}}
and
u^{\operatorname{mid}}
respectively, as
n\to\infty
).

Now that all the parameters are fixed, we proceed with the inequalities. By Hölder’s inequality and (
5.3
), if
n>K
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0}(u_{n})=\mathcal{E}_{0}(u_{n}^{\operatorname{low}}-u^{\operatorname{low}}+u^{\operatorname{low}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{mid}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}})=\mathcal{E}_{0}(u^{\operatorname{low}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}})+O(\epsilon).

By convexity, for any
w,z\in\mathbb{C}
,
|w|^{4}\geq|z|^{4}+4|z|^{2}\operatorname{Re}\overline{z}(w-z)
. Combining this inequality with (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0}(u^{\operatorname{low}}+u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{E}_{0}(u^{\operatorname{low}})+\iint\frac{|u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)|^{2}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\operatorname{Re}\left[(\overline{u^{\operatorname{low}}(x)-u^{\operatorname{low}}(y)})(u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}}(x)-u_{n}^{\operatorname{high}}(y))\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{0}(u^{\operatorname{low}})+O(\epsilon).

Finally, it follows from (
5.1
) that

\mathcal{E}_{0}(u^{\operatorname{low}})=\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)+O(\epsilon).

Overall, we proved that, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
K
such that, if
n>K
,

\mathcal{E}_{0}(u_{n})\geq\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)+O(\epsilon).

This gives the desired result!
∎

5.3.
Constrained minimization

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{P}^{*}>0
, let

\mathcal{E}^{*}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{P}^{*})=\inf\{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u),\;u\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4},\;\mathbb{P}_{+}u=u,\;\mathcal{M}(u)=\mathcal{M}^{*},\;\mathcal{P}(u)=\mathcal{P}^{*}\}.

Then this infimum is reached at a minimizer.
The same holds true if only the momentum or only the mass is fixed.

Proof.

We apply the direct method of the calculus of variations and consider a minimizing sequence
(u_{n})
such that

•

u_{n}\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
for any
n
,

•

\mathbb{P}_{+}u_{n}=u_{n}
for any
n
,

•

\mathcal{M}(u_{n})=\mathcal{M}^{*}
for any
n
,

•

\mathcal{P}(u_{n})=\mathcal{P}^{*}
for any
n
, and

•

\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u_{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.

Since
(u_{n})
is bounded in
W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
, it is precompact in
L^{p}
,
p<\infty
by the Rellich-Kondrakov theorem and there exists a subsequence (still denoted
(u_{n})
) such that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
L^{10}
and in particular
\mathcal{M}(u)=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}(u)=\mathcal{P}^{*}
. We now apply Theorem
5.2
and obtain yet another subsequence, still denoted
(u_{n})
, and a limit
\widetilde{u}\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
; however, by uniqueness of the (weak) limit, it follows that
\widetilde{u}=u
.

Overall, we have found
u\in W^{\frac{1}{4},4}
such that
\mathcal{M}(u)=\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
\mathcal{P}(u)=\mathcal{P}^{*}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{*}
. By definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{*}
, actually
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{*}
holds, which is the desired statement.
∎

The Euler-Lagrange equation satisfied by the minimizer obtained in the previous proposition is

\partial\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)=\lambda\partial\mathcal{M}(u)+\mu\partial\mathcal{P}(u),

where the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
.
Using the formula in (
A.1
) for the gradients of
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}
,
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{P}
, the Euler-Lagrange equation becomes

\frac{4}{\pi}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(u)=\lambda\Lambda^{-1}u+\mu u.

This equation is satisfied
a priori
in a weak sense, namely after pairing with a smooth function. It would be interesting to develop a regularity theory; i.e. is a solution of this equation or a minimizer necessarily smooth? The equation would then be satisfied in a stronger sense.

6.1.
General traveling waves

These have the form

\Phi(x-ct)e^{i\omega t},

where
\Phi
has positive spectrum. Plugging this ansatz in (

‣
1.1

-c\partial_{x}\Phi+\omega\Phi=\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[\Phi]

or after integrating

(6.1)

-c\Phi+\omega\Lambda^{-1}\Phi=\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[\Phi]

(in which sense this equation is satisfied depends on the smoothness of

Taking the inner product with
\overline{\Phi}
and using (

), we find the identity

-c\mathcal{P}(\Phi)+\omega\mathcal{M}(\Phi)=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(\Phi).

Lemma 6.1
.

By definition, stationary solutions are constant in time (i.e. satisfy the above with

(i)

If

(ii)

If

Proof.

This follows from the above identity and Lemma

6.2.
Explicit formulas

We know that exact traveling waves are given by

\Psi_{k}(t,x)=e^{i[kx+k(k-\sigma)t]},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, if

,

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}[e^{ikx}]=(k-\sigma)e^{ikx}.

These traveling waves are such that

\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{k})=2\pi,\qquad\mathcal{M}(\Psi_{k})=k^{-1},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(\Psi_{k})=4(k-\sigma).

Proposition 6.2
.

(i)

If
\sigma=0
, the traveling wave
\Psi_{1}
is orbitally stable.

(ii)

If
\sigma=1

and

Proof.

This follows from the conservation of

and
\mathcal{P}
and from the conservation of

∎

If

Appendix A
Variational and symplectic structures

We formally present the variational and symplectic structures of the filamentation equation. This will give a link between the symmetries and the conserved quantities that were presented in the introduction.

A.1.
General symplectic formalism

We follow the presentation in Tao
[
13
]
and start with a symplectic (antisymmetric, non-degenerate) bilinear form
\omega
on a vector space
E
.

Given a real function
F
on
E
, its differential is such that

F(u+\epsilon\varphi)=F(u)+\epsilon\,dF(u)\cdot\varphi+O(\epsilon^{2}).

By definition, the symplectic gradient
\nabla_{\omega}F
is such that

dF(u)\cdot\varphi=\omega(\nabla_{\omega}F,\varphi).

The symplectic flow associated to
F
is the evolution equation on
X
given by

\dot{u}=\nabla_{\omega}F(u).

Given data
u_{0}
, we denote the solution
u
to this evolution equation by

u(t)=\Phi^{\omega}_{F}(u_{0},t).

We consider the flow given by a specific Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}

\dot{u}=\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{H}(u).

Then the following are equivalent (Noether’s theorem):

•

the Poisson bracket vanishes:
\{\mathcal{H},F\}=\omega(\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{H},\nabla_{\omega}F)=0
;

•

F
is a conserved quantity for the flow of
\mathcal{H}
:
F(\Phi^{\omega}_{\mathcal{H}}(u_{0},t))
is independent of
t
; and

•

\Phi^{\omega}_{F}(\cdot,t)
is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
; therefore,
\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{\omega}_{F}(u_{0},t))
is independent of
t
.

A.2.
Gradients

Given a real functional
F
om complex functions
u
defined on
\mathbb{T}
with positive spectrum, we denote
\partial F(u)
as the gradient which is such that

F(u+\epsilon\varphi)=F(u)+2\epsilon\operatorname{Re}\int\partial F(u)\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+O(\epsilon^{2})

(the usual notation for this function is
\partial F(u)=\frac{\partial F(u)}{\partial u}
, where the derivative is understood in the complex sense). The gradient
\partial F(u)
becomes unique once we require that it has a negative spectrum.

The gradients of the Hamiltonian, momentum and mass are given by

(A.1)

\begin{split}&\partial\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)=\frac{4}{\pi}\overline{\mathcal{C}(u)}\\
&\partial\mathcal{P}(u)=\overline{u}\\
&\partial\mathcal{M}(u)=\Lambda^{-1}\overline{u}.\end{split}

Furthermore, the functions and their gradients are related through the inner products

(A.2)

\begin{split}&\int\overline{\mathcal{C}(u)}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}(u)}\\
&\int\overline{\partial\mathcal{P}(u)}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{P}(u)\\
&\int\overline{\partial\mathcal{M}(u)}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{M}(u).\end{split}

A.3.
Application to the filamentation equation

Consider the symplectic form on functions with positive spectrum

\omega(u,v)=\operatorname{Re}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{x}^{-1}\overline{u}\,v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

If the function
F
admits a gradient as in the previous subsection, then

\nabla_{\omega}F=2\partial_{x}\overline{\partial F}

(note that this formula makes sense even without the positive spectrum requirement, while the symplectic form does require a positive spectrum).

•

For the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}
, the gradient is
\frac{4}{\pi}\overline{\mathcal{C}(u)}
and the symplectic gradient is
\frac{8}{\pi}\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}[u]
. The associated symplectic flow is the equation (

⋆

‣
1.1
), up to time rescaling.

•

Consider the momentum
\mathcal{P}(u)=\int|u(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. Its gradient is simply
\partial\mathcal{P}=\overline{u}
, and its symplectic gradient is
\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{P}=2\partial_{x}u
. This is the generator of the translations
u(x)\mapsto u(x+x_{0})
.

•

Consider the mass
\mathcal{M}(u)=\int|\partial_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}u(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. Its gradient is
\partial\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{-1}\overline{u}
, and its symplectic gradient is
\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{M}=2iu
. This is the generator of the phase rotations
u(x)\mapsto e^{i\theta}u(x)
.

Appendix B
Computations

Lemma B.1
.

If
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1-\cos(mz)}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=2\pi|m|\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\epsilon}^{2\pi-\epsilon}\frac{1-e^{imz}}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=2\pi|m|.

Proof.

The second identity is a simple consequence of the first; furthermore, it suffices to deal with the case
m>0
, which we will focus on. The integral can be written

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1-\cos(mz)}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{S}\frac{2-z^{m}-z^{-m}}{2-z-z^{-1}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{iz},

where the contour
S
is the unit circle. The only pole of the integrated function is at
0
; the expansion of this function at the origin can be found by writing

\frac{2-z^{m}-z^{-m}}{2z-z^{2}-1}=(z^{m}+z^{-m}-2)(1+z+z^{2}+z^{3}+\dots)^{2}=(z^{m}+z^{-m}-2)(1+2z+3z^{2}+4z^{3}+\dots),

from which we see that the residue at
0
is
m
. The result therefore follows by the residue theorem.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

If
u=\mathbb{P}u
, and
u
is sufficiently smooth, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{P}\left[|u|^{2}\Lambda u-u\Lambda|u|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=k+\ell-m\\
k,\ell,m,p\geq 0\end{subarray}}e^{ipx}\left(k-|k-p|\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}.

Proof.

Expanding
u
in a Fourier series,
u(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}e^{inx}
, the left-hand side above becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell,m\geq 0\\
k+\ell-m\geq 0\end{subarray}}a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(e^{ikx}-e^{iky})(e^{i\ell x}-e^{i\ell y})(e^{-imx}-e^{-imy})}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell,m\geq 0\\
k+\ell-m\geq 0\end{subarray}}a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}e^{i(k+\ell-m)x}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(1-e^{ikz})(1-e^{i\ell z})(1-e^{-imz})}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

It remains to compute the integral. This is achieved by applying the previous lemma as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(1-e^{ikz})(1-e^{i\ell z})(1-e^{-imz})}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1-e^{ikz}-e^{i\ell z}-e^{-imz}+e^{i(k+\ell)z}+e^{i(k-m)z}+e^{i(\ell-m)z}-e^{i(k+\ell-m)z}}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\epsilon}^{2\pi-\epsilon}\left[\frac{1-e^{i(k+\ell-m)z}}{1-\cos z}+\frac{1-e^{ikz}}{1-\cos z}+\frac{1-e^{i\ell z}}{1-\cos z}+\frac{1-e^{imz}}{1-\cos z}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{e^{i(k+\ell)z}-1}{1-\cos z}+\frac{e^{i(\ell-m)z}-1}{1-\cos z}+\frac{e^{i(k-m)z}-1}{1-\cos z}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad=2\pi\left(k+\ell-m+k+\ell+m-(k+\ell)-|\ell-m|-|k-m|\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\pi\left(k+\ell-|\ell-m|-|k-m|\right)

under the assumption that
k,\ell,m,k+\ell-m\geq 0
. Denoting
p=k+\ell-m
, we have by symmetry between
k
and
\ell
,

2\pi\sum\left(k+\ell-|\ell-m|-|k-m|\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}e^{ipx}=4\pi\sum\left(k-|k-p|\right)a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}}e^{ipx}.

Coming back to physical space, this is

\mathbb{P}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}(u(x)-u(y))}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=2\mathbb{P}\left[|u|^{2}\Lambda u-u\Lambda|u|^{2}\right].

∎

Lemma B.3
.

If
u=\mathbb{P}u
and
u
is sufficiently smooth,

\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{4}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=2\pi\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[-4|u|^{2}\Lambda|u|^{2}+2|u|^{2}(\overline{u}\Lambda u+u\Lambda\overline{u}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of the previous lemma. After expanding
u
in a Fourier series, we find that

\iint_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{4}}{1-\cos(x-y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=2\pi\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell,m,n>0\\
k+\ell=m+n\end{subarray}}a_{k}a_{\ell}\overline{a_{m}a_{n}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(1-e^{ikz})(1-e^{i\ell z})(1-e^{-imz})(1-e^{-inz})}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

The integral can then be evaluated as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(1-e^{ikz})(1-e^{i\ell z})(1-e^{-imz})(1-e^{-inz})}{1-\cos z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad=2\pi\left[k+\ell+m+n-(k+\ell)-|k-m|-|k-n|-|\ell-m|-|\ell-n|-(m+n)+k+\ell+m+n\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=2\pi\left[k+\ell+m+n-|k-m|-|k-n|-|\ell-m|-|\ell-n|\right],

where we used the facts that
k,\ell,m,n\geq 0
and
k+\ell=m+n
. This gives the desired result.
∎
