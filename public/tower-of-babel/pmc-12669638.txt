The brain activation on upper extremity motor control tasks in different forces levels
Authors: Nattapat Kulwattho, Hsiao-Feng Chieh, Chien-Ju Lin, Wei-Jen Chen, Chuchart Pintavirooj, Charlie Chen Ma, Fong-Chin Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotor control is a critical process for muscle contraction initiated by nerve impulses governed by the motor cortex, which is vital for performing activities of daily living. The purpose of this study is to investigate brain activation in upper extremity motor control tasks in regulating the pushing force. Eighteen healthy young adults were asked to perform upper extremity motor control tasks, and recorded the hemodynamic signals using Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy and robotic arms. Two types of movement—static and dynamic—and three different task difficulties based on different force levels were used as force-regulating upper extremity motor control tasks. The hemodynamic response was collected in the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex (PMC), supplementary motor area (SMA), and prefrontal cortex (PFC). The results showed a decrease in HbO for PFC was greater in the static relative to dynamic movement. Moreover, contralateral (c) M1, ipsilateral (i) PFC, and PMC have a significant increment in HbO mean compared to task difficulty. These findings indicate that the upper-extremity force and movement rely on separate cortical circuits: brain activation increases with difficulty in the cM1, PMC, and iPFC, whereas only the PFC distinguishes static from dynamic movement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20727-x.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Geriatrics

Introduction

The upper extremity, or arm, can perform various movements crucial for Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), such as reaching, grasping, and manipulating objects1, which are essential for maintaining quality of life (QoL). This motor control is initiated by signals from brain regions including the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex (PMC), and supplementary motor area (SMA)2–5. Such tasks involve complex brain-muscle interactions that require precise coordination between descending motor commands from the corticospinal tract and ascending sensory feedback6—integrating proprioceptive, tactile, and visual information, which is processed through spinal interneurons7 and supraspinal circuits8 to fine-tune muscle activation patterns for accurate, goal-directed movements9. This fast and accurate brain-muscle communication, the brain sending precise motor commands while simultaneously receiving sensory feedback to adjust movements in real-time, is crucial for coordinated movement. Disruptions due to chronic illnesses, neurological conditions like Parkinson’s disease and stroke, or aging10–12 can impair movement and hinder ADLs, reducing QoL13. These everyday motor challenges become particularly evident in clinical populations: Parkinson’s disease patients show diminished activation in key motor areas like the contralateral motor cortex and supplementary motor area during simple finger tasks, compensating through increased prefrontal activity14. Similarly, stroke survivors struggle with precision grip control due to corticospinal damage in brain regions critical for motor control3, producing irregular and poorly modulated forces15. Even healthy aging shifts motor control toward greater prefrontal involvement16. These distinct patterns demonstrate how finger force control tasks can sensitively detect dexterity deficits and reveal altered brain activation strategies across different conditions, making them valuable tools for understanding motor control disruptions.

The study of motor control could help us understand how the brain transforms movement intentions into action through hierarchical processing: PMC formulates movement plans, internal models compute required joint torques, and M1 generates scaled commands to produce appropriate muscle forces17. This knowledge can lead to strategies to restore functionality through improved brain-muscle communication18, promoting independence for those facing ADLs challenges19. It can also aid in developing brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) that use brain activity to control external devices20, which are already improving independence in stroke rehabilitation21 and other cohorts. To effectively translate these neural control principles into practical solutions, it is crucial to understand the actual force requirements of daily activities. Across six common ADLs, fingertip forces range from 1.4 to 31.4 N, with most tasks requiring less than 10.5 N13,14, while peak key-pinch forces for key grasping and turning reach 24 N in the thumb and 9 N in the middle finger1,22. Moreover, isometric stabilization of up to 25 N with an extended index finger mirrors the control demands of touchscreen taps, appliance keys, etc. (0.1–5 N)23–25, while legal standards set the maximum force required for operable parts at 22–25 N26,27, which together account for most of the forces encountered in ADLs.

The relationship between forces and brain activation is particularly relevant, especially in M1 and SMA, with slightly proportional correlation28. Force control plays a significant role in motor control, and studies have shown that varying levels of force can lead to different patterns of brain activation29,30. Correspondingly, higher forces generally raise difficulty of the task31,32, requiring the sensorimotor system to recruit additional neural resources to maintain accuracy, integrating not only M1 and SMA output but also higher-order control from prefrontal region33,34. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) and SMA are responses to the evaluative signals that monitor the outcomes of arm movements35 and adjust strategy when tasks become harder31,33. PMC also exhibits distinct activation patterns during arm movement tasks, correlating with kinematic data to understand functional connectivity during physical actions36. Moreover, the motor cortex dynamics indicate that spatial and temporal parameters of arm movements are independently encoded, allowing for precise control over movement direction and speed37.

The Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) is an example of standardized measures for assessing upper extremity motor tasks in clinical settings38,39, which evaluates various components of upper limb function. However, recent advancements in neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)40, electroencephalogram (EEG)41, and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)42 have allowed researchers to investigate the brain activates in various tasks. The fNIRS, a non-invasive hemodynamic regional tissue oxygenation monitoring technique43, first introduced by Jöbsis in 197744, operate by detecting variations in light absorption by different hemoglobin types and calculating cerebral blood flow changes over time45 using the modified Beer-Lambert law46. The advantages of fNIRS encompass portability, non-invasiveness, and cost-effectiveness43, facilitating a wide range of its applications in neuroscience clinical research45 and serving as a valuable tool for asset brain activation in motor control47, making it particularly suitable for observing brain activity during movement tasks across various settings48–50. Moreover, similar established paradigms have also investigated cortical activation at comparable low-to-moderate force levels using fNIRS51,52; thus, this research employed the same technique. The robotic arm, which is a precise six-axis collaborative robot designed for various applications, particularly in upper extremity motor functional tasks, is utilized to develop an investigation method for motor control tasks in force-control situations.

Thus, the objective of this study is to examine how neural activation in four key brain regions of interest (ROIs)—the PFC, PMC, SMA, and M1—correlates with upper extremity motor control tasks at varying difficulty levels by using a robotic arm to guide force and movement in the upper extremity.

Methods

Participants

Eighteen healthy young adults (5 males and 13 females; mean age 22.33 ± 1.80 years) with no visual hand impairments, history of brain injury, or muscle tone disorders were recruited for this study. Among them, 17 participants were right-handed, and one was left-handed. All participants provided written and informed consent. The study research methodology was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of National Cheng Kung University of Approval No.: NCKU HREC-E-112-423 revision 3, ensuring compliance with Institutional Review Board (IRB) standards.

Experimental design

The UR3e robotic arm (Universal Robots, Odense, Denmark) was used in this study as a force and movement regulator. NIRScout (NSCCH-10207) fNIRS system with an 8 × 16 optode configuration, developed by NIRx Medical Technologies, Berlin, Germany. This system utilizes 8 light-emitting diodes (LED) sources that emit infrared light at wavelengths of 760 nm and 850 nm. It also includes 16 detector optodes that capture the reflected infrared light up to 30 channels, operating at a sampling frequency of 7.81 Hz. The montage setup was performed using NIRSite 2.0 software and arranged according to the EEG 10–10 system for the M1, PFC, PMC, and SMA brain regions, as shown in Fig. 1. All probes were pinned to the NIRSCap, and they were secured with Stabilizing Links. We use NIRStar 15.3 and NIRStim 4.0 for data recording, which are software tools for collecting fNIRS data and stimulus presentation.

Probe setup for fNIRS, showing the 8 source optodes (red), 16 detectors (blue), and channel numbers for measurement (line and number). The regions of interest are: Premotor cortex—PMC (highlighted in green), Prefrontal cortex—PFC (highlighted in red), Supplementary motor area—SMA (highlighted in blue), and Primary motor cortex—M1 (highlighted in yellow).

During the experiment participants were asked to sit on a chair facing the robotic arm and monitor the force bar GUI (Fig. 2b and c); the starting point of the movement path is aligned with the height of their dominant hand’s shoulder. When participants extended their index finger to gently reach and press this starting point, their elbow naturally bent to an angle of about 80 to 90 degrees. By adapting specific components from previous research by Chen53—including the robotic arm trajectory and montage design—we designed the experimental protocol to investigate the effect of force regulation on brain activation during upper extremity motor tasks, with each participant performing tasks under six different conditions. These conditions combined two types of motion—static and dynamic—with three different task difficulties: easy, moderate, and hard. The difficulty of the task was selected from the typical force range in ADLs52,54: with lower force at 4 N found in simple screen tapping tasks labelled as easy23–25, the moderate difficulty tasks were selected from the middle range force found in ADLs at 12 N13,14, and hard tasks were selected from the tasks near the regulation limits at 20 N26,27. During the dynamic movements, the robotic arm was moved along a designated path in forward and backward directions, and distance was calculated by their forearm and upper arm length; however, due to the robotic arm’s spatial limitations, we set the elbow movement path centered at 84°, flexing 30° forward and extending 30° backward at shoulder height, thereby keeping the movement within the 30°–150° ADLs range55. The force bar GUI displayed this resultant force, measured by the load cell attached to the robotic arm, which was processed using the Pythagorean theorem (Fig. 2c), and if the pressing force exceeded or fell below the target range of 2 N during the movement, the robotic arm would halt until the force returned within acceptable limits. The descriptive analytics with confidence intervals (CI) were applied to each force level to determine that the force data collected through the whole period remained within the margin of error of 2 N. Moreover, a linear mixed-effects model with maximal random intercepts for Subject, Brain Area and Condition56, and with RMSE as a covariate57, performed in RStudio using lme458 and lmerTest59 packages, showed that residual behavioral error had no measurable impact on HbO mean (p = 0.54; marginal R² = 0.0004); therefore, all samples were retained. The moving speed and acceleration of the robotic arm in this study were set at 0.1 ms− 1 and 0.1 ms− 2.

The experiment protocol consisted of a total of six different blocks, as shown in Fig. 2d, with each block containing five 30-second trials punctuated by a 30-second rest (Fig. 2a). In each subject, the condition orders were randomized, and after finishing each block, subjects rested for 1 min, except for the resting time between the third and fourth blocks (C and D), which were set to 10 min. In each block, the fNIRS data were recorded using NIRStar; also, the NIRSCap remained attached to the participant’s head throughout the experiment.

Data processing

fNIRS data post-processing

The post-processing hemodynamic data analysis was performed using MATLAB60, and Homer361,62, an open-source MATLAB graphical user interface (GUI) designed for the processing and analysis of fNIRS data developed by the Boston University Neurophotonics Center. With the preprocessing pipeline of fNIRS signals involves: Channel Pruning, Conversion to Optical Density, Motion Artifact Correction, Bandpass Filtering63, Conversion to Hemoglobin Concentration, and Block Averaging. The Hemodynamic Response Function (HRF) of the oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), and total hemoglobin (HbT) were determined by averaging the values from 5 to 30 s after the stimulus start, with the initial 5 s delay included to account for the typical delay in hemodynamic responses associated with tasks64. In addition, the 3-dimensional brain topographic map and anatomical interpretation was performed using AtlasViewer65 version 2.44.0, an open-source MATLAB GUI, created to enhance the anatomical interpretation of fNIRS data processed with Homer.

Statistical analysis

RStudio66, was used to analyze the HbO mean data, which was calculated from 5 to 30 s from each condition HRF. The rstatix package67 was used to calculate simple descriptive statistics, as well as determine outliers and the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality. In addition, a two-way repeated measure ANOVA was used with the rstatix and afex68 packages to analyze the interaction effects between force levels and movement, as well as the main effects of each parameter. Post hoc analysis was used to confirm the hypothesis; moreover, pairwise comparisons using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon test were used to compare significant main effect among different individual conditions—task difficulties and types of movement. The non-parametric ANOVA analysis was done using Aligned Rank Transform (ART) using ARTool69 package; the non-parametric pairwise comparison used the Wilcoxon test as a substitute for the paired t-test for pairwise comparison. The effects of the hemisphere of the brain, contralateral (c) versus ipsilateral (i), were compared using a paired t-test or Wilcoxon test depending on their normality. The result is considered statistically significant if the p-value is less than 0.05, with a 95% confidence level applied. The Bonferroni correction is used to account for Type I error inflation with an α value divided by 8 different brain areas.

Interaction effect between task difficulty and movement.

Interaction effect between task difficulty and movement.

An analysis of the interaction effect between task difficulty and movement types revealed no statistically significant interactions across all examined brain regions (Table 1a). After applying the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.00625), none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance. In the cM1, the interaction effect had an F-value of 2.031 with a p-value of 0.147. The iM1 showed an F-value of 0.283 (p = 0.755). For the premotor cortices, the contralateral hemisphere had an F-value of 2.562 (p = 0.110), and the ipsilateral hemisphere showed an F-value of 3.677 (p = 0.036). In the SMA, the cSMA had an F-value of 0.702 (p = 0.498), and the iSMA had an F-value of 1.061 (p = 0.343). The prefrontal cortices also showed no significant interaction effects, with the cPFC having an F-value of 0.116 (p = 0.890) and the iPFC having an F-value of 0.059 (p = 0.943).

Effect of task difficulty.

Results showed significant main effects of task difficulty in the cM1, both hemispheres of PMC, and the iPFC, with F-values ranging from 6.489 to 9.006 (p < 0.00625) (Table 1b). The cM1 showed significant effects with the highest F-value of 9.006 (p = 0.003, ges = 0.062). The PMC was the only area exhibiting significant effects in both hemispheres; the cPMC had an F-value of 8.178 (p = 0.004, ges = 0.076), and the iPMC had an F-value of 8.072 (p = 0.005, ges = 0.084). In the PFC, which was analyzed using ART due to non-normality, significant effects were observed in the ipsilateral hemisphere, showing the lowest F-value of 6.489 and the lowest p-value of 0.002. Remarkably, the remaining brain regions did not exhibit significant effects of task difficulty.

The pairwise comparison indicates that in cM1 and both hemispheres of PMC and the iPFC; the tasks, relative to hard difficulty, possess a substantial increment in value, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The cM1 exhibits a notable increase of HbO mean when comparing easy to hard tasks (p = 0.0003, statistic = -4.0544). In PMC, contralateral, an enormous rise in HbO mean was observed when comparing movements at easy to hard difficulty (p = 0.00010, statistic = -3.5802), and a lower yet significant increase of HbO average was found between moderate and hard tasks (p = 0.0050, statistic = -3.0175). In ipsilateral, there was the highest upsurge in HbO mean compared to other regions when comparing movements at easy to those at hard difficulty (p = 0.0010, statistic = -3.5151); in the comparison between moderate and hard tasks, there was also a lower but significant increase of HbO average (p = 0.0030, statistic = -3.1725). In iPFC, both easy to hard (p = 0.0010, static = 134) and moderate to hard (p = 0.0009, static = 129) comparisons show highly significant increments in HbO mean level.

Effect of tasks on brain hemisphere

The effect of tasks on brain hemisphere results, summarized in Fig. 6, revealed significant differences between brain sides in the M1 and PFC. In M1, the mean HbO concentration was relatively higher in the contralateral hemisphere at 33.555 µmol compared to -3.296 µmol in the ipsilateral hemisphere, with a statistic of 5.6995 and a very highly significant p-value of 1.07 × 10− 7. However, the PMC and SMA did not exhibit a significant hemispherical effect, as evidenced by a p-value of 0.2230, and 0.0258, respectively.

Discussion

The fNIRS study in key brain ROIs was performed to monitor the hemodynamic response (HbO) corresponding to different movements and task difficulties in the upper extremity. However, there was no significant interaction between task difficulty and movement on HbO, indicating that difficulty of the task and movement independently affect brain activation due to the separation in neural control70.

Effect of task difficulty

The regulation of forces in tasks of varying difficulty impacts the brain’s HbO level based on a two-way repeated ANOVA analysis in cM1, PMC, and iPFC. This discovery aligns with studies emphasizing the significance of those areas in regulating force during tasks of different difficulty levels28,35,36. The cM1 has been recognized for over fifty years as being directly involved in executing voluntary movements and fine-tuning muscle strength for motor activities71–73 in our study it exhibited a significant effect on the mean HbO level (p = 0.003), demonstrating a highly significant increase of 41.015 µmol from easy to hard tasks (paired t-test, p = 0.0003), thereby highlighting its essential role in controlling force during tasks of varying difficulty. Additionally, both hemispheres of the PMC are important for planning movements and adjusting the force during actions of different difficulty74,75. The results also show a significant change in HbO levels in both hemispheres (p-values ranging from 0.004 to 0.005). To extend, on both sides, the same phenomenon is exhibited with a high rise of HbO in the easy vs. hard comparison: from − 26.245 µmol to 24.971 µmol and from − 21.610 µmol to 36.875 µmol in cPMC and iPMC, respectively. Moreover, similar but smaller increases in HbO concentrations of 45.245 µmol and 48.591 µmol were found in the comparison of moderate and hard tasks in cPMC and iPMC, respectively. This finding underlined the function of the PMC in regulating force during various tasks. Many studies have revealed that, in the PFC, during muscle force generation—particularly at low to moderate difficulty levels requiring small to moderate force regulation—the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) engages and hierarchically communicates with M1 to modulate force output effectively and enhance muscle contraction capabilities76. Precisely, the ipsilateral DLPFC shows sensitivity of HbO levels in response to task difficulty and its associated force requirements77, aligning with our finding, which notes a difference in the mean HbO levels in the iPFC (p = 0.002). The HbO concentration also shows an increasing trend from lower to higher difficulty, with increments of 35.188 µmol and 30.299 µmol observed in comparisons from easy to hard and from moderate to hard tasks, respectively.

Furthermore, the comparisons between hard tasks in movements showed significant variations in various conditions of movement, while no notable distinctions were found between easy and moderate tasks. The marked variance at hard difficulty tasks, which requires regulating forces close to the maximum usually needed for ADLs1,78,79, could be linked to a significant rise in brain activity when performing tasks requiring force regulation near maximum effort levels80.

Effect of movement

Our research also noted impacts on the HbO mean response to types of movements of the PFC in both brain hemispheres, which are in line with research highlighting the role of the PFC in orchestrating and executing arm movements through the integration of motor control signals35–37. The pronounced influence observed on both sides suggests that the PFC is engaged throughout all stages of movement execution, with the increasing HbO concentration of approximately 24.02 to 25.49 µmol in dynamic movement relative to static movement, especially in the DLPFC, being regulated by self-regulation strategies81. This signifies that the PFC oversees and adopts motor plans for arm movements, highlighting its critical role in motor control and coordination of motion. Regarding the dynamic movement condition, it is worth noting that although the robotic arm maintained symmetrical back-and-forth movements at constant speed and force, the safety protocol that halted movement when force errors exceeded 2 N introduced variability in movement cycles across trials. This methodological consideration may have contributed to differential effects between extension and flexion phases within each 30-second block average, potentially influencing our hemodynamic measurements.

Effect of tasks on brain hemisphere

Moreover, we notice variations in the hemodynamic response between the sides of the brain across the brain regions, including M1 and PFC. These variations align with knowledge of how the contralateral hemisphere of the brain functions in controlling the movements on the other side of the body due to the decussation of corticospinal tracts in the medulla oblongata4. Explicitly, the heightened activity seen in cM1 is in line with its role in carrying out movements for the other side of the body73, since the HbO concentration is higher in the contralateral side at 33.555 µmol compared to -3.296 µmol in the ipsilateral side. The distinct activation pattern observed in the PFC could indicate heightened brain activity linked to motor planning82; however, in the PFC, the higher brain activation is noticed in the ipsilateral hemisphere compared to the contralateral, with a difference of 8.367 µmol. This might be underpinned by the role of the iPFC in force regulation77. Conversely, both PMC and SMA did not display a noticeable contrast between hemispheres, which suggests that both areas might be involved bilaterally in movement preparation and sequencing.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into brain activation patterns during force-modulated upper extremity movements, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Our 30-channel fNIRS configuration prioritized motor and prefrontal regions, excluding the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) due to hardware constraints. This omission is significant given that sensory feedback is integral to motor control, with S1 processing the tactile and proprioceptive information essential for precise force adjustment83, potentially overlooking critical aspects of sensorimotor integration that enable accurate force regulation84. The absence of electromyography (EMG) recordings, moreover, limited our ability to detect subtle muscular patterns beyond force measurements85,86. Additionally, our robotic arm paradigm, though providing excellent experimental control, employed simplified movement patterns that may not fully capture the complexity of naturalistic upper-limb activities typically involved in ADLs. Future investigations should incorporate expanded cortical coverage, including S1, multimodal recordings, and more naturalistic movement tasks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the neural mechanisms governing force control during functional movements.

Conclusion

This research studies the relationship between brain activation in upper extremity motor control tasks with force control at different force levels using a robotic arm and fNIRS to assess motor control tasks and brain hemodynamic response as well as brain activation. Among the six different tasks, there are two types of motion—static and dynamic— with three different task difficulties based on different force levels: easy (4 N), moderate (12 N), and hard (20 N). In conclusion, our findings indicate that movement and task difficulty are separately controlled by the brain in different regions and to varying degrees, with no significant interaction effects between task difficulty and movement on HbO levels. Moreover, we found a significant effect of task difficulty and an increase in brain activation relative to higher difficulty in cM1, iPFC, and PMC—both contralateral and ipsilateral. However, both hemispheres of the PFC also showed significant effects related to movement type, with an increase in HbO concentration during static movements.
