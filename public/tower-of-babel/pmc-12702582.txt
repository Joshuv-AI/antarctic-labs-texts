Tendon-suture complex shows better repair outcomes in the reconstruction of the medial meniscus posterior root
Authors: Yian Sun, Xu Li, Daijun Xie, Zhou Zhang, Qiang Zhao, Meng Wu, Jin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to biomechanically compare three medial meniscus posterior root tear (MMPRT) repair techniques to support their clinical application.MethodsA total of 18 porcine knee joints and 12 bovine extensor tendons were allocated into three groups: transtibial pullout repair (TPR), tendon reconstruction (TR), and tendon-suture complex (TSC). Each sample underwent cyclic loading (1 to 30 N, 1,000 cycles), followed by failure testing. Displacement, elongation, stiffness, ultimate load, and failure modes were assessed.ResultsSignificant mean differences were detected between the TPR and TSC after 100 cycles (2.27 mm (SD 0.26) vs 1.89 mm (SD 0.20), p = 0.012), and also between the TSC and TPR or TR after 500 and 1,000 cycles (500 cycles: 2.08 mm (SD 0.22) vs 2.62 mm (SD 0.27) and 2.50 mm (SD 0.13), p = 0.001 and p = 0.009, respectively; 1,000 cycles: 2.22 mm (SD 0.21) vs 2.86 mm (SD 0.31) and 2.71 mm (SD 0.20), p = 0.001 and p = 0.011, respectively), while no significant difference was detected between the TPR and TR. The ultimate failure load in the TSC was greater than that in the TR (277.43 N (SD 75.57) vs 176.88 N (SD 36.80), p = 0.038) and did not differ from that in the TPR. TR and TSC presented a lower elongation than that noted in the TPR (14.09 mm (SD 4.32) and 13.88 mm (SD 4.59) vs 26.89 mm (SD 7.27), both p = 0.003), with no difference noted between TR and TSC. Stiffness was greater with the TSC than with TPR (23.82 N/mm (SD 6.94) vs 14.34 N/mm (SD 2.39), p = 0.028) and did not differ between TPR and TR or between TR and TSC. Failure modes varied: TPR failure by suture pull-through, and TR and TSC failure by tendon pull-through.ConclusionTR and TSC outperformed TPR, with TSC resulting in better displacement, elongation, and stiffness, and a higher ultimate failure load than TR. These findings suggest that suture augmentation enhances repair strength. TSC shows favourable biomechanics, but further clinical validation and rehabilitation optimization are needed for better long-term outcomes.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(12):1148–1156.

Introduction

Meniscus posterior root tears refer to a specific type of meniscal injury that is characterized by radial tears occurring within 10 mm of the meniscal posterior root attachment site.1,2 Among these tears, medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMPRTs) are more prevalent and account for 10% of all meniscal tears.3-5 A cadaveric study on MMPRTs reported that an unrepaired tear functionally approximated a total meniscectomy.6 MMPRTs result in a reduction in the tibiofemoral contact area and an approximate 25% increase in the tibiofemoral contact pressure.6,7 MMPRTs may also exacerbate knee joint laxity, contribute to articular cartilage degeneration, and accelerate the progression of osteoarthritis.8-11

MMPRTs are commonly treated surgically and typically involve meniscectomy and meniscal root repair.12 Arthroscopic meniscectomy has been used for this purpose for many years, but its clinical outcomes remain suboptimal thus far. Krivicich et al13 reported that the progression rate of osteoarthritis was 66% in the meniscectomy group, which was significantly greater than the 22% reported in the repair group. In recent years, meniscal root repair techniques, such as transtibial pullout repair (TPR) and suture anchor repair, have gained increasing attention for the treatment of MMPRTs.14 Clinical studies have shown that both TPR and suture anchor repair achieve favourable clinical outcomes.15-17 However, these repair techniques have certain limitations. For example, the TPR may allow for suture migration along the tunnel’s longitudinal axis, resulting in a “bungee effect”,18 while suture anchor repair is associated with an increased risk of neurovascular injury.19

Tendon reconstruction (TR) of the meniscal posterior root is a novel technique that aims to restore the functional level closer to the native meniscus. Park et al20 reported that the histological characteristics of a healthy meniscal posterior root closely resemble those of normal ligaments and tendons. Liao et al21 reported that in 129 patients who underwent medial meniscus posterior root (MMPR) reconstruction with autologous hamstring tendons, 76.0% of patients (98/129) achieved complete healing within a little over two years of the follow-up period. However, in our clinical application of this technique, micromotion is observed at the meniscus-tendon interface, which potentially compromises the stability of the repair construct. Therefore, in the present study, our research team designed a novel technique called the tendon-suture complex (TSC) to reduce the potential micromotion at the meniscus-tendon interface. In the reconstruction of the MMPR with a TSC, suture augmentation was applied to reinforce fixation after the tendon was subjected to MMPR.

This study aimed to biomechanically compare TPR, TR, and TSC using in vitro experiments and evaluate the differences among them. It was hypothesized that the repair outcomes in the TR and TSC groups are superior to those in the TPR group, and that the TSC group results in superior outcomes compared to the TR group.

Methods

Specimen preparation

A total of 18 porcine knee joints and 12 bovine extensor tendons were collected from a local butcher. The porcine knee joints were randomly assigned to the following three groups, each with six samples: TPR, TR, and TSC. The knee joints were prepared by removing excess skin and muscle and then severing the connecting ligaments to remove the femur. The tibia was sectioned 10 cm below the tibial plateau, following which the lateral meniscus was excised from all joints. The research team carefully examined all the experimental samples to exclude specimens of poor quality. The final samples were stored at -20°C after dissection. The samples were thawed for 24 hours at room temperature before testing. In the TR and TSC groups, the bovine tendons were further divided randomly into two groups, with six tendons in each group. The tendons were subsequently trimmed to a length of 150 mm and a width of 7 mm. Ultrahigh-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) sutures (SC1; Tianxing Medical, China) were used to reinforce the tendon ends, which ensured equal load distribution across both ends.

Repair procedure

A sports medicine doctor with systematic orthopaedic training (YS) performed all surgical repair procedures independently, which ensured consistency throughout the experiments. Prior to commencing the repair procedures, the MMPR was sectioned from its attachment site to create a standard MMPRT model.

In the TPR group, after positioning with the anterior cruciate ligament locator, a drill sheath (Smith & Nephew, UK) was used to create a 6 mm diameter tunnel, with its origin at the meniscal tear site and terminating point 3 cm below the tibial plateau, midway between the tibial tuberosity and the medial collateral ligament. The tunnel formed a 70° angle with the tibial plateau. Next, two UHMWPE sutures were passed through the red-red zone of the meniscus within 10 mm of the tear site. As the suturing technique, a second double-locking loop suture (2DLS) fixation method was performed, as described by Anz et al.22 This suturing method provides greater strength than simple suturing techniques. The sutures were subsequently passed through a looped nitinol wire and pulled through the tunnel, and a 6 mm screw was used for fixation, during which the sutures were knotted on the screw to prevent them from being pulled toward the joint.

In the TR and TSC groups, an 8 mm tunnel was created using the same method as that used for the TPR group. A scalpel was then used to make an 8 mm longitudinal incision in the red-red zone of the medial meniscus within 10 mm of the tear site. The tendons were then passed through the meniscal incision and pulled through the tunnel. In both groups, during fixation, an 8 mm diameter screw was used (Figure 1). The difference between the TSC and TR groups was that, after the above procedures, the TSC group required an additional reinforcement suture at the site where the tendon passed through the meniscal incision; the suturing method used for this reinforcement was a single simple suture. The needle was inserted 5 mm from the meniscus-tendon contact area, passed twice through the tendon at the meniscal incision, and finally secured with a surgical knot (Figure 2).

Both the tendon reconstruction (TR) and tendon-suture complex (TSC) techniques used bovine extensor tendon graft measuring 150 mm in length and 7 mm in width for medial meniscal posterior root reconstruction. The tendon was passed through the meniscal incision and then pulled out via an 8 mm diameter bone tunnel. This tunnel originated at the site of the meniscal tear and terminated at the anteromedial tibial cortex. Fixation was achieved with an 8 mm interference screw.

Biomechanical study

All tests were performed at room temperature (24°C ± 2°C), and before testing, the medial meniscus was soaked in a saline solution to prevent drying. The instrument used for the experiment was an electric fatigue tensile-compression testing machine (KNPL-W5000N; Kaien, China). The distal tibia of the sample, which had been subjected to axial cutting, was fixed to the base of the testing equipment. The tibial tunnel and the pulling force of the testing equipment were aligned in parallel, which ensured that the applied force was more complete and concentrated (Figure 3). A customized, serrated clamp was used, with a wire wrapped around the body of the meniscus to increase friction between the meniscus and the clamp, which prevented slippage during testing. In order to prevent interference during the stiffness analysis, the meniscus was clamped 1 cm laterally from the sutures or tendons in the meniscus.1

The testing instrument was prepared with an installed sample. A custom serrated clamp was employed, where wire was wrapped around the meniscus to prevent slippage during testing. a) Front view. b) Side view.

Initially, ten cycles of preconditioning were applied to all the repaired constructs, with the cyclic loading ranging from 1 N to 10 N at 0.1 Hz. The step ensured reduced creep in the meniscal fibres. After preconditioning, the meniscus was cyclically loaded for 1,000 cycles, ranging from 10 N to 30 N at 0.5 Hz. This loading protocol has been used in previous studies to simulate the tensile forces that the MMPR might experience under a tibiofemoral load of 500 N within the neutral rotation range of 0° to 90°,23 which represents a typical range of motion and partial load during the postoperative rehabilitation protocol for meniscal root repair. The meniscus was subsequently pulled to failure at a rate of 30 mm/min.24 The displacement at 100, 500, and 1,000 cycles was recorded during the cyclic testing. Additionally, the ultimate failure load, elongation, and stiffness were measured during the failure test. Displacement was defined as the difference between the peak value of the crosshead position during the first cycle and the peak values at 100, 500, or 1,000 cycles. Meniscal stiffness was defined as the slope of the steepest curve on the load-displacement curve, which covered at least 30% of the data points between the applied load and the failure of the meniscus (Figure 4). Elongation was defined as the displacement of the suture or tendon at the ultimate failure load. The failure mode of the samples was also assessed through visual observation.

The figure was generated from data collected by the testing instrument, representing the displacement of the suture or tendon during the load application process (load-displacement curve). The horizontal axis represents the displacement of the suture or tendon, whereas the vertical axis corresponds to the applied load. Stiffness was defined as the slope of the curve encompassing at least 30% of the data points from load application to ultimate failure load. In this load–displacement curve, ‘MIN’ represents the load and displacement at the beginning of loading, ‘MAX’ represents the load and displacement at the ultimate failure load, and ‘MAX_D’ denotes the load and displacement values detected by the testing system after complete specimen failure.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v27 (IBM, USA). The data were expressed as means (SD), with significance set at p < 0.05. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality (p > 0.05), and the normally distributed data with homogeneous variance (displacement after 100, 500, and 1,000 cycles, ultimate failure load, and elongation) were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The non-normally distributed or heteroscedastic data (stiffness) were subjected to the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction. In order to minimize the Type I errors, p-values were adjusted accordingly.

When the failure modes were evaluated, all the samples in the TPR group failed because the sutures were cut through the meniscus (Figure 5a), while all the specimens in the TR and TSC groups failed because the tendons cut through the meniscus (Figures 5b and 5c). Discussion

The most interesting finding of this study was that both TR and TSC presented superior biomechanical properties compared to those noted in the TPR group, and this was particularly true for the TSC group. Specifically, the TR and TSC groups demonstrated lower displacement during cyclic loading, lower elongation, and greater stiffness under failure loading than the TPR group. However, TR resulted in a significantly lower ultimate failure load than both TPR and the TSC, whereas the ultimate failure load of the TSC group was greater than that of the TR group and was comparable to that of the TPR group. These findings support our initial hypothesis that the meniscus-tendon interface represents the weakest point in the repair construct, and that reinforcing this region could enhance repair outcomes.

Recent advancements in meniscal root repair have aimed to improve outcomes. Andrews et al25 analyzed the transition between the meniscal root and body, revealing that the root exhibited a ligament-like structure. Based on this concept, Lee et al26 introduced the tendon reconstruction technique in 2017, aiming to increase the strength of the repair construct to a level comparable to the strength of the native meniscus. Compared to traditional suture repair, TR provides greater initial fixation strength, reducing the bungee effect and enhancing the quality of healing achieved.19,26 Chen et al27 conducted an animal study to investigate the effects of patellar tendon reconstruction on the MMPR and reported that collagen expression in the repaired suture was similar to that in a normal meniscus, and the technique promoted fibrocartilage-like meniscal regeneration with the potential for tendon-to-bone healing.

In the cyclic loading tests, although the transtibial pullout repair group exhibited the greatest displacement after 100, 500, and 1,000 cycles, the displacement did not exceed 3 mm. Previous studies have indicated that a displacement threshold of 3 mm in cyclic loading tests is associated with meniscal failure.28,29 Therefore, it was concluded that this repair construct is unlikely to cause meniscal failure. LaPrade et al30 reported a mean displacement of 2.97 mm (SD 0.57) after 1,000 cycles with the use of 2DLS for meniscal root repair, under identical conditions, which was slightly greater than the values obtained in this study. This discrepancy may arise from their method of simply pulling the meniscus without fixing it to the tibia, which focuses the stress on the meniscus rather than applying this force to the entire repair construct.30 Wu et al31 reported that when tendon graft repair was used, the mean displacement noted in the cyclic loading tests after 1,000 cycles under a load range of 2 to 20 N was 2.4 mm (SD 0.9). This result was slightly smaller than the values noted for the TR group in the present study. This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher cyclic load applied in this study (10 to 30 N). Rosslenbroich et al32 conducted a biomechanical test on porcine knee joints and reported that the mean displacement of the intact meniscal posterior root was 2.8 mm (SD 0.8) after 1,000 cycles. This value is close to the displacement values noted in the TR and TSC groups after 1,000 cycles in this study, indicating that both TR and TSC demonstrated better stability and also achieved overall biomechanical properties similar to those of an intact meniscal posterior root.

In this study, the ultimate failure load of the TR group was significantly lower than that of the TPR group, which is consistent with the findings of Wu et al,31 who conducted a biomechanical study and revealed that the tendon graft technique had a significantly lower ultimate failure load than the transtibial pullout suture technique in failure load testing. This finding suggested that the biomechanical properties of TR were inferior to those of TPR before the fibrocartilage-like regeneration at the meniscus-tendon interface.27,33 Deng et al34 reported that the meniscus-root transition zone is a biomechanically weak point encountered during the healing process. The tendon reconstruction technique requires a larger incision at the meniscus-root transition zone to facilitate tendon passage, which affects the healing of this area and leads to micromotion at the meniscus-tendon interface, thereby potentially influencing the healing outcomes.35 In order to address these issues, this study designed the TSC technique, which effectively improved the ultimate failure load.

In this study, the suture materials and techniques were carefully selected to provide a robust comparison with the TR and TSC groups. Okazaki et al36 investigated the effects of different suture materials and reported that UHMWPE sutures resulted in better clinical outcomes. Mitchell et al37 conducted a biomechanical study on human cadaveric menisci and evaluated the mechanical properties of four different meniscal suture techniques. Their findings demonstrated that the “locking loop” suture resulted in a significantly greater mean failure load (194.4 N (SD 45.1)) and greater stiffness (2,909 N/mm (SD 1,373)) compared to the other techniques. Building on these findings, Anz et al22 used the 2DLS technique and achieved a higher ultimate failure load (368 N (SD 76)) compared to the other repair techniques. Therefore, in the present study, UHMWPE sutures and the 2DLS suture technique were used, as these two were expected to yield superior biomechanical outcomes.

The exploration of the TSC is considered preliminary research. Consequently, a single simple suture was used at the meniscus-tendon interface when the suturing technique was designed for the TSC group. Nonetheless, the biomechanical properties of this complex are superior to those of TR. A previous biomechanical study evaluating the different suture techniques for repairing radial meniscus tears revealed that a technique called double vertical cross-suture exhibited the best biomechanical properties (ultimate failure load: 276.8 N; stiffness: 60.7 N/mm).38 Therefore, when performing fixation at the meniscus-tendon interface, this method may be considered to further enhance the repair effectiveness of the TSC. However, more complex techniques often lead to longer operating times. Therefore, the ultimate choice of suture technique requires careful consideration, and further research on suturing techniques at the meniscus-tendon interface is essential.

Certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as an in vitro biomechanical study, it did not involve the assessment of the healing conditions of different repair constructs following MMPR surgery. Second, while the meniscus in the body is typically subjected to forces from various directions, in this study only a tensile force directed upwards and parallel to the tibial tunnel was applied, and this approach may not fully replicate the loading conditions of the knee joint in a physiological setting. However, this approach was chosen to minimize shear friction to the extent possible.39 Third, porcine knee joints and bovine extensor tendons were used to simulate human knee joints and tendons, as human samples for such a study are difficult to obtain. Nevertheless, porcine knee joints are similar to human knee joints in size and morphology, rendering them a common substitute for human joints in relevant research.32,40 Previous studies have shown that bovine extensor tendons can be reliably used as substitutes for human tendons in experiments.41

This study revealed that both TR and TSC outperform transtibial pullout repair, with the TSC exhibiting superior biomechanical performance in terms of displacement, elongation, and stiffness. However, TR resulted in a lower ultimate failure load than both transtibial pullout repair and tendon-suture complex, highlighting the meniscus-tendon interface as a critical weak point. The superior ultimate failure load achieved using the tendon-suture complex suggests that suture augmentation enhances repair strength. While tendon reconstruction exhibited promising outcomes, further clinical validation and optimized rehabilitation protocols are needed to achieve improved long-term outcomes.
