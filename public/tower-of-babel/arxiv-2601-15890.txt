Abstract
Monadic stability generalizes many tameness notions from structural graph theory such as planarity, bounded degree, bounded tree-width, and nowhere density. The sparsification conjecture predicts that the (possibly dense) monadically stable graph classes are exactly those that can be logically encoded by first-order (FO) transductions in the (always sparse) nowhere dense classes. So far this conjecture has been verified for several special cases, such as for classes of bounded shrub-depth, and for the monadically stable fragments of bounded (linear) clique-width, twin-width, and merge-width. In this work we propose the existential positive sparsification conjecture , predicting that the more restricted co-matching-free, monadically stable classes are exactly those that can be transduced from nowhere dense classes using only existential positive FO formulas. While the general conjecture remains open, we verify its truth for all known special cases of the original conjecture. Even stronger, we find the sparse preimages as subgraphs of the dense input graphs. As a key ingredient, we introduce a new combinatorial operation, called subflip , that arises as the natural co-matching-free analog of the flip operation, which is a central tool in the characterization of monadic stability. Using subflips, we characterize the co-matching-free fragment of monadic stability by appropriate strengthenings of the known flip-flatness and flipper game characterizations for monadic stability. In an attempt to generalize our results to the more expressive MSO logic, we discover (rediscover?) that on relational structures (existential) positive MSO has the same expressive power as (existential) positive FO.

Contribution 1: Existential positive sparsification

The main object of our study is the
semi-ladder-free
fragment of monadic dependence, which strictly subsumes the nowhere dense classes and is strictly contained in the monadically stable classes.
A class is semi-ladder-free if and only if it is half-graph-free and co-matching-free
[
16
]
.
Prominent examples of semi-ladder-free monadically dependent classes are the class of map graphs and fixed powers of nowhere dense classes
[
16
]
.
Analogously to the sparsification conjecture, we conjecture that the semi-ladder-free monadically dependent classes are exactly the
existential positive
transductions of nowhere dense classes.
More formally, a formula is
positive
if it contains no negation symbol
(\neg)
, and in particular no non-equality
(\neq)
. A positive formula is moreover
existential
if it contains no universal quantifier
(\forall)
. In this case we call it an
EP-formula
.
We call a transduction an
EP-transduction
if it is specified by an EP-formula.

It turns out that when studying EP-transductions it is crucial that all vertices have self-loops (i.e., every vertex is adjacent to itself).
Intuitively, the reason for this is that otherwise the information that two vertices are connected by an edge implicitly carries the non-positive information that these vertices are non-equal.
We will discuss this phenomenon in more detail in the next subsection and also in
Section

3
.
In the following, a
reflexive graph
is a graph in which every vertex has a self-loop.
For additional clarity, we mark statements requiring this assumption with a
\circlearrowleft
symbol.
On the other hand, if we do not mention reflexivity explicitly, this means we allow self-loops in our graphs, but do not require them. Sometimes we use the term
partially reflexive graph
, to stress that we are working in this more relaxed setting.

We propose the following
existential positive sparsification conjecture
.

Conjecture 1.6

(
\circlearrowleft
)
.

For every class
\mathscr{C}
of reflexive graphs, the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free and monadically dependent.

2.

\mathscr{C}
is an EP-transduction of a nowhere dense class of reflexive graphs.

Note that according to
[
28
]
and
[
16
]
the first condition that
\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free and monadically dependent is equivalent to the condition that
\mathscr{C}
is co-matching-free and monadically stable.

Again, the statement can be refined to subproperties of monadic dependence.

Conjecture 1.7

(
\circlearrowleft
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a non-trivial and transduction-closed class property. For every class
\mathscr{C}
of reflexive graphs, the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is a semi-ladder-free class with
\mathcal{P}
.

2.

\mathscr{C}
is an EP-transduction of a biclique-free class of reflexive graphs with
\mathcal{P}
.

As our first main result, we establish the implication (2.
\Rightarrow
1.) of
1.6
. For this direction no assumptions on self-loops are required.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a nowhere dense class of partially reflexive graphs.
Every EP-transduction of
\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free and monadically dependent.

As a corollary we obtain that also the implication (2.
\Rightarrow
1.) of
1.7
is true.

Corollary 1.9
.

The implication (2.
\Rightarrow
1.) of
1.7
is true.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an EP-transduction of a biclique-free class
\mathscr{D}
with some non-trivial, transduction-closed class property
\mathcal{P}
.

\mathscr{D}
must be monadically dependent, because monadic dependence is the most general non-trivial, transduction-closed class property.
Then by biclique-freeness,
\mathscr{D}
is nowhere dense. By

1.8
,
\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free. As

is transduction-closed,
\mathscr{C}
still has
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Consider the following algorithmic application of our structural result.
It was proved in

that the

Contribution 2: The monadic non-equality property

We take some time to distill the model theoretic core, that helps to explain both the connection between semi-ladder-freeness and EP-transductions, and the need to work with reflexive graphs.

Let
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
be an FO formula over the signature of colored graphs, where
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
are two tuple variables of the same length
t
.
We say that
\varphi
has the

on a graph class

if for every
\ell\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
there is a graph coloring
G_{\ell}^{+}
of a graph
G_{\ell}\in\mathscr{C}
and tuples
\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{\ell}\in V(G)^{t}
such that for all

G^{+}_{\ell}\models\varphi(\bar{a}_{i},\bar{a}_{j})\iff i\leqslant j.

(
*
)

While we have previously defined monadic stability via transductions, the original (and equivalent) definition by Baldwin and Shelah
[
2
]
states that a graph class
\mathscr{C}
is monadically stable if no formula has the monadic order-property on
\mathscr{C}
.
It is now natural to define the
monadic non-equality-property
by replacing (

∗

‣
1
) in the definition of the monadic order property with

G^{+}_{\ell}\models\varphi(\bar{a}_{i},\bar{a}_{j})\iff i\neq j.

The simple formula
\varphi(x,y)=\neg(x=y)
has the monadic non-equality-property on every graph class that contains arbitrarily large graphs.
Hence, the monadic non-equality-property is only interesting for positive formulas.
We show that for reflexive graphs, the monadic non-equality-property for EP-formulas characterizes the semi-ladder-free fragment of monadic dependence.

Theorem 1.11

(
\circlearrowleft
)
.

For every class
\mathscr{C}
of reflexive graphs the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free and monadically dependent.

2.

No EP-formula has the monadic non-equality-property on
\mathscr{C}
.

Above, assuming reflexiveness is crucial, and it is illustrative to see the difference to the general partially reflexive case, where we prove the following.

Theorem 1.12
.

For every class
\mathscr{C}
of partially reflexive graphs the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free, irreflexive-clique-free, and monadically dependent.

2.

No EP-formula has the monadic non-equality-property on
\mathscr{C}
.

Here, an
irreflexive
graph is a graph, in which no vertex has a self-loop.
We call a graph class
irreflexive-clique-free
if it does not contain all irreflexive cliques as induced subgraphs.
Clearly, on the class of irreflexive cliques, the edge relation
E(x,y)
has the monadic non-equality-property.
This hints at the reason why in the setting of positive logic, reflexive graphs behave nicer than irreflexive ones:
in irreflexive graphs, the positive information of being connected by an edge carries the negative information of being non-equal.

Contribution 3: Subflips

As a foundation for the logical results presented in Contributions 1 and 2, we initiate the systematic combinatorial study of the semi-ladder-free fragment of monadic dependence. We obtain multiple characterizations through an operation, which we call
subflip
, which we believe to be of independent interest.
Our starting point are the more general monadically stable classes (i.e., the half-graph-free fragment of monadic dependence).
Only recently, fixed-parameter tractability of the FO model checking problem was established for all monadically stable classes
[
8
,
12
]
.
Key to these algorithmic results was the emergence of a multitude of characterizations of monadic stability that are combinatorial rather than model theoretic in nature.
At the heart of these combinatorial characterizations lies the
flip
operation.
Given a graph
G
, a
k
-flip
of
G
is obtained by choosing a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
V(G)
of size at most
k
and complementing the edge relation between arbitrary pairs of parts from
\mathcal{P}
(possibly complementing parts with themselves, but preserving self-loops).
See
Figure

4
for an example.

Figure 4:
Two graphs that are
3
-flips of each other.
This is witnessed by the partition
\{P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\}
where the pairs
(P_{1},P_{2})
,
(P_{2},P_{3})
, and
(P_{3},P_{3})
were flipped.

The goal is usually to separate sets of vertices by putting them at high distance, using a
k
-flip for some small
k
.
Exemplary for this is the following characterization of monadic stability by a property called
flip-flatness
, which is one of the main tools for the algorithmic treatment of monadically stable classes.

Definition 1.13
.

A graph class
\mathscr{C}
is
flip-flat

if for every
r\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
there is a function

M_{r}:\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
and a constant
k_{r}\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
, such that for all
m\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
,
G\in\mathscr{C}
, and
W\subseteq V(G)
with

|W|\geqslant M_{r}(m)
, there is a subset
A\subseteq W
with
|A|\geqslant m

and a
k_{r}
-flip
H
of
G
, such that for every two distinct vertices

\textnormal{dist}_{H}(u,v)\geqslant r.

Theorem 1.14

(
[
11
]
)
.

A graph class is monadically stable if and only if it is flip-flat.

Intuitively, flip-flatness states that in every huge set
W
, we find a still large subset
A
whose vertices are pairwise at high distance (“well-separated”) after performing a small flip.

In this work we consider a restriction of the flip operation that we call
subflip
. A graph
H
is a
k
-subflip
of a graph
G
if it is both a
k
-flip and a subgraph of
G
.
This means subflips are only allowed to remove edges, so we can only complement the edge relation between two parts
P
and
Q
of the flip partition
\mathcal{P}
of
V(G)
if

•

P
and
Q
are the same part and the graph induced on
P=Q
is a clique, or

•

P
and
Q
are distinct parts and the graph semi-induced between
P
and
Q
is a biclique.

Being more restricted, subflips have some advantages over flips.
To give an example, the next lemma from
[
3
]
states that multiple flips of the same graph can be aggregated into a single flip that roughly achieves the same separation.
This aggregation of flips is lossy and highly non-trivial to prove.

Lemma 1.15

(
[
3

)

Let
G
be a graph and
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
be
k
-flips of

.
There is an

such that for all

and

6\cdot\textnormal{dist}_{H}(u,v)\geqslant\textnormal{dist}_{H_{i}}(u,v).

In contrast, aggregation of subflips is simple and lossless by working with the common refinement of the two flip partitions. (We later prove a strengthening of this observation in
Lemma

Observation 1.16
.

Let
G
be a graph and
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
be a
k

G

k^{m}
-subflip
H
of

.

This tameness comes at the cost of subflips being less powerful than unrestricted flips.
A
2
-flip can turn any co-matching into a matching, by flipping between the two parts of the bipartition.
This flip achieves very strong separation: after the flip, every vertex has a single neighbor and distance
\infty
to all other vertices. This is not possible to achieve with a small subflip.
However, it turns out that co-matchings are in some sense the only graphs were subflips perform worse than flips.
As one of our main results, we show that in co-matching-free classes, flips can always be approximated using subflips.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
G
be a graph that contains no semi-induced co-matching of size

Leveraging the above theorem, we obtain multiple combinatorial characterizations of semi-ladder-free monadically dependent classes, by replacing flips with subflips in known characterizations of monadic stability.
We need to make no assumptions about self-loops here.

\mathscr{C}

We use standard notation from graph theory and model theory

We write

Colored graphs

In the context of logic we treat a

Given a graph

for its vertex set and

for its edge set.
We also allow self-loops, i.e., we may have
E(v,v)
for vertices

(see
Section

3
for a discussion of this assumption).
A graph is called
reflexive
if every vertex has a self-loop and
irreflexive
if no vertex has a self-loop.
A graph
H
is a
subgraph
of
G
if
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
and

E(H)\subseteq E(G)
.
For graphs
G,H
(whose vertex sets may overlap) we write
G\cup H
for the graph with vertex set
V(G)\cup V(H)
and edge set
E(G)\cup E(H)
. We sometimes write
G\uplus H
if the vertex sets of
G
and
H
are disjoint.
We recall the following definition from the introduction.

See
1.1

The
co-matching-index
of a graph
G
is the largest number
t
such that
G
contains a semi-induced co-matching of order
t
.

A
monadic extension
of the graph signature is obtained by adding (finitely many) unary predicate symbols.
We usually do not distinguish between a relation symbol and its interpretation.
We refer to unary predicates as
colors
.
A
colored graph
is a graph expanded by a (finite) set of
colors.

If
\mathscr{C}
is a class of graphs and
\sigma^{+}
is a monadic extension of the graph signature, we write
\mathscr{C}[\sigma^{+}]
for the class of all
\sigma^{+}
-expansions of graphs from
\mathscr{C}
.

First-order logic

First-order logic over a signature
\sigma
is defined in
the usual way.
We usually write
\bar{x}
for tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

of variables,
\bar{a}
for tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell})
of elements and leave it to the context to determine the length of the tuple.
A formula is
existential
if it contains no universal quantifiers and negation is applied
only to quantifier-free subformulas
A formula is
positive
if it does not use negations (in particular it cannot use
\neq
).
We call an existential positive formula an
EP-formula
.

As a shorthand, we write
\textnormal{dist}(x,y)\leqslant r
for the existential positive formula

\exists z_{1}\ldots\exists z_{r-1}\ \big(E(x,z_{1})\wedge E(z_{1},z_{2})\wedge\ldots\wedge E(z_{r-2},z_{r-1})\wedge E(z_{r-1},y)\big),

stating that two elements in a graph have distance at most
r
.
Note that we do not require the

to be distinct, and hence also elements whose distance is strictly smaller than
r
can satisfy this formula, in particular in the presence of self-loops.

For a formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
and a (colored) graph
G
, we denote by
\varphi(G)
the set all tuples
\bar{v}
of vertices of
G
such that
G
satisfies
\varphi(\bar{v})
,
that is,
\varphi(G)=\{\bar{v}\in V(G)^{|\bar{x}|}:G\models\varphi(\bar{v})\}
.
A
simple interpretation

\mathsf{I}
of graphs in (colored) graphs is a pair
(\nu(x),\eta(x,y))
consisting of two formulas (over the signature of (colored) graphs), where
\eta
is symmetric, i.e.,

\models\eta(x,y)\leftrightarrow\eta(y,x)
.
If
G^{+}
is a (colored) graph, then
H=\mathsf{I}(G^{+})
is the graph with vertex set
V(H)=\nu(G^{+})
and edge set
E(H)=\eta(G^{+})\cap\nu(G^{+})^{2}
.

For a set
\sigma
of colors, the
coloring operation

\Gamma_{\sigma}
maps a graph

to the set
\Gamma_{\sigma}(G)
of all its
\sigma
-colorings.
A transduction

is the composition

\mathsf{I}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}
of a coloring operation
\Gamma_{\sigma}
and a simple interpretation
\mathsf{I}
of graphs in
\sigma
-colored graphs.
In other words, for every graph

we have

\mathsf{T}(G)=\{\mathsf{I}(H^{+}):H^{+}\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(G)\}
.
In many cases we will deal with transductions where the domain formula
\nu(x)
is simply true. In this case we may simply specify the formula
\eta(x,y)
, which by the color predicates it uses fully determines a transduction. We speak of an
\eta
-transduction
.
For a class
\mathscr{C}
of graphs we define
\mathsf{T}(\mathscr{C}):=\bigcup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\mathsf{T}(G)
, and we say that a class
\mathscr{D}
is a
transduction
of

, or that
\mathscr{C}

transduces

\mathscr{D}
, if there exists a transduction
\mathsf{T}
such that
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathsf{T}(\mathscr{C})
.
A transduction is an
EP-transduction
if the formulas in the simple interpretation are EP-formulas.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of graphs is
monadically dependent
if and only if it cannot transduce the class of all graphs, it is
monadically stable
if and only if it cannot transduce the class of all half-graphs
[
2
]
.
Equivalently, a class is monadically stable if and only if it is monadically dependent and half-graph-free
[
28
]
.

We next collect some properties about transductions.
It is well-known that transductions compose, see e.g.
[
17
]
.
As the next lemma shows the same is true for EP-transductions.
For the proof one can follow the lines of the proof in
[

]
and note that the composition of positive existential formulas is again a positive existential formula.

Lemma 2.1
.

The class of EP-transductions is closed under composition. More precisely, let

\mathsf{T}_{1}=\mathsf{I}_{1}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma_{1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{T}_{2}=\mathsf{I}_{2}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma_{2}}

be EP-transductions with disjoint color sets
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
.
Then there exists an EP-transduction
\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{I}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}
, where
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
, such that for every graph

,

\mathsf{T}(G)\supseteq(\mathsf{T}_{2}\circ\mathsf{T}_{1})(G).

We further show how to combine transductions that operate on parts of a graph.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every (EP-)transduction
\mathsf{T}
and

with the following property.
For every graph

Proof.

Assume
\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{I}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}
for color set

Let

consist of the following colors:

•

for each

and
\overline{U_{i}}
(intended to mark (non)membership in the set

•

for each

and each color

(intended to represent the

We define

Intuitively,

Let

We show that

On self-loops

In this section, we justify the non-standard assumption that we consider graphs with self-loops in the context of positive transductions.

3.1
Self-loops in general transductions

We first observe that in the case of general (i.e., not necessarily positive) transductions, whether we forbid/allow/enforce self-loops has no influence on the expressive power of transductions at all.
Here, whether a vertex has a self-loop or not is essentially the same as being marked with a unary predicate: we can distinguish a “regular” edge
uv
from a self-loop by checking that
\neg(u=v)
. Therefore, regarding the input graph of a transduction, self-loops can neither be used to

•

…conceal information, as the transduction can recover the graph without self-loops, nor

•

…add new information, as the transduction can add a unary predicate in the coloring step that carries the same information.

Moreover regarding its output, by using one of the following three rewritings,
a transduction
\mathsf{T}_{\varphi}
can always be assumed to produce output graphs that have …

(R1)

…no self-loops at all, by
rewriting
\varphi(x,y)
to
\neg(x=y)\wedge\varphi(x,y)
, or

(R2)

…a self-loop on every vertex, by
rewriting
\varphi(x,y)
to
(x=y)\vee\varphi(x,y)
, or even

(R3)

…self-loops on an arbitrary subset
S
of vertices, by
rewriting
\varphi(x,y)
to

\big(\neg(x=y)\wedge\varphi(x,y)\big)\vee\big((x=y)\wedge S(x)\big),

where
S
is added as a unary predicate in the coloring step of the transduction.

Hence, self-loops are not relevant in the setting of general transductions.

3.2
Self-loops in positive transductions

In the setting of positive transductions, self-loops naturally appear and seem unavoidable.
Consider for example the transduction
\mathsf{T}_{\varphi}
specified by

\varphi(x,y):=E(x,y)\vee\big(\exists z\ E(x,z)\wedge E(z,y)\big)

that creates the square of a graph.
Applied to a star, this creates self-loops on every vertex, as depicted in
Figure

5
.

Figure 5:
A star and the graph obtained by applying the transduction
\mathsf{T}_{\varphi}
to it.

Note that we cannot use
(R1)
to make
\varphi
irreflexive, due to the used negation.
Forbidding self-loops would therefore severely restrict our ability to express positive transductions that produce loopless graphs.
For our work the presence of self-loops is crucial.
One could be tempted to modify the definition of a transduction by adding a post-processing step that removes all self-loops from the output graph. Let us call this new notion an
irreflexive-transduction
.
This new notion is no remedy.
A crucial property of EP-transductions is that they are transitive (
Lemma

2.1
). For EP-irreflexive-transductions transitivity fails.
The example in
Figure

6
shows how to create co-matchings from matchings by chaining three EP-irreflexive-transductions, which is not possible by a single EP-irreflexive-transduction.
The second transduction is defined by the formula
\varphi(x,y):=\exists z\ (E(x,z)\wedge E(z,y))
.
Note that the first EP-irreflexive-transduction creates a large irreflexive clique from an independent set, which is not possible with an EP-transduction.

Figure 6:
A chain of three EP-irreflexive-transductions that produce the class of co-matchings from the class of matchings.

As mentioned in the introduction,
Theorem

1.11
fails in the absence of self-loops:
in the class of cliques without self-loops (this class is semi-ladder-free and monadically dependent) the simple formula
\varphi(x,y)=E(x,y)
has the monadic non-equality-property.

The “philosophical reason” as to why the absence self-loops involuntarily increases the expressive power of EP-formulas is the following:
An edge between two vertices that do not have self-loops carries the additional information that these two vertices are non-equal; an inherently non-positive property.

Subflips

In this section, we introduce the new notion of a
subflip
.
For this purpose, let us first review the notion of a
flip
from the literature.

4.1
Flips and partitions

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of a set
V
.
For every element
v\in V
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(v)\in\mathcal{P}
the part of
\mathcal{P}
that contains
v
.
For a set
S\subseteq V
we denote by

\mathcal{P}|_{S}:=\{P\cap S:P\in\mathcal{P}\}

the
restriction
of
\mathcal{P}
to
S
, which is a partition of
S
.
For another partition
\mathcal{Q}
on
V
we denote by

\mathcal{P}\wedge\mathcal{Q}:=\{P\cap Q:P\in\mathcal{P},Q\in\mathcal{Q}\}

the
common refinement
of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.

Fix a graph
G
and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of its vertices.
Let
F\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{2}
be a symmetric relation.
We define
H:=G\oplus(\mathcal{P},F)
to be the graph with vertex set
V(G)
,
where we define the edges between distinct vertices
u,v\in V(G)
using the following condition:

uv\in E(H)\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}uv\notin E(G)&\text{if }(\mathcal{P}(u),\mathcal{P}(v))\in F,\\
uv\in E(G)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Self-loops are preserved, i.e., for every
v\in V(G)
we have
vv\in E(H)\Leftrightarrow vv\in E(G)
.
We call
H
a
\mathcal{P}
-flip
of
G
.
If
\mathcal{P}
has at most
k

H
a
k
-flip
of
G
. See

in the introduction for an example.
We note a few basic facts about flips.

Fact 4.1
.

Let
G
,
H
,
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be graphs on a vertex set
V
, let

\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
be partitions of

, and let

.

1.

Symmetry: If
H
is a
\mathcal{P}
-flip of

, then

is also a
\mathcal{P}
-flip of
H
.

2.

is a

is a

-flip of

is a

.

3.

\mathcal{P}
-flip of

-flip of

.

4.2
Subflips

Definition 4.2

(Fully (non-)adjacent sets)
.

For a graph
G

A=B
, we say
A
and

are

(

and

are adjacent in
G
(non-adjacent in

If
A
and
B
are disjoint, this means they semi-induce a biclique in
G
(an edgeless graph in
G
).
If
A=B
, this means
A

Because we only speak about distinct vertices, the above definition makes no assumptions about self-loops.

Definition 4.3

(Subflips)
.

For a graph
G
and partition
\mathcal{P}
of
V(G)
, we define the
\mathcal{P}
-subflip of
G
to be the graph

G\ominus\mathcal{P}:=G\oplus(\mathcal{P},F)
, where
F:=\{(A,B):\text{$A$ and $B$ are fully adjacent in $G$}\}

If
\mathcal{P}
has at most
k

H
a
k

of
G
.

Note that the structure of subflip differs from that of a flip. We fix a partition

but do not supply a flip relation
F
.
Instead, we always use the relation
F
that removes the most edges.
The following crucial observation gives the subflips their name.

Observation 4.4
.

If
H
is a subflip of

H
is a subgraph of
G
.

We next establish a basic toolbox for working with subflips.
This will be guided by the symmetry, transitivity, and hereditariness properties that hold for flips (

).

4.3
Symmetry

Clearly, the subflip relation is not symmetric: the independent set

is a subflip of the clique

, but the reverse fails because
K_{n}
is no subgraph of
nK_{1}
.
To reverse the subflip operation, we introduce

as a middle ground between subflips and regular flips.

Definition 4.5

.

We call a flip
G\oplus(\mathcal{P},F)
of

pure

Observation 4.6

If

-subflip of

Crucially, in reflexive graphs we can transduce pure flips using quantifier-free, positive formulas.

(

4.4
Transitivity

The transitivity property of general flips does not hold for subflips. The finer parts of
\mathcal{P}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{P}_{2}
can be fully-adjacent, while their containing coarser parts in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are not. In this case the subflip specified by
\mathcal{P}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{P}_{2}
removes even more edges.
We can still work with refinements, using the following property.

Lemma 4.12
.

For every graph

, partition
\mathcal{P}
of

, and refinement

of

Proof.

Let

and

be distinct vertices in

and let

and
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
be the parts containing

.
If

is a non-edge in

then this also true in the two subgraphs
(G\ominus\mathcal{P})\ominus\mathcal{R}
and

and there is nothing to check.
Assume therefore
v_{1}v_{2}
an edge in
G
.

Assume
v_{1}v_{2}
is removed in
G\ominus\mathcal{R}
.
Then the parts
R_{1}
and

are fully adjacent in
G
.
If
P_{1}
and

are fully adjacent in

, then

is removed already in

.
Otherwise, the adjacency between

and

is the same in

as in

.
In particular the subparts

and

is removed in

Assume

4.5
Hereditariness

Taking a subflip does not commute with taking an induced subgraph, as shown by the following example.

Example 4.13
.

For the graph
G:=G_{1}\uplus G_{2}=2K_{2}
, where

and

are both the single edge graph

, the partition

We will work around this using the following lemma.

Lemma 4.14
.

For every graph

, partition
\mathcal{P}
of

, and

Proof.

We can restrict our attention to the set

.

stay non-edges in the two subflips.

are removed in

if and only if they are removed in

Fix any edge

in

.

Assume first that

is a non-edge

.
Then
\mathcal{P}|_{S}(u)
and

are fully adjacent in

.
If the coarser parts

and

are also fully adjacent in

, then

was already removed in

.
Otherwise, the adjacencies between

and
\mathcal{P}|_{S}(v)
are the same in

and
(G\ominus\mathcal{P})[S]

is removed in

Assume now that

is a non-edge in

.
Then either

1.

\mathcal{P}(u)
and

are fully adjacent in

, or

∎

4.6

A

The forward direction is a simple application of

Subflipper-rank

In this section we will characterize the semi-ladder-free fragment of monadic dependence as those classes that have bounded

6.1
Definition and basic properties

Given a graph

we write

as a shorthand for the graph

-neighborhood of

Definition 6.1

((Sub)flipper-rank)
.

Fix
r\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
. For the single vertex graph
K_{1}

we define the
flipper-rank

\mathrm{frk}_{r,k}(K_{1}):=0.

For every other graph
G
we define

\mathrm{frk}_{r,k}(G):=1+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$H$ is a}\\
\text{$k$-flip of $G$}\end{subarray}}\max_{v\in V(H)}\mathrm{frk}_{r,k}(\mathrm{Ball}_{r}^{H}(v)).

A graph class
\mathscr{C}
has
bounded
r
-flipper-rank
if there are
k,\ell\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
such that

for all

.
The definition of the
subflipper-rank

is derived by demanding that

is also a
k
-subflip of
G
.
We define
bounded
r
-subflipper-rank
as expected.

Intuitively, we can understand the flipper-rank (and also the subflipper-rank) as a game called the
flipper-game

[
18
]
.
Played on a graph
G
between two players

and
localizer
, in every round of the game, flipper applies a
k
-flip to the current graph (with the goal of decomposing it as fast as possible), and localizer shrinks the arena to an
r
-neighborhood (with the goal of surviving as long as possible).
The flipper-rank then denotes the number of rounds needed for flipper to finish the game.
The following theorem is a key ingredient for the first-order model checking algorithm for monadically stable classes.

Theorem 6.2

(
[
18
]
)
.

For every graph class
\mathscr{C}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathscr{C}
is monadically stable.

2.

\mathscr{C}
has bounded
r
-flipper-rank for every
r\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
.

The goal of this section is to prove the following analog for co-matching-free classes.

Theorem 6.3
.

For every graph class
\mathscr{C}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathscr{C}
is monadically stable and co-matching-free.

2.

\mathscr{C}

r
-subflipper-rank for every

, arbitrary large star

-crossings or clique

-crossings can be obtained by
k
-subflips from induced subgraphs in
\mathscr{C}
.
By

, we know already that we can obtain these patterns from
\mathscr{C}
using
k
-flips.
We will use co-matching-freeness of
\mathscr{C}
to argue that subflips are sufficient to extract them.

Assume first that
\mathscr{C}
contains for every
t
, an induced graph
G_{t}
with

C_{t}=G_{t}\oplus(\mathcal{P},F)
for some size
k
partition
\mathcal{P}
, where
C_{t}
is the clique
r
-crossings of size
t

By the bipartite structure of the patterns and Ramsey’s Theorem, we can assume that
\mathcal{P}
is the partition of

into the

L_{0},\ldots,L_{r+1}
as in the definition of a clique
r
-crossing (see
[
13
]

[
25
]
for more details on how the Ramseying of these patterns is done).
Moreover, by the pigeonhole principle we can assume that for every
t\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
the layers are flipped the same way: we use the same

for every
t\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
.
We want to prove
C_{t}=G_{t}\ominus\mathcal{P}
.
This means we have to argue that the pairs in
F
are exactly the pairs of layers that are fully adjacent in
G_{t}
.
First note that no two pairs of layers are fully adjacent in
C_{t}
. Hence, if there is a pair of fully adjacent layers in
G_{t}
, it has to be included in
F
.
It remains to prove that every pair of layers
(L_{i},L_{j})\in F
is fully adjacent in
G_{t}
.

First consider the case where
L_{i}=L_{j}
, i.e., a layer was already flipped with itself.
If
i\in\{1,r\}
, then
L_{i}
is one of the “clique layers”:
C_{t}[L_{i}]
is the disjoint union of
t
many cliques and in particular contains an induced matching of size
t
.
Then the complement
G_{t}[L_{i}]
contains a semi-induced co-matching of size
t
. Recall that
t
can be arbitrarily large, so we get a contradiction to
\mathscr{C}
being co-matching-free.
If

, then
G_{t}[L_{i}]
is an independent set so
G_{t}[L_{i}]
is a clique, and
L_{i}
is fully adjacent with itself in

as desired.

Assume now
L_{i}\neq L_{j}
. If
i
and

are not successive, then the graph semi-induced between
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
in
C_{t}
is empty, so both sets must be fully adjacent in

, and we are done.
For successive layers

and

the graph semi-induced in

contains an induced matching of size at least

(size

if an outermost layer is included:

; and size

otherwise).
As argued before,
G_{t}
must contain a semi-induced co-matching of size
t
, and we get a contradiction.

This means
C_{t}=G_{t}\ominus\mathcal{P}
, and we can extract arbitrarily large clique
r
-crossings from induced subgraphs of
\mathscr{C}
by
(r+2)
-subflips.

The case where
\mathscr{C}
contains flips of arbitrarily large star
r
-crossings

works the same way, but needs to consider one less case, as every layer

is an independent set in

.
∎

.

For every semi-ladder-free class
\mathscr{C}
of partially reflexive graphs, the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is monadically dependent.

2.

\mathscr{C}
does not EP-transduce the class of all reflexive graphs.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{C}
is monadically dependent, then it does not transduce the class of all graphs, so in particular it does not EP-transduce the class of all reflexive graphs.
Assume now
\mathscr{C}
is not monadically dependent.
By

, there are

such that
\mathscr{C}
contains all star
r
-crossings or all clique
r
-crossings as

-subflips.

Assume first it

contains all star
r
-crossings as subflips.
By first taking induced subgraphs and adding all self-loops and then applying

, we can EP-transduce the class of all reflexive star

-crossings from

.
For every reflexive graph

r

From the subdivision, we can transduce

Assume now

8
A normal form for existential positive FO

In this section we establish a normal form for EP-formulas that we will use for our further arguments.
We remark that this normal form can also be established for general relational
\Sigma
-structures by considering distances in the Gaifman graph of
G
, we refrain from doing so for consistency of presentation.

Definition 8.1

(Clique formulas)
.

A formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
is an
r
-clique formula
if for every graph
G
and every tuple
\bar{v}\in V(G)^{|\bar{x}|}

G\models\varphi(\bar{v})\quad\implies\quad G\models\bigwedge_{\mathclap{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant|\bar{x}|}}\textnormal{dist}(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant r.

This means that the (instantiations of the) free variables of
\varphi
form a
distance-
r
clique
in
G
.

We prove the following normal form.

Theorem 8.2
.

Every EP-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
with quantifier rank
q
is equivalent to an EP-formula that is a boolean combination of
2^{q}
-clique formulas
\chi_{i}(\bar{z}_{i})

of quantifier rank at most
q
, where the
\bar{z}_{i}
and are subtuples of
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
The statement is obviously true for atomic formulas
E(x,y)
,
P(x)
for unary predicates
P
, and for
x=y
.
It trivially extends to positive boolean combinations.

The interesting case is the existential quantifier.
Assume
\varphi(\bar{x})=\exists y\,\psi(\bar{x},y)
is a formula of quantifier rank
q\geqslant 1
.
Let
r:=2^{q-1}
and
t:=q+|\bar{x}|
.
The goal is to show that
\varphi
is equivalent to a positive boolean combination of
2r
-clique formulas.

By applying induction, we can assume that
\psi(\bar{x},y)
is a positive boolean combination of
r
-clique formulas.
We bring
\psi
into disjunctive normal form.
Note that for this we only have to distribute conjunctions over disjunctions by the equivalence

\zeta_{1}\wedge(\zeta_{2}\vee\zeta_{3})\;\equiv\;(\zeta_{1}\wedge\zeta_{2})\;\vee\;(\zeta_{1}\wedge\zeta_{3}),

hence positivity is preserved by this operation.
By the equivalence

\exists y\,(\zeta_{1}(y)\vee\zeta_{2}(y))\;\;\equiv\;\;(\exists y\,\zeta_{1}(y))\;\vee\;(\exists y\,\zeta_{2}(y)),

we push the existential quantifier into the disjunction.
We hence have to deal with the case

\displaystyle\varphi(\bar{x})=\exists y\big(

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(\bar{z}_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge\chi_{m}(\bar{z}_{m})\big),

where the
\chi_{i}
are
r
-clique formulas, and the
\bar{z}_{i}
and
\bar{z}^{\prime}_{i}
are subtuples of
\bar{x}y
.

Observe that if the tuple
\bar{z}_{i}
does not contain the quantified variable
y
, then we can move the conjunct
\chi_{i}(\bar{z}_{i})
out of the scope of the quantifier.
We can therefore assume that every tuple
\bar{z}_{i}
contains the quantified variable
y
.
Up to restricting the free variables of
\varphi
, we can also assume that each variable from
\bar{x}
appears in some tuple
\bar{z}_{i}
.

We claim that
\varphi
is an
r
-clique formula.
Assume that
\varphi(\bar{x})
holds.
This means there exists an element
y
such that all the
r
-clique formulas
\chi_{i}(\bar{z}_{i})
are satisfied.
Consider now any two variables
x
and
x^{\prime}
from
\bar{x}
.
Let
\bar{z}_{i}
be a tuple containing
x
and let
\bar{z}_{j}
be a tuple containing
x^{\prime}
.
The truth of the
r
-clique formula
\chi_{i}(\bar{z}_{i})
witnesses that
\textnormal{dist}(x,y)\leqslant r
(because
\{x,y\}\subseteq\bar{z}_{i}
).
Similarly, we deduce
\textnormal{dist}(x^{\prime},y)\leqslant r
, and by the triangle inequality
\textnormal{dist}(x,x^{\prime})\leqslant 2r
, as desired.
∎

To prove this lemma we require the following lemma, which can be seen as a sunflower lemma for tuples.
For a

-tuple

we write
\bar{a}[i]:=a_{i}
for its projection to the
i

\bar{a}|_{I}:=a_{i_{1}}\ldots a_{i_{p}}

Lemma 9.5
.

For all
t,m\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}}
there is

such that every set of
n
many
t
-tuples
S
, there is a subset

of

many
t
tuples and a set

such that for all distinct
\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}\in A
and

\bar{a}|_{I}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}|_{I}
are equal, and the subtuples
\bar{a}|_{J}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}|_{J}
are disjoint.

Proof.

Fix

and let

be large enough for the classical sunflower lemma for set systems of size at most
t
and sunflower size
m

(recall that a family of sets forms a sunflower with core

if all pairwise intersections equal
C
, and the sunflower lemma guarantees the existence of big sunflowers in large set systems).
Let
S
be a set of
n
many
t
-tuples.
For a tuple
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{t})
define its support
\mathrm{supp}(\bar{a}):=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\}

Applying the sunflower lemma to the family

, we obtain a subset

whose supports form a sunflower with core

.
That is, for all

\mathrm{supp}(\bar{a}_{i})\cap\mathrm{supp}(\bar{a}_{j})=C
,
and the sets

For each
\bar{a}\in A
, let

Since there are only finitely many subsets of

.
Since there is only a constant number of ways to assign indices from

On the other hand, for

.

It is folklore that Flip-depth and SC-depth are functionally equivalent.

\mathsf{M}
such that

\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{D})
contains the monotone closure of
\mathscr{D}
, that is, for every
H\in\mathscr{D}
,

\mathsf{M}(H)\supseteq\{H^{\prime}:H^{\prime}\subseteq H\text{ is a (reflexive) subgraph}\}.

In particular, for our
G^{*}\in\mathscr{D}
and every
i\in[s]
we have
G_{i}^{*}\in\mathsf{M}(G^{*})
.

Let
\mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}
be the transduction from
Theorem

10.6
(see above).
For each
i\in[s]
, consider the composition

\mathsf{R}_{i}\ :=\ \mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}\circ\mathsf{M}.

Then
\mathsf{R}_{i}
is an EP-transduction (as a composition of EP-transductions) and

G_{i}\in\mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}(G_{i}^{*})\subseteq\mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}(\mathsf{M}(G^{*}))=\mathsf{R}_{i}(G^{*}).

Thus, from
G^{*}
we can obtain each induced piece
G_{i}=G[U_{i}]
by first using
\mathsf{M}
to guess the correct subgraph
G_{i}^{*}
and then applying
\mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}
.
It remains to combine the
s
recovered graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{s}
into
G
.
Note that
V(G)=U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{s}
and
E(G)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}E(G_{i})
, as
U_{i}
is a
2
-cover. We therefore define
\mathsf{Recover}
as the EP-transduction that, on input
G^{*}
outputs the union of all
\mathsf{R}_{i}
.

Formally, one can implement this again by a simple product-coloring on the color sets needed for
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{Recover}_{k,d}
, indexed by
i\in[s]
, and interpret the final edge predicate as a disjunction over
i\in[s]
of the edge predicate of the
i
-th recovered copy.
∎

Theorem

1.10
now follows by combining
Lemma

11.10
,
Theorem

11.1
, and
Theorem

1.5
.
We will show how this works for the case of bounded clique-width, but the other cases work analogously.

Corollary 11.11
.

For every semi-ladder-free class
\mathscr{C}
of reflexive graphs of bounded clique-width, there are EP-transductions
\mathsf{Sparsify}
and
\mathsf{Recover}
such that every graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
contains a reflexive subgraph
G^{*}
such that

G^{*}\in\mathsf{Sparsify}(G)
and
G\in\mathsf{Recover}(G^{*})
,

and the class
\mathscr{D}:=\{G^{*}:G\in\mathscr{C}\}
has bounded tree-width.

Proof.

As
\mathscr{C}
is semi-ladder-free and of bounded clique-width, by
Theorem

11.1
it has structurally bounded expansion. By definition of semi-ladder-freeness,
\mathscr{C}
is also co-matching-free.
This means we can apply
Lemma

11.10
to obtain the transductions
\mathsf{Sparsify}
and
\mathsf{Recover}
, the subgraphs
G^{*}
, and the class
\mathscr{D}:=\{G^{*}:G\in\mathscr{C}\}
.
The class
\mathscr{D}
has bounded clique-width (by transduction-closure of clique-width,
Theorem

11.1
).
As
\mathscr{D}
has bounded expansion, it is biclique-free.
Now by
Theorem

1.5
,
\mathscr{D}
has bounded tree-width.
∎

12
Positive MSO

We now prove the collapse of positive MSO to positive FO.
We use the following standard monotonicity property of positive formulas, see e.g.
[
24
, Lem. 10.7]
.

Lemma 12.1
.

Let
\psi(\bar{x},Y,\bar{X})
be a formula that is
positive in
Y
, i.e., every occurrence of
Y
appears under an even number of negations.
Then for every graph
G
, every assignment
\bar{a},\bar{B}
of
\bar{x},\bar{X}
, and all sets
Y\subseteq Y^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
,

G\models\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})\ \Longrightarrow\ G\models\psi(\bar{a},Y^{\prime},\bar{B}).

Theorem 12.2
.

Every (existential) positive MSO formula
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{X})
is equivalent to an
(existential) positive FO formula.

Proof.

We eliminate the set quantifiers one by one.
First assume
\varphi=\exists Y\,\psi(\bar{x},Y,\bar{X})
where
\psi
is positive in
Y
(this holds since the whole formula is positive).
Define
\psi^{\top}(\bar{x},\bar{X})
as the formula obtained from
\psi(\bar{x},Y,\bar{X})
by replacing every atom
Y(t)
by
\top
(equivalently, interpreting
Y
as the full vertex set).
We claim that for every
G
, every tuple
\bar{a}
and tuple
\bar{B}
we have

G\models\exists Y\,\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\models\psi^{\top}(\bar{a},\bar{B}).

To see this, observe that if
G\models\exists Y\,\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})
, then by
Lemma

12.1
also
G\models\psi^{\top}(\bar{a},\bar{B})
.
Conversely, if
G\models\psi^{\top}(\bar{a},\bar{B})
, then choosing
Y=V(G)
witnesses the
existential quantifier, hence
G\models\exists Y\,\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})
.

Now assume
\varphi=\forall Y\,\psi(\bar{x},Y,\bar{X})
with
\psi
positive in
Y
.
Let
\psi^{\bot}(\bar{x},\bar{X})
be the formula obtained from
\psi(\bar{x},Y,\bar{X})
by replacing every atom
Y(t)
by
\bot
(equivalently, interpreting
Y
as the empty set). Then for every
G,\bar{a}
, and
\bar{B}
,

G\models\forall Y\,\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\models\psi^{\bot}(\bar{a},\bar{B}).

For the forward direction, take
Y=\emptyset
.
For the reverse direction, if
G\models\psi^{\bot}(\bar{a},\bar{B})
then by monotonicity (positivity in
Y
) we have
G\models\psi(\bar{a},Y,\bar{B})
for every
Y\supseteq\emptyset
, i.e., for every set
Y
, hence
G\models\forall Y\,\psi(\bar{a},Y)
.

After eliminating all set quantifiers we obtain a positive FO formula equivalent
to the original positive MSO formula.
The construction preserves the existential restriction (if present), hence the same argument yields the stated existential version.
∎
