Evaluating the role of coral trophic strategies in driving community shifts in response to heat stress via stable isotopes-based modeling
Authors: Yuanming Zheng, Qifang Wang, Caidingqian Deng, Baohua Xiao, Xinqing Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorals are increasingly threatened by global climate change, which may lead to shifts in their community on geographic scales. Reef-building corals are mixotrophic and may increase heterotrophy to bolster their resilience to environmental stress. However, many studies only focus on trophic strategies at the species or genus level, overlooking whether the change in these strategies may cause community shifts.ResultsPrior to potential summer thermal events, we collected coral samples and classified them into different groups based on the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall (SDI), life history strategy (competitive vs. non-competitive), and evolutionary trajectory (complex vs. robust). Physiological measurements showed that the corals with small polyps, a competitive life history strategy, and membership in the complex clade had higher symbiotic density and greater chlorophyll content, indicating enhanced photosynthetic capacity. By integrating stable isotope analysis (δ13C and δ15N), Bayesian mixing models, and a kernel utilization density niche approach, we found that during summer, corals with larger SDI, non-competitive life history strategies, and robust evolutionary trajectory relied more on heterotrophy, a pattern suggesting greater resilience to thermal stress. These results are consistent with field observations, where coral groups with greater reliance on heterotrophy maintained their relative abundance after a local bleaching event.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that trophic strategies, inferred from summer states, may play a role in coral resilience and provide new insights into how coral reefs may respond to climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02594-8.

Background

Global climate change and human activities have caused remarkable changes in marine environments from their pristine state [1, 2]. Animals are often more susceptible to these changes (e.g., temperature and nutrient levels) than algae and plants, possibly leading to degradation of the ecosystems built by animals (e.g., cnidarians, bivalves and sponges), termed as “marine animal forest” [3–5]. A key difference between animal-dominated and primary producer ecosystems lies in their energy-acquisition strategies. While primary producers rely on autotrophy to fulfil their metabolic needs, consumers depend primarily on ingesting organic material produced by others [6]. However, our ability to leverage this divergence in energy-acquisition strategies to predict the resilience of “marine animal forest” to environmental stress remains limited.

Reef-building corals are fundamental to coral reefs, which are recognized as biodiversity hotspots in tropical oceans [7]. Corals can obtain energy autotrophically and heterotrophically, where autotrophy is supported by their symbiotic relationship with unicellular dinoflagellate algae (i.e., Symbiodiniaceae) [8, 9]. This mixotrophic strategy not only enhances nutrient retention and cycling within holobionts in oligotrophic waters, but also serves as an essential mechanism for coping with environmental fluctuations [10, 11]. Recent studies showed that the resilience and resistance of corals to environmental stress (e.g., turbidity and temperature anomalies) increase with their reliance on heterotrophy, highlighting the pivotal role of trophic dynamics to environmental adaptation [12]. Nevertheless, corals exhibit remarkable diversity in morphology and functional traits [13], and their dominant nutritional modes can vary considerably across species [10, 14]. This variability in trophic strategy plays a crucial role in energy acquisition and stress response, thereby influencing the overall health and resilience of coral reef ecosystems.

To comprehend this complexity, ecological studies often classify coral species into distinct groups based on established biological and molecular criteria. For example, the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall (SDI) has been used as an initial metric for genus-level classification [15], while physiological traits (e.g., growth rate, reproductive mode, and fecundity) and molecular markers (e.g., 16S rDNA) can further differentiate life history strategies (competitive, weedy, generalist, and stress-tolerant) and evolutionary clades (Complexa and Robusta), respectively [16, 17]. This classification framework helps to categorize community dynamics and allows us to test if group-level traits, such as trophic strategy, are associated with observed shifts in community composition under stress [17]. For instance, in situ observations have demonstrated clear changes in coral composition worldwide [18, 19], including transitions from small-polyp to large-polyp forms, from competitive to non-competitive species, and from Complexa to Robusta clade. Unfortunately, while many studies have focused on the trophic dynamics of corals at the species or genus level [12, 20, 21], studies on their nutritional modes using the proposed classification scheme are scant. Exploring coral trophic strategies at this scale is essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying corals’ resilience to environmental stress, providing useful information for implementing conservation policies.

Traditional trophic-strategy estimating methods such as gut-content analysis, yield limited insights into corals. This limitation arises because photosynthates from algal symbionts (i.e., autotrophic sources) occur at the molecular level and heterotrophic food sources (e.g., particulate organic matter and zooplankton) are rapidly digested, hindering quantification of their nutritional contributions [22]. Thus, stable carbon (δ13C) and nitrogen (δ15N) isotope analyses have emerged as powerful tools for assessing the dietary preference of corals in complex environments. A common approach to quantify different trophic strategies is by calculating the isotopic difference between coral hosts and their symbiotic algae [23, 24], based on the distinct isotopic signatures between autotrophic and heterotrophic sources. The relationship between δ13C and δ15N can also indicate the isotopic niche of corals, which reflects their trophic niche and hence nutritional interactions [25, 26], while Bayesian-based isotope mixing models provide quantitative estimates with associated uncertainties [27]. Despite the increasing use of stable isotope methods for studying the trophic strategies of corals, different analytical approaches applied to the same sample sets can yield contrasting results [12, 20, 28]. This discrepancy underscores the need for further research to compare these methods and provide a comprehensive interpretation of stable isotope data, which can eventually improve our understanding of coral trophic strategies.

Here, we evaluated the importance of trophic strategies in shaping coral community composition across broader classification scales (i.e., SDI, life history strategy and evolutionary trajectory) among nine dominant coral genera in a marginal environment during summer. We addressed this question by using combined physiological assessments (e.g., symbiont density and chlorophyll content) with trophic strategy estimation based on stable isotope analyses, traditional methods (host-symbiont isotopic differences and Bayesian isotopic mixing models) and novel isotopic niche approaches (kernel utilization density) that require smaller sample sizes and are less sensitive to data distribution. We also collected in situ transect data before (in 2019) and after (in 2021) a recorded local bleaching event to further interpret the functional importance of coral trophic strategies. We hypothesized that, during summer, (1) the trophic strategy of corals would be governed by their classification (i.e., the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall, life history strategies and evolutionary clades), and (2) corals with greater heterotrophic reliance would demonstrate enhanced resilience to bleaching events, as evidenced by stable relative composition after stress.

Across the five study sites around the Dapeng Peninsula (Fig. 1A), the relative abundance of the targeted coral genera in the coral community changed following a local bleaching event between 2019 and 2021 (Fig. 1B). When classified by the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall (SDI), corals with lower SDI decreased in relative cover, and the opposite was observed in those with higher SDI. Discussion

Corallite size influences coral trophic strategies

The trophic strategy of corals is highly associated with their energy acquisition under different environmental conditions [9]. In this study, several methods were applied, including the innovative KUD isotopic niche analysis [25], and demonstrated a continuous range of coral trophic strategies from heterotrophy to autotrophy in marginal communities. This variation appears to be linked to the differences in coral morphology and physiology. Notably, we found that the greater shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall is associated with the shift toward heterotrophy (Fig. 4), as indicated by the higher δ15NH-S values, higher EDC values, higher relative contribution of heterotrophic nutrition, and smaller isotopic niche overlap (Figs. 3F, 5, and 6). These findings are consistent with those in previous studies that used corallite area as a proxy [12], given the positive correlation between both metrics and polyp size, which is critical for capturing heterotrophic sources [22, 30]. However, because direct measurement of polyp size is challenging due to polyps’ dynamic behavior in water, we propose that the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall can serve as a practical and effective proxy for coral trophic strategies, helping estimate corals’ resistance under stressful conditions.

Our results also suggest that the inner-edge distance of the corallite wall not only reflects polyp size, but also signifies corallite morphology that affects heterotrophic capacity. A longer inner-edge distance indicates a wider, cup-like structure that provides a larger volume for food retention. For example, through fluorescence bead experiments and mathematical modeling, it was found that the such topography of the corallite creates a localized, low-pressure zone above the polyp. As a result, the retention of particulate and suspended organic matter carried by horizontal currents is enhanced, particularly in large cup-shaped corals, like Platygyra and Dipsastraea [31]. In addition, while small-polyp branching corals secrete mucus to facilitate feeding to some extent [9], large-polyp corals tend to form thicker mucus layers on their surface [32], further increasing food retention. Collectively, these mechanisms support the use of the inner-edge distance of the corallite wall as a robust and practical proxy for estimating coral heterotrophic capacity.

The results of δ13CH-S values (lowest in S group) imply that the small corals (e.g., Goniopora) incorporate more heterotrophic carbon. However, this variation may result from enhanced carbon discrimination during photosynthesis, indicated by the lower δ13CS values [23]. We hypothesize that the daytime expansion of small polyps facilitates greater carbon exchange, thereby reducing carbon limitation in their symbiotic algae. Similar observations were documented in the Gulf of Eilat, where large-polyp corals, which extend their tentacles at night, have higher δ13CS values than small-polyp, day-expanding corals [33]. Interestingly, the extra small corals (e.g., Acropora and Montipora) were enriched in δ13CS relative to the small corals, likely due to the higher surface-area-to-volume ratio of colonies (branching or plating) and higher cellular chlorophyll content of the extra small corals, which result in higher photosynthetic efficiency [20]. Moreover, the δ13CS values of the small corals (−18.33 ± 1.88 ‰) were close to those of the heterotrophic carbon sources, such as SOM (−20.01 ± 0.07 ‰), suggesting a possible underestimation of symbiont contributions when using δ13CH-S as the sole indicator of trophic strategies. In Shenzhen’s marginal communities [29], where SOM is abundant and small corallite corals are mostly encrusting or sub-massive (i.e., Cyphastrea and Goniopora), these factors likely explain the observed variability in δ13CH-S as an indicator of coral trophic strategies.

Relationship between coral classification and trophic strategies

Life history strategy, defined by a suite of physiological traits [17], plays a pivotal role in determining the energy balance of corals and shaping their trophic strategies. Our findings indicate that competitive corals tend to rely more on autotrophy (Figs. 3I, 5E, and 6E), whereas other types of corals (i.e., stress-tolerant, weedy, or generalist) are more heterotrophic. This divergence likely stems from the differences in fecundity, growth rates, and reproductive modes. Specifically, competitive corals, which can form large colonies, grow rapidly and reproduce via spawning, mainly rely on autotrophy [14, 17]. Their algal symbionts rapidly translocate carbon-rich photosynthates to support high calcification rates and meet substantial metabolic demands [34, 35], as evidenced by the elevated chlorophyll content and enriched δ13CS values. In contrast, non-competitive corals that often employ a brooding reproductive strategy (i.e., weedy) or have higher fecundity (i.e., stress-tolerant) require higher energy input [17, 36]. These corals can compensate for nutrient deficiency in autotrophic resources [9], particularly in nitrogen and phosphorus, by increasing their reliance on heterotrophy (e.g., higher δ15NH-S and EDC values, no overlap between host and symbiotic KDE isotopic niche). Moreover, our sampling in August likely reflects tissue turnover from the pre-reproductive period (May to June) in Shenzhen coral communities [34], further linking life history strategy to trophic mode. In short, our data suggest that competitive corals rely chiefly on autotrophy, while others exhibit a higher dependency on heterotrophy, driven by their divergent physiological traits.

Although physiological traits are associated with the life history strategy of corals, few biological criteria can effectively differentiate their evolutionary trajectories. The prevailing consensus is that complex corals are generally less calcified than robust corals [37]. Here, we observed that complex corals have a higher photosynthetic capacity (Figs. 3L, 5H, 6H), as indicated by the greater areal and cellular chlorophyll levels, as well as the greater reliance on autotrophy (i.e., lower δ15NH-S values, broader 50% KDE isotopic niche overlaps, and higher autotrophic contributions). Because autotrophy supplies the energy and carbon required for calcification [38], enhanced photosynthetic capacity may in part explain differences in the degree of calcification between evolutionary trajectories. Indeed, faster skeletal extension shortens the time polyps spend at each newly formed dissepiment, which likely limits the opportunity for skeletal densification before subsequent dissepiment formation occurs [39]. For example, annual skeletal density bands can be found in corals, where the faster growth in summer can result in lower skeletal density compared to that in winter [40]. Seasonal increases in autotrophic reliance may therefore contribute to this pattern [20, 41], whereas greater heterotrophic input has been associated with increased skeletal density and reduced porosity [40]. Furthermore, greater reliance on autotrophy in complex corals may partly reflect their metabolic dependence on symbionts, due to the absence of certain biosynthetic pathways [42]. Genomic studies have shown that, unlike algal symbionts and robust corals, complex corals lack the ability to synthesize cysteine and histidine de novo [43, 44], suggesting that evolutionary gene loss may influence the dominance of nutritional modes. Further research is needed to determine if these patterns are consistent across broader spatial scales and multiple species.

Implications for using trophic strategies to infer shifts in coral communities

Heterotrophic capacity is closely linked to the resistance and resilience of corals against environmental stress, suggesting that the trophic strategy of corals adopted before summer may influence their response to the subsequent thermal events [12, 45]. Our results showed that between 2019 and 2021, the relative cover of corals among the nine genera studied decreased by ~ 2% in the XS group and ~ 7% in the S group, with competitive corals reduced by ~ 2% and complex corals reduced by ~ 5%. Although bleaching rates in 2020 were not directly measured, these findings are consistent with previous reports documenting shifts toward species with larger polyps, in the robust clade, and a non-competitive life history strategy on both global and regional scales in the context of climate change [46, 47]. Importantly, all these coral classifications (i.e., M, L, non-competitive, and robust corals) were more dependent on heterotrophy before summer, which may enhance the uptake of micronutrients (e.g., manganese, magnesium, and iron) that serve as critical elements for antioxidant protection [48]. Our in situ findings suggest that the variation in trophic strategies among different coral groups could reflect their energy budget and ability to resist thermal stress.

Conclusions

Our findings indicated that the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall (SDI) is an effective proxy for inferring the trophic strategy of corals. A novel KDE-based method was employed to estimate trophic reliance, which reduces sample requirements while yielding results consistent with other approaches. Our data obtained from nine coral genera revealed a continuous spectrum of trophic reliance in marginal communities, from autotrophy to heterotrophy. We also found that these trophic differences correlated with the life history strategies and evolutionary trajectories of corals, which explain the coral composition shifts at temporal scales to some extent. Our in situ findings suggest that variation in trophic strategies among coral groups may reflect differences in energy budget and resistance to thermal stress.

Methods

Study area and sample collection

Dapeng Peninsula (22°27′−22°36′N, 114°26′−114°37′E; Fig. 1A) is located in southeastern Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, and is an important region of coral communities along China’s southern coast [49, 50]. Sea surface temperatures range from 18.7 to 32.1 °C, photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) varies from 21.1 to 56.3 mol m−2 d−1, the diffuse attenuation coefficient at 490 nm (Kd490) is between 0.1 and 0.4 m−1, and primary production ranges from 1.0 to 4.6 mg m−3 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). In August 2023, coral fragments (approximately 5 cm in diameter or length) were collected from the study sites (Fig. 1A). This sampling captured the summer trophic strategy state of the corals. The samples, collected prior to any major thermal stress in 2023, were used to investigate the physiological traits and trophic strategies of corals across different classification groups, and thus provide the coral tissues' turnover time required for the change in isotopic signatures [34]. Coral specimens were identified to the species level, with at least five replicates per genus and colonies spaced at least 15 m apart, resulting in a total of 92 samples across nine genera. Potential heterotrophic sources for corals were also sampled [51, 52], including sedimentary organic matter (SOM) collected at the same depth and zooplankton collected via horizontal towing for 5 min using a 163-μm mesh. Additionally, 3 L of surface seawater was filtered through 0.7 μm Whatman GF/F membranes to obtain particulate organic matter (POM) [53]. All samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and stored in a freezer at − 80 °C prior to analyses.

Relative change in coral composition after exposure to thermal stress

Normally, corals along the southern China coast rarely experience extreme heat events due to the relatively high latitude of the region. However, a severe bleaching event was recorded locally in 2020. Neither coral composition data nor bleaching assessments were available from that year. To assess the coral composition prior to the bleaching event, we deployed five 50 m horizontal belt transects at various coral community sites in 2019 (Fig. 1A). We focused on studying coral species from nine dominant genera and calculated the relative coverage of each genus by dividing the number of colonies of that genus by the total number of colonies observed across all nine genera. Additionally, to estimate post-bleaching composition at the same five sites, we extracted 2021 coral cover data from a previous study [29] using WebPlotDigitizer (Version 4.2) and calculated relative cover using the same method. Thus, the 2023 summer samples were used to characterize the trophic strategies of different coral groups under pre-stress conditions, while the 2019 and 2021 community-cover data provided a temporal framework to assess how these groups responded to the documented thermal stress event in 2020 [54].

Sample pre-treatment and laboratory analysis

Coral hosts and symbionts were separated using the method described by [20]. Briefly, coral tissue was removed from the skeleton using an airbrush with DI water, while the skeleton was preserved for 3D scanning. A 10 mL aliquot was reserved and used for physiological analyses. The tissue aliquots were then homogenized with a hand-held homogenizer and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 10 min to obtain the supernatant (host tissue) and pellet (symbionts). The supernatant was centrifuged 4 times at 2,400 g for 10 min to remove residual symbionts, while the pellet was resuspended in DI water and further purified via five rounds of centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min. The centrifugation mentioned above was conducted at 4 °C. Potential heterotrophic sources were processed, following the method described by [20]. All samples were stored in a freezer at − 20 °C and then freeze-dried using a CTFD-18S vacuum freeze dryer (Yonghe Chuangxin, China) for the subsequent isotopic analysis.

Symbiont density was quantified (n = 12) using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany) coupled with a hemocytometer, with cell counts standardized as per surface area unit for density determination‌. Chlorophyll content measurement followed an optimized protocol adapted from [55]‌. A 2 mL aliquot of tissue homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min (4 °C) to isolate symbiont cellular pellets. Following supernatant removal, chlorophyll extraction was achieved through pellet resuspension in 2 mL of absolute acetone, with subsequent dark-phase storage at − 20 °C for 24 h. Chlorophyll (a + c2) concentration was calculated by measuring the absorbance of samples at 630 nm and 663 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, with turbidity correction for the absorbance at 750 nm.

Stable carbon (δ13C) and nitrogen (δ15N) isotope values of each sample were determined using a system combining an Elemental Analyzer (Vario Isotope Cube, Elementar, Germany) and an Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer (IRMS, Isoprime 100, Germany) (EA-IRMS). The δ13C and δ15N values of the samples were calculated using the international standards Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (V-PDB) for carbon (13C/12C VPDB) and atmospheric nitrogen (15N/14N air) for nitrogen isotope abundances (Rstandard) as the reference. Classification of corals

Each coral specimen was categorized into three groups based on (1) the shortest distance from the inner edge of the corallite wall (SDI) [56], (2) life history strategy [17], and (3) evolutionary trajectory [16] to investigate their trophic strategies. Specifically, SDI was measured from 3D-scanned models of individual coral skeletons (scanner software: SL3DSCAN-1.4.9.3; measurement software: Geomagic Warp-2017). For each specimen, we made six replicate measurements from approximately 3 cm2 skeletal fragments and used the mean SDI for subsequent analyses. Based on these means, we divided specimens into four SDI classes: Extra Small (XS: Montipora, Acropora, and Porites; SDI < 2 mm), (2) Small (S: Goniopora and Cyphastrea; 2 mm < SDI < 5 mm), (3) Medium (M: Galaxea and Turbinaria; 5 mm < SDI < 8 mm), and (4) Large (L: Platygyra and Dipsastraea; SDI > 8 mm). In addition, life history strategy groups were assigned using established classifications and physiological trait data (e.g., fecundity, reproductive mode and growth rate). Here, we focused on two groups: Competitive (primarily Acropora and Montipora) or Non-competitive (primarily Platygyra, Dipsastraea, Goniopora, Porites, Turbinaria, Cyphastrea and Galaxea) classification, which reflects recent global shifts in coral assemblages from competitive taxa toward stress-tolerant, weedy and generalist species [17]. Lastly, evolutionary trajectory groups were assigned following previous classification based on differences in skeletal development over evolutionary time. In our study, corals were divided into two clades: Robust (including Cyphastrea, Platygyra, and Dipsastraea) and Complex (including Acropora, Montipora, Turbinaria, Goniopora, Porites, and Galaxea). This grouping framework allows us to systematically examine how SDI, life history strategy, and evolutionary trajectory shape the trophic strategies of corals.

Estimation of coral trophic strategies

The trophic strategies of corals were evaluated using the following protocol. First, we calculated the isotopic differences between coral hosts and their symbionts using δ13C and δ15N values (δ13CH-S and δ15NH-S). Since heterotrophic sources typically have δ13C values that are 4–8‰ lower and δ15N values that are 1–5‰ higher than those of the symbionts, more negative δ13CH-S values and higher δ15NH-S values indicate greater reliance on heterotrophy, and vice versa [23, 24]. Second, we quantified the isotopic niche sizes and overlaps of coral hosts and symbionts using 50% and 95% kernel utilization density (KUD) estimators in R (rKin package) [25]. This approach can produce large niche sizes while minimizing outlier effects, providing realistic overlap estimates that reflect the degree of autotrophic interaction. Euclidean distance between the centroids (EDC) of the host and symbiont KUD niches was computed, where higher EDC values indicate greater heterotrophic reliance [12]. Third, we used Bayesian mixing models (MixSIAR v3.1.12) to estimate the relative contributions of heterotrophy (from particulate organic matter, sedimentary organic matter, and zooplankton) and autotrophy (from symbionts) in coral hosts [27]. Trophic enrichment factors (TEFs) for invertebrates (δ13C: 0.5 ± 1.3‰; δ15N: 2.3 ± 1.5‰) and symbiont-specific TEFs (δ13C: 0.5 ± 1.3‰; δ15N: 1.5 ± 1.3‰) estimated in a previous study were applied [20]. Group-specific symbionts were incorporated using non-informative priors (three chains, chain length 300,000, burn-in 200,000, thin 100). Model convergence was verified with Geweke and Gelman-Rubin statistics.

Statistical analysis

Physiological parameters (areal symbiont density, areal chlorophyll content and cellular chlorophyll), isotopic values (δ13C and δ15N), and trophic indicators (δ13CH-S and δ15NH-S) across the SDI, life history strategy, and evolutionary trajectory groups, were analyzed using One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with different classification as the fixed factor. Prior to ANOVA, the homogeneity of variances and residual normality were validated by Levene’s test and Shapiro–Wilk test, respectively. Linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between trophic strategy indicators (δ13CH-S and δ15NH-S) and SDI. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.3), and the results were reported as mean ± standard deviation.
