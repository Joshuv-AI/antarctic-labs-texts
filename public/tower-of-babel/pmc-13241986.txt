Subcutaneous immunotherapy-induced IgG1 suppresses allergic airway inflammation through FcγRIIb-mediated inhibition of group 2 innate lymphoid cell proliferation☆
Authors: Masaya Matsuda, Asuka Hirano, Momo Morishita, Ryo Fukuda, Hayato Shimora, Yuya Sannomiya, Daichi Utsumi, Katsuyo Ohashi-Doi, Takeshi Nabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT), a form of allergy immunotherapy, can alter the natural course of allergic diseases and induce tolerance to causative allergens by modulating type 2 immune responses. While the regulatory effects of SCIT on Th2 cells have been extensively studied, its effects on group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) remain poorly understood. In a murine model of asthma, we demonstrated that SCIT increased the production of allergen-specific IgG1, which is analogous to human IgG4. To clarify the role of IgG1 in the mechanisms underlying SCIT, we evaluated its effects on the development of allergic asthma and the proliferation of ILC2s.MethodsBALB/c mice were sensitized with ovalbumin (OVA) and Al(OH)3, followed by SCIT consisting of 3 subcutaneous OVA injections at a dose of 1 mg/animal. After SCIT, sera were collected, and total IgG1 was purified using the pH-gradient elution method. ILC2s were isolated from the lungs of OVA-challenged mice and stimulated with IL-33 in the presence of OVA and the purified IgG1 for 72 h, after which cell proliferation was assessed using an ATP assay. For in vivo experiments, the purified IgG1 was intratracheally administered to OVA-challenged asthmatic mice, and airway remodeling and lung leukocyte populations were subsequently analyzed. Animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Research Committee of Setsunan University (approval Nos. K21-1, K22-1, K23-1, K24-1, and K25-1) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.Results(1) IgG1+ B cells were markedly increased in the lungs of SCIT-treated asthmatic mice (p = 0.0409) and were the most abundant among the 4 IgG+ B-cell subsets. (2) Intratracheal administration of IgG1 derived from SCIT mice (SCIT-IgG1) significantly suppressed epithelial thickening and mucus accumulation, and reduced the numbers of lung ILC2s and eosinophils in the asthma model (p = 0.0034, 0.0341, 0.0246, and 0.0041, respectively). (3) FcγRIIb expression on lung ILC2s derived from asthmatic mice was significantly increased (p = 0.0004). (4) SCIT-IgG1 significantly suppressed OVA and IL-33-induced in vitro proliferation of ILC2s (p = 0.0018), and this inhibitory effect disappeared in the presence of an anti-FcγRIIb antibody.ConclusionsSCIT-IgG1 attenuates airway remodeling and limits ILC2 expansion in allergic airway inflammation. Mechanistically, SCIT-IgG1 restrains ILC2 proliferation via FcγRIIb engagement, revealing an antibody–ILC2 inhibitory axis that likely contributes to the efficacy of allergy immunotherapy and suggests therapeutic strategies that enhance inhibitory FcγR signaling to control type 2 inflammation.Keywords: Allergy immunotherapy, Asthma, Group 2 innate lymphoid cell, IgG1, Fcγ receptor IIb

Introduction

The global prevalence of allergic diseases has markedly increased, largely due to type 2 inflammation.1 This inflammation is driven by type 2 immune cells, including Th2 cells and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s).1 Upon antigen presentation, activated Th2 cells produce type 2 cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which promote antigen-specific IgE production, eosinophil infiltration, and mucus secretion. ILC2s, which lack antigen-recognition receptors, are activated by epithelial cell-derived cytokines including IL-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP).1 Importantly, ILC2s produce 10- to 100-fold higher amounts of IL-5 and IL-13 than Th2 cells,2,3 and have emerged as central drivers of airway remodeling in severe asthma.4,5 Thus, regulation of type 2 inflammation is critical for the effective treatment of allergic asthma.

Conventional treatment for allergic asthma has relied on anti-allergic drugs, such as antihistamines,6 leukotriene antagonists,7 corticosteroids,8 and monoclonal antibodies against type 2 cytokines,9,10 which mainly provide symptomatic relief. In contrast, allergy immunotherapy (AIT) is regarded as the sole intervention capable of altering the natural progression of allergic disease by inducing immune tolerance.11 AIT, administered as subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) or sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT), alters immune cell function, notably by reducing the activity of effector cells such as Th2 cells and ILC2s in both mice and humans.12 In addition, AIT markedly increases antigen-specific IgG4 levels in the sera of allergic patients.13 AIT-induced IgG4 contributes to immune suppression by inhibiting mast cell degranulation14,15 and B-cell activation,16 thereby alleviating allergic symptoms. However, the mechanisms by which AIT-induced IgG4 reduces type 2 immune cells remain incompletely understood.

Mice lack IgG4 but express 4 subclasses: IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG3.17 Among these, mouse IgG1 is considered functionally homologous to human IgG418 because: (1) it does not activate the classical complement pathway,19, 20, 21 (2) it has limited capacity for pathogen aggregation,19, 20, 21 and (3) it preferentially binds the inhibitory Fcγ receptor (FcγRIIb) over stimulatory FcγRs.17 Mouse FcγRs include 3 stimulatory receptors (FcγRI, FcγRIII, FcγRIV) and one inhibitory receptor (FcγRIIb), with IgG1 showing the highest affinity for FcγRIIb.17

We recently established a murine SCIT model, in which ovalbumin (OVA)-sensitized mice received 3 subcutaneous OVA injections (1 mg/animal), followed by 4 intratracheal OVA challenges (5 μg/animal).22 In this model, SCIT significantly suppressed airway remodeling, including epithelial thickening, mucus accumulation, type 2 cytokine production, and eosinophil infiltration.22 Moreover, serum levels of OVA-specific IgG1 were markedly increased in SCIT mice.22 These findings suggest that allergen-specific IgG1 may play an important role in suppressing allergic responses; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

In this study, we isolated total IgG1 from the serum of SCIT mice and referred to this fraction as SCIT-IgG1. Using this preparation, we aimed to clarify the functional roles of SCIT-IgG1 by investigating the following: (1) whether SCIT increases the number of IgG1+ B cells in the lung; (2) whether intratracheal administration of SCIT-IgG1, as an experimental approach to assess its local effects in the lung, attenuates airway remodeling and the expansion of type 2 immune cells; (3) whether lung ILC2s express FcγRs; and (4) whether SCIT-IgG1 suppresses the in vitro proliferation of lung ILC2s via FcγRIIb.

Methods

Sensitization, subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT), and challenges

Sensitization, SCIT, and challenges were conducted as previously described.22 Briefly, five-week-old BALB/c mice were sensitized with ovalbumin (OVA, Grade V; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) plus Al(OH)3 on days 0 and 14, followed by subcutaneous injections of OVA (1 mg/animal) on days 21, 23, and 25 as part of subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT). After SCIT, the mice were challenged with OVA (5 μg/animal) under isoflurane anesthesia (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) on days 35, 36, 37, and 40. Mice were allocated to experimental groups in a manner that balanced cage distribution, such that each cage contained one animal from each group, in order to minimize potential cage effects.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Research Committee of Setsunan University under approval numbers K21-1, K22-1, K23-1, K24-1, and K25-1.

Analyses of immune cells and epithelial cells in the lung

The lungs were collected 24 h after the fourth OVA challenge under anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of xylazine (12 mg/kg) and pentobarbital (70 mg/kg). The excised lungs were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently subjected to enzymatic digestion with collagenase type I (870 units/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C for 1 h. Following digestion, erythrocytes were eliminated using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer (155 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA/2Na). The total cell count was determined using trypan blue staining. To minimize non-specific antibody binding, lung cells were pretreated with an anti-CD16/32 (FcγRII/III) antibody. When the expression of FcγRs on ILC2s was analyzed, pre-treatment with FcγRII/III was not conducted. Cells were then washed in PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated with surface marker-specific antibodies at 4°C for 20 min. After additional washing in PBS with 2% FBS, cells were fixed and permeabilized at room temperature for 1 h, followed by staining with antibodies for intracellular molecules at room temperature for 30 min. After a final wash in PBS containing 2% FBS, flow cytometric analysis was performed using the FACSAria™ Fusion system (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Identification of specific immune cell populations was carried out as follows: B cells were defined as CD19+ cells, ILC2s as lineage− CD45+ CD90.2+ inducible T-cell costimulatory (ICOS)+ suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2)+ or lineage− CD45+ CD90.2+ killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily G member 1 (KLRG1)+ ST2+ cells, epithelial cells as CD45- epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM)+ cells, and eosinophils as siglec-F+ CD45+ cells; and Th2 cells as GATA binding protein 3 (GATA3)+ CD4+ CD3+ cells. The antibodies used for flow cytometry in the present study are shown in Table E1.

Purification of mouse IgG1

OVA-sensitized mice were treated with SCIT. Ten days after the final SCIT administration, the mice were anesthetized and then euthanized to collect whole blood samples. The collected blood was incubated on ice for 1 h and subsequently centrifuged. After centrifugation, the serum was carefully separated and stored in a deep freezer until mouse IgG1 purification. Mouse IgG1 was purified with minor modifications of a previously described method.23 The frozen serum was thawed and diluted threefold using a binding buffer containing 1.5 M glycine and 3 M NaCl at pH 8.9. This diluted sample was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The filtrate was then loaded onto a HiTrap™ Protein A HP Column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and eluted using a buffer containing 0.1 M sodium citrate at pH 6.0. The eluted fractions were neutralized with a buffer containing 1 M Tris-HCl at pH 9.0, followed by buffer exchange to PBS using a PD-10 column (Cytiva). Finally, the purified IgG1 fraction was concentrated using Amicon® Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter Devices (Merck Millipore).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and western blotting

The purity of IgG1 in the collected fractions was assessed using ELISA. Quantification of OVA-specific IgG1, total IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG3 was performed with specific ELISA kits (all from Chondrex, Redmond, WA, USA). The binding affinity of the purified IgG1 to OVA was evaluated by western blotting. OVA was combined with a sample buffer containing a reducing agent (6 × ) for SDS-PAGE (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), then heated at 98 °C for 10 min to denature proteins. The denatured samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE (4–20% gradient gels) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Blocking of the membranes was carried out using PBS supplemented with 5% non-fat dried milk and 0.1% Tween 20, followed by washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in PBS with 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% BSA. After washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 5% non-fat dried milk and 0.1% Tween 20. Protein visualization was achieved using the SuperSignal™ West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with ChemiDoc Imaging Systems (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software version 6.1.0 (Bio-Rad).

The excised lungs were homogenized in 1 mL of T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Complete Mini; Roche, Mannheim, Germany). After centrifugation, IL-33 levels in the supernatants were measured using an ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). According to the manufacturer, the intra-assay CV values were 9.6%, 5.8%, and 5.3%, and the inter-assay CV values were 10.2%, 5.8%, and 5.0%. The manufacturer also reported recovery data for mouse samples, with average recoveries of 104% (range, 91–116%) for cell culture supernatants, 90% (81–103%) for serum, 102% (96–113%) for EDTA plasma, and 99% (83–111%) for heparin plasma. However, lung homogenate supernatants were not specifically validated.

A comprehensive list of the ELISA kits and antibodies employed in this study is provided in Table E1.

Administration of mouse IgG1

SCIT-IgG1, derived from the serum of SCIT mice, was intratracheally administered 1 day before and during the OVA challenge period twice daily on days 34–40. When administered on the days of OVA challenges, intratracheal administration was conducted 1 h before and 7 h after the respective OVA challenges. Mouse myeloma-derived IgG1 (Sigma-Aldrich, clone MOPC 21) was used as control-IgG1. As vehicles for SCIT-IgG1 and control-IgG1, PBS was administered intratracheally at the corresponding times in the IgG1-treated groups.

Histological analysis

Histological analysis was conducted as previously reported.4,24,25 Briefly, 24 h following the fourth OVA challenge, the left lung lobes were isolated under anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (70 mg/kg) and xylazine (12 mg/kg). The tissues were subsequently fixed in 10% buffered formalin. After fixation, samples were embedded in paraffin, and sections with a thickness of 4 μm were prepared and stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) for light microscopic examination. Consistent with our prior studies,4,24,25 histological alterations were evaluated semi-quantitatively. Briefly, PAS-stained lung sections were analyzed for bronchi with a diameter greater than 150 μm. Epithelial thickening was quantified as the epithelial area divided by the length of the basement membrane. Mucus accumulation was assessed as the PAS-positive area within the epithelium divided by the length of the basement membrane. All morphometric analyses were conducted by a single investigator blinded to the experimental groups using Adobe Photoshop 2026 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Isolation of lung ILC2s

ILC2s were detected as lineage− CD45+ CD90.2+ KLRG1+ cells and isolated, following established protocols. Lung ILC2 isolation was performed based on methods from our prior research and previous studies.24,26 In summary, cells extracted from digested lung lobes were resuspended in 30% Percoll (Cytiva). A layer of 70% Percoll was then carefully added under the 30% Percoll layer. This gradient was centrifuged at 500 × g for 30 min at 25°C. Mononuclear cells located at the interface of the 30% and 70% Percoll layers were harvested. These cells were incubated with a biotin-conjugated anti-mouse antibody along with MojoSort™ streptavidin nanobeads (BioLegend). The cell suspensions were then subjected to magnetic separation using the MojoSort™ magnet (BioLegend) for 5 min. After separation, lineage− cells were collected and stained with specific antibodies at 4°C for 20 min. After washing with PBS containing 2% FBS, ILC2s were sorted using the FACSAria™ Fusion system. The biotinylated antibodies and fluorescent antibodies used for the isolation in the present study are shown in Table E1.

Proliferation of ILC2s

Twenty-four hours after the 4th challenge, ILC2s were purified by the method described above. Purified ILC2s were first incubated with an anti-FcγRIIb antibody (R&D Systems, clone 190907, 100 μg/mL) or rat IgG2a kappa isotype control (Thermo Fisher Scientific, clone eBR2a, 100 μg/mL) for 30 min, followed by incubation with pre-formed OVA–SCIT-IgG1 immune complexes or OVA–control IgG1 mixtures for an additional 30 min. To prepare the OVA–SCIT-IgG1 immune complexes, OVA (200 μg/mL) was pre-incubated with SCIT-IgG1 (2000 μg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C, and the resulting complexes were added to the culture at a 1:10 dilution, yielding final concentrations of 20 μg/mL OVA and 200 μg/mL SCIT-IgG1. For the control condition, OVA (200 μg/mL) was similarly pre-incubated with control IgG1 (2000 μg/mL) for 1 h at 37°C, and the resulting mixture was added at a 1:10 dilution to yield final concentrations of 20 μg/mL OVA and 200 μg/mL control IgG1. Subsequently, ILC2s were cultured in ILC2 medium (RPMI1640 containing 10% FBS, 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 10 mM HEPES) supplemented with 10 ng/mL IL-33 (BioLegend) at 37°C for 72 h. After incubation, the number of viable cells was determined by measuring ATP levels using the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To convert luminescence signals to absolute cell numbers, an ATP standard curve was generated using known numbers of ILC2s. Serially diluted cells were subjected to the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Assay under the same assay conditions, and the resulting luminescence values were plotted against cell number. Linear regression analysis showed a strong correlation between luminescence and cell number (R2 = 0.999683, R = 0.999842), and the regression equation was y = 4879.53x - 316.76, where y represents the luminescence value (RLU) and x represents cell number ( × 104 cells/mL). Based on this calibration curve, luminescence values obtained in the proliferation assay were converted to absolute cell numbers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between 2 groups, homogeneity of variances was assessed using the F test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Welch's t-test, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test. For comparisons among 3 or more groups, homogeneity of variances was assessed using the Brown–Forsythe test. Normally distributed data with equal variances were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple-comparison test, whereas normally distributed data with unequal variances were analyzed by Welch's ANOVA followed by Dunnett's T3 multiple-comparison test. If the normality assumption was not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. A probability value (p) < 0.05 was considered significant. Although no formal power analysis was conducted, our sample sizes were guided by previously published studies using comparable models, which demonstrated sufficient statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences. To ensure robustness, all in vivo experiments were conducted with a minimum of 6 animals per group (n ≥ 6), and in vitro experiments were independently replicated at least 3 times (n ≥ 3).

Results

Effect of SCIT on B cells in the lung

We previously demonstrated that the concentration of OVA-specific IgG1 in the serum was markedly upregulated by SCIT.22 However, whether the number of IgG1-producing B cells in the lung is increased by SCIT has not been fully elucidated. In this study, mice were sensitized, treated with SCIT, challenged with OVA, and analyzed on day 41 (Fig. 1A). The numbers of IgG1+, IgG2a+, IgG2b+, or IgG3+ B cells in the lung were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1B–F and Supplementary Fig. S1).

Effects of subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) on the numbers of total B cells and IgG1+, IgG2a+, IgG2b+, and IgG3+ B cells in the lungs of allergic mice. Mice were intraperitoneally sensitized with OVA + Al(OH)3 on days 0 and 14, treated with SCIT on days 21, 23, and 25 by subcutaneous administration of OVA (1 mg/animal), and then intratracheally (i.t.) challenged with OVA (5 μg/animal) on days 35, 36, 37, and 40 (A). Lung B-cell subsets were analyzed by flow cytometry on day 41. Data are shown for total Pan-B cells (B), IgG1+ B cells (C), IgG2a+ B cells (D), IgG2b+ B cells (E), and IgG3+ B cells (F). Each column represents the mean ± 95% CI of 8 animals.

When sensitized mice were treated with SCIT even without OVA challenges, the numbers of IgG1+ B cells and IgG2b+ B cells were markedly increased (p = 0.0002 and 0.0002, respectively), although the numbers of pan-B cells, IgG2a+ B cells, and IgG3+ B cells were not changed (Fig. 1B–F). In multiple OVA-challenged mice, the numbers of IgG1+ B cells and IgG2b+ B cells were also significantly higher in SCIT mice than in mice without SCIT (p = 0.0409 and p = 0.0395, respectively; Fig. 1C and E). Among these subsets, IgG1+ B cells were the most abundant (Fig. 1C).

Effect of SCIT-induced IgG1 (SCIT-IgG1) on the development of airway remodeling

To clarify the roles of SCIT-IgG1, we purified total IgG1 from SCIT mice. Table 1 shows the concentrations of immunoglobulin subclasses in the fraction purified from the sera of SCIT mice using the pH-gradient method. The purified fraction contained a large amount of IgG1, whereas the levels of other IgG subclasses (IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG3) were almost negligible. Importantly, OVA-specific IgG1 was also present at a high level, indicating that the purified fraction was predominantly composed of IgG1, including OVA-specific IgG1. Next, the binding of SCIT-IgG1 to OVA was qualitatively assessed. As shown in Fig. 2, an equal amount of OVA protein was loaded in each lane, separated by SDS–PAGE, and probed with different IgG1 antibodies. SCIT-IgG1 displayed clear binding to OVA, similar to the positive controls using OVA-specific IgG1 antibodies (clones L71 and 3G2E1D9). In contrast, the negative control IgG1 (clone MOPC21) showed no detectable binding. These results indicate that SCIT-IgG1 recognizes OVA.

Subsequently, we administered SCIT-IgG1 to OVA-induced asthmatic mice (Fig. 3A). For the control, a non-specific mouse IgG1 (clone MOPC-21) was used, which did not bind OVA (Fig. 2). As has been previously reported by us,22 epithelial thickening and mucus accumulation were induced by repeated OVA challenges (Fig. 3B–D and Supplementary Fig. S2). While administration of control IgG1 had no significant effect on these pathological changes, treatment with SCIT-IgG1 significantly suppressed epithelial thickening and mucus accumulation at a dose of 50 μg/time/animal (p = 0.0034 and 0.0341, respectively; Fig. 3C and D).

Effect of SCIT-IgG1 on numbers of ILC2s, Th2 cells, eosinophils and IL-33-producing epithelial cells

The numbers of ILC2s, Th2 cells, and eosinophils were significantly increased by multiple OVA challenges (Fig. 4A–C). Compared with the control IgG1 group, administration of SCIT-IgG1 at 50 μg/time/animal significantly reduced the numbers of ILC2s and eosinophils (p = 0.0246 and 0.0041, respectively; Fig. 4A and C), whereas it had no significant effect on the number of Th2 cells (Fig. 4B).

Mice were intraperitoneally sensitized with OVA + Al(OH)3 on days 0 and 14, and then i.t. challenged with OVA at a dose of 5 μg/animal on days 35, 36, 37, and 40. Control IgG1 or SCIT-IgG1 (15 or 50 μg/40 μL/time/animal) was intratracheally administered twice daily on days 34–40. Numbers of ILC2s (A), Th2 cells (B), eosinophils (C), and IL-33–producing epithelial cells (D) in the left lung were analyzed by flow cytometry, and IL-33 levels (E) were measured by ELISA on day 41. IL-33 is a key cytokine mainly produced by epithelial cells that activates ILC2s. As shown in Fig. 4D and E, multiple OVA challenges markedly increased both the number of IL-33-producing epithelial cells and the amount of IL-33 in the lung. Neither SCIT-IgG1 nor control IgG1 affected these parameters (Fig. 4D and E).

Expression of FcγRs on lung ILC2s

FcγR expression on lung ILC2s was then examined (Fig. 5). Among the detectable subclasses, FcγRIIb showed the highest expression (Fig. 5B). Repeated OVA challenges markedly increased the expression of both FcγRIIb and the activating receptor FcγRIII on ILC2s (p = 0.0004 and 0.0021, respectively; Fig. 5B and C) whereas FcγRI and FcγRIV were expressed at substantially lower levels. To assess the balance between inhibitory and activating inputs, we calculated the FcγRIIb-to-FcγRIII expression ratio based on mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). This ratio was significantly higher in ILC2s from OVA-challenged lungs than in those from non-challenged controls (3.90 [95% CI, 3.22–4.59] vs. 1.92 [95% CI, 1.81–2.03], p = 0.0022).

Effect of SCIT-IgG1 on the in vitro proliferation of lung ILC2s

As shown in Fig. 6A, lung ILC2s derived from multiple OVA-challenged mice showed no proliferation when treated with OVA or SCIT-IgG1 alone, whereas IL-33 alone significantly induced ILC2 proliferation (p = 0.0037). Under the IL-33-stimulated condition, the addition of OVA further enhanced ILC2 proliferation compared with IL-33 alone (p = 0.0012; Fig. 6A). In contrast, the addition of OVA together with SCIT-IgG1 markedly suppressed proliferation relative to the IL-33 plus OVA condition (p = 0.0018; Fig. 6A). OVA combined with control IgG1 also significantly suppressed ILC2 proliferation (p = 0.0359), although the inhibitory effect was more pronounced in the presence of SCIT-IgG1. Importantly, under the isotype control antibody condition, the significant suppressive effect of SCIT-IgG1 was still observed (p = 0.0424), whereas this significant suppressive effect was no longer observed in the presence of an anti-FcγRIIb antibody at 100 μg/mL (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

The roles of increased IgG1 in SCIT have not been fully elucidated. In this study, we demonstrated that: (1) IgG1+ B cells and IgG2b+ B cells were significantly increased in the lungs of SCIT-treated asthmatic mice (p = 0.0409 and p = 0.0395, respectively), with IgG1+ B cells being the most abundant among the 4 IgG+ B-cell subsets; (2) intratracheal administration of IgG1 purified from SCIT mice (SCIT-IgG1) significantly suppressed epithelial thickening, mucus accumulation, and the increases in lung ILC2s and eosinophils in the asthma model (p = 0.0034, p = 0.0341, p = 0.0246, and p = 0.0041, respectively); (3) FcγRIIb expression on lung ILC2s was significantly increased in asthmatic mice (p = 0.0004); and (4) the in vitro proliferation of ILC2s induced by OVA and IL-33 was significantly suppressed by SCIT-IgG1 (p = 0.0018). Importantly, this significant suppressive effect was no longer observed in the presence of an anti-FcγRIIb antibody. The proposed mechanism is summarized in Fig. 7. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that SCIT-IgG1 suppresses the proliferation of lung ILC2s via FcγRIIb engagement.

Graphical abstract. SCIT induced marked increases in IgG1+ and IgG2b+ B cells, with IgG1+ B cells being the predominant subset. IgG1 purified from SCIT mice (SCIT-IgG1) suppressed airway remodeling as well as the expansion of lung ILC2s and eosinophilic infiltration in an asthma model. Mechanistically, FcγRIIb was highly expressed on lung ILC2s, and the proliferation of ILC2s induced by OVA and IL-33 was significantly suppressed by SCIT-IgG1. Importantly, the significant inhibition by SCIT-IgG1 disappeared in the presence of an anti-FcγRIIb antibody. Together, these findings identify a novel mechanism by which SCIT-IgG1 regulates airway inflammation through FcγRIIb-mediated suppression of ILC2 proliferation.

Previous studies27,28 have suggested that allergy immunotherapy induces the production of allergen-specific IgG antibodies, which act as “blocking antibodies” by competing with IgE for antigen binding and thereby attenuating downstream allergic responses.14 In humans, IgG4 is thought to play this role, whereas in mice, IgG1 has been considered the functional counterpart.18,29 Although these studies have emphasized the capability of SCIT-induced IgG to interfere with IgE-mediated pathways, little attention has been paid to the direct effects of IgG on innate immune cells such as ILC2s. Our findings extend the current understanding by demonstrating that SCIT-induced IgG1 not only functions as a blocking antibody but also directly suppresses ILC2 proliferation through engagement of FcγRIIb. This novel mechanism provides a new perspective on how SCIT exerts its therapeutic effects in allergic airway inflammation.

The mechanistic basis of SCIT-IgG1–mediated suppression of ILC2s appears to involve engagement of FcγRIIb. FcγRIIb is a well-characterized inhibitory receptor that transduces negative signals through its immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM), thereby dampening cellular activation.30 Previous studies have established the role of FcγRIIb in regulating B cells,31 mast cells,14 and dendritic cells,32 where it counteracts activating FcγR signaling. In the present study, we found that FcγRIIb was highly expressed on lung ILC2s, and blockade of FcγRIIb interfered with the suppressive effect of SCIT-IgG1. These results suggest that SCIT-IgG1 may directly engage FcγRIIb on ILC2s, delivering inhibitory signals that limit their proliferation in response to IL-33 and OVA. This finding highlights a previously unrecognized role of FcγRIIb in the regulation of ILC2s, and adds to the broader understanding of FcγR-mediated immune regulation.

In considering this mechanism, it should be noted that monomeric (non-complexed) IgG1 is typically considered to bind only very weakly, if at all, to low-affinity Fcγ receptors such as FcγRIIb, FcγRIII, or FcγRIV. Indeed, several reviews33,34 indicate that FcγRI is the only murine Fcγ receptor that reliably binds monomeric IgG, whereas effective engagement of the other Fcγ receptors usually requires immune-complexed IgG. In this context, the stronger suppressive effect of SCIT-IgG1 is consistent with the possibility that SCIT-derived IgG1 forms immune complexes with OVA, thereby promoting more efficient FcγRIIb engagement on ILC2s and enhancing inhibitory signaling. Notably, control IgG1 also showed a significant suppressive effect, although the inhibition remained more pronounced with SCIT-IgG1. The reason for this modest suppression by control IgG1 is not fully clear. However, because FcγRIIb expression was markedly upregulated on lung ILC2s after repeated OVA challenges in this study, these cells may have been particularly sensitive to inhibitory inputs under the present assay conditions. Taken together, these findings support the idea that SCIT-IgG1 exerts stronger inhibition of ILC2 proliferation, most likely through more efficient FcγRIIb engagement associated with allergen-specific immune complex formation.

The expression of FcγRIIb on ILC2s was markedly upregulated by multiple OVA challenges in this study. IL-4, IL-13, and IL-33 are crucial cytokines known to enhance FcγRIIb expression on various immune cells, including dendritic cells and macrophages, in both mice and humans.35, 36, 37 We previously demonstrated that the levels of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-33 in the lungs increased progressively with repeated allergen challenges in this model.38,39 Moreover, the receptors for these cytokines are expressed on mouse ILC2s.40,41 Therefore, the cytokine milieu generated by multiple OVA challenges may synergistically promote the upregulation of FcγRIIb on ILC2s. In addition to FcγRIIb, the activating receptor FcγRIII was also significantly upregulated by repeated OVA challenges. Notably, however, the balance between these receptors shifted toward a relatively higher inhibitory tone, as reflected by an increased FcγRIIb-to-FcγRIII expression ratio observed in our analysis. These findings indicate that, despite the upregulation of both receptors, the relative expression of the inhibitory FcγRIIb predominates over FcγRIII after repeated allergen challenges. Consequently, ILC2s in allergen-challenged lungs are more prone to receiving negative signals through FcγRIIb engagement, which may serve as an intrinsic mechanism to counterbalance excessive type 2 immune activation.

Landers et al42 emphasized that allergen-specific IgG2b can exert protective functions by alleviating mast cell activation. The mechanisms underlying the induction of IgG2b during SCIT remain to be clarified, but accumulating evidence suggests that regulatory cytokines, particularly IL-10, play a role in driving class switching toward IgG2b.43 Indeed, we previously demonstrated that type 1 regulatory T (Tr1) cells, characterized by robust IL-10 production, were markedly increased in the lungs of SCIT mice in this model.22 Thus, it is plausible that the expansion of IgG2b+ B cells is promoted, at least in part, by IL-10 derived from Tr1 cells. Taken together, these findings indicate that SCIT induces both IgG1 and IgG2b responses, and these antibodies may act in concert to mediate the therapeutic effects of SCIT.

The present study also revealed that the proliferation of lung ILC2s was more strongly induced by IL-33 plus OVA than by IL-33 alone. Since OVA itself is not a known pathogen recognition receptor ligand, one plausible explanation is that trace amounts of endotoxin (LPS) contained in OVA preparations acted through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expressed on ILC2s, thereby amplifying IL-33–driven proliferation. Indeed, Mac Sharry et al44 reported that the OVA (Grade V, Sigma Chem.) that we used contained LPS at 400 ng/10 mg OVA. Importantly, SCIT-IgG1 significantly suppressed the proliferation induced by IL-33 plus OVA but did not affect the proliferation induced by IL-33 alone (data not shown). This observation suggests that SCIT-IgG1 may specifically interfere with TLR4-mediated co-stimulatory signals rather than with IL-33 signaling itself. Thus, the suppressive effect of SCIT-IgG1 on ILC2 proliferation is likely attributable, at least in part, to the inhibition of TLR4-dependent enhancement triggered by LPS contamination in OVA.

This study has several limitations. First, although murine IgG1 is often regarded as a functional counterpart of human IgG4 because both subclasses have a weak capacity to activate the classical complement pathway, important species-specific differences remain, particularly in Fcγ receptor interactions.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 In addition, human IgG4 uniquely undergoes Fab-arm exchange,45 a structural property that has not been described as a typical feature of murine IgG1. Therefore, the present findings in mice may not fully recapitulate the immunoregulatory properties of human IgG4 in human AIT. Second, although our blocking-antibody experiments support the involvement of FcγRIIb, we did not evaluate the effects of genetic FcγRIIb deficiency, and thus definitive confirmation using FcγRIIb-deficient mice will be required in future studies. Third, we did not directly quantify allergen–IgG1 immune complex formation, and therefore the relationship between immune-complex formation and FcγRIIb-mediated inhibition of ILC2 proliferation remains to be clarified. Fourth, because SCIT-IgG1 was administered intratracheally as an experimental approach to assess its local function, the safety profile of this intervention was not systematically examined. Further studies will therefore be necessary to evaluate its local and systemic safety, to determine whether comparable mechanisms operate in human ILC2s, and to explore the therapeutic applicability of SCIT-induced IgG in a clinically relevant setting.

Conclusion

Our study suggests a previously unappreciated mechanism by which SCIT-induced IgG1 contributes to the suppression of allergic airway inflammation, in part through FcγRIIb-dependent inhibition of ILC2 proliferation. These findings refine our understanding of how allergy immunotherapy exerts its effects, indicating that antibody–ILC2 interactions may represent an additional regulatory component of type 2 inflammation. While IgG1 serves this role in mice, analogous processes could involve IgG4 in allergic subjects, providing a conceptual basis for therapeutic approaches that enhance inhibitory FcγR signaling.
