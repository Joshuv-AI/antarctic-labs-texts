Effects of psychosocial stress on myocardial injury by modulating endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced autophagy and inflammation
Authors: XiaoLei Gao, GuangBiao Huang, Ran Hao, LiNa Wang, AnNa Ma, ZhaoHui Zhang, Tong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPsychosocial stress, a known trigger for depression, activates the sympathetic nervous system and serves as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), a protective response to stress, is linked to mental disorders. In depression patients, levels of NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3), nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), and interleukin-1β (IL-1) were elevated, while HRV parameters were reduced. Social defeat stress was found to activate autonomic neural activity, subsequently leading to myocardial injury through ERS modulation. Dopamine (DA), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and corticosterone (CORT) levels were measured by ELISA. The number and morphology of amygdala neurons were evaluated via Nissl and TUNEL staining. ERS-induced autophagy and inflammation were assessed using western blot and immunofluorescence staining. This study links ERS to myocardial injury, suggesting ERS modulation could represent a potential therapeutic strategy for stress-induced myocardial injury.

Introduction

Psychosocial stress, encompassing both physiological and psychological responses to perceived social threats (e.g., workplace conflict, social isolation, or financial strain), affects approximately 280 million people globally. It is a well-established contributor to mental disorders, particularly major depressive disorder.1 Depression is a leading cause of years lived with disability2 and is strongly associated with the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases.3 Both animals and humans exhibit distinct patterns of physical and psychological responses to various stressors, which lead to complex physiological adjustments adapted to environmental demands.4 These adjustments include increased sympathetic activity, activation of the sympatho-adrenal medullary and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axes, and elevated corticosterone (CORT) levels.5 Moreover, depression can reduce the expression of monoamine neurotransmitters, such as dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT), which not only impact the central nervous system but also play a pivotal role in renal and cardiac function.6,7 Cardiac injury induced by stress is likely a secondary response mediated through the brain-heart axis. However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. With the growing prevalence of psychosocial stress in modern society, further research into its impact on depression and subsequent cardiovascular damage is essential for disease prevention.

The American Heart Association recently emphasized the significance of psychological well-being and the mind-body connection in cardiovascular health, highlighting the associations between psychosocial stress and cardiovascular disease.8 Stress and depression can lead to autonomic nervous system dysregulation, increased heart rate, and myocardial injury.9 Heart rate variability (HRV), a measure of autonomic input to the heart, reflects the complex and reciprocal interactions along the brain-heart axis. Additionally, depression is associated with increased inflammatory markers. Clinical evidence suggests that decreased HRV and elevated NLRP3 inflammasome activity are linked to psychosocial stress and various psychiatric disorders, including depression.10 Furthermore, studies indicate that DA receptor activation can mitigate cardiac injury by modulating the NLRP3 inflammasome.6 The social defeat stress mouse model is a widely used and ethologically relevant approach for studying stress in rodents, closely mirroring the psychosocial stress experienced by humans. Social defeat stress induces anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors, contributing to myocardial injury by enhancing inflammatory markers.11,12 Our group’s recent research demonstrated that chronic stress induces depressive-like behaviors and cognitive impairment in mice through activation of the endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS)-NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3) signaling pathway.13 The role of the ERS signaling pathway in stress-induced cardiomyopathy and its regulation of myocardial injury warrant further investigation.

ERS plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation, depression, and various neurocognitive disorders. Autophagy, an anti-inflammatory process, is critical for mitigating inflammation.14 The ERS signaling pathway and the resultant unfolded protein response (UPR) are implicated in neuronal damage associated with neurostress.15 Upon ERS activation, the UPR triggers autophagy, beginning with the activation of inositol-requiring enzyme 1, protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6. This activation promotes the formation of autophagosomes, which can be quantified by measuring the levels of BECLIN-1, p62/sequestosome-1, and microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3).15 Numerous studies have highlighted autophagy’s anti-inflammatory role and its ability to reverse depression-like behaviors induced by social defeat stress.16,17 Additionally, autophagy-related genes have been found to be suppressed in patients with postpartum depression.18 Our recent work further demonstrated that chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS)-induced depression-like behaviors are closely linked to persistent ERS and the ERS-PERK-NLRP3 signaling pathway.13 Autophagy reduces inflammation by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation through the engulfment of inflammasome components. Moreover, several studies have reported dysregulated autophagy in animal models of depression.19 Chronic psychological stress can lead to hyperactivation of the HPA axis and elevated CORT levels, initiating ERS and impairing autophagy.20,21 However, the exact mechanisms by which ERS-induced autophagy and inflammation contribute to psychosocial stress-induced myocardial injury remain unclear. Thus, this study investigated the interactions between inflammation, autophagy, and ERS in the progression of myocardial injury induced by psychosocial stress.

Activation of the HPA axis may lead to stress-induced myocardial injury through persistent ERS. The amygdala is crucial for acquiring and processing learned anxious associations, mediating autonomic and behavioral responses related to fear and anxiety, which are linked to CORT activation.22,23 Elevated CORT levels in the amygdala may contribute to myocardial injury associated with psychosocial stress. Additionally, altered DA and 5-HT expression in the amygdala modulates ERS and contributes to depressive-like behaviors.24,25 Numerous studies have highlighted the role of ERS activation and autophagy dysfunction in stress-induced mental disorders.23,26 In depression animal models, ERS triggers inflammation and plays a pivotal role in activating the NLRP3 inflammasome.27 Meanwhile, autophagy alleviates the inflammatory response through the degradation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.28 However, the exact mechanisms by which psychosocial stress induces myocardial injury through ERS-mediated inflammation and autophagy dysfunction require further investigation.

Building on these studies, it was hypothesized that ERS-induced inflammation and autophagy dysfunction play a critical role in psychosocial stress-induced myocardial injury. This study first investigated whether inflammatory factor levels correlate with decreased HRV in depression patients. Next, the social defeat stress mouse model was employed to simulate human psychosocial stress. This study then explored whether psychosocial stress stimuli induce myocardial injury by activating the ERS-NLRP3 signaling pathway and causing autophagy dysfunction in this model. Furthermore, it examined whether inhibiting ERS can reverse myocardial injury, aiming to uncover the intrinsic mechanisms linking ERS-related signaling pathways with stress-induced myocardial injury.

Results

Descriptive data

Fifty depression patients and 45 healthy controls, meeting the study criteria, were enrolled. The average age of the depression group was 38.22 ± 1.29, and for the healthy control group, it was 36.24 ± 1.56, with no significant difference between the two groups.

Verification that the model is related to the experiment

The success of this model was verified by the social avoidance test. Significant differences were observed among the control, social defeat, social defeat + 4-phenylbutyric acid (PBA), and tunicamycin (TM) groups regarding the time spent in the interaction zone (F [3, 56] = 74.07, p < 0.01). Compared to control mice, socially defeated mice and those in the TM group spent less time in the interaction zone (Figures 2A and 2B, p < 0.05). Administration of PBA significantly increased the time spent in the interaction zone in the social defeat + PBA group (Figures 2A and 2B, p < 0.05). These data confirm the successful establishment of the social defeat stress model and show that the ERS agonist TM induced similar behavioral changes to those seen in socially defeated mice. Furthermore, the ERS inhibitor PBA altered the behavioral changes caused by social defeat stress (Figures 2A and 2B, p < 0.05).Figure 2Effects of social defeat on social avoidance test(A) Social avoidance text. Effects of social defeat stress on self-directed activity and neurotransmitter levels in socially defeated mice

Stress can induce a depression-related state in rodents, which can be assessed by measuring spontaneous grooming behavior.29 As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, coat state deterioration was observed in socially defeated mice, and this was prevented by PBA treatment (p < 0.05). PBA administration also prevented the decrease in body weight gain induced by social defeat stress (Figure 3C, p < 0.05). CORT, a marker of HPA axis activation, is associated with stress-induced myocardial injury. After exposure to social defeat stress, serum CORT levels increased, but this elevation was prevented by PBA administration (Figure 3D, p < 0.05). 5-HT and DA, key monoamine neurotransmitters, serve as critical markers for assessing depression.30 Social defeat stress significantly reduced 5-HT and DA levels in the amygdala (Figures 3E and 3F, p < 0.05) compared to the control group. This reduction was prevented by PBA. PBA alleviated social defeat stress-induced amygdala histopathological changes and neuronal damage in mice

Nissl staining revealed significant morphological changes in socially defeated and TM-treated mice, including vacuolation, pyknosis, cell loss, poorly defined Nissl granules, and a notable reduction in typical neuronal structures. In contrast, neurons in the control group exhibited a regular arrangement. PBA treatment effectively alleviated these alterations induced by social defeat stress (Figures 4A and 4B, p < 0.05). To further investigate the cellular ultrastructure in the amygdala, TEM was employed. Our findings showed that social defeat stress and TM treatment induced mitochondrial swelling, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) dilation, nuclear edema, and a reduction in autolysosomes. Notably, PBA administration mitigated the damage caused by social defeat stress (Figure S1A).

PBA attenuates social defeat stress-induced myocardial injury

Histopathological changes serve as a direct indicator of myocardial injury. In socially defeated mice, heart tissue alterations, including inflammatory cell infiltration and steatosis, were observed via H&E staining (Figures 5A and 5B). Additionally, social defeat stress induced heart fibrosis (Figure 5B), which was prevented by ERS inhibitor PBA treatment. Furthermore, social defeat stress significantly increased TUNEL-positive cells, an effect that was notably reversed by PBA treatment (Figures 5C and 5D, p < 0.05). Echocardiography revealed increased heart rate and decreased FS% and EF%, all of which were restored with PBA treatment (Figures 5E, 5F, 5G, and 5H, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the NF-κB signaling pathway interacts with NLRP3 to enhance IL-1β transcription, playing a critical role in the innate immune response and inflammation in the CNS.31 To explore the connection between ERS and the activation of inflammatory factors in the heart tissue of socially defeated mice, western blotting and immunofluorescence staining were performed. Western blot analysis revealed significant increases in p-NF-κB/NF-κB (Figures 7A and 7B, p < 0.05), TNF-α (Figures 7A–7C, p < 0.05), and BAX (Figures 7A–7E, p < 0.05) expression in both the social defeat stress and TM groups, while Bcl-2 expression was notably decreased (Figures 7A–7D, p < 0.05). PBA administration prevented the activation of these inflammatory factors induced by social defeat stress (Figures 7A, 7B, 7C, and 7D, p < 0.05). To further assess autophagic flux, morphological changes in lysosomes, mitochondria, and other organelles were examined using TEM, while co-localization of LC3 and LAMP was detected by immunofluorescence. TEM analysis, shown in Figure S1, revealed key ultrastructural alterations indicative of autophagic dysfunction: (i) lysosomal abnormalities, characterized by heterogeneous size distribution and accumulation of undigested autophagic substrates; (ii) mitochondrial pathologies, including organellar swelling, cristae fragmentation, and aggregation of dysfunctional mitochondria; and (iii) pronounced ER distension with prominent proteinaceous aggregates, consistent with unresolved ERS. Additionally, Figure 9 demonstrated that the relative fluorescence intensity of LC3 and LAMP in the social defeat stress group was notably lower than in the control group (Figures 9F–9H, 9I, p < 0.05). This decrease was reversed by PBA treatment (Figures 9F–9H, 9I, p < 0.05).

PBA improves TM-induced ERS-related inflammation and impairment of autophagy in H9C2 cells

To confirm the role of ERS in the expression of inflammatory factors and autophagy, H9C2 cells were treated with TM to induce ERS. Cell morphology was observed under an inverted phase contrast microscope, and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. In the control group, cells were adherent and displayed a fusiform or uniformly triangular shape. After exposure to TM, cellular shrinkage and nuclear condensation were observed (Figure S2A), along with reduced cell viability (Figure S2B, p < 0.05), which was restored by pretreatment with PBA. Hoechst 33342 staining was performed to assess apoptotic cells. In the TM group, chromatin margination, nuclear condensation, and a white color indicative of cell death were observed (Figure S2C), and the apoptosis rate was significantly increased (Figure S2D, p < 0.05). Pretreatment with PBA decreased nuclear fluorescence intensity and attenuated the apoptosis rate (Figures S2C and S2D). Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in the expression of GRP78 (Figures S2E and S2F, p < 0.05), CHOP (Figures S2E and S2G, p < 0.05), NLRP3 (Figures S2E and S2H, p < 0.05), and IL-1 (Figures S2E and S2I, p < 0.05) in the TM group compared to the control group, which was reversed by PBA administration. Additionally, the expression of Beclin-1 (Figures S2E and S2J, p < 0.05), P62 (Figures S2E and S2K, p < 0.05), and LC3II (Figure S2E, l, p < 0.05) was significantly decreased in the TM group, and this decrease was recovered by PBA treatment.

Discussion

Psychosocial stress can lead to mood disorders, including depression and associated behavioral changes. Recent studies have also suggested that stress contributes to the development of cardiovascular disease.1,33 However, our understanding of how stress affects aberrant brain-heart communication remains limited. Stress exposure activates the HPA axis and induces inflammation, both of which may play critical roles in stress-related myocardial injury, a process in which decreased HRV is involved.10 The current study showed that the levels of inflammatory factors, such as NLRP3, NF-κB, and IL-1, were significantly elevated in depression patients. Additionally, time-domain HRV parameters, including average RR interval, SDRR, and RMSSD, were significantly reduced in these patients. These findings suggest that inflammation and HRV are involved in stress-induced myocardial injury. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, a stress mouse model was employed in subsequent studies.

The social defeat stress model is a well-established animal model used to investigate stress in rodents, known to induce behaviors related to both depression and anxiety, as well as myocardial injury.12,29,30 The social avoidance test confirmed the successful establishment of the stressed animal model in this study. Next, self-directed activity and neurotransmitter levels were evaluated. Consistent with previous studies29,30 and our expectations, social defeat stress and the ERS agonist TM both resulted in decreased coat state scores and body weight gain. Moreover, activation of the HPA axis increased CORT levels and reduced 5-HT and DA levels. These neurotransmitters play significant roles in neuromodulation and are important indicators for evaluating mood disorders, while also contributing to decreased HRV. Although it is known that stress can induce depression and alter neurotransmitter levels, the mechanisms by which it leads to myocardial injury remain to be fully explored.

The amygdala, a brain region involved in stress, mediates its activity through pathways that include inflammation and has been identified as an independent and robust predictor of cardiovascular events.34 In this study, the effects of social defeat stress on the amygdala were investigated. Nissl staining revealed morphological changes in the amygdala following social defeat stress, which were prevented by the ERS inhibitor PBA. Similar results were observed with TUNEL staining. Additionally, ERS agonist TM also induced neuronal damage and apoptosis in the amygdala. In subsequent experiments, social defeat stress and TM were found to cause myocardial injury, as confirmed by H&E and TUNEL staining. Administration of PBA prevented these injuries. These findings suggest that amygdala activation plays a key role in stress-induced brain-heart communication, with ERS potentially mediating this process.

The ER plays a pivotal role in maintaining calcium balance, protein folding, and maturation.35 However, persistent protein misfolding, exacerbated by external stressors, can trigger ERS. Prolonged ERS disrupts ER function, initiating inflammatory and apoptotic pathways that may ultimately lead to cell death.36 Several studies have suggested that ERS-related proteins are involved in microglial inflammasome activation under chronic social defeat stress37 and can cause cellular injury in cardiomyocytes.38 However, the precise role of ERS-related inflammation and apoptosis in stress-induced myocardial injury remains unclear. This study demonstrates that the expression of p-PERK, CHOP, and GRP78 was significantly increased in the hearts of socially defeated mice, confirming that ERS plays a role in stress-induced myocardial injury. According to previous research, PERK-mediated ERS is critical for inflammation and apoptosis. GRP78 is essential for ER homeostasis, while CHOP, an indicator of ERS, can trigger apoptosis through caspase activation. The action of PERK leads to the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, initiating an inflammatory cascade.14,39 This study further investigates whether ERS induces inflammation in stress-induced myocardial injury. In the hearts of socially defeated mice, the expression of p-NF-κB, TNF-α, and NLRP3 was significantly increased, an effect that was prevented by the ERS inhibitor PBA. Additionally, the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, caspase-1, and IL-1 were elevated in the hearts of stressed mice, while Bcl-2 expression was notably decreased. These changes were also prevented by PBA treatment. TM-induced ERS caused significant neuronal injury in the amygdala and induced myocardial injury. This study demonstrates the involvement of the ERS-NF-κB-NLRP3 signaling pathway in stress-induced myocardial injury within the social defeat stress model. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that ERS is linked to autophagy through various pathways,14 and autophagy protects against myocardial injury caused by ischemia-reperfusion by inhibiting NLRP3 activity.40 Whether autophagy is involved in stress-induced myocardial injury warrants further investigation.

Autophagy is a conserved catabolic process that protects cells from damage and promotes survival during stress by facilitating degradation through lysosomes.41 Upon the activation of ERS, autophagy is initiated as a cellular response to maintain homeostasis by forming autophagosomes. However, prolonged ERS impairs autophagic function and triggers inflammatory responses.14 The relationship between ERS and autophagy was further explored in this study. Results showed that the expression of Beclin-1, LC3, and P62 was significantly reduced, a change that was prevented by the ERS inhibitor PBA. Treatment with the ERS agonist TM, which induced ERS by increasing the levels of p-PERK, CHOP, and GRP78, led to decreased expression of Beclin-1, LC3, and P62. Moreover, this study revealed that ERS induces inflammation, which subsequently causes apoptosis, while also impairing autophagy. These findings suggest that ERS plays a critical role in stress-induced myocardial injury by disrupting brain-body communication through an imbalance in inflammation and autophagy. However, the role of autophagy in stress adaptation remains debated, with some studies suggesting a protective role, while others highlight its potential detrimental effects. Our results demonstrate that autophagy acts protectively under the stress conditions studied, as evidenced by elevated LC3II and LAMP protein levels and electron microscopy (EM) observations of autophagosome formation and lysosomal integrity. To further elucidate the context-dependent mechanisms of autophagy in stress responses, future investigations should employ diverse stress paradigms, such as CUMS, and systematically vary stressor intensity and temporal dynamics. This approach will allow for a comprehensive exploration of autophagy’s dual regulatory roles, ranging from cytoprotection to potential pathological exacerbation, across different stress conditions.

This study explored the relationship between inflammation and decreased HRV in depression patients. It was demonstrated that ERS, initiated by social defeat stress, induces inflammation and causes autophagy dysfunction in a social defeat stress mouse model. These signaling pathways were associated with increased CORT levels and decreased expression of DA and 5-HT neurotransmitters. Furthermore, the amygdala, a critical brain region involved in emotion regulation, exhibited morphological changes and apoptosis. Myocardial injury was also observed in socially defeated mice. Additionally, PBA, an ERS inhibitor, restored autophagy function, prevented inflammation, enhanced DA and 5-HT neurotransmitter expression, and reduced both neuronal and myocardial damage. The ERS agonist TM induced similar changes to those seen in social defeat stress. These findings bridge preclinical mouse studies and clinical research on depression patients, suggesting that ERS-induced autophagy and inflammation mediate stress-related cardiac dysfunction. The current study links ERS-induced autophagy to stress-induced myocardial injury in both preclinical and clinical settings.

Limitations of the study

This study aimed to investigate whether ERS-induced inflammation and autophagy dysfunction are associated with stress-induced myocardial injury in mice and to explore the underlying mechanisms. However, this study has several limitations. First, only certain biochemical indicators and HRV were measured in depression patients, without incorporating imaging studies. Second, while the connection between ERS-induced inflammation and autophagy dysfunction was established in the stress mouse model and H9C2 cells, the direct link within the brain-heart axis and the specific molecular mechanisms require further exploration. Future studies will focus on elucidating the exact pathogenesis of stress-induced myocardial injury through follow-up experiments.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

This study recruited 50 male patients with depression and 45 healthy controls, all of whom met the diagnostic criteria for depression according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV). Ethical approval (XYLL-20230175) was granted by the Ethics Committee of Xinxiang Medical University, China, on March 10, 2023, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 to 60 years; (2) absence of major systemic illnesses, as determined by laboratory tests and physical examinations. Exclusion criteria: (1) pre-morbid IQ ≤ 70; (2) patients deemed too seriously ill or suicidal; (3) presence of a serious, unstable medical condition.

Ethics statement—human subjects and specimens

This study only analyzed existing, fully de-identified clinical laboratory records; no direct patient intervention or contact with identifiable personal information occurred. Therefore, this study was only reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and is provided with the corresponding number (XYLL-20230175).

Clinic and sample collection details

Blood samples and 24-h electrocardiographic were collected by medical personnel as a part of routine screening or work-up. After collection, a portion of the was placed in appropriate transport media and transported to the Henan Key Laboratory of Biological Psychiatry (HeNan XinXiang). Upon arrival, the samples were subjected to testing and cellular processing as described below. No interventions or treatments were performed in the study.

Animal studies

This study utilized 60 male C57BL/6J mice (7 weeks old, 18-22 g) and 30 male ICR mice (14 weeks old, 38-42 g), all supplied by Spiff Biotechnology (Beijing, China). After arrival, mice were acclimated in a climate-controlled room for 1 week, with constant temperature and humidity, free access to food and water, and a 12-hour light-dark cycle. The mice were then randomly assigned to the control, social defeat stress, ERS inhibitor (social defeat + PBA), and ERS agonist (TM) groups, with 15 mice in each group. In line with previous studies, the social defeat + PBA group was injected with 100 mg/kg42 of the ERS inhibitor PBA (Sigma, Shanghai, China) 30 minutes prior to daily social defeat stress via peritoneal injection. The TM group received 2 mg/kg of the ERS agonist tunicamycin (TM, Sigma, Shanghai, China) via intraperitoneal injection without further intervention.42 Following the experiments, the mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation. All animal procedures adhered to the guidelines set by the Henan Provincial Key Laboratory of Biological Psychiatry's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (HMH.No20220429AEC004) and were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting in vivo experiments.

Cell culture

H9C2 rat embryonic cardiomyocytes (purchased from Clonetics, San Diego, CA, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen Life Technologies), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine. H9C2 cells from the 4th to 9th passages were used throughout the study.

H9C2 cells were grown to 70-80% confluence in OPTI MEM medium, then incubated with TM (250 μM) for 2 hours in the presence or absence of PBA (50 μM).

Method details

Sample collection and measurement of biochemical indicators

Peripheral blood samples were collected in the morning (6:00 am to 9:00 am) from all participants. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C and stored at -80°C until use. Serum concentrations of NLRP3, IL-1, and NF-κB were measured using ELISA kits (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China).

Heart rate variability (HRV)

Patients underwent 24-hour electrocardiographic monitoring using a 3-channel Marquette 8800 recorder (Brentwood, USA). HRV, an indicator of autonomic nervous system function, was analyzed based on time-domain indices, including average RR interval, SDRR, and RMSSD (in ms).

Establishment of a stress mouse model

According to previous studies,42 the social defeat stress model, a preclinical standard for modeling chronic psychosocial adversity to investigate depression and anxiety pathogenesis, was employed in this study using ICR and C57BL/6J mice. ICR mice, being larger and more aggressive than C57BL/6J mice, were selected based on their attack latencies (attacking C57BL/6J mice within 30 seconds during three successive screening tests) to serve as aggressive residents. In both the social defeat stress and social defeat stress + PBA groups, C57BL/6J mice were exposed to different ICR aggressors daily for 10 minutes over ten consecutive days. After each 10-minute confrontation, the defeated C57BL/6J mice and their aggressors were housed together in a shared cage divided by transparent partitions, allowing continuous psychological stress through olfactory and visual contact for 24 hours. To ensure each defeated mouse was exposed to a novel aggressor, social defeat mice were systematically rotated daily throughout the 10-day protocol (see below Figure). Assessment of coat state of mice

Coat condition, which reflects the animal’s motivation for self-maintenance and is sensitive to stress, was assessed before the first aggressive encounter (initial coat state score) and after the social defeat stress (final coat state score) using a scale from 0 to 3. A score of 0 represents unhealthy and dirty, while 3 indicates healthy and clean, with intermediate states scored in 0.5-point increments.10

Social avoidance test

To further validate the social defeat stress model, a social avoidance test was conducted in a box (42 × 42 × 42 cm). The test was divided into two stages, each lasting 2.5 minutes. In the first stage, C57BL/6J mice were placed in the corner far from the interaction zone (8-cm-wide) where no ICR mouse was present. The mice were allowed to move freely within the open field, and their activity was recorded (Figure 2A). In the second stage, an unfamiliar ICR mouse was placed in the interaction zone, and the C57BL/6J mouse was positioned as in the first stage. The behavior of the C57BL/6J mice interacting with the ICR mouse was observed. To calculate the interaction ratio, spontaneous motor activity was recorded and analyzed using the spontaneous motor activity recording tracking (SMART) software (Smart Software, Spain), which tracked and analyzed the entire experimental process.

Preparation of samples

After the social avoidance test, whole hearts and brains were extracted through perfusion with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde, then fixed in paraformaldehyde solution for 24 hours. The tissues were dehydrated using a gradient ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. The hearts and brains were sectioned into 10 μm-thick coronal (or sagittal) slices using a cryostat microtome for histological examination. Additional brain and heart tissues (including the amygdala) were stored at -80°C for subsequent Western blotting or ELISA analysis. Serum, obtained by centrifugation at 3000 rpm, was also stored at -80°C until further use.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). H9C2 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After overnight adhesion, the cells were treated as described. Then, 10 μL of MTT (final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 4 hours. After removing the medium, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The blue formazan in living cells was dissolved with 150 μL DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Versa Max, Molecular Devices, USA). Cell morphology was observed with an inverted phase contrast microscope.

Evaluation of cell apoptosis by Hoechst 33342 staining

For Hoechst-33342 staining, H9C2 cells were plated in a 6-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After two days of adhesion, the cells reached 70-80% confluence in OPTI MEM medium. Following treatment as previously described, the cells were stained with Hoechst-33342 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) by adding 2 drops per milliliter of dye and incubating in the dark at 25°C for 25-30 minutes. Images of the H9C2 cells were captured using a fluorescent microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of CORT (Catalog number: EM0946; sensitivity: 1.688 ng/ml; cross-reactivity: exhibits high specificity for CORT with no significant cross-reactivity to structural analogs), 5-HT (Catalog number: EM1465; sensitivity: 0.5 ng/ml; cross-reactivity: exhibits high specificity for 5-HT with no significant cross-reactivity to structural analogs), and DA (Catalog number: EU0392; sensitivity: 0.938 ng/ml; cross-reactivity: exhibits high specificity for DA with no significant cross-reactivity to structural analogs) were measured using ELISA kits (Fine Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Histological studies

The brain and heart sections were dewaxed in xylene and ethanol, then rinsed with distilled water. The brain sections were stained using Nissl’s method, while the heart sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (G1120-3, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or Masson’s trichrome staining (G1006, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Apoptosis was detected using the Fluorescein (FITC) TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (G1501, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Finally, the stained sections were observed under a Nikon motorized microscope (Nikon, Japan) at 100 × or 200 × magnification for histological analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunohistochemistry, heart sections were equilibrated in sodium citrate antigen retrieval solution and washed with PBS. Slides were then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, followed by blocking with normal goat serum for 30 minutes. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: GRP78, p-NF-κB, and Beclin-1, CHOP and NF-κB, NLRP3, LAMP1 and Calnexin. After washing with PBS, secondary antibodies were applied at room temperature for one hour. Finally, the slides were counterstained with DAPI for five minutes, followed by observation using a motorized Nikon microscope (Japan). Images were captured at 200 × magnification, and the relative fluorescence intensity of positive cells was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

For TEM, heart and brain specimens were fixed using a TEM fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4°C overnight. The samples were then rinsed three times with 0.1M phosphate buffer solution. The specimens underwent dehydration, infiltration, and were embedded in Spurr’s resin. Ultrathin sections (60-80 nm) were prepared and post-stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 2.6% lead citrate. The stained sections were examined using an HT7800/HT7700 TEM (Japan Electronics Co., Ltd.) equipped with a charge-coupled device camera. Photographs were taken at magnifications ranging from 8,000 to 20,000.

Western blotting

At low temperatures, mouse heart tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation (10 min at 12,000 rpm) and used for further analysis. Equal protein amounts were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% milk for 1 hour, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: p-PERK, NLRP3, Caspase-1, ASC, IL-1, p-NF-κB, GRP78, BAX, Beclin-1, Bcl-2, TNF-α, and PERK, NF-κB, CHOP, LC3, and P62 while agitated in a shaker. The next day, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. β-actin served as the loading control. Protein expression levels were quantified using Image J software (v 3.0, Microsoft Windows, China).

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 2100 imaging system (Fujifilm, Toronto, CA) equipped with an 18-38 MHz linear array transducer. Mice were anesthetized with 0.5-2% inhaled isoflurane and placed on a heated pad (37°C). A left ventricular (LV) short-axis two-dimensional (2D) image was acquired at the papillary muscle level, followed by M-mode recording under 2D guidance. Using the leading-edge technique on M-mode tracings, heart rate, ejection fraction (EF%), and fractional shortening (FS%) were measured.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation). GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.) and SPSS statistics 23 (IBM) were used for data analysis. Statistical significance was assessed using an independent t-test for comparisons between two groups. For multi-group analyses, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. N indicates the number of mice, cell, and participants, unless stated otherwise. A P-value less than 0.05 represents statistical significance.
