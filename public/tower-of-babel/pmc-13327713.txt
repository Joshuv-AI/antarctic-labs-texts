Prognostic Role of Serum Neuron‐Specific Enolase at Baseline and its Early Dynamics in Metastatic Castration‐Resistant Prostate Cancer Treated With Androgen Receptor Signaling Inhibitors
Authors: Ondřej Fiala, Michaela Tkadlecová, Zuzana Váchalová, Milan Hora, Jan Králíček, Petr Stránský, Václav Liška, Matteo Santoni, Giuseppe Luigi Banna, Kristýna Pivovarčíková, Ondřej Topolčan, Jindřich Fínek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOutcomes of metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) treated with androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARSIs) vary widely and may not be fully reflected by PSA and standard clinicopathologic factors. We evaluated the prognostic value of serum neuron‐specific enolase (NSE) at baseline and early on‐treatment dynamics.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed ARSI‐treated mCRPC patients with available baseline NSE (n = 120). NSE was dichotomized by the upper limit of normal (ULN, 12.5 ng/mL). Radiographic progression‐free survival (rPFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated by Kaplan–Meier methods and compared by log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox models adjusted for age, baseline PSA (per 100 ng/mL), line of therapy (2–4 vs 1), Gleason score (> 7 vs ≤ 7), metastatic timing (synchronous vs metachronous), and visceral metastases (present vs absent). NSE dynamics were evaluated using a 28‐day landmark approach among patients alive and at risk at day 28 with available baseline and week‐4 NSE (rPFS landmark n = 47; OS landmark n = 48); follow‐up was redefined from day 28 and dynamics were modeled as log2(NSE4w/NSE0) (per doubling).ResultsMedian follow‐up was 44.7 months (95% CI 35.7–48.3). Baseline NSE > ULN was associated with shorter rPFS (9.4 vs 20.8 months) and OS (23.5 vs 38.2 months). In multivariable analyses, baseline NSE > ULN remained independently associated with inferior outcomes (rPFS HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.15–2.85, p = 0.011; OS HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.12–3.05, p = 0.017). In landmark models, higher NSE dynamics were independently associated with worse outcomes (rPFS HR 4.30, 95% CI 1.06–17.39, p = 0.041; OS HR 8.78, 95% CI 2.15–35.77, p = 0.002).ConclusionsIn ARSI‐treated mCRPC, baseline NSE above ULN and early 4‐week NSE dynamics provide independent prognostic information for rPFS and OS, supporting NSE as a pragmatic adjunct to clinical risk stratification.Keywords: androgen receptor signaling inhibitors, biomarker, castration‐resistant prostate cancer, neuron‐specific enolase, prognosis

Introduction

Prostate cancer represents a worldwide health concern and is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers in men worldwide [1]. Metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) is a clinically and therapeutically challenging form of the disease, characterized by progression due to acquired resistance to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) at castrate testosterone levels [2, 3]. Despite considerable advancements in novel systemic therapies for mCRPC, patient outcomes remain heterogeneous and reliable prognostic stratification is still needed in routine clinical practice.

Next‐generation androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARSIs), including abiraterone acetate (ABI) and enzalutamide (ENZ), constitute key treatment options in mCRPC and have demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in disease control and survival in randomized phase III trials [4, 5]. However, treatment responses vary substantially, reflecting biological diversity, and de‐novo or acquired drug resistance. In mCRPC, tumors develop reduced dependence on androgen receptor (AR) signaling and acquire features of lineage plasticity, including neuroendocrine differentiation, which has been associated with aggressive clinical behavior and poor outcomes [6, 7]. In this context, circulating biomarkers reflecting non–AR‐driven biology may provide important complementary prognostic information beyond conventional biomarkers mainly represented by prostate‐specific antigen (PSA). Notably, post‐treatment changes in PSA levels have not been shown to have a robust association with survival of mCRPC patients and have not been qualified as an endpoint to support regulatory approval [8, 9, 10]. Neuron‐specific enolase (NSE), a glycolytic enzyme expressed in neuroendocrine cells, is widely used as a serum marker in neuroendocrine malignancies and has been suggested as a blood‐based indicator of neuroendocrine features in prostate cancer, albeit with imperfect specificity [6, 7]. A growing body of evidence suggests that serum NSE is a surrogate prognostic biomarker in prostate cancer [11]. Notably, its role in mCRPC patients treated with ARSIs remains underexplored.

The aim of the present retrospective study was to evaluate the prognostic value of baseline serum NSE and its early dynamics in patients with mCRPC receiving ABI or ENZ.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

Data from mCRPC patients receiving ABI or ENZ were retrospectively analyzed. We assessed the association between outcome of patients and serum NSE at baseline and 4 weeks after treatment initiation. Clinical data were extracted from the hospital information system.

Patients and Treatment

Patients with histologically confirmed mCRPC were treated between 2007 and 2026 at the Department of Oncology and Radiotherapeutics, University Hospital in Pilsen, Czech Republic. ABI (Zytiga, Janssen Pharmaceuticals Co., Beerse, Belgium) was administered orally in the standard approved schedule (1000 mg daily) in combination with prednisone (10 mg daily). ENZ (Xtandi, Astellas Pharma Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was administered orally in the standard approved dose (160 mg daily). The therapy was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient refusal.

Outcome Assessment

The routine clinical checks including physical examination and biochemical laboratory tests were performed every 4 weeks. Radiographic controls using computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography‐CT (PET/CT) or PET‐magnetic resonance (PET/MR) were performed every three to 6 months. The radiographic progression was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [12]. Radiographic progression‐free survival (rPFS) was defined as the time from the initiation of ARSI to the progression confirmed by the aforementioned radiographic assessment or death, whichever occurred first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as time from ARSI initiation to death from any cause. Patients without an event were censored at last follow‐up.

Assessment of Serum NSE and PSA

Peripheral blood was drawn using VACUETTE Z Serum Sep tubes (Greiner Bio‐One, Kremsmünster, Austria) and allowed to clot. Serum was separated within 3 h of blood collection and analyzed. Serum NSE was measured using immunoradiometric titration method (IRMA) (Beckman‐Immunotech, USA), and ACCESS chemiluminescent assay (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, US) was used for the measurement of serum PSA.

Variables and Definitions

Baseline NSE was defined as the serum NSE value obtained prior to ARSI initiation (NSE0) and dichotomized using the upper limit of normal (ULN) 12.5 ng/mL (NSE‐high > 12.5 vs NSE‐low ≤ 12.5). An on‐treatment NSE value at approximately 4 weeks (NSE4w) was recorded, and early NSE dynamics was expressed as the ratio NSE4w/NSE0 (computed only when both values were available and > 0). For regression modeling, this ratio was log2‐transformed, such that hazard ratios (HR) can be interpreted per doubling of the week‐4‐to‐baseline NSE ratio. Covariates considered for multivariable adjustment were age at treatment initiation (years), baseline PSA (per 100 ng/mL), line of therapy (2–4 vs 1), Gleason score (> 7 vs ≤ 7), synchronous vs metachronous metastases, and visceral metastases (present vs absent).

Statistical Analysis

Standard descriptive statistics and frequencies were used to characterize the dataset. Group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier estimates were compared by log‐rank test; median follow‐up was calculated using reverse Kaplan–Meier. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The multivariable Cox model for both rPFS and OS included baseline NSE (ULN‐based high vs low) and age (per 1 year), PSA (per 100 ng/mL), line of therapy (2–4 vs 1), Gleason score (> 7 vs ≤ 7), synchronous metastases, and visceral metastases. To evaluate NSE dynamics while avoiding immortal time bias, a 28‐day landmark analysis was performed among patients with available baseline and ~4‐week NSE. Time‐to‐event was redefined from day 28, and the landmark multivariable Cox model additionally included NSE dynamics as the week‐4‐to‐baseline NSE ratio (NSE4w/NSE0), modeled on the log2 scale to yield hazard ratios per doubling of the ratio. For visualization of NSE dynamics, unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted using a median split of NSE4w/NSE0 ratio and compared by log‐rank test; and adjusted survival curves were derived from the landmark multivariable Cox model using the same median split, holding other covariates at typical values (median/mode) and displaying 95% CIs for adjusted estimates. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in Python (v3.11.2; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA), with pandas (v2.2.3), statsmodels (v0.14.3), and Matplotlib (v3.7.5).

Baseline Serum NSE and Survival Outcomes

Patients with baseline NSE above the ULN (12.5 ng/mL) experienced significantly worse outcomes than those with NSE ≤ 12.5 ng/mL. Median rPFS was 20.8 months (95% CI 12.7–28.4) in the NSE ≤ 12.5 group vs 9.4 months (95% CI 5.6–17.2) in the NSE > 12.5 group (log‐rank p = 0.009; Figure 1). Median OS was 38.2 months (95% CI 30.1–50.7) vs 23.5 months (95% CI 18.6–27.9), respectively (log‐rank p= 0.003; Figure 1). In the multivariable Cox regression model baseline NSE above ULN remained an independent predictor of both rPFS (HR 1.551, p = 0.041) and OS (HR 1.672, p = 0.031) (Table 2).

Early Serum NSE Dynamics and Survival Outcomes

In a 28‐day landmark analysis including patients with available baseline and 4‐week serum NSE values (rPFS landmark n = 47; OS landmark n = 48), a higher week‐4‐to‐baseline NSE ratio (NSE4w/NSE0) was independently associated with inferior outcomes (modeled as log2(NSE4w/NSE0), yielding hazard ratios per doubling of the ratio: rPFS HR 4.30, 95% CI 1.06–17.39, p = 0.041; OS HR 8.78, 95% CI 2.15–35.77, p = 0.002; Table 3). For visual presentation, survival from the 4‐week landmark is shown using unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves based on a median split of the NSE ratio and compared by log‐rank test (Figure 2A,B), and adjusted survival curves derived from the landmark multivariable Cox model using the same median split, with 95% confidence intervals and the Wald p‐value for the NSE‐dynamics term (Figure 2C,D). Because follow‐up in the landmark analysis begins at week 4 and includes only patients alive and at risk at the landmark with available 4‐week NSE, effect estimates from landmark models are not directly comparable to those from baseline models.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with mCRPC treated with ARSIs, baseline serum NSE above the ULN (12.5 ng/mL) was associated with inferior rPFS and OS and remained independently associated with outcomes after adjustment for established clinical factors, including baseline PSA. Moreover, early on‐treatment NSE dynamics provided additional prognostic information: in a 28‐day landmark analysis, a higher week‐4‐to‐baseline NSE ratio (NSE4w/NSE0) was independently associated with both shorter rPFS and OS. Collectively, these findings support the concept that serum NSE may capture clinically relevant aspects of tumor biology that are not fully reflected by PSA and traditional clinicopathological variables.

A plausible explanation is that elevated or rising NSE reflects neuroendocrine differentiation or a shift toward an AR‐indifferent phenotype that may emerge under therapeutic pressure on the AR axis [13, 14, 15]. Such states have been linked to aggressive clinical behavior and reduced sensitivity to AR‐directed treatment strategies, which would be consistent with the adverse outcomes observed in patients with high serum NSE. Neuroendocrine differentiation, as assessed in tumor tissue and suggested by circulating biomarkers, has been linked to prostate cancer prognosis. However, the present study does not directly examine neuroendocrine differentiation at the tissue level. Instead, it uses NSE as an easily accessible serum biomarker that may correlate with these biological processes. Previous studies investigating the prognostic relevance of serum NSE in metastatic prostate cancer suggested association between elevated pretreatment circulating neuroendocrine biomarkers and inferior survival outcomes in patients treated with ADT [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In addition, NSE has been evaluated alongside other circulating neuroendocrine biomarkers such as chromogranin A (CgA) across disease stages, including cohorts with mCRPC [20, 21, 22, 23]. In a retrospective study of 138 CRPC patients conducted by Hvamstad, elevated serum NSE and CgA were common among CRPC patients and provided prognostic information [20]. The available evidence on serum NSE specifically in mCRPC patients receiving ARSIs remains very limited and heterogeneous, with most prior studies being modest in size and often focusing on baseline measurements. A retrospective study by Heck et al focused on baseline serum NSE and CgA in a cohort of 45 mCRPC patients treated with ABI stratified in to low‐, intermediate‐ or high‐risk groups according to elevation of none, one, or both neuroendocrine serum biomarkers, respectively. They observed that the risk groups correlated with decreasing median OS (not reached vs 15.3 vs 6.6 months; p < 0.001) and decreasing median clinical or radiographic PFS (8.3 vs 4.4 vs 2.7 months; p = 0.001) [21]. Fan et al. performed a retrospective study with a similar design, including 40 mCRPC patients. They assessed baseline levels of and variations in serum NSE and CgA during treatment with ABI and found that elevated baseline CgA and NSE, as well as elevation during the first 3 months of treatment, were independent prognostic factors for OS (baseline: HR 13.71 (95% CI 1.03–183.31), p = 0.048; dynamics: HR 12.89 (95% CI 1.09–152.69), p = 0.043) and rPFS (baseline: HR 1.02 (95% CI 1.00–1.04), p = 0.041; dynamics: HR 12.89 (95% CI 1.44–24.99), p = 0.014) [22]. Notably, these studies were modest in size, used heterogeneous thresholds, and evaluated dynamics at later timepoints (e.g., 3 months), underscoring the need for additional data aligned with early routine follow‐up. Szarvas et al. reported the largest ABI/ENZ‐treated mCRPC cohort to date (n = 143) with systematic measurement of serum CgA and NSE. In their ABI/ENZ cohort, baseline NSE showed prognostic signal in univariable analyses (median split: HR 1.580, p = 0.030), and stronger associations were observed using higher thresholds (e.g., the previously published BJU cut‐off for NSE > 19 ng/mL: HR 3.457, p < 0.001) and serum biomarker combinations (≥ 1 elevated marker: HR 2.316, p = 0.001; both elevated: HR 4.216, p < 0.001). In multivariable analyses, however, the independent association was more consistent for CgA (ROC‐derived CgA > 81.29 ng/mL: HR 1.926, p = 0.047), while NSE effects attenuated (ROC‐derived NSE > 10.22 ng/mL: HR 1.239, p = 0.431; BJU cut‐off for NSE > 19 ng/mL: HR 1.984, p = 0.099). On‐treatment marker dynamics at 3 months were also more informative for CgA (> 50% increase associated with worse OS; p = 0.040), whereas NSE changes showed only a trend among patients with elevated baseline NSE (p = 0.061) [23]. However, serum CgA was not systematically available in the present cohort and therefore was not included in the current analyses. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate combined neuroendocrine biomarker models or directly compare the relative prognostic contribution of NSE and CgA. The present study was intentionally focused on NSE as a pragmatic and routinely accessible biomarker evaluated at baseline and during early on‐treatment follow‐up.

Taken together, these findings highlight both the potential and the remaining uncertainty regarding serum NSE in ARSI‐treated mCRPC, supporting the value of additional studies evaluating NSE using clinically grounded thresholds and early on‐treatment timepoints. In this context, our study adds incremental evidence by evaluating NSE both at baseline and as an early on‐treatment dynamic biomarker within a landmark framework, while accounting for standard clinical covariates including baseline PSA. Methodologically, the 28‐day landmark design used in the present study mitigates immortal time bias by restricting the dynamics analysis to patients alive and at risk at day 28 with available 4‐week NSE, thereby improving interpretability of post‐baseline biomarker changes [24, 25]. If validated, baseline and early NSE dynamics could help clinicians identify ARSI‐treated mCRPC patients at higher risk of early progression and death. This could prompt closer monitoring, earlier imaging, consideration of alternative systemic approaches, or earlier biopsy in cases suspicious for treatment‐related neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t‐NEPC) and/or aggressive variant prostate cancer (AVPC), especially when PSA kinetics appear discordant with clinical or radiographic behavior [14, 15]. However, NSE should not be interpreted in isolation: hemolysis and non‐malignant conditions can influence NSE levels, and neuroendocrine differentiation is heterogeneous and not always captured by a single circulating biomarker [11]. For clinical interpretation, NSE dynamics can be read directly as the week‐4‐to‐baseline ratio (NSE4w/NSE0), whereas the Cox model expresses this ratio on the log2 scale to yield hazard ratios per doubling. An important consideration is the evolving treatment landscape of advanced prostate cancer. In the contemporary upfront‐ARSI era, many patients receive ABI or ENZ already in the metastatic castration‐sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC) setting, potentially influencing subsequent tumor biology, AR dependence, and emergence of neuroendocrine features at the mCRPC stage. Therefore, whether baseline NSE and early NSE dynamics retain similar prognostic value in ARSI‐treated mCSPC or in patients previously exposed to ARSIs during the mCSPC phase remains uncertain and warrants prospective validation. In this context, prior ARSI exposure may represent a clinically relevant variable beyond treatment line alone in future biomarker‐oriented studies.

The present study has several strengths and limitations. Key strengths include the clinically relevant population of ARSI‐treated mCRPC, the evaluation of both baseline neuroendocrine differentiation (NSE) and early on‐treatment change, and multivariable adjustment for major prognostic covariates. The limitations include the retrospective design, the relatively modest single‐center sample size (particularly for the landmark cohort, this might limit the generalizability of the dynamic analysis, given that NSE levels can be influenced by non‐malignant conditions), potential residual confounding factors, and the lack of systematic tissue confirmation of neuroendocrine differentiation and histological correlation at disease progression with confirmation of AVPCs. In addition, the absence of systematic CgA assessment prevented evaluation of combined neuroendocrine biomarker approaches described in previous studies.

Conclusions

The present retrospective study findings suggest that baseline NSE and early NSE dynamics are associated with outcomes in ARSI‐treated mCRPC, supporting NSE as a pragmatic adjunct for risk stratification pending prospective validation and integration with markers of lineage plasticity.
