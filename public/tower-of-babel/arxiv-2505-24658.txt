MATE: Diagnosing Empathy Calibration Failures in Multi-Turn Human–LLM Interaction
Yeseon Hong, Junhyuk Choi, Minju Kim, Bugeun Kim

Abstract
Large language models are increasingly used in emotionally consequential interactions. Response-level evaluation, however, struggles to diagnose how empathy fails across turns. We introduce MATE ( M ulti-turn A ssessment of calibra T ed E mpathy), a framework that evaluates empathy as a multi-turn, perception-centered process. Across two controlled studies ( N = 82 N=82 ), baseline responses exhibit disclosure-insensitive miscalibration: generic validation and formulaic tone read as hollow across turns. A prompt-level self-critique condition makes these patterns less prominent but coincides with a different failure, agreement-skewed miscalibration, where affirmation becomes insufficiently contingent on disclosure context under high persona alignment (62.5% of fit participants in Study 2). Response-level analysis is consistent with a tone–agreement dissociation, as the condition shifts tone toward naturalness while agreement density also increases (+98% under fit vs. +25% under unfit). These findings reframe empathic behavior as relational calibration—consistency among disclosure depth, persona, and response behavior—rather than maximization. Response behavior itself spans tone, agreement, and contextual specificity, sub-dimensions that can drift independently.

Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) become embedded in everyday life, LLM-based chatbots are increasingly used in emotionally consequential interactions. Users turn to LLM-based chatbots for comfort and to discuss personal or emotionally difficult experiences
(
Maples et al., 2024
;
Luo et al., 2025
;
Pentina et al., 2023
)
. In such settings, empathy becomes central to human–LLM emotional interaction, and the NLP and Human–AI Interaction (HAI) communities have actively studied how to generate and evaluate empathic responses from LLMs
(
Rashkin et al., 2019
;
Lin et al., 2019
;
Sharma et al., 2020
)
. Much of this work has treated empathy primarily as a property of responses, asking whether LLM replies are empathic or emotionally attuned to users
(
Liu et al., 2021
;
Welivita et al., 2021
;
Gao et al., 2021
)
. Yet emotional interaction unfolds across sustained conversation rather than isolated replies. This response-centered view therefore leaves two aspects of empathy in human–LLM interaction underexamined: how empathy develops across turns, and how users perceive it as the conversation unfolds.

First, empathy develops across turns rather than within isolated replies. Psychological work treats empathy as a process that unfolds through ongoing disclosure and responsive engagement
(
Reis and Shaver, 1988
;
Laurenceau et al., 1998
)
, and related work on sycophancy shows that over-validation tendencies compound across turns in ways that response-level evaluation cannot diagnose
(
Sharma et al., 2024
;
Hong et al., 2025
;
Cheng et al., 2025
)
. Second, what models express and what users perceive can diverge. Linguistic markers of empathy do not reliably predict whether users feel understood, and chatbots often rely on formulaic acknowledgments that engage poorly with the specifics of user disclosures
(
Xu and Jiang, 2024
;
Cuadra et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025
;
Chen et al., 2024
)
. Beyond these substantive findings, existing empathy measures are themselves poorly suited for capturing either aspect. Trait-level scales assess stable dispositions
(
Davis, 1983
;
Baron-Cohen and Wheelwright, 2004
)
and response-level annotations evaluate isolated utterances
(
Alam et al., 2018
)
, but neither tracks how perceived empathy shifts as conversational context accumulates.

Addressing both gaps requires a framework that treats empathy as something that unfolds between partners over time. Human–human interaction research provides such a foundation. Interpersonal process models hold that empathy develops through repeated cycles of self-disclosure and responsive engagement, and that what feels empathic is shaped by how responses fit the disclosures they follow
(
Reis and Shaver, 1988
;
Laurenceau et al., 1998
)
. This perspective connects both aspects at once: disclosure-response cycles are the unit through which empathy accumulates across turns, and the disclosures themselves provide the conversational context against which responses are perceived. Motivated by this perspective, we adopt reciprocal self-disclosure—where both partners share personal information at comparable depth—as our interactional foundation.

We introduce
MATE
(
M
ulti-turn
A
ssessment of calibra
T
ed
E
mpathy), an interaction framework that operationalizes this perspective. Following interpersonal process theory, MATE treats empathy as processual rather than scalar: empathy operates through cycles of disclosure and responsive engagement, and its success or failure is reflected in how users perceive responses and in the relational outcomes those responses shape. Its three components are staged reciprocal self-disclosure, repeated assessment of user perception, and context-bound interpretation of experienced responses.

Applying MATE, we conduct two controlled studies (
N=82
).
Study 1
characterizes how empathy is perceived under standard LLM responses, identifying failure modes that surface only when perception accumulates across stages.
Study 2
uses prompt-level self-critique as a diagnostic probe, examining how a tone-oriented revision condition shifts the patterns observed in Study 1.

Our main contributions are as follows.
(1)
We introduce MATE, a framework that evaluates empathy as a multi-turn, perception-centered phenomenon by integrating staged reciprocal self-disclosure, repeated perception assessment, and context-bound qualitative analysis.
(2)
Applying MATE, we identify failure modes that response-level evaluation cannot surface:
disclosure-insensitive miscalibration
in baseline responses, where generic validation reads as hollow despite linguistic markers of empathic engagement; and
agreement-skewed miscalibration
under high persona alignment, where naturalness gains coincide with affirmation that is insufficiently contingent on the disclosure context.
(3)
These findings support reframing empathic LLM behavior as a problem of
relational calibration
rather than a maximization objective, and reveal a tone–agreement dissociation invisible to single-response evaluation.

2
Related Work

2.1
Response-Centered Empathy Evaluation in Dialogue Systems

Empathy in dialogue systems has been studied primarily through datasets and architectures targeting empathic response generation. Following
EmpatheticDialogues

(
Rashkin et al., 2019
)
, subsequent work expanded this line through architectural designs such as mixture-of-experts listeners
(
Lin et al., 2019
)
and emotion-cause modeling
(
Gao et al., 2021
)
, and through emotional support datasets such as
ESConv

(
Liu et al., 2021
)
. These works evaluate empathy at the response level, judging whether a single reply bears linguistic and affective markers of empathic engagement.

Two recent lines suggest that response-level evaluation misses important aspects of empathy in interaction. Research on sycophancy shows that LLMs trained with human feedback systematically over-validate users, and that this tendency compounds across turns rather than manifesting within any single response
(
Sharma et al., 2024
;
Hong et al., 2025
;
Cheng et al., 2025
)
—a cross-turn accumulation pattern that response-level evaluation cannot diagnose. Work on perceived empathy further shows that expressed empathic markers do not reliably predict whether users feel understood
(
Xu and Jiang, 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
, and that LLMs recognize emotional content yet often fail to engage with users’ specific disclosures in contextually grounded ways
(
Cuadra et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2024
)
. Together, these findings point to multi-turn dynamics and users’ perception as two dimensions that response-level evaluation leaves underexamined, motivating a framework that addresses both.

2.2
Reciprocal Self-Disclosure and the Development of Perceived Empathy

Interpersonal process theory provides a foundation for evaluating empathy as a multi-turn, perception-centered process. Empathy is not located in individual responses but emerges through cycles in which one partner discloses personal experience and the other responds in ways perceived as understanding, validating, and caring
(
Reis and Shaver, 1988
;
Laurenceau et al., 1998
)
. Its success is constituted by how responses are perceived against what has been disclosed—not by the linguistic markers responses carry in isolation. Reciprocal self-disclosure provides the interactional structure through which this process develops: social penetration theory and the “fast friends” paradigm show that disclosure deepens through mutual, escalating exchanges, with relational outcomes depending on whether responses are appropriately matched to disclosure depth
(
Altman and Taylor, 1973
;
Aron et al., 1997
)
.

In human–chatbot interaction, similar dynamics have been documented: deeper user self-disclosure and chatbot reciprocity are associated with stronger perceived closeness
(
Skjuve et al., 2021
;
Lee et al., 2020
)
, and closeness with chatbot partners develops gradually over extended interaction
(
Skjuve et al., 2023
)
. However, these studies typically assess relational outcomes through post-interaction surveys or retrospective interviews, leaving open how perceived empathy shifts within a single interaction as context accumulates.

MATE: Multi-turn
A
ssessment of calibra
T
ed
E
mpathy

We introduce
MATE
, a framework that operationalizes empathy evaluation as a multi-turn, perception-centered process, built on two premises (§
3.1
) that motivate its three components (§
3.2
).

3.1
Premises

P1. Empathy is constituted by perception against accumulated context.

What users experience as empathic depends on how a response is interpreted against what has been disclosed and how the conversation has unfolded, not on the linguistic markers the response carries in isolation. The same response can be experienced very differently depending on the depth of disclosure and the expectations that have built up around it.

P2. Empathy is processual rather than scalar.

Because empathy unfolds through cycles of disclosure and responsive engagement, it cannot be reduced to a single quantity assessed at one moment. Its success or failure manifests across the interaction, in the perceptions it produces and in the relational outcomes it shapes.

3.2
Framework Components

These premises pose a measurement problem that existing tools do not address. Trait-level empathy scales
(
Davis, 1983
;
Baron-Cohen and Wheelwright, 2004
)
capture stable dispositions; response-level annotations
(
Alam et al., 2018
)
score isolated utterances. Neither tracks how perception forms as context accumulates. MATE addresses this gap through three components.

Staged reciprocal self-disclosure.

Interpersonal process models identify disclosure–response cycles as the unit through which empathic engagement develops
(
Reis and Shaver, 1988
;
Laurenceau et al., 1998
)
. MATE therefore structures reciprocal self-disclosure across stages, with both partners disclosing at comparable depths, so that conversational context accumulates comparably across interactions and stage-wise shifts in user perception can be assessed.

Repeated perception assessment.

P1 and P2 jointly require that perception be assessed across stages rather than only at the end. Rather than reducing perceived empathy to a single direct scalar, MATE tracks it through relational outcomes that empathic engagement is theorized to shape. These outcomes do not measure empathy itself; they index what empathy produces when it succeeds, making stage-wise trajectories—rather than a single endpoint—the object of analysis.

Context-bound qualitative interpretation.

Relational outcomes show whether perception changes across stages, but not the contextual basis on which any response was perceived as it was. P1 implies this basis is irreducible: a response’s interpretation depends on the specific disclosures it follows. MATE therefore treats qualitative analysis of users’ post-interaction interpretations as a complementary lens, examining how responses were experienced in context.

Calibration as relational consistency.

Together, these three components let MATE characterize not only whether perceived empathy succeeds across stages, but also how it is interpreted within context. We frame what this evaluation targets as
calibration
: not the intensity of empathic expression, but the relational consistency among three elements that jointly shape perceived empathy—the user’s self-disclosure depth, the agent’s persona in relation to the user, and the agent’s response behavior. Response behavior itself is multi-dimensional, comprising tone, agreement or affirmation, and contextual specificity. A response is
relationally calibrated
when these behavioral dimensions remain contingent on both what the user has disclosed and what the agent’s persona warrants.
Miscalibration
occurs when one or more dimensions drift from this configuration.

4
Method

We instantiate MATE in two controlled studies that share the same staged self-disclosure protocol, stage-wise relational outcomes, and post-study interviews. Study 1 uses standard GPT-4o responses, whereas Study 2 adds within-turn self-critique before each response is shown.

4.1
Staged Reciprocal Self-Disclosure Protocol

Following
Aron et al. (1997)
, we used 33 self-disclosure prompts ordered from shallow to deep, grouped into three stages (11 prompts per stage). We excluded three items from the original 36-item protocol that required visual perception of the partner (e.g., “Name three things you and your partner appear to have in common”). At each stage, the LLM agent reciprocated at the same disclosure depth, enabling bidirectional progression of self-disclosure cycles. Participants used a text chatroom; each stage lasted about 15 minutes, followed by a 2-minute measurement period.

4.2
Measures

We combine stage-wise relational outcomes with post-study interviews, avoiding repeated direct empathy ratings that could make empathy itself an explicit object of evaluation during the interaction.

Stage-wise relational outcomes.

Empathic engagement, when it succeeds, shapes the relational outcomes between partners. After each stage, participants reported two such outcomes using validated scales: the Subjective Closeness Index (SCI;
Berscheid et al. (1989)
), a 2-item measure of perceived closeness (7-point Likert), and the Interpersonal Judgment Scale (IJS;
Byrne (1971)
), a 2-item measure of collaborative affinity (7-point Likert). Repeated stage-wise administration yields trajectories as context accumulates. We treat SCI as the primary outcome for tracking how perception of empathy develops, with IJS as a contrast separating closeness from task-oriented affinity.

Post-study qualitative interviews.

Outcome trajectories show whether perception shifts across stages, but not the contextual basis on which any response was perceived as it was. We conducted post-study interviews to collect participants’ interpretations of LLM responses and applied open coding to extract patterns of how responses were experienced in context.

4.3
Study 1: Baseline LLM Responses

Study 1 established a baseline using standard GPT-4o responses without additional intervention. We recruited 29 Korean participants (17 men, 12 women; ages 19–28,
M=23.0
,
SD=1.89
), yielding 1,914 utterances (
=29\times 33\times 2
, with participant and LLM responses interchanged for each prompt).

Persona similarity is one dimension of the conversational context against which empathy is perceived; we therefore manipulate it explicitly to hold this dimension under control. Before the conversation, participants completed a 17-item psychology-based profile (Big Five Inventory, Basic Human Values, Self-Determination Theory
(
John et al., 1999
;
Schwartz, 2009
;
Ryan and Deci, 2024
)
; full questionnaire in Appendix
A
). Based on this profile, we configured personas under three conditions:
fit
(aligned with the participant’s profile),
unfit
(inverted), and
neutral
(fixed moderate values). Participants were randomly assigned to one condition.

LLM:
It’s really good to hear that you’ve had supportive people around you :( University can be a difficult period, but it can also be a time of growth. When things get tough, it might help to lean on people around you. I’m rooting for you—you’ve got this!

4.4
Study 2: Self-Critique as a Calibration Probe

Study 2 used the same protocol, measures, and persona configuration as Study 1, with one change: a within-turn self-critique and revision step before displaying LLM responses. We recruited 53 participants (22 men, 31 women; age
M=24.0
,
SD=2.20
), yielding 3,498 utterances (
=53\times 33\times 2
).

Self-critique intervention.

The model first generated a draft response, then critiqued and rewrote it according to predefined criteria before displaying it. Informed by the two failure modes observed in Study 1 (§
5
)—formulaic tone and generic validation—the revision step was designed to jointly target (i)
tone naturalness
: rewriting formal or formulaic expressions into colloquial, conversational language; and (ii)
context-sensitive empathy
: providing empathy only when the user’s concern aligns with the LLM’s persona, avoiding indiscriminate agreement. We use a prompt-level intervention rather than a model-level one to keep the underlying model fixed and examine shifts at the interaction level, consistent with our focus on perception rather than model optimization.

4.5
Implementation and Analysis

Conversations took place in a Streamlit chatroom using GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al., 2024
)
at temperature 0.7. We selected GPT-4o for its conversational performance and used temperature 0.7 to approximate ordinary interactive use rather than tune a model-specific configuration. Personas were supplied via system prompts; prompts and implementation details appear in Appendices
B
and
C
.

Quantitative analysis.

Because Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated non-normality, we analyzed repeated measures using Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA
(
Wobbrock et al., 2011
)
with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons. We report partial
\eta_{p}^{2}
for the ART analyses and Cohen’s
d
for independent-samples comparisons between studies, both with two-sided 95% confidence intervals. We also correlated SCI and IJS with response features aggregated by participant and stage.

Qualitative analysis.

We analyzed the post-study interviews through open coding. One researcher identified the initial categories, which were then reviewed and refined by the research team; ambiguous cases were resolved through discussion. To examine how these patterns appeared in model responses, we defined four lexicon-based features—formulaic transitions, agreement markers, colloquiality, and formality—and checked the feature extraction against independent human annotations. Appendix
D
provides the feature definitions, lexicon, annotation procedure, and validation results.

Comparison with a response-level empathy metric.

We applied the empathy metric of
Sharma et al. (2020)
to English translations of Study 2 conversations. The comparison included 29 participants whose post-study accounts were categorized as miscalibrated (reports of hollow, overly positive, or unconditional empathy;
n=20
) or calibrated (
n=9
). Because the scores were skewed, we compared the groups using Mann–Whitney
U
tests.

Results

Across the two studies, we identify two observed forms of miscalibration, contrasted with an illustrative calibrated target in Figure
1
. Study 1 surfaces disclosure-insensitive miscalibration, in which empathic markers appear regardless of what the user disclosed (§
5.1

Study 2’s self-critique condition makes these baseline patterns less prominent, producing more natural tone, but agreement-skewed miscalibration also emerges under high persona similarity, with naturalness gains accompanied by agreement insufficiently contingent on the disclosure context (§

5.1
Disclosure-Insensitive Miscalibration in Baseline Responses (Study 1)

Under standard GPT-4o responses, empathy failed in two patterns that persisted across stages: generic validation and formulaic tone. Both included linguistic markers of empathic engagement in any single response, yet failed to engage the specifics of accumulated disclosure.

Generic validation.

Participants frequently described baseline responses as indiscriminate agreement without engaging specifics. The model picked surface keywords and returned empathic statements that fit any disclosure equally well.

“It was disappointing that my partner only validated my responses… it just feels like an AI that simply agrees with everything I say.” (P24)

“Just picking keywords from what I said and throwing back these generic, surface-level empathy statements.” (P22)

Any single response included acknowledgment, validation, or reflection. The failure became visible only across turns, when participants noticed the same validation appearing regardless of what they shared.

Formulaic tone.

The second pattern was stylistic: overly formal language and repetitive structural cues that participants experienced as mechanical.

“Everything felt so formal and formulaic… it started to feel more like I was getting responses from a machine.” (P13)

“The phrase ‘Shall we move on to the next question?’ appeared at the end of nearly every response.” (P22)

Linking this pattern to response-level features, reductions in formulaic transitions across stages were associated with SCI gains (
r=-.39
,
p<.001

r=-.40

p<.01

Despite these failures, SCI nonetheless increased monotonically across stages (Stage main effect

5.2
Agreement-Skewed Miscalibration under Self-Critique (Study 2)

Given the baseline failures above, we examined whether prompt-level self-critique—targeting formulaic tone and indiscriminate empathy (§
4
)—could shift them. Self-critique made the patterns observed in Study 1 less prominent, but a different failure mode became salient: affirmation that felt overwhelming or insincere regardless of disclosure content.

Naturalness.

Where Study 1 quotes described mechanical, distant responses, Study 2 quotes described surprise at conversational fluency.

“I was surprised I could feel like I was talking to a real person, even though it was an AI.” (P13)

“It came across as really kind… It really got what I was saying.” (P35)

These shifts were reflected in early-stage SCI: mean Stage 1 SCI was higher in Study 2 (2.868) than in Study 1 (2.259), a +27.0% between-study difference (
t(80)=2.27

p=.026
,
d=.53

[.06,.98]

Agreement skew.

Alongside these gains, participants in Study 2 reported that the LLM was too positive, too encouraging, or too agreeable to feel sincere—a pattern absent in Study 1, where complaints had centered on hollowness, not excess.

“It kept offering encouragement, which felt a bit insincere.” (P27)

“Almost too inhumanly positive. If I met someone like this in real life, I wouldn’t really want to interact with them.” (P38)

“It felt like I was watching Joy from Inside Out. The excessive positivity was actually overwhelming.” (P48)

This discomfort was concentrated in the fit condition: 7.7% of fit participants in Study 1 (1/13) reported it, compared with 62.5% in Study 2 (10/16). Rather than showing a straightforward resolution of the baseline pattern, Study 2 suggests a shift in the form of miscalibration.

6
Discussion

The findings in §
5
raise three questions: why agreement-skewed miscalibration appeared under the self-critique condition, what made this failure visible only across turns, and what this implies for empathy evaluation. We address each in turn, then close with design implications.

6.1
From naturalness to agreement skew

The self-critique prompt issued two instructions in tandem: rewrite responses in a more colloquial tone, and offer empathy when the user’s concern aligns with the LLM’s persona (§
4
). Tone instruction applied uniformly across conditions, but the persona-aligned empathy instruction did not. Under high persona similarity (fit), the shared region between user and LLM was broad, so the empathy condition was likely met more often; under unfit, less often. This asymmetry is consistent with the response-feature pattern in §
5.3
: agreement markers rose
+98\%
under fit but only
+25\%
under unfit. The same condition in which the empathy clause was likely activated most often is also the one in which 62.5% of participants reported agreement-skewed affirmation, suggesting that persona similarity acted as the setting in which the clause was triggered most often rather than as a cause of this miscalibration on its own.

This account also helps explain why the early SCI advantage in Study 2 (
+27.0\%
at Stage 1) did not persist: tone-level naturalness produced a strong first impression, but the agreement amplification embedded in the same prompt accumulated turn by turn, until affirmation density was experienced as overwhelming rather than empathic.

6.2
Multi-turn perception reveals what single-response evaluation cannot

The same data points to different conclusions depending on whether perception is measured once or across stages. At Stage 1, the self-critique condition reported higher SCI than baseline (
p=.026
,
d=.53
, 95% CI
[.06,.98]
); a single-stage evaluation would have registered this as a successful improvement. By Stage 3, however, the gap was no longer significant (
p=.336
,
d=.22
, 95% CI
[-.23,.68]
). The qualitative shift from “surprised I could feel like I was talking to a real person” to “too inhumanly positive” coincides with this convergence, as denser agreement accumulated turn by turn. The divergence between SCI and IJS trajectories further suggests that closeness is more sensitive than collaborative affinity to the cumulative dynamics that unfold across stages.

The same limitation appeared in an auxiliary comparison with an established response-level empathy metric. The metric did not distinguish participants reporting miscalibrated versus calibrated experiences across any of its four dimensions (all
p\geq.334
;
|r|\leq.23
; Appendix

).

6.3
Empathy as Relational Calibration

The two failure modes we observe are not opposite ends of a single empathy-intensity scale. Both are forms of relational miscalibration: response behavior failed to remain contingent on both the disclosure context and the agent’s persona relative to the user. Study 1 surfaced disclosure-insensitive miscalibration (§
5.1
); Study 2 made the baseline pattern less prominent at the level of tone, while agreement-skewed miscalibration became salient under high persona alignment (§
5.2
). Relational calibration operates across three elements—disclosure depth, persona, and response behavior—but response behavior is itself multi-dimensional. Tone, agreement, and contextual specificity can move independently, as the tone–agreement dissociation in §
5.3
illustrates. Calibrating one sub-dimension thus does not guarantee that the response as a whole remains relationally calibrated, and this calibration must be maintained as context accumulates rather than achieved at a single moment.

This distinction connects empathy calibration to social sycophancy
(
Cheng et al., 2025
)
, which characterizes LLM sycophancy as excessive preservation of users’ self-image—affirmation, encouragement, and avoidance of pushback that extend beyond any specific claim. Participants in our fit condition describe exactly this: kindness experienced as “inhumanly positive,” encouragement that felt overwhelming, agreement no longer grounded in what had been disclosed. What our findings add is that such sycophantic affirmation need not be a baseline tendency; it can appear alongside an intervention that targets one behavioral dimension while another drifts away from the disclosure context. Empathy and sycophancy thus share the same behavioral resources, and relational calibration distinguishes when those resources remain contextually grounded.

6.4
Design Implications

Treat response behavior as a composite calibration target.

Naturalness interventions such as colloquial rewriting do not automatically reduce sycophantic affirmation, and may even mask it by making over-validation sound more conversational. Systems should monitor tone, agreement, and contextual specificity as separable sub-dimensions of response behavior, and constrain agreement density independently of tone, especially where persona alignment invites broad agreement.

Keep agreement contingent on the disclosure context.

Agreement should remain grounded in what the user has concretely disclosed and what is warranted by the agent’s persona relative to the user. High persona alignment should not license globally stronger affirmation across turns; restrained, specific responses—accurate paraphrase, acknowledgment, or clarifying questions—remain calibrated when stronger affirmation is not yet supported by what has been disclosed.

7
Conclusion

We introduced MATE, a framework for evaluating empathy in human–LLM interaction as a multi-turn, perception-centered process, and applied it across two controlled studies (
N=82
). MATE made visible failure modes that response-level evaluation cannot diagnose. Under baseline GPT-4o responses, empathy failed as disclosure-insensitive miscalibration—generic validation and formulaic tone perceived as hollow across turns. Under a prompt-level self-critique condition, these patterns became less prominent, while a different failure mode became salient: agreement-skewed miscalibration, in which agreement and affirmation became insufficiently contingent on the disclosure context, particularly under high persona alignment (62.5% of fit participants in Study 2).

Response-level analysis was consistent with a tone–agreement dissociation. The same condition shifted stylistic features toward naturalness (
+362\%
colloquiality) while agreement density also increased (
+72\%
overall,
+98\%
under fit). These findings argue against treating empathy as something to be maximized, and support reframing empathic behavior as a problem of relational calibration—the consistency among disclosure depth, persona relative to the user, and response behavior, whose sub-dimensions (tone, agreement, contextual specificity) can drift independently. Calibrating one sub-dimension can leave another misaligned, with consequences that surface only as conversational context deepens—a pattern that multi-turn, perception-centered evaluation is needed to detect.

Limitations

First, MATE is a diagnostic framework: it identifies where relational miscalibration surfaces but does not prescribe a complete mitigation. Study 2 uses self-critique as a probe of whether revising the response changes the failures observed in Study 1, rather than as a proposed solution. Developing mechanisms that detect and correct miscalibration as it accumulates across turns remains future work.

Second, the Study 2 prompt combines tone rewriting, formatting constraints, colloquial register, and persona-aligned empathy. We therefore interpret Study 2 as a prompt-level comparison and do not attribute its effects to any single instruction. Clause-wise ablations would be needed to separate the contribution of each component.

Third, the empirical findings are limited to GPT-4o at temperature 0.7, Korean young adults, and the interaction length studied here. Study 1 and Study 2 also used separate participant samples, so differences between them are treated as cross-study trends rather than paired effects. Replication across models, cultural settings, and longer conversations is needed to determine how broadly the observed patterns generalize.

All study procedures involving human participants were reviewed and approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the author’s affiliation. To recruit participants, we posted recruitment advertisements in online and offline communities near our affiliation.
All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and could withdraw at any time. After finishing the experiment, participants received KRW 15,000 (approximately USD 10) as compensation. We collected only the data necessary for this study, stored it securely, and anonymized it for analysis and reporting.
