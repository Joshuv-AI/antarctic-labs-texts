Functional coupling between MC4R and Kir7.1 contributes to clozapine-induced weight gain in female mice
Authors: Li Li, Ciria C Hernandez, Luis E Gimenez, Baijie Xu, Naima S Dahir, Alys Peisley, Zan Wu, Meilin Chen, Alexandra Serrato, Rong Wan, Swati, Shari G Birnbaum, Roger D Cone, Chen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost antipsychotic drugs (APDs) cause hyperphagia and weight gain, yet the neural mechanisms underlying these metabolic side effects remain elusive, in part due to difficulties in modeling them in rodents. Here, we establish a mouse model that recapitulates clozapine-induced metabolic syndrome, enabling mechanistic investigation of this widely prescribed APD. We show that clozapine promotes obesity in female mice by driving hyperphagia, which requires functional coupling between the melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) and the Kir7.1 potassium channel. Within the broader context of clozapine’s polypharmacology, this signaling axis emerges as a critical downstream convergence node for APD-induced metabolic dysfunction. Mechanistically, clozapine inhibits MC4R-expressing neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus by enhancing MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling, thereby increasing inward potassium currents. Notably, clozapine produces this inhibition without binding the MC4R orthosteric site and without engaging canonical Gαs signaling. Genetic deletion of Kir7.1 in MC4R neurons or pharmacological inhibition of Kir7.1 reverses clozapine-induced weight gain while preserving its behavioral efficacy in established antipsychotic assays. Together, these findings reveal a G-protein-independent mechanism by which clozapine disrupts energy balance and identify MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling as a therapeutically tractable pathway for mitigating APD-associated metabolic dysfunction.Subject terms: Hypothalamus, Neuronal physiology

Introduction

Antipsychotic drugs (APDs) demonstrate broad efficacy in treating diverse neuropsychiatric conditions1. However, most APDs induce metabolic syndrome characterized by hyperphagia, weight gain, and insulin resistance2. Despite interventions such as lifestyle modification, nutritional counseling, and weight-loss pharmacotherapy3–5, these metabolic side effects remain difficult to manage and are a leading cause of treatment discontinuation.

APDs act on multiple neurotransmitter receptors throughout the brain6–8 and this polypharmacology is central to both their therapeutic benefits and metabolic liabilities. While their therapeutic efficacy primarily stems from blockade of dopamine D2 and serotonin 2a receptors9,10, the metabolic side effects of clozapine, olanzapine, and risperidone are thought to arise from convergent action across a broader receptor set that includes high-affinity antagonism at hypothalamic histamine H1, serotonin 5-HT2C, dopamine D2/D3, and muscarinic M3 receptors11–15, alongside effects on MC4R-dependent melanocortin signaling16. These actions are thought to perturb hypothalamic energy-balance circuits and promote hyperphagia and metabolic dysfunction17–19. Disentangling the contribution of any individual pathway within this polypharmacological landscape has remained a central challenge.

A major obstacle to mechanistic studies has been the inability to reliably reproduce these metabolic effects in rodent models. For example, clozapine, one of the most effective and commonly prescribed APDs, causes pronounced weight gain in both adolescents and adults20, yet chronic administration of clozapine to rodents via intraperitoneal injection or drinking water produces only modest effects on food intake and body weight21,22. In this study, we establish a mouse model that faithfully reproduces clozapine-induced weight gain and identify the interaction between the melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) and the Kir7.1 potassium channel as a key mediator of this effect. While other receptor pathways engaged by clozapine likely contribute to its overall metabolic impact, our findings establish MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling as a required convergence node within this broader polypharmacology.

Results

A mouse model of clozapine-induced metabolic syndrome

To model clozapine-induced metabolic dysfunction, we formulated a clozapine diet by compounding clozapine (50 milligrams per kilogram) into a control diet (Research Diets, Inc. D09092903). When scaled by body surface area, this dietary dose in mice is equivalent to approximately 4 mg/kg in humans (∼300 mg/day for a 70 kg individual)23, which falls within the lower end of the clinical dosing range (typically 300–600 mg/day). Consistent with this, plasma clozapine concentrations after four weeks averaged 1.12 ± 0.35 µg/mL, within the therapeutic plasma range reported in patients24. The clozapine and control diets were identical in macronutrient composition and energy density (Supplementary Table 1). Although plasma clozapine levels were markedly elevated in treated mice, they showed no preference between the two diets (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a), indicating that clozapine supplementation did not alter palatability. Previous studies have shown that the metabolic effects of APDs are more pronounced in female than in male C57BL/6 mice16,25,26 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We therefore focused on female C57BL/6 mice to assess both the chronic and acute effects of clozapine on energy metabolism.

Female mice fed the clozapine diet gained significantly more weight than controls over a 12-week period (Fig. 1b). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analyses revealed that the excessive weight gain was largely due to increased fat mass (Fig. 1c), evident in both inguinal and gonadal white adipose tissues (iWAT and gWAT respectively), but not in the brown adipose tissue (BAT, Fig. 1d). Consistent with these findings, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed increased lipid accumulation in iWAT and liver of clozapine-fed mice (Fig. 1e, f). These mice also exhibited higher plasma levels of leptin, non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA, Fig. 1g, h) and impaired glucose clearance during a glucose tolerance test (GTT, Fig. 1i). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dietary clozapine induces robust metabolic dysfunction in mice, closely recapitulating key features of clozapine-induced metabolic syndrome observed in humans.

Clozapine-induced hyperphagia drives weight gain

To elucidate the mechanism underlying clozapine-induced weight gain, we monitored food intake and energy expenditure in individual mice before and after clozapine treatment using a fully automated metabolic chamber system (TSE PhenoMaster). Mice were maintained on the control diet during acclimation and the first 2.5 days of the recording, followed by a switch to the clozapine diet for seven days. Remarkably, mice quickly developed hyperphagia following the dietary switch, consuming substantially more food during the dark phase of the day (Fig. 2a). Clozapine-fed mice also exhibited a modest increase in energy expenditure (Fig. 2b), whereas physical activity and respiratory exchange ratio (RER), an indicator of substrate utilization, remained unchanged (Fig. 2c, d). Despite the slight rise in energy expenditure (1.18 ± 0.23 kcal/day), it was insufficient to counterbalance the marked increase in energy intake (11.75 ± 3.17 kcal/day), resulting in a positive energy balance and progressive weight gain.

Clozapine does not affect canonical MC4R signaling

Genome-wide association and candidate gene studies in humans have implicated the melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R)27, among others28–33, in APD-induced weight gain. MC4R encodes a receptor for two hypothalamic neuropeptides with opposing effects on food intake: α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) activates MC4Rs to suppress food intake, whereas agouti-related peptide (AgRP) binds the same receptor to stimulate consumption34. However, whether APDs such as clozapine can directly compete for the orthosteric binding site of MC4R had not been previously reported.

In a radioligand binding assay, PF-0044668735, a selective MC4R agonist, displaced [¹²⁵I]-NDP-α-MSH (100 pM; Ki = 199 ± 37 nM, mean ± S.E.M., n = 3) from MC4Rs expressed in HEK-293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, clozapine only partially inhibited radioligand binding at the highest concentrations tested (10 and 50 µM), while olanzapine and risperidone, two other commonly prescribed APDs, had no effect (Supplementary Fig. 2a). These findings suggest that these APDs do not directly interact with the orthosteric binding site of MC4R.

The canonical MC4R signaling cascade involves Gαs-mediated activation of adenylyl cyclase and subsequent elevation of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). To determine whether APD exposure alters this pathway, we measured α-MSH–evoked cAMP responses in HEK-293 cells expressing MC4R and a genetically encoded cAMP sensor36. Clozapine, risperidone, and olanzapine (1 μM) did not affect α-MSH potency, efficacy, or the slope of the α-MSH–cAMP concentration–response curves, indicating that these APDs do not perturb MC4R–Gαs coupling or downstream adenylyl cyclase activation (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d).

Clozapine and risperidone enhance MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling

Silencing Mc4r-expressing neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (hereafter referred to as MC4RPVH neurons) induces hyperphagia and obesity in mice37,38. Interestingly, acute application of risperidone to murine hypothalamic slices inhibits these neurons in an MC4R-dependent manner16. To determine whether risperidone and clozapine exert similar effects in vivo, we injected adeno-associated viruses (AAV) expressing a Cre-dependent fluorescent calcium indicator (GCaMP8s) into the PVH of Mc4r-Cre mice (Fig. 3a). Fiber photometry recordings in freely moving mice revealed that an intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of clozapine (3 mg/kg, Fig. 3b) or risperidone (3 mg/kg, Fig. 3c) suppressed MC4RPVH neuron activity. In contrast, ziprasidone (3 mg/kg), an APD with minimal propensity to cause weight gain in patients39, had no effect (Fig. 3d).

Previous work demonstrated that risperidone inhibits MC4RPVH neurons by activating postsynaptic potassium conductance16. However, the specific potassium channel involved remained unknown. Notably, MC4RPVH neurons express the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir7.1 (encoded by the kcnj13 gene)40,41. In HEK293 cells co-expressing MC4R and Kir7.1, AgRP increases Kir7.1 conductance in an MC4R-dependent manner40,42. To test whether APDs modulate this pathway, we used a high-throughput automated patch-clamp platform (SyncroPatch 384PE; Nanion Technologies, Munich, Germany)43 to measure MC4R–Kir7.1–dependent potassium currents in HEK-293 cells. Peak current ratios (Icompound/I₀) were used to quantify compound-induced channel activation across a range of APD concentrations. Both clozapine and risperidone increased inward potassium currents in a concentration-dependent manner (0.2 nM–20 μM), with pEC₅₀ values of 1.86 nM and 0.28 nM, respectively (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). In contrast, olanzapine did not increase potassium conductance but instead triggered channel closure (decreased potassium currents compared to the reference) at the highest concentration tested (10 μM; pIC50 of 1.95 μM; Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Importantly, neither α-MSH, AgRP42, nor any of the APDs elicited potassium currents in HEK-293 cells expressing Kir7.1 alone (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c), confirming that channel activation required the presence of MC4R. We next examined whether APDs modulate AgRP-induced Kir7.1 opening44. Co-application of clozapine or risperidone (1 μM each) enhanced AgRP-induced MC4R–Kir7.1 coupling by approximately 50%, without affecting AgRP potency, efficacy, or the slope of the concentration–response curves (Fig. 3g, h). In comparison, olanzapine (1 μM) had no effect on AgRP-induced opening of Kir7.1 (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

Kir7.1 is required for clozapine-induced weight gain

We previously demonstrated that MC4R expression in PVH neurons is required for risperidone-induced weight gain, and that the selective melanocortin agonist setmelanotide reverses hyperphagia and obesity in risperidone-treated mice16. Consistent with this, chronic setmelanotide treatment reduced food intake and body weight in clozapine-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 6). To determine the role of Kir7.1 in APD-induced weight gain, we generated Kir7.1fl/fl; Mc4r-Cre+/- mice (hereafter referred to as Kir7.1Mc4r KO mice), in which Kir7.1 was selectively deleted from Mc4r-Cre-expressing neurons. In situ hybridization (RNAscopeTM) confirmed reduced Kir7.1 mRNA expression in MC4RPVH neurons (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7). Consistently, fiber photometry recordings showed diminished calcium responses in these neurons following an intraperitoneal dose of clozapine or risperidone (Fig. 4b, c). Notably, clozapine-induced weight gain was diminished in Kir7.1Mc4r KO mice (Fig. 4d). In contrast, these mice remained susceptible to olanzapine-induced increases in body weight and adiposity (Fig. 4d, e). These findings indicate that Kir7.1 in Mc4r-Cre neurons is necessary for the obesogenic effect of clozapine but not for that of olanzapine.

We next tested whether pharmacological blockade of Kir7.1 could mitigate clozapine-induced hyperphagia and weight gain using ML418, a small-molecular channel blocker with relative specificity for Kir7.145. To this end, C57BL/6 mice were maintained on the clozapine diet for 12 weeks to establish hyperphagia and obesity and then treated daily with either ML418 (5 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle while continuing the clozapine diet. ML418 treatment reduced food intake (Fig. 4f) and lowered body weight after 10 days (Fig. 4g). ML418-treated mice also showed reduced fat mass (Fig. 4h), lower plasma leptin levels (Fig. 4i), and improved glucose tolerance (Fig. 4j).

Finally, we examined whether ML418 interferes with clozapine’s antipsychotic efficacy using a rodent model of schizophrenia46. Amphetamine is well known to induce psychosis-like symptoms in healthy human subjects47 and to exacerbate symptoms in patients with schizophrenia48. Similarly, administration of amphetamine (2.5 mg/kg, i.p.) induced robust hyperactivity in C57BL/6 mice, which was significantly attenuated by pretreatment with clozapine (Fig. 4k). Importantly, we found that co-treatment with ML418 did not diminish clozapine’s ability to suppress amphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion in an open-field test (Fig. 4k). Together, these results demonstrate that Kir7.1 mediates clozapine-induced metabolic dysfunction without impairing its antipsychotic action, highlighting Kir7.1 as a promising therapeutic target to dissociate the metabolic and psychotropic effects of clozapine.

Discussion

Our study identifies a molecular mechanism underlying clozapine-induced weight gain and highlights MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating antipsychotic-induced metabolic syndrome. Using a dietary administration paradigm, we established a mouse model that recapitulates key features of clozapine’s metabolic effects, including hyperphagia, increased adiposity, and impaired glucose homeostasis. This model provides a robust platform for dissecting the neural circuits and molecular pathways that drive clozapine-induced obesity.

We found that hyperphagia emerges shortly after clozapine exposure, indicating that excessive energy intake is the primary driver of weight gain. Clozapine also enhances energy expenditure, consistent with previous reports of increased sympathetic tone and thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue49. However, the modest rise in energy expenditure is insufficient to offset the substantial increase in food intake, leading to a net positive energy balance and obesity.

Our findings implicate suppression of the central melanocortin system as a contributor to clozapine-induced hyperphagia. Specifically, we show that clozapine and risperidone inhibit the activity of MC4RPVH neurons, a key neuronal population governing energy homeostasis. Moreover, the propensity of different APDs to induce weight gain may correlate with their ability to inhibit these neurons. Consistent with this idea, ziprasidone, an APD with minimal metabolic side effects, does not suppress MC4RPVH neuronal activity. Although clozapine’s polypharmacology almost certainly contributes to its overall metabolic impact, our data identify the central melanocortin system as a necessary downstream pathway through which clozapine drives hyperphagia.

Mechanistically, clozapine-induced inhibition occurs independently of canonical MC4R-Gαs signaling and instead results from enhanced coupling between MC4R and the Kir7.1 potassium channel. Patch-clamp electrophysiology shows that clozapine and risperidone dose-dependently increase Kir7.1-mediated inward potassium currents, consistent with membrane hyperpolarization and reduced cellular excitability. These recordings were performed in heterologous HEK-293 cells, which lack the complex signaling environment of hypothalamic neurons. Although MC4R–Kir7.1 coupling has been established in native PVH neurons40, whether APDs engage this coupling in the endogenous neuronal context remains to be determined.

Importantly, clozapine- and risperidone-induced neuronal inhibition is abolished in Kir7.1Mc4r KO mice, which are resistant to clozapine-induced weight gain. These findings support a model in which certain APDs hijack melanocortin signaling by potentiating MC4R–Kir7.1 coupling to inhibit MC4R neurons, thereby contributing to hyperphagia and obesity. Our results also indicate that different APDs engage distinct neural mechanisms to induce weight gain. Although the obesogenic effect of olanzapine requires MC4R in PVH neurons16, it does not alter MC4R-Kir7.1 coupling. Consistent with this, olanzapine still causes significant weight gain in Kir7.1Mc4r KO mice. Instead, olanzapine increases Agrp expression26, which may secondarily inhibit MC4RPVH neurons through enhanced AgRP-mediated antagonism40.

Pharmacological inhibition of Kir7.1 with ML418 effectively reversed clozapine-induced hyperphagia and obesity, underscoring its therapeutic potential. Crucially, ML418 did not diminish clozapine’s antipsychotic efficacy, as clozapine continued to suppress amphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion in ML418-treated mice. Together, these findings support a role for Kir7.1 in mediating clozapine-induced metabolic dysfunction and identify MC4R–Kir7.1 signaling as a potential target to mitigate APD-associated metabolic side effects without compromising psychiatric efficacy.

In a companion study, Peisley et al. define the structural basis for ML418’s inhibition of Kir7.1. Using cryo-EM, they identify the ML418 binding site on Kir7.1 and demonstrate that intracerebroventricular administration of ML418, but not the selective Kir6.2 inhibitor VU0134992, activates MC4RPVH neurons and suppresses food intake in wild-type mice, consistent with a major central role for Kir7.1. A recognized limitation of ML418 is its additional activity against Kir6.2/SUR1, the ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel complex50, and because ML418 was administered systemically (i.p.) in our study, peripheral contributions from Kir6.2/SUR1 inhibition cannot be formally excluded. However, the directionality of KATP channel pharmacology argues against a meaningful contribution of peripheral KATP inhibition to the phenotype observed here. Sulfonylureas, which inhibit pancreatic β-cell Kir6.2/SUR1, enhance insulin secretion and are clinically associated with weight gain51,52. Conversely, the KATP channel activator diazoxide choline (Vykat XR) was recently approved by the FDA for treatment of hyperphagia in Prader-Willi syndrome, where it reduces food intake and fat mass53. Peripheral Kir6.2/SUR1 inhibition by ML418 would therefore be expected to oppose, not mimic, its observed anorexigenic and weight-reducing effects. Together with the i.c.v. data from Peisley et al., this supports attribution of ML418’s metabolic effects in our paradigm to a major role for central Kir7.1, consistent with our genetic findings in Kir7.1Mc4r KO mice. Definitive attribution will benefit from future studies using more selective Kir7.1 pharmacology as they become available.

In conclusion, we identify MC4R–Kir7.1 coupling as a critical downstream pathway necessary for clozapine-induced weight gain. These findings provide mechanistic insight into APD-induced metabolic syndrome and highlight this signaling axis as a potential therapeutic target for preventing or reversing antipsychotic-associated obesity. However, our data should not be interpreted as supporting an exclusive role for the MC4R–Kir7.1 complex in APD-induced metabolic dysfunction. Clozapine exhibits broad polypharmacology, including high-affinity antagonism at histamine H1 and serotonin 5-HT2C receptors54, both of which have been implicated in antipsychotic-induced weight gain14,15. It is therefore likely that clozapine’s obesogenic effects arise from the combined engagement of multiple parallel and intersecting neural pathways. Moreover, because Kir7.1 is expressed in peripheral tissues, systemic inhibition may raise safety concerns. Future studies should evaluate the translational feasibility of targeting Kir7.1, including the development of tissue-specific or centrally restricted modulators to minimize off-target effects, as well as strategies that selectively disrupt MC4R–Kir7.1 coupling. Determining whether this pathway contributes to metabolic disturbances induced by other APDs or across broader forms of obesity will further clarify its therapeutic potential.

Methods

Animals

All mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room in the UT Southwestern Medical Center animal facility with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle (lights on at 06:00 am, lights off at 6:00 pm), with the temperature maintained at 68–79 °F (20–26 °C) and relative humidity maintained at 30–70%. Unless otherwise noted, mice were provided standard chow (No. 2016; Harlan Teklad) and water ad libitum. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (000664). The Kir7.1fl/fl mouse line55 was generously provided by Dr. Graeme Mardon at Baylor College of Medicine. Mc4r-Cre +/-; Kir7.1fl/fl and Kir7.1/fl littermates were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center approved all mouse experiments. For i.p. injection, clozapine (Sigma, Cat #C6305), risperidone (Acros Organics, Cat #AC459390010), olanzapine (Sigma, Cat #O1141), ziprasidone (Sigma, Cat #1724408), and ML418 (MedChemExpress, Cat #HY-122697) were first dissolved in DMSO to a final concentration of 10%. The drug solutions were then mixed with PEG400 to a final concentration of 20% before being combined with saline. Chronic delivery of setmelanotide was achieved using subcutaneous osmotic mini-pumps (Alzet). Pumps were implanted under isoflurane anesthesia, followed by postoperative analgesia with carprofen. Setmelanotide (2 mg/kg/day) was delivered continuously throughout the experimental period. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at UT Southwestern Medical Center.

Genotyping

To genotype Mc4r-Cre+/−; Kir7.1fl/fl and Kir7.1fl/fl mice, the following primers were used: Mc4r-Cre primers, F/s1 (GGCAAACGGACAGAAGCA), R/s1 (TCCGGAGTCAAGAACTGAGG), and R/s2 (CAATTCATAACGCCCACACT); and Kir7.1fl/fl primers, F/s2 (AAGGGGACATGGTGGCTTG), R/s3 (ACCATTGGCAGGGAAGTCAA), F/s3 (CAATGAAAACCACTCAACAACC), and R/s4 (CTGGAAATAGCACAGTCCTCC).

Diets

All diets were prepared by ResearchDiets Inc. and shared the same macronutrients, ingredient composition, and energy density (4.76 kcal/gram). Clozapine (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-14539) was mixed into a control diet D09092903 to make the clozapine diet D21070104 (50 mg/kg). Similarly, olanzapine (ChemPacific, Cat# 356941G) was mixed into the same control diet, to make the olanzapine diet D12040807 (50 mg/kg)14.

Metabolic phenotype analysis

We used a magnetic-resonance whole-body composition analyzer (EchoMRI) to analyze the mouse’s body composition (fat mass, lean mass, and water content). For chronic studies, body weight and food intake were measured weekly. The acute effects of clozapine on energy intake and expenditure were measured using an indirect calorimetric system (TSE PhenoMaster, Germany) in the Metabolic Phenotyping Core of UT Southwestern Medical Center.

Radioligand binding experiments

Radioligand binding experiments used a HEK-293 (ATCC CRL-1573™) stable cell line expressing the human MC4R. The cells were grown in 150 mm culture dishes to prepare crude membranes until they reached 90-95% confluency. TrypLE Express (1x) cell dissociation reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to detach the cells from the growth vessels. Afterward, trypsin was neutralized with an equal volume of complete growth media. The cells were pelleted at 200 × g for 5 min, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in “Membrane Extract Buffer” (ME-buffer). This buffer contained 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgSO4, 1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM EDTA, and cOmplete™ EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel Switzerland). The initial cell pellet was homogenized using a 15 mL Dounce homogenizer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 35,000 × g for 20 min, and the process was repeated twice. The final pellet was resuspended with 10× v/w of ME-buffer supplemented with 10% sucrose, and aliquots were stored at -80°C. The total protein content from the crude membrane extracts was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay56 with a commercially available kit (Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Equilibrium competition binding experiments were performed with 4 µg of total protein from the crude membrane extract. [125I]-[Nle4, DPhe7]-ɑ-MSH (NDP-MSH, specific activity: 2200 Ci/mmol)) was used as the radioligand tracer at a concentration of 100 pM. This radioligand concentration resembles the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) determined by ligand saturation experiments. Varying concentrations of the indicated antipsychotic drugs (APD) or PF-00446687, an MC4R-specific agonist, were incubated with the crude membrane extracts in a final volume of 200 µl of Binding Assay buffer (BA-buffer: 25 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 1 mM MgSO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and cOmplete™ EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail). APD and crude membranes were preincubated for 16 hours at room temperature before adding radioligand to ensure equilibrium. The assay was incubated for an additional 120 min in the presence of the radioligand. Bound [125I]-NDP-MSH was rapidly separated from the free ligand by filtration through GF/B glass fiber filters (Brandel, Gaithersburg, MD) using a cell harvester (Brandel) manifold. During filtration, the filters were washed five times rapidly with cold 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4. IC50 values were derived from nonlinear regression fitting the data to a sigmoid function model. Ki values were derived by applying the Cheng and Prusoff transformation57.

Cellular cAMP responses

A HEK-293 (ATCC CRL-1573™) stable cell line expressing the human MC4R and a split luciferase cAMP genetic sensor based on the circularized fusion of firefly luciferase and protein kinase A regulatory cAMP binding domain (clone 22F, Promega, Madison WI) was used to determine the α-MSH-elicited cAMP responses in live cells as previously described44. The cell line identity was verified by qPCR and MC4R subtype-specific oligonucleotides58.

Briefly, twenty-four hours before the cAMP response recordings, the cells were plated in poly-D-lysine coated clear-bottom black wall 384-well plates (Corning Inc. Corning, NJ) at a density of 20,000 cells per well in growth medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 4.5 g/L D-glucose, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic™ cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 200 μg/mL hygromycin B, and 700 μg/mL Geneticin™). Two hours before the start of the experiment, the growth medium was replaced with CO2 Independent Medium™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific), containing 4% D-luciferin (Promega). The luciferase substrate permeated the cells for 120 min at 37 °C. Intracellular cAMP levels were measured using an FDSS 7000EX Functional Drug Screening System (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan). To test the effect of APD on the α-MSH concentration-response profile, the in-line addition of 1 μM (final concentration) clozapine, risperidone, or olanzapine was performed and allowed to reach equilibrium for 12 minutes. This was followed by adding 10 µL of varying 3× concentrations of α-MSH or vehicle. Luminescence responses were measured for an additional 12 min. The agonist responses were normalized to the α-MSH maximum response without APD. The data were fit to a four-parameter sigmoid model, constraining the slope to a range of 0 to 2 using GraphPad Prism.

Histology

Details for performing these experiments were described previously16,59. For brain collection, mice were deeply anesthetized with chloral hydrate (200 mg/kg, i.p.) and euthanized by transcardial perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were removed, post-fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, and sectioned using a vibratome (Leica VT1000S). RNAscope was performed using ACD RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Detection Kit version 2 following the manufacturer’s (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) protocol with the following probes Mm-Mc4r and Mm-Kir7.1.

Mouse adipose tissue and liver were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and then dehydrated through a series of ethanol baths with ascending concentrations (up to 100%). The Histo Pathology Core of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center performed paraffin embedding, sectioning, and H&E staining.

Automated patch clamp

Patch clamp recordings were conducted using a SyncroPatch 384PE system from Nanion Technologies (Munich, Germany) at the Center for Chemical Genomics at the University of Michigan. Recordings were carried out in the whole cell voltage clamp configuration employing medium resistance four-hole NPC-384 chips. Data acquisition and analysis were performed with PatchControl 384 (for data acquisition) and DataControl 384 (for data analysis), both from Nanion Technologies. The experimental setup utilized an inducible TREx HEK-293 cell line (Thermo Fisher Scientific) expressing MC4R and the inwardly rectifying K+ channel Kir7.1 mutant M125R. Cells were centrifuged at 200 g for 5 minutes, resuspended in an external solution at a density of 500,000 cells/mL, and added to the instrument cell hotel. The cell hotel was maintained at 10 °C with 200 rpm agitation until the start of the experiment. The external recording solution comprised (in mM): NaCl 140, KCl 4, CaCl2 2, MgCl2 1, HEPES 10, glucose 5; pH 7.4 (NaOH, ∼300 mOsm). Cell sealing was accomplished in a modified external solution containing 6 mM CaCl2. The internal electrode solution included (in mM): KF 110, KCl 10, NaCl 10, HEPES 10, EGTA 10; pH 7.2 (KOH, ∼280 mOsm).

To assess MC4R-dependent Kir7.1 channel closures or activations induced by antipsychotic drugs (APD), a two-stage voltage protocol was applied. This protocol involved a hyperpolarization step to −140 mV for 200 ms, starting from a holding potential of −60 mV, and it was repeated every 5 seconds. Peak Kir7.1 inward current amplitude during the hyperpolarizing step was measured and normalized to the remnant currents after adding 10 mM BaCl2 (full block). Antipsychotic drugs (APD) were delivered at various concentrations across the NPC-384 chip, followed by a Ba2+ full channel block added to each well to determine non-K+ channel currents, as the M125R Kir7.1 regains sensitivity to Ba2+ compared to the relatively insensitive wild-type Kir7.1. Responses were further normalized by determining the ratios of peak currents in the presence or absence of APDs (Icompound/I0). Concentration-response profiles were determined, and EC50 or IC50 values for activating or inhibiting APD responses were calculated. A seal quality assessment for each well was performed using the DataControl 384 application, excluding rundown wells, with a positive patch rate of 87%.

GTT

For the glucose tolerance test (GTT), mice were fasted for 7 hours with water provided ad libitum from 8 am on the experimental day. During GTT, blood glucose levels were monitored at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after an i.p. dose of glucose (Dextrose) (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose was taken from the tail vein and analyzed using a Glucometer (Johnson & Johnson).

Stereotaxic surgery

Eight-week-old female mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. The skull was exposed and leveled on a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments). A small hole was drilled in the skull for viral injection or fiber implantation. AAV9-AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP8s-WPRE (Addgene #162377-AAV9) was bilaterally injected into the PVH [anteroposterior (AP), –0.50 mm; mediolateral (ML), ±0.20 mm; dorsoventral (DV), –4.80 mm]. A total of 180 nL of the virus was injected at a rate of 30 nL/min and allowed to diffuse for eight to ten minutes before the injection needle was removed. After a three-week recovery period, a 1.25 mm ceramic ferrule-capped fiber (RWD, Cat# R-FOC-BL200C-39NA, 200 µm core, 0.39 NA) was unilaterally implanted for fiber photometry [anteroposterior (AP), –0.50 mm; mediolateral (ML), +0.10 mm; dorsoventral (DV), –4.80 mm]. As previously described60, the injection site was verified by the GCaMP8s fluorescent signal for each mouse.

Amphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion

The Rodent Behavior Core performed the amphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion in the Peter O’Donnell Jr. Brain Institute at UT Southwestern Medical Center. Briefly, C57BL/6 mice were habituated for the open field test with daily single i.p. saline injections for three days. On the third day of habituation, all mice were given D-amphetamine (2.5 mg/kg) 30 min after the saline injection. Following the habituation, all mice were randomly divided into four groups: 1) mice in the saline group received an i.p. dose of saline; 2) Mice in the ML418 group received an i.p. dose of ML418 (5 mg/kg); 3) mice in the CLZ group received an i.p. dose of clozapine (3 mg/kg); 4) mice in the ML418 + CLZ group received both clozapine (3 mg/kg) and ML418 (5 mg/kg). Locomotor activity (beam breaks) was immediately recorded after the first injection. An i.p. dose of D-amphetamine (2.5 mg/kg) was administered 30 min after the first injection.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Sample sizes were chosen to reliably measure experimental parameters while remaining in compliance with ethical guidelines for minimizing animal use, and they were similar to those reported in previous publications. All the virus expression and optic fiber implant placements were verified by post hoc histology. Replicate information is indicated in the figure legends. All results are presented as mean ± SEM and analyzed using statistical tools implemented in Prism (GraphPad, version 10). Statistical analyses were performed using the Student’s t-test and regular one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences with P < 0.05 were considered to be significant. P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***).
