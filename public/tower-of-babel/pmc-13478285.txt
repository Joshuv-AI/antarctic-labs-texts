Understanding indeterminate HIV serology through antigen-band pattern analysis of INNO-LIA and recomLine HIV confirmatory assays
Authors: Israa M Salameh, Shaden Abunasser, Nadin Younes, Parveen B Nizamuddin, Salma Younes, Sara Abdelmohsen, Affra Faiz Al-Farsi, Mazen N Abouassali, Manal Elshaikh, Ibrahim W Karimeh, Mohammed A Ibrahim, Mutaz M Ali, Samar A Mohamed, Ibrahim Al Shaar, Christian Meyer, Hadi M Yassine, Laith J Abu-Raddad, Houssein Ayoub, Gheyath K Nasrallah, Ahmed Ismail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndeterminate Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) serological results remain a major challenge in confirmatory diagnosis, delaying reporting and clinical decision-making. The INNO-LIA HIV I/II Score assay, a widely used confirmatory line immunoassay, frequently produces indeterminate profiles that complicate interpretation. This study evaluated the Mikrogen recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG assay against INNO-LIA, incorporating HIV-1 RNA PCR as an adjudicative reference within the Architect-reactive samples, with emphasis on antigen-band patterns underlying indeterminate results. A total of 180 archived serum samples previously tested by the Architect HIV Ag/Ab Combo and INNO-LIA were retested using recomLine; 80 Architect-reactive samples underwent HIV-1 RNA PCR testing. When only definitive results were considered, recomLine demonstrated complete agreement with INNO-LIA. When indeterminate results were included as non-concordant, specificity was 98.3% (114/116). INNO-LIA produced more indeterminate results than recomLine (13.3% vs. 2.8%), mainly due to isolated gp41 reactivity. RecomLine reclassified 85% of INNO-LIA indeterminate samples, all of tested PCR-negative. These findings provide insights into mechanisms underlying indeterminate HIV serology, suggesting that incomplete or isolated antigenic responses, particularly gp41, are major contributors. No HIV-2 monoinfection or HIV-1/2 dual infections were identified. Introduction

HIV remains a major global public health challenge more than four decades after its discovery. Despite substantial progress in prevention strategies and the widespread availability of antiretroviral therapy (ART), HIV continues to spread. In 2023 alone, an estimated 1.3 million new infections occurred, with 39.9 million people living with the virus and 630,000 deaths from AIDS-related illnesses1. Early diagnosis is critical, particularly during acute infection, when viral load is high and transmission risk is greatest. Timely identification enables prompt initiation of ART, improves clinical outcomes, and reduces onward transmission2.

The introduction of fourth-generation combination assays—which simultaneously detect HIV-1/2 antibodies and the p24 antigen, a viral marker present before seroconversion—has further shortened the diagnostic window to approximately two weeks2–4. Current international guidelines, including those from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Association of Public Health Laboratories, recommend these assays as the initial screening step, followed by supplemental testing to differentiate HIV-1 from HIV-2 and confirm infection5,6.

Despite these advances, confirmatory testing in many settings relies on line immunoassays such as the INNO-LIA™ HIV I/II Score assay, a widely used confirmatory assay. However, INNO-LIA frequently yields indeterminate results, necessitating additional testing such as nucleic acid testing, which may delay diagnosis and increase patient anxiety7–10. Indeterminate HIV confirmatory patterns are frequently driven by incomplete antigenic responses, particularly isolated envelope (ENV) reactivity such as gp41, reflecting either early or nonspecific antibody responses and representing a persistent diagnostic challenge11. Moreover, variable performance has been reported in certain epidemiological settings and in cases of dual HIV-1/HIV-2 infection12.

The Mikrogen recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG assay is a line immunoassay incorporating a wider range of purified recombinant antigens, including HIV-1-specific proteins gp120 and gp41 (ENV), group-specific antigen (GAG) p24 and p17, polymerase proteins (POL) p51 and p31, and HIV-2-specific proteins gp105 and gp36 (ENV), with standardized digital interpretation of results. Previous studies have primarily evaluated recomLine in early HIV infection or against other confirmatory assays10,13.

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of the recomLine assay relative to INNO-LIA, incorporating HIV-1 RNA nucleic acid testing (PCR) as an adjudicative reference within Architect-reactive samples according to the Medical Commission (MC) Laboratory HIV diagnostic algorithm used in Qatar. In this workflow, PCR is not applied universally but is used reflexively in Architect-reactive, discordant, or indeterminate serological cases to help resolve ambiguous results and detect potential acute infection. Particular emphasis was placed on evaluating recomLine’s ability to resolve INNO-LIA indeterminate results through detailed antigen-band pattern analysis. To our knowledge, while previous studies have described antigen-band reactivity in early HIV infection10, this study is among the first to systematically evaluate antigen-band patterns in both recomLine and INNO-LIA and to examine how recomLine resolves indeterminate INNO-LIA samples within a structured diagnostic algorithm.

Methods

Ethical approval

Archived serum samples from previous studies were used14. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the IRB at Qatar University under approval number QU-IRB 017/2024-E. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of archived, anonymized human serum samples, the requirement for obtaining informed consent was waived by the IRB.

HIV diagnostic workflow at the medical commission (MC) laboratory, Qatar

In Qatar, mandatory HIV screening for expatriates is conducted through the MC under the Ministry of Public Health. The MC Laboratory HIV diagnostic algorithm follows a stepwise approach beginning with a fourth-generation HIV-1/2 Ag/Ab screening assay (ARCHITECT®, Abbott Diagnostics, Abbott Park, IL, USA)15. Samples that are initially reactive undergo duplicate repeat testing. If either repeat test remains reactive, supplemental antibody differentiation is performed using the INNO-LIA™ HIV I/II Score assay.

When INNO-LIA results are discordant or indeterminate, reflex nucleic acid testing for HIV-1 RNA is performed using the Roche cobas® platform on a newly collected sample. If HIV-1 RNA is not detected in samples with indeterminate serological results, repeat testing is performed after four weeks in accordance with the MC protocol. Within this diagnostic algorithm, HIV-1 RNA PCR is applied as a reflex adjudicative tool in Architect-reactive cases to resolve discordant serological findings and detect potential acute infection prior to complete seroconversion, rather than as a primary screening method (Fig. 1).

National HIV Testing Algorithm Used by the Medical Commission (Qatar). Flowchart illustrating the stepwise HIV diagnostic workflow for mandatory screening. Initial testing is performed using the fourth-generation Architect HIV-1/2 Ag/Ab Combo assay. Nonreactive samples are reported as HIV-negative. Reactive samples undergo supplemental antibody differentiation using the INNO-LIA™ HIV-1/2 assay together with reflex HIV-1 RNA PCR testing. Samples with detectable HIV-1 RNA or positive INNO-LIA results are classified as HIV-positive. If INNO-LIA results are indeterminate and HIV-1 RNA is not detected, a new sample is collected after four weeks, and the testing sequence is repeated in accordance with the MC protocol. Study population and sample selection

For this study, 180 archived, de-identified HIV-1/2 serum samples were collected over a defined three-month period. The sample panel was intentionally enriched with Architect-reactive samples and INNO-LIA-discordant or -indeterminate samples to evaluate assay performance in diagnostically challenging scenarios rather than reflect routine screening prevalence.

The cohort consisted of 80 Architect-reactive and 100 Architect-nonreactive samples. In accordance with the MC diagnostic algorithm, all Architect-reactive samples underwent HIV-1 RNA PCR testing, whereas Architect-nonreactive samples did not.

Among the Architect-reactive samples, 40 were INNO-LIA-positive, 20 were INNO-LIA-negative, and 20 were INNO-LIA-indeterminate. All 180 samples were subsequently tested using the recomLine assay to assess diagnostic performance relative to the MC Laboratory HIV diagnostic algorithm (Fig. 2).

Fujirebio INNO-LIA™ HIV I/II Score

The INNO-LIA™ HIV I/II Score (Fujirebio Europe N.V, Ghent, Belgium) is a qualitative line immunoassay for the detection of antibodies to HIV-1 (including group O) and HIV-2 in human serum or plasma14,16,17. The assay includes five HIV-1 antigens (sgp120, gp41, p31, p24, and p17) and two HIV-2 antigens (gp36 and sgp105), along with internal control bands for assay validity. In this study, INNO-LIA strips were interpreted using the LiRAS™ software for infectious diseases17,18, and classified as positive, negative, or indeterminate based on the manufacturer’s interpretation criteria (Table 2).

Mikrogen recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG

The recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG assay (Mikrogen Diagnostik, Neuried, Germany) is a qualitative line immunoassay for the detection of HIV-1/2 IgG antibodies in human serum or plasma. The test strips include HIV-1 antigens (gp120, gp41, p24, p31, and p51) and HIV-2 antigens (gp36 and gp105), along with internal control bands. Results were interpreted using recomScan software according to the manufacturer’s criteria and categorized as positive, negative, or borderline19. For a harmonized comparison with INNO-LIA, borderline recomLine results were categorized as indeterminate (Table 2).

COBAS® AmpliPrep/COBAS® TaqMan® HIV-1 Test, version 2.0

HIV-1 RNA testing was performed using the COBAS® AmpliPrep/COBAS® TaqMan® HIV-1 Test, version 2.0 (Roche Diagnostics) using newly collected samples20.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed for categorical variables. Diagnostic concordance between recomLine, INNO-LIA, and PCR results was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, overall percent agreement (OPA), positive percent agreement (PPA), negative percent agreement (NPA), and Cohen’s kappa coefficient21,22. All diagnostic accuracy metrics were calculated using strict binary reference classifications (positive vs. negative) derived from standard 2 × 2 contingency tables. Indeterminate results were predefined as a separate outcome category and excluded from primary binary accuracy calculations, in accordance with the STARD 2015 recommendations23. These outcomes were analyzed descriptively in a secondary framework focused on the resolution of inconclusive results.

Kappa values were interpreted as follows: <0 = no agreement, 0.00–0.20 = slight, 0.21–0.40 = fair, 0.41–0.60 = moderate, 0.61–0.80 = substantial, and 0.81–1.00 = almost perfect agreement24,25. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software, version 10.4.1 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Because the sample panel was intentionally enriched with diagnostically challenging cases, the dataset was designed to evaluate assay performance across diverse serological profiles, rather than estimate population prevalence. Although no formal prospective power calculation was performed, a sample size of 180 samples was selected to ensure adequate representation across diagnostic categories and to provide reasonably precise estimates of diagnostic performance, consistent with recommendations for diagnostic accuracy studies26.

Results

Diagnostic performance of recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG compared with INNO-LIA™ HIV I/II Score

Following the predefined statistical analysis plan, 24 INNO-LIA indeterminate cases were excluded from the primary diagnostic accuracy analysis. This resulted in 156 samples with definitive INNO-LIA classifications (40 positive and 116 negative).

Among the 40 INNO-LIA-positive samples, recomLine correctly identified all 40 samples as positive, yielding a sensitivity of 100.0%. Among the 116 INNO-LIA-negative samples, recomLine classified 114 as negative and 2 as indeterminate. When indeterminate results were treated as non-concordant outcomes, this corresponded to an apparent specificity of 98.3% (114/116).

For the primary analysis, only samples with definitive classifications for both assays were included, and indeterminate results were excluded from sensitivity and specificity calculations. When restricted to these definitive classifications, recomLine demonstrated complete concordance with INNO-LIA (Table 3).

Interpretation of discordant serological results using HIV-1 RNA PCR

HIV-1 RNA PCR testing was performed on all Architect-reactive samples (n = 80). Among these, 40 samples were PCR-positive, all of which corresponded to the Architect-reactive/INNO-LIA-positive subgroup. No HIV-1 RNA was detected among the Architect-reactive/INNO-LIA-negative samples (n = 20) or the Architect-reactive/INNO-LIA-indeterminate samples (n = 20), indicating that these discordant serological patterns were not associated with detectable HIV-1 infection.

When evaluated against HIV-1 RNA PCR results, recomLine correctly classified all PCR-positive samples as positive (100%; 40/40), with no false-negative or indeterminate results. Among the PCR-negative samples (n = 40), recomLine classified 36 (90.0%) as negative and 4 (10%) as indeterminate, with no false-positive results observed (Table 4).

Antigenic reactivity patterns in INNO-LIA indeterminate samples

INNO-LIA™ yielded a higher proportion of indeterminate results (13.3%; 24/180), comprising 20 PI (Architect-reactive/INNO-LIA-indeterminate) and 4 NI (Architect-nonreactive/INNO-LIA-indeterminate) samples, compared with recomLine (2.8%; 5/180). HIV-1 RNA PCR data were available only for the PI subgroup (20/20) and were included in antigen pattern and PCR correlation analyses.

INNO-LIA antigen reactivity

Within the PI subgroup, gp41 was the most frequently detected antigen, present in 90.0% (18/20) of samples, generally exhibiting weak-to-moderate reactivity (1 + to 2+). Other antigens were infrequently reactive, including p17 (10.0%, 2/20) and isolated reactivity to gp120, p24, or p31 (5.0%). No HIV-2-specific antigens (gp105 or gp36) were detected by INNO-LIA, indicating that these indeterminate profiles were predominantly characterized by limited HIV-1 envelope reactivity (Table 6).

recomLine antigen reactivity

In the same PI subgroup, recomLine demonstrated a broader antigen detection profile. gp41 remained the most frequently reactive antigen (80.0%, 16/20), followed by p24 (45.0%, 9/20). Additional low-frequency reactivity was observed for gp105 (25.0%, 5/20), gp36 (10.0%, 2/20), p17 (5.0%, 1/20), and p51 (5.0%, 1/20) (Table 6).

Diagnostic classification and PCR correlation

recomLine reclassified 85.0% (17/20) of PI samples as negative; all of these were PCR-negative. These samples predominantly exhibited isolated gp41 reactivity on INNO-LIA. Despite occasional weak reactivity to gp105, gp36, or p24 on recomLine, the overall interpretation remained negative (Table 6).

The remaining 15% (3/20) were indeterminate by recomLine and were likewise PCR-negative, suggesting persistent low-level or non-specific antibody activity. Four samples with isolated gp41 reactivity on both assays highlight that this pattern alone does not reliably indicate true infection (Table 6). No confirmed HIV-2-only or HIV-1/2 dual infections were identified in this cohort.

Discussion

Accurate and timely HIV diagnosis remains essential for effective patient management and public health control. In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of the recomLine assay relative to INNO-LIA, incorporating HIV-1 RNA PCR within the Architect-reactive samples as an adjudicative reference, with particular emphasis on indeterminate results, an enduring challenge in confirmatory testing.

When analyses were restricted to samples with definitive reference classifications, recomLine demonstrated high concordance with INNO-LIA, with a specificity of 98.3% (114/116) among INNO-LIA-negative samples (Table 3and Supplementary Table S1). Importantly, recomLine substantially reduced the frequency of indeterminate results (2.8%; 5/180) compared with INNO-LIA (13.3%; 24/180). This reduction is clinically relevant, as minimizing inconclusive outcomes may facilitate more timely diagnostic resolution and linkage to care. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies, where INNO-LIA has been associated with higher rates of indeterminate results, particularly during early seroconversion or in specific screening contexts27–29.

In accordance with STARD 2015 recommendations, indeterminate results were predefined as a separate outcome category and excluded from the primary sensitivity and specificity calculations23. Instead, these cases were evaluated in terms of how frequently recomLine could resolve indeterminate INNO-LIA profiles. This approach is consistent with STARD 2015 and prior methodological guidance23,30. Under conservative assumptions in which indeterminate outcomes are treated as false positives, specificity estimates would decrease modestly; however, the primary analysis reflects the predefined statistical framework.

Two distinct subgroups of INNO-LIA indeterminate results were identified: PI (Architect-reactive/INNO-LIA-indeterminate) and NI (Architect-nonreactive/INNO-LIA-indeterminate). The NI subgroup, being nonreactive on the screening assay, is more likely to reflect nonspecific confirmatory band reactivity rather than acute infection. All four NI samples were negative by recomLine, supporting this interpretation. Within the PI subgroup, isolated or predominant gp41 reactivity was the dominant pattern (90.0%; 18/20). This finding is consistent with known causes of indeterminate immunoblot profiles, where limited ENV reactivity occurs in the absence of coordinated response to GAG or POL antigens (Table 6). recomLine applies stricter pattern-based interpretive criteria requiring reactivity across multiple antigen groups (ENV together with GAG and/or POL). Using this approach, 85.0% (17/20) of PI cases were classified as negative, and all were PCR-negative. These findings suggest that many INNO-LIA indeterminate results are driven by incomplete or isolated antigenic responses, particularly gp41, rather than true infection. In this context, recomLine’s pattern-based interpretation may reduce diagnostic ambiguity by requiring broader antigenic confirmation.

Although biologic cross-reactivity is a well-recognized explanation for isolated gp41 responses, alternative mechanisms should also be considered. In individuals with long-term viral suppression, whether due to sustained ART or elite controller status, reduced antigenic stimulation may result in waning antibody responses. This can result in the loss of GAG and POL reactivity while ENV antibodies such as gp41 persist31,32. Because samples in this study were anonymized and lacked linked clinical data, including treatment history and virologic status, this possibility could not be evaluated. Therefore, isolated gp41 reactivity in some cases may reflect altered antibody kinetics rather than purely nonspecific cross-reactivity.

The improved diagnostic clarity observed with recomLine may also be attributed to methodological features of the assay. These include the use of highly purified recombinant antigens, broader antigenic coverage (inclusion of the HIV-1 POL antigen p51), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-based detection chemistry, and automated digital interpretation. HRP-based systems have been reported to enhance detection of low-abundance antibodies compared with alkaline phosphatase-based methods33, while automated digital interpretation may reduce subjectivity associated with visually read line immunoassays. Together, these features may contribute to more consistent and interpretable results.

Previous studies have also demonstrated gp41 cross-reactivity associated with non-HIV-related immune responses, including antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 and commensal microbial antigens34,35. Western blot studies similarly indicate that isolated ENV band reactivity, such as gp41, gp160, or gp120 rarely indicates true infection36. The findings of this study are consistent with this broader immunologic context and suggest that stricter band-pattern interpretation may reduce indeterminate classifications, particularly in low-prevalence screening settings. From a diagnostic algorithm perspective, these findings support the use of confirmatory assays that require multi-antigen reactivity, as well as integrating nucleic acid testing in cases with isolated gp41 reactivity to reduce the risk of false-positive interpretations.

Pre-analytical factors may also influence weak or nonspecific banding patterns37. In this study, INNO-LIA testing was performed on fresh clinical samples as part of routine diagnostics, whereas recomLine testing was conducted retrospectively on archived samples stored at − 80 °C and subjected to an additional freeze–thaw cycle. Differences in sample handling could potentially affect low-level antibody reactivity and should be considered when interpreting comparative performance.

Overall, recomLine may offer several potential advantages over INNO-LIA, including expanded antigenic coverage, stricter interpretation criteria, improved detection chemistry, and automated analysis. These features may contribute to fewer indeterminate outcomes and improved interpretive clarity, supporting the potential role of recomLine as a reliable confirmatory assay in HIV diagnostic workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of indeterminate cases may limit generalizability, although similar sample sizes have been reported in previous studies investigating indeterminate results36,38. Second, no confirmed HIV-2 mono-infection or HIV-1/HIV-2 dual infection cases were identified, and only HIV-1 RNA PCR was available as the molecular reference test. As a result, the findings primarily reflect HIV-1 detection and may not be directly applicable to settings with higher HIV-2 prevalence or dual infections. Because only HIV-1 RNA PCR was performed, HIV-2 infection cannot be completely excluded in PCR-negative samples.

Third, PCR testing was performed only on Architect-reactive samples according to the MC diagnostic algorithm. As a result, four Architect-nonreactive/INNO-LIA indeterminate samples lacked PCR confirmation and were excluded from PCR-based analyses. Although these samples were also negative by recomLine, suggesting nonspecific reactivity, very low-level or occult infection cannot be completely excluded in the absence of PCR or HIV-2–specific testing. This limitation should be considered when interpreting recomLine’s ability to resolve all indeterminate profiles. Fourth, indeterminate results were excluded from primary binary accuracy analyses, and although evaluated separately, alternative classification strategies could modestly affect performance estimates.

Fifth, the lack of clinical data, including treatment status, viral suppression status, and immunologic history, precluded evaluation of factors that may influence antibody profiles. Sixth, differences in sample handling could have influenced weak band reactivity in a subset of samples. Finally, viral subtyping, genomic sequencing, and longitudinal follow-up were not performed, limiting assessment of subtype-related effects and seroconversion dynamics.

Future studies incorporating viral sequencing, HIV-2-specific molecular testing, longitudinal follow-up, and larger multi-epidemiologic cohorts are warranted to further evaluate the diagnostic performance of recomLine across diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

The recomLine HIV-1/2 IgG assay demonstrated strong concordance with INNO-LIA and HIV-1 RNA PCR within a structured diagnostic algorithm, while substantially reducing the frequency of indeterminate serological outcomes. This improvement appears to be driven by stricter pattern-based interpretation, which effectively resolves cases characterized by isolated gp41 reactivity, a common source of indeterminate confirmatory results in low-prevalence settings. While analyses were restricted to samples with definitive classifications, specificity remained high, with indeterminate results analyzed separately in accordance with predefined statistical criteria. Because no confirmed HIV-2 or dual infections were identified, these findings primarily reflect performance in HIV-1 infection. Within these limitations, recomLine represents a reliable confirmatory alternative that may improve interpretive clarity and reducing ambiguous results in routine HIV-1 diagnostic workflows.
