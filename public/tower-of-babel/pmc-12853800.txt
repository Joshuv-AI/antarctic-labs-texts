Mapping lactation education in health care curricula in Belgium: a quantitative observational study
Authors: Hannelore Permentier, Jill Reyskens, An Eerdekens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreastfeeding is widely acknowledged as essential for infant health and maternal well-being. Yet, breastfeeding rates in Belgium remain below global targets. In Flanders, only 36.4% of infants receive any breastfeeding at six months, and national figures remain unavailable. Professional support is vital to initiate and sustain breastfeeding, and this depends on adequate, multidisciplinary education for healthcare providers. The extent to which lactation is integrated into Belgian health-related higher education curricula is unclear. This study explores current practices and identifies areas for improvement while positioning findings within a broader international context.MethodsA quantitative observational study was conducted in January – March 2025 using a structured questionnaire developed in Dutch, French, and German. The survey targeted curricula in nursing, midwifery, and allied health professions across 29 Belgian higher education institutions. A total of 124 program coordinators were contacted. The study assessed the presence, structure, and depth of lactation education.ResultsAmong contacted programs, 45 responded (36%) and 29 fully completed the questionnaire (64% of respondents). Lactation education duration varied from 30 min to 40 h. Less than half of the programs addressed internationally recognized frameworks such as the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative or Mother-Friendly care. Respondents consistently identified midwives as essential breastfeeding experts, while other professions were less emphasized—despite broad agreement on the need for interdisciplinary approaches.ConclusionsLactation education across Belgian health care curricula is fragmented and varies greatly in scope and quality. The absence of unified standards risks inconsistent breastfeeding support and undermines efforts to promote optimal infant feeding practices. These findings reflect a wider international challenge. As breastfeeding remains a global public health priority, establishing coherent, evidence-based educational frameworks across disciplines and borders is critical. This study adds to the international dialogue by identifying systemic gaps and reinforcing the importance of collaboration in strengthening professional training related to lactation.Keywords: Lactation education, Health care curricula, Breastfeeding support, Midwifery training, Global public health

Background

In Flanders, the Dutch-speaking part of Belgium, 82.6% of newborns are breastfed on their first day of life. However, breastfeeding rates decline steeply over time: by six months, only 36.4% of infants are breastfed, and a mere 10% receive exclusive breastfeeding [1]. These figures reveal a substantial gap between the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendations and actual practice. Globally, exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months reached 48% in 2023, approaching the WHO target of 50% by 2025 [2]. The Flemish data illustrate the challenges of sustaining breastfeeding in high-income countries, where initiation rates are high but continuation and exclusivity remain limited. This discrepancy underscores the need for targeted strategies to support mothers in maintaining breastfeeding, aligning national practice with international health priorities. Early discontinuation is often attributed to concerns over maternal and infant health, practical challenges such as pumping, and notably, return to work, which is a leading reason for cessation. Post-return breastfeeding rates are estimated at only 25% globally [3, 4]. Support from healthcare professionals has proven to be critical in helping mothers navigate these challenges [5].

Research emphasizes the importance of consistent guidance, breastfeeding education, and practical support in improving breastfeeding outcomes, especially when paired with early discharge from maternity care [6]. Effective promotion of breastfeeding requires multidisciplinary collaboration grounded in physiological and evidence-based approaches. Initiatives like the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) have demonstrated substantial benefits for breastfeeding initiation, exclusivity, and duration [7]. Integrating breastfeeding education into healthcare curricula has also been linked to improved outcomes [8]. Despite these gains, a structural, science-based approach to breastfeeding education has not yet been established in healthcare training programs. Inconsistent advice, often stemming from inadequate professional preparation, continues to hinder effective breastfeeding support [9]. Furthermore, international studies highlight wide variation and lack of standardization in educational content and evaluation methods across institutions, leading to insufficient professional knowledge and confidence [10]. The purpose of this study is to explore the scope, structure, and consistency of breastfeeding education within nursing, midwifery, and allied health programmes in Belgian higher education. The integration of lactation-related content into professional curricula plays a critical role in preparing healthcare providers to support breastfeeding effectively. Despite international recognition of breastfeeding as a public health priority, the educational approaches to human lactation remain highly variable across disciplines and institutions. This variability may contribute to inconsistent support for breastfeeding mothers and hinder efforts to promote optimal infant feeding practices. By mapping current curricular practices, this study aims to identify systemic gaps and inform future strategies for strengthening professional training in breastfeeding support. The findings are positioned within a broader international context, contributing to the ongoing dialogue on evidence-based, interdisciplinary education in maternal and child health.

Methods

Study design and scope

This study aimed to map the structure, content, and consistency of breastfeeding education across healthcare curricula in Belgian higher education institutions .A quantitative observational study was conducted in January – March 2025 to examine the structure and content of breastfeeding education within the core curricula of healthcare training programs in Belgian higher education through a self-developed online questionnaire. Due to Belgium’s decentralized education and breastfeeding policies—governed separately by the Flemish, French, and German-speaking communities—a nationwide, multilingual approach was essential to capture the full educational landscape. Therefore, the survey was available in Dutch, French, and German to ensure coverage across Belgium’s three linguistic communities and to enable intercommunity analysis of lactation education.

A total of 29 institutions were contacted (20 university colleges and 9 universities), of which 124 program coordinators were invited to participate. In Belgium, universities are research‑intensive institutions offering academic degrees up to PhD level, while university colleges (hogescholen/hautes écoles) focus on professional bachelor’s programmes and applied training, comparable to universities of applied sciences in other countries. The questionnaire was distributed to program coordinators or designated faculty members responsible for curriculum content in nursing, midwifery, and allied health programmes. When direct contact details were unavailable, inquiries were directed to the general administrative offices of the relevant faculties, which then assigned the most appropriate respondent. Thus, the answers reflect the perspectives of individuals with responsibility for curriculum design and/or delivery within their institution.

Instrument

To assess how breastfeeding education is integrated into healthcare curricula, a structured questionnaire was developed based on the content of the International Board Certified Lactation Consultant (IBCLC) training by the study authors. Given the national scope of the study, the questionnaire was developed in Belgium’s three official languages. The primary version was drafted in Dutch. A French version was created based on internal language proficiency and subsequently underwent backward translation using DeepL Translate. Final validation was performed by French-speaking experts and a certified IBCLC active in the French Community. The German version was fully translated via DeepL Translate and reviewed for linguistic and content accuracy by German-speaking experts affiliated with the University Hospital of Basel. The questionnaire underwent expert review to ensure face and content validity across linguistic and disciplinary contexts. All contributors to the survey design are acknowledged in the manuscript. Following finalization, the questionnaire was distributed via email. Each institution received an invitation to participate, including a hyperlink to the Google Forms survey and an attached information and consent form. The survey was open from 10 March to 31 March 2025. Reminder emails and follow-up phone calls were used to encourage participation among non-respondents. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 30). Descriptive statistics were applied to dichotomous, nominal, and ordinal variables. Missing data were excluded listwise. The dataset included dichotomous, nominal, and ordinal variables, which were reported in both absolute and relative terms. The primary outcome measure was the structure and content of breastfeeding education within the core curricula of healthcare training programs in Belgian higher education. Results were presented at the national level, and further stratified by professional discipline, linguistic community, and region. To ensure institutional anonymity, individual institutions were not identifiable in the published findings.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University Leuven (reference number MP033085). All participating institutions provided signed informed consent outlining the study’s objectives and data handling procedures. Institutional consent was obtained prior to survey distribution. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time without justification. During the study, all survey data were stored securely on password-protected computers with two-factor authentication. Data were pseudonymized. All procedures complied with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applicable within the European Union.

Institutional characteristics, programmes and curriculum hours

A total of 29 higher education institutions participated in the survey, comprising 2 universities (7%) and 27 university colleges (93%). The majority belonged to the Dutch‑speaking Community (76%), followed by the French‑speaking (21%) and German‑speaking (3%) communities. Breastfeeding education was most commonly integrated into Midwifery (n = 12) and Nursing (n = 9) programmes, with smaller numbers in Nutrition and Dietetics, Speech Therapy, Medicine, and Pediatrics. The number of hours varied substantially, with Midwifery reporting a median of 20 h (range: 6–40 h) and Nursing only 1.5 h (range: 0–4 h). Nutrition and Dietetics reported a median of 3 h, while Medicine and Pediatrics reported 1.5 and 20 h respectively. In‑person lectures were the most common teaching format (90%), followed by practical sessions (41%) and self‑study (35%). Exams were the primary evaluation method (86%), while 17% reported no formal assessment. Discussion

Overview and alignment with study objectives

This study aimed to examine the structure, scope, and consistency of breastfeeding education within healthcare curricula in Belgian higher education. The findings reveal substantial variation in how lactation is taught across disciplines, institutions, and linguistic communities. While midwifery programmes consistently offer more extensive and integrated breastfeeding education, other healthcare programmes—particularly nursing and allied health—demonstrate limited curricular attention to this topic. While the study included institutions from all three language communities, the modest response rate limits the generalizability of the findings. Interpretations regarding national representativeness should therefore be made with caution.

Disciplinary disparities and fragmentation

Midwifery curricula approach breastfeeding from a broad, interdisciplinary perspective, embedding it within modules on perinatal care, nutrition, pharmacology, and postpartum support. In contrast, nursing programmes often allocate minimal instructional time and frame breastfeeding within a more clinical or pathological context. This disparity may reflect differing professional expectations, but it also raises concerns about the preparedness of non-midwifery professionals who frequently interact with breastfeeding mothers.

The limited inclusion of lactation education in allied health programmes such as nutrition, speech therapy, and medicine further underscores the fragmented nature of training. Despite the multidisciplinary nature of maternal and infant care, breastfeeding education remains siloed, with midwives bearing disproportionate responsibility for support and guidance.

Instructor qualifications and educational depth

The study also highlights inconsistencies in instructor qualifications. While many educators have backgrounds in midwifery or lactation consultancy, others lack specific training in breastfeeding. This heterogeneity may compromise the scientific rigor and practical relevance of the education provided. Notably, only a small proportion of instructors reported direct clinical experience in breastfeeding support. Although no institution indicated “no experience,” the limited number with hands-on lactation expertise underscores a critical gap that may hinder the delivery of high-quality, practice-oriented education. The absence of standardized teaching qualifications or competencies in lactation education reflects a broader gap in professional development frameworks. These findings align with international literature that identifies a lack of standardization in breastfeeding education as a barrier to effective support. Structured training has been shown to improve provider knowledge, attitudes, and clinical skills, ultimately enhancing breastfeeding outcomes. However, lactation remains underrepresented in many medical and health curricula, often overshadowed by topics perceived as more clinically urgent [10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that implementation of the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) significantly improves breastfeeding outcomes and institutional practices [7, 11–13]. Yet, the integration of BFHI principles into educational curricula remains inconsistent, and the limited clinical experience among instructors further highlights the need for reform. It is noteworthy that only three respondents identified lactation consultants as the professional group best equipped to support mothers in their breastfeeding journey. This relatively low rate likely reflects the Belgian educational context, where lactation consultancy is not a standard component of undergraduate curricula but rather a postgraduate specialization. As the majority of respondents represented midwifery programmes, it is unsurprising that midwives were most frequently identified as breastfeeding experts. Nevertheless, the limited recognition of lactation consultants highlights a potential gap in interdisciplinary training and suggests that their expertise may be underutilized in the current educational framework.

Gendered gaps

Although gender was not the central focus of this study, it remains an important contextual factor in understanding how breastfeeding education is positioned within healthcare curricula. Breastfeeding, as a biologically female and socially complex process, is frequently marginalized in curricula that prioritize acute care and biomedical interventions [14, 15]. Acknowledging this dynamic is essential when interpreting curricular gaps and advocating for more inclusive and equitable training frameworks [16]. Moreover, the responsibility for breastfeeding should not rest solely with the mother. Lactation must be framed as a shared societal responsibility, supported and promoted by governments, public health systems, healthcare institutions, and educational bodies. In current practice, this responsibility remains disproportionately maternal [17].

Limitations and response bias

A key limitation of this study is the modest response rate, which may indicate limited institutional prioritization of breastfeeding education. The possibility of response bias—wherein institutions with stronger interest in lactation education were more likely to participate—must be acknowledged. As such, the findings may not fully capture the perspectives of less engaged stakeholders. Future research should explore institutional attitudes and barriers to curricular integration in greater depth. Another limitation of this study is its exclusive focus on formal higher education curricula. Globally, breastfeeding counseling is often delivered through postgraduate or in-service training, including WHO-endorsed programmes. These forms of continuing education were not examined in this study. As such, the findings reflect only the foundational training provided prior to professional practice, and may not capture the full scope of lactation education available to healthcare providers. Future research should explore the integration and impact of in-service training to provide a more comprehensive understanding of professional preparedness.

Recommendations for reform

To address the systemic gaps identified, we recommend the development of multidisciplinary, evidence-based guidelines for breastfeeding education in higher education. These should be aligned with international frameworks such as those proposed by the Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine [18], while remaining adaptable to national and linguistic contexts. Such guidelines should define core competencies, minimum instructional hours, and recommended teaching formats across disciplines. Moreover, breastfeeding education should be framed as a shared professional responsibility, not one confined to midwifery. All healthcare providers who interact with mothers and infants—whether in clinical, community, or educational settings—should be equipped with foundational knowledge and practical skills to support breastfeeding effectively.

Conclusion

Breastfeeding is a fundamental aspect of maternal and child health, and its promotion requires coordinated, interdisciplinary support. This study underscores the urgent need for structural reform in healthcare education to ensure consistent, high-quality lactation training across all relevant programmes. By establishing coherent educational standards and fostering collaborative approaches, institutions can better prepare future professionals to uphold breastfeeding as a public health priority.
