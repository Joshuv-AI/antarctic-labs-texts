Continuous nonlinear adaptive experimental design with gradient flow
Ruhui Jin, Qin Li, Stephen Mussmann, Stephen J. Wright

Abstract
In computational inverse problems, the optimal experimental design (OED) problem seeks the best locations in time and space at which to take measurements. We investigate the nonlinear OED problem in the context of continuously-indexed design space for the measurements. In contrast to traditional approaches that select experiments from a finite measurement set, a continuous design space is often a better reflection of practical experimental options, where there is considerable flexibility concerning where and when to take measurements. The continuously-indexed space introduces computational challenges, and we address them by employing gradient-flow and optimal transport techniques, complemented by an adaptive strategy for bi-level optimization. Numerical results on the Lorenz 63 system and Schrödinger equation demonstrate that our solver identifies good measurement times / locations and achieves improved reconstruction of unknown parameters in inverse problems.

Introduction

In the mathematical models of physical systems, unknown parameters in the differential equations can be inferred from carefully chosen measurements of the system.
An optimization formulation is often used to reconstruct the unknown parameter
[
54
,
30
]
.
This formulation can be stated generically as follows:

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}:=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma};\rho]\,\equiv\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})-\text{data}(\theta)\right)^{2}\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta\,,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the parameter to be recovered and the other elements of the formulation are described in the rest of this paragraph.
The notation
\theta
indicates the design variable, selected from a space
\Omega
of possible experimental design choices.
Specifically,
\theta
describes the configuration of the experiment, for instance, source and detector locations and observation times.
Often,
\theta
is naturally “continuously indexed," as usually seen in regression models
[
40
,
38
]
and medical imaging
[
41
,
45
]
.
For instance, the time at which a measurement is taken can be
any
time within a certain interval, not just one of a finite number of choices.

\rho
denotes a probability measure over
\Omega
that guides selection of experiments.
Empirically, more measurements are selected for a value
\theta
when its density
\rho(\theta)
is larger.
The formulation (
1
) incorporates a Parameter-to-Output (PtO) map
\mathcal{M},
where
\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})
gives the output at measurement index
\theta\in\Omega
when the system parameter is
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
Corresponding to this map is the experimental measurement
\text{data}(\theta)
which in contrast to
\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, may contain measurement noise.
Specifically, we assume that
\text{data}(\theta)
is generated via ground-truth parameters
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}}
with additive random noise
\epsilon
, that is,
\text{data}(\theta)=\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}})+\epsilon
.

When the design measure
\rho
is fixed, the solution of (
1
) is a parameter
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
that finds the smallest weighted
L^{2}
mismatch between the output predicted by the model and the actual observations.
This value
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
is taken to be a good approximation to the ground-truth parameter
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}}
.
Reconstruction quality depends in general on the choice of
\rho
.
Considering that experimental data and field measurements are often expensive or time-consuming to collect, it is crucial to choose
\rho
in the most effective possible way.
In this paper, we develop an efficient solver to find the optimal design
\rho
that yields high-quality reconstruction of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
at low cost.
This
optimal experimental design (OED)
problem
[
6
,
48
,
32
]
has broad applications in clinical
[
29
,
12
]
, engineering
[
12
]
and other natural sciences.
A critical aspect of OED is the optimality criterion that indicates desirability of a given
\rho
, such as A-optimality and D-optimality
[
39
]
.

A major feature of our work is that we allow the design variable
\theta
to be continuously indexed, so that the design space
\Omega
can represent objects such as spatial, temporal, or angular coordinates.
Consequently,
\Omega
is a set of uncountably many configurations.
This formulation differs significantly from the classical setting in which
|\Omega|<\infty
, where
\rho
can be represented by a finite-dimensional vector.
Our formulation requires
\rho
to belong to a space
\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\cdot)
, which is supported on a continuous domain
\Omega
:

\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)=\left\{\rho~\Big|~\rho(\theta)\geq 0,\forall~\theta\in\Omega,~\int_{\Omega}\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta=1,~\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta<\infty\right\}\,.

(2)

Our optimal design problem is thus formulated as the following bilevel optimization problem, with the outer level being a minimization problem over
\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
and inner layer minimizing over
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
:

\rho^{\text{opt}}:=\arg\min_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]~~\text{being~the~optimum~of~\eqref{eqn: continuous loss}}\,.

(3)

Here the functional
F
is a certain criterion that measures the quality of the experimental design.
We use such classical criteria as the A- and D-optimal designs
[
39
]
, both of which are defined in terms of the spectrum of Hessian matrix of the loss function (
1
).
Loosely speaking, a better-conditioned Hessian leads to more stable reconstruction against measurement error, due to local strong convexity in (
1
).

The continuous OED problem (
3
) raises several important issues.

•

The outer loop in (
3
) is an optimization posed over the probability measure space
\textbf{Pr}_{2}
over the continuous space
\Omega
.
Since
\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
is an infinite-dimensional function space, we cannot use implementations of gradient descent over Euclidean or Sobolev spaces in a straightforward way.
We instead propose a modified gradient-based optimization solver suited to
\textbf{Pr}_{2}
.

•

The bilevel form of (
3
) becomes difficult to handle when the PtO map
\mathcal{M}
is nonlinear.
In a brute-force computation, each outer-loop update requires the inner loop
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
to be obtained by solving (
1
), which can be costly.
We develop a solver that avoids the need to perform this inner-loop optimization repeatedly.

In recent years, there has been significant progress in optimal transport
[
21
]
and gradient flow
[
50
]
.
Techniques for optimizing over
\textbf{Pr}_{2}
(
2
) have been developed and can be applied directly to continuous OED (
3
).
By applying the implicit function theorem, we can pass the dynamics of
\rho
to that of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
, eliminating the need to solve the inner-loop optimization for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
at each iteration.
A combination of these two strategies leads to an efficient bilevel optimization solver for the design problem (
3
).

There is a rich literature on the optimal design problem, which we sketch briefly in Section
1.1
, followed by an outline of our contributions in Section
1.2
.
Our method combines the classical optimal design and the novel gradient flow technique; we discuss these elements in Section
2
.
Section
3
describes the new developments of this work.
We expand on the formulation (
3
) and present first the brute-force computational method and then our approach.
Numerical tests are presented in Sections
4
and
Section

5
for the Lorenz 63 system and the Schrödinger model, respectively.

1.2
Our contributions

In this paper we develop a computational scheme for continuous nonlinear optimal design.
To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to address nonlinear OED by optimizing a continuous design distribution
\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
.
Our bilevel approach alternates between optimization of the continuous design measure
\rho
and optimization of the inference parameters
\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Gradient flow is used in conjunction with the Monte-Carlo particle method, which captures the design measure
\rho
, facilitating the search for those measurements that carry maximal information volume.
We describe both “brute-force" and relaxed one-step approaches for updating
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, the difference being in how accurately we solve the inner problem of the bilevel formulation.
These two strategies are specified in
Algorithm

1
(
Section

3.2
) and
Algorithm

2
(
Section

3.3
).
The current work can be viewed as a nonlinear extension of the paper
[
37
]
, which addresses the case of continuous
linear
OED models.
Nonlinearity requires us to add an inner layer of optimization for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
(
1
).

We provide computational results for
our algorithms on various nonlinear inverse problem models, including the Lorenz 63 dynamical system
[
44
]
(
Section

4
) and the steady-state Schrödinger equation
[
56
]
(
Section

5
).
Besides achieving the best design criteria, the optimal design measures reveal patterns that have interesting correspondences to features of the physical systems.

2
Preliminaries

We review briefly two main elements of our work: Optimal experimental design (specifically the A- and D-optimal design) and Wasserstein gradient flow.

2.1
Optimal design for linear models

In the linear setting, OED is often formulated via a data matrix, with the OED goal being to select matrix rows that are most valuable for computing the solutions.
The linear model is

\mathcal{M}(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,,

(5)

where the “matrix"
\bf A
may have infinitely many (possibly uncountably many) rows, as when the index
\theta
is continuously valued.
The task here is to select a weighted subset of the rows in
\bf A
that allows
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
to be recovered stably via linear inversion.

Consider the unknowns
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a design measure
\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
. Similar to (
4
), supposing that we reweight the experiment
{\bf A}(\theta,:)
with the density
\rho(\theta)
, the reconstruction problem (
1
) becomes
\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\Omega}({\bf A}(\theta,:)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\text{data}(\theta))^{2}\rho(\theta)\differential\theta
has an explicit analytical solution:

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{r}[\rho]\,,

(6)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)^{\top}\text{data}(\theta)\rho(\theta)\differential\theta

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since both
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]
and
\mathbf{r}[\rho]
have the form of expectations, one can employ Monte-Carlo sampling to approximate them numerically.

Uncertainties and errors in
\text{data}(\theta)
are amplified by application of the matrix inverse
(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}
.
Recalling that we assume that
\data(\theta)={\bf A}(\theta,:)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}}+\epsilon
with Gaussian noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\delta^{2})
, we have that

\text{data}(\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{A}(\theta,:)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}},\delta^{2}\right)
for all
\theta
.
Moreover, the value
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
obtained from (
6
) is also a Gaussian random variable with a modified covariance:
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}},\delta^{2}\,(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\right)
.
To minimize the sensitivity of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
to perturbations in data, it is crucial to choose
\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
so that
({\bf A^{\top}A}[\rho])^{-1}
is “small."
Two popular OED design criteria, known as A-optimal and D-optimal designs, quantify “smallness" of
({\bf A^{\top}A}[\rho])^{-1}
in different ways: They optimize the trace and determinant of the variance matrix, respectively.
We have

\displaystyle\text{A-optimal:}\quad\rho^{A}

\displaystyle:=\arg\min_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{A}[\rho]\equiv\Tr\left((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho])^{-1}\right)\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\text{D-optimal:}\quad\rho^{D}

\displaystyle:=\arg\max_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{D}[\rho]\equiv\log(\text{Det}(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}[\rho]))\,.

(7b)

Details are discussed in
[
37
, Section 3.1]
.

In this paper, we look for the counterpart of this linear design scheme for a nonlinear map
\mathcal{M}
.
When
\mathcal{M}
has the linear form (
5
), we have
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma})={\bf A}^{\top}(\theta,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the minimization problem in (
1
) has Hessian
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]
.
For the inverse problem (
1
) with
nonlinear

\mathcal{M}
, we are thus motivated to consider the Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma})
,
using this quantity in place of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}[\rho]
in the OED criteria.

2.2
Gradient flow and particle method

As seen in (
3
), the OED problem is a constrained optimization problem over the probability measure space
\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
.
It calls for a numerical strategy that honors the geometry of
\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
.

Given the optimization problem

\min_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F[\rho]\,,

a gradient-based strategy demands a proper definition of the “gradient".
We can equip the probability measure space
\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)
with the Wasserstein-2 metric, for which distance can be defined using the following standard definition:

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\,\differential\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/2}.

(8)

The formula for Wasserstein-2 gradient flow is thus

\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla_{W_{2}}F[\rho]=\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho]}{\delta\rho}\right)\,,

(9)

where
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the Fréchet derivative, and
\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
is usually termed the velocity field.
A nice property of this formula is
that it can be represented easily by the motion of discrete particles.
Denoting the empirical measure on selected particles
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by

\rho=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta_{i}}\,,

(10)

the McKean-Vlasov formulation states that each particle
\theta_{i}
moves according to the flow:

\dot{\theta}_{i}=-\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}(\theta_{i}),\,\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(11)

The complication may arise that (
10
) is a measure-valued solution, so that (
9
) has to be interpreted in the weak sense; see
[
5
]
for a discussion of this point.
Although the numerical computation is done via finite particle simulation, the
W_{2}
gradient flow (
15
) is fundamental to solving our continuous OED problem and encompasses both continuous and discrete types of probability distribution
\rho
.

3
Methodology for nonlinear design

We now describe our approaches for the nonlinear OED problem (
3
), with the objective
F
defined by nonlinear extensions of the familiar criteria (
7
) from the linear OED setting.
Gradient flow (described in
Section

2.2
) is used for the outer-level optimization in (
3
); we provide the formulae for calculating the gradients.
The Monte Carlo particle method is instrumental in the outer-level calculation, with particle velocities (
11
) being evaluated at the solution
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
of the inner-level optimization of (
3
).
We describe two approaches, a “brute-force" approach (
Algorithm

1
) in which the inner-level optimization problem is solved accurately, and a “streamlined" approach (
Algorithm

2
) in which just one step of gradient descent is taken at the inner level, for each outer-level update.

3.1
Nonlinear design formulation

The Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]
for the mapping
\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]&=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{C}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\\
\mbox{where}\quad\mathcal{C}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma})&=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,.\end{split}

(12)

As mentioned above, the matrix
\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]
(sometimes also referred to in this context as the Gauss-Newton Hessian) is a natural extension of the matrix
{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf A}[\rho]
that arises in the case of linear OED, which is the basis of the A-optimality and D-optimality criteria.
These criteria can be extended to the nonlinear case as follows (cf. (
7
)):

F^{A}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]\equiv\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]\right)^{-1}\,,\quad F^{D}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]\equiv\log(\text{Det} \left(\I[\rho; \vecsigma] \right))\,.

(13)

When
\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]
is evaluated at the inverse problem solution
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
(
1
), it is proportional to the variance of error in the solution when data is polluted by Gaussian noise.
(The proof of this claim follows the linear case of
Section

2.1
.)
The nonlinear experimental design problems can thus be written as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{A-optimal:}\quad\rho^{A}

\displaystyle:=\arg\min_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{A}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]\,,

(14a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{D-optimal:}\quad\rho^{D}

\displaystyle:=\arg\max_{\rho\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}(\Omega)}F^{D}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]\,,

(14b)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
is obtained from the reconstruction (
1
).
Because the nonlinear OED criteria (
14
) depends only on the Fisher information at the global optimal point
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast}[\rho]
of the loss function, it captures the local sensitivity close to this point.

3.2
Brute-force algorithm

In our first solver for the nonlinear OED problem (
14
), we apply the gradient flow method (
9
) to the outer-level problem, yielding the formula

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]}{\delta\rho}\right)\,,

(15)

where
F
denotes
F^{A}
or
F^{D}
from (
13
), or some other criterion.
Note that evaluation of the right-hand side requires knowledge of the solution
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
of the inner-level problem (
1
).
To obtain a tight approximation to this point, for each each iterate
\rho
arising from the outer-level gradient-flow process, we run a fixed number
T^{\prime}
of gradient-descent iterations for (
1
).
We stress that (
15
) may be highly nonlinear, as both
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
and the velocity field
\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]}{\delta\rho}
depend on
\rho
in nontrivial ways.
For the two design objectives in (
13
), the velocity fields are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-2\left(\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)(\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}])^{-2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{\emph{\text{dim}}(\Omega)},

(16a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)

\displaystyle=2\,\left(\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)(\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}])^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{\emph{\text{dim}}(\Omega)}\,,

(16b)

where
\mathcal{I}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the Fisher information matrix of (
12
).
See
[
37
, Proposition 3.1]
for details of this calculation in the case of linear
\mathcal{M}
.

To implement gradient flow PDE (
15
), we use the particle method described in
Section

2.2
, restated here:

\rho=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta_{i}}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\dot{\theta}_{i}=-\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]]}{\delta\rho}(\theta_{i}),\;\;i=1,2,\dotsc,N.

(17)

A forward-Euler time discretization for (
17
) with step size
\Delta t
yields

\begin{split}\theta_{i}^{t+1}&=\theta_{i}^{t}+\Delta t\,\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F[\rho^{t};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho^{t}]]}{\delta\rho}(\theta^{t}_{i})\,~~\text{for}~i=1,\dots,N,\\
\mbox{where}\;\;\rho^{t}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta_{i}^{t}}.\end{split}

(18)

By using the particles
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we can restate the velocity fields (
16
) as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{A}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)=-2\Big(\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\Big)\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{-2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{\emph{\text{dim}}(\Omega)},

(19a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\delta F^{D}[\rho;\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}{\delta\rho}(\theta)=2\Big(\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\Big)\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{\emph{\text{dim}}(\Omega)}\,,

(19b)

where
\hat{\mathcal{I}}
is the discrete version of Fisher information (
12
):

{\hat{\mathcal{I}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{C}(\theta_{i};\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

These formulae represent a nonlinear extension of
[
37
, Proposition 3.4]
, where
{\bf A}(\theta,:)
in
[
37
]
is the gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})
in the linear case.

The complete algorithm is stated next.
The outer-level iteration appears in steps 2-3, whereas the inner-level iteration is steps 4-8.

)) and corresponding inference parameter

3.3
Streamlined algorithm

A major part of the computation in
Algorithm

1
is the inner-loop computation of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast,t+1}
.
On later iterations, when the step from
\rho^{t}
to
\rho^{t+1}
is small, we would expect that few steps of the inner loop would be required to find a sufficiently accurate approximation to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast,t+1}
.
Accordingly, we describe a streamlined approach in which the inner iteration is reduced to a single step, which is an Euler-type step based on the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
, using the implicit function theorem to capture its dependence on
\rho
.

To derive the approach, we note that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast}[\rho]
satisfies first-order conditions for (
1
), which are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho];\rho]=2\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})\,\left(\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})-\text{data}(\theta))\right)\,\rho(\theta)\,\differential\theta={\bf{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This equation implicitly links
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast}[\rho]
with
\rho
.
To outline our use of the implicit function theorem
[
42
]
, consider
f(x,y(x))=0
, where
f
is a smooth map.
By differentiating through with respect to
x
, we obtain
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dx}=0
.
Multiplying by
\frac{dx}{dt}
and using the chain rule, we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}=0
, which we rearrange to obtain
\dot{y}=-(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y})^{-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dot{x}
.
Setting
x=\rho
,
y=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\ast}[\rho]
and
f=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho];\rho]
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\rho]\right)^{-1}\,\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\rho])}{\delta\rho}(\theta)\,\partial_{t}\rho(\theta)\differential\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(21)

Here
\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\rho]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the Hessian of the loss function (
1
) with input
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
.
The formula (
21
) can be used to evolve
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
in time after each forward timestep has been taken in
\rho
.

As in
Algorithm

1
, we represent the gradient flow (
15
) using the particle method with (
18
).
The particle form of formula (

) is

\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}}=-\left(\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\{\theta_{j}\})\right)^{-1}\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,\text{Loss}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\{\theta_{j}\}))}{\partial\theta_{i}}\,\dot{\theta_{i}}.

(22)

Here we denote
\text{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\rho]
by
\text{Loss}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\{\theta_{j}\})
for the empirical measure
\rho=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\delta_{\theta_{j}}
.
The particle-based evolution equation (

) takes the velocity of the particles
\dot{\theta}_{j}
given by (
19

The Hessian and gradient terms in (

\displaystyle\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\text{Loss}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\{\theta_{j}\})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta_{j};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{\top}(\theta_{j};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})

(23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle~~~+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta_{j};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})\left(\mathcal{M}(\theta_{j};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})-\text{data}(\theta_{j})\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\text{Loss}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*};\{\theta_{j}\})}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle~~~+\frac{2}{N}\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta_{i};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})(\mathcal{M}(\theta_{i};\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})-\text{data}(\theta_{i})),\;\;i=1,2,\dotsc,N.

4
Numerical experiment: Lorenz 63 system

We demonstrate the performance of our numerical algorithms for optimal design based on the Lorenz 63 model
[
44
]
.
This physical system is a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection that is nonlinear and aperiodic.
Though deterministic in our set up, the model solution exhibits chaotic behavior, which is why it is widely studied in many scientific domains.

Suppose we can take measurements along the trajectory of the solution to infer the parameters in the model. At which time points should we probe the model so that the collected data yields the most stable recovery?
We apply the proposed algorithms of
Section

3
to thoroughly address this experimental design question.

Model formulation and design.

The Lorenz 63 model is three-dimensional and is defined by the following nonlinear differential equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 3.00003pt\displaystyle\frac{\differential x}{\differential\tau}=\alpha(y-x)\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\displaystyle\frac{\differential y}{\differential\tau}=x(\gamma-z)-y\\
\displaystyle\frac{\differential z}{\differential\tau}=xy-\beta z,\end{array}\right.

(25)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~(d=3)
are the unknown parameters to be inferred, and
x,y,z
are the state variables to be observed.
The variables
x,y,z
stand respectively for the convection rate and horizontal and vertical temperature variations.
We use
\tau
to denote the time variable in the Lorenz model.
The model parameters
\alpha,\gamma,\beta
are proportional to Prandtl number, Rayleigh number, and dimension of the physical layer, respectively.
The ground-truth parameters are
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{true}}=(\alpha_{\mathrm{true}},\gamma_{\mathrm{true}},\beta_{\mathrm{true}})=(10,28,8/3)
.
The initial values of the variables are fixed as
x(0)=1.5
,
y(0)=-1.5
, and
z(0)=25
.
Numerical simulation of the model (
25
) is conducted using forward Euler with step-size
\Delta\tau=10^{-4}
.

Next, we describe experimental design for Lorenz model.
To reconstruct the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)
, the PtO map
\mathcal{M}
(
1
) is defined as follows:

\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,,\quad\text{with }\theta=(c,\tau)\in\Omega=\{x,y,z\}\times[0,3],\,\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Here the design variable
\theta=(c,\tau)
has two layers of choices: the notion
c
indicates which one of the three state variables
x
,
y
, or
z
is to be measured, and
\tau
denotes the corresponding observation time from the interval
[0,3]
.
In other words,
\mathcal{M}
captures one of the quantities
x(\tau)
or
y(\tau)
or
z(\tau)
obtained from the model simulation (
25
) based on the given parameters
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)
.
The design goal therefore is to select the best time points at which to observe the state variables. Consequently, we express the design measure
\rho
as a combination of marginal distributions
\rho_{x},\rho_{y},\rho_{z}\in\textbf{Pr}_{2}([0,3])
, all supported on the observation time interval.
Further details about computation of Lorenz model design are given in
A
.

In

5
Numerical experiment: Schrödinger equation

We turn next to a simplified Schrödinger equation model
[
25
]
, for which we use
Algorithm

2
to identify important measurements.
Unlike the Lorenz system, which evolves in time, the simplified Schrödinger equation describes a steady-state solution, with measurements taken
in space
.
In experimental design, we are tasked to find the measurement locations that contain the best information to recover the unknown potential.

Model formulation and design.

The steady state Schrödinger model with zero boundary condition is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u^{\prime\prime}+\sigma\,u=S(x)\,,\quad x\in[0,1]\\
u\Big|_{x=0,1}=0\,,\end{array}\right.

(26)

where we set the domain
x\in[0,1]
.
Different sources
S(x)
are injected into the model and the corresponding model solutions
u(x)
are taken to infer the unknown potential function
\sigma\in L^{2}([0,1])
.
In our numerical experiment, we set the ground-truth potential
\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}
to be a Gaussian mixture; details are given below.

For the purpose of experimental design, we can exploit the form of the source
S(x)
and the detector location. Specifically we can inject source at a location
\theta^{1}\in[0,1]
by setting
S(x)=\delta_{\theta^{1}}
, and measure the solution of the resulting model (
26
), denoted by
u_{\theta^{1}}
, at location
\theta^{2}\in[0,1]
.
The design variable is therefore
\theta=(\theta^{1},\theta^{2})\in\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
and the simulated measurement is in the form:

\mathcal{M}(\theta;\sigma)=u_{\theta^{1}}(\theta^{2})\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Using a classical calculus-of-variable argument, one can compute the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{M}
with respect to
\sigma
to be

\frac{\delta\mathcal{M}(\theta;\sigma)}{\delta\sigma}=u_{\theta^{1}}\cdot v_{\theta^{2}}\,,

where
u_{\theta^{1}}
and
v_{\theta^{2}}
solve the forward and adjoint models, which are obtained from the following equations:

\text{forward}:~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{\theta^{1}}^{\prime\prime}+\sigma\,u_{\theta^{1}}=\delta_{\theta^{1}},&x\in[0,1]\\
u_{\theta^{1}}=0,&x=0,1,\end{array}\right.\quad\text{adjoint}:~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\theta^{2}}^{\prime\prime}+\sigma\,v_{\theta^{2}}=\delta_{\theta^{2}},&x\in[0,1]\\
v_{\theta^{2}}=0,&x=0,1.\end{array}\right.

We use the finite-difference method to solve these equations numerically.
We represent the function
\sigma(x)
as a
d
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
via a basis function set
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
, namely
\sigma(x)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{k}\,\psi_{k}(x),~~\text{with}~\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})^{\top}
. In the discrete setting, the above Fréchet derivative
\frac{\delta\mathcal{M}}{\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
transfers to a gradient vector with respect to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose
k
-th coordinate is

\partial_{\sigma_{k}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)_{k}=\left\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{M}(\theta;\sigma)}{\delta\sigma},\psi_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{\theta^{1}}\cdot v_{\theta^{2}},~\psi_{k}\right\rangle,\quad\text{for}~k=1,\dots,d.

We decompose the domain
[0,1]
into
d
equi-spaced cells and set the basis
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
to be characteristic functions over these cells.
The discrete potentials are
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\equiv\sigma(x_{k})
, where
x_{k}
is the center of the
k
-th cell for
k=1,\dots,d
.
Throughout this section, we set
d=100
.

Algorithm setup.

The streamlined gradient flow method,
Algorithm

2
, alternates between updates for the design particles
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the reconstruction discrete vector
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For both design criteria (A- and D-optimal), we choose a ground truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{true}}
, and define the observation data to be
\text{data}(\theta)=\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}})+\epsilon
, with the additive noise
\epsilon
drawn from
0.1\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
We consider two types of choices for the initial design measure
\rho^{0}
: a uniform distribution on
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
and a warm-start distribution.
Given
\rho^{0}
, the initial reconstruction result
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*,0}
(
1
) is obtained by running the inner loop of
Algorithm

1
until convergence to a critical point, starting from a random guess drawn from
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\sim\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}}+2\,U[0,1]^{100}
.
In one simulation, we randomly draw
N
particles
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to realize the initial design measure
\rho^{0}
.
We conduct
10
independent simulations for each numerical experiment and plot their averaged results in the figures below (with the shading around curve representing the standard deviation of the algorithm runs).

In the main loop of
Algorithm

2
, to reduce the computation burden, we utilize an approximate calculation for the velocity term
\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}
(
22
).
Recognizing that the distance between
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
and the ground-truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{true}}
is small, so that the discrepancy
\mathcal{M}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})-\text{data}(\theta)
is small, we drop the second lines of both formulas (
23
) and (
24
).
By doing so, we avoid the nontrivial calculations of terms
\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}
and
\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}.

6
Conclusions and future directions

Efficient data acquisition is crucial for learning physical systems.
By computing an optimal probability distribution that quantifies the importance of data, we can weight the data accordingly to best extract reconstruction of unknown parameters.
Novel gradient flow and particle simulation methods can calculate experimental designs over probability measure space
\textbf{Pr}_{2}
.
Since most physical models are highly nonlinear, the design measure
\rho
and the vector
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
of parameters to be recovered are co-dependent.
We proposed a bilevel optimization scheme that adaptively solves for
\rho
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}.

The difference between our two algorithmic approaches — brute-force and streamlined — lies in the update of unknowns
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
for a given measure
\rho
.
The streamlined formulation employs the one-step update for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
, reducing computational cost.
We applied these methods to the Lorenz dynamical system and a Schrödinger model.
Our algorithms returned optimal design measures and revealed interesting physics phenomena.

Despite promising empirical performance, the current work still has several limitations described below, which will be the subjects of future studies.

1.

Convergence theory.
The convergence properties of the adaptive gradient flow method (
Algorithm

2
) need to be understood better.
Open problems include the dependence of these properties on the sample size
N
and number of iterations
T
.
The dependence between the design distribution
\rho
and the reconstruction
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
prompts analytical challenges.
Is it possible to examine the convergence of
Algorithm

2
via studying the coupled updating system of
\rho
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}
?
Does evolution of the design measure
\rho
lead to the reconstruction
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}[\rho]
converging to the ground-truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{true}}?

2.

Efficiency improvement.

In our tests, hyperparameters such as
T
and
\Delta t
are tuned manually, but these should be chosen automatically and adaptively in practice.
Moreover, when the sample particle
\theta
gets updated, the algorithm has no further use for the measurement
\text{data}(\theta)
.
Since these observations are costly, can we develop a scheme to make better use of the measurements and recycle them in certain way?

They are all very interesting questions that need to be answered to fully understand the computational and sample complexity of the algorithm and its effectiveness. We leave these questions for future research.

Appendix A
Computation details for the Lorenz model

We provide some computational details concerning the Lorenz 63 model.

The experimental design objectives (
14
) rely on the gradient term
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with respect to the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)
.
This gradient can be computed explicitly as one of the rows of the following
3\times 3
matrix depending on the observable
x,y,z:

J:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\vskip 2.84526pt\frac{\partial x}{\partial\alpha}&\frac{\partial x}{\partial\gamma}&\frac{\partial x}{\partial\beta}\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\frac{\partial y}{\partial\alpha}&\frac{\partial y}{\partial\gamma}&\frac{\partial y}{\partial\beta}\\
\frac{\partial z}{\partial\alpha}&\frac{\partial z}{\partial\gamma}&\frac{\partial z}{\partial\beta}\end{array}\right]\,.

(27)

Differentiating the Lorenz model (
25
) on both sides with respect to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}J=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\alpha&\alpha&0\\
\gamma-z&-1&-x\\
y&x&-\beta\end{array}\right]\cdot J+\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}y-x&0&0\\
0&x&0\\
0&0&-z\\
\end{array}\right]\,.

(28)

Since the initialization for the state variables are fixed, the initial value for these derivatives are all 0, meaning that
J(\tau=0)={\bf 0}
.
Solving this dynamical system gives us the evaluation of
J
for all times
t
.
In
Fig.

25
we plot the trajectory of Lorenz 63 model with three state variables and their corresponding gradients w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, evaluated at the ground-truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{true}}
.
A similar strategy applies to computing the second-order term
\text{Hess}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}
in (
23
).

In the implementation of our particle gradient flow algorithms, we also need the gradients w.r.t. the design variable
\theta
, including the terms
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{M}
and
\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}
. Once the observable
c\in\{x,y,z\}
is determined, the differentiation of
\theta=(c,\tau)
is simplified as the time derivative
\frac{\differential}{\differential\tau}(\cdot)
. Hence
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{M}
and
\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}
can be extracted respectively from the left-hand sides of the dynamical systems (
25
) and (
28
).

) (left) and its gradient terms w.r.t.

) (right).
