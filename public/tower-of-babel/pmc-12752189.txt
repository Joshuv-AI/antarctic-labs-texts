Recombinant attenuated Salmonella vaccine promotes CD8+ T cell-dependent antitumor immunity via IFN-γ-induced IRF-1-mediated upregulation of IL-7
Authors: Jae-Hyeon Jeong, Dae-Joon Kim, Chae-Won Kim, Youngjoo Han, Ah-Ra Jang, Jong-Hwan Park, Nara Tae, Jae-Hee Ahn, Jaewon Cho, Sun Shim Choi, Mi-Na Kweon, Dae Hee Kim, Hyun-Jeong Ko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is a leading cause of mortality worldwide, necessitating the development of novel therapeutic targets and strategies to prevent recurrence and metastasis. In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether a recombinant attenuated Salmonella vaccine (RASV) can stimulate antitumor immunity and prevent cancer progression in vivo.MethodsWe established a mouse model by transplanting cancer cells subcutaneously 4 weeks after oral RASV inoculation and analyzed bone marrow (BM) cells and tumor-infiltrated immune cells by flow cytometry. Further, we established a 4 T-1-induced metastatic cancer model to evaluate the RASV-mediated prevention of tumor metastasis.ResultsAdjuvant RASV significantly reduced tumor growth by enhancing CD8+ T cell activity and inducing changes in BM progenitor cells, which contributed combinatorically to the antitumor effects of RASV by increasing the number of common lymphoid progenitor cells. Moreover, interferon-γ-stimulated genes, including Irf1, were upregulated in the BM of RASV-treated mice, thus mediating IL-7 expression. Furthermore, RASV inoculation prevented lung metastasis in mice with breast cancer.ConclusionsRASV inoculation can promote marked alterations in the BM microenvironment, thus reshaping the anticancer immune response in vivo. Background

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with increasing global incidence attributed to aging populations among other factors [1]. Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, tumor recurrence and metastasis remain clinically challenging [2]. Moreover, current adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with lymph node metastasis after surgical tumor resection shows limited efficacy [3], emphasizing the need for developing innovative therapeutic strategies. Immune surveillance by T cell-mediated and innate immune responses protects against cancer recurrence and metastasis by recognizing and eliminating cancer cells [4–7]. Thus, enhancing immune surveillance by targeting the tumor microenvironment with immunomodulatory agents or immune checkpoint inhibitors can improve immunotherapy outcomes [8].

Interleukin (IL)-7 is a crucial cytokine for the homeostasis, development, and survival of lymphocytes [9]. IL-7 is highly expressed in lymphoid organs, such as the bone marrow (BM), spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes; it is also expressed in nonlymphoid organs, including the liver, lungs, and skin [10]. In the BM, IL-7 promotes the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) into common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) that eventually become T or B cell precursors [11], which then migrate to the thymus or peripheral tissues to complete their development. Indeed, IL-7 is essential for the survival of thymocytes, homeostasis of naïve and memory T cells, and activation of effector T cells [12].

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which are responsible for eliminating virus-infected and cancerous host cells [13], mature in the thymus and acquire antigen-specific functions in secondary lymphoid organs by interacting with antigen-presenting cells [14]. Strategies for enhancing the tumor-specific cytotoxic potential of these cells, including genetic modifications and cytokine treatments, have been investigated to improve CD8+ tumor-killing activity [13]. Moreover, IL-7 was found to enhance CD8+ T cell survival and function [15, 16]. However, the effects of IL-7-mediated preconditioning of the BM on thymic selection, T cell activation, and bystander T cell-driven antitumor responses remain unexplored.

We previously showed that the intratumoral administration of recombinant attenuated Salmonella vaccine (RASV) exerts significant antitumor effects by increasing CD8+ T cell responses [17]. Herein, we investigated whether administering RASV prior to tumor onset promotes anticancer resistance mechanisms. Our data indicates that RASV inoculation enhances anticancer activity in the BM via IL-7-dependent hematopoiesis in association with CLPs, resulting in increased CD8+ T cell responses. Notably, elevated IL-7 expression was associated with enhanced interferon regulatory factor (IRF)-1 activity via interferon (IFN)-γ-induced responses. These findings suggest a potential role for IL-7, post-RASV vaccination, in enhancing anticancer CD8+ T cell activity to prevent tumor recurrence and metastasis through the expansion of CLPs.

Methods

Animal model and bacteria

Six-week-old specific pathogen-free C57BL/6 and BALB/c female mice weighing 16–18 g were purchased from Koatech Co. Ltd. (Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea). C57BL/6 CD45.1 mice originally sourced from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were bred and housed at the Animal Laboratory Center of Kangwon National University under specific pathogen-free conditions. C57BL/6 TLR2-deficient mice were originally sourced from Prof. Shizuo Akira at Osaka University, and C57BL/6 NOD2-deficient mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. All mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups prior to the commencement of the study, and the sample sizes of the control and experimental groups were initially equal. Mice that died due to tumor growth were excluded from the analyses. Tumor volume was measured using a digital caliper, with dimensions recorded in two orthogonal planes: the longest diameter (length, L) and the shortest diameter (width, W). The volume (V) was calculated using the formula V = W2 × L/2, where W and L are expressed in millimeters (mm). All animal protocols were approved and performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kangwon National University (permit numbers: KW-210412–2 and KW-240111–1). RASV χ9241 (ΔpabA1516 ΔpabB232 ΔasdA16 ΔaraBAD23 ΔrelA198::araCPBADlacI (ATG) TT containing pYA3620) was provided by Roy Curtiss, III (Arizona State University, USA). Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) was provided by Sung Jae Shin (Yonsei University, Republic of Korea). RASV was cultured in Luria–Bertani medium at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. Mice were inoculated with either RASV (1 × 109 colony-forming unit [CFU]) or PBS orally and BCG (1 × 106 CFU) subcutaneously. Anti-CD8 (αCD8, 2.43, BioXcell, Lebanon, NH, USA) and anti-IL-7R (αIL-7R, A7R34; BioXcell) antibodies were intraperitoneally administered at 200 and 100 μg/mouse, respectively, after RASV inoculation. After 28 days, the mice were subcutaneously injected with tumor cells (B16F10, Her2/CT26, or MC38). The presence of RASV was examined 4 weeks after inoculation, and no bacteria were detected in the spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes, liver, or BM (Supplementary Fig. 1). All animal studies were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kangwon National University.

Cell lines and culture

The murine melanoma and breast cancer cell lines B16F10 and 4 T-1, respectively, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Human HER-2/neu-expressing CT26 colon carcinoma (Her2/CT26) was developed by transducing CT26 cells using a retroviral vector system [18, 19]. Cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea). Murine BM stromal cell line M2-10B4 was obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB; Seoul, Republic of Korea) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 10 mM HEPES (Welgene), and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Welgene). All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

IL-7 production

A flexible GGGGS linker was used to fuse IL-7 with a Fc backbone by overlap extension PCR. The resulting construct was cloned into the expression vector pCEP4 and confirmed via DNA sequencing. The purified plasmid DNA was transfected into FreeStyle 293-F cells (Gibco) using FectoPro (Polyplus, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cultured for 9 days after transfection; supernatants from the transfected cells were purified by open-column affinity chromatography using Protein A resin (Repligen, Waltham, MA, USA). Elution fractions were collected and dialyzed against PBS using Slide-A-Lyzer cassettes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Protein purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining (iNtRON, Seongnam, Republic of Korea).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

M2-10B4 cells (1 × 107) were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, which was then quenched with 125 mM glycine. These cells were washed with ice-cold PBS thrice and resuspended in chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay buffer (50 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate). The cells were sonicated thrice at 30% amp at 10 s intervals (Fisherbrand Model 120 Sonic Dismembrator, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunoclearing was performed using protein A bead (PRT-7100, Repligen) slurry for 2 h at 4 °C. The slurry was centrifuged at 2000 × g, and the supernatant was reacted with nonspecific IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and rabbit-α-IRF-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, 50 μL of protein A-coated slurry was added for conjugation with the antibodies. After centrifugation, the beads were washed with low-salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl), high-salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl, 500 mM NaCl), LiCl (0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl), and TE (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA) buffers. DNA was eluted using elution buffer (1% SDS, 100 mM NaHCO3). Reverse crosslinking was performed overnight at 65 °C. DNA was purified using a PCR purification kit (Macherey–Nagel, Düren, Germany). The following primers were used to quantify the precipitated DNA: IRF-1-ChIP-F: 5′-TGTGCCCCCAACTAATACGA-3′; IRF-1-ChIP-R: 5′-TGATTAAGAGGGTCACGCCC-3′. The results were calculated using the formula % IP = 100 × 2(Ct of specific Ab−Ct of input).

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in PRO-PREP protein extraction solution (iNtRON) containing 1% protease (GenDEPOT, Altair, TX, USA) and phosphatase inhibitors (GenDEPOT). The cell lysates were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Abbkine, Atlanta, GA, USA) and then resolved by SDS-PAGE. The proteins were transferred onto Immobilon-P PVDF Membranes (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). All antibodies were diluted in 5% skim milk in TBST buffer. Anti-IRF-1 (α-IRF-1) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) was diluted at 1:1000. Anti-β-actin (α-β-actin) mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA) was diluted at 1:5000. Anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Santa Cruz) and anti-mouse IgG-HRP secondary antibodies (ENZO, Farmingdale, NY, USA) were diluted at 1:5000.

Isolation of mononuclear cells from tumor tissues

Tumors were extracted after the mice were euthanized and were minced using C-tubes (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in 5 mL of digestion buffer (RPMI-1640 [Corning]-containing 2% heat-inactivated FBS [Gibco], 10 mM HEPES [Welgene], 1% penicillin–streptomycin [Welgene], 0.01 mg/mL DNase I [Roche, Basel, Switzerland], and 400 U/mL collagenase IV [Worthington, Lakewood, NJ, USA]). The samples were digested for 1 h in a 37 °C, 200 rpm shaking incubator. The collected cells were suspended in 40% of Percoll (Cytiva, MA, USA) and placed on 70% Percoll. The Percoll gradient was generated by centrifugation at 2,000 rpm for 20 min without brakes. Mononuclear cells were isolated from the interphase and used in subsequent experiments. Tumor tissue was homogenized in PBS at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the supernatant, as previously described [20].

Isolation of splenocytes and bone marrow cells

Spleens were extracted from the euthanized mice and were minced on a strainer with 100 μm pore size (SPL, Pocheon, Republic of Korea). Red blood cells (RBCs) were removed using RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). The splenocytes were collected for use in subsequent experiments. To obtain BM cells, femur and tibia were extracted from mice, and the BM was flushed out with PBS and filtered through a strainer (100 μm pore size). After RBC removal, the BM cells were collected for use in subsequent experiments.

Bone marrow transplantation

C57BL/6 WT mice were orally administered PBS or RASV. After 28 days, the mice were euthanized. BM was extracted, and isolated BM cells (1 × 107) were transplanted via intravenous injection into C57BL/6 CD45.1 mice subjected to γ-irradiation (8 Gy) using a Gammacell 40 Exactor (Kanata, Canada).

Differentiation of BM cells into dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages

Isolated BM cells were cultured for 7 days in 20% L929-conditioned growth medium (RPMI-1640 [Corning]-containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS [Gibco], 10 mM HEPES [Welgene], 1% penicillin–streptomycin [Welgene], 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol [Gibco], and 20 μg/mL gentamicin [Gibco]) or GM-CSF (20 ng/mL, Peprotech, New Jersey, US)-containing medium to differentiate into BM-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and BM-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), respectively. The cells (2 × 105) were seeded in 48-well plates and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 100 ng/mL, Merck) for 3 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The supernatant was collected and used for ELISA.

DC vaccination and in vivo CTL assay

For antigen sensitization, differentiated BMDCs from RASV- or PBS-inoculated mice were cultured with 50 μg/mL ovalbumin (OVA; Merck) in the presence of LPS (100 ng/mL, Merck) for 18 h. The sensitized BMDCs (2 × 105) were intravenously injected into mice for vaccination against OVA. After 2 weeks, splenocytes from donor mice were divided into two groups: one pulsed with SIINFEKL peptide (Peptron, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 30 min and the other left unpulsed as a control. For the Her2/CT26 antigen-inoculated mouse model, splenocytes were pulsed with an epitope peptide (TYLPTNASL; Peptron). The splenocytes were labeled with different concentrations of Celltrace Violet (CTV, Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA)—CTVhi (5 μM) for pulsed and CTVlow (0.5 μM) for unpulsed cells—and mixed equally. Mixed splenocytes (5 × 106) were injected intravenously into vaccinated mice. Recipient mice were euthanized 18 h postinjection, and the spleens were extracted. CTV+ cells in excised spleens were analyzed using FACSVerse. The target cell lysis ratio (r) was calculated as (% CTVhi/CTVlow), and the percentage of lysis (%) was calculated as [1–(rpulsed/runpulsed)] × 100.

ELISA

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-10 levels in tumor supernatant and supernatant of LPS-stimulated BMDMs and BMDCs were measured using ELISA kits (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

mRNA quantification

mRNA from BM cells was isolated using an RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands), and cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions. cDNA was amplified using SYBR Green pre-MIX (Promega). Target gene expression was normalized to Actin levels. Primers used for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) were as follows: Actin (Forward, 5′-TGGAATCCTGTGGCATCCATGAAA-3′, Reverse, 5′-TAAAACGCAGCTCAGTAACAGTCCG-3′), Irf1 (Forward, 5′-ATGCCAATCACTCGAATGCG-3′, Reverse, 5′-TTGTATCGGCCTGTGTGAATG-3′), and Il7 (Forward, 5′-ATTGCCCGAATAATGAACCA-3′, Reverse, 5′-ACCAGTGTTTGTGTGCCTTG-3′). All primers were synthesized by Macrogen Inc (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

RNA sequencing

BM cells of PBS- and RASV-inoculated mice were harvested in 500 μL of TRIzol (Invitrogen) and maintained at − 80 °C for further experiments. The frozen samples were delivered to ATG Lifetech Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) for bulk RNA sequencing analysis. Total RNA (250 ng) was reverse transcribed; cDNA synthesis for next-generation sequencing (NGS) library preparation was performed using TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing (50 bp paired-end) was performed on an Illumina NextSeq sequencing platform, yielding an average read depth of ~ 600 M reads per sample, to obtain NGS data. Subsequent analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.3). Read counts were normalized using DESeq2. Criteria for considering differences in gene expression as significant were padj < 0.1 and fold change > 1.5 or < − 1.5.

Flow cytometry

The collected cells were incubated with αCD16/CD32 (2.4G2; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) at room temperature for 10 min to block Fc receptors. The cells were stained by incubating with PBS containing various combinations of fluorophore-labeled antibodies in the dark for 30 min at 4 °C. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail plus Protein Transporter Inhibitor (Invitrogen) for 3 h at 37 °C, under 5% CO2, in growth medium prior to staining with antibodies. The cells were fixed with IC Fixation Buffer (Invitrogen) for cytokine staining and Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (Invitrogen) for Foxp3 staining in the dark for 30 min at 4 °C. The cells were then washed with a permeabilization buffer (Invitrogen). Cytokines were stained with the permeabilization buffer containing fluorophore-conjugated antibodies in the dark for 30 min at 4 °C. The stained cells were analyzed using FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) at the Core Facility for Innovative Cancer Drug Discovery at Kangwon National University. The data were analyzed using the FlowJo Version 10.5.3 software (BD Biosciences). Information on all purchased fluorophore-conjugated antibodies and reagents is listed (Supplementary Table).

Metastatic cancer model

Female BALB/c mice were orally administered PBS or RASV. After 28 days, the fourth mammary gland of each mouse was injected with 5 × 105 4 T-1 cancer cells under anesthesia induced by isoflurane (Hana Pharm. Co. Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea) via inhalation. Tumors were excised when they were approximately 200–400 mm3. The incision was sutured using stitches and Reflex 7-mm wound clips (Cellpoint Scientific, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Blood vessel removal and hemostasis during surgery were achieved with a Gemini cautery kit (Cellpoint Scientific). Fifteen days post-surgery, mice were scanned using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT; Quantum GX2 MicroCT, Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) to obtain transverse, sagittal, and dorsal images. These images were processed into 3D models using Caliper Analyze version 12.0 software (Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups when the data had a Gaussian distribution with similar variances. One-way ANOVA was used to compare more than two groups, followed by the t-test. Two-way ANOVA was used to compare variable changes among the groups, followed by a post-hoc test (Bonferroni test). The threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.05, with 95% confidence intervals for all analyses.

Results

Prophylactic RASV administration induces CD8+ T cell-dependent antitumor immunity

We investigated whether RASV administration before tumor transplantation provides resistance against cancer. In vivo oral administration of RASV prior to B16F10 cell transplantation (Fig. 1A) significantly reduced tumor volume and mass as compared with PBS-treated counterparts (Fig. 1B, C). To assess immunological changes, the mice were euthanized 20 days post-tumor injection, and immune cells infiltrating the spleen and tumor tissues were analyzed. The abundance of activated CD8+ T cells (CD8+CD44+) in the spleens from RASV-administered mice was comparable with that in the PBS-treated group; however, their number in tumors was significantly increased (Fig. 1D). PD-1, an inhibitory immune checkpoint molecule, was significantly downregulated in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from RASV-treated mice (Fig. 1E). In contrast, the expression of the activation marker ICOS and the production of antitumor cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ, were markedly increased (Fig. 1F, G). Although no significant difference was observed between myeloid-derived suppressor cells in the spleens and tumors, the percentage of regulatory T cells in the tumors from RASV-administered mice was decreased (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, BCG did not yield significant antitumor effects (Supplementary Fig. 3B and C). RASV inoculation activates certain pattern-recognition receptors, such as nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2 (NOD2) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), triggering an immune response and inflammation, which exert anticancer effects [21, 22]. However, despite the absence of NOD2 or TLR4, RASV-induced anticancer effects were evident in mice (Supplementary Fig. 3D–G). This result indicates that the antitumor effect is orchestrated by an immune response triggered through intricate pathways involving NOD2 or TLR4, rather than via independent activation. We examined whether RASV-induced tumor growth inhibition could be ascribed to CD8+ T cells by depleting them. RASV-administered mice were implanted with Her2/CT26 cells and treated with an anti-CD8 antibody, and tumor size and in vivo CTL activity were analyzed. Of note, antibody-induced depletion of CD8+ T cells in RASV-administered mice curtailed the previously observed antitumor effects (Fig. 1H) and in vivo CTL activity (Fig. 1I). These results indicate that RASV pretreatment can induce antitumor effects with enhanced CTL activity in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner.

RASV induces hematopoiesis and antitumor CD8+ T cell responses

Inflammation or infection can alter the BM environment through cytokines or pathogen-associated molecules, thereby modulating HSC activity and influencing downstream lymphoid development [23]. In our model, RASV-induced hematopoietic activation in the BM may have affected thymic progenitor dynamics and peripheral T-cell homeostasis. Thymic analysis at four weeks after RASV immunization revealed no significant differences in CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ single-positive thymocyte subsets; however, DN1 cells were increased whereas DN4 cells were decreased, indicating altered early thymic progenitor maturation (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, total T cells and CD8⁺ T cells were increased in the spleens of RASV-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 5A). This splenic T-cell expansion likely reflects the combined effects of enhanced hematopoiesis and cytokine-driven peripheral T-cell survival and proliferation following RASV administration. To ascertain the potential of BM cells obtained from RASV-inoculated mice in preventive antitumor effects, we conducted a bulk RNA analysis of these cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of BM cells from RASV-treated mice revealed significant enrichment of gene ontology (GO) terms associated with hematopoiesis, with notable upregulation of IL-7R signaling-related gene clusters linked to lymphopoiesis and myelopoiesis (Fig. 2A). BM precursor cells were evaluated 4 weeks after RASV inoculation, in which multipotent progenitor (MPP), common myeloid progenitor (CMP), common dendritic cell progenitor (CDP), and CLP cells were analyzed to investigate potential correlations between enhanced CTL activity and BM reconstitution. Total BM cell populations in the PBS- and RASV-treated groups were comparable. However, the numbers of MPP, CMP, and CLP were increased in the RASV-administered group, whereas that of CDP was not significantly altered (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 6). In comparison with a previous report on enhanced innate immune cells trained after BCG treatment [24], we differentiated BM cells into BMDMs and BMDCs and then stimulated them with LPS to test whether RASV inoculation increased the activity of myeloid cells differentiated from progenitors of myeloid cells. No significant differences were observed in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, in the supernatant of BMDMs and BMDCs cultures between PBS- and RASV-administered mice (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Next, we intravenously vaccinated mice with OVA-primed BMDCs obtained from PBS- or RASV-administered mice and conducted in vivo CTL assays (Supplementary Fig. 5C). The vaccination efficacies of the RASV- and PBS-administered groups did not differ, indicating that RASV inoculation did not affect the myeloid progenitor cells. To verify that the RASV-induced anticancer effect was associated with HSCs, we transplanted BM cells from PBS- or RASV-inoculated mice to γ-irradiated CD45.1 recipient mice, which were then challenged with B16F10 cells 28 days post-BM cell transplantation (Fig. 2C). The tumor size was significantly decreased in mice that received RASV-treated BM cells compared to those transplanted with PBS-treated control BM cells (Fig. 2D). Production of IFN-γ and granzyme B by intratumoral CD8+ T cells was increased in mice transplanted with RASV-treated BM cells (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these results suggest that RASV inoculation induces alterations in BM progenitor cells that support anticancer effects.

Impact of IL-7 on CLP induction and anticancer effects enhanced by RASV inoculation

IL-7 plays a crucial role in CLP lymphopoiesis, as well as in the survival and differentiation of IL-7Rhi CLPs into naïve T cells in the BM [10, 25]. Of note, with increased CLP, Il7 expression in total BM cells was found to be increased in RASV-inoculated mice (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we hypothesized that RASV inoculation could trigger IL-7 expression, potentially augmenting CLPs in RASV-inoculated mice and consequently boosting CTL activity. Thus, RASV-inoculated mice were treated with αIL-7R antibody twice per week until euthanasia, and the precursor cells in the BM were analyzed 28 days post-RASV inoculation (Fig. 3B). Although no difference in total BM was observed among all groups and the numbers of MPP, CMP, and CDP in αIL-7R-treated groups were comparable with those in controls (Supplementary Fig. 7A), the CLP number was dramatically decreased after αIL-7R antibody treatment in both PBS- and RASV-treated groups (Fig. 3C). To explore the effect of IL-7 on anticancer responses in conjunction with CLPs, we administered recombinant IL-7-Fc into tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 3D), which substantially reduced tumor size (Fig. 3E) along with a notable increase in IFN-γ and granzyme B expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3F). While the overall number of BM precursor cells remained unchanged in the IL-7-Fc-treated group compared with the controls (Supplementary Fig. 7B), only CLPs exhibited a significant increase (Fig. 3G). These findings reveal the role of RASV-treatment-induced IL-7 in CLP elevation and enhanced anticancer effects.

IL-7-driven CLP expansion enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses in RASV-inoculated mice

Next, we assessed the effect of RASV-induced IL-7 on increased CLP levels and on antitumor responses. Since mice administered αIL-7R antibodies twice per week showed consistently decreasing CLP levels, they were then subcutaneously inoculated with MC38 cells and euthanized 21 days post-tumor inoculation (Fig. 4A). Tumor size was lower in the RASV-inoculated group than in the PBS-treated group, but the RASV-induced antitumor effects disappeared after αIL-7R treatment (Fig. 4B). Moreover, αIL-7R administration markedly reduced RASV-induced IFN-γ production by CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4C). However, no differences in programmed cell death protein 1 levels were observed (Supplementary Fig. 8A). IFN-γ expression in natural killer (NK) and CD4+ T cells, and CD80 and CD86 expression on antigen-presenting cells in tumors were comparable between the αIL-7R-treated RASV and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 8B and C). The levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α in tumors were increased, whereas those of IL-10 were decreased in the RASV-treated groups, and these effects were abolished by αIL-7R treatment and were similar to those in the PBS group (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these results indicate that IL-7-induced increase in CTL responses may be responsible for the enhanced antitumor effects in mice following RASV administration.

IFN-γ-induced IRF-1 expression enhances IL-7 levels in BM stromal cells following RASV treatment

To identify transcriptomic changes associated with increased IL-7 expression in the BM after RASV treatment, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of BM samples from PBS- and RASV-administered mice. We identified 382 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B and C) and performed GO enrichment analysis to investigate any functional preferences between up- or down-regulated genes. Briefly, the upregulated genes were associated with GO terms related to immune system processes, cellular response to IFN-β, innate immune responses, response to bacteria, and cellular response to IFN-γ. By contrast, the downregulated genes were mainly associated with GO terms for extracellular matrix formation or remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 9D). GSEA further revealed enrichment of IFN-γ-inducible genes, including interferon regulatory factor 1 (Irf1), in RASV-administered BM (Supplementary Fig. 9E). The DEGs were subjected to network analysis and categorized by biological process with clueGO, which revealed that increased Irf1 expression in RASV-treated BM was associated with immune system processes, regulation of immune system processes, leukocyte activation, and response to cytokines. STRING analysis for protein–protein interaction assessment showed that Irf1 expression in the RASV group was correlated with the upregulation of Stat1, Socs3, and Gbp2 expression (Supplementary Fig. 9F and G). These data indicate that RASV oral administration affected the BM transcription profile in response to IFN-γ and induced Irf1, which showed significant upregulation as compared with other Irf genes (Fig. 5A). IL-7 expression in nonimmune cells, such as intestinal epithelial or BM stromal cells, can be induced by IFN regulatory factors [26, 27] and is primarily induced by IFN-γ [28]. Considering that T cells are a major source of IFN-γ among immune cells [29], we analyzed CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the BM of RASV-treated mice. Significantly increased IFN-γ expression in CD4+ T cells and higher percentage in CD45+ population, but not in CD8+ T cells, were observed (Fig. 5B). Consequently, to investigate whether IFN-γ induces IL-7 secretion in association with IRF-1 in BM stromal cells, we stimulated M2-10B4 cells with IFN-γ and analyzed the transcription of Irf1 and Il7. Their levels were significantly increased in IFN-γ-treated M2-10B4 cells (Fig. 5C), along with IRF-1 protein expression (Fig. 5D). Considering that IRF-1 upregulates IL-7 in human intestinal epithelial cells upon IFN-γ stimulation [26], we hypothesized that IRF-1 can function as a transcription factor for IL-7 in BM stromal cells. ChIP assays revealed that IFN-γ initiated binding of IRF-1 to IL-7 promoter sequences (IRF-1 binding sites: 5′-GGGAGGAAA-3′), which enhanced Il7 transcription in mouse BM stromal cells (Fig. 5E and F). Thus, IFN-γ-induced IRF-1 expression functions as a transcription factor of IL-7 in mouse BM stromal cells.

RASV administration reduces lung metastasis in a murine breast cancer model

To explore the potential of RASV treatment in mitigating cancer metastasis, we inoculated 4 T-1 cancer cells in the fourth mammary glands of mice 28 days after RASV or PBS administration. After 25 days of inoculation, when the tumor size was approximately 200–400 mm3, tumor tissue was surgically excised. Subsequently, mice were scanned with micro-CT on day 40 and euthanized on day 45 to collect the lungs (Fig. 6A). Hematoxylin–eosin staining revealed fewer lung lesions in RASV-treated mice (Fig. 6B and C). Micro-CT cross-sectional images showed that control mice exhibited extensive loss of aerated lung areas, whereas RASV-treated mice retained well-aerated regions with markedly reduced tumor involvement (Fig. 6D). Three-dimensional reconstructions further demonstrated that control lungs displayed irregular, collapsed surfaces caused by tumor-induced tissue destruction, while RASV-treated lungs maintained smoother surfaces and preserved lobular architecture (Fig. 6E). These findings indicate that RASV administration has the potential to prevent breast cancer metastasis in the lungs.

Discussion

Intratumoral RASV administration can significantly inhibit the growth of Her-2/neu-expressing CT26 tumors by increasing the number of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment [17]. Thus, RASV can mitigate immunosuppressive conditions and stimulate robust immunogenic responses. However, whether RASV also confers prophylactic antitumor effects remained unexplored. Herein, we demonstrate that RASV administration prior to tumor implantation can enhance immune reconstitution and antitumor immunity. RASV treatment promoted IL-7-dependent increase in CLP levels in the BM and strengthened CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses via an IFN-γ-driven mechanism. Enhanced hematopoiesis was evident in RASV-treated mice, with BM cell transplantation from these mice into irradiated recipients significantly boosting antitumor activity. Moreover, upregulation of Irf1 expression in the BM of RASV-treated mice was found to be linked to IL-7 induction, underscoring a robust immune response. Lastly, RASV administration showed potential to prevent metastatic tumor growth. Hence, these findings indicate that IL-7-driven immune reconstitution through RASV, which relies on CLP expansion, is essential for enhancing CD8+ T cell-mediated cancer immune surveillance, thus supporting its prophylactic potential against cancer.

Activation of T cells via cytokines (such as type I IFN, IL-15, or IL-18) or other signals released by tumor cells or antigen-presenting cells plays a crucial role in the antitumor immune response [30–33], as they recognize and attack cancer cells expressing other antigens, leading to an antigen-nonspecific immune response against cancer [34–36]. Microbial infection induces the expression of diverse cytokines, which are associated with activating these bystander T cells, to establish adaptive immunity [37]. In our model, enhanced cytokine production following RASV inoculation activated bystander T cells, contributing to noticeable anticancer effects. Although IL-7-mediated bystander T cell activation has not been explicitly studied, IL-7 may play a crucial role in the survival of bystander T cells, rather than directly activating them. Accordingly, RASV-induced activation of bystander T cells may be associated with enhanced antitumor immunity through IL-7-mediated HSC reconstitution in the bone marrow.

In this study, we primarily focused on the prophylactic antitumor efficacy of RASV within four weeks after inoculation, and the durability of this effect beyond this period remains to be determined. Previous studies have reported that oral administration of RASV did not exhibit a significant therapeutic effect once tumors were established [17], suggesting that its efficacy depends largely on early immune reprogramming and prophylactic immune training rather than on direct tumor regression. Future studies are warranted to evaluate whether RASV-induced hematopoietic and immune modulation confer long-term protection and whether CLP reconstitution in the bone marrow is directly linked to the activation of antitumor bystander T cells.

Despite the advances in breast cancer treatment, recurrence is still associated with a poor prognosis, with only 29% patients surviving 4 years after early breast cancer recurrence (≤ 10 years after primary diagnosis) [38–40]. Chemotherapy can reduce the recurrence risk but is used in only 30% of patients [41]. A clinical trial using pembrolizumab to reduce breast cancer recurrence after surgery showed significantly increased patient survival and suggested the possibility of immunotherapy for preventing recurrence [42]. We observed that pretreatment with IL-7-Fc increased CD44+, which is a bystander marker, in CD8+ T cells and maintained the phenotype in the long term [15]. These factors were responsible for the increased sensitivity of IL-7R, which significantly enhanced the antitumor effects even after treatment with a low IL-7-Fc dose after tumor onset. We suggest that IL-7, either through direct administration or RASV-induced expression, could serve as a novel immunotherapy strategy to prevent breast cancer recurrence via the immune response.

The T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire is critical for effective immune surveillance against specific pathogens and cancer cells, and age-associated decline in its diversity renders the host vulnerable to various diseases [43–45]. Notably, IL-7, which promotes T cell expansion and increases the abundance of the TCR repertoire, holds potential for mitigating TCR diversity decline [46]. In our study, thymic analysis revealed a significant increase in DN1 cells and a decrease in DN4 cells following RASV immunization, indicating altered early thymic progenitor dynamics. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-7R signaling promotes the proliferation of double negative (DN) 1 thymocytes, consistent with the possibility that elevated IL-7 levels in RASV-inoculated mice enhance thymopoiesis [47, 48]. These findings suggest that IL-7 can enhance T cell expansion and promote the establishment of efficient adaptive immunity triggered by RASV, thus contributing to cancer resistance. Moreover, IL-7 can improve the functionality of tumor-infiltrating T cells, promote their proliferation and survival, and enhance the efficacy of T cell-based immunotherapies [16, 49]. Thus, IL-7 is a promising immunotherapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of cancer recurrence, and its safety and efficacy are being evaluated in clinical trials. Furthermore, the role of IL-7 in maintaining T cell homeostasis extends beyond its effects on the TCR repertoire. IL-7 also plays a critical role in the proliferation of CLPs and maintenance of memory T cells [50]. Together with the bystander T cell concept, these findings indicate that IL-7 plays a broad role in innate and adaptive immune responses.

IL-7-producing stromal cells within the BM are important for the differentiation of hematopoietic progenitor cells into multilineage stem cells [51]. The critical role of IL-7 in maintaining the survival and proliferation of memory T cells, particularly in the long term, has been established, as IL-7R deficiency leads to memory T cell loss [45, 52]. Salmonella vaccine increases the memory T cell population [53], which may induce the BM to express IL-7 and maintain memory T cell survival in the RASV inoculation model [54, 55]. Moreover, significant upregulation of Il7 expression and increased CLPs in the BM of RASV-inoculated mice indicate a possible role of RASV in regulating IL-7 expression in the BM. Transplantation of BM cells from RASV-immunized mice into naïve recipient mice resulted in increased anticancer effects. Conversely, blocking the IL-7 receptor reduced CLPs and the anticancer effects, indicating a central role for CLPs within the BM in enhancing cancer resistance. The BM is a reservoir of memory T cells that colocalize with the niche of IL-7+ stromal cells, but activated T cells also maintain hematopoiesis in the BM [55, 56]. We observed increased IFN-γ expression in T cells, which may be responsible for inducing IL-7 secretion in the BM. Considering that activation of IFN-induced transcription factors (such as IRF-1, IRF-2, IRF-7, and IRF-9) promotes IL-7 expression in nonimmune cells, such as epithelial or stromal cells [26, 27], IFN-γ-expressing T cells in the BM may stimulate stromal cells to produce IL-7 through IFN-γ signaling, supporting their survival and potentially leading to an increase in CLP.

Upregulation of IL-7 expression increased the number of CLPs in RASV-inoculated mice; however, the mechanisms by which IL-7-stimulated changes in CLPs affect thymic selection remain challenging to elucidate. We hypothesize that IL-7 enhances the survival and differentiation of T cells, promoting the progression from DN and double-positive (DP) stages to CD8+ single-positive (SP) T cells. Notch signaling is crucial for IL-7Rα expression in DN T cells during early T cell maturation [57]. IL-7Rα is typically downregulated during the transition from DN4 to DP stages and reappears after positive selection, whereas IL-7 responsiveness promotes the differentiation of DP cells into CD8+ SP T cells rather than CD4+ SP T cells [58]. In our bulk RNA sequencing analysis of BM cells, Notch expression was slightly elevated in RASV-inoculated mice, suggesting an association between Notch signaling and CD8+ T cell amplification following RASV treatment. Thus, Notch activity, possibly through maintaining or enhancing IL-7Rα expression in CLPs during thymic selection, may contribute to a CD8+ T cell-biased differentiation and enhanced CD8+ T cell function.

Pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs), including TLRs and NOD proteins, are important for immune system activation [59]. TLR signaling regulates IL-7 expression in various nonlymphoid tissues [60], whereas NOD signaling is involved in thymocyte selection and shaping the TCR repertoire [61]. Herein, TLR4 knockout (KO) mice exhibited significant anticancer effects following RASV administration, and similar effects were observed in NOD2 KO mice. The anticancer effects of RASV were not solely dependent on immune pathways mediated by either TLR4 or NOD2. Considering that RASV elicited robust anticancer responses even when TLR4 or NOD2 was individually disrupted, we hypothesized that multiple PRRs may act in concert to mediate the overall anticancer effects of RASV. Our results suggest that the enhanced anticancer immunity observed after RASV treatment is driven by increased IFN-γ production, which may be a downstream consequence of PRR-mediated responses.

Conclusions

We show that IFN-γ induces IL-7 expression in BM stromal cells and highlight the role of IFN-γ-mediated IL-7 production in maintaining lymphopoiesis and mitigating cancer metastasis. Elevated IL-7 levels were observed in RASV-inoculated mice alongside increased IFN-γ, suggesting a potential link between RASV administration and enhanced lymphopoiesis. However, further investigation is needed to clarify the precise mechanisms by which RASV regulates IL-7 expression in the BM microenvironment and its functional contribution to lymphopoiesis. A comprehensive understanding of these regulatory pathways may provide insights for developing novel immune-based anticancer strategies.
