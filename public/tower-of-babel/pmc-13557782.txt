Discussion: Preference Signaling—Does It Work? A Multispecialty Review of the Data
Authors: Sanjana Challa, Dorien I. Schonebaum, Samuel J. Lin
Published: 2026

Preference signaling is a relatively new feature of the medical residency match process that allows applicants to indicate their top-choice programs. This system has, however, existed in other fields for approximately 2 decades. The first official use of preference signaling was in 2006, when the American Economic Association introduced it as part of the recruitment process for graduate economics students.1 The American Economic Association granted its applicants 2 signals. These signals were considered scarce, and therefore, programs that received a signal perceived this as a significant opportunity cost because that applicant would not be able to signal another program. The concept of preference signaling has existed for much longer and has been studied extensively in the field of economics.2 It is believed that preference signaling is a tactic to reduce application congestion in highly sought-after markets, such as the residency match.

The residency application process is extremely competitive and often taxing for medical students. Historically, applicants were encouraged to apply to a broad range of programs to maximize their chances of matching into a residency position, especially in competitive specialties. However, this advice has led to application inflation and created challenges for both applicants and residency programs, especially noted during the COVID-19 pandemic when interviews were all conducted virtually for several years; the virtual interview setting allowed applicants to interview at a higher-than-average number of programs, which resulted in fewer interviews being given to applicants overall. Orthopedic surgery was the first specialty to propose preference signaling in 2017. In 2018, otolaryngology proposed a similar system called the star system. Both systems hypothesized that preference signaling would increase transparency and simplicity of the match process, reduce the number of applications, and ultimately improve outcomes for programs and applicants. In 2019, Whipple et al3 tested this theory by performing a computer simulation model. They concluded that preference signaling would enable programs to evaluate applicants more holistically.3 Signaling theoretically gives less competitive applicants, who otherwise might have been screened out by standardized test score cutoffs, more visibility in the application process and allows for more holistic review.4 This model improved the number of practical interview invitations per applicant. However, it is important to note that many programs primarily focus on applicants who signal them, which may lead to qualified applicants being overlooked. In 2021, otolaryngology decided to pilot preference signaling, and currently, most specialties use a form of preference signaling.

Recently, Elemosho et al5 published “Preference Signaling: Does It Work? A Multispecialty Review of the Data.” This article offers timely insight into the impact of preference signaling on residency application data across several specialties, including plastic surgery.5 They used the Electronic Residency Application Service and the National Resident Matching Program databases to evaluate match cycles from 2 years before and 2 years after the adoption of preference signaling. The main outcomes analyzed were the number of applicants, the average number of applications per applicant, and match rates. These metrics were compared between the presignaling and postsignaling eras to determine the effect of preference signaling.

Results showed that some specialties with more than 10 signals (ENT and neurosurgery) had a decrease in the number of applications per applicant, which was consistent with the intended goal of preference signaling. However, the reduction in applications was not uniform across all specialties. Other high-signal specialties (anesthesiology and obstetrics and gynecology) showed slight increases in applications per applicant. This trend possibly reflects an adjustment phase where applicants learn how to use signaling. Interestingly, specialties with fewer than 10 signals saw large reductions in applications per applicant. Integrated plastic surgery had a drastic 18.2% drop in applications per applicant, a notable finding given the historically high volume of applications in this competitive specialty.5–7 These results were based on application data from the Electronic Residency Application Service and National Resident Matching Program for the 2020–2021, 2021–2022, 2022–2023, and 2023–2024 cycles. During this time, plastic surgery designed its own application system called the Plastic Surgery Common Application. The impact of this new application system on the total number of applications per applicant is unknown.8

A change in match rates was also observed. In the high-signal group, ENT showed an increase in match rate of 12%. A slight increase in match rates was also seen in anesthesiology, orthopedic surgery, and neurosurgery, varying from 0.2% to 0.9%. Not all high-signaling programs showed an increase in match rates; obstetrics and gynecology and dermatology showed decreases of 3.2% and 10.2%, respectively. Specialties in the small-signaling group also saw an increase in match rates for most participating programs. In fact, plastic surgery had a 4.5% increase in match rate.

These findings suggest that preference signaling can be especially beneficial in competitive specialties where applicants are typically encouraged to apply to many programs in hopes of matching. By only signaling programs that align with their personal and professional goals, applicants may reduce unnecessary applications, decrease application costs, and strategically focus on programs they are genuinely interested in. This is the first study reporting on outcomes of preference signaling, and a few limitations must be noted. The authors suggest that the results may be slightly skewed because the data from the presignaling era were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic. Previous studies have shown that 59% of medical students felt that COVID-19 impacted their residency choice.9 The pandemic changed the interview process tremendously due to online interviewing, which possibly impacted match rates in the presignaling data used in this study.10,11

Thus far, most program directors and applicants view the signaling system positively.12 From our perspective, the advent of signaling is a positive change in the match process. Signals allow applicants to apply to fewer programs, focus on programs that are truly aligned with their career goals, and therefore reduce application costs. Additionally, signals indicate genuine interest in programs and tend to reduce the number of interview invites per applicant, which reduces the financial burden associated with attending in-person interviews. Although the overall number of interviews has decreased, applicants have more preparation time for interviews at their optimal programs. For plastic surgery specifically, perhaps the optimal number of signals is approximately 20, as this is approximately 25% of all programs and gives applicants a fair chance of an adequate number of interviews to match.

In conclusion, Elemosho et al5 present a timely article as the first pre- and postsignaling comparison within the field of plastic surgery. Their analysis showed changes in the number of applications per applicant and match rates. In the field of plastic surgery, this new change to the application process will likely be further refined in the future for the benefit of both applicants and programs.
