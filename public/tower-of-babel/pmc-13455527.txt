The Role of a Biological Membrane in Healing Full‐Thickness Cutaneous Wounds in Rats
Authors: Ali Ghazi Atiyah, Thamer Jaddoa Shihab, Ismael I Hasan, Lipi Buch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFull‐thickness wounds pose significant healing challenges due to their reduced regenerative capacity and prolonged inflammation.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the healing potential of decellularized amniotic membrane (AM) as a biological dressing for the treatment of full‐thickness cutaneous wounds.MethodsIn this study, 18 healthy rats were evenly distributed between the control and treatment groups. A 3 × 3 cm full‐thickness wound was made on the dorsal side of the back. The treated group received decellularized ovine amniotic membrane (OAM). This membrane was then applied to the affected area. The control group, however, did not get any treatment at all. The animals were euthanized at 7, 14, and 21 days after the operation so the histological analysis could be conducted.ResultScanning electron microscopy images showed that the decellularized AM had a uniformly smooth surface, whereas the undecellularized AM appeared to contain organic and cellular material, resulting in a rougher surface. Histological and macroscopic examinations revealed that the treated group healed wounds more quickly than the control group.ConclusionsThe biological membrane can induce tissue re‐epithelialization, stimulate collagen synthesis, reduce inflammatory reactions, and prevent immunological rejection, making it an attractive, biocompatible scaffold and a promising option in regenerative medicine.Keywords: amniotic membrane, biological membrane, cutaneous wound, decellularized, scaffold

1. Introduction

The largest organ in the body, the skin, covers all surfaces and protects us from germs, chemicals, and mechanical stress. Numerous factors, such as microbes, high temperatures, chemicals, and mechanical stress, can damage the skin. Wounds can be classified into three primary types based on the depth of damage: epidermal, partial thickness, and full thickness. The body’s natural healing processes can swiftly repair the outer layer of skin that is torn by a tiny cut, known as an epidermal wound. In a partial‐thickness wound, the dermis and a small amount of epidermal tissue are damaged. Complete destruction of dermal epithelial regeneration components occurs in full‐thickness wounds. For this reason, sterile wound dressings are necessary to prevent large scars [1, 2].

Traditionally, autografts, allografts, and xenografts have been used because of the significant tissue loss that occurs in trauma or skin‐burn lesions. The danger of immune rejection and primary contraction are two limitations of these therapies [3]. Skin substitutes are designed to mimic the skin’s natural functions and the structural features of the extracellular matrix (ECM). They should also protect against protein loss, improve the wound’s appearance, and prevent the growth of microbes not naturally present [4]. The use of biological replacements to treat skin damage is a relatively new concept. Collagen, gelatin, hyaluronic acid, fibronectin/fibrin, chitosan, alginate, polyglycolic acid, polylactic acid, and polycaprolactone are some of the natural and synthetic substrates from which these alternatives can be produced; they can therefore facilitate cell migration, differentiation, and growth. On the other hand, biological scaffolds derived from the ECM of decellularized tissues can serve as viable alternatives [5]. Decellularized tissues and organs have many practical applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, and the methods used to accomplish this aim are varied and varied in kind [6]. Tissue engineering seeks to build three‐dimensional biomaterial and cell complexes. The primary focus of tissue engineering was reprogramming cells to respond to morphogens, scaffolds, and signals. Therefore, biocompatible scaffolds that support cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation are the building blocks of tissue engineering [7].

Recent improvements in wound‐healing research have also centered on the creation of composite scaffolds, hydrogels, electrospun nanofibers, and polymeric membranes as substitute wound scaffolds for full‐thickness skin defects. Fabricated from polyvinyl alcohol, polyurethane, or polycaprolactone, polymeric membranes offer regulated biodegradation characteristics, good mechanical strength, and the ability to retain moisture; these qualities help wounds heal faster and tissues regenerate more quickly [8]. Moreover, electrospun nanofibers possess significant interest because of their nanoscale structure, which closely mimics the ECM, hence promoting cell adhesion, migration, and angiogenesis. Natural and synthetic nanofiber‐based scaffolds containing collagen, gelatin, chitosan, or synthetic polymers have shown favorable results in mitigating inflammation and enhancing re‐epithelialization in experimental wound settings [9]. Besides, hydrogel‐based scaffolds and bioengineered composite materials infused with growth factors or antibacterial agents have been studied to ensure prolonged biological activity and improved healing efficacy. [10]. However, many synthetic and hybrid wound dressing biomaterials continue to struggle with issues including poor biocompatibility, rapid disintegration, high production costs, and poor structural integrity, even with these improvements. Therefore, biological scaffolds derived from decellularized tissues continue to be an attractive option due to their intrinsic regenerative ability and original ECM.

The novelty of this study lies in the use of ovine amniotic membrane (OAM) as a naturally sourced wound‐healing scaffold and the application of an optimized decellularization protocol that effectively removes cells while maintaining the integrity of the native ECM structure and its bioactive constituents. Instead of using synthetic polymers, electrospun nanofibers, or hydrogel systems, which require substantial material modification or external bioactive incorporation, the proposed scaffold takes advantage of the inherent biological characteristics of a matrix derived from native tissue, which is different from current wound‐healing methods. This study makes use of a comprehensive multiscale evaluation approach that integrates histological examination, high‐resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies, and temporal macroscopic wound‐closure measurements. Inflammatory control, cellular infiltration, collagen organization, and dermal remodeling are all molecular mechanisms that may be studied in detail using this all‐encompassing method. Therefore, the findings show that decellularized OAM offers a natural, scalable, biocompatible, low‐immunogenic alternative to current synthetic and hybrid wound‐dressing approaches. It also retains its structural integrity.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Ethical Approval

The experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the College of Veterinary Medicine, Tikrit University, with approval license number Tu. Vet. 107 (October 2024).

2.2. Experimental Animals

Eighteen clinically healthy male albino rats, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 200–250 g, were obtained from the animal housing center at the College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Tikrit. All animals were housed in sterilized polypropylene cages under a controlled environment (temperature, 24 ± 2°C; humidity, 55%; 12/12 h light/dark cycle). A regular rat diet and drinkable water were given to all animals throughout the study. The experimental animals were randomly assigned to control and treated groups (n = 9 per group) to minimize selection bias.

2.3. Preparation of OAM

The OAM was procured from a local butcher shop in Tikrit City. All samples were obtained from clinically healthy male sheep aged (6–9) months, specifically from the small intestines. The small intestine was sectioned into approximately 10 cm segments and subsequently washed in saline. After the mechanical excision of the tunica serosa and tunica muscularis, the amniotic membrane (AM) was purified through continuous irrigation with a saline solution. After thoroughly cleansing the intestine, it was cut into two segments measuring approximately (3 × 3) cm each (Figure 1A).

(A) OAM before decellularization. (B) OAM after decellularization.

2.4. Method of Decellularization

The decellularization process was performed using a modified technique mentioned by Ji et al. [11]. The membrane was immersed in a methanol‐chloroform solution (1:1, v/v) for 12 h under a fume hood to remove lipids and cellular components. After this step, the samples were rinsed multiple times with deionized water to eliminate residual solvents. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated in a solution containing 0.05% trypsin and 0.05% EDTA at 37°C for 12 h to facilitate cellular detachment. The samples were then washed thoroughly with saline solution to remove enzymatic residues. Then, the membrane was treated with 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) in 0.9% sodium chloride for 4 h under continuous agitation to remove cellular material. Following this, the samples were washed several times with normal saline to remove any remaining detergent. Finally, the decellularized membranes were stored at −20°C until use (Figure 1B).

2.5. Surgical Procedure

A combination of xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine HCL (90 mg/kg) was administered intramuscularly to induce general anesthesia [12]. The dorsal surface of each animal was shaved with an electric clipper and disinfected with 70% ethanol under aseptic conditions. A full‐thickness excisional wound measuring approximately (3 × 3) cm was created on the dorsal midline using a sterile scalpel (blade No. 15), extending through the skin layers down to the underlying muscle fascia. Hemostasis was achieved using sterile gauze. The animals in the treated group were treated with decolonized OAM, while the animals in the control group were left untreated (Figure 2). Subsequently, a (0/5) polyglycolic acid suture was applied to secure the membrane to the skin.

(A) Surgical procedure for creating full‐thickness skin wounds and application of decellularized OAM in rats. (B) The dorsal surface of each rat was shaved, disinfected, and marked for incision. (C) In the control group, wounds were left untreated. (D) In the treated group, the wound was immediately covered with a decellularized OAM.

3. Animals’ Evaluations

3.1. Wound Contraction Assessments

On Days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21 postsurgery, the area of the wound was measured using a caliper after surgery. The percentage of the wound was then calculated. Afterward, the following formula was used to determine the modifications in wound area due to contraction [13].

3.2. Macroscopic Assessments

Photographs of the injured area of each animal were taken at 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21 days postsurgery to assess for indications of inflammation, erythema, edema, necrosis, or hemorrhage. The durations of wound contraction and closure were documented as well.

3.3. Histopathological Assessments

Skin samples were collected from wound sites on Days 7, 14, and 21 postsurgeries, subsequent to the euthanasia of the animals using a fatal intramuscular dosage of ketamine HCL (Alfasan, Holland). A complete‐thickness specimen was carefully dissected using both sharp and blunt instruments. The specimen was subsequently fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, rinsed with xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. A microtome (Leica SP 1600; Leica Microsystems, Germany) was used to produce 5‐μm‐thick serial sections, which were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin. A certified pathologist conducted a histopathological investigation utilizing an Olympus light microscope (Japan) with a scale bar (μm) [14].

3.4. SEM

The SEM analysis technique used in this study was the (Tuscan Vega third Generation from England), and the picture was obtained at an alternative magnification to analyze the sample′s surface morphology.

3.5. Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (interquartile range, IQR) to offer a thorough characterization of data distribution. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normality of the data. Comparisons between the control and treated groups at each time point for normally distributed data were conducted using independent‐samples t‐tests. All tests were bilateral, and p values ≤ 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Alongside p values, effect sizes (mean differences) were computed to provide a clearer understanding of the extent of the change between groups. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) were computed to assess the accuracy of the results. All analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5). Statistical reporting according to the SAMPL criteria.

4. Results

4.1. SEM of OAM

The SEM revealed the pre and postdecellularization surface morphology of the OAM matrix. The SEM results of the OAM matrix predecellularization revealed a dense network of regular fibers with irregular spacing, containing many particles associated with biological debris (Figure 3). Additionally, the decellularized AM was unaffected and undisturbed. These findings validated the removal of organic residues and demonstrated that the membrane’s micro and ultrastructural properties were maintained following decellularization (Figure 4). A comparison of the surface morphology before and after implantation of the decellularized OAM over surgical skin incisions was shown using SEM. The SEM analysis taken after the implant showed that the surface structure had changed and that the membrane porosity had increased. The presence of active cellular infiltration and molecular remodeling activities at the wound site most likely caused these modifications. The changes that were seen show that the OAM kept the structure intact while allowing for the vital biological interactions that are happening throughout the healing process of a wound (Figure 5).

(A) magnification 15.000X and (B) 30.000X show the SEM image analysis of the decellularized amniotic membrane revealed a matrix free of cell debris and organic residues, as well as a homogeneous, smooth surface.

(A) magnification 130X and (B) 500X shows the SEM images of the decellularized OAM after implantation over a surgical skin wound. The micrographs reveal enhanced porosity and significant modifications in surface morphology relative to the preimplantation condition. These changes show that cells are moving into the wound and that the extracellular matrix is changing shape. This suggests that the scaffold is biocompatible and helps tissue regeneration.

4.2. Macroscopic Evaluation

In the first 3 days after injury, the treated group showed signs of redness, swelling, and inflammation. These signs persisted in the control group for up to 10 days postinjury. No bleeding or necrosis was observed in either group throughout the experimental period. By Day 21, the treated group exhibited complete wound closure, whereas the control group required longer to heal (Figure 6). The quantitative analysis of wound contraction (Table 1) and (Figure 7) showed that the treated group achieved greater wound closure compared to the control group at all evaluated time points. At Day 3, no significant difference was observed between the treated group (mean: 6.6 ± 8.2%, median: 5% [IQR: 1–10]) and the control group (mean: 3.3 ± 5.9%, median: 3% [IQR: 0–7]) (p = 0.34), although the treated group showed a slightly higher mean value, with a mean difference of 3.3%.

At Day 7, wound contraction was significantly higher in the treated group. The treated group’s mean was 33.3 ± 15.7%, with a median of 30% (IQR: 20–45). In comparison, the control group had a mean of 13.3 ± 11.3% and a median of 12% (IQR: 5–20) (p = 0.007). The mean difference was 20.0%. By Day 14, this difference grew. The treated group reached 66.6 ± 15.7% (median: 65% [IQR: 55–80]). The control group reached 33.3 ± 15.7% (median: 30% [IQR: 20–45]) (p < 0.001), and the mean difference was 33.3%. At Day 21, the treated group showed near‐complete wound closure, with a mean of 94.0 ± 7.9% (median: 95% [IQR: 90–100]). This remained significantly higher than the control group, which had a mean of 66.0 ± 15.79% and a median of 65% (IQR: 50–80) (p < 0.001), with a mean difference of 28.0%. The 95% CIs supported the reliability of these differences, especially at later time points.

4.3. Histopathological Evaluation

In the control group, at 7 days following surgery, a thin epithelial tongue consisting of a single layer of immature keratinocytes was observed. The tongue was small and thick, and it quickly protruded above the dermal layer. At this time point, a strong collagen matrix with a few fibroblasts and several multinucleated inflammatory cells infiltrated the dermal layer (Figure 8A). At 14 days postsurgery, the epidermal tongue halted its progression, and the remaining wound defect was covered by dense fibrin, with a serocellular crust concealing the injured area. The dermal layer at this stage contained granulation tissue invaded by multinucleated inflammatory cells, with many fibroblasts situated within a dense, irregularly oriented collagen fiber matrix (Figure 8C). At 21 days after surgery, the epidermal layer consisted of a dense, keratinized stratum with hypertrophic keratinocytes and no moving epithelial tongue. The dermal layer displayed a dense, irregularly oriented collagen fiber matrix, with deeply embedded fibroblasts and multinucleated inflammatory cells. Furthermore, blood vessels were distended and filled with hemolyzed blood (Figure 8E and Figure 9A).

The histological sections in the treated group exhibited a pronounced epithelial tongue, with keratinocytes encapsulated in vacuoles inside the basal layer, while spindle‐shaped keratinocytes resided in the stratum spinosum layer. Mature keratinocytes, identified by black granules within the stratum granulosum layer, were also observed as the epithelial tongue stretched beneath the decellularized matrix membrane (Figure 8B). Also, multinucleated inflammatory cells and deeply embedded fibroblasts were seen to have entered the dermal layer together with a thin, irregularly oriented collagen fiber. A great deal of freshly created blood vessels was also seen (Figure 8B). At 14 days after surgery, a thick layer of keratinocyte‐rich tissue, covered with an exterior thin layer of keratinized tissue, marked complete re‐epithelialization of the epidermal layer. Furthermore, the dermal layer contained a few multinucleated inflammatory cells and collagen fibers rich in fibroblasts, arranged in thin, slightly regular orientations (Figure 8D). At 21 days after surgery, invasion of the epidermal papillae fibers caused the epidermal layer to be deepithelialized, making it look delicate and vulnerable, while other sections showed fewer inflammatory cells in the dermal layer, which contained fibrocytes embedded in thin, regularly oriented collagen. Additionally, newly formed hair follicles with hair bulbs encased in epithelial sheaths rich in epithelial cells were observed (Figure 8F) and (Figure 9B).

5. Discussion

A full‐thickness skin wound needs a prolonged period for self‐healing. Hypertrophy, decreased dermal elasticity, and an undesirable aesthetic appearance are common consequences of scar formation [15]. In the present study, the treated group exhibited significantly accelerated wound healing compared to the control group, as evidenced by higher wound contraction percentages at Days 7, 14, and 21. Specifically, wound contraction reached 33.3 ± 15.7% at Day 7 and increased to 66.6 ± 15.7% and 94.0 ± 7.9% at Days 14 and 21, respectively, indicating a progressive and enhanced healing response. Thus, the duration of recovery and complications related to scarring from full‐thickness skin AM are reduced [16]. The results of the present study align with prior publications indicating that decellularized AM improves wound healing by modulating inflammation and facilitating tissue regeneration [17, 18]. Consistent with these findings, the present research found that the decellularized membrane had the ability to regenerate, since the treated group exhibited quicker re‐epithelialization and considerably better wound contraction than the control group, especially at later time points.

Similar to our results, these studies reported accelerated re‐epithelialization and improved collagen organization. However, unlike some studies that incorporated molecular analyses, the current work primarily relied on histopathological observations, which may limit detailed mechanistic interpretation [17]. The quantitative results of this study further support the observed histopathological improvements, where reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and enhanced collagen organization were associated with significantly higher wound contraction percentages in the treated group. The relatively higher variability observed at early time points may reflect individual biological differences during the inflammatory phase of wound healing. The pattern of mean differences observed across time points revealed a progressive increase from Day 3 to Day 14, followed by a slight reduction at Day 21. This trend reflects the natural phases of wound healing, where minimal differences are expected during the early inflammatory phase, followed by pronounced differences during the proliferative phase due to enhanced fibroblast activity and collagen deposition. The slight reduction at the later stage may be attributed to the progression of healing in both groups toward wound closure during the remodeling phase. Because of its biocompatibility, suitable porosity structure, and lack of immunogenicity, the AM causes minimal inflammatory reactions and has minimal immunogenicity [19]. Furthermore, we concur with the prior research indicating that acellular animal membrane products are more economical, more often used for extensive skin lesions, and demonstrate a reduced healing duration compared to traditional healing methods [20].

Previous studies have validated the reliability of SEM evaluations by employing comparable protocols for the removal, preparation, and imaging of the AM [18]. The current findings show that the smooth surface observed in decellularized samples, as visualized under SEM, is indicative of effective removal of cellular components and preservation of the ECM [21]. Much research has shown that a reduced matrix rejection rate is an additional advantage of cell‐free processing [22, 23]. Furthermore, decellularization did not harm the matrix, and these findings are consistent with previous research using the same method [24, 25].

The comparison with the control group in this study was intended to establish a baseline for natural wound healing and to clearly demonstrate the regenerative potential of the decellularized membrane. Also, the significant differences observed between groups, particularly at Days 7, 14, and 21, highlight the effectiveness of the decellularized membrane in accelerating tissue repair. The improved healing shown in the treated group might be explained by the fact that the decellularized membrane still has its ECM structure, creating an ideal setting for cell migration and proliferation. According to Song et al. [26], a significant factor in avoiding chronic wound diseases is the modulation of the inflammatory phase of wound healing by the scaffold. This might be explained by the decreased inflammatory cell infiltration. For this reason, the ideal AM for use in wound healing would have the following characteristics: the ability to adhere to wound surfaces, to grow and differentiate cells, and to have suitable mechanical and physical qualities [27]. Although the present study did not evaluate molecular biomarkers such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), histopathological findings showed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and increased collagen deposition in the treated group. These observations are commonly associated with decreased proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6, and increased expression of growth factors, such as TGF‐β, which play a critical role in tissue regeneration and remodeling. Therefore, the results indirectly support the involvement of these pathways in the improved healing process. Future studies are recommended to include molecular analyses to confirm these mechanisms.

In addition, histological analysis showed that the treated group had an abundance of living fibroblasts, which may suggest that these cells might grow, multiply, and migrate on top of the treated group. As time went on, these fibroblasts and the excess collagen began to replace the inflammatory leukocytes. Consequently, we concur with prior research that has shown the AM can strongly attach to the surface of the wounded area and initiate the healing process in cases of acute tissue damage [20]. Thus, wounds are strengthened, and extracellular microfibrils are better cross‐linked when collagen production increases; a second study indicated that biological membranes promoted fibroblast proliferation, which in turn boosted collagen levels at the wound site [28].

Notwithstanding the encouraging results of this investigation, numerous limitations must be acknowledged. Initially, despite the random allocation of animals to groups, blinding was not maintained during histological assessment, which may have resulted in observer bias. The histological evaluation was predominantly descriptive, lacking a standardized grading system or quantitative image analysis. Third, the absence of molecular and immunohistochemical investigations, including the assessment of IL‐6, TGF‐β, and angiogenic markers, constrains the mechanistic understanding of the healing process. Subsequent research ought to rectify these shortcomings by integrating quantitative histological scoring, molecular biomarkers, and blinded evaluations to enhance the validity and reproducibility of the results. A further disadvantage of this study is the lack of a standard or positive control group, such as traditional wound dressings. The untreated control, which evaluated natural healing and included a clinically relevant comparator, would enhance the evidence of therapy efficacy. Subsequent research should integrate standardized wound care products to enhance clinical relevance and facilitate comparison. The findings endorse the efficacy of decellularized OAM as a viable scaffold for wound healing.

6. Conclusion

This research illustrates that the decellularized OAM markedly improves the healing of full‐thickness cutaneous lesions in rats. The treated group exhibited expedited wound contraction and enhanced re‐epithelialization. They exhibited a less inflammatory response in comparison to the untreated control group. Histological examination validated enhanced tissue regeneration. This was characterized by structured collagen deposition, heightened fibroblast activity, and the development of epidermal appendages, including hair follicles. SEM confirmed the structural integrity and successful decellularization of the OAM. This ensured that it was suitable as a framework that is compatible with living organisms. Decellularized OAM has the makings of a cheap biological scaffold with promising healing properties, little immunogenicity, and easy production. It may be used for therapeutic wound care and tissue regeneration.
