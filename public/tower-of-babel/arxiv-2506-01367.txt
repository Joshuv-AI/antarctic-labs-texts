MMD-Flagger : Leveraging Maximum Mean Discrepancy to Detect Hallucinations
Kensuke Mitsuzawa, Damien Garreau

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have become pervasive in our everyday life. Yet, a fundamental obstacle prevents their use in many critical applications: their propensity to generate fluent, human-quality content that is not grounded in reality. The detection of such hallucinations is thus of the highest importance. In this work, we propose a new method to flag hallucinated content, MMD-Flagger . It relies on Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), a non-parametric distance between distributions. On a high-level perspective, MMD-Flagger tracks the MMD between the generated documents and documents generated with various temperature parameters. We show empirically that inspecting the shape of this trajectory is sufficient to detect most hallucinations. This novel method is benchmarked on two machine translation datasets, on which it outperforms natural competitors.



1
Introduction

Neural probabilistic modeling
(
bengio_et_al_2003
)
in an auto-regressive framework has become the
de facto
paradigm for natural language processing.
Powered by the transformer architecture
(
Vaswani-2017-attention
)
, such neural networks have achieved impressive performances in text translation
(
kocmi_et_al_2024
)
, abstractive text summarization
(
shakil_farooq_kalita_2024
)
, generative question-answering
(
daull_et_al_2023
)
, and many other applications.
More recently, the trend has been to bring forward so-called Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT
(
openai_2024
)
, which can tackle simultaneously nearly all these different tasks.
Popularized by ChatGPT (a chatbot version of GPT), they are used daily by dozens of millions of users
(
Yuyang-2023-BigData
)
.

However, LLMs have a key limitation which prevents their use in critical applications: their propensity to generate
hallucinations
.
Commonly characterized by the generation of fluent but factually inaccurate or unsubstantiated content, these are observed in all models
(
ji_et_al_2023
)
, although rare
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
.
Within the domain of machine translation, the focus of this work, this phenomenon manifests as translations that, while grammatically correct, deviate semantically from the contextual information present in the source language
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
.
We provide an example in Table
1
.
Hallucinated content is a critical drawback, especially in domains where information accuracy is essential, such as law
Benkirane-2024-machine
.
In these fields, getting wrong (yet seemingly correct) information even once can undermine users’ trust permanently.
Detecting hallucinations at test time remains a challenging task.

Focusing on standard encoder-decoder architecture, it is believed that the decoding step is one of the main causes of hallucinations
(
ji_et_al_2023
)
.
In practice,
beam search
is commonly used to generate the output token sequence, given its superior performance, even with respect to maximum a posteriori
(
Meister-etal-2020-beam
)
.
This is particularly true for machine translation tasks
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
;
Xu-etal-2023-understanding
)
.
Nevertheless, beam search tends to suffer from the so-called
likelihood trap

(
das-balke-2022-quantifying
)
: a high-likelihood token sequence often results in a low-quality token sequence.

An alternative approach is simply to sample the next token according to the probability distribution given by the decoder’s softmax layer at each step.
The main advantage in doing so is the
stochasticity
of outputs, giving rise to many diverse output documents from a single input.
A further refinement is to introduce a
temperature parameter
(
\tau
in our notation), which controls the randomness of the next token prediction; a low value (say
<0.1
) corresponding roughly to a probability distribution concentrated on the most likely token, and a higher value (say
>1
) making the distribution near uniform.
Thus, it is not surprising that, in practice, low temperatures are reported to induce low variability in the sampled token sequences
(
Peeperkorn-etal-2024
)
,
while high temperatures result increase in the hallucination rate
(
Renze-2024-effect
;
Huang-2025
)
.

The central objective of this study is to compare the default output of a language model (generated using beam search) with stochastic samples obtained under varying temperature.
At low temperature settings, we observe that
the sampled outputs are highly similar to the default output if the model is not hallucinating, and moderately dissimilar if it is
.
We believe this occurs because of the following reason: when a clear optimal candidate token exists, both sampling (at low temperatures) and beam search tend to favor the most probable token.
However, in cases where no single candidate clearly dominates (often indicative of hallucination), the outputs are more likely to diverge.
In contrast,
at high temperature, generation becomes increasingly random, and sampled outputs tend to be substantially different from the default output regardless
of the model’s behavior.
This pattern is illustrated in Table
1
.
As a consequence, plotting the similarity between the default output and the stochastic outputs yields two distinct curves: either a monotonously increasing trajectory in the non-hallucinating scenario, or a
U
-shape curve in the hallucinating scenario.
This can be visualized in Figure
1
.

Drawing on these findings, we introduce a novel hallucination detection method:
MMD-Flagger
.
The key idea of the method is to simply i) plot the similarity between default output and stochastic samples as a function of the temperature, and ii) automatically detect whether this curve is monotonously increasing or
U
-shaped.
In the latter case, we flag the output as hallucination.
As a measure of similarity, we propose to use the
Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)

(
Gretton-2012a
)
.
As a shape detection mechanism, we simply estimate the inflection point of the trajectory and check whether it is located near
0
(see Figure
1
).

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we detail the operating procedure of
MMD-Flagger
after recalling the necessary background on temperature sampling and MMD.
We benchmark our method against several natural competitors in Section
3
, before concluding in Section
5
.
The codebase is available at
https://github.com/Kensuke-Mitsuzawa/MMD-Flagger
.

1.1
Related work

Hallucination detection is a very active field, with a wide range of approaches proposed in recent years.
Let us review the most related work;
We refer to
(
ji_et_al_2023
)
for a more complete survey of mitigation methods in various domains.

Uncertainty quantification.

Following the taxonomy outlined by
(
Huang-2025
)
,
MMD-Flagger
falls under the umbrella of
uncertainty estimation
, which aims to quantify a model’s confidence in its output.
Stemming from the observation that models are more confident when they do not hallucinate, the Monte-Carlo dropout-based
MC-Dsim

(
Fomicheva-2020-unsupervised
;
Zerva-2021-unbabel
)
estimates the stability of generated token sequences.
A similar approach is to inspect the log-probability of output sequences
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
and to flag as hallucination if this log-probability is too low (
Seq-Logprob
).
Alternatively, quality estimation methods
(
Rei-etal-2022-cometkiwi
;
Feng-etal-2022-language
)
correlate low-quality translations with hallucinations, though they are not originally designed for hallucination detection.
Empirical evidence also suggests that combining multiple detectors improves detection accuracy
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
;
Himmi-2024-enhanced
)
.
In the context of QA,
(
Varshney-2023stitchtimesavesnine
)
proposes to use the minimum token probability as an indicator of uncertainty, based on the premise that lower token probabilities suggest higher uncertainty.
We compare
MMD-Flagger
to
MC-Dsim
and
Seq-Logprob
in Section
3
.

External knowledge.

Beyond uncertainty estimation, several approaches aim to detect hallucination by leveraging external knowledge.
For example,
(
Min-etal-2023-factscore
)
introduce factuality scores derived from knowledge graphs. In machine translation,
(
Xu-etal-2023-understanding
)
propose a supervised hallucination detection model trained on synthetic pseudo-hallucination datasets. The
LMvLM
framework
(
Cohen-etal-2023-lm
)
employs an examiner language model to interrogate an examinee model through multi-turn interactions, identifying potential inconsistencies. Similarly, the
Chain-of-Verification
method
(
dhuliawala-etal-2024-chain
)
makes the model’s reasoning process explicit, enabling humans to assess the validity of its outputs.
We do not compare against such methods since
MMD-Flagger
does not use external information.

Model internals.

Building on the assumption that models “know when they hallucinate”
(
duan_et_al_2024
)
, other approaches look at models’ internal states to detect hallucinations.

(
Ferrando-etal-2022-towards
;
Dale-2022-detecting
)
measure how much the input contributes to the output and show that one can detect hallucination when the source sentence contribution is low.
It is also possible to analyze input-output relationships from an approach using optimal transport
(
Guerreiro-etal-2023-optimal
)
.
We showcase in Appendix
G
how
MMD-Flagger
can be adapted to leverage information from the model internals.
However, the more consistent experimental results are achieved using only the model’s outputs.

Kernel methods and LLMs.

Another key ingredient of our approach is the use of MMD to compute similarities between outputs of LLMs.
To the best of our knowledge, only one prior study connects MMD with LLMs.

(
Zhangs-2024MMDMP
)
propose a two-sample testing framework that employs MMD as the test statistic to distinguish between human-written and machine-generated texts.
Thus, their goal is not hallucination detection, but rather authenticity classification, making the application of MMD to hallucination detection a novel contribution of our work.
More similar to our approach,
(
Nikitin-2024-kernel
)
proposes kernel language entropy, a kernel-based method to quantify the uncertainty in LLM outcomes,
and hallucination detection is proposed based on the entropy score
(
Farquhar-2024detecting
)
.

2
Methodology

In this section, we describe the proposed method,
MMD-Flagger
.
Given an input document
x
, the starting point of the method is to generate the output
y=f(x)
to be inspected, using beam search.
The next step is to generate additional documents by stochastic sampling at various temperature parameters, which is detailed in Section
2.1
.
Then, the MMD between
y
and the newly created groups is computed for each temperature parameter.
We detail this computation in Section
2.2
.
At this point, for a given input / output pair
(x,y)
, we have obtained a series of MMD values corresponding to different temperature settings.
We explain our procedure to inspect this trajectory in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Stochastic sampling and temperature

In this section, we present the encoder-decoder architecture considered in this work and detail the role of the temperature in stochastic sampling.

Encoder-decoder architecture.

We consider the encoder-decoder architecture
(
Sutskever-2014
)
with self-attention mechanism
(
Vaswani-2017-attention
)
.
Given an input token sequence
x
, the encoder
e
, which consists in multiple layers of self-attention and feed-forward networks encodes the sequence into a representation with fixed length
T
.
The decoder then generates the output sequence autoregressively, conditioned on both the encoded fixed-length vector and the already processed tokens.
More precisely, each output token is generated as

Y_{t}=\Phi(Y_{t-1},\ldots,Y_{1},e(x))\,,

where
\Phi
is a (potentially) stochastic sampler.
This process iterates until the
EOS
token is sampled.
In our framework, the way sampling operates is based on the assumption that the decoder is equipped with a softmax layer converting logits scores
\mathbb{R}^{T\times V}
into a discrete probability distribution
P\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, where
V
is the size of the dictionary.

Argmax.

Arguably, the most natural thing to do is to select the token associated with the highest
P_{i}
as output.
We refer to this as
argmax
.
Simply picking the most likely token comes with a lot of rigidity, since some tokens can attract a lot of mass, such as the
EOS
token.
Alternatively, it is very common to
sample
a random token using this distribution.
There are many ways to do so.

Stochastic sampling.

A first possibility is simply to
sample
according to
P
.
That is, sample a random variable with distribution given by
P
and select the associated token.
We denote
Y_{\text{sto}}=\text{LLM}_{\text{sto}}(x)
the random output obtained by stochastic sampling.

Temperature parameter.

A variation on the previous theme is to introduce a
temperature
parameter
\tau>0
.
The softmax transformation is modified into

P_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{exp}(z_{i}/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{V}\mathrm{exp}(z_{j}/\tau)}\,,

(1)

where
z_{i}
represents the logit score for the
i
-th token.
As recalled in Section
1
, high temperature gives a lot of variability
(
Yehuda-etal-2024
)
(equivalent to uniform sampling in the limit), while low temperature degrades to argmax sampling.

Beam Search.

Rather than considering only the most likely next token, beam search allows for a broader search over possible sequences by keeping multiple candidates.
This principle underlies beam search, a deterministic decoding algorithm widely used in the literature
(
Meister-etal-2020-beam
)
.
Formally,

\begin{split}S_{t}&=\operatorname{argmax}_{S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{t},|S^{\prime}|=w}\log p_{\theta}(S^{\prime}|x),\\
\text{where}\qquad\mathcal{B}_{t}&=\{s_{t-1}\circ s\mid s\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }s_{t-1}\in S_{t-1}\}\,.\end{split}

In the previous display,
S
is the stack of candidate token sequences maintained during beam search,
S_{t}
is the stack at the
t
-th time step,
\mathcal{B}_{t}
is the candidate set of next possible token sequences, and
\mathcal{V}
indicates the vocabulary list, and
s_{t-1}\circ s
is the operation of adding a sampled token
s
.
Beam search usually starts with
S_{0}=\{\text{BOS}\}
, and holds
w
-candidates of token sequences in
S
.
Beam search can keep the
w
-best path of tokens in selecting the
t
-th token, while the argmax sampling simply chooses the token with the highest probability value.
We denote by
y_{\text{beam}}=\text{LLM}_{\text{beam}}(x)
the token sequence generated by beam search.
Note that it is also possible to use beam-search with a temperature parameter, but we do not do so in this work, as is typical.
While aiming to find the most likely token sequence, beam search is known to sometimes generate low-quality outputs having a high likelihood
(
das-balke-2022-quantifying
)
.
Stochastic sampling offers a potential mitigation for this issue by introducing diversity into the generation process.
We aim to compare the sequences generated by beam search with those produced through stochastic sampling.
For a temperature parameter
\tau\in T
, we collect a set of token sequences
Y^{\tau}_{\rm sto}=\{\text{LLM}^{\tau}_{\text{sto}}(x)\}_{n=1}^{N}
generated by stochastic sampling.
To summarize, in this work
we compare
y_{\text{beam}}
and
Y^{\tau}_{\rm sto}
for various values of
\tau
.

Alternative sampling strategies.

To conclude this section, we note that there exist many other alternative sampling strategies, such as top-
k

(
fan-etal-2018-hierarchical
)
or
nucleus sampling

(
Holtzman-2020
)
.
They can mitigate the hallucinations by placing constraints on sampling the next tokens
(
Huang-2025
)
.

2.2
Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)

Figure 2
:

Illustration of the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD).
MMD measures the distance between two probability distributions by mapping them into a
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(RKHS) and computing the distance.

Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is a measure of distance between two probability distributions.
It relies on a positive semi-definite kernel
(
Hofmann-2008
;
Smola-2009
)
to map the distributions into a new space, a
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(RKHS), where probability distributions are represented as data points
(
Muandet-2017
)
.
A large MMD value indicates significant differences between two distributions.
We depict an intuitive presentation of the MMD in Figure
2
.

Formal definition.

Formally, for a given kernel
k
, the MMD between two distributions
P
and
Q
is defined as

\begin{split}{\rm MMD}_{k}^{2}(P,Q):=\ \mathbb{E}_{A,A^{\prime}\sim P}[k(A,A^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}_{B,B^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(B,B^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P,\ B\sim Q}[k(A,B)]\,,\end{split}

(2)

where
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
are independent random vectors drawn from
P
, and
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
are drawn from
Q
.

Empirical estimate.

Given i.i.d. samples
{\bf A}=\{A^{1},\dots,A^{n}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P
and
{\bf B}=\{B^{1},\dots,B^{m}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}Q
, one can estimate the MMD by replacing the expectations in Eq. (
2
) by the corresponding empirical averages:

\begin{split}&\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{U}({\bf A},{\bf B}):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq i^{\prime}\leq n}k(A^{i},A^{i^{\prime}})\\
&+\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq m}k(B^{j},B^{j^{\prime}})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(A^{i},B^{j}).\end{split}

(3)

This is an unbiased estimate of the MMD Eq. (
2
), and converges at the rate
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(\min(n,m)^{-1/2})
as
n,m\to\infty
provided that
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,x)<\infty

(
Gretton-2012a
, Theorem 10)
.

MMD in our setting.

Recall that our goal is to measure the similarity between the outputs of
\text{LLM}_{\rm beam}
and those of
\text{LLM}_{\rm sto}^{\tau}
.
To do this, we consider
\text{LLM}_{\rm beam}
and
\text{LLM}_{\rm sto}^{\tau}
as two probability distributions, and use Eq. (
3
) to get an estimate of the MMD.
Note that, since
\text{LLM}_{\rm beam}
deterministically generates a sequence, it is simply a constant random variable.

Vector representation of outputs.

Since most kernels (especially the ones used in this work) take vector values as input, we need to convert outputs into vector representation.
The most natural way is to resort to the word embedding matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times D}
used by our encoder-decoder.
Thus each token sequence
y
is associated with a sequence of embedding vectors

(e_{1},\ldots,e_{T})=E_{y_{1},:},\ldots,E_{y_{T},:}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}\,.

We introduce two aggregations of
T
-length vector representations and convert them into a fixed-length vector
h
;
avg
and
concat
.
The
avg
aggregation is computing the average over
T
vectors, namely

h=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\,.

The other possibility we investigate is the
concat
aggregation joins the
T
embeddings to form

h=[e_{1};e_{2};\ldots;e_{T};\underbrace{e_{\rm pad};e_{\rm pad};\ldots;e_{\rm pad}}_{T_{\max}-T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{\max}\cdot D}\,,

where
e_{\rm pad}=\mathbf{0}_{D}
, and
T_{\rm max}
is given as a hyperparameter.
Having the fixed-length vector representation, we denote the set of fixed-length vectors as
H:=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\}
.

Kernel choice.

In this work, we focus on the linear and the Gaussian kernel.
The linear kernel (
Dot
in the following)
k^{\text{lin}}(a,b)=a^{\top}b
is the simplest choice, and gives rise to simple measures of similarity.
Indeed, it is easy to show that the MMD in that case corresponds to the
L^{2}
distance between
y_{\text{beam}}
and the barycenter of
Y^{\tau}_{\rm sto}
.
On the other side, the Gaussian kernel is often preferred in machine learning applications, notably because of the flexibility of its parameter tuning.
For any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, it is defined as

k^{\text{Gau}}(a,b)=\exp\left(\frac{-\|a-b\|^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right)\,.

(4)

In the previous display, the bandwidth parameter
\gamma>0
controls the scaling of input distances before taking the exponential.
The median heuristic
(
Garreau2018-largesampleanalysismedian
)
, which sets the length scale to the median of pairwise distances, is commonly used in kernel methods due to its empirical effectiveness.
We explored various settings of length scale selections, and selected the best percentile at
25
-th percentile, which is reported in Appendix
F
.

To conclude this section, we note that MMD is a good choice when the data dimensionality is high and the available sample size is limited
(
Gretton-2012a
)
.
These MMD’s advantages are well-suited to our setting since the vectorized outputs can have quite a high dimension (especially in the
concat
setting), while the number of sampled sequences
H^{\tau}_{\text{sto}}
is strongly limited by our compute budget.

2.3
MMD Flagger

Now, we are equipped with all the machinery required to introduce our proposed method.

MMD-Flagger
is a simple rule-based approach checking MMD distances
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau}_{\rm sto})
for a grid of temperature parameters
T=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau\}
.
By default, we propose to take
T=\{0.1,\ldots,1.0\}
with a step of
0.1
.
For each
\tau\in T
, we query the model to sample token sequences
Y^{\tau}_{\rm sto}:=\{{y^{\tau}_{\rm sto}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and its vector representations
H^{\tau}_{\rm sto}:=\{h_{\rm sto}^{\tau}\}_{n=1}^{N}
by the stochastic sampling with temperature parameter
\tau
, as described in Section
2.1
.
We then compute the MMD distances defined by Eq. (
3
).
Namely,

\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{MMD}}:=(\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto}),\ldots,\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau}_{\rm sto}))\,.

Since an empirical MMD estimate (Eq
3
) converges to the true MMD as the sample size increases, the variability in the computed MMD trajectory gradually disappears, as empirically shown in Appendix
H
.

After computing these values, we detect the inflection point of this trajectory and flag it as a hallucination if it is close to
0
.
To detect this inflection point, we proceed in the following way:
first, we consider a minimal temperature
\tau_{0}
, set by default at
0.11
.
Then we compute
\tau_{\text{min}}
the temperature parameter realizing the minimal MMD distance between the reference and the stochastic samples.
In other words,
\tau_{\min}
is the index of
\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{MMD}}
with the minimal value.
Finally, we flag as hallucination if
\tau_{\min}
is greater than or equal to
\tau_{0}
.
Figure
1
showcases an example of the method at work in both the hallucination and the non-hallucination scenario.
We collect the operating procedure in Algorithm
1
.

Input:

LLM, source input
x
, temperature parameters
T
, number of samples
N
, a minimum temperature for detecting the hallucination
\tau_{0}

Obtain beam-search translation
h_{\text{beam}}=\text{LLM}_{\text{beam}}(x)

foreach

\tau\in T

do

Repeat stochastic translation in
N
times and obtain
H_{\text{sto}}^{\tau}=\text{LLM}_{\text{sto}}^{\tau}(x)

Compute the MMD distance
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau}_{\rm sto})
using Eq. (
3
)

end foreach

\tau_{\min}=\arg\min(\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{MMD}})

if

\tau_{\min}>\tau_{0}

then

return

hallucination

3.1
Datasets and metrics

LFAN-HALL

(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
is a hallucination detection benchmark dataset for the machine translation task from German to English.
The dataset consists in
3,413
machine translations obtained by a transformer-based encoder-decoder model relying on the
fairseq
package
Ott-2019-fairseq
and beam search,
together with associated reference translation and human-annotated hallucination labels.
Since we observed some inaccurate annotation labels, we undertook a review of the annotated labels and made corrections.
The relabeling process is detailed in Appendix
A
.
The dataset defines three types of hallucination labels occupying approximately
8\%
of all translations, and we unify these three labels as “hallucination.”
In this dataset, the beam search width is fixed to
5
.

Halomi

(
Dale-2022-detecting
)
is a hallucination detection benchmark dataset consisting over
3,000
sentences in
9
languages.
Similarly to
LFAN-HALL
,
Halomi
consists of beam search translations manually annotated for hallucination.
The dataset defines three types of hallucinations that occupy approximately
26\%
of the dataset, and we again unify them as “hallucination.”
Following the setting of
(
Himmi-2024-enhanced
)
, we use a distilled version of the multilingual translation model
(
Costa-2022-no
)
, totaling 600M parameters.
We use the open source Hugging Face implementation.
Beam search width was also set to
5
.

Evaluation Metrics.

We use recall and precision as evaluation metrics.
They are computed based on the binary labels associated with hallucination / not hallucination given by the datasets.
We treat the “hallucination” label as
true
label.
Recall is defined as
\frac{T_{p}}{T_{p}+F_{n}}
, and precision is defined as
\frac{T_{p}}{T_{p}+F_{p}},
where
T_{p}
denotes the number of true positives,
F_{p}
the number of false positives, and
F_{n}
the number of false negatives.

3.2
MMD-Flagger
settings

As temperature parameters, we set
T=\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.9,1.0\}
.
Previous work
(
Peeperkorn-etal-2024
)
used a smaller value
\tau<0.1
, however, in our setting, the minimum
\tau
is set to
\tau=0.1
since the
fairseq
package runs into numerical problems whenever
\tau<0.1
.
The set of stochastic sampling sequences
Y_{\text{sto}}^{\tau}
and corresponding vector representations
H_{\rm sto}^{\tau}
consist of
25
examples for each temperature parameter.
We obtain the embedding representation from the word embedding layer of the given LLM.
The embedding dimensions are
512
for
LFAN-HALL
and
1,024
for
Halomi
.

As described in Section
2.2
, we considered the linear(
Dot
) and Gaussian (
Gaussian
) kernels in our experiments.
To pick the bandwidth
\gamma
for the Gaussian kernel, we randomly select
200
(correct) translations as calibration data.
The
T_{\rm max}
of
concat
is set to the maximum token length among
200
translations.
We conduct experiments with various configurations of choosing the length scale and select the
25
-th percentile of
L^{2}
-distance, instead of the usual median heuristic
(
Garreau2018-largesampleanalysismedian
)
.
Supporting experiments for this choice can be found in Appendix
F
.
For the
Halomi
dataset, selecting the calibration data and calculating the length scale is done per source and target language pair.

We also benchmarked
MMD-Flagger
on internal representations of the model (namely, taking as
h
vectors of the intermediate hidden layers).
We did not find a consistent pattern in our results, while some internal representations yield better experimental results, see Appendix
G
.

3.3
Baseline Methods

We considered the baselines proposed by
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
; one rule-based method (
TNG
) and two uncertainty estimation methods (
MC-DSim, Seq-Logprob
).
Let us describe them briefly.

Top n-gram count
(
TNG
)
(
Raunak-etal-2021-curious
)
is a rule-based method that compares frequencies of
n
-grams between the source and the translation text.

TNG
flags the translation as the hallucination if certain
n
-gram counts have significant differences.

TNG
is designed to detect the oscillation type of hallucination; thus, it struggles to flag strong or fully-detached hallucination cases.
We set
n=4
following
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
.

Dissimilarity of Monte-Carlo hypotheses
(
MC-DSim
)
(
Fomicheva-2020-unsupervised
;
Zerva-2021-unbabel
)
is the average similarity scores between
y_{\text{beam}}
and
Y_{\rm MC}
, defined by

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{SIM}(y_{\text{beam}},Y_{\rm MC}),

where
Y_{\rm MC}
is a token sequence generated by the beam search using Monte-Carlo Dropout
(
Gal-2016-dropout
)
, and
N
is the number of Monte-Carlo Dropout generations.
Following
(
Guerreiro-2023-looking
)
, we set
N=10
and use the
METEOR
metric
(
Banerjee-2005-meteor
)
of the
nltk
package
(
Bird-loper-2004-nltk
)
to compute the similarity score.

Sequence log-probability
(
Seq-Logprob
) is the average of log-probability, defined by
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\log\mathbb{P}\left(y_{k}\middle|y_{<k},x,\theta\right),
where
x
is the source text,
y_{k}
is the
k
-th token in the translation using the beam search,
y_{<k}
is the previous tokens,
T
is the length of tokens in the translation, and
\theta
is the model parameters of a LLM.

MC-DSim
and
Seq-Logprob
are both using a fixed threshold to detect hallucination.
As
Guerreiro-2023-looking
suggests, we fix the threshold at the
40
-th percentile among a given dataset.
On the
LFAN-HALL
dataset, the threshold selection is performed using the whole dataset, while we compute the threshold per language pair of the source and target language on the
Halomi
dataset.
The threshold selection is described in more depth in Appendix
C.2
.

3.4
Discussion

Experimental results.

Table
2
lists the precision and recall scores on the benchmark datasets.

MMD-Flagger (concat-Gaussian)
matches the best baseline in terms of recall on the
LFAN-HALL
dataset (
Seq-Logprob
), and outperforms it (again
Seq-Logprob
) in terms of recall and precision on the
Halomi
dataset.

We see these results as very promising, and observe that they are robust with respect to changes in the kernel and aggregation method.

Concatenation versus averaging.

When comparing
avg
and
concat
, the
concat
aggregation generally yielded slightly superior or comparable performance to
avg
.
This trend is plausible, as
concat
retains all individual embeddings, whereas
avg
aggregates information through a mean operation, potentially losing finer-grained details.
Indeed,
concat
relies on a much larger vector representation of the output documents, of size
D\cdot T_{\text{max}}
.
This can become an issue for longer outputs or if the dimension of the word embedding is too high.
For instance, in our experiments with the
LFAN-HALL
dataset, the selected maximum token length (
T_{\text{max}}
) by the calibration data is
105
tokens.
Given that the dimensionality of a word embedding vector
D=512
, the resulting
concat
vectors have dimension
53,760
.
In contrast, the
avg
aggregation maintains a fixed-length vector size regardless of the token length.
Thus, the
avg
aggregation is generally more practical, even though detection scores can be slightly lower
than those of the
concat
aggregation.

4
Discussion

Limitations.

The first limitation of
MMD-Flagger
lies in its reliance on the source of hallucination.
The method is designed to detect hallucinations by identifying discrepancies between beam search and stochastic sampling outputs, as reflected in the trajectory of MMD distances.
Consequently, if the sampled sequences
Y_{\text{sto}}^{\tau}
are highly similar to the beam search output
y_{\text{beam}}
for low temperatures, hallucinations can go undetected.
It may be the case for an irreducible proportion of hallucinations.

Another limitation is the computational cost of
MMD-Flagger
, as it requires repeated stochastic sampling across multiple temperature settings.
This cost scales with the size of LLM and the length of the generated sequence, making the method less suitable for very large models or long outputs.
The computational cost of the MMD estimate can also be high, since it is quadratic in the number of generated sequences.
A practical workaround is to reduce the number of samples or temperature values used when computing the MMD trajectory.
However, the stability and reliability of
MMD-Flagger
under such reduced settings remain an open question.

Broader Impact.

Our work contributes to the development of safer and more widespread applications of LLMs.
Indeed,
MMD-Flagger
can enhance translation-related tasks by identifying hallucinations in LLMs outputs.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
MMD-Flagger
, a method for hallucination detection based on the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) metric.
The key idea is to compare output sequences generated by beam search and stochastic sampling across varying temperature settings.
By measuring the distributional distance between these sequences using MMD,
MMD-Flagger
achieves detection performance on par with the strongest uncertainty-based estimation methods on two machine translation benchmarks.
The method’s performance is robust with respect to changes in the output vectorization and choice of the kernel.

Future work includes extending
MMD-Flagger
to other generation tasks such as summarization or question-answering, using LLMs such as Qwen
(
Yang-2024-Qwen
)
or Llama
(
Grattafiori-2024llama
)
as models to inspect.
Benchmark datasets are available
(
Maynez-etal-2020-faithfulness
;
Bao-2025-naacl
)
, and the sampling strategy is the same.
The challenge here is the size of the models at hand and the outputs.

Another promising research direction is to investigate the interpretability of
MMD-Flagger
.
One possibility is to leverage recent work in feature importance for MMD-based testing
sutherland2017
;
mitsuzawa-2023
.
This would allow to pinpoint embedding features responsible for the detection of hallucinated content by
MMD-Flagger
.
Once such variables are located in intermediate layers, interpretability techniques such as Layerwise Relevance Propagation
(
Montavon-2019
)
could be applied to analyze how these variables relate to specific encoder layers or segments of the input.

More intuition on
MMD-Flagger

In detecting the hallucination by
MMD-Flagger

is the principal factor of forming the U-shape in the MMD distances trajectory, as shown in Figure

\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto})
is larger than
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau}_{\rm sto})

Figure

These distributions represent clear differences by the annotation labels; Comparing these distributions,
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto})
with the hallucination label represents the high volume in the center, while the distribution with the correct label depicts the high volume at the quite low
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto})
value.
In case of the machine translation errors, the distribution of
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto})
looks quite different from the distribution of the correct label.
There may be room for employing
MMD-Flagger
for detecting the machine translations; However, further studies are required.

Appendix H
Detection Stability of MMD-Hallucination-Flagger

In this section, we discuss the stability of
MMD-Flagger
in detecting hallucinations, focusing on the impact of randomness and sample size.
The detection process involves stochastic sampling, where a random selection of the next token is made based on the probability distribution generated by the softmax function, as detailed in Eq.(
1
).
Our empirical findings suggest that
MMD-Flagger
is effective in identifying hallucinations with sequences comprising just
25
tokens.

We used a subset of the
LFAN-HALL
dataset to iteratively generate token sequences, performing this process 10 times with sample sizes of
\{10,25,50,100\}
.
This subset consists of translation examples that all have hallucination labels, totaling
294
examples; consequently, we only report the recall score.

The vector representation is set to
avg
, and the kernel employed is a Gaussian kernel.
The length scale is determined to be the
25
-th percentile of
200
calibration data, following the same method described in Section
3.2
.
The settings for the Gaussian kernel remain consistent across all experiments, ensuring that the computed MMD distances are comparable.

Figure
6
illustrates the bar plots representing recall scores.
Notably, the range of recall scores for a sample size of
25
is closely aligned with that of
100
samples.
This observation suggests that a sample size of
25
is adequate for effectively detecting hallucinations.

A trajectory derived from a sample size of

The MMD distance
\widehat{\rm MMD}^{2}_{n}(H_{\rm beam},H^{\tau=0.1}_{\rm sto})

\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{MMD}}
