One Health-related risk factors and seasonal variations in zoonotic bacteriological contamination of livestock water sources
Authors: Chalachew Yenew, Abathun Temesgen, Asaye Alamneh Gebeyehu, Anley Shiferaw Enawgaw, Getaneh Atikilt Yemata, Amare Genetu Ejigu, Tilahun Degu Tsega, Almaw Genet Yeshiwas, Rahel Mulatie Anteneh, Zeamanuel Anteneh Yigzaw, Getasew Yirdaw, Sintayehu Simie Tsega, Abebaw Molla Kebede, Berhanu Abebaw Mekonnen, Meron Asmamaw Alemayehu, Habitamu Mekonen, Ahmed Fentaw Ahmed, Zufan Alamrie Asmare, Gashaw Melkie Bayeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater contamination by pathogens threatens livestock health, productivity, and food security. The study aims to assess bacterial contamination in livestock water, seasonal variation, and identify key One Health risk factors. A laboratory-based cross-sectional study analyzed 420 water samples from randomly selected animal farms. Standard microbial culture techniques identify zoonotic pathogens (Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Acinetobacter, and Escherichia coli (E. coli)). The analysis, which combined descriptive and multiple logistic regression with innovative visualizations, including a map of the South Gondar Zone, waffle plots, radial plots, and forest plots, was conducted using R version 4.5.1. This study employs a One Health framework to identify the primary factors driving zoonotic bacterial contamination in livestock feed and drinking water. Through descriptive analytics and multiple logistic regression, we identify three critical dimensions: seasonal variation (rainy season), animal species (ruminant, including goats and sheep, and poultry), and risk practices (wastewater discharge and shallow well use). Results reveal significantly higher contamination during rainy periods (e.g., Acinetobacter increased by 60% in livestock drinking water), while ownership of chickens (aOR = 2.52, *p* = 0.002), goats (aOR = 2.41, *p* = 0.001), and sheep (aOR = 1.72, *p* = 0.01) markedly elevated contamination odds. Wastewater discharge (aOR = 1.85, *p* = 0.03) and shallow well use (aOR = 1.45, *p* = 0.02) were significant anthropogenic risk factors. Visualization includes radial seasonal comparisons, diverging water-use bars, and sorted forest plots that highlight these synergies. This study highlights significant zoonotic bacteriological contamination in water used for livestock and feed production, with elevated levels during the rainy season. Key contributing factors include livestock type, wastewater discharge, shallow water sources, and seasonal variations. Adopting a One Health approach is crucial for mitigating contamination risks, focusing on enhanced water management, targeted interventions, and risk reduction strategies to protect both public and environmental health.Keywords: One Health, Zoonotic pathogens, Seasonal variation, Ruminants, Risk factors, Water contamination, Logistic regression, Visual analyticsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Microbiology, Planetary science, Biomarkers, Diseases, Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

Water quality is a critical component in ensuring the health and productivity of livestock, particularly in regions where agriculture and animal husbandry are closely linked to public health. In Ethiopia, the livestock sector plays a vital role in the economy, with a significant reliance on water sources for drinking and the production of mixed concentrate feed1. However, the contamination of water with harmful bacteria, including zoonotic pathogens, presents substantial health risks to both animals and humans. Zoonotic diseases, which are transmitted between animals and humans, are a growing concern in Ethiopia because of the lack of adequate water quality control and surveillance mechanisms. Understanding the bacteriological contamination levels in these water sources is essential for developing interventions to improve public health outcomes and promote food safety2.

Several studies have documented the presence of bacterial contamination in the water sources used for animal husbandry in developing countries, including Ethiopia. Pathogens such as Escherichia coli (E.coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), and Acinetobacter are commonly found in water used for both animal consumption and feed production, contributing to the spread of diseases such as diarrhea, mastitis, and foodborne illnesses in livestock and humans These three bacteria (E. coli, S. aureus, Acinetobacter spp.) were selected as key indicator and zoonotic pathogens of global concern that are commonly associated with livestock, water contamination, and a significant burden of human disease, aligning with the core One Health objective of the study3. Additionally, the One Health approach, which recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, has been increasingly adopted in studies addressing zoonotic diseases and waterborne pathogens. However, gaps remain in understanding the extent of bacteriological contamination in water used for drinking and feed production on livestock farms, particularly in regions such as the South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia. Despite the growing body of research on waterborne pathogens in Ethiopia, several knowledge gaps remain. Few studies have comprehensively assessed zoonotic bacteriological contamination in water sources used for both mixed concentrate feed production and drinking water in the same geographic region4. Existing studies often focus on either drinking water or feed production independently, overlooking the potential risks posed by the water used for mixed feed, which may have relatively high contamination levels5. Additionally, there remains limited research on how environmental conditions, social practices, and farm management factors collectively influence bacterial contamination levels in livestock water sources—particularly in rural and peri-urban contexts such as South Gondar Zone. This region is characterized by high livestock density and significant economic reliance on agriculture, yet it has been underrepresented in previous studies. The absence of systematic surveillance and the limited application of a One Health framework further exacerbate critical knowledge gaps in understanding and managing zoonotic water contamination risks in such settings. These factors collectively underscore the relevance of the South Gondar Zone as an ideal model for investigating this multifaceted public and animal health challenge6.

This study aims to address the existing gaps by providing a comprehensive analysis of bacteriological contamination in water used for both livestock drinking and feed production, with a specific focus on the South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia. By employing a One Health approach, research will examine how contamination in water sources impacts both animal and human health, taking into account farm management practices and environmental factors7. The findings will contribute to the understanding of the full extent of waterborne contamination risk and provide evidence for the development of integrated, multispectral interventions that address water quality in livestock farming, thereby improving public health and food safety in Ethiopia. This research also highlights the need for policy reforms to incorporate water quality management into livestock farming practices.

Methods and materials

Study design period

This study employed a laboratory-based cross-sectional design to evaluate the bacteriological contamination of water used in the production of mixed concentrated livestock feed and for drinking in the South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia. The cross-sectional design enables a snapshot of water quality at a specific point in time, which is ideal for identifying the prevalence of bacterial contamination and its associated factors. Given the nature of the study, the focus is on identifying zoonotic pathogens in water sources, particularly Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Acinetobacter, which are indicators of fecal contamination and potential health risks to both livestock and humans from February 2024 to June 2024.

Study area

The study was conducted in South Gondar Zone, a region in the Amhara administrative zone of Ethiopia. The South Gondar Zone is an area renowned for its diverse farming systems, where both livestock production and crop cultivation are prevalent. The region’s diverse water sources, including rivers, boreholes, and wells, serve as critical water supplies for livestock and agricultural activities. The study area was specifically chosen because of its agricultural relevance and the known challenges in maintaining water quality in rural farm settings. The geographical location and environmental conditions of this region also make it an ideal setting for evaluating the One Health approach, as human, animal, and environmental health are closely interlinked (Fig. 1).

Map of South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia.

Administrative boundaries were processed and visualized using R statistical software (version 4.5.1; https://www.R-project.org/) with the following packages: sf (version 1.0–14) for spatial data handling and ggplot2 (version 3.4.2) for visualization. Administrative boundaries were sourced from the Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency (2013). The map was created specifically for this study by the authors (Fig. 1).

Sample collection

A total of 420 water samples were collected from randomly selected animal farms in the study area. Of these, 210 samples were obtained from water used for livestock drinking, and 210 samples were taken from water used in mixed concentrate animal feed production. The farms were selected using a two-stage random sampling technique. In the first stage, kebeles (the smallest administrative units) were randomly selected from a list of all kebeles in the study zone. In the second stage, a list of all households with livestock in the selected kebeles was obtained from agricultural offices, and the required number of households (farms) was selected using a computer-generated random number table. The sampling was conducted across different seasons to account for potential seasonal variations in water quality. Seasonal variations in water quality were considered as a potential factor influencing contamination levels. Data collection spanned multiple seasons, and stratification was performed by wet and dry seasons. Rainfall patterns and agricultural activities during each season were documented to provide context for understanding the dynamics of contamination. We also used a structured questionnaire and observational checklist to collect data on farm management practices, water source types, and other One Health-related risk factors. To ensure standardization and minimize contamination, water samples were collected in sterile containers and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions (4 °C) within six hours of collection. Care was taken to follow proper hygiene and safety protocols during sample collection to prevent external contamination.

The sample size for the study was calculated using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies, which takes into account the population size, the expected proportion of contamination, and the desired confidence level. Given that no prior study in the region has estimated the prevalence of bacteriological contamination in these specific water sources, a conservative estimate of 50% prevalence was used, which maximizes the sample size8. The final sample size was 420. This number was determined by adjusting the initial calculated sample size of 384 to account for the design effect associated with the clustered sampling method.

Sample analysis

The water samples were analyzed for the presence of Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Acinetobacter species using standard microbial culture methods. Samples were cultured on selective media—MacConkey agar for E. coli, mannitol salt agar for S. aureus, and Acinetobacter selective agar for Acinetobacter spp. Following incubation, colony counts were performed, and bacterial species were confirmed through a series of biochemical tests. These included coagulase and catalase tests for S. aureus, oxidase and motility tests for Acinetobacter, and IMViC tests (Indole, Methyl Red, Voges-Proskauer, and Citrate) for E. coli. It is important to note that while this study isolated and identified bacterial species known to contain pathogenic strains, the primary focus was on assessing contamination levels as a risk factor for zoonotic transmission rather than confirming pathogenicity through molecular characterization of virulence genes9.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using R version 4.5.1. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions, were used to summarize the bacterial contamination levels for each water source. Multiple logistic regression was used to identify factors significantly associated with contamination levels, including farm management practices, water source type, and seasonal factors. Statistical significance was determined at the 5% level.

After which a series of advanced visualization techniques including a waffle chart for population characteristics to intuitively display proportional data, radial charts for seasonal comparisons to emphasize cyclical patterns and dramatic percentage changes, diverging bar charts for direct comparison between water uses to starkly illustrate contrasts in contamination levels, and a sorted forest plot for risk factor analysis to create an unambiguous, hierarchical visualization of effect sizes and statistical significance were constructed to transform complex statistical outcomes into accessible, visually engaging, and scientifically rigorous narratives for diverse audiences.

Results

Profile of the study population

Among the study population, the majority (67.9%) had over three years of experience in livestock holding. Chickens were the most common livestock class (48.5%), while 65.5% of the respondents reported wastewater discharge practices around their area. Water containers were predominantly cleaned within one day (34.5%), and shallow wells were the primary source of water (77.8%). Jerri Cans were the most used water storage containers (94%), reflecting widespread reliance on this container type. Table 2 illustrates the seasonal variations in zoonotic bacteriological contamination levels in water used for the preparation of mixed concentrates and livestock drinking, focusing on three types of bacteria: Acinetobacter, S. aureus, and E. coli. The contamination levels for Acinetobacter were notably higher during the rainy season compared to the dry season. Specifically, the mean contamination for water used in the preparation of mixed concentrates was 21.5 (SD = 2.2) in the rainy season, which decreased to 13.4 (SD = 1.5) during the dry season. For S. aureus, a similar trend was observed, with contamination being higher in the rainy season. The mean contamination in water for mixed concentrates was 3.2 (SD = 0.9) during the rainy season, dropping to 0.8 (SD = 0.3) in the dry season. In water used for livestock drinking, contamination levels were 22.4 (SD = 2.4) during the rainy season, decreasing to 16.1 (SD = 1.8) during the dry season. This further emphasizes the significant seasonal influence on bacterial contamination, particularly in water sources during wetter conditions (Table 2).

E. coli contamination also exhibited higher levels in the rainy season. The mean contamination for mixed concentrate water was 4.9 (SD = 1.3) during the rainy season, and 1.5 (SD = 0.6) in the dry season. Similarly, for water used for livestock drinking, contamination was 9.8 (SD = 1.7) in the rainy season, compared to 5.2 (SD = 1.3) in the dry season. These findings reflect the impact of seasonal weather changes, with the rainy season contributing to a greater microbial load (Table 2).

Finally, when considering the total contamination across all three bacterial types, the results also align with the seasonal pattern. During the rainy season, the total contamination for water used in mixed concentrates was 24.0 (SD = 3.1), while for livestock drinking water it was 25.6 (SD = 3.1). In contrast, during the dry season, the totals were significantly lower, at 14.8 (SD = 2.0) for mixed concentrate water and 7.5 (SD = 2.1) for livestock drinking water. This highlights the higher overall bacterial contamination levels in the rainy season, with a marked reduction in the dry season (Table 2).

The overall bacterial contamination levels across both waters used for preparing mixed concentrates and livestock drinking water show significant variation. For mixed concentrates water, the mean contamination was 17.4 (SD = 2.0), ranging from 10.2 to 25.6, while for livestock drinking water, the mean contamination was 20.9 (SD = 2.6), ranging from 12.5 to 30.5. Across all sample types, the total mean contamination was higher in water used for livestock drinking compared to water for mixed concentrates, with bacterial levels of 20.9 (SD = 2.6) for livestock water and 17.4 (SD = 2.0) for mixed concentrates. This indicates that livestock drinking water generally exhibited more significant contamination than water used for preparing mixed concentrates (Table 2). Risk factors for zoonotic bacteriological mean prevalence of contamination with water used for livestock feed production and drinking

The multiple logistic regression analysis reveals several significant determinants of zoonotic bacteriological contamination levels in water. Livestock holding experience, specifically having less than three years of experience, was not significantly associated with water contamination (AOR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.55–1.32, p = 0.15). However, the class of livestock played a critical role, with owning sheep (AOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.31–2.80, p = 0.01), chickens (AOR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.67–3.87, p = 0.002), and goats (AOR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.51–3.50, p = 0.001) significantly increasing the odds of water contamination compared to owning dairy cows. Wastewater discharge also emerged as a significant factor, with those practicing wastewater discharge (AOR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.02–2.44, p = 0.03) exhibiting higher odds of contamination than those who did not. The frequency of cleaning water containers showed a slight but non-significant trend, with cleaning within one day (aOR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.68–2.11, p = 0.04) and two to three days (AOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.78–2.98, p = 0.06) associated with higher odds of contamination. Water sourced from shallow wells (AOR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.92–2.54, p = 0.02) was also significantly more likely to be contaminated compared to tap water. The type of water container, specifically JerriCans (AOR = 1.70, 95% CI: 0.89–2.01, p = 0.07), showed a trend towards increased contamination but was not statistically significant. Finally, the rainy season (AOR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.11–2.05, p = 0.01) was associated with significantly higher odds of water contamination compared to the dry season. In summary, livestock type, wastewater discharge practices, shallow water sources, and the rainy season were all significant contributors to higher levels of zoonotic bacteriological contamination in water (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the bacteriological contamination levels of water used for livestock drinking and the production of mixed concentrate feed, as well as the associated determinants. Understanding these contamination risks is essential for informing interventions to safeguard livestock and public health. The findings highlighted significant associations between E. coli contamination and factors such as livestock type, water source, and frequency of container cleaning, providing valuable insights into the drivers of microbial risk.

The observed high contamination with Acinetobacter and E. coli aligns with previous studies in similar settings, which reported substantial microbial contamination in water sources used for livestock10. For example, research in rural sub-Saharan Africa revealed that shallow wells are particularly vulnerable to fecal contamination, which is consistent with our findings of higher contamination levels in these sources11. However, unlike some studies that reported negligible differences in contamination between container types, this study highlighted a trend (although not statistically significant) toward greater contamination in JerriCans than in plastic buckets12.

The findings of this study highlight critical gaps in understanding the factors contributing to bacterial contamination of water used for livestock drinking and feed production in Ethiopia. The association between livestock types and contamination risk, particularly the elevated odds observed in chickens, highlights the need for a more in-depth investigation into poultry-specific management practices. Chickens are often raised in densely populated environments, which can lead to rapid waste accumulation and create favorable conditions for bacterial proliferation. Additionally, inadequate waste disposal and poor housing conditions in poultry farming could further exacerbate contamination risks. This would provide valuable insights for designing targeted interventions to mitigate contamination in poultry farming systems. The significant association between shallow wells and higher contamination risk reflects vulnerabilities in the water infrastructure and environmental management. Shallow wells are particularly susceptible to contamination from surface runoff, often containing agricultural and domestic waste, as well as from inadequate sealing or maintenance. These factors increase the likelihood of bacterial infiltration, especially in regions with poor sanitation infrastructure. A more thorough discussion on these infrastructural and environmental vulnerabilities is necessary to inform policy and practice. Addressing these issues through improved well design, regular maintenance, and protective measures, alongside community-based education on water hygiene, would contribute to reducing contamination risks and enhancing the sustainability of livestock water resources13.

These findings underscore the need for policies promoting proper livestock management, regular water container hygiene, and improved water source protection. Programs should prioritize training livestock owners on the risks of infrequent cleaning and encourage the use of covered, secure water sources such as tap water. The high levels of E. coli contamination indicate risks of fecal contamination, necessitating targeted interventions to address wastewater discharge practices near water sources1.

Strengths and limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study was effective for determining the prevalence of bacteriological contamination and identifying associated risk factors at a specific point in time. However, this design inherently limits our ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and contamination trends or to observe temporal dynamics. While our data provides a valuable snapshot of seasonal variations, a longitudinal study would be necessary to validate these findings and more definitively explore how contamination fluctuates over extended periods in response to climatic and management changes. Furthermore, in the absence of molecular confirmation of virulent genes, we have explicitly avoided making claims of direct pathogenicity for the isolated bacterial species. This remains a limitation of the study, and the findings should be interpreted as indicating a risk of contamination and potential for zoonotic transmission rather than confirmed pathogenicity.

Conclusion and recommendation

Conclusion

This study conclusively demonstrates that zoonotic bacteriological contamination in livestock water is driven by the convergence of environmental, animal, and human behavioral factors within a One Health framework. The rainy season significantly amplifies contamination levels across all bacterial species, particularly S. aureus and E. coli. Animal species play a crucial role, with chickens, goats, and sheep posing substantially higher risks than dairy cows. Critical risk practices include wastewater discharge near livestock areas, reliance on shallow well water sources, and the use of JerriCan containers. These findings underscore the interconnection between ecological conditions, animal husbandry practices, and human management decisions in creating public health risks.
