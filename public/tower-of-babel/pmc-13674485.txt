Transcriptional control of meiotic recombination by DREAM complex components
Authors: Hasibe Tunçay, Xing Ma, Katja Müller, Yuxuan Guo, Valerio Filippo Toccaceli, Huiqi Fu, Franziska Böwer, Lucas Lang, David Latrasse, Yingxiang Wang, Moussa Benhamed, Arp Schnittger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Meiotic chromosome segregation and recombination, essential for sexual reproduction and evolution, require a specialized gene set. Despite its importance, the transcriptional regulators that directly orchestrate meiotic gene expression remain unidentified in plants. We identify the MuvB core components known from the DREAM complex as key transcriptional regulators of meiotic recombination in Arabidopsis. Loss of DREAM components TCX5/6 and ALY1/2 leads to impaired recombination and fertility defects. Meiocyte-specific transcriptome profiling and genome-wide binding analyses reveal that core recombination genes, including the crossover-promoting helicase MER3, are direct targets. Consistently, MER3 overexpression partially rescues recombination defects in DREAM-compromised plants. These findings establish the DREAM complex components as important regulators of the meiotic program in plants. Given that DREAM dysfunction causes reproductive defects in animals, this regulatory role may be conserved across multicellular eukaryotes.

INTRODUCTION

The DREAM complex is an ancient transcriptional regulator across eukaryotes, with conserved components spanning from humans to flowering plants (1–5). Pioneering studies in Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila, and mammalian cells revealed a conserved and crucial role of the DREAM complex [named after dimerization partner (DP), RB-like, E2F, and MuvB core] in regulating genes involved in cell cycle and development (2). In mammals, the complex operates in functionally distinct forms: The canonical repressive DREAM complex (MuvB, DP, Rb-like, E2F) silences cell cycle genes during quiescence, while the activator complexes MuvB-Myb (MMB) and MuvB-FOXM1 drive gene expression during S phase and mitosis, respectively (6, 7). The capacity to act as both a transcriptional repressor and activator is a conserved property of the complex, as also demonstrated in Drosophila, where the dREAM/MMB complex exhibits both positive and negative activities on distinct target gene sets in proliferating cells (8).

Recently, the full complement of the animal DREAM complex has been identified in the flowering plant Arabidopsis, including homologs of the MuvB-core components LIN9 known as ALWAYS EARLY (ALY1, ALY2, and ALY3), the LIN54/TESMIN/TSO1-LIKE CXC homologs (TCX5 and TCX6), homologs of LIN52 (LIN52A and LIN52B), and homologs of ABNORMAL CELL LINEAGE PROTEIN 37 (LIN37A and LIN37B) (3, 4, 9). In addition, plant-specific components have been identified, including BTE1/DRC2, which mediates DREAM-dependent transcriptional repression by antagonizing H3K4me3 deposition at target gene promoters (3, 10, 11), but it is currently not clear to what extent there are different DREAM/MuvB complexes with specific cell cycle or developmental roles. In plants, the activator/repressor switch operates through MYB3R transcription factors. MYB3R4 acts as a transcriptional activator, while MYB3R3 and MYB3R5 act as repressors, forming distinct DREAM-like complexes in a mutually exclusive manner (9). Notably, no plant homologs of B-Myb and FOXM1 have been identified, suggesting that the plant DREAM complex achieves activator/repressor switching through a distinct mechanism compared to animals (1, 9). Functional studies have shown that the Arabidopsis DREAM complex transcriptionally represses genes related to the cell cycle and maintenance of DNA methylation in somatic tissues (4, 9). In addition, it was found to restrict plant growth following DNA damage (3).

Whether the DREAM complex plays any role in reproductive development and meiosis in plants, however, has remained unexplored. Given that DREAM dysfunction causes fertility defects across animals (8, 12–15) and that precise transcriptional control of meiotic gene expression is essential for faithful recombination and chromosome segregation, we investigated whether the DREAM complex regulates meiotic gene expression in Arabidopsis. Here, we show that core DREAM complex components ALY1, ALY2, TCX5, and TCX6 are required for crossover (CO) formation, that TCX5 directly binds and regulates key meiotic recombination genes including MER3, and that it acts as both a transcriptional repressor and activator in a tissue-specific manner.

RESULTS

DREAM mutants exhibit meiotic defects that compromise fertility

To explore a possible role of the DREAM complex in reproductive development, we analyzed single mutants of the core MuvB components, i.e., aly1, aly2, aly3, tcx5, tcx6, lin37a, lin37b, lin52a, and lin52b (fig. S1). As none of them differed notably from wild-type (WT) plants, we generated double mutants of presumed redundant components. While lin37a/b, lin52a/b, aly1/3, and aly2/3 double mutants were indistinguishable from WT, aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants exhibited severe sterility, with reduced seed sets (WT, 60 ± 0.4; aly1/2, 35 ± 1.8; tcx5/6, 11 ± 1.6; Fig. 1, A and B) and pollen viability (WT, 99.5 ± 0.2%; aly1/2, 91 ± 1.0%; tcx5/6, 86 ± 2.2%; P < 0.0001 for all comparisons against WT; Fig. 1, C and D).

Furthermore, both aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants displayed pollen size variation (Fig. 1C), hinting at gametophytic and/or meiotic defects. However, the transmission efficiency of tcx6 was unaffected when tcx5 homozygous/tcx6 heterozygous plants were crossed with WT (male: 102%; female: 106%; table S1), indicating a sporophytic origin of the defects.

To assess whether female meiosis and male meiosis are differentially affected in tcx5/6, we performed reciprocal crosses between tcx5/6 and WT plants and quantified seed set in F1 siliques. Both crossing directions resulted in significantly reduced seed numbers compared to WT × WT crosses (WT × WT: 41.6 ± 5.9; tcx5/6 × WT: 21.9 ± 8.3; WT × tcx5/6: 31.9 ± 11.4; P < 0.0001 and P = 0.0152, respectively, fig. S2, A and B). Notably, the reduction in seed set when tcx5/6 was used as the male parent exceeds what would be expected from the 14% pollen viability alone, suggesting that a fraction of apparently viable tcx5/6 pollen carries meiotic defects that only manifest as fertilization failure or early seed abortion. The seed set was more severely reduced when tcx5/6 was used as the female parent, and the difference between the two crossing directions was significant (P = 0.01), suggesting that female meiosis is more strongly affected than male meiosis in tcx5/6. Consistent with this, chromosome spreads and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining of whole ovules revealed univalent formation at metaphase I in tcx5/6 female meiocytes (fig. S2, C and D), demonstrating that female meiosis is also impaired in tcx5/6.

Given that male meiocytes are more experimentally accessible, we analyzed meiotic progression by chromosome spreads to characterize the underlying defects in detail. Both double mutants showed unpaired chromosomal regions at pachytene (Fig. 1E, g and m, arrowheads), whereas WT chromosomes were fully paired. Pairing defects are followed by incomplete synapsis at late pachytene (fig. S3A). At diakinesis and metaphase I, univalents were observed in the mutants (Fig. 1E, h, i, n, and o) instead of the five bivalents seen in WT (Fig. 1E, b and c). The average numbers of bivalence were 5.0 in WT, 3.8 in aly1/2, and 3.3 in tcx5/6 (n = 81, 48, and 46, respectively; aberrant metaphase I cells showing chromosome entanglements were excluded from this calculation). At interkinesis, we identified lagging chromosomes and unbalanced chromosome pools (Fig. 1E, j, k, p, and r, white arrowheads), and after meiosis II, the formation of triads and polyads could be observed (Fig. 1E, l and s, and fig. S3B) instead of balanced tetrads as in WT (Fig. 1E, f).

These meiotic abnormalities indicated severe defects in recombination in DREAM complex mutants. The average number of chiasmata, the physical manifestation of CO, during metaphase I (Fig. 1F) was significantly reduced in both aly1/2 (6.5 ± 0.5) and tcx5/6 (5.3 ± 0.5) compared to WT (9.5 ± 1.7; n = 59, 47, 39; P < 0.0001 for all comparisons against WT). Notably, 11% of aly1/2 and 15% of tcx5/6 meiocytes lacked chiasmata entirely, demonstrating that the DREAM complex components ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 are important for CO formation and faithful chromosome segregation in Arabidopsis.

DREAM complex components are required for class I CO formation

To determine which step in the recombination pathway was impaired in DREAM mutants, we assessed meiotic double-strand break (DSB) formation. DSBs are catalyzed by the SPO11 complex (16, 17) in the context of the chromosome axis, a proteinaceous structure, from which DNA loops emerge. The axis includes integral axis proteins such as ASYNAPTIC3 (ASY3) as well as axis-associated proteins such as ASY1 (18, 19), which build a scaffold for SPO11. SPO11-mediated DSBs generate 3′ single-stranded DNA overhangs coated by RAD51 and DMC1 to promote strand invasion and joint molecule formation (20, 21). Quantification of RAD51 foci at zygotene revealed no significant difference between aly1/2, tcx5/6, and WT [P = 0.64 for aly1/2 versus WT and P = 0.96 for tcx5/6 versus WT, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); fig. S3, C and D], suggesting that DSB formation and their initial processing are unaffected in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants.

A subset of processed DSBs is resolved as COs by two pathways: class I and class II (22–24). Class I COs, which account for ∼85% of total COs in Arabidopsis, rely on the so-called ZMM proteins such as MER3 (25), PTD (26), SHOC1 (27), ZIP4 (28), MSH4/5 (29), and HEI10 (30). Assessing class I COs by quantifying HEI10 foci in late pachytene revealed a significant reduction in aly1/2 (6.2 ± 0.5) and tcx5/6 (5.8 ± 0.6) compared to WT (10.0 ± 0.2; n = 10 for all genotypes; P < 0.0001 for all comparisons against WT; Fig. 1, G and H), indicating that ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 function is in particular required for class I CO formation.

To further dissect how CO formation pathways are affected in the DREAM mutant background, we generated genetic combinations of tcx5/6 with key CO pathway components. Triple mutants of tcx5/6 spo11-1 were indistinguishable from spo11-1 single mutants, with both showing a complete absence of COs at metaphase I (Fig. 2, A and B), placing TCX5/6 function downstream of SPO11-1–dependent DSB formation.

To assess the contribution of class I COs to the residual CO formation in tcx5/6, we generated the tcx5/6 msh4 triple mutant, which significantly reduced the average bivalence number from 3.2 in tcx5/6 to 1.1 in the triple mutant (P < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 2), with the fraction of cells showing complete CO loss increasing from 15% in tcx5/6 to 46% in tcx5/6 msh4. These results demonstrate that while class I CO formation is severely compromised in tcx5/6, a residual class I CO remains. Combining tcx5/6 with mus81 similarly reduced average bivalence numbers to 2.2, with 42% of cells showing complete CO loss, indicating that a fraction of the remaining COs in tcx5/6 are class II/MUS81–dependent (Fig. 2, A and B). As mus81 single mutants show bivalent numbers comparable to WT (5.0), the significant reduction of average bivalence number seen in tcx5/6 mus81 compared to the tcx5/6 background (2.2 and 3.2, respectively, P < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA) indicates that residual CO formation in tcx5/6 becomes disproportionately dependent on the MUS81-dependent class II pathway.

To investigate whether ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 act in the same pathway, we generated the aly1/2 tcx5/6 quadruple mutant. Notably, the quadruple mutant displayed a markedly enhanced CO loss phenotype compared to tcx5/6, with average bivalence dropping to 1.1 (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA) and more than 60% of metaphase I cells showing a complete absence of COs (Fig. 2). This near-complete loss of CO formation demonstrates that MuvB activity is not completely abolished in neither aly1/2 nor tcx5/6 mutants, consistent with the finding that the aly1/2/3 triple mutant is lethal (4). In addition, these results corroborate the involvement of a MuvB core–containing complex in controlling meiotic recombination in Arabidopsis.

Consistent with a function in meiosis, we found that ALY1, TCX5, and TCX6 localized to meiotic chromosomes, as revealed by genomic reporter lines (Fig. 1I and fig. S4, A to C). Colocalization of TCX6 with the chromosome axis protein ASY3 confirms that DREAM complex components localize to meiotic chromosomes (fig. S4D). The enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)-TCX5 reporter nearly completely restored fertility in tcx5/6 (the average bivalent numbers from 3.3 to 5.0; fig. S5, A and B), validating its functionality. The ALY1-EGFP reporter largely rescued aly1/2, increasing bivalent numbers from 3.8 to 4.8, close to the WT level of 5.0 (fig. S5B), although residual meiotic defects suggest that ALY1-EGFP may not be fully functional.

Meiotic transcriptome analysis reveals deregulation of cell cycle and DNA repair genes

To pinpoint the genes responsible for the meiotic defects in aly1/2 and tcx5/6, we compared the transcriptomes of male meiocytes from both mutant combinations with WT. Whole-transcriptome sequencing yielded more than 71 million reads per sample (three replicates per genotype), with >91% of the reads achieving 99.9% base-calling accuracy (table S2). A total of 17,775 genes were identified to be transcribed in WT, 17,840 in aly1/2, and 18,110 in tcx5/6. Of these, 17,176 genes were commonly expressed across all genotypes (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis revealed 655 up-regulated and 256 down-regulated genes in aly1/2 and 1358 up-regulated and 685 down-regulated genes in tcx5/6 compared to the WT (Fig. 3, B and C). The greater number and magnitude of gene expression changes in tcx5/6 (Fig. 3, B and C, and table S3) correlate with its more severe meiotic defects relative to aly1/2 (Fig. 1, F and H). Possibly, ALY3 partially compensates for the loss of ALY1/2, consistent with the embryonic lethality of aly1/2/3 triple mutants (4).

To identify the biological pathways represented by the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we performed a gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the deregulated genes in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 compared to WT. Among the up-regulated genes, both mutants showed a significant enrichment for the GO term cell cycle, with 53 genes in aly1/2 [false discovery rate (FDR) = 1.50 × 10−15] and 123 in tcx5/6 (FDR = 5.60 × 10−37; Fig. 3, D and E), consistent with a conserved role of the DREAM complex in cell cycle regulation (1, 7, 31). In addition, GO terms related to DNA repair and DNA metabolic process are significantly enriched in tcx5/6 [62 genes (FDR = 3.30 × 10−17) and 104 genes (FDR = 5.10 × 10−27), respectively; Fig. 3, D and E]. By contrast, GO enrichment analysis of the down-regulated genes did not identify any terms directly associated with the meiotic defects observed in the mutants (fig. S6).

To uncover the molecular basis of reduced COs in mutants of DREAM components, we focused on the expression of genes involved in meiotic recombination. Two observations may account for the CO defects: (i) up-regulation of CO-suppressing factors, such as FANCM, RECQ4A, and RMI1, and (ii) down-regulation of the CO-promoting factor MER3 (Table 1).

The CO-suppressing factor FANCM promotes DSB repair via synthesis-dependent strand annealing (32), while RECQ4A/B, being part of the RTR complex together with TOP3 α and RMI1, resolves double Holliday junctions into noncrossovers (NCOs) (33, 34). Mutants in FANCM, RECQ4, and RMI1 exhibit elevated CO numbers because of increased class II COs (32, 34, 35). Although up-regulation of these suppressors has not previously been associated with reduced CO levels, it is tempting to speculate that their elevated expression contributes to the CO defects observed in DREAM mutants.

MER3, a conserved eukaryotic helicase, promotes CO formation by catalyzing D-loop migration and double Holliday junction formation. Like other ZMM mutants, mer3 exhibits decreased levels of COs, leading to univalent formation, recapitulating the defects seen in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 (36).

Together, our expression analyses of isolated mutant meiocytes revealed the deregulation of several central recombination regulators in the absence of core DREAM components. We hence hypothesized that the deregulation of one or likely more of these genes is the cause for the observed recombination defect in aly1/2 and tcx5/6.

TCX5 directly binds promoters of cell cycle and DNA repair genes

To address whether the transcriptional deregulation of the above-identified meiotic recombination regulators in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 could be a result of the direct regulation by the DREAM complex components, we performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) using anthers of plants harboring a functional EGFP-TCX5 reporter (fig. S5). This resulted in the identification of 3470 TCX5-enriched genes. Among these, 271 were up-regulated and 98 were down-regulated in tcx5/6, showing a significant overlap between TCX5-enriched targets and DEGs in the mutant (P = 1.1 × 10−25 and P = 0.001, respectively; Fig. 3F).

TCX5 CUT&Tag reads were predominantly enriched around transcription start sites (TSSs), as expected for DREAM complex binding (Fig. 3, G and H, and fig. S7A) (4, 8, 12, 37). HOMER motif enrichment analysis of these regions identified several overrepresented motifs, with the most enriched being the GC-rich motif (P = 1 × 10−858), followed by a G-box motif (P = 1 × 10−246; Fig. 3I). We also detected motifs recognized by tesmin/TSO1–like CXC domain–containing proteins (P = 1 × 10−36), E2Fs (P = 1 × 10−21), and MYB3Rs (P = 1 × 10−25; Fig. 3I), which have been shown to be plant DREAM–associated transcription factors (3, 4).

A GO term enrichment analysis of genes both TCX5-enriched and up-regulated in tcx5/6 revealed significant enrichment for categories related to DNA repair and cell cycle (Fig. 3J), while TCX5-enriched and down-regulated genes in tcx5/6 did not yield GO terms obviously related to the observed meiotic defects (fig. S7B). Reflecting shared meiotic defects of aly1/2 and tcx5/6, 141 of the 655 genes up-regulated in aly1/2 were direct TCX5 targets, and 119 of these were up-regulated in tcx5/6 (fig. S7, C and D). GO term enrichment analysis of these 119 genes similarly highlighted cell cycle and DNA repair categories (fig. S7E).

Notably, we found no evidence of TCX5 binding at the promoter of FANCM, indicating that its up-regulation is unlikely to be directly mediated by TCX5. In contrast, TCX5 was detected at the promoter of RECQ4A and RMI1 (table S3). While RECQ4A expression was found to be increased in tcx5/6 and in aly1/2 meiocytes, RMI1 transcript levels were only elevated in tcx5/6 meiocytes (Table 1). In addition, no transcriptional up-regulation was observed for TOP3α, the third core component of the RTR complex (table S3). These findings suggest that the RTR complex is unlikely to account for the reduced COs observed in aly1/2 and tcx5/6.

Transcriptional activation of MER3 by DREAM components promotes CO formation

Among the TCX5-bound genes differentially expressed in DREAM mutants, the down-regulation of the CO-promoting helicase MER3 stood out as a candidate directly linking TCX5 activity to reduced CO formation. To test whether MER3 down-regulation functionally contributes to the CO defects in DREAM mutants, we first characterized TCX5 binding at the MER3 promoter in detail. TCX5 binding was detected 315 base pairs (bp) upstream of the MER3 start codon (Fig. 4A), and within this peak region, we identified the TCX/CXC-domain binding motif found in this study, as well as motifs recognized by MYB3R and E2F transcription factors and the previously described TCX5 binding motif (4).

To assess the functional importance of this TCX5-bound region for MER3 transcriptional regulation, we performed a transient dual-luciferase assay in tobacco leaves using different versions of the MER3 promoter fused to a luciferase reporter, with TCX5 and TCX6 as effectors (Fig. 4B). When TCX5 and TCX6 were coexpressed, the full MER3 promoter (1016 bp upstream of the start codon) showed a significant reduction in luciferase signal (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA), while TCX5 alone showed a reduction trend that did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06), suggesting that TCX5 and TCX6 act cooperatively to repress MER3 transcription (Fig. 4C). A promoter fragment containing only the TCX5 peak region was sufficient to recapitulate this down-regulation, demonstrating that TCX5 binding to this region is sufficient to mediate repression. Conversely, deletion of the TCX5 peak from the full promoter resulted in significantly elevated luciferase signal (P < 0.0001) compared to the 1016-bp promoter, confirming that this region is necessary for full repression. Consistently, a short promoter version (315 bp upstream) lacking the TCX5 peak and any TCX binding motifs similarly showed significantly elevated luciferase activity (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4C). Together, these results establish that the TCX5-bound region in the MER3 promoter is both necessary and sufficient for TCX5/TCX6–mediated transcriptional regulation. We note that because this experiment was performed in tobacco leaves, which lack a meiotic context, it reflects the repressive function of the DREAM complex characteristic of somatic tissues. Nevertheless, it directly demonstrates that TCX5 and TCX6 bind and functionally regulate the MER3 promoter through this specific region, supporting the conclusion that MER3 is a direct transcriptional target of the DREAM complex components TCX5 and TCX6.

Having established that the TCX5-bound region in the MER3 promoter is functionally important for its transcriptional regulation, we next sought to directly test whether MER3 down-regulation contributes to the CO defects observed in DREAM mutants. We therefore generated a construct in which the genomic sequence of MER3 fused to EGFP was driven by the meiosis-specific DMC1 promoter. Notably, DMC1 expression was up-regulated in aly1/2 and tcx5/6, suggesting that the DMC1 promoter might confer a strong expression of MER3 in meiocytes (Fig. 5A and Table 1).

We observed that MER3-EGFP localization was confined to male meiocytes (Fig. 5B), and the pDMC1-MER3-EGFP construct fully restored recombination in homozygous mer3 mutants (Fig. 5, C and D, and fig. S8A), validating its functionality. We next introduced this construct into tcx5−/− tcx6+/− and aly1−/− aly2+/− mutants to assess whether increased MER3 expression could restore CO formation defects. In contrast to its meiocyte-restricted localization in mer3, MER3-EGFP was additionally detected in the tapetal cells surrounding the meiocytes in both aly1/2 and tcx5/6 anthers (Fig. 5B and fig. S8B). Given that DMC1 is a direct TCX5 target (Fig. 5A) and TCX5 binding is lost in DREAM mutants, this ectopic tapetal expression reflects the derepression of the DMC1 promoter in somatic anther cells upon loss of DREAM function, consistent with its canonical repressive role in somatic tissues (4, 9). This observation directly demonstrates that the DREAM complex restricts DMC1 promoter activity, and potentially that of other meiotic genes, to meiocytes within the anther, thereby enforcing the spatial specificity of meiotic gene expression.

Notably, MER3-EGFP expression in aly1/2 increased the proportion of meiocytes with five bivalents from 40 to 55% (Fig. 5C), and cells exhibiting two, one, or no bivalents were no longer observed. MER3-EGFP expression in tcx5/6 reduced the fraction of cells with no bivalents from 15 to 1% and those with one bivalent from 9 to 4% (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, MER3-EGFP expression significantly increased the average bivalent numbers from 3.8 to 4.8 in aly1/2 (P < 0.0001) and from 3.3 to 4.1 in tcx5/6 (P < 0.008; Fig. 5, C and D, e and g). However, it also elevated the incidence of chromosome entanglements, potentially contributing to the chromosome fragmentation seen in anaphase I (Fig. 5D, l and n). The partial but significant rescue of CO formation identifies MER3 as a major, although not the sole, target of the DREAM complex components in meiosis and suggests that additional targets of these components contribute to the full complement of COs in Arabidopsis.

The meiotic DREAM complex has a specialized configuration

Seeing that ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 directly promote the expression of MER3 in meiocytes and that, conversely, the restoration of MER3 expression profoundly promotes a WT-like recombination pattern in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants indicated that ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 not only have repressive but also activating functions in meiocytes. Previous work has indicated that the three-repeat MYB proteins MYB3R1 and MYB3R4 are part of an activating DREAM complex in Arabidopsis in a manner analogous to FOXM1-containing complexes. We therefore analyzed myb3r1/4 double mutants but did not observe any recombination defects similar to aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants (fig. S9, A and B). We then wondered whether bona fide repressive components genetically contribute to the defects seen in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 mutants. Therefore, we also analyzed triple mutants in the repressively acting MYBs (myb3r1/3/5) but again did not observe recombination defects (fig. S9, A and B). Last, we also generated transgenic plants in which we mutated the previously found RBR1-binding LxCxE motif (3) to AxAxA in the genomic TCX5/6 reporter. Notably, expression of these versions fully restored a WT-like meiotic phenotype in tcx5/6 mutants (fig. S9, B to E). Thus, it seems likely that ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 participate in a specialized DREAM complex in meiosis that neither contains the known activating nor the established repressive components of the canonical somatic DREAM complex.

The DREAM complex components act as a context-dependent transcriptional repressor and activator

In animals, the DREAM core complex has been shown to act as both a transcriptional repressor and activator depending on the developmental context (8, 12). Our data now reveal that DREAM complex components function as both transcriptional repressors and activators in male meiocytes. To explore this dual role, we integrated our meiocyte transcriptome data with previously published tcx5/6 and WT seedling transcriptome data (4), together with TCX5 binding profiles obtained from seedlings [chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq)] (4) and anthers (CUT&Tag; this study). We identified 1011 shared TCX5 target genes, with 2459 and 1732 genes uniquely bound in anthers and seedlings, respectively (fig. S10A). Among genes up-regulated in tcx5/6 meiocytes and seedlings, 126 were common TCX5 targets enriched for cell cycle–related GO terms, consistent with the conserved repressive role of the DREAM complex (fig. S10, B and C) (1, 37, 38). In contrast, TCX5-bound genes showed no significant overlap with genes down-regulated in tcx5/6 seedlings (fig. S10D), whereas a large overlap exists in tcx5/6 male meiocyte down-regulated genes and TCX5-bound genes in anthers (Fig. 3F). Moreover, no down-regulated genes in tcx5/6 meiocytes and seedlings were shared TCX5 targets (fig. S10E). These findings led to the interesting hypothesis that TCX5’s activating role is specific to reproductive tissues.

We note that this comparison integrates CUT&Tag data from anthers with ChIP-seq data from seedlings, and the difference in experimental technique may limit direct interpretation of differences in binding profiles. However, CUT&Tag data have been shown to recover the strongest and most functionally relevant ChIP-seq peaks with equivalent biological enrichments (39), supporting the biological relevance of the tissue-specific differences we observe.

Regulatory framework for meiotic gene expression controlled by DREAM complex components

To further explore the role of the DREAM complex components in reproductive development, particularly in meiosis, we examined 135 genes with established meiotic functions that are transcriptionally active in meiocytes (table S3). To this end, we assessed their transcriptional regulation and TCX5 binding in seedlings (4) and meiocytes/anthers. In seedlings, TCX5 binds to 93 of these genes. Of these, 84 were transcriptionally repressed in DREAM complex mutants, including key regulators of class I and II CO formation, DSB formation and repair, and synaptonemal complex assembly (table S3). These results suggest that the DREAM complex broadly represses meiotic gene expression in nonreproductive tissues, confining their activation to the appropriate developmental context. This finding is consistent with an accompanying paper by Guo et al. (40) that shows that the DREAM complex represses meiotic recombination in somatic cells.

To then analyze the transcriptional regulation of these 135 genes in reproductive tissues, we adopted a more permissive cutoff for TCX5 enrichment, i.e., fold change >2 versus >4 used in the genome-wide CUT&Tag analysis. This approach accounts for the low proportion of meiocytes in anther tissue, which may dilute meiocyte-specific signals. Nonetheless, we cannot fully exclude the fact that some TCX5 binding sites unique to meiocytes may remain undetected.

In reproductive tissues, we identified five distinct regulatory patterns that illustrate how the DREAM complex components control reproductive gene expression (Fig. 6, A and B, and table S3): (i) Fourteen genes were not bound by TCX5 in neither anthers nor seedlings, indicating that they are not TCX5 targets in either context. (ii) Sixteen genes were not bound by TCX5 in anthers but were bound (and either repressed or unregulated) in seedlings, including the ZMM genes HEI10 and MSH5, suggesting their release from DREAM-mediated repression in reproductive tissues. This pattern supports the above observation that the canonical DREAM complex is a key regulator of reproduction-specific gene expression. (iii) Fifty-nine genes were bound by TCX5 in anthers but not differentially expressed in meiocytes, such as ASY1 and ASY3 (chromosome axis formation) as well as SPO11-1 and SPO11-2 (DSB initiation). Possibly, these genes represent canonical DREAM complex targets whose expression is suppressed in the somatic cells of the anther but is already derepressed in meiocytes. Consequently, loss of TCX5/6 function would not further alter their expression in meiocytes, as the repression would already be lifted. (iv) Forty-four genes were bound by TCX5 in anthers and repressed in meiocytes, including RAD51, X-RAY REPAIR CROSS COMPLEMENTING 3 (XRCC3), MEIOSIS DEFECTIVE 1 (MEI1), RMI1, and RECQ4A (table S3). These are largely associated with DNA repair (41, 42) and may be subjected to phase-specific regulation, e.g., during DSB formation and initial repair in leptotene and zygotene. Notably, DMC1 also falls into this category. As shown above, the loss of TCX5/6 causes both the ectopic expression of DMC1 in the anther tissues surrounding the meiocytes and its up-regulation within the meiocytes (Fig. 5, A and B). Class IV therefore may encompass two regulatory modes: temporal repression (e.g., DMC1 down-regulation after early prophase) and spatial restriction to meiocytes, reflecting the multiple mechanisms through which TCX5 governs development-specific gene expression. (v) Two genes, MER3 and PS1, the latter of which is important for proper meiotic spindle formation (43), were bound by TCX5 in anthers and transcriptionally activated in meiocytes. Given MER3’s essential role in class I CO formation, this highlights an activator function of the DREAM complex components in reproductive tissue and suggests a direct link to the control of key meiotic processes.

DREAM complex–mediated regulation of meiotic genes.

(A) Classification of 135 meiotic genes with established meiotic roles that are expressed in meiocytes. Class I: no TCX5 enrichment in either seedlings or anthers, indicating they are not direct DREAM targets. Class II: not bound by TCX5 in anthers but bound in seedlings; their transcription is repressed or unchanged in both seedlings and meiocytes. Class III: enriched for TCX5 binding in anthers but with no transcriptional changes in meiocytes. Class IV: TCX5-bound in anthers and transcriptionally repressed in meiocytes. Class V: TCX5-bound in anthers and transcriptionally activated in meiocytes. (B) Hypothetical model of DREAM-mediated regulation of meiotic genes across somatic and reproductive tissues. In seedlings, meiotic gene expression is repressed by the DREAM complex. In reproductive tissues, class II genes are derepressed, indicating reprogramming toward a reproductive state. Class III genes appear regulated at the anther level rather than in meiocytes, potentially restricting their expression to meiocytes. The repression of class IV genes in meiocytes suggests phase-specific control that confines their expression to a particular stage of meiosis and/or spatial restriction to meiocytes. Class V genes, MER3 and PS1, highlight an unusual activator function of the DREAM complex in reproductive tissue.

DISCUSSION

Here, we identify the DREAM complex components ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 as transcriptional regulators of meiotic gene expression in Arabidopsis. A key target of their action is MER3. Partial restoration of bivalent formation by MER3 expression, particularly in tcx5/6, suggests that additional targets of these components contribute to meiotic recombination. Moreover, the chromosome entanglements seen in particular in tcx5/6 further implicate ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 in broader meiotic functions.

Notably, no transcription factor has yet been found that directly controls meiotic gene expression in Arabidopsis or any other plant species. Our work shows that the DREAM complex components ALY1/2 and TCX5/6 function as both a repressor and activator of transcription during reproduction. Their repressive role is consistent with prior studies in somatic tissues of Arabidopsis (3, 4, 40) and with the conserved function of DREAM complexes in other species (37, 38, 44). Together with Guo et al. (40), our work highlights that the DREAM complex represses a large subset of meiotic genes in somatic cells (e.g., seedlings), mirroring the somatic repression of germline genes observed for dREAM in C. elegans (38, 40, 45). Furthermore, in the reproductive context, the DREAM complex components fine-tune gene expression within anthers, restricting the expression of recombination genes, including DMC1, specifically to meiocytes. Possibly, this repression is even crucial to establish temporal regulation during meiosis, for example, restricting the expression of recombination genes to meiotic prophase I.

We uncover that DREAM complex components activate key meiotic genes, including MER3, and this activator role appears to be specific for reproductive processes, raising the possibility of a specialized DREAM variant in reproduction. In somatic tissues, the Arabidopsis DREAM complex switches between repressor and activator states through MYB3R transcription factors, with MYB3R3/5 acting as a repressor and MYB3R4 as an activator (9).

However, neither myb3r1/4 activator nor myb3r1/3/5 repressor mutants display CO defects (fig. S9, A and B), suggesting that MYB3Rs are not involved in TCX5/6- and ALY1/2-mediated meiotic gene regulation. Furthermore, TCX5/6 variants carrying mutations in the RBR1-binding LxCxE domain do not exhibit meiotic defects (fig. S9, B and C), indicating that TCX5/6 and RBR1 interaction is dispensable for meiotic gene regulation. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that RBR1 participates in a meiotic DREAM variant through alternative interaction interfaces or via other complex components. These mutant variants are properly expressed and show the same meiotic localization pattern as their respective WT proteins (fig. S9, D and E).

Together, these results further highlight the possibility that a specialized DREAM complex variant, distinct in composition from the canonical somatic complex, governs meiotic gene expression in reproductive tissues. The composition of such a variant and its potential meiosis-specific interactors remain to be determined.

Notably, the DREAM-like complexes are required for fertility across different animals, e.g., C. elegans, Drosophila, and mice (8, 12–15). In mice, the testis-specific TCX5 homolog TESMIN/METALLOTHIONIEN-LIKE 5 (MTL5) interacts with LIN9, LIN37, and MYBL1, forming the MYBL1-MuvB complex (46); loss of either TESMIN and MYBL1 causes male sterility resulting from pachytene arrest, and MYBL1 was shown to activate genes involved in male meiosis (47). In Drosophila, the testis Meiotic Arrest Complex (tMAC), a testis-specific paralog composed of dREAM components alongside testis-specific factors, activates genes essential for the G2/meiosis I transition and spermatid differentiation in primary spermatocytes, while the canonical dREAM complex represses genes not expressed in spermatocytes (13, 44, 48–51). This division of labor between a canonical repressive complex and a reproductive tissue-specific activating variant is notably analogous to what we propose here for Arabidopsis.

In C. elegans, the DRM complex, a functional analog of DREAM, regulates germ line–specific transcriptional programs (12, 45), and its loss results in sterility and ectopic germ line–like expression of DNA repair genes in somatic cells (38). A complementary study by Guo et al. (40) now directly demonstrates this phenomenon in Arabidopsis: TCX5/6 repress meiotic DSB-formation genes in somatic tissues through limiting H3K4me3 deposition at their promoters, and loss of this repression leads to ectopic DSB formation and genomic instability. Consistent with this, our data show that the DREAM complex restricts DMC1 promoter activity to meiocytes; when MER3 is expressed under the DMC1 promoter, ectopic expression of MER3-EGFP in tapetal cells is observed in aly1/2 and tcx5/6 but not in WT.

In summary, our study identifies a possible DREAM complex variant as a key transcriptional regulator of meiotic gene expression in plants, operating through a context-dependent dual function that activates and represses distinct subsets of meiotic genes to ensure their expression at the right time and place during reproductive development. Given that DREAM dysfunction causes reproductive defects across animals and that testis-specific DREAM variants have evolved in both mammals and Drosophila to fulfill activating roles in meiosis, the regulatory logic uncovered here is likely conserved across multicellular eukaryotes. Our identification of MER3 as a direct target of DREAM complex components, whose function in meiocytes may involve a specialized complex variant, provides the first mechanistic understanding into how these components control the meiotic recombination program and provides additional insights into understanding how the composition and context specificity of DREAM complexes are tuned to meet the transcriptional requirements of reproductive development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

The Arabidopsis thaliana accession Columbia (Col-0) was used as a WT reference throughout this study. All mutants and transgenic lines used in this study were in the Col-0 background. The transferred DNA insertion lines tcx5-1 (SALK_047165), tcx6-1 (GK-453H07), aly1-1 (SALK_073108), aly2-2 (SALK_118765C), aly3-4 (SALK_125138C), lin37a-2 (SALK_057175), lin37b-3 (SALK_103139C), lin52b-1 (GK_854A11), mer3-2 (SALK_091560), spo11-1-3 (SALK_146172), mus81-2 (SALK_107515), and msh4-1 (SALK_136296) were isolated from GABI-KAT and SALK transferred DNA collections and described previously (3). The lin52a-c1 mutant is generated via CRISPR-Cas9 and described in our previous study (3). The myb3r1-1, myb3r4-1, myb3r3-1, and myb3r5-1 alleles, and the myb3r1-1 myb3r4-1 and myb3r1-1 myb3r3-1 myb3r5-1 higher-order mutant combinations were previously described by Kobayashi et al. (9). Transgenic lines harboring PRORPS5A:TagRFP:TUB5 (45) and PROASY3:ASY3:RFP (52) reporter constructs were crossed with EGFP-TCX5, EGFP-TCX6, and EGFP-ALY1 transgenic reporter lines generated in this study. All plants were grown in growth chambers with a 16-hour light/21°C and 8-hour/18°C dark cycle.

Plasmid construction and plant transformation

To generate PROTCX5:EGFP:TCX5 and PROTCX6:EGFP:TCX6 reporter constructs, genomic sequences of TCX5 (5557 bp) and TCX6 (5157 bp) were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and subsequently cloned into the pENTR vector via the SLiCE reaction. A Sma I restriction site was introduced right after the start codon by PCR. The resulting construct was linearized by Sma I restriction and ligated with EGFP. To generate the PROALY1:ALY1-EGFP construct, the genomic sequence of the ALY1 (9252 bp) sequence was amplified as two fragments by PCR and inserted into the pENTR vector via the SLiCE reaction. A Sma I restriction site was introduced right before the stop codon by PCR. The resulting construct was linearized by Sma I restriction and ligated with EGFP. These constructs were subsequently transferred into the binary expression vector pGWB501 (53) by the Gateway LR reaction.

To generate the RBR1-binding domain mutant versions of TCX5 and TCX6, the LxCxE motif was mutated to AxAxA by reamplifying pDONR221-TCX5 or pDONR221-TCX6 with primers carrying the respective substitutions. The subsequent steps of EGFP fusion and cloning into the binary expression vector were performed as described above for the respective WT reporter constructs.

To generate PRODMC1:MER3:EGFP:TERREC8, the genomic sequences of MER3 covering the coding sequence (6259 bp) and an EGFP fragment were amplified by PCR and incorporated by the SLiCE reaction into the pENTR-PRODMC1:SmaI:TERREC8 vector linearized by Sma I restriction. The resulting fusion construct was then transferred into the binary expression vector pGWB501 by the Gateway LR reaction. All primers used for cloning are listed in table S4. A. thaliana transgenic lines were generated by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (54).

Pollen and seed viability assessment

Pollen viability was assessed as previously described (55). Three open flowers per plant were dipped into 12 μl of Peterson staining solution (10% ethanol, 0.001% malachite green, 25% glycerol, 0.005% acid fuchsin, 0.0005% orange G, and 4% acetic acid). Slides were covered with a coverslip, incubated on a preheated hot plate at 80°C for 5 min, and screened using a light microscope (Axioskop, Carl Zeiss GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) and the 20× objective. Six hundred pollen grains of five different plants were counted per line. To assess seed viability, seeds were evaluated from five siliques of at least five different plants per line.

Cell spreads

Flower buds from transgenic lines were fixed as previously described (56). Briefly, fresh flower buds were fixed in ethanol/acetic acid (3:1) for 48 hours at 4°C, washed with 75% ethanol three times, and stored in 70% ethanol at 4°C. For male meiotic spreads, flower buds between 0.3 and 0.5 mm in size were digested in an enzyme solution (10 mM citrate buffer containing 1.5% cellulose, 1.5% pectolyase, and 1.5% cytohelicase) for 3 hours at 37°C. Three digested flower buds were transferred onto a glass slide and mashed with a bent needle. The cell suspension was spread on an adhesive slide on a 46°C hot plate after adding 10 μl of 45% acetic acid. The slide was then rinsed with an ice-cold ethanol/acetic acid (3:1) solution and mounted in 15 μl of VECTASHIELD medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories).

For female meiocyte spreads, fixed flowers were washed twice with distilled water. Carpels were isolated, washed once with the citrate buffer, and transferred onto a sealing film. Fifteen to 20 μl of enzyme solution was added, and samples were covered to prevent evaporation and incubated at 37°C for 1.5 hours. After enzymatic digestion, samples were washed once with distilled water, transferred onto glass slides, and gently squashed in a small drop of distilled water. Slides were treated with precooled 60% acetic acid, gently mixed, incubated on a 45°C hot plate for 1 to 2 min, and then immediately fixed with precooled Carnoy’s solution (60% ethanol, 30% chloroform, and 10% glacial acetic acid). After air drying for 10 min, slides were stained with DAPI. Slides were observed using an Axiophot microscope (Zeiss) with a 63× oil-immersion objective.

DAPI staining of whole ovaries

To assess female meiotic progression, fixed flowers were washed twice with distilled water. The outer tissue of the carpel was carefully removed using fine forceps to expose the ovules. A drop of DAPI solution was applied to the ovules, which were subsequently covered with a coverslip and observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunostaining

Immunostaining was performed as previously described (57). Briefly, flower buds were dissected using needles, and anthers were transferred to a droplet of artificial pond water (0.5 mM NaCl, 0.4 mM CaCl2, 0.2 mM NaHCO3, and 0.05 mM KCl in deionized water) on a polylysine-coated slide. Anthers were gently squeezed to release meiocytes. The isolated meiocytes were collected into a 1.5-ml tube on ice and subsequently digested in 15 μl of digestion solution (1% cytohelicase, 1.5% sucrose, and 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone) in the dark for 30 min. Following digestion, meiocytes were transferred to a clean, charged microscope slide. Next, 20 μl of 1% Lipsol was added, and the sample was spread by tilting the slide. After a 4-min incubation at room temperature, 24 μl of 4% formaldehyde was added, and the slide was left to air dry. The slides were washed twice with 2× SCC (0.3 M NaCl and 30 mM Na3C6H5O7) for 5 min each. The primary antibody mix was then added, and the slide was covered by a piece of parafilm. Slides were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humid chamber. Slides were washed twice with 2× SCC for 5 min, followed by addition of the secondary antibody mix. Slides were again covered with parafilm and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a humid chamber. Last, slides were washed twice with 2× SCC for 5 min in the dark. Fifteen microliters of VECTASHIELD medium with DAPI was added to the slide and covered with a glass coverslip and sealed using nail polish. The following dilutions were used for the primary and secondary antibodies: anti-ASY1 1:10,000 (57), anti-RAD51 1:300 (58), anti-ZYP1 1:500 (29), anti-HEI10 1:300 (57), anti-guinea pig Alexa 488 1:400 (Abcam, no. ab150185), anti-rabbit Alexa 568 1:400 (Abcam, no. ab175471), and anti-rat Alexa 647 (Abcam, no. ab150159).

Microscopy

To detect protein localization in reporter lines, young anthers were dissected from floral buds in a drop of artificial pond water and imaged directly using an upright Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope equipped with a 60× water-immersion objective. Meiotic stages were determined on the basis of chromosome morphology, cell shape, or microtubule pattern visualized by the PRORPS5A:TagRFP:TUA5 marker. Green fluorescent protein (GFP) was excited at 488 nm and detected at 495 to 560 nm, and TagRFP was excited at 561 nm and detected at 570 to 650 nm.

For imaging immunohistochemical stained samples, high-resolution imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with an Airyscan detector (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), using a 63×/1.4–numerical aperture oil-immersion objective. Airyscan mode was used to improve the resolution and signal-to-noise ratio. Z-stacks were acquired at optimal intervals for three-dimensional reconstruction and processed using Zeiss Zen Black software (ZEN 2.3 SP1).

Male meiocyte isolation and RNA extraction

Anthers at developmental stages 6 to 11 covering all meiotic stages were dissected and collected in artificial pond water supplemented with RNasin Ribonuclease Inhibitor (1 U/μl; Promega, N2511). Anthers were gently squashed to release meiotic columns, and ∼300 columns per sample were transferred into ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes (Zymo Research, S6003-50) containing lysis buffer from the RNAqueous-Micro Total RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM1931) and then immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Dissections and collections were performed at 4°C from 16 plants per genotype for each replicate across three biological replicates. Samples were homogenized using a mechanical shaker, and total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the NanoChip Kit, and all samples showed high integrity (RNA integrity number >8.0).

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared by Novogene and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform using 150-bp paired-end reads (PE150). Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC. The reference genome index was built with HISAT2 (version 2.0.5), and paired-end reads were aligned to the genome using the same version. Gene-level read counts were obtained using featureCounts (version 1.5.0-p3). Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie (version 1.3.3b), and gene expression levels were reported as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM). Next, we used DESeq2 to identify DEGs with the thresholds: adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.5. GO enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using agriGO (59) version 2.0 (https://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/). GO terms with an adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

CUT&Tag sample preparation from Arabidopsis anthers

Anthers at stages 6 to 11 from the PROTCX5:EGFP:TCX5 tcx5 transgenic line were dissected to capture all stages of meiosis and collected in Nuclei Extraction Buffer [0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM MgCl2, and 1% Triton X-100] supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete ULTRA Tablets, Roche, 05892791001). Anthers were homogenized using a glass Dounce tissue grinder and incubated on a rotating wheel at 4°C for 10 min. The homogenate was filtered through 50-μm filters, and nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min at 4°C. Nuclei were washed three times with the same extraction buffer. The final pellet was resuspended in sucrose buffer [0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and 10 mM MgCl2] containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete ULTRA Tablets, Roche, 05892791001) and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide. Extracted nuclei were cryopreserved at −80°C in a controlled-rate freezing container. CUT&Tag was performed using the CUT&Tag-IT Assay Kit, Anti-Rabbit (Active Motif, 53160), with an anti-GFP antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11122) used to target EGFP-tagged TCX5. Library preparation was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

CUT&TAG data analysis

Paired-end reads of CUT&Tag raw data were trimmed by using Fastp (version 0.23.2). Using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.4) (60), the cleaned reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis reference genome assembly TAIR10. Duplicated reads were removed, and reads were filtered to keep only high-quality alignments (mapping quality score >30) using SAMtools (version 1.9) (61). Afterward, peaks were called using MACS3 (https://github.com/macs3-project/MACS) (62) with the threshold q value <0.05. ChIPseeker from the R package was used to investigate genomic distribution of CUT&Tag peaks. For genome-wide analyses, TCX5 targets were defined as regions with a fold enrichment >4. For analyses focusing on the promoters of the 135 meiotic genes (table S3), the fold-enrichment threshold was set to >2 to reduce the possibility of false negatives, given the low proportion of meiotic cells in anther tissues. To visualize the signals of CUT&Tag, RPGC-normalized bigWig files were generated using the bamCoverage option from Deeptools (version 3.5.6) (63). Motif enrichment analysis of TCX5 binding sites was conducted using findMotifsGenome.pl from HOMER, identifying motifs enriched with P < 0.05, which was determined by a binomial test.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To assess the transcriptional regulatory activity of TCX5 and TCX6 on the MER3 promoter, different promoter fragments were individually cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase (LUC) reporter gene in the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector, which additionally carries a Renilla luciferase (REN) gene under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter as an internal reference. TCX5 and TCX6 were used as effectors in transient coexpression assays carried out in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. LUC and REN activities were subsequently measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910), and LUC signals were normalized to REN activity to account for variation in transformation efficiency. The assay was performed in three independent biological replicates. Primers used for amplification of the MER3 promoter fragments are listed in table S4.

Image processing and HEI10 and RAD51 focus quantification

For image analysis of RAD51 and HEI10 immunostaining, maximum intensity projections were generated using the Z Project function in Fiji/ImageJ (version 2.14.0/1.54f). RAD51and HEI10 foci were manually counted on merged channels. Only RAD51 and HEI10 foci colocalizing with the chromosome markers (ASY1 and ZYP1, respectively) were included in the analysis. To ensure consistency across samples, all images were adjusted with identical brightness and contrast settings.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses (as indicated in the figure legends) were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software version 10.4.1). Error bars indicate standard deviations.
