Talking with a ghost: semi-virtual coupled levitated oscillators
Ronghao Yin, Yugang Ren, Deok Young Seo, Anoushka Sinha, Jonathan D. Pritchett, Qiongyuan Wu, James Millen

Abstract
Mesoscopic particles levitated by optical, electrical or magnetic fields act as mechanical oscillators with a range of surprising properties, such as tuneable oscillation frequencies, access to rotational motion, and remarkable quality factors. Coupled levitated particles display rich dynamics and non-reciprocal interactions, with applications in sensing and the exploration of non-equilibrium and quantum physics. In this work, we present a single levitated particle displaying coupled-oscillator dynamics by generating an interaction with a virtual or “ghost” particle. This ghost levitated particle is simulated on an analogue computer, and hence its prperties can be dynamically varied. Our work represents a new angle on measurement-based bath engineering and physical simulation, and in the future could lead to the generation of novel cooling mechanisms and complex physical simulation.



Keywords:
Levitated oscillators; virtual particle; analogue computer; semi-virtual coupling.

In this work, we present a novel coupled levitated oscillator formed of a ”real” levitated charged microsphere interacting with a virtual, or ”ghost”, oscillator generated via an analogue computer. We demonstrate the simulation of a levitated oscillator in an electronic analogue device, and explore the synthesis of a two-way Coulomb interaction with a charged microsphere levitated by a Paul trap in vacuum.

The experimental system used to create semi-virtual coupled oscillators is illustrated in Figure
1

, where a single levitated particle (real) is coupled to a virtual particle (ghost) simulated on an analogue computer. The real particle is a silica sphere of diameter

\times

Via a tracking algorithm, the EBC records the real particle position in 2D, which is streamed as an analogue voltage in real-time via an FPGA (Red Pitaya STEMlab 125-14). The details of the Paul trap geometry and EBC tracking can be found in Refs.

The motion of the real particle in 1D is described by the Langevin equation
[
19
]
:

\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{r}}=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{r}}-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{r}}+\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}},

(1)

where
q_{\mathrm{r}}
is the position of the real particle,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}
is its momentum damping rate,
\omega_{\mathrm{r}}
is the oscillation frequency,
M_{\mathrm{r}}
is its mass, and
\xi_{\mathrm{r}}=\sqrt{2\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}k_{B}T_{\mathrm{r}}M_{\mathrm{r}}}\eta(t)
is Brownian noise due to collisions with gas molecules and residual voltage noise such that
\braket{\eta(t)}=0
and
\braket{\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})}=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and
T_{\mathrm{r}}
is the centre-of-mass temperature of the real particle. We only consider motion along the direction perpendicular to the surface of the endcap electrodes. The power spectral density (PSD) of the real particle motion has the form:

S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)=\frac{k_{B}T_{\mathrm{r}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}/\pi M_{\mathrm{r}}}{(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}})^{2}}+c_{\mathrm{r}},

(2)

where
c_{\mathrm{r}}
represents measurement noise when we record the motion
q_{\mathrm{r}}
of the real particle.

The ghost particle’s 1D dynamics are simulated on an analogue computer (Anabrid
The Analog Thing
), which is a collection of analogue integrators, summers, multipliers and potentiometers that can be used to continuously solve differential equations. The analogue computer solves the Langevin equation of motion of a damped-driven harmonic oscillator in real-time to simulate the ghost particle:

\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{g}}=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}\times\left(-M_{\mathrm{g}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{g}}-M_{\mathrm{g}}\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{g}}+\xi_{\mathrm{g}}\right),

(3)

where
q_{\mathrm{g}}
is the position of the ghost particle,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}
is the momentum damping rate,
\omega_{\mathrm{g}}
is the oscillation frequency and
M_{\mathrm{g}}
is the effective mass of the oscillator. The noise term
\xi_{\mathrm{g}}=\sqrt{2\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}k_{B}T_{\mathrm{g}}M_{\mathrm{g}}}\chi(t)
is white noise added to the analogue computer to simulate Brownian motion such that
\braket{\chi(t)}=0
and
\braket{\chi(t)\chi(t^{\prime})}=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and
T_{\mathrm{g}}
is the effective temperature of the ghost particle. An illustration of the operation of the analogue computer is shown in Figure
1
b
, with the detailed configuration given in Appendix
A
. We are able to tune the ghost particle’s dynamics using potentiometers on the analogue computer:
\small1⃝
the initial position
q_{0}
,
\small2⃝

M_{\mathrm{g}}\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}
,
\small3⃝

M_{\mathrm{g}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}
,
\small4⃝

1/M_{\mathrm{g}}
. The amplitude of the white noise
\xi_{\mathrm{g}}
is controlled using a separate function generator (SRS DS335).

We demonstrate a semi-virtual coupling between the real and ghost particles to simulate the dynamics of coupled harmonic oscillators. The position signals from the real
q_{\mathrm{r}}
and ghost
q_{\mathrm{g}}
particles are combined and used to synthesize forces acting on each system, as shown in Figure
1
b
. We approximate a simulation of two charged particles interacting via the Coulomb force by considering a coupling which is linearly dependent on the distance between the two particles,
F_{i}=k_{i}(q_{\mathrm{r}}-q_{\mathrm{g}})
, where
i=\{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{g}\}
and
k_{i}
is the coupling strength. This is a reasonable approximation for the physically realistic scenario of small particle displacements compared to their separation.

The force acting on the real particle is created by amplifying (TTi WA301) the voltage output
F_{\mathrm{g}}
and applying it to an electrode placed
1.15\,
mm from the real particle. The force acting on the ghost particle is implemented on the analogue computer by inverting
q_{\mathrm{r}}
and summing it with
q_{\mathrm{g}}
, as illustrated in Figure
1
b
. Therefore, the dynamics of the coupled semi-virtual system can be described by the equations of motion:

\displaystyle\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{r}}-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{r}}+\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}(q_{\mathrm{g}}(t-\tau)-q_{\mathrm{r}}),

(4a)

\displaystyle\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{g}}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{g}}-\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{g}}+\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}(q_{\mathrm{r}}(t-\tau)-q_{\mathrm{g}}),

(4b)

where
\tau
is the time delay due to signal processing which introduces a phase delay between the real and ghost particles’ motions.
This semi-virtual system simulates two harmonic oscillators coupled through a spring-like interaction. However, unlike two real particles coupled through a physical interaction, one of the oscillators is virtual and the coupling is mediated through measurement.

3
Results

We first characterize the dynamics of the ghost oscillator simulated on the analogue computer, followed by the introduction of a coupling between the real and ghost particles. Finally, we show that by tuning the frequency of the ghost particle, one can synthesize a tunable coupling between the two oscillators.

4
Discussion

We have demonstrated tuneable coupling between two semi-virtual oscillators, one of which is a real charged microsphere levitated in vacuum and the other a near-identical ghost oscillator synthesized on an analogue computer. This proof-of-principle work represents a new toolbox for levitated particle manipulation, by considering it as hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) of an electronic control system. Charged particles levitated in a Paul trap are an ideal platform for physical analogue simulation, due to a high degree of environmental isolation in vacuum, and the possibility to engineer a user-defined environment via electrical forces
[
18
]
. The real-time feedback we use to generate an interaction between a single real particle and the analogue ghost is scaleable to arrays of particles or multiple degrees-of-freedom simultaneously
[
29
]
.
In this work we have only varied the relative oscillation frequency between the semi-virtual coupled oscillators, and the magnitude of linear coupling between the particle. Future work will consider oscillators with different properties, for example by sythesizing enhanced dissipation for the ghost to enable sympathetic cooling of the real particle. By unbalancing the interaction between the semi-virtual oscillators (
k_{\mathrm{r}}\neq k_{\mathrm{g}}
), it will be possible to synthesize non-reciprocal Coulomb interactions in our HIL system, which cannot be physically generated. Finally, we have only explored a linear coupling between the oscillators, yet it is possible to synthesize arbitrary interactions, which may potentially not be feasible to digitally simulate. This work is in the tradition of physical analogue engineering, which can be used to explore inaccessible areas of physics
[
35
]
and enable efficient information processing
[
40
]
.

Appendix B
PSDs for coupled harmonic oscillators

By rearrangin
Eq.

4
and taking the Fourier transform, the coupled system in the frequency domain reads

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}q_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}q_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{r}}\right)q_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{r}}q_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)}{M_{\mathrm{r}}},

(9a)

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}q_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}q_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{g}}\right)q_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{g}}q_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}.

(9b)

For simplicity, this system of equations can be written in the matrix format
\mathbf{D}(\omega)\mathbf{q}(\omega)=\mathbf{M}{\bm{\xi}}(\omega)
with
\mathbf{q}(\omega)=\left(q_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega),q_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)\right)^{T}
,
{\bm{\xi}}(\omega)=\left(\xi_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega),\xi_{\mathrm{g}}(\omega)\right)^{T}
and

\mathbf{D}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}-\omega^{2}+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{r}}\right)&-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{r}}\\
-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{g}}&-\omega^{2}+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{g}}\right)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

This is a linear system with the solution

\mathbf{q}(\omega)=\mathbf{G}(\omega){\bm{\xi}}(\omega)

(11)

where
\mathbf{G}(\omega)=\mathbf{D}(\omega)^{-1}\mathbf{M}
and

\mathbf{D}(\omega)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\Delta(\omega)}\begin{pmatrix}D_{\mathrm{gg}}(\omega)&-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}\\
-e^{-i\omega\tau}\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}&D_{\mathrm{rr}}(\omega)\end{pmatrix},

(12)

with
D_{\mathrm{rr}}(\omega)=-\omega^{2}+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{r}}\right)
,
D_{\mathrm{gg}}(\omega)=-\omega^{2}+i\omega\Gamma_{\mathrm{g}}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{g}}\right)
and
\Delta(\omega)=D_{\mathrm{rr}}(\omega)D_{\mathrm{gg}}(\omega)-e^{-2i\omega\tau}k_{\mathrm{r}}k_{\mathrm{g}}/M_{\mathrm{r}}M_{\mathrm{g}}
that one can read from
Eq.

10
.

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(\omega)\mathbf{S}_{\xi\xi}\mathbf{G}(\omega)^{\dagger}.

(13)

Given the white noise definition,

is a diagonal matrix with the diagonal terms

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,r}}(\omega)

(14a)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,g}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\Delta(\omega)\rvert^{2}}\left(\lvert D_{\mathrm{rr}}(\omega)\rvert^{2}\frac{\braket{\xi_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}\frac{\braket{\xi_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}}\right),

(14b)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,g}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\Delta(\omega)\rvert^{2}}\left(e^{i\omega\tau}D_{\mathrm{gg}}(\omega)\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}\frac{\braket{\xi_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}}+e^{-i\omega\tau}D_{\mathrm{rr}}^{\ast}(\omega)\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}\frac{\braket{\xi_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}\right),

(14c)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,r}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,g}}(\omega)^{\ast},

(14d)

which returns

2
and

If we are interested in the in-phase and out-of-phase modes
\{q_{\mathrm{i}}=q_{\mathrm{r}}+q_{\mathrm{g}},\ q_{\mathrm{o}}=q_{\mathrm{r}}-q_{\mathrm{g}}\}
, the corresponding PSDs for these modes can be obtained by following the above steps and taking the transformation

\mathbf{S}^{\prime}_{qq}=\braket{\mathbf{T}\mathbf{q}(\omega)\mathbf{q}(\omega)^{\dagger}\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}},\qquad\text{with }\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

which gives the corresponding terms

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\prime\,\mathrm{i,i}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,r}}(\omega)+S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,g}}(\omega)+S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,g}}(\omega)+S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,r}}(\omega),

(16a)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\prime\,\mathrm{o,o}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,r}}(\omega)+S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,g}}(\omega)-S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,g}}(\omega)-S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,r}}(\omega),

(16b)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\prime\,\mathrm{i,o}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,r}}(\omega)-S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,g}}(\omega)-S_{qq}^{\mathrm{r,g}}(\omega)+S_{qq}^{\mathrm{g,r}}(\omega),

(16c)

\displaystyle S_{qq}^{\prime\,\mathrm{o,i}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{qq}^{\mathrm{i,o}}(\omega)^{\ast}.

(16d)

In general, these resonant frequencies obtained from

\Delta(\omega)\equiv\omega^{4}-(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+\Omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2})\omega^{2}+\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}-\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}M_{\mathrm{g}}}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{o}}^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+\Omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2})^{2}-4\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}-\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}M_{\mathrm{g}}}\right)},

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}-\Omega_{\mathrm{g}}^{2})^{2}+4\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}M_{\mathrm{g}}}},

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{M_{\mathrm{r}}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{g}}}{M_{\mathrm{g}}}\right)^{2}},
