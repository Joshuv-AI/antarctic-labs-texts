Designing and validating a clinical competency logbook for workplace-based assessment in undergraduate medical education: A foundational psychometric analysis
Authors: Tayseer Mansour, Marwa Zalat, Ayat R Abdallah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe implementation of competency-based medical education (CBME) demands robust, validated workplace-based assessment (WBA) tools. In this study, we aimed to develop and conduct an initial validation of the clinical competency logbook (CCL) as a structured instrument designed to assess the observed clinical competencies of undergraduate medical students in authentic practice settings.MethodsA prospective validation study was conducted during the 2024–2025 academic year with 129 fourth-year medical students. The CCL was systematically constructed through literature review and expert consensus using a two-round Delphi process to establish content validity. Students were assessed using the CCL during two consecutive 4-week clinical rotations. Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way random-effects model. Exploratory fairness analysis was conducted based on subgroup comparisons between male and female students.ResultsThe CCL exhibited strong content validity throughout its expert-driven development process. Overall test–retest reliability was moderate to good (ICC = 0.711, 95% confidence interval [0.591, 0.796]). However, the reliability varied significantly by gender subgroups: ICC = 0.507 for males and ICC = 0.346 for females, which was attributed to ceiling effects and restricted score variance in the female subgroup. The mean scores differed significantly between genders, highlighting the need for further investigations of potential rater bias or other unmeasured variables, although these findings remain strictly exploratory.ConclusionThe CCL exhibited acceptable psychometric properties for formative assessment in undergraduate medical education. The strong content validity and moderate overall reliability (though highly dependent on score distribution and varying by subgroup) support its use for tracking student progress and providing structured feedback. These findings highlight the importance of contextual interpretation of reliability coefficients and suggest that caution is required for high-stakes summative decisions. In this study, we produced an evidence-informed instrument and methodological framework for developing WBA instruments in CBME programs.Keywords: Clinical competence assessment, Competency-based medical education (CBME), Instrument validation, Medical education, Psychometric properties, Test-retest reliability, Workplace-based assessment (WBA)

Introduction

The global adoption of competency-based medical education (CBME) has led to an urgent need for robust, psychometrically supported assessment tools that are capable of accurately measuring clinical competencies within authentic workplace settings.1,2 The principles of CBME are widely accepted but their successful implementation depends on the quality of the assessment instruments used to inform educational decisions.3 A significant gap persists between the demand for high-quality workplace-based assessment (WBA) and the availability of tools with documented psychometric integrity, and this challenge can undermine the foundation of entrustment decisions.4,5 Without rigorous, evidence-based validation, assessment data remain open to interpretation, potentially leading to inaccurate judgments of a learner's readiness for unsupervised clinical practice.6

To address this gap, the clinical competency logbook (CCL) was systematically constructed to serve as a comprehensive instrument for the direct observation and evaluation of undergraduate medical students. The development of the CCL was a multi-stage, evidence-based process designed to establish a strong foundation for content validity from its inception. The process began with a thorough review of established competency frameworks, including the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Core Competencies and the CanMEDS framework, to generate an initial item pool grounded in internationally recognized standards.7,8 This item pool was then refined through a formal two-round Delphi process with a panel of eight medical education experts. This rigorous method ensured that the final instrument's content was sufficiently relevant, representative, and comprehensive for its intended purpose, as a critical first step in any robust validation argument.9

The importance of the CCL extends beyond simple experience logging because it was designed as a structured evaluation instrument that provides standardized, quantifiable data on competency development. This is paramount for a CBME model, where learner progression is determined by demonstrated ability rather than time in training.10 The CCL is a comprehensive instrument encompassing a broad range of clinical domains, including history taking, case presentation, formal examination, whole consultation, hospital procedures, prescribing skills, and clinical debrief. The instrument was specifically designed for fourth-year undergraduate medical students who are at a stage of training focused on mastering foundational cognitive and communicative competencies. At this level, students are not yet expected or permitted to perform invasive clinical procedures independently, and thus the assessment framework prioritizes the depth of knowledge, clinical reasoning, and application to patient care. Performance is evaluated using a multi-level, behaviorally anchored scoring system (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10), where a score of 10 indicates “outstanding knowledge, enthusiastic/inspiring learner, logical and clear thinking process,” intermediate scores (2, 4, 6, 8) reflect progressive levels of knowledge application and clinical linkage, and a score of 0 indicates “very poor knowledge base and understanding.” This scale was deliberately chosen over an entrustment-based scale to align with the developmental stage and learning objectives of undergraduate students.11

In this study, we conducted a foundational validation of the CCL within a modern validity framework, where validity is understood as a unified concept supported by multiple sources of evidence.5,12 This study represents a foundational validation effort within Messick's unified framework. We primarily focused on content validity, reliability (temporal stability), and relationships to other variables, but we acknowledge that a comprehensive validation argument requires the ongoing accumulation of evidence across all five sources, including response processes and consequences.

Our primary objective was to accumulate robust evidence to support the validity and reliability of the CCL for its intended use in formative assessment. In particular, we aimed to: (1) provide evidence of content validity through the detailed description of our systematic instrument construction process; (2) examine the tool's reliability by evaluating its temporal stability using test–retest analysis; and (3) conduct exploratory fairness analysis by comparing scores across demographic subgroups to test for potential bias. By presenting this multifaceted validation evidence, we aim to contribute a psychometrically sound instrument to the field and provide a practical framework for the development and validation of WBA tools in the CBME era.

Materials and Methods

In this study, a prospective cohort design was employed to gather and analyze validity evidence for the CCL in accordance with modern validation frameworks.5,12 The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at our institution.

Participants and setting

The study cohort consisted of all 129 fourth-year medical students enrolled during the 2024–2025 academic year at the Faculty of Medicine, Suez Canal University, Egypt. Participation was a required component of their clinical curriculum. The assessments were conducted during two consecutive 4-week mandatory clinical rotations in internal medicine and surgery.

Instrument construction and content validity evidence

The development of the CCL was a systematic process designed to establish strong content validity from the outset. An initial blueprint and item pool were generated following a comprehensive review of established competency frameworks, including the ACGME Core Competencies and the CanMEDS framework, to ensure alignment with internationally recognized standards of medical education.7,8

This draft instrument was then subjected to a formal, two-round Delphi process involving a panel of eight senior medical education experts and clinical supervisors from our institution. The expert panel evaluated each item in terms of its relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness when assessing the core clinical competencies of a fourth-year medical student. Items were retained, revised, or eliminated based on predefined consensus criteria, which is a standard methodology for ensuring content validity in instrument development.13 The final logbook evaluation form was a comprehensive instrument scored out of 100 marks, encompassing a broad range of clinical domains, including history taking, case presentation, formal examination, whole consultation, hospital procedures, prescribing skills, and clinical debrief. Instead of using a simple Likert scale, performance was assessed with a multi-level, behaviorally anchored scoring system (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10). For example, in the “Learning from randomly selected items” section, a score of 10 indicated “Outstanding knowledge, Enthusiastic/inspiring learner, Logical and clear thinking process,” whereas a score of 2 indicated “Struggles to correctly/accurately find correct knowledge with poor linkage to patient care.” The complete logbook evaluation form, detailing all domains and behavioral anchors, is provided in Supplementary Appendix A.

Data collection procedures

Students were provided with the CCL at the beginning of each 4-week rotation. Clinical supervisors who were responsible for evaluating the students received a standardized 30-min orientation on the purpose of the CCL and the behavioral anchors. The objective of this training process was to enhance the consistency of ratings by establishing a shared mental model among raters. During the session, supervisors were introduced to the purpose and overall structure of the logbook evaluation form, guided through the specific domains (including history taking, examination, procedures, and prescribing), and familiarized with the detailed behavioral anchors associated with the multi-level scoring system (scale of 0–10), thereby reducing idiosyncratic interpretations and minimizing inter-rater variability. Supervisors completed the CCL for each student at the conclusion of the first rotation (Time 1) and again at the conclusion of the subsequent rotation (Time 2), and thus there was a 4-week test–retest interval.

Data analysis and validity framework

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Our analysis was structured to gather evidence for multiple facets of validity, as conceptualized in the unified framework of validity.5,12

Evidence for reliability

Evidence for the temporal stability of the CCL was primarily assessed through its reliability. Test–retest reliability: The temporal stability of the CCL scores was evaluated by comparing scores at Time 1 and Time 2. We calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using a two-way random-effects model based on a single-rating, absolute-agreement definition. This model was selected because it accounts for systematic errors between raters and is appropriate when the same set of supervisors is not guaranteed for each student across both rotations.14 ICC values were interpreted according to established guidelines: <0.5 (poor), 0.5–0.75 (moderate), 0.75–0.9 (good), and >0.9 (excellent).14

Evidence for relationships to other variables (exploratory fairness analysis)

We conducted subgroup comparisons to explore the relationships between CCL scores and demographic variables. We compared the mean scores for male and female students using a paired t-test. No difference was hypothesized based on clinical competence, but this analysis served as an exploratory investigation into potential differential functioning of the instrument across demographic subgroups, which can be an important source of validity evidence.5 A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Suez Canal University - Faculty of Medicine (reference number: 6275, February 2025). All participants provided informed consent, and data confidentiality was maintained throughout the study process.

Results

In total, 129 fourth-year medical students from the 2024–2025 academic cohort participated in the study, comprising 65 males (50.4%) and 64 females (49.6%). The analysis focused on accumulating validity evidence for the CCL by examining its reliability (temporal stability) and relationships with other variables.

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons

The mean CCL scores for male and female students at both Time 1 and Time 2 are presented in Table 1. Independent samples t-tests detected statistically significant differences in the mean scores for male and female students at both time points. At Time 1, the mean score for female students (mean = 92.4, SD = 6.3) was significantly higher than that for male students (mean = 79.0, SD = 9.0; p < 0.001). This finding provides initial evidence for the CCL's ability to detect variations between demographic subgroups, serving as an exploratory fairness analysis. The overall test–retest reliability for the entire cohort was ICC = 0.711 (95% confidence interval [0.591, 0.796]), indicating moderate to good reliability according to established psychometric guidelines,14 thereby suggesting that the CCL provided reasonably consistent scores over time for the student population.

However, subgroup analysis indicated significant variation in reliability between genders. The ICC was 0.507 for male students, indicating moderate reliability. By contrast, the ICC was 0.346 for female students, suggesting poor reliability. This discrepancy is visualized in the scatter plot in Figure 2, which shows that the pattern of scores around the line of perfect agreement was more diffuse for females compared with males. Figure 3 further contextualizes this difference by comparing the ICC values for each group against standard reliability benchmarks.14 To investigate the source of this discrepancy in reliability, the score distributions for each subgroup were analyzed. As shown in the histograms in Figure 4, the scores for female students were more homogeneous and clustered at the higher end of the scale (a ceiling effect) at both Time 1 (mean = 92.4, SD = 6.3) and Time 2 (mean = 89.9, SD = 5.6). This restricted variance in the scores for female students mathematically constrained the potential value of the ICC, providing a statistical explanation for the lower coefficient observed in that group.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a foundational validation of the CCL, providing crucial evidence for its use in the era of CBME. The present discussion is framed around the sources of validity evidence as outlined by modern validation theory, recognizing that this represents a foundational step and that complete validation requires accumulating ongoing evidence across all five of Messick's elements. Our findings support the use of the CCL as a psychometrically sound instrument for its intended purpose of formative assessment, while also highlighting the nuanced interpretation required for validation evidence in medical education.5,12

Evidence for content validity

It is important to contextualize the design of the CCL's assessment scale within the specific educational level that it targets. Entrustment-based scales are increasingly advocated in CBME, particularly for postgraduate training, but they are not developmentally appropriate for undergraduate medical students. At the fourth-year undergraduate level, students are primarily focused on acquiring foundational clinical knowledge and developing the ability to apply it in patient encounters. Due to patient safety regulations and their current level of training, undergraduate students are generally not expected or permitted to independently perform invasive clinical procedures, such as intravenous catheterization or nasogastric tube insertion. Therefore, an entrustment scale culminating in “performed independently” would be misaligned with their learning stage and could lead to unrealistic performance expectations. The CCL's multi-level, behaviorally anchored scoring system (0–10) provides a more developmentally appropriate and valid measure of undergraduate clinical competence. Future iterations of the tool, designed for postgraduate trainees, may incorporate appropriate entrustment-based scales for procedural domains.

The strongest evidence for the validity of the CCL came from its content. The systematic, multi-stage construction process, which included a comprehensive literature review of established competency frameworks and a formal Delphi process with content experts, ensured that the instrument's items were relevant, representative, and comprehensive.9,13 This rigorous development process directly addresses the call for more robust and evidence-based approaches to assessment design in CBME,3,4 providing a strong foundation for the instrument's use.

Evidence for reliability (temporal stability) and its nuances

The overall test–retest reliability of the CCL was moderate to good (ICC = 0.711), suggesting that the instrument provided consistent scores over time for the student cohort. This level of reliability is consistent with findings for other WBA tools and is generally considered adequate for formative assessment purposes.15,16 However, the most salient finding regarding the CCL's reliability was the significant discrepancy between male (ICC = 0.507) and female (ICC = 0.346) students. Reliability coefficients are highly dependent on the score variance within a sample.14,17 The score variance was significantly lower in the female subgroup, and thus there was a ceiling effect, which mathematically constrained the ICC. Relying on a single ICC value without examining the underlying data can be profoundly misleading.18 Furthermore, we acknowledge that true internal structure evidence, such as dimensionality via factor analysis, was not assessed in this study and remains a critical area for future research.

Relationships to other variables: exploring group differences

Our analysis detected an unexpected but statistically significant difference in the mean scores for male and female students. This finding highlights the instrument's sensitivity to detecting group variations and prompts a critical inquiry into the potential sources of this variance, such as unconscious rater bias, differences in clinical rotation experiences, or other unmeasured confounding variables.19 Recent research has increasingly highlighted the potential for gender bias in clinical grading and narrative evaluations, demonstrating the need to critically examine score disparities.20,21 However, it is important to emphasize that this subgroup analysis was exploratory. Thus, the observed differences should be interpreted with caution and serve primarily as a catalyst for future, targeted investigations rather than being treated as definitive evidence of bias.

Implications for use in a CBME program

The accumulated evidence strongly supports the use of the CCL for formative assessment. Its strong content validity ensures that feedback is aligned with curricular goals, and its moderate reliability indicates that it is sufficiently consistent to monitor learner progress and facilitate meaningful feedback conversations.16 However, the CCL should be used with caution for high-stakes summative decisions, and as part of a broader program of assessment.3,10 The reliability estimate of 0.711 is acceptable for low-stakes use, but falls short of the high standards (typically >0.90) required for making critical decisions about individual learners.22 This aligns with a growing consensus in the literature that regardless of the tool applied, single assessment encounters often lack the reliability for summative entrustment decisions, and that a programmatic approach is required by aggregating multiple data points.4,23

Implications for instrument design

The ceiling effect observed among female students suggests that the discriminatory capacity of the instrument is limited at the higher end of the scale. This clustering of scores indicates a need to refine the scoring anchors. Future iterations of the CCL should expand the behavioral descriptors at the higher end (e.g., scores 8 and 10) to better differentiate between competent and exceptional performance, thereby improving its utility for providing formative feedback and making progression decisions.

Limitations and future directions

This study had some limitations that highlight potential future research. As a single-institution study, our findings require replication in diverse settings to establish generalizability. The unexpected gender difference also necessitates further investigation into potential sources of bias in rater-based assessments.

In addition, the current scoring system combines elements of knowledge, reasoning, and affective traits (such as enthusiasm) within single descriptors. This blending of constructs introduces the risk of halo effects and reduces the interpretability of scores. Future versions of the tool should aim to ensure clearer separation of domains (e.g., evaluating knowledge application separately from professional enthusiasm) and stronger alignment with observable behaviors to enhance construct clarity and usability.

Furthermore, rater-related factors were insufficiently addressed in this study. WBAs are highly susceptible to the idiosyncrasies of raters, including leniency bias, halo effects, and varying interpretations of behavioral anchors.19 Faculty orientation was provided for 30 min but its effectiveness was not evaluated, and there was no assessment of inter-rater reliability. This represents a notable limitation given the established influence of rater cognition and variability in WBA. Thus, future studies should implement structured rater calibration sessions, such as frame-of-reference training, and formally evaluate inter-rater reliability.

The current logbook evaluation form utilizes a comprehensive behaviorally anchored scale of 0–10, but future iterations should consider incorporating a 4-point entrustment scale specifically for the “hospital procedures” and “skill lab procedures” domains. The most critical next step is conducting a formal generalizability study (G-study) to simultaneously examine multiple sources of measurement error.24 Furthermore, future studies should seek to gather convergent validity evidence by correlating CCL scores with other established measures of clinical competence, such as objective structured clinical examination performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we performed a promising initial validation of the CCL, supporting its potential use as a psychometrically sound instrument for the formative assessment of undergraduate medical students. The CCL's strong content validity based on its development in a systematic and expert-driven construction process ensures its alignment with the core tenets of CBME. The moderate to good overall test–retest reliability (although highly dependent on the score distribution and varying by subgroup) supports its use for tracking learner progress and providing structured, competency-based feedback.

Critically, this research also serves as a practical demonstration of modern validity theory, demonstrating that validation is an ongoing process involving the accumulation of evidence, and that psychometric properties such as reliability must be interpreted with nuance and context. The CCL is not a standalone solution for all assessment needs but is a valuable component of a comprehensive, programmatic approach to assessment in CBME.

We recommend the CCL for formative use in undergraduate medical programs that aim to enhance the quality and consistency of their WBA. Future research should focus on multi-institutional studies to determine generalizability, and further explore the tool's fairness and equity across diverse learner populations. The initial development and foundational validation of the CCL address the call for more rigorous, evidence-based assessment tools and provide a clear framework for other institutions to follow in the continuous pursuit of excellence in medical education.
