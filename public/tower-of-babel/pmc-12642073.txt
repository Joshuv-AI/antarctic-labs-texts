Facilitators and challenges of using eHealth for primary health care delivery during disasters: a qualitative study of health care providers’ perceptions and experiences
Authors: Maryam Nikfard, Abbas Ali Dehghani Tafti, Ibrahim Salmani, Mohammad Reza Khajehaminian, Hamid Reza Dehghan, Mahsa Khodayarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe frequency and intensity of disasters and their impacts on global population health are on the rise. Health systems often face disruptions during disasters due to infrastructure damage and the sudden surge in patient numbers. These disruptions are exacerbated by the lack of sufficient capacity, resources, and tools needed to respond effectively to disasters. Primary Health Care (PHC), as the first point of public contact with the health system, plays a key role in providing integrated health services. Also eHealth technologies help ensure the continuity of essential health services and fill existing gaps in health care delivery. This research aims to explore the perceptions and experiences of the health care providers to understand the facilitators and challenges of using eHealth to deliver primary health care in times of disasters and health emergencies.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted through semi-structured interviews with senior managers, experts, and health care providers, selected through purposive sampling. The data were analyzed using thematic content analysis with inductive approach in MAXQDA 2020.ResultsThe facilitators were categorized into six areas: health network infrastructure, the health information system (SIB), health care providers, mobile health capabilities, community participation, and intersectoral collaborations. The associated challenges were identified in six categories: challenges related to the SIB system, technical challenges, health system challenges, health care providers, community challenges, and the inherent aspects of disasters. ConclusionsStrengthening the health information system, improving technical infrastructure, fostering intersectoral collaborations, and increasing community engagement are essential steps. Additionally, empowering HCPs and addressing the inherent challenges of disasters can improve the resilience and responsiveness of health systems to deliver PHC in disasters and health emergencies. This understanding of facilitators and challenges provides a framework for improving PHC delivery through eHealth in future disaster scenarios, ensuring resilient, equitable, continuous, and accessible health services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03089-1.Keywords: Primary health care, EHealth, Disaster, Health emergency, Health care providers, Challenge, Facilitator

Background

The frequency and intensity of disasters, along with their consequences on global public health are escalating. Disasters directly affect health by causing injuries, diseases, fatalities, and impairing the delivery of shelter, clean water, sanitation, hygiene, health care, essential public services, and utilities to those impacted [1]. A significant indirect consequence of disasters is the disruption of health care systems due to infrastructure damage and increased demand for services. This loss of functionality and coordination is exacerbated by the insufficient capacities, capabilities, tools, and resources within health systems to effectively respond [2].

Primary health care (PHC), as the initial point of contact between the public and the health system, is crucial in disaster contexts for maintaining access to essential services and minimizing non-emergency-related morbidity and mortality. PHC is vital in conducting rapid assessments, identifying vulnerable populations, surveillance of the community health, and providing essential health services such as vaccination, chronic disease management, mental health, and maternal and child care [3]. However, disasters often overwhelm these functions, leading to decreased service continuity and heightened risks of long-term physical and mental health for affected populations, especially the vulnerable [1, 4].

Moreover, eHealth plays a vital role in public health emergencies by enabling remote assessment, decision support, triage, and monitoring during the initial critical phase [1]. eHealth technologies have the capacity to maintain essential health services even amid healthcare system disruptions, aiding in early diagnosis and proactive disease detection during biological disasters, reducing the burden of hospitalization, and enhancing community involvement [5]. As the emergency response progresses, the emphasis shifts from acute care to long-term primary care and the ongoing monitoring of the affected population’s physical and mental health. This transition ensures accurate prognosis and clear communication of health risks to patients, caregivers, and relief organizations. eHealth also supports the management of individuals with mild symptoms or psychological distress, offering essential guidance to healthcare professionals and first responders. Although there are logistical and regulatory challenges, integrating eHealth into emergency response systems helps address gaps in healthcare delivery [1].

Global institutions are increasingly calling for action. In 2018, the World Health Assembly resolution on digital health emphasized the significance of digital technologies in advancing Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [6]. A 2021 World Bank report, examining the state of PHC services after the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasized the necessity of incorporating information technology into PHC systems and the urgent need to create new platforms that deliver integrated, responsive, continuous, and community-focused care in the wake of the pandemic [5].

Several studies have examined the role of PHC in disaster preparedness, and adoption of digital technologies in health care [7–9]. Most of the existing literature focuses on technological design, system-level integration, or conceptual modeling. However, a limited number of qualitative studies have explored the primary health care providers’ experience in using eHealth during emergencies [10, 11]. There remains limited understanding of how eHealth is actually experienced, used, and perceived by frontline health care providers during disasters through a qualitative research design. HCPs’ insights are essential to identifying both the operational barriers and enablers that shape effective use of eHealth in PHC settings.

This study tried to address a gap in the literature by exploring the lived experiences of healthcare providers who used eHealth to deliver primary health care during disasters through qualitative research methodology. By focusing on facilitators and challenges at the service delivery level, the research offered practical and context-sensitive insights to extend existing frameworks. Understanding the real-world perspectives on the facilitators and challenges is essential to inform evidence-based policy decisions and to design systems that are technically sound, feasible, acceptable, and sustainable in disaster-affected settings.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design to explore the facilitators and challenges of using eHealth for delivering PHC during disasters and used the criteria in COREQ checklist (consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research) [12].

Setting

The study was conducted at the Department of Health Affairs of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences and Community Health Centers in Yazd province, in central Iran. These settings provided a comprehensive view of eHealth implementation from both administrative and field perspectives. The field settings included community health centers, which offered diverse insights into the practical challenges and opportunities of eHealth use in different contexts. Interviews were conducted at the offices and health centers in person from April 2022 to May 2023 encompassing COVID-19 pandemic response and recovery phases.

Sampling and participants

Purposive sampling was used to select participants who had practical experience and knowledge about primary health care delivery during disasters considering maximum variation sampling [13]. The inclusion criteria for the study consisted of primary health care providers, including health sector senior and mid-level managers and health care workers employed in comprehensive health centers, who had experience in disaster and emergency response, had a minimum of five years of service in the health sector, and had worked with the SIB system, the health information system used in health sector. We tried to conduct interviews with specialists from various PHC services. The sample size was determined during the study and continued until data saturation was reached and all categories were covered.

The first interview was performed with a key informant who introduced other professionals with rich experience in health delivery during disasters in administrative level. The recommended individuals were screened by the research team in terms of their field of work and experience before being invited. Participants were invited in-person and via phone call making an appointment for the interview session (Table 1). Data saturation was achieved after 18 interviews with 16 participants, as no new themes or information emerged, allowing us to clearly define the categories [14]. Interview duration ranged from 18 (for a supplementary interview) to 46 min.

Data collection

Data were collected through key informant interviews using a semi-structured format. We developed an interview guide based on the research questions to explore the facilitators and challenges of using eHealth for primary health care delivery during disasters. The guide served as a flexible framework rather than a rigid questionnaire, allowing for open-ended and in-depth discussions through probing, specific, clarifying, reflective, and narrative questions (Supplementary File 1). After conducting the first interview, the guide was reviewed and refined collaboratively with the research team to ensure clarity and relevance to the study objectives. Interviews were conducted face-to-face individually.

The interviews started with general questions regarding the participants’ experience of providing PHC services in disasters, and gradually shifted to more specific questions about the use of eHealth in providing PHC services, as well as the facilitators and challenges in this regard. All the interviews were conducted in Persian and audio-recorded using the Voice Memos application. The audio recordings were immediately transcribed verbatim in Persian into Microsoft Word 2013. Reflective notes were taken to supplement the transcripts. Translation into English was performed during the article development stage.

Observational data were collected to provide contextual understanding of the settings in which eHealth-supported PHC services were delivered. Observations focused on physical infrastructure, equipment, and software. The purpose was to better understand the workflow and to gain general awareness and recognition of the system. These notes were not analyzed separately but were used to triangulate and enrich interpretation of interview data.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted step by step, following the stages proposed by Graneheim and Lundman [15]. Thematic content analysis with an inductive approach was applied to analyze the interview data using MAXQDA 2020 software. The unit of analysis consisted of each interview transcript as a whole and the researcher’s notes. During the coding process, meaning units, defined as words, sentences, or paragraphs related to the same central meaning were identified and analyzed. These meaning units were then condensed, coded, and grouped into subcategories and categories.

Both the manifest content (transcribed interview texts) and the latent content (non-verbal content) were explored. The transcripts were read multiple times to gain familiarity and deeply engage with the data and to develop a comprehensive understanding of the texts. Then, using the researcher’s notes and deeper analysis of the data, meaning units were identified and condensed while preserving the core meaning. Condensed meaning units were labeled with codes. Codes were generated inductively from the data using in vivo coding (participants’ own words), process coding (capturing actions), and open coding (labeling concepts found in the data without pre-set categories). By comparing the similarities and differences among the codes, they were grouped into subcategories and then into broader categories based on their relationship in terms of content or context [16]. These categories were reviewed and refined multiple times through discussions with research team members to ensure accuracy and relevance. Memos were used to capture analytic decisions, and all steps were documented to allow replication.

Coding and categorizing were conducted iteratively. Initial coding was performed by the PI. A second researcher independently reviewed a subset of transcripts and codes. Discrepancies were discussed with a third team member until consensus was achieved. All final codes and categories were agreed upon unanimously by the research team. Also negative case analyses were addressed through follow-up interviews with the same participant and by discussing the controversial issue with subsequent participants.

Trustworthiness

In this study, the four criteria of credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability, as proposed by Guba and Lincoln, were used to evaluate the trustworthiness of the data.

To establish internal validity or credibility, in addition to data saturation, the triangulation method was employed. Triangulation refers to the use of different sources of information or methods of data collection to confirm the findings and make the results more reliable. In this study, it involved a combination of semi-structured interviews, participant and environmental observations, multiple sessions with the research team, and the analysis of related official documents to collect comprehensive data. Data were gathered from various sources, including administrative and field settings, to encompass a broad range of perspectives. Member checks and peer checks were used throughout data collection and analysis. Additionally, prolonged engagement and persistent observation in the research environment, along with 13 months of data collection and analysis, were other methods employed to enhance trustworthiness.

For respondent validation, after the interviews, the information was returned to some participants for feedback and, if necessary, corrections. Neutrality was maintained regarding the study data and the views about the findings were expressed through memos and bracketing.

To ensure reliability or dependability, the researcher’s notes were used during the interviews, and all interviews were conducted by the same researcher. The review of the materials was carried out at two stages, midway and at the end of the process.

External validity or transferability was ensured through the diversity of participants in terms of their areas of expertise in primary health care, covering roles such as senior and mid-level managers and health care providers.

To ensure confirmability, all stages of the research, particularly the data analysis process, were thoroughly documented.

Results

In all, 16 health care providers were interviewed through 18 sessions. Supplementary interviews were conducted for two key informants following the revision of the interview guide. Ten participants held administrative roles at the University Department of Health Affairs from the divisions of Administration, Adolescent and School Health, Family Health, Disaster Risk Reduction, Health Education and Promotion, Communicable Diseases, Non-Communicable Diseases, and Mental Health. Six participants were based in Community Health Centers, including one center manager and five health care providers. All participants had practical experience providing PHC during COVID-19. Other significant health emergencies and disasters experienced by the participants included earthquakes, floods, and outbreaks of H1N1, Eltor cholera, measles, and influenza. Facilitators

Regarding the experience and perception of the participants, 64 codes were identified as the facilitators and drivers of eHealth adoption to deliver PHC during disasters in the Iranian context. These were defined under six categories: (1) Health network infrastructure, (2) National health information system (SIB, in native language), (3) Health care providers’ (HCPs) capacity, (4) mHealth functionalities, (5) Community engagement, and (6) Intersectoral collaborations, as detailed in Table 2.

Collectively, these categories reflect both structural enablers, such as Iran’s widespread health network and the integration of SIB with national civil registration and operational enablers, including trained health workers, mHealth-based two-way communication, and active community liaison roles.

Participants emphasized the role of Iran’s extensive health network, which ensured service reach into rural and underserved areas, and the adaptability of the national health system in adopting new protocols and care packages during disasters. The SIB system emerged as a pivotal asset, offering comprehensive patient records, vaccination tracking, and the capacity to integrate health education materials for both preventive and emergency purposes.

From the workforce perspective, HCPs’ prior training and experience facilitated the swift adoption of telehealth protocols and guideline updates. In parallel, mHealth tools particularly targeted messaging and interactive virtual groups were credited with enabling timely health warnings, two-way communication, and remote disease monitoring via smartphones. Community engagement was reinforced through the acceptance of mobile applications, especially among younger users, and through community health liaisons who bridged the gap between HCPs and clients using social media channels. Finally, intersectoral collaborations, including the SIB system’s integration with the National Organization for Civil Registration and partnerships with the General Administration of Education, allowed for efficient school health service delivery and streamlined population-level health tracking during emergencies.

Challenges and barriers

In total, 256 codes were identified for the challenges and barriers to implementing eHealth for PHC delivery during disasters under six categories. While many reflect challenges well documented in the eHealth literature such as digital literacy, infrastructure reliability, and interoperability, our study participants provided disaster-specific insights into based on lived experiences. The challenges were categorized as: (1) Existing national health information system (SIB) challenges, (2) Technical considerations, (3) Health system and policy challenges, (4) Health care providers’ challenges, (5) Community-related challenges, and (6) Inherent aspects of disasters (Table 3).

Existing health information system challenges

Participants highlighted several issues with the existing national health information system, known as SIB in native language. Being used by the majority of health networks in Iran, SIB system was launched in 2016 with the aim of digitizing patients/clients’ documents at health centers and organizing paperless delivery and registration of PHC in its all aspects. It records specific primary health care services for infants, children, teenagers, youth, middle-aged adults, elderly, and women, covering services from birth to death.

Participants noted that SIB’s current design, while comprehensive for routine PHC, lacked the agility and disaster-readiness needed for emergency response. Problems included inflexibility in updating forms, inability to integrate with secondary/tertiary care systems, and a one-way communication model that limited patient engagement. For example, unnecessary referrals in chronic disease care (e.g., asthma) were cited as increasing workload and reducing efficiency during disasters.

– According to the participants, SIB system had problems with functionality including lack of flexibility, not being connected to the clinics and higher levels of healthcare delivery, and inappropriate referrals. “There are way too many unnecessary referrals. Take asthma care, for example. Every question gets answered with a ‘No.’ The student has zero risk factors for asthma, but still gets bumped up to higher care. We end up clicking the mouse a bunch more times.” (P13).

Technical challenges

Participants described both infrastructure limitations (e.g., insufficient bandwidth, lack of remote monitoring tools) and data security concerns as significant barriers. While technical readiness affects eHealth adoption, the data show that disasters could worsen the problem in emergency primary healthcare.

– Participants reported lack of monitoring tools at the health centers and with the community to monitor the health status remotely or at the centers. “To provide remote services, you need a strong and high-speed internet connection, especially in a disaster, so tasks can be handled quickly. For delivering NCD care remotely, families should have the necessary equipment, like a blood pressure monitor and a glucose test device. Well, these are crucial. The infrastructure must be set up to make this possible.” (P14).

Providing remote services sometimes encounters problems due to the need for more equipment and better internet infrastructure.” (P01).

Health system and policy challenges

Some challenges, such as the dominance of treatment-oriented priorities and centralized decision-making, are not unique to eHealth. However, the data suggest that such systemic orientations directly slow the uptake and integration of eHealth tools for PHC in emergencies. For example, participants noted that limited intersectoral data sharing undermined the potential for SIB to serve as a unified disaster response platform.

– Lack of intersectoral coordination was recurred in interviews, as the organizations each used their own data base and there was no integrated system to collect and utilize data. “If SIB data was shared with the governorates, especially during the census that happens every five years, it would keep our information updated and would also be beneficial for the Ministry of Interior to have health statistics.” (P02).

– Lack of intersectoral coordination was recurred in interviews, as the organizations each used their own data base and there was no integrated system to collect and utilize data. “If SIB data was shared with the governorates, especially during the census that happens every five years, it would keep our information updated and would also be beneficial for the Ministry of Interior to have health statistics.” (P02).

– The inherent nature of PHC, which traditionally relies on in-person face-to-face interactions, posed significant barriers to remote care. “In cases of natural disasters with high trauma, it’s not acceptable for someone to provide services remotely. The affected individuals would say, “You’re sitting comfortably in your armchairs, trying to offer me services. How can you understand my situation when I don’t have drinking water, food, clothing, or shelter?” (P03).

Burnout, insufficient training for telecare, and inconsistent data entry practices emerged as key issues. While provider fatigue and training needs are common in eHealth studies, participants stressed that these problems increase during disasters when personnel are reassigned (e.g., to vaccination centers), yet still expected to manage remote consultations without adequate preparation.

– According to the participants, HCPs failed to regularly update or complete the clients’ information on SIB system. But then, there are others who don’t bother to enter new clients’ addresses or contact numbers, and sometimes even write the phone number wrong.” (P02).

Discussion

The results of this study highlighted the facilitators and challenges of eHealth implementation for PHC delivery during disasters. The supporting facilitators were categorized as health network infrastructure, national health information system, health care providers, mHealth functionalities, community engagement, and intersectoral collaborations. On the other hand, the challenges and barriers were grouped under categories of challenges related to existing health information system (SIB), technical considerations, health system and policy, health care providers, community, and inherent aspects of disasters.

While many identified facilitators and barriers align with existing eHealth research, these findings add a disaster-specific perspective, showing how existing PHC systems, health information platforms, and community engagement strategies can either support or struggle during disasters.

This study identified a range of facilitators and challenges influencing the implementation of eHealth in PHC delivery during disasters in Iran. The findings contribute to the growing body of evidence on disaster-resilient health systems and underscore the need for integrated, community-centered, and technically robust approaches.

According to Golinelli et al.’s (2020), rapid digital adoption during COVID-19 underlined the potential of emergencies to accelerate innovation but also exposed persistent challenges, including regulatory gaps, data privacy issues, and funding constraints [8].

Facilitators

According to the findings of this study, in the Iranian health system, the widespread geographic distribution of community health centers across urban and rural areas plays a crucial role in maintaining access to health care facilities during disasters and is considered a strength of the health system. That is while geographic distribution and poor coverage in certain areas were leading challenges in other records [17, 18]. The flexibility of the health system in adopting new protocols and the provision of psychosocial support packages for COVID-19 survivors were other enablers, which is in line with WHO’s emphasis on adaptable health systems during disasters [19]. The national health information system in Iran provides a rich source of community demographic and health data, facilitating the implementation of eHealth interventions for various age-groups, as pointed out by Kalkhajeh et al. [20]. The presence of trained and experienced health workers, known as Behvarz in Iran, who could implement new guidelines was a significant advantage, which was also noted by Malekafzali, as the strengths of Iranian health system [21]. This will be a significant factor in the adoption of eHealth and new technologies in service delivery. On the other hand, the mHealth capabilities and ubiquity of smartphones present a potential opportunity to further expand eHealth initiatives and enhance the coverage and quality of health services, as emphasized by WHO [6]. Community engagement, in form of health liaisons, is another positive aspect of the system. Health liaisons facilitate the connection between the health system and target populations, maintaining community-based PHC services, which are of high importance in disasters [7, 19]. Malekafzali also appreciated this capability in the Iranian health system, although he stated that the community was not empowered enough to contribute to decision-making [21].

Intersectoral collaborations between some organizations, such as the connection of the SIB system with the National Organization for Civil Registration, and the health system’s relationship with the General Administration of Education for school health delivery, were acknowledged. Collaboration with other organizations involved directly or indirectly was also mentioned as a factor facilitating telemedicine development by PAHO and WHO [18].

Challenges

Despite these strengths, the study revealed substantial barriers to effective eHealth deployment.From the perspective of frontline implementation, Sharon et al. (2021) reported that Australian PHC nurses valued telehealth for maintaining continuity of care during COVID-19 but faced barriers such as insufficient training, workflow disruptions, and equity concerns for vulnerable populations [10]. Similarly, a qualitative study from Sweden on the use of a digital patient–staff communication tool in primary care found that while digital triage enhanced workflow efficiency and access, issues related to usability, workload distribution, and patient safety influenced its sustained adoption [11].

SIB system

Despite the merits, the interviews revealed several drawbacks with the existing health information system. The complicated design of the SIB system with numerous fields to be completed for the clients, unnecessary referrals, and limited user-friendliness creates problems for using this system as an eHealth platform. One-way flow of information has turned SIB into a large data-keeping source where the output is not shared with the people, and they do not have access to their health profiles. Community data is not regularly updated due to the of lack of effective relationships; updates occur when the client come in personally and answers the provider’s questions, making them passive recipients of services [22]. This limited interaction hinders effective health delivery, particularly during disasters when connections are lost.

Technical challenges

Technical challenges, including insufficient monitoring tools and devices at the health centers and with the community, inadequate infrastructure for eHealth interventions, and concerns about data security during infrastructure failures, were major obstacles. The need for robust internet connections and sufficient equipment was emphasized. Mahdizdeh et al. also argued that technical challenges when using these systems necessitates assessing the technical infrastructure, equipment, and standards before adopting a health information system [23].

Multisectoral and intersectoral collaborations

Lack of integrated intersectoral systems was a notable challenge the health system faced, as the organizations each used their own database, limiting coordinated disaster response. Malekafzali also believed that intersectoral collaborations are not institutionalized and well-organized in Iran [21].

WHO addresses multisectoral and intersectoral collaborations as essential building blocks to be respected during emergencies [19]. Implementing eHealth for PHC during disasters requires collaborations across sectors of Health, Information and Communications Technology, Education, Telecommunications, Red Crescent, and Disaster Management. Parallel data across different sectors leads to data accumulation and less effective use of data in disaster preparedness and risk reduction measures. Regarding multisectoral nature of health, centralized data contributes to better coordination, more efficient resource allocation, and improved disaster response strategies.

In-person nature of PHC

Another issue raised in the interviews was the in-person nature of PHC which produced substantial barriers. An appreciable effort in the Iranian Health System was labeling the services as “non in-person” during the COVID-19 pandemic, although this was rolled back after the pandemic. Despite the limited number of services labeled as such, this helps to redefine the services and identify those capable of being delivered remotely during disasters.

Priority of treatment over prevention, weak implementation of the referral system

Another underlying problem was the weak implementation of the referral system and family physician plan. When the referral system is not systematically implemented, PHC centers are considered unnecessary, and people choose to refer to higher levels of care based on their own diagnosis, leading to work overload on the secondary and tertiary health facilities. This issue is rooted in the prioritization of treatment over prevention, which has been systematically ingrained in the culture, mindset, and practice at all macro, meso, and micro levels of the society. From policymakers to the individuals, there is a preference to invest in treatment rather than preventive health. This was also argued by Behera et al., where “concentrated focus on curative health services rather than preventive and promotive health-care services” was listed as a challenge for implementation of PHC [17]. The footprint of this phenomenon is seen in the lack of appeal of the health centers for the community, which are not equipped with monitoring tools and lack physical space needed for age-specific care. Health centers are not attractive enough to draw people to receive care even at no charge. Overall, it seems that complete implementation of the referral system and family physician plan can be beneficial for organizing the primary health system. The issue of prioritizing treatment over prevention has also been discussed in other sources [5, 17].

Health care providers

Challenges were identified with HCPs at the centers including the family physicians and health workers, such as updating the clients’ records, and workforce shortage especially in times of prolonged disasters like COVID-19. Burnout among HPs was reported due to the extensive time required to record data and fill out numerous forms in the SIB system, which limited their ability for consultation and face-to-face dialogue with clients. HPs felt like data recorders rather than health consultants. It seems that digitization, which was meant to lower workload, has become a source of time consumption for the providers.

During COVID-19, HPs made phone calls to follow up with clients but were not justified and trained on how to provide services remotely as they were in trouble with time constrains and had to summarize their health messages, which was thought to be inefficient. As emphasized by WHO, recent epidemics highlighted the importance of investing in a strong, skilled, and fit-for-purpose health workforce aligned with PHC principles [19]. Workforce readiness in terms of capacity and education/training is critical to the proper functioning of the health system in emergencies [24]. Disaster management principles such as active patient finding, patient tracking, and risk communication can be considered in training courses for HCPs.

In addition, the epidemiological and demographic transition, which has led to an increase in non-communicable diseases and an aging population, has elevated primary health care from simple vaccination, communicable disease control, and basic education to a more technical level. Health care providers now need to offer more specialized consultations on diseases such as cardiovascular failure, diabetes, and lifestyle changes. Therefore, as health care providers require higher levels of training, the public also needs to become familiar with the principles of self-care.

Community

The next category of challenges was related to community. Limited cultural acceptance and trust in digital health solutions and primary health care, low health literacy, and lack of digital and media literacy among the public were critical barriers. As mentioned before, the culture of prioritizing treatment over prevention manifests in a lack of trust in primary health sector. This was an important context-specific cultural barrier identified in this study, as recommended by WHO [19].

No-shows and service withdrawal

No-shows and service withdrawal were frequently reported by the interviewees. On the other hand, people do not trust digital solutions, although this is improving. People are unaware of the age-specific service provided in the public health centers at no charge and prefer to visit the specialists personally, bypassing the referral system. Financial constraints to afford remote monitoring tools and smartphones, and heightened risk perception in disasters due to fear of the unknown also impact eHealth uptake.

People have a high perceived sensitivity, but due to low self-efficacy, lack of trust in the healthcare system, excessive fear, and other factors, they avoid seeking health services when needed and do not engage in health-seeking behaviors. WHO states that development of people-centered PHC requires empowered populations [22]. We need to empower our people in health, digital, and risk reduction literacy to achieve people-centered eHealth use during emergencies and disasters [19].

Inherent aspects of disasters

Finally inherent and inseparable aspects of disasters were grouped as a challenge category. The type of disaster significantly influenced eHealth implementation. As mentioned in the results of this study, disasters whether natural or human-made, acute or prolonged, affects the primary health care required to be delivered, as well as the role of HCPs, infrastructure disruption, and community cooperation. As outlined by the participants, survivors’ psychological distress varied across different disasters impacting service delivery, particularly remote care. Disasters like pandemics with unclear start and end point and consequent waves of the disease outbreak make the health system more exhausted and affected [24].

Normally during disasters, attentions is focused on response and saving the lives of victims, where PHC services are neglected. Recent reports on COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on global health needs show a significant decline in health indicators, especially in underprivileged areas [25]. Essential services were often suspended, and non-COVID care was severely limited, putting immense pressure on secondary and tertiary health centers [26]. De Vera et al., in their scoping review, stated that during the pandemic, access to primary care centers or primary care physicians was particularly important, as this level serves as the first point of contact for patients with the health care system. They acknowledged that appropriate primary care can help reduce visits to secondary or tertiary hospitals, which are providing care for COVID-19 patients [27].

Another prevalent phenomenon in disasters is infodemics, where an overload of information can lead to public confusion, anxiety, and mismanagement. Risk communication principles can greatly mitigate infodemics. Delayed consequence of non-communicable diseases in disasters can also cause death due to inadequate care which underscores the importance of maintaining continuity of care and monitoring for NCD patients even during disasters and emergencies. And finally, chaotic nature of disasters with infrastructure disruptions is a considerable challenge and might interfere providing remote services, hence the need for preplanned scenarios for different types of disasters and the services that could be provided accordingly. WHO also mentions that the emergencies disrupting critical infrastructure and elements pose significant challenges to health service provision during emergencies [28].

These findings illustrate the multifaceted nature of the challenges associated with eHealth implementation in PHC during disasters. Addressing these barriers requires a comprehensive approach that considers technical, systemic, HCPs, and community-related factors, taking into account the inherent characteristics of disasters to improve the resilience and effectiveness of PHC services in emergency settings.

Limitations

This study faced certain limitations and shortcomings. Since primary health care is provided nationally through the health network in Iran, and the SIB system is used as a national information system in most medical universities, the interviews were conducted only in the province of Yazd. This presents a limitation for generalizing the findings. It should be noted, however, that Yazd province, with 100% coverage of primary health care, is one of the leading provinces in terms of facilitating access to primary health care. Additionally, developmental indicators show that Yazd ranks among the top provinces in terms of epidemiological transition. Therefore, the results obtained from this study might be applicable to the settings with similar levels of development as Yazd, and implementable for those gradually reaching the same level.

Given that the aim of the qualitative study was to explore the experiences of primary health care providers regarding the use of eHealth services in disasters, participants were selected from among senior and mid-level managers, health care providers, and disaster management specialists in health sector. No interviews were conducted with specialists in the field of information technology. Assessing technical aspects of the system was beyond the scope of this study and can be addressed in future research.

Conclusions

The results of this study highlighted both the facilitators and challenges of eHealth implementation for PHC delivery during disasters. Key facilitators included robust health network infrastructure, a national health information system, skilled HCPs, mHealth capabilities, community engagement, and intersectoral collaborations. However, significant challenges were identified, such as the limitations of the existing health information system (SIB), technical considerations, systemic and policy issues, HCPs constraints, community-related barriers, and the inherent complexities of disasters.

Higher levels of education have increased availability of information, and ubiquity of smartphones and digital technologies are changing community expectations of health care delivery. In this regard, digital health integrated with primary health care can increase the capacity of communities to prevent disasters and respond effectively. Strengthening the health information system, improving technical infrastructure, fostering intersectoral collaborations, and increasing community engagement are essential steps. Additionally, empowering HCPs and addressing the inherent challenges of disasters can improve the resilience and responsiveness of health systems to deliver PHC in disasters and health emergencies. This understanding of facilitators and challenges provides a framework for improving PHC delivery through eHealth in future disaster scenarios, ensuring resilient, equitable, continuous, and accessible health services. These recommendations are in line with PHC pillars of empowered people and communities; multisectoral policy and action for health; and strong and integrated health services.
