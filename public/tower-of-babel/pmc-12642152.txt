Anti- inflammatory effects of Andrographis paniculata (Burm.f.) Wall. Ex Nees leaf in LPS-Induced RAW264.7 macrophages
Authors: Young-Ah Jang, Ue-Kyoung Hwang, Yong-Jin Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The inflammatory response is a protective biological response to harmful stimuli involving immune cells and inflammatory mediators. However, chronic inflammation can trigger a variety of inflammatory diseases. In this study, we screened natural products for anti-inflammatory materials. Andrographis paniculata (Burm.f.) Wall. ex Nees (A. paniculata) is used to treat inflammatory diseases in Asia. However, the mechanism of its anti-inflammatory effect is unclear. We analyzed the anti-inflammatory effect of A. paniculata boiling water extract (APW) and A. paniculata 70% EtOH extract (AP70E) and the underlying mechanisms of action. Interestingly, 100 µg/mL AP70E suppressed lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and nitric oxide (NO) production to a significantly greater degree than APW. In addition, 100 µg/mL AP70E significantly inhibited the LPS-induced production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 and suppressed the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and Janus kinase (JAK)/signaling transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling axes. Moreover, it significantly reduced the activity of the JAK/STAT axis independently of its reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine production. In conclusion, AP70E has potential as an anti-inflammatory agent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05143-y.

Background

The immune response is a mechanism of defense against pathogens or antigens [1]. Immune cells recognize invading pathogens or antigens and in response recruit a variety of immune-cell types [2]. These immune cells release signaling molecules, thus inducing inflammation [3]. Because inflammation is an essential defense response, an appropriate inflammatory response is essential for maintaining good health. However, uncontrolled immune responses and chronic inflammatory responses can trigger inflammatory diseases, including dermatitis, atopy, and degenerative arthritis [4, 5].

Macrophages, the principal cellular mediators of innate immunity, not only phagocytose bacteria, foreign cells, and damaged or dead cells but also secrete substances that attract other immune cells to the site of infection [6]. Upon stimulation by LPS, Toll-like receptor 4 on macrophages activates the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB)/inhibitor of NF-κB-α (IκBα), MAPK, and JAK/STAT signaling axes [7, 8]. Transcription factors activated by LPS trigger the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and that of inflammation-related genes such as iNOS and COX-2, resulting in the production of NO and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [9, 10]. Because an excessive inflammatory reaction is linked to the onset of inflammatory diseases, it is important to investigate the mechanism of anti-inflammatory activity and to discover compounds with therapeutic potential for inflammatory diseases.

Ancient books such as the Chinese Academy of Chinese Medicine Library and Donguibogam record the use of the leaves, stems, and roots of plants for hundreds of years in Asia to treat or alleviate inflammatory diseases [11, 12]. The therapeutic effects of various plants have been demonstrated in in vitro and in vivo models, but the mechanisms of action of many plants are still unclear [13–15]. Therefore, we screened natural substances with therapeutic potential for inflammatory diseases and evaluated their detailed mechanisms of action.

A. paniculata is a medicinal plant traditionally used in Southern and Southeastern Asia countries, including India, Sri Lanka, and Thailand [16, 17]. Its leaves and roots are used as a botanical herb to treat inflammatory diseases; it was used to help treat coronavirus disease 2019 in Thailand [18]. It contains a variety of bioactive compounds, including andrographolide (which has the greatest bioactivities and abundance), 4-deoxy-11,12 didehydroandrographolide, andrographosterin, and stigmasterol, which have been used for multiple pharmacological purposes [18–20]. A. paniculata is used for various inflammatory diseases, but the detailed mechanism of action for the inflammatory effects is still unknown. Therefore, we analyzed the mechanism of the anti-inflammatory effects of A. paniculata using LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages.

To evaluate the mechanism of its anti-inflammatory effect, we extracted A. paniculata leaves with 70% ethanol and boiling water. The protein and mRNA levels of iNOS and COX-2 and NO production were analyzed in both extracts. In addition, we evaluated the effect of the two extracts on the NF-κB/IκBα, MAPK, and JAK/STAT signaling axes and the production of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α).

Methods

The horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-tagged anti-rabbit antibody (ADI-SAB-300, Enzo Life Science, Farmingdale, NY, USA), HRP-tagged anti-mouse antibody (ADI-SAB-100, Enzo Life Science).

Reagents

The reagents used in this study are as follows: 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-Yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reagent (M1415, Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands), Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, L3012, Sigma- Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), Griess reagent (G4410, Sigma-Aldrich), Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye reagent (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA),

Extraction process of A. Paniculata

The plant name of Andrographis Paniculata (Burm.f.) Wall. ex Nees (A. paniculata) was checked on the Plants of the World Online website (https://powo.science.kew.org/). The plant material of A. paniculata was purchased from Herbmaul Co., Ltd. (Cheongju, Chungcheongbuk-do, Republic of Korea). A. paniculata boiling water extract (APW) was extracted in 99℃ deionized water (200 g of A. paniculata leaf powder:1800 ml of deionized water) twice for 3 h, and A. Paniculata 70% EtOH extracts (AP70E) were extracted with 70% EtOH (200 g of A. paniculata leaf powder:1800 ml of 70% EtOH) twice for 24 h at room temperature (22–24 ℃). Each of the two supernatants was filtered using a paper filter (Whatman, Tokyo, Japan), concentrated using a vacuum rotary evaporator (EYELA, Tokyo, Japan), and freeze-dried using a freeze dryer (ilShinbiobase, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) for 96 h. A total of 15.493 g (7.746%) of APE extract and 16.713 g (8.356%) of APW extract were obtained.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) fingerprint analysis

A. Paniculata extracts were identified using an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (Agilent Technologies, Inc, Santa Clara, CA, USA; 4.6 × 150 nm, 5 µM particle size). 0.2% Formic acid in deionized water was used as mobile phase A, 0.1% Formic acid in acetonitrile was used as mobile phase B (elution conditions: 0–5 min, 5% B; 5–15 min, 25% B; 15–25 min, 50% B; 25–55 min, 100% B; flow rate, 1.0 ml/min; injection volume, 20 µl; wavelength, 254 nm; column temperature, 25 ℃). Before injection, the standard and sample were dissolved in 50% MeOH and filtered through an 0.45 µM PVDF membrane filter. All solvents and mobile phases were of HPLC grade.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained using DMEM (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany) containing 10% FBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Capricorn Scientific GmbH). Cells were incubated in humidified incubator (Vision Science Co., Ltd., Daegu, Republic of Korea) containing 5% CO2 at 37 ℃.

Cell viability assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of APW or AP70E for 24 h. After treatment, cells were treated 1 mg/ml MTT reagent for 2 h. Purple formazan crystals were solubilized with DMSO and measured at 570 nm using an Infinite 200 pro plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Nitric oxide (NO) assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and pre-treated with various concentrations of APW or AP70E for 2 h. After treatment, 200 ng/ml LPS was treated for 16 h. To measure NO production, 100 µl of the supernatant was mixed with 100 µl of 1× Griess reagent and incubated for 15 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an Infinite 200 pro plate reader.

Collection of conditioned medium (CM)

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with or without 200 ng/ml LPS for 4 h. After treatment, the medium was washed out using PBS, replaced with a fresh medium, and further incubated for 4 h. After incubation, control-conditioned media (CON-CM) and LPS-conditioned media (LPS-CM) was collected and treated with the cells. The heat-inactivated CM was prepared by boiling LPS-CM at 100 ℃ for 20 min to denature various secretory molecules.

Protein analysis with western blot

To analyze protein expression, cells were lysed using 1% Triton-X lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice. After incubation for 20 min, lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 RPM (15,700 RCF) for 20 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. The protein contents of the total cell lysates were measured using Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye reagent (Bio-Rad). The same amounts of protein were loaded on SDS-polyacrylamide gels, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA). The transfered membranes were blocked in 5% skim-milk (LPS Solution, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies (1:500–2,000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, Membranes were washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000–10,000 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature, and then visualized using the ECL chemiluminescence kit (Biomax, Seoul, Republic of Korea).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

To analyze mRNA expression, total RNA was isolated using the RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan), and cDNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace™ qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instruction (https://www.toyobo-global.com/). qPCR was performed using the BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (Applied Biological Materials, Richmond, Canada), and fluorescent signal was detected by CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). The sequence of primers used in this study are as follows: iNOS forward, 5`-CAGCACAGGAAATGTTTCAGC-3`, iNOS reverse, 5`-TAGCCAGCGTACCGGATGA-3`, COX-2 forward, 5`-TTTGGTCTGGTGCCTGGTC-3`, COX-2 reverse, 5`-CTGCTGGTTTGGAATAGTTGCTC-3`, TNF-α forward, 5`-TATGGCTCAGGGTCCAACTC-3`, TNF-α reverse, 5`-CTCCCTTTGCAGAACTCAGG-3`, IL-1β forward, 5`-TTGACGGACCCCAAAAGATG-3`, IL-1β reverse, 5`-AGAAGGTGCTCATGTCCTCA-3`, IL-6 forward, 5`-GGTGACAACCACGGCCTTCCC-3`, IL-6 reverse, 5`-AAGCCTCCGACTTGTGAAGTGGT-3`, GAPDH forward, 5`-GCAAATTCAACGGCACAG-3`, GAPDH reverse, 5`-CACCAGTAGACTCCACGAC-3`.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and pre-treated with various concentrations of APW or AP70E for 4 h. After treatment, 200 ng/ml LPS was treated for 16 h. The cell cultured media were harvested and cytokine contents of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α were measured using the EliKine™ Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (KTE7005, Abbkine, Wuhan, China), EliKine™ Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (KTE7009, Abbkine) and EliKine™ Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (KTE7015, Abbkine) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (https://www.abbkine.com/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2019 software (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) or GraphPad Prism v.8 software (Graphpad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. All significance was calculated by unpaired Student’s t-test and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Extraction method, HPLC, and cytotoxicity of A. Paniculata extracts

To demonstrate the anti-inflammatory efficacy of A. paniculata extracts according to difference of extraction methods, we extracted A. paniculata leaves using 99℃ deionized water and 70% EtOH, followed by filtration, concentration, and freeze-drying. The yield of A70E was 7.746% and that of APW was 8.356% (Fig. 1A, B). HPLC analysis, the content of andrographolide (a marker of A. paniculate) was higher in AP70E than in APW (Fig. 1C, D). We analyzed the anti-inflammatory effects of the two A. paniculata extracts using LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages. AP70E and APW at ≥ 200 and 400 µg/mL, respectively, showed significant toxicity in RAW264.7 cells. Prior to the study, the cell cytotoxicity of AP70E and APW was confirmed in RAW264.7 cells. AP70E showed significant cell toxicity at 200 µg/mL, but APW showed no toxicity until 400 µg/mL (Fig. 1E). AP70E and APW were used at 50 and 100 µg/mL in subsequent experiments. AP70E reduced LPS-induced cell death modestlyat 50 µg/mL and significantly at 100 µg/mL, and APW at 100 µg/mL modestlyreduced cell death (Fig. 1F). Therefore, AP70E is more cytotoxic than APW but significantly alleviated the LPS-induced death of RAW264.7 cells.

AP70E has a greater inhibitory effect on LPS-induced inflammation than APW

RAW264.7 macrophages elongate and expand when activated by LPS, effects reversed by treatment with anti-inflammatory agents. We evaluated the effects of AP70E and APW on LPS-induced morphological changes. AP70E at 100 µg/mL significantly alleviated LPS-induced macrophage activation but APW did not (Fig. 2A). In addition, AP70E at 50 and 100 µg/mL significantly prevented LPS-mediated NO production in RAW264.7 cells, and APW at 100 µg/mL only modestly (Fig. 2B). AP70E at 100 µg/mL significantly reversed the LPS-induced increases in the iNOS and COX-2 protein and mRNA levels, whereas APW at 100 µg/mL modestlyreversed the increase in iNOS but not COX-2 (Fig. 2C–D). These results suggest that AP70E exhibits significantly stronger inhibitory effect on LPS-induced inflammatory responses compared to APW.

AP70E inhibits LPS-induced production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6

Invasion by pathogens triggers the production of inflammatory cytokines; however, excessive increases in the levels of inflammatory cytokines can trigger inflammatory diseases and/or adverse reactions. Therefore, there is a need for natural substances that suppress the production of inflammatory cytokines. AP70E significantly decreased the mRNA and protein levels of IL-1β (Fig. 3A, B) and IL-6 (Fig. 3C, D) in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells in a dose-dependent manner; however, the mRNA and protein levels of TNF-α were unaffected (Fig. 3E, F). These results suggest that AP70E prevents LPS-induced inflammation by inhibiting the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6.

AP70E inhibits LPS-mediated activation of the MAPK and JAK/STAT signaling axes

To investigate the mechanism underlying the anti-inflammatory activity of AP70E, we investigated its effect on the JAK/STAT, MAPK, and IκBα/NF-κB inflammatory signaling axes in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells. Interleukins such as IL-6 activate the JAK/STAT axis, and TNF-α activates the IκBα/NF-κB axis. Because AP70E significantly inhibited LPS-induced IL-6 and IL-1β production, we evaluated its effect on the JAK/STAT axis. AP70E inhibited the LPS-induced phosphorylation of JAK1, 2 and STAT1, 3 (Fig. 4A). In addition, AP70E inhibited the LPS-induced phosphorylation of ERK, p38, and JNK (Fig. 4B). Because AP70E did not reverse the LPS-induced increase in TNF-α production, the phosphorylation of NF-κB was not inhibited and that of IκBα was modestlyinhibited (Fig. 4C). Therefore, AP70E prevents LPS-induced inflammation by inhibiting the JAK/STAT and MAPK signaling axes.

The AP70E-mediated inhibition of JAK/STAT axis activation is not mediated by its effect on the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines

The activation of NF-κB and MAPK is induced by the interaction between LPS and TLR4 on macrophages, and the JAK/STAT axis is activated by the IL-6R and gp130 produced in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines. We hypothesized that AP70E inhibits LPS-induced activation of the JAK/STAT axis by suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6) or by some other mechanism. To evaluate whether AP70E directly inhibits activation of the JAK/STAT axis, we harvested LPS-CM containing inflammatory cytokines from LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells; heat-inactivated LPS-CM was used as the negative control (Fig. 5A). The JAK/STAT axis was not activated by fresh medium, CON-CM, and heat-inactivated LPS-CM but was markedly activated by LPS-CM. Interestingly, AP70E significantly inhibited the LPS-CM–mediated phosphorylation of the JAK/STAT axis. (Fig. 5B, C). These results suggest that AP70E inhibits activation of the JAK/STAT axis independently of its effect on the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Discussion

Inflammation protects against pathogens and antigens but an abnormal or persistent immune response can trigger chronic inflammatory diseases [1, 3]. Chronic inflammatory diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, and the World Health Organization (WHO) considers them to be the greatest threat to human health [21]. The prevalences of diseases associated with chronic inflammation are expected to increase steadily over the next 30 years in the United States [22]. Modern life can involve irregular mealtimes, obesity, stress, and insomnia, factors that can trigger inflammatory responses [23–25]. An excessive immune response can cause inflammatory diseases such as sprains, menstrual cramps, rheumatoid arthritis, migraines, and headaches, as well as other conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, inflammatory kidney disease, and inflammatory bowel disease [21]. Because the prevalences of inflammatory diseases are increasing worldwide, the development of therapeutic agents for inflammation is important.

Macrophages engulf bacteria, foreign cells, and damaged or dead cells and secrete cytokines and chemokines that attract white blood cells to the site of infection, thus initiating an acquired immune response [6, 26]. However, excessive immune responses or overly high levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines can cause adverse effects by over-activating downstream pathways such as the JAK/STAT and MAPK axes. Numerous drugs for the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases are under development, and several drugs that target JAK/STAT activation, such as Tofacitinib, Peficitinib, Baricitinib, and Itacitinib, have received approval from the FDA or are undergoing clinical trials for psoriatic arthritis, atopic dermatitis, and inflammatory bowel diseases [27]. Recent studies reported that Trametinib, an inhibitor of MEK kinase, suppresses LPS-induced production of TNF-α, and SCH772984, an inhibitor of ERK, inhibits LPS-induced production of TNF-α, IL-6, LCN2, and CCL2 [28, 29]. Furthermore, Hispidin, a polyphenolic compound, inhibits the LPS-mediated activation of the JAK/STAT and MAPK axes, suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy [30]. Therefore, agents that inhibit the JAK/STAT and MAPK axes have anti-inflammatory potential. In this study, we demonstrated that AP70E alleviated the inflammatory response by inhibiting LPS-induced activation of the JAK/STAT and MAPK axes. Therefore, AP70E has therapeutic and prophylactic potential for diseases related to inflammation.

Ayurveda is an ancient Indian system of alternative medicine that continues to be widely practiced in India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka [31]. A. paniculata is an Ayurvedic medicinal plant belonging to the family Acanthaceae that is traditionally used to treat a variety of inflammatory or infectious diseases, including skin infections, malaria, herpes, helminth infections, and liver infections [16]. In addition, it has been used to treat diabetes, alopecia, dyspepsia, snakebites, jaundice, and cough [17]. Andrographolide is a member of the diterpenoid family present in the leaves and stems of A. paniculata. Andrographolide inhibits several inflammatory signaling axes, and reportedly suppresses the growth of breast cancer, ovarian cancer, HCC, and GBM [32, 33]. The STAT and MAPK signaling axes promote tumor migration, invasion, proliferation, and stemness, possibly explaining the antitumor activity of the A. paniculata extracts [34–36].

Conclusions

AP70E inhibited LPS-induced iNOS and COX-2 expression and NO production to a greater degree than APW. AP70E significantly alleviated the LPS-induced activation of the JAK/STAT and MAPK axes and suppressed the production of IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 6). In addition, AP70E attenuated LPS-induced JAK/STAT activation in a manner not involving its effect on the production of pro-inflammatory factors (Fig. 6). Our findings suggest that AP70EL inhibits excessive inflammatory responses by suppressing the JAK/STAT and MAPK signaling axes.
